## Executive Summary

This report, submitted to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University), presents an otolith-based age estimation study of the critically endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) from [[Lake Mohave]], a mainstream reservoir on the lower Colorado River formed by [[Davis Dam]]. Because decades of hatchery repatriation stocking (beginning 1981) have made it nearly impossible to visually distinguish wild from hatchery-origin adults, the study used sacrificed specimens (n=17) to extract and read otoliths, aiming to estimate individual age and back-calculate year class. Fish assignable to an **1980 or 1981 year class** (pre-dating the first hatchery stocking) could be confidently identified as **wild, naturally produced individuals**. Results indicate that wild recruitment has persisted in Lake Mohave, with an estimated 10% to potentially two-thirds of examined fish being of natural origin, underscoring the population's continued (if limited) capacity for natural reproduction despite massive hatchery augmentation efforts.

## Background & Description

- **Total Length (TL):** Overall mean = 48.4 cm (range 44.5–54.5 cm)
  - Males: 47.7 ± 1.8 cm SD (n=9, range 45.3–51.0 cm)
  - Females: 49.1 ± 3.8 cm SD (n=8, range 44.5–54.5 cm)
- **Sex ratio of sample:** 9 males, 8 females (total n=17)
- **Age range (otolith-derived):** 7–13 years across all fish
- **No significant relationship between TL and age** was found — plotting TL (dependent variable) against age produced no graphable curve, for combined sexes or either sex alone. This decoupling is hypothesized to result from differential growth rates between protected/fed hatchery fish and wild fish of equivalent age.
- **Otolith structure:** Presumed annular (yearly) growth rings were used as the aging criterion, identified via polishing (1500 grit silicon carbide) and microscopic examination (40–450X).
- Size overlap was extensive between the aged sample and historically documented wild adults captured in 1979–1981 (females avg. 51.8 ± 2.8 cm TL, n=6; males 45.4–48.0 cm TL, n=5), demonstrating that **body size alone cannot discriminate wild vs. hatchery-origin adults**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic range:** Formerly common-to-abundant throughout mainstream channels and major tributaries of the [[Colorado River]] system, from Mexico north into Wyoming.
- **Current range:** Restricted to sporadic, low-abundance collections in the upper Colorado River basin (e.g., Cataract Canyon, Green River) and to [[Lake Mohave]] — the only site where the species can still be reliably captured, and even there only infrequently, restricted to spring–early summer.
- **Population status:** Estimated in the hundreds to low thousands; precise abundance estimation not possible due to low encounter rates.
- **Threats:** Major water development (impoundment, mainstem alteration) throughout the 20th century is cited as the primary driver of range collapse; the species remains federally listed as endangered despite decades of conservation investment (Recovery Plan 1990; upper basin Recovery Implementation Plan).
- **Reproductive ecology / recruitment:** Evidence from this study supports continued, if limited, in-reservoir natural reproduction — a critical ecological finding given the species' dependence on hatchery augmentation since 1981.
- **Hatchery interaction:** Extensive supplementation program from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (NM) and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (AZ) has released 202,622 fish into Lake Mohave (1981–1996), using various life stages (larvae, juveniles, adults) and marking methods (oxytetracycline, PIT tags, fin clips, coded wire tags, sonic tags), described fully in Appendix A of the source report.

## Key Findings

- **Otolith aging is currently the only practicable method** for estimating age of *Gila elegans*, but it is lethal, damages the specimen (often precluding museum accession), and has limited validation (only one known-age fish available: true age 11 years, estimated by otolith reading as 9 years — indicating a conservative bias in estimates).
- **Three fish (IDs 7, 8A/8B, 11)** were confidently assigned to 1980 or 1981 year classes, confirming they are **wild, naturally produced** bonytail — direct evidence of persistent natural recruitment in Lake Mohave post-repatriation.
- Up to **8 additional fish** assigned to 1982–1983 year classes could plausibly belong to 1981 or earlier, given inter-reader disagreement in specific age estimates (though ranges among readers generally agreed).
- Overall, examined fish year classes spanned **1980–1988**, based on combined mean age estimates and known death dates (1992, 1993, 1995).
- **Discrimination reliability decreases over time**: As more years elapse post-stocking and additional stocking cohorts accumulate, distinguishing wild from hatchery-origin individuals via otolith-derived year class becomes increasingly difficult.
- Age determination is more reliable for identifying **relative "old age"** (e.g., >10 years) than for pinpointing an exact age, per author assessment.
- **Recommendations:**
  1. Maintain accurate, centralized, and complete individual fish records (capture, transfer, holding, release, death) to support future age/origin analyses.
  2. Explore **non-lethal genetic methodologies** to determine parentage and origin (wild vs. hatchery) as an alternative or complement to otolith extraction.
  3. Validate the otolith-aging technique using a larger series of **known-age hatchery fish** spanning multiple year classes.
  4. Apply otolith aging opportunistically to **incidental mortalities** from field collections rather than through intentional sacrifice.
- Confirmatory identification of hatchery-origin fish is only possible via **physical tag recovery** (e.g., PIT tags, oxytetracycline marks), not otolith analysis alone.
