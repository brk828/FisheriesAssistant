# The Use of Scales of the Brown Trout (Salmo trutta) for Back-Calculation of Growth

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #freshwater-biology #fish-scales #growth-back-calculation

## Executive Summary

This paper by Charlotte Kipling investigates the relationship between scale length and body length in [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) to test the fundamental assumption underlying [[Back-Calculation of Growth]]: that scale growth and fish body growth are directly proportional (isometric). Using samples from a wide variety of freshwater environments (lakes, hatcheries, mountain streams, limestone streams, and moorland tarns), the study demonstrates that growth is frequently **allometric** rather than isometric, particularly in fish under 10 cm. The degree of allometry appears correlated with growth rate, with faster-growing populations (e.g., hatchery fish) approaching isometry, while slower-growing populations (e.g., moorland becks) show strongly allometric relationships. The paper quantifies the errors introduced by wrongly assuming direct proportion in back-calculations and provides correction guidance for future studies, particularly for estimating "Length at Age 1."

## Background & Description

- **Scale sampling location**: Scales taken from below the dorsal fin, near the lateral line (standard laboratory protocol); Haweswater sample scales taken from the "shoulder" region (a less well-defined area).
- **Scale measurement**: "Scale length" defined as the distance from the scale center (focus) to the anterior edge — the region used for back-calculation.
- **Preparation**: Scales cleaned in 5% sodium hydroxide solution, mounted between glass slides (typically 3–4 scales per fish).
- **Magnification**: Standardized to ×73 using [[Gilson Scale-Reading Apparatus]]; measurements taken to the nearest millimetre (fish fork length also to nearest mm, except Haweswater fish measured to nearest cm).
- **Intra-individual scale variation** ("within fish"):
  - 52 scales from one 39.8 cm Windermere trout: variance ≈ 0.001 (log scale), SD ≈ 0.03 (normally distributed).
  - Confirmed via 10 scales from each of 5 Windermere trout (SD = 0.030) and 5 scales from each of 8 hatchery trout (SD = 0.028).
- **Inter-individual scale variation** ("between fish"): Significantly greater than within-fish variation; total variance ≈ 0.003 for most wild samples, ≈ 0.002 for hatchery fish (suggesting greater uniformity in hatchery conditions), and ≈ 0.005 for Haweswater (attributed to imprecise scale-sampling location and coarser length measurement).
- **Sexual dimorphism**: No significant difference found between fish length/scale length regression slopes for males vs. females in hatchery and Windermere samples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitats sampled**: Windermere (lake, two independent samples), a hatchery, mountain streams (Raise Beck, Walker Beck, Kingswell Beck), a limestone stream, moorland tarns (Three Dubs Tarn, Wiseen Tarn and its inflow), and Haweswater.
- **Life-history staging relative to scale development**:
  1. **No scales** (fish length ≈ 0.3 cm)
  2. **Scales appearing and growing rapidly** ("catching up" to cover body; fish length ≈ 3–10 cm)
  3. **Scales growing proportionally with the fish** (fish length > 10 cm)
- **Growth rate correlation**: The body length/scale length relationship (regression coefficient byx) appears linked to population growth rate — slow-growing environments (e.g., [[Raise Beck]]) show the most allometric relationships (lowest byx), while fast-growing environments (e.g., the hatchery) approach isometry (byx near 1).
- **Migratory considerations**: Notes potential sampling bias in migratory populations, where larger fish may migrate from stream to lake earlier than smaller fish (per [[Ball and Jones 1962]]), affecting unbiased estimation of age-group mean lengths.
- **Sampling caveat**: Electric shocker sampling may under-represent very small fish unless repeated shocking is performed (per unpublished LE CREN data).

## Key Findings

1. **Non-linear raw relationship**: Plots of fish length vs. scale length on arithmetic axes were curvilinear with increasing scatter at larger sizes; log-log transformation produced a linear relationship (except for smallest fish), consistent with the power function Y = AXᵇ (i.e., log Y = log A + b log X).

2. **Allometry is common, not exceptional**: Of 12 samples analyzed (Table 1), regression coefficients (byx) ranged from **0.53 to 1.05**. Only **2 of 12 samples** ([[Kingswell Beck]] >10 cm and [[Hatchery I]] >10 cm) had byx statistically indistinguishable from 1 (isometric growth), justifying direct-proportion back-calculation. All others showed significant allometry.

3. **Size-dependent relationship**: Fish **under ~10 cm** consistently showed lower byx (0.53–0.64) than fish over 10 cm, indicating disproportionately fast-growing scales in juveniles ("catching up" growth). This 10 cm threshold was chosen as a practical (not necessarily biological) dividing line.

4. **Three homogeneous statistical groups** identified among the 12 samples:
   - Group 1 (samples 1–4, fish <10 cm): byx 0.53–0.64
   - Group 2 (samples 5–8): byx 0.70–0.84 (slower-growing waters, e.g., Raise Beck, Haweswater, Three Dubs Tarn, Wiseen Tarn)
   - Group 3 (samples 9–12): byx 0.90–1.05 (faster-growing waters, e.g., Windermere I & II, Kingswell Beck >10 cm, Hatchery I)
   - Groups differ very significantly from each other but are internally homogeneous — this permits use of a representative equation from a similar water body when site-specific data are unavailable.

5. **Growth rate correlation**: Ranking of samples by byx broadly matches independently known ranking of growth rates — [[Raise Beck]] (slowest growth, lowest byx) to hatchery fish (fastest growth, highest byx). This relationship held even when comparing fast vs. slow growers *within* the same water body (Windermere), though sample sizes were too small to confirm conclusively.

6. **Quantified back-calculation errors** (Table 2/3): Using direct proportion instead of correcting for allometry:
   - Errors were **always negative** (calculated lengths underestimate true lengths) when using direct proportion.
   - Maximum absolute error in worked examples: **2.4 cm**; maximum percentage error: **43%**.
   - The [[Rounsefell and Everhart Formula]] (correcting only for length at scale formation) reduced errors but introduced both positive and negative deviations, complicating comparative interpretation.

7. **Practical case study**: A sample of 67 one-year-old [[Kingswell Beck]] trout (true mean length 7.1 cm) would, if back-calculated by direct proportion from a future length of 25 cm, yield a false mean of 5.5 cm — an error of 1.6 cm (23%), with an even larger implied error in body weight (fish of 5.5 cm weigh less than half those of 7.1 cm).

8. **Effect on growth-rate comparisons**: Incorrect assumption of isometry can distort comparisons of growth between waters or between size classes within the same water. Example: comparing Three Dubs Tarn vs. Kingswell Beck trout at true age-2 lengths of 15 cm and 20 cm (true difference 5 cm) — back-calculation by direct proportion from 25 cm fish yielded a false difference of 7.1 cm.

9. **General error magnitude**: Errors from wrongly assuming isometry rarely exceeded **2.5 cm**; differences larger than this in back-calculated comparisons can generally be considered real/biologically meaningful.

10. **Practical recommendation**: Despite these caveats, [[Back-Calculation of Growth]] remains a valuable technique. Site-specific correction curves/equations (or equations from statistically homogeneous comparable groups) can be derived once per water body and applied efficiently using simple correction apparatus (cf. [[Le Cren 1947]]; [[Frost and Kipling 1959]]).
