# Utah Lake Ecosystem Monitoring to Support June Sucker Conservation Efforts

#ichthyology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #ecosystem-restoration #shallow-lakes

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[June Sucker]] (*Chasmistes liorus*) is a threatened endemic catostomid in [[Utah Lake]], challenged by degraded ecosystem conditions. The species relies on zooplankton and macroinvertebrates as primary prey (May et al. 2021). Spawning abundance has increased in recent years, likely due to improved stocking efficiency, though natural recruitment of young-of-year remains undetected in annual sampling. [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), an invasive benthivore introduced in the late 1800s, has been the target of mechanical removal since 2009. Carp population biomass was substantially reduced by 2016 and remained low through 2019 (approaching the 25% reduction target), but increased in recent years due to compensatory recruitment during high lake levels. Carp age structure is estimated using dorsal spines; population biomass estimates derive from an age-structured integrated population model (Walsworth et al. 2023). Other prominent species include [[White Bass]] (*Morone chrysops*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), and [[Walleye]] (*Sander vitreus*). Fecundity, growth parameters, and maturity schedules are not reported for June Sucker in this document.

**Habitat Associations**: Utah Lake is a shallow (mean depth ~3 m at full pool), hypereutrophic system in an urbanized mountain valley. Historically, the lake was clearer and heavily vegetated with emergent, floating, and submerged macrophytes (Janetski 1990; King 2019). Current conditions feature high turbidity (mean 218 NTU in 2022), low Secchi depth (mean 0.25 m in 2022), and elevated total phosphorus (mean 0.17 mg/L in 2022). Nearshore habitats include monotypic stands of invasive [[Phragmites]] (*Phragmites australis*), native emergent taxa ([[Hardstem Bulrush]] *Scirpus acutus*, [[Cattail]] *Typha latifolia*, [[Alkali Bulrush]] *Bolboschoenus maritimus*), and limited submerged vegetation ([[Sago Pondweed]] *Stuckenia pectinata*, [[Coontail]] *Ceratophyllum demersum*). Submerged macrophyte coverage remains <1% of lake area across all years (2012–2022). Enclosed bays (Provo Bay, southern Goshen Bay) show higher probabilities of submerged vegetation during low lake levels. Lake level fluctuations are extreme, driven by arid-land hydrology and human water demands; depth below full pool (compromise level ~1368.3 m above sea level) varies substantially among years. Macrophyte-associated habitats support higher densities of juvenile and small-bodied fishes, including non-natives, than open water.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Not reported for June Sucker in this document. Carp demonstrate compensatory recruitment responses to high lake levels, with large recruitment events allowing population recovery despite ongoing removal (Walsworth et al. 2020, 2023).

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: June Sucker diets are dominated by [[Chironomid]] larvae (macroinvertebrates) and large-bodied zooplankton, particularly [[Daphnia]]. Electivity analyses show 83% of individual June Sucker select for Chironomids; approximately half select for Daphnia, Cyclopoid copepods, and Ceriodaphnia, while the other half select against these zooplankton taxa. Diet composition is highly similar to Common Carp, suggesting strong potential for exploitative competition. White Bass and Channel Catfish also consume Chironomids and Daphnia, though White Bass shows less reliance on small-bodied zooplankton and Channel Catfish diets are ~75% Chironomids with minimal zooplankton. Carp benthic foraging mobilizes sediment-borne nutrients, increases turbidity, and directly impacts macroinvertebrates and macrophytes through bioturbation. Reduced carp biomass correlates with increased biomass of Chironomids and Oligochaeta (macroinvertebrates), as well as large-bodied zooplankton (Daphnia, Calanoid copepods). A shift in zooplankton community structure favoring large-bodied over small-bodied taxa was initiated in 2017, the year of lowest carp biomass. Phytoplankton community shows increased densities of [[Chlorophyta]] (green algae, a preferred food for zooplankton) correlated with low carp biomass, though [[Cyanophyta]] (blue-green algae/cyanobacteria) densities have not declined. Macrophyte species richness at sampling sites increased beginning in 2016, coinciding with carp biomass reduction, but diversity and submerged macrophyte densities decreased after 2017 as carp biomass recovered. Macroinvertebrate biomass is substantially higher in macrophyte habitats than bare sediment; biomass increased 2014–2016 in response to increased macrophyte richness but stabilized thereafter. Macrophyte-associated habitats are dominated by non-native small-bodied and juvenile fishes (black bullhead, carp, fathead minnow, green sunfish, bluegill, black crappie), while open water supports more June Sucker, yellow perch, and white bass. Size-spectra analysis shows steeper negative slopes (greater abundance of small-bodied individuals) in vegetation (slope = -0.79) and edge of vegetation (-0.78) than open water (-0.45). Seasonal lake level drawdown reduces inundated macrophyte habitat, concentrating juveniles at vegetation edges and increasing predation vulnerability.

**Environmental Drivers**: Lake level is the primary driver of macrophyte habitat availability at the lake scale. Low lake levels promote submerged vegetation in shallow enclosed areas but also cause these areas to dry first, eliminating established stands. High lake levels support increased proportional coverage of emergent plants; submerged plant coverage increases at lower lake levels. Multiple regression shows lake level has stronger effects than carp biomass on most water quality parameters and individual zooplankton taxa biomass. Low lake levels drive significant increases in nutrient, chlorophyll a, and total dissolved solids (TDS) concentrations, and decreases in Secchi depth. Low lake levels also increase catch rates of benthic fish species (carp, black bullhead, channel catfish) and improve their body condition. Conversely, elevated lake levels improve White Bass body condition. Phragmites treatment efforts (herbicide and mechanical trampling) kill most macrophytes initially, followed by recolonization dictated partly by lake level. Wind and wave action limit submerged vegetation persistence, particularly in eastern lake areas. Principal components analysis (PCA) shows lake level (PC1, 19% variance) and carp biomass (PC2, 15% variance) operate synergistically to drive ecosystem changes. A substantial ecosystem shift occurred 2016–2017 (lowest carp biomass), with increased macroinvertebrate biomass and submerged vegetation. Ecosystem structure reverted toward 2012 conditions through 2021, then shifted back toward 2017 conditions in 2022 due to extremely low lake levels.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: June Sucker is listed as Threatened (formerly Endangered). Spawning abundance has increased in recent years, likely due to improved stocking efficiency. However, annual sampling by state biologists has not yet demonstrated an increase in young-of-year June Sucker, indicating natural recruitment remains limited. Adult June Sucker body condition improved significantly during low carp biomass conditions, potentially due to competitive release for preferred prey (Chironomids, Daphnia). No juvenile June Sucker were captured in habitat association sampling (2021–2022), though adults (>300 mm) were observed in all habitat types. Common Carp population biomass was reduced to near the 25% target (2016–2019) but has increased well above target levels in recent years due to compensatory recruitment during high lake levels. White Bass is the only non-carp species showing significant increase in biomass catch rates correlated with carp removal; relative abundance of White Bass increased substantially over time (declined in 2022). Channel Catfish and White Bass body condition have increased since 2012.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: June Sucker recovery is challenged by multiple interacting anthropogenic stressors: degraded water quality (hypereutrophic conditions, high turbidity, elevated nutrients), limited submerged macrophyte coverage (<1% lake area), extreme lake level fluctuations, invasive species (Common Carp, Phragmites), and competition/predation from non-native fishes. Lack of predator refugia (macrophyte cover) is believed the primary cause of declining June Sucker populations (Crowl et al. 1998). Macrophyte-associated habitats are dominated by non-native fishes, suggesting juvenile June Sucker (if present) would encounter interspecific competition and predation. Cyanobacteria blooms (sometimes toxic) have not declined despite carp removal. Total phosphorus concentrations remain high (no decrease over study period), reflecting continued nutrient loading from urbanized watershed. Water clarity has not improved (Secchi depth remains low, turbidity high). Recent increases in carp biomass (2020–2022) have reversed some positive ecosystem changes observed during the lowest carp biomass period (2016–2019). The limited response of submerged macrophyte coverage is identified as a critical missing response to restoration efforts. Lake level fluctuations can mask or counteract benefits of carp removal; low lake levels degrade water quality and eliminate inundated macrophyte habitat, while high lake levels promote carp recruitment.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: (1) **Continued carp suppression**: Maintain carp population biomass at or below the 25% reduction target to sustain improvements in zooplankton community structure (large-bodied taxa), macroinvertebrate biomass (Chironomids, Oligochaeta), phytoplankton composition (increased Chlorophyta), and fish body condition. Recent carp biomass increases have reversed positive ecosystem changes. (2) **Direct macrophyte restoration**: Implement efforts to support submerged macrophyte growth directly, coupled with carp suppression. Submerged macrophyte coverage remains the critical missing ecosystem response; increased coverage would provide predator refugia for juvenile June Sucker, stabilize sediments, improve water clarity, and support macroinvertebrate biomass. (3) **Nutrient control**: Apply additional nutrient control protocols in tandem with carp removal to address anthropogenic nutrient sources (agriculture, development). Carp removal alone has not reduced total phosphorus or cyanobacteria densities; other systems required combined fish removal and nutrient control to improve water quality (Jeppesen et al. 2007; Gulati et al. 2008; Hobbs et al. 2012). (4) **Water level management**: Evaluate water management strategies to maximize inundated macrophyte habitat availability, reducing predation on juveniles by maintaining refugia. Low lake levels eliminate established macrophyte stands and degrade water quality; high lake levels promote carp recruitment. (5) **Non-native fish control**: Implement measures to control non-native fishes (particularly in macrophyte habitats) to reduce interspecific competition and predation on juvenile June Sucker. (6) **Phragmites management**: Continue Phragmites treatment efforts to promote native macrophyte recolonization, though treatments temporarily eliminate all vegetation and effects are modulated by lake level. (7) **Adaptive monitoring**: Continue long-term ecosystem monitoring to detect delayed responses (e.g., enhanced recruitment of sport fish not yet evident due to gear selectivity) and guide adaptive management. (8) **Stocking optimization**: Maintain improved stocking efficiency that has increased adult spawning abundance; consider stocking strategies that account for limited natural recruitment and habitat constraints.

**Biological Reference Points**: Carp population biomass target: 25% of initial 2009 population level (~1,000 metric tons or less). This target was approached 2016–2019 but not sustained. Current (2022) carp biomass is well above target. Water quality standards: Total phosphorus exceeds state health standards (mean 0.17 mg/L in 2022); no target reduction achieved. Secchi depth remains low (mean 0.25 m in 2022); no improvement detected. Turbidity remains high (mean 218 NTU in 2022). Macrophyte coverage: Submerged vegetation <1% of lake area across all years; emergent vegetation <10% of lake area. No quantitative restoration targets are specified, but substantial increases in submerged macrophyte coverage are needed. Zooplankton: Shift toward large-bodied taxa (Daphnia, Calanoid copepods) initiated in 2017 at lowest carp biomass; this shift is a positive indicator. Macroinvertebrate biomass: Increased 2014–2016 but stabilized thereafter; continued increases depend on macrophyte diversity. Fish body condition: June Sucker, White Bass, Channel Catfish, Black Crappie, and Carp all show improved condition at low carp biomass. June Sucker recruitment: No increase in young-of-year detected in annual sampling; natural recruitment remains a critical unmet goal.
