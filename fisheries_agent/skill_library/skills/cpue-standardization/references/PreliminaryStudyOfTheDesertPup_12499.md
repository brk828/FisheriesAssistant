# Desert Pupfish and Their Habitat at Quitobaquito Springs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae #desert-ecology #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This technical report presents a comprehensive baseline study of the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) population at [[Quitobaquito Springs]], [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], Arizona, conducted from March 1975 to August 1976. The study documented population demography, physico-chemical habitat characteristics, seasonal habitat utilization, and breeding biology of this unique desert fish fauna. The spring-fed pool represents a critical oasis habitat in the lower [[Sonoran Desert]]. Population estimates revealed approximately 7,986 fish in October 1975, declining to 3,592 by June 1976—a 50% winter mortality. Water quality remained generally excellent for fish habitat throughout the year, with temperatures ranging from 4°C to 33°C and dissolved oxygen levels adequate except in dense vegetation during warm nights. Breeding occurred from late April through August, with males showing strong preference for shallow (10-15 cm), warm, firm-substrate territories along pool edges. The study identified critical management needs including potential habitat enhancement and regular population monitoring protocols.

## Background & Description

[[Quitobaquito Springs]] is a spring-fed pool in the lower [[Sonoran Desert]] that has been altered from its original configuration of springs, seeps, and shallow marsh. In February 1962, the [[National Park Service]] deepened the marsh area to create a pool of fairly uniform 90-100 cm depth, leaving only two shallow ledges on the northeast and east sides. By 1964, dense aquatic vascular plant growth (*Chara* and *Potamogeton*) covered most of the bottom.

The [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) represents the special fish fauna of this unique oasis. Historical data from Bryan (1925) documented spring temperature of 26.8°C and flow of 43 gal/min in 1917. The present study found spring waters approximately 2°C cooler (24.5°C) with significantly diminished flow by 1975, though water chemistry remained remarkably constant over 60 years.

The pool measures approximately 1000 linear feet around its periphery, with the main pool fed by an east spring through 500 feet of plastic pipe. An observation pool receives warm spring water before it enters the main pool. The pool bottom consists largely of precipitated CaCO₃ marl from *Chara* activity, with varying substrate types including solid rock, sand, and soft muck in different areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water Quality Characteristics:**
The spring-fed pool exhibited excellent buffering capacity with high alkalinity (295 mg/l HCO₃) and total dissolved solids averaging 700-800 mg/l—much lower than the nearby Rio Sonoyta (1450 mg/l TDS). Monthly monitoring revealed:

- pH: averaged 9.0 in pool (7.0-7.5 in spring), well-buffered with minimal diel variation
- Temperature: spring constant at 24.5°C; pool ranged 4°C-33°C daily maximum
- Dissolved oxygen: generally 5-16 mg/l, dropping to 0.5 mg/l in dense vegetation during warm summer nights
- Nutrients: no pollution levels of nitrates or phosphates; peaked in winter months
- Major ions: Ca 30 mg/l, Mg 28 mg/l, Na 270 mg/l, Cl 148 mg/l

The photic zone extended to the pool bottom throughout the study except during a slight algal bloom from May 20 through July 16, 1976. Water level remained stable except for a 5.0 cm drop in fall 1975, recovering by July rains.

**Habitat Types:**
1. **Shallow edge habitat** (12-15 cm depth): solid substrate of plant roots and earth, warmest temperatures, open water, DO 7+ mg/l
2. **Deep dense vegetation** (60-100 cm): muck bottom of CaCO₃ and plant debris, cooler temperatures, low nighttime DO (0.5 mg/l), dense *Chara* and *Potamogeton*
3. **Deep open water**: solid rock bottom in western area, good water quality but limited extent
4. **East shore**: shallow sloping muck bottom, intermediate vegetation, subject to wind/wave scouring

**Seasonal Habitat Utilization:**
Fish distribution varied dramatically by season. In December and April, 90% of activity concentrated in the northern one-third of the pool in shallow waters. By July, fish dispersed throughout the pool with 69% of catches in deep water traps. Fish showed strong diel activity patterns, active only during daylight hours. Temperature strongly influenced activity in winter (inactive below 13°C) and spring, with peak catches during warmest periods. Summer activity showed bimodal peaks (morning and afternoon) with midday depression.

Dissolved oxygen did not limit distribution, likely due to the species' exceptional low-oxygen tolerance (0.13 mg/l). Fish consistently preferred shallow traps over vegetation or substrate; floating traps in open water caught no fish, confirming fish only swim shallow over vegetation or substrate.

## Key Findings

**Population Demography:**
- October 1975 population: 7,986 fish (95% CI: 6,228-8,589); 90.5% markable size (>22 mm)
- June 1976 population: 3,592 fish (95% CI: 3,073-3,996); 93.3% markable size
- **50% winter mortality** between October and June, affecting juveniles and adults
- Maximum lifespan: 3 years
- Age structure (June): 70% age-0, 18% age-I, 4% age-II, minimal age-III
- Growth rate: Age-I averaged 31.5 mm TL; Age-II 40.2 mm (8.7 mm increase); Age-III 48 mm (7.8 mm increase)
- Sex ratio (≥32 mm): 43% males, 57% females (not significantly different from 50:50, χ²=3.14, p>0.05)
- Mean catch rate: 18.6 fish/trap/hr in October 1975

**Breeding Biology:**
- **Breeding season**: late April through August (approximately 4 months)
- Initiation temperature: 24°C daily maximum (75°F)
- First fry observed: April 29, 1975 (24.5°C); May 20, 1976 (28.3°C)
- Peak breeding: May-June, with dramatic decline in territorial males after June
- Territorial male density: approximately 3 males per linear meter along preferred shallow edge
- Estimated 1,114 males seeking territories with only 700-800 feet of preferred shallow habitat available

**Habitat Preferences (Territorial Males):**
1. **Depth**: Strong preference for shallow water (10-15 cm); all 14 experimental males chose ≤15 cm depth
2. **Substrate**: Definite preference for sand over silt (χ²=3.76, p<0.10); firm bottom over soft muck
3. **Cover**: Preference for open, visually unobstructed areas; 9 of 13 males avoided pool wall
4. **Temperature**: Warmest available areas; males concentrated where water warmed earliest
5. **Vegetation**: Avoided dense vegetation for territory establishment; preferred open water over solid substrate

**Breeding Habitat Utilization:**
Early breeding season (May-June): Males occupied both shallow edge and deep dense vegetation, with competition forcing less dominant males to suboptimal deep-water territories. Late season (July-August): Sufficient shallow edge habitat available; virtually all territorial males in preferred shallow edge habitat.

**Water Quality and Breeding:**
Shallow edge breeding habitat characterized by: (1) solid substrate, (2) DO 7+ mg/l even during summer lows, (3) warmest temperatures, (4) open water for male visibility. Deep vegetation areas had: (1) muck bottom, (2) cooler temperatures with nighttime DO as low as 0.5 mg/l, (3) dense vegetation obscuring males, (4) poor egg survival habitat on bottom.

**Management Recommendations:**

1. **Restore warm-water breeding habitat**: Clear 30-foot reed-choked drainage between observation pool and main pool; install small concrete ditch with sand covering to provide access to warm spring waters (24.5°C). This would extend breeding season by 2-3 months (February-March), restoring reproductive flexibility lost when spring waters were enclosed in pipe.

2. **Population monitoring protocol**: Establish annual fall monitoring using 20 screened minnow traps placed in standardized locations. Calculate catch/trap/hr as relative abundance index (baseline: 18.6 fish/trap/hr, October 1975). This simple technique would detect population trends over years.

3. **Water quality monitoring**: Implement seasonal monitoring for pesticides and general water quality due to agricultural development and pumping activities one mile south in Mexico. Monitor pool water level as indicator of aquatic health.

4. **Avoid pool alteration**: Do not create additional shallow beach areas in main pool despite breeding habitat scarcity; fish have adapted to current conditions and long-term survival in artificial alterations is unknown.

**Conservation Concerns:**
- Limited optimal breeding habitat (700-800 feet) insufficient for estimated 1,114 breeding males
- 50% winter mortality of unknown cause (not temperature or oxygen related)
- Significantly reduced spring flow compared to historical levels (1917)
- Potential impacts from Mexican agricultural activities and groundwater pumping
- Loss of warm-water spring habitat when waters were enclosed in pipe, reducing breeding season by approximately 50%

**Comparative Context:**
[[Quitobaquito]] pupfish show exceptional low-oxygen tolerance (0.13 mg/l) compared to most fish species, an adaptation critical for survival in desert pools with dense vegetation and warm summer conditions. The population's 3-year maximum lifespan and rapid first-year growth (31.5 mm) are typical of desert pupfish life history strategies. The 50% winter mortality rate represents a significant population bottleneck requiring further investigation.
