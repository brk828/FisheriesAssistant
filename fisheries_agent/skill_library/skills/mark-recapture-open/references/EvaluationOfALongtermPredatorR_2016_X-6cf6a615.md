# Evaluation of a Long-Term Predator Removal Program: Abundance and Population Dynamics of Invasive Northern Pike in the Yampa River, Colorado

#ichthyology #Esocidae #invasive_species #predator_removal

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) is a large-bodied, invasive piscivore established in the [[Yampa River]] basin since the 1970s. Captured individuals ranged 59–1,120 mm TL (mean = 465 mm TL). Mean TL was greatest downstream (Maybell–Sunbeam reach: 519 mm) and smallest in the middle reach (South Beach–Little Yampa Canyon–Juniper: 411 mm). Age, maturity, and fecundity parameters not explicitly reported. Growth parameters inferred from within-year recaptures: mean growth was 4 mm for fish recaptured in the same year, indicating slow growth for larger individuals. Proportions of large Northern Pike (>450 mm and >600 mm TL) declined throughout 2004–2010 in all reaches, with fish >600 mm TL declining to less than half of 2004 levels by 2010 in some reaches. Survival rates for mean-TL fish (465 mm) were length-dependent, peaking at 625 mm TL then declining for larger individuals. Annual survival rates varied by reach: highest downstream (mean = 0.54; range = 0.36–0.71), intermediate in the middle reach (mean = 0.32; range = 0.18–0.48), and lowest upstream (mean = 0.25; range = 0.12–0.38). Mortality rates (death or emigration) averaged 70–75% in upstream and middle reaches. Tag loss rate estimated at 12.2%, though likely an overestimate due to variation in tagging techniques among years.

**Habitat Associations**: Northern Pike occupy riverine habitats throughout the [[Yampa River]] (175 river kilometers sampled) and have expanded into the [[Green River]] basin. Upstream reaches (Hayden to Craig, South Beach–Little Yampa Canyon–Juniper) support highest abundances and densities. Downstream reach (Maybell–Sunbeam) has lower densities but still substantial populations. Northern Pike also inhabit off-channel reservoirs ([[Lake Catamount]], [[Stagecoach Reservoir]], [[Elkhead Reservoir]]) and ponds, which serve as propagule sources. Spawning occurs in upstream tributaries (e.g., Walton Creek confluence) and off-channel habitats. The species demonstrates high adaptability to varied riverine and lentic environments within the basin.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Movement analysis of 1,911 individually tagged Northern Pike (2001–2012) revealed predominantly downstream dispersal (90% of reach-changing fish moved downstream). Total distance traveled ranged 0.0–388.5 km (mean = 19.3 km). Of fish with multiple captures, 21% changed reaches, 73% remained in initial capture reach, and 6% escaped translocation sites and were recaptured. Upstream reaches had highest emigration rates: 34% of fish initially captured above Hayden left that reach, 19% from Hayden to Craig. Downstream movements covered one to eight reaches; upstream movements were shorter (one to two reaches). Longest documented movement: 388.5 km from Lake Catamount downstream to middle Green River. Northern Pike from Yampa River moved into Green River (both upstream into Lodore Canyon and downstream), establishing reproducing populations. Movement was sometimes extensive and promoted establishment of additional populations. Time elapsed between initial and final captures ranged 1 day to over 8 years. Net distance traveled (first to last capture location) and total distance traveled (sum of all movement legs) documented for all individuals. Of 121 translocated fish recaptured, 80% were found in the adjacent Hayden to Craig reach, 3% upstream, and 17% downstream including Green River. Movement patterns indicate high dispersal potential and risk of continued range expansion.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Northern Pike function as apex predators, consuming multiple life stages of native fishes. A 758-mm TL Northern Pike consumed an adult [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] estimated at 500 mm TL, demonstrating predation on large native species. Bioenergetics modeling ranked [[Smallmouth Bass]] as the foremost problematic invasive species and Northern Pike as second-most problematic due to high abundances, habitat overlap with native fishes, and ability to consume wide variety of life stages. Northern Pike have replaced Colorado Pikeminnow as the top predator in the middle Yampa River. Trophic adaptability allows Northern Pike to consume various species when preferred prey become less abundant, affecting multiple native fish species simultaneously. Competition with and predation on endangered fishes ([[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]]) impedes recovery efforts. Northern Pike densities were 4–58 times higher than current Colorado Pikeminnow density (1.2 fish/km), indicating substantial competitive and predatory pressure on the native apex predator.

**Environmental Drivers**: The Yampa River has a relatively natural hydrograph driven by spring snowmelt runoff. Small upstream reservoirs (Stagecoach Reservoir, Lake Catamount, Elkhead Reservoir) provide limited flow regulation. Mean May discharge was included as a covariate in capture probability models but was not retained in top-ranked models. Capture probability varied by year, pass, and reach, with mean capture probability across all reaches, passes, and years of 0.17 (range = 0.03–0.51) for 465-mm fish. Highest mean capture probability per pass occurred in 2008 (0.22); lowest in 2009 (0.13). Environmental conditions affecting Northern Pike reproduction and recruitment not fully elucidated but likely include spawning habitat availability, discharge levels, and temperature. Unlimited sources of annual production from reservoir escapement and abundant local reproductive habitat sustain populations despite high mortality rates. Climate change and environmental disturbances may facilitate continued expansion of invasive species.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Annual abundance estimates (2004–2010) were highest in upstream reach (Hayden to Craig: 1,144–4,078 fish; 19–69 fish/km), intermediate in middle reach (South Beach–Little Yampa Canyon–Juniper: 819–3,499 fish; 12–50 fish/km), and lowest downstream (Maybell–Sunbeam: 232–746 fish; 5–15 fish/km). Years of highest abundance: 2004, 2005, and 2009 upstream; 2004 and 2009 in middle reach; 2004, 2007, and 2009 downstream. Abundance estimates were relatively low in all reaches 2006–2008. After removal each year, Northern Pike abundance increased annually by nearly 1,000% in upstream reach (mean = 993%; range = 136–3,974%), over 875% in middle reach (mean = 878%; range = 57–2,988%), and over 375% downstream (mean = 388%; range = 52–975%) due to recruitment and immigration. Densities in upstream and middle reaches declined after 2005 and 2004, respectively, remained relatively low until 2009, then increased substantially. Downstream reach showed more variable pattern with abundance lower than predicted in two of six years. Population growth between removal periods consistently exceeded removal and mortality, indicating inadequate control. Colorado Pikeminnow abundance declined in Yampa River from 2000–2003 to 2006–2008 and continues to decline, contrasting with increases in four other Green River basin population areas.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Northern Pike pose substantial predatory and competitive threat to endangered fishes in the upper Colorado River basin. Invasive status confirmed; species impedes recovery of four federally endangered fishes: Colorado Pikeminnow, Razorback Sucker, Humpback Chub, and Bonytail. Current removal rates inadequate to reduce abundance because recruitment and immigration exceed removal and mortality. Removal rates averaged 31% (range = 9–48%) upstream, 30% (range = 13–52%) in middle reach, and 32% (range = 19–44%) downstream. Removal resulted in only 7% mean decrease in survival rates upstream and 8% decrease in middle reach; downstream removal produced larger decreases (mean = 16%; range = 13–21%) and was likely primary mechanism reducing numbers there. Mortality due to death or emigration was greater upstream and declined going downstream. Propagule pressure from reservoir escapement (Lake Catamount, Stagecoach Reservoir, Elkhead Reservoir) and off-channel sources continues. Over 5,500 Northern Pike removed from Lake Catamount 2008–2010 (twice the number removed from three Yampa River study reaches), demonstrating persistent threat from reservoir populations. Translocation of approximately 9,000 Northern Pike to off-channel ponds (2001–2012) resulted in 121 subsequent recaptures in river, indicating escapement from translocation sites. Predominantly downstream movement (90%) increases risk of establishment in additional portions of upper Colorado River basin. Long-distance movements documented (up to 388.5 km), facilitating range expansion. Upstream reaches serve as important sources of Northern Pike production; 36% of age-0 or age-1 fish captured in upper reach (2012 sampling). Spawning locations identified (e.g., Walton Creek confluence) produce many times the number of age-0 fish captured during weeks of downstream sampling.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Current removal efforts must be increased and supplemented with additional control measures to effectively reduce Northern Pike abundance. Mechanical removal remains important population control tool and should continue, but removal alone is inadequate. Specific recommendations include: (1) Investigation of reproduction and recruitment patterns, including well-timed, targeted sampling of age-0 and age-1 Northern Pike under various environmental conditions to identify sources and estimate recruitment rates. (2) Source management: control of off-channel propagule sources via screening, enclosure nets, or elimination; continued removal efforts in Lake Catamount and other reservoirs. (3) Spawning disruption: identification of Northern Pike spawning locations and implementation of spawning disruption measures; investigation of relationships among spawning, discharge, and temperature levels to stem reproduction and recruitment. (4) Increased removal: higher removal rates needed to exceed population increases from immigration and recruitment, particularly in upstream and middle reaches. (5) Translocation cessation: translocation of Northern Pike has ceased (as of study period), reducing one source of escapement. (6) Comprehensive, sustained effort: long-term commitment to control program with adequate evaluation components to allow efficient allocation of resources. (7) Adaptive management: use of demographic parameters and movement data to guide management decisions and evaluate efficacy. Target density of 1.9 fish/km (or equivalent to current Colorado Pikeminnow density of 1.2 fish/km, whichever lower) likely too high given residual propagule pressure; revised targets needed. Removal efforts should focus on reducing densities in critical habitat for endangered fishes while addressing upstream sources. Tagging should continue to allow robust evaluation of removal effects; decisions to forego tagging should be made carefully.
