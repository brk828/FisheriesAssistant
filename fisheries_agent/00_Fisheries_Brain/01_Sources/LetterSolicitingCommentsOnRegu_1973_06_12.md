## Executive Summary
This document is not a primary scientific study but a **regulatory solicitation letter** issued by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife) on June 12, 1973. It requests expert and public comment on proposed mechanisms to regulate the importation of potentially **injurious exotic fishes** under the authority of the [[Lacey Act]] (18 U.S.C. 42e-hh; 62 Stat. 687). The letter does not report original morphological, ecological, or population data on any specific fish species; instead, it outlines administrative and policy context for classifying species as "injurious" or "low risk" for import control purposes. Two reference lists — the **AFS Exotic Species Committee List** and the **Collette et al. List** — were enclosed as the basis for soliciting scientific input, though their taxonomic contents are not reproduced in the text provided.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
While no direct ecological data (habitat, trophic role, distribution) are given, the letter implicitly addresses **invasion ecology and biosecurity risk**, stating that:

- Imported [[Exotic Species]] (vertebrates, mollusks, crustaceans) pose demonstrated risks to:
  - Human health and safety
  - Agriculture, horticulture, and forestry
  - Native [[Wildlife]] and wildlife resources of the United States
- "Current information shows that injury caused by imported wildlife is more widespread and serious than previously believed."
- Growing volume of live wildlife imports is cited as a compounding risk factor for ecological disruption.
- Once established, **pest species control or eradication programs are described as "costly and usually unsuccessful,"** implying recognition of the practical difficulty of reversing [[Biological Invasion|biological invasions]] once introduced into native ecosystems.

This framing situates the letter within the broader ecological concern of **introduced/invasive fish species** disrupting native aquatic community structure, though no specific niche, habitat, or species interaction data are detailed.

## Key Findings
- **Legal Basis**: The [[Lacey Act]] empowers the Secretary of the Interior to regulate importation of exotic vertebrates, mollusks, and crustaceans found to be injurious to designated interests (human health, agriculture, forestry, wildlife).
- **Proposed Regulatory Mechanisms** (two systems under consideration):
  1. A **"black list"** approach — listing species *known* to pose serious threats (all listed species restricted).
  2. A **"white list" / "low risk" list** approach — species *not* listed are presumed injurious and restricted by default.
- **Permit System**: Under either system, species classified as injurious could still be imported under permit for **scientific, educational, medical, or zoological purposes**, contingent on "proper showing of responsibility."
- **Dynamic Listing**: Any resulting injurious/low-risk species lists would be subject to **continual review and revision** as new ecological data become available.
- **Stakeholder Engagement**: The Service explicitly solicited comments from ichthyologists and fisheries scientists on two enclosed reference lists:
  - [[AFS Exotic Species Committee List]]
  - [[Collette et al. List]]
- **Procedural Timeline**:
  - Comment deadline: **July 13, 1973**
  - Final regulatory framework to be published in the **Federal Register** as a "Proposed Rule Making," with at least two public hearings to follow.
- **Precedent Context**: The letter cites unspecified "recent examples of injury" from exotic wildlife introductions domestically and internationally as justification for tightened import controls, reflecting early institutional recognition of what is now understood as [[Invasive Species Management|invasive species risk assessment]] in fisheries and wildlife policy.
