# Key to the Nymphs of the British Species of Ephemeroptera

#ephemeroptera #freshwater-ecology #entomology

## Executive Summary

This Freshwater Biological Association publication, authored by [[T. T. Macan]] (1961), is a dichotomous key to the nymphs (larvae) of the British mayflies, order Ephemeroptera. It extends earlier family- and genus-level keys by [[D. E. Kimmins]] to species level. Keys and descriptions exist for 45 of the 47 British species, and the two remaining species are covered using features described by continental workers. Two closely related species pairs cannot be separated by nymphal characters. The key is supported by notes on collecting, preservation, [[Life Histories]] and [[Distribution Maps]] for all species.

## Background & Description

The Ephemeroptera life cycle begins with eggs laid in water, followed by a nymphal stage that grows through many moults. A winged form then emerges and moults once more to become the sexually mature adult, which generally dies after mating and egg-laying. The nymph's body is divided into a head with antennae, large eyes and mouthparts; a thorax with three pairs of legs; and a ten-segment abdomen. Abdominal gills are used to diagnose species, and three caudal filaments (tails) occur at the end of the abdomen.

Mouthparts consist of a labrum, mandibles (in *Baétis*, bearing a prostheca), maxillae with three-jointed palps, and a labium with glossae and paraglossae. Identification relies on gill form, leg spination, abdominal markings, tail banding, and mouthpart shape. Smaller nymphs are harder to identify because they resemble related species more closely.

**Collecting and preservation:** A standard pond-net is the most useful tool, with a dredge and sieve preferred when lightness is not a priority. Live nymphs are easier to identify in some species because tail angle is diagnostic. Recommended preservatives were 2½% formalin, or a mixture of 500 ml of 70% alcohol, 10 ml of 40% formalin and 5 ml of glycerin.

**Terminology:** The author notes the use of "nymph" versus "larva" is inconsistent. Stages were grouped as "tiny," "half-grown" and "full-grown" nymphs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Species occupy a range of habitats. Among the most frequently cited are:

- **Stony streams and rivers:** [[Ecdyonurus Venosus]], [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]], [[Baétis Rhodani]] and [[Baétis Pumilus]], which the author identifies as typical of small stony streams.
- **Sandy or muddy substrates:** [[Ephemera Danica]] (lakes and rivers with sandy or gravelly beds), [[Ephemera Vulgata]] (rivers with muddy beds), and [[Caenis Horaria]] (soft mud in still and flowing water).
- **Lakes, tarns and ponds:** [[Leptophlebia Vespertina]] (especially in lime-poor districts), [[Cloeon Dipterum]] (small rich ponds, lakes and slow river sections) and [[Siphlonurus Lacustris]] (tarns, slow becks and lakes).
- **High-altitude streams:** [[Ameletus Inopinatus]], generally a stream species confined to high altitudes.
- **Calcareous conditions:** Several species, including *Ecdyonurus insignis* and *Siphlonurus* species, are suggested to prefer calcareous or lime-rich water, though the author notes the evidence is tentative.

The author describes the distribution of *Siphlonurus* as "inexplicably sporadic." Distribution records were compiled from authoritative records, the author's unpublished data and [[Harris (1952)]] for Ireland, which used a dot-map approach that complicated conversion to [[Vice-County Recording]] units.

## Key Findings

**Scope and taxonomy**
- The key covers 47 British species. Descriptions and keys were published for 45 of them in the previous two decades, mostly by Macan.
- Keys are provided for the families Ephemeridae, Potamanthidae, Caenidae, Ephemerellidae, Ecdyonuridae, Leptophlebiidae, Siphlonuridae and Baetidae, with species-level keys within each.
- Two species pairs (*Heptagenia*-related and *Rhithrogena semicolorata/R. haarupi*) could not be separated by nymphal characters. *Rhithrogena semicolorata* and *R. haarupi* are reported as indistinguishable as nymphs.
- Taxonomic notes include the distinction of [[Caenis Moesta]] from [[Caenis Macrura]] and the separation of [[Procloeon Pseudorufulum]] from *Procloeon rufulum*.

**Notable identification characters**
- *Ephemera*: abdominal tergal markings on segments 7–9 and foreleg tibia shape separate *E. vulgata*, *E. danica* and *E. lineata*.
- *Caenis*: the number of spines on the fore femora (6–11 across species), pronotum shape, and cockade-like scale counts separate the four or five species.
- *Baétis*: tail banding, spination on the femora, labrum bristles and gill shape distinguish the ten species.

**Life histories (Life Histories section)**
- [[Ephemera Danica]] completes one generation per year, as supported by Pleskot (1958). Most other British authors' claims of two-year cycles were judged unsupported by data.
- [[Ephemerella Ignita]] spends about 10 months in the egg stage, hatching mid-summer, though the flight period is longer in England than in an Austrian stream.
- *Caenis horaria* nymphs appear towards the end of July and emerge 11–12 months later (Moon 1939). The author cautions that Moon's data may include *C. moesta*.
- [[Baétis Rhodani]] and [[Baétis Pumilus]] each have two generations per year, a slow-growing winter generation and a fast-growing summer generation, with two peaks of hatching.
- [[Ameletus Inopinatus]] grows through both winter and summer and has two hatching bursts, one in autumn and one early in the year.
- [[Heptagenia Lateralis]] does not grow in the coldest part of the year and has a lower upper lethal temperature than *Rhithrogena semicolorata*, which may explain its narrower range.
- The author describes a new feature in the Life Histories section, intended to aid understanding of the group's ecological significance. Diagrams for 16 species are presented in Figures 28 and 29, and the author states that some parts are speculative.

**Distribution**
- Figures 30–36 map 47 species across Britain and Ireland, with records restricted to recognised authorities or verifiable identifications.
- Lake District records are noted for many species, for example *Ecdyonurus dispar* as "common and abundant" and *Baétis pumilus* as "common and often abundant."
- Several species have scattered or historical records, such as *Brachycercus harrisella* (Old records from Berkshire and London) and *Heptagenia longicauda* (Berkshire 1868, Middlesex 1904, Surrey 1933).

**References**
- The reference list includes about 80 works, including key contributions by Macan (1949–1960), Kimmins (1932–1957), Harker (1952), Moon (1939) and Rawlinson (1939).
- An index lists species names with page numbers for both key and life-history sections.
