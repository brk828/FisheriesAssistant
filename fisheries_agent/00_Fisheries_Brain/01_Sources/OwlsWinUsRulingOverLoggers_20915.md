# Mexican Spotted Owl Critical Habitat Ruling and Southwestern Logging

#ornithology #conservation-biology #strix-occidentalis-lucida

## Executive Summary

A federal court order in June required the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (FWS) to establish a timetable for designating [[Critical Habitat]] for the [[Mexican Spotted Owl]], a subspecies listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Environmentalists argued that the protections could bar logging on up to 3 million acres of Southwestern forest. Timber industry representatives argued the listing had already caused heavy losses and that the ruling might have little immediate effect. The article focuses on the legal and economic conflict rather than on owl biology.

## Background & Description

- **Subject:** [[Mexican Spotted Owl]] (*Strix occidentalis lucida*), one of three [[Spotted Owl]] subspecies. The source includes a distribution map showing it separately from the [[Northern Spotted Owl]] and [[California Spotted Owl]].
- **Legal status:** Defined as threatened with extinction in April 1993 (per the article).
- **Population:** An estimated 2,160 owls in Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado and Utah.
- **Petitioning:** [[Robin Silver]] of Phoenix first petitioned the government to protect the owl in December 1989.
- **Litigation:** Environmentalists sued in March against FWS, claiming it violated the Endangered Species Act by failing to designate protected habitat by the 1 November 1993 deadline. U.S. District Judge Carl Muecke agreed (the source spells the name "Muccke" and "Muecke" through OCR).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat preference:** Environmentalists maintain the owl thrives best in old-growth forests. Timber firms counter that owls are thriving in areas that have been cut and are regrowing.

**Affected lands:** The ruling could place up to 3 million acres of forest in Arizona and New Mexico off-limits to future logging. The article compares the area to the size of Connecticut. Protected areas would include:
- Ancient forests
- Stands of trees never previously logged
- The state's last remaining large [[Ponderosa Pine]] stands

**Management concerns:** Matson argued that areas placed off-limits could eventually die off from fire, insects or disease and be lost for perhaps hundreds of years. He said designations should rely on the best available science.

**Forest structure and industry:** Suckling predicted the ruling might help smaller [[Sawmill]] operations compete with larger ones, because smaller mills could still bid on thinning sales in overgrown forests while large trees were spared.

**Timber harvest effects:** Federal authorities reported that protecting the owl had so far produced a 15 percent reduction in timber harvests.

## Key Findings

1. **Court-ordered habitat designation:** Judge Muecke ordered FWS, on June 22, to produce a timetable for establishing critical habitat by 16 September. Suckling said the judge could impose his own timetable if FWS failed to produce an appropriate one.

2. **Scope of protection:** Designation could bar logging on up to 3 million acres. Fish and Wildlife officials must determine whether timber sales would harm currently occupied areas or unoccupied areas into which owls could expand.

3. **Industry impact:** Matson said the listing had already hurt the industry badly and that new protections might have little immediate effect, since many forest lands were already closed to logging. Kaibab Forest Products Co. had no work in Arizona during the week before the article, the first time since 1946.

4. **Timeliness dispute:** Silver said FWS was more than three years late in designating critical habitat. Environmentalists feared FWS would continue to delay. An FWS spokeswoman in Albuquerque said the agency was already working to define critical habitat.

5. **Scientific basis:** Suckling said the owls had been studied extensively, their nesting locations were known, and the lands to protect were clear. Matson argued the designation should use the best available science.

6. **Stakeholders:** The article quotes [[Kieran Suckling]] of the [[Southwest Center For Biodiversity]] (Silver City, N.M.) and [[Jim Matson]], chairman of the [[Southwest Natural Resources Council]], which represents six logging companies in Arizona and New Mexico.
