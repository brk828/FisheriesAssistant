# Diffuse Branchiomycosis in Lacustrine Fish Fauna

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #fish-pathology #branchiomycosis

## Executive Summary

This 1973 study from the [[Istituto Italiano di Idrobiologia]] reports a widespread gill infection by a fungus attributed to the genus [[Branchiomyces]] (order [[Saprolegniales]]) across lakes in Northern and Central Italy and in Swiss lakes north of the Alps. Infection was found in 21 of 24 examined species. Mortality, however, was documented only in [[Bleak]], and infection in most hosts did not produce overt disease. The authors link the fungus's shift from saprophytism to parasitism to lake [[Eutrophication]], and they describe the first pure culture of the fungus and its experimental infection of bleak. Serological tests place the fungus close to, but not identical with, [[Saprolegnia]].

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** The authors argue that fish pathology and population ecology have rarely been integrated, and that fungi are under-considered as causes of morbidity in natural fish populations. The recent spread of Saprolegniaceae dermatitis in European running-water salmonids and the appearance of Branchiomyces in lakes motivated the study.
- **Classical hosts:** Branchiomycosis, known in carp as "Kiemenfäule" or gill rot, was previously described mainly in carp and tench (and secondarily pike) in small, warm, organically enriched waters such as carp ponds, with algal blooms.
- **Infection scale:** Gill infection was graded as slight (scattered punctiform foci), medium (numerous foci over much of the filaments) or severe (abundant branching filamentous foci, sometimes with necrosis of distal filaments).
- **Structure of the paper:** Part I covers epidemiology in lake environments; Part II covers culture requirements, experimental transmission and taxonomic affinity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lago Maggiore (1972 field survey).** Bleak showed 100% infection at every sampling date. Severe infection declined over the autumn: the proportion of severely infected bleak was 38.5% (29 Aug), 30.0% (31 Aug), 13.3% (11 Sep) and 15.9% (6 Nov). Sample sizes were 65, 100, 61 and 4 individuals for the four dates in Table 1, with the 6 Nov sample the smallest.

**Other Lago Maggiore species (Table 2, 17 species surveyed).** Infection intensity was highest in cyprinids (except the [[Vimba]]-like "savetta"), in [[Pumpkinseed]]-type centrarchids (sun perch), and in trout among salmonids. Pike showed slight infection. European eel showed infection in 2 of 4 individuals. Coregonid "bondella" had the lowest incidence (166 of 211 infected). Burbot, perch and agone showed no infection in this lake.

**Experimental species in captivity.** Young black bullheads (about 1.5–3.0 cm, about 50 fish) died within days in a tank, with gills blackened by fungus. Loach died more slowly. These two species were excluded from the comparative table because captivity may have made them unrepresentative.

**Other northern Italian lakes (Table 3, bleak as indicator).** Infection was found in every lake examined, including Varese, Lugano, Como, Iseo, Garda and Ledro, plus Mergozzo and Endine (examined using rudd). Varese bleak showed 24.0% severe infection and Iseo 72.7%. Mergozzo whitefish were uninfected, while Mergozzo char showed slight infection, and perch and burbot, apparently free in Lago Maggiore, showed slight infection in Lake Lugano.

**Central Italian lakes.** Trasimeno, Nemi and Piediluco were sampled. At Trasimeno the infection was heaviest in [[Atherina]] (sand smelt) and triotto, and perch were uninfected. At Nemi and Piediluco infection was slight but present in every individual of every species examined.

**Swiss lakes (Table 4, Alburnus alburnus).** Infection occurred in all four lakes: Zugersee (n=30), Zürichsee (n=20), Greifensee (n=20) and Bielersee (n=20). No mortality was reported. In January 1973 Zugersee bleak showed granular, weakly melanised structures resembling those seen only during die-offs in Italy. Char from Zugersee (n=20) were uninfected, while all 20 roach from Zürichsee were infected.

**High-altitude Gotthard lakes.** Lago Tom, Lago Stabbio and Lago Scuro, which are oligotrophic and hydrologically isolated, contained trout and char with no infection.

**Temperature.** In culture, growth was slow up to about 12 °C, accelerated up to about 27 °C, and fell sharply above that. In an aquarium experiment, bleak with subclinical infection that were warmed from 6 °C to 20 °C over one week suffered mortality for about 15 days. Fish held at a constant 7–8 °C had only rare deaths.

## Key Findings

1. **Near-universal infection:** Branchiomycosis was documented in 21 of 24 species, across Central and Northern Italy and Swiss lakes north of the Alps.
2. **Bleak as principal victim:** Bleak was the only species in which mortality was recorded during the study period, and it showed the most severe infections.
3. **Differential susceptibility:** Infection intensity and pattern differed among species. Coregonid "bondella" had the lowest incidence. Burbot, perch and agone were uninfected in Lago Maggiore.
4. **Historical absence:** Histological material from 1961–1964 was negative, so the first documented outbreaks appear to be the 1964 bleak die-offs at Avigliana and Varese.
5. **Eutrophication hypothesis:** The authors propose that the fungus converted from a saprophyte to a parasite in eutrophic lakes such as Varese, then spread to less eutrophic lakes such as Maggiore and Garda, with cyprinid increase in eutrophication possibly aiding its spread. Oligotrophic, isolated Gotthard lakes were free of infection.
6. **Spread via Fiume Bardello:** The first bleak die-off in Lago Maggiore (1968) began where the Bardello river enters the lake, four years after the first event at Varese.
7. **Pure culture obtained:** The fungus was grown free of bacteria using antibiotic media, low-pH media and transfer of young hyphal tips. Sporangia were induced on salmon-extract medium and on hemp seed medium, but sexual structures were not found.
8. **Thermal optimum:** Colony growth peaked in the upper range of 12–27 °C, consistent with summer epidemics when water reaches about 25–26 °C.
9. **Experimental pathogenicity:** Of 10 bleak inoculated with cultured material, 7 died within five days, and hyphae were found in the gill epithelium. The fungus was re-isolated and matched the inoculum.
10. **Taxonomic position:** Immunodiffusion tests with four [[Saprolegnia]] species (S. parasitica, S. ferax, S. delica, S. diclina) showed strong antigenic affinity but non-identity, supporting placement in the Saprolegniales as a distinct genus. The classification remains unresolved, with [[Branchiomyces sanguinis]] and [[Branchiomyces demigrans]] requiring comparison.
11. **Sublethal effects:** The authors suggest that even without visible disease, gill infection may impair respiration and metabolism, affecting population dynamics.
