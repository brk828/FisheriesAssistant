# Seeking Solutions: Exploring the Applicability of ADR for Resolving Water Issues in the West

#water-policy #alternative-dispute-resolution #western-us

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by [[Gail Bingham]] of RESOLVE, Inc. for the Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission (October 1997), assesses how [[Alternative Dispute Resolution]] (ADR) has been applied to water resources conflicts in the western United States. It defines ADR as procedures that involve a formal role for a neutral, primarily [[Mediation]], and briefly describes [[Arbitration]], [[Early Neutral Evaluation]] and [[Mini-Trial]] processes. The report argues that water disputes stem from both efficiency concerns (time and cost) and quality concerns (outcomes that poorly meet parties' needs). It reviews documented cases, institutional mechanisms, lessons learned and open questions, and concludes that ADR is a useful but not universal tool that works best under clear objectives, voluntary and inclusive participation, adequate resources and reasonable deadlines.

## Background & Description

**Scope.** The report considers western water matters broadly, including water rights, dam operations, flood control, endangered species, wetlands, water quality permitting, fisheries management and recreation access. It takes a narrower view of ADR, focusing on processes with a formal neutral. Traditional settlement negotiations without a neutral are acknowledged as often effective but are not treated as ADR.

**Purpose.** The paper asks why people seek new dispute approaches, what ADR has accomplished, what concerns exist, and when ADR should or should not be used. It emphasizes that ADR is not only an alternative to [[Litigation]]; administrative rulemaking, legislative action and environmental review also handle these disputes.

**Process types described:**
- **Mediation and facilitation:** the most common form. The mediator does not decide outcomes; decision-making power stays with the parties. Mediators convene parties, design processes, record agreements and help anticipate implementation problems.
- **Variants:** [[Negotiated Rulemaking]], policy dialogues, [[Joint Fact-Finding]], facilitation and partnering (mainly in construction contracts).
- **Special masters and referees:** used in adjudications, for example in the Gila and Little Colorado River adjudications in Arizona.
- **Early neutral evaluation and mini-trials:** tools for overcoming differing predictions of court outcomes.

**Institutional context.** The [[Negotiated Rulemaking Act]] (1990) and the [[Administrative Dispute Resolution Act]] encourage federal agencies to use ADR. Montana amended its stream adjudication statute in 1997 to let the [[Montana Water Court]] assign cases to mediators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report is focused on governance and dispute resolution rather than ecology, but it repeatedly ties disputes to aquatic resources:

- **[[Cui-Ui]]:** an endangered fish central to the culture of the Pyramid Lake Paiutes. It is long-lived and does not need to spawn every year. Non-Indian diversions in the Truckee and Carson systems have made spawning almost impossible. A negotiated arrangement between Sierra Pacific and the Tribe stored claimed water in upstream reservoirs for release as spawning flows in wet years, with Sierra Pacific using it for urban supply in drought years.
- **Pacific salmon:** central to the [[Endangered Species Act]] disputes on the mid-Columbia River and to the historically rich salmon fishery supporting the Umatilla, Cayuse and Walla Walla tribes.
- **Chinook fishery:** downstream of the Don Pedro project, where instream flows were debated against municipal supply.
- **Fish passage at Red Bluff:** the Bureau of Reclamation's Sacramento River diversion dam had fish ladders, but populations were declining to levels of concern.
- **Other habitat issues:** American River cottonwood stands, nutrient loading in the Patuxent River, and endangered-species habitat under the [[Watershed Approach]].

The report also notes that forecasts of ecosystem impacts are controversial because ecological understanding is still developing and more information could always be gathered.

## Key Findings

**1. Why ADR is sought.** Two concerns motivate it: efficiency (time and cost to reach decisions) and quality (decisions that do not meet parties' needs). Pressures include [[Winters Doctrine]] Indian water rights claims and environmental demands for water for threatened and endangered species in watersheds that are fully or over-allocated.

**2. Litigation timing data.** A 1983 Judicial Conference search cited in the report found a median duration of 10 months for environmental cases filed in U.S. District Court (top 10% took three to four years). For cases that went to trial, the median was 23 months (top 10% took five to six years). The report also notes that 90% of all lawsuits eventually settle.

**3. Scale of ADR use.** Between 1974 and 1985, nearly 200 environmental disputes were mediated in the U.S., about 10% involving water. Virtually no environmental matters were arbitrated. Growth since then is not quantified because no comprehensive database has been maintained. The formal mediation of environmental disputes began in 1974 with the Snoqualmie River flood control dispute in Washington.

**4. Scale of parties.** The Gila River adjudication involves approximately 24,000 parties, and the Snake River adjudication may involve 150,000. Mediated negotiations typically reduce the table through group representation, which raises fairness and transparency concerns.

**5. Selected successful cases:**
- **[[San Luis Rey Settlement]]:** mediated by Clyde A. Romney; settlement principles were embodied in P.L. 100-675 (1988) and a $30 million trust fund was appropriated.
- **[[Umatilla Basin Project]]:** mediation after objections to Oregon approvals allowed the project to proceed and guaranteed Columbia River water for fish flows.
- **Red Bluff fish passage:** CDR Associates facilitated two meetings, producing agreement on a plan development approach and interim gate operations.
- **Mid-Columbia Habitat Conservation Plan:** three public utility districts developed 50-year plans with federal, state and tribal participation.
- **[[Clark Fork Relicensing]]:** convened by Washington Water Power with RESOLVE assistance in July 1996, involving about 25 entities. Four working groups meet almost monthly. The license application is due February 1999.
- **[[Don Pedro Hydroelectric Project]]:** FERC used Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service mediators; the license was amended to incorporate the settlement.
- **[[Puyallup River Watershed]]:** the Washington Department of Ecology, U.S. EPA and the Puyallup Indian Tribe convened a Water Quality Mediation Committee addressing TMDLs, loading capacity and NPDES permit limits.
- **[[Patuxent River]]:** a two-stage process (scientists' joint report, then about 40 stakeholders) produced a compromise on nutrient control. This example is outside the West.
- **San Diego emergency storage:** a 27-member Emergency Storage Working Committee met 7 or 8 times; 32 options were narrowed to 13 and then to four alternatives for NEPA/CEQA review.
- **[[Drinking Water Standards]] negotiation:** EPA convened 17 parties in 1992-1993, who met ten times over ten months and reached agreement on three rules (DBP Rule, Enhanced Surface Water Treatment Rule, Information Collection Rule).
- **[[Lower American River Task Force]]:** formed February 1993 with 34 members, growing to 41; completed six phases, each ending in agreement. Its first phase was completed in November 1996 and passed the January 1997 "storm of record" without flood damage.

**6. Limitations acknowledged.** The second round of [[Truckee Carson Settlement]] negotiations led by the report's author did not reach the intended region-wide agreement. ADR is not a panacea; it will not resolve all cases, and it is unsuitable for legal interpretation or avoiding legal requirements.

**7. Consensus principles.** The report's guiding principles include Purpose Driven, Inclusive, Voluntary, Self Design, Flexibility, Equal Opportunity, Respect for Diverse Interests, Accountability, Time Limits and Implementation. It draws on [[Getting to Yes]] (interests over positions) and Canadian Round Tables on the Environment and Economy.

**8. Specific challenges.** Water disputes involve intra-organizational complexity, boundary-crossing flows, unclear incentives, finite supply, scientific uncertainty, unequal resources (including pro se parties) and public/political dimensions. Governance laws such as the [[Federal Advisory Committee Act]] and FOIA are seen as improving perceived legitimacy rather than inhibiting ADR.

**9. Open questions.** The report lists unresolved issues: criteria for when ADR is appropriate, mandatory mediation, cultural biases, safeguards for public involvement, government roles, payment, mediator qualifications, and whether institutionalization will reduce flexibility.

**10. Recommendations.**
- *State governments:* enact ADR authorization laws similar to the federal ADRA, with voluntary participation, confidentiality, neutral disclosure and state offices of dispute resolution.
- *Congress:* amend the Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act and NEPA at reauthorization to allow (not require) ADR at identified decision points, with FACA compliance and funding for joint fact-finding.
- *Research:* fund evaluation of water ADR through bodies such as the National Academy of Sciences and the National Science Foundation.
- *Pilot programs:* fund mediation pilots at the Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, National Marine Fisheries Service and state agencies.
- *General practice:* engage early, define objectives collaboratively, set groundrules, ensure all affected interests can participate, invest in joint fact-finding, and require neutrals to be skilled and continually trained.

**11. Core conclusion.** ADR has shown positive results for water disputes when objectives are clear, participation is voluntary and inclusive, incentives to settle exist, resources are adequate, constituencies are kept informed and deadlines are reasonable. Not every attempt will succeed, and the report argues that the field should embrace scrutiny to improve.
