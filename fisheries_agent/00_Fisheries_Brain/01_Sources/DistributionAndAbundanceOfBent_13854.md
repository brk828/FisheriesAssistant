# Distribution and Abundance of Benthic Invertebrates in a Sonoran Desert Stream

#benthic-invertebrates #freshwater-ecology #entomology

## Executive Summary

This study examined the seasonal and longitudinal distribution of [[Benthic Invertebrates]] in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a low-elevation hot-desert stream in the [[Sonoran Desert]] of Arizona. The creek had escaped channelization, impoundment and desiccation, and no earlier published work existed on Sonoran Desert stream benthos. Sampling ran for 13 months (April 1975 to April 1976) at eight stations above, within and below a canyon.

Invertebrate numbers and biomass tracked standing crops of [[Filamentous Algae]], mainly [[Cladophora Glomerata]], which suggests that autochthonous production is important in the stream's food web. [[Flash Floods]] in summer sharply reduced populations, but recovery was consistent. Annual mean standing crops were comparable to those of streams in regions with less frequent and less intense floods.

## Background & Description

The authors note that Sonoran Desert streams were disappearing under increasing human activity, which made baseline ecological data urgent. Aravaipa Creek drains about 1500 km² and has a permanent reach rising from sand and gravel about 6 km northwest of Klondyke, Arizona, at 1010 m elevation. Most of its channels are dry except after heavy rain. In flood, the stream reaches the [[San Pedro River]] at 660 m.

The stream runs through a steep-walled canyon cut in Tertiary andesites and Precambrian rocks, with walls often over 250 m high. Upland vegetation grades from desert grassland to Lower Sonoran Desert, with [[Riparian Vegetation]] of deciduous woodland and shrubs along the banks.

Air temperatures range from under 5 °C to over 40 °C. Mean annual precipitation at Klondyke (1952–72) was 34.7 cm, with a range of 19.4 to 54.9 cm. Rainfall peaks in July and August. Pools are scarce, and most habitat consists of riffles and runs.

**Methods (summary):** Benthic invertebrates were collected about every six weeks with a [[Surber Sampler]] (929 cm², 228 µm mesh), taking two samples from gravel and cobble at each station (one from coarse sand at Station I). Samples were preserved in 10% formalin, sorted by air elutriation, and identified with keys of Usinger (1956) and Edmondson (1959). Physical and chemical variables were recorded, including diel dissolved oxygen, temperature, pH and conductance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical setting**
- Water temperature reached 25–28 °C at most stations, with a maximum of 35 °C near Station VIII. This exceeds the thermal tolerances of resident fishes.
- Discharge is highly seasonal. Summer cyclonic storms produce spates, and on 25 July 1975 discharge rose from 1.16 to 8.63 m³/s in 15 minutes.
- Canyon walls concentrate flood energy, while desert side-canyon runoff often affects only part of the mainstream.
- Water is circumneutral near the source and becomes alkaline downstream (pH 7–9 to 8.9). Conductance is generally 400–500 µmhos/cm at 25 °C.

**Biological setting**
- Seven native fish species occur: [[Gila Mountain-Sucker]], [[Gila Coarse-Scaled Sucker]], [[Colorado Chub]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Gila Spikedace]]. The [[Green Sunfish]] is the only non-native species that persists. Benthic invertebrates are the principal food of all fishes except the mountain-sucker, which grazes periphyton.
- [[Watercress]] is the dominant emergent macrophyte. It declines in summer floods and regrows in autumn.
- Diatoms are common on stony substrate. Filamentous algae are scarce in the canyon, most abundant downstream, and common above the gorge where light is strong.

## Key Findings

- **Community size:** 63 invertebrate taxa were collected, 56 of them insects. At least 60 insect species were estimated from riffles, a diversity comparable to most streams in Patrick's (1975) survey.
- **Density and biomass:** Station I on coarse sand had the highest values, at 62,289 individuals/m² and 29.9 g/m². These were significantly higher (95% confidence) than downstream riffle stations. The highest riffle density was 20,448 individuals/m² at Station VII, and the greatest biomass was 10.3 g/m² at Station III.
- **Algae-invertebrate link:** Numbers and biomass rose with filamentous algae. Invertebrates were much less abundant in heavily shaded canyon reaches (Stations IV–VI) than in exposed reaches (Stations VII–VIII), with a statistically significant difference at 95% confidence.
- **Light as a driver:** Canyon walls and riparian shade limit [[Cladophora Glomerata]] growth and therefore indirectly reduce the benthos.
- **Flood effects:** The July 1975 flood severely reduced [[Baetis]] spp., *Choroterpes inornata*, [[Tricorythodes]] spp., *Ochrotrichia* spp., [[Chironomidae]] and Oligochaeta. Baetis, *C. inornata*, chironomids and oligochaetes recovered by December. *Tricorythodes* and *Ochrotrichia* did not recover to earlier levels.
- **Simuliid response:** [[Simuliidae]] larvae increased in July and September despite flooding, likely because of abundant fine detritus in transport.
- **Seasonal life cycle:** Numbers rose from October to December as early instars hatched, then fell through March as mortality occurred. Biomass rose as animals matured. A sharp biomass drop in April indicates that many insects had emerged. Densities stayed roughly constant because new hatching balanced emergence.
- **Detritus:** Summer floods import large amounts of fine detritus. Much of it is retained in the substrate and stays relatively constant over time.
- **Comparison with other streams:** Aravaipa Creek's annual mean density was 12,240/m², which exceeds most streams in Table 4. Its biomass (6.9 g/m²) is somewhat low. The authors attribute the high numbers partly to fine-mesh sampling (228 µm), which retains small invertebrates that coarse nets miss. Small body size may also be favored by flooding.
- **Authors' conclusions:** Populations fluctuated with current, substrate, light and algae, seasonal life cycles, and flood disturbance. Flooding may act as a selective force favoring short-lived, small-bodied species, and the authors predict unusually rapid generation times in these taxa.
