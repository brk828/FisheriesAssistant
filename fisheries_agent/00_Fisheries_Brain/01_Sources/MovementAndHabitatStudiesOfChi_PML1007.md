# Chinook Salmon and White Sturgeon Movement and Habitat Studies in the Columbia Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #acipenseridae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 technical report by J. M. Haynes of [[Pacific Northwest Laboratory]], prepared for the U.S. Department of Energy, presents two radiotelemetry studies in the Columbia River Basin. Part 1 examines the depth distribution, movements and dam passage of adult [[Chinook Salmon]] in the lower [[Snake River]] during 1975–1977, with attention to gas supersaturation from spill over hydroelectric dams. Part 2 examines the seasonal movements and habitat preferences of [[White Sturgeon]] in the mid-Columbia River (Hanford reach) in 1975–1977. The report also assesses the effects of transmitter attachment and tagging method on fish behavior and survival, including tag loss.

## Background & Description

The report is organized in two chapters, each with its own introduction, methods, results and discussion.

- **Chapter 1 (Chinook Salmon):** Studies of adult *Oncorhynchus tshawytscha* were conducted between [[Ice Harbor Dam]] and [[Lower Granite Dam]], with releases at Texas Rapids and above [[Little Goose Dam]]. Salmon were captured at the Little Goose Dam fish ladder, radiotagged and released. Some 64 additional salmon served as tagged-but-unradiotagged controls.
- **Chapter 2 (White Sturgeon):** Radiotagged *Acipenser transmontanus* (fork-length and weight data reported in the chapter) were monitored in the [[Hanford Reach]] near [[White Bluffs Pool]], Washington, from 1975 to 1977.
- **Equipment:** Transmitters were built by the [[University of Minnesota Bioelectronics Laboratory]], operated at 53 MHz, and distinguished over 100 discrete crystal-tuned frequencies. Transmitter life was 10–20 days for salmon; sturgeon transmitters lasted about 1–6 months.

Background context includes the decline of Snake River chinook runs linked to [[Gas Bubble Disease]], which arises when river water supersaturated with dissolved gas from spill over dams enters fish tissues.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Gas supersaturation:** Spring run-off spilling over dams raised total dissolved gas to 120–130% in spring 1976 (values recorded below Little Goose Dam in Table 1 ranged from 101% to 129%). Gas supersaturation falls by about 10% per meter of depth due to hydrostatic pressure. Saturation below 110% was not considered harmful to adult salmon.
- **Temperature:** Columbia River temperatures rose from about 9.0 to 14.5 °C during the spring salmon study. Transmitter pulse rates were not noticeably affected by normal river temperature ranges.
- **Sturgeon thermal ecology:** Movements began in June once water temperatures exceeded about 13 °C and ceased in autumn as temperatures fell back to 13 °C. Sturgeon moved into shallow warm water to feed in late afternoon and evening, returning to cooler mid-channel areas after midnight.
- **Hanford Reach habitat:** The mid-Columbia is largely free-flowing; [[White Bluffs Pool]] contains islands, deep channels and back eddies. Sloughs and back-eddies reached 2–3 °C above the main current in summer.

## Key Findings

**Chinook Salmon (Chapter 1)**

- Mean swimming depths of spring 1976 externally tagged salmon were significantly greater than those of fall 1976 and spring 1977 fish (p < 0.05). With internally tagged salmon excluded, spring 1976 and spring 1977 depths differed highly significantly (p < 0.01).
- Mean depths were 4.83 m (spring 1976 internal), 5.49 m (spring 1976 external), 3.99 m (fall 1976) and 3.68 m (spring 1977); the HSD value was 0.87 m.
- Under spill-generated supersaturation (120–130%), salmon travelled at deeper depths; when gas levels fell below 108%, average travel depths were 3.0 and 4.0 m, below the critical zone.
- Externally tagged salmon passed Little Goose Dam more successfully than internally tagged fish (spring 1975 and 1977 data in [[Percent Passage]] table). In spring 1977, 54% of controls and 83% of externally radiotagged salmon were known to cross Little Goose Dam after release at Texas Rapids (values as reported in the text).
- No internally radiotagged salmon crossed Little Goose Dam in spring 1976; 40% of such fish moved to Little Goose Dam area.
- Passage at [[Lower Granite Dam]] was comparable to NMFS studies, and travel times from Little Goose to Lower Granite were similar between tagged and control groups.
- Internal tag insertion was deeper than the NMFS method, probably contributing to passage failure, and high fall 1976 water temperatures added handling stress.
- Salmon released above Little Goose Dam showed extended delays (2–6 weeks), repeated drop-back episodes and deeper swimming in the spill basin, suggesting a detrimental interaction with the dam.
- Authors suggest reasons for the Little Goose delays: pumped-water fish ladder, possible sensory disruption by spill, alarm substances from crowded fish, and avoidance of the trap mechanism.
- Electronic drift in transmitters was eliminated by changes in design in spring 1977; depth precision improved from ±0.5 m to ±0.2 m.
- Tag attachment: External and internal attachment were judged equally reliable when properly installed, with no significant differences in percent return or travel times between controls and externally tagged fish.

**White Sturgeon (Chapter 2)**

- Radiotagged sturgeon in the study (mean length 129.5 ± 19.1 cm for the group reported) showed three movement groups: downstream movers (mean length 94.7 ± 14.5 cm), upstream movers (182.9 ± 32.7 cm) and local movers (about 129 cm); size differences among groups were significant.
- Downstream-moving sturgeon averaged 35.5 ± 32.7 km net and 59.3 ± 23.9 km total distance; upstream-moving sturgeon averaged 12.2 ± 14.5 km net and 25.1 ± 17.3 km total distance (as reported).
- Movements were seasonal, from June to October, and ceased when river temperatures fell below 13 °C.
- Sturgeon environmental temperatures were significantly higher in late afternoon and evening, indicating movements into shallow warm areas for feeding.
- Tag losses occurred 1–8 months after release; one sturgeon was recaptured two years after release with no visible tag scars.
- The authors note that hydroelectric dams likely preclude long-distance movements for sturgeon in the Columbia River.

**Overall Conclusions**

- Telemetry revealed behaviours that catch counts alone cannot, but the authors caution that tagging itself can alter fish movements; hence control groups and tag-type comparisons were essential.
- Dam operations, especially spill and turbine regimes, strongly shaped salmon depths and passage delays.
