# Some Physiological Consequences of Angling Stress in Muskellunge

## Executive Summary
This study (Beggs, Holeton & Crossman, 1980) examined the physiological effects of angling-induced stress on wild [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy*) captured near Bobcaygeon, Ontario, in 1975–1976. Fish were angled, anaesthetized, cannulated, and monitored for blood acid–base status, lactate, haematology, and plasma electrolytes over recovery periods of up to 84 h. Angling produced a marked but transient metabolic **acidaemia** (blood pH dropping to ~7.15, versus a predicted normal of ~7.7 at 23°C) accompanied by elevated **lactic acid**, and reductions in **total CO₂** and **bicarbonate**. Full acid–base recovery took 12–18 h, but this was paradoxically accompanied by a 22% decline in haemoglobin and 40% decline in haematocrit. Plasma sodium and potassium remained largely stable in survivors, suggesting minimal ionoregulatory disruption. Notably, 30% of angled muskellunge died post-capture — a comparatively high mortality rate — despite blood lactate levels that were lower than typically reported in other exhaustively exercised fish species. This suggests that mortality was not simply attributable to lactic acidosis, and that muskellunge, though a "sedentary" species, are not as poorly adapted to angling-induced exercise stress as originally hypothesized, although actual field angling durations (often >30 min for large fish) could produce more severe outcomes than the experimental conditions tested.

## Background & Description
- **Body/muscle composition:** Axial (white) muscle comprises approximately **56% of total body weight** (P. Webb, pers. comm.), composed almost exclusively of fast-twitch white fibres characteristic of "sprinter" locomotory morphology, adapted for short bursts of high-speed swimming rather than sustained activity.
- **Size range sampled:** 1.25–4.25 kg body mass; 61.9–91.8 cm total length.
- **Circulatory parameters (baseline/recovery, hour 0):**
  - Haemoglobin: ~5.7–5.8 g/100 ml
  - Haematocrit: ~25%
  - Estimated total blood volume: ~5% of body weight ([[Randall 1970]] estimate)
- **Buffering characteristics:** Non-bicarbonate buffer value (β) estimated at 8 meq/l/pH unit ([[Piiper Et Al 1972]]), consistent with values used for other elasmobranch/teleost comparisons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** [[Muskellunge]] are largely **sedentary**, occupying vegetated, shallow-water habitats or remaining motionless within cover; they are not adapted to prolonged high-intensity activity.
- **Behavioral activity patterns:**
  1. Short sprint bursts (once or twice daily) for predation on fish prey.
  2. Moderately long directed movements (1–3 days) once yearly around spawning at low water temperatures.
- **Thermal regime:** Occupies habitats reaching maximum temperatures of ~30°C in August; experimental respirometer water temperatures averaged 19.7°C (range 11.5–24.5°C).
- **Life-history traits:** Slow-growing, long-lived, late to reach sexual maturity — traits that necessitate minimum size regulations in sport fisheries, resulting in frequent catch-and-release angling encounters and repeated exposure to angling stress.
- **Trophic role:** Apex/opportunistic piscivore relying on short, powerful sprint attacks rather than sustained pursuit, consistent with its white-muscle-dominated physiology.
- Comparisons drawn to related [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) and other species such as [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*), and [[Starry Flounder]] (*Platichthys stellatus*) regarding lactate/pH stress dynamics.

## Key Findings

### Acid–Base and Metabolic Response
- Angling capture caused venous blood pH to drop to as low as **~7.15** (vs. predicted normal ~7.7 at 23°C), representing significant **metabolic acidaemia**.
- Acidaemia was **most severe immediately post-capture** and began recovering **before** blood lactate peaked — an important temporal dissociation between H⁺ and lactate kinetics.
- Blood lactate levels in muskellunge were **lower than typically reported for fatigue** in other actively-exercised teleosts (e.g., trout, salmon), possibly indicating more efficient lactate elimination/clearance or slower release from white muscle — potentially an adaptation to burst-swimming lifestyle, similar to patterns in some marine flatfish ([[Wood Et Al 1977]]; [[Wardle 1978]]).
- Total plasma CO₂ initially decreased, then increased and stabilized during later recovery; the bicarbonate fraction rose to ~96% of total CO₂.
- Estimated buffering: non-bicarbonate buffers accounted for ~4.4 meq H⁺/l, bicarbonate buffering ~26 meq H⁺/l; total combined buffering ≈ **7.0 meq H⁺/l**, exceeding the ~2.5 meq H⁺/l attributable to measured dissociated lactate — suggesting **uneven distribution of H⁺ and lactate ions** between muscle and blood compartments, consistent with findings in [[Scyliorhinus Stellaris]] (larger spotted dogfish) studied by Piiper et al. (1972) and Holeton & Heisler (1978).
- Full acid–base recovery required **12–18 h**; oxygen consumption rose ~87% above minimal resting recovery levels shortly after angling, likely fueling lactate metabolism and CO₂ production for bicarbonate restoration.

### Haematological Changes
- Haemoglobin concentration declined by **22%** and haematocrit by **40%** during recovery — changes far exceeding what could be attributed to experimental blood sampling losses (~5% of total blood volume).
- These declines could not be fully explained by haemodilution (via branchial water influx, per [[Stevens 1968]]; [[Stevens 1972]]) since plasma ion concentrations remained stable, which would be expected to drop under simple dilution.
- A brief **haemoconcentration** was noted shortly after operative procedures (second anaesthetization alone increased haemoglobin by ~0.6 g/100 ml and haematocrit by ~3%), distinct from the much larger subsequent declines attributable to angling stress itself.

### Electrolyte Balance
- Plasma **sodium** concentrations remained relatively stable throughout recovery in both survivors and dying fish, and were higher than reported for [[Northern Pike]] (Soivio & Oikari, 1976).
- Plasma **potassium** fluctuated without clear trend in survivors but was **elevated in dying fish**, especially during early recovery and up to the point of death — potassium elevation combined with declining pH characterized the terminal stages preceding mortality.
- Overall, stable ion levels indicate **no severe ionoregulatory dysfunction** resulted from angling stress, in survivors or (for sodium) even in fish that ultimately died.

### Mortality Patterns
- **30% mortality** among angled/cannulated muskellunge (8 of 25) — notably higher than reported for some other sportfish.
- Mortality classified as:
  - **Immediate** (within 6 h of stress): characterized by abrupt pH decline with minimum pH recorded immediately prior to death.
  - **Delayed** (after 36 h): blood pH initially rose (similar to survivors) but declined after ~24 h until death; lactate peaks were generally higher and occurred later than in survivors.
  - No mortalities occurred between 6–36 h post-capture.
- Cause of death was **not conclusively identified**; severe lactic acidosis alone did not appear sufficient to explain mortality, since lactate levels in dying fish did not differ significantly from survivors and were lower than lethal thresholds reported in comparable studies (e.g., [[Parker Et Al 1959]]; [[Miles Et Al 1974]]).
- Terminal physiological signature: **declining blood pH + elevated plasma potassium**.
- Field validation: 73% of released, tagged fish were recaptured up to one year later via trap-netting/electro-shocking, indicating good long-term survival for fish that survived the initial post-release period.
- Authors caution that real-world angling (with longer fight durations, especially for larger fish, often >30 min) could produce **higher mortality** than observed under the controlled experimental conditions (mean handling time 22 min, mean struggle time 4 min).

### Methodological Controls
- Effects of **anaesthesia and cannulation alone** (via re-anaesthetization trials) were minor compared to angling stress: pH dropped only ~0.07 units (vs. ~0.65 units from angling + anaesthesia), confirming that observed physiological disturbances were driven primarily by the angling/capture stress itself rather than experimental procedures.

### Comparative Context
- Findings align with, but also diverge from, prior angling-stress studies in [[Chinook Salmon]] (Parker & Black, 1959; Parker et al., 1959) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (Bouck & Ball, 1966; Wydoski et al., 1976), extending this physiological stress framework to a large-bodied, white-muscle-dominated freshwater [[Esocidae|esocid]] species for the first time.
- Supports growing evidence (e.g., in [[Scyliorhinus Stellaris]] and [[Starry Flounder]]) that **lactate and H⁺ ions can become kinetically decoupled** during post-exercise recovery in fish, implicating gill/branchial ion exchange mechanisms (e.g., bicarbonate uptake) as a compensatory pathway.
