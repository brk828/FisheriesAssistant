# Central Arizona Project and Nonnative Aquatic Species in the Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #nonnative-species #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) background document, dated April 17, 2001, supports a biological opinion to the Bureau of Reclamation on how transporting and delivering water through the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) may introduce and spread nonnative aquatic species in the [[Gila River Basin]] (excluding the Santa Cruz River subbasin). The opinion addresses eight listed fishes and the [[Bald Eagle]]. The document concludes that CAP will substantially increase opportunities for nonnative species to invade native fish habitats, and that the likelihood of controlling invaders to prevent serious loss of listed species diminishes substantially with CAP operating. It sets out the consultation history, the status of each listed species, the existing nonnative fauna, dispersal mechanisms, and likely invasion routes, and it describes the conservation measures that were adopted.

## Background & Description

The document was prepared to accompany a reinitiated consultation requested by Reclamation on January 3, 2001. It replaces the 1994 biological opinion, which the courts found to be partly flawed, and it summarizes 45 informal and 17 formal consultations on CAP since 1983 covering 13 listed species, six of them fishes.

Listed species considered:
- **Endangered:** [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Gila Trout]]
- **Threatened:** [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Apache Trout]], [[Bald Eagle]]

Key species descriptions:
- **Spikedace** is a small silvery fish with a spine in the dorsal fin. It lives about two years, feeds on insects, and is now known only from the Verde, middle Gila and upper Gila Rivers, and Aravaipa and Eagle Creeks. It is common only in Aravaipa Creek.
- **Loach minnow** is a small bottom-dweller of swift water. Its range has been reduced by about 85 percent. It is common only in Aravaipa Creek, the Blue River, and limited portions of the San Francisco, upper Gila and Tularosa Rivers.
- **Gila topminnow** is a small live-bearing poeciliid. Historically one of the most common fishes in the basin, it now survives in 12 natural populations, of which only three (Cienega Creek, Monkey Spring, Cottonwood Spring) are free of nonnative fish and considered secure. It is described as "predator and competitor-naive."
- **Razorback sucker** can exceed 60 cm and may live about 50 years. The largest wild population, in Lake Mohave, declined from over 60,000 individuals in the early 1980s to fewer than 10,000 in the late 1990s.
- **Desert pupfish** was extirpated from the Gila basin by the mid-1900s. Only two populations are extant in the basin (Boyce-Thompson Arboretum and Cold Springs Seep).
- **Colorado squawfish** was extirpated from the basin by 1970 and has been reintroduced to the Verde and upper Salt Rivers with limited success.
- **Gila trout** and **Apache trout** have recovered through removal of nonnative trout and repatriation. Hybridization with rainbow trout has been a primary threat.
- **Bald eagle** in Arizona nests mostly on cliffs. Upper Salt River productivity fell from 0.67 young per nest in the 1980s to 0.26 in the 1990s, which the document links to the replacement of native suckers by catfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Environmental baseline.** The document reports 40 nonnative fish species established and reproducing in the Gila River basin, with at least 24 more reported but not established or of unknown status. Other nonnative groups include:
- **Amphibians:** bullfrog, Rio Grande leopard frog, tiger salamander, and African clawed frog (recorded, with concern it may spread).
- **Reptiles:** several turtles (some established), American alligator, spectacled caiman, and water monitor.
- **Invertebrates:** Asian clam (widespread), northern crayfish, red swamp crayfish (lower Gila), a nonnative zooplankton (*Daphnia lumholtzi*) and tubificid worm in Salt River reservoirs.
- **Plants:** water cress (ubiquitous), Eurasian water-milfoil, salt cedar and other riparian species.
- **Pathogens:** Asian tapeworm (recently found in the Gila River below Ashurst-Hayden Dam), anchor worm, and 68 fish pathogens recorded from the basin.

**Pathways of spread.** The document identifies these mechanisms: interbasin water transfer (the primary CAP mechanism), sport stocking, aquaculture and aquarium releases (about 26% of past U.S. fish introductions), bait-bucket release (Ludwig and Leitch found 28.5% of commercial bait purchases contained illegal species), and biological control. The CAP aqueduct links formerly fragmented subbasins, which the document says will mainly benefit nonnative species.

**Environmental degradation.** Human population growth tied to CAP water, groundwater pumping, and recreation are expected to deepen habitat loss and increase nonnative introductions. Reclamation's position that growth would occur without CAP is disputed in the document.

## Key Findings

- **Scope and history:** CAP-related consultation began in 1983. The 1994 opinion (found to be partly flawed by the courts in 2000) is being reconsidered.
- **Conservation measures:** The 2001 project description adopts the 1994 reasonable and prudent alternative plus additional measures. These include physical barriers, monitoring, "recovery in-lieu of threat removal," nonnative management, and education. The document accepts a "last ditch stand" concept that focuses protection on native fish habitats rather than on the aqueduct.
- **Documented CAP invasions:** Striped bass entered the aqueduct and reached Lake Pleasant (first found 1993). White bass moved along the aqueduct to the Brady pumping plant. Pacu appeared in Lake Pleasant by late 1999. The Asian clam is common in the aqueduct, and Asian tapeworm was found in the Gila River in 1998. Fish from the Colorado River pass through the intake pumps.
- **Probability over time:** The document calculates that a bait-bucket release probability of 0.0117 per angler-day reaches 99.6 percent certainty across a year with 19 million angler-days, and that CAP-mediated introductions will become near certain within the 100-year project life (280–350 delivery days per year).
- **Predicted effects:** The document concludes that nonnative species delivered by CAP will have an enormous potential to harm spikedace, loach minnow, Gila topminnow, razorback sucker, desert pupfish and Colorado squawfish. Extirpation of one or more populations of spikedace, loach minnow and Gila topminnow is likely over the project life.
- **Bald eagle risk:** About 24% of desert-nesting bald eagle territories are in areas of direct impact from CAP-mediated nonnative species.
- **Priority threats:** Giant salvinia (found in the lower Colorado in 1999) could extirpate Gila topminnow and desert pupfish. Red swamp crayfish, zebra mussel, swamp eel and inland silverside are listed as high-concern potential invaders.
- **Legal framework:** Executive Orders 11987 (1977) and 13112 (1999) and the Nonindigenous Aquatic Species Prevention and Control Act of 1990 guide federal policy against introductions.
- **Core conclusion:** Control of nonnative species is "the single most important requirement" for native fish survival in the basin, and CAP makes that control substantially harder.
