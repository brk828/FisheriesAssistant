# Woundfin Recovery Plan (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, 1979)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The Woundfin Recovery Plan, prepared by the Woundfin Recovery Team and dated April 15, 1979, sets out a management strategy for [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), a cyprinid minnow listed as endangered since 1967. The Virgin River in Utah, Arizona and Nevada is the only stream where the species is now viable. The plan's overall objective is to prevent extinction and secure survival through protection of the Virgin River population, restoration of at least two additional self-sustaining populations in separate drainages, and staged downlisting to Threatened status followed by delisting. The document also contains the team's literature cited, a step-down outline, a costed action schedule (about $320,000 over five years), agency review letters with responses, a population monitoring protocol, reintroduction criteria, and the 1977–1979 critical habitat rulemaking history.

## Background & Description

The species is a streamlined, highly silvery minnow with a flattened head, reduced eyes, sensory buds and a sharp, solidified dorsal spine that gives it its name. It is essentially scaleless and rarely exceeds 75 mm standard length. The type specimen was described by [[Edward Drinker Cope]] in 1874. The woundfin belongs to the tribe Plagopterini, which contains three genera (*Meda*, *Plagopterus* and *Lepidomeda*), and the group is endemic to the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] and its former tributary, the White River.

Historic range included the Salt River near Tempe, the Gila River near Yuma, the lower Colorado River, the Virgin River system and LaVerkin Creek. Four transplant attempts in Arizona and Utah have failed. The species was first listed in 1967 under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Woundfin tolerate harsh conditions, including summer temperatures up to 37.6°C and high turbidity, which limit competition and predation from exotic fishes. Adults prefer runs over sand bottoms at mean depths of about 25 cm (±19) and mean current of 0.42 m/s (±0.28) (Cross, 1975). Larvae use shallow inshore backwaters before moving to deeper, swifter water.

Associated native fishes include [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Virgin Spinedace]] and [[Gila Robusta]]. The introduced [[Red Shiner]] largely replaces woundfin in modified reaches. Predators include piscivorous birds, soft-shelled turtles, and non-native [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]].

Food is largely aquatic insect larvae (especially Diptera), sand, amorphous organic matter and filamentous algae. Reproduction is thought to begin in late May, with spawning reported at 14.5°C in April 1977. Highest juvenile mortality occurs from October through December, linked to low flows and low temperatures.

Habitat loss is the main limiting factor: six dams on the Salt and Verde rivers, two on the Gila, Hoover Dam and Lake Mead, irrigation diversions, canals, and return flows with exotic species and pollutants have removed or degraded most habitat.

## Key Findings

- **Status and goal:** The only viable population is in the Virgin River. The plan aims to downlist to Threatened once Virgin River habitat is stabilized and one more self-sustaining population exists; delisting requires a third population.
- **Self-sustaining criterion:** Transplanted populations must exceed 100,000 adults and persist at least 10 years. Reviewers questioned how this number was derived.
- **Monitoring:** Seven stations on the Virgin River are sampled three times a year (June, September, March), with seining continued until catches fall to 10% or less of the first haul.
- **Research priorities:** Two-year population studies (about $20,000/yr each), a habitat study (about $36,000 over two years) and a hydrologic study (about $30,000/yr for three years) were recommended.
- **Reintroduction sites:** The Gila River above Safford and the Verde River above Horseshoe Reservoir were identified as first-priority sites, based on criteria requiring permanent medium-to-large low-desert streams with few exotic fishes.
- **Budget:** Total estimated cost is $320,000 over five years; the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service carries the largest share ($146,000), followed by the Bureau of Reclamation ($96,000) and Arizona Game and Fish ($36,000).
- **Critical habitat:** The Virgin River from LaVerkin Springs to Lake Mead and lower LaVerkin Creek were proposed on November 2, 1977; the proposal was withdrawn on March 6, 1979 under the 1978 Endangered Species Act amendments and must be re-proposed.
- **Agency review:** Reviewers (Arizona Game and Fish, Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, Bureau of Reclamation, Bureau of Land Management, Forest Service) raised concerns about cost basis, unclear agency funding shares, the feasibility of reintroductions without knowing why the species disappeared, and the need for a single Virgin River habitat management plan. The team's responses accepted many suggestions and declined others, such as removing transplant sites from critical habitat.
