# Workshop Proceedings on Fish Eggs and Larvae: Preservation, Sorting, and Identification of Larval Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #larval-fish

## Executive Summary

This document is a compiled technical publication (National Marine Fisheries Service, Middle Atlantic Coastal Fisheries Center, Technical Publication No. 1, published August 1973). It assembles proceedings from a June 1968 workshop on [[Fish Eggs and Larvae]] held at Bears Bluff Laboratories, Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina, sponsored by the [[Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission]] and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and edited by Anthony L. Pacheco. It contains three main papers: methods for preserving and preparing larval fish and plankton samples; a practical account of sorting costs and procedures; and a guide to morphological characters useful for identifying larval fishes, with discussion transcripts and a bibliography.

## Background & Description

The publication was issued as a service to marine scientists working with fishery resources of the Atlantic shelf. Contributions reflect staff of the Sandy Hook Laboratory and the Miami and Tropical Atlantic Biological Laboratory of the [[Bureau of Commercial Fisheries]], and the document notes that individual contributions represent the authors' own work rather than the center as a whole.

The first paper, *Preserving and Preparing Larval Fishes for Study* (William J. Richards and Frederick H. Berry), covers preservation chemistry, initial and subsequent sorting, storage vials, and special preparations such as clearing and staining, radiography, and illustration.

The second part, the workshop text on [[Sorting Costs]] and subsequent sorting, addresses the economics and curatorial practice of separating eggs and larvae from plankton.

The third paper, *Characters Useful to the Study of Larval Fishes* (Frederick H. Berry and William J. Richards), presents a "dynamic" approach to larval description, covering myomeres and vertebrae, fins, caudal fin ray counts, branchiostegal rays, photophores, gill rakers, head and body features, pigmentation, and morphometrics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document is oriented to plankton-net collections, the main source of larval fish samples. It treats the sample as an assemblage of fish larvae embedded in other plankton, including chaetognaths and filamentous algae, which affect sorting accuracy. Species-level identification depends on knowing the juvenile and adult forms of the local ichthyofauna, because larvae of distantly related forms may look alike while closely related ones may share early appearance.

Preservation is also environmentally framed: heat during shipboard storage, especially in the tropics, can damage collections, and evaporation and acidity threaten long-term holdings. Mesopelagic and bathypelagic fishes are noted as especially difficult to culture and obtain, so taxonomic identification of their eggs and larvae is likely to remain necessary.

## Key Findings

**Preservation**
- Formalin is strongly recommended over [[Ethyl Alcohol]] for preserving plankton and larval samples. Ethanol at 70% causes shrinkage, distortion, and evaporation problems, and the authors regard it as more expensive.
- Routine formalin use is 5 to 10%. Concentrated formalin (37 to 40% formaldehyde) should be diluted at about 1 part to 9 to 19 parts ambient water, yielding roughly a 5 to 10% working solution.
- Borax (sodium tetraborate) is a good buffer: adding 2.5 g per liter to 5% formalin provides immediate buffering, with a useful period of 4 to 5 years. Excess borax can bleach tissues, and pH above 8.0 to 8.3 should be avoided.
- Hexamine (hexamethylenetetramine) is strongly disapproved because it crystallizes around specimens, bleaches larvae, and produces an unstable preservative.
- Adding 1 ml of 1% FD&C Red No. 40 dye per 100 cc of sample helps retain red pigments in fish larvae.
- A sugar-based solution developed by Joseph J. Graham preserves larvae in good condition but not color; the formula includes tap water, NaCl, KCl, boric acid, NaOH, formalin, and sugar, and remains under research.
- Preserved plankton samples should be checked at least once a year for neutral pH. Acidic samples should receive fresh buffered solution rather than buffer added to the sample.

**Storage and labeling**
- Labels placed inside sample containers can be obliterated by agitation, so labels should sit on top of the liquid, with external tags as well.
- Containers should be filled completely to limit sloshing.
- Plankton should be kept away from heat, and temperature-controlled shipboard storage is recommended for material going ashore in good condition.
- Larvae are stored in 5% buffered formalin in 5-dram clear glass vials with nylon cap liners; screw-top vials without liners allow nearly inevitable evaporation.
- Identified larvae can be kept in small vials (about 1.7 x 0.5 cm) with cotton plugs, although larvae adhering to plugs can be overlooked on reexamination.

**Sorting**
- Fish eggs and larvae should be removed before any splitting or subsampling, since fish larvae make up a small proportion of most plankton samples.
- Large fishes (organisms larger than 5 or 10 mm) should be retained with larvae because they aid identification by comparison.
- Larvae must be removed under magnification using eyedroppers, wire loops, or microdissection forceps.
- Well-trained sorters typically overlook less than 5% of fish larvae; the document also records a sorter who missed as many as 50%.
- Sorting costs in one survey ranged from $20 to $56 per sample (100 cc of plankton). A representative cost at the La Jolla Fishery Oceanography Center was about $20 to $25 per sample, covering removal, counting, and identification and measurement of eggs and larvae of three abundant species. Two other large groups reported $50 to $56 per sample for removal and counting only.
- Larval samples should not be separated into smaller subsets until major components and most species are known, because very small specimens may be separated from conspecific larger specimens.

**Identification and morphology**
- Two approaches exist: working backward from adults, or working forward from fertilized eggs from known parents. The authors encourage rearing marine fishes through embryonic and larval stages.
- Myomere-vertebrae counts are generally more useful than caudal fin meristics, because myomere complement is completed early in the larval stage in most species.
- The caudal fin is usually the second component to be completely formed after myomeres. Principal caudal ray counts are conservative within groups; for example, the typical percoid count is 9 + 8, while plectognaths usually have 6 + 6 and synentognaths 7 + 8.
- Gill rakers are of little value for larval identification, while branchiostegal ray counts can be useful at the generic and higher levels.
- Morphometric data should be taken in the sagittal plane and standardized. Measurement schemes should include standard length, notochord length, and body depth, and the authors recommend that the dynamic (developmental series) approach be used.
- Pigmentation changes with growth and preservation; yellow, red, and blue chromatophores fade rapidly after death, so pigment should be examined on living or recently preserved specimens.

**Permanent storage and documentation**
- Larvae can be cleared and stained for cartilage and bone, then kept in pure glycerine with thymol. Taylor's (1967) enzyme method for clearing and staining is cited as excellent.
- Radiography of larvae requires X-raying in vacuum and high-resolution film (1,000 lines per mm), with higher cost than for adult fishes.
- Illustrations of larvae require careful attention to proportion and character accuracy, and should note when several specimens were combined.

**Discussion highlights**
- Participants noted that 40% isopropyl alcohol is useful for larvae but collapses eggs.
- A Battelle Institute study used LDH enzyme electrophoresis to distinguish walleye strains using larvae and eggs, though the authors cautioned that this enzyme is unstable unless frozen.
- Participants recognized an ongoing Smithsonian invertebrate preservation study and a related experiment on plankton preservation.

**Bibliography**: The appended literature list includes key references on larval fish techniques, such as Ahlstrom and Counts (1958), Taylor (1967), Mansueti (1958), and Fretholer (1966), and a bibliography containing about 1,158 citations on fish eggs and larvae.
