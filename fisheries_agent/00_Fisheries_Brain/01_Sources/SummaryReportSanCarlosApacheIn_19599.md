# Fishery Management Program, San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation, Arizona (1965 Summary Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Centrarchidae #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary
This 1966 summary report from the U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife reviews 1965 fishery management on the 1,643,939-acre San Carlos Reservation. The work followed a 1958 agreement among the San Carlos Apache Tribe, the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the Bureau. Management covered 110 stock ponds, three streams and one lake. Total fishing pressure rose from 12,670 to 13,153 man-days, even though use of [[Seneca Lake]] fell. The report documents trout stocking, a new Arizona state-record [[Bluegill]], the loss of stocked [[Mozambique Tilapia]] to cold floodwater, and summer trout die-off in Seneca Lake from heat and low oxygen.

## Background & Description
- Almost all Reservation waters are small livestock [[Stock Tanks]] on intermittent drainages. The Bureau stocks them, and the Tribe opens them to the public on a permit basis.
- Under the 1958 agreement the Bureau provides technical assistance and the fish for stocking.
- Fishing pressure estimates came from Tribal permit sales and visual observations.
- Trout tanks (Table 1) are managed mainly for [[Rainbow Trout]], with [[Channel Catfish]] added in some. Warm-water tanks (Table 2) hold [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Golden Shiner]] and, in one tank, [[Threadfin Shad]].
- The stream fishery is confined to the [[Black River]], [[Salt River]] and [[San Carlos River]], all in rough, largely inaccessible terrain.
- [[Seneca Lake]] was built in 1963, covers 27.5 surface acres and is managed as a put-and-take trout fishery. The Tribe runs a small concession there.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Heavy rain in January 1965 filled most reservoirs and secured summer water supply. The resulting turbid water delayed fishing success until late spring, and cool spring weather also depressed early interest.
- Unusually heavy December 1965 rain and snowmelt filled most tanks again. It washed out the trout tanks Freezeout, Cienega, Geronomo and Deadman, and the warm-water tank Turkey Roost.
- [[Mozambique Tilapia]] planted in 1964 at Warm Springs on the San Carlos River had built nests and appeared to be reproducing. They did not survive the early-1965 cold floodwater, being sensitive to low temperature.
- Seneca Lake on July 21, 1965: surface 81° F, 74° F at 9 ft, 70° F at 10 ft, 67° F at 12 ft and 56° F at 19 ft. Surface pH had reached 9.5.
- Dissolved oxygen measured on September 15, 1965 was insufficient to support trout below 8 ft.
- In Big Boni Tank, young-of-the-year [[Largemouth Bass]] from a successful spawn began competing with rainbow trout for food.

## Key Findings
- **Fishing pressure:** Seneca Lake use fell from 7,072 days (1964) to an estimated 6,000 (1965). Reservation-wide pressure rose from 12,670 to 13,153 man-days because of more stock-tank fishing.
- **Trout program expansion:** Tanks managed for trout increased from 34 to 55, mostly newly surveyed tanks or tanks switched from warm-water species. Most additions were stocked with fingerlings in fall 1965 and so provided no fishing that year. Armstrong, Bone, Big Bonito and Skeleton were dropped because of competition from other fish, poor trout survival and/or inadequate water. Big Boni Tank was moved to the warm-water list.
- **Trout stocking:** 21,100 rainbow trout, mostly large fingerlings, went into tanks. An experimental 400 brook trout fingerlings went into Windy Bob Tank. Seneca Lake received 14,500 catchable rainbow trout (last plant June 11, 1965). Examples from Table 1 include Dry Lake (150 acres, 5,000 rainbow trout), Midnight (1,700 rainbow trout, 600 channel catfish) and Lower Windy (2,300 rainbow trout).
- **Channel catfish:** 3,225 were stocked in trout tanks. They serve as predators that crop stunted panfish but do not reproduce in the tanks, so they do not add to overpopulation.
- **Warm-water tanks:** Stocked with 600 channel catfish, 506 black crappie, 1,740 largemouth bass and 1,000 rainbow trout. Little angling occurs there. Interest rose where rainbow trout or large bluegills could be caught, notably at Big Boni and Soldiers Hole.
- **State record:** An Arizona record [[Bluegill]] came from Soldiers Hole Tank. It was 14 1/8 in long, 15 3/4 in in girth and weighed 3 lb 5 oz, twice the previous record. Many other bluegills there also exceeded the old record.
- **Streams:** 964 largemouth bass were stocked in the San Carlos River and 7,000 smallmouth bass in the Black River. Smallmouth bass planted in 1964 appear to be doing well. They were seen in the Black River as far upstream as Ten of Diamonds Ranch and in the Salt River as far downstream as the Highway 60 crossing.
- **Seneca Lake:** Trout were restricted to water below about 9 ft, and the deeper water lacked oxygen, so rainbow trout apparently did not survive the summer. Fishing dropped sharply in mid-summer. Channel catfish and golden shiners survived, and catfish averaged 9.78 in by September 1965. Golden shiners compete with trout for food and have become numerous enough that control measures are recommended.
- **Outlook:** No significant rise in fishing pressure is expected until the Tribe builds lakes designed for fishing or opens the Black River to access. Campgrounds are needed at Seneca Lake to support the concession. Stock ponds are too small and too inaccessible to anchor a recreational enterprise.
- **Authorship:** Report by Richard J. Nava, Fishery Management Biologist, dated January 29, 1966.
