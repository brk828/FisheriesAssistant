# Occurrence of the Freshwater Jellyfish, *Craspedacusta sowerbyi*, in Lake Mead, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Hydrozoa #Craspedacusta #freshwater-invertebrates #limnology

## Executive Summary
This note documents a 1962–1963 range-extension record of the freshwater medusa **[[Craspedacusta sowerbyi]]** in **[[Lake Mead]]**, Nevada — a significant western find given that the vast majority of the ~100 known North American localities lie east of the Mississippi River. The population appeared abruptly in medusa form during the fall of 1962, peaked in abundance, and then rapidly declined to undetectable levels by February 1963, illustrating the characteristically **sporadic, boom-and-bust occurrence** pattern of this species' free-swimming polymorphic life stage.

## Background & Description
- **Taxon:** *Craspedacusta sowerbyi* Lankester (freshwater hydrozoan; colloquially termed "moonfish" by local observers, though not a true fish).
- Note: Source text does not provide detailed morphometric/anatomical descriptions of the medusa itself (bell diameter, tentacle counts, gonad structure), as the paper is a faunistic/distributional note rather than a taxonomic description.
- **Life stage discussed:** Free-swimming **medusa stage** only; the sessile **polyp stage** (which persists between sporadic medusa "blooms") is referenced implicitly via the species' known life cycle but not directly observed/discussed in this collection.
- Sex ratio determination noted as an area of ongoing/planned study at time of publication (not reported here).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat specificity:** Medusae were found to be restricted largely to **clear, unsilted water** within **deep rocky coves** of the reservoir — suggesting sensitivity to turbidity/sedimentation.
- **Behavioral pattern:** Medusae were most **active during periods of high light intensity**, indicating a photic/diel activity pattern consistent with other cladonematid/olindiid hydrozoans.
- **Population dynamics:** Classic boom-bust seasonality:
  - First sighted (per SCUBA diver reports): **June 1962**
  - Became abundant: **September–October 1962**
  - Peak collections: **up to 500 individuals** per outing at ~weekly intervals
  - Sharp decline by **25 January 1963** (only 12 individuals collected with major effort)
  - **Complete disappearance** of medusa stage by **8 February 1963**
- **Historical recurrence:** Anecdotal report from Park Service Ranger Captain Gene Gatzke of a prior sighting in Lake Mead **8–10 years earlier**, supporting the idea of intermittent, multi-year blooming cycles typical of this species (consistent with observations in [[R.W. Pennak]]'s *Freshwater Invertebrates of the United States*, 1953).
- **Biogeographic context:** Prior to this record, the closest known western population was in **central California** (per C.F. Lytle, personal communication); records west of the Rocky Mountains had only been documented in **British Columbia, Washington, Oregon, and California**, making the Lake Mead, Nevada occurrence a substantial eastward/southward extension within the western range.

## Key Findings
1. **First confirmed record** of *Craspedacusta sowerbyi* in Nevada, representing a significant geographic range extension for the species in the western United States/interior Southwest.
2. Medusa population exhibited **strong microhabitat selectivity** — restricted to clear, unsilted, rocky cove environments, implying sedimentation/turbidity as a limiting ecological factor.
3. Activity was **light-dependent**, with medusae most observable/active during high-light periods.
4. The medusa stage displayed a **highly transient seasonal bloom pattern**: appearing in early summer, peaking in fall, and vanishing entirely by the following February — consistent with the known irregular/episodic appearance of the medusa form across this species' broader North American distribution (see [[Lytle 1959]], [[Lytle 1962]]).
5. Anecdotal historical sighting data suggest **multi-year (8–10 year) recurrence intervals** for medusa blooms in the same water body, hinting at long-lived, cryptic polyp populations that intermittently produce free-swimming medusae under favorable conditions.
6. Findings underscore the value of **citizen/diver observation networks** (SCUBA divers, Park Service personnel) in detecting and dating rare invertebrate bloom events in large reservoir systems.
