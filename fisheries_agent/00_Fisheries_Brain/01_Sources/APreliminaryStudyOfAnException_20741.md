# A Preliminary Study of an Exceptionally Productive Trout Water, Fish Lake, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1935 paper by A. S. Hazzard of the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]] reports a preliminary limnological survey of [[Fish Lake]], Utah, a high-elevation lake known to anglers as exceptionally productive. The survey ran from July 21 to 31, 1933, in cooperation with the [[Utah Fish And Game Department]]. A 13-day creel check in 1934 recorded 17,521 trout weighing 13,844 pounds. The author estimates the seasonal yield at about 38.3 pounds per acre. The study links this productivity to abundant aquatic plants, a rich [[Freshwater Scud]] and [[Cladocerans]] food base, and heavy, consistent trout stocking. It also documents the competitive threat posed by introduced [[Chub]]s. The paper argues that detailed studies of poor, average and highly productive trout waters are needed to set management yardsticks.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The paper calls for detailed studies of trout waters that anglers rate as poor, average or exceptionally productive. A [[Creel Census]] would classify such waters, and [[Limnology]] of several waters in each class would provide reliable benchmarks for evaluating and improving other lakes.

**Catch and economic value.** The author cites a 13-day check of the first part of the 1934 season:
- 17,521 trout, total weight 13,844 lb.
- Anglers spent $27,246.21 on travel, meals and supplies, about $2 per pound of trout.
- Assuming a season of about 90 days, the projected yield is 95,850 lb, worth an estimated $191,700 to local businesses.
- This equals 38.3 lb per acre.

**Physical setting.**
- Elevation 8,800 ft, in a region of past volcanic and glacial activity.
- About 5.25 miles long and three-quarters of a mile wide on average, roughly 2,500 acres. The long axis runs northeast to southwest.
- Shorelines contrast sharply: steep, wooded, rocky slopes on the southeast; gentler hills on the northwest; marshy flats at each end.
- Six spring-fed inlet streams. Only one offers any trout spawning area, and that is under half a mile long.
- The author attributes the fishing mainly to consistently heavy plantings by the state, not to natural reproduction.

**Bathymetry.** A contour map was built from 140 soundings. About 70% of the lake is deeper than 90 ft, with a maximum depth of 117 ft. The main shoals and weed beds lie at the ends and along the northwest side.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal and optical conditions.**
- Surface temperatures ranged from 64 to 69 °F.
- A distinct [[Thermocline]] lay between 25 and 60 ft. Below it, temperature fell to about 40 °F.
- Water was white in color and quite clear. A six-inch white disk ([[Secchi Disk]]) disappeared at 41 to 45 ft.
- Beyond the 30 ft contour, the bottom is mostly mud mixed with decaying plants. Shoals are gravel, peat and mud, densely vegetated in places.

**Chemistry.**
- [[Dissolved Oxygen]] was adequate, 6.5 to 10.7 ppm, except in water deeper than 100 ft: 2.2 ppm at 103 ft and 0.9 ppm at 113 ft.
- Carbon dioxide rose from zero at the surface to 17.5 ppm at 113 ft.
- pH was acidic (6.4) in deep water and alkaline (7.4 to 8.5) in water shallower than 100 ft.
- Methyl orange alkalinity ranged from 50 to 63.5 ppm. Phenolphthalein alkalinity appeared only at the surface at one station and in the outlet.
- Based on bicarbonate content, the author placed Fish Lake between the [[Hard And Soft Water Classes]] proposed by Ricker (1932).

**Aquatic plants.** Table 1 lists the plant species identified by Dr. Walter P. Cottam of the University of Utah. Key taxa include:
- Very abundant: [[Elodea]] and [[Zannichellia]] at the north and south ends (5 to 10 ft).
- Common: [[Potamogeton]], [[Myriophyllum]] and [[Ceratophyllum]] around the border (8 to 25 ft), plus [[Lemna]] at the ends and [[Chara]] over much of the lake (10 to 75 ft).
- Abundant: [[Nostoc]] along the shores (0 to 60 ft).

The author attributes the abundant vegetation to the bottom type, soil richness and sheltered shoal areas.

**Food base.**
- The main food item was [[Gammarus Limnaeus]], the freshwater "shrimp" or scud. Up to 25 were counted under one 6-inch stone. Dredge samples suggested 200 to 500 per square foot in dense weed beds, and some were taken at 77 ft.
- Other shore invertebrates included snails, midges, leeches, mayflies, caddisflies and damselflies.
- Plankton was abundant. Vertical hauls at six stations yielded 1.4 to 6.6 cc of plankton.
- Deep-water bottom organisms were sparse, at 0.2 to 1.4 cc per Ekman sample.

## Key Findings

**1. Productivity is high and economically significant.** The study estimates a yield of 38.3 lb/acre and a local cash value of about $191,700 per season, based on a 13-day 1934 check.

**2. Aquatic vegetation and food production are central drivers.** Dr. Hildebrand and Towers (1927) had suggested that plant abundance caused high trout production. This study supports that view and identifies bottom type, soil richness and sheltered shoals as causes. Scuds and [[Cladocerans]] form the core of the food web.

**3. Hard water is not essential for a heavy yield.** Fish Lake is intermediate in hardness, yet it is highly productive. The author concludes that hard water may not be required for abundant crustacean food and trout.

**4. Stocking, not natural reproduction, sustains the fishery.** Stocking records from January 1930 to July 13, 1935, document 2,043,088 [[Rainbow Trout]] (small fingerlings to 14-inch fish), 3,887,180 [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (fingerlings and eyed eggs) and 305,000 silver and chinook salmon fingerlings. Native [[Cutthroat Trout]] have been largely displaced, with about 20 reported in spawning-station traps in spring 1933.

**5. Species-specific performance:**

| Species | Gill-net catch rate | Condition factor (a) | Growth (inches at annuli 1 to 5) | Key diet |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[Rainbow Trout]] | 11.3 fish/hr | 1.414 | 2.9, 7.5, 12.4, 15.4, 17.6 | Shrimp 33.8% of food volume; present in all 39 stomachs |
| [[Eastern Brook Trout]] | 2.3 fish/hr | 1.446 | 4.6, 8.3, 10.6, 12.9 (to 4 years) | [[Cladocerans]] in 64.5% of stomachs, 44.8% of volume |
| [[Chub]] | 19 fish/hr | N/A | 1.3, 3.5, 5.5, 6.6, 7.4 | Competes directly with trout |

- Rainbow trout were the most abundant game fish. The average weight was about 2 lb, with a maximum of 9 lb.
- Brook trout once commonly reached 5 lb, but average size has declined. The author cites intensive angling and rising rainbow numbers as likely causes.
- Brook trout growth was satisfactory but slower than rainbow growth.

**6. [[Mackinaw Trout]] are self-sustaining and valuable.** Introduced about twenty years earlier, they reproduce naturally and reach 35 lb. They use the large deep-water area that other species do not fully exploit. Stomach analysis of three large fish taken in November (9 to 13 lb) found one to three brook trout (5 to 10 inches) in each, suggesting predation on brook trout.

**7. Stomach analysis shows a diverse trout diet.**
- Rainbow trout (39 fish, 7.6 to 19.6 in): shrimp 33.8% of volume; plants including algae 33%, with about 20% higher aquatics such as pondweeds and Elodea; mollusks (mainly snails) third, followed by cladocerans.
- Brook trout (17 fish, 7.7 to 13.9 in): cladocerans dominant, followed by shrimp, dipteran larvae, pupae and adults. Mollusks were minor.

**8. Introduced chubs are an ecological and angling problem.** [[Chub]] (*Gila atraria*) and shiners were introduced by anglers using them as [[Live Bait]]. Chubs are now numerous enough to annoy fly fishermen, with 19 caught per hour in nets. Their slow growth is attributed to cold water and a short growing season. Stomach contents show direct competition with trout, with no evidence that trout eat chubs. The author notes that competition appears uncompensated and that further study of its extent and control is underway.

**9. Native fauna.** [[Bullhead]] (*Cottus* sp.) are native but not abundant.

**10. Conclusion and recommendation.** The author calls for more intensive studies of waters like Fish Lake, combined with accurate catch and stocking records, to guide management of trout lakes across the country.
