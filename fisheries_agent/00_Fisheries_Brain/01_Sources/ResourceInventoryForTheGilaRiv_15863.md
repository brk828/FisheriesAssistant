# Resource Inventory for the Gila River Complex, Eastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1979 baseline resource inventory, prepared by [[Arizona State University]] for the [[Bureau of Land Management]] Safford District, documents the physical, chemical, bacteriological, and biological condition of the Gila River Complex in eastern Arizona over October 1976 to September 1978. Its purpose was to supply baseline data for management planning ahead of possible water development, mining, grazing, recreation, and geothermal activity. Fishes are treated only briefly within a broader aquatic study; the bulk of the report covers flora, birds, mammals, herpetofauna, water chemistry, and algae.

Key conclusions: man's impact on water quality was small but locally evident from mining, grazing, and recreation; the region is biologically diverse because it lies in a transition zone between the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] and [[Sonoran Desert]]; and [[Riparian Habitat]] is the most important habitat type, supporting a large share of the fauna and flora.

## Background & Description

The Gila River Complex lies northeast of Safford, Arizona, within the Basin and Range Physiographic Province. It drains portions of the [[Upper Gila River Basin]], whose principal tributaries are the [[San Francisco River]], [[Eagle Creek]], and [[Bonita Creek]]. The upper basin covers about 20,450 km², of which roughly 50.8% is Gila River drainage and 35% is San Francisco River drainage (Table II-1). Ephemeral drainages make up only 1.7% of the total, reflecting dependence on high-mountain water supplies.

Geologically the area consists of fault-block mountain ranges separated by alluvial basins, including the [[Safford Valley]] and Duncan-Virden Valley. Basin fills reach more than 1,100 m thick in parts of the Safford Valley. The modern drainage incised through Quaternary fills, with terraces recording former base levels.

Climate is bi-seasonal: winter frontal storms, spring drought, summer convective thunderstorms, and autumn drought. Evaporation rates from water surfaces are roughly 7 to 10 times annual precipitation (about 23 cm/year at Safford). Floods are major ecological events; the Gila River near Clifton crested at 799 m³/s in 1941, more than 150 times its mean discharge, and the San Francisco River reached an estimated 4,065 m³/s in 1906.

Historical accounts from [[Kearny Expedition]] members (1846) describe clear, fish-rich streams lined with cottonwoods and willows. Emory recorded fish resembling trout that were identified as the [[Colorado River Chub]] (*Gila robusta*). Widespread riparian destruction occurred during the 1904 to 1917 floods, and the channel widened roughly twelvefold relative to 1875.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Physico-chemical, bacteriological, and algal sampling covered four mainstream Gila River sites, three San Francisco River sites, three Eagle Creek sites, Bonita Creek, Hot Springs Canyon, and numerous springs. Measured variables included temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, conductance, major ions, nutrients, and trace metals. Coliform and fecal streptococci counts were used to assess contamination. Flooding in August 1977 and winter 1978-79 strongly affected turbidity and channel form.

**Vegetation.** The flora comprises 88 families, 277 genera, 389 species, and 394 taxa; the authors estimate they recorded 65 to 85% of plant taxa present. Ten major vegetation formations were mapped, with Forest or Woodland covering about 70% of the basin and Desertscrub about 5%. Riparian communities, including [[Mesquite Bosque]] and broad-leafed stands of cottonwood, sycamore, and walnut, form corridors through arid lands. Saltcedar has not established in the reach, apparently because flooding destroys seedlings.

**Birds.** A total of 183 species was observed, 88 of them nesting species; 58 of the nesting species are permanent residents. Foliage height diversity and bird species diversity were positively correlated (P>.01) across 14 transects. Riparian habitats hold the greatest densities and diversity. Notable raptors include [[Black Hawk]] (Group III, with 3 to 4 pairs in Bonita Creek Canyon and 4 to 7 in Eagle Creek Canyon) and [[Zone-tailed Hawk]].

**Mammals.** Fifty-eight non-domesticated taxa were confirmed, plus 13 more likely present. Small-mammal trapping across roughly 34,560 trap days yielded only 1.85% trap success, very low for the region, which the authors attribute to prolonged drought and grazing. Cactus mouse dominated most habitats. Three aquatic mammals are recorded or likely present; [[Beaver]] was active along Eagle Creek and the San Francisco River.

**Herpetofauna.** Twenty-three lizard species, roughly 25 snakes, several toads and frogs, and two common turtles were recorded or expected. The [[Bat Cave]] maternity colony of [[Mexican Free-tailed Bat]] declined from about 25 million (1964) to about 600,000 (1970), probably from pesticide residues. [[Spiny Softshell Turtle]] is introduced in the Gila River, and [[Bullfrog]] is introduced and a threat to native amphibians and fishes.

**Fishes.** The aquatic study addressed native fishes, including chubs of the genus *Gila* and the [[Colorado River Chub]]. Common Merganser specimens had consumed channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*). Fish status on federal and state lists of depleted or endangered species was tabulated, but fish-specific results are limited in the text supplied.

## Key Findings

- Little human impact on water quality was demonstrated; localized effects from mining, grazing, and recreation were evident.
- Riparian areas are the most important habitats, sustaining a large fraction of the regional fauna and flora.
- The biota is diverse and complex because the area lies in a Chihuahuan-Sonoran transition zone.
- Historical channel widening (1904-1917) destroyed much riparian vegetation; cottonwood reproduction has declined since the 1930s.
- Drought and flooding strongly limited sampling; small-mammal densities were abnormally low in 1977-78.
- Four plant taxa proposed for federal endangered listing occur in the complex: *Echeveria rusbyi*, *Echinocereus triglochidiatus* var. *arizonicus*, *Fraxinus anomala* var. *lowellii*, and *Perityle lemmoni*.
- Bird diversity correlates positively with foliage height diversity, though other factors also influence populations.
- Management recommendations emphasize protecting microhabitats, riparian corridors, and sensitive species, and monitoring the effects of water development, mining, grazing, and geothermal activity.
