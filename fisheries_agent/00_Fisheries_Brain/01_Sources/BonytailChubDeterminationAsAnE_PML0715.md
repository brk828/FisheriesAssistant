# Bonytail Chub Listed as an Endangered Species (Federal Register, April 23, 1980)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] issued a final rule determining the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) to be an Endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. The fish has been extirpated from most of its historical range in the [[Colorado River Basin]], no reproduction has been documented in recent years, and the species is considered in immediate danger of extinction. The rule took effect May 23, 1980. No [[Critical Habitat]] was designated because no known area then met all requirements for successful reproduction.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The bonytail's status was confused in earlier literature, where it was treated either as a full species or as a subspecies of the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*). A 1979 study by Smith, Miller and Sable, published in *Scientific Research in the National Parks*, concluded that the roundtail chub, bonytail chub and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) are all distinct species. The Service accepted this finding.
- **Historical distribution:** The species was once found in large turbid mainstream rivers of the Colorado Basin, including the Green River in Wyoming and Utah, the Yampa and Gunnison rivers in Colorado, the Colorado River in Utah, Arizona, Nevada and California, and the Gila and Salt rivers in Arizona. Fish were most frequently associated with eddies adjacent to swift current.
- **Habitat preference:** Historically it occupied a mix of swift, torrential canyon reaches and slow, meandering sand-bottomed stretches.
- **Proposal history:** The rule was first proposed in the *Federal Register* on April 24, 1978 (43 FR 17375-17377). Comments were accepted through April 15, 1980.
- **Regulatory context:** Section 9(a)(1) prohibitions under 50 CFR 17.21 apply, including restrictions on taking, importing, exporting, interstate commerce sales, and possession of illegally taken specimens. Permits are available for scientific purposes or propagation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat loss:** Mainstream impoundments and diversions, including [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (closed 1962), eliminated much of the original habitat. The lower basin became a series of reservoirs and cold tailwaters. Tailwaters lack the roughly 65°F (about 18°C) temperature needed for spawning, so they offer no usable habitat.
- **Lower basin reservoirs:** Adults were historically found in Lakes Mead, Mohave and Havasu, and spawning was observed, but no young were found. [[Lake Mohave]] is the only reservoir known to hold an adult population, which is composed of elderly individuals and is expected to disappear as the fish die.
- **Dewatering:** Parts of the lower basin, particularly the Gila River system, were dewatered by irrigation projects.
- **Upper basin decline:** The decline in the upper basin was noticed in the 1960s, and the Green River population apparently began declining after 1960, once the reservoir stabilized. The most recent record of an adult came from Dinosaur National Monument (Canyon of the Green River, Utah).
- **Exotic species:** Predation by introduced species (such as [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Red Shiner]]) and competition with introduced fishes are considered likely major factors in the decline. Loss of young fish to predation and interspecific competition is also implicated.
- **Water quality and flow:** Reduced in-stream flows and altered physical and chemical habitat conditions are believed to prevent reproduction.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** The bonytail chub is threatened with extinction throughout all of its range. The Director determined this under Section 4(a)(1) of the Act.
- **Reproduction:** No reproduction was documented anywhere in the Colorado River Basin in recent years. The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] noted in December 1979 that "very few bonytails have been observed recently."
- **Public comments:** Sixteen written responses were received: 2 from federal agencies, 7 from state agencies and 7 from other organizations. Six supported the listing, five opposed it, two asked that the proposal be held in abeyance, and three took no position. No new quantitative data were submitted.
- **Opposition concerns:** Opponents cited potential impacts on hydroelectric generation, industrial water use (including [[Shale Oil Development]]), interbasin export, and agriculture. They also questioned the adequacy of the biological data and the Service's past role in rough fish eradication.
- **Survey evidence:** A White River Shale Project survey collected 1,800 fish with no bonytails. Arizona surveys of Lake Havasu found no chubs, and specimens observed were all small, indicating a non-reproducing population.
- **Critical Habitat:** Not designated. The Salt River Project identified the Salt, Verde, White and Black rivers as unsuitable, citing dams, dewatering, and cold water temperatures.
- **Other responses:** The Bureau of Reclamation disputed the claim that minor water-quality changes would cause a decline. The Tosco Corporation reported "no significant differences" in sixteen water-quality parameters before and after Flaming Gorge Dam (no analysis was provided). The Colorado River Water Conservation District argued the listing should not apply to Colorado.
- **Recovery and international status:** The Service will evaluate whether the species should be proposed for listing under [[CITES]] (the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species).
- **Environmental review:** An environmental assessment found the listing would not significantly affect the quality of the human environment under NEPA Section 102(2)(c).
- **Federal responsibilities:** Under Section 7(a) of the Act, federal agencies must ensure their actions are not likely to jeopardize the species. No federal actions were known to be affected by this rule at the time.
