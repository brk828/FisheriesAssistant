# Reader Letters to the Editor (Degraded Scan)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This *Wildlife Views* (July 1980) reader-letters page contains four letters and an editor's note. The most substantive item is a letter from Don Ellison of Taylor, Arizona, who recalls large numbers of [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Roosevelt Lake]] during the 1920s and 1930s and asks why the species disappeared. The editor replies that a feature article on native fishes is planned. Other letters report a handsize [[Pumpkinseed]] (*Lepomis gibbosus*) at [[Alamo Lake]] and comment on the funding of hunting and fishing programs. No formal survey data, sample sizes or population statistics are reported.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Reader correspondence and editor's note in the Arizona Game and Fish Department magazine *Wildlife Views*, July 1980. Page 1 is only OCR debris.
- **Fish named in the letters:**
  - *Humpback suckers*, described as large, abundant suckers that were thrown onto the bank in piles by hand-line fishermen. Scientific name is added here for reference; the letter gives only the common name.
  - *Salmon*, glossed by the writer as "squaw fish?", a tentative identification. This is probably a [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] or related large minnow, but the text does not confirm it.
  - *Pumpkinseed* (sunfish), reported by Alan Millward from Alamo Lake.
- **Notable catch:** Buster Ellison, the writer's uncle, caught an 18-pound fish by trolling with a hand line in Roosevelt Lake a few years after the reservoir was completed. The species is not named.
- **People and roles:** [[Bill Silvey]], Fisheries Biologist, reviewed the Ellison letter and commented on its accuracy. Bill Sizer is the author of a *Wildlife Views* article on Arizona's smaller sunfishes, which Millward references.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical abundance:** Ellison recalls that as a boy of about 16, in the 1930s, he rode horses into Roosevelt Lake waters where suckers were so thick that horses' ankles were bumped and the animals threw fits. He also describes fishermen using weighted treble hooks and no bait to catch suckers that were left to rot on the bank.
- **Decline:** Ellison has not seen a humpback sucker since the 1930s and asks whether the decline was caused by poisoning or disease. The text offers no answer.
- **Cost and access:** Don Underwood argues that sportsmen should not pay such a large share of conservation costs, since off-road vehicles cause more damage than hunters or anglers yet need no permits or fees.
- **Hunting permits:** The editor notes that drawings for permits are unpopular with applicants, but no better way has been found to allocate the available permits.

## Key Findings

- **Historical abundance of humpback suckers in Roosevelt Lake:** Large numbers were present in the 1920s and 1930s and were discarded by anglers. The species is no longer seen by this writer.
- **Causes of decline:** Unknown in the text. Poisoning and disease are raised as hypotheses only.
- **Editor's plan:** An article on Arizona's native fishes is planned for an upcoming issue. Fisheries Biologist Bill Silvey verified the letter's accuracy.
- **Pumpkinseed record:** A handsize pumpkinseed was seen at Alamo Lake on May 30 (the year is not given explicitly, but the context is 1980). The writer had not seen one in years.
- **Funding equity:** Reader Don Underwood argues that users who cause the most habitat damage, such as off-road vehicle drivers, should share costs. The magazine staff is praised for its work.
- **Readership:** An overseas Arizona-born reader requests back issues and notes the magazine helps keep him informed about hunting and fishing while he is in Europe.
