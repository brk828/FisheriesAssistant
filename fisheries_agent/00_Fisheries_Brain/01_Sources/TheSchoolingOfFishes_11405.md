# The Schooling of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #schooling-behavior

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1962 *Scientific American* article by [[Evelyn Shaw]] examines how fishes form schools, a social organization defined by parallel orientation, near-constant spacing, and synchronized movement. Shaw's laboratory work on [[Menidia]] silversides (whitebait) shows that schooling develops gradually from the interaction of pairs of fry, shifting from head-on avoidance to head-to-tail parallel swimming as the fish grow. The article reviews candidate sensory cues ([[Visual Attraction]], [[Lateral Line]], hearing and chemical senses), evaluates proposed adaptive advantages, and concludes that the function of schooling remains unresolved.

## Background & Description

Schools are more than crowds. Members of a school keep a stable geometric arrangement: they head the same way, swim parallel, and keep nearly equal spacing, so that a school can appear as "a huge single animal" in motion. Schools have no fixed leaders; fish at the front rotate to the flanks during turns. Schools maintain their shape during most activities, but break up during feeding. Members of a school are similar in size, since speed increases with size and fish sort themselves accordingly. Schools may be a few fish or up to a million members deep. The [[Atlantic Menhaden]] is cited as recognizable from the air by its "giant amoeboid shadow" school shape.

Shaw argues that "instinct" is not an explanation in itself, and that developmental study is needed. Schooling is documented in roughly 2,000 marine species and a further 2,000 species in the mostly freshwater order [[Cypriniformes]], which includes minnows and characins. Major marine schooling orders are:
- [[Clupeiformes]] (herrings, menhaden)
- [[Mugiliformes]] (mullets, silversides, barracuda)
- [[Perciformes]] (jacks, pompanos, bluefish, mackerels, tuna, some snappers and grunts)

Schooling fishes share a sleek, silvery body and small, flattened pectoral fins with limited mobility. [[C. M. Breder]] observed that these fishes cannot swim backward, which Shaw suggests keeps schools moving forward. The fossil record does not show whether schooling is a primitive or advanced trait.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most schooling species begin life in the plankton. Eggs are spawned and fertilized by the school, then drift unattended. Hatched fry, about 5 mm long, are difficult to study in the open ocean because plankton nets disrupt natural behavior. Shaw's field work was therefore limited to nearshore species, especially Menidia off Cape Cod. Menidia spawn adhesive eggs on rocks and marine vegetation. In summer 1960, the team observed an estimated 10,000 small fishes in the plankton of shallow waters near Woods Hole.

The author notes that observed schools are stable across varied environmental conditions, which suggests internal regulation rather than external stimuli such as food or light changes. Shaw states that the author's fieldwork and laboratory work were carried out at the [[Marine Biological Laboratory]], [[Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution]], the [[Bermuda Biological Station]], and the [[Lerner Marine Laboratory]].

## Key Findings

**Laboratory rearing of Menidia fry (Shaw, 1960 and 1961 studies):**
- Fry reared from the egg in a doughnut-shaped tank (three-inch channel) began schooling when they reached the same size as the smallest schooling fry seen in the sea.
- Schooling develops in stages tied to body length:
  - 5–7 mm: fry dart away from head, tail, or side approaches within 5 mm.
  - 8–9 mm: tail-on approaches allow two fry to swim parallel for a second or two; head-on approaches still prompt flight.
  - About 9 mm: head-to-tail approach becomes predominant, with parallel swimming for 5–10 seconds.
  - 10–10.5 mm: paired fry briefly vibrate their bodies, then swim in tandem or parallel for 30–60 seconds; small groups of three or four form.
  - 11–12 mm: about 10 fry school in a ragged formation with 10–35 mm spacing.
  - 14 mm: spacing narrows to 10–15 mm.
- Wild fry at 11–12 mm formed schools of 30–50 individuals.

**Isolation experiments:**
- Of 400 fry isolated in the first season, four survived to schooling size. In the second season, nine of 87 survived.
- Isolated fry appeared to starve because they did not begin feeding as community-reared fry did (two or three days post-hatch, while still carrying a yolk sac).
- Fry isolated from species-mates schooled within 10 minutes after being placed together. Fry that spent their first week with species-mates needed at least 150 minutes. Shaw suggests early contact may set up an inhibitory process.
- Isolated fish placed in community tanks became indistinguishable from others after four hours.

**Visual cues:**
- Very young fry did not respond to a fry across a glass barrier. Responsiveness grew with age, and fry eventually oriented in parallel and vibrated their bodies.
- Adult fishes placed on either side of a glass partition swam toward each other. Blinded fish could not school. A fish blinded in one eye lined up only on the side of its intact eye.
- Movement outweighs color and species in initial attraction.
- Shaw and [[Edward R. Baylor]] found many Cape Cod schooling species are farsighted, with retinal structures suited to contrast and motion detection. Contact lenses produced no conclusive effects on schooling distance.
- [[Albert E. Parr]] (1927) proposed that spacing reflects a balance of visual attraction and repulsion.

**Other sensory cues (inconclusive):**
- [[James M. Moulton]] recorded hydrodynamic sounds from schooling species that may help maintain the school, but not individual position.
- Taste and smell are unlikely to matter in open oceanic water.
- The [[Lateral Line]] is the sensory system thought most likely to aid fish-to-fish orientation, based on work by [[van Bergeijk and Harris]].

**Proposed adaptive advantages (none confirmed as critical):**
- Predator confusion: [[Carl Welty]] found goldfish ate fewer Daphnia when fed larger numbers. [[Vernon E. Brock]] and [[Robert H. Riffenburgh]] showed a school cannot be decimated once it exceeds a certain size. Shaw notes that schools may also serve as easy prey.
- Foraging: only peripheral fish are well placed to find food, so the benefit is unclear. Social feeding may speed growth of young.
- Reproduction: schooling species show no courtship. Males and females are often indistinguishable, and eggs and sperm are released into plankton. Shaw found some schools were entirely male or entirely female.
- Hydrodynamics: followers may save energy by using turbulence from fish ahead, though leaders may not.

**Overall conclusion:** Shaw states that schooling is widespread and evidently successful, but its evolutionary function is still unknown. The article ends by noting that the questions raised suggest further observational and experimental research.
