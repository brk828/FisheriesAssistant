# Humpback Chub and Native Colorado River Fishes: Population, Movement, Survival and Predation in the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (contract BOR-1-FC-90-10490, dated March 31, 1996) presents five years (July 1991 to June 1995) of research by [[Arizona State University]] on four indigenous big-river fishes of the Grand Canyon region: [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*, federally endangered), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, endangered), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*, candidate for listing) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*, considered secure). The work was part of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program, which asked whether flow releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] could be modified to reduce downstream impacts. Study sites were the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and its confluence with the mainstem, about 99 river-kilometers below the dam.

The report has four sections: population estimates and movements of humpback chub; predation on native fishes by introduced species; population estimates and survival of catostomids; and a five-year survival analysis for humpback chub. Together they argue that the LCR is now a critical resident refuge for humpback chub, and that predation by introduced fishes and altered water clarity are serious threats.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin holds a distinctive fish fauna with species-level endemism near 70%. Its fishes evolved under highly variable, turbulent flows, and many are now endangered. The report frames the conflict as one between development and conservation, citing [[Minckley and Douglas]] (1991) on the "blink of the eye" rate of native fish extinction.

*Gila cypha* is described as the most specialized minnow in western North America. It is known only from the Colorado River and major swift tributaries, occurs sporadically, and is seldom locally abundant. Adults can live over 20 years (Minckley 1991). The Grand Canyon population at the LCR confluence was not recognized as reproducing until 1975. The species was described in 1946 from a type specimen caught in 1932.

The big-river fauna comprises seven species (four cyprinids and three catostomids). Four are endangered, one is a candidate, one is under review and one is considered secure. The authors note that baseline data for the two catostomids are sparse because research has focused on threatened species, and because remote, rugged terrain makes consistent sampling difficult.

The LCR drains 141,155 km² and flows 412 km to its confluence with the Colorado River. Its lower 21 km are perennial, fed by springs such as Blue Springs (discharge 6.1–6.6 m³/s). At base flow the river is saline (conductivity above 5,000 µmhos/cm) and travertine-forming, which gives it a turquoise color and complex water chemistry.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Glen Canyon Dam (completed 1963) reduced the basin's large-river habitat from about 2,400 km to 965 km. It cut sediment and temperature variation, reduced salinity, and altered flow regimes. Cold-water species such as rainbow and brown trout came to dominate the downstream fish community. Within Grand Canyon National Park, five of eight indigenous fish species persist, mostly in tributaries and backwaters.

The LCR, by contrast, retains temperature and flow conditions similar to the pre-dam mainstem. Base camps were established at three sites: the confluence (RKM 0.55), Powell Canyon (RKM 3.1) and Salt Canyon (RKM 10.8). A series of travertine falls at RKM 14.9 (the Atomizer Falls complex) marks the upstream limit of humpback chub distribution. Riparian vegetation is a mix of native and nonnative species, including tamarisk and giant reed.

Introduced fishes are a major concern. The report notes about 70 non-native fishes introduced to the Colorado basin and 20 known from Grand Canyon. Nearly 26 million fish have been stocked in the region, including roughly 19.8 million into [[Lake Powell]] since 1963.

## Key Findings

**Section 1: Population estimates and movements of *Gila cypha***
- Adult humpback chub (over 150 mm TL) were tagged with PIT tags during 19 trips at three reaches (July 1991 to December 1992).
- The best-fitting closed-population estimate for the entire 19-month study was 4,508 individuals (Pollock and Otis estimator, Mh). Monthly estimates summed over reaches averaged 2,992, and monthly estimates for the whole LCR averaged 2,434.
- Estimates for May 1992 were 1,320 at the confluence, 4,346 for the whole 14.9 km LCR, and 4,602 when summed over the three reaches. These were lower than the 1987 and 1988 confluence estimates (1,800 and 2,900) and the 25,000 estimated for 1989.
- Upstream movement occurred in spring, with staging at the confluence followed by movement into the LCR. Downstream, postreproductive movement was slow and diffuse.
- Within-reach stasis dominated in summer through winter: about 70% of recorded 1992 movements were within the same reach. Between-reach movement was minimal (36 of 3,272 recaptures in 1991; 1 of 4,030 in 1992).
- The authors conclude that adults overwinter in the LCR and may use it as a warm-water refuge, a possible post-dam life-history shift.

**Section 2: Predation by introduced fishes**
- Humpback chub remains occurred in 13 of 408 stomachs (3.2%) across five predator species.
- Rainbow trout (3 of 174) and channel catfish (8 of 202) were the main predators. Channel catfish ate chubs 85–200 mm TL.
- Estimated consumption: a predator population of 1,000 taking one chub per week would remove about 3,588 chubs annually. Predation may limit recruitment by removing juveniles and increase adult mortality.
- No larval or small post-larval fish were found in predator stomachs, which the authors attribute to the transient nature of these remains.

**Section 3: Catostomid populations, movements and survival**
- *C. latipinnis* and *C. discobolus* were tagged with PIT tags and analyzed using Cormack-Jolly-Seber (CJS) open-population models over 49 months.
- Recapture rates were 41.8% for *C. latipinnis* (1,550 of 32,739) and 11.8% for *C. discobolus* (484 of 4,097), so *C. discobolus* estimates had much larger variances.
- *C. latipinnis* estimates varied significantly among seasons (F = 2.44, p < 0.01). Summer and autumn differed (p < 0.04), as did autumn and spring (p < 0.03).
- *C. discobolus* estimates declined steadily from month 23 to 45.
- *Xyrauchen texanus* was too rare for reliable estimates (fewer than 12 individuals per estimate; 32 capture records, 25 unique individuals, 7 recaptures). Most captures were in spring and most individuals were male.
- Survival was uniform across size classes in 1991–1993 but was lowest in 1994 for both species, which the authors link to flood-driven changes in water clarity.

**Section 4: Five-year survival of *Gila cypha***
- Recaptures made up 58% of 18,626 records (10,795 recaptures; the maximum was 20 recaptures per individual).
- Survival estimates were uniform across size classes for 1991–1993. In 1994 survival was markedly reduced, with no size class above 47%, compared with a minimum of 52% in earlier years.
- Monthly CJS estimates fell sharply after month 27 (summer 1993). The highest adjusted estimate was 9,848 and the lowest was 442 at month 49.
- Predation by rainbow trout and channel catfish is a possible cause, but it does not explain uniform declines across all size classes. The authors suggest reduced carbonate content may have altered behavior, or that increased water clarity exposed fish to predators.

**Overall conclusions and recommendations**
- The LCR is a critical refuge and breeding area for humpback chub, and the species' use of it is a critical management concern.
- Long-term monitoring is needed at a level that can separate normal movement variability from declines.
- Genetic studies are needed for all three native species, especially to assess hybridization between razorback and flannelmouth suckers.
- Control of introduced predators, which the agencies have been reluctant to pursue, is essential for native fish recovery.
