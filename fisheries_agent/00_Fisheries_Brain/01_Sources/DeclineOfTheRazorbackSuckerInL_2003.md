# Decline of the Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, Colorado River, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #catostomidae #endangered_species #population_ecology

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is an endemic "big-river" fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The Lake Mohave population consists primarily of fish produced in the early 1950s when young were abundant; most remaining adults are estimated to exceed 50 years old. Age estimation methods for this species have been documented by McCarthy and Minckley (1987). Sexual maturity and spawning behavior occur in adults, with fish staging and spawning at specific shoreline areas in springtime (March). Sex categories include juvenile (sexually immature), male, female, and unknown (adults whose sex could not be reliably determined). The long-term effective female population was estimated via molecular techniques to have been in the hundreds of thousands historically, likely derived from a basinwide population numbering in the millions.

**Habitat Associations**: Historically widely distributed and abundant to common in mainstream and major tributaries from Wyoming through Colorado, Utah, Nevada, Arizona, California, and into Mexico (Gulf of California, Sonora and Baja). The species occupied riverine environments prior to impoundment. Currently, the largest remaining population occurs in [[Lake Mohave]], a 105-km-long, 11,400-ha run-of-the-river reservoir impounded between [[Hoover Dam]] (north) and [[Davis Dam]] (south). Primary sampling sites include shallow shorelines, reefs, and spits in coves and inlets at depths of 2–6 m. Specific locations include Owl Cove (RK 45), Yuma and Gold coves (RK 39), Arizona Bay (RK 38), Carp Cove (RK 32), Cottonwood Cove East (RK 32) on the Arizona side, and Cottonwood Cove West to Tequila Cove (RK 35), Hog Farm Cove (RK 31), Six Mile Cove (RK 30), Nine Mile to Half-way coves (RK 27), and Nine Mile Cove (RK 26) on the Nevada side.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Adult razorback suckers consistently visit specific shoreline areas to stage and spawn in springtime. Telemetry studies (Mueller et al. 2000) have shown that razorback suckers disperse great distances after release and randomly mix into the population. Fish captured and marked demonstrate movement patterns consistent with spawning site fidelity during the breeding season.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: The primary ecological threat is predation on early life stages (larvae) by nonnative fishes. Marsh and Langhorst (1988) documented feeding and fate of wild larval razorback suckers, demonstrating that predation by introduced species precludes measurable recruitment. Juveniles are absent from collections, indicating complete recruitment failure since the early 1950s, with only one record of natural recruitment documented since that time.

**Environmental Drivers**: Population declines are attributed to establishment of nonnative fishes and water development impacts. The transition from riverine to reservoir environment (impoundment) fundamentally altered habitat conditions. [[Lake Mohave]] represents a relatively stable, closed environment, but this stability has not prevented population collapse due to biotic factors (nonnative predation).

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Catastrophic decline documented over multiple decades. Historical abundance in the hundreds of thousands declined to 60,000–75,000 in the 1980s, 44,000 in 1991, fewer than 25,000 in the mid-1990s, and fewer than 3,000 in 2001. The 2001 population estimate (N* = 2,698; 95% CL: 1,573–5,081) represents only 13% of the 1992 estimate and a >94% decline over the 1991–2002 period. Mark-recapture data show significant downward trend (R² = 0.67). The greatest apparent decline occurred between 1991 and 1992, though this may reflect small sample size (only 51 fish marked in 1991) rather than a catastrophic event.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: [[Population Viability Analysis]] predicts rapid progression toward functional extirpation. Mean time to extinction estimated at 14.65 years (95% CL: 9.84–20.40) for threshold of 50 individuals, 12.10 years (95% CL: 8.13–16.84) for 100 individuals, and 6.19 years (95% CL: 4.16–8.62) for 500 individuals. Median time to extinction similar to mean values. Probability of ultimate extinction for all thresholds could be realized within 24 years from 2003. Population growth rate (λ) estimated at 0.78 (95% CL: 0.69–0.87), indicating average population decrease. The 5% probability of persistence would be attained in approximately 20, 17, and 9 years for thresholds of 50, 100, and 500 individuals, respectively. Extirpation was anticipated within the 2000s decade at current rate of decline. The population is limited primarily to large, old adults because predation on larvae by nonnative fishes has precluded measurable recruitment for nearly half a century. There is no practicable method at this time to remove the continuing threat of nonnative predators to razorback sucker larvae.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: The [[Native Fish Work Group]] initiated a repatriation program in 1991 using wild-produced larvae reared in captivity and returned to the lake at sizes less susceptible to predation (30 cm or longer). The goal is to establish a population of at least 50,000 repatriated razorback suckers to replace the aging and nearly extirpated adult stock. To date (2003), approximately 70,000 fish have been released, with 811 recaptures documented. Low recapture rates may reflect time needed for fish to grow to maturity and become available for capture on spawning grounds. Approximately 100 fish were removed from the population during the last decade for reproductive services at national fish hatcheries or for participation in telemetry and other studies. The intensive conservation effort based upon repatriated fish aims to perpetuate the species and its genetic variability until threats can be ameliorated or removed, enabling re-establishment of self-sustaining populations into historical habitats.

**Biological Reference Points**: Population estimates calculated using Chapman modification of Peterson method (adjusted Peterson-method formula): N* = ([M + 1] × [C + 1])/(R + 1), where N* is annual single-census population estimate for marking year, M is number of fish marked during marking year, C is combined number of marked and unmarked fish captured in sampling year, and R is number of marked fish captured in sampling year that were originally marked in marking year. Confidence limits based on Poisson frequency distribution with R as entering variable; if R > 50, Pearson's formula used: 0.5x + 1.92 ± 1.960√(x + 1.0). PVA parameters: μ (population growth parameter) = -0.27, σ² (variance) = 0.036. Statistical bias noted for marking year 1999 (R < 4 fish makes bias probable). Condition for unbiased estimate (M + C) > N* not met for any years, resulting in negative bias and underestimation of population size.
