# Stream Surveys of the Guadalupe and San Antonio Rivers, Texas (1951–1953)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-community-survey

## Executive Summary

This Texas Game and Fish Commission report (Inland Fisheries Division, IF Report Series No. 1, January 1955, by Aquatic Biologist Robert A. Kuehne) presents two stream surveys. Section I covers the [[Guadalupe River]], surveyed from July 5, 1951 to October 17, 1952. Section II covers the [[San Antonio River]], surveyed from June 24 to August 14, 1953. Both surveys used seining and gill netting to determine which fish species were present, how abundant they were, and how fish distribution related to water quality, pollution, irrigation and watershed condition. The Guadalupe survey recorded 66 species and found rough fish dominant throughout most of the river. The central conclusion is that the decline of game fish is driven mainly by flood-driven siltation caused by poor land management, so the report recommends [[Rotenone Treatment]] to remove rough fish, restocking, and land conservation.

## Background & Description

The report is the first in a series of surveys of Texas lakes and streams, begun in July 1951 to determine the status of the state's fresh waters. The two surveys were financed with state funds. Later surveys were funded under the [[Dingell-Johnson Program]], under which the federal government paid 75 percent of project costs and the state paid 25 percent. The work was supported by Game Wardens and staff, and specimen identification was checked by Dr. Clark Hubbs of the University of Texas.

**Guadalupe River.** The river rises in the [[Edwards Plateau]] west of Kerrville at nearly 2,000 feet and flows about 400 river miles southeast to San Antonio Bay. It crosses five natural subdivisions of Texas. The headwaters are swift, shallow, clear and spring-fed. East of the [[Balcones Fault Zone]] near New Braunfels, the river enters the Blackland Prairie and becomes a broad, flat, meandering channel. The [[Comal River]] adds a large, nearly constant spring-fed supply, and the [[San Marcos River]] enters near Gonzales.

**San Antonio River.** The river rises in San Antonio and flows about 100 miles southeast before joining the Guadalupe a few miles from San Antonio Bay. Its main tributaries are the Medina River and Cibolo Creek. Annual rainfall ranges from about 27 inches in the west to 32 inches near the mouth. Historically, large springs in the Olmos Creek valley fed the river, but these are now virtually extinct, and flow is maintained by a shallow well, surplus artesian well water, and treated sewage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat zonation.** The headwaters are clear, with aquatic plants, and support sunfish, bass, catfish, minnows and darters. Downstream, murkiness increases, pools lengthen, and shad, suckers and gar become dominant. Below Gonzales the river is always murky.

**Water chemistry.** Of the properties tested, only temperature, turbidity and salinity correlated with fish distribution. Dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide and alkalinity varied sharply between nearby stations with similar fish collections. Key values include:
- Alkalinity consistently above 100 ppm, with enough calcium carbonate to support high production of crustaceans and aquatic insects, the main food of young game fish.
- Water consistently basic (one neutral sample).
- Free carbon dioxide up to 30 ppm at the main North Fork springs, and none in warm, moving water.
- Normal chloride in the upper Guadalupe of 10–20 ppm (probably never above 30 ppm). At low water at the Gonzales water works, chloride is usually about 250 ppm, with more than 1,000 ppm recorded several times.
- Sufficient oxygen under normal conditions, though localized fish kills occur.

**Temperature-limited species.** The roundnose minnow, Gambusia sp., fountain darter, rock bass and Rio Grande perch are restricted to or near spring areas with near-constant temperatures. The rock bass cannot tolerate warm summers, and the sailfin molly and Rio Grande perch are harmed by winter cold.

**Siltation and flooding.** Siltation is the main form of pollution in the Guadalupe basin. Floods scour the stream bed, deposit silt and strip shoreline vegetation. Pre-1900 floods reached about six feet in Kerr County, but floods of 20 to 30 feet have been recorded in recent years. Silt removes the insect and crustacean food of young game fish and favors bottom-feeding suckers and buffalo.

**Other pollution and water use.**
- Brine and heavy oil from the Luling Oil Field, entering via the San Marcos River, are the primary industrial pollution in the Guadalupe watershed.
- Sand and gravel wash water discharged directly into the river keeps two or three miles murky near Kerrville.
- Irrigation has at times reduced summer flow on the upper Guadalupe to a dangerous level. Rice irrigation on the lower river is small.
- The San Antonio River's very restricted flow makes untreated sewage and sludge particularly harmful, and fine mud coats the bed for many miles below the city.
- Summertime spring flow has declined; the large Landa Park spring went dry in summer 1952.

**Introduced and invasive species.** The [[Rio Grande Perch]] spread through the lower basin after introduction to [[Landa Park Lake]] and crowded out other fish. The [[Sailfin Molly]] has multiplied in San Antonio headwaters to outnumber other species in many places. The Rio Grande tetra was introduced from the Rio Grande about 30 years earlier and is established in headwater areas kept warm by artesian flow. Northern trout introductions have failed and are unlikely to persist.

## Key Findings

**Guadalupe River (Section I)**

- **Collection effort:** 52 seining collections and 46 gill net sets. Seining recorded 51 species, and gill netting 22 species. Sixty-six species were recorded in total, including some brackish and saltwater forms.
- **Rough fish dominance:** The average rough-to-game fish ratio across quarterly gill net samples was 89.6% rough fish. Rough fish dominated even in the clear headwaters, though gill nets catch more active rough species.
- **Rotenone experiment:** In September and October 1951, a 20-mile stretch above New Braunfels was treated. Rough fish made up over 80% of fish other than minnows and small darters. [[Longnose Gar]] were usually 60–70% of the population. Gizzard shad, Texas gray redhorse and smallmouth buffalo were usually 10–20%. Channel and flathead catfish were about 10% and 2–3% respectively. Largemouth bass were under 1%.
- **Fishing pressure ruled out:** Accessible pools fished by many visitors showed no marked population difference from the most remote treated pool, so overfishing was not the cause of low game fish numbers.
- **Habitat is the controlling factor:** Siltation is the main limit on habitat suitability. Clear water is preferred by most game fish and essential to some.
- **Widespread species:** The redhorse shiner (the most abundant and widely distributed minnow), mosquitofish, longear sunfish and bluegill are the most broadly distributed. The [[Channel Catfish]] was the best-distributed game fish.
- **Largemouth bass:** Bass were common in the clear headwaters, with rapid declines in murky water. Near New Braunfels, the few bass were mostly Texas spotted bass, and the population showed few intermediate-size fish, suggesting poor spawning or survival of young.
- **Hybridization:** Three hybrid crosses were found, including Notropis venustus × N. lutrensis near Kerrville.

**San Antonio River (Section II)**

- **Fish community:** Most native species were either widely distributed or showed strong habitat preferences. Exotic carp, Rio Grande perch, sailfin molly and Rio Grande tetra have crowded some native species into minor roles in the upper basin.
- **Channel and blue catfish:** These species are the basin's most important game fish. Channel catfish tolerate murky water and were abundant in the lower basin, and the blue catfish nearly replaced them below Goliad County.
- **Water quality:** Waters are all alkaline with consistently high pH. Turbidity, alkalinity, pH and chlorides tend to increase downstream. After seining, turbidity commonly reached about 500 ppm.
- **Pollution:** No contamination was observed in the city, though past pollution seriously degraded the river. Chloride pollution from an abandoned oil field at Canvasback Lake is known.
- **Irrigation:** The Medina River is so heavily used for irrigation that it rarely contributes much flow.

**Management recommendations (Guadalupe)**

- **Rotenone treatment:** Considered the most promising rough fish control method, applied in heavily infested areas followed by restocking. Summer or early fall is best. It requires advance planning and hatchery capacity.
- **Commercial fishing:** Large-scale buffalo operations similar to those at Lake Texoma could remove rough fish. Gill nets could help in headwater pools, though Kerr County restrictions apply.
- **Land management:** The primary cause of the fish decline is poor land use that produces scouring floods and silt. Recommended practices include restoring grass cover, brush control, terracing, grassed waterways, and controlled grazing across the Edwards Plateau, Blackland Prairie, Post Oak belt and Coastal Prairie.
- **Dams:** Self-clearing low water dams are beneficial above Kerr County, while large reservoirs are considered risky because siltation would rapidly restore rough fish dominance. The proposed Canyon Reservoir in Comal County was judged the most acceptable of the proposed dams.

**Conclusions (San Antonio)**

- Channel and flathead catfish would benefit most from large-scale removal, but rough fish and Rio Grande perch would rapidly re-establish unless treatment extended from San Antonio to the mouth of Cibolo Creek.
- Below Cibolo Creek, flow is somewhat increased, silt is less severe, and exotic species are less problematic. Catfish populations would limit the benefit of fish poisoning there.
- Recommended techniques include feasible headwater dams (especially on Cibolo Creek, which dries to pools most of the year), strategic rough fish removal and restocking, abatement of pollution and excessive irrigation, and encouragement of commercial fishing for rough species. The author concludes that soil and water conservation is the main hope for long-term stream management.
