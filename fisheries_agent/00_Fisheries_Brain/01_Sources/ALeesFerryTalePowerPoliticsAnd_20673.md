# Lee's Ferry Fishery and the Colorado River Below Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Autumn 1991 Trout Unlimited feature traces the history of the [[Colorado River]] at [[Lee's Ferry]], Arizona, and argues that the [[Lee's Ferry Fishery]], a wild and stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] tailwater, is being degraded by the peaking-power operations of [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The article covers the river's exploration and settlement history, the rise of federal reclamation and the [[Law of the River]], the environmental studies that followed closure of the dam, and the 1990–1991 political fight over interim flows. It concludes that the fishery and the [[Grand Canyon]] cannot be protected without a change in how the dam is operated, which requires Congressional action.

## Background & Description

The piece is a narrative and advocacy article rather than a research paper, and it reports no original field survey. Its ecological content relies on agency figures (principally [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]) and on quotations from guides, anglers, scientists and legislators.

Key historical anchors:

- **Early exploration:** Spanish padres [[Dominguez and Escalante]] crossed near Lee's Ferry in 1776. [[John Wesley Powell]] passed through in 1869 and again in 1871–1872, and was the first to map the region in detail.
- **Settlement:** Mormon pioneers established a ferry run by [[John Doyle Lee]], who was later executed for his role in the [[Mountain Meadows Massacre]] of 1857. The Mormon Church sold the ferry in 1909. The ferry was closed after its 1928 capsizing, when [[Navajo Bridge]] was completed.
- **Reclamation:** The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR) was created by the [[Newlands Reclamation Act]] of 1902. The [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922 apportioned water between the upper and lower basins, with Lee's Ferry as the "Compact Point."
- **Glen Canyon Dam:** Construction began in October 1956 under the [[Colorado River Storage Project Act]] (1956). The dam was closed in 1963, and [[Lake Powell]] filled by 1980.

Lee's Ferry is the reference point for all river mileages (mile zero), and it sits exactly midway between the river's mountain origins and its mouth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dam and river physics**

- Glen Canyon Dam is 710 feet high and contains about five million cubic yards of concrete. It impounds a lake 186 miles long with 2,000 miles of shoreline.
- Before closure the river carried heavy silt and was warm and turbid. After closure it ran clear and cold, creating conditions suited to salmonids.
- Water temperatures of 46–48°F were reported in the 1960s and 1970s. The article describes the current average as 47°F, "just to the cool side of ideal" for rainbows.
- The dam was converted to a [[Peaking Power]] facility after the 1982 turbine upgrade. Flows now track electricity demand. Winter minimums can drop to about 1,000 cfs during the rainbow spawning season, and summer releases can reach 31,500 cfs.
- Daily fluctuations can raise or lower the river by up to 13 feet. The article says these swings strand trout, desiccate [[Redds]] (spawning nests), wash out eggs and fry, scour beaches, and dry algae beds that support food organisms.

**Fishery and aquatic community**

- The [[Lee's Ferry Fishery]] covers 15.8 miles. It is managed under Arizona's "Blue Ribbon" concept.
- Stocking began after closure. The Arizona Game and Fish Department planted "catchable" rainbows (Bel Air and Kamloops strains), freshwater shrimp (*Gammarus lacustris*) were introduced as food in 1968, rainbow fingerlings were stocked from 1976, and brook trout from 1977.
- Browns and cutthroats occasionally appear but are not a managed component.
- The cold, clear water pushed the native [[Humpback Chub]] downstream to the mouth of the Little Colorado River. The [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were listed as endangered. Historic native fishes also included the [[Colorado Squawfish]].
- Scuds and mayflies are the main forage. The article reports that anglers and guides consider an additional food source, such as a mayfly, desirable.
- Arizona's coldwater resource totals 1,490 miles of streams and 2,923 acres of lakes. Only 211 acres of lakes and 23 miles of streams (including the 15.8 miles of Lee's Ferry) carry Blue Ribbon status.

**Governance and legal framework**

- The [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (1969) requires an [[Environmental Impact Statement]] (EIS) for federal projects that significantly affect the environment.
- [[Public Law 90-537]] (1968) prohibits construction or study of dams in Grand Canyon without Congressional approval.
- The [[Grand Canyon Enlargement Act]] (1975) placed Grand and Marble Canyons within Grand Canyon National Park.
- Operating priorities for Glen Canyon Dam were stated as flood control, river regulation, consumptive use, reclamation, and hydropower. Power generation was originally ranked last but became the dominant priority in practice.
- The article's authors argue that the Law of the River does not legally require the Bureau to run the dam for power alone.

## Key Findings

**1. Dam operations drive fishery decline.** The article presents peaking-power fluctuations as the central cause of the Lee's Ferry fishery's problems, including stranding, redd desiccation, egg and fry loss, and reduced food availability.

**2. Growth and reproduction have fallen.** Arizona Game and Fish Department figures cited in the article show:
- Rainbow growth fell from about one inch per month around 1980 to about one-half inch per month at the time of writing.
- Only 27 percent of the rainbow trout in the river are naturally reproduced.

**3. Study flows made conditions worse.** Three-day flows of 5,000 cfs in June 1990 were reported to have caused emaciated, "cigar-shaped" trout by autumn. Parasites were found in the intestines of the thin fish. Guide Larry Riley summarized the problem: "We ended up having more fish than groceries."

**4. The 1988 GCES report confirmed known damage.** The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) final report of 1988 documented the damage. The article notes that a [[National Research Council]] review (1987, *River and Dam Management*) criticized the Bureau for unclear objectives and resistance to change.

**5. Political opposition blocked interim relief.** The article describes the following:
- A December 1988 lawsuit by [[Trout Unlimited]] and allied groups.
- A April 1989 injunction by Judge Thomas J. Greene against long-term power contracts until an EIS was prepared.
- The August 1989 order by Interior Secretary Manuel Lujan for an EIS.
- Representative George Miller's HR 4498 (April 4, 1990), which had 29 co-sponsors and called for interim flows.
- Senator John McCain's Grand Canyon Protection Act (SB 2807, 1990), which died in the 1990 session. It was reintroduced as SB 144 on January 14, 1991.

**6. Proposed release criteria.** The study's scientists recommended a maximum flow of 20,000 cfs (versus 31,500 cfs then permitted), a minimum above 5,000 cfs, and a daily fluctuation limit of 5,000 cfs.

**7. Interim flow test announced.** The Bureau of Reclamation announced a 90-day interim flow test beginning August 1, 1991, following the end of research flows on July 31, 1991. Its parameters were:
- Maximum flow of 20,000 cfs.
- Minimum flow of 8,000 cfs from 7 AM to 7 PM and 5,000 cfs at night.
- Hourly ramp limits of +2,500 cfs and −1,500 cfs.
- Maximum daily fluctuation of 5,000–8,000 cfs, depending on monthly volume.

The Bureau said the interim flows would remain in effect until the Glen Canyon Dam EIS is completed, expected in late 1993. A draft EIS was due for public comment in mid-1992.

**8. Angler regulations and ethics.**
- The Arizona "catch-and-release" policy was described as "anything under six dead fish." Trout Unlimited's Lee's Ferry Chapter and Arizona Trout Unlimited (AZTU) sought true no-kill rules, especially during the October–April spawning season.
- The 1991 regulations set a 16- to 22-inch slot and a two-fish limit, with only one allowed over 22 inches.
- Guides reported that roughly 75 percent of their clients fished with fly rods.
- Guides also opposed the "San Juan Shuffle" (shuffling feet to dislodge insects), which they described as "a meat hunter's game."

**9. Guides' recommendations for recovery.** Veteran guide Terry Gunn listed three requirements for the fishery to regain world-class status: a regular, moderately fluctuating flow of 8,000 cfs or more, no-kill regulations, and an additional food supply such as a mayfly.

**10. Policy position of the article.** The article concludes that the fishery and the Grand Canyon are "national values" that must be protected, and that only Congressional action can force the Bureau to operate the dam for ecological purposes. It urges readers to act.

**11. Status at publication.** The Grand Canyon Protection Act had not yet passed. The Miller House version passed in the spring of 1991, but McCain's Senate measure was stalled in committee and tied to an omnibus water bill.
