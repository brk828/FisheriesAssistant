# Landlocked Salmon and Brook Trout of Cold Stream Drainage, Penobscot County, Maine

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Game report (Dingell-Johnson Project F-5-R, Fishery Research and Management Division, by Lyndon H. Bond and Stuart E. DeRoche) examines how far [[Landlocked Salmon]] and [[Eastern Brook Trout]] use the outlet streams of the Cold Stream drainage (about 48 square miles) as spawning and nursery areas. Two-way weir traps operated from 1951 to 1953 measured migration, tagging and fin-clipping recorded survival and returns, and creel censuses and scale analyses documented angler catch and growth. The main conclusions are that the lake outlet is valuable salmon spawning and nursery habitat, that a fishway at the Cold Stream Pond outlet dam was justified and built in November 1952, and that stocked brook trout contributed nothing to the angler fishery.

## Background & Description

The drainage holds three ponds and their outlets. The Upper Cold Stream Ponds are separated by a road culvert, and their outlet, Smelt Brook, flows into Cold Stream Pond.

- **Upper Cold Stream Ponds:** The upper pond has a maximum depth of 32 feet and covers 186 acres. The lower pond reaches 62 feet and covers 499 acres. Both are oligotrophic, but the upper pond has low deep-water oxygen and light brown water. Thermal stratification occurs at 20 feet (upper) and 30 feet (lower).
- **[[Cold Stream Pond]]:** A typical oligotrophic lake of 3,628 acres with a maximum depth of 104 feet and high oxygen throughout. Stratification occurs at about 30 feet in summer, surface temperatures rarely exceed 70 °F, and cooler layers average about 50 °F. Aquatic vegetation is sparse, limited to one cove of about 5 acres.

The Upper Cold Stream Ponds hold 22 fish species, including landlocked salmon, brook trout, [[White Perch]] (the most abundant game species by trap and netting records), [[Yellow Perch]], [[Chain Pickerel]], [[American Smelt]], [[American Eel]], [[White Sucker]], [[Common Shiner]], [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]] and [[Freshwater Sculpin]]. Cold Stream Pond also contains [[Lake Trout]] and [[Burbot]] (cusk). Smallmouth bass are present in deadwater areas about a mile below the outlet. Salmonid spawning is confined to the outlet stream, and the Cold Stream Pond outlet dam originally had no fishway.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Migration trapping design.** Three two-way weirs were run:

1. [[Culvert Trap]]: May 4, 1951 to May 31, 1952, between the two Upper Cold Stream Ponds.
2. [[Upper Cold Stream Ponds Outlet Trap]]: May 21, 1951 to May 13, 1953.
3. [[Cold Stream Pond Outlet Trap]]: June 13, 1951 to June 28, 1953, in Enfield, about 100 yards below the outlet dam.

Fish over 8 inches were tagged with monel metal jaw tags. Smaller fish were fin-clipped. Anesthesia used urethane (1.7 grams per gallon). Water height and temperature were recorded at each trap.

**Species movement patterns.** Movement peaked during each species' spawning period. Ten species moved downstream in sufficient numbers to be able to colonize waters below the drainage if habitat was suitable. Smelt was the only species present in the traps every month. Its largest runs occurred in late April and early May.

**Habitat constraints.** The outlet stream substrate is mainly unproductive sand with a few boulders, and Smelt Brook is hard to reach, which makes stream improvement difficult.

## Key Findings

**Trap totals**
- The Culvert Trap recorded 10,627 upstream and 8,134 downstream fish over 13 months. Common shiner dominated (9,553 total). Brook trout totaled 29 fish, and all but 3 were hatchery plants above the weir.
- The Upper Cold Stream Ponds Outlet Trap recorded 19 species moving downstream but only 10 significant upstream. Smelt (1,172) and redbellied dace (990) were the most abundant. Brook trout totaled 360, and only white sucker exceeded them in upstream movement. Trout runs were 173 down and 152 up in 1951–52, and 30 down and 5 up in 1952–53, which was linked to low water.
- The Cold Stream Pond Outlet Trap recorded 19 species. Landlocked salmon was most abundant (1,135), followed by smelt (750). Excluding lake-dwelling species, upstream movement was 950 and downstream movement was 982. White sucker made up over 12 percent of downstream migrants.

**Salmon production in the lake outlet**
- Table 1 showed 1.8 to 5.9 inch fish moving 323 down and 222 up, and 6.0 to 9.9 inch fish moving 117 down and 227 up. Upstream movement of the 6.0 to 9.9 inch class was therefore about double the downstream movement. Most salmon were not ready to ascend to the lake until about 6 inches.
- 449 immature salmon (1.8 to 9.9 inches) were fin-clipped and moved to the lake.
- 150 mature salmon used the outlet for spawning across two seasons. Of these, 96 returned to the lake, a 64 percent return rate. This compares with 29.5 percent survival to a second spawning in the Fish River Lakes study (Warner, 1954).
- Adult return migration to the lake began at 45 °F in 1951 and 48 °F in 1952. Sixty-six percent of the 1951 run and 50 percent of the 1952 run returned in May, 6 to 8 months after spawning.
- A fishway at the outlet dam was operational on November 15, 1952. From July 1, 1952 to June 30, 1953, it passed 126 fish downstream and 359 upstream.

**Survival of stocked brook trout**
- On June 8, 1951, 1,006 fin-clipped brook trout (1949 brood year) were stocked above the uppermost trap, and 250 were stocked in Smelt Brook. A total of 1,256 fish were stocked.
- The recovery table covers 24 upstream and 16 downstream movements. No stocked trout were recovered by anglers. None were taken in traps after November 1951. Vandals broke open one trap on June 9, 1951.

**Lake-tagged adult salmon**
- 18 mature salmon were jaw-tagged at Webb Cove in October 1952. Four (22 percent) were caught by anglers in spring 1953, and all had spawned the previous fall. The report states that lake spawning or spawning in small tributaries may be occurring, but this was not confirmed. Seining small tributaries produced no young salmon.
- Anglers exploited about 29 percent of tagged salmon in the lake. This is a minimum figure because tag loss and natural mortality were not accounted for. The lake trout exploitation rate was 35 percent.

**Creel census (Cold Stream Pond, 1952–1953)**
- A total of 2,935 anglers were checked. Salmon catch was 465 fish overall (123 in 1952, 342 in 1953). Most were taken in April and May.
- Total catch of other species: lake trout, landlocked salmon, brook trout, chain pickerel and white perch (see Table 2 of the report).

**Age, growth and catch**
- Spawning population: 1951 dominant age groups were II to IV. The 1952 run was older, with age groups III, IV and V dominant. The 1952 run included fish up to age VII. Mature females of up to V annuli in the 9.0 to 14.0 inch range showed that stream-confined salmon grow more slowly than pond-accessible salmon.
- Angler catch: age group III was dominant in 1953 (about 77 percent of catch). Age groups III and IV dominated overall.
- Growth: Cold Stream salmon reached the 14-inch legal length during the third year, compared with the fourth year at Square Lake (Warner, 1955). Cold Stream growth was slightly above the Maine average (Rupp, 1954) for year classes IV and III and slightly below for older groups.
- Stream-dwelling salmon: 92 fish were sampled on July 1, 1952 and 162 on October 13, 1952. Mean total length after two summers was 6.19 inches. Growth showed no significant change from 1951 to 1952.

**Returns from hatchery stocking**
- Stocking and returns showed no correlation between the number of salmon stocked and the angler catch for a given brood year. For example, 40,000 fish stocked in 1949 (1948 brood year) returned 64 fish, and 41,000 stocked in 1950 (1949 brood year) returned 184.
- Salmon generally did not reach the 14-inch legal size until age three, and after five years they contributed little to the fishery.

**Summary of conclusions**
- The lake outlet has significant value as salmon spawning and nursery habitat.
- A fishway at the Cold Stream Pond outlet dam was justified and was built as a result of the study.
- Stocked brook trout returned nothing to anglers.
- The growth rate of Cold Stream salmon compares favorably with other Maine lakes.
