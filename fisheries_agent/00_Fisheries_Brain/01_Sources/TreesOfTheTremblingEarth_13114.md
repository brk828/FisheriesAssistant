# Trees of the Trembling Earth: Southern Beech Regeneration in Chile's Lake District

#botany #forest-ecology #nothofagus

## Executive Summary

This article examines why [[Southern Beech]] trees (genus [[Nothofagus]]) are common as very large adults and as seedlings but rare at intermediate sizes in mature forests of the Chilean [[Lake District]]. The author's research, carried out over about a decade, argues that the beeches depend on large-scale natural disturbances such as [[Landslides]], [[Volcanic Ash]] deposition, floods, windstorms, and fire. These events create open ground where beech seedlings can establish, while shade-tolerant competitors are suppressed. Field evidence from 1976 to 1984 supported this view, although the author notes that the long-term question of regeneration in intact forests still requires decades of monitoring.

## Background & Description

- **Trees:** Four common beech species, known locally as [[Coigue]], [[Coigue De Chiloé]], [[Roble]], and [[Rauli]], dominate mature forests from the lowlands up to about 3,000 feet. The two evergreen coigues occur where conditions are wettest, while the deciduous roble and rauli prevail in slightly warmer, less saturated conditions.
- **Size:** Mature beeches are typically several feet in diameter and more than 150 feet tall, standing 30 to 60 feet above the rest of the canopy. Their crowns often span 60 feet or more. Individual trees live about four or five centuries.
- **Puzzle:** In undisturbed forests, beeches occur mainly as huge trees or as seedlings under six inches tall. Intermediate sizes are exceedingly rare, although other trees grow in all sizes. No evidence suggests the beeches are disappearing, which raised the question of how they regenerate.
- **Setting:** The region lies between 38° and 42° S. Its Andes are old granitic rock overlain by volcanic deposits from the last 65 million years, dissected by Ice Age glaciers into steep valleys now filled with crystal blue lakes. Glaciated slopes are blanketed with volcanic ash.
- **Climate:** Pacific air masses release 150 to 200 inches of precipitation per year on the western flanks of the Andes. Forty to 50 percent falls from June through August, while the "dry" summer months of January through March receive four to eight inches each. Temperatures are mild, similar to the Pacific Northwest of the United States.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seismic and volcanic disturbance.** Southern Chile is one of the most seismically active regions in the world. The 1960 earthquake, measured at 8.7 on the Richter scale, destroyed or seriously damaged 60 percent of the buildings in [[Valdivia]]. It was part of an eleven-shock swarm over four days. Coastal uplift and subsidence (about six feet up to the north, five feet down to the south) let seawater inundate approximately 99,000 acres. Thousands of landslides collapsed hillsides, and [[Puyehue Volcano]] blanketed a 54,000-acre area with about two inches of pumice, sand, and gravel. From 1520 to 1946, forty-seven notable earthquakes struck the region, seven roughly comparable to 1960. Quakes partly destroyed Valdivia in 1575, 1737, 1837, and 1907. In 1575, a mudflow blocked the outflow of [[Lake Ranco]], and the breach about four and a half months later caused catastrophic floods. [[Villarrica Volcano]] has had nine notable eruptions in the past two centuries. A mild 1972 eruption dropped nearly six tons of ash per acre at a site fifteen miles away.

**Other disturbances.** Windstorms exceeding 100 miles per hour can fell whole stands. Logging, burning, avalanches, and fire all open ground. Fires are sometimes fueled by [[Bamboo]], which flowers and dies at intervals of several decades, leaving heavy dry fuel loads.

**Landforms.** Earthquake-triggered debris flows are now thought to have carried many valley-bottom boulders that were previously attributed to glaciers. Older landslides buried former surface soils under several yards of debris.

## Key Findings

1. **Beeches colonize disturbed ground quickly.** Small beech trees on landslide sites began growing at most sites within two years of the slide, based on ring counts from cored wood. Beech seedlings and small trees were among the most abundant woody plants on these sites. Their light, winged seeds disperse 300 feet or more from parent trees.

2. **Substrate quality shapes colonization.** Shrubs and trees grew densest and fastest on the most thoroughly weathered volcanic ash with the smallest particles. Sites dominated by granite bedrock or rocky rubble had sparse cover and slow tree growth.

3. **Shade-tolerant competitors are excluded early.** On bare sites, tepa, trevo, and maiiio were absent or extremely rare despite nearby seed sources. Dense, pure beech stands grew to twenty feet and more within a few hundred feet of seed trees.

4. **Succession follows a multi-century sequence.** Pure beech thickets may persist for roughly a century. Shade-tolerant seedlings then sprout in the understory and rise as beeches thin, creating gaps. A mature structure of sparse giant beeches above a dense shade-tolerant canopy takes three centuries or more. Tree-ring patterns match this sequence: rapid, then declining beech growth, and bursts of accelerated growth in the other trees.

5. **Beeches need large-scale disturbance.** Beech seedlings survived in the understory for only two years in three-year plot observations. A 1983 to 1984 survey found no beeches using tree-fall gaps, which averaged about 4,000 square feet. Gaps were instead filled by shade-tolerant trees and bamboo, which shoot up eighteen to thirty feet in one growing season after canopy openings form.

6. **Disturbance is frequent enough to matter.** The author concludes that large disturbances are both highly favorable to beeches and frequent enough to shape forest composition and structure. A hypothetical beechless forest would take more than a millennium to develop. None was found, so the last major disturbance must have been too recent for that succession to occur.

7. **Monitoring is ongoing.** Permanent plots were installed in 1977 to test whether mature-forest regeneration could sustain the sparse big-beech population. The author describes results as tentative and notes that a definitive answer needs several decades of monitoring.

**Research team and timeline:** The author, [[Thomas T. Veblen]], spent a four-year stay from 1975 in southern Chile. In 1976 he collaborated with [[David Ashton]], an Australian botanist, on landslide-site sampling. Field work continued through 1979, with permanent plots established in 1977 and a survey in 1983 to 1984.
