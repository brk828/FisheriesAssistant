# Fisheries Investigation of Lake Mead, Lake Mohave and the Colorado River Below Davis Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Nevada Fish and Game Commission final report (Dingell-Johnson Project F-1-8, by Robert C. Sumner, Project Leader and Fisheries Technician) documents a multi-year comparative study of reservoirs created by impoundment on the lower [[Colorado River]]. The work covered [[Lake Mead]] (formed by [[Hoover Dam]], completed 1935), [[Lake Mohave]] (formed by [[Davis Dam]], filled 1950 to 1951), and the river reach below Davis Dam. The study aimed to describe limnology, food organisms, predators, fish populations, angling use and regulations, and to recommend management for sustained fishing.

The investigation found that wide annual water-level fluctuation at Lake Mead prevented establishment of rooted aquatic plants and limited the benthic insect fauna, while largemouth bass and sport fish supported an angling fishery that rose after 1951 and declined later. Lake Mohave became a trout and bass fishery, with rainbow trout planted heavily but returning poorly, and a 1952 outbreak of the parasite Lernia affected trout. Recommendations emphasized maintaining the current liberal regulations, experimental creel-size trout plantings, possible brush-shelter habitat, and caution with introductions of new species.

## Background & Description

The Lake Mead and Lake Mohave region lies in the Lower Sonoran life zone of the Mojave Desert, with hot summers, very low precipitation (typically 3 to 5 inches per year), and frequent flash floods. The study area forms a continuous boundary between Nevada and Arizona, and is accessed by U.S. highways 91, 93, 95 and 466 and several secondary roads. Nearby population centers include Las Vegas and Boulder City, Nevada, Kingman, Arizona, and Needles, California.

The Colorado River basin above Lake Mead drains roughly 158,000 square miles across seven states (the report lists six, Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, New Mexico, Arizona and Nevada), with the most significant contributing area in the upper basin. Lake Mead at peak storage covers 229 square miles, has about 550 miles of shoreline, and a maximum depth of roughly 500 feet at the time of construction. Lake Mohave, formed behind Davis Dam, covers about 28,500 acres and has roughly 200 miles of irregular shoreline, with a normal operating surface elevation near 641 feet and an active storage capacity of about 1,600,000 acre-feet.

Historically, the river supported native fishes such as [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Humpback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Non-native sport fishes including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Crappie]] and [[Bluegill]] were stocked into Lake Mead between 1935 and 1942.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water fluctuation.** Lake Mead's surface fluctuated widely, averaging about 33.9 feet annually between 1938 and 1951, with extremes from about 2.5 to 79.5 feet. This drawdown exposed shoreline and eliminated rooted vegetation. Submerged aquatic plants such as sago pondweed and muskgrass were lost from the lower lake after filling because of increased depth and reduced light penetration.

**Water chemistry.** Lake Mead was judged moderately low in basic fertility, with dissolved solids declining over the study. Dissolved oxygen ranged from 5.0 to 10.0 parts per million and free carbon dioxide from 0 to 5 parts per million, so no deficiency was found. Water temperatures in Lake Mead ranged roughly from 50°F to 79°F, with a cold-water layer persisting below about 30 feet in summer.

**Temperature below Davis Dam.** Releases from Hoover Dam were cold (about 55°F) through the lower gates, and warm-water flows occurred when the upper gates were used. Downstream of Davis Dam, the river was clear and silt-free, with temperatures typically between 45°F and 70°F.

**Biological communities.** Aquatic insects (mayflies, dragonflies, damselflies, midges) were abundant in Lake Mead only in some years, and were strongly reduced by water fluctuation. Freshwater shrimp, snails and crayfish occurred at low levels. Algae (blue-green, green and diatoms) dominated plankton and food webs, and zooplankton samples were small. Terrestrial insects washed into the reservoir contributed food for trout.

**Predators.** Double-crested cormorants and great blue herons were the most important fish-eating birds; the report judged that their predation was not a serious threat to game fish populations overall. Soft-shelled turtles were rarely caught and only occasionally noted as a predator on fish.

## Key Findings

**Fish population composition (Lake Mead, 1953-1954 gill net and angling data).**
- Thirteen species were recorded, with [[Largemouth Bass]] rated abundant, and channel catfish, black crappie, bluegill and green sunfish common or rare depending on species.
- Native fishes were humpback sucker and bonytail chub (common or abundant), while carp were the most numerous coarse fish.
- Bass made up about 95 percent of the sport catch in some periods; catch percentages from the gill-net sample were recorded in the study tables.

**Largemouth bass (Lake Mead).**
- Bass were the most important game fish. Good fishing occurred around 1939, declined around 1941, improved again, and declined after the late 1940s.
- The largest bass sampled were 21 inches long and weighed about 10 pounds; average length of angler-caught bass was about 12.2 inches in 1952.
- Bass food shifted from young bluegill and shad to other fishes and crayfish, with the lack of aquatic plants cited as a limiting factor for growth.

**Channel catfish.** Catfish spawned in June and July, grew to 13 to 14 inches by the end of the second year, and the largest recorded fish was 20 pounds. Catfish were omnivorous, feeding on algae, insects and detritus.

**Black crappie.** Crappie were introduced between 1935 and 1940 and became an important angling species by 1942. Spawning occurred in April and May, and crappie were heavily taken by anglers in the 1950s.

**Rainbow trout (Lake Mohave and below Davis Dam).**
- Trout planting between 1951 and 1954 included fingerling, fry and creel-size fish, with the largest stocking events exceeding 1 million fry.
- Tagged trout below Davis Dam grew about six inches per year in the first year, and 39 percent of returns from one tagging group came from the taggers' area.
- A 1952 outbreak of the parasite Lernia on trout was noted, although its direct role in mortality was doubtful.

**Lake Mohave operation.** Lake Mohave's surface was lowered 20 feet in 1953, which reduced power head at Davis Dam, and the operating level was later raised to 571 feet. Cold water from the lower intake gates kept trout habitat viable.

**Recommendations.**
- Maintain the existing liberal creel and possession regulations, which were judged adequate; the minimum size limit on bass was discontinued in 1952.
- Consider experimental creel-size rainbow trout plantings for at least three years below Davis Dam.
- Test brush-shelter habitat near shallow shorelines in Lake Mohave, to be installed in early spring.
- Use caution with introductions of new species such as northern smallmouth bass or walleye, pending further study.
- Maintain cold-water releases to favor trout survival and prevent parasite outbreaks.

**Economic value.** Angler-day estimates for Lake Mead and Lake Mohave in 1951 and 1952 were derived from license sales, with Clark County license sales used as the primary indicator. The report notes that the sale of licenses represents only a fraction of funds spent by anglers.
