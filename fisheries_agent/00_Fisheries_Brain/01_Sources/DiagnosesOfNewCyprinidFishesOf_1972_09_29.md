## Executive Summary

This 1972 taxonomic paper by Hubbs and Miller formally diagnoses **one new genus, two new species, and six new subspecies** of cyprinid (minnow) fishes endemic to isolated, relictual waters of the Great Basin of the western United States. These taxa represent surviving remnants of a once-widespread fish fauna that inhabited extensive Pleistocene "pluvial" lakes and connected drainages, now reduced to isolated springs and spring-fed creeks following late-Pleistocene desiccation. The paper is explicitly framed as part of a broader research program correlating **hydrographic history** with **fish speciation and differentiation** in endorheic (closed) desert basins. Central to the description is [[Relictus Solitarius]], a newly erected monotypic genus with distinctive osteological, meristic, and karyotypic features, alongside new species and subspecies within the genera [[Gila]] and [[Rhinichthys]].

## Background & Description

The Great Basin of western North America contains numerous endorheic (internally drained) basins that, during the late Pleistocene, held extensive "pluvial" lakes (e.g., Lakes Gale, Franklin, Steptoe, Waring, Alvord, Newark, Clover, Gilbert). As the climate warmed and these lakes desiccated into isolated springs and small streams, resident fish populations became geographically isolated and diverged morphologically, producing a mosaic of endemic species and subspecies confined to single springs or small drainage remnants.

This paper is an early, detailed documentation of that differentiation, supplementing a 1948 summary (Hubbs & Miller) and anticipating a larger monograph (Hubbs & Miller, in press) on the "Hydrographic history and relict fishes of the north-central Great Basin." All type specimens were deposited at the **University of Michigan Museum of Zoology (UMMZ)**.

### Taxa Diagnosed

**[[Relictus Solitarius]] (new genus and species)**
- Type locality: hillside spring, Kirkpatrick Ranch, Butte Valley, Elko County, Nevada (basins of pluvial Lakes Gale, Franklin, Steptoe, and Waring).
- Diagnostic osteology: greatly expanded dorsal crest of maxilla; slender cleithrum; elongate supraethmoid (laterally expanded at front, resembling *Rhinichthys*); long, narrow urohyal.
- Vertebrae: 35–39. Pharyngeal teeth 4–4 (rarely 5–4 or 4–3), arch thin/lacy medially.
- Gill-rakers: 7–12 (usually 8–11).
- Mouth oblique, terminal, no horny cutting edges, no frenum or barbel.
- Lateral line obsolescent/disrupted (3–29 pores total); supratemporal canal rarely complete.
- Scales small (50–70 transverse rows), irregular, numerous radii.
- Uniquely paddlelike pelvic fins; dorsal and pelvic fins both displaced posteriorly, beginning at same vertical (as in *Siphateles*/*Gila*).
- Distinctive nuptial tuberculation pattern on head, infraorbital canal, and pectoral rays.
- Karyotype: 2n = 50, with 2 large + 8 small acrocentrics, 12 metacentrics, 28 subtelocentric/submetacentric.

**[[Gila Alvordensis]] (new species)**
- Type locality: Trout Creek, tributary to Alvord Desert, Harney County, Oregon (basin of pluvial Lake Alvord).
- A chub related to subgenus *Siphateles*, but with notably reduced, embedded scales bearing radii all around (convergent with *Rhinichthys*/*Relictus*).
- Pharyngeal teeth uniserial, normally 5–4.
- Distinctive strong nuptial tuberculation on flattened, twisted pectoral fin of males.
- Fin rays: dorsal 7–10 (mode 7), anal 6–9 (mode 7), caudal 17–20 (mode 19), pelvic 7–9 (mode 8).
- Gill-rakers: 16–22.
- Notably dwarfed in the Borax Lake population.

**[[Gila Bicolor Newarkensis]] (new subspecies)**
- Type locality: spring, Newark Valley, White Pine County, Nevada (Lake Newark basin).
- Medium-small chub (max ~97 mm); dark, uniform coloration with scale-margin pigmentation forming stripes; turgid, rounded body; broadly rounded muzzle; low anal-ray counts (mode 7); few, short gill-rakers (mode 12); pharyngeal teeth usually 5–4.

**[[Gila Bicolor Euchila]] (new subspecies)**
- Type locality: Fish Creek Springs, Eureka County, Nevada (Lake Newark basin).
- Unusually large chub (males to 114 mm, females to 149 mm). Distinct sexual coloration (moss-green females; gilt/rosy/orange males). Larger, more pointed head; larger, more oblique mouth; low scale-row counts; more posteriorly inserted dorsal fin; supratemporal canal more often complete than in most *G. bicolor* forms.

**[[Gila Bicolor Isolata]] (new subspecies)**
- Type locality: Warm Springs, Independence Valley, Elko County, Nevada (edge of pluvial Lake Clover bed).
- Somewhat dwarfed (max: males 73 mm, females 91 mm). Wide unpigmented ventral band; distinctive black midventral speck at caudal base. Lateral line frequently incomplete on peduncle; supratemporal canal regularly complete (unique among subspecies studied). Anal rays predominantly 7, pelvic rays predominantly 8; low vertebral/scale counts; few gill-rakers (8–14, mean 11.14).

**[[Rhinichthys Osculus Reliquus]] (new subspecies)**
- Type locality: spring-fed creek, Grass Valley, Lander County, Nevada (Lake Gilbert basin); population apparently now extinct.
- Relatively large dace (to 82 mm). Broad, solid dark lateral band; conspicuous deep-lying melanophores on lower sides; dark wedge on lower caudal peduncle; heavily punctate lower lip; barbel almost always absent; supratemporal canal commissure consistently, widely interrupted; most posterior pelvic-fin insertion among forms studied; extreme sexual dimorphism in pectoral length; reversed pattern of dorsal-fin sexual dimorphism relative to other *Rhinichthys*.

**[[Rhinichthys Osculus Oligoporus]] (new subspecies)**
- Type locality: Warm Springs, Clover Valley, Elko County, Nevada (Lake Clover basin).
- Average-sized dace; extensively speckled body; reduced/absent lower lateral band; reduced/disrupted basicaudal wedge; rounded head/body form; barbel invariably absent (n=51); extreme reduction of body lateral line; lower scale counts than *R. o. reliquus*.

**[[Rhinichthys Osculus Lethoporus]] (new subspecies)**
- Type locality: Warm Springs, Independence Valley, Nevada (same collection as *Gila bicolor isolata* holotype).
- Most dwarfed *Rhinichthys* in the region (largest male 34 mm, largest female 39 mm, n=101). Fine dark speckling extending onto caudal peduncle; distinctly compressed body form (body-width ratio ~2.0 vs. ~1.5 in *R. o. robustus*); barbel almost always absent; reduced lateral line (intermediate between the other two new subspecies); strongly oblique, nearly straight mouth; low pectoral-ray count (mean 12.72); reduced vertebrae and scale rows.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

All taxa described are restricted to **highly isolated, small-volume aquatic habitats**—springs, spring-fed creeks, and remnant streams—situated within the beds or margins of former Pleistocene pluvial lakes (Lakes Gale, Franklin, Steptoe, Waring, Alvord, Newark, Clover, and Gilbert). These endorheic basins have no outlet to the ocean, and the fish faunas they harbor are products of vicariance: as pluvial lakes contracted during the late Pleistocene and Holocene, previously continuous or interconnected populations were isolated in disjunct spring systems, each subsequently diverging under unique selective and genetic-drift regimes.

Key environmental correlates highlighted include:
- **Extreme habitat isolation**, sometimes to a single spring, producing severe dwarfism (e.g., *R. o. lethoporus*, *G. b. isolata*) or, conversely, gigantism relative to typical isolated populations (e.g., *G. b. euchila*).
- **Thermal/spring habitats** (many type localities are warm springs), which likely constrain body size, growth, and possibly favor loss of structures like barbels and lateral-line completeness.
- **Loss or reduction of dispersal-related traits** (e.g., reduced lateral line, absent barbels) potentially reflecting relaxed selection in stable, low-flow, isolated spring habitats.
- The described taxa collectively illustrate a **parallel and independent morphological divergence** occurring across multiple, separate basins in the genera [[Gila]] and [[Rhinichthys]], as well as the emergence of a wholly distinct lineage, [[Relictus Solitarius]], underscoring the evolutionary significance of Great Basin desert spring systems as reservoirs of cryptic endemic biodiversity.
- At least one lineage (*Rhinichthys osculus reliquus*) is noted as **apparently extinct** at the time of writing, reflecting the fragility of these microendemic populations to habitat alteration or desiccation.

This work exemplifies the broader theme of studying **relict aquatic faunas** as biological archives of paleohydrography, linking contemporary fish morphology and genetics to Pleistocene lake histories (see [[Pluvial Lakes]], [[Endorheic Basins]]).

## Key Findings

- **New genus erected**: [[Relictus Solitarius]], distinguished by unique osteology (maxilla, cleithrum, supraethmoid, urohyal), paddle-like pelvic fins, distinctive nuptial tuberculation, and a karyotype with an unusual balance of acrocentric and metacentric chromosomes (2n=50).
- **Two new species**: *Relictus solitarius* (Lakes Gale/Franklin/Steptoe/Waring basins) and [[Gila Alvordensis]] (Lake Alvord basin, including a dwarfed Borax Lake population).
- **Six new subspecies** described across two genera:
  - *Gila bicolor*: newarkensis, euchila (both Lake Newark basin), and isolata (Lake Clover basin).
  - *Rhinichthys osculus*: reliquus (Lake Gilbert basin, likely extinct), oligoporus and lethoporus (both Lake Clover basin).
- Diagnostic characters used to separate taxa include: pharyngeal tooth counts, gill-raker number/shape, scale-row counts, lateral-line completeness/pore counts, supratemporal canal completeness, fin-ray counts, body/head shape, nuptial tuberculation patterns, coloration, and (for *Relictus*) karyotype.
- Findings reinforce the concept that **isolated Great Basin spring systems act as evolutionary laboratories**, generating measurable morphological divergence (including extreme dwarfism, distinct pigmentation, and structural reduction) within short evolutionary timeframes tied to Pleistocene-to-Holocene lake desiccation.
- The study explicitly connects to a forthcoming, more comprehensive treatise (Hubbs & Miller, in press) on the hydrographic history and relict fishes of the north-central Great Basin, positioning this paper as a taxonomic foundation for that broader biogeographic synthesis.
