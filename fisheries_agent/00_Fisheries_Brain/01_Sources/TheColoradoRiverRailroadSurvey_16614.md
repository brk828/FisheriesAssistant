# Down the Colorado: Robert Brewster Stanton's 1889–90 Railroad Survey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #history-of-exploration

## Executive Summary

This historical article describes the 1889–90 expedition led by [[Robert Brewster Stanton]] down the [[Colorado River]]. The expedition was commissioned to survey the river's canyons and determine whether a railroad could be built along its full length. It was only the second party to run the entire river, following [[John Wesley Powell]]'s first journey roughly 20 years earlier. The expedition was marked by tragedy: three men drowned, one was seriously injured, and Stanton nearly drowned. The article also covers the survey's conclusions and the later fate of the river.

## Background & Description

The railroad idea originated with prospector S. S. Harper around 1870, who envisioned moving coal from southwestern Colorado to California. Denver businessman [[Frank M. Brown]] organized the [[Denver, Colorado Canyon and Pacific Railroad]] to test the scheme and became its president. He hired Stanton, then in his early 40s, as chief engineer. Stanton had worked as a division engineer for the [[Union Pacific]], where he built the Devil's Gate Viaduct (Georgetown Loop), and he had long admired Powell's exploits.

The main party left [[Green River Station]] on May 25, 1889, with 16 men in six boats: five cedar boats (each about 15 feet long, 40 inches wide, and 18 inches deep) and one boat under [[Frank C. Kendrick]], who had earlier surveyed the Grand River (now the Colorado) from Grand Junction to the Green River confluence. Stanton had warned against the thin cedar planking and had asked for life preservers, but Brown rejected the request.

Key events of the first journey:

- The cedar boats were repeatedly damaged, and provisions dwindled. Food planned for 75 days had shrunk to a six-day supply by June 13.
- Brown split the party to speed the survey. On July 9, at [[Marble Canyon]], Brown was thrown from his boat in a whirlpool and drowned. His body was never recovered.
- Crew members Peter Hansbrough and Henry Richards drowned the next day in a rough rapid. Stanton noted that all three deaths could have been avoided with life preservers.
- Stanton temporarily left the river, then returned to Denver to secure funding. He personally spent about $12,500 on the expedition.

The second journey used three oak boats riveted with copper rivets, cork life preservers for every crew member, and watertight rubber bags for supplies. The party of 12 included Stanton, assistant engineer John Hislop, and photographer F. A. Nims from the first party. It departed on December 10, 1889, after wagon transport to the mouth of Crescent Creek in [[Glen Canyon]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article offers little direct ecological data. It describes the Colorado River as a river with a descent of about 4,200 feet in under 500 miles, containing 520 rapids, falls, and cataracts. Stanton's party encountered the rapids of Cataract Canyon, Narrow Canyon, Glen Canyon, Marble Canyon, and the Grand Canyon, including [[Separation Rapid]] in the Grand Canyon.

The only species mentioned is the Colorado River squawfish, described as "now endangered and much-maligned." The expedition ate it for Christmas dinner at [[Lee's Ferry]] in December 1889. This is a historical reference, and the article provides no population data.

Stanton's report and the article's closing note on later developments point to a major ecological change. Dams, diversions, and hydroelectric plants were later built along the river, reducing it to "a comparative trickle at its terminal point," and burying many of the rapids Stanton's crew had struggled to pass.

## Key Findings

- **Survey conclusion:** Stanton concluded that a railroad "is neither impossible or impracticable" and could be built at a reasonable cost compared with other transcontinental lines. He argued that only the first 200 feet of a canyon's walls affect railroad engineering.
- **Operational claims:** He suggested the line could run on electricity generated by the river and offered advantages in grades, distance, and roadbed permanence. He did not assess profitability and left that to investors.
- **Human cost:** Three men drowned (Brown, Hansbrough, Richards), one photographer (Nims) suffered a broken leg and internal injuries after a 20-foot fall on January 1, 1890, and Stanton nearly drowned at Separation Rapid on March 13, 1890.
- **Safety lessons:** Stanton repeatedly noted that the deaths could have been prevented with life preservers, and that the cedar boats were unsuitable. The second expedition's oak boats and cork preservers reflected these lessons.
- **Press controversy:** The [[Denver Times]] reported that a quarter of the party had "perished in the gloom of Marble Canyon," claiming a whole boat's crew was lost. Other Denver papers disputed the claim, and Stanton later said the telegram was fabricated. Nims's arrival in Denver ended the Times's account.
- **Documentation:** Stanton took about 1,600 photographs, sent negatives to [[William H. Jackson]] for development, and kept detailed notes. He wrote a 1,038-page history of the Colorado River, finished in 1920, which was considered too long for publication.
- **Outcome:** The second party reached tidewater at the head of the [[Gulf of California]] on April 26, 1890, becoming only the second expedition to run the entire river. The railroad was never built.
- **Legacy:** Stanton, who died in 1922 in New Canaan, Connecticut, at age 75, is overshadowed by Powell in river history. The article presents him as a dedicated, cautious engineer whose survey became a symbol of frontier ambition.
