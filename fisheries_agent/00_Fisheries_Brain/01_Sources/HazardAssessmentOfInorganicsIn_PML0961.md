# Hazard Assessment of Inorganics, Individually and in Mixtures, to Two Endangered Fish in the San Juan River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study by [[Hamilton and Buhl]] (1997, *Environmental Toxicology and Water Quality*) measured 96-h acute toxicity of five individual inorganics (arsenate, copper, selenate, selenite, zinc) and five environmentally derived inorganic mixtures to larval [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], two species listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Tests used reconstituted [[San Juan River]] water. Razorback suckers were generally more sensitive than Colorado squawfish. Copper was the main toxic component of the mixtures, and the hazard ratios for copper and all five mixtures indicated a high potential hazard to larval fish at sites receiving irrigation return flows and nonpoint discharges.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*), both endangered and dependent on the San Juan River for remaining habitat. Critical life stages run from fertilized egg through the first year, and young-of-year fish use shallow embayments.
- **Regulatory context:** The study followed the [[Department of the Interior]] (DOI) irrigation drainwater program, launched after selenium contamination was discovered in the [[San Joaquin Valley]] in 1982.
- **Study area:** The San Juan River basin, including the Animas, La Plata and Mancos rivers, between Navajo Dam and Lake Powell. Irrigation return flows, particularly from the Navajo Indian Irrigation Project (possibly about 15% of the annual flow), influence water quality.
- **Fish source and husbandry:** Eggs came from Dexter National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico, and were reared at the Yankton Field Research Station, South Dakota. Water was maintained at 20 ± 2 °C during culture and 25 ± 1 °C during testing. Fish were not fed during tests.
- **Test ages:** Colorado squawfish at 32–40 days posthatch; razorback sucker at 13–23 days posthatch, the ages previously identified as most sensitive.
- **Dilution water:** Reconstituted water simulating the San Juan River at Shiprock, NM (pH 8.06 ± 0.04; hardness 144 mg/L as CaCO3), without trace elements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical status:** After the closure of Navajo Dam, few individuals of either species were collected, and both were reported as rare or extirpated. Platania et al. (1991) later confirmed adult and multi-age Colorado squawfish, and a single reproductively active male razorback sucker, but no larvae.
- **Recruitment:** No razorback sucker recruitment has been found in the San Juan River, and Colorado squawfish recruitment is limited.
- **Prior contamination data:** Earlier DOI investigations ([[Blanchard et al. 1993]]; [[Butler et al. 1994]]) reported elevated selenium and other inorganics in water, sediment and biota at irrigation-associated sites.
- **Exposure sites:** Five monitored sites were used to define the test mixtures: Ojo Amarillo Canyon, Gallegos Canyon, Hogback East Drain, Mancos River and McElmo Creek. Backwater areas near the mouths of tributaries and drains are used by young fish and are exposed to these mixtures.
- **Exposure routes:** Fish are exposed to waterborne, sedimentary and dietary toxicants together. Dietary selenium and vanadium were reported as more toxic than waterborne exposure in other studies.

## Key Findings

**Individual toxicants (96-h LC50, mg/L)**
- Razorback sucker were significantly more sensitive than Colorado squawfish to arsenate, selenate and selenite.
- Colorado squawfish were less sensitive to selenate (96-h LC50 values of 246 and 66 mg/L across the tests reported) and to selenite (12.8 and 14 mg/L). Razorback sucker were more sensitive to selenate (25.2 and 48 mg/L) and selenite (16.8 and 14 mg/L, as reported).
- Copper was the most toxic individual inorganic to both species. Copper LC50 values for larval fish were 0.293 mg/L (Colorado squawfish) and 0.385 mg/L (razorback sucker) at 96 h (within the reported ranges).
- Zinc was less toxic than copper.

**Mixture toxicity**
- Gallegos Canyon mixture: synergistic toxicity for both species.
- Mancos River and McElmo Creek mixtures: additive toxicity.
- Ojo Amarillo Canyon mixture: antagonistic toxicity (more toxic than expected from its components in one case; interactions were antagonistic overall).
- Hogback East Drain mixture: additive for Colorado squawfish, synergistic for razorback sucker.
- Copper was the primary toxic component in the Gallegos Canyon, Mancos River, McElmo Creek and Hogback East Drain mixtures.
- Razorback sucker were more sensitive overall to the five mixtures.

**Hazard assessment**
- Margins of uncertainty (96-h LC50 divided by environmental concentration) were below 100 for copper and all five mixtures, indicating a high potential hazard.
- Margins were less than 40 for the Gallegos Canyon, Mancos River and McElmo Creek mixtures, and less than or close to 100 for Hogback East Drain. Ojo Amarillo was slightly above 100, partly because of antagonism.
- For individual inorganics, margins were very large (e.g., above 1,000 for arsenic and selenium), suggesting low risk when considered alone.
- Waterborne copper was the only individual inorganic of concern at the sites.
- National water quality criteria for arsenic, copper, selenium and zinc were not exceeded in most cases, but these criteria do not account for mixtures.

**Conclusions**
- The authors concluded the margins of uncertainty for copper and all mixtures indicate a moderate to high hazard to the endangered fish.
- Individually, national criteria would protect these fish, but they do not protect against mixture toxicity.
- Adverse effects of inorganic mixtures may contribute to the decline of these endangered fish in the San Juan River, particularly in backwaters affected by irrigation drainwater.
