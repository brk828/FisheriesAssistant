# Reconnaissance of Headwater Springs in the Gila River Drainage Basin, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrogeology

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Water-Supply Paper (1619-H, 1963) by J. H. Feth and J. D. Hem reports a reconnaissance of the ground-water system and springs of the [[Mogollon Rim]] region in central Arizona, the source of much of the water in the Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers. The study, carried out between 1950 and 1953 with later additions, mapped spring locations, measured discharge fluctuations, characterized geology and structure, and assessed water chemistry. The report is a hydrogeological and geological survey; it contains no fish data, and no fish species are discussed.

## Background & Description

The Mogollon Rim is a structural escarpment formed on northward-dipping, mainly Paleozoic rocks. It extends about 200 miles northwestward from near Clifton and Morenci, and it is up to nearly 2,000 feet high in the central part between Payson and Show Low. The rim region covers roughly 10,000 to 15,000 square miles and is divided into western, central, and eastern divisions for discussion. Main drainages run north onto the [[Colorado Plateau]] and south or southwest below the rim toward the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]].

Stated objectives of the investigation were to:
1. locate springs contributing flow to the Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers;
2. document discharge fluctuations of principal springs;
3. determine water-bearing properties of spring-discharge formations;
4. relate spring chemistry to source formations;
5. relate geologic structure to spring localization;
6. identify sources of highly mineralized water detrimental to irrigation;
7. identify areas where spring flow could be increased by development.

Previous work includes general studies by [[Darton]] and others and [[Wilson]] (1939), plus stratigraphic and ore-deposit studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and precipitation.** Mean annual temperatures range from 55.5°F at Winslow (4,880 ft) to 45°F at Flagstaff (6,993 ft). Precipitation ranges from 6.96 to 25.79 inches north of the rim and from 13.81 to 29.60 inches on and south of the rim, with strong orographic control.

**Water budget.** Assuming a conservative mean annual precipitation of 16 inches, about 8,500,000 acre-feet fall annually on the plateau portion of the rim region. Measured spring discharge is about 150,000 acre-feet per year, so less than 2 percent (estimated range 1 to 4 percent) of precipitation appears as spring flow. The rest leaves by evapotranspiration, flood runoff, and subsurface discharge.

**Recharge.** Recharge occurs mainly by direct infiltration of rain and snowmelt through jointed, shattered [[Basalt]] and the jointed, channeled [[Kaibab Limestone]]. Clay layers in marshes and "parks" retard percolation. Cienegas (marshes) and natural lakes such as [[Mormon Lake]] and [[Ashurst Lake]] lose considerable water and are proposed as potential artificial recharge sites.

**Faulting and spring localization.** High-angle, mostly normal faults are the main structural features. Springs localize at fault intersections, at permeable-impermeable rock contacts, and in grabens. In the Payson area, flow in several streams sinks to zero along a line about 3 miles south of the rim, which is interpreted as a fault zone bringing cavernous [[Redwall Limestone]] to or near the surface.

**Lake deposits.** Tertiary and Quaternary lacustrine sediments cover more than 1,000 square miles across four basins (Verde, Payson, Tonto, San Carlos). The Verde formation exceeds 300 square miles and 2,000 feet in thickness. The Verde Valley is interpreted as a graben, and the four basins align northwestward parallel to the rim. A large deposit of sodium sulfate minerals (thenardite, mirabilite, glauberite) occurs near Camp Verde.

**Travertine.** Significant travertine deposits were seen at 10 localities; active deposition occurs at four: [[Tonto Natural Bridge]], [[Fossil Creek]], [[Salt Banks]], and [[Alchesay Spring]] on the White River. The Fossil Creek inactive terrace is nearly a mile long and about 100 feet high at its cliff face.

**Seismicity.** Between 1868 and 1938, 25 earthquakes were reported in the region; the largest, on September 23, 1910, north of Flagstaff, was intensity 8 to 9 on the Mercalli scale. The region is considered to have mild ongoing seismic activity.

## Key Findings

**Spring discharge and major springs**
- The [[Naco Formation]] (Pennsylvanian) supplies the largest spring group, Fossil Creek, at 18,500 gpm.
- Fossil Creek springs discharge about 71°F and show little variation, suggesting a large storage area.
- Alchesay Spring (White River) yields about 7,500 gpm, emerging from the Fort Apache member of the Supai Formation.
- Summers Spring (Sycamore Canyon) contributes about half of Sycamore Creek's flow, which ranged 4,500 to 5,125 gpm (1946 to 1952 measurements).
- Oak Creek flood-plain springs together yield about 22,500 gpm, at 67°F to 71°F.
- Blue Spring on the Little Colorado River discharges about 200 cfs, nearly equal to all southward-flowing springs measured in the rim region.
- Measured springs in the rim region total roughly 85,000 gpm southward and about 5,500 gpm northward. About 5/6 of southward flow comes from the western and central divisions.

**Water quality (by J. D. Hem)**
- Salt Banks springs (Precambrian quartzite, Salt River) contain up to 37,000 ppm dissolved solids, mostly sodium and chloride, near sea-water concentration. Their sulfate-to-bicarbonate ratio is far below that of sea water.
- Paleozoic-rock springs generally contain less than 500 ppm, mainly calcium, magnesium, and bicarbonate.
- Basalt springs near the rim are the best in the region, some below 100 ppm dissolved solids.
- Clifton Hot Springs (about 1,000 gpm) add about 50 tons of dissolved solids per day (18,000 tons per year) to the San Francisco River.
- White River Salt Springs (about 950 gpm) add about 12 tons per day (4,500 tons per year).
- Salt Banks were earlier estimated to add about 140 tons per day (about 50,000 tons per year). March 1959 sampling suggested a much smaller discharge. In March 1959 the Salt Banks' salt input had a negligible effect on Salt River quality.

**Geology and structure**
- Precambrian granite, Paleozoic sedimentary rocks, and Tertiary-Quaternary basalt are the principal units.
- Paleozoic marker beds (base of the Martin formation) vary by more than 2,000 feet across the rim, interpreted as transverse faulting or folding.
- The Holbrook structure is the largest known fold. The Silver Creek valley is an artesian basin with the Coconino sandstone as aquifer.
- Four major orogenic periods are identified: Precambrian, Miocene(?), late Tertiary (possibly Pliocene), and at or near the beginning of the Quaternary. Laramide structures are only weakly expressed.

**Recommendations**
- Detailed studies are recommended at Fossil Creek (tunneling or well-drilling to lower head), Summers Spring, Alchesay Spring, and the Oak Creek springs reach to increase flow.
- Clearing debris from small spring orifices is recommended, since about half of Arsenic Cave Spring's discharge is lost to seepage.
- Artificial recharge through piercing clay seals and detaining snowmelt is proposed. Prescribed burning on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation was reported to restart springs that had been dry for up to 30 years (older Indian reports, unverified).
- Diverting highly mineralized water at its source (Clifton Hot Springs, Salt River salt springs) is proposed to improve reservoir water quality.

**Limitations**
- Precipitation and discharge data are incomplete, and the water budget is a rough estimate.
- The Verde Basin's geologic controls are described as "puzzling," and conditions south of the rim were not investigated for recharge.
