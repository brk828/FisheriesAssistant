# 2009 Gila Science Forum: Effects of Flow Modification on the Upper Gila River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the raw working record (presentation notes, transcripts, planning memos, correspondence, panelist bios, and draft reports) of the [[2009 Gila Science Forum]] held 3 June 2009 at Western New Mexico University, Silver City. Six panelists addressed four questions on the potential effects of flow modification on aquatic and riparian resources of the [[Gila River]], the tools and methods available to assess them, how data could be integrated into an ecosystem-based assessment, and how to use roughly one year and $1 million to address the most pressing needs. The forum was convened to inform New Mexico's use of water under the [[Arizona Water Settlements Act]] of 2004 and its [[Consumptive Use and Forbearance Agreement]] (CUFA). The panel's consensus was that flow modification will produce effects at multiple spatial and temporal scales, that data gaps and uncertainty are substantial, and that an integrated, iterative, multi-year framework linking stakeholder values to policy options is needed.

## Background & Description

The Gila River is the largest tributary of the [[Colorado River]] in Arizona and New Mexico. Its headwaters, the East, Middle, and West forks, arise in the [[Gila National Forest]] and the [[Gila Wilderness]]; the upper watershed is largely federal land and the lower reaches a mix of private, state, and federal ownership. From its origins to the Arizona border the river is free-flowing. Flow is highly variable, with high flows typically in late winter and early spring and monsoon storm peaks in summer.

Documented fauna in the Cliff-Gila Valley includes native fishes: longfin dace, [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], Sonora sucker, and desert sucker (the text cites 13 native species, 8 of them listed or of concern in the upper river). Non-native species are present; a 1989–2004 dataset of about 110,000 fish records is cited for upper basin non-native species. Birds of concern include the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] and [[Yellow-Billed Cuckoo]]. Notes also mention nearly 250 species of plants and animals in the system, including invertebrates, reptiles, and amphibians.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**CUFA conditions.** Under the CUFA, New Mexico may withdraw water on a given day only when combined flows at the Gila River near Gila and San Francisco River near Clifton gages exceed monthly bypass amounts (for example 82.5 cfs in January and 442.5 cfs in summer months, per the table). Withdrawals may not exceed 350 cfs at any time; annual consumptive use may not exceed 64k acre-feet, with a maximum of 140k acre-feet in any 10-year period; San Francisco drainage consumptive use is capped at 4k acre-feet per year; and San Carlos Reservoir must hold at least 30k acre-feet before diversion can begin in New Mexico in a given year. The likely reach for withdrawal is the upper Cliff-Gila Valley, though no means or location had been determined.

**Existing use (from panelist data requests).** Agricultural depletion is cited at an annual average of about 27k acre-feet, against a 30k acre-foot limit; about 14k acre-feet annually enters from the wilderness area. Phelps Dodge transfers are reported at about 6,000 AF/yr, with about 2,000 AF/yr used. Several irrigation ditches divert nearly all year; groundwater-surface water exchange is described as variable and unknown for certain.

**Ecological effects discussed.** Flow reduction was described as causing desiccation (flow to zero) with severe effects on aquatic life, and sub-lethal effects on growth, reproduction, and community composition. Panelists noted that native fish abundance and distribution rise with flow and fall with flow decline, that reduced connectivity increases extirpation risk, and that some effects may favor non-native species. Riparian cottonwood regeneration depends on sediment-rich, high-flow events, and cottonwood stands are reported to be declining.

**Hydrologic reference.** The Gila River near Redrock 3-year hydrograph was shown to illustrate variability; a February 2005 flood is documented in aerial imagery with sediment deposition and channel shifts.

## Key Findings

1. **Effects are scale-dependent.** Panelists agreed that modifying flow on the upper Gila will produce effects at multiple spatial and temporal scales, both biological and hydrological.
2. **Critical thresholds and hysteresis.** Systems can be pushed past thresholds beyond which they cannot recover; identifying them requires complete models and post-hoc monitoring. Recovery of some species may not follow reversal of flow reduction.
3. **Data gaps are the core constraint.** Needed information includes current water use by reach, groundwater interactions, stream gauge records, irrigation diversion statistics, habitat requirements of non-native species at each life stage, and baseline distributions.
4. **Baseline and time.** A one-year biological study was judged insufficient to capture interannual variability. Long-term data (paleoclimate, tree rings, gauge records) and baseline conditions were emphasized.
5. **Climate uncertainty.** Climate models disagree on future precipitation; southern New Mexico and Arizona may experience more extreme monsoons and droughts. Climate change should be part of any analysis.
6. **Tools.** Options include conceptual and process-based models, hierarchical models, scenario modeling, sensitivity and uncertainty analysis, meta-analysis, and tradeoff or agent-based models (such as [[Evoland]]). Models are only as good as the data populating them; satellite and GIS data now expand possibilities.
7. **Integration.** An integrated, iterative framework should link biological, hydrological, geomorphological, socioeconomic, and public-interest information. Stakeholder aspirations and constraints should define the scenarios. Experts working as a group, with a facilitating body, were preferred to independent consultants.
8. **Adaptive management.** Post-change monitoring is essential; agencies often do not fund it. Adaptive management is needed to reverse course if outcomes differ from predictions.
9. **Uniqueness.** The upper Gila is one of the few relatively unmodified large southwestern rivers and offers a control for future studies, with substantial existing data.
10. **Planning recommendations (draft report).** The draft report proposes a flexible, adaptable, science-based "white box" framework linking stakeholder values and constraints to ranked water-management policies. Its phased approach begins with synthesis of existing information and identification of data gaps, followed by RFP-based studies and spiral development. It includes an integrated team, a half-to-full-time coordinator, an oversight committee, and peer review. Timelines and cost assessments were left to be developed.
11. **Consensus points (draft).** Flow modification will cause change; change has already occurred in the system; some changes may be valued by stakeholder subgroups; and data, baseline, and understanding of linkages are limited.
12. **Process concerns.** Panelists and the facilitator noted that the forum was too broad to produce specific tool or method guidance, that stakeholder values were not yet defined, and that the audience wanted more discrete advice.

Participant administrative records (contracts, tax forms, and agendas) are summarized here only as context and contain no scientific findings.
