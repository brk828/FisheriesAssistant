# Larval Razorback Sucker and Bonytail Survival in a Reset Floodplain Wetland

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #razorback-sucker #bonytail

## Executive Summary

This report from the [[Utah Division Of Wildlife Resources]] and the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] tests whether larval [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]], both endangered, can survive in a floodplain wetland that has been reset by drought. Reset sites begin the first inundation with few [[Nonnative Fish]]. Larvae were stocked at two densities into 0.10 ha enclosures containing roughly equal numbers of nonnative fish, with a control enclosure lacking them. Larval razorback survival was 0.67% in the low-density enclosure and 0.37% in the high-density enclosure, against 9.8% in the control. Bonytail survival in the source text is reported inconsistently: the executive summary gives 7.7% and 13%, while the results section gives 1.7% (low) and 1.3% (high), against 17.1% in the control. Over 3,000 razorback suckers survived despite the low rates.

## Background & Description

Razorback Sucker and Bonytail are endangered species endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Razorback suckers were listed as endangered in 1991 and bonytail in 1980. Wild bonytail are essentially extirpated, and most bonytail in the Green River are hatchery-stocked fish. A small wild razorback population reproduces in the middle Green River, but survival beyond the larval stage has rarely been observed. Authors attribute the lack of recruitment to habitat loss and predation on larvae and early juveniles by nonnative fish.

Floodplain wetlands are considered important rearing habitat for both species. However, [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations have reduced floodplain inundation frequency, which limits access for endangered fish. Earlier work in the [[Levee Removal Study]] recorded no larval razorback survival, likely because adult numbers were very low and nonnative populations were large, the result of several cohorts reproducing over years. The study was prompted by the [[Reset Theory]]: drought dewatered floodplains in 2001 and eliminated nonnative populations, so the first inundation may offer the best opportunity for larval survival.

The report combines two independent projects: a UDWR razorback enclosure study and a Vernal FWS bonytail study, both conducted at The Stirrup because river flows were too low to fill the intended sites.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and enclosures.** The Stirrup floodplain ranges from 9.1 to 13.9 surface hectares at river flows of 13,000 to 24,000 cfs. Peak flows had not exceeded 14,000 cfs in recent years, and the site dried completely in 2001. In 2002, river water was pumped in, producing a depth of about 1.0 m. Two 0.10 ha enclosures (22.9 m × 45.7 m) and one 4.9 m × 4.9 m control enclosure were built of steel posts and layered fencing (tarp, window screen, hardware cloth, and wire panels). The tarp was removed once fish were large enough to be contained by screen.

**Water quality.** Spring peak flow reached 7,570 cfs, well below the roughly 13,500 cfs needed to connect the river to the floodplain. Temperatures ranged from 19 to 29 °C, averaging 25.4 °C, which favored warm-water fish growth. Dissolved oxygen rarely fell below 5.0 mg/L.

**Zooplankton.** Fish presence strongly shaped [[Zooplankton]] density. Outside the enclosures, densities rose to about 400 per liter in spring, then fell to 30–50 per liter after vascular plants appeared. In July, densities averaged 7.6 per liter (high-density enclosure), 5.6 per liter (low-density enclosure), and 86.3 per liter outside the enclosures. Enclosure zooplankton remained low because fish consumed the prey.

**Nonnative fish.** Stocked nonnative fish were sourced from the Green River and local ponds and included [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Common Carp]]. Their numbers were based on densities observed in the Levee Removal Study. Black bullhead, green sunfish, and fathead minnow populations grew to exceed 40,000 individuals in each enclosure. Carp and red shiner were too rare for population estimates.

## Key Findings

**Stocking.**
- Razorback larvae: 60,373 (low-density) and 457,193 (high-density), plus 1,000 in the control, sourced from the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]].
- Bonytail larvae: about 21,250 (low-density), about 71,500 (high-density), and 5,250 (control), sourced from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- Non-native fish: 149 (low-density) and 154 (high-density) fish, with 0 in the control.

**Razorback sucker survival.**
- Low-density enclosure: 0.67%; high-density enclosure: 0.37%; control: 9.8% (98 fish recovered).
- Depletion-based survival ranged from 0.62 to 0.87% (low-density) and 0.37 to 0.41% (high-density).
- Escapement: the Schumacher and Eschmeyer estimate for fish outside the enclosures was 1,113 (95% CI 761–2,070).
- The authors call survival of over 3,000 razorback suckers encouraging, the first detected larval razorback survival in the middle Green River since 1996.

**Bonytail survival.**
- Executive summary: 7.7% (low) and 13% (high). Results section: 1.7% (low), 1.3% (high), and 17.1% (control; 898 fish harvested).
- Estimated abundance: 952 in the high-density enclosure and 368 in the low-density enclosure.
- About 11,553 bonytail (95% CI 9,649–13,915) were estimated outside the enclosures, with roughly 12% of introduced fish having escaped. The authors note within-enclosure survival estimates are therefore conservative.

**Razorback growth.**
- June 27 means: 27.7 mm (high-density) and 33.2 mm (low-density), indicating density dependence.
- Outside the enclosures on July 23–30: about 90 mm.
- During depletion sampling: 69 mm (low-density), 58 mm (high-density), 69 mm (control).
- October 8: released fish averaged 163 mm (range 95–294 mm), growing 1.1 mm/day over 139 days.

**Bonytail growth.**
- June 22 means: 47.2 mm (high-density) and 46.5 mm (low-density), with no significant difference.
- Growth of 0.8 mm/day over 51 days.
- Final mean lengths: 63.0 mm (high-density), 57.4 mm (low-density), 51.3 mm (control), and 95.0 mm (outside enclosures).

**Predator behavior.** Razorback larvae often schooled in open water near large predators and did not use available cover, consistent with [[Johnson Et Al]] (1993) conclusions that larvae are predator naive. Underwater video confirmed this.

**Interpretation.** The authors suggest the reset theory works because few nonnative fish enter at connection, and larvae gained a size advantage over age-0 predators after the zooplankton peak. Projected contribution is 1,000–2,000 sub-adult razorback suckers to the river, though the authors caution against this extrapolation.

**Conclusions and recommendations.**
- Larvae survived and grew in a reset floodplain with a simulated predator load. The minimum density for target survival was not established.
- Continue managing floodplains under the reset theory.
- Test larval introductions into nonnative-free floodplains during the next high-flow year.
- Evaluate methods to increase larval entrainment (connection configuration, location, and river flows).
- Evaluate conditions that allow razorback suckers and bonytail to recruit back to the river.
