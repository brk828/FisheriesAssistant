# Desert Fishes Council Proceedings (1998 Symposium): Razorback Sucker Growth and Roundtail Chub Predation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a two-paper excerpt from the Proceedings of the [[Desert Fishes Council]], Volume XXX (1998 Symposium), published November 15, 1999. Both papers concern native fishes of the American Southwest, with abstracts in English and Spanish.

1. **Growth of wild adult [[Razorback Sucker]]s in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada** (Marsh and co-author, Arizona State University, Tempe). Mark-recapture data indicated that mean monthly growth of adults was not significantly different from zero.
2. **Potential predation on native [[Roundtail Chub]] by non-native fishes in the [[Verde River]], Arizona** (Parmley and Brouder, Arizona Game and Fish Department, Phoenix). Stomach analysis of 535 fish from six non-native species found no roundtail chub remains, while crayfish and aquatic insects were the dominant prey.

## Background & Description

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)** is a long-lived catostomid of the Colorado River system. The study examined wild adults in Lake Mohave, a reservoir on the Arizona-Nevada border, using capture-recapture methods to estimate growth.

**Roundtail Chub (*Gila robusta*)** is a native cyprinid of Arizona. Although not federally listed as threatened or endangered, it is considered a species of concern in Arizona because of documented population declines. Proposed causes include habitat loss, competition with non-native fishes, and predation by them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Growth study setting:** Wild adult razorback suckers in a large reservoir, with growth measured over intervals between capture and recapture.
- **Predation study setting:** The Verde River, where six non-native species were collected by backpack electrofishing.
- **Concern:** Both studies address threats to native desert fishes, namely habitat change, interactions with introduced species, and low or stagnant population growth.

## Key Findings

### Razorback Sucker Growth (Lake Mohave)

- **Sampling:** Wild adults were tracked through capture-recapture (mark-recapture) monitoring.
- **Time at liberty:** Intervals between capture and recapture ranged from 37 to 2,516 days for females and from 34 to 2,563 days for males. These were standardized to months (30.4 days/month).
- **Monthly growth range:** Individual monthly growth rates ranged from roughly -7.2 mm to +44 mm for females (upper value partly illegible in scan) and from -16.2 mm to +31.6 mm for males.
- **Positive, zero, and negative growth:** Individuals of both sexes showed positive, null, and negative growth.
- **Overall result:** The mean monthly growth of all organisms of both sexes was **not different from zero**. Standard errors were relatively large, which the authors attributed to measurement error.
- **Approximate means (scan partly illegible):** Mean monthly change of about 0.2 mm (SE about 1.5 mm) for females and about 0.1 mm (SE about 2.2 mm) for males.

### Roundtail Chub Predation (Verde River)

- **Method:** Six non-native species were collected with a backpack electrofishing unit. A total of **535 fish** were sacrificed, and their stomachs were removed, preserved, and analyzed in the laboratory. Diets were quantified by percent composition by number and by weight.
- **Crayfish (Cambaridae)** were the dominant prey by weight for all species, ranging from **38%** in [[Largemouth Bass]] to **94%** in [[Flathead Catfish]]. Crayfish also made up the highest percentage by number in flathead catfish.
- **Other five species:** Aquatic insects dominated by number, mainly Ephemeroptera (exact percentage illegible in scan) and Corixidae (**19%**).
- **Roundtail chub:** **None were found** in any stomach.
- **Native suckers** (*Catostomus insignis* or *Pantosteus clarkii*) made up **17%** by weight of [[Smallmouth Bass]] diets.
- **Implication:** Predation on adult roundtail chub by these non-native species was not detected in this sample. The main prey were crayfish and insects, although native suckers were eaten by smallmouth bass.
