# Risk Assessment on Black Carp (Mylopharyngodon piceus)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey report, prepared by Leo G. Nico and James D. Williams in October 1996 for the Risk Assessment and Management (RAM) Committee under the Aquatic Nuisance Species Task Force, tests the RAM "Generic Nonindigenous Aquatic Organism Risk Analysis Review Process" using the [[Black Carp]] as a test organism. It is a single-organism assessment, not a pathway assessment. It compiles a comprehensive species profile (taxonomy, morphology, distribution, [[Biology And Natural History]], diseases, [[Aquaculture]] history, introductions and biological control use) and then rates the organism's risk of establishment and impact in the United States. The authors conclude that the species poses an unacceptable (Medium-to-High) organism risk potential and recommend that all non-broodstock fish be certified triploids, with restrictions on release.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** Black carp was described as *Leuciscus piceus* by Richardson (1846) from the Canton (Guangzhou) area. Five later researchers described it as new species from different localities before the forms were synonymized after 1930. It is the only recognized species in the genus *Mylopharyngodon* within the subfamily Cyprininae, though Chinese researchers often place it in Leuciscinae. Its common names number more than 30 across languages, including "snail carp" and "black amur."

**Morphology.** The body is black, with all fins black (color varies from blue-gray to dark brown in some reports). Adults exceed 1 m; the largest reported weight is 60–70 kg, and Yangtze River fish commonly weigh 15–40 kg. Key identifying features include a terminal, small, oblique mouth without barbels, cycloid scales, 39–45 lateral line scales, and massive, molar-like pharyngeal teeth without grooves or hooks. Black carp are superficially very similar to [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), and juveniles are difficult to separate. Both have a diploid chromosome number of 2N=48. Diagnostic differences are body color, pharyngeal tooth morphology and intestinal length (about 1.25 versus 2.5 body lengths).

**Native distribution.** The native range spans most major Pacific drainages of eastern Asia from about 22° N to 51° N, including the [[Amur River]], [[Yellow River]], Huai, [[Yangtze River]] and [[Pearl River]] basins. Reported occurrences in Taiwan, northern Vietnam (Red River), Japan (Tone River) and Yugoslavia/Ukraine are considered introductions or possible introductions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and behavior.** Black carp are lowland fish, mostly below 200 m elevation, inhabiting lakes and lower river reaches. They spawn in rivers, and the eggs are semipelagic and drift downstream. Adults stay in the middle to lower water column and near the bottom, and in winter they remain in deep water. Eggs require sufficient river flow (reported as above about 0.6–0.8 m/s) and long drift distances before hatching.

**Diet.** Black carp are gape-limited molluscivores. Larvae feed on zooplankton; juveniles shift to chironomid larvae and then to snails and clams as their pharyngeal teeth develop. Adults prey heavily on snails (e.g., *Viviparus*) and bivalves. Reported consumption in pond experiments included 1.4–1.8 kg per day of zebra mussels for four-year-old fish. Mouth gape (7–25 mm for 100–500 mm fish, per [[Shelton Et Al 1995]]) limits prey size.

**Life history.** Maturity is reported between 3 and 11 years depending on region and source, with females usually later than males. Fecundity estimates range from about 129,000 to 1.5 million eggs per female. Maximum age is probably in excess of 14 years. Spawning in the Yangtze runs from May into July and is affected by dams such as the [[Gezhouba Dam]] and [[Danjiangkou Dam]].

**Parasites and disease.** Black carp are reported to be resistant to many snail-borne parasites, which makes them a probable carrier of parasites that use mollusks as intermediate hosts. Recorded agents include *Aspidogaster*, *Khawia sinensis*, *Lernaea*, *Trichodina* and *Dactylogyrus*. A 1990 Taiwanese study of 185 cultured fish found *Lernaea* in 51% and *Trichodina* in 37%. Hemorrhagic and infectious pancreatic necrosis viruses have also been reported.

**Use in the United States.** The species entered the United States in the early 1970s as a contaminant of imported grass carp stock in Arkansas. A second introduction in the early 1980s was made for yellow grub control and food-fish culture. By 1995–1996, black carp were held in aquaculture facilities in six states (Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, North Carolina, Oklahoma, Texas). The first and only known open-water introduction occurred in April 1994, when 30 or more black carp escaped into the Osage River in Missouri during a flood.

## Key Findings

**Biological control value is uncertain.** Trials were mostly small and inconclusive. In North Carolina, black carp at about 25 per acre could not control burrowing snails and did not control snails in vegetated ponds. In Arkansas, about 5 per acre was reported sufficient in farm ponds, depending on infestation level. Black carp were unable to control adult apple snails (*Pomacea*) in 1989–1994 trials in Louisiana. A 1996 study showed a non-significant effect on *Helisoma*.

**Zebra mussel control is doubtful.** Ukrainian proposals for black carp control of zebra mussels rest on pond experiments with unclear design. The authors argue that the species, being toothless and gape-limited, cannot break apart zebra mussel rafts, and that control would require removal of all mussels given the species' high reproductive rate.

**Risk ratings (Section III.A).**
- Probability of being on or in the pathway: High (Very Certain).
- Survival in transit: High (Very Certain).
- Establishment (colonization and maintenance): Medium (Reasonably Certain).
- Spread beyond the colonized area: High (Reasonably Certain).
- Economic impact if established: Low (Moderately Certain).
- Environmental impact if established: High (Very Certain).
- Social and political influence: Medium (Moderately Certain).

**Organism Risk Potential.** The combined rating is Medium, with high environmental consequence. The report classes this as unacceptable risk requiring mitigation. Environmental concern is driven by the 213 of 297 native freshwater mussel taxa (71.7%) considered endangered, threatened or of special concern, and by competition with snail-eating native fishes, turtles and birds.

**Establishment and spread.** Appropriate habitat and climate occur across most of the continental United States. The closest relative, grass carp, has established naturally reproducing populations in the Mississippi basin and first arrived in 1963 in Alabama and Arkansas. Black carp could be mistaken for grass carp, increasing the risk of unintentional introduction.

**Triploidy as mitigation.** Triploids are considered sterile, and no evidence was found of triploids reverting to diploids. Escape of diploid broodstock is therefore the main risk. Triploid certification for grass carp is voluntary and does not act as law enforcement.

**Recommendations (Section IV).**
1. All non-broodstock black carp must be certified triploids.
2. Broodstock must be held where escape or flooding is essentially impossible.
3. Develop a system to verify the location and distribution of all live black carp.
4. Restrict experimental use against disease-carrying snails to triploids.
5. Prohibit release of triploids into open waters until research shows benefit and no significant harm to native mussels and snails.
6. Investigate black carp as a disease pathway and add precautions for any new imports.
7. Produce an identification guide to separate black carp from grass carp.
8. Establish a quality assurance and education program.

**Scope.** The report explicitly does not make regulatory decisions; its purpose is to test and refine the risk process, and it notes that the species' fragmented literature motivated a comprehensive profile.
