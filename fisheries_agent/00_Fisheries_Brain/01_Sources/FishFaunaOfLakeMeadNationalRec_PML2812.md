# Fish Fauna of Lake Mead National Recreational Area and Growth Rates of Four Missouri River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This note combines two fisheries papers. The first surveys the status of the fish fauna of the [[Lake Mead National Recreational Area]] on the lower [[Colorado River]], Arizona-Nevada, which has been reshaped by [[Hoover Dam]] and [[Davis Dam]]. The author recorded 3 native and 11 exotic species from six families, with six further native fishes and two alien forms possibly present. Native fishes appear to be declining under competition from exotic species and altered river conditions. Exotic game fish are now firmly established, so a closed fishing season is considered unnecessary. The author recommends against new introductions and against further stocking of [[Largemouth Black Bass]] or forage fish in [[Lake Mead]], while continued [[Rainbow Trout]] planting below Hoover Dam is judged necessary.

The second paper reports age and growth of four species from a previously unstudied reach of the [[Missouri River]] in Montana, based on surveys in summer 1948 and 1949. Trout grew faster than suckers, and most trout were in age groups I–III, while suckers were mostly in groups IV–VIII.

## Background & Description

**Lake Mead fauna (Wattis).** The [[Colorado River]] flows for nearly 225 miles through the recreational area, and its character has been changed by dam construction. Earlier workers (Evermann and Rutter 1895; Gilbert and Scofield 1898; Snyder 1915; Dill 1944; Miller 1946a, b) described the native Colorado River fauna as species-poor but highly [[Endemism|endemic]] and in urgent need of study. The report aims to document the current fish composition and to inform management of the sport fishery.

**Missouri River growth study (Karlein).** The study was undertaken because no previous fisheries investigations of this section of the river were known. Its goal was to support evaluation of management problems for the most important species. It was published as a contribution from the Montana State College Agricultural Experiment Station (Project No. 231 Journal Series, Paper No. 231).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fishes in decline.** Native species are thought to be declining because of competition with [[Introduced Species]] and changed ecological conditions in the river. The [[Squawfish]] was formerly abundant in river channels as far north as the base of the Rocky Mountains in Colorado, but it is now less numerous. The [[Bonytail]] was more abundant in earlier days and is now rarely seen, although Moffett (1943) reported large schools in 1941. The author notes that one endemic minnow, a species whose name is not legible in the scan, has not been collected in any other locality. Protection is seen as the main way to save such fishes from the fate of the other indigenous species of the Colorado Basin.

**Introduced fishes.** Introductions increased after Hoover Dam was built. Exotic game fish dominate the sport fishery, and some species arrived as bait or through unintentional introduction, such as the [[Green Sunfish]], which probably came in with the 1937 bluegill planting. Several species were noted but not confirmed. A [[Smallmouth Black Bass]] was seen near the mouth of the Moapa River in 1942, and [[Goldfish]] may have become established, though no records were found.

**Missouri River conditions.** The study reach is a flowing trout-and-sucker system. The paper provides no additional environmental data.

## Key Findings

### Lake Mead National Recreational Area (Wattis)

- **Fauna totals:** 3 native and 11 exotic species, from six families. Six additional native Colorado Basin fishes and two other alien forms may occur in the area.
- **Game fish:** [[Largemouth Black Bass]] is the most important game species in Lake Mead. [[Rainbow Trout]] is the leading sport fish in [[Lake Mohave]], about 300 miles below Hoover Dam.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - A closed fishing season is probably unnecessary because exotic game fish are firmly established.
  - No further alien species should be introduced.
  - Further stocking of largemouth bass or forage fish in Lake Mead is discouraged.
  - Continued rainbow trout planting below Hoover Dam is needed to meet angler demand.
  - More investigation of the sport fishery is desirable.

**Species accounts (summary):**

- **Rainbow trout:** Nearly a million fingerlings were planted according to National Park Service records. The initial stocking was in 1935, fish were protected until the first season opened in 1940, and Eicher (1947) estimated a harvest of 20,000 pounds. The record fish measured 32½ inches and weighed 18 pounds (taken 1950). Most are under 25 inches.
- **Brown trout:** A single record from Las Vegas Wash (April 6, 1949) weighed 3 pounds 6 ounces, measured 24¾ inches, and was in its seventh year. It likely drifted downstream from plantings farther upriver.
- **Largemouth black bass:** Plantings began in July 1935; 466,000 fingerlings were released from 1935 to 1940. The record fish weighed 13 pounds 14 ounces (November 26, 1939), won first place in the 1939 national Field and Stream contest, and is one of many Lake Mead winners. Further stocking is judged unlikely to improve fishing.
- **Channel catfish:** The first introductions (1892–1893) involved 722 adults and yearlings released by the Arizona Fish Commission. No recent stocking has been made. The record is a 20-pound, 31-inch fish caught near Pierce Ferry in April 1945.
- **Black bullhead:** A single specimen (159 mm standard length) was collected in Hemenway Wash on August 2, 1937, and none had been recently reported at landings.
- **Western gambusia:** Present in the Moapa River and shallow coves and washes along the shoreline. It may have entered via the Moapa or Virgin River.
- **Black crappie:** Probably stocked between 1935 and 1937. It is common throughout Lake Mead but rare below Hoover Dam. Typical fish weigh 1–2 pounds and are under 12 inches.
- **Bluegill:** A single planting of 25,000 two-inch fingerlings was made in 1937. It is now abundant and important in Lake Mead.
- **Green sunfish:** Nearly as numerous and widespread as the bluegill. Considered less desirable because it attains a small size and competes for food.
- **Squawfish:** Historically reached a reputed 80 pounds and 5 feet, but no such giant has been recorded recently. A 27-pound fish was brought to a dock in 1945, and a 34-pound "salmon" was credited to H. Armitage.
- **Bonytail (Gila robusta elegans):** Native to Lake Mead and below Hoover Dam; locally called "Gila or Verde trout."
- **Bonneville mountain sucker (Pantosteus platyrhynchus):** A single exotic specimen was collected in Lake Mead on September 8, 1938, probably brought in as bait.

### Missouri River, Holter Dam to Cascade, Montana (Karlein)

- **Species studied:** [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Longnose Sucker]] (*Catostomus catostomus*), and [[Western White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*).
- **Sample sizes:** The condition coefficient was calculated from 481 rainbow trout and 117 brown trout.
- **Age ranges:**
  - Rainbow trout: groups I–VII
  - Brown trout: groups I–IX
  - Longnose sucker: groups III–VIII
  - Western white sucker: groups I–VIII
- **Dominant age groups:** Group II for rainbow and brown trout, group VI for longnose suckers, and group III for western white suckers.
- **Growth:** Greatest growth occurred in the second year for rainbow and brown trout, and in the first and third years for longnose and western white suckers. Trout grew more rapidly than suckers.
- **Age structure:** Most trout were in groups I–III, while suckers were mostly in groups IV–VIII.
- **Condition coefficient:** 37.2 for rainbow trout and 34.6 for brown trout.
