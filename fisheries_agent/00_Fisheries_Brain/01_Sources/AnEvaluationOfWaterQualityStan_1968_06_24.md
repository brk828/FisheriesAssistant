# An Evaluation of Water Quality Standards (Lake Erie, 1968)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #water-quality #freshwater-ecology #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 conference paper by R.S. Burd (Federal Water Pollution Control Administration) and F.E.J. Fry (University of Toronto) presents a dual analysis of U.S. water quality standards following the Water Quality Act of 1965, with special focus on [[Lake Erie]]. Burd's section covers the policy and administrative framework of state-federal standard setting, defining "interstate waters," treatment requirements, and the "anti-degradation" policy for high-quality waters. Fry's section translates the recommendations of the National Technical Advisory Committee on Water Quality Criteria into biologically grounded thresholds for aquatic life, citing empirical fish physiology and toxicology studies to justify criteria for pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen, free CO₂, turbidity, and toxic substances. The paper is a key historical document illustrating the scientific basis underlying early U.S. water quality regulation as it pertains to fish survival, growth, and reproduction.

## Background & Description

- **Context**: Following the Water Quality Act of 1965, all 50 U.S. states submitted water quality standards for federal review by June 30, 1967; 34 states (including all Lake Erie basin states) had received approval by the time of this paper.
- **Standard components** (as defined in the Act):
  1. Determination of designated water uses.
  2. Water quality criteria to protect those uses.
  3. An implementation plan to achieve and maintain those criteria.
- **Interstate waters definition** included boundary waters, designated tributaries, all Great Lakes waters, and coastal waters to the territorial limit/tidal influence.
- **Treatment policy**: "Best practicable treatment or control," generally interpreted as a minimum of secondary treatment, with compliance targeted for 1972 in the [[Lake Erie Basin]] states (New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Michigan).
- **Anti-degradation policy**: High-quality waters exceeding minimum standards should not be degraded except where new discharges are shown to be socially/economically necessary and receive best practicable treatment first.
- **Scientific basis**: An 84-member National Technical Advisory Committee on Water Quality Criteria synthesized the technical literature to underpin numeric criteria, later applied in negotiations over the Lake Erie standards.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Erie zonation for standards**: Criteria were differentiated across *open water*, *shore/swimming areas*, and *harbor* zones, with Michigan additionally distinguishing designated enclosed harbors (e.g., Presque Isle Bay, Erie Harbor, Lorain, Cleveland, Ashtabula harbors).
- **Bacterial (coliform) limits**: Ranged from median coliform ≤1000–5000/100 mL depending on state, season, and zone, reflecting recreational and aquatic-life use protections.
- **Dissolved oxygen (DO) standards**: Generally required a daily average ≥5.0 mg/L with short-term minima (4.0 ppm non-trout, 5.0 ppm trout waters); New York specified DO ≥5.0 mg/L for at least 16 hours in any 24-hour period, dropping to ≥3.0 mg/L at any time.
- **Temperature standards**: Varied by lake zone and season (e.g., 90°F/93°F allowable May–Nov in open water vs. 60°F/73°F Dec–May), generally allowing no more than a 5°F change from natural background; trout waters required no thermal discharge causing adverse effects.
- **Chlorides / Total Dissolved Solids (TDS)**: Monthly average limits of ~500 mg/L, with peak allowances up to 700–1000 mg/L depending on zone and state.
- **pH standards**: Range of 6.5–8.5 (Lake Erie states) with a maximum allowable shift of 0.5 pH units from natural conditions.
- **Nutrients**: All Lake Erie states deferred to criteria set by the Lake Erie Enforcement Technical Committee (6/1/67); nitrate (NO₃) concentrations were required to conform to USPHS Drinking Water Standards.
- **Biological rationale (Fry's contribution)** links these regulatory numbers to physiological thresholds in fish and other aquatic organisms, drawing on classic and contemporary ecotoxicology literature (Ellis 1937; Alabaster & Downing 1966; Dahlberg et al. 1968; Stewart et al. 1967).

## Key Findings

### I. Dissolved Materials (Non-Toxic)
- Recommended addition limit: ≤50 milliosmoles (~2500 ppm at MW 50), primarily to control osmotic stress on aquatic organisms.

### II. pH, Alkalinity, Acidity
- Target range: pH 6–9; alkalinity not to be artificially reduced below 20 ppm (to preserve bicarbonate reservoir for photosynthesis).
- [[Rainbow Trout]] pH tolerance data (Lloyd & Jordan 1964; Jordan & Lloyd 1964) show that free CO₂ presence sharply narrows tolerance to low pH — an important synergistic toxicity effect.

### III. Temperature
- Warmwater fish six-hour peak thresholds:
  - North/Upland: 83–86°F
  - Central/Lowland: 86–90°F
  - South: 90–96°F
- Coldwater species: no summer temperature increase permitted; winter max of 55°F to protect developing eggs.
- Field validation: Potomac River heated effluent studies (Elser 1965) show fish vacate water above ~86°F threshold, matching the central-lowland criterion.
- British fish studies (Alabaster & Downing 1966) show avoidance of water >77°F (25°C); [[Brown Trout]] essentially absent above 68°F (20°C), reinforcing coldwater sensitivity.

### IV. Dissolved Oxygen
- Warmwater fauna: minimum 5 ppm (short-term extreme 4 ppm).
- Coldwater fauna: 6 ppm minimum; oligotrophic lake hypolimnion should not fall below 6 ppm.
- Physiological basis (Dahlberg et al. 1968): swimming performance in [[Largemouth Bass]] (warmwater) and [[Coho Salmon]] (coldwater) begins to decline almost precisely at these 5/6 ppm thresholds.
- Growth effects (Stewart et al. 1967): bass exposed to daily DO minima of ~2 ppm showed severely checked growth even when mean daily DO exceeded 6 ppm — underscoring the importance of short-term minima, not just averages.

### V. Free Carbon Dioxide
- Tentative upper limit: 25 ppm (data-limited).
- Fry (1960) data on [[Goldfish]] and trout show CO₂ effects on active metabolism, though acclimation effects remain unstudied.

### VI. Turbidity
- Limits: ≤50 Jackson units (warmwater streams), ≤25 units (lakes), ≤10 units (coldwater streams) — far below lethal turbidity thresholds (~50,000 units), instead targeting productivity impacts.
- Buck (1956): turbidity reduces fish production in farm ponds.
- Herbert et al. (1961): china-clay waste turbidity drastically damaged bottom fauna in Cornish trout streams, at levels well above recommended criteria.

### VII. Nutrients / Eutrophication
- No quantitative criteria proposed by the Committee.
- Langford (unpublished, cited) data show fertilizer additions to oligotrophic headwater lakes in the late 1940s caused hypolimnetic oxygen depletion with effects persisting with little recovery through 1963 — illustrating long-lasting eutrophication impacts.

### VIII. Toxicity
- **General principle**: All effluents with foreign materials should be bioassayed; chlorinated hydrocarbon pesticides should be avoided entirely.
- **Detergents**: ABS ≤1/7 of 48-hr TLm; LAS ≤0.2 mg/L or 1/7 of 48-hr TLm.
- **Non-cumulative toxins**: ≤1/10 of 96-hr TLm at any time; 24-hr average ≤1/20 TLm.
- **Cumulative toxins**: 24-hr mean ≤1/100 of 96-hr TLm.
- **Resistance vs. tolerance distinction** (Fry 1947): short-term lethal bioassays (resistance) differ from long-term sublethal tolerance limits; many toxicants show additive toxicity at tolerance levels (Lloyd 1961; Lloyd & Jordan 1964; Sprague & Ramsay 1965).
- **Temperature-toxicity interaction** (Brown et al. 1967 on phenol/[[Rainbow Trout]]; Mount 1966 on copper/[[Fathead Minnow]]): acute lethality increases with temperature, but long-term tolerable concentrations may be higher than short-term lethal thresholds suggest.
- **Sublethal detergent effects**: 0.5 ppm ABS (less than 1/20 of TLm) damaged chemoreceptors in the [[Yellow Bullhead]] (Bardach et al. 1965); sublethal ABS also slowed feeding response in [[Zebrafish]] (Cairns & Loos 1967).
- **Pesticide effects**: DDT progressively depressed marine algal growth rate across a ~50-fold concentration range before reaching lethal levels (Wurster 1968), demonstrating sublethal ecological risk well below acute toxicity thresholds.
