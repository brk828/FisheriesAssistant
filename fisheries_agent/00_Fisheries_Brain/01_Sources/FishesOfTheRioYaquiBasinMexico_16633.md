# Fishes of the Rio Yaqui Basin, Mexico and United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Poeciliidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This survey, conducted in spring and summer 1978 and combined with a review of literature and museum specimens, documents the fish fauna of the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] in northwestern Mexico and its small United States portion. The drainage, covering about 73,000 km², supports 35 native fish species in 25 genera and 16 families, the richest ichthyofauna of any drainage in the American Southwest. Roughly 30% of recorded species are introduced, mostly lentic-adapted sport and bait fishes. Several US populations of native species have been extirpated, and the authors identify introduced fishes and reservoir expansion as the main threats to the remaining native fauna.

## Background & Description

The Rio Yaqui rises in the Rio Papigochic system of western Chihuahua, flows through eastern Sonora as the Rios Aros-Sirupa, receives the Rio de Bavispe, and enters the [[Sea of Cortez]] near Ciudad Obregón. The basin lies between parallels 27° and 32° N and meridians 107° and 110° 40' W.

The landscape spans three physiographic provinces:
- **[[Sierra Madre Occidental]]**: deeply incised "barrancas" in the west and the high, flat Mesa del Norte plateau in the east, with peaks rising above 1,800 m and endorheic basins at 1,500–2,000 m.
- **Parallel Ranges and Valleys**: north–south structural troughs that extend toward the [[Basin and Range]] of southeastern Arizona.
- **Sonoran Desert Province**: arid, low crystalline mountains and coastal plains bordering the delta.

Geological history includes Laramide orogeny, Tertiary lava flows, the Baucarit Formation and Gila Conglomerate deposits, and Pliocene–Pleistocene drainage reorganization. Trans-basin stream captures and headward erosion linked the Yaqui with the [[Gila River]] system, the Rio Grande (via the Rio Conchos), and the Rio Mayo.

Climate ranges from mean annual air temperatures above 25 °C on the coast to below 15 °C on the Plateau. Precipitation rarely exceeds 50 mm/year in the lowlands. Vegetation grades from [[Sinaloan Thornscrub]] and Sinaloan Deciduous Forest through Madrean evergreen woodland to Petran montane conifer forest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sampling used backpack electrofishing (150 V), seines (4.6 m and 1.2 m), gill nets, and a 91 m trammel net. Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin and stored in 70% ethanol at [[Arizona State University]] and the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]]. Localities (1–90 authors' sites; 101–173 literature/museum sites) were mapped from 1:250,000 scale charts.

Habitat use varies by species:
- Upland creeks with pools, riffles, and cover (Yaqui trout, Mexican stoneroller, Yaqui sucker).
- Large rivers with deep pools and swift runs (roundtail chub, Yaqui catfish, Yaqui sucker).
- Intermittent desert streams (longfin dace, Sonoran topminnow).
- Artificial reservoirs and stock tanks, which host mostly introduced species.

Water conditions in sampled sites ranged from clear, algae-free mountain streams to turbid, mud-bottomed reservoirs. The authors describe much of the basin as relatively undisturbed. Logging is largely by hand and horse, grazing is severe only near towns, and mining effects are localized. The three main mainstream reservoirs (Presa La Angostura, Presa Novillo, Presa Álvaro Obregón) are not yet operated for peaking power, and tributaries sustain flow heterogeneity.

## Key Findings

**Fauna composition and status**
- 35 native species (25 genera, 16 families) and 22 introduced species (7 genera) are recorded, with about 30% of taxa introduced.
- The fauna includes primary freshwater fishes, euryhaline species, diadromous fishes, and marine fishes that enter fresh water.
- Endemism is low. The [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*) is probably confined to the Rio Yaqui system and the new *Cyprinodon* species from the Rio Papigochic may be endemic.
- Of U.S.-portion species, only [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) appears rare in Mexico. It was collected once during the survey (Rio Moctezuma at Cumpas) and absent from Cabullonas despite intensive sampling.

**Taxonomic notes**
- *Campostoma pricei* is synonymized with *C. ornatum* (Burr 1976).
- *Gila minacae* is synonymized with *G. robusta*.
- *Notropis mearnsi* is synonymized with *N. formosus* (or *N. lutrensis*, per Contreras 1978); the authors retain *N. formosus*.
- *Catostomus bernardini* is closely related to, perhaps conspecific with, *C. insignis* of the Gila basin.
- Two undescribed *Catostomus* species occur in the upper Rio de Bavispe and southern headwaters.
- The taxonomic status of the Yaqui *Agosia* form is unresolved.

**Introductions and threats**
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) was recorded from the Mexican basin for the first time, at 2 localities, and was relatively rare.
- Mosquitofish is a known threat to [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) in the Gila basin. The authors predict decline of the topminnow here if mosquitofish spreads.
- Blue and channel catfishes (*Ictalurus furcatus*, *I. punctatus*) may hybridize or compete with Yaqui catfish.
- Introduction of *Notropis lutrensis* as bait could cause genetic swamping of *N. formosus*.
- Rainbow trout stocked in [[Rucker Canyon]] interact with native fishes in the upper Chiricahua region.

**Specific species notes**
- *Dorosoma smithi* (Pacific shad) extends the known range about 200 km north into the interior, from Presa Novillo.
- Yaqui trout (*Salmo* sp.) are widespread in high-elevation tributaries; two native forms are recognized (northern Rio Gavilán and southern Rio Mayo-related).
- The all-female *Poeciliopsis monacha-occidentalis* is of hybrid origin and depends on *P. occidentalis* males for sperm. It reached 38% of topminnow individuals at a southern locality.
- *Codoma ornata* (ornate minnow) occurs mostly above 1,500 m in the Rio Papigochic and is morphologically similar to the fathead minnow.
- *Cichlasoma beani* (Sinaloan cichlid) is confined to the southernmost basin.

**Conservation priorities**
- Native fishes persist in the Mexican basin, but expansion of reservoirs and introduced fishes will likely be the main impact.
- The authors call for monitoring of introduced species, the native [[Yaqui Catfish]] status, and the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] and mosquitofish interaction.
- Management of Forest Service lands in southeastern Arizona (Rickel 1979) is cited as a model for native-fish protection.
