# Fresh-Water Molluscs from New Mexico and Vicinity

#ichthyology #marine-biology #malacology #hydrobiidae #littoridinidae #assimineidae #sphaeriidae

## Executive Summary

This bulletin (Bulletin 116, [[New Mexico Bureau of Mines & Mineral Resources]], Socorro, 1987) by Dwight W. Taylor of Oregon State University describes 22 freshwater mollusc species from New Mexico and adjacent areas. It describes nine new species from New Mexico, four from Arizona, two from Utah, and five from Texas, and redescribes two more from New Mexico and Texas. The species are placed in Assimineidae (one species), Hydrobiidae (11 species of [[Fontelicella]] and two monotypic new genera), Littoridinidae (seven species of [[Tryonia]]), and Sphaeriidae (one species of Pisidium). Most are narrowly local forms considered actually or potentially endangered. The document's main scientific argument is that these spring-restricted snails record the drainage history of the region, supporting evolution in separate drainages during Pliocene or older times that were later united into present river systems.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and support.** The studies were supported by the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] to assess potentially threatened and endangered State fauna, and by the New Mexico Bureau of Mines and Mineral Resources to prepare a regional manual of the freshwater molluscan fauna of New Mexico and adjacent areas, including the [[Rio Grande]] drainage. Fieldwork in the Diamond Y Draw area of Texas was partly supported by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], Office of Endangered Species.

**Methods.** Classification relies on penial structure and glandular patterns (a terminology developed for the genus, see Fig. 3), along with shell measurements, operculum measurements, and bursa copulatrix/albumen gland ratios. Tables give means, ranges, standard deviations, and standard errors for each species.

**Taxonomic overview.**
- **[[Assiminea pecos]]** (Assimineidae): a minute, chestnut-brown conical snail, named for the [[Pecos River]]; the inland population is more remote from the sea than any other known Assiminea.
- **[[Fontelicella]]** (Hydrobiidae): the most widespread and diversified hydrobiid genus in the American West. Species are grouped into informal groups: davisi, gilae, kolobensis, neomexicana, pecosensis, thermalis, and trivialis, plus stearnsiana and californiensis series in the broader genus.
- **[[Apachecoccus]]** and **[[Yaquicoccus]]**: new monotypic genera from Arizona and Arizona/Sonora drainages.
- **[[Tryonia]]** (Littoridinidae): with the new subgenus **[[Paupertryonia]]**, which lacks basal penial papillae.
- **[[Pisidium sanguinichristi]]** (Sphaeriidae): a new fingernail clam from a Sangre de Cristo cirque lake.

**Diagnostic and descriptive highlights from the text.**
- *Assiminea pecos*: spire angle 55–60°; up to 4½ whorls; umbilicus contained about 9 times in shell diameter; females slightly larger than males.
- *Fontelicella davisi*: Y-shaped penial gland (DP) characteristic of the davisi group; bursa/albumen gland ratio 0.45–0.76 in five specimens (mean 0.60).
- *Fontelicella roswellensis*: unique within the genus for an operculum with internal calcareous thickening.
- *Fontelicella pecosensis*: operculum with raised spiral coiling on the external face; a dorsal distal lobule characteristic of the species.
- *Fontelicella thermalis*: the most distinctive species of the genus; shell W/L may exceed 1.0; horseshoe-shaped terminal gland; lacks the granular patch behind the eyes.
- *Tryonia stocktonensis*: spiral sculpture of raised threads; attains shell length 5.5 mm.
- *Pisidium sanguinichristi*: holotype length 2.4 mm, height 2.0 mm, diameter 1.6 mm.

**Species checklist by status.** The text states that the fauna is largely narrowly local and considered actually or potentially endangered. Several taxa are recorded in the document as New Mexico Department of Game and Fish or U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service candidate endangered species, including the Pecos spring snail (*Fontelicella pecosensis*), Roswell spring snail (*F. roswellensis*), Gila spring snail (*F. gilae*), Chupadera spring snail (*F. chupaderae*), Socorro-area [[Fontelicella neomexicana]], New Mexico hot spring snail (*F. thermalis*), Alamosa spring snail (*[[Tryonia alamosae]]*), Koster's spring snail (*[[Tryonia kosteri]]*), and the Sangre de Cristo pea clam (*Pisidium sanguinichristi*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainage history as the core theme.** The author argues that spring-restricted hydrobiids and littoridinids are useful indicators of past river drainages, provided species are restricted to perennial water at all life stages, their habitat and dispersal are known, and their affinities are understood. Shell features are usually not indicators of relationships, and the fossil record is unlikely to be illuminating. Thermal-spring species are considered generally ancient, perhaps mid-Tertiary. As a rule of thumb, *Fontelicella* may be Pliocene and *Tryonia* Miocene in age, although whether this reflects intrinsic speciation rates or habitat (grazers on exposed surfaces versus sediment-dwelling particle selectors) remains to be studied.

**Biogeographic boundary.** Spring and spring-brook snails show a clear dividing line running southeast along the [[Mogollon Highlands]] of Arizona into Chihuahua, parallel to the Rio Grande. All *Tryonia* (*Paupertryonia*) are northeast of this line. Fontelicellas northeast of the line belong to the *F. californiensis* series, related to Utah species west of the Colorado River. Southwest of the line, *Tryonia* (s.s.) and Fontelicellas are of the *F. stearnsiana* group.

**Regional geology.** Drainage evolution is attributed to epiorogenic uplift of the southern Colorado Plateau–eastern Basin and Range region and differential movement on basin-range faults (Gile & Hatton, 1983 as cited), with headward erosion by master streams (Rio Grande, Gila River) at the expense of interior streams.

- **[[Upper Rio Grande]]:** Molluscan evidence is consistent with the view that the river was an interior drainage that became tributary to internal basins of northern Mexico by latest Pliocene–early Pleistocene and joined the lower Rio Grande only in mid-Pleistocene. Three living species are restricted to the upper Rio Grande. The Alamosa Formation (early Pleistocene) fossils show no hint of a rich perennial-stream fauna.
- **[[Pecos River and Lower Rio Grande]]:** Local gastropods are restricted to southeastern New Mexico and Trans-Pecos Texas, concentrated in the Roswell and Delaware basins. The author concludes these basins were not part of the [[Ogallala Formation]] alluvial-fan sequence and were already in a different environment and drainage. Northward progression of solution-subsidence basins is proposed to provide isolating mechanisms.
- **[[Gila River]]:** Endemic forms of eastern Arizona (*Tryonia gilae* and *Apachecoccus*) are related to species in the Rio Santa Maria, Chihuahua. Species of the upper Gila in New Mexico (*F. gilae*, *F. thermalis*) are sharply distinct. Their occurrence in the former separate [[Mimbres River]] drainage is suggested as an isolating mechanism, based on an alluvial divide and incised lower Sapillo Creek.

**Habitat preferences recorded in the text.**
- *Assiminea pecos*: moist earth beside seepages or spring-brooks, never beside standing water; beneath salt grass or sedges.
- *Fontelicella* (generally): abundant among vegetation, on mud and rock, in spring outflows; some populations (e.g., Torreon Springs) on rootlets in tiny source pools.
- *Tryonia*: sediment-dwelling; most abundant in soft mud; *T. kosteri* in gypsum-rich waters of Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge.
- *Thermal springs*: *F. thermalis* at 33–35°C in sheets or trickles, with upper temperature limit recorded as 38°C; *Apachecoccus* sources ranged 26–32°C.
- *Pisidium sanguinichristi*: Middle Fork Lake, a glacial cirque lake at 10,845 ft (3,306 m) with no submergent vegetation.

**Other notes from the habitat accounts.** The text notes that snails at the Naegele Springs outflow were at 24°C; at Blue Spring, *F. pecosensis* occurred on pebbles and mud at the source; the Diamond Y Draw system is described as associated with [[Physa mexicana]] and [[Pisidium casertanum]]. Water-chemistry and flow data are referenced to Summers (1976) and Henry (1979). Human modification (irrigation impoundment, canal construction, flooding, and burning of sedges at Cuatro Cienegas) is repeatedly described as reducing or eliminating populations, with several species recorded as extinct or nearly so (e.g., *Assiminea pecos* at a Bitter Lake seepage that became extinct between 1981 and 1984; *F. neomexicana* at its type locality).

## Key Findings

- **Species count and classification:** 22 species in total, classified as Assimineidae (1), Hydrobiidae (11 *Fontelicella* plus two new monotypic genera, *Apachecoccus* and *Yaquicoccus*), Littoridinidae (7 *Tryonia*), and Sphaeriidae (1 *Pisidium*). Nine new species from New Mexico, four from Arizona, two from Utah, five from Texas; redescriptions of two more from New Mexico and Texas.
- **Endemism and conservation:** Virtually all species are narrowly local and considered actually or potentially endangered.
- **Drainage history:** The fauna is consistent with separate drainages evolving during Pliocene or older times that were later united into present stream systems.
- **Upper Rio Grande isolation:** Molluscan evidence supports a late integration of the upper Rio Grande with the lower Rio Grande (mid-Pleistocene), and does not support an ancient greater Rio Grande.
- **Miocene Santa Fe Group:** The author argues that Miocene depositional environments in the Rio Grande rift were characterized by closed basins with central playa lakes, not through-going fluvial systems, contrary to Rogers et al. (1985).
- **Ogallala Formation:** The Roswell and Delaware basin springs are argued to be inconsistent with an Ogallala alluvial blanket covering the Pecos Valley.
- **Biogeographic line:** A clear dividing line separates *Tryonia* (*Paupertryonia*) and *Fontelicella californiensis* series to the northeast from *Tryonia* (s.s.) and *Fontelicella stearnsiana* group to the southwest.
- **Taxonomic methodology:** The author developed a symbolic notation and terminology for penial ornament (Fig. 3), with classification based on penis structure and glands; female reproductive features (bursa/albumen gland ratio) are secondary.
- **Relative rates:** Preliminary "rule of thumb" that *Fontelicella* is Pliocene and *Tryonia* Miocene in age, with the cause (intrinsic speciation rates versus habitat) left unresolved.
- **Notable species results:**
  - *Assiminea pecos*: type locality on Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge, Chaves County, NM; holotype LACM 2088; a population at Roswell Country Club recorded as one empty shell representing an extinct population; a live animal found at only one locality in Coahuila (Rio Churince, 10-VII-1975).
  - *Fontelicella neomexicana*: original type from Socorro warm springs; now extinct at type locality; Torreon Springs population estimated at about 5,000 individuals in 24-IV-1981 (the author notes the 1971 Fish & Wildlife status is doubtful).
  - *Tryonia alamosae*: restricted to springs forming the source of Alamosa Creek; males and females markedly dimorphic (mean length 1.41 mm vs 2.30 mm).
  - *Tryonia cheatumi*: outflows of Phantom Lake and San Solomon Springs; of 80 males examined, 79 had two penial papillae and one had only one.
  - *Pisidium sanguinichristi*: described from Middle Fork Lake (Taos County, NM); one of nine Sangre de Cristo lakes sampled for Pisidium in which it was found.
- **Management implications (from the text):** Protection is maintained at the Sago Spring type locality of *Tryonia kosteri* in Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge; the Roswell Country Club population of *T. kosteri* and *F. roswellensis* is described as doubtful in future. The author also notes trematode infestation and parasitic castration are high at Sago Spring.
- **Research gaps:** Whether thermal-spring species are ancient, whether *F. thermalis* occurs in cooler water, and whether the Pisidium species occurs beyond Middle Fork Lake are left for further study.
