# Ash Meadows Preservation Committee Commendation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1986 letter and commendation from the [[Desert Fishes Council]] recognizes the [[Ash Meadows Preservation Committee]] for its central role in protecting [[Ash Meadows]], Nevada. The [[California-Nevada Chapter]] of the [[American Fisheries Society]] gave the committee its Conservation Achievement Award at the 1986 Annual Meeting. The document traces the area's decline and reprieve, then its acquisition for public ownership, and names the individuals whose advocacy made it possible. It contains no quantitative data on fish populations.

## Background & Description

The letter, signed by [[P. Pister]] as Executive Secretary and dated January 28, 1986, was addressed to committee members [[Barbara Kelley]], [[Cindy Williams]], [[Tasker Edmiston]], [[Beula Edmiston]] and [[Dave Livermore]]. The award consisted of a walnut plaque bearing the Society's insignia and an engraved brass plate. A narrative read at the awards ceremony was enclosed as a commendation.

The commendation describes two North American areas with the highest levels of endemism:

- The [[Cuatrociénegas Basin]] of Coahuila, Mexico, which the document says may soon be lost to development because protective legislation is absent.
- Ash Meadows, in Nye County, southwestern Nevada, near [[Death Valley National Monument]].

Both areas support endemic plants, invertebrates and fishes, many listed as endangered or threatened. The document does not name any fish species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document presents Ash Meadows as an "enormously valuable ecosystem" with a dependent fauna and flora. The main threat was groundwater decline:

- **Late 1960s:** Large land tracts were exchanged to allow private development, around the time the environmental movement was rising and the scientific community was becoming aware of the area's value.
- **Agricultural pumping:** Development lowered the water table until spring levels were becoming "dangerously and disastrously low."
- **1976:** Legal action by the Desert Fishes Council and the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] produced a reprieve through the [[Devils Hole]] decision of the [[U.S. Supreme Court]].
- **Later reprieve loss:** The land was sold to a real estate developer, and streets were bulldozed and the site levelled for subdivision.

## Key Findings

- **Endemism:** Ash Meadows and the Cuatrociénegas Basin are among the most endemic areas in North America, with many endangered or threatened species.
- **Protection history:** The 1976 Devils Hole Supreme Court decision gave the area a temporary reprieve. Development then resumed before the committee intervened.
- **Committee role:** The Desert Fishes Council formed the Ash Meadows Preservation Committee, which worked with minimal donation-based funding. Its work included lobbying legislators, organizing public hearings, and drafting the funding legislation.
- **Acquisition:** [[Dave Livermore]] of the [[Nature Conservancy]] negotiated the purchase. Federal funds were appropriated through legislators including [[Paul Laxalt]] and [[Alan Cranston]], and escrow papers were signed in December 1983.
- **Outcome:** Ash Meadows now has a fully protected status through public ownership, which the document describes as assuring its future.
- **Recognition:** The award honors Barbara Kelley's persistence, supported by Cindy Williams and Tasker and Beula Edmiston.
