# Literature Review and Impact Report on the Fauna of Several Streams in East-Central Arizona at Transecting Points of a Proposed Power Line

#ichthyology #environmental-impact #aquatic-ecology #riparian-ecology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1973 literature review and impact assessment examines the vertebrate and invertebrate fauna of several streams and rivers in east-central Arizona at proposed power line crossing points. Prepared by J. N. Rinne (Arizona State University) for Dames & Moore environmental consultants, the report synthesizes published literature, unpublished reports, field notes, and verbal communications to characterize aquatic habitats and their fauna. The assessment covers fishes, aquatic insects, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals across multiple water bodies including the [[Little Colorado River]], [[Leonard Canyon]], [[Canyon Creek]], [[Carizzo Creek]], [[Cibeque Creek]], [[Salt River]], [[Gila River]], and [[San Pedro River]]. While most crossings would have minimal detrimental effects, several locations—particularly [[Leonard Canyon]] and upper [[Canyon Creek]]—harbor rare, threatened, or scientifically significant species that could be greatly affected. Re-routing the transmission line at these sensitive locations is recommended.

## Background & Description

The proposed power transmission line traverses multiple aquatic habitats in east-central Arizona, each with distinct hydrological characteristics and faunal assemblages. Flow data from USGS records reveal highly variable discharge patterns influenced by irrigation diversions, reservoirs, and characteristic Sonoran Desert cyclonic patterns.

**Hydrological Context:**
- [[Little Colorado River]] at Holbrook: Mean flow 210.5 m³/min (22 years of record), with extremely variable discharge from <2.0 to >100,000 m³/min
- [[Leonard Canyon]]: Permanent flow of cool to cold water at higher elevation
- [[Canyon Creek]], [[Carizzo Creek]], [[Cibeque Creek]]: Mean flow ~60 m³/min (12 years of record)
- [[Salt River]]: Mean flow 1,400 m³/min (57 years of record), maximum flows to 200,000 m³/min
- [[Gila River]] below Coolidge Dam: Flow regulated since 1928; pre-dam maximum ~220,000 m³/min reduced to 2,200 m³/min post-dam
- [[San Pedro River]]: Highly variable, zero flow recorded May-June 1970, ~300 m³/min in August

The report acknowledges vast changes in aquatic habitats and faunas over the last century in the American Southwest due to irrigation, damming, and climate factors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic Insects:**
The report provides extensive taxonomic lists (Tables 1-3) of aquatic insects by order and family, including [[Coleoptera]] (beetles), [[Diptera]] (flies), [[Odonata]] (dragonflies and damselflies), [[Hemiptera]] (true bugs), [[Plecoptera]] (stoneflies), [[Neuroptera]], [[Trichoptera]] (caddisflies), and [[Ephemeroptera]] (mayflies). Species lists are based on Arizona State University museum records and field notes. Several new species of water pennies (Family [[Psephenidae]]) were being described from the White Mountains region adjacent to study streams.

**Fishes:**
Native fish assemblages have been severely impacted by habitat modification. Key native species include:
- [[Lepidomeda vittata]] (Little Colorado River spinedace): Rare and threatened, nearly wiped out over most of its range, now largely confined to [[East Clear Creek]] drainage
- [[Rhinichthys osculus]] (speckled dace): Highly variable species with potentially unique form in [[Leonard Canyon]] under study at Arizona State University
- [[Gila robusta]] (roundtail chub): Multiple subspecies present
- [[Pantosteus delphinus]], [[Catostomus latipinnis]], and undescribed [[Catostomus]] sp.: Native suckers
- [[Agosia chrysogaster]]: Desert-adapted minnow

Introduced species dominate many locations, including [[Lepomis cyanellus]] (green sunfish), [[Lepomis macrochirus]] (bluegill), [[Ictalurus punctatus]] (channel catfish), [[Gambusia affinis]] (mosquitofish), [[Cyprinus carpio]] (carp), [[Salmo gairdneri]] (rainbow trout), and [[Salmo trutta]] (brown trout).

**Amphibians and Reptiles:**
Approximately 25 species of aquatic or semi-aquatic herptiles may occur at crossing points (Table 4). [[Rana pipiens]] (leopard frog) comprises several distinct forms, perhaps separate species based on morphological, call, and blood data. The report notes that while no species are on rare and endangered lists, some are considered rare by scientists.

**Birds:**
Table 5 lists aquatic and semi-aquatic avifauna organized by habitat preference (open water vs. water's edge) and seasonal occurrence. Species include grebes, cormorants, herons, egrets, waterfowl, rails, shorebirds, and riparian-dependent passerines. The [[Belted Kingfisher]], [[Black Phoebe]], [[Dipper]], and various warblers depend on streamside vegetation.

**Mammals:**
Aquatic mammals include [[Castor canadensis]] (beaver), [[Ondatra zibethicus]] (muskrat), shrews (Family [[Soricidae]]), and possibly [[Zapus]] sp. (jumping mice) in upper reaches of [[Cibeque Creek]], [[Canyon Creek]], [[Carizzo Creek]], and [[Leonard Canyon]]. Many terrestrial mammals (raccoon, bats, cottontail, pocket gophers, mice, gray fox, badger, skunks, deer, javelina, bobcat) frequent watercourses due to riparian vegetation concentrating food and cover.

## Key Findings

1. **Minimal Impact Locations:** Most power line crossings will have minimal detrimental effects on aquatic organisms. The [[Little Colorado River]] near Holbrook, [[Salt River]] at Gleason Flats, [[Gila River]] below Coolidge Dam, and [[San Pedro River]] near Winkelman contain largely introduced or widespread species that can withstand construction disturbance.

2. **Critical Concern - Leonard Canyon:** This tributary harbors the rare and threatened [[Lepidomeda vittata]] (Little Colorado River spinedace), a potentially unique population of [[Rhinichthys osculus]] under study at Arizona State University, and possibly an undescribed [[Catostomus]] species. The report strongly recommends: "extensive consideration and caution should be practiced in manipulating this environment. If possible, it would be recommended to avoid crossing this mountain stream."

3. **Critical Concern - Upper Canyon Creek:** The proposed route "passes over or very near" a newly established Arizona Game and Fish hatchery on upper [[Canyon Creek]]. This location requires definitive determination and likely avoidance.

4. **Re-routing Recommendation:** "In several cases, Leonard Canyon and upper Canyon Creek, re-routing of the trans-line might be both advised and expedient."

5. **Aquatic Insects:** Comprehensive species lists provided for three habitat groupings (rim complex, Salt River, Gila/San Pedro rivers). Actual field sampling is "requisite to substantiate, modify, and/or add to, and in instances, negate, data reported."

6. **Fish Community Changes:** Native large-river fishes including [[Xyrauchen texanus]] (humpback sucker), [[Gila elegans]] (bonytail chub), and [[Ptychocheilus lucius]] (Colorado squawfish) once inhabited the [[San Pedro River]] but are now extirpated from the study area.

7. **Amphibians and Reptiles:** Impact "will be mostly in the form of habitat destruction during construction activities" but should be minimal on populations overall.

8. **Birds:** Species inhabiting water's edge "might be more permanently affected by construction activities than the open water species" because destroyed vegetation requires longer replacement time than flowing water. Rare or uncommon species may experience greater disturbance due to small population sizes.

9. **Mammals:** Beavers in upper [[Cibeque Creek]], [[Canyon Creek]], and [[Carizzo Creek]] "could be especially disturbed by construction activities which might be sufficient to displace them." However, reproductive potential of small rodents and insectivores would be sufficient to replace temporary losses.

10. **Data Limitations:** The report explicitly states that "on the spot, contemporary sampling is requisite" and that species lists "are not, nor are they intended to be exhaustive." Botanical data could not be provided within the time frame.

11. **Scientific Significance:** Beyond immediate conservation concerns, the report emphasizes potential impacts "both in the actual and scientific sense," recognizing that unique populations under active study (e.g., [[Rhinichthys osculus]] in [[Leonard Canyon]]) have research value beyond their conservation status.
