## Executive Summary

This 1989 attachment is a status update on the **Propagation/Genetics Management Plan** for endangered fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], authored by the Propagation Coordinator for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. It is not a species morphology or ecology study, but an administrative/scientific management document outlining the strategy for using **artificial propagation, genetic conservation, and habitat protection** to recover populations of the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (historically "Colorado squawfish"), the [[Razorback Sucker]], and a Gila-complex species (referred to as "Gila sp.", likely [[Bonytail Chub]] or related [[Gila Chub]] taxa). The Plan is under review by the Management and Technical Groups of the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP) and involves coordination among USFWS Regions 2 and 6, an ad hoc Propagation Work Group (PWG), a hatchery advisory team, and field stations in Grand Junction, Colorado and Vernal, Utah.

## Key Findings

- The **Propagation/Genetics Management Plan** is framed as part of a broader **ecosystem-based recovery strategy** for endangered fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin, integrating habitat protection/modification with artificial propagation and genetic conservation principles.
- **Seven major objectives** (subdivided into tasks) structure the Plan, including:
  1. Formation of a technical/professional group for Plan development and implementation.
  2. Identification of **short-term fish needs** (current year), including Kenney Reservoir issues, marking/tagging studies, Gila sp. and razorback sucker genetics studies, refugia/broodstock development, and contaminants studies for razorback sucker.
  3. Identification of **long-term fish needs** projected over 15 years in 5-year increments.
  4. Identification of facilities (new and existing) needed to meet short- and long-term fish needs, including site evaluation/design criteria and construction as necessary.
  5. Development of a **Genetics Management Plan** for rare fishes of the upper basin, including initiation of appropriate genetic studies.
  6. Development of **rare fish germplasm/gene banks**.
  7. Development of an **integrated recovery management framework** linking recovery goals, fish needs/problems, and the conservation "status" of identifiable fish populations — from which an array of recovery/action options (including artificial propagation) can be prioritized based on specificity, priority, and feasibility.
- **Artificial propagation** is explicitly defined as a "tool," not an end goal — used to initiate/sustain wild reproduction and to maintain rare gene pools in captivity. **Fish stocking** is characterized as a subset of artificial propagation activities.
- Major capability areas of artificial propagation identified: **refugia, research and development (R/D), and production for stocking** (experimental releases, augmentation, and reintroduction).
- The **FY 1990 Work Plan** includes six concrete propagation/genetics activities:
  1. Genetics studies of Gila sp. and razorback sucker.
  2. Development of Colorado squawfish (pikeminnow) and razorback sucker refugia populations.
  3. Development of prototype facility design/construction criteria for refugia, broodstock development, R/D, and production, including site visits/evaluations — with assistance from the **Colorado Water Conservation Board**.
  4. Grow-out feeding trials for Colorado squawfish (CDOW, Fort Collins, CO) and razorback sucker (USFWS, Ouray NWR, UT).
  5. Marking and tagging studies to determine origins/uses of fish stocking program outputs (USFWS Dexter National Fish Hatchery, NM, and Grand Junction, CO Colorado River Fishery Project).
  6. Development of **alternate production technologies**.
- Institutional coordination is emphasized as essential: the Propagation Coordinator depends on guidance from the **Technical Group**, advice from the **Propagation Work Group (PWG)**, and ad hoc hatchery advisory groups, with USFWS field stations in **Grand Junction, CO** and **Vernal, UT** identified as vital to implementation.
- The overarching genetic conservation principle stated is to **prevent loss of unique genetic resources and disruption of natural patterns of genetic diversity**, framed as the most efficient approach to preserving evolutionary potential and long-term species survival.
