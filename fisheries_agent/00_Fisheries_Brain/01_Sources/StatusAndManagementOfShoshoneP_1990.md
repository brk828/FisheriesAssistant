# Status and Management of Shoshone Pupfish (Cyprinodon nevadensis shoshone) at Shoshone Spring, Inyo County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary
[[Shoshone Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis shoshone*), a subspecies endemic to [[Shoshone Spring]] in Inyo County, California, was considered extinct as of 1969 but rediscovered in 1986 in the spring's outflow. This 1988 survey (15–18 April, 26 May, 19 November) mapped the highly altered spring system, assessed habitat quality, and quantified fish distribution and abundance. Researchers found pupfish numbers drastically reduced compared to 1986 observations, with only large adult fish captured (suggesting an aging remnant population lacking recruitment), and vastly outnumbered by invasive [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). Habitat area had shrunk 33–66% since 1937 estimates, driven by water diversion, channelization, tamarisk invasion, and chlorine pollution from a nearby swimming pool. Given the population's precarious status, researchers removed 10 pupfish to establish a captive refuge population at UC Davis for future reintroduction, pending mosquitofish eradication and habitat restoration.

## Background & Description
- **Species status**: Subspecies of *Cyprinodon nevadensis* (Death Valley pupfish complex), closely related to [[Amargosa Pupfish]] (*C. n. amargosae*).
- **Size range captured (16 April 1988)**: Standard length 40–54 mm; total length 46–62 mm (Table 1); only large adults were captured — no juveniles or small size classes observed, contrasting with the mixed size/age structure reported in 1986.
- **Sex ratio in samples**: Mixed males and females captured across Middle and Lower spring sections (e.g., 6 males/3 females on 26 May 1988).
- **Comparative note**: A related species, [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*C. diabolis*), was documented to attain larger size and altered morphology after several generations in an artificial refuge (Williams 1977), raising concerns about phenotypic drift in captive-held Shoshone pupfish.
- **Taxonomic ambiguity**: Rediscovered fish (Taylor et al. 1988) did not match historical Shoshone pupfish morphology exactly; possible explanations include a genetic bottleneck, morphological shift due to river residency, or hybridization/introgression with Amargosa pupfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type**: Highly modified desert spring system divided into three sections (Upper, Middle, Lower) by Old State Highway and State Highway 127, further subdivided by spring boxes, culverts, cement ditches, and pipes that create partial barriers to fish movement.
- **Physicochemical conditions (17 April 1988)**:
  - Water temperature: 32°C at source, decreasing downstream to 21.6°C (air temp 20.3°C)
  - Specific conductance: 1458–1755 μmhos·cm⁻³
  - pH: 6.7–7.4
  - Total dissolved solids: 1005–1207 mg·L⁻¹
  - Dissolved oxygen: 6.25 ppm at 30°C (cement ditch, 1230 h)
  - Flow rate: 3270 cm³·sec⁻¹ (cement ditch, 16 April 1988)
- **Vegetation**: Historically bordered by cattails (*Typha* sp.) and mesquite (*Prosopis* sp.); now dominated by invasive tamarisk (*Tamarix chinensis*), which may alter water/nutrient balance and shade spring channels. Bulrush (*Scirpus* sp.) also present.
- **Habitat area loss**: Estimated 1100–2200 m² of pupfish habitat in 1937 (per Miller 1948) vs. 740 m² in April 1988 — a 33–66% reduction. Maximum depth also halved (<30 cm vs. historical up to 60 cm), suggesting even greater loss in habitat *volume* than area.
- **Refuge quality by section**:
  - **Upper section**: Relies on spring box leakage/overflow; no fish found; unsuitable as refuge without direct flow restoration.
  - **Middle section**: Includes cement ditch (best remaining refuge) but threatened by chlorine pollution from pool cleaning, vegetation overgrowth, and mosquitofish presence.
  - **Lower section**: Sandy substrate, prone to drying; floodplain connection to Amargosa River during high water may allow fish exchange between river and spring.
- **Threats**:
  - Habitat alteration (channelization, water diversion since at least 1985, all channels now artificial)
  - Chlorine/bleach pollution events (e.g., 17 November 1988 pool draining/bleaching killed 359 mosquitofish and 1 pupfish)
  - Predation/competition from introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (present since between 1938–1967)
  - Tamarisk invasion altering riparian structure
- **Interspecific interactions**: Documented parallel to [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) decline under mosquitofish pressure (Minckley 1969) — shift from mixed-size population to few large old individuals, mirrored in Shoshone pupfish population structure.

## Key Findings
- **Population status**: Only 7 pupfish captured vs. 2,625 mosquitofish during 16–17 April 1988 survey — mosquitofish dominate all sampled habitats where pupfish were found.
- **Demographic shift**: Population structure changed from mixed sizes/ages (observed 1986 by Taylor et al.) to only large adults (1988), consistent with a population in decline lacking recruitment, potentially due to mosquitofish predation on fry and reduced female survivorship (per Schoenherr 1981 mechanism).
- **No pupfish or mosquitofish found in Upper section**, despite historical presence (Miller 1948, 1938 collection) — indicates total habitat loss/unsuitability in that section.
- **Pollution mortality event**: 19 November 1988 — chlorine/bleach discharge from pool cleaning caused mass mosquitofish kill (359 dead) and killed at least one pupfish in the cement ditch; incomplete mortality suggests either partial exposure or reinvasion from unaffected downstream sections.
- **Conservation action taken**: 10 pupfish (7 males, 3 females total across visits) removed and transferred to University of California, Davis to establish a captive refuge/breeding population for future reintroduction.
- **Management recommendations**:
  1. Eliminate mosquitofish before reintroducing pupfish.
  2. Restore historical habitat conditions (larger pools, mesquite/cattail vegetation, removal of tamarisk).
  3. Increase available habitat area/volume and stabilize flow.
  4. Protect remaining refugia (especially the cement ditch) from chemical pollutants, trash, and water diversion.
  5. Conduct further research on the Amargosa River drainage to clarify the taxonomic/genetic relationship between Shoshone pupfish and [[Amargosa Pupfish]], given unresolved questions about the "reappearance" mechanism (invasion, introduction, or non-detection hypotheses per Taylor et al. 1988).
- **Broader significance**: This case exemplifies challenges in desert fish conservation across the [[Death Valley System]], paralleling documented mosquitofish-driven declines in other native cyprinodontiforms (e.g., [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], Gila topminnow), and underscores the fragility of isolated spring endemics facing simultaneous anthropogenic water development and invasive species pressure.
