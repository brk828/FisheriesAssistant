# Ichthyoplankton of Lake Havasu

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game study describes larval fish (ichthyoplankton) sampled in [[Lake Havasu]] on the lower [[Colorado River]] in 1985 and 1986, with sampling running from March through June. The survey collected 6,617 larvae (5,126 in 1985; 1,491 in 1986) representing eight species in six families. [[Common Carp]] and [[Sunfish Lepomis|Lepomis spp.]] (mostly [[Bluegill]]) dominated the catch, with [[Threadfin Shad]] second in 1986. Larval abundance tracked water temperature, reflecting predictable spawning phenology. The study also found that [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae were present but too small to have hatched upstream in [[Lake Mohave]], suggesting that natural reproduction occurred in the study area, although no documented razorback reproduction had been recorded since the 1940s–1950s.

## Background & Description

The study area is a relatively long reach of the lower Colorado River, including Lake Havasu and the river sections above it. The fish fauna is dominated by non-native warm-water species. The authors note that 27 fish species have been recorded from the area (Minckley 1979; Marsh et al. 1988), and the larvae identified in this study represent only a subset of these.

Several groups of fishes were of particular interest:

- **Native species:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is extirpated from the area, and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) is extremely rare. The razorback sucker is the key native species for which larval evidence was sought.
- **Presumed-absent non-natives:** White sturgeon, brown bullhead, white crappie, and mottled sculpin were relegated to hypothetical status by Minckley (1979). Self-sustaining populations of these fishes have not been established, so larvae were not expected.
- **Stocked fishes:** Rainbow trout below [[Davis Dam]] are maintained by stocking and natural reproduction.
- **Baitfish:** Goldfish, golden shiner, red shiner, and fathead minnow have been widely used as bait in the lower Colorado River. Only the red shiner was noted as producing insignificant numbers.
- **Other species not encountered:** Flathead catfish, black and yellow bullheads, mosquitofish, smallmouth bass, green sunfish, and redear sunfish were absent from collections. The authors attribute this to their preference for backwater or marginal habitats, their rarity in the area, or the difficulty of identifying [[Lepomis Species|Lepomis]] larvae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reservoir versus river.** All species found in the river were also found in the reservoir. However, all were notably more abundant in the reservoir. The authors suggest that the strongly flowing, relatively cold river is apparently not favored for spawning by these predominantly warm-water fishes.

**Water temperature and spawning.** Larval abundance followed water temperature closely, based on literature values and in-lake measurements:

| Species | Reported spawning temperature |
|---|---|
| [[Threadfin Shad]] | Spring, when water reaches about 21 °C |
| [[Common Carp]] | Move into shallows at about 16 °C; peak activity at about 18–20 °C |
| [[Bluegill]] | Protracted season, extending from spring through autumn |
| [[Largemouth Bass]] | 16–24 °C |
| [[Black Crappie]] | 14–18 °C (value partly unclear in source text) |

Midwater temperature in Lake Havasu was below 13 °C when sampling began in 1985. It rose above 21 °C after mid-April and remained above that level after the first week of May. In 1986, temperatures were near 10 °C at the start of sampling, reached 18 °C by 1 April, 20 °C by 1 May, and remained about 21 °C after mid-May.

**Phenology.** [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae appeared earliest, at the beginning of April, and were the first to disappear, with few individuals collected after mid-May. Other taxa appeared in April or May and persisted into June. A similar pattern has been observed in Lake Mohave (Langhorst and Marsh 1986).

## Key Findings

**1. Catch composition**
- Eight species in six families were identified from 6,617 larvae. Bluegill was the only species identified to species level with confidence, though other Lepomis were present.
- [[Common Carp]] was the most abundant taxon in both years, at 49% (1985) and 55% (1986) of the catch.
- Lepomis spp. made up 29% of the 1985 catch and a much smaller share in 1986.
- In 1986, [[Threadfin Shad]] was the second most numerous fish, at 37% of the catch.
- Centrarchids were much less abundant in 1985, comprising only about 3% of the total.
- [[Striped Bass]] and razorback sucker each made up less than 2% of the total in each year.

**2. Catch per unit effort (CPE)**
- Total CPE in 1986 was about one-third of the 1985 value.
- Threadfin shad CPE in 1986 was about 80% of the 1985 value, and carp CPE was about 34% of the 1985 value. The centrarchid comparison is not clearly legible in the source.
- The authors suggest these changes may reflect differences in reproductive success, spatial distribution of spawning, or timing of spawning, incubation, and hatching.

**3. Razorback sucker evidence**
- Larvae were collected in the study area, which the authors interpret as evidence of natural reproduction.
- Passive downstream travel from [[Lake Mohave]] to [[Parker Dam]] (136 km) takes at least 13.2 days. Larvae hatched above Davis Dam would need to be about 23 days old, 12–15 mm long, and near the metalarval stage on arrival.
- All collected razorback sucker specimens were proto- or mesolarvae. They averaged about 9.5 mm in length (n = 23), with the largest at 10.8 mm.
- The authors conclude it is improbable that these larvae were produced upstream in Lake Mohave.
- Razorback suckers were reported spawning below Davis Dam and in Lake Havasu in the 1940s and 1950s, but no documented reproduction has been recorded since then.

**4. Methodological notes**
- Several Lepomis species are notoriously difficult to identify at the larval stage, so some may have gone undetected.
- Larval identification relied on published keys and atlases, including Snyder (1979, 1981), Wang (1981), and Winn and Miller (1954).
