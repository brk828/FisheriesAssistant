# Some Optical Properties of Aquaria and Their Application to Lighting and Design

#aquarium-design #public-aquarium #exhibit-technology #optics

## Executive Summary

This technical report documents a 1958-59 study of lighting systems at the [[Cleveland Aquarium]], analyzing how glass-sided aquaria behave optically as liquid-filled prisms. The study established five fundamental optical principles governing light behavior in aquarium tanks and applied these principles to develop two innovations: (1) a front-lighting system using total internal reflection to eliminate ventral shadowing on fish, and (2) an "invisible" hexagonal tank design that minimizes artificial appearance through strategic use of refraction and reflection. The hexagonal design was implemented in a 1,300-gallon exhibit tank and provides improved visitor traffic flow and increased linear exhibit space.

## Background & Description

The study examined optical phenomena in aquarium exhibits, treating glass-sided tanks and their water-air interfaces as optical systems. Five empirical principles were established through ray tracing methods:

1. Light entering an aquarium at incident angles greater than zero refracts toward the normal
2. Light striking glass sides or water/air interfaces at large angles undergoes total internal reflection
3. Interference colors in fishes (e.g., brilliant blue of [[Neon Tetra]] *Hyphessobrycon innesi*) and scale reflections display optimally under point-source illumination such as unfrosted incandescent lamps
4. External light incident to the water/air surface interface at very large angles is mostly reflected
5. Objects not located along perpendicular sight lines appear displaced from true position due to refraction

Previous work by Coates and Atz (1949) identified limitations of side-lighting through aquarium walls, attributing failures to fish disturbance and algal occlusion. Garnaud (1952) at Monaco noted that tank sides sloping at approximately 45° become invisible to viewers due to refraction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses the visual presentation of aquatic organisms in public aquarium settings. Traditional rectangular "fish in a box" tank designs create artificial appearances by making sides, back, bottom, and corners visible to observers. The optical properties of water-glass interfaces significantly affect how fish coloration appears to viewers, particularly interference-based colors versus pigmentation.

Dorsal lighting creates ventral shadowing on fish specimens, reducing visibility of anatomical features. The positioning and type of light sources directly influence the display quality of fish scales and structural coloration. Algal growth on glass surfaces can occlude light entry points in conventional side-lighting arrangements.

## Key Findings

**Front Lighting System Using Total Internal Reflection:**
- Conventional overhead lighting illuminates fish dorsally but creates ventral shadows
- Previous attempts at side-lighting through aquarium walls proved unsuccessful due to fish disturbance and algal occlusion
- A new arrangement positions an additional light source (L) to transmit light toward the front glass where it undergoes total internal reflection, illuminating the front side of fish
- Unfrosted incandescent bulbs as point sources optimize display of interference colors and scale reflections
- This system produces "spectacular improvement in the appearance of the displayed fishes"

**"Invisible" Hexagonal Tank Design:**
- Hexagonal configuration with 30° side walls minimizes visible tank structure
- Walls A and B appear to fold back out of sight through refraction
- Bottom surface with shallow front-to-rear slope appears very steep due to refraction
- Back three walls (C, D, E) remain nearly unlighted and become invisible when painted dark
- A 1,300-gallon hexagonal tank implementing this design was operational at [[Cleveland Aquarium]]

**Visitor Experience Improvements:**
- Hexagonal tanks "nest" well together in exhibition halls
- Tank rotation creates automatic traffic patterns: visitors see active exhibits on right side entering, then on left side returning
- Label panels (L) positioned adjacent to tank fronts allow studious readers to step out of main traffic flow
- Design increases linear exhibit space length
- Provides service access to two rear sides of each tank

**Diorama Integration:**
- Smaller tanks with 30° side walls accommodate curved rear dioramas viewable through glass and water layers
- Water layer provides magnification of background diorama from visitor viewpoint near front glass
- Skillful arrangement creates convincing impression of large "seascape" when background blends with living animals in foreground

The report concludes that further investigation of relationships between optical properties and aquarium design is warranted to enhance exhibit appearance, recognizing that visiting public aquaria is primarily a visual experience.
