# Effects of River Regulation on Fish Populations Below Cow Green Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how impoundment and flow regulation of the River Tees, following closure of the [[Cow Green Reservoir]] dam in June 1970, altered populations of [[Bullhead]] (*Cottus gobio*) and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*). Before-and-after comparisons in the regulated Tees were paired with an unregulated tributary, [[Maize Beck]], which served as a control. Regulation was associated with higher population densities and biomass of both species and a threefold rise in bullhead production, while individual growth of trout changed little. The authors conclude that temperature, rather than food, largely limits growth, so fish benefited mainly through greater numbers rather than faster growth.

## Background & Description

The study was conducted by Crisp, Mann and Cubby (1983) at the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] and the River Laboratory, East Stoke. The Cow Green dam regulates downstream discharge. Stations were located in the Tees below [[Cauldron Snout]], a 34 m waterfall 322 m downstream of the dam, above the confluence with Maize Beck, and in the lower reaches of Maize Beck.

Sampling was by [[Electrofishing]] in spring, summer and autumn. Data were grouped into three periods: pre-regulation (August 1967 to May 1970), transition (August and October 1970), and post-regulation (May 1971 onwards). Most post-regulation comparisons used 1972–75 data, with later data from 1975–80 used where appropriate. Methods included scale and otolith ageing, back-calculated growth, fecundity counts, survivorship curves, and production estimates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Regulation substantially reduced diel water temperature fluctuations in the Tees. During experimental releases in July 1976, Tees temperatures ranged from 11.5 to 18.5 °C, whereas Maize Beck ranged from 13.1 to 27.5 °C and exceeded 18 °C, a level associated with high temperature stress in brown trout.

Exceptionally mild winters during 1971–75 coincided with the post-regulation period. Mean winter air temperature at Moor House was 0.07 ± 0.571 °C for 1952–70, compared with 1.31 ± 0.358 °C for 1970–76. The authors judged these winters not causally linked to regulation and used Maize Beck as a control to separate their effects. Armitage (1978), cited in the paper, reported that benthic standing crop increased in the Tees after regulation.

## Key Findings

- **Population density:** Trout density (excluding O group) rose from 3.5 to 4.9 per 100 m². Bullhead densities rose markedly for I group (9.5 to 81.5 per 100 m²) and II group (5.2 to 24.4 per 100 m²). Both species were significantly more numerous in the Tees than in Maize Beck in both periods (P < 0.05).
- **Mortality:** Annual instantaneous mortality for bullheads in the Tees increased from 0.67 ± 0.21 to 1.30 ± 0.68 after impoundment. Maize Beck values were 0.68 ± 0.25 and 0.81 ± 0.22.
- **Fecundity:** Individual female bullhead fecundity fell in the Tees but was unchanged in Maize Beck. Total population egg output in the Tees rose from 3.70 to 8.42 eggs per m² (P < 0.05), while Maize Beck values (2.04 and 2.28) did not differ significantly.
- **Production:** Bullhead production in the Tees rose from 1.056 to 3.638 g m⁻² yr⁻¹, mainly through O and I group fish. Maize Beck rose only slightly (0.401 to 0.547 g m⁻² yr⁻¹). Trout production estimates were very approximate: 0.3 and 0.4 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ in the Tees before and after regulation, and 0.15 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ in Maize Beck.
- **Growth:** Trout growth changed only slightly, with a statistically significant but small increase. Bullhead O group lengths fell in the Tees, which the authors attribute to later spawning (only 22% of mature bullheads had spawned by late April/early May, versus about 85% in Maize Beck; χ² = 67.9, P < 0.001).
- **Maturity:** Age at first maturity did not change in trout. In bullheads, the proportion of mature I group fish in the Tees was 20% before and after regulation, though II group maturity was reported differently across periods.
- **Interpretation:** Growth in both species appears limited mainly by water temperature. Regulation therefore benefited fish chiefly through higher population density and biomass rather than faster individual growth. The authors note that food supply may also have improved, but this was not proven.
- **Limitations:** Electrofishing equipment operated near its useful limit in the larger Tees, and high-discharge sampling was less reliable. Population estimates were variable, and the authors judged that before-and-after comparisons were likely to underestimate, rather than overstate, real changes.
