# Habitat Requirements of Juvenile Colorado Squawfish in the Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 report, prepared by [[Paul B. Holden]] for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under contract 14-16-0009-77-050, examines the habitat requirements of rare fishes in the Green River between Jensen and Green River, Utah, with emphasis on young [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The study addresses the four endangered or threatened-candidate species of the upper [[Colorado River Basin]]: Colorado squawfish, [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Field work used electivity curves, a method from the [[Instream Flow Methodology]], to link habitat selection to physical and chemical conditions. The study concludes that backwaters are the critical habitat for young rare fishes, that spawning was confined largely to the lower reach in a low-water year, and that pump intake placement and backwater management should account for these needs.

## Background & Description

- **Rare species:** Colorado squawfish and humpback chub were listed as endangered. Bonytail chub and razorback sucker were under consideration for listing as endangered and threatened, respectively.
- **Core research need:** Determining the subsistence requirements of these fishes to support recovery and to guide natural-resource development in the upper basin.
- **Two key factors:** (1) adequate spawning and rearing habitat, and (2) interaction with exotic fishes.
- **Reference reach:** The lower and middle Green River in Utah held reproducing squawfish populations and was the least altered large-river habitat in the upper basin.
- **Objectives:** Determine physical and chemical habitat parameters; determine whether and where spawning occurs; determine movements by size class; identify associated fishes; and assess the relative importance of river habitats.

**Methods (summary):** Sampling ran in August 1977 (Jensen to Sand Wash, using boats) and September 1977 (Sand Wash to Green River, using a rubber raft). Crews measured velocity (Marsh-McBirney current meter), depth (graduated wading rod), substrate, and temperature, and recorded chemistry with YSI and Bausch & Lomb instruments. Fish were collected by seine, electrofishing and fry shovel, with seines used most often. Rare fish over about 100 mm were tagged with numbered finger-ring tags; juveniles under 100 mm were fin-clipped.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish community.** Over 330 samples yielded more than 19,500 specimens of 19 species. The introduced [[Red Shiner]] was by far the most abundant fish. The [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] was the most abundant native species. The bonytail chub was the rarest native fish, with only two individuals caught. The introduced white sucker, redside shiner and creek chub were rare and mostly upstream, where the river is cooler.

**Hydrology and temperature.** Daily temperature swings were 3 to 5 °C, with backwaters the warmest habitats (up to 3 to 8 °C above the river). Daily stage fluctuation from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] releases reached 16 inches at Jensen but only 3 to 4 inches in [[Desolation Canyon]]. Flows were lower than normal in 1977, which reduced backwater availability.

**Water chemistry.** Dissolved oxygen, pH, ammonia, nitrate, nitrite, sulphate, alkalinity, conductivity and salinity changed little during the study and remained within expected ranges. Turbidity peaked after rainstorms, and native fishes appear adapted to these sudden changes.

**Backwaters.** Backwaters were typified by no current, firm silt substrate, and a depth of 1 to 2 feet. They are nutrient-rich, warm, and less affected by stage changes than other habitats. Two types were distinguished: short-lived (changing with small level shifts) and long-lived (stable from early summer to the next spring high flows).

## Key Findings

**Habitat selection (electivity curves)**
- Young-of-the-year squawfish (30–64 mm) were found only in backwaters with no current, a firm silt bottom, and a depth of 1 to 2 feet.
- Juvenile squawfish (65–200 mm) also preferred backwaters, but were less selective of depth.
- Subadult and adult squawfish (200+ mm) used a range of velocities and depths, preferring sandy-bottomed eddies and runs.
- All squawfish sizes showed little temperature preference.
- Six young-of-the-year razorback suckers were tentatively identified, preferring eddies at about 0.2 fps, about 1.0 foot depth and a soft silt bottom. These are described as the first wild young razorbacks on record.
- Young humpback chub preferred backwaters with no current, a firm silt bottom and about 2 feet depth, but used a wider range of habitats. Adult humpbacks showed no clear depth or velocity preference.

**Distribution and spawning**
- Young-of-the-year squawfish occurred only in the lower study area. Juveniles were concentrated at the upper and lower ends, with fewer in the middle reach (Ouray to Sand Wash).
- Spawning was inferred for squawfish in Gray Canyon, and no young were found above the point of major concentration. Razorback spawning was inferred near and below Ouray. Humpback spawning was inferred for Desolation and Gray canyons.
- No young-of-the-year were found from Jensen to Ouray. The authors reason that 1977 low flows and cool temperatures may explain this.

**Movement**
- Twenty juvenile squawfish were tagged, plus 13 more anal-fin clipped and four subadults. One tagged juvenile was recaptured at its release site after 4.5 hours.
- A two-and-a-half-day study in one backwater marked 31, 29, 11 and 12 young-of-the-year squawfish across five samples, with 4 and 1 recaptures in the first and later samples. Marked fish were not found elsewhere. The authors conclude movement occurred within the backwater, but its extent is unknown.

**Competition and predation**
- Exotic species dominated young-of-the-year collections with squawfish and humpback chub. Red shiners, fathead minnows and channel catfish were the most frequent associates of young squawfish.
- The introduced [[Smallmouth Bass]] adds a predator, though spring turbidity and river rigor should limit its reproduction.

**Management implications**
- Pump intakes for irrigation and refuge flooding on the Ouray National Wildlife Refuge should avoid slow water, especially backwaters. Swift runs are the better option.
- Artificially constructed long-lived backwaters could increase exotic fish populations, so short-lived backwaters may suffice for young squawfish.
- Future studies in normal flow years are needed to confirm whether low flows limited reproduction.

**Reliability of results**
- Under [[Cooperative Instream Flow Service Group]] criteria, young-of-the-year squawfish and humpback chub curves were rated "good," and the others "fair." Several years of data are needed for "excellent" reliability in juvenile and adult fish.
