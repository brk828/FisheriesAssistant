# Physical Limnology and Profundal Bottom Fauna of Mountain Lake, Virginia

#limnology #freshwater-benthos #tubificidae

## Executive Summary

This Virginia Agricultural Experiment Station technical bulletin (Roth and Neff, 1964) reports a year-long study of the physical and chemical limnology and profundal bottom fauna of [[Mountain Lake]], a 1,180 m elevation lake in the Ridge and Valley province of western Virginia. Sampling ran twice monthly from January 1962 to January 19, 1963, at a 22 m station, with an August 1962 depth-distribution series. The lake is classed as a second-class, temperate, [[Dimictic Lake]] (possibly monomictic in some years). Its oxygen budget indicates oligotrophy, but its morphometry suggests eutrophy, and its bottom fauna is dominated by tubificid worms, which conventional typology would associate with productive waters. The authors conclude that traditional concepts of productive lake typology "must be critically re-examined."

## Background & Description

Mountain Lake is a small, soft-water lake formed when a natural rockslide dammed a mountain stream valley. Earlier work included [[Hutchinson and Pickford]] (1932), who sampled in July–August 1931; Coker and Hayes (1940) and Grover and Coker (1940) on net plankton; and Platt and Shoup (1950), who used a thermistor in 1947.

**Morphometry (Table 1):**
- Maximum length 870 m; maximum width 266 m
- Surface area 189,000 m² (about 0.2 km²)
- Maximum depth 31.5 m; mean depth 9.75 m
- Volume 1,850,000 m³
- Shoreline length 2,160 m; shoreline development 1.37
- Mean basin slope 15.0%
- Drainage basin 0.95 km², a basin-to-lake area ratio of 5.02

The basin is regular and shallowest at the south end, with a single maximum depression near the north outlet. Half the volume lies above the 6 m contour, and 99% of area and volume lie in the upper 22 m.

**Setting:** The lake sits in a breach of a Silurian [[Clinch Sandstone]] anticline, within [[Martinsburg Shale]] of Ordovician age. The drainage basin is ancient, resistant rock under virgin hemlock–northern hardwood forest, which the authors credit for the lack of suspended matter. Water comes from seepage and underwater springs. The outlet is Pond Drain, a tributary of Little Stony Creek.

**Methods:** Temperature and conductivity were read at 1 m intervals with a thermistor. Water samples were taken at 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 m. Dissolved oxygen was determined by the unmodified [[Winkler Method]]; pH was measured in the field with a Hellige comparator. Transparency was measured with a 20 cm [[Secchi Disc]] and a USGS turbidity rod. Bottom samples were collected with a 15.2 × 15.2 cm [[Ekman Dredge]], three hauls per sampling day, and washed through #30 and #40 screens (0.589 and 0.417 mm). Taxonomic identifications were made by Dr. R. O. Brinkhurst (oligochaetes) and Dr. G. A. Cole (ostracods).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime:** Four thermal seasons were recognised: autumnal circulation (about 6 weeks), winter stratification (about 8 weeks, with inverse stratification), vernal circulation (about 6 weeks), and summer stratification (about 28 weeks). Winter warming under ice was attributed to solar radiation, bottom-mud heat, and underwater springs at about 5 °C. Hypolimnetic temperatures stayed near 5–6 °C through summer.

**Chemistry:** Conductivity was 9–18 µmhos with no appreciable vertical gradient, and the water is very soft. Bicarbonate alkalinity was 6–8 mg/L, with no phenolphthalein alkalinity. The pH range was 6.4–7.6, and the surface was slightly alkaline in most seasons. Turbidity was below 7 mg/L silica.

**Oxygen:** During summer, the metalimnion showed a positive heterograde maximum (115% saturation at 10 m on August 6, 1962), with the hypolimnion falling to 70% saturation. At the end of stagnation (November 5), the bottom layer reached a minimum of 5.5 mg/L (57% saturation). The authors judged that oxygen does not limit benthos.

**Benthic habitat:** The littoral zone extends to about 10 m and is dominated by a [[Nitella]] bed. Rhododendron and Kalmia overhang the rocky shore. Soft mud appears only below 10 m. The sediment at 22 m is two-layered: a superficial ooze (organic content 18.76%) over clay (8.14% organic), with about 10–11% of each layer soluble in HCl.

## Key Findings

**1. Trophic status is mixed.**
- Areal hypolimnetic oxygen deficit: 0.0035 mg/cm² per day over 113 days stagnation (0.404 mg/cm² consumed). This is "well within the range denoting oligotrophy."
- Oxygen-curve shape suggested secondary (morphometric) eutrophy because of the small mean depth.
- Profundal mud and fauna also indicated oligotrophy.

**2. Profundal fauna is qualitatively poor but quantitatively rich.**
- Typical profundal fauna comprised tubificid oligochaetes, [[Chaoborus]] larvae, tendipedid larvae, and ostracods.
- Tubificids were the most numerous and most massive animals, comprising at least 95% of volume and mass in most samples.
- Total dry mass of profundal organisms never exceeded 5 g/m² in the depth series and was usually much less.

**3. Species accounts.**
- [[Tubifex Templetoni]] was the most numerous organism at all profundal depths.
- [[Limnodrilus Hoffmeisteri]] made up about 10–15% of oligochaetes at 16 m.
- [[Candona]] sp. was intermediate between *C. caudata* and *C. intermedia* and could not be assigned to either.
- [[Tendipes Attenuatus]] was the largest and most common tendipedid. [[Tendipes Modestus]] and [[Procladius Culiciformis]] were also present.
- Five profundal insect species were found in total.

**4. Seasonal and depth patterns at the 22 m station.**
- Oligochaetes: about 4,000/m² in winter, rising to over 10,000/m² in late autumn. Dry weight ranged from about 250 to over 4,500 mg/m².
- Ostracods: usually 200–1,000/m². A major peak of about 5,000/m² occurred on August 20, and a smaller peak of about 3,500/m² on March 2.
- Insects: about 300/m² on average, with a March 2 maximum above 1,100/m².
- Chaoborus: generally under 100/m²; absent from deep water in midsummer.

**5. Depth distribution (August 1962).**
- A transitional sublittoral fauna occurred at 10 and 13 m, with each taxon usually under 30/m².
- Concentration zone at 13 m: ostracods almost 9,000/m² and tendipedids over 700/m².
- Tubificids rose from under 2,000/m² at 10 m to over 8,000/m² at 24 m.
- Chaoborus occurred from 10 to 19 m, but not below.

**6. Statistical results.**
- Analyses of variance (Appendix VI) showed significant differences at the 1% level between seasons and between depths.
- The authors concluded that animals were not randomly distributed within depth zones and that Poisson variation was not satisfied.

**7. Typology paradoxes.**
- Classical European typology, based on tendipedid oxygen tolerance (e.g., *Chironomus* vs. *Tanytarsus* lakes), would predict productive conditions from the tubificid-dominated fauna.
- The authors list unresolved questions, including why [[Sphaeriidae]] are restricted to shallow water, why the "oligotrophic" [[Calopsectra]] is limited to the littoral and sublittoral, and why Chaoborus leaves the hypolimnion in midsummer even without oxygen depletion.
- They conclude that "traditional concepts of productive lake typology must be critically re-examined" and recommend further research on [[Lake Typology]].

**8. Data caveats.**
- The source gives conflicting annual temperature figures. The summary lists a mean range of 2.21–18.04 °C, while the Temperature Summary and Appendix I give 2.21–13.04 °C. Appendix I supports 13.04 °C.
- The summary gives 28 weeks of summer stratification, while the text also describes 7 months (April to November) in 1962.
- Technical limits of the dredge and screening, together with the small sample size (three hauls per date), are acknowledged. Statistical treatment is described as "not warranted" beyond the analyses presented.
