# Scientists Struggle to Save Rapidly Diminishing Species: The Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This newspaper feature by Steve Daniels (Western Arizona Bureau) reports on the decline of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large native fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]] that was once common in Arizona and southern California but has been reduced to a few surviving adults, mostly in [[Lake Mohave]]. The article describes a recovery effort in which wild fish were taken to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico for breeding, and hatchery-raised fry were released into the Salt, Gila and Verde rivers. It also covers the [[Bonytail Sucker]], which is considered among the most seriously endangered fish in North America. The article notes that the razorback has not been federally listed, a decision attributed to political concerns.

## Background & Description

- **Identity:** The razorback is one of four native fish of the Colorado River Basin considered threatened with extinction. The others are the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], the [[Humpback Chub]] and the bonytail sucker. Unlike the other three, the razorback is not on the endangered species list.
- **Appearance:** It resembles a large carp, with a dorsal fin behind the head (the source of its name), an elongated snout and a cylindrical body. It is reddish-brown with a yellow underside.
- **Size:** Adults grow up to 8 feet long and weigh up to 14 pounds.
- **Historical abundance:** Until the mid-1940s the species was common in Arizona and in southern California irrigation canals fed by the Colorado River. Fifty years before the article, it was so abundant in the Phoenix canals and the [[Salt River]] above [[Roosevelt Lake]] that wagonloads were collected.
- **Commercial fishery:** As late as 1949, a commercial razorback fishery operated on [[Saguaro Lake]]. The lake was drained in 1966, and no razorbacks survived.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline:** Predation by other fish is believed to be the primary cause. Predators include [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Catfish]], both introduced into the Colorado River.

**Lake Mohave population:**
- Lake Mohave held the only large population, believed to number in the thousands at the time of reporting.
- [[W.L. Minckley]], an Arizona State University zoologist, believes the surviving fish hatched after the reservoir filled in 1950–52. The population has produced no offspring since.
- The surviving fish are estimated to be about 25 years old and are described as the last native razorback suckers in the Southwest.
- Minckley's statistics: more than 30 percent are blind in at least one eye, and 4 percent have external tumors, a sign of old age.
- Minckley expected the last of the razorbacks to die within about five years.

**Analogy:** [[Jerry Burton]], an endangered-species biologist with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], compared the population to "a bunch of 80-year-old people around."

**Status of other native fish:** According to Minckley, the Colorado River squawfish and humpback chub, once found throughout the basin, no longer exist there.

## Key Findings

1. **Rescue capture:** In January, federal and state biologists and ASU researchers netted 136 razorbacks from Lake Mohave and sent them to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in Dexter, New Mexico, as [[Brood Stock]].
2. **Hatchery fry releases:** In June and again in September, a total of 16,000 razorback fry bred at Dexter were released at three sites on the Salt and Gila rivers and at two sites on the [[Verde River]]. The fry could not yet reproduce, and it was unknown whether they would breed in the wild.
3. **Brood stock replacement:** Brood stock at the hatchery was dying off. A four-day trammel-net capture in Lake Mohave took about 70 razorbacks to replace them.
4. **Recovery target:** The program aims to produce 100,000 razorbacks a year at Dexter for 10 years, so that about 1 million could eventually be [[Reintroduction|reintroduced]] in Arizona and surrounding states.
5. **Listing withheld:** Although the razorback is a strong candidate for the federal threatened or endangered list, federal officials withheld its listing. Burton said listing would make each new introduction a fight. He attributed the reluctance of agencies to the political problems caused by listed species, pointing to the [[Tellico Dam]] and [[Snail Darter]] controversy.
6. **Bonytail sucker:** Fewer than 24 bonytails had been recovered from the Colorado River system in the past decade. Minckley and Burton called it "the most seriously endangered fish species in North America, if not the world." In 1979 a survey found six females but no males in Lake Mohave, and researchers feared the species was lost. In March 1981 four male bonytails were found. They were used in a successful spawn, and 42,000 bonytail fingerlings were reintroduced to Lake Mohave the previous month.

**Conclusion:** The article presents the razorback as a species at serious risk. Its survival depends on captive breeding and reintroduction, and the effort is complicated by the lack of federal listing and by the aging, unreproductive remnant population in Lake Mohave.
