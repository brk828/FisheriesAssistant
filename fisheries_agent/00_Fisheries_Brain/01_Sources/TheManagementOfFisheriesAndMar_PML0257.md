# Fisheries Management and Marine Ecosystems: Overfishing, Indirect Effects and Physical Forcing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This document is a reprint of a 1997 [[Science]] review by Botsford, Castilla and Peterson on the [[Management of Fisheries]] and [[Marine Ecosystem Management]]. It argues that the global marine fish catch is near its upper limit and that management has failed to prevent overfishing. The authors attribute much of this failure to a [[Ratchet Effect]], in which political pressure for higher harvests combines with uncertainty in predicting collapse. They call for a more holistic approach that includes [[Multispecies Interactions]] and [[Physical Environment]] influences, and for changes in the management process itself. The page sequence also includes a short fragment from [[Dobson et al. (1997)]] on [[Restoration Ecology]] and [[Habitat Conversion]]. The bulk of the remaining text is reference lists and scan debris from unrelated soil biology articles and is not summarized here.

## Background & Description

Fishing provides direct employment to about 200 million people and supplies roughly 19% of animal protein for humans. Global first-sale fishery revenues are about US$20 billion. Landings have leveled off in the 1990s at about 100 million tons. Almost half of individual fish stocks are fully exploited, and others are overexploited. The authors note that fishery scientists have rarely applied an [[Ecosystem Approach]] because marine systems are complex and difficult to sample.

The [[Ecological Society of America]] defines ecosystem management as holistic consideration of interactions among ecosystem components to achieve sustainability through [[Adaptive Management]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Direct and indirect effects of fishing**

- Fisheries remove about 8% of global marine primary production, but they require 25 to 55% of upwelling and continental shelf production.
- Overfishing is most serious for long-lived, low-reproductive species. Classic collapses include the [[Sardine]] stocks off California and Japan in the late 1940s, and the [[Anchoveta]] off Peru and Chile in 1972.
- Sardines, anchovies and related small pelagic species form a dominant part of world catches.
- Size-selective fishing reduces average age, size at age and genetic diversity. Bycatch of juvenile and non-target species is substantial, and nonselective gear and high prices for a few target species encourage waste.
- Bottom-contact gear (dredging, trawling, long-lining and explosives) destroys benthic structural habitat, including coral reefs, polychaete reefs, seagrass beds and other epibenthic organisms.

**Trophic cascades**

- Top-down control in coastal systems typically follows loss of a top predator, such as sea otters or lobsters. Released prey can then dominate rock surfaces and displace less competitive species.
- Overfishing of herbivorous fishes in the Caribbean, together with eutrophication, allows [[Macroalgae]] to overgrow and kill coral.
- In Chile, removal of the predatory gastropod *Concholepas concholepas* (loco) in the rocky intertidal allowed its mussel prey (*Perumytilus purpuratus*) to dominate. After harvest was stopped in 1981, loco density rose within a couple of years and mussels were largely eliminated. After 12 years, three barnacle species and various macroalgae had replaced the mussels.
- Baleen whale removal may have released zooplankton that fueled alternative energy pathways. Fishing on sardines, anchovies and other forage species also harms seabirds and marine mammals, which depend on them during the energetically demanding rearing of young near coastal colonies.
- Discarded fish feed aggressive scavenging seabirds, which disrupt the wider seabird community through nest predation.

**Physical forcing**

- [[El Niño-Southern Oscillation]] (ENSO) causes warming and changes in coastal circulation. Effects include reduced primary productivity near the equator, lower zooplankton productivity in the California Current, and reduced survival and growth of salmon, mackerel and anchovies.
- A [[Regime Shift]] occurred in the North Pacific in the mid-1970s, when the Aleutian low-pressure system intensified and the region shifted from cooler to warmer conditions in 1976. Associated changes included increased mixed-layer depth, higher chlorophyll concentrations, increased Alaskan salmon catches, and a shift from shrimp to fish (gadoids and flatfish) dominance in the northern Gulf of Alaska.
- Shifts in sardine and anchovy stocks worldwide may reflect long-term, large-scale physical change rather than fishing alone.
- Finer-scale work shows that weekly fluctuations in upwelling winds affect primary productivity, that calm periods allow larval feeding, and that mesoscale circulation controls larval transport and recruitment. This variability drives spatial patterns of [[Metapopulation]] dynamics.

**Restoration ecology (Dobson et al.)**

- Habitat conversion to agriculture, industry and cities is the major threat to biodiversity. Industrial accidents produce sudden, large but localized change, while habitat conversion acts chronically over long periods and builds an "extinction debt."
- Restoration ecology is presented as a complement to nature reserves for preserving biodiversity.

## Key Findings

1. **Overfishing is driven mainly by management behavior.** The authors attribute most overfishing to the Ratchet Effect. Managers under political pressure allow harvest increases until scientists cannot rule out collapse, and the burden of proof falls on managers rather than the industry.
2. **Uncertainty is intrinsic.** Limited understanding of marine complexity, the cost of sampling, and environmental variability mean predictions of harvest effects will always be uncertain. Reducing harvest rates therefore requires reducing political pressure, for example through lower overcapitalization, reduced subsidies, and controlling open access.
3. **Maximum sustained yield (MSY) is criticized.** Since the 1970s, MSY has been challenged for risking populations, ignoring spatial variability and non-target species, counting benefits but not costs, and being sensitive to politics. The authors note that the criticism concerned the goal's narrowness, not sustainability itself.
4. **Thresholds guide current policy.** Fishery agencies now set limits below which rebuilding actions are required. In the United States, about 100 federal plans define overfishing and prescribe remedies. Thresholds rely on empirical comparisons because recruitment collapse points are unknown until they occur.
5. **Holism requires multispecies and physical inputs.** Greater holism means considering species interactions, broad-scale physical forcing, and management responses to pressure. Tools include multispecies stock assessment, food web models, and interaction webs that incorporate physical drivers.
6. **Better management is proposed.** Recommendations include a precautionary approach in which the burden of proof lies with those favoring risky actions, adaptive management, stakeholder inclusion, and insulation of management from short-term political pressure. Community-based and individual transferable quota systems are discussed as options for local fisheries.
7. **Regime shifts complicate interpretation.** Population changes that appear to reflect fishing may instead reflect decadal physical regime shifts. Both must be considered together.
8. **Conservation and fisheries science should collaborate.** Developing management thresholds for low-abundance stocks offers a shared opportunity with [[Endangered Species]] researchers.
9. **Restoration and reserves complement each other.** In the Dobson et al. fragment, restoration must occur before substantial biodiversity losses, and cleanup of industrial accidents should minimize biodiversity impacts.
