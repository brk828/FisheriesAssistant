## Key Findings

- The [[Endangered Species Act]] was passed nearly unanimously in 1973 (92–0 Senate; 355–4 House) and initially protected 109 species; by 1998 this had grown to 1,177 species, ~60% of which are plants.
- Only 27 species have ever been delisted, and of those, 16 were removed due to extinction or erroneous original listing — a statistic cited by critics as evidence of the law's ineffectiveness in achieving species **recovery**, as opposed to mere **protection**.
- The **snail darter** case (Tellico River, TN, 1977) is identified as the first high-profile confrontation under the ESA, halting a nearly-completed TVA dam. Congress ultimately overrode the law via legislative exemption, and the dam was completed — establishing an early precedent for legislative override of ESA protections.
- This case is explicitly noted as damaging the law's public image: conservationist Mark Van Putten states the snail darter "was not the best poster child for the Endangered Species Act," as it created a perception of an "inflexible act" prioritizing an obscure fish over major infrastructure and economic interests.
- In 1982, Congress amended the ESA to introduce **Habitat Conservation Plans (HCPs)**, permitting "incidental take" of species during otherwise lawful land development — a flexibility mechanism directly influenced by conflicts like the snail darter dispute.
- By 1998, 243 HCP agreements covered 6.2 million acres across 16 states, reflecting continued use of negotiated, case-by-case approaches to reconcile species protection (including aquatic species) with land development.
- The article underscores that freshwater fish species, though less charismatic than flagship terrestrial megafauna (bald eagle, grizzly bear, whooping crane), have played an outsized role in shaping ESA jurisprudence and public controversy, foreshadowing later aquatic-species conflicts (e.g., listing debates over other freshwater and riverine taxa).
