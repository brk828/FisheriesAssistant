# Use of Impounded Water for Fish Culture

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Research Report (No. 35, 1953) by Harlan E. Johnson and Richard F. Brice reports experiments at the [[Dorena Dam Experimental Laboratory]], Cottage Grove, Oregon, from September 1950 to November 1952. The study tested whether water from [[Dorena Reservoir]] could be used to incubate eggs and rear fingerlings of [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Silver Salmon]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Cutthroat Trout]]. The motivation was the scarcity of hatchery sites in the [[Columbia River Basin]], where dams block migrations and alter flows.

The authors conclude that reservoir water contained no toxic substance harmful to salmonids. Its main limitations were low dissolved oxygen in deep water, which [[Aeration]] corrected, and high summer and fall temperatures. Temperature was the critical factor for chinook egg survival and for outbreaks of [[Columnaris Disease]].

## Background & Description

Dams in the Columbia system delay adult migrations, flood spawning areas, injure seaward migrants, and alter conditions within and below impoundments. Hatchery sites with suitable water supplies are limited. Using reservoir water would widen hatchery options, and hatcheries below dams could avoid transporting fish around barriers and provide a steadier flow than natural streams.

Doubts about reservoir water arose mainly from mortality of silver-salmon eggs at a hatchery below [[Ariel Dam]] on the [[Lewis River]] in Washington. Dorena was chosen because it was under construction when the question arose, and its intake could be installed during construction. The authors acknowledge that a small reservoir with little winter storage is not fully representative, but they consider it broadly comparable to others in the region.

**Dam and reservoir (from the Corps of Engineers regulation manual):**
- Earth-fill dam with concrete spillway on the [[Row River]], an upper tributary of the [[Willamette River]]; completed 1949.
- Reservoir at normal pool: 77,500 acre-feet gross capacity, 1,835 acres surface area, extending 4.6 miles upstream.
- Permanent pool of 7,000 acre-feet below elevation 770.5 ft; net usable storage 70,500 acre-feet.
- Basin of 265 square miles; normal annual precipitation 54 inches, about 70 percent from November through March.
- The dam has no fishways and is a total barrier to upstream migration. The Row River had apparently never supported anadromous runs.

**Hatchery facilities:** The laboratory was in a temporary building near the spillway, with 22 deep troughs (16 × 16 × 16 in.) and 24 circular tanks (6 ft diameter, 18 in. deep). Three intakes at 805, 785, and 765 ft elevation drew water through 12-inch pipes reduced to 8-inch lines running about 600 feet to the building.

**Methods:** Water was tested for dissolved oxygen (Winkler method), alkalinity, pH, and ammonia. Temperatures were recorded at the dam face and at stations in the reservoir. Experiments combined water from the three intakes and an "optimum" supply held at 55° to 60° F, with aerated and unaerated versions of each.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime:** The reservoir stratified in summer. A thermocline first appeared in late May at about 17 to 27 ft depth and descended through the season. Stratification broke down in fall as the reservoir was drawn down and surface water cooled.

**Dissolved oxygen:** Surface water stayed high through winter. Bottom water became depleted after stratification developed. In 1951 bottom oxygen reached 0.1 ppm at station 2 on September 27 and was exhausted at station 1 on October 3. Oxygen recovered when oxygen-depleted water was discharged, not through natural overturn.

**pH and alkalinity:** Surface pH ranged from 6.7 to 7.4. The lowest value, 6.4, occurred in midsummer. Alkalinity (as CaCO₃) was 16.5 to 31.5 ppm across supplies, due to bicarbonates. Free ammonia reached at most 0.07 ppm, far below harmful levels.

**Fish community:** Rainbow and cutthroat trout stocked by the Oregon State Game Commission supported a sport fishery. Cyprinids (dace and shiners) remained after rotenone treatment in 1949. Unverified reports of catfish existed.

**Temperature in the reservoir:** Lower-intake water ranged from 34° to 68° F over November 1950 to November 1952. Middle-intake water ranged from 39° to 69° F. Upper-intake water ranged from 44° to 73° F in 1951–1952. The reservoir's maximum recorded temperature was 78° F at 3 ft depth on August 12, 1952. Row River temperatures above the dam exceeded 70° F for much of July and August 1950, which the authors suggest may explain the absence of native spring chinook.

## Key Findings

**Water quality conclusions (numbered as in the report):**
1. No toxic substance in harmful concentration was found in Dorena water, based on analyses and hatchery experiments.
2. Low dissolved oxygen in lower-reservoir water during summer and fall required aeration.
3. When adequately aerated, lower-intake water was satisfactory for rearing fingerling chinook and silver salmon and rainbow and cutthroat trout. Warm years increased the risk of bacterial disease, especially columnaris.
4. Lower-intake water was suitable for incubating silver salmon, rainbow trout, and cutthroat trout eggs. Abnormal losses were attributed to factors other than water quality.
5. Fall water from all three intakes was too warm for chinook egg incubation. Cooling to below 55° F made it suitable.
6. Fingerlings held above 65° F for extended periods contracted columnaris, and mortality was high. Sulfamerazine partly controlled the disease.

**Fall chinook salmon (3 lots):**
- Lot 4 (1950 brood, 27,618 green eggs): 73% mortality; attributed to incubation temperatures of 60° to 64° F for three weeks.
- Lot 10 (1951 brood): mortality of 12.1% (control), 17.5% (lower-intake water), and 9.5% (cooled water), indicating temperature was the critical factor.
- Lot 16 (1952 brood, 5,320 green eggs): 100% mortality in lower-intake water (61.5° to 67° F); 3.6% in cooled water versus 3.7% in the control at Little White Salmon Hatchery. Cooled water (54° to 55° F) was judged suitable.

**Spring chinook salmon (5 lots):**
- Lot 1 (2,122 fingerlings, 1949 brood): about 12% kidney-disease loss; 8.3% transport loss excluded. Final weight 17.2 g.
- Lot 3 (18,975 green eggs, 1950 brood): over 95% mortality in incubation at temperatures above 60° F.
- Lot 7 (16,108 fingerlings): good growth in lower-intake water (excellent results); columnaris in warm upper-intake and optimum supplies caused losses of over 50% in the upper-intake tanks.
- Lot 11 (1951 brood): control 43.1%, cooled water 45.0%, lower-intake 51.3%, upper-intake 79.6% egg mortality. Temperature was judged the sole cause.
- Lot 15 (1952 brood, 7,370 green eggs): control 2.3%, cooled water 2.6%, lower-intake 100%; lower-intake water had daily means of 62° F or higher.

**Silver salmon (4 lots):**
- Lot 2 (about 2,455 fingerlings): 2.8% mortality, September 1950–May 1951, with good growth (8.8 g to 34.2 g).
- Lot 5A (about 28,893 green eggs, December 1950): 12.3% transport mortality, probably from milt toxicity.
- Lot 5B (28,229 green eggs, hauled in burlap): 20.2% transport mortality; high fingerling losses from bacterial infection.
- Lot 6A/6B (Coos River stock): bacterial infection killed about 25% of lot 6A fingerlings; lot 6B egg mortality was 28%.
- Lot 12 (14,061 green eggs, November 1951): 13.7% egg mortality; upper-intake aerated water lost 42.4% to columnaris.

**Rainbow trout (2 lots):**
- Lot 9 (23,300 eyed eggs, March 1951): 46.5% loss in egg, fry, and early fingerling stages, cause undetermined.
- Lot 13 (29,700 eyed eggs, February 1952): 3.5% loss in egg and fry stages; 2.5% in lower-intake water and 5.6% in optimum water during summer rearing.

**Cutthroat trout (2 lots):**
- Lot 8 (25,300 eyed eggs, March 1951): 87.1% loss to July 11; upper-intake fish lost 52% to columnaris.
- Lot 14 (18,500 eyed eggs, March 1952): 3.4% loss to fry stage; 7.2% (lower-intake) and 2.7% (optimum) loss in summer rearing.

**Diseases and treatments:**
- Kidney disease in yearling spring chinook and silver salmon (spring 1951); sulfamerazine had no apparent benefit.
- A bacterial (tentatively myxobacterial) infection killed about 16% to 25% of silver salmon fingerlings in April and May 1951. Sulfamerazine checked it at first, but losses rose again, apparently from drug toxicity to small fish. Lot 5A and 6B showed no infection.
- [[Columnaris Disease]] (caused by *Chondrococcus columnaris*) was the most damaging disease, appearing in 1951 and again in 1952 in warm upper-intake water. Water above 65° F for extended periods was the critical factor.

**Overall conclusion:** The authors recommend reservoir water for hatchery use with aeration for deep-intake water and cooling for chinook egg incubation in warm fall periods. They note that the fishery literature does not definitively establish optimal rearing temperatures.
