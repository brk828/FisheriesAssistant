# Dragonflies Collected in Kentucky, Tennessee, North and South Carolina, and Georgia in 1931

#odonata

> **Note:** This source is an entomological (non-fish) faunal survey of [[Odonata]] (dragonflies and damselflies) from the southeastern United States. No fish species data are present in the text; the note below synthesizes the freshwater aquatic invertebrate and habitat data actually reported, per instructions to follow the source faithfully.

## Executive Summary

This 1934 paper by [[E. B. Williamson]] reports results of a three-week collecting expedition (September 22–October 8, 1931) through Tennessee, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, and Kentucky, documenting **60 species of Odonata** (dragonflies and damselflies) across **42 discrete collecting localities**. The survey emphasizes late-season (autumn) phenology — a period under-sampled relative to typical summer entomological fieldwork tied to school vacation schedules — and provides detailed habitat descriptions of streams, rivers, mill ponds, and seepage bogs. Several taxonomically significant observations are included: intraspecific melanism variation in *Enallagma geminatum*, morphological differentiation between *Enallagma doubledayi* and *E. civile* females, behavioral notes on *Stylurus* mating and predation avoidance, and possible undescribed subspecific variation in *Stylurus scudderi*.

## Background & Description

- **Study design:** Opportunistic road-based collecting trip by four naturalists (E. B. Williamson, W. H. Ditzler, and Ditzler's wife and daughter), covering new-to-them terrain in the [[Piedmont Upland]] and [[Blue Ridge Province]] (per Fenneman's physiographic classification).
- **Temporal scope:** Late September through early October — explicitly noted as an unusual and valuable collecting window, since most Odonata records cluster around the May–August "school vacation" period.
- **Geographic scope:** 42 numbered localities spanning:
  - [[Little Tennessee River]] (Blount County, TN)
  - [[Shop Creek]] (Blount County, TN, ~2000 ft elevation)
  - Streams near [[Robbinsville]] and the [[Nantahala River]] watershed (Otter Creek, Queen's Creek, ~2650 ft elevation), Swain and Macon Counties, NC
  - [[Oconaluftee River]] near Smokemont, NC
  - [[Ivy Log Creek]], [[Nottely River]], [[Chestatee River]], and [[Baggs Creek]], Union/White County, GA
  - [[Little River (Georgia)]], [[Mill Pond Creek]]/[[Lokey Pond]] complex, McDuffie County, GA
  - [[Brier Creek]] and [[Butler's Mill Pond]], Burke/Jefferson County, GA
  - [[Saluda River]] and [[North Saluda River]], Greenville/Laurens/Greenwood County, SC
  - [[Shelton Laurel Creek]], Madison County, NC
  - Two roadside ponds near [[Whitesburg]], Letcher County, KY
- **Habitat types documented:** high-gradient rocky mountain torrents, slow lowland rivers, sandy creeks, spring-fed seepage bogs, artificial mill ponds and mill races, swamp forest blackwater streams (e.g., Brier Creek), and roadside pasture ponds.
- **Elevation range noted:** ~1926–2650 ft in the mountain localities (Bryson City, Nantahala, Topton).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat–species associations:** Certain species were tightly linked to specific stream morphologies:
  - *[[Boyeria grafiana]]* and *[[Boyeria vinosa]]* co-occurred but were separable in flight by wing height and abdominal spot coloration/size; *vinosa* was described as broadly tolerant of varied stream conditions and weather, while *grafiana* favored cooler, rockier torrents (e.g., Shop Creek, Queen's Creek, Location 8).
  - *[[Enallagma weewa]]* was found in small, sun-exposed, low-elevation creek habitats, contrasting with the larger-stream preference of its close relative *[[Enallagma exsulans]]*; individuals sought shade and used a "protoneurine" perching behavior near dense, bush-overhung pools.
  - *Enallagma dubium* and *[[Enallagma concisum]]* co-dominated at [[Lokey Pond]], with daily catch ratios varying dramatically (12% vs. 69% of combined catch on two different dates) despite similar weather — an unexplained temporal fluctuation in relative abundance.
  - *[[Ischnura kellicotti]]* was unusually abundant among water lilies (both native yellow lilies in deep water and white lilies in shallow, muddy/hog-trampled habitat), exceeding typical northern population densities.
  - *[[Nehalennia integricollis]]* was restricted to dense low sedge/grass stands in shallow, full-sun pools at the pond margins.
  - *[[Lestes vigilax]]* was observed in large numbers pairing and ovipositing in shallow water just inside alder borders at Lokey Pond.
- **Reproductive/behavioral observations:**
  - *[[Boyeria vinosa]]* females were observed ovipositing in damp wood a few inches above the water line (Locations 24 and 37).
  - *[[Stylurus plagiatus]]* and *Stylurus ivae* exhibited a repeated water-plunging behavior (2–5 dips) before ascending into trees.
  - An interspecific mating attempt (misalliance) was documented between a male *Stylurus plagiatus* and a female *[[Dromogomphus armatus]]*, with copulation structurally prevented despite successful coupling — noted as a rare direct field observation of odonate reproductive isolation mechanisms.
- **Water chemistry/physical habitat notes:** Streams ranged from cold, rocky, heavily shaded mountain torrents (Shop Creek: "a cold, dark, mountain torrent") to warm, slow, sandy/muddy lowland rivers (Chestatee River, Little River) to stagnant, vegetation-choked swamp streams (Brier Creek: "deep, dark, cold... choked with logs and dead tree tops"). Aquatic macrophytes noted include yellow and white water lilies (*Nymphaea*), *Cyperus*, and *Utricularia* (bladderwort), indicating mesotrophic to eutrophic pond conditions at several localities (e.g., Location 33, Butler's Mill Pond).

## Key Findings

- **Total species recorded:** 60 Odonata species across the full trip; four species (*[[Tramea lacerata]]*, *[[Pantala hymenaea]]*, *[[Pantala flavescens]]*, and *[[Celithemis eponina]]*) were field-identified visually but not collected as specimens due to time constraints and low taxonomic/geographic value of vouchering common, wide-ranging species.
- **Taxonomic notes:**
  - *[[Enallagma geminatum]]* specimens from Georgia showed pronounced, asymmetrical melanism relative to northern populations, including divided antehumeral stripes, reduced humeral markings, and expanded black areas on segments 8–9 — described in detail as a basis for geographic variation.
  - Female *[[Enallagma doubledayi]]* and *Enallagma civile* were previously unseparable by existing keys; the author proposes new diagnostic characters based on prothorax hind-border coloration and antehumeral stripe geometry relative to the mesostigmal lamina.
  - *[[Stylurus scudderi]]* specimens from this trip were larger and bluer (vs. yellower) than northern series, suggesting possible undescribed subspecific differentiation.
  - The taxonomic status of *[[Calopteryx apicale]]* vs. *Calopteryx dimidiata* remains unresolved by the author, who tentatively considers them conspecific.
  - *Enallagma vesperum*: a single male specimen showed possible southern subspecific divergence (lemon-yellow vs. blue segment 10, wider humeral stripe) but was insufficient for confirmation.
- **Notable abundance/rarity patterns:**
  - *[[Celithemis fasciata]]* was absent on a first visit to Lokey Pond but present (including mating pairs) on a return visit, with wing pigmentation more reduced than in previously seen specimens.
  - *[[Perithemis tenera]]* was "abundant" at Butler's Mill Pond (Location 25).
  - Only single specimens of *[[Somatochlora tenebrosa]]* (2 total) and *[[Somatochlora linearis]]* (2 total) were seen/collected during the entire trip, suggesting rarity or poor seasonal timing for these species.
- **Methodological/phenological value:** The authors explicitly frame the record's chief value as temporal (documenting autumn Odonata activity, a poorly studied season) rather than geographic, given the trip's short duration and non-systematic site selection.
- **Site recommendation:** The [[North Saluda River]] (Location 37) is highlighted as the most promising site for future, more extensive seasonal collecting, described as "the finest stream seen on our trip."
