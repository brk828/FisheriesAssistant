## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary morphological or ecological research paper, but an administrative **roster and planning memorandum** for the Lower Basin Recovery Implementation Scientific Workgroup (LBRISW), a scientific advisory body concerned with native fish recovery in the Lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. The roster lists academic, state, and federal ichthyologists and resource managers, their institutional affiliations, and the specific recovery/management programs each contributes to (e.g., [[Glen Canyon Dam Adaptive Management Program]], [[Central Arizona Project Native Fishes Program]], [[San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program]], [[Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Implementation Plan]]). The final section outlines foundational assumptions, stakeholder interests, and procedural tenets for developing a native fish recovery "Plan" for the Lower Colorado River, rather than presenting original species-level biological data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While species-specific ecological data are absent, the document defines the **institutional and geographic ecological context** for native fish recovery efforts:

- **Geographic scope**: Lower Colorado River Basin, including [[Lake Mead]], and connections to Upper Basin programs (Glen Canyon, San Juan River).
- **Habitat management arenas**:
  - [[Glen Canyon Dam]] tailwater and mainstem reaches (Glen Canyon Monitoring & Research Planning, tied to Biological Opinion compliance).
  - [[Lake Mead]] reservoir habitat (Lake Mead Native Fishes Research & Management).
  - Central Arizona Project canal and associated native fish refugia (Central Arizona Project Native Fishes Program).
  - Lower Colorado River mainstem fisheries management under Biological Opinion-related planning (Multispecies Habitat Conservation Planning & Native Fishes Workgroup).
- **Jurisdictional/stakeholder ecology**: The note explicitly maps the human and institutional "niche" surrounding recovery efforts, including:
  - Federal agencies: [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]], [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[International Boundary and Water Commission]], U.S. Military, [[National Park Service]].
  - State agencies: Arizona, California, and Nevada wildlife/game departments.
  - Water and power interests: hydropower production/distribution, irrigation districts, state land and water resources divisions, counties, and municipal domestic water users.
  - Tribal nations with reserved water and land interests: [[Chemehuevi Nation]], [[Cocopah Tribe]], [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]], [[Fort Mohave Tribe]], [[Hopi Nation]], [[Hualapai Nation]], [[Navajo Nation]], with possible inclusion of the [[San Carlos Apache]] and [[White Mountain Apache]] tribes.

This multi-stakeholder framing underscores that native fish recovery in this system is inseparable from water allocation, tribal water rights, and multi-agency regulatory compliance (notably under the Endangered Species Act "Biological Opinion" process).

## Key Findings

- The **Lower Basin Recovery Implementation Scientific Workgroup** comprises a small group of academic ichthyologists (e.g., [[James E. Deacon]], [[Paul Marsh]], [[William Matthews]], [[W.L. Minckley]], [[Dave Propst]]) and agency biologists (e.g., [[Owen T. Gorman]], [[C.O. Minckley]], [[Gordon Mueller]], [[Jon Sjoberg]], [[Kirk Young]], [[Chris Hayes]]) drawn from federal agencies ([[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Geological Survey]]), state agencies (Arizona, California, Nevada), and universities ([[University of Nevada Las Vegas]], [[Arizona State University]], [[University of Oklahoma]]).
- Each member is linked to specific programmatic responsibilities spanning the Upper and Lower Colorado River Basins, indicating a **coordinated, basin-wide approach** to native fish recovery rather than isolated single-site management.
- **Foundational planning assumptions** stated before substantive work begins include:
  1. Responsible agencies are genuinely committed to recovery at some level.
  2. Recovery is biologically feasible, supportable, and desired.
  3. Adequate founder stocks exist for establishing new populations without being blocked by genetic or disease concerns that have stalled past actions.
  4. The Workgroup commits to producing a publicly transmitted "Plan" concurrently with delivery to a separate stakeholder-based "Recovery Implementation Team," reflecting a commitment to transparency and open scientific publication.
- The workgroup explicitly frames **recovery definition** as needing to be biologically rigorous while contextualized within real-world river conditions, water use, and user conflicts — emphasizing that a purely biological recovery target must be reconciled with sustained human demands on the river system.
- The note calls for quantifying **sustainability estimates** around any recovery definition and producing a management narrative capable of sustaining population quantity, distribution, and quality over time — indicating a population viability/adaptive management orientation rather than a single fixed recovery number.
- The document highlights the **complex jurisdictional landscape** (federal, state, tribal, and private stakeholders) that any Lower Colorado River native fish recovery plan must navigate, underscoring that recovery planning in this basin is as much a socio-political and legal exercise as a biological one.
