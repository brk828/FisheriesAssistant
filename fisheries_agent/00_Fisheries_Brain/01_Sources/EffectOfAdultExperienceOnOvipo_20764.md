# Effect of Adult Experience on Oviposition Choice and Short-Distance Attraction in Drosophila buzzatii

#entomology #behavioral-ecology #drosophila

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether early adult experience with naturally occurring yeasts alters oviposition choice and short-distance attraction in inbred lines of [[Drosophila Buzzatii]], a [[Cactophilic Drosophila]] species. Flies were exposed to [[Candida Sonorensis]] or [[Clavispora Opuntiae]], two yeasts abundant in cactus rots and preferred for oviposition. Across four experiments, the authors found **no consistent effect of adult experience** on either oviposition choice or Y-tube attraction. The null result held when flies were starved, when choice was scored over two consecutive days, and in the olfactometer assay. The authors attribute the outcome to possible limits of the test systems, the uncontrolled length of experience, and the possibility that flies cannot learn yeast-based preferences at all.

## Background & Description

Genetic variation in natural populations is often attributed to adaptation to heterogeneous environments. Maintenance of polymorphism is more likely when genotypes choose resources where they have high fitness, and when prior experience strengthens those choices (Jaenike, 1988). Earlier work suggested that early adult experience influences oviposition on different foods in Drosophila. However, Hoffmann (1985) found strong experience effects in small petri dishes but none in a population cage, so the authors argue that experience must be tested across multiple experimental systems.

The natural habitat of *D. buzzatii* is rotting cactus, where yeasts are a major food resource. Yeast communities vary among localities, between seasons, among rots at one locality, and within individual rots. *D. buzzatii* shows differential preferences among yeast species for feeding and oviposition, and earlier studies suggested genetically based habitat selection on yeasts (Barker, 1990). Earlier experiments used naive flies. This study asked whether previous exposure to yeasts changes that choice, a question motivated by evidence that Diptera learn cues associated with hosts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Host system:** The [[Cactus-Yeast-Drosophila Model System]] links *Opuntia* cacti, their necrotic yeast and bacterial communities, and associated cactophilic Drosophila. Field-collected *Opuntia stricta* cladodes were used to prepare experimental media.
- **Microbial community:** Each yeast was combined with a bacterial community of six strains, including *Xanthomonas* sp. and *Micrococcus kristinae*, to approximate natural rots.
- **Population sources:** Inbred lines came from four Australian populations: Masthead Island, Hemmant, Metz Gorge, and O'Hara. All were maintained on autoclaved sucrose-yeast-cactus medium seeded with [[Saccharomyces Cerevisiae]].
- **Experimental setting:** Tests ran at 25 °C in dark incubators. Oviposition chambers used 15-cm petri dishes with filter paper moistened with sterile water.

## Key Findings

**Experiment 1: Yeast choice across inbred lines**
- Twenty-four inbred lines (six from each of four localities) were tested in four replicates.
- The mean proportion of eggs laid on *C. sonorensis* ranged from 0.376 to 0.728, with an overall mean of 0.567.
- ANOVA showed a significant effect of line within locality (P < 0.05). The locality effect was not significant.
- Four lines were selected for later experiments: O'Hara 5 (high, 0.728), Masthead 3 (low, 0.450), and Masthead 4 and O'Hara 6 (intermediate, 0.611 and 0.609).

**Experiment 2: Starved females, single-day choice**
- Fifteen females per line × treatment combination were tested individually. Females were treated with bacteria only, *C. sonorensis* plus bacteria, or *Cl. opuntiae* plus bacteria.
- Starvation occurred because the treatment medium supported sparse microbial growth.
- Between 9 and 15 females per combination laid eggs (average 13.4).
- Mean proportions on *C. sonorensis* were 0.533 (bacteria only), 0.509 (*C. sonorensis* experience), and 0.523 (*Cl. opuntiae* experience). ANOVA found no significant effects of line, treatment, or their interaction.
- Excluding females that laid fewer than 10 eggs (average 11.4 females per combination) did not change the results.
- Females laid an average of 22.3 eggs each.

**Experiment 3: Well-fed females, two-day choice**
- The treatment medium supported luxuriant growth, and flies were scored on Day 1 and Day 2.
- Females given bacteria only laid significantly fewer eggs on both days.
- Mean egg counts were 40.4 per female on Day 1 and 31.9 on Day 2.
- ANOVA showed no significant effects of experience on either day.
- Regression of Day 2 proportions on Day 1 proportions gave a pooled coefficient of 0.189 (P = 0.069) and a pooled correlation of 0.406.

**Experiment 4: Y-tube olfactometer attraction**
- Flies were conditioned as in Experiment 3, then placed in a Y-tube olfactometer with *C. sonorensis* and *Cl. opuntiae* traps. Each tube held 45 flies of one sex from one line.
- Twenty-four Y-tubes were run (four lines × two sexes × three replicates).
- The overall mean proportion choosing *C. sonorensis* was 0.532 (SE 0.025). Line averages ranged from 0.485 to 0.628.
- Responding flies averaged 12.4 males and 14.1 females.
- ANOVA showed no significant effects of experience.

**Interpretation and conclusions**
- Across all experiments, the authors found no consistent effect of experience. Some lines showed the expected positive pattern, but others showed reversals.
- Line rankings for preference changed between experiments. This was most marked for O'Hara 5, the line chosen for its high initial preference. The authors suggest that prior maintenance on *S. cerevisiae* and differing starvation status may affect choice.
- Proposed explanations include: (1) flies could not differentiate the yeasts, which the authors reject given significant among-line variation and earlier work on yeast preference (Vacek et al., 1985); (2) the test system missed a transient experience effect; (3) bacterial flora, rather than yeasts, may provide the cues learned in nature; and (4) *D. buzzatii* may lack the ability to use past experience to choose yeasts.
- The authors conclude that experience is not a universal factor and may not relax the conditions for balanced polymorphism in all species.
