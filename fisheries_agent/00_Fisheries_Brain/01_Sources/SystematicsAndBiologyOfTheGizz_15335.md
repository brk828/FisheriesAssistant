# Systematics and Biology of the Gizzard Shad (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) and Related Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Fishery Bulletin (No. 173, Volume 60, 1960) by [[Robert Rush Miller]] reviews the systematics and biology of the gizzard shads, marine and freshwater herrings of the subfamily [[Dorosomatinae]]. It provides keys to the seven known genera and to the five species of the American genus [[Dorosoma]], and treats the [[Eastern Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) in detail. The biological summary draws largely on a critical survey of published and unpublished literature. It is intended to help inland-water fishery biologists weigh the species' dual role as a valuable forage fish and, in some waters, a nuisance that requires costly control.

## Background & Description

- **Classification:** Gizzard shads were first recognized as Chatoessina by Günther (1868), raised to family rank as Dorosomidae by Gill (1872), and later treated mostly as a subfamily of Clupeidae. Regan (1917) placed the group in Clupeinae; Miller considers Old and New World relationships tentative.
- **Genera:** Seven genera are recognized. Only *Dorosoma* (including subgenus *Signalosa*) occurs in the New World; the others occur in Asia and the Indo-Australian region. [[Anodontostoma]] and [[Gonialosa]] lack the filamentous last dorsal ray.
- **Diagnostic traits:** Inferior, subterminal or terminal toothless mouth (adults); gizzardlike muscular stomach; very long, convoluted intestine with numerous pyloric caeca; cycloid scales; bony abdominal scutes; fine, numerous gill rakers used for filter feeding; and a prolonged last dorsal ray in the adult.
- **Species of *Dorosoma*:** Five are recognized: four in subgenus *Dorosoma* (*D. cepedianum*, *D. anale*, *D. chavesi*, *D. smithi*) and one, *D. petenense* (threadfin shad), in subgenus *Signalosa*. Mouth position and scale counts are the most reliable distinguishing characters.
- **Morphology of *D. cepedianum*:** Lateral scales 52–70 (mean 61.06); scales around body 36–45; vertebrae 48–51 (mean 49.83); dorsal rays 10–18; anal rays 25–36. Body depth 2.3–3.1 in standard length. The dorsal filament is short in young fish, lengthens to about 200 mm, then becomes relatively shorter.
- **Subspecies:** Three nominal subspecies were once recognized (*cepedianum*, *heterurum*, *exile*). Miller finds no reliable basis for them, and no subspecies are recognized.
- **Color:** Silvery bluish back, milky white abdomen, six to eight dark horizontal stripes on the upper sides, and a large purple shoulder spot that is prominent in young and half-grown fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Range:** Found from southeastern South Dakota, central Minnesota, the Great Lakes drainage, and extreme southern New York, south through the Mississippi system and along the Atlantic slope to the Gulf coast and the Rio Panuco basin. Low temperatures limit its distribution, which excludes upper Lake Huron, Lake Michigan, and Lake Superior.
- **Habitat:** Inhabits large rivers, reservoirs, lakes, swamps, estuaries, and quiet open waters. It is essentially an open-water species, usually near the surface, and tolerates temperatures up to about 85 °C in the cited study.
- **Salinity:** Along the Texas coast, individuals were found in waters of 2.0 to 33.7 ppt salinity; the smallest fish occurred in the freshest water.
- **Spawning:** Occurs in fresh water from mid-March to late August, mostly in April–June at about 50–70 °F. Eggs are sticky, demersal, and may smother aquatic vegetation. The fertilized egg measures about 0.75 mm.
- **Development:** Hatching takes about 95 hours at 62 °F (about 36 hours at 80 °F). Larvae undergo alternate sinking and rising behavior for their first two days.
- **Growth and life span:** Matures usually in its second or third year and typically lives 5–7 years, with a maximum reported at 10 years. Largest known individual: 20.5 inches, 3 lb 7 oz. Typical adults are 10–14 inches; 10–13 inches weigh about 1 lb. Most annual growth (70–80%) occurs in June–August or July–September.
- **Diet:** Almost entirely herbivorous after the first few weeks, feeding on phytoplankton and algae (Tiffany identified 150 species and varieties of nonfilamentous algae). Early larvae eat protozoa and entomostraca such as *Bosmina* and copepods. Mud and sand may be ingested, with sand aiding gizzard maceration.
- **Predators:** At least 17 important game fishes feed on it, mostly on young-of-the-year 2–5 inches long. Examples include [[Largemouth Bass]], [[White Bass]], [[Walleye]], crappies, channel catfish, and freshwater drum. Waterfowl also depend on shad die-offs and young shad.
- **Parasites:** Generally free of parasites, which is attributed to its herbivorous diet. Heavy infestations of an encysting sporozoan occur in young fish at Buckeye Lake, Ohio.
- **Mortality:** Winter die-offs are common. The largest documented event was more than 1,000 tons removed from Lake Erie at Erie, Pennsylvania, in January–February 1953, linked to abrupt warming in Presque Isle Bay's West Basin (55–60 °F versus 34–35 °F in the East Basin). Mechanical deaths also occurred in an intake pipe at Detroit in 1952.

## Key Findings

- **Dual role in fisheries:** The gizzard shad is an important forage fish: it converts phytoplankton directly into fish biomass, reproduces prolifically, is largely parasite-free, and grows rapidly. Yet it often overpopulates warm, shallow, turbid, mud-bottomed lakes with few predators, suppressing game fish.
- **Overpopulation cases:**
  - *Black Hawk Lake, Iowa*: Shad suppressed game fish; after intensive netting (1951–57), the shad share of catch fell from 98.1% to 2.8%, while carp rose from 1.8% to 96.3%.
  - *Clearwater Lake, Missouri*: Shad rose from 2.0% (1949) to 57.8% (1950) of the forage fish.
  - *Lake Apopka, Florida*: Shad exceeded 80% of the fish population; about 3.5 million lb were killed with rotenone in 1957.
  - *Carpenter Lake, Kentucky*: 285 lb per acre were killed in 1954, against an anticipated 50 lb.
- **Oklahoma comparison:** A lake with shad had a standing crop of 1,043 lb per acre (466 lb desirable size) versus 655 lb (608 lb desirable) in a lake without shad. Sunfish condition was below the state average where shad were present, indicating competition.
- **Ideal conditions for forage use:** Deep, clear fluctuating impoundments (e.g., Norris Reservoir, Tennessee) with abrupt shorelines, little littoral vegetation, and adequate predators make shad a valuable forage base.
- **Utilization:** Rarely eaten by humans because of soft flesh and many fine bones. Uses include hog feed, fertilizer, guano, fur-animal feed (with thiaminase concerns; about 15% protein and 12% fat), and occasional bait. Chesapeake Bay catch in 1920 was 72,852 lb worth $2,013. Great Lakes commercial catch rose erratically (126,415 lb total, 1939–57, excluding Lake Superior).
- **Management implications:** Miller advises avoiding introduction of shad into shallow, warm, turbid waters where game-fish production is a priority, and notes that the species can be very valuable where it is part of the native fauna alongside predators.
