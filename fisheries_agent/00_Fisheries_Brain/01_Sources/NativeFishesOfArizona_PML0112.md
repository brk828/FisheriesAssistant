# Native Fishes of Arizona: Species Accounts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This document compiles species accounts for Arizona's native fishes, covering twelve species from the [[Gila River Basin]], [[Colorado River Basin]] and adjacent Sonoran and New Mexico drainages. Each account summarizes description, distribution, habitat, food, reproduction and growth, and management implications. The cover sheet presents a status checklist of roughly 40 native species, with federal and state listings ranging from Extinct and Endangered to Category 2 candidates. The common thread across all accounts is decline: dams, flow alteration, groundwater pumping, habitat degradation and especially [[Nonnative Fishes]] have reduced most species to a small fraction of their historic ranges.

## Background & Description

The accounts draw on a standard set of references, including [[Lee et al. 1980]] (Atlas of North American Freshwater Fishes), [[Minckley 1973]] (Fishes of Arizona), [[Minckley and Deacon 1991]] (Battle Against Extinction) and [[Rinne and Minckley 1991]] (Native Fishes of Arid Lands). Most accounts also cite [[Sublette et al. 1990]] (The Fishes of New Mexico) and [[Rinne 1992]] on physical habitat use in a Sonoran Desert stream.

Key traits:
- **Cyprinids (minnows and dace)** are mostly small, with the exception of the large Colorado Squawfish.
- **Catostomids (suckers)** have ventral mouths. Desert Sucker and Sonora Sucker are primarily herbivorous or omnivorous; Razorback Sucker feeds from the bottom with a subterminal mouth.
- **Salmonids** (Gila Trout) are cold-water specialists of small headwater streams.
- **Poeciliids** (Gila Topminnow) are livebearers that resemble [[Mosquitofish]], a nonnative competitor.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat preferences vary widely:
- **Shallow, sandy desert streams**: Longfin Dace tolerates warm water and moves rapidly with precipitation-driven flow. Its preferred habitat has actually expanded with erosion-driven sand deposition.
- **Pools, cienegas and quiet waters**: Gila Chub is highly secretive and associated with cienegas and marshlands. Gila Topminnow prefers shallow, warm, quiet water with dense algae and debris.
- **Mid-elevation rivers and pools**: Roundtail Chub uses pools near swifter riffles, with overhead cover such as boulders and undercut banks. Speckled Dace is most abundant in swift, pool-and-riffle creeks between 5,000 and 10,000 feet.
- **Riffles and fast water**: Spikedace occupies midwater habitats of runs and eddies. Loach Minnow is restricted almost exclusively to cobble riffles, typically 6 to 7 inches deep at 2 to 2.5 feet per second.
- **Large, deep rivers**: Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker require large channels and flooded bottomlands, and Razorback Sucker historically used strong currents over sand.
- **Cold, clear headwaters**: Gila Trout occupies streams where water temperatures seldom exceed 70°F, with boulder pools and stable, vegetated banks.

Thermal tolerances are also varied. Desert Sucker tolerates temperatures up to 90°F, and Speckled Dace can acclimate to 98°F but prefers about 60°F. Longfin Dace adults congregate in shade when water exceeds 75°F.

Diet ranges from insectivory (Spikedace, Loach Minnow, Speckled Dace) to algal grazing (Desert Sucker, Longfin Dace when mayflies are scarce), to piscivory in large adults (Colorado Squawfish). Gila Chub feeds on both terrestrial and aquatic invertebrates, small fishes, algae and debris.

Major threats recur across the accounts:
- **Impoundment and flow alteration** from [[Reservoirs]] and dams, which dewater, cool or inundate habitat.
- **Groundwater pumping and lowered water tables**, which drain cienegas and streams.
- **Watershed erosion and sedimentation**, which fills riffles and pools (Speckled Dace, Sonora Sucker, Spikedace).
- **Nonnative predators and competitors**, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and Mosquitofish.
- **Grazing, riparian degradation and loss of large old trees**, which reduce cover and bank stability.

## Key Findings

**Longfin Dace** (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- Native to the Yaqui, Magdalena, Sonoyta, Gila and Bill Williams drainages; introduced to the Virgin and Mimbres rivers.
- Spawns December through July, perhaps into September. Nests can number up to 20 per square yard; incubation takes about 4 days above 75°F.
- Lifespan is rarely longer than three years. Thermal mortality has rarely been observed.
- The [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] recommended Category 2 status.

**Gila Chub** (*Gila intermedia*)
- Once widespread in the headwaters of the major Gila tributaries. Rediscovered in the San Pedro drainage in Sonora, where it had not been collected since 1857.
- Extirpated in New Mexico. Populations were rapidly eliminated after largemouth bass were stocked.
- Reintroduction into suitable habitat is identified as an appropriate management action.

**Roundtail Chub** (*Gila robusta*)
- Still occurs in the Verde and Salt rivers, but populations have declined considerably. It has disappeared from the San Francisco River and from the Gila River below Redrock.
- Typical maximum size is 10 to 16 inches; individuals up to 20 inches may occur.
- Predation by flathead catfish and smallmouth bass is implicated in population losses. Adults are a sport fish.

**Spikedace** (*Meda fulgida*)
- Restricted to less than 6% of its historic range. In Arizona it occurs in Aravaipa and Eagle creeks and the upper Verde River between Sullivan Lake and Tapco; in New Mexico, the mainstem Gila above Redrock and the East, Middle and West forks of the Gila.
- Breeds April to June. Females produce 90 to 150 ova; first-year fish average 1.5 inches and second-year fish 2.5 inches.
- Status: Arizona Threatened; New Mexico Endangered.

**Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- Once so abundant that hundreds were pitchforked from irrigation ditches before about 1911. Now occupies only about 25% of its former range.
- Last adult in the Gila system was captured at Roosevelt in 1937.
- Spawns early July to mid-August; survival is highest at 68°F. Barrier dams block many spawning areas.
- Hatchery reintroductions since 1985 into the Salt and Verde systems are designated "experimental, nonessential."

**Speckled Dace** (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- The most ubiquitous freshwater fish in the western United States. Extirpated from the San Pedro River but persists in Aravaipa Creek.
- Peak abundance occurs at 5,000 to 10,000 feet; preferred temperature is about 60°F.
- Spawning occurs in spring and late summer; eggs hatch in six days at 65°F. Feeding peaks between 9 P.M. and 1 A.M.
- Populations can crash in drought and rebound strongly after high runoff.

**Loach Minnow** (*Tiaroga cobitis*)
- Present range represents about 15% of its former range. Not collected on the Prescott National Forest since the early 1900s.
- Spawns March to June on the underside of cobble. Females carry 150 to 250 ova; eggs incubate 5 to 6 days.
- Longevity is typically 15 to 24 months, rarely 36. Dams and reservoirs eliminate it for many miles.

**Desert Sucker** (*Catostomus clarki*)
- Occurs in the Bill Williams, Salt, Gila, San Francisco and Verde drainages at roughly 1,000 to 6,000 feet.
- Adults are herbivorous, scraping algae; juveniles feed on midge larvae. Tolerates high temperatures and possibly low oxygen.
- Category 2 species, per the Desert Fishes Recovery Team.

**Sonora Sucker** (*Catostomus insignis*)
- Widely distributed between 1,000 and 6,500 feet in the Gila, Verde, Bill Williams and San Francisco basins. Extirpated from the San Pedro and Santa Cruz except Aravaipa Creek.
- Spawns February to July. Intolerant of reservoir conditions.
- Used historically as food and remains a sport fish. Recommended as Category 2.

**Gila Trout** (*Oncorhynchus gilae*)
- Four relict and six reestablished populations remain in the Gila, San Francisco and Mimbres drainages in New Mexico. The Gap Creek, Arizona population existed until 1990.
- Spawns April to June when water exceeds about 45°F; females produce 75 to 150 eggs.
- Declined through introduction of brown and rainbow trout and riparian disturbance. Highly vulnerable to wildfire and floods.
- Downlisting to Threatened is contingent on reestablishment in Main Diamond Creek, which is currently barren after the Divide fire.

**Gila Topminnow** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*)
- Once among the most common fish in southern Arizona in the 1940s. Now gone from all riverine habitats; remains in eight natural sites (two on public lands) and some transplanted sites.
- Tolerates pH 6.6 to 8.9, dissolved oxygen 2.2 to 11 ppm, and salinities from fresh to seawater. Gestation is 24 to 28 days; lifespan about one year.
- Mosquitofish, introduced to Arizona in 1926, can eliminate a population within a few years. In 1982 to 1983, 24 sites on the Prescott National Forest were stocked; only two remained by August 1991.
- Recovery plan under revision. Unlikely to be delisted, but could be downlisted to Threatened.

**Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- Formerly abundant across the Colorado basin. Largest remaining population is in [[Lake Mohave]], with smaller populations in the Green River and upper Colorado subbasin.
- Females produce about 75,000 to 144,000 eggs; no nest or parental care. Fish up to 44 years old have been collected.
- No evidence of successful recruitment into the Lake Mohave population for more than two decades.
- Since 1910, 15 dams have been built on the lower Colorado and its major tributaries. About 40 nonnative species are established in the Colorado River.
- More than 13 million hatchery fish were stocked over the past decade with scarce recapture; survival appears dependent on establishing populations in predator-free areas.

**Cross-cutting management conclusions**
- Protecting remaining unaltered reaches (upper Verde, Salt and their tributaries) is vital.
- Nonnative fish removal and predator-free refugia are repeatedly recommended.
- Surveys and monitoring are needed for several Category 2 species.
- Resource activities that affect water quality, water levels and riparian cover should be avoided or corrected.
