# Intraspecific Macroevolution: Functional Biology of the Polymorphic Cichlid *Cichlasoma minckleyi*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter (Liem and Kaufman) examines [[Cichlasoma Minckleyi]], a freshwater cichlid whose two trophic morphs, the papilliform and the molariform, differ in pharyngeal jaw structure, head width, gut length and branchial musculature to an extent that exceeds that seen among many sympatric cichlid species. The authors compare both morphs with [[Cichlasoma Cyanoguttatum]], which they treat as the primitive sister group, using high-speed cinematography, electromyography and laboratory feeding trials. Prey capture and mastication patterns are identical across the three forms, so functional shifts in the molariform morph arise from morphological change rather than altered motor output. Feeding preferences diverge only under low food abundance, when the molariform morph switches to snails. The authors interpret the polymorphism as an incipient intraspecific macroevolutionary transition.

## Background & Description

The chapter opens with the recognition, supported by [[Kornfield Et Al 1982]] and [[Kornfield And Taylor 1983]], that *C. minckleyi* is a distinctly polymorphic species. Earlier workers had described the two morphs as separate species. The authors frame the system around four evolutionary implications: a possible link to macroevolution and species flocks, a test of simple epigenetic mechanisms producing rapid structural change, a model of incipient functional differentiation within a species, and a case of ecological tracking of trophic resources by a polymorphic species.

**Morphological differences (molariform vs. papilliform):**
- Lower pharyngeal jaw: more delicate, narrower and with smaller horns in the papilliform morph.
- Gut: significantly shorter in the molariform morph.
- Upper pharyngeal jaw: more massive and expanded posteriorly and laterally in the molariform morph.
- Head width: significantly greater in the molariform morph.
- Fourth epibranchial bone: enlarged with a concave posterior outline in the molariform morph.
- Branchial musculature: levator externus 4, levator posterior and retractor dorsalis show a large increase in cross-sectional area and complex tendinous architecture in the molariform morph. Levatores interni 1 and 2 are drastically reduced.
- Ventral branchial musculature is identical between the morphs.

Specimens for the morphological figures measured 6.9 to 7.4 cm standard length. Partial molarization of the pharyngeal apparatus occurs sporadically in other populations, but without the full set of molariform specializations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Feeding repertoire.** Four prey-capture patterns were recorded in all three forms: horizontal suction (most frequent, midwater and surface prey), downwards-directed suction (bottom feeding on tubifex worms, chironomid larvae and snails), high-velocity suction (pursuit of agile fish, feeding cycle under 50 msec) and manipulating (dislodging sessile prey such as snails).

**Mastication.** A stereotypic crushing cycle, governed by an apparent pharyngeal jaw pattern generator, was used for soft, tough and hard prey alike. Electromyography showed that the levator posterior and levator externus 4 dominate the activity patterns, and that the molariform morph lacks the shearing phase typical of other cichlids.

**Field ecology.** [[Smith 1982]], working at Cuatro Cienegas, Coahuila, reported a lack of dietary specialization when food was presumably most abundant, ecological differences smaller than trophic morphology would predict, and snails acting as a competitive refugium for molariform fish when food was scarce.

**Comparative taxa.** Molariform specializations are nearly identical in several African mollusc-crushing cichlids, including Lamprologus tretocephalus, Tylochromis polylepis, Astatoreochromis vanderhorsti, Astatoreochromis alluaudi, Cyrtocara placodon and several Labrochromis species.

## Key Findings

1. **Feeding kinematics are conserved.** The papilliform and molariform morphs and *C. cyanoguttatum* showed no major differences in prey-capture or mastication kinematics or electromyographic patterns.

2. **Feeding preferences diverge only under scarcity.** Under abundant food, no significant between-morph differences appeared in paired-food or five-food trials (for example, five-food, both morphs, abundant food: between-morph chi square = 22.23, p = .33). Under low food, between-morph heterogeneity was highly significant (chi square = 54.83, p < .001). The papilliform morph took more tubifex and flakes, while the molariform morph concentrated on snails.

3. **Snails are the least preferred food under abundance.** When one morph was offered five food types in abundance, snails were the least desirable for both morphs (Table 1.1 totals: 37 of 475 papilliform and 41 of 438 molariform).

4. **Snail-eating pattern under low food.** When choosing between snails and shrimp at low abundance, the papilliform morph fed on shrimp while the molariform morph switched to snails (chi square = 23.56; p < .005).

5. **Morphology drives function.** The authors conclude that specialization in the pharyngeal mill proceeds through morphological variation, because neuromuscular stereotypy constrains motor output. The shift to a purely crushing apparatus is achieved by structural changes rather than new motor patterns.

6. **Developmental interplay.** The molariform morph is hypothesized to arise from genetically induced hypertrophy of the pharyngeal jaws, triggering correlated changes in head width and musculature. Pharyngeal specialization occurs first in ontogeny, followed by changes in surrounding structures.

7. **Polymorphism as incipient macroevolution.** The authors interpret the molariform morph as a specialized daughter form. The polymorphism shows no assortative mating (Kornfield et al. 1982), and the authors propose that trophically based habitat differences may arise during ecological bottlenecks.

8. **Community responsiveness.** The authors argue, citing Kornfield et al. (1982), that communities of polymorphic species may respond more strongly to environmental and biotic perturbations than communities of conventional species. The capacity to exploit secondary foods such as snails during scarcity is presented as insuring the survival of the polymorphic species.

9. **Competing origin hypotheses.** The authors favour divergence from a recessive molariform allele over introgression or hybridization, although a hybridization origin is not ruled out given the molarization seen in *C. regani* and *C. labridens*.
