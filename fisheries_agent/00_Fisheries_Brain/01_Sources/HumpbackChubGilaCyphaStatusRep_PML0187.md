# Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*) Species Status Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This status report, prepared as a species account for [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), assesses a fish listed as **Endangered** and described as the most critically endangered species of the Colorado River drainage. The document is undated in the OCR text (the preparation date appears as "May 19-?3"). Its central conclusions are that pure *G. cypha* has virtually disappeared from its former range, that taxonomic identification is complicated by [[Hybridization]] with the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*), and that [[Dam Construction]] and environmental modification of the Colorado River are the principal causes of decline. Recovery work is judged nearly impossible until a pure or near-pure remnant population is located.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic identity.** The species belongs to the order Cypriniformes and family Cyprinidae. It was described by [[Miller 1946]] from a single specimen caught in Grand Canyon, Arizona. Its validity was later reconsidered by [[Holden and Stalnaker]] (1970), who confirmed that *G. robusta* and *G. elegans* are two valid species (previously treated as subspecies of *G. robusta*), but found that *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* lack clear-cut separation.

**Diagnostic characters.** The traditional diagnostic trait is a pronounced dorsal hump. Holden (1968) and Holden and Stalnaker (1970) showed this is not reliable on its own. Relative to *G. elegans*, *G. cypha* typically has:
- the most pronounced, abrupt hump;
- a fleshy snout projecting well beyond the jaw;
- a thicker caudal peduncle;
- a longer upper jaw.

Because most recent specimens appear intermediate between the two species, recognizing pure *G. cypha* is "somewhat problematical."

**Life history.** Food, feeding, reproduction and interactions with other species are all recorded as unknown. The species was surmised to inhabit large river environments of canyon areas with strong flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical range.** Probably limited to large river and canyon areas of the Colorado basin, from Grand Canyon, Arizona, to the Green River in Wyoming (Flaming Gorge area).

**Present status.**
- Holden's and Stalnaker's collections in the Yampa and Green Rivers yielded very few specimens; the authors stated that "little can be said about it" and that its presence in the Yampa River is "definitely important."
- The species has been eliminated from the upper Green River by [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].
- Collections from [[Lake Powell]] in 1966 were intermediate between *G. cypha* and *G. elegans*. No *Gila* chubs were taken there after 1970 (Steve Gloss, Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, personal communication).
- Holden's samplings below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1970–72 found no humpback chub.

**Cause of decline.** Holden attributed the loss to the altered environment below Glen Canyon Dam, which has colder, clearer water and 5–6 ft daily fluctuations. He concluded that this has produced an essentially sterile river in Grand Canyon by eliminating productive backwater nursery habitat. The "big river" fishes *G. cypha*, *G. elegans* and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) have also been eliminated from that reach.

**Observations below Glen Canyon Dam.** Joe Stone (Arizona Game and Fish Department) studied the area from 1964 to 1968 and agreed that the altered environment had doomed the humpback chub and squawfish there. He nevertheless recorded the humpback chub (or a *G. cypha* × *G. elegans* hybrid) as relatively abundant during his study, and noted an apparent upstream movement from Grand Canyon toward Glen Canyon Dam in October–November.

**Expedition data.** James Deacon (University of Nevada, Las Vegas) reported intensive sampling in Grand Canyon in 1968 using electric shockers, nets and seines. It yielded **4 humpback chub** (2 adults and 2 small specimens).

**Ownership.** The lower Yampa River lies within Dinosaur National Monument. A few *G. cypha* may persist there and possibly in Grand Canyon National Park.

**Competition and threats.** As with other "big river" Colorado endemics, water development and environmental alteration drove rapid decline. These changes appear to have triggered hybridization with *G. elegans*, itself endangered, which accelerated the loss of pure individuals.

## Key Findings

- **Rarity:** Pure *G. cypha* is "virtually gone" from its former range. Holden and Stalnaker's most recent collections yielded very few individuals, and no chubs were taken from Lake Powell after 1970.
- **Taxonomic uncertainty:** The species is difficult to diagnose because specimens may reflect introgressive hybridization with *G. elegans*. The most likely explanation is that *G. cypha* was originally restricted to the more torrential canyon reaches of the Colorado and Green Rivers, and that dam construction and environmental change stimulated hybridization.
- **Breeding:** Whether a breeding population persists in Grand Canyon "appears dim."
- **Habitat loss:** Glen Canyon Dam's colder, clearer, fluctuating flows are judged to have eliminated nursery backwaters, producing an essentially sterile reach.
- **Sampling effort:** The 1968 expedition recovered only 4 individuals, and no 1970–72 sampling below Glen Canyon Dam found any.
- **Management recommendation:** Further sampling in Grand Canyon or above Lake Powell may locate a remnant population. Any population found must first be tested for hybridization with *G. elegans*. If a pure or virtually pure population is found, research on life history, feeding, reproduction and especially artificial propagation could proceed.
- **Authorities consulted:** Paul Holden and Clare Stalnaker (Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit, Utah State University); R. R. Miller (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology); and Joseph Stone (Arizona Game and Fish).
- **Conclusion:** The authors conclude that the species "seems doomed to extinction."
