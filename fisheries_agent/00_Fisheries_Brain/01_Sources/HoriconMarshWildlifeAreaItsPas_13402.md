# Horicon Marsh Wildlife Area: Its Past, Its Present, Its Future

#freshwater-ecology #waterfowl #wildlife-management

## Executive Summary

This 1952 Wisconsin Conservation Department publication (Pub. 350-52, July 1952, manager W. C. Truax) traces the history, condition and management outlook of [[Horicon Marsh]], a large shallow freshwater wetland in Dodge County, Wisconsin. It describes how the marsh was repeatedly altered by dams, market hunting, drainage and eventual public restoration, then reports its mid-century state and sets out a management philosophy centred on water-level control, controlled burning, muskrat and carp harvest, and applied research on [[Waterfowl]] and [[Muskrat]] populations.

The document is largely narrative rather than a research paper. Its most concrete content concerns harvest figures, acreage, and the ecological consequences of the ditching and water-level manipulation.

## Background & Description

**Setting and geology.** The marsh lies in north-central Dodge County, about 14 miles long and 3 to 4 miles wide, in the upper reaches of the [[Rock River]]. It was formed by the Green Bay lobe of the [[Wisconsin Glacier]] in Pleistocene time. Underlying geology includes Cambrian sandstone and Lower Magnesium limestone, and the region is dotted with glacial drumlins that support a gravel-washing industry. The glacial scour once produced a lake of about 51 square miles, which became a marsh after moraine scouring and alluvial deposition.

**Cultural history.** Over 400 Indian mounds were reported on the marsh periphery, indicating a long history of hunting and fishing use centred on wild rice, fish and game. Early settlers built a brush, log and mud dam in 1845-46, which held back nine feet of water and created a lake noted for [[Bass]], [[Northern Pike]], pickerel, bullheads and [[Muskellunge]]. Some 140 tons of pickerel were shipped to eastern markets in 1856.

**Dam, lawsuits and drainage.** Water-damage suits by neighbouring farmers led the Wisconsin Supreme Court to uphold their claims in 1861, and the dam was removed in 1869. Land speculators later bought about 20,000 acres for six to ten cents per acre. Hunting clubs leased the marsh in the 1870s at $100 per year, and market hunting shipped barrels of waterfowl to Milwaukee, where ducks sold for 10 cents each. A drainage district petition was filed in 1904, and ditching began in 1909 despite a court ruling that the river and bays were navigable. The Main Ditch and several laterals were completed around 1914-18, and reclaimed land sold for up to $400 per acre.

**Restoration.** Conservation advocates began a campaign in 1921. Over 115,000 names were reportedly gathered in support, and state legislation appropriated $250,000 for land acquisition and $10,000 for construction. Public ownership eventually reached nearly 30,000 acres of marsh and marsh edge, held by state and federal governments under the [[Pittman-Robertson Act]] framework. Federal dike and dam development later extended the project.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Present condition (c. 1952).** The state-owned portion covers over 10,000 acres, of which only about 6,000 acres in the marsh centre are considered productive. Mean water depth in the productive zone is about two feet, ranging from a few inches at the edges to three feet in deeper areas. Open water is under five per cent of the state area. Vegetation includes [[Cattail]], bur reed, river bulrush, coontail and encroaching scrub willow, with submerged plants often choking channels.

**Drainage legacy.** The [[Main Ditch]] is about six feet deep and 40 feet wide, with four lateral ditches. Cottonwoods lining the banks, roughly 25 to 30 years old at the time, mark the dredging era. The old river channel had become secondary in importance to the ditch.

**Water-level management.** A planned federal dike and dam near the State-Federal line was expected to raise the water level to 77 feet, flooding about 7,000 acres, or over one-third of the marsh's roughly 20,000-acre total.

**Wildlife.** Breeding and resident species include [[Black-Crowned Night Heron]] at the rookery on Four Mile Island, [[Great Blue Heron]], [[American Bittern]], [[Least Bittern]], [[Black Tern]], coots, gallinules, red-winged and yellow-headed blackbirds, mallards, blue-winged teal and black ducks. Migrant [[Canada Goose]] numbers were significant, with about 25,000 present in spring before northward migration and more than 40,000 reported in 1949. The marsh is also noted for its [[Muskrat]] and [[Mink]] populations.

**Fur harvest.** Trapping was permitted under permit only, with each trapper allowed 125 traps. Muskrat takes were reported as follows: about 9,000 (1943), 4,200 (1944), 1,016 (1945), 8,243 (1946), 9,535 (1947), 24,654 (1948) and nearly 30,000 (1949). Half of all pelts became state property.

**Fish.** The [[Common Carp]] was a management target, removed periodically through an in-house trap at the "Y" and by ice-season netting, because carp disturbed bottom sediments and submerged plant beds.

## Key Findings

1. **Historic productivity was high but probably periodic.** Old-timer accounts claim the marsh once produced about half a million ducks a year, hundreds of geese, 50,000 or more muskrats and tons of northern pike. The author doubts these figures could be matched.

2. **Irreversible degradation.** Silting, pollution and watershed alteration cannot be undone. Management was therefore framed as slowing degradation through two approaches: halting adverse inputs (pollution abatement, erosion control with the Soil Conservation Service) and manipulating controllable factors on site.

3. **Water level is the primary lever.** Raising water could dilute pollution and offset silting and basin filling. Upcoming federal dam work was expected to change conditions substantially.

4. **Controlled burning and harvest.** Burning retards weed, grass and sedge build-up and can open holes in peat. Muskrat harvest and accelerated carp removal are intended to control marsh plants and aid bottom settling.

5. **Hunting regulation.** Limited seasons, refuges and bag limits were the main hunting controls, though their value was described as questionable. The stated aim is to protect breeding stocks of ducks, pheasants and upland game.

6. **Research integration.** A waterfowl research project studies duck ecology, censusing, banding and artificial propagation, while a fur research project studies muskrat tagging, disease, predators, food habits and reproduction. The author argues that research must inform management and that sportsmen need to accept those findings.

7. **Habitat restoration plan.** Recommended measures include auxiliary impoundments, ditching with drag-lines and dynamite, water control to reduce unproductive edges, strip cropping and contour plowing on surrounding uplands, and hedgerows and food patches for pheasants.

8. **Management philosophy.** The publication closes by stressing that progress will be gradual, that the aims are clear but the best procedures remain uncertain, and that competent, persistent staff are essential. It is framed within the [[Conservation Pledge]] of the Wisconsin Conservation Department.
