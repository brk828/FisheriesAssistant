# Monitoring the Effects of Introduced Flathead Catfish on Sport Fish Populations in the Altamaha River, Georgia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This conference paper by Matthew E. Thomas of the [[Georgia Department Of Natural Resources]] reports a standardized stream monitoring program run on the [[Altamaha River]] from 1987 to 1992 (electrofishing data analysed for 1988 to 1992). The study tracked the invasion of [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), which were introduced by an unauthorized stocking in the [[Ocmulgee River]] in 1973. Flathead catfish catch rates rose sharply, while native sport fish, notably [[Bullhead Catfish]] and [[Redbreast Sunfish]], declined markedly. The author concludes that the invasion and rapid expansion of flathead catfish have affected native sport fish populations, and that the declines are considered major.

## Background & Description

The Altamaha River is the largest inland fishery in southeast Georgia, supporting upwards of 60,000 sport fishing trips annually (Hottel et al. 1983). Flathead catfish were first reported in the early 1980s, when Michaels and Williamson (1982) captured two individuals. The species had moved about 180 river kilometres downstream from the Ocmulgee River stocking. Managers were concerned that this piscivorous predator could alter the resident sport fish community, a concern supported by earlier literature on its predatory habits (Hackney 1965; Minckley and Deacon 1959; Guier et al. 1981; Davis 1985).

Earlier surveys by Coomer and Holder (1980) and Hottel et al. (1983) provided baseline data, but their descriptive, one-time design could not reveal population trends. Quinn (1987) argued that regular sampling before and after establishment was needed to assess impacts. The present program was designed to build a database for detecting sport fish trends, and to justify future research and management needs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Ten stations were used, with two randomly chosen from each of five equal river sections, to avoid station clustering. Centrarchids were sampled each fall by boat-mounted [[Electrofishing]] over about one hour per station, moving upstream along outside bends. Ictalurids were sampled each summer under low flow using a boat-mounted Electrofisher Pulser Model 3-A ([[Mod-3A Electrofisher]]) with a chase boat. Indices included length-frequency distributions, [[Proportional Stock Density]] (PSD), [[Relative Stock Density]] (RSD), [[Relative Weight]] (Wr), relative composition, and mean length and weight. [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (CPUE) was compared between years and sites using [[Analysis Of Variance]], with P < 0.05 treated as significant. Because station standardization only began in 1988, comparisons cover 1988 to 1992. A non-uniform access [[Creel Survey]] ran annually from 1987 to 1992 to supplement electrofishing.

**Predator-prey context.** Flathead catfish are described as opportunistic feeders that select prey by abundance and ease of capture (Minckley and Deacon 1959; Turner and Summerfelt 1970; Quinn 1987). Sunfish were the dominant forage in [[Bluestone Reservoir]], West Virginia (Edmundson 1974), and sunfish and ictalurids were the most common prey families in the [[Cape Fear River]], North Carolina (Guier et al. 1981).

**Barriers and comparison sites.** Evans (1991) found bullheads at high catch rates above a dam that blocked flathead invasion, but almost none below it where flathead were present. Mickey and Simpson (1988) reported a similar pattern on the [[Yadkin River]], North Carolina. The redbreast decline is also documented below [[Juliette Dam]] on the Ocmulgee River (Evans 1991).

## Key Findings

**Flathead catfish expansion**
- A total of 3,043 flathead catfish were collected from 1988 to 1992, out of about 11,780 game fish and 5,000 angler interviews.
- Electrofishing CPUE rose from 20.7 fish/hr in 1988 to 84.0 fish/hr in 1992. The abstract instead reports a peak CPUE of 90 fish/hr in 1990. CPUE differed significantly (P < 0.05) between years and between sites.
- Weight per hour was about 10 times greater in 1992 (212 kg/hr) than in 1988 (20 kg/hr).
- The share of flathead catfish in ictalurid samples rose from 32% (1988) to 83% (1992).
- Mean total length rose from 334 mm (1988) to 515 mm (1992). PSD ranged 49.0–64.0 and RSD 11.6–18.2.
- The creel survey showed no discernible increase in flathead harvest from 1987 to 1992.
- The author notes that flathead catfish went from scarce in 1979–1982 to the dominant predator in mainstream habitat in about 10 years. A similar timeline occurred in the Cape Fear River (Guier et al. 1981).

**Bullhead decline**
- Only five bullheads were collected from 1988 to 1992. The highest CPUE was 0.3 fish/hr in 1990, and none were collected in 1991 or 1992.
- Estimated bullhead harvest fell from 38,342 (Hottel et al. 1983) to 15,500 in 1987, when bullheads made up 34% of the ictalurid harvest. By 1991–1992 they made up about 2%.
- The author attributes the absence to a real population collapse rather than gear failure, citing Evans (1991) and Mickey and Simpson (1988).

**Redbreast sunfish decline**
- Redbreast sunfish were the most abundant centrarchid in fall samples (N = 3,386). CPUE fell from 105 fish/hr (1988) to 32 fish/hr (1992), and relative composition fell from 59% to 35%.
- CPUE differed significantly between years but not between sites.
- PSD and RSD rose as CPUE fell, and PSD and RSD more than doubled over the study period. The inverse relationship between CPUE and PSD/RSD was significant (Probst 1991).
- Creel overall success fell from 0.594 fish/hr (1987) to 0.256 fish/hr (1992). Fished-for success fell from 3.715 to 1.257 fish/hr.
- Fish under 70 mm showed no major reproduction failure, but CPUE of 70–100 mm fish fell by more than 90%.
- Overharvest is judged unlikely as the main cause, because only 0.1% of Georgia sunfish anglers creel a daily limit (Hess 1991) and the CPUE–PSD/RSD relationship points elsewhere.

**Interpretation**
- The author attributes the redbreast decline mainly to the rise in flathead catfish, possibly through a diet shift after the bullhead forage base collapsed.
- In 1987, redbreast and bullheads together made up 45% of the sport fishery by number and 38% by weight. Redbreast harvest is now about half its 1987 value, and bullheads rarely appear in the creel.
- Flathead catfish remain a minor component of the sport fishery, but their abundance keeps rising. The author concludes that documented declines in native sport fish are major, and monitoring will continue to clarify the full impact.
