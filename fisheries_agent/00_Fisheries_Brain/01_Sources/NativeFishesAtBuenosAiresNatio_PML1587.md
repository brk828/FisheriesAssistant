# Native Fishes at Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge and Arizona State University Research Park

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper (Harsh, Arizona State University) reports the stocking and monitoring of three imperiled Colorado River fishes, [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], in two artificial systems in Arizona: the ponds of the [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] (NWR) and the lakes of the [[Arizona State University Research Park]] (ASU). The goal was to test whether these ponds could serve as grow-out space before fish are planted into the wild, and whether the native fishes could grow at ASU despite co-occurring with non-native species.

Survival and growth of bonytail and razorback sucker were excellent at both sites. Razorback suckers grew to 436 mm TL by autumn 1987. The Buenos Aires ponds were judged clearly suitable for rearing fish to substantial size. At ASU, growth showed no detectable suppression by non-native fishes, although the author notes that exotics were largely absent at stocking time. Colorado squawfish were rarely captured, probably because of sampling-gear selectivity, so their status remains unknown.

## Background & Description

The three species are endemic, big-river fishes of the Colorado River system in southwestern North America. All are imperiled because of habitat alteration and destruction and interactions with non-native fishes, and each is listed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and/or several southwestern states (Johnson 1987).

- **Bonytail:** Often regarded as the most critically endangered fish in the United States. Its recovery plan calls for holding all wild individuals collected in refugia. Reintroductions have occurred only in [[Lake Mohave]], to augment a remnant adult population.
- **Razorback sucker:** Adults remain abundant in Lake Mohave, but recruitment there is essentially nil, despite annual production of large numbers of larvae. Populations in the upper Colorado basin are small, and recruitment is low.
- **Colorado squawfish:** Extirpated from the lower Colorado basin but still persists in reproducing populations in the upper basin. Reintroductions to the Salt and Verde rivers in Arizona began in 1981 under nonessential experimental population status.

Earlier work by Harsh and Brooks (in review) and Brooks (unpublished) on post-stocking dispersal and predation found that: (1) fish generally dispersed downstream at night from stocking areas; (2) predation by non-native [[Ictalurid Catfish]] was a potentially significant source of mortality, at least for juvenile razorback suckers; and (3) recapture rates, and therefore apparent long-term survival, were low. Harsh and Brooks recommended that razorback suckers be reared to about two years of age before planting, when they would be less available to most predators. The author argues that bonytail and Colorado squawfish would also benefit from initial rearing to larger size.

Hatchery production had relied on the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service National Fish Hatchery in Dexter, New Mexico, with some batches moved to the Arizona Game and Fish Department Page Springs State Fish Hatchery. Space at both was limited, so additional grow-out facilities were sought. The Buenos Aires NWR ponds were identified as numerous, available and apparently suitable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites**

- **Buenos Aires NWR:** About 4,000 ha of grassland and rocky desert mountains in extreme southern Arizona, mostly in the headwaters of the north-flowing Altar Valley (Santa Cruz River system). Aquatic habitats are mainly rain-dependent, shallow, mud-bottomed cattle "tanks" on impounded washes, ranging from about 0.1 to 1.5 ha. Many are ephemeral; others hold water year-round. The only perennial stream, Arivaca Creek, was historically fishless.
- **ASU Research Park:** Three lakes (North, South and Middle) built in the early 1980s and filled in May 1985, interconnected by large pipes and continuously aerated. The grounds have no outlets and receive make-up water from the Salt River Project canal system.

**Non-native fishes**

Preliminary Buenos Aires surveys (1985 and 1986) found only non-native species, including fathead minnow, largemouth bass, yellow bullhead, black bullhead and mosquitofish, along with tiger salamanders and ranid frogs. No native fishes were collected.

At ASU, the lakes began fishless but were quickly invaded or stocked. [[Redbelly Tilapia]] were stocked in May 1986 to control algae and reproduced successfully. [[Red Shiner]] entered from the canals. Four tilapia taxa were recorded, the last three presumably contaminants of the initial stocking. Green sunfish, bluegill and mosquitofish were found in small numbers, and largemouth bass were reported by an unidentified local resident. Crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii*) also invaded.

## Key Findings

### Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge

- **Stocking (November 1986 to March 1987):** About 3,000 juvenile razorback suckers (mean 176 mm TL, 14 g) and bonytail (mean 76 mm TL, 6 g) were planted in fishless tanks, including Mormon Lake, King's, Big Bertha, Road Camp, Rock and Tequila tanks. A second razorback stocking of fry (about 11 mm) was made on 31 March 1987.
- **January–February 1987 (about 3 months post-stocking):** Sixteen razorback suckers from Mormon Lake averaged 166 mm TL (106–217 mm) and 6.5 g, all in good condition. Bonytail across tanks averaged 101 mm TL (63–178 mm) and 10 g (3–53 g), with no significant size differences among tanks.
- **February 1987 (about 4 months):** Seventeen razorback suckers averaged 173 mm TL and 58 g. Bonytail (n = 71) averaged 130 mm TL and 31 g.
- **August 1987:** Mormon Lake yielded 84 razorback suckers averaging 97 mm TL (71–133 mm) and 9 g, all from the second stocking. Trammel nets on 31 August–1 September captured seven razorbacks from the initial planting, confirming survival and substantial growth.
- **Road Camp Tank:** A single seine haul yielded 114 bonytail. They showed no appreciable growth since February.
- **King's and Big Bertha tanks:** Both had shrunk considerably, and no bonytail were found, so these stocks were considered extirpated. Possible causes include stress and mortality from deoxygenation (organic-rich substrates, dense phytoplankton and macroalgae, depths below 0.3 m), and predation by piscivorous birds and mammals as water levels fell.
- **Rock and Tequila tanks:** Each received about 3,000 razorback suckers in March. After five months, fish were robust and in excellent condition, averaging 113 mm (Rock) and 96 mm (Tequila).
- **October 1987 (about 6.5 months):** Bonytail in Road Camp Tank averaged 188 mm (145–285 mm, n = 23). Razorback suckers from Rock, Tequila and Mormon Lake averaged 157 mm (n = 163).

### ASU Research Park

- **Stocking (October–November 1986):** About 1,100 Colorado squawfish (about 76 mm TL), roughly 1,000 razorback suckers and 879 bonytail (76 mm TL) were planted. About half of each species went into Middle Lake, and the rest was split between North and South lakes.
- **Winter die-off (January 1987):** Thousands of tilapia were aggregated in sunlit shallows, and many hundreds of small fish were moribund. Many dead fish had been beheaded, presumably by crayfish.
- **February 1987:** One threadfin shad and one bonytail (about 130 mm, partly consumed by scavengers) were captured. Three decomposing razorback suckers were found in South Lake, with no other fish species found dead.
- **April 1987:** Trammel nets captured razorback suckers as the majority of 113 fish. Razorbacks measured 165–325 mm TL and averaged about 3xx g, and catch per unit effort (CPUE) for razorbacks ranged narrowly among lakes (about 7 to 9.3), suggesting similar abundances. All razorbacks were in excellent health and free of anchor worm (*Lernaea cyprinacea*), a common affliction in wild and captive stocks. Three bonytail (314 and 316 mm TL) also appeared in sound condition. Seine catches (overall mean CPUE of 3.6) were dominated by red shiner, followed by mosquitofish and tilapia. Colorado squawfish (n = 3) measured about 98 and 101 mm TL.
- **Species richness (April 1987):** 19 species were found. Only four were intentionally stocked (bonytail, Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, redbelly tilapia); the rest arrived as contaminants or invaders.
- **September 1987:** Trammel nets collected 11 species, including yellow bass and black crappie (the crappie had not been recorded from the system since 1969). Total CPUE was roughly double April's. Razorback suckers were the most abundant fish, with mean TL of about 363 mm (n = 60), and had grown remarkably since the previous sampling. Redbelly tilapia (the only cichlid collected) and bonytail made up small shares of the catch.
- **Bonytail (September 1987):** Nine bonytail averaged 381 mm TL (180–370 mm) and 155 g (n = 6, 88–345 g). They were free of external parasites and showed no sign of compromised health. Survival could not be reliably estimated because of gear avoidance and mesh selectivity.
- **Colorado squawfish:** Status remains unknown. Gear selectivity likely explains their scarcity: fish were too large and agile for seines yet too small to be caught in trammel nets. Electroshocking may be needed.

### Summary and Conclusions

- Survival and growth of bonytail and razorback sucker were excellent in both artificial systems. Growth met or exceeded that of wild Lake Mohave fish and hatchery stocks at Dexter, Page Springs and Niland state hatcheries (unpublished data).
- Health was excellent; anchor worm, a problem at some facilities, was not found on planted fish.
- The Buenos Aires stock tanks are clearly effective for grow-out. Fish reached substantial size in under a year, which should improve survival after reintroduction to natural habitats. Additional refuge waters were identified, some requiring reclamation to remove exotics. The author recommends stocking them with bonytail and razorback sucker as soon as fish are available, and detailing plans for later wild distribution.
- At ASU, native fishes grew remarkably despite co-occurrence with exotics that are often implicated in native declines. The author attributes this to planting fish beyond the range of available predators; larval stocking would likely have failed. Introduced fishes may still reduce recruitment through predation on larvae, but they showed little apparent effect on the growth of these two large species. Abundant larval cichlids and other fishes may serve as food.
- Both bonytail and razorback sucker were approaching sizes at which sexual maturity is reached. Controlled studies of exotic fish effects on native reproduction and recruitment were proposed as a future opportunity.
- Public awareness was raised by print and broadcast media coverage, numerous telephone inquiries, and interpretive signs at the ASU Research Park. The signs ask anglers to return any protected fish caught alive to the water and to report it.
