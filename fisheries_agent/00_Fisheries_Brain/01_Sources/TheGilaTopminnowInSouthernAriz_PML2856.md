# Gila Topminnow in Southern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This presentation, given to the Arizona Riparian Council in October 1994 by an Arizona Game and Fish Department biologist, summarizes the status, biology, and conservation of the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*[[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]]* subsp. *occidentalis*), a small live-bearing fish native to the [[Gila River Basin]]. The species has declined sharply because of [[Habitat Destruction]] and competition and predation by the introduced [[Western Mosquitofish]]. It is listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The document covers historic and current distribution, life history, threats, and recovery actions.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Originally described as *Heterandria occidentalis* by Girard (1853) from a specimen collected near Tucson, Arizona, and redescribed by Hubbs and Miller (1941).
- **Morphology:** Olive-bodied with a white belly. Breeding males are blackened and bear a modified anal fin called a [[Gonopodium]]. Adults usually reach 30 to 45 mm standard length (SL), with males seldom exceeding 25 mm SL.
- **Taxonomic position:** It is the only member of the family Poeciliidae native to the Gila River drainage. Other poeciliids in the basin, including the [[Western Mosquitofish]], are introduced.
- **Range:** Historically it occupied the northernmost limit of the genus *Poeciliopsis*, with populations in the Gila River system, the Santa Cruz River, and northern Sonora. Records from the upper Santa Cruz extend from near Lochiel, Arizona, into Sonora. Tributaries such as [[Sonoita Creek]], [[Cienega Creek]], and Sabino Canyon also supported it.
- **Current distribution:** Eleven of the remaining localities are in the Santa Cruz River system, including [[Redrock Canyon]], Cottonwood Spring, [[Monkey Spring]], Fresno Canyon, Coal Mine Canyon, and Sharp Spring. The other two, [[Bylas Springs]] and Middle Spring, lie beside the Gila River on the San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and tolerance**
- Prefers shallow, warm, fairly quiet water but tolerates a wide range of conditions.
- Temperatures from near freezing under ice to 37°C were reported, with a maximum tolerance of 43°C for brief periods (Heath 1962).
- Recorded pH ranged from 6.6 to 8.9, dissolved oxygen from 2.2 to 11 mg/L (Meffe et al. 1983), and salinities from fresh water to sea water (Schoenherr 1974).
- Can burrow into mud and survive almost total loss of water for 1 to 2 days.
- Occupies the upper third of the water column. Young prefer the warmest, shallowest areas. Pools, glides, and backwaters are used more than marshes or fast flow.
- Historically occupied marshes, sloughs, backwaters, and river edges, which have been lost. The species now persists mainly in spring-heads and smaller creeks, where nonnative fish impacts have been lower.

**Reproduction**
- Viviparous, with internal fertilization through spermatophores delivered by the male's gonopodium.
- Females store sperm for several months and can produce up to 10 broods after isolation from males (Schultz 1961). [[Superfetation]] occurs, though most *Poeciliopsis* carry two embryo stages.
- Mean interval between broods is 21.5 days (Schoenherr 1974). Brood size ranges from 1 to 31, depending on female size.
- Sexual maturity is reached from about two to 11 months after birth, depending on season of birth. Some females matured at 22 mm SL, about four months old.
- Breeding occurs mainly January through August, but young may be produced year-round in thermally constant springs. Up to 98% of mature females are pregnant at peak season.
- Dominant males (over about 24 mm SL) turn black, defend territories, and court females. Subordinate males use a "sneaking" strategy with a relatively long gonopodium.
- Sex ratios in stabilized populations favor females (1.5 to 6.3 per male), while ratios at birth approximate 1.0.

**Growth, diet, and behavior**
- Males stop growing after reaching maturity; females continue growing. Females can reach 50 mm SL and typically outlive males, which live slightly over one year.
- Opportunistic omnivores with a short gut (1.5 to 2 times SL), weak spatulate teeth, and a diet of detritus, vegetation, algae, and insect larvae, with some fish.
- Food supply and photoperiod influence breeding and fecundity.

**Interactions with nonnative species**
- Introduced nonnative species include the [[Asian Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*), probably introduced via the [[Central Arizona Project]] canal, and [[Salt Cedar]] (*Tamarix ramosissima*), which alters habitat.
- Predatory sport fish (e.g., [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]]) are also threats.
- The [[Bullfrog]] is widespread and known to feed on fishes.
- Mosquitofish have replaced topminnows across much of their range, tolerating similar conditions and occupying similar habitats.

## Key Findings

**Historic decline and habitat loss**
- Overgrazing, wood cutting, and a drought in 1891–1893 caused widespread loss of vegetation. Floods then incised watercourses, lowered water tables, and turned permanent streams into intermittent arroyos.
- The [[San Pedro River]] was incised from its mouth for 125 miles upstream in about 10 years.
- Groundwater pumping, water diversions, and dams further degraded habitat.

**Mosquitofish competition and predation**
- Mosquitofish were first collected in Arizona in 1926. Large-scale topminnow reductions correspond closely with their spread.
- Schoenherr (1974) and others documented more than 20 populations severely reduced or eliminated within three years of mosquitofish invasion.
- Mosquitofish prey directly on young topminnows and cause adult deaths through fin damage and infection. Their open cephalic canals improve prey detection.
- Mosquitofish avoid areas topminnows use, such as thick plant mats and clear, carbonate-rich springs, which are the refuges where topminnows persist.
- Coexistence is linked to habitat complexity and flood frequency. Flooding removes a larger proportion of mosquitofish. At Sharp Spring, mosquitofish fell from a substantial share of the fauna before flooding to 0.7% after flooding (OCR-garbled figure; verify against the original).
- In areas not prone to flooding, coexistence rarely exceeded three years. In the Santa Cruz River, topminnows persisted alongside mosquitofish for more than 30 years.
- Extreme floods have also removed reestablished populations, including those at Camp and Cave Creeks, Tule Creek, and Seven Springs.

**Recovery and conservation status**
- Human-assisted movements began in 1936 for mosquito control. Reintroductions for conservation followed.
- Seventeen reestablished populations persist in the wild within historic range.
- Most reintroductions since 1981 used fish from [[Boyce-Thompson Arboretum]], a captive population believed to descend from Bylas Springs, Cocio Wash, and Monkey Spring. Some wild populations, such as Monkey Spring fish at Cold Springs, are pure natural stocks.
- A reestablished population at Mud Springs survived 16 years in a cement watering trough before being eliminated.
- Mid-Spring was lost to drought in 1989 and reestablished with fish from the Roper Lake State Park holding population.
- Renovation efforts (mosquitofish removal) at Bylas Springs, Salt Creek, Roper Lake State Park, and Boyce-Thompson Arboretum had limited success. Bylas Springs had been poisoned three times without success before a later renovation by the USFWS San Carlos Fishery Assistance Office succeeded.
- Cottonwood Spring is protected by a Partners for Wildlife agreement with an exclosure and grazing exclusion. Redrock Canyon has road closures, exclosures, and revised grazing plans. Lower Sonoita Creek, Fresno Canyon, and Coal Mine Canyon are part of the Sonoita Creek State Natural Area.
- Future threats include grazing, mining, timber cutting, recreation, groundwater pumping, urban growth, channelization, and pollution. Habitats identified for recovery lack statutory protection, and budgets for maintaining constructed habitats may be limited.
- Because mosquitofish now have a nearly cosmopolitan distribution, the document concludes that removing this threat from the historic range is unlikely.
