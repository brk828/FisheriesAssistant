# Native Fishes of Arid Lands: A Dwindling Resource of the Desert Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #catostomidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Forest Service General Technical Report (RM-206, September 1991), by [[John N. Rinne]] (photographs) and [[W. L. Minckley]] (text), documents the native fishes of the arid American Southwest and northern Mexico. It describes the aquatic systems of mountains and deserts, the habitats native fishes depend on, and the forces driving their decline. The report includes color photographs of 44 species, many published for the first time. Its central argument is that human alteration of rivers (dams, diversion, groundwater pumping, grazing, logging, and introduced predators) has reduced native fish faunas, and that biological interactions with non-native fishes are the ultimate cause of native fish disappearance in the United States.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The "American Southwest" (Figure 1) covers parts or all of Baja California Norte and Sur, Chihuahua, Coahuila, and Sonora in Mexico, and Arizona, California, Colorado, Texas, New Mexico, Nevada, and Utah in the United States.
- **Purpose:** Raise public awareness of threatened native fishes, update the status of species, and correct confusion over common and scientific names. The text is based on more than 30 years of field experience by Minckley.
- **Structure:** Sections cover the setting and origins of desertlands, water and human impacts, high-mountain streams, intermediate-elevation streams, low-desert streams and rivers, desert oases, marine species, and conclusions.
- **Appendices:** Appendix I lists about 50 selected references. Appendix II lists common and scientific names of native and non-native species, grouped by family (roughly 60 native and 40 non-native entries).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and historic change.** Desert rivers were characterized by highly variable discharge: spring snowmelt floods, winter rains and late-summer monsoons, followed by drought trickles. Floodplains supported cottonwood-willow gallery forests and marshes, and water tables were near the surface even in the driest basins. Dams, reservoirs, canals, and groundwater pumping replaced this regime. Arroyo cutting, driven partly by overgrazing, drained water tables further, and riparian communities persist only as remnants. The Colorado River's pre-regulation flows ranged from less than 0.35 to more than 7000 cubic meters per second.

**Mountain streams ("Creeks in the Pines").** Summer temperatures often exclude temperate fishes and allow native trout to thrive. Streams are heavily shaded, so food webs are heterotrophic, based on detritus from pine needles and leaves. Beaver ponds once supported large native trout populations; beaver overtrapping contributed to habitat loss.

**Intermediate-elevation streams.** Water temperatures reach about 30°C in afternoon and drop to 21°C or lower at daybreak. Channels carry heavy sediment loads, and most annual water yield comes in flash floods. Chemistry generally stays within healthy-fish limits, though total dissolved solids are higher than in mountain streams.

**Low-desert systems.** Most low-desert streams are now diverted or dewatered. The lower Colorado River historically had extensive delta marshes, riparian forests, and tidal influence, with tides exceeding 9.0 m at the river mouth. The Colorado River Delta has been largely dried by water diversions.

**Marine and estuarine influence.** Some species (for example, striped mullet, Pacific tenpounder, Pacific shad, and the giant totoaba) entered lower river reaches from the Gulf of California. The totoaba, endemic to the upper Gulf of California, has nearly disappeared from commercial catches.

**Desert oases and springs.** Springs fed by subsurface sources support stable, low-variability environments in which fish reproduce year-round at constant rates. Some springs hold fish found nowhere else.

## Key Findings

- **Species diversity and endemism:** Of 41 strictly freshwater fishes discussed, 10 are reported from the United States only, 12 from Mexico only (according to the text's summary wording), and 19 are shared; the report notes that 32 species are shared by the two countries and that many are local endemics. In 1989, 28 species (out of about 41 taxa) were officially recognized as threatened, endangered, or of special concern by the American Fisheries Society.
- **Trout:** [[Apache Trout]] (threatened) was reduced to less than 5.0% of its former range by the 1970s through hybridization and competition with introduced trout. [[Gila Trout]] (endangered) persists naturally in only five streams. Both were displaced by introduced rainbow, cutthroat, brown, and brook trout. Both are federally listed.
- **Hybridization and introduced species:** The report identifies hybridization as the most serious threat to species integrity. Non-native trout, bass, and other predators and competitors are the central threat across all groups.
- **Habitat degradation:** Logging, livestock grazing, sawmill wastes, and groundwater pumping have opened stream channels, increased sedimentation, and removed riparian vegetation.
- **Endangered minnows and suckers:** [[Humpback Chub]] (endangered) lives in Grand Canyon whitewater and is long-lived (possibly more than 30 years). [[Razorback Sucker]] shows over two decades of no recruitment into the Lake Mohave population, with larvae disappearing before 15 mm. [[Bonytail]] is nearly extinct in the wild, with specimens found to be 35 to 40 years old, and is considered by many workers to be the most endangered freshwater fish in western North America. [[Woundfin]] is now restricted to the Virgin River mainstream.
- **Colorado squawfish:** This is the largest North American minnow, reaching almost 1.8 m and perhaps 41 kg. It is now extirpated from the lower Colorado basin.
- **Small-bodied species:** [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], and [[Desert Pupfish]] illustrate the range of life histories, from rapid dispersal and drought resistance to territorial spawning behavior.
- **Special-status springfishes and pupfish:** The [[Moapa Dace]] (endangered) lives only in thermal pools of the upper Moapa River. The [[White River Springfish]] persists in thermal springs of Nevada. Several topminnow and pupfish populations have been lost to introduced predators such as largemouth bass.
- **Conservation tools:** Recovery plans focus on isolating native populations in wilderness areas and using hatchery strains for restocking. Non-native trout exclusion is recommended. Captive broodstock programs are underway for bonytail and razorback.
- **Core conclusion:** The authors hypothesize that biological interactions with introduced fishes (through competition, predation, and hybridization) are the ultimate factor eliminating native fishes in the United States, even where physical and chemical conditions resemble natural states. Mexican systems remain less altered but face the same pressures as introductions expand.
- **Evolutionary context:** The native fauna developed over 5 to 10 million years with few native predators and no carnivores like gars, pikes, or bass, leaving it poorly prepared for introduced predators.
- **Research gap:** The mechanisms of species replacement are not experimentally tested and are left to future research.
