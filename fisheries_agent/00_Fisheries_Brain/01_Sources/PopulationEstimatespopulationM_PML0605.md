# Population Estimates and Movements of the Endangered Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*) in the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study estimates the abundance and movements of adult [[Gila Cypha]] (humpback chub), an endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system, within the lower [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. The LCR confluence holds the largest known population of the species. Fish were tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging]] (PIT) tags during 19 trips between July 1991 and December 1992 and analyzed with [[Capture-Recapture]] models. The authors test the hypothesis that adult residency and local movement in the LCR are restricted to the reproductive period. The data reject this hypothesis. Adults move upriver in spring, then move slowly downstream after spawning, and many remain in the LCR for long periods, possibly all year. The authors link this residency to [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (completed 1963) and suggest a possible shift in the species' life-history strategy.

## Background & Description

*Gila cypha* is one of the most specialized minnows in western North America. It is known from the Colorado River and its large, swift tributaries, where it occurs sporadically and is rarely locally abundant. Its morphology reflects adaptation to boulder-strewn, high-velocity rapids. Its lineage dates to the Miocene. The species was described in 1946 from a specimen caught in 1932 near [[Bright Angel Creek]], about 141.3 river km below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Its life history remained poorly known, and the Grand Canyon population was among the least studied. Chub at the LCR confluence were not recognized as a reproducing population until 1975.

The Colorado River basin combines high economic value with extraordinary fish diversity. Species-level endemism is approximately 75%, or 93% if undescribed forms and subspecies are included (Minckley 1991; Carlson and Muth 1989). Most of the endemic fishes are endangered or candidates for listing because of habitat modification. The authors frame the conflict as a classic one between development and conservation.

The study addressed three questions:
- How many adult chub occupy the LCR?
- What is the nature and extent of their movements within the river, where reproduction occurs annually?
- Are local movement and residency restricted to the reproductive period?

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study river.** The LCR flows 412 km from headwaters to its confluence with the Colorado River. Most of its upper 390 km is seasonally dry. The lower 21 km is perennial, fed by groundwater springs. The largest spring, [[Blue Springs]] at LCR river km (RKM) 21, discharges 6.1–6.6 m³/s. At base flow the river is saline (conductivity above 5000 µmhos/cm) and travertine-forming. Carbonate precipitation gives it a turquoise color and builds pools, runs, rapids, and scalloped falls. Broad sandbars shift seasonally with flooding. Riparian vegetation includes native species such as catclaw acacia, honey mesquite, coyote willow, and arrowweed, alongside nonnative tamarisk and camelthorn. A series of travertine falls at RKM 14.9, [[Atomizer Falls]], marks the upstream limit of *G. cypha* distribution.

**Dam effects.** Dam construction and dewatering greatly reduced the river's ecological character. Suitable riverine habitat for large-river fishes fell from 2400 km to 965 km (Miller 1982). [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Powell]] now cover former river sections. Closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963 cooled the river, reduced sediment and salinity, and altered flow. Downstream, the fish community shifted toward cold-water species such as [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]. Of eight indigenous fishes in Grand Canyon National Park, five still persist, usually in warmer tributaries and backwaters. Adult *G. cypha* persisted in Lake Powell but could not reproduce there.

**Thermal refugium.** Temperature and flow in the LCR resemble those of the pre-dam mainstem. The LCR therefore may serve as a warm-water refuge for the species, a point central to the authors' interpretation.

**Habitat use.** Adults occupy deep, swirling eddies along canyon walls and turbulent zones near boulders. They are primarily nocturnal and, in the LCR, reside in deep water along cut banks, rock outcrops, and pools during the day. The species also associates with riprap from railroad and highway construction elsewhere in the basin.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sampling**
- Adults were sampled in three reaches: the [[Confluence Staging Area]] (RKM 0.55), [[Powell Canyon]] (RKM 3.1), and [[Salt Canyon]] (RKM 10.8). Sampling used hoop nets and trammel nets over 19 trips of 6–14 days each at roughly monthly intervals.
- Adults (>150 mm TL) received PIT tags and were released at capture sites.
- Population estimates were derived using [[Closed Population Models]]. This analysis produced 57 capture-history matrices (three camps across 19 trips). Nine closed-population estimates were generated per matrix using the CAPTURE program.

**Population estimates**
- The best-fitting model for the full 19-month dataset was the [[Pollock And Otto Estimator]] (Mₕ), with a criterion of 0.61. It estimated **4508 adults** (SD 120; 95% CI 4330–4811).
- Average monthly estimates summed over reaches (2993) and calculated by month (2434) were not significantly different (ANOVA F = 1.15, P > 0.7). The authors treat the 4508 estimate as superior but caution that it is affected by closure violations.
- In May 1992, the confluence held an estimated **1320 adults**, compared with 1800 (1987) and 2900 (1988). The paper reports these as reductions of 21% and 54%; the 1800 comparison computes to about 27%. The entire LCR in May 1992 held an estimated **4346 adults** (summed reach estimate 4602), compared with 25,000 in 1989.
- Population estimates peaked in early March through June 1992 and rose again in autumn of both years.

**Reach differences**
- Fishing effort differed significantly among reaches (F = 6.40; P < 0.0035). Effort at Salt Canyon exceeded that at the confluence.
- Normalized population estimates also differed (F = 4.19; P < 0.01). The confluence and Salt Canyon were statistically similar, and both exceeded Powell Canyon after adjusting for effort.

**Seasonal movement (1992)**
- Upstream movement made up **21%** of total recorded movements. For example, 49% (n = 47) of confluence fish tagged in winter and recaptured moved upstream, and 51% (n = 96) of spring confluence recaptures did the same.
- Downstream movement made up **9%** of recaptures. Downstream movement from Powell in winter was 21% (n = 8), and from Powell in spring 16% (n = 30).
- Residency (movement within reach) made up **70%** of recorded movements. Confluence fish tagged in summer were recaptured there summer through winter at 76% (n = 54). Salt Canyon fish tagged in summer were recaptured there at 93% (n = 159).

**Between-reach movement during sampling**
- Movement between reaches during a sampling trip was negligible. In 1991, 36 of 3272 fish (0.01%) were recaptured in a different reach. In 1992, one of 4030 fish (0.0003%) was recaptured in a different reach. The authors use this to support closure during sampling.

**Interpretation**
- Adults stage at the confluence before spring upstream movement to spawn. The authors consider this a staging pattern.
- Postspawning downstream movement is slow and diffuse, not an explosive reproductive movement.
- Long-term residency, especially summer through winter, indicates that many adults overwinter in the LCR. The authors propose two competing hypotheses. Residency may be a pre-dam trait, or the species may have shifted its life history after dam closure, increasing residency to avoid cold mainstem water. Larger, presumably older adults make most movements into and out of the LCR, and adults under 200 mm TL are seldom found in the mainstem. The authors cite these as anecdotal support for the post-dam hypothesis, but neither hypothesis can yet be tested.
- The authors suggest that flow regulation from Glen Canyon Dam could potentially mimic a natural hydrograph and enhance survival of the species, though political and economic constraints may limit such management.

**Conservation implications**
- If *G. cypha* now depends more heavily on the LCR, its long-term persistence in Grand Canyon depends more on that river than previously assumed.
- The LCR population's reproductive success, residency, and response to flow management are priority questions for recovery planning.
