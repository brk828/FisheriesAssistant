## Executive Summary

This 1941 study by William C. Beckman (Michigan Institute for Fisheries Research) documents a field experiment testing whether reducing the density of a stunted fish population improves individual growth rates. In 1937, the south basin (16.0 acres) of Standard Lake, Michigan — a lake notorious for poor fishing due to overcrowding with undersized fish — was treated with rotenone (derris root) to poison and remove the resident fish population. A pre-treatment census documented an extremely dense but stunted population dominated by [[Rock Bass]] and [[Yellow Perch]], with very few legal-sized game fish (2.7 per acre). Following poisoning, unharmed fish from the connected north basin recolonized the whole lake at lower density. Scale-based age and growth analysis of rock bass collected in 1939 and 1940 showed a marked, statistically consistent increase in growth rate across all age groups compared to pre-poisoning (1937) fish, supporting density reduction as a viable fisheries management tool for lakes with stunted, overcrowded populations unsuitable for coldwater species like trout.

## Background & Description

- **Species examined:** [[Rock Bass]] (*Ambloplites rupestris*), family [[Centrarchidae]]
- **Aging method:** Scale annuli counts; "key" scales taken from a standardized body location; validity of the annulus as a true year-mark in rock bass confirmed by Hile (1941)
- **Body-scale relationship:** Linear, with an x-intercept at 15 mm standard length
- **Growth back-calculation formula:**

  L_n = 15 + S_n × (L_t − 15) / S_t

  where L_n = calculated fish length at end of year *n*, S_n = scale radius within the *n*th annulus, L_t = standard length at capture, S_t = total scale radius measurement
- **Sexual dimorphism in growth:** Only difference between sexes was more rapid growth of males in later years of life; otherwise growth patterns were similar
- **Size structure pre-poisoning (1937 sample):** Average rock bass weight 9.70 oz; average age-group I standard length ~52–57 mm; population strongly skewed toward small, undersized individuals

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Standard Lake, 32.5 acres total, max depth 31 ft, divided into a north basin (16.5 acres) and south basin (16.0 acres) connected by a shallow (~1 ft), ~200 ft-wide channel permitting free fish passage
- **Community composition (south basin, pre-treatment census, Table 1):**
  - [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*): 4,827 individuals, 146.4 lbs
  - [[Rock Bass]] (*Ambloplites rupestris*): 1,233 individuals, 53.9 lbs
  - "Forage fish" (combined small cyprinids/others): 14,054 individuals, 62.9 lbs — including [[Red-Bellied Dace]], [[Fine-Scaled Dace]], [[Iowa Darter]], [[Mud Minnow]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Black-Nosed Shiner]], [[Blunt-Nosed Minnow]]
  - [[Common Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*): 78 individuals, 85.5 lbs
  - **Total:** 20,192 fish (1,262/acre), 348.7 lbs (21.8 lbs/acre)
- **Trophic/management context:** Lake deemed unsuitable for coldwater species (e.g., trout) due to conditions, unlike lakes in Eschmeyer's earlier perch studies where complete removal allowed successful trout stocking
- **Population density issue:** Only 2.7 legal-sized game fish per acre pre-treatment (2.4 perch, 0.3 rock bass) — entire basin held only 42 legal fish, less than two angling limit catches
- **Recovery mechanism:** Unpoisoned north basin fish freely recolonized the treated south basin once the channel blockade (sand bags) was removed, naturally re-establishing a population at reduced density

## Key Findings

1. **Dense stunted populations were the norm:** Standard Lake's south basin held 1,262 fish/acre but only 21.8 lbs/acre biomass — indicative of severe stunting rather than true overpopulation by biomass.
2. **Rotenone treatment successfully reduced density** without eliminating the population lakewide, since the unpoisoned north basin served as a source for recolonization.
3. **Growth rate increased significantly post-treatment:** Rock bass collected in 1939–1940 were notably larger at a given age than pre-poisoning (1937) fish of the same age and sex (Table 3), despite ~2/3 of 1939 samples being collected in May/June (early season, before most annual growth) versus the 1937 sample taken in September (near-complete seasonal growth) — making the observed size increase even more conservative/significant.
4. **Consistency ruled out chance/normal fluctuation:** Year-by-year calculated growth increments (Tables 4–5) were highly consistent across age groups for the 5 years *prior* to poisoning (1933–1937), then increased markedly in 1938–1939, arguing against the increased growth being attributable to natural annual growth-rate variability (of the type documented by [[Hile 1941]] for rock bass and [[Hubbs & Hubbs 1933]] for [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]], and their hybrids).
5. **Alternative explanations were ruled out:** The hypothesis that the 1937 sample was unrepresentative (e.g., reflecting a distinct, slower-growing sub-population confined to the treated basin) was rejected because 1939–1940 growth calculations for pre-1937 years matched the 1937 data closely — divergence appeared only after poisoning.
6. **Age-group I data flagged as unreliable:** The apparent difference in growth was largest for age-group I, but this was attributed to gear selectivity of the experimental gill nets (capturing only larger individuals of that age class), corroborated by comparison with [[Hile 1941]]'s length-distribution data from 1¼-inch mesh gill nets.
7. **Management implication:** Reducing population density via poisoning/removal is proposed as a sound, effective fisheries management strategy for stunted-fish lakes unsuitable for coldwater species conversion, in contrast to counterproductive practices such as legally protecting undersized fish ("runts") or continuing to stock already overcrowded waters.
