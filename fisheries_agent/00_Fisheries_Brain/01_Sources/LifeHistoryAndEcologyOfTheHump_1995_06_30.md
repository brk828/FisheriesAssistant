# Life History and Ecology of the Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*) — Colorado River, Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #[[Gila Cypha]]

## Executive Summary
This document synthesizes a five-year (1990–1995) BIO/WEST, Inc. research program contracted by the Bureau of Reclamation to characterize the distribution, habitat use, movement, growth, diet, reproduction, and survival of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the Colorado River through Grand Canyon. The study also produced an integrated fisheries database (GCFIN), a population model framework, a flow-routing model, a data collection plan, and a photographic archive of adult fish. Findings establish that the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) is the primary recruitment source sustaining most mainstem aggregations, that non-native fishes dominate mainstem biomass and prey heavily on subadult chub, and that habitat use shifts ontogenetically from shoreline to eddy-complex environments.

## Background & Description
- **Size classes**: Subadults defined as <200 mm total length (TL); adults as >200 mm TL.
- **Growth rates**:
  - Subadults in the mainstem: 4.00 mm/30 days (slower).
  - Subadults in the LCR: 10.30 mm/30 days (faster).
  - Adults in the mainstem: 1.2–2.79 mm/30 days (potentially faster than LCR adults).
  - Adults in the LCR: <1–1.42 mm/30 days.
- **Age determination**: Scale-based aging was feasible for subadults; disruption of scale circuli observed when fish moved from warmer (20°C+) LCR waters to cooler (10°C) mainstem waters. Validation against otoliths recommended.
- **Post-larval specimens**: ~100 individuals ranging 18–31 mm TL discovered in a warm spring (21.5°C) at River Mile (RM) 30.6, representing rare evidence of mainstem spawning.
- **Photographic voucher record**: 197 adult humpback chub documented via 35 mm color slides, each linked to PIT tag number, date, sex, capture location (river mile), weight, total length, and standard length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Distribution and Abundance
- Nine distinct aggregations identified from RM 30 to RM 213.
- The largest aggregation occurs at the **[[Little Colorado River]] inflow**, supporting 3,000–3,500 adults and an estimated 65,980–230,930 subadults (with a peak estimate of 857,750 in 1993).
- Seven other downstream aggregations are likely sustained by LCR-origin recruits.
- The **30-Mile aggregation** (~50 adults) is considered a mainstem relict population, potentially dating to fish hatched around 1970.
- Mortality of subadults in the mainstem is very high; recruitment into the mainstem population is believed to be driven largely by small adults emigrating from the LCR.

### Habitat Use
- **Ontogenetic shift**: Subadults (<200 mm TL) occupy shoreline habitats; adults (>200 mm TL) occupy large recirculating eddy complexes. This transition begins after year 1 and completes by year 3.
- Eddy complexes comprise only ~20% of river surface area but contained 88% of captured adults and 77% of radio-telemetry contacts.
- Highest adult densities correspond to regions with frequent debris-fan-formed eddy complexes (e.g., LCR inflow, [[Middle Granite Gorge]]).
- Subadult densities were highest along vegetated shorelines, talus slopes, and debris fans (in descending order).
- Western Grand Canyon habitat (below [[Granite Park]]) may be structurally suitable but potentially food-limited, especially for subadults.

### Movement and Behavior
- Home range of the LCR inflow aggregation: 13.5 km in the mainstem + 14.9 km in the LCR = **28.4 km total**.
- Average net displacement: 1.49 km (radio-tagged adults) and 0.99 km (PIT-tagged adults).
- Extremely low inter-aggregation movement: only 2 of 280 recaptured adults (out of 1,524 marked) moved outside their home aggregation.
- Activity patterns:
  - Highest at night and during high-turbidity conditions.
  - High water clarity (<30 NTU) significantly suppressed near-surface activity.
  - Movement increased significantly when flow changes exceeded 300 cfs/hr at discharges >10,000 cfs.
- Spawning-related seasonal movement: local aggregation in eddy complexes (February) → congregation at LCR inflow (March–April) → ascent into LCR for spawning (March–May).

### Diet and Trophic Ecology
- **Cladophora** (filamentous algae) composed 87–95% of gut volume — the dominant food resource.
- Invertebrate diet composition (overall): 40% simuliids (blackflies), 45% *[[Gammarus]]* (amphipod), 5% chironomids, 9% terrestrial invertebrates, 1% other aquatic invertebrates.
- Diet varied spatially: In Middle Granite Gorge (RM 125), terrestrial invertebrates rose to 30% of diet, indicating greater reliance on allochthonous food downstream.
- Diet varied seasonally:
  - Spring: *Gammarus* (51%), simuliids (24%), terrestrials (23%).
  - Summer: Simuliids (47%), *Gammarus* (30%), terrestrials (14%).
  - Fall: Simuliids and *Gammarus* roughly equal (44% each).
- Drift dynamics: Cladophora volume in drift increased during upramping flows and in downstream reaches; invertebrate volume increased during downramping flows and decreased downstream.
- Adults employ dual foraging strategies: midwater feeding on drift material in eddies, and benthic feeding on woody debris, sand riffles, and boulders. Subadults rely primarily on shoreline-available food.
- **Dietary overlap with [[Rainbow Trout]]** suggests potential interspecific competition for food resources.

### Reproduction
- Only direct evidence of mainstem spawning: post-larval humpback chub found in the RM 30.6 warm spring (21.5°C).
- Mainstem reproduction is presumed to occur sporadically in other warm springs/tributary inflows but is considered demographically insignificant to overall population.
- Spawning readiness timing differs by aggregation: mainstem fish reach readiness May–June; LCR-spawning fish are ready March–April.

### Survival and Population Dynamics
- Estimated annual survival rates: **subadults = 0.100**; **adults = 0.755**.
- Of the 3,000–3,500 LCR-inflow adults, 738–858 individuals are lost annually.
- To replace this loss, an estimated **738,000–858,000 young-of-year (YOY)** must successfully recruit into the mainstem population each year — highlighting an extraordinarily high early-life mortality bottleneck.

### Species Interactions
- Humpback chub co-occur with 10 non-native and 3 native fish species; most frequently with [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Common Carp]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Fathead Minnow]].
- **Predation impact** (estimated subadult humpback chub consumed annually):
  - Brown Trout: ~227,760
  - Rainbow Trout: ~27,373
  - Channel Catfish: ~2,738
  - **Total: ~250,000 humpback chub consumed annually by non-native predators.**
- Parasite prevalence: Asian tapeworm (*[[Bothriocephalus acheilognathi]]*) found in 4% of examined chub; *Lernaea cyprinacea* (anchor worm) found in 1%.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] populations also showed signs of low reproduction/recruitment based on length distributions and catch rates.

## Key Findings
- **Non-native dominance**: Rainbow trout and carp constitute ~80% of total mainstem fish biomass; of 34 non-native species documented historically in Grand Canyon, 11 were detected during this study.
- **LCR as recruitment engine**: The Little Colorado River is the primary source of recruitment for most mainstem humpback chub aggregations; mainstem spawning is rare and ecologically minor.
- **High juvenile mortality**: Subadult survival (0.100 annually) is drastically lower than adult survival (0.755), pointing to early life stages as the critical population bottleneck.
- **Habitat partitioning by life stage**: A clear ontogenetic shift from shoreline to eddy-complex habitat occurs between years 1–3 of life, with implications for flow-management strategies affecting each life stage differently.
- **Flow-management sensitivity**: Fish movement and subadult shoreline habitat stability are closely tied to flow fluctuation magnitude and rate, suggesting dam operations at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] directly influence chub behavior and habitat quality.
- **Predation as a major mortality source**: Non-native predatory fish (especially [[Brown Trout]]) account for an estimated loss of ~250,000 subadult humpback chub annually, rivaling or exceeding natural mortality sources.
- **Research infrastructure legacy**: The project established foundational tools (GCFIN database, population model framework, flow routing model) still relevant to long-term monitoring and adaptive management of the [[Grand Canyon]] Colorado River ecosystem.
