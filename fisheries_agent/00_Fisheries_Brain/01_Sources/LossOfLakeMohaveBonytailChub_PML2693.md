# Loss of Endangered Bonytail Chub During Broodstock Capture and Transfer from Lake Mohave, 1993

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bonytail-chub

## Executive Summary

This internal memorandum (1993) reports the loss of most of a group of endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] collected from [[Lake Mohave]] as potential broodstock for [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center (NFH&TC). Of 16 fish netted in May 1993, 15 were held at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and then shipped to Dexter. Seven of those fish died after arrival, leaving four alive in the raceway at the time of writing. The memo attributes the losses to poor handling, delayed veterinary involvement, a communication breakdown, and the absence of a written protocol for capturing and holding endangered species. A new protocol was being drafted for submission to the Regional Office by June 18, 1993.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** Bonytail chub is listed as endangered. Historically, very few are caught annually in Lake Mohave, often four or fewer per year.
- **Origin of broodstock:** A survey by an Arizona university project, begun around 1987, netted bonytail during the summer. Captured fish were considered valuable because they could increase the genetic variability of the broodstock held at Dexter. Some fish from the original broodstock came from Lake Mohave, and some fish at Dexter were transferred from Willow Beach.
- **Prior losses:** Some fish in the Dexter population had been captured in earlier years, and some had not spawned. Most fish held at Dexter or Willow Beach had been captured before.
- **Personnel:** The effort was led by Chuck Minckley (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service), Paul Marsh (Arizona State University), and Kirk Young (Arizona Game and Fish). The Willow Beach hatchery manager, Glenn Green, held and treated the fish. Roger Hamman and Phillip Soda transported fish to Dexter.
- **Notification:** The hatchery manager was informed by telephone several months before the effort. A memorandum about the effort was sent to all interested parties on May 7 (signed May 10).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The memo focuses on handling and husbandry rather than population ecology. The relevant environmental and biological conditions it describes are:

- **Netting conditions:** Nets were set on Lake Mohave on the evening of May 12 and pulled for the last time on May 13. The nets were fished through the morning and evening.
- **Water temperature change:** Fish were moved from lake water (about 72°F) to hatchery water over roughly one hour. Exact temperatures are partly illegible in the source.
- **Disease and injury:** Fish arrived with apparent fungal growth and were treated for fungus with [[Potassium Permanganate]]. Staff also reported that the fish were badly marked up by the netting.
- **Post-mortem observations:** The dead fish did not appear fungal. They appeared to slough off necrotic tissue, which the memo suggests may reflect bacterial infection or injury from handling and transport.

## Key Findings

**1. Capture and handling**
- 16 bonytail were netted on the Lake Mohave sampling dates in May 1993.
- One fish already carried a [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag]] (PIT tag) and was released back into the lake.
- The other 15 fish were measured, weighed, photographed, and placed in larger Nalgene tanks for transport to Willow Beach. Stress-reduction chemicals were used during transport.

**2. Holding and treatment at Willow Beach**
- Fish were held at Willow Beach for about two weeks before shipping. One fish died during this period.
- Treatments were given to reduce the potential for fungus. The hatchery manager was off duty on the weekend and this was not communicated to weekend staff, so the fish were not treated or checked on those days.
- Weekend staff reported that the fish looked fine on May 18 and 19. By the time the manager returned, the fish appeared heavily fungused.
- Seven fish were found to be over-stressed, and treatment was reduced by a bath in potassium permanganate. A fish health specialist provided a stress-reduction formula.

**3. Decision to ship**
- On May 24 the decision was made to ship the fish to Dexter. The [[Fish Health Center]] and Dexter staff were consulted.
- The Willow Beach crew believed the fish were in good enough condition to travel. The Dexter staff, although skeptical, felt there was a better chance of saving the fish at Dexter than at Willow Beach.
- The fish were transported and arrived in late May.

**4. Losses**
- One additional fish died in transit to Dexter. Seven fish died after arriving at Dexter, and four fish were alive in the raceway at the time of writing.
- Surviving fish were described as having no visible fungus but with sloughing skin, which suggests bacterial infection or handling injury.

**5. Root causes identified**
- **No written protocol:** There was no written protocol for capturing and holding endangered species. The only relevant rule that existed was ignored: no fish are to be moved to a hatchery without examination by a hatchery biologist.
- **Communication breakdown:** Weekend staff were not informed of the treatment schedule.
- **Handling practices:** The memo suggests the losses reflect poor handling and a lack of communication.

**6. Corrective actions**
- A protocol for all handling of endangered species was being drafted.
- The protocol was to be submitted to the Regional Office by June 18, 1993, and was to be reviewed and ready for general distribution by June 26, 1993.
