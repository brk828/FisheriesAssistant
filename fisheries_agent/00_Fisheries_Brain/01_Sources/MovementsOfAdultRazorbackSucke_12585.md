# Movements of Adult Razorback Suckers in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #catostomidae #endangered-species #telemetry #movement-ecology

## Executive Summary

This master's thesis documents a 17-month telemetry study (November 1994–August 1996) tracking movements of 20 adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a 108-km regulatory reservoir on the [[Colorado River]]. Ten individuals (4 males, 6 females) survived the study duration and were tracked bi-weekly using surgically implanted sonic transmitters. The study revealed sex-specific movement patterns, spawning-site fidelity, and predictable seasonal migrations to a highly productive mixing zone. Females exhibited greater mobility than males (261±65 km vs. 154±51 km gross movement), while males showed stronger association with inshore spawning habitats. Both sexes visited multiple spawning aggregations during single spawning periods (November–March) and demonstrated fidelity to specific sites across years. A notable post-spawning migration of 15–20 km to a thermally stratified [[Mixing Zone]] occurred in late spring, where fish remained 1–2 months, likely exploiting elevated zooplankton productivity. These findings provide critical baseline data for managing the largest extant population of this endangered [[Catostomidae]] species.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large, monotypic catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] drainage, representing one of the most distinctive native fishes in western North America. Adults reach maximum sizes of ~75 cm total length and 5–6 kg. The species is characterized by a laterally compressed, sharp-edged keel rising abruptly from the nape to the dorsal fin—the basis for both common and scientific names (*Xyrauchen* = "razor-nape"). 

Sexual dimorphism is pronounced: females are larger-bodied with enlarged urogenital papillae during breeding; males are smaller with sharper pre-dorsal keels, relatively longer anal fins, and strong nuptial tubercles on anal and caudal fins. Unlike most suckers, razorback suckers possess highly protrusible mouths and fine gill rakers adapted for planktivory, feeding on zooplankton and detritus.

Historically abundant throughout the Colorado River basin from Wyoming and Colorado to Mexico, the species has declined drastically due to habitat alteration (impoundment, channelization, flow regulation) and exotic species introductions. Listed as endangered in 1991, razorback suckers are now rare in the upper basin (estimated ~500 individuals in mid-Green River, early 1990s) and common only in [[Lake Mohave]], which harbors the largest extant population. However, even this population declined from ~73,500 to ~23,300 individuals between 1988 and 1992. Populations consist predominantly of old adults (40–50 year life expectancy) with negligible recruitment, primarily due to larval predation by non-native fishes.

[[Lake Mohave]] is a 108-km-long run-of-the-river reservoir impounded by [[Davis Dam]] in 1954, constrained by canyon walls for most of its length. At full pool (197 m elevation), maximum depth is 42 m, surface area 115 km², volume 1.85×10⁹ m³, and mean turnover 68 days. The reservoir is relatively clear year-round, receiving cold hypolimnetic water (12–13°C) from [[Hoover Dam]]. It is mesotrophic relative to other Colorado River reservoirs. The fish fauna consists almost entirely of exotic species except for razorback sucker and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Razorback suckers in [[Lake Mohave]] aggregate to spawn from November through March (sometimes into April–May). Spawning aggregations of tens to hundreds of fish occur at consistent locations annually, including sites in the lotic [[Black Canyon]] reach. Males congregate inshore over wave-washed gravel and cobble substrates at 0.5–5 m depth. Females remain in deeper water until ready to spawn, then approach spawning grounds singly. Spawning involves one female and one to several males in bottom convulsions lasting seconds to minutes. Females spawn repeatedly over hours, days, or weeks with no apparent diel pattern, then return to deeper water while males rejoin their groups.

Despite successful spawning, recruitment is negligible. Predation on larvae by non-native species is the primary limiting factor. A [[Native Fish Work Group]] formed in 1990 implemented a conservation strategy: capturing naturally spawned larvae, rearing them in predator-free backwaters, and releasing juveniles at ~25 cm TL (a size relatively immune to piscine predation). Between 1991 and 1997, ~6,700 juveniles were released, with 125 recaptured, indicating partial success in replacing the aging population.

The reservoir's limnology strongly influences razorback sucker ecology. [[Lake Mohave]] becomes thermally stratified in late spring. Cold, nutrient-rich hypolimnetic water from [[Lake Mead]] passes under warmer stratified water, creating a turbulent [[Mixing Zone]] (approximately waypoints 17–27, or 15–20 km uplake from mid-reservoir) that becomes the most productive area for phytoplankton and zooplankton. This zone appears critical to razorback sucker foraging ecology given their planktivorous adaptations.

## Key Findings

**Transmitter Performance and Study Duration:**
- 20 adult razorback suckers (10 males, 10 females) were surgically implanted with sonic transmitters in November 1994
- 10 individuals (4 males, 6 females) survived the 17-month study and provided analyzable data
- 6 fish stopped moving within weeks (presumed tag expulsion or mortality); 2 lost contact after several months (transmitter malfunction); 2 contacted only once
- Expected 14-month battery life was exceeded by 14 of 20 tags, which functioned for 21 months
- Average contacts per fish: 55 for females (range 38–66), 57 for males (range 51–59)

**Movement Patterns and Sex Differences:**
- Females exhibited significantly greater gross movement (261±65 km) than males (154±51 km; P<0.05)
- Excluding one anomalous male (no. 2228), male gross movement averaged only 127±18 km
- Female movement rates averaged 776±1392 m·d⁻¹ (first spawning period) and 617±1689 m·d⁻¹ (second spawning period)
- Male movement rates averaged 1214±860 m·d⁻¹ (second spawning period), but only 189±322 m·d⁻¹ when excluding no. 2228
- Most females moved 100–500 m·d⁻¹; most males moved <100 m·d⁻¹
- Movement rate differences between sexes were significant for spawning and first non-spawning periods (P<0.001) but not the final non-spawning period (P>0.10)

**Geographic Range:**
- Individual locations ranged over 75 km: from within 5 km of [[Hoover Dam]] (upstream limit) to within 20 km of [[Davis Dam]] (downstream limit)
- Distance between maximum up- and downstream contacts: 20–63.6 km (variable among individuals)
- Females ranged more widely than males, though differences were not statistically significant (P>0.25)

**Spawning Behavior and Site Fidelity:**
- Both sexes visited multiple spawning aggregations during single spawning periods
- Individuals returned to the same aggregations in both 1994–95 and 1995–96 spawning seasons
- Known spawning aggregations included: [[Horseshoe Rapids]], [[Owl Cove]] area, [[Tequilla Cove]], [[Yuma Cove]] area, and [[Six and Nine Mile]] area
- Males stayed at single aggregations longer (mean 70 days) than females (mean 20±12 days; P<0.001)
- Females visited slightly more aggregations than males and moved more frequently between sites
- Duration of stay differences remained significant even when visits extending beyond defined spawning period were excluded

**Habitat Preferences:**
- Both sexes showed strong preference for inshore habitats during spawning and non-spawning periods (P<0.001)
- Males were inshore more often than females (P<0.001), especially during spawning
- Temperature data (based on tag temperature intervals) suggested females preferred deeper, colder water (P<0.001), though sample sizes were small (mean 10 observations per individual)
- Most individuals located in 16–25°C water when stratified

**Seasonal Migration to Mixing Zone:**
- Seven individuals migrated 15–20 km uplake to the [[Mixing Zone]] after spawning in 1995; five repeated this in 1996
- Fish remained in [[Mixing Zone]] for mean 57±27 days (range 14–98 days), from late May through July–August
- Groups of hundreds of razorback suckers observed near surface in [[Mixing Zone]] during late spring–early summer in both study years
- Migration timing coincided with thermal stratification and peak zooplankton productivity in the [[Mixing Zone]]
- This pattern likely represents foraging migration to exploit elevated food resources, consistent with planktivorous feeding morphology

**Geographic Distribution by Season:**
- During spawning (November–March): individuals concentrated in Little Basin and Big Basin, associated with known spawning grounds
- Post-spawning (late spring–summer): concentration in [[Mixing Zone]]
- Gradual downstream movement following [[Mixing Zone]] residency

**Recaptures:**
- Four telemetered individuals captured during March 1995 spawning (2 by trammel net, 2 by electroshocking) at [[Horseshoe Rapids]], Great West Cove, and [[Yuma Cove]]
- One fish trammel-netted at Gold Cove in April 1996
- All were in good condition with healed surgical wounds (some retained sutures)
- Capture and handling did not appear to affect subsequent movements

**Management Implications:**
- Razorback suckers exhibit predictable spatio-temporal movement patterns
- Spawning-site fidelity and seasonal migrations to [[Mixing Zone]] provide opportunities for targeted monitoring and management
- Future changes in [[Hoover Dam]] and [[Davis Dam]] operations could alter [[Mixing Zone]] productivity and affect razorback sucker nutrition
- Telemetry of additional adults and juveniles recommended to confirm patterns and examine life-stage differences
- Understanding juvenile movement patterns critical for evaluating success of repatriation efforts (6,700 juveniles released 1991–1997)
