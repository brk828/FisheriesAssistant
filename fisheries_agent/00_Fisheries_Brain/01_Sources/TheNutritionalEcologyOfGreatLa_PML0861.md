# Nutritional Ecology of Great Lakes *Cladophora glomerata*

#phycology #limnology #cladophora-glomerata

## Executive Summary

This EPA Environmental Research Laboratory (Duluth, MN) project summary reports a study of the nutritional ecology of the nuisance filamentous green alga [[Cladophora Glomerata]] in Green Bay and Lake Michigan. The work, conducted by Gerald C. Gerloff and J. Vic Muth of the University of Wisconsin, Madison, covers the period May 1, 1976 to September 30, 1978. Using [[Plant Tissue Analysis]] and [[Differential Nutrient Enrichment]] bioassays, the authors concluded that [[Phosphorus Limitation]] is the most likely key nutrient controlling nuisance growths, although [[Vitamin B12]] could not be ruled out as a critical factor.

## Background & Description

*Cladophora glomerata* is a filamentous green alga. Its nuisance growths in the Great Lakes are linked to municipal and industrial pollution. The stated goal was to identify the inorganic and organic nutrients most likely to limit and control its growth in Lake Michigan, and to continue experiments on its nutrition.

Sampling covered five Lake Michigan sites: four on the Green Bay side of Door County and one on the open Lake Michigan side. The sites were chosen to span a range of pollution and fertility from the [[Fox River]] inflow into lower Green Bay. Healthy green filaments were clipped from heavy growths on rocks, shorelines, and rock breakwaters, rinsed in distilled water, refrigerated, oven-dried, and ground. Nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and boron were measured by standard quantitative methods.

Methods included:
- **Plant analysis bioassay:** a tissue concentration at or below the [[Critical Concentration]] indicates a growth-limiting nutrient supply.
- **Differential nutrient enrichment:** lake water from each site was enriched with nutrients, with one or more omitted per treatment. Cultures were incubated for 21 days under continuous fluorescent light at 23±1°C. An element was judged unlikely to limit growth if lake water supported at least 50% of the yield in complete medium.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study frames *C. glomerata* as an indicator of nutrient enrichment in a system affected by municipal and industrial inputs. Its growth is controlled by specific nutrients in the water, so nutrient management is the proposed route to reducing nuisance growths. The authors also examined whether other algae in Lake Michigan produce growth-inhibiting compounds that might regulate *C. glomerata* populations.

Nutrient conditions were addressed through several lines of evidence:
- Phosphorus, nitrogen, and boron were assessed in tissue and water.
- Potassium and calcium were tested as solution concentrations in culture.
- Trace elements and vitamin B12 were tested in enrichment experiments.
- [[Nitzschia Palea]], a common Great Lakes diatom, was cultured to examine its silicon requirement.

## Key Findings

**Limiting nutrients**
- Phosphorus is very likely the critical nutrient in nuisance *C. glomerata* growths. Phosphorus supply is sometimes reduced to growth-limiting levels.
- Three bioassays (total P, hot-water-extractable P, and alkaline phosphatase activity) agreed and consistently indicated phosphorus limitation.
- Vitamin B12 may at times be critical, and this possibility could not be eliminated.
- In the enrichment tests, four nutrients (N, P, Fe, and B12) were most likely to limit growth. Calcium, magnesium, manganese, molybdenum, zinc, copper, boron, sulfur, and B12 were initially screened out as critical elements, though B12 was later reconsidered.

**Plant analysis validation**
- Critical nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations were relatively constant across different algal ages and environmental conditions. This supports plant analysis as a reliable nutrient bioassay.

**Solution concentration thresholds**
- Growth becomes less than optimal when phosphorus solution concentration falls to 0.014 ppm or below, even with adequate total phosphorus.
- Growth declines at potassium concentrations below 0.18 ppm and calcium concentrations below 0.24 ppm.

**Unusual nutritional requirements**
- *C. glomerata* has an unusual requirement for vitamin B12 and very high requirements for sulfur and boron. The sulfur requirement is unique among the green and blue-green algae compared.
- These sulfur and boron requirements are not considered critical in the field, and no sulfur deficiency was indicated in Great Lakes *Cladophora*.
- Lake water gave slightly better yields than distilled water. This suggests either an unidentified essential trace nutrient in lake water or non-optimal nutrient ratios in the synthetic medium.
- A mixture of suspected essential trace elements significantly increased yields, but systematic removal of individual elements did not identify a single responsible element. The authors describe the situation as "more complex than anticipated."

**Algal interactions and culture**
- Algae from Lake Michigan did not produce growth inhibitors at levels suggesting they are important in *C. glomerata* ecology.
- Silicon concentrations well above those in common media were essential for laboratory growth of *N. palea*. Media used for blue-green and green algae contain inadequate silicon for the diatom.
- Laboratory studies on heavy metal toxicity to *C. glomerata* were initiated but are not reported in detail.

**Conclusions**
1. *C. glomerata* becomes phosphorus deficient in parts of Green Bay during late summer. Phosphorus appears to be the key nutrient in nuisance growths.
2. A close correlation exists between nutrient inputs to Green Bay and phosphorus deficiency in *C. glomerata*.
3. Simple bioassays are available for evaluating nutrient supply for this alga.
4. Inhibitor production by common algae is probably not a major factor in its ecology.
