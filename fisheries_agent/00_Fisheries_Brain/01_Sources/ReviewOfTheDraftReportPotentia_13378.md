# Review Comments on the Draft Report on Non-native Fish Transfer via Central Arizona Project Canal Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #Cichlidae

## Executive Summary
This is a January 1991 transmittal letter from the Bureau of Reclamation (Arizona Projects Office) to Dr. Bill Matter (University of Arizona). It forwards agency and expert comments on the draft report "Potential for Transfer of Non-native Fish in Central Arizona Project Canal Waters to the Gila River System." Reclamation praises the report as objective and well written. It asks for clearer, more specific recommendations, especially on whether to modify [[Ashurst-Hayden Dam]], stop releases below it, or stop delivering CAP water. It also requests finalization by January 28, 1991, because irrigation water deliveries were pending. Enclosures from the [[Bureau of Land Management]], the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and [[W. L. Minckley]] add ecological and policy points.

## Background & Description
- The [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) canal carries Colorado River water across central Arizona. The concern is that non-native fishes in the canal could reach the [[Gila River]] and [[San Pedro River]] drainages and harm native fishes.
- The draft report examined five fish species of concern, including [[Striped Bass]], [[White Bass]], [[Yellow Bass]] and [[Tilapia]]. The BLM notes that all of them have appeared, or may soon appear, in Arizona in this century.
- The report's objectives 4 and 5 appear in the scope of work but were omitted from the draft. Objective 4 is to predict biological impact, with emphasis on native fish and prey species of the [[Bald Eagle]] (primarily two sucker species, carp and catfish). Objective 5 is to recommend procedures to reduce or eliminate the spread of non-native fish.
- Existing safeguards are the [[China Wash Electrical Fish Barrier]] and an electric barrier in the Pima Lateral Canal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Bald eagle foraging (Enclosure 2, Tom Gatz):**
  - White bass occur in Lake Pleasant (Arizona) and Caballo Reservoir (New Mexico), both with bald eagle nesting territories. At Caballo, carp were the main prey observed; catfish, coots, rabbits, packrats and carrion were also taken.
  - About 40,000 striped bass are stocked in [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]] every other year, and some enter Caballo.
  - Eagles fledged young from the Caballo nest in two of three active years. The Lake Pleasant nest has never succeeded since its discovery in 1984, for unknown reasons.
  - Yellow bass occur in Salt River reservoirs below Roosevelt Lake, with the largest populations in Saguaro and Apache Lakes. They made up 24 percent of fish prey remains at the Saguaro Lake nest in 1987 and 1988, and were taken mainly when dead or dying after spawning.
  - Tilapia occur at Lake Pleasant, Saguaro Lake and Alamo Lake. Three were recovered from the Alamo nest's prey remains, and eagles have fledged from the Alamo nest every active year since 1988.
  - Eagles are described as having a "flexible foraging strategy," taking whatever prey is available.
- **Gila River conditions:** Citing Kepner et al. (1983), Reclamation notes that fish numbers in the Gila below Coolidge Dam were low. High irrigation flows cause scouring and turbidity, which limit primary productivity and habitat, so tilapia "proliferating" there seems unlikely.
- **Other expert views:** [[W. L. Minckley]] considers the report largely academic because most species studied are too large for the small waters of concern. He sees small, non-native fishes as the greater threat to isolated native-fish habitats such as [[Aravaipa Creek]]: sunfishes ([[Lepomis]] spp.), [[Red Shiner]], catfishes and poeciliids. He also stresses human transfer and fish-farm escapes, and suggests the long-term problem may be plants and insects.

## Key Findings
- **General request:** the recommendations should state specifically what should or should not be done.
- **Tilapia:** the draft's conclusion that tilapia are the only species likely to establish self-sustaining populations, but that it is "not clear that they will cause significant harm," is ambiguous. Reclamation asks for further analysis of likely impacts.
- **China Wash barrier:** given the apparently low threat (per the study and Minckley), Reclamation asks whether the barrier should be discontinued given operation, maintenance and safety costs. If kept, should it be maintained for the project life or reevaluated periodically?
- **Pima Lateral barrier:** the draft says it serves no significant function, so Reclamation asks how CAP water should be delivered to the Florence-Casa Grande Canal.
- **Monitoring (point 3):** Reclamation wants guidance on where, when, how often and for how many years to sample. It proposes:
  - starting in the CAP canal, farm delivery canals, sumps, siphons and the Gila below Ashurst/Hayden Dam, then working upstream as findings dictate;
  - sampling in fall during the canal outage;
  - reevaluating every 2 or 3 years.
- **Public education (point 4):** if it is meant as a recommendation, the report should say so.
- **Point 5 (the key recommendation)** weighs three options:
  1. Structural changes to Ashurst-Hayden Dam, estimated by Reclamation at about $500,000.
  2. Stopping releases below the diversion dam. This may be feasible for irrigator-controlled releases, but flows originating above the dam, such as the San Pedro, cannot be stopped without a very large dam.
  3. Not delivering CAP water near the Gila drainage, which is possible but may not be politically practical.
  
  The report judged these costs probably not commensurate with the probable harm, and Reclamation wants a clearer recommendation.
- **Corrections:**
  - A 27-pound striped bass reported from a Mesa lake was a hoax; the angler brought the fish from another source, assumed to be the Colorado River.
  - Figure 2 should be moved.
  - Mixed English and metric units should be harmonized, and misspellings fixed ("releassed," "pyholimnion" should be hypolimnion, "white white" should be white bass).
- **AGFD additions:**
  - A 10-inch striped bass, an Arizona inland record, was caught in December 1990 at the 99th Avenue overpass in Peoria by a 5-year-old girl. The resulting publicity could increase angling and illegal human transport of striped bass from CAP waters.
  - White bass have moved up the Agua Fria River above Lake Pleasant during high spring runoff, last about 11 years earlier.
- **BLM concerns:**
  - The report is too narrow for a comprehensive assessment. It should address long-term threats, future species becoming available to the CAP, and effects on reintroducing "big river" native fishes in the upper San Pedro.
  - It should consider channel and flathead catfish.
  - Close proximity of canals to natural waters and illegal stocking should be added as a sixth condition affecting fish entry to natural waters.
  - BLM suggests that modifying Ashurst/Hayden Dam, or isolating intact native-fish streams with barrier dams, may be prudent.
  - Timing of forage availability is critical to eagle reproductive success, and white and striped bass may not become available at the proper time.
- **Minckley:** he generally concurs with the conclusions and warns of homogenization of Arizona's fish fauna through interconnected waterways. He notes that rainbow smelt, if ever introduced, would likely find central Arizona waters too severe to survive.
