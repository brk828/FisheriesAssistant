# Irrigation of the Gills in Fishes: Studies of the Mechanism of Branchial Irrigation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Teleostei

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by Richard L. Saunders, published in the [[Canadian Journal of Zoology]], examines how teleost fishes move water over their gills. The author developed a [[Cannulation Technique]] that measures hydrostatic pressure in the oral and opercular cavities without interfering with normal breathing. The work covers three freshwater species: [[White Sucker]], [[Brown Bullhead]], and [[Common Carp]]. The study finds that water flows over the gills almost continuously, with only brief back-pressure reversals. It also reports that the slits between adjacent gill arches open rhythmically during moderate and heavy breathing, a feature the author argues earlier workers overlooked.

## Background & Description

**Branchial irrigation** is the passage of water over the gills for respiration. In teleosts it is driven by active respiratory movements and, in some species, by forward swimming. The author describes the [[Branchial Pump]] as two coupled pumps:

- **Oral cavity (force pump):** Pushes water over the gills. Velar [[Oral Valves]] (maxillary or mandibular) prevent regurgitation.
- **Opercular cavity (suction pump):** Draws water over the gills as the opercula abduct. Flexible posterior opercular extensions act as valves against the cleithra.

The gills are supported by four pairs of bony arches. Each arch carries two rows of filaments (hemibranchiae) bearing secondary lamellae. The prevailing view, associated with [[Bijtel]] and others, held that the filament tips of adjacent hemibranchiae touch throughout quiet breathing, so water passes only through interlamellar pores.

Earlier pressure studies used hypodermic cannulae placed between the lips or behind the opercular valves. These methods were disruptive: fish struggled, cannulae were hard to hold, and placement interfered with the membranous valves. Prior investigators included [[Woskoboinikoff]], [[Hughes and Shelton]], and [[Van Dam]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study conditions**

- Species were held outdoors in an 850-litre plywood tank lined with black neoprene to reduce abrasion.
- Winter temperatures were 12–16 °C and summer temperatures 15–19 °C.
- Fish were acclimated to 20 °C by daily 1 °C increments, for a minimum of 4 weeks.
- Recordings used an aquarium of 23 litres with running water, in which dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide could be varied.
- Heavy breathing was induced by raising the partial pressure of free carbon dioxide.

**Anaesthesia and recovery**

- Fish were immobilised in a 1:20,000 solution of [[Tricaine Methanesulphonate]] (MS-222) in under 3 minutes.
- They resumed breathing and swimming within 2 minutes of return to fresh water.
- After surgery, fish recovered in running tap water at 20 °C and were invariably resting and breathing quietly the next morning.
- Small wounds from cannula removal healed quickly, and fish could be reused after about 2 months.

**Comparative context**

The author cites marine species studied by [[Hughes]] (1960). Back-pressure phases were found in herring, whiting, horse mackerel, wrasse, and five-bearded rockling. They were absent in the bottom-living butterfly blenny, plaice, and merry sole. Back-pressure phases were also absent in the carp and in certain marine flatfishes during heavy breathing.

## Key Findings

### Methodology

- **Cannula design:** Polyethylene tubing (Intramedic PE 160; outside diameter 0.152 mm, inside diameter 0.114 mm) was inserted through the [[Cleithrum]] into the opercular cavity. A second cannula entered the oral cavity through the rostrum, in the midline anterior to the nostrils.
- **Fixation:** A heated flange held the opercular cannula against the posterior wall of the cavity. A plastic washer on the oral cannula prevented it from being pulled out.
- **Recording system:** Cannulae were connected to [[Segment Tambour]] diaphragms carrying small mirrors. An [[Optical Lever]] projected the reflected light onto a revolving drum to record pressure over time.
- **Calibration:** Each tambour was calibrated against a U-tube water manometer. The author's worked example gives a sensitivity factor of 1.21 (7.5 mm versus 6.2 mm deflection at 10 cm water).
- **Gill-slit observation:** [[Cinematography]] at high shutter speeds (8 mm and 16 mm cameras) recorded transient openings between gill arches.

### Quiet Breathing

- Oral-cavity pressure was predominantly positive relative to the opercular cavity in all three species. This positive gradient is required for water to flow from mouth to opercula.
- A brief back-pressure phase, in which opercular pressure exceeded oral pressure, occurred in each cycle. It occupied a small fraction of the cycle in bullheads and carp, and a slightly larger fraction in suckers.
- Flow over the gills was described as nearly continuous.
- The **opercular suction pump** was dominant in sucker and bullhead. In carp, the **oral pressure pump** contributed slightly more.

### Species-Specific Breathing Patterns

- **Quiet breathing rates:** about 65 per minute (sucker), 38 (bullhead), and 24 (carp).
- **Apnoea:** bullheads paused for up to 30 seconds; suckers rarely paused for 10–15 seconds.
- **Carp pattern:** trains of two or three rapid movements followed by an apnoeic pause. One cycle lasted about 5 seconds, and the pattern persisted for up to 5 minutes. Oral pressure built for about 14 seconds and then attenuated over the following 34 seconds.
- **Pressure amplitude:** the sucker's excursions were more than twice those of the bullhead. The carp's amplitude was more than twice that of the sucker (fish of equal size).

### Heavy Breathing

- Breathing rates under elevated carbon dioxide were approximately 120 (sucker), 50 (bullhead), and 110 (carp) per minute.
- Heavy breathing was more regular, with fewer pauses and amplitude changes.
- In suckers and bullheads, back-pressure phases persisted but were shorter and lower in magnitude.
- The carp switched from its slow, intermittent pattern to a rapid, regular one. Oral pressure stayed above opercular pressure through the whole cycle, so no back-pressure reversal occurred.
- Sucker and bullhead patterns closely resembled those of [[Brown Trout]], [[Tench]], and [[Roach]] recorded by Hughes and Shelton. The carp pattern did not resemble that of the tench or roach, which belong to the same family, Cyprinidae.

### Rhythmic Opening of Gill Slits

- Openings appeared consistently between hemibranchiae of adjacent gill arches while the opercular valve was open during moderate and heavy breathing in all three species.
- Quietly breathing fish could not be observed this way because the opercular opening did not widen enough to expose the gills.
- Ciné sequences (Plate II, Fig. 9) show open slits in carp and sucker.

### Interpretation

- **Gill resistance:** The author argues that gill slits stay closed during quiet breathing. At higher flow rates they open, either forced by water rushing through the pharynx or actively during arch flexure. Resistance falls as slits open, which explains the decline in gill resistance reported by Hughes and Shelton.
- **Safety valve:** Slit opening may reduce the oral–opercular pressure difference, protecting delicate lamellae from excessive pressure during heavy breathing.
- **Swimming:** Slit opening could matter most during swimming, when large volumes of water pass over the gills. The author cites [[Atlantic Mackerel]] (Hall, 1930), which asphyxiates if prevented from swimming, and suggests that closed slits could add drag.
- **Efficiency:** The author suggests that the species maintaining the most constant positive pressure gradient across the gills would be best at taking up oxygen. Slit opening could regulate the upper limit of water velocity through the interlamellar spaces.

### Conclusions

1. Pressure relationships in the branchial pump agree with earlier findings: oral pressure is positive relative to opercular pressure except during a brief back-pressure phase.
2. The carp has a distinctive breathing pattern of rapid trains followed by apnoea, lasting about 5 seconds per cycle.
3. Gill slits open rhythmically during moderate and heavy breathing. This allows part of the water to bypass the interlamellar spaces, which earlier workers considered the only route.
