# Biogeography in the Death Valley Region: Evidence from Springsnails (Hydrobiidae: Tryonia)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This study uses allozyme electrophoresis and mitochondrial COI DNA sequences to reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships of the nine [[Tryonia]] springsnail species endemic to the [[Death Valley System]]. Both data sets show that the regional fauna is non-monophyletic. Upper Amargosa basin species form a clade sister to a snail from the [[Colorado Basin]], while lower Amargosa basin species have a complex history, with two species closely related to the estuarine *Tryonia imitator* of coastal California. The authors find the same pattern in the co-occurring [[Death Valley Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon*), and interpret both faunas against a proposed model of early [[Lower Colorado River]] history involving an integrated Amargosa-Colorado paleoriver.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** The Death Valley system comprises the isolated Amargosa, Mohave and Owens basins, linked during the late Pleistocene pluvial period via overflowing lakes that terminated in Lake Manly.
- **Genus Tryonia:** Small, gill-breathing, benthic, obligately aquatic snails with direct development and limited dispersal. Nineteen described western species are mostly restricted to single springs or drainages. Their distribution is assumed to reflect hydrographic history.
- **Death Valley fauna:** Nine species, all but one endemic. Four occur in the upper Amargosa River (three endemic to [[Ash Meadows]]: *T. angulata*, *T. elata*, *T. ericae*, plus *T. variegata*). Four are restricted to the lower Amargosa basin (*T. robusta*, *T. salina*, *T. margae*, *T. rowlandsi*). *T. protea* is parthenogenetic, while other species are sexual.
- **Competing hypotheses:** Taylor (1985) proposed that Amargosa Tryonia were allied with *T. clathrata*; Hershler (1989) proposed that Amargosa Tryonia were monophyletic with *T. protea* closely allied to one of them.
- **Outgroups:** *T. imitator* (estuarine, coastal California), *T. clathrata* (southern Nevada, along the pluvial White River) and *T. brevissima* (peninsular Florida, used to root trees).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Tryonia and pupfish are confined to the upper Owens basin and the Amargosa basin; both groups are absent from the lower Owens-drainage valleys (Indian Wells, Searles, Panamint).
- Tryonia in the Death Valley system typically occur in thermal, highly mineralized springs, often co-occurring with pupfish.
- Allozyme diversity in these snails is low, reflecting founder effects and isolation typical of desert spring hydrobiids, comparable to other surveys (Hamlin, 1996; Ponder et al., 1996).
- Pupfish are thought to have colonized stressful desert waters resembling marginal marine habitats (Smith, 1981), paralleling the estuarine affinity of some snails.
- Geological context: the Colorado River incised the Grand Canyon and attained its modern course less than 5 Ma; the early Amargosa drainage was presumably disrupted by Pliocene diversion into the Death Valley trough.

## Key Findings

- **Non-monophyly:** Allozyme and mtDNA data both show that the nine Death Valley Tryonia species are non-monophyletic, owing to placement of *T. clathrata* and *T. imitator* within the in-group.
- **Upper Amargosa clade:** *T. angulata*, *T. elata*, *T. ericae* and *T. variegata* form a clade, to which *T. clathrata* (Colorado basin) is sister; this cluster is more similar to the Owens basin *T. protea* than to lower Amargosa species.
- **Lower Amargosa complexity:** *T. margae* and *T. salina*, together with *T. imitator*, form a clade sister to *T. protea*. *T. robusta* is the most divergent Death Valley species and occupies a basal position.
- **Pupfish parallel:** Mitochondrial evidence places the Owens pupfish *C. radiosus* closer to the lower Colorado basin *C. macularius* than to the three Amargosa pupfish, which form a monophyletic unit. The authors note that a diphyletic origin of the pupfish fauna is "an equally if not more tenable hypothesis."
- **Taxonomic revision:** Genetic distances suggest *T. angulata* and *T. variegata* are confused. Populations of *T. variegata* from Amargosa Valley (Shoshone, Saratoga Springs) were substantially differentiated from Ash Meadows populations and probably warrant separate specific status. The Saratoga Spring sample was more similar to *T. angulata* (p=0.67%) than to Devils Hole *T. variegata* (p=5.2%), indicating the species needs revision.
- **Allozyme divergence:** Two *T. robusta* samples were five times more divergent (D=0.09) than any other conspecific comparison; the authors cite Woodruff et al. (1988), who suggest D<0.10 is typical within species and D>0.50 between species for mollusks.
- **Molecular data:** The 600 bp COI alignment had 135 variable and 82 phylogenetically informative sites. Parsimony gave four equally parsimonious trees of 211 steps (CI=0.73, RI=0.69). Tree-length distribution was significantly skewed (g1 = -1.09), indicating a strong phylogenetic signal.
- **Biogeographic model:** The authors favor Howard's (1996) hypothesis that an older integrated Amargosa-Colorado paleoriver allowed transfer of snails and pupfish before incision of the Grand Canyon. The alternative of westward drainage from Owens Valley into San Joaquin Valley is also discussed as a route to coastal faunas.
- **Fossil support:** Fossil Tryonia are known from Pleistocene Death Valley deposits and Plio-Pleistocene Tulare Formation strata (as *Calipyrgula*), and a Miocene Mint Canyon Formation record was identified as *T. imitator*. A late Miocene pupfish fossil from northern Death Valley provides a minimum pupfish date.
- **Limitations:** Only three of the 13 extra-limital species were analyzed, so monophyly of the fauna was not fully tested. Allozyme pooling was required for the smallest species (*T. elata*, *T. ericae*).
