# Fishery Survey of the Colorado River Below Boulder Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #rainbow-trout

## Executive Summary

This 1942 survey by [[James W. Morrert]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) describes the [[Colorado River]] for roughly 25 miles below [[Boulder Dam]] and proposes a tentative fishery management plan. The dam's reservoir, [[Lake Mead]], traps silt, and water is released from deep, cold levels. These conditions create a clear, cold, and productive stretch of river that supports a stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery. The river carries a dense algal blanket, and the trout feed mainly on that algae and the insects living in it. The author found no clear evidence of natural trout reproduction. The report therefore recommends continued stocking of larger fish, no closed season, and further study of reproduction and growth.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Boulder Dam closes a canyon on the Colorado River. The reservoir behind it captures sediment from a drainage of about 167,000 square miles, so water released downstream is free of silt.
- **Purpose:** The report presents data from a preliminary survey and proposes a management plan. Sportsmen's groups, civic clubs, and agencies in Nevada and Arizona requested the work.
- **Prior work:** The author drew on unpublished reports by [[Davis and Needham]] (1941), Foster, and Madsen. Davis and Needham reported on stomach contents of nine trout, and Madsen visited the river in October 1940.
- **Access:** Canyon walls rise 400 to 500 feet. Only a few washes reach the river, including [[Ringbolt Rapids]] (about 4 miles below the dam) and [[Willow Beach]] (10 miles below). The only river access on the Nevada side is at [[Eldorado Canyon]]. Boats need at least 10-horsepower motors to run the large rapids upstream. Flash floods can wash out access roads without warning.
- **Species recorded:** The author listed seven fish species present in or observed from the river. Four are introduced: rainbow trout, largemouth bass, carp, and channel catfish. The report notes that other species are probably present and that a seine survey at low water is needed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water release and temperature**
- Water can leave the reservoir from the surface spillways, the 1,050-foot level (about 120 to 170 feet deep), or the 900-foot level (about 270 to 320 feet deep).
- In 1940 and 1941, discharge from the 900-foot level ranged from 54° to 57° F, and from the 1,050-foot level from 55° to 61° F.
- Monthly records at Willow Beach (1936 to 1941) showed little fluctuation in 1939 and 1940. Spillway discharge in August to November 1941 raised the average to 61° to 69.5° F, with a maximum of 77° F in August.
- On October 31, 1941, the water was 67° F at Eldorado Canyon, where discharge at the dam was 64° F. Air temperature was 73° to 79° F. Summer air temperatures in the region reach 110° to 126° F.

**Flow and physical structure**
- Flow at the time of the study was 27,000 to 35,000 cubic feet per second (cfs). Madsen recorded 4,000 to 12,000 cfs in 1940.
- Assuming 20,000 cfs, the river averages about 500 feet wide. The author estimated about 1,600 acres of trout water.
- The gradient is about 3 feet per mile. Some rapids drop 3 to 6 feet within a quarter mile, and quiet stretches have 1 to 2 feet per mile. Flow averages about 4 miles per hour.
- Gravel shoals are visible at depths of 15 to 20 feet in riffles and quieter areas.
- The lower limit of managed trout water is set at Eldorado Canyon, where silt bars and high turbidity reduce fish food production. Some trout production likely continues below it.

**Water chemistry**
- Dissolved oxygen was 9.3 parts per million (ppm) at Willow Beach. Rapids and turbulence keep oxygen high downstream.
- pH was a constant 8.2, so the river was highly alkaline. No carbon dioxide was detected.
- Carbonates and bicarbonates measured 121 ppm. Total dissolved solids were 650 ppm (reported by Leo Dunbar, U.S. Bureau of Reclamation). These are mainly calcium sulfate (gypsum), magnesium sulfate, and sodium salts, with small amounts of sodium chloride.

**Vegetation and food base**
- Aquatic plants are scarce apart from algae. The main alga is [[Cladophora]], with some green and blue-green algae. Wherever riffles and gravel bars exist, Cladophora forms a near-continuous blanket.
- Reeds, cattails, and bulrushes grow in quiet water. Roots of cottonwood, tamarisk, and willow along the banks provide fish cover.
- Quantitative bottom sampling was limited by high water. Sandy areas were barren. The riffles supported most food organisms, including mayfly nymphs (mainly [[Callibaetis]]), midge larvae and pupae, microcaddis larvae and pupae, and stonefly nymphs (rare). Daphnia and Diaptomus occurred among the algal filaments.
- In April 1941, 100,000 freshwater shrimp were planted upstream of Willow Beach. None were found in November 1941 or in trout stomachs. High water may have hindered the search.

**Growth and condition**
- The growing season is long, with stable temperatures and year-round algal and insect production. Scale growth was uniform, and the author judged growth to be continuous and rapid, though this was not proven.

## Key Findings

**Trout growth and condition (1941)**
- Thirty-seven trout measuring 9½ to 17¼ inches total length were examined for scales.
- Six fish of 10 to 11 inches averaged 7.4 ounces with a coefficient of condition ([[Coefficient of Condition]]) of 1.09.
- Three fish of 11 to 12 inches averaged 8.2 ounces with a coefficient of 1.00.
- One 13½-inch fish weighed 13.5 ounces (coefficient 1.01).
- Five fish of 14 to 15 inches averaged 1 lb 0.8 oz (coefficient 1.00).
- The longest fish, 17¼ inches, weighed about 1 lb 6 oz (the OCR for the ounce figure is unclear).
- Scale age determination failed because growth rings were too uniform. Some scales showed possible spawning checks near the margin, which may reflect a fall spawning habit inherited from hatchery stock.

**Diet**
- Forty-three trout collected from August 27 to November 16, 1941 fed heavily on algae. In all but two stomachs, algae made up 70 to 98 percent of contents.
- Mayfly nymphs and subimagoes (Callibaetis) occurred in 81 percent of stomachs. Midges occurred in 84 percent. Daphnia and Diaptomus appeared in 13 and 9 stomachs. Microcaddis occurred in 50 percent.
- Terrestrial invertebrates were uncommon. Angleworms and salmon eggs used as bait appeared in many stomachs.
- The author suggests trout eat algae for the aquatic life living on it, but this was not proven.

**Reproduction**
- No definitive evidence of natural spawning was found. Fishermen reported small trout in January and February 1941, when only yearlings were being stocked.
- Gravel is plentiful, and daily fluctuation was under one foot during the study. However, flows over six years ranged from 2,500 to 35,000 cfs, and flow was cut off entirely in February 1935. The author considers reproduction research essential for long-term management.

**Stocking history ([[Stocking Records]])**
- The first rainbow trout were planted on November 7, 1935: 25,000 four-inch fish below the dam.
- Total plantings to 1941 included 21,000 six-inch fish, 50,000 four-inch fish, and 148,000 fish of 1 to 2½ inches. All came from the U.S. Fish Hatchery at Springville, Utah.
- In December 1941, 62,000 three-inch rainbows were delivered to the Las Vegas Hatchery for overwinter holding and spring planting.
- Table 1 lists the individual plantings. Most were made below Boulder Dam, at Eldorado Canyon, or at Willow Beach.

**Management recommendations**
1. No closed season on trout fishing for now. Season rules should stay unchanged until natural spawning is better understood.
2. Creel limits are satisfactory. Arizona and Nevada regulations should be made uniform for this water. A minimum size limit of seven inches should be imposed.
3. Further attempts to introduce food organisms are unnecessary.
4. If angling pressure does not rise and natural reproduction remains unknown, stock 30,000 five- to seven-inch rainbows annually. Fish should be reared to 5 to 7 inches before planting, since 50,000 four-inch fish yield fewer catchable fish than 50,000 six-inch fish. If that is not possible, plant 60,000 to 75,000 two- to three-inch fingerlings. Rearing to yearling size reduces the heavy predation that small fish face when stocked among large fish.
