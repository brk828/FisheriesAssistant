# Letter on Endangered Species Recovery Policy and "Johnny Applefish" Approach

#conservation-biology #endangered-species-policy #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1987 letter from Donald W. to Dr. W.L. Minckley discusses concerns about the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's endangered species recovery approach, particularly the practice of spreading rare species across multiple locations ("Johnny Applefish" approach) and over-reliance on aquarium facilities and hatcheries as substitutes for habitat protection and in-situ conservation.

## Background & Description

The correspondence addresses a shift in endangered species management philosophy within the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] during the 1980s. The author critiques what he terms the "Johnny Applefish" approach—a strategy of establishing multiple populations of rare species in non-native locations as a means of avoiding formal [[Endangered Species Act]] listing requirements and addressing recovery obligations.

The letter specifically mentions [[Razorback Sucker]] as a case where delays in implementing necessary conservation programs may have foreclosed recovery options. The author expresses concern that bureaucratic avoidance of controversy takes precedence over congressionally mandated conservation purposes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The letter reflects broader tensions in endangered species management between ex-situ conservation (hatcheries, aquarium facilities, refugia) and in-situ habitat protection. The author acknowledges that hatchery and refugium populations serve important roles as genetic depositories to prevent extinction, but argues they should be used within their limitations rather than as primary conservation tools.

The correspondence indicates that transplantation outside native ranges was being used as justification for species recovery, raising concerns about the ecological appropriateness and long-term viability of such approaches. The author suggests this represents a degradation of conservation priorities, where facilities meant to serve as temporary genetic repositories become endpoints rather than tools supporting wild population recovery.

## Key Findings

**Policy Concerns:**
- The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] was increasingly relying on captive facilities and translocation as primary conservation strategies
- This approach was being used to avoid listing species and to circumvent difficult habitat protection decisions
- The strategy represented a shift away from addressing root causes of species decline

**Conservation Implications:**
- Over-reliance on ex-situ conservation may lead to delayed action until recovery options are foreclosed
- Aquarium facilities risk becoming "oddities" vulnerable to budget cuts rather than valued genetic repositories
- The approach may foster complacency among managers who believe they are accomplishing meaningful conservation

**Specific Case:**
- [[Razorback Sucker]] cited as example where prolonged delays potentially eliminated viable recovery options

The author offers to provide additional examples from the region where hatcheries and transplantation outside native ranges were used as justification for recovery programs.
