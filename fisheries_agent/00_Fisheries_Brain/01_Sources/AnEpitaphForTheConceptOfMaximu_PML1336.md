# An Epitaph for the Concept of Maximum Sustained Yield

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

P. A. Larkin's 1977 keynote address to the [[American Fisheries Society]] (published in *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, Vol. 106, No. 1) is a critical retrospective on [[Maximum Sustained Yield]] (MSY), the dominant fisheries management paradigm of the mid-twentieth century. Larkin traces the intellectual history of MSY from early catch equations through [[Beverton-Holt Model]] theory, argues that MSY is biologically unattainable for single species or mixed communities, is economically irrelevant or harmful under open entry, and is too vague to guide decisions when reframed as [[Optimum Yield]]. The essay ends with a symbolic "epitaph" for MSY and a call for management that accepts risk, complexity, and social tradeoffs.

## Background & Description

The paper is a personal, historically framed keynote rather than a research study. It reviews:

- **Early foundations:** Baranov (1918) catch equation; Russell (1931) and Graham (1935) dynamic pool models; Thompson and Bell (1934) on Pacific halibut; Hile (1936) on Wisconsin cisco.
- **Golden age (post-WWII):** Ricker (1948, 1958) handbooks; Fry (1947) virtual population analysis; Schaefer (1954) surplus production model; the [[Beverton-Holt Model]] (Beverton and Holt 1957), described by Larkin as the "crowning achievement" of the MSY movement.
- **Later refinements:** Gulland (1969) manual; Doubleday (1976) on optimum rather than maximum yields; Ricker (1973) on growth versus recruitment overfishing.
- **Context:** Rise of the FAO as an international actor; world fisheries catch of about 20 million tons at the time of MSY's consolidation; the 1976 draft U.S. Fishery Conservation and Management Act; and the 1976 Canadian fisheries policy statement.

Larkin frames the essay with a retrospective on his own graduate training and on debates between fisheries biologists and limnologists (e.g., the Langlois–Van Oosten debate over [[Lake Erie]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biological complications**
- For most species, the critical harvest age is close to first maturity, because growth in weight is rapid and natural mortality low before maturity. Intensive fishing therefore leaves spawning stocks dominated by young, first-time spawners.
- Egg quality may decline in such stocks (Nikolskiy 1965; Bagenal 1973), and loss of older age classes makes egg or larval failure far more damaging. [[Clupeid Fisheries]] are cited as prime examples.
- Depensatory mortality, possibly linked to predation at low prey densities (Neave 1954; Larkin 1973; Holling 1973; Clark 1974), can prevent recovery of depressed stocks.
- Genetic variability among local subpopulations is a concern. [[Pacific Salmon]] are the main example: the aggregate run is now less than half its size of a century earlier (per Larkin), with many substocks lost. Loftus (1976) argues removal of less productive components is widespread, including in [[Lake Trout]] and [[Whitefish]].

**Mixed fisheries and communities**
- Multispecies gear progressively eliminates less productive species as the most productive species is harvested to MSY. The [[Great Lakes]] are cited as a warning case (Regier et al. 1969; Smith 1968).
- Predator–prey and competitive interactions (Volterra 1931; Lett and Koehler 1976 on [[Atlantic Herring]] and mackerel) make species-by-species MSY management impractical.
- Limnological work (Hrbáček et al. 1961; Brooks and Dodson 1965) showed that fish predation shapes zooplankton community composition, supporting a whole-ecosystem view.

**Economic context**
- Under open entry, commercial fisheries tend toward or beyond biological MSY and toward lower economic yields, reaching a "bionomic" equilibrium where profit is dissipated (Gordon 1954; Clark and Munro 1975).
- Recreational fisheries generally have little relevance to MSY, as their aims are recreation and aesthetic satisfaction.

## Key Findings

1. **MSY is not attainable for single species in practice.** Historic-statistics-based MSY estimates are unlikely to be sustainable; if MSY exists, it is a yield associated with high risk of recruitment failure, given depleted or eliminated substocks.
2. **Mixed-species fisheries cannot achieve MSY for each species.** The aggregate yield at most equals what can persist when less productive species are reduced below their individual MSYs. Setting species-specific gear regulation would cost more than the resource is worth.
3. **Fishery statistics are imprecise.** Larkin describes world fisheries statistics as "more precise than accurate," with incomplete data and guesses.
4. **Economic MSY is distinct from biological MSY.** Maximum economic yield occurs at lower effort levels; MSY is an "indicator of biological pressure" rather than a holy duty. The best known constraint, limiting entry, is rarely applied to commercial fisheries.
5. **Recreational fisheries are poorly suited to MSY.** Recreational goals (aesthetics, ethics, distribution of catch) make MSY of little meaning.
6. **Optimum yield is too vague to be operational.** Roedel's (1975) definitions of optimum yield and optimum sustained yield are described as "virtually meaningless" and as having no operational basis, given the diversity of natural and social systems.
7. **Two extreme management paths exist.** A technocentric approach (fish first, economics second, social problems third) and a minimal-intervention market approach. Most current policy, including the 1976 Canadian statement, is a "middle-of-the-road" mix that Larkin views as preserving flexibility at the cost of clarity.
8. **Larkin's personal position:** a technocentric approach with the fish first, emphasizing caution with resources, documented reasons for decisions, and intergenerational responsibility.
9. **Epitaph:** MSY is retired as a sole guide for management, and the concept is acknowledged as having curbed many early fishery problems.
