## Executive Summary
This short field-note paper by Calvin R. Fremling (1960) documents a behavioral observation of the subimaginal molt (ecdysis from subimago to imago) in the mayfly **[[Caenis Hilaris]]** (Say), family **[[Caenidae]]**, order **[[Ephemeroptera]]**. The observation, made at Lock 19 near Keokuk, Iowa on the Mississippi River, supports and extends earlier work by **[[Edmunds 1956]]**, who argued that *Caenis* mayflies only *appear* to molt in flight because the subimaginal exuviae remain attached to the imaginal cerci after the insect has actually landed and molted at rest. This paper is not a fish study per se, but was funded and published under the auspices of the **[[Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]]**, reflecting the broader ecological context of aquatic insect studies supporting fisheries science.

## Background & Description
- **Taxon:** *Caenis hilaris* (Say), family Caenidae, order Ephemeroptera (mayflies).
- **Life stage examined:** Subimago transitioning to imago (the final molt in mayfly ontogeny).
- **Key structure:** Imaginal **cerci** — the subimaginal exuviae (shed cuticle) frequently remained clinging to these paired tail filaments after molting, creating the visual illusion of mid-flight exuviation.
- **Wing posture during molt:** Wings held horizontally immediately upon alighting, prior to and during exuviation.
- No quantitative morphometric data (e.g., body length, wing span) are reported; the paper is behavioral/observational in scope.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** Emergent adult mayflies were observed molting on a solid substrate — an overturned lifeboat — near a lock and dam structure (Lock 19) on the Mississippi River at Keokuk, Iowa, indicating use of artificial/anthropogenic surfaces as molting perches near large river systems.
- **Behavioral ecology:** Subimagoes exhibited a stereotyped emergence sequence: landing → wing extension → rapid exuviation → flight as imago. This is a terrestrial/aerial resting behavior tied closely to the aquatic larval-to-adult transition typical of **[[Ephemeroptera]]** life cycles.
- **Temporal pattern:** Molting activity was tied to **crepuscular/evening conditions** (observations from 7:00–7:20 p.m. CST), with molting speed increasing as darkness approached — suggesting light level or approaching darkness as a proximate trigger accelerating exuviation.
- **Population context:** "Large numbers" of subimagoes were molting simultaneously, and activity continued in "undiminished numbers" until observation was cut short by darkness, indicating a synchronized mass emergence event typical of many Ephemeroptera species (a phenomenon relevant to aquatic food-web dynamics and as a prey resource for fish, per the **[[Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]]** sponsorship).

## Key Findings
- **Molting occurs at rest, not truly in flight:** Direct observation confirms **[[Edmunds 1956]]**'s hypothesis that *Caenis* subimagoes land on a substrate, molt, and only then take flight as imagoes — contradicting the appearance of in-flight molting caused by exuviae clinging to the cerci.
- **Rapid ecdysis timing:** The full process of landing, molting, and departing as an imago ranged from **8 to 65 seconds**, with times shortening (frequently <10 seconds) as darkness increased after 7:10 p.m.
- **Exuviae retention:** Subimaginal exuviae commonly remained attached to the **imaginal cerci** even after the imago flew off, which is the likely source of prior misinterpretations of "flight molting" in *Caenis* and related genera.
- **Environmental trigger:** Onset of darkness appears to hasten the exuviation process, suggesting light-dependent regulation of this final ecdysis event.
- **Broader relevance:** As a mayfly species, *Caenis hilaris* is ecologically significant within freshwater systems such as the Mississippi River as a component of aquatic invertebrate communities and a food source underpinning fisheries productivity, consistent with the paper's institutional support from fisheries research programs.
