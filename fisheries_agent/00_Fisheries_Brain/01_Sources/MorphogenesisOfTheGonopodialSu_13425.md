# Morphogenesis of the Gonopodial Suspensorium in *Gambusia affinis* and Induction of Male Suspensorial Characters in Females by Androgenic Hormones

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1942 study by C. L. Turner, published in the [[Journal of Experimental Zoology]], examines how the skeletal support of the male anal fin (the gonopodial suspensorium) develops in [[Gambusia affinis]] and whether it can be induced in females. The author homologizes suspensorial elements between sexes, describes the staged development of the male suspensorium, and tests how female suspensoria respond to [[Ethynyl Testosterone]] and [[Methyl Testosterone]] at graded concentrations. Female suspensoria developed many male-type characters under hormone treatment, but the fin itself did not shift position, and the dose–response pattern differed among structures.

## Background & Description

- **Sexual dimorphism:** In poeciliid fishes the male anal fin is modified into a copulatory organ, the [[Gonopodium]], and other fins often show male-specific structures.
- **Suspensorium:** The hemal and interhemal ray complexes suspend the gonopodium from the vertebral column. The term "gonopodial suspensorium" was introduced by [[Howell Rivero and Hubbs]] (1936). Suspensorial characters are constant within a species and differ among species, so they are useful taxonomically.
- **Prior work:** Structure was figured by [[Langer]] (1913), [[Collier]] (1936) and [[Hollister]] (1940), but the morphogenesis and homologies had not been worked out in detail for both sexes.
- **Experimental conditions:** Earlier work by the author showed that structural modification depends on fish age and temperature. All experiments used females 23–27 mm long at approximately 23 °C. Hormone solutions were renewed every 2 days, and exposure continued until gonopodial metamorphosis was complete or development ceased.
- **Methods:** Specimens were cleared and stained with alizarin, destained in acid alcohol, cleared in 2% KOH, and mounted. Stages in males were identified by the number of segments in the third ray of the anal fin. Hormones were supplied by the Schering Corporation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a laboratory developmental study. It does not report field habitat, distribution, or natural populations beyond noting that the species is viviparous and that related taxa (e.g., [[Belonesox belizanus]], [[Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus]], [[Gambusia affinis holbrookii]]) occur in other regions. Temperature (about 23 °C) and hormone concentration were the controlled environmental variables. The author also notes that the Bermuda shallow-water survey by Hollister (1940) is a source of comparative data.

## Key Findings

**Homology and anatomy**
- Females and males share the same basic set: four hemal spines attached to vertebrae 13–16 and ten interhemals supporting the ten anal rays.
- In males, hemal spines I–III become [[Gonapophyses]] with spinous processes; the first hemal spine is detached from vertebra 13 and reduced to a ossicle within the suspensory ligament, which is homologous in both sexes.
- In males, interhemals 2, 3, 4 and 5 fuse into a compound interhemal with a distal [[Collar Structure]].
- The number of uncinatoid processes varies among poeciliids: they occur on all three gonapophyses in some species, while in *G. affinis* they are limited to gonapophyses II and III.

**Male developmental sequence**
- Development is staged by the segment count in ray 3 (11-, 14-, 17-, 23-, 26-, 33- and 39-segment stages).
- Early events: interhemal elongation, detachment of the first hemal spine and its forward shift, and onset of spinous and uncinatoid processes.
- Later events: collar formation (first seen at the 23-segment stage), interhemal fusion, and further gonapophysis extension. The third gonapophysis' uncinatoid processes vary greatly in timing and are absent in some old males.
- The author proposes that a hormone-responsive, genetically determined field controls each developmental area, and that susceptibility differs along an anterior–posterior gradient.

**Hormone induction in females**
- The female suspensorium responded to hormone with male-type characters, but the fin did not shift forward as it does in males.
- Ethynyl testosterone thresholds (1 mg per volume of water, as given in the source):
  - 1 mg/20,000,000 cc: elongation of interhemals 2–5, first response.
  - 1 mg/10,000,000 cc: spinous processes and the first collar indications appear; comparable to the 23-segment male stage.
  - 1 mg/5,000,000 cc: comparable to the 26-segment male stage; first hemal spine largely dissolved.
  - 1 mg/1,500,000 cc: stated optimum for gonapophysis development; uncinatoid processes on the third gonapophysis appear as blunt masses.
  - 1 mg/20,000 cc: abnormal; spinous and uncinatoid processes are poorly defined and shorter than at optimal dose.
- Interhemal elongation is inhibited at higher doses, and high concentrations inhibit the elongation of the processes.

**Methyl vs. ethynyl testosterone**
- Methyl testosterone produced nearly identical structural results at lower concentrations: the first response occurred at 1 mg/42,000,000 cc (vs. 1 mg/20,000,000 cc for ethynyl testosterone).
- The author concludes that methyl testosterone is two to four times as potent as ethynyl testosterone, consistent with the earlier gonopodium study.

**Comparison with the gonopodium**
- The effective range of concentrations for suspensorial tissues (roughly 1 mg/20,000,000 to 1 mg/20,000 cc) is wider than the range for gonopodial tissues.
- Gonopodial optimum was about 1 mg/2,000,000 cc, and the initial gonopodial response was at about 1 mg/13,000,000 cc.

**Summary conclusions**
- Genetic potential for all male suspensorial structures exists in females, and all characters except the forward fin shift can be induced by hormone.
- Structures that appear early in male development respond to low hormone levels in females; late structures need high levels.
- Concentrations above optimum inhibit growth and produce abnormal structures.
