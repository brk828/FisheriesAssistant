# Gila Topminnow Extirpation Sites in Arizona: 1994-95 Field Season Results

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) technical report evaluates [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) reintroduction sites for extirpation during the 1994-95 field season. Since 1981, AGFD, the [[Bureau of Land Management]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[U.S. Forest Service]] have cooperated on recovery, with topminnows reintroduced at more than 200 sites, many of which failed. The report applies draft extirpation criteria (Abarca et al. 1994) to reduce an administrative backlog created by sites that no longer support the species, because formal extirpation removes [[Section 7 Consultation]] obligations.

Of the sites evaluated, the report presents 27 extirpation reports (a group of sites meeting criteria during 1994, followed by sites meeting criteria before 1994), and a separate section covering sites that did not meet criteria or that warrant further management. Most sites were recommended for extirpation and no restocking; several others were recommended for augmentation.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow is a small live-bearing poeciliid fish native to the Gila River drainage. Reintroduction failures were long attributed to poor site selection. Site selection was initially opportunistic, selecting any available, possibly permanent water within historical range with little likelihood of land-use conflict. The [[Reintroduction Criteria]] (Abarca et al. 1994) now specify:

- Drainage area ≤1.0 km²
- Elevation below about 1,600 m
- Perennial, lotic, sheltered flow (≥0.1 m³/sec)
- Stream gradient <3%; channel classes C-2 to C-5 (Rosgen 1980)
- Pond surface area <2 ha; depth ≤2 m
- Little or no channelization; complex, heterogeneous, protected habitat free of major recurring flash floods
- Moderate to abundant aquatic vegetation
- Only native, nonpredatory fishes present
- Low or no development potential
- Location within the Gila River drainage

Extirpation criteria are provisional. A population is considered provisionally extirpated when qualified biologists fail to collect topminnows in at least five consecutive surveys (natural populations) or three consecutive surveys (reintroduced populations), with surveys covering most available habitat, conducted mainly June through November, with mosquitofish vouchers preserved where present, and with nearby surface waters also searched if the site is dry. Provisional declarations require a site report, review by AGFD and other biologists, and a final decision by AGFD and USFWS with the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]]. The draft recovery plan has not been finalized by USFWS.

Survey thoroughness and percent habitat surveyed were inconsistently documented in historical records, so the authors estimated them from personal knowledge and communication with experienced collectors, including [[James Brooks]], [[Lee Simons]], [[Brian Bagley]], [[Francisco Abarca]] and [[Matt Brown]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The sites reflect a recurring set of threats:

- **Desiccation**: the most frequently cited cause, especially at stock tanks, ephemeral washes and artesian wells whose flow failed.
- **Flooding and scouring**: common in steep, narrow canyons (e.g., Castle Creek, Seven Springs, Humbug Creek, Buehman Canyon).
- **Temperature extremes**: cold water at high-elevation sites (e.g., El Pilar, Indian Spring #2, Lower Nogales-area sites) and possible overheating.
- **Vegetation and algae**: dense cattails and algae in tanks reduced habitat quality.
- **Predation and competition**: non-native fishes such as [[Green Sunfish]], [[Goldfish]], [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Longfin Dace]] (native but noted as a potential predator of juveniles) present at several sites.
- **Illegal introductions**: unauthorized stocking by locals and anglers was a concern at Martin Well and Green Tanks.
- **Human activity**: livestock use, recreation and highway runoff affected several sites.

Sites were often stocked with fish from [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]], originating from [[Monkey Spring]], Cocio, and Bylas springs populations.

## Key Findings

**Extirpation recommendations (sites meeting criteria, with no restocking unless noted):**

- Roughly 25 extirpation reports covered sites evaluated for the 1994-95 season and earlier, with stocking dates ranging from 1964 to 1993.
- Bench Well, Castle Creek, Happy Camp Spring, Mesquite Flat Tank, Mesquite Spring Tank, Nogales Spring, Reed Spring, Hutt Spring, Shute Springs, Holly Spring, Alambre Tank and Pilot Tank were among the sites recommended for extirpation.
- Several sites persisted for extended periods before failing: Cedar Spring, Chalky Butte Well and Artesian Well #3 each supported topminnows through the mid-1980s or later; Upper Horrell Spring and Artesian Well #4 were also documented extirpated.
- Desiccation was the most commonly identified cause, followed by flooding.

**Sites not meeting extirpation criteria or recommended for further action:**

- **Horse Creek**: not meeting criteria; recommended for augmentation stocking because flooding and desiccation are occasional, not persistent.
- **Humbug Creek** and **Indian Spring #1** (Category #1 sites): recommended for augmentation from a natural population lacking replicates.
- **Seven Springs**: recommended for augmentation despite frequent flood damage, heavy recreation and non-native fish.
- **Green Tanks**: qualified for extirpation, but site enhancement could make it suitable; discussions with BLM and the rancher were recommended.
- **Rincon Site #122 (Saguaro National Monument)**: upper tinajas may be perennial; re-evaluation recommended.
- **Peoples Canyon**: flood-removed population; a thorough search was recommended before any decision.
- **Buehman Canyon**: upstream reaches should be sampled before any augmentation.

**Out-of-range sites:** Los Alamos (Site #36) and Peoples Canyon were outside historical range and should not be restocked; desert pupfish extirpation criteria were not defined in the 1993 Desert Pupfish Recovery Plan.
