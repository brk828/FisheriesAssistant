# Colorado Squawfish Recovery Team Letter on the San Juan River and Reclassification Recommendations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This April 16, 1990 letter from the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] to the Regional Director of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Denver, Colorado) responds to a March 23, 1990 draft of the [[Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan]]. The team concluded that the [[San Juan River]] should be considered as a potential recovery area and included in the recovery plan objectives for both downlisting and delisting [[Colorado Squawfish]]. It also recommended revisions to the plan's reclassification and delisting criteria, a comprehensive management plan, and evaluation of [[Navajo Dam]] reoperation.

## Background & Description

The letter is written by Steve Petersburg, Team Leader, and copied to all team members. It summarizes a March 28, 1990 meeting at which the team reviewed the draft plan and discussed designating the San Juan River as a recovery area. The Colorado Squawfish is a large cyprinid fish of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The species is listed as endangered, and the letter proposes changing its status to threatened under specified conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The team's biological summary for the San Juan River includes these points:

- Squawfish have been collected in the San Juan River year-round, during all three seasons, in collections from 1988 to 1989. Fish were taken from [[Lake Powell]] upstream some 90 miles.
- The river appears to hold a total population that is unknown. The team considers it presumably habitable for the species.
- The San Juan River is one of only three known reproductive areas documented in the Upper Colorado River system.
- New hydrology data show flexibility within the system to maintain or enhance flows (habitat), which correlates with successful downstream recruitment.
- Suitable habitat appears available for all life stages.
- Existing populations appear depressed in the Colorado subbasin, and the status in the [[Green River]] is considered secure.
- Vulnerability to oil spills and natural catastrophic events, such as the [[Yampa River]] and [[Westwater Canyon]] episodes, poses a continuing threat to recovery.
- Compared with other Upper Basin rivers, the San Juan supports larger numbers of native fishes, although several exotic species appear to be present.
- Water quality problems exist, including oil spills and fish eradication, but opportunities exist to improve water quality.
- The southern tributary's temperature characteristics appear to favor long-term native fish survival.
- The squawfish population, though small in number, may be important as a unique genetic stock in a separate subbasin.

## Key Findings

**Team conclusions**

1. There is sufficient new information to warrant further consideration of the San Juan River as a potential recovery area.
2. The San Juan River should be included in the recovery plan objectives for both downlisting and delisting.
3. Enhancing San Juan River fish numbers and habitat would increase recovery potential over a larger portion of the fish's historic range.

**Recommended revisions to the recovery plan**

- **Downlisting, Option 1 (preferred by majority):** Reclassify to threatened when naturally self-sustaining populations are maintained in three areas:
  - (a) The Green River from its confluence with the Colorado River upstream to Echo Park, the lower 220 km (137.5 miles) of the [[Yampa River]], and the lower 240 km (150 miles) of the [[White River]].
  - (b) The Colorado River from Lake Powell upstream to Palisade, Colorado.
  - (c) The San Juan River from Lake Powell upstream to River Mile 183.
- **Downlisting, Option 2 (alternative):** The same three areas, with the Green River basin criteria applied more flexibly, so that a population in one area can substitute if recovery proves exceedingly difficult in an existing area.
- **Voting:** The team unanimously agreed that recovery in all three areas should be required for downlisting. Representatives of [[Arizona]] and [[New Mexico]] were absent, and the team expected they would have supported Option 1. A record of the vote will be included in the meeting minutes.
- **Delisting criteria:** Item (d) should be revised to read: "Other identifiable threats, if any, which may significantly affect the populations are removed."
- **New delisting item (e):** A population in either the [[Salt River]] (from a diversion dam at the outlet of Roosevelt Lake to Apache Falls, 70 river miles) or the [[Verde River]] (from Horseshoe Reservoir upstream to Paulden, Arizona, 150 river miles) should be reestablished, with habitats and flows protected. Feasibility is to be reevaluated at the conclusion of the stocking program in 1995.
- **Taylor Draw Dam passage:** Regarding the Yampa River passage paragraph, the team recommends either (a) presenting scientific data showing that loss of this range and associated habitat will not impair recovery, or (b) requiring passage upstream and downstream of [[Taylor Draw Dam]].
- **Deletion:** Delete the last paragraph on pages 20 and 21.

**Recommended management actions**

1. Develop a comprehensive management plan directing all recovery and related resource activities, drafted by an interagency team as soon as possible. The team would like to review a draft once prepared.
2. Apply all appropriate protection measures currently afforded Colorado squawfish populations elsewhere in the Upper Colorado River Basin to the San Juan River population.
3. Coordinate San Juan River recovery activities closely with ongoing efforts in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] and with the [[Recovery Implementation Program]].
4. Because recovery opportunities exist in the San Juan River, evaluate the potential for reoperating [[Navajo Dam]] and Reservoir to realize them.
