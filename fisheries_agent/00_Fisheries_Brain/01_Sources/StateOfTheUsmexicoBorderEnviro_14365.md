# Linking Grassroots to National Policy: U.S.-Mexico Border Environmental Plan Public Advisory Committee Report to EPA Administrator

#policy #environmental-management #transboundary-ecosystems #border-region #water-resources

## Executive Summary

This letter report from the EPA Border Environmental Plan Public Advisory Committee to Administrator Carol Browner (July 22, 1993) documents critical environmental and public health concerns in the [[U.S.-Mexico Border Region]]. The Committee, working with the [[Udall Center for Studies in Public Policy]], provides a grassroots perspective on transboundary environmental problems that have been historically neglected. The border region faces severe environmental degradation from rapid industrialization, urbanization, and population growth (9.2 million in 1990, projected to double in 22 years) without adequate infrastructure investment. The report emphasizes that border environmental problems—including water contamination, air pollution, toxic waste, and resulting health impacts—require immediate attention independent of [[North American Free Trade Agreement]] (NAFTA) outcomes. The Committee proposes creation of a new binational commission with exclusive mandate for border environmental protection, increased funding commensurate with problems, greater community participation, and emphasis on environmental health and equity.

## Background & Description

The U.S.-Mexico border region encompasses 28 counties, 21 designated as distressed, with 56.9% ethnic minority population (versus 19.7% nationally) and 20.7% poverty rate (versus 12.4% nationally). The region is characterized by 14 pairs of twin cities sharing common airsheds and transnational river basins, most notably the [[Rio Grande]]/Rio Bravo. The [[Trinity Edwards Aquifer]] provides 50% of river flow during low-flow periods in the 100 miles upstream of [[Amistad Reservoir]]. The region supports 460 endangered, threatened, proposed, or candidate species and includes shared ecosystems in the [[Gulf of Mexico]] and [[Gulf of California]].

Environmental problems are binational and more severe than domestic ones due to shared water resources (surface and groundwater), airsheds, and ecosystems. The [[International Boundary and Water Commission]] (IBWC) has historically focused on water quantity and allocation, while the [[La Paz Agreement]] of 1983 provides coordination mechanisms but lacks mandatory provisions. The [[Integrated Border Environmental Plan]] (IBEP) prepared by EPA and Mexico's SEDESOL is criticized as a non-integrated "wish list" that ignores long-term water supply, lacks state/local participation, and fails to guide Congressional appropriations effectively.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The border region experiences severe environmental degradation affecting both aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems. Transboundary river basins and shared aquifers face depletion and contamination. The 100-km border zone defined in the La Paz Agreement is considered arbitrary; the Committee recommends defining "border region" as any area where events in one country impact the ecosystem or population of the other, typically encompassing entire border states and complete airsheds/watersheds.

Aquatic ecosystems face multiple stressors: untreated sewage discharge, contaminated drinking water sources, toxic waste causing water pollution, and inadequate wastewater collection and treatment infrastructure, particularly on the Mexican side and in U.S. "colonias." Coastal zones in the Gulf of Mexico receive contamination carried by rivers, threatening rich marine ecosystems. The region lacks comprehensive binational water-quality data despite nearly ten years since the La Paz Agreement.

Air quality problems include particulate matter from unpaved roads, fumes from trash burning at dumps, dust from slag heaps and lime quarries, toxic emissions, mobile sources at border crossings, and pesticide drift from agricultural activities. Pollutants move freely across the international boundary through shared airsheds.

The border population faces disproportionate exposure to environmental hazards: pesticides, toxic materials, air pollution, untreated sewage, contaminated drinking water, uncollected garbage, smoke from burning dumps, blowing dust, and unsafe working conditions. Environmental diseases documented include birth defects, pulmonary diseases, shortened life span, cardiac disease, and skin diseases. Over 30% of mothers surveyed in [[Nogales, Arizona]] believed contaminated water and dirty air adversely impacted their family's health.

## Key Findings

**Critical Institutional Needs:**

1. **New Binational Commission Required**: The Committee recommends creating a binational institution with exclusive mandated responsibility for protecting border region environmental quality. This commission would have oversight jurisdiction over water (surface/groundwater, quantity/quality), air quality, hazardous material and toxic waste management, and other environmental quality and health matters. It would coordinate efforts of EPA, SEDESOL, IBWC/CILA, and other federal, state, and local entities. The commission would have two co-chairs appointed by the Presidents of Mexico and the United States, plus representatives from federal agencies, border state governors, and a Border Council of Governments ensuring community representation.

2. **Better Data Coordination**: Critical need exists for better, more efficient, and nonduplicative binational coordination and data-gathering to support monitoring and enforcement. Common technical standards must be developed between both countries as basis for integrated planning. The commission would collect and publicly disseminate all current border environmental and health data, provide technical assistance, and direct loans/grants to areas with critical binational problems.

3. **Integrated Planning**: The region needs institutional arrangements for environmentally and financially sustainable economic development planning with at least 25-year horizon moving in 5-year increments. A 5-year financial plan from both countries outlining sources and uses of funds for planned capital improvements and operation/maintenance programs is necessary. Infrastructure plans should contain fee schedules based on user-pay principles for self-sustaining facilities.

**Funding Priorities:**

Environmental infrastructure requirements through 2003 for water, sewage, solid and hazardous waste may cost as much as $6.5 billion, not including health and other human needs costs. Current funding is inadequate and arrives too late. The Committee recommends:

- Creation of special fees along the border tied to commerce and trade, dedicated to environmental management and as source of grants and low-interest loans to border cities
- Direct EPA grants to border cities and counties with prepared planning, cost-sharing, and pay-back arrangements based on user-pays principles
- Greater emphasis on sustainable management and design of user fees allowing border infrastructure to become more self-supporting
- Funding allocations driven by identified needs rather than political influence or emergency response

**Research Priorities:**

The [[Southwest Center for Environmental Research and Policy]] (SCERP) received Congressional funding before significant research questions were identified and without thorough search for appropriate expertise. Research must be coupled more closely to physical and administrative problems and community participation. Existing data are scattered and not publicly available; consolidation and open access are needed. Research should be undertaken only when aimed at meeting locally identified needs, with open professional peer review process.

**Topics Requiring Highest Consideration:**

1. **Pollution Prevention and Cleanup**: Control marketing of environmentally obsolete and "dirty" technologies; promote best available appropriate technologies; provide federal assistance for prototype pollution prevention ordinances. Cleanup of existing environmental problems, including some in Mexico linked to U.S. investment, has received no IBEP commitment.

2. **Wastewater Collection and Treatment**: Basic infrastructural and public health problem requiring resolution on Mexican side and for U.S. colonias. EPA and SEDESOL should continue constructing wastewater treatment plants in Mexican sister cities.

3. **Surface and Groundwater Management**: These waters represent already limited resources threatened by increased consumption and pollution. Basic information on extent, flow, vulnerability, and significance of binational aquifers is needed. New treaty with emphasis on shared aquifers is high priority. Immediate regulatory mechanisms needed to protect water quality from further contamination. Moratorium recommended on hazardous and radioactive waste disposal in 100-km La Paz Zone or wherever transboundary impacts may occur.

4. **Water Supply and Safe Drinking Water**: Urgent attention needed for colonias. Long-term protection and management of surface and groundwater sources required. Immediate binational water-quality testing and management programs necessary.

5. **Hazardous and Solid Wastes**: Ensure proper management to prevent future "Love Canal" conditions with outrageous binational Superfund costs. Particular concern for hazardous wastes from maquiladoras and industrial activities. Municipal solid wastes must be reduced, properly collected, and safely disposed. Nonhazardous waste-to-energy conversion technologies should be encouraged.

6. **Land-use Planning and Conservation of Natural Resources**: IBEP overemphasizes urban areas and neglects rural and natural areas. Shared ecosystems should be protected, including transboundary wildlife and marine life to assure biodiversity. Recent establishment of new binational biosphere reserve is applauded; such efforts should extend to other areas.

7. **Air Quality**: Monitoring needed for priority pollutants, traffic on unpaved roads, fumes from trash burning, dust from slag heaps and lime quarries, toxic emissions, mobile sources, and pesticides. Binational airsheds should be defined locally and regionally. Health effects screening process should be implemented and adapted to monitor all population segments. Annexes 4 and 5 of La Paz Agreement are insufficient; new approach needed.

8. **Environmental Baselines**: Reliable data must be collected to define environmental profiles as references for calculating future progress indicators. Continuously updated baselines needed as management tools providing public basis to make contributions and monitor action effectiveness.

9. **Environmental Health**: Perhaps most pivotal principle underlying all topics of critical attention. Health institutions should have access to EPA and border environmental committees. Environmental investigations and actions should contain environmental health as major component. More resources needed for collecting and aggregating baseline health data specific to border area. Information needed about cross-media exposure to contaminants and cumulative effects. Binational health assessments and remediation/prevention strategies must be closely linked.

**Omitted Topics in IBEP:**

1. **Environmental Equity**: Most U.S. border counties have high proportion of Latino and minority residents. Federal and state agencies must incorporate racial composition and socioeconomic status in actions on equitable ruling, enforcement, permitting, public participation, and particularly siting of disproportionate number of proposed waste facilities along border, especially in Texas.

2. **Radioactive Materials and Wastes**: Texas-Chihuahua region experienced consequences of mismanaged discarded medical equipment laden with Cobalt-60. Potential impact could be as disastrous as mismanaged hazardous wastes.

3. **Downstream/Downwind Impacts**: Effects from actions or lack of actions at particular points can have detrimental consequences. Contamination carried by rivers into coastal zones threatens rich ecosystems. Air pollution sources of particulates or gases can have macroregional effects. The 100-km border zone is arbitrary; "border region" should be defined as any area where event or action in one country can impact ecosystem or population of the other.

4. **Early Education**: Real future of region is in hands of forthcoming generations. Major responsibility exists in providing children opportunities to learn about their environment. Both countries must work actively to promote earth literacy in educational curricula and community outreach, especially to K-12 teachers.

**Community Participation and Environmental Justice:**

Border population is less educated (40.9% high school dropout rate versus 20.6% nationally). Educational and assistance programs must be developed to reach affected individuals through bilingual, multimedia notification programs including public-service announcements, publications read by ethnic minorities, and postings at churches, union halls, jobsites, and public buildings.

Federal agencies including EPA must review enforcement efforts to ensure resources are applied with no greater vigor in primarily minority-populated border areas than elsewhere. Right-to-know policies promoted in UN Rio Declarations, IBEP, and NAFTA preamble and embodied in U.S. law must be implemented in practice in Mexico.

Border residents have reacted creatively through establishment of nongovernmental, binational health and environmental councils: [[San Luis Rio Colorado]]-Yuma Council, [[Nogales Health Coalition]], Northeast Sonora Cochise County Health Council, Valley Proud Environmental Council, and Comité Consenso Regional. These grassroots volunteer groups collect data, evaluate conditions, and attempt to control exposure to agrochemical and other contaminants. EPA should support and work with such councils as part of strategy to increase bottom-up participation in setting priorities responsive to health concerns.

**Committee Conclusion:**

The Committee concludes with "a sense of fading hope," feeling that border communities are "like long-lost, distant relatives to the nation who have recently been rediscovered only to be orphaned." There is disconnect between policies being devised and actual community needs. Border environmental conditions and public health will be aggravated by free trade and are likely to worsen unless policies change. Progress made in environmental cleanup in rest of country has not occurred on border; instead conditions have deteriorated. The U.S.-Mexico border region is "truly a region under stress" requiring immediate attention to transboundary natural resources and environmental issues independent of NAFTA's eventual success.
