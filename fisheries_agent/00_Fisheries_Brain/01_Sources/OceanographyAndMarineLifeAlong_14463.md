# Oceanography and Marine Life along the Pacific Coast of Middle America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #eastern-pacific-oceanography

## Executive Summary

This Scripps Institution of Oceanography paper by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Gunnar I. Roden]] reviews the physical oceanography of the eastern Pacific coast of Mexico and Central America and links it to the marine life that pre-Columbian peoples exploited. It covers major currents, upwelling, the thermocline, the [[Golfo de California]] basin, tides, hurricanes, and sea-surface temperature history, then surveys how coastal people used shellfish, crustaceans, fishes, sea turtles, birds, marine mammals and other marine products, and how the sea served trade and travel. Midden evidence, including radiocarbon dates from [[Punta Minitas]], is used to argue that seafood use along the region extends back at least 8,000 years.

## Background & Description

The authors open with the premise that the seas shaped human life through food, materials, climate moderation, commerce and imagination. [[Aschmann]] (1959) is quoted on the value of coastal and land resources combined, which reduced seasonal feast-to-famine cycles for aboriginal rancherias in Baja California.

The region is defined by two major current systems: the cool, low-salinity, oxygen-rich [[California Current]] from the north, and the warm, saline, low-oxygen [[North Equatorial Current]] from the south. Their meeting near Cabo San Lucas and Cabo Corrientes produces sharp temperature and salinity gradients.

Oceanographic exploration was recent. Early work by McEwen (1916) on northern Baja California was followed by cruises into Golfo de Panama and [[Golfo de California]] (1939-1940), the Scripps and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service surveys from 1949, eastern tropical Pacific cruises (1949-1954), the 1955 Eastropic Expedition, and the 1957 Island Current Survey by the Inter-American Tropical Tuna Commission.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water column and forcing.** Subsurface water forms three layers: a mixed layer to about 20 m, a thermocline and halocline between roughly 20 and 60 m, and deeper water with low dissolved oxygen. Trade winds drive the major currents. The zone of weak, variable winds between the trade systems provides breeding ground for tropical cyclones.

**Upwelling.** Cool, saline, low-oxygen water rises along the [[Baja California]] coast, notably at [[Punta Banda]] (Arbolitos), where summer surface temperatures can run 5°-9°C, occasionally 12°C, cooler than on the other side of the point only 3 km away. Upwelling also occurs at Punta Descanso, Cabo Colnett, Punta Eugenia, and Bahia Magdalena. Ekman-based explanations of upwelling are disputed, and no theory has been fully tested because observations are inadequate.

**Temperature and salinity regime.** The northwestern Baja coast is cool-temperate. Seasonal temperature range is generally under 5°C outside Baja California, about 9°C near Cabo San Lucas, and more than 15°C (up to 22°C extremes) in the Gulf. Salinity is shaped by the balance of evaporation (about 250 cm/year) against precipitation (about 25 cm/year) in the Gulf.

**Golfo de California.** The Gulf is an evaporation basin with a surface area of about 250,000 sq. km. Strong tidal mixing around Tiburon and Angel de la Guarda keeps the bottom largely free of soft sediment. The biota is limited in species but contains many endemics, a mix of tropical and temperate forms, and Pleistocene relicts. Its surface waters often look like "thick soup" from plankton blooms, and it is called the Vermilion Sea.

**Tides.** Tides are mixed and strongly diurnal, with mean ranges of about 0.5 m along the open coast from San Blas to Acapulco and 1.2-1.8 m along the west coast of Baja California. Ranges exceed 10 m at the head of the Gulf, with a tidal bore in the Colorado River reaching 3 m. Tidal mixing and intertidal exposure shaped the rocky shore fauna and aided shellfish gathering and tapo shrimp netting.

**Hurricanes.** Eastern Pacific storms ("chubascos") peak in September, with about two storms on average. The October 1959 storm killed about 2,000 people around Manzanillo, mainly through sea-level rise and rainfall. Some storms also strand shellfish or bring rain to desert regions.

**Biogeography.** The Pacific and Atlantic biotas were connected until the Miocene to Pliocene closure of the Central American straits. The Pacific side has a narrower tropical belt and greater latitudinal diversity, weak coral reef development (only Clipperton Island as an atoll), abundant rocky shore habitat, and rich lagoon and sandy beach fauna. The Panamanian biota and the Mid-Riff region of the Gulf are important biogeographic features.

**Climate history.** Sea temperatures were generally warmer than at present through most of Recent time. A cool period dated about 1,600-600 years ago allowed the giant chiton (Cryptochiton stelleri) to be harvested on this coast, and in the coldest areas it persisted until about 100 years ago. Sea level rise helped maintain reefs and coastal inlets.

## Key Findings

- Shellfish gatherers probably used the Baja California coast about 8,000 years ago; midden dates from Punta Minitas range from 7,020 to 1,510 years B.P., and Gulf and west-shore shells date to over 6,000 and about 7,000 years B.P.
- Upwelling areas support cool-temperate fish faunas (rockfishes, Sebastodes, embiotocids) and abundant giant kelp, California mussel, pismo clam, and abalone.
- The Gulf of California is highly productive, with extreme seasonal temperature ranges, low-oxygen deep water, and a biota that combines endemics, tropical forms, and temperate relicts.
- Marine resources were used extensively: mollusks (shell mounds more than 6 m deep in central Baja California), oysters, shrimp (tapo weirs), spiny lobster and crabs, sheephead and rockfishes, corbinas, and sea turtles (Pacific green turtle is most important).
- Marine mammals were used locally, mainly sea lions and stranded cetaceans; sea otters were extensively hunted until the early 19th century.
- Shells were traded inland as far as Yucatan, and shell tools, lime, dye (from Purpura patula pansa), asphaltum, and beach cobbles were all marine-derived materials.
- Coastal navigation and trade were extensive, with evidence of seagoing canoes and of ocean travel in the eastern Pacific. Islands far offshore were apparently not settled by aboriginal peoples, except the Galapagos.
- Eastern Pacific fish kills are confined mainly to the Atlantic side per the authors; Gulf of California is not seriously affected by lethal red tides.
- Pacific and Atlantic faunas diverged after the Central American isthmus formed, with Pacific shores characterized by rocky habitats and strong tides and Atlantic shores by coral reefs and depositional coasts.
