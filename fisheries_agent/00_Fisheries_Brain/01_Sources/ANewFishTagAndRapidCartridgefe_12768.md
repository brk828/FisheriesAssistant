# A New Fish Tag and Rapid, Cartridge-Fed Applicator

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This short paper from the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 97, No. 1, 1968) introduces a new [[Internal Anchor Tag]] and a [[Cartridge-Fed Tagging Gun]] for rapid, mass tagging of fish. The tag is molded of [[Nylon]] and is inserted with a hollow needle. Preliminary 1967 trials on [[Rainbow Trout]] at the [[University Of Washington College Of Fisheries]] found the applicator was two to three times faster than the [[Petersen Disk Tag]]. After two months, the internal anchor tag had a higher return rate (29.5%) than the disk tag (22.0%), but the difference was not statistically significant. The author reports the tag as an early, promising design and notes that labeling and needle length still needed work.

## Background & Description

The tag and applicator were first developed by the [[Dennison Manufacturing Company]] for the clothing industry. The company's officials suggested the idea of fish tagging to the American Fisheries Society, which passed it to Paul Lyon of the [[Floy Tag Company]] for further development. The work was supported by the U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries (Contract No. 14-17-0007-557) and was published as Contribution No. 263 of the College of Fisheries.

**Tag design**
- A molded nylon tag consisting of a T-bar and a rectangular pendant joined by a slender filament.
- Pendant about 5 mm long and 11 mm wide; T-bar 10 mm long.
- Available in three overall lengths (18 mm, 66 mm, 132 mm) and eleven colors.
- Supplied in cartridges of 25 to 100 tags, attached in a row on a nylon mounting strip that is part of the original molding.

**Labeling options**
- Hand-labeling with ink or dye failed because nylon resists all tested dyes and inks.
- Die-casting an agency code into the tag was possible, but its cost was unknown.
- A vibrograver electric pencil produced permanent labels at about 100 tags per hour (three letters and three numbers), but characters were hard to read in the field because of poor color contrast.
- The Floy Tag Company's method removes the pendant and glues a 76 mm piece of machine-labeled plastic tubing to the filament.

**Applicator**
- A gun-shaped tool of plastic, steel, and aluminum.
- Squeezing the lever drives a piston through a hollow slotted needle. The piston shears one tag from the cartridge strip and pushes the T-bar through the needle, with the filament sliding through the needle slot.
- Releasing the lever resets the mechanism and advances the cartridge for the next tag.
- The piston action helps keep the needle clear of fish flesh and debris, and the needle can be replaced without tools.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The preliminary tests were carried out on small salmonids preserved in formalin (10 to 15 cm fork length), then on live [[Rainbow Trout]] (26 to 41 cm) held near [[Lake Union]]. The study addresses mark-recapture methods for freshwater salmonids. It does not analyze population dynamics, water chemistry, or habitat beyond the tagging site.

**Tag placement**
- Tags were inserted in the dorsal musculature just below the dorsal fin.
- The needle (18 mm) gave a diagonal insertion at about 45° to the body. Depth was set by extruding the tag as the needle point touched the skin on the opposite side, so the tag lay just under that skin.
- The needle slot faced the fish's anterior, so the T-bar emerged parallel to the skin.
- In final position, the T-bar formed about 30° with the filament, and the T-bar sat at a right angle to the interneural bones of the dorsal fin.
- Full-body insertion through the dorsal area was rejected because the filament could rotate and the puncture wounds could enlarge on both sides.

## Key Findings

**Field trial (9 February 1967)**
- 200 live [[Rainbow Trout]] were tagged with the 66 mm internal anchor tag and released into Lake Union.
- A control group of 200 trout was tagged with the [[Petersen Disk Tag]].
- All fish were anesthetized with [[MS-222]] before tagging.
- Half of the anchor tags were inserted perpendicular to the body and half diagonally. The standard 18 mm needle could not reach past the interneural bones on a diagonal in fish of this size, so a longer needle was under investigation.

**Return rates (as of 9 April 1967, about two months)**

| Group | Returned | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Internal anchor tags (all) | 59 of 200 | 29.5% |
| Petersen disk tags (control) | 44 of 200 | 22.0% |
| Anchor tags, perpendicular | 24 of 96 | 25.0% |
| Anchor tags, diagonal | 35 of 104 | 33.7% |

- The difference between anchor and disk tags, and between perpendicular and diagonal insertion, was not significant by chi-square test at the 95% level.
- Fishermen reported that tags were firmly attached and wounds were minimal.
- One tagged trout was recaptured 19 days after release at the college ponds. The external wound was slight and healing.

**Application speed**
- The applicator worked "two to three times as rapidly" as the disk tag.

**Conclusions from the author**
- Diagonal attachment is needed to minimize wear on the tag wound.
- The tag could potentially be placed in the abdominal wall, anal fin, or jaw, and would suit marking dead specimens for laboratory examination.
- The applicator keeps the serial order of tags, allows uniform placement by inexperienced taggers, and works with cold hands.
- Remaining issues were a longer needle for diagonal insertion in larger fish, and a practical labeling method, since no method was yet fully satisfactory.
