# Effectiveness of Electrical Fish Barriers Associated with the Central Arizona Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper evaluates the operation, maintenance, and effectiveness of [[Electrical Fish Barrier]] systems built to stop nonindigenous fishes from moving upstream out of the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) canal into the [[Gila River Basin]]. Author Robert W. Clarkson (U.S. Bureau of Reclamation) reviews barrier operation from 1988 to 2000/2001. Documented outages totaled more than 100 h, less than 0.001% of downtime since installation. Remote monitoring outages exceeded 400 d, about 3% of the operating period. Grass carp crossed the barriers while they were operating to design criteria, showing that the barriers do not totally block upstream-migrating fish. The author concludes that barrier complexity may always preclude absolute effectiveness, and that managers must weigh the cost of further refinements.

## Background & Description

Electrical fields were first applied in North America in the 1950s to block upstream spawning migrations of [[Sea Lamprey]] in Great Lakes tributaries. The aim was to protect declining [[Lake Trout]] stocks. The program was only partly successful and was largely replaced in 1960 by the selective lampricide [[Lampricide TFM]] (3-trifluoromethyl-4-nitrophenol). Pulsed-DC barrier systems were refined in the 1980s and revived as part of an integrated pest management approach.

The CAP is a 540-km aqueduct delivering [[Colorado River]] water to the Gila River basin. Its planning raised concerns that entrained organisms could harm native fishes and established sport fisheries. Keeping organisms out of the canal was judged technically and economically infeasible. Instead, managers chose to stop fishes from moving upstream once outside the canal. Electrical barriers were built where low-head barriers were infeasible, or where direct CAP connections existed.

**Barrier design.** Systems were designed by [[Smith-Root Inc]] and included:
- Six pulse generators per barrier, connected in series, with a designed minimum field of 1.0 V/cm at the water surface
- Pulse width of 25 ms at 2 pulses/s, adjustable to 8–32 ms at 1–13 pulses/s
- Seven electrodes per barrier, evenly spaced at 1 m intervals
- Concrete weirs in canal bottoms (water depth about 1.2 m at maximum flows)
- A 50 kVA backup generator, with a 16-s power interruption before backup starts
- Electronic remote monitoring (fitted January 1992) and [[Standard Operating Procedures]] manuals

**Study sites.**
- [[Arizona Canal]]: 18.5 m wide, [[South Canal]]: 15.8 m wide, both about 2.5 m deep, concrete-lined. Annual flows were 7.24 × 10^8 m³ (587,000 acre-ft) for the Arizona canal and 4.93 × 10^8 m³ (400,000 acre-ft) for the South canal, with maximum rates near 34.0 m³/s.
- [[Pima Lateral Canal]] (24.5 km downstream of the diversion, 1.65 × 10^8 m³ per year): barrier installed 1990, operations ceased 1992 after interconnects made it redundant.
- [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]] (unlined, 15–30 m wide, average depth 1 m, 3.79 × 10^8 m³ per year): barrier installed 1990, 4.2 km below the Gila River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Gila River basin historically supported 20 native fish species. At present, one is recently extinct, seven are endangered, one is proposed endangered, three are threatened, and the rest have declined. Five species have been extirpated. Introduced sport and bait fishes now greatly outnumber natives: Table 2 lists 34 introduced taxa. Nonnative fishes dominate or have displaced native assemblages in most basin waters. The CAP canal is occupied exclusively by nonindigenous fishes, and only two to four natives remain in other canals and tributaries below regulating dams. The major impacts of nonnative fishes include predation, competition, hybridization, and parasite and disease transmission. The problem is shaped by the [[Colorado River]] main-stem dams, which fostered introductions of sport fishes.

**Fish responses to electric fields.** Following Snyder (1992), the author describes responses that generally occur in order of increasing field intensity toward the anode: reactive detection, inhibited or undirected motion, taxis, narcosis (petit mal seizure), and tetanus (grand mal seizure). Fish that swim upstream toward an electrified weir either turn away at the reactive detection zone or continue until other responses overwhelm their orientation, causing them to drift downstream. The physiological mechanism remains controversial.

**Related applications.** Electrical barriers have also been used for escapement estimation and prevention of entrainment. Much of this work is in gray literature and focuses on short-term effectiveness, with little long-term reliability data.

## Key Findings

1. **Outages were common and varied in cause.** Documented barrier outages totaled more than 100 h (less than 0.001% downtime). The first documented outage occurred on the [[Pima Lateral Canal]] on 2 June 1990, when the backup generator ran out of fuel, lasting 12–36 h. Causes included lightning strikes, component breakdowns, backup generator failures, failure to follow maintenance schedules, lack of redundancy, inadequate training, and unknown causes.

2. **Lightning was a recurring problem.** Lightning damaged pulse generators and electrical components several times, including a 2 September 1994 strike on the South canal that damaged several pulsators. Lightning-arresting measures were added, but damage continued.

3. **Remote monitoring is a weak point.** Known remote monitoring outages totaled more than 400 d, about 3% of the operating period. Because these outages hid potential barrier failures, the author calls for [[Remote Monitoring Systems]] reevaluation, modem redundancy, and independent verification of alarm reports.

4. **Barriers did not block all upstream movement.** [[Grass Carp]], a species found below but not above the barriers, were captured above the [[Salt River Project]] barriers during monitoring. Captures included two grass carp above the South canal barrier on 23 October 1995 (no power interruption recorded) and grass carp above the Arizona canal on 8 January 2001. Captures occurred during low flows (5–8 cm depth), when large fish may not absorb enough current to be stunned. A 13-cm vertical steel plate was added across the Arizona canal weir in 2001 as a remedy.

5. **Field strength was marginal for small fish.** Voltages near center electrodes read 1.3–1.6 V/cm, above the designed 1.0 V/cm. In a test in October 1999, about a dozen 3–4 cm [[Red Shiner]] released into the Florence-Casa Grande barrier field swam erratically, never tetanized, and some passed upstream. Reported tetany thresholds for freshwater fishes range from 0.05 to 5.5 V/cm, suggesting that higher outputs may be needed.

6. **SOP development improved response.** The [[Salt River Project]] SOP was first produced in early 1990 and revised in 1991 and later. Revisions added inspection procedures, tightened failure scenarios, auxiliary generator crank tests, and battery-voltage alarms. The [[San Carlos Irrigation Project]] SOP, modeled on the 1990 Salt River SOP, followed in early 1991. Management agency review was critical to addressing biological issues such as managing fish in canal reaches between a barrier and natural waters after a failure.

7. **Costs constrain upgrades.** Annual operation and maintenance costs average US$12,700–$14,500 per barrier. Recommended upgrades cost approximately $100,000 per barrier, and a new canal barrier system about $500,000 (including $330,000 for construction). Agencies have been hesitant to fund such replacements.

8. **Post-outage canal clearing was not supported.** Agencies declined to fund comprehensive removal of fishes from canal reaches between diversions and barriers after outages, because it would require stopping water deliveries and could breach contracts. Fishes that pass the barriers can reach upstream river systems.

9. **Conclusion.** The author considers CAP electrical barriers "halfway technologies" (after Frazer 1992; Meffe 1992). They cannot fully solve the problem of nonnative fish omnipresence, so the need for barriers remains until that problem is addressed. Because a single transgressing pair of fish could render a barrier ineffective, the author argues that continued refinement and independent periodic review are needed.
