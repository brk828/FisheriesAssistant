# The Colorado River: Lifeline of the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Clarence A. Carlson]] and [[Robert T. Muth]] (Colorado State University) treats the [[Colorado River]] as a single ecosystem. The authors argue that in less than a century the river has been transformed from a free-flowing, highly variable system into a chain of regulated, man-made segments dominated by non-native organisms. The [[Hoover Dam]] closure in 1935 is identified as the end of the free-flowing river. The paper covers geology and history, the pre-1935 physical and biological condition, native fish assemblages, management and water allocation, non-native introductions, riparian change, and recommendations for the future. The authors conclude that endemic fishes are incompatible with present stream regulation and non-native species, and that future water shortages will limit allocations for ecological needs.

## Background & Description

**Basin and geography.** The Colorado heads in Rocky Mountain National Park, Colorado, and flows 2320 km to the Gulf of California. The [[Green River]] originates in the Wind River Range, Wyoming, 2735 km above the Gulf. The drainage covers 632,000 km² in the United States and Mexico and ranges from sea level to 1450 m. The upper basin (above [[Lee Ferry]]) comprises the Green (115,800 km²), Upper Mainstem (68,600 km²), and San Juan (99,200 km²) sub-basins. Lower-basin sub-basins include the Little Colorado (67,000 km²), Virgin (28,500 km²), Bill Williams (14,000 km²), and Gila (145,000 km²).

**Geology.** The drainage spans the Rocky Mountains, Colorado Plateau, and Basin and Range provinces. The river's present northern drainage was established by the end of the Miocene, and the modern route through the [[Grand Canyon]] followed uplift of the Kaibab Upwarp. The region was lowered about 3.2 km since the middle Oligocene.

**Human history.** Agricultural cultures occupied the canyon country for about 4000 years. Spanish explorers reached the lower river in 1540, and the name "Colorado" was applied on a 1705 map. [[John Wesley Powell]] began scientific investigations in 1869, floating from Green River, Wyoming, through the Grand Canyon.

**Pre-1935 condition.** Before regulation the river ran unchecked, with flows at Yuma ranging from near zero to over 7,000 m³/s. Mean annual virgin flow from the upper basin over four centuries was 16.65 km³. Sediment transport peaked at 25 million t on 13 September 1927 at 3540 m³/s in the Grand Canyon. The lower river was a "broad, meandering, sandy-bottomed" stream with oxbow lakes and marshes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fish fauna.** The basin supports 33 native species (22 subspecies), 20 genera, and 9 families. Of the freshwater species, 20 are endemic and form a highly specialized assemblage. Endemic fishes make up 61% of all native species and 35% of native genera, one of the highest endemism levels in North America. Centers of endemism are the [[Virgin River]] and [[Gila River]] drainages. Average native richness per major drainage was about 10 species, ranging from 5 ([[Bill Williams River]]) to 15 (Gila River).

**The "big water" fishes.** [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] were largely restricted to large main-channel habitats.

**Riparian vegetation.** Upper-elevation riparian zones were dominated by willows. Lower zones held willows, cottonwood, mesquite, seepwillow, and arrowweed. In 1600s-era estimates, at least 2023 ha of cottonwood communities existed along 322 km of the lower river. Non-native [[Tamarisk]] (salt cedar) was established by 1935 and has spread northward at about 20 km/yr.

**Benthic invertebrates.** Pre-regulation lower-river benthos was simple, dominated by chironomids and oligochaetes. Regulated tailwaters now support [[Cladophora Glomerata]] mats, which provide food for introduced fishes, and the non-native amphipod *Gammarus lacustris*. The Colorado system lacks Unionacean clams, and isopods are usually absent from lotic sites. The introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*) occurs as far upstream as Lake Mead.

## Key Findings

- **Flow regulation:** Hoover Dam closure (1935) ended the free-flowing river. About 12% of flow evaporated from reservoirs by the 1970s and another 3% was diverted from the basin. Less than 1% of virgin flow reaches the river mouth.
- **Thermal and sediment change:** Regulation lowered mainstream summer temperatures by 10 to 15°C and warmed winters below dams. Sediment is trapped in reservoirs, and channels below dams have degraded and armoured.
- **Salinity:** Natural salt at Lee Ferry (about 250 mg/L) had doubled by 1957. Salinity reached 825 mg/L at [[Imperial Dam]]. Sulfate makes up nearly half of TDS. The [[Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Act]] (1976) addresses this.
- **Fish declines:** Nearly all endemic species are in severe decline. One species (Pahranagat spinedace) is believed extinct, 21 are struggling, and the bonytail is functionally extinct. Colorado squawfish are extirpated from the lower basin, and humpback chub persist tenuously.
- **Causes of decline:** The authors identify habitat modification from dams and dewatering, and competition and predation from roughly 67 introduced non-native fish species in 16 families.
- **Rotenone incident (1962):** Treatment of 362 km of the Green River above Flaming Gorge killed rare endemic fishes in Dinosaur National Monument. Some species and mayflies had not recolonized after 2 years.
- **Fisheries value:** Lake Mead fisheries were valued at $69 million annually (Martin et al. 1982). Trout, sunfish, and striped bass are the major fisheries.
- **Water budget:** Dependable annual supply was estimated at 15.67–18.26 km³. Welsh (1985) projected a surface water shortage after 2000 unless supplies are augmented.
- **Cottonwood loss:** Only about 1130 ha of cottonwood-willow communities remain on the lower river, and fewer than 202 ha are pure cottonwood.
- **Recommendations:** The authors call for a basin water budget, coordinated basin-wide recovery teams, monitoring of threatened fishes, peer-reviewed research, and public education. They conclude that endangered endemic fishes are incompatible with stream regulation and non-native species.
