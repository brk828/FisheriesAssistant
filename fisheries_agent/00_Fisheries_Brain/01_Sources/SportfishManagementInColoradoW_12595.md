# Sportfish Management in Colorado: With Emphasis on Hatchery-Reared Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This is a presentation by Eddie Kochman, Aquatic Manager of the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] (CDOW), given on May 19, 1994, at the "Wild Trout and Planted Trout: Balancing the Scale" workshop in Denver. It defends the central role of [[Hatchery-Reared Fish]] in Colorado sportfish management, arguing that a growing angler population, limited aquatic habitat, and the impracticality of restoring [[Native Cutthroat Trout]] across most waters make stocking essential. It also reframes "wild trout management" as habitat-based management that accepts periodic stocking, and it argues that hatchery critics misrepresent managers and hatchery staff.

## Background & Description

- **Population context:** Colorado's population of 3.3 million was expected to rise to 4.2 million by 2010, producing more anglers and greater demands on lakes and streams.
- **Market research:** A Market Research Report completed in 1986 (periodically updated) provided public attitude data for fishery and game resources.
- **Angler survey results:** 53% rated their Colorado fishing experience "very favorable," 30% "favorable," and 17% "neutral or unfavorable." Preferred methods were bait (47%), lures (28%) and flies (25%). Only 13% usually caught their limit, and 43% caught four fish or fewer on a typical day. Anglers valued beauty, companionship and access, and most supported habitat protection and were willing to pay for it.
- **Definition of wild trout management:** Managers define it as managing selected habitats to encourage natural reproduction and maintain resident trout, with stocking, selective harvest and habitat modification where needed. The speaker rejects the strict view that only naturally raised, native fish count and that all non-native trout should be excluded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stream habitat:** Colorado has more than 34,000 miles of streams, about 13,000 of which contain trout. Quality ranges from marginal to Gold Medal fisheries. Factors degrading quality include mining, channelization, erosion, pollution and de-watering. Heavy metal discharge from past mining has had the greatest impact, with long-term problems in reaches of the Arkansas, Animas and Eagle rivers.
- **Self-sustaining potential:** Of the 13,000 trout miles, the speaker estimates only about five percent or less can ever maintain high-quality self-sustaining wild trout fisheries.
- **Still and managed waters:** Colorado has over 2,400 natural lakes and reservoirs covering 203,000 surface acres. CDOW manages 180,100 total acres, including 143,300 acres of lakes and reservoirs open to public fishing (about 77% of annual fishing days) and 36,800 acres of streams (about 23%). Roughly 740,000 licensed anglers and 350,000 unlicensed youth and senior anglers use these waters.
- **Reservoir aging:** Many reservoirs are of marginal quality, fluctuate in level, become overpopulated with undesirable fish, and lose productivity with age. New reservoir construction has ended, so aging reservoirs will need intensive stocking of both cold and warmwater species.
- **Notable waters:** Spinney Mountain and Stagecoach Reservoir were cited as examples of fast growth in new reservoirs. Blue Mesa Reservoir (about 9,000 surface acres, 96 miles of shoreline) was the subject of an economic study. Cherry Creek Reservoir in Southeast Denver receives over 80,000 hatchery rainbows per year and provides over 160,000 angler days.
- **Regional patterns:** The East Slope (Atlantic drainage) holds 48% of the water but produces 69% of recreation days (3,472,200). The West Slope (Pacific drainage) holds 52% of the water and produces 31% (1,557,000). Northeast Region recreation is 84% dependent on hatchery trout, and the Central Region, with over 60% of the population, has 6% of lakes and 5% of streams yet provides over 20% of recreation days.

## Key Findings

- **Hatchery capacity:** CDOW operates 15 hatchery and rearing units (from the large Rifle Hatchery to small units such as Chatfield). Collectively they stock approximately 15 million coldwater and 48 million warmwater fish annually, with totals falling as fish are raised to larger sizes. About 94% go to lakes and reservoirs and 6% to streams. Hatcheries also supply over 27 million eggs and fish to other agencies and private hatcheries. [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] hatcheries supplement the state system.
- **Stocking decisions:** Field biologists determine species, size and number for each water based on angler use, fish population, habitat conditions and capacity to support wild fisheries. Hatchery managers then rear and stock the fish. The speaker states the stocking decision is not made by hatchery managers.
- **Habitat management categories:**
  - *Intensive Management* provides maximum recreation within facility and environmental limits, mainly with catchable (about 10-inch) rainbows.
  - *Optimum Management* produces fish within habitat limits, with stocking similar to natural production and harvest regulations.
  - *Special Management* preserves or enhances selected species, including threatened or unique fish, using special regulations.

- **Central Region regulations:** Most waters allow a daily possession limit of 8 fish. Some urban waters use minimum size limits and reduced bag limits to improve catch rates and fish size.
- **Central Region stocking:** About 900,000 rainbows are stocked annually, mostly in waters over 10 acres near Denver. Angler surveys over the last 16 years (text reads "164 years," likely OCR error) show returns to creel usually above 90%.
- **Economic value:** Estimated annual hunting and fishing expenditures total about $590.6 million for hunting and $920.4 million for fishing, including a 1.2 local service multiplier. The speaker attributes much of the fishing value to stocking.
- **Blue Mesa Reservoir study (Colorado Water Resources Research Institute, 1987):** About 165,000 anglers fished the lake in 1986. Gross economic value to anglers was $5.43 million, comprising $2.64 million in trip expenditures and $2.79 million in net value above spending. Fish stocking cost about $308,000 and management about $132,000 in 1986. The speaker says the benefit-cost ratio is higher in 1993.
- **Native species:** Management of the threatened [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] is cited as a successful native-fish recovery effort. CDOW is developing a statewide long-term native cutthroat plan with federal agencies and plans a native-species hatchery for sensitive sport and non-sport fish.
- **Policy position:** Restoring native cutthroat to 100% of historic range is "wishful thinking." Stocking will continue for lakes, reservoirs and streams that cannot sustain natural reproduction, with attention to wild trout protection. The speaker calls for resolution of the hatchery-versus-wild debate and avoidance of interstate comparisons that ignore habitat and angler differences.
- **Conclusion:** The speaker sees no alternative to continued use of hatchery-reared fish, including catchable rainbows and sub-catchable cutthroat, to serve a growing angler population over the next 15 years.
