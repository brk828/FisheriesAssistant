# Quinaldine as a New Anesthetic for Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 note by Bruce Muench of the [[Illinois Department of Conservation]] reports [[Quinaldine]] (2-methylquinoline), a chemical not previously recognized as a fish anesthetic, as a candidate for handling and transporting fish, and possibly for anesthetizing fish populations in their natural habitat. Effective concentrations were low (5 to 12 p.p.m.), anesthesia developed within 45 seconds to 6 minutes, and recovery in fresh water took 1 to 10 minutes. Fish tolerated exposures of several days, and some species tolerated concentrations far above the anesthetic dose. The work was partly drawn from a master's thesis in the Department of Zoology at [[Southern Illinois University]].

## Background & Description

The article opens by listing anesthetics then in use for fish transport and handling: sodium amytal, thiopental sodium, urethane, [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (M.S. 222), chlorobutanol, ether, and carbon dioxide. The aim was a safer, more effective and cheaper drug that could allow anesthetization of wild fish populations. Screening many compounds identified quinaldine as meeting these criteria.

Quinaldine (2-methylquinoline) occurs in coal tar. It is not used in medicine, although other quinolines have antiseptic, antimalarial and pesticidal uses. Quinolines are also used in making dyes and explosives. Its density is 1.059 and it is slightly soluble in water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The drug acts as a central nervous system depressant, producing a state of quiescence similar to that caused by barbiturates. Anesthetized fish rest in a position that varies with species and concentration, with muscles relaxed and movement limited mostly to breathing. They can be moved by hand without resistance, though rough handling can provoke a short swim.

Water temperature had little effect on potency. Anesthesia at 50° to 54° F was about as effective as at 74° to 79° F, though recovery at the lower temperatures appeared to take longer. Persistence is a practical limit: a 10 p.p.m. solution stayed effective for 6 days in a laboratory aquarium, but the author doubted this would hold under field conditions.

Toxicity data for other animals are given from [[Spector (1956)]]: oral LD50 in rats of 1,230 mg/kg and cutaneous LD50 in rabbits of 1,870 mg/kg, indicating relatively low toxicity. No cancer tests were known.

## Key Findings

- **Effective concentration:** Anesthesia was produced at 5 to 12 p.p.m. in the tested species; the table lists exposure concentrations for green sunfish, white crappie and yellow bullhead, with time to anesthetize, time held in solution and recovery time.
- **Speed of action:** Cessation of movement occurred within 45 seconds to 6 minutes.
- **Recovery:** Recovery in fresh water began immediately and was complete in 1 to 10 minutes; recovery time rose with exposure length and concentration.
- **Safety margin:** [[Green Sunfish]] held at 20 p.p.m. for 11 hours showed no ill effects, three times the anesthetic concentration for that species.
- **Prolonged exposure:** Some fish were held in test solutions for 2 to 3 days and recovered within minutes; they were observed for several weeks with no detectable harm.
- **Solvent:** Quinaldine was usually applied in acetone at no more than 1:10,000 final concentration. Acetone alone had no effect on fish. Quinaldine without solvent was equally effective but distributed less evenly.
- **Temperature:** Efficacy was similar at 50° to 54° F and 74° to 79° F.
- **Scope:** The author concluded the drug was effective enough to be considered for large-scale anesthetization of fish populations, although the report does not describe any field trial.
