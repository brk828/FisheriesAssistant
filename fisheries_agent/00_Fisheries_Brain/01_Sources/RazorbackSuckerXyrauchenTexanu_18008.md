# Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish Reintroduction and Monitoring, Salt and Verde Rivers, Arizona, 1991-1992

#ichthyology #endangered-species #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #reintroduction #radiotelemetry

## Executive Summary

This special report documents a reintroduction and monitoring program for two endangered native fishes, [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), in the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] systems of Arizona during 1991-1992. Both species were historically endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] but were extirpated from the [[Gila River Basin]] by 1960. The study stocked 501 razorback suckers (355-660 mm) and 10 squawfish (562-669 mm) into the Verde River, with 18 razorbacks and all 10 squawfish implanted with radio-transmitters. Monitoring employed electroshocking, seining, netting, and radiotelemetry to track post-stocking dispersal, survivorship, movement patterns, and habitat use. Survivorship was low: only 22% of radio-tagged razorbacks survived to 266 days, and 40% of squawfish to 185 days. Both species exhibited greater downstream than upstream movement, with razorbacks showing diel differences in activity (more active at night) while squawfish did not. Habitat analysis revealed razorbacks preferred deep, slow-flowing pools and glides with fine substrates, while squawfish utilized similar habitats but with slightly higher velocities.

## Background & Description

[[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] are endemic to the Colorado River basin and formerly occupied both the Colorado and Gila river drainages in Arizona. Their historical presence in the [[Gila River Basin]] extended northward to Perkinsville on the Verde River, eastward to Ft. Thomas on the Gila River, and south to the delta in Mexico. Evidence suggests both species engaged in seasonal migrations. By 1960, both were extirpated from the Gila Basin and lowermost Colorado River. Razorbacks were last collected from the Verde River basin at Peck's Lake in 1954 and from the Salt River drainage at Saguaro Lake around 1949. Squawfish were last collected from the Gila River system at Salt River Canyon in 1958.

Both species persist in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] in the Green, Yampa, and Colorado rivers of Utah, Colorado, and Wyoming, and in the San Juan River of New Mexico and Utah. Natural populations of razorbacks still exist in the Lower Colorado River in [[Grand Canyon]] below Glen Canyon Dam and in Lakes Mead, Mohave, and Havasu, though these populations consist primarily of older adults indicating greatly reduced reproductive success. Population declines resulted from physical and chemical alteration of natural systems and introduction of non-native fish species, leading to the listing of squawfish as endangered in 1967 and razorback as endangered in 1991.

The reintroduction program began in 1981 under a 10-year Memorandum of Understanding between the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS). Initial efforts using juvenile razorbacks from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico showed low survivorship due to introduced predators. By the end of 1990, over 600,000 squawfish and 11,800,000 razorbacks had been stocked, but predator-induced mortality remained high, especially for smaller fish. The current study utilized larger fish suitable for radio-transmitter implantation to track movement patterns and habitat preferences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study Areas:**
The [[Verde River]] is 286 km long, draining approximately 16,896 km². It exhibits fairly constant base flow through upper reaches, with more seasonally variable flows below the Verde Valley. The study area extended from Sullivan Lake (RKM 145) to Horseshoe Reservoir (RKM 0). Native fish species include [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), and the federally threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). Introduced species include red shiner, fathead minnow, mosquitofish, yellow bullhead, carp, channel catfish, rock bass, smallmouth bass, rainbow trout, and brown trout. Native species dominance declines below Perkinsville.

The [[Salt River]] study area extended from Highway 60 bridge (RKM 90) to Gleason Flat (RKM 60). Only five native fishes occur naturally: longfin dace, Sonora sucker, speckled dace, roundtail chub, and desert sucker. Eighteen exotic species are present, including various catfish, bass, buffalo, and sunfish species.

**Habitat Characteristics:**
Available habitats in the Verde River comprised: pools (36%), runs (39%), glides (15%), riffles (8%), backwaters (1%), and eddies (1%). Mean depth was 0.49 m (range 0.02-2.00 m), mean current velocity 0.27 m/s (range -0.08 to 1.37 m/s), with substrate primarily cobble or sand.

**Water Quality:**
Dissolved oxygen showed pronounced daily fluctuations September-November and April-May due to photosynthetic activity. pH ranged from 7.48 (December) to 8.74 (April) with minimal daily fluctuations. Water temperature followed daily and seasonal air temperature patterns. Conductivity was higher in warmer months and lower in winter, with December-March drops reflecting dilution from flooding.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and Monitoring:**
- Seven release efforts in Verde River from June 1991-July 1992
- Total stocked: 501 razorback suckers (355-660 mm) and 10 squawfish (562-669 mm)
- All fish injected with PIT (passive integrated transponder) tags
- 18 razorback adults and all 10 squawfish implanted with radio-transmitters
- Salt River stocking prevented by high conductivity interfering with telemetry and electroshocking equipment
- All stocking concentrated at Perkinsville auto bridge (RKM 121.6) on Verde River

**Survivorship:**
- Razorback suckers: 22% of radio-tagged fish survived to 266 days (battery life)
- Colorado squawfish: 40% survived to 185 days
- Fish released during late spring/summer showed shortest survivorship (20-78 days)
- Fish released during late fall survived longer (49-210 days, with one exception of 6 days)
- Only two razorbacks recaptured during fish surveys (from 1920 stocked 1/14/91)
- Greater Lernaea spp. parasite infestation observed in reintroduced fish compared to natives

**Movement Patterns:**
- Razorback suckers: mean cumulative distance 16.22 km/fish over monitoring period
  - Mean downstream movement: 1.11 km/week
  - Mean upstream movement: 0.76 km/week
  - Greater downstream tendency overall
- Colorado squawfish: mean cumulative distance 13.24 km/fish
  - Mean downstream movement: 1.01 km/week
  - Mean upstream movement: 0.42 km/week
- February 1992 flooding caused dramatic movements in some individuals (38.5-66.8 km downstream)
- Excluding flood effects, both species restricted movements to within 8 km of release point
- Razorbacks showed significant diel differences: 8.6 m/h daytime vs. 16.8 m/h nighttime (F=35.6443, p<.001)
- Squawfish showed no diel differences: 14.6 m/h daytime vs. 13.7 m/h nighttime (F=1.0750, p>.30)

**Habitat Use:**
- Razorback suckers utilized:
  - Mean depth: 0.74 m (range 0.18-1.65 m)
  - Mean current velocity: 0.05 m/s (range 0.0-0.58 m/s)
  - Primary substrate: sand or pebble; secondary: silt or sand
  - Habitat types: pools (57.5%), glides (34.7%), runs (3.7%), backwaters (2.0%), eddies (1.8%)
  - Significantly different from available habitat (K-S tests, all p<.01)
- Colorado squawfish utilized:
  - Mean depth: 0.87 m (range 0.04-1.22 m)
  - Mean current velocity: 0.25 m/s (range 0.0-0.67 m/s)
  - Primary substrate: silt; secondary: pebble
  - Habitat types: glides (50%), pools (32.2%), runs (17.7%)
  - Significantly different from available habitat (K-S tests, all p<.01)
- Both species preferred deeper, slower habitats than generally available

**Surgical Methods:**
- Lateral incision method superior to midline incision for transmitter implantation
- Transmitters: 17 g for squawfish, 12 g for razorbacks (40.502-40.763 MHz range)
- Healing required 6-8 weeks before release
- Fish treated with Chloramine T (external bactericide) and oxytetracycline (systemic antibiotic)
- Ethicon coated Vicryl sutures removed at 3-4 weeks to reduce infection risk

**Telemetry Accuracy:**
- Triangulation method 95% confidence interval: 2.73-4.27 m longitudinally, 2.69-4.17 m across width
- No significant differences among three test subjects (F=0.0720, p>.05)
- Aerial telemetry conducted weekly using loop antennas on aircraft wings
- Ground telemetry used triangulation from shoreline bearings

**Factors Affecting Survival:**
- Surgical trauma may adversely impact fish
- Warmer release temperatures (late spring/summer) associated with lower survival
- Lernaea spp. parasite infestation higher in reintroduced fish
- Combination of factors likely contributes to low survivorship
- Too few non-implanted fish recaptured to compare survival rates between groups

**Conclusions:**
Reintroduction efforts for larger, radio-tagged razorback suckers and Colorado squawfish were largely unsuccessful, with low survivorship rates. The study successfully documented movement patterns and habitat preferences, showing both species prefer deep, slow-flowing habitats and tend to remain within limited ranges of their release points. However, the combination of surgical stress, seasonal temperature effects, and parasite infestation appears to significantly impact survival of reintroduced individuals.
