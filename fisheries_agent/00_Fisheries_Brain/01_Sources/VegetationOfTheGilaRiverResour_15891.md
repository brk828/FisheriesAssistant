# Vegetation of the Gila River Resource Area, Eastern Arizona

#botany #plant-ecology #Prosopis-juliflora

## Executive Summary

This baseline study by [[W. L. Minckley]] and Thomas O. Clark (Arizona State University, 1981) describes the perennial vegetation of the [[Gila River Resource Area]] in eastern Arizona. Fieldwork ran from October 1976 through September 1979 under a [[Bureau of Land Management]] contract, with the aim of documenting conditions before potential development. The document covers plants only. It contains no fish, aquatic invertebrate, or water chemistry data beyond general hydrological context, so the note records vegetation and its environmental setting.

The region is a transition zone between the [[Sonoran Desert]] and [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. Desertscrub dominates the uplands and lowlands, while mesquite bosques and broad-leafed riparian communities line perennial and intermittent streams.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Mostly below 1,500 m elevation, within the [[Basin and Range Province]]. Relatively short, parallel mountain ranges are separated by alluvial valleys, including the Safford and Duncan valleys.
- **Geology and soils:** Upland soils are derived from granites, gneisses, schists and limestones, with [[Gray Desert Soils]] common. Alluvial soils are thicker, with zones of salt deposition.
- **Climate:** Semi-arid, with mean annual precipitation of 25.4–38.1 cm and strongly bi-seasonal rainfall (winter rain, spring drought, summer–autumn [[Monsoon]] rains). At Clifton (1893–1965), winter precipitation was 12.3 ± 7.4 cm, spring 1.9 ± 1.8 cm, and summer–autumn 18.2 ± 5.8 cm, giving a mean annual total of 32.3 ± 9.9 cm. Evaporation from water surfaces is 7–10 times annual precipitation.
- **Methods:** Permanent transects (Table 1, lengths typically 1,050 m) were sampled for frequency of occurrence, direct tree counts within 15 m belts, and foliage volume using a visual-obstruction method. Spot sampling used 250 m² circular plots at about 1 km intervals. Devil's cholla clones and mesquite clumps required special counting rules. Identifications followed [[Lehr 1978]] and [[McGill 1979]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Upland vegetation.** Between 1,250 and 1,750 m, [[Semi-desert Grassland]] predominates, but pure grass stands are rare because shrubs and cacti are common. Grasses include grama, brome, and cotton-top. Shrub invasion over the last century is linked to overgrazing. [[Interior Chaparral]] elements occur at higher elevations, and [[Madrean Evergreen Woodland]] fringes reach downslope, including junipers and piñon pine.

**Desertscrub.** Below about 1,250 m, desertscrub is dominated by creosote-bush, ocotillo, and prickly pear, with mesquite and blue palo-verde as sub-dominants on dissected valley fill. [[Devil's Cholla]] and Christmas cholla are sub-dominants on level surfaces.

**Terraces.** Flat valley-fill terraces are often sparsely vegetated. Heavy livestock use, impervious soils, caliche and clays, and drought all shape their plant cover. Brushy, multi-stemmed mesquite is common where livestock browse and disperse seed.

**Washes and side canyons.** Ephemeral washes support desertscrub enhancements. Side canyons with perennial water support broad-leafed trees, including willow, walnut, box elder, cottonwood and hackberry. Seepwillow and burro-bush are rare in perennial canyons.

**Mesquite bosques.** Mesquite made up 95–99% of trees on four mainstream Gila River transects. Along the mainstream, most mesquite was under 35 cm in diameter, which the authors interpret as a 60–90 year age for the habitat. Bosques were largely destroyed during 1888–1917. Floods in 1978–79 removed many more stands. Riparian mesquite covered 26% of 465 ha from Solomon to near Bonita Creek mouth, 56% of 637 ha within the study area, and 43% of 780 ha upstream to the New Mexico boundary. About 66% (234 ha) of mapped mesquite stands had more than 50% ground cover.

**Broad-leafed riparian.** Bonita Creek had 29 taxa in 46 quadrats. Mesquite occurred in 91.3% of plots, and sycamore in 43.5%. Eagle Creek Canyon hosts the best-developed cottonwood galleries, with some trees over 30 m tall. Cottonwood–willow stands covered only 5.7% of mapped riparian area along the Gila River.

**Invasive and absent species.** [[Salt-cedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) occurs only as scattered individuals and is kept in check by flooding, since its seedlings were destroyed. [[Arrowweed]] was not found. Blue palo-verde is absent east of Turtle Mountain.

## Key Findings

- **Transition zone:** The Gila River Resource Area is a [[Biogeographic Transition Zone]] where Sonoran and Chihuahuan taxa co-occur, shown in a table of indicator species (Table 12).
- **Desertscrub dominance:** Perennial vegetation is mainly desertscrub. Creosote-bush dominates at Transect GL-7 (84.50%) and B-5 (88.86%) (Table 6).
- **Riparian diversity:** Narrow riparian threads raise landscape diversity far beyond their area. Riparian zones act as corridors between higher elevations and permanent streams.
- **Mesquite dominance in bosques:** Mesquite is the principal riparian tree on recent alluvium, with reproduction present on all sampled bosques (Table 9).
- **Flood impacts:** Flooding in 1978–79 removed or altered many bosques and riparian stands along Eagle Creek, the San Francisco River, and the mainstream Gila River. These effects were assessed qualitatively only.
- **Grazing effects:** Livestock grazing suppresses understory growth, limits cottonwood and sycamore reproduction, and spreads mesquite seed.
- **Baseline purpose:** The data are intended as a pre-development baseline for the Bureau of Land Management.
