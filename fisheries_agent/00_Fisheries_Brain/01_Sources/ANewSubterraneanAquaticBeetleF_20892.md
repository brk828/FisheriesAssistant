# A New Subterranean Aquatic Beetle from Texas

#entomology #coleoptera #dytiscidae

## Executive Summary

This paper describes *Haideoporus texanus* gen. et sp. nov., the first blind, depigmented, aquifer-adapted water beetle of the family [[Dytiscidae]] recorded from North America. The species was collected from an artesian well at the [[Aquatic Station]] of [[Southwest Texas State University]] near San Marcos, Texas. It resembles the Japanese *[[Morimotoa Phreatica]]* superficially, but differs in its minute, apparently nonfunctional eyes, sensory setae, tarsal structure, and male genitalia. The authors interpret shared coxal and prosternal traits among *Haideoporus*, *Morimotoa*, and *[[Siettitia Balsentensis]]* as parallel adaptations to subterranean life, not as evidence of close relationship. The paper also describes the well's habitat within the [[Edwards Aquifer]] and the troglobitic fauna it supports.

## Background & Description

Cave and aquifer-adapted aquatic beetles are very rare. The authors list the known examples: *Siettitia balsentensis* (France, 1904), a second *Siettitia* species from France, a *Uvarus* from Africa, *Morimotoa phreatica* from Japan, *Trogloguignotus concii* from Venezuela, a cave elmid from the Congo, and the noterid *Phreatodytes relictus* from Japan. Most other aquatic beetles reported from caves or wells are thought to have been washed in by streams or floods.

Several surface dytiscids show incipient subterranean adaptation. *[[Hydroporus]] rufiplanulus* has reduced eyes and is somewhat depigmented. Members of the subgenus *Sternoporus* burrow into sandy or gravelly stream and spring bottoms and can be dug out during dry periods.

**Diagnosis of the genus *Haideoporus*:** Blind and depigmented, lacking hind wings, with elongated body and leg setae and enlarged, globular anterior and middle coxae. The prosternal process does not reach the metasternum. Small degenerate eyes sit at the sides of the head. The anterior tarsi of both sexes are squarely articulated with rows of adhesive pads, unlike *Morimotoa*, whose tarsi are angulate with fewer pads. The male genitalia are symmetrical, with a simple aedeagus, unlike the asymmetrical parameres and bifurcate aedeagus of *Morimotoa*.

**Holotype male measurements:** total length 3.68 mm; elytron length 2.48 mm; pronotum length 0.72 mm; prosternal process length 0.32 mm. The allotype female is 3.4 mm long. Paratypes range from about 3.6 to 3.7 mm. Body colour is very light yellowish brown, with translucent integument. The specimens are considered fully pigmented adults, not teneral.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The type habitat is the subterranean water of the San Marcos pool of the Edwards Aquifer. This aquifer lies along the [[Balcones Escarpment]], a line of faulted Cretaceous limestone that holds the largest concentration of caverns in Texas. Many caverns are partly water-filled. The authors describe the aquifer's water quality as excellent.

The well is an artesian well drilled in 1895. Its water comes from a cavern about 1.5 m across at a depth of 59.5 m. The cavern is one of many along the Balcones Escarpment, and the nearby [[San Marcos Fault]] is the major structure in the area. The well and [[San Marcos Springs]] (a first-magnitude spring about 0.6 km away) draw on the same aquifer. Springs flow is slightly more than 200 times that of the well, so the authors estimate that the springs also discharge about 200 times as many organisms.

The well supports a diverse troglobitic community: one turbellarian, two gastropods (one undescribed), one ostracod, two copepods, two isopods, one thermosbaenacean, eight amphipods (five undescribed), one decapod shrimp, and the [[Texas Blind Salamander]] (*Eurycea rathbuni*).

**Water chemistry (Table 1, two sampling dates in 1974):** Temperature was 23.0 °C on both dates. pH was 6.91–6.94, dissolved oxygen was 5.62–5.82 mg/l, and specific conductance was about 600–605 µmhos. Turbidity was 0.0–1.0 JTU, nitrate-nitrogen was 1.19–1.69 mg/l, and phosphate values were low (0.01 mg/l).

## Key Findings

- *Haideoporus texanus* is the first blind, aquifer-adapted dytiscid recorded from North America. It is described as a new genus and species.
- Its closest-looking relative is *Morimotoa phreatica* from Japan. The two differ in eye reduction, tarsal pad arrangement, and male genitalia.
- The shared enlarged coxae and shortened prosternal process of *Haideoporus*, *Morimotoa*, and *Siettitia* are interpreted as **convergent adaptations** to restricted underground spaces, paralleling those in cave carabids such as *Pseudanophthalmus*.
- Because these wingless, aquifer-adapted beetles could not easily cross a sea, the authors argue that a shared ancestry would require distribution before the separation of Japan and North America from Eurasia. They reject that interpretation and favour convergence.
- The Texas find suggests that other cave or aquifer-adapted Dytiscidae may occur in North America. The authors point to related Appalachian hypogean relicts, such as *Agabetes* and *Hoperius*.
- Collection method: a 500-mesh nylon net placed over the flowing artesian well discharge captured the specimens. Earlier workers had trapped *Siettitia* with baited traps in deep wells and captured *Morimotoa* and *Phreatodytes* by pumping well water through a plankton net.
- The holotype was collected 12 June 1974 by Joe Kolb. About 27 paratypes were collected from 1973 to 1976 (tallied from the paper's collection-date list). Types are deposited in the [[Smithsonian Institution]] (National Museum of Natural History), and paratypes are distributed to more than 15 museums worldwide.
- The authors conclude that the wider subterranean aquatic fauna, including the associates of *Haideoporus*, is poorly known and that new collecting methods may reveal more remarkable subterranean insects.
