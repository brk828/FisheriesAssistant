# Habitat Use and Movement of Rare Fishes in the Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study, by Paul B. Holden of [[BIO/WEST, Inc.]] (Logan, Utah), examined habitat use and movement of four rare large-river fishes in the Green River of Utah: the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]], and the rare [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Young fish of these species favored low-velocity backwaters with silt substrates and depths over one foot. A short mark-recapture study of young-of-the-year squawfish was inconclusive about movement. The study also documented young-of-the-year razorback suckers, tentatively identified as the first wild young of this species recorded from the Upper Colorado Basin. Exotic species were more common than natives in samples containing young rare fishes.

## Background & Description

The upper Colorado River basin holds two fishes listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]: the Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and the Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*). The Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*) and Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) were rare and under consideration for endangered and threatened status, respectively. The stated research need was to define subsistence requirements to support recovery and to guide natural resource development so that rare fish populations and their habitat are maintained.

The author identified two factors as central to self-sustaining populations: (1) adequate spawning and rearing habitat, and (2) interaction with exotic fishes. The lower and middle Green River in Utah was described as the least altered large-river habitat in the basin and the site of reproducing squawfish populations. Adults occur elsewhere, but recruitment is lacking, suggesting unmet spawning and rearing requirements.

**Methods (summary):** Sampling used seines (used most often, since the focus was on small fish), electrofishing and a fry shovel. Habitat variables recorded at each sample included depth, velocity, substrate (firm silt versus soft silt, with seine clogging as a field indicator), habitat type and temperature (pocket thermometers). Fish were enumerated and measured for length-frequency analysis, and most were released. Larger rare fish (usually over 100 mm) were tagged with numbered plastic fingerling tags, and juvenile squawfish were marked by clipping the anal fin. Preferences were expressed as [[Electivity Curves]]. Sites were plotted on [[U.S.G.S. Quadrangle Maps]] and river miles followed [[Green River Mile System]] conventions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study reach covered Desolation and Gray canyons and the area from Jensen to Ouray. Historically, fish community data were compiled from earlier surveys, including the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], [[Vanicek]] (1967, 1970) and [[Holden and Stalnaker]] (1975). Turbidity during spring and early summer, together with the river's rigorous flow regime, was judged likely to limit reproduction of the introduced [[Smallmouth Bass]], a piscivore that is a potential predator of rare fishes. Competition and predation between rare species and exotics were considered possible but not quantified.

## Key Findings

- **Fish sampling:** More than 330 samples were collected: 125 in runs, 121 in backwaters, 59 in eddies and 19 in riffles, roughly proportional to habitat availability.
- **Community:** Nineteen species totaling over 19,500 specimens were collected. The introduced Red Shiner was the most abundant fish (13,717 specimens). The Flannelmouth Sucker was the most abundant native species. The Bonytail Chub was the rarest native, with only two specimens caught.
- **Colorado Squawfish:** Young-of-the-year (35-64 mm) occurred only in backwaters with no current, firm silt, and depths of one to two feet. Juveniles (65-200 mm) also preferred backwaters with no current and silt bottoms but were less selective for depth. Subadults and adults (200+ mm) used a range of velocities and depths but favored sandy eddies and runs over backwaters. No temperature preference was evident.
- **Razorback Sucker:** Six young-of-the-year fish (lateral line scales 80-88) were tentatively identified in the middle study area, with the only two adults seen. They preferred eddies at about 0.2 fps, 1.0 ft depth and soft silt. These are considered the first wild young razorbacks recorded from the Upper Colorado Basin, pending taxonomic confirmation.
- **Humpback Chub:** Young-of-the-year preferred backwaters with no current, firm silt and about two feet of depth but also used eddies and runs at 0.4-0.8 fps. Juveniles favored no-current, silt areas at about two feet. Adults were found mainly in sandy-bottomed eddies and runs across varied velocities and depths. Temperature was not selected by any size class. Adults were caught at 2.5 fps and 20 ft depth.
- **Bonytail Chub:** One adult was collected in the upper study area and one juvenile in Desolation Canyon, identified by fin ray counts (10-10) and body shape.
- **Movement study:** Five seine samples over two days yielded 4, 31, 29, 11 and 12 young-of-the-year squawfish. Marked fish (anal fin clip, first four samples) were recaptured only three times, all within the same backwater, with none found in adjacent areas. The author concluded the 2.5-day study was inconclusive, suggesting limited movement between backwater and main channel but some movement within the backwater. Sampling efficiency was excellent in the upper backwater, good in the middle and poor near the mouth.
- **Tagging:** One adult and one juvenile bonytail were tagged but not recaptured. Fourteen adult and nine juvenile chubs from Desolation and Gray canyons were tagged, with no recaptures.
- **Associated fishes:** Exotic species dominated samples with young rare fish. Red Shiner appeared in 100% of samples with young-of-the-year squawfish and 75-96% of samples with other rare-fish age classes. Fathead Minnow and juvenile carp were also frequent. Juvenile squawfish were more often associated with native species such as juvenile roundtail chub and young suckers. Competition and predation were flagged as possible limits, but their significance was unknown.
- **Population status:** Earlier and recent surveys suggested rare fish distribution and relative abundance had changed little. Squawfish apparently spawned only in the extreme lower study area in 1977. The author concluded the rare fishes appeared to be maintaining populations in the study area.
- **Taxonomy:** Chub specimens from Desolation and Gray canyons (dorsal-anal fin rays 9-9 and 9-10) were provisionally referred to Humpback Chub, pending further analysis. They may represent a population distinct from the Jensen group.
- **Seasonal habitat shift:** Larger squawfish may use backwaters during high water (spring and early summer) and eddies and runs during low water (fall).
