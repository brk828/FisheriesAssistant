# Draft Proposal: Habitat Use by Humpback Chub in the Little Colorado River and Other Tributaries of the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This undated draft research proposal, written for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Bureau of Reclamation context, plans to quantify how [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) use habitat in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and in several tributaries of the [[Colorado River]] in the [[Grand Canyon]]. It also aims to assess how [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations, which control flows, may affect these populations. The study partly addresses Conservation Measures 5 and 7 of the recovery program. Its work includes seasonal microhabitat and macrohabitat sampling, streamflow analysis, and statistical tests of habitat preference. The attached budget lists three annual requests, and the revised totals shown are about $99,556 for the first year, about $205,397 for FY1993, and a FY1992 total that is illegible in the scan.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and discovery:** The species was described by Miller (1946) from a single specimen collected in Grand Canyon. Miller's 1946 paper is the source of the original description, and the document calls it a "remarkable new species of cyprinid."
- **Historical range:** Fish remains in archaeological sites near Hoover Dam indicate that the species was long established in the basin. Its historical abundance was greatest in the mainstem Colorado, Green, White, Yampa, and Little Colorado rivers.
- **Current range:** The native range has contracted and population densities have declined. The species is now restricted to canyon reaches of the Green River (Desolation, Gray, and Labyrinth Canyons), Dinosaur National Monument (Yampa and Green rivers), the Colorado River in Black Rocks, Westwater, and De Beque Canyons, Marble and Grand Canyons, and the lower [[Little Colorado River]]. The document notes the species has been declared endangered.
- **Spawning:** In the lower Colorado River, most or all successful spawning occurs in the Little Colorado River (Kaeding and Zimmerman 1983; Minckley 1987). The document states that survival of lower-river populations appears to depend on this single population.
- **Knowledge gaps:** Habitat-use information covers mainly juveniles and adults, mostly collected April through October. Earlier work focused on locating populations, and life history and ecology have been poorly studied.
- **Reported adult preferences:** Adults have been associated with fast current and/or deep channels (Holden and Stalnaker 1975; Kidd 1977; Seethaler et al. 1979). Valdez et al. (1982) and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1986) reported preferred adult habitat in water less than 9.1 m deep over silt, sand, boulder, or bedrock, at velocities below 30 cm/s. Minckley et al. (1981) found adults in pools next to eddies, areas with little or no current, and travertine dams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regulation:** The proposal states that river flow fluctuations are controlled by rainstorm intensity and duration, snowmelt, basin characteristics, and other factors. The mainstem below Glen Canyon Dam is regulated, so dam operations are a central concern.
- **Habitat-availability questions:** Habitat availability at changing discharges is central to the study, because it determines whether flood stages and flow volumes alter habitat for juvenile and adult chub.
- **Tributary context:** Tributaries are considered potential sites for a second spawning population in the Grand Canyon. The study will evaluate their suitability for recovery.
- **Associated species:** The sampling is designed to capture humpback chub together with other native and introduced fishes. The document names the [[Leopard Darter]] (*Percina pantherina*) as a comparison species in cited habitat studies, but it is not a study target.

## Key Findings

### Study Objectives
1. Identify and quantify preferred habitats of juvenile and adult humpback chub and other species in the LCR.
2. Identify and quantify seasonal habitat use patterns in the LCR.
3. Identify potential humpback chub habitat in the Colorado River tributaries and evaluate its suitability for recovery.
4. Develop discharge-frequency and flow-duration curves at LCR sites to determine how flood stages affect habitat.
5. Identify information and research needed to enhance environmental conditions for fish and wildlife in the LCR and other tributaries.

### Working Hypotheses
- **H1:** Juvenile and adult humpback chub are randomly distributed throughout available habitats.
- **H2:** Habitat use by juveniles and adults does not vary seasonally.
- **H3:** Habitat use does not vary among differing age classes.
- **H4:** Habitat availability and habitat use do not vary over a continuum of flow volumes.

### Microhabitat Sampling in the LCR
- **Study area:** From the confluence with the Colorado River upstream about 21 river kilometers to Blue Springs.
- **Design:** The area is divided into four 5.25 km subreaches. Within each subreach, three randomly selected 1 km reaches are sampled for two days each (12 reaches total). Seasonal trips last 12 days.
- **Measurements:** Water temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, turbidity, and conductivity are measured at depth, sampled during day, night, and crepuscular periods.
- **Gear:** The proposal uses gill and trammel nets, seines, hoop and fyke nets, and minnow traps. Gill and trammel nets are placed in gangs of three to five at 25 m intervals. Seines are used in all seasons, with higher catch rates reported in summer.
- **Recorded data:** Fish total length, standard length, individual biomass, depth, and orientation are recorded. Habitat data include water velocity, depth, and substrate complexity.
- **Analysis:** The proposal plans to use [[Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test]] to test whether densities across habitat variables differ from uniform.

### Macrohabitat and Tributary Sampling
- **LCR transects:** 22 permanent transects run perpendicular to flow at 1 km intervals from the confluence to LCR km 21, measured during low-flow periods.
- **Measurements:** Velocity is measured at 0.6 depth with a Marsh McBirney or equivalent meter. Depth is measured with a wading rod, and substrate is classified by the Modified Wentworth scale.
- **Tributaries:** Paria River, Bright Angel, Shinumo, Tapeats, Deer, Kanab, and Havasu creeks will be surveyed with analogous methods. Transects will extend upstream from confluences to points where channel morphology blocks fish passage.

### Streamflow Analysis
- The proposal outlines a three-stage approach: statistical analysis of available records, synthetic streamflow generation, and water surface profile computation.
- Streamflow analysis based on records of 50 years or longer is described as the most reliable.
- Candidate models include the SWRRB (Williams et al. 1985), HEC-1, and HEC-2 programs from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers.
- Eleven planned steps run from mapping gages and watershed boundaries through analysis of future irrigation effects on low flows.

### Data Analysis Plan
- Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests compare depth, velocity, and substrate use with availability.
- Contrast analysis with ANOVA tests seasonal depth preferences. Dunn's procedure with Kruskal-Wallis tests evaluates seasonal substrate and velocity preferences.
- Principal Components Analysis and discriminant function analysis are planned to separate habitat variables that distinguish presence from absence of chub.
