# Endangered Arizona Fishes For Future Generations

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This article documents the critical endangerment of three native Arizona fish species—[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)—that were once so abundant in the [[Colorado River]] Valley that settlers harvested them with pitchforks from irrigation canals. Arizona State University researchers W.L. Minckley and Paul Marsh are leading recovery efforts to study these species' life cycles and bolster their populations. The bonytail chub is identified as "the most critically endangered fish in the Southwest," with only an estimated few hundred individuals remaining in [[Lake Mohave]]. The broader context reveals that 16 of Arizona's 25 native fish species are endangered, representing a dramatic decline from abundance in the 1940s to near-extinction within 40 years.

## Background & Description

The three focal species were historically abundant throughout the [[Colorado River]] system, ranging from Wyoming south to Mexico in the Colorado River and east to the New Mexico border in the [[Gila River]]. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is described as large and pike-like. All three species possess unique adaptations allowing them to thrive in harsh aquatic environments characterized by warm, swift-flowing waters and natural cycles of flooding and drought.

Current population status varies dramatically among species:
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Most critically endangered; found rarely and only in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada border, 40 miles south of Las Vegas). Since 1976, Minckley found only 24 specimens during research. Estimated population: a few hundred individuals.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Also restricted to [[Lake Mohave]]; estimated population of approximately 50,000.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: Many thousands remain in the wild, but distribution is restricted to the [[Colorado River]].

The article notes that 16 of Arizona's 25 native fish species are on the endangered list, representing a broader conservation crisis for Southwestern endemic fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The decline of these species is attributed to two primary factors:

**Habitat Disruption from Dam Construction**: Dam construction has fundamentally altered river ecosystems. Natural annual cycles of flooding and drought are now completely controlled, with river flow dictated by electric power and irrigation demands. Dams create lakes and ponds upstream and reduce flow to a trickle downstream in some instances, creating habitats "far different from those in which these fishes lived for millions of years." The transformation from warm, swift-flowing rivers to impounded waters represents a complete habitat shift.

**Predation by Non-Native Species**: More than 70 non-native fish species have been introduced to Arizona waters as sport and baitfish. Species mentioned include [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], and several types of trout. These introductions may contribute to the disappearance of native species through predation and competition.

The researchers compare the population collapse to the near-extinction of bison in the last century, emphasizing this is "not a nickel-and-dime change" but rather a shift from abundance to near-extinction within living memory (since the 1940s).

## Key Findings

**Research Infrastructure**: As of Autumn 1986, researchers established experimental populations at the [[ASU Research Park]] (Price and Elliot roads, five miles southeast of ASU). Three interconnected lakes covering 18 surface acres were stocked with 1,000 individuals each of [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The largest lake covers eight acres and reaches approximately 20 feet deep.

**Research Advantages**: The controlled environment at the Research Park provides:
- Elimination of 500-mile round trips to [[Lake Mohave]] or longer treks to upper [[Colorado River]] basin
- Ability to conduct intensive studies on populations that are difficult or impossible to find in the wild
- Safer capture methods compared to whitewater habitats
- Controlled conditions to determine causes of wild population decline
- Maintenance of genetic diversity through established refuge populations

**Conservation Strategy**: The recovery effort is funded by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service with cooperation from the Arizona Game and Fish Department. Researchers emphasize that habitat protection is the long-term answer to preserving Southwestern fishes, requiring increased public awareness and education of both scientists and the general public.

**Public Engagement**: Fishing is permitted at the Research Park lakes, but protective measures include plexiglass-encased posters instructing anglers to return any rare fishes to the water and contact ASU Center for Environmental Studies or Arizona Game and Fish Department with research-related information.

**Research Gaps**: Little is known about these fishes' life cycles or specific causes of decline because "they became rare before scientists could study them closely." The controlled populations aim to address these knowledge gaps.
