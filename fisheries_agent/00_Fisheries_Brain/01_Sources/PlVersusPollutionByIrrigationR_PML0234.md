# PL 92-500 Versus Pollution by Irrigation Return Flow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #water-quality-policy

## Executive Summary

This 1977 ASCE journal paper, written by three staff members of the EPA [[National Enforcement Investigations Center]] in Denver, examines how the [[National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System]] (NPDES) permit program under [[Public Law 92-500]] (the Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments of 1972) can be applied to [[Irrigation Return Flow]] (IRF). The authors argue that the municipal and industrial treatment-oriented permit approach fits poorly with irrigation, that conventional end-of-pipe treatment is largely impractical, and that control must rely on "housekeeping" measures, optimum water application, and flexible, area-based permitting. They conclude that the existing monitoring-only "first generation" permit approach will not produce meaningful data or achieve the statutory goals.

## Background & Description

- **Legal framework:** Section 301(b) requires [[Best Practicable Control Technology]] (BPT) by July 1, 1977, and [[Best Available Technology]] (BAT) by July 1, 1983, for point sources other than publicly owned treatment works. Section 304(b) directs EPA to issue effluent guidelines; Section 306 sets new-source performance standards. The January 16, 1973 Federal Register listed the minimum of 27 new-source categories.
- **Scale of irrigation:** More than 48,000,000 acres are irrigated in the US, all but 5,000,000 of them in 17 western states. Irrigation accounts for 35% of withdrawals from streams and aquifers and 84% of freshwater consumption.
- **Application methods:** Flood irrigation is most common; sprinkler and trickle (including perforated-pipe and subsurface) systems are increasingly used.
- **Water balance:** About half of applied water is consumed by transpiration and evaporation or lost to deep percolation; the remainder returns as tailwater, base flow, seepage, tile drainage, or spills.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

IRF degrades receiving waters in several ways, with the authors describing irrigation-related pollution as equal in national importance to municipal and industrial waste, measured by degradation of receiving waters in water-short areas:

- **Salinity:** Salt loading (leaching, pumped groundwater, displaced saline groundwater) and salt concentrating (transpiration, evaporation, stream abstraction) raise stream salinity. Annual salt yields in the Colorado River Basin range from 8.5 tons/acre (Price River Valley) to under 0.1 ton/acre (Green River Valley). Total dissolved solids in the lower Colorado River approach or exceed 1,000 mg/l; the salinity hazard at Imperial Dam (1973 data) is rated C3-S1 "high," predicted to become "very high" if TDS reaches 1,300 mg/l by 2000. Boron approaches the 0.33 ppm limit for sensitive crops such as citrus.
- **Nutrients:** Phosphate from natural sources combined with nitrogen from irrigation drains stimulates nuisance aquatic growth in parts of the Northwest, causing eutrophication, impaired fisheries, depressed oxygen, and taste and odor problems. Notable affected waters include the Snake River impoundments and the San Joaquin River.
- **Pesticides:** Pesticides enter waters via drift, runoff, subsurface drainage, equipment cleaning, and direct aquatic applications. In 1971, 10% of reported fish kills in the five western EPA Regions were attributed to pesticides, but 30% in Region IX (Arizona, California, Nevada). Organochlorines persist and bioaccumulate; organophosphates are more acutely toxic but less persistent.
- **Temperature:** Tailwater warming is likely but poorly quantified; subsurface return flow is usually not warm enough to cause serious problems.
- **Suspended and settleable solids:** Erosion from fields, canals, and drains blankets stream and reservoir bottoms, impairing benthic life, navigation, storage, and recreation. Problems are worst in areas with unlined canals and silty soils.

## Key Findings

**1. Control techniques and their limits**
- Most available controls are "housekeeping" measures (canal lining, optimized water and agrochemical quantities, leveling, scheduling, elimination of tailwater, subsurface and trickle delivery, tile drainage, pump-back systems) rather than end-of-pipe treatment.
- End-of-pipe options such as settling ponds are only marginally successful, unsuited to water-short or saline areas, costly in land, and can remobilize adsorbed pesticides under anaerobic conditions. Advanced treatment and desalination are economically prohibitive for irrigation sources.
- Pump-back systems can cause intolerable salt buildup in marginally saline soils and are often impractical on certain layouts (e.g., center-pivot systems).

**2. Water rights conflict**
- Western "beneficial use" and prior-appropriation law leads irrigators to use their full allocation, much of it unnecessary for optimum yield. Applying only the water needed for leaching and crop requirements would reduce salt leaching, preserve dilution flows, retain solids and agrochemicals on fields, and lower treatment costs.
- The authors note that court decisions on "duty of water" increasingly hold that water beyond optimum crop needs is not beneficial use, though challenges are expected.

**3. Responsibility for permits is difficult to assign**
- Drainage quality on one farm may depend on upstream or neighboring practices (the paper cites a Farm H example receiving underflow from Farms E, G, and I). Districts often lack police powers, and federal ownership of drains complicates permit responsibility.

**4. Regulatory history**
- EPA's 1973 guidance exempted operators with under 3,000 acres and required only monitoring in the first permit generation.
- In *NRDC v. Train*, the Federal District Court for the District of Columbia (June 10, 1975) negated the exemption and required regulation of all point sources, while suggesting flexible tools such as generic or area permits and minimal-requirement categories.
- EPA's proposed February 1976 agricultural regulations treat controlled irrigation discharges to navigable waters as point sources, while natural precipitation runoff is excluded. Discharges must contain pollutants, result from controlled application, flow from a discernible conveyance, and enter navigable waters.

**5. The monitoring approach is failing**
- Few permits had been issued by late 1975, and monitoring was only just starting on a limited scale (notable exceptions in Idaho and the Yakima Valley). Permits typically require only temperature, pH, specific conductance, and sometimes suspended solids, sampled quarterly, often only during the irrigation season.
- The authors conclude this will yield few data points and that changing practices will make them meaningless, so it cannot achieve BPT by 1977 or BAT by 1983.

**6. Projected consequences of inaction**
- Continued degradation of Western and Southern waters, especially salinity and nutrients, rising domestic treatment costs, health effects from consumption, fisheries decline in the Northwest, and loss of citrus and other investments. Federal water-use laws might eventually be imposed on state water rights frameworks.

**7. Recommended program**
- Acceptance of optimum water application, with education to show economic gains from higher yields and lower fertilizer costs.
- Flexible designation of permittees: the owner of a common drain for a large district, or individual irrigators where smaller drains discharge directly.
- EPA-funded grants and contracts to run optimum-practice demonstration plots in each major irrigated area, with site-specific permit limits derived from their results.
- Bureau of Reclamation collaboration on optimum operation in new projects, and legislation giving districts police powers to act as permittees.
- Technical assistance for unorganized operators and state legislation requiring districts to meet BPT and BAT.

**Abstract summary:** Pollutant discharges from IRF can be controlled through optimum water use, carefully controlled pesticide and fertilizer applications, elimination of tailwater, and water reuse, implemented via NPDES permits linked to EPA-funded demonstration plots in each major irrigated area.
