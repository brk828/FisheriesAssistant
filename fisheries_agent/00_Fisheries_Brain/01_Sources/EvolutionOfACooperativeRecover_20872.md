# Evolution of a Cooperative Recovery Program for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This review traces how the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for four endemic [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes came about. Large dams, water diversion and introduced fishes altered the river and drove declines in the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Federal laws, especially the [[Endangered Species Act]], then forced water developers, agencies and conservation groups into negotiation. After about four years of talks, the program was finalized in 1987 and set a fifteen-year recovery goal for the upper basin.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Four endemic large-river fishes, collectively called the endangered Colorado River fishes. Three (squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail) were federally listed as endangered. The razorback sucker was a candidate for listing and was considered endangered in Colorado.
- **Endemism:** The native fauna has 74% endemism (Miller 1958), which reflects long isolation.
- **Historic habitat:** The river was warm and turbid in summer, with large changes in volume and velocity. Its headwaters are clear and cold, flowing from the Rocky, Uinta and Wind River mountains.
- **Water context:** The Colorado receives less water per unit of drainage area than any other major U.S. river, yet supplies about 15 million people. It is governed by the [[Colorado River Compact]] (1922) and the 1948 Upper Colorado River Compact.
- **Dam construction:** The [[Colorado River Storage Project]] (authorized 1956) created six upper-basin reservoirs. Main-stem dam construction was completed in 1980 with the filling of Lake Powell. Construction began in the lower basin with Hoover Dam in 1935.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat alteration.** Dams and water developments changed natural flow and temperature regimes and turbidity. They inundated stream habitat, blocked migration routes and replaced high spring flows and low winter flows with stabilized seasonal flows that fluctuate daily in some areas.

**Introduced species.** Introductions added predators and competitors. The reported proportion of introduced fishes rose over time:

| Source | Native | Introduced |
|---|---|---|
| Richardson 1976 (Colorado River Wildlife Council), 50 species | 20 (40%) | 30 (60%) |
| Holden and Stalnaker 1975 | 10 (34.5%) | 19 (65.5%) |
| Tyus et al. 1982a, 55 fishes | N/A | 76% |

**Chemical and cold-water effects.** The largest rotenone treatment in the United States, made in 1962 on the Green River before [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closed, eliminated the squawfish and probably the bonytail and humpback chub from the reservoir basin. The chemical moved downstream into Dinosaur National Monument. Binns (1965) judged that habitat change from the dam was likely more important than the poisoning. Cold releases below the dam eliminated some large native fishes from a 105-km reach. The Yampa River confluence moderated temperatures, and the endangered fishes persisted downstream of it.

**Lower basin declines.** After Hoover Dam, the squawfish was extirpated in the lower basin. Bonytail, humpback chub and razorback sucker were drastically reduced. A viable humpback chub population persisted in the [[Little Colorado River]].

## Key Findings

**Historical timeline of research and policy**

- **Early 1960s:** Pre-impoundment and post-impoundment studies focused on water quality and game fish. The [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] studied the Green River below Flaming Gorge Dam from 1963 to 1966.
- **1968:** The U.S. Water Resources Council found enough water for upper-basin fishing needs if minimum flows and reservoir pools were kept for game fish. The report ignored the endangered fishes.
- **1975–1976:** The Colorado Squawfish Recovery Team was formed in December 1975 and expanded in 1976 to cover all four species.
- **Mid-1970s:** Life-history studies on the razorback sucker (McAda 1977) and squawfish (Seethaler 1978) followed.
- **1979:** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] signed a Memorandum of Understanding. This created the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]], described as the largest single fishery project for endangered fishes in the United States. Its initial scope covered 965 km of the Colorado and Green rivers and tributaries.
- **1981:** The Memorandum of Understanding was amended to add humpback chub studies in the Little Colorado River and studies of the Dolores and Gunnison rivers.
- **1981:** The Service's [[Windy Gap Process]] for [[Section 7 Consultation]] was developed. It required project sponsors to make one-time monetary contributions. The process raised nearly $1.3 million for fish conservation between 1981 and 1987, against an estimated implementation cost of $25 million.
- **After 1985:** The Windy Gap Process was dropped for large depletion projects after environmental groups objected to its "excessive and unnecessary risks of extinction." Interim procedures replaced it.
- **1984:** The Service had issued nearly 100 biological opinions finding that cumulative water depletions were likely to jeopardize the fishes. A draft Conservation Plan proposed minimum flows based on historic conditions. Upper-basin states reacted strongly against it.
- **March 1984:** The [[Upper Colorado River Coordinating Committee]] was formed with federal and state agencies, conservation groups and water users.
- **September 1987:** The Recovery Implementation Program was finalized after nearly four years of negotiation and an environmental assessment.

**Recovery program structure**

The program has a ten-member [[Upper Colorado River Implementation Committee]]. Voting members include the Service, the Bureau of Reclamation, the Western Area Power Administration, the states of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, an environmental representative, and private water interests. It sets five recovery elements:

1. **Provision of instream flows:** Quantify required flows, protect releases from federal reservoirs, and acquire water rights in the Yampa and White rivers.
2. **Habitat development and maintenance:** Research whether backwaters, jetties and fish ladders help recovery.
3. **Native fish stocking:** Run hatchery and rearing programs to supply research fish and augment populations.
4. **Management of non-native species and sportfishing:** Control predators and competitors where feasible.
5. **Research, monitoring and data management:** Review progress and guide future research.

**Goals and funding**

- The goal is to recover and delist the squawfish, humpback chub and bonytail and to free the razorback sucker from ESA protection. The initial timeframe is fifteen years from 1987.
- The projected annual budget is $2.3 million, adjusted for inflation.
- Congress appropriated $1 million in 1988 for instream flow water acquisition.
- Two capital funds are planned: at least $10 million for water rights and $5 million for facilities such as fish passageways and rearing facilities.
- Water depletions may be offset by a one-time contribution of $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion, plus legal protection of instream flows under state law. This contribution is applied only case by case, depending on progress toward instream flow protection.

**Main conclusions and open issues**

- The program is described as a milestone. It is the most comprehensive endangered-fish recovery effort in the United States and gives the first long-term funding commitment for acquiring and protecting instream flows.
- Its most controversial parts are habitat improvement, predator and competitor control, reservoir operations for fish habitat, and the Section 7 consultation process. The program does not set a consultation process for direct impacts such as migration barriers, inundation and temperature changes. Those are handled case by case.
- The authors state that recovery must rest on sound biological principles and that water must be balanced among developers, power users, environmentalists and anglers.
