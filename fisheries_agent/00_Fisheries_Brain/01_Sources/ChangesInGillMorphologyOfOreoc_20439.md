# Changes in Gill Morphology of Oreochromis mossambicus Subjected to Heat Stress

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study examines how acute heat stress alters the gill ultrastructure of the [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (*Oreochromis mossambicus*, family [[Cichlidae]]). Fish acclimated to 25°C for 14 days were exposed to 30, 35, 40 and 45°C. Gill epithelia were progressively damaged between 30 and 40°C, and the authors link these morphological changes to the hypoxia that causes heat-induced mortality. The study uses both [[Scanning Electron Microscopy]] (SEM) and, for the first time in this context, [[Transmission Electron Microscopy]] (TEM). The authors conclude that gill structural damage, rather than the microridge surface, is the main driver of heat-related death.

## Background & Description

Little prior work described the physical effects of thermal stress on teleost gills. [[Rombough and Garside 1977]] reported subepithelial edema, lamellar capillary congestion and delamination of the respiratory epithelium in the [[Banded Killifish]] (*Fundulus diaphanus*), and argued that these injuries cause fatal hypoxia. Earlier SEM work by the senior author's group, including [[Jacobs et al. 1981]] on [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) and [[Tilney et al. 1983]] on *O. mossambicus*, showed reduced gill surface area through loss of microridges and shrinkage of secondary lamellae.

The fish used were 92–170 mm total length, collected near the Amalinda hatchery. Acclimation was at 1°C per day to a control temperature of 25°C, followed by 14 days at that temperature under a 12/12 h light/dark photoperiod. Test groups were moved directly to 30, 35, 40 or 45°C (±1°C). Three fish per group were sampled after a 7-day exposure or at death. Gill arches were perfused with cold 5% phosphate-buffered [[Glutaraldehyde]], then processed for TEM (ultrathin sections) and SEM (critical point dried, gold coated). Table 1 of the source lists the procedural steps (fixation, dehydration, embedding, sectioning, examination and photography) for both methods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses [[Hypoxia]] in fish at elevated temperature. Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen, and metabolic demand rises, so gill function becomes critical. The authors also discuss [[Osmoregulation]]: sudden temperature changes disrupt ionic balance across gill epithelia, and maintaining that balance under stress needs extra energy. Mucus on the gills is considered as a barrier against ectoparasites, a defence against toxicants such as heavy metals, and a factor in oxygen permeability. The paper also refers to the [[Root Effect]], in which elevated CO2 reduces blood oxygen-carrying capacity. Its relevance to power-station thermal effluent is implied by the cited behavioural review ([[Stauffer 1980]]), but the document does not describe any field thermal discharge directly.

## Key Findings

- **Control (25°C):** Secondary lamellae were well defined, with a single-cell-layer epithelium, large lamellar blood spaces and a thin mucous film. Microridges were sparse on the gill filaments.
- **30°C:** Morphology resembled the control. Folded basement membrane suggested slight shrinkage. No tissue damage was recorded.
- **35°C:** Filament microridges remained well defined. The lamellar epithelium thickened and folded. The authors describe this as a degree of stress rather than tissue damage.
- **40°C:** Extensive damage included epithelial edema, shortened secondary lamellae, blood cysts, breakdown of [[Pillar Cells]], shrinkage of lamellar blood spaces and a highly convoluted basement membrane. Many organelles, probably mitochondria, were present in epithelial cells.
- **45°C:** Microridges broke down, with widespread edema, delamination of the epithelium from the basement membrane, and mucus lifting from the cell surfaces. The authors note less damage than at 40°C, attributing this to shorter survival time at 45°C.
- **Lethal limit:** The cited upper lethal temperature for this species ([[Allanson and Noble 1964]]) is about 38.20–38.25°C. Fish at 40 and 45°C died shortly after transfer, and morphological results matched the behavioural observations.
- **Microridge function:** Secondary lamellae of *O. mossambicus* had very few or no microridges. The only ridges were those formed by intercellular adhesion zones. The authors conclude that microridges serve mucus retention or water turbulence rather than respiration in this species, and that they are of secondary importance for respiration.
- **Mechanism of death at 40°C:** The authors propose that hypoxia results from a longer oxygen diffusion distance, caused by epithelial edema, secondary lamellar shrinkage and collapse of lamellar blood spaces after pillar-cell breakdown. This supports the hypothesis of [[Jacobs et al. 1981]].
- **Acclimation:** A companion experiment showed the species adapts well to 35°C when acclimated at 1°C per day.
- **Limitations:** The sample was small (three fish per temperature group at the sampling point), and the authors note that mucus may be lost during electron-microscope preparation.
