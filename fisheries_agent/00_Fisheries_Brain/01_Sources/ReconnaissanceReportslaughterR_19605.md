# Hydraulic Reconnaissance Report - Slaughter Ranch Water Resources

#hydrology #water-resources #artesian-aquifer #springs

## Executive Summary

This reconnaissance report documents water resources at the [[Slaughter Ranch]] in [[Douglas, Arizona]], conducted June 26-29, 1978, by hydraulic engineer Henry Edgar. The ranch contains multiple springs and artesian wells originating from an 1887 earthquake-induced fault system creating an artesian reservoir. Water rights claims have been filed for six springs and ten artesian wells with priority dates ranging from 1882 to 1943. The water is of excellent quality, historically bottled and sold commercially until the 1930s.

## Background & Description

The [[Slaughter Ranch]], under ownership of Mr. Paul Ramsower in 1978, contains an extensive artesian aquifer system. The geological formation resulted from an 1887 earthquake that created a fault south of the ranch in Mexico, forming an impervious barrier and artesian reservoir centered just above the border in the ranch's central sector. The artesian aquifer extends north into the [[Stevens Ranch]] (60 acres of alfalfa irrigation) and south into Mexico.

Water quality is excellent with low total dissolved solids. The water was commercially bottled and sold to Douglas residents from the mid-1920s through early 1930s prior to municipal system development. Springs and artesian wells have reportedly flowed essentially undiminished since 1926.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The water resources support multiple uses including irrigation, stockwatering, domestic supply, and recreational purposes. One artesian well is equipped with a pump for sprinkler irrigation. Production is influenced by three irrigation wells on the [[Stevens Ranch]] and two wells in Mexico, all used for alfalfa irrigation.

The springs include:
- [[Headquarters Spring]] (San Bernardino Spring): 100 acre-feet/annum
- Headquarters Domestic Spring: 10,000 gallons/annum (19 gpm)
- [[Barn Spring]]: 8,000,000 gallons/annum (15 gpm)
- South Mesa (Bathtub Spring): 5,000,000 gallons/annum (9.5 gpm)
- [[Cattail Spring]] & [[Figtree Springs]]: 26,000,000 gallons/annum (49 gpm)
- Mesa Seep Spring: 2,000,000 gallons/annum (3.8 gpm)

## Key Findings

**Water Rights Claims**: Through attorney Ben F. Williams Jr., claims were filed with the Arizona State Land Department for:
- Six springs with priority dates of January 1, 1882 (Headquarters Spring) and January 1, 1884 (five springs)
- Ten artesian wells (Wells #1-10), each claiming 250 acre-feet/annum with priority dates of January 1, 1903 (Wells #1-7, #9-10) and January 1, 1943 (Well #8)

**Geological Origin**: The artesian system originated from an 1887 earthquake-induced fault in Mexico creating an impervious barrier and artesian reservoir.

**Water Quality**: Excellent quality, low total dissolved solids, suitable for commercial bottling (historical use mid-1920s to early 1930s).

**Flow Stability**: Springs and artesian wells have maintained essentially undiminished flow since 1926 according to current and historical owners.

**Cross-Border Hydrology**: The aquifer system extends across the U.S.-Mexico border, with production influenced by irrigation wells on both sides.

**Survey Details**: Field reconnaissance conducted June 29, 1978, 9:00 a.m. to 2:30 p.m., with property owner Paul Ramsower.
