# Black and White Crappies in Clear Lake, Iowa, 1950-1961

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study in the *Iowa State Journal of Science* by Richard A. Neal documents a population shift in [[Crappie]] species in [[Clear Lake Iowa]], a shallow eutrophic lake of 3,643 acres in north-central Iowa. Catches moved from a population dominated by [[Black Crappie]] (1948-1956) to one dominated by [[White Crappie]] by 1961. The shift coincided with increased turbidity and decreased water levels. Condition factors of black crappies declined steadily, while those of white crappies stayed fairly constant. Growth indices for the two species were negatively correlated, and neither species' growth correlated with air temperature or water level. Year-class strength showed little of the dominance often reported for crappies.

## Background & Description

Clear Lake was studied through gill nets, seines, an electric shocker, a trawl, and fyke nets, with the Fisheries Research Unit and the Iowa Lake Survey Crew supplying data. Crappies made up approximately 28% of the fish taken by anglers in Clear Lake during summer 1961. In the 1940s, black crappies were the more abundant species (Bailey and Harrison, 1945; Erickson, 1952). Bailey and Harrison (1945) listed white crappies as "rare," and about 20,000 white crappies planted between 1933 and 1939 apparently did not reproduce. Erickson (1952) reported a ratio of five black to one white crappie in collections made from 1941 to 1950.

Scales were read by plastic impressions under a microprojector, following the [[Carlander Reading Procedure]]. First- and second-reading agreement was 83% for white crappies and 67% for black crappies. Growth was back-calculated using the [[Hile Growth Index]] and the Van Oosten (1929) method, with intercepts of 1.6 inches (white) and 1.7 inches (black).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat partitioning.** Gear ratios in 1961 suggested that black crappies favour shallow, weedy inshore areas and white crappies favour open water. Black crappies were usually taken nearer shore, which the author links to vegetation. Bailey and Harrison (1945) noted that black crappies prefer vegetated waters.

**Turbidity and vegetation.** Secchi readings show a turbid period in 1948-1950, a clear period in 1951-1954, and a turbid period in 1956-1961. Aquatic vegetation was sparse in 1948-1950 (following [[Forney 1955]] and [[Parsons 1950]]), and the clearest water coincided with moderately abundant vegetation in 1951-1954. Carp, bullheads, and boating were considered as turbidity sources, but none showed drastic change in the preceding decade.

**Water level.** Lake level tracked Secchi readings closely. Low water exposes former sediment areas to wave action, which resuspends material. A crowding effect from lower water (1955-1959) may have affected reproduction (Johnson, 1945).

**Temperature.** Mean April-October air temperatures at Mason City showed no trend and no clear link to crappie abundance or growth.

**Related species.** [[Yellow Bass]] growth and condition declined over the same period (Buchholz, 1960), and the [[Yellow Perch]] and bullhead populations are discussed in the wider Clear Lake literature.

## Key Findings

- **Population shift:** Black crappies comprised 68-100% of catches in 1948-1953, falling to 14% in 1960 and 5% in 1961. Black crappies made up 9.1% of young-of-the-year crappies in 1960 and 4.7% in 1961.
- **Gear selectivity:** The 1961 black:white ratio was 4:100 for experimental gill nets, 186:100 for small seines, 17:100 for the electric shocker, and 3:100 for the trawl. Overall was 6:100.
- **Catch per gill-net hour:** White crappies rose from 0.001 (1953) to 0.276 (1961). Black crappies fell from 0.109 to 0.011 over the same period.
- **Condition factor:** Mean black crappie condition declined steadily from 58.1 (1951) to 48.9 (1960), then rose to 52.8 in 1961. White crappie condition varied little (44.0-51.2).
- **Growth (Hile index):** Black and white indices were negatively correlated (r = -0.656, significant at 5%). White crappie growth was below average in 1948-1953 and above average in 1954-1960.
- **Lee's phenomenon:** Present in black crappies in later years but absent in white crappies. The cause is unknown.
- **Year-class strength:** No strongly dominant year classes were found. The 1950 black and 1957 white year classes were strong but not dominant.
- **Water level and turbidity:** Mean water level correlated with the percentage of black crappies (r = -0.749, significant at 1%). Turbidity (Secchi) and percentage black were not significantly correlated (r = 0.289).
- **Temperature:** No significant correlation with Hile indices for either species.
- **Conclusion:** The author attributes the shift to turbidity and vegetation changes linked to water level, with white crappies replacing black crappies as turbidity increased. The author notes that white crappies are more tolerant of turbid water (Hubbs and Lagler, 1947; Harlan and Speaker, 1951). The lake was expected to return to outlet flow in 1962 after heavy rain, and future population changes were left for monitoring.
