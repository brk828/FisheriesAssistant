# The Comparative Returns of Various Sizes of Trout Stocked in Massachusetts Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #fisheries-management #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1956 study by James W. Mullan (Massachusetts Division of Fisheries and Game), conducted under the Dingell-Johnson stream investigational project, examined whether the size of hatchery-reared [[Trout]] at stocking influences their return rate to anglers in a put-and-take fishery. Using jaw-tag recovery data from 62,680 tagged [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Rainbow Trout]] stocked across five major Massachusetts stream drainages (1952–1954), the study found a clear, statistically apparent trend: larger stocked trout yielded significantly higher angler return rates than smaller trout. The findings support economic and management arguments for prioritizing the stocking of larger trout size classes over larger numbers of small yearlings, echoing earlier survival work by [[Miller 1952]] and [[Miller 1954]] on hatchery-reared [[Cutthroat Trout]].

## Background & Description
The study employed an arbitrary Massachusetts hatchery grading system based on total length and growth rate, rather than strict age classes:

| Grade | Size Range | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 6-8's | Up to 8 inches (TL) | Yearling trout |
| 8-9's | 8–9 inches (TL) | Fast-growing yearlings or slow-growing 2-year-olds |
| 9-12's | 9–12 inches (TL) | 2-year-olds |
| 12+'s | Greater than 12 inches (TL) | Discarded brood stock |

- Measurements: Individual length measurements were taken on all or representative samples of each size lot.
- Tagging method: Circular **monel-metal jaw tags**, size-matched to fish, attached to the mandible.
- Anesthesia: Fish were immobilized with a **0.5% urethane solution** prior to tagging.
- A referenced companion finding (Miller 1952) noted differential mortality by size/age: of 199 three-year-old [[Cutthroat Trout]] (12–15 in. fork length), about one-third died shortly after stocking, compared to nearly all of 201 two-year-olds (5–8 in. fork length).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Lotic freshwater systems — specifically five major Massachusetts stream drainages: [[Westfield River]] (1952), [[Millers River]] (1953), [[Squannacook River]] (1953), and the [[Merrimac River]] & [[Ipswich River]] drainages (1954).
- **Stocking context**: Trout were released as part of a put-and-take recreational fishery model, with stocking occurring primarily in March (with limited in-season stocking also assessed).
- **Survival factors**: The paper proposes that larger trout possess a physiological/ecological advantage — greater body size enables better tolerance of stream velocity and competitive pressures (e.g., against wild fish and other hatchery trout), leading to longer survival post-stocking and greater cumulative exposure to angling pressure.
- **Confounding ecological variables**: Return rates varied substantially between drainages due to differences in runoff, angling pressure, watershed characteristics, and the presence/absence of tag-return boxes (used on the [[Millers River]], [[Ipswich River]], and [[Merrimac River]] drainages), limiting valid comparisons to within-drainage, within-species contrasts only.

## Key Findings
1. **Size-dependent return rate**: Across 62,680 tagged trout (brook, brown, rainbow) stocked in March over 3 years, larger fish showed significantly greater angler return rates than smaller fish.
2. **Return hierarchy by size class**:
   - 6–8 inch yearlings: **poorest return**
   - 8–9 inch (yearling/2-yr-old): intermediate return
   - 9–12 inch 2-year-olds: **highest return**
   - 12+ inch brood stock: comparable to 9–12 inch fish (but excluded from economic comparisons as non-economical surplus stock)
3. **Quantified increment**: Average actual return was **21% for 6–8 inch trout** versus **31% for trout above 8 inches** — approximately a 50% relative increase in return for larger fish.
4. **Tag-recovery caveat**: Reported percentages represent raw tag recoveries with **no correction for angler non-reporting**, meaning true survival/harvest differentials could differ from raw recovery figures.
5. **Alternative explanations considered**: The authors considered but downplayed the possibility that anglers simply preferentially reported tags from larger, more "interesting" fish, concluding this was insufficient to explain the magnitude of difference, especially in drainages using systematic tag-return boxes.
6. **Preferred explanation**: Differential post-stocking survival — larger trout better withstand stream conditions and competition, resulting in longer residency and greater cumulative angling vulnerability — consistent with [[Miller 1952]] and [[Miller 1954]] survival research on hatchery vs. wild [[Cutthroat Trout]].
7. **Economic/management implication**: In 1954, ~1 million legal-size trout (6+ inches) were stocked in Massachusetts, with ~500,000 being small (6–8 in., averaging <7 in.) yearlings. The authors calculate that reducing numerical stocking by one-third while stocking an equivalent poundage of 8+ inch fish could maintain angler harvest numbers while **increasing total creel weight by 33%**.
8. **Practical recommendation**: Achieving this shift would require artificial induction of early spawning (via light manipulation) and holding smaller fish for in-season stocking timed to avoid high-water mortality periods preceding the Massachusetts trout season.
