# Evaluation of the Effects of Tusher Wash Diversion Dam on Movement and Survival of Juvenile and Subadult Native Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 2000 final report by Peter A. Cavalli (Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, [[Recovery Implementation Program]] Project CAP-15) evaluates whether [[Tusher Wash Diversion Dam]] on the [[Green River]] near Green River, Utah, blocks upstream movement of subadult native fish, entrains native fish into canals and power facilities, and needs modification. Field work was conducted in 1998. A second year of fieldwork was not funded, and 1998 flows were roughly twice normal during the low-flow period, so the first two objectives were only partly addressed.

Native fish were entrained into the canal system. Subadult [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] were not found in the canals, but one hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] died on a trash rack at the power plant. Tagged fish passed the dam at about 4,820 cfs, yet average-year flows fall below this level, so passage at low flow remains unknown. The authors conclude that entrainment and probable mortality need attention, and that reducing canal diversion may be a cheaper alternative to costly dam modifications.

## Background & Description

**Structure and operation.** The dam is about 840 feet long and spans the full river width, about 8 river miles north of the town of Green River. It was built in 1906 from timber cribbing filled with rock, with a concrete cap added in 1936. It is roughly 9 feet high. A sluiceway on the west side feeds a water wheel on the east side, and a central notch is about 0.5 feet deep.

**Diversions.** No flow meters are installed on the facilities. Estimates put roughly 715 cfs entering the west-side canal system at full capacity. About 200 cfs reaches the [[Thayn Power Plant]] and pump station, where 35 cfs is pumped uphill to the 42-foot ditch ([[Thayne Ditch]]). The [[Green River Canal]] was carrying about 90 cfs during the irrigation season. A 1999 court decision limited legal flow through the power plant and pumping station to a maximum of 425 cfs (as read from the scanned text). Total diversions at the dam ranged from 5.9% to 29.3% of average monthly flow in recent years.

**Study species.** The study focused on [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. Humpback chub and bonytail are also native to the Upper Colorado River Basin and were captured upstream and downstream of the dam. Earlier work documented adult and subadult Colorado pikeminnow moving upstream past the dam.

**Objectives.**
1. Determine whether the dam prevents upstream movement of subadult natives, especially at low flow.
2. Determine whether the raceway or Green River Canal entrains native fish.
3. Determine whether the dam needs modification to better accommodate native fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Larval entrainment.** Earlier monitoring (UDWR, unpublished) recorded larvae of speckled dace, unidentified suckers, [[Red Shiner]], and [[Channel Catfish]] in the canal system, with the same assemblage captured in the Green River.

**Flow and temperature.** The 1998 hydrograph was well above average during the low-flow season, which the report links to the low larval catch. Water temperatures in the river were monitored with a thermograph during the drift periods (Figure 4).

**Canal conditions.** The canal was dry at times, allowing searches for stranded fish. Debris was abundant. Predators and scavengers (birds, mammals, and people fishing) were noted in the canal, which may have reduced fish counts. Northern leopard frogs and crayfish were also found in the canal system.

**Power plant intake.** In February 1999 a concrete wall was built in front of the intake to increase water delivered to the Green River Canal. This raised water velocities at the trash grate, impinging large fish. A turbine was shut off on May 20, 1999, and the intake was removed on June 30, 1999, to reduce velocities.

## Key Findings

**Larval drift netting (1998).**
- Drift nets were set May 5–26 (targeting razorback sucker larvae) and June 29–August 22 (targeting Colorado pikeminnow larvae).
- No razorback sucker larvae were captured.
- Colorado pikeminnow catch was significantly higher in the Green River than in the canal, but with so few larvae the validity of this result is questionable.
- Only 12 Colorado pikeminnow larvae were captured in the Green River during the study, compared with over 100 caught with similar effort between 1991 and 1996. The report attributes the low catch to high late-summer flows.
- Other native species were captured in both locations, and catch rates did not differ significantly for other species.

**Subadult and adult entrainment.**
- The canal was drained for the 1998 gate closure and searched on August 6, and the canal was searched again on November 30, 1998. Few fish were found (one juvenile carp, one speckled dace, and one channel catfish were recorded).
- A hatchery-reared razorback sucker stocked in October 1998 was found dead on the grate in May 1999.
- Large numbers of other fish were removed from the grate, including flannelmouth and bluehead suckers, about 85 common carp, and other unidentified species.
- The report estimates that the dam and canal system are responsible for the loss of roughly 20% of razorback sucker larvae and over 50% of Colorado pikeminnow larvae (values as read from the scanned text).

**Tagging and electrofishing.**
- Electrofishing produced 34 Colorado pikeminnow and several Gila species, including roundtail chub, humpback chub, and unidentified chubs.
- PIT tags were implanted in 31 Colorado pikeminnow and in common carp and other species. Anchor tags were applied to about 231 flannelmouth suckers, about 192 bluehead suckers, channel catfish, and black bullhead.
- None of the tagged Colorado pikeminnow or Gila species were recaptured.
- Electrofishing catch rates for Gila species were significantly higher upstream of the dam (p = 0.0007).
- Common carp catch rates were significantly higher downstream of the dam (p = 0.0061).
- Other size and catch-rate differences were not significant, and the authors attribute them to small sample sizes, especially for Colorado pikeminnow (only three captured upstream).

**Radio telemetry.**
- Radio transmitters were implanted in seven subadult Colorado pikeminnow and five subadult bluehead suckers. Flannelmouth suckers were also radio-tagged (Table 10).
- None of the radio-tagged Colorado pikeminnow moved upstream past the dam. Most moved downstream soon after release, consistent with [[Cavalli]] (1999).
- Three flannelmouth suckers moved upstream past the dam, two of them at flows near 4,800 cfs.
- The longest bluehead sucker movement recorded was about 21.9 miles. Contact was lost for three of the five tagged bluehead suckers after several weeks.

**Fish passage conclusions.**
- The dam is not a complete barrier to upstream subadult movement when flows reach at least about 4,800 cfs.
- Mean catch rates and sizes of most species did not differ between upstream and downstream sites.
- Average base flow in recent years is about 2,500 cfs, and it is unknown whether subadult natives can pass at that level.
- A fish passage structure may eventually be needed, but no data support or reject the assumption that occasional passage is sufficient.

**Recommendations.**
- Install screens small enough to exclude larval fish at the canal and power plant intakes, though debris and sediment may make screens impractical. Larger screens or vertical louvers may suit larger fish.
- Consider other options, such as rotating drums that return larvae to the river and reduced diversion during larval drift periods. Reducing diversion would protect all life stages but could reduce crop production and power generation, so a cost comparison is recommended.
- Collect passage data when flows are near 2,500 cfs to decide whether a fish ladder is needed.
- Use electronic flow meters in future drift netting, because mechanical meters failed frequently in 1998.

**Overall conclusion.** Native fish, including Colorado pikeminnow and Gila species, were entrained in the Green River Canal system. Passage at the dam is confirmed only at high flows, so the effects of the dam at average low flows remain unresolved.
