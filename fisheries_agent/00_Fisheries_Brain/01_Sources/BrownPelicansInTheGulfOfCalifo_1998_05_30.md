## Executive Summary
This 1998 field report from D. Anderson, J. Keith, E. Palacios, and colleagues (UC Davis) documents a severe, ENSO-linked reproductive failure among colonial seabirds nesting on islands in the [[Gulf of California]] during the strong 1997-98 El Niño event. Although sea surface temperatures had largely normalized by spring 1998, the residual effects of the prior winter's anomalously warm water manifested as widespread nest abandonment, delayed breeding phenology, and elevated adult mortality across multiple species, most notably the [[Brown Pelican]]. While the source text is centered on avian breeding ecology rather than direct fish sampling, it provides indirect but important evidence of ENSO-driven collapse in the small pelagic forage-fish base that underpins the region's seabird food web — a key link between [[El Niño Southern Oscillation]] oceanographic anomalies and marine trophic disruption in the [[Sea of Cortez]].

## Background & Description
No direct fish morphological data are presented in this source. The only fish-relevant biological detail concerns the [[Osprey]] (*Pandion haliaetus*), described as "the most resident of the breeding seabirds in the study area," which feeds largely on **resident, local species of fish** rather than on pelagic or migratory prey stocks. This dietary specialization on nearshore, non-migratory fish populations is proposed as the likely explanation for the Osprey's comparatively higher nesting success relative to pelagic-foraging species during the 1998 El Niño.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Trophic structure**: Seabird breeding success in the Gulf of California is tightly coupled to the abundance and availability of small pelagic forage fish (e.g., sardines, anchovies) and other prey typically enhanced by cold, nutrient-rich upwelling. ENSO-driven warming disrupts this upwelling, reducing prey availability for pelagic-foraging species such as [[Brown Pelican]], boobies, cormorants, terns, and storm-petrels.
- **Resident vs. pelagic foragers**: The contrast between Osprey (feeding on resident local fish stocks, ~0.3 fledglings/nest attempt — the most successful breeder in 1998) and pelagic-foraging seabirds (near-total nesting failure) suggests that ENSO impacts were concentrated on offshore/pelagic fish prey populations rather than uniformly across all nearshore fish communities.
- **Nesting colonies affected**: Isla San Pedro Mártir, San Lorenzo area, Puerto Refugio, Isla Rasa, Isla Gemelos, Isla San Luis, Isla Smith, and Laguna San Ignacio (Pacific side) — spanning both the Midriff Islands region of the Gulf and the Pacific coast of Baja California.
- **Indicator species for prey collapse**: Storm-petrels (least and black), normally the most abundant birds on pelagic surveys in the Midriff Islands during March-April, were "very scarce," implying substantial depletion or redistribution of their zooplankton/small-fish prey base during peak El Niño conditions, with partial recovery by May.
- **Wintering seabird mortality**: Eared grebes, Pacific loons, and common loons showed elevated mortality and emaciation, along with delayed plumage development — consistent with a broader collapse in fish/invertebrate prey availability along the wintering range extending to the Pacific coast of Baja California.

## Key Findings
- **1998 Brown Pelican nesting failure** was the most extensive El Niño-related nesting failure recorded in Baja California/Gulf of California pelicans since long-term monitoring began in 1970, with all but four nests deserted colony-wide by mid-May.
- Colony-level declines were dramatic: Isla San Pedro Mártir fell from a normal 80-8,000 nests to a fraction of that; San Lorenzo area from 20,000 to 280 nests; Puerto Refugio from ~3,000 to 8 nests.
- **Phenological delays** of 4-5 weeks were observed across multiple species (Heermann's Gull, Elegant Tern, Royal Tern), consistent with delayed onset of favorable foraging/prey conditions.
- **Osprey**, feeding on resident local fish rather than pelagic stocks, had the highest relative breeding success of any species studied in 1998 (~0.3 fledglings/nest), despite still representing the lowest Osprey productivity recorded since 1971 — indicating that even resident nearshore fish populations were somewhat impacted, though far less severely than pelagic prey.
- Elevated adult **Blue-footed Booby** mortality was noted in beach surveys, and a distinct pelican die-off zone was identified from Bahía Magdalena north to Laguna San Ignacio, with carcass condition indicating mortality occurred in January-February 1998 — coincident with peak ENSO warming and presumed prey-base collapse.
- The study represents a continuation of a multi-decadal dataset (D.W. Anderson) spanning four major and three lesser ENSO events, providing a valuable long-term baseline for correlating [[Gulf of California]] fish/forage prey dynamics with seabird reproductive output under recurring [[El Niño Southern Oscillation]] cycles.

> **Note:** This source is fundamentally an ornithological/oceanographic field report on ENSO impacts to seabird colonies; it contains no direct fish population counts, species identifications, or morphometric data. Fish-related inferences here are indirect, derived from seabird foraging ecology and breeding success as proxies for regional forage-fish abundance.
