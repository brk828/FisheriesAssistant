# Weed Control Possibilities in Massachusetts

#aquatic-ecology #fisheries-management #invasive-species #water-quality #policy

## Executive Summary

This 1950s-era bulletin from the [[Massachusetts Division of Fisheries and Game]] addresses the growing problem of aquatic weed infestations in Commonwealth inland waters and evaluates various control methods. The document establishes that the Division does not assume responsibility for weed control (as funds derive from hunting and fishing licenses, not general taxation) and that most weed problems affect swimming and boating interests before fishing. The bulletin systematically reviews biological, mechanical, and chemical control methods, finding most ineffective, prohibitively expensive, or harmful to fish populations. Only water level manipulation, fertilization, dye application, and limited use of 2,4-D show promise under specific conditions. The document emphasizes that [[Pickerel]] populations—Massachusetts' most sought-after game fish—depend on rooted vegetation for reproduction, creating inherent conflicts between weed control and fisheries management.

## Background & Description

The bulletin identifies that many Massachusetts inland waters have become infested with various water-weeds, some to densities that prevent swimming and boating. These conditions typically exist in very clear, shallow waters that historically supported minimal vegetation. The document attributes weed proliferation primarily to human activities: fragments transferred on boat bottoms, ill-advised introductions by property owners responding to advertising promises of enhanced waterfowl or fish production, aquarium dumping by pet owners, and past government agency encouragement of weed introductions.

The authors note that while certain fishes—particularly [[Chain Pickerel]] and relatives, and possibly certain minnows—require rooted aquatic vegetation for successful reproduction, the need for water-weeds in other fish production has been "greatly overemphasized." The Division explicitly states it "neither sponsors nor condones the introduction of water-weeds for purposes of fish production of any kind."

The document establishes clear policy: the Division does not assume responsibility for weed existence or control, as its license-funded mandate focuses on fish and game problems rather than swimming and boating interests. Effective weed control would likely eliminate flourishing pickerel populations, creating management conflicts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The bulletin provides ecological context for understanding aquatic vegetation dynamics in Massachusetts waters. Clear, shallow waters with adequate light penetration support dense rooted vegetation. The document notes that acid, bog waters with brown coloration naturally have few weeds due to reduced light penetration from suspended materials.

Regarding fish-vegetation relationships, the authors emphasize that [[Chain Pickerel]] require rooted aquatic vegetation for spawning success, making this species vulnerable to weed control efforts. The document suggests that most other fish species do not require extensive rooted vegetation, contrary to popular belief.

The bulletin describes how water-weed fragments can regenerate from cuttings, enabling rapid vegetative propagation and spread throughout water bodies. This characteristic makes mechanical control methods counterproductive, as cutting operations create numerous viable fragments that establish new colonies.

Environmental conditions affecting weed control include water chemistry, depth, clarity, and the presence of certain primitive plants like [[Chara]] that can remove dyes from water. The document notes that fertilization creates dense algal blooms that shade out rooted vegetation by limiting light penetration—essentially mimicking natural bog water conditions.

## Key Findings

**Biological Control Methods (Generally Ineffective):**

1. **Crayfish introduction**: Widely tried throughout the United States with no success despite vegetative feeding habits. Modern understanding recognizes crayfish value as forage for [[Smallmouth Bass]] but not for vegetation control.

2. **Other animals** (turtles, muskrats, Nutria): Despite feeding on water-weeds, these animals are most abundant in heavily vegetated areas that remain choked with vegetation year after year.

3. **Carp**: Fairly effective in controlling rooted submerged vegetation through feeding behavior that suspends bottom muds, creating perpetual turbidity that blocks sunlight. However, this method is prohibited because it destroys native fish habitat, decimates game fish populations (which feed by sight), eliminates spawning beds, and cannot be confined to single water bodies. Carp stocking requires permits that will not be granted (General Laws, Chapter 131, Section 47).

4. **Parasites and diseases**: Limited agricultural success with biological control has not translated to aquatic weeds. British correspondence revealed that the disappearance of [[Elodea]] (Anacharis) from Thames Estuary was not due to parasitic fungi as erroneously suggested; actual causes remain unknown. Only two fungi recorded on Elodea: one observed once in 1889 and never again, the other harmless.

**Mechanical Methods (Generally Counterproductive):**

1. **Cutting and pulling**: Hand cutting with sickles/scythes or power mowing devices are "generally worse than useless besides being extremely laborious and expensive." Almost all submerged water-weeds regenerate from cuttings; fragments spread with currents to establish new colonies. U.S. Army Corps of Engineers' extensive power cutting operations failed, with weed problems worsening to require continuous cutting to maintain narrow channels for only days at a time. Massachusetts experience confirms these disadvantages.

   Exception: Continuous cutting of lily pads for 3-4 consecutive years (preventing any stalks from reaching surface) will exhaust root food supplies and cause death. However, this typically results in rapid invasion by dense submerged weeds once shading effect is removed.

2. **Water level control**: Fluctuating impoundments do not support rooted vegetation. Winter drawdown of 8-10 feet minimum, allowing pond bottom freezing, can kill vegetation. Complete drainage may be very effective but risks fish population damage or total destruction. Arbitrary drainage of public fishing waters constitutes serious legal offense (fish are Commonwealth property; see General Laws, Chapter 131, Sections 38 & 117).

   Conversely, raising water levels several feet through dam construction can provide partial control by exceeding depth tolerance of various plant species, killing deeper weeds and opening central clear areas. New margins will become infested. Generally impractical in Massachusetts due to shoreline development and resulting property damage lawsuits.

3. **Scraping or flushing**: Removing fertile pond-bottom muds after drainage has theoretical merit but costs "several hundred dollars an acre" with technical problems likely insurmountable. Doubtful that effects would be long-maintained even if technically feasible.

**Chemical Methods:**

1. **Copper sulphate**: Widely used in drinking water treatment for algae control at quantities only slightly below fish-lethal concentrations. Quantities sufficient to destroy rooted vegetation would kill all aquatic life and prevent reestablishment for long periods. Massachusetts water supplies treated with copper sulphate support far fewer fish per acre than untreated public waters, with extremely low numbers of available game fish, probably due to poor food production from copper toxicity. **Cannot be permitted** for general weed control.

2. **Sodium arsenite**: More effective than copper sulphate for rooted weeds and relatively non-destructive to fish, but extremely poisonous to dogs, cattle, horses, humans, and all warm-blooded animals. **Cannot be permitted** as public menace.

3. **Chlorinated benzenes**: Various benzene compounds (marketed under trade names combining syllables from "benzene" and "chlorine") effectively kill weeds but only temporarily. Extreme cost ($50-75 per acre for materials alone, requiring 2+ treatments annually) and fish toxicity make them impractical. Several Massachusetts ponds treated heavily (one case: several thousand dollars expended) achieved control lasting only weeks. Pre-knowledge of similar failures elsewhere could have prevented wastage. Not all compounds tested; research may uncover satisfactory chemical from this group.

4. **2,4-D**: Limited success controlling surface-floating vegetation. Massachusetts achieved some success controlling [[Water Chestnut]] by spraying with 2,4-D and No. 2 fuel oil mixture, though hand pulling also required. Reproductive nut-like bodies may be unaffected. Limited success in aquaria and small Canadian ponds using water solutions. Very temporary effects require 2-3 treatments annually at ~$45 per acre for materials (labor additional). Almost completely non-toxic to fish at application rates. **Promising for small private ponds; use would be approved by Division.**

5. **Dyes**: Utilizes principle that brown-colored (acid bog) waters have few weeds due to suspended material reducing light penetration. Water-soluble, non-toxic dyes can effect control under specific conditions:
   - Pond must be completely landlocked or lack outlet
   - Near absence of [[Chara]] (primitive plant that removes dye from water)
   
   Results in somewhat black water with reduced light penetration; rooted aquatics wilt and die. Coloring lasts ~2 years under Massachusetts conditions, diminished effect through third year as sun action modifies chemical. Current cost: $7.50/acre for ponds ≥20 acres, $10/acre for smaller ponds (materials only; negligible labor). Annual cost effectively $3.75-5.00/acre. Dye non-toxic to all animals, does not stain skin or hair. **Use would be approved subject to noted limitations in severe infestation cases.**

6. **Fertilization**: "Perhaps the most effective and satisfactory" method except possibly dye method under specified conditions. Involves commercial agricultural fertilizers applied directly to water at rates up to half ton per acre annually in 10-12 equal applications at bi-weekly intervals during spring/summer. Produces dense pea-green "bloom" of microscopic algae that clouds water continuously, limiting sunlight penetration so rooted vegetation cannot develop or existing plants wilt and die.

   Extremely successful in many U.S. regions but generally limited to small ponds due to total expense. Current fertilizer cost: ~$30/acre/year (possibly reducible to $20/acre). Treatment must continue annually, though later years may allow reduced rates. Fastidious swimmers will find "pea-soup" conditions undesirable, but choice is clear: such conditions or no swimming.

   Added benefit: much greater fish poundage produced due to larger food supply. In natural waters with dozen+ fish species, cannot always predict which will flourish most. Fairly certain that fertilization will eliminate pickerel due to vegetation loss needed for reproduction. Unknown whether artificial spawning aids could substitute.

**Legal Framework:**

All methods require compliance with Massachusetts General Laws, Chapter 131:
- **Section 28**: Director may prohibit/regulate discharge of substances injuring fisheries
- **Section 29**: Prohibits poisoning fish; violators liable for twice damages to municipalities and private fishery rights holders
- **Section 38**: Establishes fishing rights in non-great ponds
- **Section 47**: Requires Director's permit for fish stocking; carp permits will not be granted
- **Section 117A**: Requires 10-day written notice to department before draining water bodies to enable fish salvage

**No chemicals can be legally introduced into Commonwealth inland waters without written permit from Director** (Sections 28 & 29). Most applications denied because chemicals cannot be controlled properly, will not perform as claimed, and/or mean permanent elimination of valuable fish populations that are Commonwealth property.

**Summary Classifications:**

**Appendix I - Methods Known Ineffective (Cannot be condoned):**
- Crayfish encouragement
- Turtle/muskrat encouragement  
- Cutting and pulling
- Scraping/flushing pond bottoms

**Methods Known Harmful (Cannot be permitted):**
- Copper sulphate
- Sodium arsenite
- Chlorinated benzenes (as demonstrated)
- Carp, Nutria, other exotic animal introduction

**Methods Temporarily Useful Within Limitations (Could be authorized with legal safeguards):**

| Method | Approximate Materials Cost/Acre/Year |
|--------|--------------------------------------|
| Water level changes | Negligible to prohibitive |
| 2,4-D solutions | $150.00 |
| Dye introduction | $5.00-$10.00 |
| Fertilization | $30.00 |

*Labor costs additional for all methods*

The bulletin concludes that no generally applicable methods offer inexpensive, effective, permanent control. Best available methods (water level control, dye introduction, fertilization) possible only under special circumstances or at considerable annual expense. Fertilization is "the best and cheapest available tool under the widest range of conditions."
