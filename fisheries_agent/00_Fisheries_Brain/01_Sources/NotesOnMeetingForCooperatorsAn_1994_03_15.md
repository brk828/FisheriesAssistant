# Notes on Meeting for Cooperators and Interested Parties: Glen Canyon Draft Biological Opinion

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #ConservationBiology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document summarizes a March 14, 1994 interagency meeting (Arizona Projects Office, Phoenix) convened to review scientific research underpinning the Draft Biological Opinion (2-21-93-F-167) on Glen Canyon Dam operations, and to reconcile findings with the March 2, 1994 scientists' meeting in Las Vegas. The central subject is the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) population in the Grand Canyon reach of the Colorado River, with secondary consideration of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. Discussion centered on whether the Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) flow regime — versus the Modified Low Fluctuating Flow (MLFF) alternative — would sufficiently mitigate jeopardy to native fishes caused by dam operations. Key driving variables identified include flooding magnitude/timing, thermal regime, flow variability, backwater/sediment dynamics, food base (drift), and nonnative species interactions (predation/competition). Researchers broadly agreed that neither flow scenario alone would fully "remove jeopardy," and that temperature modification (e.g., via a multi-level intake structure) is likely necessary to substantially increase mainstem population numbers, though flow manipulation still meaningfully affects populations.

## Morphological / Life-History Characteristics

- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*, family Cyprinidae): Adults classified as fish >175 mm; this size class shows little detectable response to flow magnitude, duration, or hydrograph shape. Early life stages (eggs, larvae, juveniles, and fish <150 mm) are the demographically sensitive stages and the primary focus for recruitment-based management.
  - Scale and otolith analyses used to estimate age/growth; annual growth checks (rings) visible at ages 4–5, associated with a physiological disruption when fish move from ~20°C (Little Colorado River) to ~10°C (mainstem) water — potentially indicating a minimum survivable size for mainstem-reared fish.
  - Larval light traps proposed as a survey method to detect survival across life stages.
  - Fat storage capacity in the species is limited, and spawning success is linked to annual food resource availability (energetics not fully understood).
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]**: Described as sight-feeders; predation efficacy is reduced by turbidity.
- **[[Brown Trout]]**: Documented as a predator of juvenile/young humpback chub (unlike rainbow trout, per field observations by Bill Leibfried).
- **[[Fathead Minnow]]**: A nonnative competitor/prey species; humpback chub were observed feeding on fathead minnows in the prior year's data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Population structure**: The Grand Canyon humpback chub population is the largest range-wide, split into two components — (1) fish residing permanently in the [[Little Colorado River]], and (2) fish that move between the Little Colorado River and the mainstem Colorado River. Approximately 90% of humpback chub occur within a ~20-mile reach centered on the Little Colorado River confluence; ten additional smaller aggregations occur canyon-wide, associated with warm springs, tributaries, or comparable habitat features.
- **Seasonal movement**: Adults aggregate in the mainstem and migrate into the Little Colorado River in spring; young descend into the mainstem during summer. High flows may disperse humpback chub further downstream.
- **Thermal constraints**: Cold mainstem temperatures (post-dam) are believed to prevent humpback chub from reoccupying historic mainstem habitats; the Little Colorado River offers a warmer thermal refuge (~15°C pre-flood, per Cataract Canyon comparison) suitable for spawning.
- **Habitat drivers** (from March 2 meeting driver/parameter framework):
  - **Flooding** — categorized as Maintenance (<31,000 cfs, ~1/yr), Habitat-building (~45,000 cfs ±, ~1/8 yrs), and Uncontrolled (>31,000 cfs + tributary flow, turbid, ~1/100 yrs). Floods are needed to create/maintain habitat but affect early life stages far more than adults.
  - **Backwaters** — important nursery habitat; relative importance possibly increased post-dam due to lowered mainstem temperatures; young backwaters are less productive than mature ones; annual maintenance floods not strictly required to sustain backwaters or eddy return channels.
  - **Food base/drift** — highly fluctuating flows produce the most drift; steady flows reduce drift availability, potentially limiting food for young fish, though no evidence yet that current operations limit adult food supply.
  - **Turbidity** — reduces sight-based predation (e.g., by rainbow trout); its overall importance is predator-species-dependent.
  - **Nonnative interactions** — Nonnative fish catch rates drop sharply during flood events in comparable canyon systems but rebound once flows recede; native fishes are comparatively flood-adapted. An estimated 3 orders of magnitude above base flow would be needed to meaningfully displace nonnatives in the Grand Canyon — a magnitude considered unlikely to ever be implemented.
  - **Contaminants/disease** — Selenium was raised as a potential concern for breeding, possibly mitigated by settling with sediments in Lake Powell; Asian tapeworm and other disease/parasite dynamics flagged as an ongoing concern under altered flow operations.
- **Proposed second spawning population sites**: Candidate mainstem locations discussed for establishing a second self-sustaining spawning aggregation include River Mile 30, [[Shinumo Creek]], and River Mile 126–129.

## Key Findings

1. **Jeopardy determination**: Fish and Wildlife Service position (per Frank Baucom) is that Glen Canyon Dam operations jeopardize humpback chub by preventing mainstem spawning, leaving the Little Colorado River as essentially the sole spawning site; conditions in the Little Colorado River are considered part of the environmental baseline, not a separable risk factor.
2. **Flow vs. temperature**: Flow manipulation affects humpback chub populations, but temperature is considered the more critical limiting factor; jeopardy likely cannot be removed without temperature modification (e.g., a Multi-Level Intake structure), which was recommended for further study, including effects of warmer releases on nonnative species.
3. **RPA vs. MLFF**: Both flow scenarios fall within the general physiological tolerance range of humpback chub, but neither alone is expected to remove jeopardy. A majority of researchers favored the RPA as having a higher probability of benefiting humpback chub/native fish populations relative to MLFF, though consensus was not unanimous.
4. **Recruitment focus**: Because adult humpback chub show minimal detectable response to flow variables, management and research recommendations should prioritize recruitment and early life stages (spawning, larval drift, egg/larval migration, young-of-year, juveniles).
5. **Data/statistical limitations**: Given system complexity and time constraints, rigorous statistical comparison between flow scenarios was deemed largely infeasible; decisions must rely on best professional judgment.
6. **Distribution as a trend indicator**: Humpback chub distribution has apparently remained stable since at least 1977 in surveyed locations, but reproductive/recruitment status at those sites remains unknown, limiting the strength of "population stabilization" claims in the Biological Opinion.
7. **Experimental design gaps**: Concerns were raised (Larry Stevens, Bob Lynch, Jan Balsom) about the RPA experimental flows lacking a true control/comparison design; suggestions included river-fluctuation-with-controlled-backwater experiments and laboratory-based studies.
8. **Other native species**: Data on [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] are not well defined; measures benefiting humpback chub are expected to carry over benefits to these species.
9. **Monitoring vs. research distinction**: Participants noted the RPA's flow criteria blur research and monitoring objectives, complicating evaluation of experimental success.
10. **Historical baseline**: Post-dam fish community data indicate native species composition; three native fish species have been lost from the system historically, per discussion (source of quantitative data was questioned during the meeting).
