# Better Stocking Methods and Trout Nutrition: The Progressive Fish Culturist, August 1936

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1936 issue of *The Progressive Fish Culturist*, published by the [[Bureau of Fisheries]] (U.S. Department of Commerce), contains practical hatchery and stocking guidance plus trout nutrition material. Its lead article by S. U. Kell, Stream Improvement Supervisor, argues that the survival of hatchery-reared fish after planting, not the number reared, is the only measure of success. The issue then covers transport and planting procedures, equipment such as the pack can, feeding diets based on fresh meats, dry products and canned foods, condemned packing-house products, reader correspondence, book reviews, and a Japanese study on carrot and vitamin-rich soybean oil in trout diets.

## Background & Description

The issue is a numbered technical bulletin (No. 9, August 1936) written for hatchery workers and field staff. Its author notes that "hundreds of millions" of publicly and privately reared fish had been planted with little or no result, because of poor understanding of environmental requirements and careless handling after delivery to applicants.

The article describes a typical failure sequence: a sportsmen's group receives a shipment, the transport arrangements are poorly planned, fish are dumped into water far from the planned site, and most die. Kell compares the outcome to planting potatoes on a rock pile and expecting a crop.

The remaining sections are:
- Stocking methods (planning, transport in cans, acclimation, aeration, and planting)
- Trout feeding (fresh meat, dry products, mixed diets, canned foods)
- Condemned packing-house products for fish food
- Reader correspondence from the Neosho (Missouri) station and a commercial hatchery
- Book and article reviews (fish diseases in the Pacific countries; Japanese trout experiment)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water temperature matching:** Water in transport cans is usually colder than the stream in spring, late fall and summer conditions differ, so temperatures must be equalized gradually by replacing can water with stream water. Fish from a hatchery whose water chemistry differs from the destination should be acclimated slowly.
- **Aeration:** Fish crowding at the surface signal low oxygen. Operators are told to dip water and pour it back from a height to aerate.
- **Habitat selection:** Fish should be planted in pools with cover, away from falls or strong current, and in quiet water where they can recover before dispersing. Predators and birds are noted as threats to newly planted fish.
- **Stream restoration context:** The author links stocking to pollution elimination, stream restoration, habitat rehabilitation and selective breeding as parts of a wider conservation effort.
- **Overfishing:** A small stream can be depleted by "a half dozen anglers in a week," and a badly depleted water may take a lifetime to recover naturally.

## Key Findings

**1. Stocking success is the real measure of hatchery work.** Kell argues that fish must survive and be catchable. He recommends reducing the number reared if necessary so that enough funds and labor remain for proper planting. He suggests that rearing about half the fish currently produced, with assured survival, would give better results.

**2. Proper planting requires trained personnel and planning.** Planting should be done under the direct supervision of trained, interested workers. Agencies such as deputy game wardens, forest and park rangers, and fire wardens are useful but often too busy with emergency duties. A general plan, equipment list and advance scouting of the stream are recommended.

**3. Transport and acclimation guidelines.**
- Clean cans with boiling water; carry spare cans.
- Keep water sloshing in cans to reduce crowding.
- Aerate with a dipper when fish rise to the surface.
- Equalize temperature gradually, ideally checking with a thermometer.
- Avoid planting in fast current, below falls or in the open sun.

**4. Pack cans for rough terrain.** The [[Pack Can]] is an equipment design, built of light sheet metal with shoulder straps and a perforated lid. It can carry about half a can of water, nearly doubling the fish carried per trip compared with conventional cans. The article reports one carrier moved as many as 50 trout (5 inches) per can for 10 miles. Specifications were available from the Bureau of Fisheries.

**5. Diet recommendations for trout.**
- Fresh meat and offal are standard, but fish used as feed must be perfectly fresh. Partly decayed fish caused serious losses.
- Long-term use of a single fresh-meat diet gives unsatisfactory results.
- Dry products are more economical per unit of nutrition than fresh meat. Fresh meat (about 70% water) must be multiplied by about 3 for cost comparison with dry meal (under 10% water).
- Dry meals (animal origin such as salmon egg meal, meat meal, fish meal, dried milk) should be mixed with raw liver. Fine grinding improves the mix.
- Vegetable meals support slower growth and may be used for economy at a 10 to 20% level.
- Feeding should be restricted, because concentrated diets can kill fish if overfed.

**6. Suggested diet formulas (by weight).** Several mixtures were proposed, most with about 50 to 60% pig or sheep liver, 20 to 30% salmon egg meal or meat meal, and 20 to 30% fish meal or dried milk. An example: 50 pounds liver, 20 pounds dry salmon egg meal and 30 pounds dry meat meal make 100 pounds of diet.

**7. Condemned packing-house products.** The Bureau of Fisheries was testing condemned packing-house products (livers, lights, hearts) for fish food, with treated condemned liver costing several cents per pound less than fresh or frozen standard liver.

**8. Reader correspondence.** Neosho station data reported that a ration of 65% horse meat and 35% sheep liver gave better growth in rainbow trout than straight liver. A commercial rainbow trout hatchery used 66% beef and 34% sheep liver plus cereal supplements. A proposed iodine solution (5 g iodine, 10 g potassium iodide, in water) was mentioned. Sheep liver price rose to $3.95 per hundredweight in the previous quarter, with a further increase expected.

**9. Book and article reviews.** [[H. S. Davis]] reviewed recent discoveries in fish diseases in Canada, Japan and the United States, including furunculosis among wild Rocky Mountain whitefish and cutthroat trout at Elk Lake, British Columbia. A Japanese study by K. Hatta tested carrots and vitamin-rich soybean oil in one-to-one diets for three-year-old rainbow and brook trout over eleven months (May 1932 to April 1933). The text reports growth and egg-production values for the control and experimental groups, though the table data are partly illegible in the scan.
