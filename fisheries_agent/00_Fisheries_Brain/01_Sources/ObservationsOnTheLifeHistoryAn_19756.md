# Observations on the Life History and Movement of Cutthroat Trout, *Salmo clarki*, in Flathead River Drainage, Montana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by Howard E. Johnson, published in the *Proceedings of the Montana Academy of Sciences*, examines the [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*) in the upper [[Flathead River]] drainage of northwestern Montana, one of the few remaining cutthroat fisheries in the state. Field work ran through summer 1959 and spring–summer 1960, chiefly on the [[North Fork Flathead River]]. The study covers spawning behaviour, fecundity, early development, fry and fingerling distribution, movement via tagging, and age and growth from scale analysis. Results indicate that both resident and migratory populations exist, that tributary streams are key nursery habitat, and that tributary-raised fish grow more slowly than river and lake fish during early life.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*), a native salmonid historically abundant across Montana's Rocky Mountain waters.
- **Study area:** The upper Flathead drainage, mainly in Flathead County and [[Glacier National Park]], comprising the main Flathead River, the North, South and Middle Forks (arising on the Continental Divide at 8,000–10,000 ft), and numerous small tributaries. The main river flows 50 miles south through an agricultural valley into [[Flathead Lake]].
- **Major alteration:** [[Hungry Horse Dam]], completed on the South Fork in 1953, blocks fish movement and created a reservoir about 34 miles long with an estimated surface area of 25,000 acres.
- **Associated species:** [[Dolly Varden]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], longnose and largescale suckers, peamouth, redside shiner, northern squawfish and sculpins (*Cottus* spp.). Introduced or stocked fish include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Arctic Grayling]], [[Kokanee]] and [[Lake Whitefish]].
- **Methods:** Electrofishing, traps, minnow seines, angling and cresol, supplemented by angler-caught fish. Fish were measured to 0.1 inch and weighed to 0.01 lb, scale samples were taken, and plastic jaw tags were applied.
- **Prior work:** Studies by Echo (1956), Block (1955), Hanzel (1960) and others in Montana, Utah, Idaho, California, Oregon, British Columbia and Yellowstone.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stocking history:** Plantings began around 1910 near Glacier Park. Rainbow trout were introduced until 1950, after which stocking was restricted to avoid contaminating native cutthroat stocks. Yellowstone Lake cutthroat (or progeny) have been planted since 1915; their establishment or hybridisation with native fish is unknown. Only four rainbow trout were encountered during this study, and some fish showed rainbow × cutthroat hybrid traits.
- **Distribution:** Hanzel (1960) recorded cutthroat trout in 95 of 98 streams and lakes surveyed.
- **Spawning hydrology:** At Mud Lake's inlet, spawning was constrained to spring runoff because the shallow stream was inaccessible at other times. Stream temperature stayed at 39 °F or less during spawning, supplied by melting snow and springs at 40–45 °F.
- **Stream size and reproduction:** Fry and fingerlings were found almost exclusively in smaller streams, while young whitefish and dolly varden dominated larger ones. Severe spring scouring in large streams may reduce spawning success. Streams with headwater lakes (Logging, Langford and Cyclone Creeks) showed abundant fry, likely due to moderated flows and temperatures.
- **Growth environment:** Growth was slowest in tributaries, faster in rivers, and fastest in lakes during early years. Growing seasons are short at high elevations.

## Key Findings

**Spawning**
- Spawners were found between June 16 and June 24, 1959, and between April 25 and June 29, 1960, with late ripe males in August. Peak spawning in tributaries probably occurs in mid-June.
- Totals for both years: 16 males (all ripe) and 33 females (26 green, 5 ripe, 2 spent).
- At Mud Lake's inlet (April 21 to June 1, 1960), a trap captured 13 females and 23 males. Spawning was first observed on May 13, and as many as six pairs spawned in an area under four square feet. Females averaged 12.5 in and 0.86 lb; males averaged 11.0 in and 0.46 lb.
- Resident and migratory populations both appear to exist. Small resident trout (4–10 in) were abundant in tributaries year-round, and resident spawning was confirmed in Langford Creek. Large trout (12.0–19.3 in) appeared in tributaries only during spawning, presumably migrating from larger streams.

**Fecundity**
- Egg counts from 18 green females (mean 14.6 in, 1.01 lb) gave a mean of 1,482 eggs. The maximum was 1,874 (15.5 in) and the minimum 877 (13.3 in). A 6.3-in fish produced 183 eggs.
- Mean eggs by length group: 13–14 in, 1,236; 14–15 in, 1,548; 15–16 in, 1,625.

**Early Development**
- Four redds in the Mud Lake inlet: eyed eggs first appeared 46 days after spawning, and fry (mean 17 mm) at 60 days. Recently emerged fry (mean 23 mm) appeared at 77 days.
- Incubation water was 35–39 °F for 57 days, then rose to a maximum of 50 °F.
- Embryos in screened boxes suffered heavy mortality from fine sediment. Those in a spring hatched at about 50 days, and fry were free-swimming by 81 days (mean 26 mm at 89 days).

**Fry and Fingerling Stages**
- Fry and fingerlings were sampled in streams ranging from under 1 cfs to 258 cfs.
- Emergence extended through summer, with fry sizes ranging widely by August.
- Few cutthroat fry were found in the North Fork main stem: only one fish (38 mm) was caught, over two miles from the nearest tributary, though 3–8 in fish were abundant in summer pools.
- A culvert pool near Deep Creek held an estimated 70–100 trout (3–8 in), most displaced downstream during high water. Eight larger fish were 2–3 years old.

**Movement**
- A total of 175 cutthroat (≥6 in) was tagged: 108 in the North Fork, 23 in its tributaries, and 4 in the upper six miles of the main Flathead.
- Of 13 returns, 12 were fishermen returns and one came from the tagging crew. Eight of 13 were caught downstream of release points (10–40 miles). None showed upstream movement.
- Block (1955) proposed late-summer migration out of the North Fork, but Johnson's data suggest some fish remain year-round.

**Age and Growth**
- Scales were taken from 766 fish: 106 from small tributaries, 101 from lakes and 559 from rivers.
- Tributary fish grew slowest; the grand average was 2.3 in at age I, 4.5 in at II and 7.0 in at III. Fish of age IV averaged 2.6–2.8 in less than river and lake fish.
- River fish showed rapid growth in years 3–4 and widely spaced circuli after the second or third annulus, consistent with 2–3 years of tributary residence before river entry.
- Lake fish grew fastest early on (grand average 2.5 in at age I and 5.2 in at II). Growth rates were generally lower than in Logan River, Utah, and Yellowstone Lake, but comparable to coastal cutthroat in California.
- Age composition of 38 spawners: Age III 3%, IV 60%, V 24%, VI 13%. Mud Lake spawners matured earlier than river fish.
- Annulus formation varied by location. Some lower-river fish had new circuli by May 24, while high-elevation tributary fish still lacked new growth on July 4.

**Conclusions**
- Tributary streams serve as critical spawning and nursery habitat, while larger rivers and lakes support growth of older fish.
- Both resident and migratory life-history strategies coexist in the drainage.
- Management implications include limiting non-native stocking, protecting spawning tributaries from high-water scour, and recognising culverts as barriers.
