# Habitat Use by Humpback Chub in the Little Colorado River and Grand Canyon Tributaries

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #habitat-assessment

## Executive Summary

This 1992 annual report documents research on habitat use by [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and other native fishes in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and tributaries of the [[Colorado River]] within [[Grand Canyon]]. The study aimed to determine habitat availability and use patterns, identify spawning habitat, and evaluate potential effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on native fish populations. Despite ambitious sampling efforts (69 field days, 615 ASU hoopnets, 913 FWS minihoop nets, 599 minnow traps, 128 seine samples, 306 habitat transects), fish capture rates were extremely low due to continuous flooding throughout 1992. [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Humpback Chub]] remained the predominant species. The strong 1991 year class of humpback chub persisted throughout 1992 despite near-complete reproductive failure, demonstrating juvenile resilience to prolonged flooding conditions. Preliminary habitat analyses characterized depth, current, and substrate patterns from Blue Springs (km 21) to the confluence (km 0), with principal component analysis revealing cross-sectional and longitudinal gradients in habitat structure.

## Background & Description

The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] conducted this research as part of the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) Phase II program. Principal investigators Owen T. Gorman and Stuart C. Leon (Arizona Fishery Resources Office, Pinetop) and Q. Eugene Maughan (Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit, University of Arizona) led the effort. The study focused on the [[Little Colorado River]], a critical tributary supporting one of the few remaining spawning aggregations of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]]. Research objectives included: (1) measuring and mapping habitat availability in the LCR and other tributaries; (2) determining seasonal habitat use patterns by juvenile and adult fishes; (3) identifying spawning habitat; and (4) predicting effects of high discharges on habitat availability through modeling.

The LCR study area extended from the confluence with the [[Colorado River]] (km 0) to [[Blue Springs]] (km 21), the major source of perennial base flow. Two primary study areas were established: Powell Camp reach (km 2.5-3.5) and Salt Camp reach (km 10.5-11.5). Habitat variables measured included depth (21 categories from 0->200 cm), current velocity (6 categories from 0=no current to 5=torrent >1.2 m/s), and substrate (11 categories from silt to travertine/bedrock, modified Wentworth scale).

Nine research trips were completed in 1992 (February, March, April, June, July, August, September, November), totaling 69 field days. Sampling methods included ASU hoopnets, FWS minihoop nets, minnow traps, seining, and habitat transect measurements. Additional surveys covered smaller tributaries including [[Paria River]], [[Bright Angel Creek]], [[Shinumo Creek]], [[Tapeats Creek]], [[Deer Creek]], [[Kanab Creek]], and [[Havasu Creek]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The 1992 hydrological year contrasted dramatically with 1991. While 1991 remained at base flow from spring through late fall, 1992 experienced regular and prolonged flooding starting in December 1991. The LCR was above base flow throughout most of 1992, with regular spates and periods of prolonged flooding. During February-April, discharge typically ranged from 1000-3000 cfs. A brief pre-monsoon dry period in June allowed base flow (~200 cfs) for several weeks. With monsoon arrival, the river flushed regularly with peak discharges >3000 cfs. The largest flood occurred September 20, peaking over 6000 cfs.

Water quality parameters reflected flood conditions: water was generally very turbid and silt-laden, dissolved oxygen levels remained at saturation, water temperatures ranged from 8°C to 26°C, and conductivity ranged from 200-4000 µMHOs (typically ≤1000 as runoff diluted saline bicarbonate waters from Blue Springs). The continuous flooding transported tremendous sediment loads into the lower river channel, minimizing Blue Springs' influence. Travertine formation was not apparent while erosion of old travertine was evident.

Fish community composition in the LCR included native species ([[Humpback Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]) and exotic species ([[Fathead Minnow]], [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Rainbow Trout]]). Total captures in 1992: 693 humpback chub, 1560 speckled dace, 46 bluehead sucker, 17 flannelmouth sucker, 32 fathead minnow, 14 carp, 7 channel catfish. Most speckled dace captured in August-September came from the Atomizer Falls to Blue Springs reach (km 14.5-21). No humpback chub were taken above Chute Falls (~km 15), but 3 were captured near Chute Falls (~km 14.5). During sustained flooding, humpback chub was the predominant species captured, suggesting greater activity than other species during high turbidity conditions.

Tributary surveys documented native fishes in most systems. Notable captures included a large adult humpback chub (435 mm TL) with yellow Carlin tag (#668 or #8997) and a juvenile (100 mm TL) in [[Shinumo Creek]] confluence. Two large channel catfish (593 and 650 mm TL) were seined from [[Kanab Creek]]; the larger contained a partially digested bluehead sucker (245 mm TL).

## Key Findings

**Habitat Characterization**: Principal component analysis of 1991 habitat transect data revealed three major gradients: (1) PC1 (37.5% variance) represented a cross-sectional gradient from shallow, still, fine-substrate edges to deeper, faster, larger-substrate center channel; (2) PC2 (26.4% variance) represented an upstream-downstream longitudinal gradient of pool types—upstream pools were deeper with smaller substrates (sand-bottomed pools along vertical canyon walls and below travertine dams), while downstream pools were shallower with larger substrates (boulders and travertine reefs); (3) PC3 (23.1% variance) represented an upstream-downstream gradient of travertine dam/reef/riffle habitat types, with more expansive complexes in middle and upper LCR (especially km 11-15).

**Habitat Patterns by River Reach**: Mean habitat values revealed distinct reaches: Blue Springs reach (km 20-21) had shallow depths (36-39 cm), slow-moderate currents (2.4-2.5), small substrates (2.6-3.4), characterized by sand and boulder substrates with numerous springs. Below Blue Springs (km 17-19) depths increased (51-58 cm) with deep sand-bottomed pools against vertical canyon walls and fast boulder riffles. Above Chute Falls (km 15-16) travertine formations first appeared; the dam effect created a widened, sand-choked channel with decreased depths (36-38 cm), currents (1.6-2.6), and substrates (2.0-3.2). Big Canyon-Atomizer Falls reach (km 11-14) contained all large travertine dam complexes with increased depths (53-68 cm) and substrates (2.2-5.8). Sipapu to Salt Camp reach (km 7-10) was notably shallower (38-58 cm) with smaller substrates (2.7-5.0). The confluence (km 0) was markedly different, showing [[Colorado River]] backwater influence with greater depth (62 cm), much slower currents (1.3), and smaller substrates (3.3).

**Study Area Representativeness**: Percent similarity (PS) measures showed Powell Camp and Salt Camp study areas were most similar to neighboring reaches (PS values typically 0.73-0.74 for combined dimensions). Mean similarity for depth across all sections was 0.78, for current 0.77, for substrate 0.66, and for combined dimensions 0.74. The confluence stood out as very different from upstream reaches. Scatterplots of habitat data on principal components confirmed study areas represented subsets of their associated river sections.

**Fish Sampling Results**: Despite ambitious efforts, catch rates were extremely low, typically <0.5 fish/set in minihoop nets. Capture rates were highest in June when the LCR was at base flow for several weeks before monsoons (1159 fish captured). Monthly totals: February (66), June (1159), July (335), August (213), September (508), November (89). Three fish were lost to incidental mortality (two juvenile chubs, one speckled dace).

**Reproductive Failure in 1992**: While spring 1992 sampling by ASU researchers indicated humpback chub had spawned, 1992 reproduction of native fishes appeared to be a near-complete failure, evidently tied to regular and prolonged flooding. Despite this failure, the strong 1991 year class of humpback chub remained abundant throughout 1992, demonstrating juvenile capacity to dwell and thrive under continuous flooding conditions.

**Exotic Species Patterns**: Increased abundance of fathead minnows and presence of young-of-year common carp and channel catfish in September sampling (immediately following the largest flood >6000 cfs) suggested these exotics reproduce in upstream portions of the LCR drainage and are flushed into the perennially flowing lower portion by monsoon-triggered spates. Most were captured above Chute Falls.

**Fish Behavior During Flooding**: High turbidity and silt loads affected fish activity levels and habitat use. During periods of high discharge and high silt loads, passive sampling devices captured very few fish. During short base flow periods (June 1992), catch rates increased 4-20 fold. Most fish in September and November trips were sampled by active methods (seines). During declining sediment loads and turbidity, humpback chub were the first species to show increased activity. Researchers hypothesized that high sediment loads may place respiratory stress on resident fishes, requiring increased mucus secretion to clear gill filaments, reducing respiratory capacity and activity levels—essentially, when the LCR floods, fishes "hunker-down."

**Habitat Arrays 1991 vs 1992**: Although data were not fully analyzed, habitat arrays differed dramatically between years. Stream habitats in 1992 were deeper, faster, and dominated by sand and silt substrates compared to 1991.

**Tributary Survey Results**: All small tributaries were sampled at least once; [[Paria River]] was sampled monthly. Native fishes ([[Speckled Dace]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]) were relatively common and abundant in most tributaries. Common widespread exotics included brown and rainbow trout. Total tributary captures: 2 humpback chub, 3359 speckled dace, 357 bluehead sucker, 157 flannelmouth sucker, 38 fathead minnow, 5 channel catfish, 3 common carp, 220 brown trout, 239 rainbow trout, 413 cutthroat trout.

**Data Management and Analysis Plan**: All USFWS habitat and fish data were entered into dBASE IV files. A comprehensive analysis plan was outlined including: (1) descriptive statistics of transect data; (2) multivariate statistical analysis of transect data; (3) habitat measurements from fish sampling devices; (4) multivariate analysis of habitat from fish sampling; (5) habitat availability/sampling bias analysis; (6) analysis of fish habitat use data including selectivity, niche overlap, niche shifts, and ontogenetic changes; (7) comparison of tributary habitat and fish community structure to LCR patterns.

**Future Directions**: The final field season for USFWS involvement in GCES Phase II research would occur in 1993, focusing on initiating river hydraulic modeling research and evaluating feasibility of developing habitat models using instream flow methodologies. Critical habitat use data remained uncollected, with no matching fish data for 1991 habitat measurements. Success in 1993 would depend on more normal hydrological conditions and increased cooperation between research teams (USFWS, ASU, AZGF).
