# Review of the Draft Federal Long-Term Monitoring Plan for the Colorado River below Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riverine-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1994 pre-publication review by the [[National Research Council]] (NRC) Committee to Review the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] of the Bureau of Reclamation's *Draft Federal Long-Term Monitoring Plan* for the Colorado River below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The committee judges the plan important, because it may serve as a model for riverine monitoring in the United States and abroad, but finds that the April 1993 draft does not yet meet criteria for being well designed, clearly explained, and efficient. The committee's main objections are that the plan has no cost estimates, no firm research recommendations, weak specificity on sites, frequencies and methods, unclear administration, and a monitoring objective tied too narrowly to the preferred alternative in the draft [[Environmental Impact Statement]] (EIS). The committee recommends a rewrite, a comprehensive summary table, explicit cost estimates, a complementary research program, administrative independence, open contracting, and greater use of [[Non-Invasive Monitoring]] methods.

## Background & Description

**Document type:** NRC review report (pre-publication copy, Washington, D.C., 1994). It reproduces the Bureau of Reclamation draft plan as Appendix B, titled *Long-Term Monitoring in Glen and Grand Canyon: Response to Operations of Glen Canyon Dam*, by Duncan T. Patten, PhD, Chief Scientist, GCES, dated May 1993 (Advance Draft 7/93). Support came from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under Cooperative Agreement Number 6-FC-40-04240.

**Dam and legislative context:**
- [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed in 1963, authorized by the [[Colorado River Storage Project Act]] of 1956.
- Operating rules originally emphasized water delivery and peaking hydropower revenue.
- The [[Colorado River Basin Project Act]] of 1968 added water quality, recreation, and fish and wildlife purposes.
- The [[Grand Canyon Protection Act of 1992]] requires protection, mitigation, and improvement of natural and cultural resources of [[Grand Canyon National Park]] and [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]].

**Program history:**
- [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) began in 1982 under Bureau of Reclamation sponsorship.
- By 1986 the study area extended over the 255 miles from the Lake Powell forebay to the upper end of [[Lake Mead]].
- GCES peaked at $11-$12 million per year in 1990-1993.
- Major contributing agencies: Bureau of Reclamation, [[National Park Service]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], Western Area Power Administration, [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and U.S. Geological Survey. Indian tribes and consultants also contributed.
- The NRC committee has reviewed GCES since 1986 (NRC 1987; NRC 1990 symposium volume).
- A workshop on long-term monitoring was held in October 1992. The NRC invited 50 participants, selected for direct experience with long-term monitoring or measurement of environmental variables below the dam.

**Committee:** Chaired by William M. Lewis, Jr. (University of Colorado, Boulder; limnologist). Members include Clark Hubbs (University of Texas, Austin; ichthyologist with endangered-fish experience), William Graf (fluvial geomorphology), Robert C. Euler (anthropology), and other economists, hydrologists, and legal scholars.

**Plan scope (as reviewed):** The review covers scope of work, data acquisition and archiving protocols, organization, justification, and implementation. Plan components include Lake Powell, mainstem water and sediment, water chemistry, tributaries, sediment dynamics, aquatic food base, fishes, vegetation, terrestrial organisms, physical and cultural sites, tribal values, recreation, and power and economics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Setting:** The Colorado River through [[Grand Canyon]] is described in the plan as "the most heavily regulated large river in North America." Since the 1963 closure, its flow, sediment load, and dissolved constituents have changed dramatically (Appendix B, Introduction).

**Dam-driven processes the plan targets:**
- Discharge regime, including daily fluctuations and spills, which control sediment dynamics, habitat development, and biotic recruitment and survival.
- Sediment storage in channels, eddies, and elevated deposits such as beaches. Sediment is the primary substrate for many canyon biological processes.
- Nutrient trapping by the dam, nutrient transport changes within [[Lake Powell]], and water chemistry and temperature changes below the dam.
- Loss of [[Trout]] spawning habitat (redds) and stranding of young through fluctuating flows. Predicted enhancement of native fish recruitment by reduced flushing of larval fish from tributaries.

**Aquatic and riparian components:**
- Aquatic food base: phyto- and zoobenthos, and phyto- and zooplankton, with standing crop and dominance as proposed measures.
- Fishes: native species, including endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and non-native warmwater, coolwater, and trout populations.
- Riparian vegetation in three mainstem communities: old high water zone (OHWZ), new high water zone (NHWZ), and near-shoreline marshes. The OHWZ is maintained by habitat maintenance flows.
- Tributaries (Paria River, [[Little Colorado River]], Kanab, Bright Angel, and Havasu Creeks) serve as refugia for native fishes and trout, and as controls for mainstem changes.

**Contaminant pathways:** The committee notes that sediment moves heavy metals, radionuclides, herbicides, and pesticides through the system. Measurements at Lake Powell showed high concentrations of mercury and selenium. The [[Church Rock Spill]] of July 16, 1989 released about 100 million gallons of uranium mill tailings solids and liquids containing thorium-230 and radium-222 into the lower Colorado River system.

**Hydrologic benchmarks cited:**
- Maximum powerplant discharge: 31,500 cfs.
- Maximum regulated discharge and mean annual pre-dam peak flow: 100,000 cfs (proposed lower bound for the monitored zone).
- Maximum pre-dam flood: 220,000-300,000 cfs.
- Beach-building flows (controlled floods) are likely to exceed 30,000 cfs, and possibly be considerably higher.

**Spatial units:** Thirteen GIS reaches of 2 to 12 miles, mapped at 1:2400, were established by GCES. The geomorphic classification referenced is [[Schmidt and Graf Classification]] (1990). Monitoring ends at Separation Canyon (River Mile 240), the generally accepted head of Lake Mead.

**Wilderness conflict:** Under the [[Wilderness Act of 1964]], works of man in the inner canyon should not be visible. The committee stresses that monitoring infrastructure, such as cableways and towers, conflicts with this.

## Key Findings

**Overall assessment**
- The NRC committee believes the draft plan will be of great importance and could serve as a model for riverine monitoring elsewhere.
- It judges the present draft does not yet meet criteria for being well designed, clearly explained, and efficient.
- The plan is described as "confusing" and in need of extensive revision for clarity, logical cohesion, and specificity.

**Missing elements**
- No cost estimates. The committee recommends tabulating costs for each data collection type. Without them, the program may be arbitrarily underfunded.
- No firm research recommendations to complement monitoring. The committee expects monitoring to yield unexpected results needing explanation.
- No administrative, management, or contracting proposal.
- No comprehensive summary table listing information type, frequency, and number and location of sites.

**Monitoring objectives**
- The draft ties monitoring to validating the preferred alternative. The committee calls this "too narrow" and recommends a purpose of producing reliable basic information for assessing environmental change under any operating regime, actual or hypothetical.
- The committee considers long-term monitoring "critical for adaptive management."
- The NPS objective of simulating pre-dam ecosystems is judged inconsistent with endorsing daily fluctuating flows for hydropower.

**Frequency and sampling concerns**
- Quarterly Lake Powell sampling is insufficient. The committee says samples must be taken at least monthly.
- Seasonal or quarterly water chemistry and temperature measurements are insufficient. The committee recommends at least bimonthly, and probably monthly, sampling.
- Measurements at some sites are 'seasonal' in the draft; the committee recommends increasing frequency where long gaps reduce usefulness.

**Water chemistry and biota recommendations**
- Nitrogen: total N, nitrate N, and dissolved organic N.
- Phosphorus: particulate P, total soluble P, and soluble reactive P.
- Add chlorophyll a and algal counts.
- Zooplankton and phytoplankton taxon-specific enumeration should not be replaced by chlorophyll a surrogates.
- Dissolved organic matter should be monitored at Lake Powell as well as in the river.

**Sediment and contaminants**
- Suspended and bed sediments should be collected annually for chemical analysis at gaging sites, the head of Lake Mead, and Lake Powell.
- A small number of fish tissue samples should be assessed each year for contaminants.
- Modeling "cannot substitute for collection of data." Field sediment and discharge data should continue indefinitely.
- Tributary sediment transport should not be bound to a specific agency (the plan names USGS).

**Gaging and infrastructure**
- No gaging sites should be removed. The committee agrees the National Canyon gage is least important, but says it should remain for the foreseeable future given its record.
- No new gage needs to be added, unless the Bureau justifies one.
- Towers and cables should be removed and replaced with alternative methods such as boats attached to temporary cables, and eye bolts in canyon walls. A specific transition date should be set.

**Fishes**
- Monitoring must address endangered fish welfare more explicitly, given USFWS concerns about daily flow fluctuations.
- Trout monitoring based only on creel census and fishing guide surveys is inadequate. Growth rates, feeding habits, distribution, age structure, and spawning success should be measured directly.
- Pre-adult life stages are to be treated as "less complete," which the committee considers vague.
- Draft sampling schedule: adults of long-lived taxa annually, moving to a four-year cycle as information improves. Short-lived species and young-of-the-year sampled twice a year (spring larval period, and after summer flooding).

**Vegetation and terrestrial organisms**
- The plan appears to confuse quadrants with quadrats.
- "Statistically significant number of sampling sites" is meaningless; the plan must specify numbers.
- Terrestrial vertebrate and invertebrate monitoring is hypothetical ("if determined to be essential"). The committee wants a determination made now.

**Cultural resources and tribal concerns**
- Cultural monitoring must specify which resources, how often, and which techniques.
- Historic sites should receive substantial weight, as the plan implies they are as important as prehistoric ones.
- Tribal values should be monitored through standardized, quantitative methods. The term "visitations" is not scientific.
- The draft adopts the Section 106 Programmatic Agreement design. Sites directly impacted by river discharge would be monitored quarterly; about 20% of other sites visited annually; sites with rapid degradation monitored weekly by time-lapse cameras.

**Recreation and economics**
- Recreation data should be collected directly, not merely transferred from other agencies. The long-term program should "assume direct responsibility in perpetuity" for specific information.
- Recreation values to be surveyed every five years or after unusual events.
- Nonuse values need a specific subheading and more extensive treatment.
- Hydropower generation to be monitored hourly.

**Administration and contracting**
- The draft's proposed committee of federal cooperators would be ineffective. The program should be administered independently and report to a higher authority.
- Restricting operations to issuing contracts, archiving data, and coordinating review is probably most cost-efficient.
- Open, competitive contracting should extend beyond cooperating agencies to any qualified public or private entity.
- Contracts should not be "held captive" for permits, access authorization, or use of public equipment.
- An outside review panel should evaluate proposed program changes.

**Recommendations list (Section 4 of the review)**
1. Add a comprehensive table by category of information, frequency, and number and location of sites.
2. Broaden objectives to cover responses to any operation of the dam and other environmental changes.
3. Increase frequency of seasonal or quarterly measurements.
4. Add an explicit research program complementing monitoring.
5. Assign the long-term program direct, perpetual responsibility for specific data kinds.
6. Emphasize non-invasive methods and remove permanent cableways.
7. Define administration, review, contracting, and cost estimates, favoring administrative independence and open contracting.

**Appendix B draft plan: stated objectives (for reference)**
- Monitor the effectiveness of prescribed dam operations against EIS, 1992 Act, and agency objectives.
- Use monitoring as regular feedback for adaptive management, via an Adaptive Management Work Group.
- Provide a data archive for the program, with GIS-based information management, and GCES Scientific Information Management system (SIM) as an input.
- Monitor 10 critical attribute groups: water quantity and quality; sediment dynamics; fish; vegetation; wildlife; endangered species; cultural resources; recreation; power supply; and nonuse values.
