# Stream Survey of the San Francisco River

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #Ictaluridae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This first report to the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] documents a comprehensive stream survey of the [[San Francisco River]] conducted between May 27 and June 15, 1977. Twelve sampling stations were established between [[Frisco Hot Springs]] and the mouth of the [[Blue River]] in Arizona, covering approximately 45 km of river. The survey assessed fish populations, aquatic invertebrates, water quality, and physical habitat characteristics. Native species including [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiroga cobitis*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) were documented alongside introduced species such as [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), and [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*). The survey revealed significant habitat degradation and displacement of native species by non-natives in the main river, while tributaries like [[Harden Cieniga]] and the [[Blue River]] retained more intact native fish assemblages.

## Background & Description

The [[San Francisco River]] flows through the [[Gila National Forest]] in southwestern New Mexico and eastern Arizona. The survey was conducted by Richard Anderson (graduate student) and Paul Turner (Assistant Professor of Fishery Science) from the Department of Fishery and Wildlife Sciences at [[New Mexico State University]].

**Sampling Methods:**
- Electrofishing with 120-volt, 1500-watt generator over approximately 100-m sections
- Collection using dip nets, 15-foot minnow seine, 50-foot bag seine, and gill nets
- Estimated sampling efficiency: 50-75% of fish within stations
- Physical parameters measured: percent pool, percent shallow riffle (<20 cm depth), percent deep riffle (>20 cm depth), bottom substrate composition
- Pools defined as >0.8 m depth and ≥2 m width

**Water Quality Analysis:**
- Field measurements: dissolved oxygen, pH (using Hach reagents)
- Laboratory analysis: alkalinity, hardness, sulfate, nitrate, turbidity, conductivity (Hach DR Photocolorimeter)
- Total dissolved solids and total soluble salts analyzed by [[New Mexico State University]] Soil and Water Testing Laboratory

**Invertebrate Sampling:**
- Three Surber samples per station
- Identification to order level
- Relative abundance determined by biomass

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Characteristics:**

Station 1B (near [[Frisco Hot Springs]]) exhibited the highest species diversity with ten species, including five native and five introduced species. This station featured excellent riffle habitat with rubble bottoms preferred by [[Loach Minnow]]. Native species were clearly dominant at this upstream location.

**Habitat Preferences:**
- [[Sonora Sucker]] preferred still or slow-moving pools, quiet areas around submerged logs, and undercut banks
- [[Desert Sucker]] dominated pools and riffles with current, especially swift rubble riffles
- [[Loach Minnow]] highly adapted to shallow (<20 cm), rubble riffles
- [[Longfin Dace]] occupied diverse habitats from riffles to low-velocity areas with aquatic macrophytes

**Water Quality:**

Alkalinity ranged from 136-234 mg/L (total), hardness 88-234 mg/L, pH 7.6-9.0, dissolved oxygen 6.0-8.4 ppm, and conductivity 180-500 micromhos. Water temperatures ranged from 57-91°F (air) and 64-86°F (water). Hot springs at Station 1A reached 105°F, 32°F above river temperature.

**Aquatic Invertebrates:**

Ephemeroptera (mayflies) comprised 19.9% of total biomass across stations, with families including Tricorythidae, Heptageniidae, Siphlonuridae, Leptophlebiidae, Ephemerellidae, and Baetidae. Megaloptera (dobsonflies, family Corydalidae) represented 40.4% of biomass. Trichoptera (caddisflies, primarily Hydropsychidae) comprised 12.0%, and Diptera 13.7%. Plecoptera (stoneflies) were rare, found only at Station 1B (10% of biomass).

**Habitat Degradation:**

The survey documented extensive vehicular use of the river bottom, particularly at riffles used as crossing points. On Memorial Day weekend (May 30), 33 vehicles were counted in a 45-minute interval between [[Mule Creek]] and Highway 180. Continued vehicular traffic may disrupt riffle stability. Heavy cattle use also appeared to reduce bank stability and increase erosion, with negative impacts on riparian understory vegetation.

**Wildlife Observations:**

- 22 bighorn sheep (all ewes) with 11 kids observed near Station 1B
- 5 Rocky Mountain bighorn sheep (all rams) sighted near Station 6
- 3 black hawks in [[Mule Creek]] canyon, including a calling pair
- Zone-tailed hawk (*Buteo albonotatus*) in [[Dix Creek]]
- 2 adult and 2 young javelina in [[Harden Cieniga]]
- Spiny softshell turtle (*Trionyx spiniferus*) at Station 9, with 2 additional individuals observed

## Key Findings

**Native Species Distribution:**

1. **Station 1B** (1 km below [[Frisco Hot Springs]]): Highest diversity with 10 species. Native species dominant. Collections included 27 large fish (>120 mm) from first pool: 22 [[Sonora Sucker]], 1 [[Desert Sucker]], 1 [[Common Carp]] (404 mm TL), 1 [[Channel Catfish]] (274 mm TL), 1 [[Smallmouth Bass]] (269 mm TL - only specimen collected during survey). Second pool yielded 63 fish: 75% *C. clarki*, 25% *C. insignis*. Total biomass of 63 suckers: 7.5 kg. Estimated total sucker biomass in 100 m: 14 kg (representing 50-75% of actual population).

2. **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiroga cobitis*): Dominant cyprinid in shallow rubble riffles at Station 1B (65 collected). Scarce downstream from [[Big Dry Creek]] due to lack of suitable riffle habitat. Found at Stations 2B (1 specimen), 3 (2 y-o-y), 5 (7 specimens), 8 (3), and mouth of [[Mule Creek]] (1). In [[Blue River]]: 1 specimen at 150 m upstream, 3 at 500 m, and 8 at 3.5 km upstream.

3. **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta grahami*): Found only in [[Harden Cieniga]], where it was the most abundant species. Over 250 individuals counted in one 300-m stretch. Previously collected from [[San Francisco River]] near [[Frisco Hot Springs]] in the 1950s by Drs. Koster and Miller, but absent from main river during this survey.

4. **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*): Not collected during survey. No documented collections in [[San Francisco River]] since 1950 (review of collection records by Drs. W.J. Koster, R.R. Miller, J. LaBounty, and P.R. Turner).

5. **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*): Not collected. Disappeared from river since 1955.

**Introduced Species Impact:**

1. **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*): Widespread and abundant. Station 4 pool (30 × 2.5 m) had highest density: 12 specimens (392-512 mm, mean 455 mm) plus 1 [[Flathead Catfish]]. Station 11 yielded 22 channel catfish (129-456 mm, mean 341 mm). Total of 14 channel catfish (209-426 mm, mean 370 mm) at Station 8.

2. **[[Flathead Catfish]]** (*Pylodictis olivaris*): Highly piscivorous. Largest specimen: 791 mm (>13 lbs) at Station 6. Station 2A angler reported recently catching 14-lb flathead. Presence correlated with absence of large suckers (>160 mm) at Station 2A.

3. **[[Common Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*): Present at most stations. Station 5 yielded 6 carp (461-554 mm) and 9 carp (382-520 mm) from pool. Young-of-year collected at Stations 3 and mouth of [[Mule Creek]].

4. **[[Red Shiner]]** (*Notropis lutrensis*): Increasingly abundant downstream. Station 11 riffle yielded 51 specimens with many more escaping. Seven collected at Station 1B from sand-gravel riffles.

5. **[[Western Mosquitofish]]** (*Gambusia affinis*): Rare, collected only at Station 1A hot spring (28 specimens) and one at Station 1B (150 m downstream). Absence despite apparently suitable habitat may be due to periodic floods. Rarity enhances possibility of reintroducing [[Gila Topminnow]].

**Tributary Comparisons:**

1. **[[Mule Creek]]**: Surveyed 4.1 km upstream. Clear water, 5°C cooler than river, mostly rubble bottom, high aquatic invertebrate production, dense riparian shade. [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Sonora Sucker]] common in lower stretch. Approximately 1.6 km upstream, bottom changed to bedrock with numerous pools containing only large suckers. Appearance of good trout stream but water temperatures too high.

2. **[[Harden Cieniga]]**: Surveyed 3 km upstream. Isolated from river by 120 m of dry streambed. Only native species present. [[Roundtail Chub]] most abundant (>250 counted in 300 m). [[Speckled Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Sonora Sucker]] also common. Stream averaged 16 cm depth, 1.2 m width, 20% pools/80% riffles, rubble and gravel bottom. Dense willow (*Salix* sp.) along stream with Arizona sycamore and cottonwood overstory.

3. **[[Dix Creek]]**: In 20-m section, collected 37 [[Desert Sucker]], 9 [[Sonora Sucker]], 28 [[Speckled Dace]], and 13 [[Longfin Dace]].

4. **[[Blue River]]**: Similar appearance to [[San Francisco River]] but with more shallow rubble riffles (10-20% of bottom). Minimal non-native impact despite no physical barriers: only 2 [[Channel Catfish]] (320 mm) collected 3.5 km upstream. Native fauna largely intact. At 150 m upstream: 1 [[Loach Minnow]], 20 [[Speckled Dace]], 21 [[Longfin Dace]], 20 [[Desert Sucker]], 5 [[Sonora Sucker]]. At 500 m: 3 [[Loach Minnow]], 5 [[Speckled Dace]], 231 [[Longfin Dace]], 32 [[Sonora Sucker]], 5 [[Desert Sucker]]. At 3.5 km: 8 [[Loach Minnow]], 10 [[Speckled Dace]], 21 [[Longfin Dace]], 5 [[Sonora Sucker]], 1 [[Desert Sucker]].

**Species Interactions:**

Large catfish and large suckers typically occupied different pools, except at Stations 11 and 12. At Station 11, 4 [[Sonora Sucker]] (383-460 mm) were collected with 4 [[Flathead Catfish]] (366-621 mm, mean 473 mm) and 22 [[Channel Catfish]] (129-456 mm, mean 341 mm). At Station 12, channel catfish occupied deep riffle where large [[Sonora Sucker]] were absent; largest sucker in riffle was 196 mm.

**Conservation Implications:**

Three native species ([[Spikedace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Gila Topminnow]]) have either disappeared or become very rare in vicinity of [[Frisco Hot Springs]]. No documented collections of these species in [[San Francisco River]] since 1955. Native fish fauna heavily influenced by invasion of non-native species from [[Blue River]] mouth upstream to [[Frisco Hot Springs]]. In contrast, [[Blue River]] native fauna remains largely intact despite absence of physical barriers to upstream movement by non-natives.

Suitable riffle habitat for [[Loach Minnow]] very rare downstream from [[Big Dry Creek]]. Unknown whether riffle habitat has always been limited or has decreased in recent times. Possible causes of riffle destruction: vehicular use of river bottom (crossings typically selected at riffles) and extensive cattle use reducing bank stability and increasing erosion.
