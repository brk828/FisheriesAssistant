# Water Temperature, Dissolved Oxygen, and Turbidity Control in Reservoir Releases

#ichthyology #marine-biology #reservoir-limnology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by Richard A. Cassidy reviews engineering and management methods for controlling the quality of reservoir water and reservoir releases, focusing on [[Water Temperature]], [[Dissolved Oxygen]], and abiogenic [[Turbidity]]. It argues that the choice of technology is site-specific and that the two most important reservoir water-quality parameters are temperature and dissolved oxygen. The chapter traces the history of [[Selective Withdrawal]] from the 1594 Tibi Dam to modern U.S. projects, describes the numerical models used to predict reservoir thermal regimes, and summarizes aeration and oxygenation techniques, including [[Artificial Circulation]], [[Hypolimnetic Aeration]], and turbine venting. Downstream effects on aquatic biota, such as temperature targets for anadromous fisheries, are a recurring concern.

## Background & Description

**Scope and reservoir types.** Reservoirs are classed as single-purpose (water supply, irrigation, flood control) or multiple-purpose impoundments, which often combine consumptive uses (irrigation, water supply) with nonconsumptive uses (flood control, hydropower, navigation). Conflicts such as hydropower versus fisheries are noted. Secondary purposes such as recreation, fish enhancement and water quality enhancement are increasingly important.

**Size classification.** The transition from pond to reservoir is generally at a mean depth above 3 m or surface area above 10 km². The transition from small to large reservoir occurs at mean depth above 10 m, surface area above 100 km², or volume above 1.0 × 10⁹ m³. Key inventory figures:
- About 2,650 U.S. reservoirs and controlled natural lakes (more than 6.17 × 10⁶ m³ storage), holding 5.92 × 10¹¹ m³, with almost 90% of storage in only 574 of the largest.
- About 50,000 smaller reservoirs and roughly 2 million farm ponds.
- Ownership and management: the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers operates 584 reservoirs, the Bureau of Reclamation 275 projects, and the TVA 53 reservoirs; the Forest Service, Bureau of Indian Affairs, National Park Service, Fish and Wildlife Service and Department of Energy manage many smaller dams.

**Thermal stratification and retention time.** Theoretical retention time is described as the key to reservoir water quality. Reservoirs with retention times above 1 year (about 20% of U.S. reservoirs) are comparable to natural lakes; about 35% have 115 to 365 days; about 45% have less than 115 days. Almost all lakes and reservoirs deeper than 5 m stratify horizontally during parts of summer. The chapter notes that limnological terms (epilimnion, hypolimnion, thermocline) are applied to reservoirs even though their basic characteristics differ from natural lakes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Biological effects:** Water temperature governs metabolic rates and physiological responses of aquatic biota and the rates of chemical and biogeochemical reactions. Dissolved oxygen affects respiration, distribution, abundance and behavior, and controls the release of nutrients from sediments.
- **Turbidity and productivity:** Inorganic (abiogenic) particulates from the watershed influence density currents and productivity; biogenic particulates (algae, plankton) influence temperature and oxygen regimes. The chapter limits itself to abiogenic turbidity.
- **Gas supersaturation:** Spillway and dam releases can become supersaturated with dissolved gases, causing gas bubble disease in fish.
- **Anadromous fisheries:** The Rogue River Basin supports the second largest anadromous fishery in Oregon, and selective withdrawal is credited with reducing dam-related environmental effects there.
- **Density and turbidity zones:** Thornton et al. describe riverine, transition and lacustrine zones within reservoirs, each with distinct light, sediment and productivity regimes.

## Key Findings

**1. Selective withdrawal history and status**
- Early example: Tibi Dam (Alicante, Spain, 1594) had a 143 m vertical outlet shaft with multiple outlets.
- The U.S. modern concept began with Arrowrock Dam (Boise River, completed 1915), with 25 outlets in 3 tiers.
- Selective withdrawal is credited to Harleman et al. at MIT, who introduced it for cool-water withdrawal from deep reservoir levels.
- More than 70 U.S. Corps reservoirs, 15 Bureau of Reclamation and 4 TVA reservoirs had selective withdrawal capability at the time of writing.

**2. Institutional drivers**
- 1958 amendment to the Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act required consultation with fisheries agencies, leading to selective withdrawal outlets at new Corps dams.
- Public Law 87-88 (1961), the National Environmental Policy Act (1969), and President Carter's 1978 water resources policy each increased federal attention to downstream biota and water-quality standards.

**3. Modeling progress**
- Models evolved from Edinger and Geyer's one-dimensional work and Orlob's digital computation to the WRE, WESTEX, EIKER and HEC-5Q models, and later CE-QUAL-R1, CE-QUAL-W2 and two- and three-dimensional models.
- Marcinski (1975) found the WRE model generally best among four one-dimensional models used by the Corps.

**4. Design and performance**
- Early 1960s designs often used rule-of-thumb, equally sized high-middle-low outlets.
- Corps Ohio River Division led with 33 structures; five of six Fort Worth District dams built in the 1980s had selective withdrawal.
- Selective withdrawal cannot consistently meet state temperature standards: spring releases often lack warm water, fall releases cool too slowly, and warm monomictic Rogue River reservoirs rarely meet Oregon winter standards.

**5. Target temperatures**
- Lost Creek and Applegate Lakes (Rogue River, Oregon) had target temperatures revised after postimpoundment fisheries studies (Figures 6 and 7 give monthly targets for 1987).
- Wynoochee Dam (Washington) target range was 7.3 to 14.8°C, with a fishery optimum of 7 to 12.8°C (November to May) and 10 to 14°C (June to October).
- Libby Dam (Kootenai River) advisory group set a maximum of 14.4°C from mid-July to mid-September and a band of 10 to 12.8°C from mid-September to November; the band was revised in 1983 because April and May releases could not meet the 1978 guidelines.

**6. Dissolved oxygen techniques**
- Artificial circulation: Pastorok et al. reviewed 47 sites; 62 mechanical and diffused-air cases produced a temperature differential of 3°C or less, and 79% of 57 mixing experiments (45 cases) raised bottom-water dissolved oxygen.
- Hypolimnetic aeration: of 15 case studies, dissolved oxygen in the hypolimnion increased in 11 (73%); thermal changes were under 4°C.
- Gated outlet works: 12 Corps dams raised dissolved oxygen from 80–100% of saturation regardless of inflow conditions.
- Turbine venting: Wisconsin dams raised dissolved oxygen by about 1 mg/L; Catawba River tests reached nearly 3 mg/L; TVA hub baffles at Norris Dam raised it by 2–3 mg/L, cutting aeration power-loss costs from almost $500,000 to $70,000 annually.
- Russell Dam oxygenation was estimated to need about 5,500 t of oxygen annually to meet a 6 mg/L downstream objective.

**7. TVA dissolved oxygen problems**
- By 1978, low dissolved oxygen in TVA releases adversely affected 547 km of streams.
- Releases failed state and federal criteria for 3 days per year (Upper Bear Creek) up to 183 days per year (Tims Ford).

**8. Turbidity**
- Water density is maximal at 4°C; about 300 mg/L dissolved solids or 450 mg/L suspended solids equal a 1°C temperature change at 25°C.
- Density currents (overflow, interflow, underflow) determine where inflows travel. Turbidity is treated as a conservative parameter, not subject to decay.
- Lost Creek Lake (Oregon): storm turbidity of 5.7 × 10⁴ m³ with peaks near 70 JTU was reduced to under 5 JTU within 6 weeks using the turbidity conduit.
- Spada Lake (Washington): a December 1979 storm raised turbidity to nearly 50 NTU; limited selective withdrawal reduced it to under 5 NTU in 4.5 months.
- Lake Red Rock (Iowa): turbidity currents shortened the average residence time of storm water from 7 days to 2–3 days.

**9. Conclusions**
- Reservoir management must be site-specific, combining selective withdrawal, aeration and operational scheduling.
- Turbidity modeling and controlling biogenic turbidity remain research needs; Harleman (1982) called for further study of turbidity and suspended-sediment modeling.
