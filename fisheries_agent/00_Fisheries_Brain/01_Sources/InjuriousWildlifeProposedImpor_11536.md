# Proposed Rule: Injurious Wildlife Importation and Shipment Requirements (50 CFR Part 16, 1977)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #injurious-wildlife

## Executive Summary

This document is a Federal Register notice (Vol. 42, No. 44, Monday, March 7, 1977, pp. 12972–12978) from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] proposing a third revision of 50 CFR Part 16, the regulations governing the importation and interstate shipment of [[Injurious Wildlife]]. The rule is issued under the [[Lacey Act]] (18 U.S.C. § 42). The Service proposes to keep the existing "dirty list" structure, which bans listed species, and to add new species to that list. It rejects the "clean list" approach of earlier drafts. Among the additions are several fish groups, including venomous, electric, parasitic, predatory, and competitively dominant species. The notice also sets out permit rules, exemptions, and special conditions. Comments were due May 6, 1977.

## Background & Description

Under 18 U.S.C. § 42, the Secretary may designate wild mammals, birds, fish (including mollusks and crustaceans), amphibians, reptiles, and their eggs or offspring as "prescribed wildlife" if they are injurious to human beings, agriculture, horticulture, forestry, wildlife, or wildlife resources of the United States. Apart from limited exceptions, injurious wildlife may not be imported into the United States or shipped between the continental United States, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and other possessions.

The notice is the third proposal in a rulemaking history:

- **First proposal:** published December 20, 1973 (38 FR 34970). It treated all wildlife as potentially injurious and proposed a "low risk" list of species exempt from the ban. A draft environmental impact statement followed on June 5, 1974 (39 FR 19969). Public hearings were held in August 1974 in Washington, D.C., Kansas City, Miami, and San Francisco. The Service received 4,315 public comments.
- **Second proposal:** published February 24, 1976 (40 FR 7935), with a draft environmental impact statement. The comment period closed April 10, 1975 (as printed). It drew 1,162 responses.
- **Third proposal (this notice):** adds species to the injurious list and revises the structure of Part 16 for clarity.

The Secretary states that all wildlife outside its native habitat is potentially injurious, but that the degree of risk varies by species. Nine criteria are used to justify listing a species:

1. It occupies an ecological niche that substantially overlaps that of a native species.
2. It is a close relative of a native species it might compete with or interbreed with.
3. Its behavior, feeding, or ecological requirements could be disruptive or destructive to natural communities.
4. It feeds on crops, agricultural products, or harvested natural resources.
5. It hosts a parasite or disease vector harmful to humans, domestic animals, or native wildlife.
6. It is dangerously venomous, toxic, or otherwise noxious.
7. It occupies disturbed or urbanized habitats as a major part of its range.
8. It has shown an ability to establish, colonize, or disperse, or has reproductive traits suggesting it could establish without normal population controls.
9. It is a close relative of a species in any of the above categories.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Public comment themes (second proposal):** Comments came from government agencies, research and medical groups, zoos and aquaria, the pet industry, conservation organizations, the general public, aviculturists, game bird breeders, falconers, aquarium hobbyists, aquaculture interests, herpetology hobbyists, and big game breeders. Key concerns included:

- Coordination with state and federal agencies.
- Administrative burden of permits. Biological supply houses noted that because [[Rana Pipiens]] and [[Rana Catesbeiana]] were not on the [[Low Risk List]], routine frog use in laboratories would require permits.
- Discrepancies in the low-risk list, where closely related species were treated differently.
- Objections that the Secretary lacked sufficient proof that all wildlife is injurious.
- Requests for an economic impact statement.
- Aquaculture objection that the giant prawn, [[Giant Prawn]] (*Macrobrachium rosenbergii*), was not on the low-risk list.
- Requests to reconsider restrictions on importing [[Brine Shrimp]] cysts.
- Conservation groups considered the low-risk list too permissive.

**Species-specific ecology cited in the rule:**

- **[[Pike Killifish]] (*Belonesox*):** an aggressive predator up to eight inches long. In Florida it reportedly impairs natural control of mosquito larvae by the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).
- **[[Climbing Perch]] (*Anabas*):** aggressive, prolific, and air-breathing, able to move over land.
- **Cyprinid genera** (*Aristichthys*, *Ctenopharyngodon*, *Hypophthalmichthys*, *Leuciscus*, *Mylopharyngodon*, *Opsariichthys*): prolific and aggressive. Through competition for food and space, they could eliminate native fishes with similar niches.
- **[[Electric Fishes]]:** the electric eel (*Electrophorus*, Gymnotidae), electric catfishes (*Malapterurus*, Malapteruridae), and electric rays (Torpedinidae). Electric eel discharges reach up to 600 volts, with an average output of 350 volts. Other electric fishes usually produce less than 200 volts. Electric eels are largely air-breathing, which complicates control.
- **[[Candiru]]:** small South American catfishes that are often parasitic on other fishes. Their opercular and preopercular spines can lodge in objects, and they can penetrate the urogenital openings of swimmers, causing severe pain that sometimes requires surgery.
- **[[Venomous Fishes]]:** toadfishes (*Opsanus*, *Thalassophryne*), stingrays (Dasyatidae and Potamotrygonidae), catfishes of the genus *Plotosus*, scorpionfishes (*Brachirus*, *Dendrochirus*, *Pterois*, *Synanceia*, *Scorpaena*), and weeverfishes (*Trachinus*). These are dangerous to fishermen and swimmers.
- **[[Piranhas]]:** most piranhas rarely exceed 18 inches but are among the most dangerous. Some large predatory listed species reach three to four feet and have powerful, well-toothed jaws.
- **[[African Clawed Frog]]** and **[[Cane Toad]]:** both established in the United States, both prey on native wildlife. The cane toad's parotid gland poison is harmful to pets and potential predators.
- **[[Salmonidae]]:** not listed as injurious. Imports are exempt if certified free of *Myxosoma cerebralis* (the causative agent of [[Whirling Disease]]) and of the virus causing [[Viral Hemorrhagic Septicemia]] ("Egtved disease").

## Key Findings

**Scope of the fish listings:**

- The proposal lists 50 genera of fishes from 22 families. The rule states these fishes are parasitic, venomous, electric, large aggressive predators, or superior competitors, and that there are currently no known safe and efficient control methods if they become established.
- A subset of 28 genera from nine families is listed for aggressive predation, competition, and habitat disruption. Families include Centropomidae, Characidae, Cichlidae, Citharinidae, Ctenoluciidae, Erythrinidae, Hepsetidae, Hemiodontidae (Leblasinidae), and Ophiocephalidae.
- The full list also includes the Anabantidae, Cyprinidae, Poeciliidae, Trachinidae, Plotosidae, Scorpaenidae, Batrachoididae, Dasyatidae, Potamotrygonidae, and Torpedinidae families, among others.

**Other proposed additions (summarized):**

- Vampire bats (feed on blood; rabies carriers).
- Ferrets, stoats, weasels, and mink (predation and competition).
- Bulbuls, starlings, mynas, and white-eyes (established or spreading in the United States, Canada, or Hawaii).
- Newts and salamanders with toxic skin secretions.
- Venomous snakes, including the Viperidae, Elapidae, and Colubridae families.
- Frogs and toads (African clawed frog, cane toad).

**Regulatory provisions:**

- **Prohibitions (§§ 16.21, 16.22):** importation into the United States, Puerto Rico, or any possession, and interstate shipment of listed wildlife.
- **Permits (§ 16.31):** issued for zoological, educational, medical, or scientific purposes. Applicants must provide species lists, justification, facility descriptions, precautions, disposal methods, and technical expertise. Permits may not exceed two years.
- **Permit conditions:** complete records and annual summaries due within 30 days of each one-year period. Escapes must be reported by telephone, letter, or telegraph within 24 hours, with a full written report within 40 days.
- **Other exceptions:** Federal agencies for their own use (§ 16.32); dead natural history specimens (§ 16.33); domesticated canaries, parrots, and psittacine birds (§ 16.34); and certified salmonids (§ 16.36).
- **Definitions (§ 16.30):** "Zoological purpose" requires public display or propagation/husbandry technology, and the comments note that applicants may need to meet both criteria, which few hobbyists could do.
- **Amendment process (§ 16.12):** the list may be revised as new data arise. Any person may petition the Director to review a species' status.

**Status and next steps:**

- An environmental assessment was in preparation at the time of publication.
- Written comments were due by May 6, 1977, to the Director, Fish and Wildlife Service. Comments were available for inspection in Suite 600 at 1612 K Street, N.W., Washington, D.C.
- The principal author is David R. Endres, legal specialist. The notice is dated February 25, 1977, and signed by Acting Director Harvey K. Nelson.
