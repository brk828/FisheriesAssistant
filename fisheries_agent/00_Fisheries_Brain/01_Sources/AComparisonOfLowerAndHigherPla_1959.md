## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by Eville Gorham compares the radioactivity of fallout-derived isotopes accumulated in the ash of three plant groups — lichens, mosses (bryophytes), and angiosperms — collected from the English Lake District in early 1958. Using standardized ashing and radioactivity counting protocols, the study found that lower plants (mosses and lichens) accumulated significantly more radioactivity per unit ash than higher plants (angiosperms), despite comparable ash (mineral) content across groups. The work extends an earlier paper (Gorham, 1958) linking fallout accumulation to plant mineral content, and has downstream implications for radioisotope transfer into herbivores such as reindeer.

## Background & Description

Not applicable to fish. Relevant morphological/structural comparisons in the source concern plant growth form and surface area:

- **Lichens and mosses**: Presumed to have greater **surface area per unit dry weight** compared to angiosperms, which likely enhances trapping of particulate fallout and absorption of soluble fallout components from surface runoff.
- **Growth habit**: Mosses and lichens often grow as **mats or cushions** directly on the soil surface, a structural trait proposed to enhance fallout accumulation from runoff, in contrast to the more elevated, less surface-contacting growth form of angiosperms.
- **Ash content**: All three groups were deliberately selected to have closely similar ash (mineral) content (~4.5–4.6% antilog mean), isolating plant-type effects from mineral-content effects on radioactivity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat sampled**: English Lake District, including terrestrial woodland and semi-aquatic fen/peatland environments; comparison samples of *Sphagnum papillosum* were also collected from North Wales and northwest Scotland.
- **Substrate/microhabitat**: Mosses were subdivided into woodland species, semi-aquatic fen species, and *Sphagnum* vs. non-*Sphagnum* taxa — no significant radioactivity differences were found among these subcategories.
- **Hydrological context**: The similarity in radioactivity between terrestrial (wooded slope) mosses and semi-aquatic (wet valley-bottom fen) mosses suggests fallout is **strongly retained/held by vegetation** rather than being extensively leached from slopes into peaty, water-saturated valley bottoms via runoff.
- **Comparative water chemistry**: Plant ash radioactivity was **23 times greater** than that of ash from evaporated stream waters in the same region (per Gorham 1958), indicating vegetation acts as a major fallout reservoir relative to the aquatic (stream) compartment of this freshwater-influenced landscape.
- **Trophic linkage**: The elevated radioactivity of lichens and mosses has implications for herbivores that graze on them (see [[Radioactive Fallout Bioaccumulation]] and [[Strontium-90 In Food Chains]]), representing a terrestrial-to-animal trophic transfer pathway analogous to bioaccumulation processes studied in aquatic food webs.

## Key Findings

- **Radioactivity by plant group** (mean antilog counts per minute per 50 mg ash):
  - Angiosperms (n=10): **63 c.p.m.**
  - Mosses (n=20): **152 c.p.m.**
  - Lichens (n=3): **183 c.p.m.**
- The difference between angiosperms and mosses was **statistically significant (p < 0.05)**; the difference between mosses and lichens was **not significant**.
- Lower plants (lichens, mosses) showed **substantially higher fallout radioactivity** than higher plants (angiosperms) despite matched ash content, implicating plant-type-specific traits (surface area, growth habit) rather than mineral content as the driver.
- **Decay curve analysis**: Radioactivity decay in *Sphagnum papillosum* ash followed a **curvilinear pattern** on a log scale over time (Feb–July), indicating a **mixture of radioisotopes** rather than a single isotope; the relatively rapid decay rate is consistent with fallout from **thermonuclear weapons testing** rather than a slower-decaying source.
- The October 1957 **Windscale plutonium plant accident**, though geographically close to the study area, was **not implicated** as the source of elevated radioactivity — *Sphagnum papillosum* samples from geographically distant sites (North Wales, northwest Scotland) showed comparably high count rates to those near Windscale.
- **Practical/ecological implication**: Animals grazing on mosses and lichens (e.g., reindeer) may accumulate higher levels of radioactive fallout. This is supported by cited external data showing **higher strontium-90 concentrations in Norwegian reindeer bones** compared to sheep bones from the same region (Hvinden, unpublished report).
- Methodological note: no correction for self-absorption was applied and specific isotopes were not identified, so results represent **relative, arbitrary comparisons** of total radioactivity under standardized counting conditions rather than absolute isotope quantification.
