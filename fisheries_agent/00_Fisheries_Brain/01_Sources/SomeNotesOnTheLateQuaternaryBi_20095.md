# Some Notes on the Late Quaternary Biogeography of the Mohave Desert

#paleobiogeography #quaternary-ecology #pinaceae #cupressaceae

## Executive Summary

This document is an interim research report (Report No. 11) by Peter J. Mehringer, Jr., of the [[University of Arizona]] Geochronology Laboratories. It is explicitly tentative and not a publication. It reconstructs probable vegetation changes in the [[Mohave Desert]] during the [[Late Wisconsin]] and proposes a Pleistocene [[Pleistocene Woodland Corridor]] linking mountain ranges from southern Nevada to the San Bernardino Mountains of southern California. The document contains no fish data. Its biological content concerns plants, woodland-dependent small mammals, and the possible routes of early human migration.

## Background & Description

The report draws on fossil pollen and plant remains from [[Tule Springs]] (Las Vegas Valley), [[Gypsum Cave]], and [[Rampart Cave]]. It discusses how vegetation zones shifted vertically and southward during cooler, wetter Pleistocene conditions. Funding is credited to National Science Foundation Grants G-21944, GB-1959 (to P. S. Martin) and GS-23 (to R. Shutler, Jr.).

The author cautions that vertical displacement of vegetation zones is only a descriptive tool. Present-day plant associations may not have held in the past, and "mixtures" of species from currently separate communities may have existed. The author states that more information on fossil species associations is needed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation depression.** Fossil evidence from Tule Springs, Rampart Cave and Gypsum Cave indicates a depression of vegetation zones of about 1000 m during the Late Wisconsin. [[Wells and Jorgensen]] (1964) suggested about 600 m for the juniper woodland. The author stresses that the magnitude of change across wide areas remains uncertain.

**Mapped displacement.** For the [[Spring Range]] of southern Nevada, the author allowed:
- a 1000 m lowering of the juniper-pinyon lower limit (present 1800 m), and
- a 900 m lowering of the ponderosa pine-white fir lower limit (present 2200 m).

The 1000 m figure is described as a minimum for sites where soil, slope and exposure permit woodland today. Johnson et al. (1948) place the New York-Providence juniper-pinyon lower limit at about 1550 m, which is 250 m lower than the Spring Range value. The map shows only one gap of less than 15 km, between the Bristol and Bullion mountains, in an otherwise continuous woodland chain.

**Dated evidence.**
- [[Packrat Middens]] from the Turtle Mountains (UCLA-756, 13,900 ± 200) contain [[Utah Juniper]] and [[Pinyon Pine]]. The highest point of the range is about 1315 m, and the midden lies at about 730 m, which the author treats as an equivalent of the 1000 m displacement.
- A midden from Negro Butte in Lucerne Valley (UCLA-757, 9140 ± 140) contains Utah juniper.
- Juniper remains from Frenchman Flat were radiocarbon dated by Wells and Jorgensen (1964).

**Suitable habitat for ponderosa pine and white fir.** No fossil evidence for either species is known from the New York, McCullough or Providence mountains. The author argues that modern habitats in nearby ranges would suit them if conditions were slightly cooler and moister. Both species occur today on Potosi Mountain, [[Clark Mountain]] and Kingston Peak. Clark Mountain is the southernmost present locality for ponderosa pine and white fir on the Spring-Clark-Providence axis.

**Area-elevation analysis.** Using the [[Spring Range]] area-elevation curve, the author estimates that if the lower limit for boreal species fell by 610 m (to 2440 m), the target area above 3050 m would be about nine times larger. This would increase the chance of colonization by long-range dispersal and could support greater species diversity.

**Pleistocene lakes.** Four high-stand lakes are mapped (from Snyder et al., 1964). Dates include:
- [[Lake Manix]]: 19,300 and 19,500 B.P. (tufa) and 13,800 B.P. (Anodonta shells).
- [[Lake Mohave]]: ten dates from 8350 to 13,670 B.P.
- Lakes Mesquite and Ivanpah are undated, but the author infers similar high-stand timing from regional chronologies.

The author concludes that these dates place the mapped lake levels near the Late Wisconsin pluvial maximum.

**Gypsum Cave.** Joshua trees (Yucca brevifolia) and Utah juniper grew near the cave during the Wisconsin, according to Laudermilk and Munz (1934). The cave lies at 610 m, and the two species now grow together about 1000 m higher.

**Fauna.** A pocket of ponderosa pine at 1220 m in Pine Creek Canyon grows 800 to 1000 m below its average lower limit in the Spring Range. Fossil [[Pika]], [[Golden-Mantled Ground Squirrel]] and marmot are known from the [[Mescal Range]]. The author suggests the pika and ground squirrel could have moved through north-south ranges at least to the Mescal Range, and possibly to the Providence Mountains. The other three species probably occupied lower-elevation habitats.

## Key Findings

1. **Vegetation displacement.** The Late Wisconsin lowering of vegetation zones was at least about 1000 m in the Mohave Desert (Tule Springs, Rampart and Gypsum caves). Estimates vary, and the magnitude is considered open to question.
2. **Woodland corridor.** Mapped lowering of 1000 m for juniper-pinyon and 900 m for ponderosa pine-white fir would have produced a nearly continuous woodland corridor from the Spring Range to the San Bernardino Mountains. The map covers roughly 22,000 to 12,500 B.P.
3. **Limited dating.** Radiocarbon dates from packrat middens and lake deposits support the timing of expansion and the Late Wisconsin pluvial maximum, but the sample is small and the author notes the dates are preliminary.
4. **Species associations.** Past plant communities may not have matched present ones. More fossil association data are needed.
5. **Colonization potential.** Ponderosa pine and white fir may have expanded southward, but this is only a probability until fossil or biogeographic evidence confirms it.
6. **Human migration.** The same corridor could have served as a migration route for big-game hunters who entered the New World about 12,000 radiocarbon years ago. Warming and drying from about 12,000 to 7000 B.P. would have pushed travel routes toward the mountains. Mountain cores, with pinyon seed, game and water, would have been important refuges.
7. **Status.** All conclusions are provisional. The report is an interim document and may be revised by future work.
