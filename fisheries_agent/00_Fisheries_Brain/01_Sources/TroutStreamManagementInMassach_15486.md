# Trout Stream Management in Massachusetts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 bulletin by aquatic biologist James W. Mullan, issued by the [[Massachusetts Division of Fisheries and Game]], assesses the state's trout stream management. The Division stocks slightly over one million catchable trout a year, mostly into streams, under a "put-and-take" program. The author argues that most Massachusetts streams offer only borderline trout habitat because of temperature, oxygen, siltation, flooding and land-use history, and that hatchery trout are not a substitute for wild trout. The document recommends a biologically and economically grounded stocking policy, habitat restoration, watershed protection, pollution abatement, selective [[Reclamation]] of streams, and revised regulations and financing.

## Background & Description

The bulletin's preface traces the first trout stream law to 1849 and the first hatchery plantings a few years later. The three species considered are [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], which are the only ones commonly raised for put-and-take fishing. The author notes that the work draws on research by several scientists, including Dr. Edwin L. Cooper, Dr. Paul R. Needham and Dr. Karl F. Lagler, and on Dingell-Johnson projects F-1-R and F-14-R.

The document covers trout requirements (temperature, oxygen, food, spawning gravel, pH), competition with warmwater fishes, pollution, flooding, predators, dams, stream and watershed formation, historical fish and fishing, hatchery trout performance, stocking policy, access, regulations, habitat improvement, pollution abatement and reclamation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and oxygen.** Flourishing trout populations are not found where temperatures exceed 70 °F for extended periods. Trout may survive short exposures to 87 °F. Optimum growth and survival occur at about 55 to 65 °F. Trout require at least 5 ppm dissolved oxygen.

**Competition.** Competition for food and space is critical. Competition between trout and warmwater fishes rises with the proportion of warmwater habitat. Bottom organisms require several pounds of plankton per pound produced, and 5 to 10 pounds of bottom food are needed per pound of fish.

**Pollution.** Pollutants include industrial-chemical wastes, organic sewage and eroded silt. Silt smothers spawning gravel, cover and bottom fauna, and it can widen channels and remove shade, which raises temperatures.

**Flooding and land use.** Glaciation left Massachusetts with thin, poorly permeable soils, so most streams depend on surface runoff and "freeze and boil, flood and dry up." Clearing, burning, grazing and tillage reduced infiltration and increased flashy floods. Forest cover is now about 80 to 85 percent of the state, but the forest differs from the original.

**Shade.** Streamside shade is a major control on summer temperature. A North Carolina study found unshaded streams averaged 9 to 20 °F warmer than comparable shaded streams. Shaded streams in the region rarely exceed 68 to 70 °F.

**Dams.** Dams can warm water and introduce trash fish, but they can also provide winter refuge and stabilize flows. Trout can complete their life cycle within roughly 200 feet of stream.

**Predators.** The bulletin judges predation a small loss, balanced by pollution control. Snapping turtles, black watersnakes, herons, mergansers, kingfishers and otters are discussed; for example, only seven of 106 black watersnakes from trout streams had eaten trout.

**Fish communities.** Headwater brooks support 25 to 65 pounds of fish per surface acre, of which trout make up 7 to 51 percent (3 to 15 pounds). Larger rivers are dominated by trash fish such as suckers, carp and fallfish.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking scale:** Slightly over one million catchable trout are stocked annually. Hatchery trout make up 90 to 95 percent or more of the trout harvested on main-stream areas, and 75 percent or less on headwater brooks.
- **Survival:** Anglers catch only about half the trout stocked. Field experiments in Canada found that 65 percent of larger hatchery trout survived the first summer, compared with 15 to 34 percent of same-size or smaller hatchery trout. Survival over winter was negligible.
- **Competition effects:** In a Canadian test, 13 to 16 percent of stocked trout died without competition. Competition was linked to additional mortality and weight loss.
- **Angling quality:** Massachusetts stream catch rates were far below the national average of one fish per hour. Trout took 1¼ to 1¾ hours on sea-run streams of outer Cape Cod, and three to five hours on main streams. About 70 percent of license buyers fish for trout, fewer than 45 percent succeed, and 10 percent of anglers take about half the trout stocked.
- **Cost:** Raising trout costs about $1 per pound (private hatcheries $1.95). About 50 cents of each license dollar directly funds trout raising, and total trout program costs approach 75 cents.
- **Stocking size and timing:** Stocking 8 to 12 inch trout was the policy, since 8 to 12 inch fish returned 50 percent more than 6 to 8 inch fish. Spring or in-season stocking is recommended over fall stocking, because in-season recovery averaged 41.3 percent while fall plants averaged 16.8 percent.
- **Species mix:** The author suggests an annual catchable production of about 45 percent brook, 45 percent brown and 10 percent rainbow trout.
- **Regulations:** The bulletin recommends moving the opening day from the third Saturday to the last Saturday in April, reducing the daily bag limit from 12 to 6 fish during early season, and introducing a trout stamp of at least $2. It opposes closed breeder streams, post-stocking closures and flyfishing-only areas.
- **Access:** About 2,500 miles of stocked streams are accessible to the public, and 90 percent or more is private property. The author suggests dedicating 10 percent of the propagation budget (about $30,000 a year) to leasing and purchase of fishing areas.
- **Habitat:** The document recommends a 50-foot uncut strip on each side of streams, fencing against cattle, streambank planting of tall trees, and cool-water "oases" at spring and tributary inputs. Stream improvement structures are useful only where the watershed problem is corrected.
- **Reclamation pilot (Deerfield River, 1959):** 39 miles of main stream and 176 surface acres of impoundments were treated with [[Rotenone]], killing about 75,000 pounds of fish, of which trout were 1.4 percent (1,014 pounds). The upper 20-mile test section had 1946-47 and 1959 harvest comparisons showing about 17,548 wild trout harvested in 1960, versus about 1,000 annually before. Restocking with fingerlings produced an estimated 16 percent return.
- **Conclusion:** Trout fishing in Massachusetts streams can be improved, but only at a cost. The author states that the balance between water, soil, forest and trout "will never again be what it once was."
