# Recent Vegetation Changes Along the Colorado River Between Glen Canyon Dam and Lake Mead, Arizona

#botany #riparian-ecology #colorado-river #plant-distribution #photo-documentation

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey professional paper (Turner and Karpiscak, 1980) compares historical photographs taken between 1872 and 1963 with matched photographs taken in 1972–1976 along the [[Colorado River]] from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to [[Lake Mead]]. The authors document a marked increase in riparian plant density after the dam's completion in 1963, which altered the river's flow, sediment load and temperature. Exotic species such as [[Saltcedar]] and [[Camelthorn]], and native riparian shrubs such as [[Sandbar Willow]] and [[Arrowweed]], now form a new riparian community along much of the channel. The paper also maps the distribution of 25 plant species and summarises the hydrologic and geomorphic changes that drive these shifts.

## Background & Description

The study addresses the [[Grand Canyon]] reach, measured in kilometres from [[Lees Ferry]] (km 0) to Pearce Ferry (about km 450). The authors note that the river's long-term average annual discharge is 16,600 hm³, and that the river's ecological importance far exceeds its size for recreation, power and water supply. Before dam regulation, flooding was frequent and scoured the banks, so the predam valley lacked the dense riparian community typical of other regional streams.

Methods combined:
- Matching of 48 sets of repeat photographs (predam photographs from the 1870s–1950s, repeats from 1972–1976), with camera stations listed in Table 3.
- Hydrologic records from Lees Ferry and near Grand Canyon.
- Plant distribution observations from boat-based surveys in September 1976, supplemented by earlier collections (e.g., [[Clover and Jotter]], 1938–1939).

Photographic pioneers include J. K. Hillers, T. H. O'Sullivan, William Bell, F. A. Nims, R. B. Stanton and E. C. LaRue. The authors describe the first Powell expedition (1869) and the second (1871–1872) as the origin of much of the early visual record.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrologic regime changes.** Dam construction smoothed the flow regime:
- The annual maximum stage at Lees Ferry averaged 5.04 m before the dam and 3.48 m afterward (1963–1976, excluding 1965). The coefficient of variation fell from 0.19 to 0.06.
- Average annual maximum flows fell from about 2,486 m³/s to 803 m³/s, and median discharge rose from about 210 m³/s to 346 m³/s.
- Average diurnal stage fluctuation increased from a few centimetres to several metres.
- Water temperature ranged from 0.2–28 °C (1949–1962) before the dam and 5.5–18 °C afterward.
- Median sediment concentration dropped from 1,500 ppm to 7 ppm.
- The maximum monthly to minimum ratio fell from more than 10:1 to 1.8:1.

**Sediment and channel changes.** Channel degradation below the dam removed about 9.87 × 10⁶ m³ of bottom sediment through 1975 within the 26-km reach to the [[Paria River]], leading to gravel armouring and stabilisation. [[Laursen and Others]] (1976) predicted that more than 200 years would pass before most beaches below Lees Ferry disappear.

**Riparian zonation.** [[Dolan and Others]] (1974) recognised three zones:
1. Postdam fluvial sediments at the river's edge, dominated by arrowweed, saltcedar, coyote willow and Bermuda grass.
2. Predam fluvial sediments reworked by wind, up to 5.5 m above high water, with arrowweed, red brome, camelthorn and Russian thistle.
3. Predam flood terraces, eolian deposits and talus slopes up to 9 m above high water, with Apache plume, catclaw, western honey mesquite and desert broom.

**Human influence.** The number of people travelling the canyon by boat rose from 185 (1869–1955) to about 15,000 per year in the 1970s. The authors note that trampling at campsites and trail cutting disturb the upper zone, and that boat passengers may move plant propagules between beaches.

## Key Findings

- **Species distribution:** The study maps 25 plant species (the text also refers to 24 in one passage). Five are introduced: Bermuda grass, Russian olive, saltcedar, elm and camelthorn.
- **Exotic species:** [[Russian Olive]] was first recorded in the Grand Canyon at Kanab Creek in 1973. Elm, first recorded near Glen Canyon Dam in 1976, was previously unknown along the Grand Canyon reach.
- **Saltcedar:** Now occurs nearly continuously along the postdam river bars. Plants may produce as many as 17 viable seeds per square centimetre per season, and the authors attribute its density to moisture on river bars and a stable habitat.
- **Camelthorn:** First reported in 1970 at km 269.5, it spreads by underground rootstocks and often occurs above the zone of daily inundation.
- **Catclaw and mesquite:** Catclaw is a near-constant component of the predam flood-line community. Seedlings below the old boundary (22 seedlings counted on 100 m²) suggest a future dense thicket. Western honey mesquite first appears at km 63.4 and is infected by [[Mistletoe]] from km 282.9.
- **Fremont cottonwood:** Still seen only infrequently along the river and, in the authors' view, heavily browsed by [[Beaver]]. Beaver cutting is a potential factor limiting its occurrence.
- **Stability of talus vegetation:** Photographs do not support earlier claims that landslides prevented climax vegetation on talus slopes. Most talus is judged stable.
- **Species turnover:** Rabbitbrush and desert broom show decline in older predam flood-line stands, while saltcedar, sandbar willow and arrowweed expand.
- **Future outlook:** The authors conclude that the riparian community has not reached equilibrium. Future flows depend on power demand, water supply and weather, and that uncertainty makes long-term prediction difficult. Their 1977 example shows that drought can reduce flows below predictions, stranding river parties.
