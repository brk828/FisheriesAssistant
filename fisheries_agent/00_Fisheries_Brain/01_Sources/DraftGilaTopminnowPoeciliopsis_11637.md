# Gila Topminnow Recovery Plan (Revised Draft 1993)

#ichthyology #poeciliidae #endangered-species #conservation #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This revised recovery plan addresses the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), one of two subspecies of [[Sonoran Topminnow]] found in Arizona and northern Mexico. Listed as endangered in 1967, the species was historically among the most abundant fishes in the lower [[Colorado River]] watershed but has declined dramatically due to habitat loss and predation/competition from exotic [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). As of 1992, only 9 natural populations persist in Arizona, primarily in the [[Santa Cruz River]] system, with additional populations in the [[Rio Sonora]] and [[Rio de la Concepción]] in Mexico. The plan establishes criteria for downlisting (not delisting) based on securing natural populations, establishing replicate populations within historic range, implementing genetic protocols to restore diversity, and long-term monitoring. Over 208 reintroductions have been attempted at 178 wild locations since the 1980s, with limited success (<15% persistence). The plan emphasizes genetic management, recognizing that Arizona populations show low heterozygosity due to recent bottlenecks, while Mexican populations retain higher genetic diversity.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] is a small live-bearing fish of the family [[Poeciliidae]], originally described by Baird and Girard in 1853 from specimens collected in the [[Santa Cruz River]] near Tucson. Males seldom exceed 25 mm standard length; females average 30-45 mm. They are tan to olive bodied with darkly outlined dorsal scales. Breeding males turn black with gold on the pre-dorsal midline, orange at the gonopodium base, and bright yellow pelvic, pectoral, and caudal fins. The species can be distinguished from [[Mosquitofish]] by the absence of a dark sub-orbital bar and black spots on fins; topminnow males have relatively longer gonopodia reaching beyond the snout when in copulatory position.

Historically, [[Gila Topminnow]] were widespread in the [[Gila River]] drainage below 1800 m elevation, from the [[San Francisco River]] at Frisco Hot Springs, New Mexico, west to the mainstem Gila near Yuma, Arizona. They occurred in the [[Salt River]] upstream to present-day Roosevelt Lake, throughout the [[Santa Cruz River]] system including [[Sonoita Creek]], and in northern Sonora's [[Rio de la Concepción]] and [[Rio Sonora]]. The species formed an almost continuous population at low elevations, dispersing during wet years and retreating to springs during droughts.

Genetic analyses using allozymes revealed three natural Arizona populations ([[Cienega Creek]], [[Monkey Spring]], [[Bylas Springs]]) were homozygous for all 25 loci tested, while [[Sharp Spring]] showed 3.7% mean heterozygosity. Mitochondrial DNA studies suggest the Gila basin historically harbored a single, basin-wide panmictic population, with current geographic differences resulting from recent anthropogenic bottlenecks rather than historical isolation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Gila Topminnow]] inhabit shallow, warm, fairly quiet waters but tolerate a wide range of conditions: temperatures from near freezing to 37°C, pH 6.6-8.9, dissolved oxygen 2.2-11 mg/l, and salinities from freshwater to seawater. They occupy springs, cienegas, marshes, permanent and intermittent streams, and historically river margins. Preferred habitat contains dense mats of algae and debris along stream margins or below riffles with sandy substrates. Topminnows occupy the upper third of the water column, with densities recorded as high as 566 fish/m² in [[Cienega Creek]].

The species has internal fertilization with males defending territories (large males) or employing "sneaking" strategies (small males). Sexual maturity occurs at 2-11 months depending on birth season. Breeding occurs primarily January-August, with year-round reproduction in thermally constant springs. Females carry 2-3 broods simultaneously, with brood sizes of 1-90 (average 5-15) and intervals of 24-28 days. Females average 2-5 broods per year and can store sperm for future fertilization.

Diet is omnivorous, including detritus, vegetation, amphipods, and insect larvae. Growth rate depends on age, sexual maturity, and available resources. Males stop growing at sexual maturity (rarely exceeding 30 mm SL), while females continue growing throughout life (up to 50 mm SL).

The species is more tolerant of moderate flooding than [[Mosquitofish]], with floods removing larger percentages of the exotic species. However, extreme flooding has eliminated several reintroduced populations. In habitats prone to flooding, topminnows have coexisted with mosquitofish for over 29 years, but in stable habitats, coexistence rarely exceeds 3 years.

## Key Findings

**Current Status and Threats:**
- Only 9 natural populations remain in the United States (down from 11 in 1984)
- Two populations lost since 1984: [[Salt Creek]] (mosquitofish) and [[Middle Spring]] (lack of water)
- Seven natural populations occur in the [[Santa Cruz River]] system; two on the [[San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation]]
- Only three natural populations ([[Monkey Spring]], [[Cottonwood Spring]], [[Cienega Creek]]) lack mosquitofish contamination
- Habitat destruction from late 1800s-early 1900s overgrazing, drought (1891-1893), and subsequent flooding caused widespread arroyo cutting and water table lowering
- Introduction of [[Mosquitofish]] (first collected 1926) corresponds strongly with topminnow decline through direct predation on young and fin-shredding of adults
- Elimination by mosquitofish can occur in <2-3 years

**Reintroduction Program Results:**
- Over 208 reintroductions attempted at 178 wild locations since early 1980s
- As of 1992, only 20 wild populations persist (19 within historic range)
- Success rate remains low (<15% persistence)
- Failure causes: 51% desiccation, 20% flooding, 20% unknown, 2% mosquitofish, 7% cattle overuse/dredging/low oxygen
- Thirteen reintroduced populations considered secure enough to persist into foreseeable future
- Most early reintroductions used [[Boyce-Thompson Arboretum]] stock of mixed and uncertain genetic origin

**Genetic Considerations:**
- Three Arizona populations completely homozygous at 25 allozyme loci
- [[Sharp Spring]] population shows higher heterozygosity (3.7%), survival, growth, fecundity, and developmental stability
- Mexican populations ([[Rio de la Concepción]], [[Rio Sonora]]) retain higher genetic diversity
- MtDNA evidence suggests historic basin-wide panmixia, with current differences from recent bottlenecks
- Genetic diversity positively correlated with fitness in experimental comparisons

**Recovery Criteria for Downlisting:**
1. Secure remaining natural populations and habitats at nine locations in four metapopulations: [[San Rafael Valley]] (Sharp Spring + uppermost Santa Cruz), [[Sonoita Creek]] complex (5 sites), [[Cienega Creek]], [[Bylas Springs]] complex
2. Replicate natural populations within historic range
3. Improve and maintain genetic diversity through protocol allowing gene flow among populations
4. Develop and implement monitoring plans for populations, habitats, and genetic integrity
5. Replicated populations must persist minimum 10 years to be considered established
6. Delisting not considered feasible in foreseeable future

**Recommended Recovery Actions:**
1. **Protect remaining populations:** Identify full geographic distribution, acquire habitats/conservation easements, remove nonnatives where feasible, protect from detrimental land/water practices
2. **Reestablish populations:** Use experimental design testing fitness of pure stocks vs. hybrids; stock sites near parental populations with same lineage; use isolated sites for experimental crosses; consider Mexican stocks to restore heterozygosity
3. **Monitor populations:** Develop standardized protocol; monitor natural populations twice yearly (March-April, September-October); monitor reintroduced populations within 2-year cycles
4. **Genetic protocol:** Develop computer model of population genetics; conduct mesocosm experiments with pure and hybrid stocks; implement field experiments; conduct periodic genetic monitoring at 5-year intervals
5. **Maintain captive populations:** Minimum 500 overwintering adults per refugium; maintain separate facilities for each natural population; assess genetic diversity every 5 years
6. **Research:** Study minimum viable population size, survival/reproduction success, temperature thresholds, interactions with native/nonnative species, disease incidence, movement patterns
7. **Education:** Develop materials in English and Spanish; conduct training seminars on sampling and identification; hold periodic interagency meetings

**Site-Specific Reintroduction Guidelines:**
- High-quality sites: permanent water, no mosquitofish, habitat complexity, preferably high-carbonate springheads, lack of detrimental human activities
- Stock large numbers (≥500) distributed among diverse habitat types
- Use [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] or selected refugia as sources
- Avoid direct use of wild populations to prevent mosquitofish contamination
- Coordinate all activities with [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]
- Population categories: Category 1 (expected to persist indefinitely without support), Category 2 (persist with support), Category 3 (low numbers/limited habitat), Category 4 (threatened by exotics)

**Captive Populations:**
- Eleven captive populations persisted in 1992
- Major refugia: [[Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum]], [[Boyce-Thompson Arboretum]] (not recommended for reintroductions due to mixed origin), [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], [[Roper Lake State Park]]
- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] switched from [[Monkey Spring]] to [[Sharp Spring]] stock in 1986 based on fitness studies
