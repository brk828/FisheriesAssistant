# Feeding Activity and Spawning Time of Striped Bass in the Colorado River Inlet, Lake Powell, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary
This study (Persons & Bulkley, 1982) investigated the spawning migration, reproductive timing, and feeding ecology of [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) in the Colorado River inlet of Lake Powell, Utah, during 1980 and 1981. Striped bass, an anadromous species introduced to Lake Powell in 1974, have established a naturally reproducing population. The research aimed to determine whether spawning adults migrated through [[Cataract Canyon]] rapids and whether they preyed upon native, threatened, or endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]] such as the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] during their spawning run. Results indicate that striped bass spawned in or near the river-reservoir mixing zone (Gypsum Canyon area) and did not ascend through Cataract Canyon rapids. Of 321 stomachs examined, only 30% contained food, dominated by [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), with no evidence of predation on native endangered species.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Morone saxatilis* — an anadromous coastal species successfully introduced into freshwater reservoirs, including [[Lake Powell]] (Glen Canyon Reservoir) in 1974.
- **Growth & Maturity** (per Gustaveson et al. 1980):
  - Males mature at age II, total length ~440 mm.
  - Females mature at age IV, total length ~670 mm.
- **Sex ratio in catch**: More than twice as many males as females captured at Gypsum Canyon across both study years (male ripe:spent ratio of 2.3:1 suggested males arrive earlier and predominate on spawning grounds).
- **Size range of associated squawfish**: Small *Ptychocheilus lucius* individuals captured measured 67, 72, 106, and 106 mm (juveniles); one juvenile at 251 mm captured in 1981 — indicating suitable prey-sized native fish were present but unconsumed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Striped bass occupied the river-reservoir mixing zone of the Colorado River inlet at Lake Powell, particularly near **Gypsum Canyon** (river km 317), just below the last rapid of Cataract Canyon. This zone featured strong currents, back eddies, and mud/silt bars, considered optimal for the semibuoyant, current-dependent eggs typical of [[Striped Bass Reproduction]].
- **Spawning conditions**:
  - Optimal spawning temperature range: 10–25°C, most frequently 15.6–17.8°C (Hardy 1978).
  - Egg incubation period: 48–72 hours at 15.6–17.8°C.
  - Spawning requires turbulent, silt-laden water with sufficient current to keep semibuoyant eggs suspended and prevent silting/smothering.
- **Migration behavior**: Unlike many populations that travel up to 320 km upstream from salt water in native ranges, Lake Powell striped bass did **not** migrate through Cataract Canyon rapids (river km 343, Spanish Bottom) in either sampling year — no striped bass or eggs were captured above the rapids.
- **Hydrology influence**: 
  - 1980 was a high-water year (river discharge 680–1,800 m³/sec; reservoir at max elevation 1,128 m).
  - 1981 was a low-water year (discharge 200–650 m³/sec; reservoir at 1,122 m), with a narrower, shallower channel and altered mixing zone (exposed mud/silt bars, reduced eddies).
- **Sympatric species**: Common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), walleye (*Stizostedion vitreum*), and channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*) were also frequently captured. Endangered natives — [[Razorback Sucker]] (6 in 1980, 10 in 1981) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (45 in 1980, 6 in 1981) — co-occurred in the same gill-net samples as striped bass.
- **Trophic role**: Striped bass are highly piscivorous; the study's central ecological concern was potential predation pressure on native endangered fish species sharing the spawning habitat.

## Key Findings

### Spawning Timing and Location
- Water temperatures at Gypsum Canyon were within/above the striped bass spawning range (16–18°C) during May 5–9, May 20–24, and after June 9 in 1980, and throughout the entire 1981 sampling period.
- Catch rates tracked temperature patterns: 1980 showed three catch peaks (early May, mid-May, mid-June); 1981 showed two peaks (early June, late June) — consistent with reports of multiple spawning peaks correlated with rising temperature or falling water levels (Hardy 1978).
- **No striped bass were captured above Cataract Canyon rapids** (Spanish Bottom) in either year, and no bass eggs were found in plankton tows there — confirming spawning occurred at or below Gypsum Canyon, not upstream in Cataract Canyon or the Green/Colorado confluence.
- Four striped bass eggs were collected via plankton net in the Gypsum Canyon area in 1980 (three viable, one apparently unfertilized/dead); none were collected in 1981, though later fall surveys indicated good reproductive success that year.
- Early arrival and dominance of ripe males (2.3:1 ratio) at Gypsum Canyon further supports a nearby spawning location.

### Sex, Maturity, and Timing
- In 1980, sexually mature males were captured as soon as sampling began (water temp 13°C, April 26); mature females were not captured until May 20 (16°C).
- Spent females first appeared June 11, 1980; spent males appeared later.
- In 1981, sampling began later in the spawning cycle — the first fish captured was already a spent female, and spawning had evidently begun before sampling started.
- More than 40% of 1981 catch was spent, versus <10% in 1980 — attributed to warmer water temperatures and a later sampling window in 1981.
- Grand totals: 403 fish sexed (282 males [70%], 121 females [30%]).

### Feeding Ecology
- Of 321 adult striped bass stomachs examined over both years:
  - **70% were empty**.
  - **28% (90 fish) contained threadfin shad** (*Dorosoma petenense*) — the dominant prey item.
  - Only 4 stomachs contained red shiner (*Notropis lutrensis*) and 2 contained unidentified fish remains.
  - **No native threatened or endangered Colorado River fish species were found in any stomach**, despite their co-occurrence in the sampling area.
- Feeding activity varied by maturity stage: ~42% of immature/maturing fish stomachs contained food, vs. 25% of ripe (sexually mature) fish, and 33% of spent fish — consistent with known patterns that striped bass feed less during active spawning condition (Raney 1952; Trent and Hassler 1966).
- Similar percentages of stomachs (30%) and intestinal tracts (41%) contained food, suggesting regurgitation from gill-netting was not a major confounding factor in diet composition estimates.

### Conservation & Management Implications
- No evidence supports predation by adult striped bass on native endangered fishes ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]]) or the rare [[Razorback Sucker]] during the spring/early-summer spawning migration, despite co-occurrence and availability of suitably-sized squawfish prey.
- [[Threadfin Shad]] currently constitute the primary forage base for striped bass in Lake Powell; as long as shad remain abundant, heavy predation on native/endemic species is considered unlikely.
- Natural reproduction confirmed in Lake Powell (in addition to [[Lake Mead]]) led to termination of striped bass fry stocking in 1980.
- Findings suggest striped bass, though normally anadromous, can adapt to an **adfluvial life history**, spawning entirely within reservoir/river inlet systems without requiring access to salt water or extensive upstream migration.
- Fall gill-net surveys for young-of-year striped bass were noted as a more effective method for assessing reproductive success than plankton-net egg/larvae sampling, which was hampered by heavy debris load in the sampling area.
