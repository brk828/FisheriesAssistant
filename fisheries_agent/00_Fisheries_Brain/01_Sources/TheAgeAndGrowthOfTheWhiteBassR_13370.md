# The age and growth of the white bass, Roccus chrysops, of Herrington Lake, Kentucky

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Moronidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 Kentucky Division of Game and Fish study documents the age, growth, maturity, and length-weight relationship of the [[White Bass]] (*Morone chrysops*) population in [[Herrington Lake]], a deep, rocky Kentucky reservoir. Based on 1,978 fish collected during the spring spawning run, the study found that white bass in Herrington Lake exhibit unusually rapid growth compared to populations in [[Lake Erie]], [[Spirit Lake]] (Iowa), and [[Clear Lake]] (Iowa), reach sexual maturity early, and have short lifespans (maximum Age Group IV, no fish surviving past their fifth summer). The species had become the dominant game fish in the reservoir since its introduction around 1930, supported primarily by an abundant forage base of [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*). The authors concluded that existing fishing regulations (15-fish creel limit, no size limit) were adequate given the species' fast growth and short life cycle.

## Background & Description

- **Water body:** [[Herrington Lake]] — a 2,940-acre reservoir in the Bluegrass region of Central Kentucky, formed in 1925 by a hydroelectric dam on the [[Dix River]], a tributary of the [[Kentucky River]].
- **Physical characteristics:** Average depth of 73 feet; deep river gorge morphology; rocky, precipitous banks; notable lack of shoal habitat and aquatic vegetation.
- **Population monitoring:** Kentucky Division of Game and Fish has conducted annual population studies since 1948.
- **Community composition (rotenone sample, 20 acres):**
  - [[White Bass]]: ~7.6% of total population weight (most abundant game fish, except in 1949)
  - [[Black Bass]] (various species): ~3.8% of total weight
  - [[Sunfish]] species (combined): <7% of total weight
  - [[Gizzard Shad]]: ~53% of total weight — the dominant forage fish, comprising ~90% of white bass diet by volume
- **Introduction history:** White bass were absent from Herrington Lake proper from 1925–1928 despite presence in the Kentucky River. Large numbers began appearing in the tailwaters of the dam thereafter (possibly reflecting migratory behavior similar to that noted by Eschmeyer (1942) in the [[Clinch River]] below Norris Dam). Anglers stocked "a few hundred" fish directly into the reservoir. By 1930 onward, white bass popularity as a game fish grew rapidly, rivaling black bass.
- **Spawning run:** Fish undertake a well-known annual spawning migration up the [[Dix River]] each April, attracting a significant recreational fishery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Trophic role:** White bass function as a key predatory game fish supported almost entirely by [[Gizzard Shad]] forage populations, indicating a strong predator-prey linkage central to reservoir fisheries management.
- **Habitat limitations:** The lake's steep-sided, deep morphology and lack of shoal/vegetated habitat likely shape spawning behavior, driving the species to migrate into river headwaters (Dix River) for reproduction rather than spawning within the reservoir itself.
- **Population turnover:** Rapid growth coupled with early maturity and short lifespan (max Age Group IV) suggests a population structure with high annual turnover — most mortality occurring in the third year of life.
- **Comparative growth environments:** Compared to [[Lake Erie]] and Iowa lakes, Herrington Lake's white bass show superior growth rates, possibly reflecting favorable forage abundance (gizzard shad) and/or reservoir productivity despite limited vegetative habitat.

## Key Findings

### Body-Scale Relationship
- Regression line fit for total length vs. anterior scale radius (X33): Y-intercept = 1.12, slope = 1.74 (n = 2,169 fish, including 191 young fish from June/August 1951).
- Comparable literature correction factors: Van Oosten (Lake Erie) = 1.18"; Sigler (Spirit Lake, Iowa) = 1.21".
- Correction formula applied: `Lx = 1.1 + [Sx(L - 1.1)/S]`

### Age Composition
- Sample: 1,978 fish (April 18–28, 1951).
- No fish older than **Age Group IV** found.
- **54.9%** of fish were in Age Group I.
- **38%** were Age Group 0 (young-of-year).
- Only **7.1%** belonged to Age Groups II–IV combined.
- Comparable to Van Oosten's Lake Erie findings (only 4.7% older than 3 years), indicating a young-dominated population typical of white bass generally.
- Majority of mortality occurs during the third year of life.

### Growth Rates (Calculated Total Length, inches)
| Sex | End Yr 1 | End Yr 2 | End Yr 3 | End Yr 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Male (n=684) | ~8.4 | ~13.2 | ~14.9 | — |
| Female (n=543) | 8.5 | 13.6 | 15.6 | 17.1 |
| **Combined avg** | **8.4** | **13.4** | **15.1** | **16.6** |

- Females exceeded males in calculated length **every year**, as well as in annual growth increment.
- Growth was notably faster than reported for [[Lake Erie]], [[Spirit Lake]] (Iowa), and [[Clear Lake]] (Iowa) populations (Table V comparison).
- Only 13 comparison specimens available from [[Kentucky Lake]], averaging 8.7 inches at end of year 2.

### Sexual Maturity
- **Males:** Average maturity length = **8.5 inches**. Of 658 Age-0 males examined, 526 (80%) were mature. No mature males <8.1 in.; no immature males >8.9 in.
- **Females:** Average maturity length = **10.4 inches**. Smallest ripe female = 8.2 in.; all females >11.4 in. were mature. Of 93 Age-0 females, only 14 (15%) were ripe.
- High percentage of mature Age-0 males reflects sampling bias — faster-growing individuals disproportionately represented in the spawning run catch.

### Length-Weight Relationship
- Equation: **W = 3.712 × 10⁻⁶ L^2.977**
- Logarithmic form: **Log W = -3.4304 + 3.077 log L**
- Herrington Lake white bass were found to be less "plump" (lower condition) than fish studied by Van Oosten (Lake Erie) and Sigler (Spirit Lake).

### Management Recommendations
- Existing regulations (creel limit of 15, no size limit) were deemed adequate.
- Given rapid growth, short lifespan, and early maturity, harvest at maximum rate was encouraged.
- Creel limit retained primarily to deter poaching/commercial exploitation rather than for population protection.
- If future size limit needed, recommended minimum = **11 inches** for near-complete reproductive protection.
