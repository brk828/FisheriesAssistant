# Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center: Annual Report, Fiscal Year 2001

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #hatchery-management

## Executive Summary

This annual report for Fiscal Year 2001 covers the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center in the Pecos River Valley of southeastern New Mexico. The facility functions as a refugium and recovery center for imperiled fishes of the American Southwest, holding 16 listed species and one species of special concern in FY 2001. Its work includes captive maintenance of genetically diverse broodstock, production for reintroduction under recovery programs, facility upgrades, staffing, budgeting, and public outreach. The report also documents the first culture of [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] at the station.

## Background & Description

- **Authorization and history:** Authorized under the White Act of 1930. Station land (640 acres, Section 16, T13S, R26E, Chaves County) was acquired by the Federal Government on August 31, 1931, for $3,265.90. Construction used Public Works Administration and Works Progress Administration assistance; operations began in 1932.
- **Mission shift:** After the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, the station was selected in 1974 as the site for an imperiled fishes recovery program and by 1978 raised only threatened and endangered species. It was designated a Technology Center in FY 1991, one of only seven in the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
- **Goals:** Establish a refuge for critically imperiled southwestern fish; develop spawning and culture techniques; and produce fish for reintroduction programs.
- **Objectives:** Maintain protected gene pools; develop culture methods; study biological requirements; supply specimens to agencies; exchange expertise; publish findings; and run a public information program.
- **Location and setting:** About 200 miles southeast of Albuquerque and 20 miles south of Roswell, on the northern fringe of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. Elevation is 3,500 ft, average rainfall about 12 inches, and the growing season lasts 180 to 210 days.
- **Water supply:** Five shallow aquifer wells (150 ft deep), pumping a combined 2,000+ gallons per minute. Water is a constant 64°F, pH 7.5, total hardness 2,100 ppm, and total dissolved solids 3,500 ppm. Culture wastewater is collected in two sumps isolated from other surface waters.
- **Facilities:** 46 earthen ponds (0.1 to 1.0 acre), four 6' x 40' concrete raceways, four 6' x 40' and twenty 2' x 12' fiberglass raceways, more than 40 four-foot and 50 three-foot fiberglass circular tanks, and numerous aquaria, plus administration, culture, visitor, shop, storage, and feed buildings and housing trailers.
- **Staff:** The report lists 11 permanent and temporary staff with experience ranging from one to 25+ years, including an Assistant Center Director, a Geneticist, a Fishery Biologist, and a Genetics Fisheries Biologist.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The station lies in a region with a high concentration of rare fishes. The report states that no other place in the world has such a concentration of rare and endangered fishes at a single locality. Of the 16 listed species held in FY 2001, all but one (Pahranagat roundtail chub, maintained for USFWS Region 1) originate from New Mexico, neighboring states (Arizona, Utah, Colorado, Texas), or Mexican states (Sonora and Chihuahua).

The northwest portion of the property is used for operations. The remainder is managed as a Wildlife Management Area, where the sumps receive hatchery effluent. This area is open to seasonal hunting of upland game and migratory birds. Station staff also participated in the Middle-Upper Rio Grande and Pecos Ecosystem teams.

## Key Findings

**Species maintenance and distribution (FY 2001)**

- **[[Big Bend Gambusia]]** (endangered): Refugium since 1974; the only other known population is at Big Bend National Park, Texas. No fish distributed in 2001.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (endangered): Refugium since 1976. No fish distributed in 2001.
- **[[Pecos Gambusia]]** (endangered): Refugium since 1999, held in aquaria. No fish distributed in 2001.
- **[[Leon Springs Pupfish]]** (endangered): Refugium since 1974; the only other population is at Diamond Y/Leon Creek, Texas. 90 fish went to the University of New Mexico and 5,404 to Diamond Y/Leon Creek.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (endangered): Refugium since 1983. No fish distributed in 2001.
- **[[Comanche Springs Pupfish]]** (endangered): Refugium since 1999. 10 fish went to Oklahoma State University for genetic verification of stock purity.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (endangered): Refugium and production since 1981. 32,745 fish went to the University of New Mexico for rearing and stocking in the San Juan River.
- **[[Yaqui Sucker]]** (special concern): Refugium since 1996. No fish distributed in 2001.
- **[[Guzman Beautiful Shiner]]** (threatened): Refugium since 1978. No fish distributed in 2001.
- **[[Chihuahua Chub]]** (threatened): Refugium and production since 1979. 4,797 fish went to the Mimbres River, New Mexico.
- **[[Woundfin]]** (endangered): Refugium and production since 1979. 5,173 fish went to the Nevada Department of Wildlife.
- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]** (endangered): Refugium and production since 1980; three unique populations maintained (Yampa River 1974, Green/Colorado River 1981, Colorado River 1991). 148 fish went to the San Juan River.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (endangered): Refugium and production since 1981, described as the most endangered fish in North America. Dexter holds the largest known captive population. Distributions included 15,957 eggs to Mora NFH & TC; 110,825 fish to Wahweap SFH, Utah; 60,500 to the Mumma Native Aquatic Species Restoration Facility, Colorado; 56,472 to Willow Beach NFH; 255 to the National Wildlife Health Center, Wisconsin; and 8,379 subadults to Lake Mohave. A five-year broodstock replacement program entered its second year.
- **[[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]]**: Refugium since 1985. No fish distributed in 2001 (the report text reads "2007", likely an OCR error).
- **[[Virgin River Chub]]** (endangered): Refugium since 1989. No fish distributed in 2001.
- **[[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]]** (endangered): First brought to Dexter in 2001. 525 three-year-old broodstock adults arrived in May, followed by 80,500 fry from June to September. More than 11,000 fish were produced for Rio Grande restoration. Survival was described as satisfactory by warm-water culture standards, but consistent high-survival methods are still needed.

**Facility and infrastructure**

- The final Phase II construction project was completed in 2001. HI Plains Drilling re-drilled five wells and installed new pumps at a cost of $453,000.
- Flow increased from roughly 250 gallons per minute to a projected 2,850 gallons per minute. Tested and limit capacities varied by well (for example, Well #4 tested at 950 gpm with a 750 gpm limit).
- Fleet replacements included a 2001 Dodge Dakota, a 2001 John Deere utility vehicle, a 2001 Chevrolet extended cab, and a 2001 John Deere tractor.
- FY 2002 funding was made available for 25 new ponds, four raceways, lining of two larger ponds, and paving and landscaping.

**Planned work (FY 2002)**

- Assist in developing a propagation plan and coordinate culture with cooperating agencies.
- Refine culture methods for consistent production of quality animals.
- Conduct genetic screening of incoming and outgoing stock, including developing five microsatellite markers for quality control.
- Develop captive spawning, incubation, and larval rearing techniques for refugia and research.
- Publish a production guide for intensive and extensive Rio Grande Silvery Minnow culture.

**Outreach and review**

- A Technology Center Program Review was conducted in March 2001 by a six-member USFWS and USGS team.
- Staff presented at the Robust Redhorse Conservation Committee and at regional hatchery and technology meetings, focusing on genetics and [[Razorback Sucker]] management.
- About 400 students and many visitors toured the facility. The station hosted a display at the Bitter Lake NWR Dragonfly Festival in Roswell (August 11-12, 2001).
- Press coverage included a bonytail spawning article and a June 2001 feature in *New Mexico* magazine on state hatcheries.
