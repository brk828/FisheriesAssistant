# Gear Selectivity Models, Mortality Profiles and the Interpretation of Archaeological Fish Remains: A Harney Basin Case Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper evaluates whether [[Gear Selectivity Models]] and [[Catastrophic Mortality Profiles]] can reliably tell natural fish kills from human fishing, and which fishing gear produced an archaeological fish assemblage. The case study is a set of [[Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor*) remains from the [[Harney Dune Site]] in the Harney Basin, northwestern [[Great Basin]], Oregon. The author concludes that the models are a useful framework but are limited unless applied with attention to fish population structure, behaviour, taphonomy and archaeological context. The size data are consistent with deliberate, size-selective fishing, most plausibly with [[Gill Nets]].

## Background & Description

Archaeological fish assemblages often contain many bones with no cultural modification, and no direct evidence of fishing technology. Interpretation therefore relies on species composition, skeletal part representation and size range. The paper focuses on two questions: whether an assemblage is cultural or natural in origin, and what gear produced it if it is cultural.

The tui chub is a schooling cyprinid of lacustrine and marsh habitats. Adults move offshore into deeper water in winter and inshore in spring, while young fish remain in shallows. Dense aggregations of adults occur in nearshore water during summer spawning. Many fish species, including tui chub, lay thousands of eggs per season and show high juvenile mortality. Tui chubs grow rapidly in early years, then growth tapers off.

Key methodological terms include:
- **Gill net selectivity:** unimodal, roughly normal curve set by mesh size.
- **Seine and dip nets:** sharp cut-off on the small-size side.
- **Baited hooks:** unimodal but broader than gill net curves.
- **Catastrophic (living structure) profile** versus **attritional (true mortality) profile.**

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population structure.** A living lacustrine tui chub population is multimodal and contains many small individuals. Eagle Lake data from [[Davis 1986]] show individuals reaching about 340 mm by roughly 8 years, with some living more than 20 years. Size and age profiles differ in shape and should not be substituted for one another.

**Mortality models.** The author criticises earlier fish studies by [[Balme 1983]] and [[Butler 1996]] for using diagonal catastrophic profiles without justification. These profiles assume a constant mortality rate and were derived from large mammal models, which the author argues are not transferable to fish with high juvenile mortality. Butler's model also treats age as a proxy for size, which is inappropriate because growth is not constant through life.

**Taphonomy and natural kills.** Natural fish kills can be non-selective or selectively affect young or spawned-out adults. Small and immature bones are poorly preserved and underrepresented. Birds scavenging a fish kill may carry off small fish, leaving larger ones to enter the archaeological record. Both processes can mimic cultural size selection.

**Site setting.** The Harney Dune Site (35HA718) is a lithic scatter on the lakeward side of Harney Dune, on the north shore of Harney Lake, managed by the [[Malheur National Wildlife Refuge]]. The Harney Basin has internal drainage. Radiocarbon dates and projectile points place occupation within the last 500 years. The site lies along an alkaline lake with little fuel for natural brush fires.

**Ethnographic fishing.** [[Northern Paiute]] groups used gill nets, dip nets, seine nets, bag nets, basket traps and hook and line. Gill nets were weighted with notched stones. Sites in the area yielded net weights and bipointed bone gorges. The [[Cattail-Eater Northern Paiute]] at Stillwater Marsh took large tui chubs with gill nets, traps and hook and line, and small ones with dip nets and basket scoops.

## Key Findings

1. **Model limits.** Gear selectivity and mortality models are useful only when population structure, schooling, seasonal movement, taphonomy and archaeological context are considered together.
2. **Critique of prior work.** Diagonal catastrophic profiles used by Balme and Butler lack theoretical justification for fish and conflate age with size.
3. **Size reconstruction.** Total lengths were estimated from regression equations based on 44 modern specimens, using the hyomandibular and basioccipital bones. Regressions had R² values of 0.985 and 0.964.
4. **Adult populations.** All measured specimens were adults. Estimated total lengths ranged from 130 to 342 mm.
5. **Feature differences.** Summary statistics (total length, mm):

| Feature | N | Min | Max | Mean | SD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 68 | 86 | 130 | 228 | 184 | 20.7 |
| 89 | 93 | 161 | 321 | 227 | 24.0 |
| 66 | 17 | 240 | 342 | 277 | 26.7 |

6. **Distribution shape.** Features 68 and 89 are unimodal and roughly normal, consistent with gill net capture. Feature 66 (N=17) is not clearly unimodal, and its larger fish plus three bone gorges suggest possible hook and line fishing.
7. **Statistical difference.** Features 68 and 89 have significantly different means (t = −12.78, P<0.001), which the author takes as evidence of two different nets. Radiocarbon dates suggest the features may differ in age by up to a few hundred years.
8. **Cultural origin.** Support includes association of fish bones with cultural deposits, burning in an area with little natural fuel, and net weights and gorges on site. The author regards these lines of evidence as stronger than population structure alone.
9. **Comparative size.** Harney Dune specimens are large relative to Stillwater Marsh (SL 52–143 mm, mean 89 mm) and Nevada cave assemblages. Measurement types (TL, SL, FL) differ, so comparisons are approximate.
10. **Processing and optimal foraging.** Small tui chubs could be dried whole or eaten bones-and-all, reducing processing time. Lindstrom's diet breadth model ranked spawning fish captured by basketry scoop 20th of 58 when processing time was included, versus top rank without it. Small fish may therefore have been an efficient resource.
11. **Hypothesis.** Deliberate, size-selective tui chub fishing with gill nets at Harney Dune is supported, though the author notes that other gear, seasonal effects and multiple fishing events cannot be fully excluded. Incremental growth ring analysis might help determine season of death.
