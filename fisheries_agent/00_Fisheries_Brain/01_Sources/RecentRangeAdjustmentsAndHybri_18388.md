# Recent Range Adjustments and Hybridization of Notropis lutrensis and Notropis spilopterus in Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 paper by [[Lawrence M. Page]] and [[Richard L. Smith]] examines hybridization and competitive displacement among Illinois minnows of the subgenus *Cyprinella* (genus *Notropis*). The authors report that [[Notropis Lutrensis]] has expanded into streams long occupied by [[Notropis Spilopterus]], leading to hybrid swarms and the decline of *N. spilopterus* across much of the Mississippi drainage. Hybridization appears to depend on [[Turbidity]], which may both facilitate invasion and break down [[Reproductive Isolation]]. Comparison of 1908 and 1950–1969 records supports a pattern of competitive displacement.

## Background & Description

*Notropis lutrensis* and *N. spilopterus* are closely related minnows within the subgenus *Cyprinella* (see [[Gibbs 1957]]). Both species are separated by body depth to standard length ratio, lateral-line scale counts, and anal-ray counts, which the authors treat as characters that could reveal introgression ([[Hubbs and Lagler Methods]]).

Sampling was carried out on October 5 and 26, 1969, in the middle [[Green River]] system, an area with a high incidence of hybrids. Specimens were preserved in 10 percent formalin at capture and supplemented with [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] collections. Comparative samples were:

- 100 hybrids (Green River area)
- 100 *N. lutrensis* and 100 *N. spilopterus* from near the hybrid locality (within 50 miles)
- 100 *N. lutrensis* from central and southern Illinois (Mississippi and Illinois drainages)
- 100 *N. spilopterus* from eastern Illinois (Wabash drainage)

Historical distributions are based on [[Forbes and Richardson 1908]] and a current survey of Illinois fishes. Figures 1 to 3 map the 1908 and 1950–1969 records, but these are not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Notropis lutrensis.** This species occupies a wide range of habitats, from small intermittent streams to medium and occasionally large rivers. Its largest populations occur in small to medium streams. It tolerates bottoms from clean sand or gravel to almost pure silt, and the authors consider this silt tolerance a major factor in its abundance in Illinois agricultural areas.

**Notropis spilopterus.** This species is most often found over sand or gravel bars in streams of moderate current. It remains abundant in the Wabash drainage and northern Illinois, including small, turbid northern streams.

**Green River lowlands.** The middle Green River lies in a lowland sand area with slow to moderate current. Its tributaries are mainly sand-bottomed but have been silted by surrounding agriculture. Forbes and Richardson's five collections included 56 species, among them clear-water forms such as *Notropis rubellus*, *N. heterodon*, *N. boops*, [[Erimyzon Oblongus]], and [[Etheostoma Exile]]. Eighteen collections from 1950 to the present yielded only 38 species, none of the clear-water forms listed above. Silt-tolerant species such as [[Common Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Black Nose Dace]] are now present but were not recorded earlier.

**Other Cyprinella.** [[Notropis venustus]] shares a drainage with *N. lutrensis* in southern Illinois, where the two species hybridize and coexist. [[Notropis whipplei]] shows range reduction in areas now occupied by *N. lutrensis* but no evidence of hybridization.

## Key Findings

1. **Range shifts.** *N. lutrensis* is now extremely common across its Illinois range and has extended its range east and north from its 1908 distribution. *N. spilopterus* was formerly widespread except along the Mississippi in the southern half of the state, but has been nearly extirpated from the Mississippi drainage of the southern three-quarters of Illinois. Recent lower Mississippi records consist of six from the Sangamon River system and one from the Kaskaskia River system.

2. **Displacement.** Many streams invaded by *N. lutrensis* were previously occupied by *N. spilopterus*, which disappeared after the invasion. The complementary (parapatric) present ranges indicate strong competition, and at range convergence *N. lutrensis* eventually displaces *N. spilopterus*.

3. **Hybrid swarms.** Hybrid swarms occur in two areas, including the Green River system. The authors report that hybrids were not all F1 progeny and were probably partially fertile, with backcrosses likely represented.

4. **Morphology.** Mean lateral-line and anal-ray counts of the 100 hybrids were intermediate between the parent species, with anal-ray counts closer to *N. spilopterus*. Mean body depth to standard length ratio was lower than in the two *N. lutrensis* samples and one *N. spilopterus* sample, but higher than the non-Green River *N. spilopterus* sample. The table values were partly garbled in the scan, so specific figures are not reproduced here.

5. **Turbidity and isolation.** Breeding males show bright yellow fins (*N. spilopterus*) and bright red fins (*N. lutrensis*), suggesting visual species recognition. Courtship sounds in *Cyprinella* suggest acoustic recognition as well. Heavy silt in the breeding habitat could disrupt both light and sound and lead to mismatched spawning partners.

6. **Proposed sequence.** The authors propose the following sequence: initial occupation by *N. spilopterus*, invasion by *N. lutrensis*, hybridization, increasing *N. lutrensis* influence through successive invasions, and eventual loss of *N. spilopterus* and hybrids. Hybrid vigor may explain the initial fitness of hybrids, but later replacement is more likely due to displacement by the invader than to loss of fitness.

7. **No permanent genetic imprint.** Specimens of *N. lutrensis* from areas formerly occupied by *N. spilopterus* show no morphological influence from it. Introgression near the Green River swarm is possible but no permanent effect is discernible.

8. **Future outlook.** The authors expect *N. lutrensis* to complete its displacement of *N. spilopterus* in the Mississippi drainage and suggest it may eventually become statewide. The Wabash tributaries carry less silt, which could reduce the basis for competitive exclusion there.

9. **Other species.** Hybrids between *N. venustus* and *N. lutrensis* are fairly common, but no competitive displacement is expected. *N. whipplei* appears to find *N. lutrensis* ecologically incompatible, as *N. spilopterus* does, though no hybridization was found.
