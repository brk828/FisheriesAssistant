# Age Composition and Growth of Fish and Fisherman Success in Utah's High Uinta Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Sigler and Low (1949–1950, *Proceedings of the Utah Academy of Sciences, Arts and Letters*) report a 16-day pack trip (August 19 to September 3, 1947) through the primitive area of the high [[Uinta Mountains]] in northeastern Utah. The study sampled trout from 13 alpine lakes, aged 104 fish from 4 lakes by scale annuli, and recorded fishing effort and catch. The authors describe the region as a large, largely unexploited fishery whose trout grow slowly, whose fish are readily caught, and whose cutthroat trout dominate. The paper is explicitly exploratory: it aims to highlight the fishery resource and to identify questions for future investigation.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Uinta Mountains form a single east-west range, roughly 160 miles long and 45 miles wide, with King's Peak (13,525 ft) the highest point in Utah. The range was heavily glaciated, and glacial deposition created many of its lakes. The authors estimate that more than 1,000 lakes lie in the region, classed as temperate lakes with summer surface temperatures near 4 °C.
- **Study lakes:** Data came from 13 lakes near the range center, mostly at or near timberline. Lake elevations were mostly 9,500 to 11,500 ft. Some snow from the previous winter remained in protected spots in late August 1947.
- **Species:** Cutthroat trout ([[Salmo clarkii]]) and [[Brook Trout]] ([[Salvelinus fontinalis]]) were the aged species. [[Rainbow Trout]] ([[Salmo gairdnerii irideus]]) were present in the catches but were not aged.
- **Historical stocking:** According to the local fish and game warden, most high lakes had no fish until the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources|Utah Fish and Game Department]] planted trout. Lakes below timberline generally had larger fish and a higher proportion of stocked populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry (after [[Pennak 1945]]):** Average values for eight alpine lakes above 3,200 m were:
- Total residue: 25.73 mg/L
- Ash content: 20.35 mg/L
- Total organic content: 5.38 mg/L
- pH range: 6.6 to 7.5

These are classed as soft-water lakes. Pennak (1949) argued that plankton production estimates should be based on year-round sampling and that seston is the most reliable and least variable measure of mean annual plankton standing crop.

**Primary production and plankton:** Larger aquatic vegetation is sparse or absent. The water is cold and clear, and microscopic plant life is scarce. According to [[Tanner 1931]], some Uinta lakes share diatom and desmid species with Arctic and European waters.

**Climate and hydrology:**
- Tanner (1931) estimated annual precipitation at 25 to 35 inches, based on snowfall records.
- Of 86 lakes sounded by Tanner, only 11 exceeded 25 ft in maximum depth. Surface temperatures therefore track air temperature closely.
- Many lakes are frozen for about six months of the year.

**Diet:** Most stomachs examined were empty. Where food was present, the main items were winged ants and other terrestrial insects. Tanner's (1931) analysis of 176 Uinta trout gave terrestrial life 50%, plankton 25%, and other items (*Gammarus* sp., caddis flies, sticks, rocks) 25%.

Age composition (Table 2, counts per age group):
- **Kay's Brook (n = 21):** age 2 = 6; age 3 = 15
- **White Rock and Ted's (n = 22):** age 2 = 4; age 3 = 17; age 4 = 1
- **Island (n = 61):** counts of 21, 36, 3 and 1 (column headers partly lost; age 2 is 21 and age 3 is 36 on the most plausible reading)

The authors attribute the small size of brook and cutthroat trout, and the predominance of two- and three-year-old fish, partly to the fact that most fish were taken from shore with a fly rod. Island Lake and Twin Lakes were fished from a boat.

Growth was slow compared with the same species in warmer Utah waters. The authors consider this expected given the short growing season and apparent food scarcity.

### 2. Fishing success (13 lakes, August 20 to September 1, 1947)

- Overall average success was **2.45 fish per hour**, which the authors regard as remarkably high.
- Individual lakes ranged widely. Examples (fish per hour): Twin 2.95 (65 trout, 22 manhours); Island 6.30 (63 cutthroat, 10 manhours); Kay's 10.50; Unnamed lake 3.17; White Rock 1.81; Red Castle 1.92 (48 fish, 25 manhours); Upper Five Points 1.70 (41 fish, 24 manhours); Two Unnamed Lakes 2.73 (60 fish, 22 manhours).
- The full table (Table 4) also lists lakes with lower values, including Lost (1.00) and Queant (1.17). Data for lakes where no fish were taken are omitted.
- For comparison, [[Thoreson 1949]] reported average success of about 0.5 fish per hour in the [[Logan River]].

The authors offer three explanations for the high success: a large fish population, high vulnerability to angling, or both. They suggest food scarcity may have made fish bite readily. Ray Johnson (personal communication, Minnesota Department of Conservation) noted that good fishing can be hard to obtain when fish are numerous, and that hungry fish strike readily at likely objects.

### 3. Gear, bait and lure preference

Fly casting was the only method used. Artificial flies were the only bait, apart from a few grasshoppers. A dark-colored artificial fly was taken more readily than any other lure, which the authors link to the prevalence of terrestrial insects in stomachs.

### 4. Species dominance

Cutthroat trout were the most abundant species in 10 of the 13 lakes fished. The authors could not determine whether this reflects adaptability or the effects of stocking.

### 5. Methodological caveats

The [[Scale Aging Method]] rests on three assumptions (after [[Van Oosten 1930]]): scale number is constant and scales retain identity; scale growth is proportional to body growth; and annuli form yearly at a consistent time. The authors accept the first two for their purposes. They state that the data do not prove annuli form yearly or at a fixed time, and that this assumption is presumed rather than demonstrated. Calculated lengths for cutthroat and brook trout agreed closely, though the authors note this does not prove annual annulus formation.

### 6. Conclusions and research directions

The authors describe the high Uinta lakes as a substantial but largely unstudied fishery with high angler success and slow growth. They list the following as needing investigation: whether annuli form yearly; how stocking history shapes species composition; the relationship between food supply, fish density and catch rates; and the role of year-round plankton sampling.
