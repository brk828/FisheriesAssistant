# Mollusca on the University of Kansas Natural History Reservation

#mollusca #gastropoda #pulmonata #malacology

## Executive Summary

This 1952 [[Kansas University Science Bulletin]] paper by A. Byron Leonard and C. Raymond Goble is the first in a planned series cataloguing the invertebrate and vertebrate fauna of the [[University of Kansas Natural History Reservation]]. The authors surveyed the land snails and freshwater mollusks at ten collecting stations (A–J) between October 1948 and June 1949, shortly after cultivation and grazing ceased. They recorded 25 molluscan taxa: four aquatic gastropods, two bivalves, and 21 terrestrial gastropods (the abstract and summary count terrestrial and aquatic species differently, so the totals are reported as the authors gave them). The paper provides species accounts with recognition characters, type localities, distributions, habitat ecology, and station-level abundance data.

## Background & Description

The Reservation covers about one square mile of wooded, hilly land on the north slope of the [[Kansas River Valley]] in [[Douglas County Kansas]]. The land was formerly grazed and cultivated. Grazing ended on most timbered areas before 1938, and a 1936 CCC program built check dams in a meadow to halt erosion. After 1948 the administration adopted a policy of letting the Reservation revert to natural conditions.

The study had three stated purposes:

- To record the kinds of mollusks present.
- To serve as a baseline for detecting changes in molluscan populations as vegetation and the wider fauna recovered.
- To document the site's position near the southern and western range margins of many northern and eastern snails, and the northern margin of some southern species.

Methods included field inspection in September 1948, collection by Goble from October 1, 1948 until mid-November, and regular trips from March 1 to June 20, 1949. Snails were collected into vials, cleaned, and catalogued in the Mollusk Collections of the [[University of Kansas Museum of Natural History]]. Field equipment included an aspirator, screens for sifting soil and gravel, a 10x hand lens, and a one-foot quadrat frame used for population estimates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate.** Kansas has extreme temperature and precipitation swings. In 1936 Lawrence recorded an average summer monthly rainfall of 0.84 inch and a July high of 113°F. Douglas County annual rainfall was 42.93 inches in 1935 and 22.12 inches in 1936, the driest year on record for the state. The authors note that hot, dry periods strongly limit which species can persist: aquatic snails must tolerate ephemeral water, and terrestrial snails must either aestivate or stay in the most humid microhabitats.

**Aspect and vegetation.** South-facing slopes are drier and wind-exposed, and they support elm, honey locust, osage orange, and yellow oak. North-facing slopes are moister and support shagbark hickory, black oak, white ash, and black walnut. The molluscan fauna follows this moisture gradient. Historic big bluestem grassland has largely been replaced by woody cover since settlement.

**Stations.** The ten stations were chosen for differences in local habitat:

- **Station A (small stream):** a seepage-fed stream with crayfish (*Orconectes nais*) and small fish. It supported a large colony of [[Physa hawni]] and some [[Pisidium Compressum]] shells.
- **Station B (silted pond):** a pond that had filled with silt and was dry for much of the year. It held shells of [[Helisoma Trivolvis]], [[Sphaerium]], and [[Succinea Concordialis]], but no living snails were found.
- **Station C (stream banks):** steep, humid banks, where [[Vertigo ovata]] and [[Lymnaea parva]] occurred only at this station.
- **Station D (decaying logs):** 13 species, with [[Zonitoides arboreus]] especially abundant. One eight-foot log held 26 living individuals.
- **Station E (north-facing slope):** 14 species, with 18–25 snails per square foot in the yellow oak–hickory–walnut area.
- **Station F (west-facing slope):** steep and moist, with [[Gastrocopta holzingeri]] the most numerous species and [[Pupoides albilabris]] abundant on well-drained slopes.
- **Station G (south-facing slope):** the highest number of snails (average 46 per square foot), but only 11 species.
- **Station H (ungrazed grassland):** big bluestem prairie, less favorable for snails. Fewer than half the Reservation's species occurred there.
- **Station I (grazed meadow):** snails nearly absent after cattle removal in March 1949. A colony of [[Bulimulus dealbatus]] was found in a fenced weed patch.
- **Station J (limestone ledges):** exposed, arid summit habitat.

## Key Findings

**Species inventory and status**

- Four aquatic mollusks were recorded: *Physa hawni*, *Helisoma trivolvis lentum*, *Sphaerium* sp., and *Pisidium* cf. *compressum*. The authors state that all four can survive long periods of drought.
- Three terrestrial species were restricted to moist situations near water: *Succinea concordialis*, *Vertigo ovata*, and *Lymnaea parva*.
- Only *Bulimulus dealbatus* was found exclusively in open areas. All other species occurred on at least one wooded slope.
- The Reservation is near the northern limit for *Bulimulus dealbatus*, and on the edge of the range for *Gastrocopta procera* and other species.

**Dominance and abundance**

- Three species, [[Gastrocopta Armifera]], [[Retinella Indentata]], and [[Vallonia Parvula]], made up 60–80% of the molluscan population across most of the Reservation.
- [[Gastrocopta Armifera]] was the most widespread snail, found in every terrestrial habitat, and it averaged about eight per square foot in some areas of Station G.
- [[Vallonia Parvula]] was the most numerous terrestrial species, reaching an average of 19 per square foot at Station G.
- At Station G, [[Gastrocopta Armifera]], [[Vallonia Parvula]], and [[Retinella Indentata]] comprised 80% of the fauna.
- [[Gastrocopta Holzingeri]] was unusually common on Station F, at 30–35 per square foot.
- South- and west-facing slopes (SF, SG) averaged 30–50 specimens per square foot. North-facing slopes had more species, but fewer than five individuals per square foot in the poorly drained lower areas.

**Habitat associations**

- [[Strobilops Labyrinthica]] and [[Retinella Electrina]] are woodland snails tied to decaying logs and moist humus.
- [[Zonitoides Arboreus]] was common on north-facing slopes, but scarce where decaying logs were absent.
- [[Stenotrema Monodon Aliciae]] was most abundant around decaying logs on protected slopes.
- [[Deroceras Laeve]] was most abundant in poorly drained north-facing slopes.
- [[Hawaiia Minuscula]] was abundant only along the stream banks.
- [[Succinea Avara]] was restricted to moist habitats, but probably under-collected because its shell is often coated with dirt.

**Taxonomic notes**

- The authors conclude that the characters separating *Gastrocopta pentodon* and *G. tappaniana* are inadequate for specific rank, and they treat *G. tappaniana* as a synonym of *G. pentodon*.
- Two "forms" of *G. pentodon* (a broad ovoid and a slender elongate type) may merit subspecific status once more ecological data are available.

**Ecological and conservation points**

- Shell abundance in the pond (Station B) indicated a recent large population, but the pond was dry during the study period, so no living snails were recovered.
- The authors note that the scarcity of *Zonitoides arboreus* on many slopes probably reflects the lack of decaying logs.
- The authors identify the molluscan fauna as a baseline for detecting future shifts as the Reservation reverts to natural conditions.
- A grazed meadow held almost no snails, apart from a single [[Gastrocopta Procera]] under cow dung, which the authors note as the only occurrence in the meadow far from the timbered edge.
