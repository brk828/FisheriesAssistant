# Late Season Reproduction by Big-River Catostomidae in Grand Canyon (Arizona)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested for late-season spawning by endemic big-river catostomids at the confluence of the [[Colorado River]] and [[Havasu Creek]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]], Arizona, during late September and early October 1998. Ripe female [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*) and newly hatched fry confirmed late-season (early October) spawning by this species. Indirect evidence and a single possible larva suggested that [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered species not considered a constituent member of the endemic Grand Canyon fish community, or a hybrid with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*C. latipinnis*), may also have spawned. Flannelmouth Sucker abundance was significantly associated with [[Havasu Creek]] flows but not with [[Colorado River]] mainstem flows.

## Background & Description

Many freshwater fishes show a defined spawning period, and late spawning is generally thought to reflect individuals that missed the normal reproductive window. The authors note that late-season reproduction has been proposed as a bet-hedging mechanism against catastrophic early life-history mortality, but support for this idea is largely anecdotal. Late-season reproduction had not been verified for large-river endemic fishes of western North America prior to this work.

The study species belong to the [[Catostomidae]] (suckers). Key taxa are:
- **Flannelmouth Sucker** (*C. latipinnis*): the most abundant species captured.
- **Bluehead Sucker** (*C. discobolus*): females were observed expressing ova, and fry were collected.
- **Razorback Sucker** (*X. texanus*): a single larva was tentatively identified.
- **Humpback Chub** (*[[Gila cypha]]*): mentioned in comparative literature on spawning cues.

Havasu Creek is the second largest tributary in Grand Canyon, draining 782 km² of the Coconino Plateau. It is perennial from 19 km upstream and has travertine dams, pools, and waterfalls. Access from the river is obstructed above 400 m by natural travertine falls.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** The Colorado River was accessed at Lee's Ferry (river km 0) and Havasu Creek (rkm 262.4). Flows were obtained from the [[United States Geological Survey]] for Phantom Ranch (rkm 87.8) and Havasu Creek. Sampling used a 15.3 m × 1.8 m trammel net (inner mesh 2.5 cm, outer mesh 30 cm) fished in the alcove at the creek-river confluence from 2–20 October 1998 (18 sampling days; total effort 39.25 net hours). Big-river endemics over 150 mm TL were tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) tags. Population estimates were generated using the [[Cormack-Jolly-Seber]] open population model (POPAN-5), and relationships with flow and effort were tested with ANCOVA after Box-Cox-Bartlett transformation.

**Flows and environment.** Colorado River maximum September flows at Phantom Ranch averaged 30,582 m³/min (range 23,616–36,698), and October maxima averaged 26,192 m³/min (21,917–30,922). Havasu Creek flows averaged 97 m³/min in September and 102 m³/min in October. Water temperatures on 14 October were: Colorado River 16 °C, eddy at confluence 17 °C, Havasu Creek 18.5 °C, and the mainstem air temperature 27 °C.

**Regional spawning context.** Spawning cues for big-river fishes are linked to hydrograph shape. [[Humpback Chub]] spawn on the descending hydrograph in the Little Colorado River, while [[Razorback Sucker]] spawn on the ascending hydrograph. Monsoon-driven late-summer floods are a potential late-season cue in the lower basin. Havasu Creek flooding has been most frequent in summer, and some events were catastrophic.

## Key Findings

- **Late-season spawning confirmed for Bluehead Sucker.** Females expressed ova on 7 and 11 October 1998. On 9 October, unidentified fry were observed along the northwest wall of the Havasu Creek alcove. Seven were preserved; six were identified as *C. discobolus*.
- **Possible Razorback Sucker or hybrid larva.** The seventh larva had sparse pigmentation, little yolk for its size, and a well-developed gut. It was tentatively classified as *X. texanus* (?). Hybrid origin with *C. latipinnis* cannot be excluded. The authors advise that DNA analysis would be required for confirmation.
- **Catch composition.** Total captures comprised 102 *C. latipinnis* (137–540 mm TL, mean 4S), 16 *C. discobolus* (168–344 mm TL, mean 209), 11 [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*; 200–270 mm TL, mean 229, five with parr marks), one [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*; 286 mm TL), and one Humpback Chub (130 mm TL).
- **Population and tagging.** Of 102 adult *C. latipinnis*, 82 (80%) were untagged and 20 were recaptures. Population estimates were not calculated for two of 18 sampling periods due to lack of recaptures. Only 7.8% (n = 8) of tagged *C. latipinnis* were recaptured.
- **Flow relationships.** Colorado River daily flows (P > 0.86) and fishing effort (P > 0.87) were not significantly related to *C. latipinnis* population estimates. Havasu Creek flows were significantly related (F = 12.94, P < 0.005), while effort was not (P > 0.08). Total catches were not significantly related to riverine (P > 0.82) or creek flows (P > 0.31).
- **Spawning behaviour.** Many *C. latipinnis* had abraded anal fins, and 70% were tuberculate. Individuals were caught in close proximity at the bottom of the net. The authors interpret these observations as evidence of spawning on gravel beds at the Havasu Creek outflow.
- **Population decline context.** Estimates for *C. latipinnis* were eight- to tenfold lower than for a comparable period six to eight years earlier at the [[Little Colorado River]]. The authors suggest that Havasu Creek's watershed is only 6% the size of the Little Colorado River's, and its narrow, canyon-bound confluence may restrict fish access at low-to-moderate mainstem flows.
- **Management implications.** The authors argue that long-term monitoring of endemic fishes should focus on tributary confluences. The research framework for [[Grand Canyon]] management is dominated by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations, which leaves tributary influences underemphasized. Operation of Glen Canyon Dam has eliminated the historic spring hydrograph for more than 20 years.
- **Study limitations.** The authors state that the study duration was too short to support broad generalisations about aggregations and flow characteristics. Tubercles alone are insufficient to diagnose reproduction, and expressible ova in females are a positive indicator.
