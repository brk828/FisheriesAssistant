# Age and Growth Differentiation Between the Sexes of the Largemouth Black Bass, *Micropterus salmoides* (Lacepède)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 study, reprinted from the *Journal of the Tennessee Academy of Science*, tested the widely held view that nearly all large, old [[Largemouth Black Bass]] are female. It examined scales from 287 bass, 186 from [[Lake Auburn]] (drained in 1949) and 101 from [[Silver Lake]] (poisoned with [[Rotenone]] in 1949). Sex was determined from gonads. The author also developed a practical [[Waterglass-Glycerine Mount]] method for reading annuli with a [[Microprojector]]. The study found a differential growth rate between the sexes, particularly at Silver Lake, and clear differential mortality, with no males older than six years but females reaching nine years at Lake Auburn. It concluded that females survive better under adverse conditions and that the life span of bass was shorter where growth conditions were poor.

## Background & Description

The paper is a thesis-derived study written by James H. Paprie, Jr., of the Tennessee State Game and Fish Commission, Jackson, Tennessee. It was submitted to the Graduate Faculty of the Alabama Polytechnic Institute, Auburn, in partial fulfillment of a Master of Science in Fish Management (March 16, 1950).

The stated objectives were:
1. Develop a more practical method than those in use for preparing temporary scale mounts.
2. Determine sex differences in (a) growth in length, (b) growth in weight, (c) periods of fastest growth, and (d) longevity.

The introduction reviews earlier work. [[Bennett]] (1937) suspected differential growth in Wisconsin bass. [[Stroud]] (1948) reported similar growth of the sexes in [[Norris Reservoir]] through age three and found only a female in the oldest (seven-year) group. [[Eschmeyer]] (1939) reported only slightly faster male growth. The review also covers related centrarchids and other fishes, such as [[Hubbs and Cooper]] (1935) on centrarchid males growing faster, [[Van Oosten]] (1937) on the longjaw, [[Spoor]] (1938) on the white sucker, [[Raney and Lachner]] (1946) on the hog sucker, and [[Hile]] (1936) on the cisco. Across these taxa, the author notes a common theme: slow growth tends to correlate with longer life, and females often outlive males.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Auburn (Alabama):** A private club-owned, artificial impoundment about four miles south of [[Auburn, Alabama]], near Chewacla State Park. It covers 12 acres, was built in 1931, and was stocked in 1932 as a lake-management experiment. A dam washout in 1938 wiped out the population, and the lake was restocked after repairs. It was fertilized and managed by the Farm Ponds Department. In 1949 it was drained to restart management. The standing crop was 199.8 pounds of fish per acre. Other species included [[Bluegill]], white crappie, yellow bullhead, and red-eared sunfish.

**Silver Lake (Georgia):** A natural, 50-acre lake owned by the International Paper Company on Southlands Plantation near Bainbridge, Georgia. It formed about twenty years earlier from overflow of the [[Flint River]] and had never been managed for fish production. Fishing had declined, so it was treated with rotenone in 1949 to remove the population before restocking. The estimated standing crop was 61 pounds per acre, of which 61% was non-sport fish (buffalo, gar, carp, and shad) and 39% sport fish (bluegill, crappie, pickerel, largemouth bass, and speckled bullhead).

The contrast between a managed, fertilized impoundment and an unmanaged natural lake is central to the paper's interpretation of growth and longevity differences.

## Key Findings

### Materials and Methods
- **Sample:** 287 largemouth bass: 186 from Lake Auburn (collected on draining, September 27, 1949) and 101 from Silver Lake (collected after poisoning, October 15, 1949).
- **Measurements:** Standard and total lengths (millimeters, later converted to inches), weights (pounds and ounces), and sex from gonads. All specimens were mature.
- **Scale sampling:** Scales taken from the left side, between the dorsal and pectoral fins, above or below the lateral line. A dozen or more were usually removed per fish.
- **Mounting:** The simple moist-scale compression method failed on large scales, so the author used [[Syracuse Watch Glass]] mounts in a waterglass-glycerine solution. The solution was 60 g sodium silicate in 100 cc boiled water, filtered, then mixed with 20 cc glycerine and 40 cc of the solution. Doubling the glycerine reduced scale shifting, and this became the method used for all remaining scales.
- **Age determination and back-calculation:** Annuli were measured on the projected image and standard lengths at each annulus were back-calculated using the [[Scale Method]] formula, which assumes a constant ratio between scale-field growth and body growth.

### Growth in Length
- **Silver Lake:** Females grew at a higher rate than males in every year except the sixth. Males and females grew at similar rates through the first few age groups. The author attributes the exception to small sample size.
- **Lake Auburn:** Females grew slightly faster than males in age-groups I, II, and VI. Groups III to V showed little difference.
- **Between lakes:** Lake Auburn females were longer than Silver Lake females in each age group through group V. Above group V, Silver Lake females were longer. The largest Lake Auburn female averaged 19.5 inches at the end of year nine, while the largest Silver Lake female reached 19.4 inches in seven years.
- **Interpretation:** More food may have been available to young Lake Auburn females, possibly due to management.

### Growth in Weight
- Males and females at each location gained weight at about the same rate per unit of length, so a single length-weight curve was drawn for each lake ([[Length-Weight Relationship]] figures).
- At Lake Auburn, males and females were of similar weight across age groups.
- At Silver Lake, females were heavier than males in each age group.
- Length increased more in the first year than the second, but weight gains showed the reverse pattern.

### Differential Mortality and Sex Ratio
- **Lake Auburn:** Males dominated age-groups II to V. Females dominated groups I and VI to IX. No males appeared in groups VII to IX. The oldest male was six years old and the oldest female was nine.
- **Silver Lake:** Males dominated groups III and IV. Females dominated groups V to VII. The oldest male was six years old and the oldest female was seven.
- **Longevity by environment:** Fourteen percent of Lake Auburn males were in age-group V, compared with four percent of Silver Lake males. Females in both lakes lived longer than males, and only two percent of the Lake Auburn sample were in group IX.

### Supporting Literature Cited in the Study
- [[Gentry]] (1949) examined 167 bass weighing 3 to 12 pounds. Males made up 15% of fish of 3 to 5 pounds and 5% of fish of 5 to 7 pounds, and no males were found above 7 pounds.

### Summary Conclusions
1. The scale mounting method described is practical and recommended for age determination in other fishes.
2. A differential growth rate existed between the sexes, with females relatively longer in each age group. This was most evident under poor growth conditions and only slight under good conditions.
3. Weight increased at similar rates per unit length in both sexes. Silver Lake females were heavier each year, while Lake Auburn females were only slightly heavier.
4. Differential mortality occurred, with more females in the upper age groups. No male exceeded six years, while females reached nine, supporting the belief that most old, large bass are female.
5. Bass lived shorter lives at Silver Lake, where growth conditions were poor, than at Lake Auburn, where conditions were favorable.
6. Females appear better suited to survive adverse conditions. Under poor growth conditions, males grew more slowly and died at a higher rate. Under favorable conditions, males grew at about the same rate as females, but still had higher mortality in the upper age groups.
7. Faster-growing individuals of each sex lived longer than slower-growing ones.

### Significance
The study provides early evidence that old, large largemouth bass are predominantly female, and links this to both growth environment and sex-specific mortality. Its scale-mounting technique and back-calculation approach are presented as methodological contributions for fisheries management.
