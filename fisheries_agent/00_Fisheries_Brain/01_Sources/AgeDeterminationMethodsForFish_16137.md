# Age-Determination Methods for Fishes Studied by the Groundfish Program at the Pacific Biological Station

#ichthyology #marine-biology #groundfish-aging #otoliths #fin-rays

## Executive Summary

This Canadian Special Publication (Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences 60, 1982) is a laboratory manual by [[Doris E. Chilton]] and [[Richard J. Beamish]] of the Pacific Biological Station, Nanaimo, B.C. It describes methods and equipment for estimating the ages of groundfish species off Canada's west coast. Procedures cover annuli identification on [[Scales]], [[Otoliths]], [[Fin-Ray Sections]], and [[Spines]], with species-specific protocols for 17 species across seven families. The authors stress that the manual is preliminary, that many methods are unvalidated, and that accuracy cannot be assumed without [[Validation Methods|validation]] for all age-classes. The manual's central finding is that many groundfish, especially [[Rockfish]], are far older than previously believed.

## Background & Description

The manual is written for fisheries biologists and aging laboratories. Its introduction argues that accurate aging is essential to understanding fish population dynamics. Its general procedures recommend treating every stock as if aged for the first time, comparing several structures, and examining juvenile fish to locate the first annulus.

Aging structures used in the laboratory:
- **Scales**: [[Ctenoid Scales]] (spiny-rayed fish) and [[Cycloid Scales]] (soft-rayed fish); flatfish carry both. Scales are unreliable for older fish.
- **Otoliths** (sagittae, the largest of three otolith pairs): read as whole surfaces, transverse sections, or broken and burnt halves. Otoliths are considered the best permanent growth record because bone resorption does not occur.
- **Fin-ray sections**: dorsal, pectoral, and pelvic rays, sectioned on a [[Bronwill Sectioning Machine]] and embedded in epoxy.
- **Spines**: second dorsal spine of spiny dogfish.

Other bones (cleithra, opercula, interopercula) are not used. Equipment includes the Bronwill sectioning machine, a Buehler isomet low-speed saw, an Eberbach microprojector, microfiche reader-printers, CCTV/video microscopy, and Wild stereomicroscopes and compound microscopes.

Annuli are defined as translucent (hyaline) zones formed during slow growth, with opaque zones representing summer growth. Ages are recorded with a January 1 birthdate convention and a notation for partial-year growth; for example, 3(4) means age 3 with the fourth year's growth complete.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Growth patterns are linked to annual growth cycles, and changes in growth after maturity can alter structure appearance. Several ecological features affect aging:
- Growth slows after maturity, especially in males, causing crowded annuli and underestimated ages.
- Exploitation may alter the appearance of the first annulus among stocks.
- Some species show distinct geographic and stock-level differences (e.g., Pacific hake in the Strait of Georgia versus offshore stocks; Pacific ocean perch among geographic areas and sexes).
- Sablefish larvae are believed to remain at hatching depths of about 500 m for the first ~100 days, then move toward surface waters; juveniles of 5–15 cm were seen in surface waters of Queen Charlotte Sound in August.
- Dover sole spend their first year pelagically, possibly contributing to a poorly defined first annulus.
- Checks are non-annual marks linked to environmental or physiological changes.

## Key Findings

**Methodological principles**
- Age determinations must be validated for all age-classes, for each species, and often for each stock. The authors call failure to validate "one of the most serious mistakes" in fisheries biology.
- Validation relies on mark-recapture studies, including injection of [[Oxytetracycline]] (OTC) into fish before release, which fluoresces under UV light.
- Where validation is absent, ages should be considered rough approximations, and multiple structures should be compared.
- Precision is measured with an "average percent error" index (Beamish and Fournier 1981). Percent agreement alone is misleading because a ±1 yr error means different things for short- and long-lived species.
- Scales should not be used for older fish; they tend to underestimate age beyond maturity.

**Species-specific protocols and findings**
- **[[Sablefish]]**: Earlier studies underestimated age using scales and otolith surfaces. Thin and burnt otolith sections suggested fish could be 40–50 yr old. OTC tagging validated annual zones. Daily increments were studied with acetate impressions; the first annulus was placed after ~275–300 daily increments.
- **[[Pacific Cod]]**: Otoliths were judged of little use, and the scale method was suspended in 1978 because it likely underaged older fish. Second dorsal and pectoral fin rays gave similar ages for young fish and appear more reliable for older fish. Length-frequency analysis may suit younger fish.
- **[[Pacific Hake]]**: Surface otolith ages underaged older and thicker otoliths. The authors routinely section or break and burn all hake otoliths. Breaking and burning is reported to be as clear as sectioning and less time-consuming, but sections remain necessary for difficult or very old otoliths.
- **[[Walleye Pollock]]**: Pectoral fin-ray sections proved most useful; otoliths were obscured by annuli problems in fish larger than ~35 cm. Sections should be cut 0.6–1.0 mm from the base.
- **[[Lingcod]]**: Cross sections of the 4th–8th second dorsal fin rays provide an aging method (published 1977). Scale ages are acceptable to about age 5–6 and may underestimate age by up to 8 yr thereafter.
- **[[Flatfish]] (Pleuronectidae)**: Surface readings are satisfactory to about 7 yr; breaking and burning, or thin sections, is recommended for older fish. Flatfish otoliths are thickened mainly by internal deposition.
- **[[Arrowtooth Flounder]]**: Surface reading works for the first few years; sections are needed for older fish.
- **[[Pacific Halibut]]**: Preliminary results suggest very old halibut may be underestimated by surface reading. Study ongoing with the International Pacific Halibut Commission.
- **[[Rock Sole]]**: Surface ages suit young fish; cross sections are needed for older fish. Growth zones gradually narrow after about 6 yr.
- **[[Dover Sole]]**: Scales gave the lowest ages and are difficult to sample; cross sections gave much older ages than surface readings. Surface ages are satisfactory to 7–8 yr.
- **[[English Sole]]**: Burnt cross sections were used for older individuals, with maximum age reported as 21 yr.
- **[[Albacore]]**: Dorsal and anal fin rays were most satisfactory. The method is rapid but not validated; the first annulus is unconfirmed and checks complicate counts.
- **[[Rockfish]] (Scorpaenidae)**: Surface ages may be accurate only to about 15–20 yr (Beamish 1979b). Thin and burnt sections indicate ages of 80–140 yr. Breaking and burning is now routine. Scales should not be used.
  - [[Pacific Ocean Perch]]: Uniform growth zones on the internal surface indicate very old age.
  - [[Silvergray Rockfish]]: Burnt sections show clear annuli; surfaces develop fingerlike projections after 12–15 yr.
  - [[Yellowtail Rockfish]]: Large first-year growth; the first annulus lies far from the nucleus.
  - [[Canary Rockfish]]: One otolith was aged at >30 yr; surface counting is advised from nucleus down the dorsal side.
  - [[Yellowmouth Rockfish]]: Surface ages are reliable only to roughly 25–30 yr.
- **[[Spiny Dogfish]]**: Second dorsal spine is used. About 2000 spines are aged each year. Worn tips require a Ketchen (1975) correction curve. An OTC-tagged fish at liberty for 10 months showed new growth beyond the mark. Maximum age reported as 66 yr.

**Maximum ages (Table 1)**
The manual lists maximum ages for 25 species. Notable values include rougheye rockfish 140 yr (described as one of the oldest recorded ages for any fish), shortraker rockfish 120 yr, Pacific ocean perch 90 yr, sablefish 53 yr, and spiny dogfish 66 yr. The authors report that 43% of species range from 50 to over 100 yr, 48% from 29 to 49 yr, and only 9% have maximum ages under 20 yr.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Prior assumptions that most fish live 5–15 yr likely reflected scale-based reading errors and unsupported interpretations of checks.
- Extreme longevity is supported by OTC validation for some groups and by natural radionuclide (lead-210/radium-226) measurements in rockfish otoliths (Bennett et al. 1982).
- Failure to recognize long lifespans has important management consequences, particularly for exploitation strategies (Leaman and Beamish 1982).
- Aging should never be taken for granted; all techniques are qualitative to some degree and require ongoing evaluation.
- The manual is a preliminary draft to be revised as validation proceeds.
