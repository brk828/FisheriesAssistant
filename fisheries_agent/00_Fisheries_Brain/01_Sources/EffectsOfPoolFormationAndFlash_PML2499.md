# Effects of Pool Formation and Flash Flooding on Young-of-Year Flannelmouth Sucker in the Paria River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines why [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) recruit poorly to adult size in the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The authors hypothesized that a lack of persistent warm rearing areas for [[Young-Of-Year Fish]] (YOY) limits recruitment. They studied the mouth of the [[Paria River]], a tributary of the Colorado River, during 1996 and 1997, and analyzed catch data from 1991 to 1996. Two factors emerged: flash flooding in the Paria River during the critical rearing period was strongly associated with low YOY abundance, and [[Pool Formation]] in the tributary mouth was associated with YOY rearing and with successful year-classes. The authors conclude that recruitment may be improved by enhancing pool formation, and they note that pool formation cannot be predicted from Colorado River flows alone.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] is endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin in the western United States. Its range has contracted in the lower basin, where it has been extirpated from the lower Salt, San Pedro and Gila rivers. In the upper basin, hybridization with [[White Sucker]] (*C. commersoni*) threatens its genetic purity. The species was listed as a "species of concern" in 1991.
- **Recruitment concern:** Surveys indicate that the species remains relatively common in [[Grand Canyon]]. However, few YOY (<90 mm total length) and sub-adults (90–360 mm) have been captured near the Paria River, and mean length of spawning adults rose significantly between 1981 and 1992–1993, suggesting low recruitment to age 1 or spawning size.
- **Temperature limitation:** Hypolimnetic releases from Glen Canyon Dam keep the river cold. Water is clear and held at about 8–12 °C, compared with historical seasonal ranges of 2–26 °C. Cold water limits larval growth and swimming performance. Larval flannelmouth suckers reared at 10 °C were about 20 mm long at 90 days, versus about 40 mm at 20 °C (Clarkson and Childs, 2000).
- **Study area:** The lower 6 km of the Paria River and its confluence with the Colorado River, about 25 km below Glen Canyon Dam. The Paria River drains 3730 km² from the Pink Cliffs of southern Utah. Its mean instantaneous discharge is 0.77 m³/s, its mean annual flood is 88 m³/s, and its lower 16 km are perennial and spring-fed. The stream is uniformly shallow (<0.3 m deep) and channel width is <20 m at base flow. Flash floods carry heavy sediment loads into the Colorado River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tributary mouths as rearing habitat.** Tributary mouths provide YOY rearing areas in other systems. In the Colorado River, high discharge can create large pools in tributary mouths. An experimental flood in 1996 (about 1274 m³/s) created a pool more than 600 m long at the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]]. Clarkson et al. (1994) suggested sustained high spring flows to impound tributary mouths so that YOY native fishes would be retained in warmer water.

**Effects of dam operations.** Glen Canyon Dam has eliminated the high spring flows that historically impounded tributary mouths for weeks at a time. Daily flow fluctuations (142–849 m³/s) now dewater and inundate nearshore areas and backwaters. Flows exceeding 2265 m³/s occurred only in 1984 and 1985 after dam closure in 1963. Thus, tributary mouths are less likely to be impounded now, reducing rearing areas for [[Larval Fish]] and juveniles. Similar losses of nursery habitat were documented in the Sinnamary River (French Guiana) after the [[Petit-Saut Dam]] was built, where tributary-mouth velocities rose and fish richness and abundance fell.

**Sediment dynamics.** Pool formation depends on Colorado River flow, Paria River bed elevation, and antecedent sediment deposition. Prolonged high Colorado River flows can act as a dam, impounding the Paria mouth and depositing sediment until flash floods flush it out. Historically, spring impoundment was followed by summer monsoon and winter frontal flash floods that scoured the mouth.

**Thermal and size constraints.** Warmer mainstem reaches, such as western Grand Canyon (about 14–16 °C in May–June), hold more YOY flannelmouth suckers. Clarkson and Childs (2000) found that very young larvae enter cold coma within 5–10 seconds when moved from 20 °C to 10 °C water. Older YOY tolerate the transition better. For [[Humpback Chub]], Valdez and Ryel (1995) estimated a minimum survival size of 52 mm TL.

## Key Findings

### 1. Abundance and growth (1996–1997)

- A total of 576 YOY were captured in 1996, with the majority from the impounded Paria mouth. [[Catch-Per-Unit-Effort]] (CPUE) was significantly higher at the mouth than at upstream Paria sites (RM ANOVA, Wilks' Lambda = 21L.34, df = 1,7, p < 0.0002).
- YOY grew about 0.52 mm/day (R² = 0.78, F = 1002.1, df = 2, p < 0.0001).
- In 1996, YOY averaged 47 mm (S.D. = 5.02, n = 160) on 19 June, and the mean temperature in the mouth ranged from 16 to 32 °C while YOY were present.
- In 1997, no YOY were caught in June, but sampling was ineffective because of silt in the mouth. Two adults (85 and 88 mm) were caught in February 1997, presumably from the 1996 year-class. On 13 May 1997, YOY averaged 16.9 mm (S.E. = 3.14, n = 19).

### 2. Pool formation in the Paria mouth

- **1996:** High Colorado River flows (never below 336 m³/s from 9 April to 15 July) inundated the mouth. A slackwater area fluctuated between 20 m and more than 200 m long and was present from May to mid-July. Flows in the Paria River were low (mean 0.16 m³/s).
- **1997:** Steady high Colorado flows (566–736 m³/s) from February to June inundated the mouth initially, creating a pool about 150–200 m long and up to 1 m deep. Suspended sediment then deposited 0.5–1.0 m in the mouth, and the pool was completely filled by May. On 13 May 1997, despite Colorado flows of 566 m³/s, no pool formed. A pool formed on 14 June 1997 after flows rose to 737 m³/s.
- The authors conclude that pool formation cannot be inferred from Colorado River flow alone.

### 3. Flash floods and recruitment (1991–1996)

- Maximum daily Paria discharge during 15 March–30 June was inversely correlated with mean YOY CPUE (Spearman's rho = −0.9856, p = 0.0003).
- Years with flash floods during the critical rearing period (1992, 1993, 1995) had low YOY abundance. CPUE was highest in 1994 (14.5 fish/100 m²) and 1996 (4.1 fish/100 m²), when flash floods were absent.
- In 1994, a pool apparently formed at the mouth on 15 June, when the depth was 0.36 m compared with 0.09 m upstream.
- Sub-adults captured in 1996 included 18 individuals, of which 78% were presumed to be from the 1994 year-class based on size (151–215 mm) and capture month. The Conclusions section instead cites 14 sub-adults. The last sub-adult previously recorded near the Paria River was captured in October 1984.

### 4. Critical rearing period

- The critical rearing period was defined as 15 March–30 June, based on spawning in the Paria River (15 March–15 April) and YOY presence in the [[Little Colorado River]] through June.
- The authors suggest the end of June is a reasonable endpoint, because YOY are then large enough to move into the mainstem.

### 5. Management implications

- Managers cannot control flash flooding, so the focus should be on pool formation.
- The authors recommend a high Colorado River flow during the critical rearing period, combined with lower flows when sediment input is high (summer monsoons and winter fronts).
- An alternative is an artificial pool at the Paria mouth.
- A [[Multi-Level Intake Structure]] could warm Colorado River water released from Glen Canyon Dam in spring and summer. The authors caution that warmer releases could favor non-native fishes and expand the range of parasites found in endangered fishes.

### 6. Conclusions

- A strong YOY year-class formed in 1996 because no flash floods occurred and an impounded rearing area formed at the mouth.
- Flash floods during the rearing period appear to prevent virtually any recruitment.
- Recruitment may be enhanced by pool formation at the tributary mouth. Further monitoring is needed to clarify the relative roles of flash flooding and pool formation.
