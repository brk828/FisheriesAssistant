# A Brief History of Ecology: Part I, Pre-Nineteenth Century to 1919

#ecology-history #limnology #history-of-science

## Executive Summary

This occasional paper by [[Richard Brewer]] (Department of Biology, [[Western Michigan University]]) is the first part of a chronological survey of the development of [[Ecology]] from antiquity to 1919. It was published on November 22, 1960, as Number 1 of the *Occasional Papers of the [[C. C. Adams Center for Ecological Studies]]* (pp. 1-18). The paper argues that ecology did not emerge as a distinct discipline until the late nineteenth century, although observations of organism-environment relationships date to a Chinese calendar of 700 B.C. It traces the field through European plant geography, the founding of [[Limnology]] and [[Oceanography]], and the American schools of [[Chicago School of Ecology]] and [[University of Illinois Ecology]], ending with the founding of ecological societies and journals in 1913-1920.

## Background & Description

- **Scope and method:** Treatment is chronological, except where tracing an investigator's career or a concept's development was more appropriate. No person whose first ecological paper appeared after 1949 is included. Omissions are acknowledged as the result of author bias and incomplete knowledge.
- **Periods covered:** Pre-nineteenth century; 1800-1890; 1890-1909; 1910-1919.
- **Sources:** The bibliography lists about 150 works, several examined only secondhand (marked "ne.").
- **Acknowledgments:** The author credits courses and conversations with [[W. C. Allee]]-era figures such as [[V. E. Shelford]], [[S. C. Kendeigh]], and [[A. G. Vestal]], and manuscript comments from several ecologists, including [[R. H. Whittaker]].

Key early contributors noted in the paper include:

- [[Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon]] (1742, 1749-1769): demonstrated knowledge of forest succession.
- [[Thomas Malthus]] (1798): stated the tendency of populations to increase geometrically, with some understanding of food and competition as limits.
- [[Alexander von Humboldt]] (1805): described vegetation by growth form rather than floristically.
- [[August Grisebach]] (1838): coined the term "formation" and is considered the first to note the integrated nature of plant communities.
- [[Eugenius Warming]] (1895): *Plantesamfund*, considered the first ecological textbook.
- [[Andreas Schimper]] (1898): *Pflanzengeographie auf physiologischer Grundlage*.
- [[Ernst Haeckel]] (1869/1870): credited with coining "ecology" and defining it as the body of knowledge on an organism's total relations with its organic and inorganic environment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper traces several conceptual threads that shaped modern ecology:

- **Succession and climax:** Early recognition of succession by King (1685), Dureau de la Malle (1825), and Steenstrup (1842), followed by [[Frederic Clements]]'s monoclimax concept and [[Henry Cowles]]'s work on Lake Michigan dunes and Chicago's physiography (1899, 1901).
- **Uniformitarianism:** [[Charles Darwin]] and [[Charles Lyell]] are described as central to its assimilation into science; the paper states that uniformitarianism "is basic to most ecological reasoning."
- **Animal communities:** [[Karl Möbius]] (1877) proposed *biocoenosis* for interacting animal communities. [[Karl Semper]] (1881) described pyramids of biomass and numbers.
- **Plankton and quantitative methods:** [[Victor Hensen]] (1887) introduced the term *plankton* and quantitative sampling; [[C. G. J. Petersen]] (1889-1893) applied quantitative methods to bottom fauna.
- **Limnology:** [[François-Alphonse Forel]] (1869; *Le Léman* monograph, 1892-1904) established limnology as a branch of science. [[Edward Birge]] and [[Chancey Juday]] worked on thermoclines and dissolved gases in Wisconsin lakes.
- **Paleoecology:** Peat-horizon studies by Steenstrup and [[Carl Vaupell]], and pollen analysis by [[Gunnar Lagerheim]] and [[Lennart von Post]] (first applied in 1916).
- **Population ecology:** [[Leland Howard]] and [[William Fiske]] (1911, gypsy moth) distinguished catastrophic from facultative (density-dependent) mortality, anticipating later categories.

## Key Findings

1. **Antiquity of ecological observation:** Phenological records and Aristotelian and Theophrastean classifications contained ecological elements long before ecology was a discipline.
2. **Origin of the name and early definition:** Haeckel's 1869 coinage was defined in 1870; the term reached general literary use only near the end of the nineteenth century.
3. **Founders of plant ecology:** Warming and Schimper, who assumed plant distribution depends on environment, are considered the most direct intellectual ancestors of Cowles and Clements.
4. **Forbes's ecosystem thinking:** [[Stephen Forbes]] published more than 20 papers in 1880-1890. His "On some interactions of organisms" (1880) and "The lake as a microcosm" (1887) addressed predator-prey relationships and an ecosystem-like concept. He is credited with introducing stomach analysis for food habit studies. The paper notes his influence on limnology and economic entomology was greater than on ecology proper.
5. **Fish physiology:** [[Shelford]] and [[W. C. Allee]] (1913), "The reactions of fishes to gradients of dissolved atmospheric gases" (*J. Exper. Zool.*, 14: 207-266), was an early paper in Shelford's response-ecology series. Allee's later work on animal aggregations and sociality grew from this research.
6. **Founding of societies:** The [[British Ecological Society]] was formed in 1913, with Tansley as first president, and produced the *Journal of Ecology*. The [[Ecological Society of America]] was organized in 1915 (Shelford first president). Its journal *Ecology* was planned in 1919 and initiated in 1920, with [[B. Moore]] as first editor.
7. **Community concept:** Vestal (1914) argued that plant and animal assemblages are "coextensive parts of a biotic association," forming the real terrestrial community.
8. **Critiques of key systems:** Merriam's life-zone system (1894), though widely adopted, was judged unsound because of its single-factor approach. Cooper (1926) argued that a vegetation unit is an organism only "in the figurative sense."
9. **Ecosystem concept:** [[William S. Cooper]]'s unfinished textbook planned a chapter titled "Community and environment: the ecosystem," showing early awareness of the concept within the Cowles lineage.
10. **Authorship limits:** The paper explicitly excludes investigators whose first ecological paper appeared after 1949 and states that its omissions reflect author bias and incomplete knowledge, inviting corrections.
