# Virgin Spinedace Listing Withdrawal and Whooping Crane Experimental Population Rule (Federal Register, February 6, 1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register proposed rule, issued by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Service) on February 6, 1996, withdraws two earlier proposals concerning the [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*), a small cyprinid endemic to the [[Virgin River Drainage]] of the southwestern United States. The May 18, 1994 proposal to list the species as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and the April 5, 1995 proposed critical habitat designation (shared with the [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin River Chub]]) are both withdrawn. The decision rests on the [[Virgin Spinedace Conservation Agreement]], signed by eight parties in 1995, which is expected to prevent the species from becoming endangered in the foreseeable future. The species is retained as a "species of concern" pending evaluation. The document also contains a second, unrelated rule amending 50 CFR 17.95(b) to establish a nonessential experimental population of [[Whooping Crane]] in Florida's Kissimmee Prairie.

## Background & Description

The Virgin spinedace belongs to a unique, endemic tribe of western cyprinids, the Plagopterini. Adults measure 80–120 mm (3–5 in), with a broad, flat silvery body and brassy sheen. They inhabit clear, cool streams with pools, runs, and riffles, often near undercut banks, debris, or boulders, and feed primarily on aquatic insects. Its historical distribution is poorly documented, but it was once common to abundant in Virgin River tributaries and in some mainstem reaches above Pah Tempe (La Verkin) Springs near Hurricane, Utah. It was less abundant in the mainstem below those springs, except near the mouths of Quail Creek and Beaver Dam Wash.

Petitions to list the species were received from the [[Bonneville Chapter of the American Fisheries Society]] (June 15, 1992) and the [[Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance]] (August 17, 1992). The Service published a 90-day finding on March 16, 1993, that the petitions presented substantial information. The proposed threatened listing followed on May 18, 1994.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat loss.** Approximately 37–40 percent (83 km or 52 mi) of historical habitat has been lost to human impacts, including introduced nonnative fishes, agricultural dewatering, mining, and urban development. About 60 percent of the loss occurred after the 1950s and is linked to dams and diversions. Another cited estimate holds that about 68 percent of loss occurred in the last 45 years. Dewatered reaches include parts of the East Fork of Beaver Dam Wash, the Santa Clara River below Gunlock Reservoir, Ash Creek near Toquerville, and the mainstem Virgin River between Quail Creek Diversion and Pah Tempe Springs.

**Nonnative species and parasites.** Introduced fishes have contributed to the decline of native fish. The [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), introduced in 1986, weakens hosts and makes them vulnerable to low water levels, though it has not yet been confirmed in Virgin spinedace. An unidentified parasite was found in Ash Creek fish.

**Grazing.** Livestock grazing has declined since the 1930s–1940s, but 18,000 head of cattle remained in Washington County alone. Valdez et al. (1991) identified 10 of 13 populations as potentially threatened by riparian grazing and cattle feedlot runoff.

**Drought and regulation.** Drought dewaters reaches and isolates populations, and artificial barriers now prevent fish from retreating to refuge waters. The species is listed as state endangered in Utah and Arizona and state protected in Nevada.

## Key Findings

- **Listing withdrawn:** The Service withdrew the proposed threatened listing and the critical habitat designation for the Virgin spinedace, concluding that the Agreement, when fully implemented, will prevent the five listing factors from making the species endangered in the foreseeable future.
- **Agreement provisions:** The Agreement, developed by the Utah Department of Natural Resources with multiple agencies, aims to reestablish about 39 km (24 mi) of historical habitat, restore at least 50 percent of lost habitat, maintain instream flows (including 5 km / 3 mi below Quail Creek Diversion and about 31 km / 19 mi on the Santa Clara River), selectively control nonnative fishes, and enhance riparian zones through fencing and grazing management.
- **Species of concern:** Until the Agreement's success is evaluated, the species is considered a species of concern. If the Agreement fails, it will be reevaluated for listing.
- **Peer review:** Three independent fish specialists all supported the threatened listing and found the listing package biologically supportable.
- **Public comment:** Of 41 comments (1 federal agency, 4 state agencies, 11 city or county governments, 22 private individuals or groups, 3 scientific representatives), 8 supported listing, 32 opposed, and 1 was neutral. A public hearing was held in St. George, Utah, on July 13, 1994, with 19 parties testifying.
- **Issues addressed:** The Service responded to 16 issues, including critical habitat timing, habitat loss, climatic causes, water-rights effects, distribution in Kane County, dewatering, livestock grazing, and economic objections. The Service stated that listing would not have modified water rights and that Section 10(a)(1)(B) permits can address incidental take.
- **Mitigation:** Future projects affecting occupied habitat will generally require one-to-one replacement of historical habitat.
- **Whooping crane rule:** The companion rule amends 50 CFR 17.95(b) to establish a nonessential experimental population of [[Whooping Crane]] in the entire State of Florida, with the release site on the Kissimmee Prairie portions of Polk, Highlands, and Okeechobee counties. The Service expects the population to remain within the prairie region of central Florida, and it notes that no other extant populations come into contact with it. The rule also amends critical habitat maps for the whooping crane in Idaho, Colorado, and New Mexico.
