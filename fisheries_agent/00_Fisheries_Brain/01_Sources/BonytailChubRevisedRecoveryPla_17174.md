# Bonytail Chub Revised Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila

## Executive Summary

This revised [[Recovery Plan]] for the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) was prepared by the Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service), Region 6. The species was listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] on April 23, 1980. The original plan was approved May 16, 1984. The species is very rare: few individuals have been found in the last decade, and recruitment is apparently nonexistent or extremely low. The largest known concentration is in [[Lake Mohave]]. Decline has been attributed to stream alteration by dams, flow depletion from irrigation and other uses, hybridization with other *Gila*, and introduction of nonnative fishes.

The short-term goal is to prevent extinction. Long-term goals (downlisting and delisting criteria) will be set once the immediate threat is removed. The plan's major actions are: establishing a genetically diverse captive population for reintroduction; obtaining life history and habitat information; resolving the taxonomy of the Colorado River chubs; and developing quantitative recovery goals and a habitat protection strategy.

## Background & Description

**Listing and priority.** Critical habitat was not designated at listing because it could not be defined. The recovery priority is ranked 5C, indicating high threat, low recovery potential, and conflict with economic activity.

**Nomenclature.** "Bonytail" is the accepted common name (Robins et al. 1980), but "bonytail chub" is used in the plan because biologists in the basin commonly use it. The original description was by [[Baird and Girard]] (1853). The type locality was given as the Zuni River, New Mexico, but Smith et al. (1979) suggested the specimens came from the Little Colorado River at Grand Falls.

**Morphology.** Adults are gray or olive on the back with silvery sides and a white belly. Breeding males show bright red-orange lateral slashes and head tubercles, which are more subdued in females. Typical total length is 300–350 mm (12–14 in.), with specimens up to 600 mm (24 in.) recorded from Lakes Mohave and Havasu. The body is elongated and laterally compressed with a narrow caudal peduncle. Key diagnostic traits:

- Dorsal and anal fin rays usually 10 and 10 (vs. 9–9 in [[Roundtail Chub]] and 9–10 in [[Humpback Chub]]).
- Gill rakers on the anterior row of the second arch usually 18 (range 15–21), vs. 15 (13–17) in humpback and 13 (12–15) in roundtail chub.
- Head length to caudal peduncle depth ratio generally greater than five (under five in roundtail and humpback chubs).
- The nuchal hump rises smoothly from a concave skull, unlike the more abrupt hump of the humpback chub.

The snout-overhang character once proposed for separating bonytail from humpback chub was tested by Karp and Tyus (1989) and found not useful. Field identification of juveniles is often "tentative," and identification within the *Gila* complex remains a problem where the three species are sympatric and hybridize (artificial bonytail × roundtail and bonytail × humpback crosses have been produced).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic distribution.** Records span the Colorado and Gila rivers, the Green River (from southern Wyoming through Dinosaur National Monument, Desolation and Gray Canyons, and Utah), the San Juan, Gunnison, Salt, and Verde rivers, and the Gulf of California. The species entered the Salton Sea basin during 1905–07 but disappeared when salinity became intolerable. It was reported abundant in the late 1800s, then declined in the Lower Basin, disappearing from the Salt and upper Gila rivers before 1926 and absent from the Yuma area by 1950.

**Present distribution.** In the Lower Basin, individuals are occasionally caught in [[Lake Havasu]] and [[Lake Mohave]], where 32 adults were collected from 1974 to 1987 and 16 more in 1988–1989. No successful reproduction has been documented there. In the Upper Basin, recent captures are sparse:

- [[Dinosaur National Monument]]: 91 specimens collected 1962–1966; no adults reported 1981–1983.
- [[Cataract Canyon]]: suspected bonytails captured in 1985 (two), 1986 (one), and 1987 (two), including juveniles.
- [[Desolation Canyon]] and Gray Canyon: isolated adults and one juvenile reported in the 1970s; one tentative bonytail among 523 *Gila* captured 1982–1985.
- A single adult was taken from [[Lake Powell]] area waters in 1985.

**Habitat and behavior.** Bonytail are big-river, mainstem species. Adults occupy pools and eddies rather than fast current. Spawning has not been observed in a river; extrapolation from ripe fish suggests late June to early July at about 18°C (64°F) in Dinosaur National Monument.

**Growth.** Back-calculated lengths from scales in the Green River: 55 mm (first season), 100 mm (second), 158 mm (third). The largest handled was 388 mm and 7 years old. Three Lake Mohave specimens were estimated at 34–49 years by otolith examination.

**Diet.** Young chubs (bonytail and roundtail) fed mainly on chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs, shifting to floating and more diverse insect prey as they grew. Adults fed on terrestrial insects taken from the surface. No fish remains were found in stomachs. Kirsch (1888) reported gastropods and caddisfly larvae in Gila River fish.

**Threats.** Habitat alteration by dams, flow depletion, hybridization, and nonnative fish, as summarized in the Executive Summary. Recruitment is apparently absent, so surviving fish are old and not reproducing.

## Key Findings

**Status and goals**
- The species is considered the most endangered fish in the Colorado River basin.
- Short-term goal: prevent extinction and maintain the gene pool. Tasks 111–114 are Priority 1.
- Long-term goal: quantitative downlisting and delisting criteria, to be set after the immediate threat is removed.

**Recovery actions**
- **Refugia:** Maintain the existing refugium at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico) and establish at least one more. Candidate sites include Page Springs Hatchery (Arizona), Ouray National Wildlife Refuge (Utah), Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge (Arizona), Palm Lake at the Hassayampa River Preserve (Arizona), and Niland Native Fish Ponds (California).
- **Captive propagation:** Maximize [[Genetic Diversity]], preserving both Upper and Lower Basin gene pools. At least 10 years of captive propagation is anticipated. Rear fish to a size that maximizes post-stocking survival.
- **Experimental stocking:** Use [[Radiotelemetry]] on adults to identify habitat requirements, with Dinosaur National Monument as the highest-priority study site.
- **Priority recovery sites:** (1) Green and Yampa rivers at Dinosaur National Monument; (2) Green River at Desolation/Gray Canyon; (3) Colorado River at Cataract Canyon; (4) San Juan River, if suitable habitat exists. Lake Mohave is also a high priority, serving as a wild refugium and broodstock source.
- **Nonnative species:** Establish a cooperative agreement among the seven basin states and the Service to prohibit further introductions that could compete with or prey on bonytail.
- **Habitat protection:** Protect reaches that prove necessary for survival or recovery; the plan notes that significant future funding will be needed.
- **Compliance:** Inform agencies of their obligations under the Endangered Species Act, Clean Water Act, and Lacey Act, and ensure [[Section 7 Consultation]] so federal actions do not jeopardize the species.
- **Taxonomy:** Resolve whether bonytail, humpback, and roundtail chubs are distinct species, with an estimated four-year timeframe.

**Coordinated programs**
- The [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for the Upper Basin, signed January 1988, is the stepdown mechanism for this plan. Its goal is to recover Colorado River fishes by 2003 at an estimated cost of $59 million, excluding the San Juan River. The projected annual budget is $2,300,000. Five elements: instream flows; habitat development; rearing and stocking of native fish; nonnative species and sportfishing management; and research, monitoring, and data management.
- Funding plans include a $10 million request for water-rights purchases to protect instream flows, a $5 million request for facilities, and a one-time contribution of $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion from private water projects.
- A Lower Basin Recovery Action Plan (covering [[Colorado Squawfish]], bonytail, humpback chub, and [[Razorback Sucker]]) was being drafted. Its recovery cost and date were still to be determined.

**Knowledge gaps**
- Life history and habitat requirements are largely unknown. Studies are needed to determine the roles of predation, competition, habitat modification, and water management in limiting survival and reproduction.
- Juvenile identification remains uncertain, so recruitment is hard to verify.
