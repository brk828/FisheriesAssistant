# Little Colorado Spinedace in the Arboretum Pond: Spawning, Growth, Diet and Behavior (1992–1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report, prepared by [[Northern Arizona University]] researchers (Blinn, Pradetto and Runck) for the [[Coconino National Forest]] in September 1994, documents a three-year study of a introduced population of [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*), a federally listed threatened cyprinid endemic to eastern Arizona. Forty-four fish were transplanted from Rudd Creek into a stream/pond system at the [[Arboretum at Flagstaff]] on 2 September 1992. The study examined spawning cues, reproductive condition, growth, condition factors, diet, predation and distribution, alongside water chemistry and light conditions in the pond.

## Background & Description

The [[Arboretum at Flagstaff Pond]] is a modified cattle tank in Sinclair Wash, about 6.5 km SSW of Flagstaff (2,167 m elevation). The oval pond measures roughly 38 m × 47 m, with a surface area of 1408 m² and a maximum depth of 3.6 m. An adjoining stream (ca. 35 m long, 1–2 m wide, 0.6 m deep) is fed by water pumped from the pond. Before the introduction there were no fish in the habitat, although numerous [[Tiger Salamander]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) larvae were present.

Transplanted fish had total lengths of 58–120 mm (mean 85.9 mm). Growth data indicated that fish over 70 mm were at least one year old at introduction, while the two smallest (58 and 60 mm) likely hatched in May 1992.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal stratification:** The pond stayed sharply stratified through summer. Surface temperatures never exceeded 26 °C, and bottom temperatures stayed below 17 °C. A dense stand of [[Elodea]] contributed to stratification and anoxic bottom layers.
- **Dissolved oxygen and pH:** Oxygen exceeded 8.0 mg/L in the epilimnion, while the bottom 0.75 m was often anoxic. pH rose from about 7.2 in April–May to above 9.0 by 5 July, driven by photosynthesis.
- **Water transparency:** Secchi depth was ≤75 cm in April–May 1994 and exceeded 250 cm by mid-June. Light extinction coefficients were 2.9 before 1 June and 0.68 late in summer.
- **Nutrients (August 1994):** NO₃ about 8 ppb, NH₃ about 186 ppb, and PO₄ ≤100 ppb. A [[Cyanobacteria]] bloom (*Anabaena spiroides*) subsided after Elodea established.
- **Food base:** Chironomid larvae and oligochaetes dominated sediment invertebrates. Benthic food energy fell by nearly an order of magnitude from summer to November.
- **Predators:** Adult [[Belted Kingfisher]] predation, garter snake predation in enclosures, raccoon activity, and introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Green Sunfish]] predation were noted in the broader literature cited.

## Key Findings

**Spawning and cues**
- In 1993, three cohorts were produced between mid-May and mid-July. Spawning followed rises in water level and occurred at water temperatures above 18 °C and low transparency.
- In 1994, spawning began between 16 and 20 May in the stream, mainly between 1230 and 1530 h, when the stream mouth was in direct sunlight. Water temperatures were 16–20 °C, Secchi depth was below 100 cm, and daylength was about 13.5 h.
- Spawning ceased in early June 1994, when transparency rose to more than 250 cm. The authors conclude that **water transparency is the primary proximate cue**, with water temperature and photoperiod as important supporting factors.
- Spawning sites were fine gravels (mean stone size 4.9 × 6.4 mm), current velocity 14.2 cm/s, and depth 3.8 cm. Spawning did not occur in pools with fine sediment, in large cobbles, or in vegetation.

**Reproduction**
- Males became ripe near 70 mm TL and females were usually gravid from 80 mm TL. The largest ripe male was 116 mm TL and the largest gravid female 137 mm TL.
- On 26 April 1994, 74% of males were ripe but no females were gravid. By 6 May, over 90% of males and 41% of females were ripe or gravid.
- The sex ratio was about 2:1 males to females overall. Most fish over 100 mm TL were female.
- Egg incubation was about 5 days at 19–22 °C. Newly hatched fry were 6–7 mm TL and weighed 0.004 g or less.

**Growth**
- Modal size increased about 4.2 mm TL per week in the 1994 cohorts between 23 May and 25 August.
- Fish reached about 50% of average maximum length within two months of hatching.
- Length-weight and length-length regressions were calculated (for example, SL = 0.809 TL + 0.992, r² = 0.996).
- Condition factors were about 1.3 before spawning (April) and about 1.1 after spawning (June–August). They never fell below 1.0 for any size class.

**Population estimates**
- About 500 adult fish (70–90 mm TL) were estimated on 5 July 1994.
- About 960 fish over 70 mm and about 9,000 fish under 60 mm were estimated on 1 September 1994, using mark-recapture (212 marked, 251 captured, 6 recaptured).

**Diet and feeding**
- Adult dipterans (over 56%) and unidentified eggs (over 16%) made up most of the summer 1994 diet. Use of adult flying diptera increased through summer.
- Spinedace fed at the surface, especially at dawn and dusk.
- Baited and unbaited minnow traps caught similar numbers (averaging 5 fish per trap), with no significant difference (p > 0.05).

**Predation and distribution**
- Adult spinedace were believed to cannibalize fry. Juveniles stayed in shallow shoreline water, and adults occupied deeper water near Elodea. Vertical net tows in the centre of the pond caught no juveniles.
- Tiger salamander stomach analyses (48–128 mm SVL) showed no spinedace or spinedace eggs as a significant diet component. The authors nevertheless believe spinedace had a major negative impact on the salamander population, with salamander seine catches dropping from 4–5 animals per 20 m haul in 1993 to negligible numbers in 1994.
- Spinedace distribution was highly unpredictable from day to day, which the authors interpret as a possible evolved anti-predator strategy.

**Management implications**
- The authors recommend further study of water transparency, temperature and the salamander–spinedace interaction. They note that spinedace may respond to natural spring spate cues in other southwestern streams.
