# Dexter National Fish Hatchery Annual Narrative Report, Calendar Year 1984

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #Cyprinodontidae #Poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This annual narrative report, submitted on December 6, 1984 by Hatchery Manager Buddy Lee Jensen, covers operations at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in the Pecos River Valley of southeastern New Mexico. The station was built in 1931 and began rearing fish in 1932, originally to meet demand for warmwater game fish. By 1984 its program consisted almost entirely of holding, spawning, rearing, and distributing imperiled southwestern fishes for recovery and restocking in the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], and the mainstem [[Colorado River]] near Grand Junction, Colorado.

The report documents spawning and rearing results for [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Chihuahua Chub]], [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Desert Pupfish]], along with a tapeworm and parasite problem, cooperative work with state and academic partners, facility projects, and visitor activity.

## Background & Description

**Station history and purpose.** Construction began in 1931, and fish rearing started in 1932. The hatchery's mission has shifted with Service policy and regional needs, and by 1984 it focused on endangered and imperiled native species of the Southwest.

**Staffing (1984).** The station employed seven permanent Federal Pay (FPT) positions and eight seasonal or temporary employees. Permanent staff included:

- Buddy Jensen, Hatchery Manager (GS-12)
- Troy Mihalan (Assistant Manager, GS-9)
- Roger Hinman (Fish Culturist, GS-9)
- Connie Foster (Fishery Biologist, GS-7)
- Joe Owens (Maintenance Worker, WG-8)
- Philippe Sosa (Maintenance Worker, WG-7)
- Sharon Coats (Office Assistant, GS-5)

Other staff included Tom Maxwell (YCC group leader) and YCC enrollees. Philippe Sosa returned to the station in October. Connie Foster had been on extended leave without pay (LWOP) due to illness.

**Planned 1984 work.** The plan called for producing fish of several imperiled species, supplying refuge stock, surveying bullfrog and other techniques, providing fish for anglers and research, and supporting the Grand Canyon region recovery program. The report notes that recovery efforts for the Gila River species were a critical priority.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The hatchery's species are native to the Colorado River system and the Rio Grande and Pecos drainages. Many are federally listed as endangered or threatened, and their wild populations are restricted to small or fragmented habitats.

**Parasites and disease.** Lernaea (anchor worm) infestations persisted in bonytail brood tanks. Staff cleaned up the Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish facilities and hoped to eliminate the parasite before winter. A tapeworm, whose genus is partly illegible in the scan, was also found on Colorado Squawfish and [[Beautiful Shiner]]. Three-year-old squawfish were fed Di-N-Butyl-Tin-Oxide (DNBTO) in gelatin capsules and on feed in an attempt to eradicate it. The effect of the treatment is unknown. Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish were cleared for stocking in Colorado River system waters after the annual disease inspection, conducted by Hatchery Biologist Ron Major.

**Habitat and contamination.** The Gila topminnow holding pond was contaminated by Pecos gambusia, and topminnow numbers were greatly reduced. Staff collected more than 500 additional topminnows in Arizona to supplement the stock, and more than 5,000 were moved to a new pond for overwintering. The station also maintained populations of Comanche Springs pupfish, Leon Springs pupfish, and Pecos pupfish, and of Big Bend gambusia.

**Extinction report.** Clark Hubbs and Jensen's report on the extinction of [[Amistad Gambusia]] was published in *Copeia* in February 1984.

## Key Findings

### 1. Spawning and Fecundity

- **Razorback Sucker:** 25 wild and 10 domestic females were spawned using chorionic gonadotropin (CG). Total fecundity was reported as 3,570,191 eggs, and total swimup fry numbered 1,646,057 (65.2% of expected fry). The 70 three-year-old domestic brood fish averaged 2.6 pounds and produced 22,160 eggs per pound of body weight. Total fecundity for this group was 4,455,191.
- **Colorado Squawfish:** Fish averaged 3.2 pounds and were induced with carp pituitary. They produced 20,656 eggs per pound of body weight. Total fecundity was 595,612, and mean fecundity was 66,185. Fertility (viability) was 48.9%. Swimup fry represented 78.8% of total fecundity. Many fry were lost in holding tanks before swimup, which staff addressed with a trial of earlier stocking.
- **Bonytail:** Fish averaged 0.72 pounds and produced 23,910 eggs per pound of body weight. Total fecundity was 181,108, and swimup fry numbered 16,464 (about 64.1% of expected fry).

### 2. Fry Stocking and Survival

- Staff stocked both swimup and pre-swimup Razorback Sucker fry. Pre-swimup fry were stocked five days earlier than swimup fry. Swimup fry returned 98% (75,080 advanced fry), and pre-swimup fry returned 87.9% (72,594 advanced fry). Pre-swimup fry had about 10% lower survival, which the report judged acceptable given the tank losses avoided.
- Advanced Razorback Sucker fry were transferred to ponds for further rearing and stocking. Pond-reared fry were distributed to Arizona waters, the Verde and Gila rivers, and Nevada and California.
- Stocking totals were reported as: Dexter ponds (500,107), Page Springs Hatchery, Arizona (100,000), and Salt River and tributaries (3,020,786).

### 3. Distribution Summary

Table 2 in the report records 1984 distribution by species and destination. Key destinations included the Cherry Creek and Canyon Creek areas, the Salt River, Page Springs Hatchery, the Gila River, the Verde River, and state and university facilities in Utah, California, Nevada, and New Mexico. The Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker fry were the largest shipments.

### 4. Preserved Specimens and Partners

- Demand for preserved specimens was lower than in previous years but remained important. Requesters included the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish, Arizona State University, and the University of Texas.
- Dr. W. L. Minckley spent 12 to 14 months at Dexter under an IPA agreement with Arizona State University. Minckley and Jensen assisted the Arizona Game and Fish Department with a post-stocking dispersal study of Razorback Sucker in the Gila River.
- Dexter served as a "water change" station on long hauls and held 5,000 fish from Inks Dam National Fish Hatchery.
- Jensen served on the Rio Grande Fishes Recovery Team with several state, federal, academic, and Mexican partners.

### 5. Facilities and Projects

- The Jobs Bill pond subdivision was nearly complete, and raceways were finished. An addition to the East Sump was nearing completion. A 60 kW standby power supply was received but not yet installed.
- A two-line telephone system and a dedicated computer modem line were installed. A new one-ton Dodge pickup and a hot water cleaner were purchased.
- Cyclical maintenance projects listed for the year included water valve maintenance ($1,500), water well and pump maintenance ($6,000), septic tank pumping ($400), pond maintenance ($5,000), raceway construction ($21,500), and a standby power supply ($15,000).

### 6. Outreach and Visitors

- Approximately 1,500 visitors toured the hatchery in 1984. Notable visitors included Senator Pete Domenici and several regional and central office Service staff.
- Staff presented papers at the Arizona-New Mexico AFS and Wildlife Society meeting in Silver City and the Western Association of Fish and Wildlife Agencies meeting in Victoria, British Columbia. Roger Hinman submitted three papers for publication.
- Staff were certified as Class 5 Aquatic Pest Control applicators, and a Regional Field Station Evaluation team completed an administrative inspection.
