# The Susceptibility of Salmonid Fish to Poisons Under Estuarine Conditions—I Zinc Sulphate

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #toxicology #estuarine-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1964 experimental study by Herbert and Wakeford at the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] investigated how salinity affects the toxicity of [[Zinc Sulphate]] to [[Atlantic Salmon]] smolts and yearling [[Rainbow Trout]]. The research addresses a critical management question: how to assess toxicity risks to migrating salmon in estuaries where industrial effluents may be discharged. Results demonstrate that resistance to zinc poisoning increases dramatically with salinity up to 30-40% sea water, where trout can withstand 15 times and salmon 13 times the zinc concentration tolerated in fresh water. Beyond this optimal salinity range, tolerance decreases. Salmon smolts proved consistently more susceptible than rainbow trout at all salinities tested, suggesting caution when extrapolating trout toxicity data to salmon conservation.

## Background & Description

The study focused on two salmonid species: [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar* L.) smolts averaging 14.8 cm fork length and yearling [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii* Richardson) averaging 14.5 cm fork length. Salmon were obtained as 2-year-old parr in April 1963 and had assumed silvery smolt dress by testing in May-June. Rainbow trout were selected as a potential surrogate species for routine toxicity testing because they are cheaper, available year-round, and more tolerant of sea water than native [[Brown Trout]].

Test conditions employed hard fresh water (320 ppm hardness, 240 ppm alkalinity as CaCO₃) from laboratory boreholes and sea water pumped from [[Dovercourt]] near [[Harwich]] with salinities of 31.4-33.2 g/kg. All solutions were continuously aerated with air-CO₂ mixtures maintaining pH at approximately 7.8 (range 7.70-7.80 in controls), though zinc sulphate addition lowered pH values. Temperatures were held between 12.5-13.5°C.

Fish were acclimatized systematically: those destined for ≤25% sea water spent 4 days at test salinity; those for 25-50% spent 4 days at 25% then 4 days at final concentration; those for higher salinities received stepwise acclimatization through 25%, 50%, and final concentration (4 days each). Groups of 8-10 individuals were immersed in 40 L solutions at various zinc sulphate concentrations, transferred to fresh solutions after 24 hours, and tested for 48 hours total.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The research addresses the practical problem of [[Estuarine Pollution]] where industrial waste waters containing heavy metals are discharged into migration corridors used by [[Anadromous Fish]]. [[Atlantic Salmon]] traverse estuaries as smolts migrating to sea or as adults returning to spawn, encountering waters ranging from fresh to full-strength sea water.

The study revealed that 31% sea water is isotonic with the blood of acclimatized Atlantic salmon smolts (calculated from Parry 1960 and Harvey 1955 data). Maximum resistance to zinc poisoning occurred at salinities slightly above this isotonic point (30-40% sea water). This pattern parallels earlier work by Erichsen Jones (1940) showing that [[Minnows]] (*Phoxinus phoxinus*) exhibited maximum resistance to [[Mercuric Chloride]] at isotonic sodium chloride concentrations.

The mechanism likely involves reduced inward diffusion of water (and dissolved toxins) through [[Gill Epithelium]] at isotonic salinities. Zinc exerts toxicity primarily by destroying gill epithelial cells (Lloyd 1962), and poisons generally enter fish mainly through gills. Strontium uptake studies (Ophel and Judd 1962) and cadmium-115m absorption research (Shiras and Mori 1961) support the conclusion that absorption of dissolved substances from water is reduced in marine versus fresh-water conditions.

At 35% sea water, zinc precipitation occurred (up to 24% of added zinc as basic salts), and supplementary experiments confirmed that suspended precipitated zinc salts retained toxic properties, agreeing with Lloyd's (1960) findings in fresh water.

## Key Findings

**Salinity-Toxicity Relationships:**
- In fresh water, the 48-hour TLm (median tolerance limit) for rainbow trout was approximately 6-7 ppm Zn
- Resistance increased progressively with salinity, peaking at 30-40% sea water
- At 30-40% sea water, rainbow trout could withstand approximately 100 ppm Zn (15× fresh water tolerance)
- At 30-40% sea water, salmon smolts could withstand approximately 80 ppm Zn (13× fresh water tolerance)
- Beyond 35% sea water, tolerance declined; at 72% sea water, trout resistance was still much higher than fresh water, but salmon showed greater susceptibility reduction

**Species Comparisons:**
- [[Atlantic Salmon]] smolts were consistently more susceptible to zinc poisoning than [[Rainbow Trout]] at all salinities tested
- The ratio of trout to salmon tolerance ranged from 1.2 to 2.2 times depending on salinity
- This species difference has important implications: toxicity trials with acclimatized rainbow trout cannot be directly applied to salmon without safety factors

**Physiological Interpretations:**
- Maximum resistance occurred near the isotonic point (31% sea water for salmon smolts)
- The degree of resistance reduction at high salinity (72% sea water) varied by species: greatest in exclusively fresh-water [[Minnows]], least in euryhaline salmon smolts in migratory condition, intermediate in rainbow trout
- This pattern suggests that osmotic stress at hypersaline conditions may increase susceptibility to toxins

**Practical Implications:**
- Zinc pollution should not pose serious problems in seaward portions of estuaries due to the large volumes of water requiring economically prohibitive quantities of zinc to reach dangerous concentrations
- Much greater zinc concentrations can be tolerated in moderately saline estuarine waters than in upstream fresh water
- Caution is required when using rainbow trout as surrogates for salmon in toxicity assessments

**Methodological Notes:**
- No fish died during acclimatization periods
- No control fish died during 48-hour tests
- Zinc remained in solution at concentrations near the 48-hr TLm except in 35% sea water
- Precipitated basic zinc salts in suspension retained toxicity
- The dithizone method was used for zinc analysis in fresh water; EDTA method for saline water
