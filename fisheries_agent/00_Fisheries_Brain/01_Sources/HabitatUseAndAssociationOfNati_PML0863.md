# Habitat Use and Association of Native and Nonnative Fishes in the San Juan River, New Mexico and Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Gido and Propst (1999, *Copeia*) examines habitat use and interspecific association among native and nonnative fishes in [[Secondary Channels]] of the [[San Juan River]], which is a regulated tributary of the [[Colorado River]]. Most species and age classes used low-velocity habitats with silt substrate. Habitat use overlapped broadly among species, and older age classes tended to occupy faster, deeper water. Discriminant analysis classified species and age classes correctly only 23.4% of the time, indicating high overlap. Native juvenile fishes showed the greatest positive association with nonnative fishes, while adult and subadult natives showed the least. The authors conclude that, depending on resource availability, this overlap may produce negative interactions, and they recommend managing to suppress nonnative fish abundance while refining reservoir release patterns to benefit natives.

## Background & Description

Native riverine fish assemblages in the American Southwest have declined because of [[Stream Impoundment]] and the establishment of [[Nonnative Species Invasion|nonnative fishes]], which in some rivers now outnumber natives. The authors frame management around enhancing habitat for natives while reducing habitat suitability for nonnatives, following Elton's (1958) call to dampen, rather than eradicate, invasive outbreaks.

Because many southwestern rivers are regulated by reservoir releases, [[Flow Regulation]] is a potential conservation tool. Discharge changes can alter depth, velocity, substrate and cover, and persistent low flows can armor substrata and eliminate ephemeral habitats. Recommending release patterns requires habitat-use data for each species and age class, since ontogenetic shifts in habitat use are common.

The study was part of a long-term, multidisciplinary project on the San Juan River, where reservoir releases mimicked natural flow regimes. It follows Gido et al. (1997), which described species distributions among the same four secondary channels over time.

**Study species** (13 species collected; abbreviations as used in the source):

- Nonnatives: [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*), and [[Plains Killifish]] (*Fundulus zebrinus*), among others.
- Natives: [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The San Juan River drains approximately 99,200 km² across Colorado, Utah, Arizona and New Mexico. Operation of [[Navajo Dam]], about 100 km upstream of the study sites, reduces natural flow variability by diminishing floods during spring runoff and elevating base flows in late summer, autumn and winter. Snowmelt releases and unregulated tributary flows still maintained a quasi-natural pattern of high spring-early summer flows followed by low late-summer to winter flows.

**Secondary channels.** These floodplain channels are abundant on the San Juan River and provide habitat used extensively by native and nonnative fishes. Study sites (115–300 m long) were in four channels that were 0.9–3.4 km in length.

**Sampling methods.** Sampling ran from July 1993 to May 1995 (almost two years). Sites were sampled every three weeks from peak spring runoff through November, then every six weeks until the next spring runoff. Turbidity precluded direct observation, so fish were sampled by [[Seining]] (97% of habitats, using a 1.6 mm mesh drag seine, 2.5 × 1.2 m) or by [[Electrofishing]] where seining was ineffective. Depth, velocity, dominant substrate and cover were recorded at nine points per habitat, and velocity was measured at 60% depth. Habitat availability was quantified from 100 points per site on each sample date. Fish were classified into three age classes: larvae, juveniles and subadult/adult. Specimens over 100 mm TL were released in the field; the remainder were preserved in 10% formalin and accessioned into the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish collection.

**Analytical approaches.** Analyses included stepwise [[Discriminant Function Analysis]] (50% of habitats used for training, 50% for testing), [[Hurlbert's Coefficient of Interspecific Association]] (C₇, ranging from −1 to +1, with values near zero indicating random coexistence), contingency chi-square tests with Bonferroni-adjusted alpha, and [[Spearman Rank Correlation]] of abundance across habitats.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and catch**
- 1,132 habitats were sampled, of which 929 (82%) were occupied by fish.
- Each site was visited 17 times, except site 4, which was sampled 15 times.
- 55,590 specimens of 13 species were collected.
- Three nonnatives (*C. lutrensis*, *P. promelas*, *G. affinis*) were the first, second and fourth most abundant and made up 77.0% of the total catch.
- Native *R. osculus*, *C. latipinnis* and *C. discobolus* were the third, fifth and sixth most abundant and made up 17.8% of the catch.

**Habitat use**
- Mean fish density was greatest in low-velocity (<20 cm/s), silt-bottomed habitats.
- Density showed a slight trend toward lower values in deeper habitats (>60 cm).
- Adult *R. osculus* occupied the fastest habitats (mean velocity 48.9 cm/s). Adult and subadult *C. discobolus* also used higher-velocity habitats than other species.
- Sampled habitats differed significantly from available habitats at two of four sites. The greatest deviation was at site 4, where sampled habitats were shallower, slower and siltier.
- Peak abundance of each age class usually occurred in late summer. Densities of adult and juvenile *C. latipinnis* and *C. discobolus* did not peak in late summer in both years.

**Discriminant analysis**
- The first two axes explained 77% of variation in habitat use (57.4% and 20.1%). Axis 1 contrasted low-velocity, shallow, silt habitats with fast, deep habitats.
- The model was significant but classified species and age classes correctly only 23.4% of the time.
- Native groups were classified correctly 10.9% of the time and nonnatives 26.8%. *R. osculus* was predicted most accurately (54.1%).
- Combining species into age classes raised accuracy to 50.4%. Nonnative age classes were classified correctly 50.8% of the time, versus 14.3% for native age classes.

**Interspecific association**
- Of 99 possible native–nonnative comparisons, 42 (42%) were significantly positive, and only seven were significantly negative (six involving *R. osculus*).
- Native juveniles showed the greatest positive association with nonnatives; adult and subadult natives showed the least.
- Among 36 native–native comparisons, 18 (50%) were significantly positive, and only one was negative (adult *R. osculus* with juvenile *C. discobolus*).

**Abundance correlations**
- Larval *C. latipinnis* was negatively correlated with juvenile *P. promelas* (r = −0.54, P < 0.01) and larval *C. lutrensis* (r = −0.48, P = 0.05), despite positive presence/absence associations.
- Larval *C. discobolus* was positively correlated with juvenile *C. lutrensis* (r = 0.30, P < 0.01) and *C. carpio* (r = 0.47, P < 0.01).

**Temporal patterns**
- Native larvae appeared and peaked earlier than nonnative larvae. *Catostomus* larvae were present when water temperature was below 18 °C, whereas larval *C. lutrensis* and *P. promelas* did not appear until water exceeded 21 °C.
- Fish densities exceeded 50 individuals m⁻² in many low-velocity habitats during late summer–autumn low flows, and most were juveniles.

**Interpretation and management**
- Classification success was low, indicating high habitat overlap, especially among larvae and juveniles.
- Native adult *R. osculus* had the least overlap with nonnatives because it occupied riffles with high velocity, and no riffle-dwelling nonnative had established in the river.
- Temporal separation of spawning (native fishes spawn in cool spring runoff, nonnatives later) may reduce larval overlap.
- Negative interactions are plausible during low flows, when habitat is limited and densities peak. Nonnative *C. lutrensis* is considered more aggressive and capable of displacing natives elsewhere.
- Predation by adult nonnatives on native larvae in secondary channels may also reduce native survival.
- The authors conclude that control of nonnative fish abundance is probably critical to native fish conservation, and that prudent management should include measures to suppress nonnatives until competition and predation are better defined.
