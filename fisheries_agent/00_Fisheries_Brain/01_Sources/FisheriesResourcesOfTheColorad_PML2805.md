# Lower Colorado River Wildlife and Fisheries Resources (Project 60-R, 1952 Completion Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Commission Wildlife Restoration Division completion report (Project 60-R, January 1951 to July 1952, prepared by Project Leader R. A. Wagner) surveys the wildlife and fisheries of the [[Lower Colorado River]] from [[Hoover Dam]] to the International Border. It is organized as four job reports: waterfowl, small game, big game, and fisheries. The report's central argument is that the river is managed by seven separate, often opposed agencies with no common management foundation, so a comprehensive plan cannot be formulated until uniform seasons, bag limits, enforcement, biological staffing and predator control policy are agreed upon. Fisheries use is described as only a fraction of the resource's potential.

## Background & Description

**Scope and administration.** The project area is about 344 miles long and ranges from under 50 feet to 10 miles wide. Seven agencies share jurisdiction, including the Mexican government, the states of Arizona and California, the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]], the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs. The report identifies three management variations: deer season date and length, law enforcement activity, and predator control concepts.

**Physical geography.** Key reaches, in river miles, include Hoover Dam to Davis Dam (67.4), Davis Dam to Parker Dam (88.2), and Imperial Dam to Laguna Dam (22.1 per Table I). Major impoundments are [[Lake Mohave]] (behind [[Davis Dam]]), [[Havasu Lake]] (behind [[Parker Dam]]), and Lake Moovalya (behind Headgate Rock Dam). Below [[Palo Verde Weir]], the river meanders across an alluvial flood plain forming sloughs and oxbow lakes, including [[Cibola Lake]] (about 140 acres open water and over 2,600 acres of slough, marsh and bog) and [[Mittry Lake]].

**Climate.** The area lies in the lower Sonoran life zone, with low rainfall (roughly 5 inches annually), evaporation of about 7 to 10 feet, a growing season of about 300 days, and a mean annual temperature of about 72 °F.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Water released from Lake Mead carries about 500 ppm total salts, rising to roughly 150 percent of that at Yuma as irrigation return flows are added. Total soluble salts range from about 550 ppm in the upper project area to about 800 ppm in the lower part (excluding Mittry Lake). Average pH is 8.2, dissolved nitrates 1.5 ppm, and phosphate 0.1 ppm. Silt load was historically very high; before Hoover Dam, 20 to over 300 million tons per year were carried downstream.

**Water fluctuation.** The report judges that fluctuations of less than four feet per year have little effect on plant growth, while larger variations retard or destroy plant life. Dam operations cause scouring surges in some reaches (reducing plant growth) and help control tule vegetation in others.

**Habitat condition.** Habitat changes are driven by sediment. Upper reaches are shifting sand bars and side channels; the Imperial to Laguna reach is being rapidly overgrown with cattail; the Laguna to Morales reach has very little water much of the year; and below Morales Dam the river becomes a shallow sand-bed stream where vehicles cross the channel.

**Biological communities.** Waterfowl use the river as part of the [[Pacific Flyway]]. Food plants include pondweed, bulrush, wigeongrass, muskgrass and spikerush for ducks and geese. Small game depends on desert shrubs and cultivated crops. Big game (bighorn sheep, desert mule deer) depends on water holes, browse such as mesquite and catclaw, and aquatic emergents like cattail and bulrush.

## Key Findings

**1. Waterfowl (Job 1).**
- Three goose species were recorded. Of the total goose population, about 45 percent were Canadian honkers, 35 percent speckled geese, 10 percent snow geese, and 10 percent other species.
- The report estimates about 10.5 thousand geese and 244 thousand ducks for the area, as read from a garbled summary line, so treat these figures with caution.
- Duck arrival ran from pintails in mid-August through green-winged teal in late December. Peak numbers occurred in November and December.
- Food supply in 1951 was judged roughly ten times greater than in each of the two previous years, except at Lake Mohave.
- Hunting pressure was estimated at about 6,000 hunters on Havasu Lake National Wildlife Refuge, about 5,000 between Cibola and Parker Dam, and 1,960 on the Imperial National Wildlife Refuge. Utilization was no more than 40 percent of potential.
- Recommendations include retaining public lands, planting or reseeding, improving Mittry Lake with higher-quality water, guaranteeing water deliveries to Upper Topock Swamp, improving road access, and enforcing game laws more rigidly during peak population periods.

**2. Small Game (Job 2).**
- Four resident or migratory species were studied: [[Gambel Quail]], Desert Cottontail, [[Mourning Dove]], and [[White-Winged Dove]].
- Mourning doves were estimated at about 30,000 between the International Border and Imperial Dam, with other concentrations of 10,000 to 12,000 in several sections. Over 90 percent had moved south by late September.
- Rabbits cause heavy crop damage, with "many thousands of dollars" claimed in losses, especially in the Yuma Valley.
- Hunting pressure was rated at 40 to 85 percent of potential across sections, with many sections at 60 percent.
- Recommendations include continuing a 1080 predator program, better road maintenance, and using a 1080 program where rabbit damage is high.

**3. Big Game (Job 3).**
- Observed [[Bighorn Sheep]] totalled 164 in the Table I survey, while the summary cites approximately 436 desert bighorn under normal conditions.
- Observed [[Desert Mule Deer]] totalled roughly 309 in the Table II survey, with the Arizona side reportedly supporting over five times as many deer as the California side.
- Heavy deer hunting and poaching were noted: about 260 deer hunters in the 1949 season, roughly 40 deer taken in 1950, and only about 20 bagged in the 1950 season, with many animals "run to death."
- Bighorn are protected by law, with no open season.
- Recommendations include extending the deer season to a week in the Cibola to Colorado River Indian Reservation reach, a predator control program (especially in the Mohave and Black Mountains), and developing or improving water tanks.

**4. Fisheries (Job 4).**
- The inventory lists 18 fish species, including native and sport fish. Native species in conspicuous numbers include [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Introduced or established species include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], crappie, bluegill, sunfish, bullheads, [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], mullet, and gambusia.
- [[Lake Mohave]] is split by temperature: cold water from Hoover Dam supports trout in the upper section, while the warm surface water supports bass and other warm-water species. Introduced forage improvements are discussed, and mixing of populations is undesirable.
- Over 90 percent of users on the river are fishermen. The area receives over 250,000 angler, camper and hunter man-days per year, but the report says this is no more than 20 percent of potential use.
- Lake Mohave fishing grew from over 68,235 anglers in 1950 to a 61 percent increase in 1951, with a further increase in 1952.
- Major concerns are raw sewage discharge from Hoover Dam and Yuma, unstable channel conditions, lack of cover for bass fry, and a need for a forage fish.
- Recommendations include public camp sites, improved public access, forage fish introduction, closing spawning areas to fishing, legalizing bowfishing and bullfrog taking, and regulating boat safety standards.

**5. Overall Conclusion.**
The report concludes that the entire river should be managed as a unit under a single regulatory body, which it acknowledges is probably impossible. Until then, it recommends uniform seasons and bag limits, increased law enforcement, an equalized biological staff, and a uniform predator control policy. Only after these are in place can a comprehensive management plan be formulated.
