## Executive Summary

This document is a 1990 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region 2) interagency memorandum critically reviewing a Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (UDOW) proposal to introduce non-native [[Rainbow Smelt]] into [[Lake Powell]] as forage for a collapsed [[Striped Bass]] fishery. The reviewers argue the proposal fails to address the root cause of the [[Threadfin Shad]] population crash — namely, that reproducing Striped Bass had exceeded the reservoir's carrying capacity and demolished the existing forage base. The memo raises strong ecological, legal (NEPA/ESA), and management objections, concluding that introducing smelt is unlikely to succeed and poses unacceptable risk to endangered native fishes, including the [[Humpback Chub]], in the Colorado River Basin.

## Background & Description

The source text is a policy/management memorandum and contains minimal direct morphological data. Limited relevant physical/life-history notes include:

- **Rainbow Smelt**: Described as "predatory to some extent," with early spawning phenology relative to most native fishes in the system — small smelt are noted to be capable of preying on small native fish shortly after hatching.
- **Striped Bass**: Characterized functionally as "an eating machine," with subadults (>6-8 inches) questioned as being able to sustain "good body condition foraging strictly on large zooplankton" — reviewers suggest they instead prey on young-of-year fish (y-o-y) of other predatory species and small shad, indicating subadults are not restricted to the pelagic zone.
- **Threadfin Shad**: Noted as efficient converters of plankton into fish biomass ("effective converters of plankton to fish flesh"), and as a population that is cyclical in nature, particularly sensitive to low winter water temperatures.
- **Walleye**: Described as a bottom/near-bottom feeder, a trait linked to potential competitive/predatory impact on native bottom-dwelling fish species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Trophic structure of Lake Powell**: The existing (pre-disruption) energy pathway was nutrients → plankton → [[Threadfin Shad]] → [[Striped Bass]], described as an efficient food chain. Introducing [[Rainbow Smelt]] as an additional trophic link was argued to make this chain "more complex and less efficient," diluting rather than repairing the flawed system.
- **Reservoir fertility**: Lake Powell and other Colorado River reservoirs are characterized as relatively sterile/infertile compared to productive systems like [[Lake Texoma]] and the [[Santee-Cooper Reservoirs]], which benefit from agricultural runoff, standing timber, sloughs, and shallow-water habitat that Lake Powell lacks.
- **Boom-bust reservoir dynamics**: A classic post-impoundment pattern is described — high initial fertility and excellent fishery performance following reservoir filling and predator stocking, followed by an inevitable decline as forage is depleted by the introduced predator population.
- **Zone of influence and endangered species**: Four rare fish species (three federally endangered, one candidate for listing) occupy the zone potentially affected by smelt introduction, including juvenile [[Humpback Chub]] near the mouth of the Little Colorado River, Arizona. Concerns center on smelt as both a competitor and potential predator of these native fishes.
- **Anticipated geographic spread**: The proposal itself conceded that smelt would not remain contained within Lake Powell, with expected establishment from Lake Powell downstream to the [[Gulf of California]], implicating Arizona, California, Nevada, New Mexico, and Mexico in potential ecological impacts.
- **Precedent from the Great Lakes**: Cited research (via Dr. Paul Marsh) links [[Rainbow Smelt]] introductions in the [[Great Lakes]] to declines in recruitment of indigenous fish species, used as evidence for likely negative impacts in the Colorado River system.
- **Largemouth Bass and Walleye niches**: Largemouth Bass establishment issues in the reservoir are attributed less to lack of brush cover (uncommon in arid environments) and more to predation pressure exacerbated by Striped Bass depleting the shared forage base. Walleye are suggested to have contributed positively to the fishery pre-Striped Bass introduction but may also have contributed to native species decline via bottom-feeding overlap.

## Key Findings

- **Root cause misdiagnosis**: The core criticism is that UDOW's proposal treats the Threadfin Shad crash as a cause requiring a new forage species, when in fact it is a symptom of Striped Bass overpopulation exceeding carrying capacity — the "broken link" that needs repair, not compounding with a new introduction.
- **Low likelihood of success**: Reviewers assess the probability of successful, sustained smelt establishment as "very low," and note that even under a "best scenario," a temporary condition improvement in Striped Bass would only trigger renewed over-reproduction and re-collapse of the forage base — a repeating cycle.
- **Unrealistic management goals**: UDOW's goal of restoring 1980s peak-era Striped Bass catch rates is called unrealistic; achieving a stated target of approximately 4,000,000 lbs of Striped Bass production would require over 20,000,000 lbs of annual forage production, deemed impractical for this reservoir's fertility level.
- **Regulatory non-compliance concerns**: The action is argued to not comply with NEPA or ESA given anticipated interstate/international range expansion and impacts to endangered species, and Section 7 consultation is recommended.
- **Broad multi-agency opposition**: The proposal was formally opposed in writing by the National Park Service, Arizona State University staff, the [[Desert Fishes Council]], Arizona Game and Fish Department, and the Fish and Wildlife Service.
- **Risk-benefit conclusion**: Echoing Arizona Game and Fish Director Shroufe, the memo concludes "the chances of an environmental mistake are greater than the potential benefits," recommending against the introduction.
- **Alternative framing of the problem**: Reviewers suggest the actual underlying issue is that Striped Bass — an evolutionarily predation-adapted, high-forage-demand species — is fundamentally poorly suited to the naturally low-fertility Colorado River reservoir system, rather than a simple forage-species deficiency solvable by adding [[Rainbow Smelt]].
- **Native species jeopardy**: Native/endemic fish are described as likely capable of completing life cycles in tributary rivers entering the lake if exotic predators (Striped Bass, Walleye) were absent, implying that exotic species introductions — not just reservoir conversion — are the primary driver of native fish decline.
- **Commercial fishery outlook**: Even if smelt establish, benefits to the Striped Bass fishery are predicted to be short-lived and contingent on an "unlimited food supply" for Striped Bass, a condition deemed unlikely to be met.
