# West Turkey Creek Yaqui Chub Restoration

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Project 99-017-024), submitted by [[Stephanie M. Coleman]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] to the [[National Fish and Wildlife Foundation]] and dated 15 March 2000, documents the renovation of [[West Turkey Creek]] in the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] of southeastern Arizona. The project removed non-native fishes, restocked two imperiled natives, the [[Yaqui Chub]] and an [[Unnamed Dace]], and built structures to prevent reinvasion. Two of three objectives were achieved by the report date. The third, long-term enhancement of the native community, awaited spring 2001 monitoring. Drought in 2000 was a major setback, but native fish reproduced successfully after renovation and drought, and no non-natives had been found by January 2001.

## Background & Description

The [[Rio Yaqui]] drainage of northwestern Mexico barely enters the USA in southeastern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico. It originally contributed nine fish species to Arizona's fauna, more than 25% of the state's freshwater fish fauna, and five of these were known in the USA only from this drainage. Five Yaqui species were extirpated from the USA by the 1980s, which led to the formation of the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] and a hatchery and reintroduction program.

West Turkey Creek is a former connection of the Rio Yaqui system. Two native fishes occur there:

- **Yaqui chub** (*Gila purpurea*): highly restricted in distribution, historically known only near the refuge and West Turkey Creek, and listed as Endangered by the USFWS (1996).
- **Unnamed dace** (*Agosia* sp.; Hendrickson & Minckley, in preparation): not listed, but rare. It was known from no more than five small US watersheds and now from three. West Turkey Creek supports the last of four US populations.

The area is also part of the recovery area for the Threatened [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*).

Non-native species targeted for removal were [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]], plus [[Bullfrog]]s, which competed with or preyed on the natives.

The watershed is managed through the [[Habitat Conservation Plan]] (1998), a partnership among [[El Coronado Ranch]], the [[USDA Forest Service]], the USFWS, and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. It was Arizona's first habitat conservation plan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Setting:** The Chiricahua Mountains are sky islands of mesic forest above desert grassland. Their northwestern slopes drain to the closed [[Sulphur Springs Valley]], which once held pluvial Lake Cochise and now has only the ephemeral Willcox Playa.
- **Origin of natives:** Both native fishes likely invaded via the Rio Yaqui before the valley was isolated. The chub probably disappeared before the 1900s. The dace survives.
- **Hydrology:** Except for the extreme headwaters, the creek and tributaries are intermittent. Dry reaches alternate with short flowing sections, and dry channel increases downstream. Existing diversions and dry stretches served as natural barriers.
- **Stressors:** Drought, flood, and forest fire were historic natural threats. Logging, livestock grazing, and non-native introductions were human-caused threats. Fatheads probably entered as discarded bait.
- **Drought and monsoon:** Drought in winter and spring 2000 dried the creek and seven connected ponds. Early monsoon rains in summer and autumn 2000 restored flow and refilled the ponds. Flooding in July and August 1999 overflowed the canal-fed ponds and washed one out.

## Key Findings

**Project objectives**

1. **Eradicate non-natives** from an ~8.0-mile perennial reach, intermittent tributaries, and 14 creek- and runoff-fed ponds (~4.0 acres total). Completed summer 1999, before this project was funded.
2. **Secure viable native populations.** Completed. Stocks were held in multiple places, reintroduced after renovation, and reproduced in the creek and 2 of 9 connected ponds. Yaqui catfish were stocked in 2 runoff-fed ponds in 1999.
3. **Long-term enhancement of the native community.** Not yet achieved. Reproduction was noted in 3 ponds in autumn and early winter 2000, but no new status data were available until spring 2001.

**Renovation methods**

- The [[Habitat Conservation Plan]] pre-authorized renovation on private land. Decision in November 1998 and implementation in just over six months was described as a world record by the parties.
- Native stocks were held in isolated ponds and at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Arizona State University]]. About 1,200 chubs were retained at the ranch, with more available at the refuge. About 500 dace were secured, split among three locations.
- Canal intakes were closed and siphons installed to lower pond levels. Water was spread on fields where possible, and screened intakes protected turtles and other native aquatic species.
- Piscicides were applied under a certified applicator. [[Rotenone]] was used in ponds and [[Fintrol]] (Antimycin-A) mainly in flowing water. Both were neutralized with potassium permanganate or a similar oxidant. A detoxification station was staffed downstream.
- The first renovation treated all surface water twice, two days apart. A second whole-system application followed in early June over three days.
- A fathead population survived in one deep pond, probably in layered, non-mixing water. The pond was retreated.
- Rainfall in July and August 1999 caused flooding, and fatheads reappeared in two formerly dry ponds connected by floodwater from a marsh missed in earlier treatments. Green sunfish later appeared in the most downstream pond, which was pumped dry.
- A repatriation error occurred. Fatheads were mixed with the held natives. Only adults were released to avoid restocking misidentified non-native young. More than 1,000 chubs and 300 dace were recovered and released.

**Post-renovation results**

- No non-native fish survival was found after the final treatments.
- Hundreds of juvenile chubs and dace were seen in upstream ponds in late September 1999.
- Native fish survived the 2000 drought and reproduced after both renovation and drought.
- As of January 2001, no fatheads or green sunfish had been seen since renovation.

**Infrastructure**

- A diversion was rebuilt to add an [[Infiltration Gallery]], a vertical-drop barrier of about one meter, and improved water control. The gallery allows water withdrawal while preventing entrainment of eggs, larvae, and adults into a refuge pond.
- The plan is to install similar galleries on three more diversions, dividing the system into four fish management units. The first gallery was completed and operating by late 2000.

**Funding**

- NFWF contributed $10,700.00. It paid for a high-volume pump used to dry a pond where green sunfish could not be removed chemically, and for materials for the diversion barrier and gallery.
- Cost share was 100% private, from El Coronado Ranch to [[Arizona State University]]. It covered equipment, part-time staff time by Stephanie Coleman from 1 July 1999 to 30 June 2000, and diversion reconstruction costs.

**Management outlook**

- The system is closed to fishing, with information signs planned.
- Monitoring under the habitat conservation plan will continue, and intake devices are being installed to block non-natives from creek to canal-fed ponds.
- The authors expect no further whole-system renovation will be needed.
