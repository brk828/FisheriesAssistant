# Swimming Speed of the Western Sucker (Catostomus occidentalis)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #freshwater-biology #fish-passage

## Executive Summary
This 1950 field note by J. H. Wares documents a rare direct observation of maximum sustained swimming speed in the [[Western Sucker]] (*Catostomus occidentalis*) attempting to ascend a concrete culvert on Salt Creek, a tributary entering [[Shasta Lake]] in Shasta County, California. Approximately 10 adult suckers (estimated 12–14 inches) repeatedly attempted to ascend a highly turbulent, smooth-floored culvert against water velocities of 7.2–8.6 ft/sec to reach spawning habitat upstream. The fish were able to advance 15–25 feet against the current before exhaustion forced retreat, cyclically retrying the ascent. The data are used to estimate maximum burst swimming speed (~10–10.6 ft/sec) and to compare sucker locomotor performance against published trout swimming capacity data (Denil, 1938), with implications for fish-barrier design intended to exclude "rough fish" from trout streams.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Catostomus occidentalis* Ayres — [[Western Sucker]], family [[Catostomidae]]
- **Estimated size of observed individuals**: 12–14 inches (adult specimens); none captured for direct measurement
- **Life stage**: Adults in pre-spawning migratory condition
- No detailed external morphological description provided beyond size class and maturity status (spawning migrants)

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context**: Lotic-lentic interface — the mouth of [[Salt Creek]] where it enters the impounding reservoir of [[Shasta Lake]] via a concrete highway culvert (under U.S. Highway 99)
- **Behavioral ecology**: Adult suckers exhibited upstream **spawning migration** behavior, attempting repeated bursts up a hydraulically challenging culvert to reach spawning grounds in the stream above
- **Migration behavior pattern**: Fish would dart from a resting pool at the stream/reservoir interface, ascend against high-velocity flow until exhausted, drop back downstream, pause, and repeat — suggesting possible rotation among different groups of individuals
- **Physical habitat parameters** (Salt Creek culvert, April 13, 1950):
  - Culvert: half-round concrete, floor 12 ft wide, 187 ft long
  - Longitudinal floor slope: 1.2%
  - Stream discharge: 36 c.f.s.
  - Water depth: lower end 0–7.5 in (side to center); upper end ~2–9.5 in (side to center)
  - Flow velocity: **8.6 ft/sec** (lower culvert), **7.2 ft/sec** (upper culvert), measured with a Price current meter
- **Relevance to fisheries management**: The study is framed within the broader context of designing water-velocity **fish barriers** to selectively exclude non-game "rough fish" (e.g., suckers) from trout-bearing streams, a management technique of interest to [[California Division of Fish and Game]]

## Key Findings
- Adult Western Suckers sustained upstream advancement of **15–25 feet** through a culvert section with flow velocity of **8.6 ft/sec**, despite ultimately being forced back by exhaustion.
- Net forward progress against the current was estimated at **~2 ft/sec**, implying an absolute (ground-relative) burst swimming speed of approximately **10 ft/sec** (8.6 + 2 ft/sec) for short exertions.
- Extrapolating, the author estimates the theoretical maximum flow velocity a 12-inch sucker could resist (holding position only, ~5 sec) is about **10.6 ft/sec**.
- Comparative data from [[Denil (1938)]] indicate a 13.5-inch trout (*Salmo trutta*) achieves burst swimming speeds of **11.4 ft/sec** (3.5 m/sec) in short sudden efforts.
- Denil's force-based framework: trout can develop force ≈1.4× body weight in sudden efforts (salmon ≈1.2×); cyprinids ≈0.5× body weight sustained for 50–60 sec, up to ≈1.0× body weight for brief bursts — suckers appear to perform closer to salmonid capability than expected.
- The similarity in maximum swimming speeds between suckers (~10.6 ft/sec) and trout (~11.4 ft/sec) suggests that **water velocity alone is a poor discriminator** for barriers meant to selectively exclude suckers/rough fish while allowing trout passage.
- Practical implication: as stream velocity decreases below these species-specific maxima (e.g., 5.6 ft/sec), both suckers and trout gain net upstream headway (e.g., a sucker could advance ~25 feet in 5 seconds at 5.6 ft/sec flow), reducing the effectiveness of velocity-based selective barriers.
- The author notes uncertainty regarding the **duration** either species can sustain high swimming velocities, identifying this as a key gap for future barrier design research.
