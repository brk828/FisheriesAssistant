# Fossil Creek Crayfish Removal Project

#decapoda #invasive-species #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by Jim Walters under the [[Central Arizona Project Funds Transfer Program]], evaluates trap types and capture methods for removing invasive crayfish from [[Fossil Creek]], a perennial, spring-fed stream in central Arizona. Trapping and dipnetting were conducted from 2001 to 2004 in reaches above and below the [[Irving Power Plant]]. The study found that [[Baited Trapping]] was the main effective method, that [[Dipnetting]] was useful mainly for young-of-year crayfish, and that crayfish activity varied with season and [[High Flow Event]]s. The author concludes that physical removal is labor-intensive and nearly impossible in remote Arizona streams, and recommends developing a licensed chemical control, removal within barrier-defined reaches, and density and biomass monitoring.

## Background & Description

**Invasive crayfish.** Crayfish are not native to Arizona. [[Orconectes Virilis]] and [[Procambarus Clarkii]] are widespread in the state's streams, lakes, and reservoirs. Invasive crayfish consume fish eggs and larvae, prey on frogs and snakes, alter food webs, and reduce aquatic plant and periphyton biomass. Manual trapping has been the most common control method, but its effect on population structure is uncertain.

**Study site.** Fossil Creek flows into the Verde River in central Arizona and is perennial along its roughly 14-mile length. Its spring source historically provided about 43 cfs. The creek is known for [[Travertine Formation]], in which calcium-carbonate-rich spring water degasses downstream and deposits travertine dams and pools. These features provide extensive habitat for macroinvertebrates and rearing habitat for small fish. The creek still supports a native fish community, including [[Roundtail Chub]], desert sucker, Sonoran sucker, and speckled dace.

**Hydropower history.** Water users diverted nearly 98% of the flow just below the spring source around 1900. The [[Childs Power Plant]] (built 1909, three 800 kW generators) and the Irving plant (built 1916, one 1600 kW generator) together produced 4.2 MW for the Jerome/Clarkdale mining area. [[Arizona Public Service]] (APS) agreed in November 1999 to cease power production and restore full flow to the creek, with site restoration due by December 2009. Negotiations also led to a collaborative native fish salvage: about 10 miles of creek were treated with icthyocide, involving more than 90 individuals and volunteers and four federal agencies.

**Objectives.** The study compared trap types and capture devices, set removal goals, and identified the effort needed to significantly reduce crayfish populations. It also examined whether native chubs prey on young crayfish, a question still unresolved in the field.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** Two reaches, separated by natural falls 18–25 ft high that block upstream crayfish migration, were sampled: a 3 km section above Irving and a 2.5 km section below the falls, into the Irving outlet pool.

**Trap types.** Five trap types were tested:
- Commercial pillow traps (3/4" plastic-coated chicken wire, 2" funnel)
- Modified Gee minnow traps (2" funnel)
- Pillow traps of 3/4" chicken wire
- Commercial collapsible minnow traps (2" funnel)
- LiNi traps imported from Sweden

Collapsible minnow traps were the most practical: they were light, easy to carry into inaccessible areas, and easy to bait and empty. Pillow traps were bulkier and harder to transport, and some traps allowed smaller crayfish to escape. A "trap night" was defined as a 12-hour set.

**Bait and handling.** Traps were baited with about 25 g of dry dog food, plus fish, chicken, or crayfish scraps, and positioned in pools, runs, and near overhanging banks. Traps were checked and emptied each morning and evening. Captured crayfish were recorded by sex, carapace length (nearest mm), and weight, using a calibrated scale.

**Diel and seasonal activity.** Crayfish were primarily nocturnal. Daytime trap sets yielded only 43 crayfish (2.2% of the total). Ovigerous females were seen only in March and April, and reproduction occurs once a year in spring. The species has a maximum lifespan of about 3 years. CPUE declined in winter both above and below Irving, with the effect stronger above Irving, where water temperature is lower and more variable. The author notes this does not fit the temperature-driven pattern reported by [[Somers and Green]] for Ontario lakes, and suggests a seasonal activity cycle independent of temperature.

**Bycatch.** Despite crayfish sharing traps with native fish, no fish mortality or observed damage occurred among the 232 native fish captured, which is important given the declining status of southwestern fishes. See [[Bycatch Management]].

**Habitat and flow.** Below Irving, water temperature and quality are more constant and there is more habitat because of increased flow, which may explain higher crayfish abundance there. The author expects that after the plant closure, conditions below Irving will extend to the reach above the dam, creating prime habitat for crayfish growth.

**Dipnetting constraints.** Turbidity after rains, leaf litter and tannins in fall, and boulder substrate limited dipnet effectiveness. The method worked best in shallow pools and runs (under about 1 m deep) with bedrock, sand, or cobble, and required fine mesh (1/4" or smaller). Dipnets wore out after 6–8 hours of use.

## Key Findings

**Trapping results (2001–2004)**
- Overall catch varied strongly by year. The text reports 79 crayfish in 121 trap nights in 2001, rising to 1,019 crayfish in 1,100 trap nights in 2003 (OCR-uncertain figures). CPUE was lower in 2001, possibly due to an inexperienced trapper and traps that let small crayfish escape.
- From 2002 to 2004, CPUE declined while the average weight of crayfish increased in 2004. The author links this to reduced trapping pressure, which may reduce competition and speed growth. Brown et al. (1995) and Momot (1990) are cited as supporting density-dependent growth and biomass regulation.
- Sex ratio: overall, 45.5% males and 54.5% females. This contradicts the male dominance reported by [[Momot]] and [[Stuecheli]] (1991), suggesting different behavior in lotic and lentic systems.
- Paired-date CPUE comparisons (Table 5) showed higher catches below Irving in several sampling periods.

**Dipnetting results**
- Dipnetting accounted for about 32% of all crayfish caught.
- Dipnetted crayfish were much smaller: above Irving, mean weight was 9.5 g versus 29.4 g for trapped crayfish.
- Dipnetting is the only practical method for young-of-year crayfish, which stay in very shallow water (<1 cm observed along the bank) and do not enter traps.

**High flow effects**
- In July–August 2001, large numbers of young-of-year crayfish were caught by dipnet before a high flow event (about 1 m higher than normal flow over much of the area above Irving).
- After the event, dipnet CPUE dropped from 28/hr to 8/hr, and mean weight rose from 5.2 g to 20.1 g. The author suggests small crayfish were swept downstream.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Physical removal is labor-intensive and nearly impossible in remote Arizona streams. The search for a chemical control needs to be intensified.
- Intensive control may reduce crayfish size and make them more vulnerable to fish predation.
- High flow events may be the only natural limit on crayfish populations; natural flows and flooding should be allowed.
- Crayfish moved back into intensively trapped pools within a month, suggesting active movement in the water column. No crayfish were observed out of the water.
- Specific recommendations:
  - Fund development and licensing of a chemical crayfish control.
  - Focus removal on discrete reaches defined by barriers to crayfish movement.
  - Pair removal efforts with density and biomass monitoring to assess effectiveness.
- Further study is needed on crayfish movement and on barriers that could block migration.
