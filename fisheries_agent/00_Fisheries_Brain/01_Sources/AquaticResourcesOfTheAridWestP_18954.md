# Aquatic Resources of the Arid West: Perspectives on Fishes and Wilderness Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by John W. Sigler and William F. Sigler examines the fishes and aquatic ecosystems of the arid interior western United States and argues that their management must be treated as a biological problem, not just an administrative one. It describes extreme water events of the 1980s, the physical limits of desert aquatic life, and the decline of native fish through habitat loss, dams, diversions, and introduced species. Its central conclusion is that the region needs a new [[Biological Policy]] grounded in science and adequate funding, with priority given to [[Wilderness Areas]] that hold endemic fish.

## Background & Description

The chapter opens with flood and drought examples from the Bible and then turns to recent extremes. From 1983 to 1985, snowpack and flood waters in Utah, Nevada, Arizona, New Mexico, and parts of California and Idaho exceeded 200% of normal. By 1988 and 1989 conditions had reversed, and the Utah legislature convened a [[Bear River Legislative Task Force]] to study new dams. The authors argue that water crises in the western deserts will become continual by the end of the century.

Deserts are defined in several ways. One parameter is annual precipitation generally below 250 to 300 mm. Arizona (average annual precipitation 33 cm) and Utah (30 cm) are compared with Mississippi (142 cm). Average precipitation is misleading because most falls at high elevations, and evaporation from water bodies may exceed one meter per year. Utah has 64 listed fish species, of which 27 are native. Arizona has 99 listed species, many of them non-native.

Desert fishes show distinct body forms and tolerances:
- **Spring fishes** are chubby, with rounded fins, suited to slow or still water.
- **Stream fishes** have fusiform bodies and pointed fins.
- **Colorado River fast-water fishes** ([[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]]) have flattened heads and specialized humps.
- The **chiselmouth** (25 to 30 cm) scrapes periphyton with a chisel-like lower jaw.
- The **western mosquitofish** survived in 42°C spring water, but not at 49°C.
- Fishes of [[Pyramid Lake]] tolerate total dissolved solids of 5.5 ppt or more.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat loss and introduced species.** Early industry and agriculture across the West caused native fish habitat to be reduced or degraded, and exotic fishes outcompeted, preyed on, or spread disease to natives. In the mid-1800s, commercial fishing at [[Utah Lake]] was largely unrestricted. [[Cutthroat Trout]] disappeared first, then the June sucker and Utah Lake sculpin in the drought years of the mid-1930s. The authors attribute the cutthroat's fate mainly to habitat degradation rather than overfishing.

**Water diversions.** The [[Newlands Project]], completed in 1905, was the first federal irrigation system in the United States. It diverted water from the Truckee River to the Carson River. [[Derby Dam]] reduced the Lahontan cutthroat's range from 193 km in the main stem, plus about 95 tributary km, to 48 km between the dam and Pyramid Lake. Today the Pyramid Lake system supports one endangered species (cui-ui) and one threatened species (Lahontan cutthroat trout).

**The Colorado River.** The river's watershed covers 629,000 square km and flows 2190 km to the Gulf of California. Dams and diversions have transformed it, and the four large native fishes are now endangered or declining. The authors describe the change as too drastic and too rapid for them to adjust to. Flaming Gorge Lake favors lake trout, brown trout, and kokanee, and downstream reservoirs favor [[Largemouth Bass]], black crappie, bluegill, and striped bass.

**Potholes.** Isolated spring pools are especially vulnerable. Pumping of nearby aquifers lowered the water level in [[Devils Hole]], where the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] spawns on a shelf of about 19 square meters. A U.S. Supreme Court decision ordered the pumping terminated to protect the species.

**Resource competition.** Wilderness and public lands are managed by federal, state, and private entities. Arizona has two national parks, five national wildlife refuges, and six national forests. Utah has five national parks, two national wildlife refuges, and six national forests. Nonconsumptive wildlife users aged 16 and older rose from 93.2 million in 1980 to 134.7 million in 1985, an increase of 44.5%.

## Key Findings

- **Water extremes are the new normal.** The 1983–1985 floods and the later drought show that moving water between excess and shortage areas will be a core management task in the twenty-first century. Projects such as the [[Central Utah Project]] and the [[Central Arizona Project]] illustrate the scale of human alteration.
- **Desert fishes are highly specialized and fragile.** Many have narrow temperature ranges, small habitats, and short life spans. The [[Amargosa Pupfish]] lives two to three years and can produce eight to ten broods a year. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] lives twelve to fifteen years and breeds in a three- to four-week window.
- **Dams and diversions drove most native declines.** The Colorado River fishes and the Lahontan cutthroat trout lost habitat and connectivity. The authors argue these losses were not reversible by simple adjustment.
- **Introduced species often replaced natives.** Common carp was widely stocked in the late 1800s and is now often destructive in western marshes. The authors note that about ten of Utah's 37 introduced species have substantial recreational benefit, and that stocking policies are sometimes set without adequate knowledge of their effects.
- **Some species have benefited.** The [[Utah Chub]] thrives in fluctuating impoundments and ditches, with a range extended by human introduction. Its native range is the ancient Lake Bonneville basin.
- **Endemic lake fishes face local threats.** [[Bear Lake]] has four endemic fishes: the [[Bonneville Cisco]], Bonneville whitefish, Bear Lake whitefish, and Bear Lake sculpin. Dam-regulated outflows may be reducing lake nutrient levels, and tributary cutthroat habitat has been greatly reduced.
- **Threats are few but serious.** Species face extinction through predation, disease, competition and hybridization with exotics, and chemical poisoning. Ecosystems face destruction by filling, draining, pumping, and dredging, as well as thermal and flow changes.
- **Management is fragmented.** Overlapping federal, state, and local agencies produce conflicting policies. The authors reject a single-agency solution as a bureaucratic answer to a biological problem.
- **Recommended direction.** The authors call for a new biological policy that is science-based, adequately funded, and implemented quickly enough to halt extinctions and habitat loss. Priority should go to wilderness areas where resource values are most cherished and where small impacts could have large consequences.
