# Longfin Darter and Barcheek Darter Confirmed as Egg-Clustering Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This short note by [[Lawrence M. Page]], [[W. Lawrence Keller]], and [[Lynn E. Cordes]] (Illinois Natural History Survey) reports the first documented egg-clustering and male guarding behavior in two North American darters: the [[Longfin Darter]] (*Etheostoma longimanum*) and the [[Barcheek Darter]] (*E. obeyense*). Both were observed in May 1980 in stream nests located on the undersides of stones. The authors use these records to strengthen a broader review of [[Egg-Clustering Behavior]] across the genus [[Etheostoma]], which they argue has evolved independently in at least three subgenera.

## Background & Description

Darters (genus [[Etheostoma]], family [[Percidae]]) show several breeding strategies. Some bury eggs in substrate and abandon them, some attach eggs singly or in small groups to rocks or plants, and others deposit eggs in clusters beneath stones or logs, where the male guards them until hatching. Egg-clustering involves parental care and is considered the most complex form of breeding behavior in darters.

The authors state that egg-clustering has evolved in at least three non-sister subgenera: [[Boleosoma]], [[Nothonotus]], and [[Catonotus]]. It characterizes all species of Catonotus and Boleosoma and some, but not all, species of Nothonotus. Within Boleosoma, the note lists egg-clustering in *E. olmstedi*, *E. nigrum*, *E. perlongum*, and *E. longimanum*, and presumes it for the sibling *E. podostemone*. Within Catonotus, documented species include *E. flabellare*, *E. squamiceps*, *E. kennicotti*, *E. smithi*, *E. neopterum*, *E. striatulum*, *E. olivaceum*, *E. virgatum*, and *E. obeyense*. In Nothonotus, *E. maculatum* clusters its eggs, whereas *E. tippecanoe*, *E. camurum*, and *E. rufilineatum* bury them; the breeding habits of the remaining nine species are unknown.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Longfin Darter (Craig Creek, Virginia):** Searches were conducted on 23 May 1980 above and below New Castle, Craig County. Only one nest was found, despite capturing several large males in breeding coloration. Most females captured appeared to have already spawned. The nest was on the underside of a stone in moderately swift water at 17.5 °C and about 50 cm depth.

**Barcheek Darter (Smith Creek, Kentucky):** Searches were conducted on 21 May 1980 at the Route 696 bridge, 3 km SE of Albany, Clinton County. Most captured individuals were ripe females, suggesting the 1980 spawning peak had not yet occurred. Two large breeding males were found under slab stones. One had apparently established a territory but had no eggs present. The other guarded a nest in slow-flowing water at 18 °C and 40 cm depth.

## Key Findings

- **First documented egg-clustering in *E. longimanum*:** One nest was found in Craig Creek, containing about 600 eggs in a single-layer cluster, averaging 1.5 mm in diameter. The guarding male was almost entirely black and measured 56 mm.
- **First documented egg-clustering in *E. obeyense*:** A nest of about 100 eggs was guarded by a 64 mm SL male. The eggs were about 2 mm in diameter, and many contained eyed embryos.
- **Nest site preference:** Both species deposited eggs on the underside of stones, in moderate to slow current at depths of 40–50 cm and temperatures of 17.5–18 °C.
- **Phylogenetic relevance:** The authors argue that the distribution of clustering and guarding behavior is informative for reconstructing darter phylogenetic relationships, and that its independent evolution in at least three subgenera points to its importance for reproductive success.
- **Prior literature gaps:** [[Raney and Lachner]] (1943) studied the life history of *E. longimanum* but did not describe its egg deposition site.
