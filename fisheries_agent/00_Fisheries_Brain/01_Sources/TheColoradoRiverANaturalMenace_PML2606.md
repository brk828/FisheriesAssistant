# Colorado River Basin Water Resources Development Program (c. 1944–1946 Planning Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This scanned federal planning compilation, assembled by multiple agencies of the U.S. Government, sets out a coordinated program for the water resources of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It covers stream gaging, ground-water investigation, water quality, reservoir siting, recreation, fish and wildlife, grazing, mineral resources, Indian irrigation projects, land classification, forest watersheds, and hydropower. Its core message is that reliable, unbiased, long-term hydrologic data are a prerequisite for planning, operating, and allocating the basin's water among the states and Mexico. Fish and wildlife content is one section among many; the document is primarily a water-resources and multiple-use planning report rather than an ichthyological study.

## Background & Description

The report opens with the principle that factual information on the quantity and quality of water must be systematically collected, without bias toward any use or agency, over many years. The [[United States Geological Survey]] is presented as the investigatory body whose records are regarded as reliable, funded by Congress and supplemented by cooperating states, municipalities and other federal agencies. Field offices are maintained in the basin (Las Vegas, Phoenix, Safford, Tucson) and outside it (Salt Lake City, Logan, Cheyenne, Denver, Santa Fe, Albuquerque, Los Angeles).

The basin is divided into an Upper Basin (about 110,000 square miles, defined by the [[Colorado River Compact]]) and a Lower Basin. The report is organized by subject (surface water, ground water, quality, utilization) and by agency section. Sections cover the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[National Park Service]], [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Grazing Service]], [[Bureau of Mines]], [[Office of Indian Affairs]], [[Federal Power Commission]], [[General Land Office]] and [[Forest Service]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fish and wildlife content describes the basin's cold-water trout streams in the upper reaches of the Green, Colorado, Gunnison and San Juan rivers. Cutthroat and brook trout dominate high-elevation headwaters, rainbow trout lower reaches, and brown trout occur in several places, notably the Gunnison River. Channel catfish replace trout downstream, and the lower Colorado supports largemouth bass, carp, catfish and sunfish in reservoirs. The report warns that reservoirs with large fluctuations and turbid, shifting river bottoms yield little fish food and poor spawning habitat.

Other environmental concerns include accelerated erosion and gullying, salinity increases, evaporation losses, and the loss of valley-bottom water to non-beneficial vegetation (phreatophytes such as salt cedar). Mineral-related pollution from mine wastes and smelters is noted as a local threat. Forest watersheds are described as producing most of the basin's annual water yield, and riparian vegetation and grazing practices are linked to stream flow and sediment.

## Key Findings

- **Data mandate**: Systematic, unbiased, multi-year stream, reservoir and ground-water records are essential; the USGS is the designated neutral collector.
- **Gaging network (Upper Basin)**: 252 stream-gaging stations were operating, with an estimated 85 additional stations needed; annual operating cost of roughly $600 to $1,000 per station was cited. Records are published in Water Supply Papers.
- **Lower Basin network**: 101 gaging stations were operating in 1944, with 29 additional stations recommended and about $112,000 per year needed for operation.
- **Estimated costs**: A three-year Upper Basin program was costed in tables, and a Lower Basin program was costed for five years, with individual items for surface water, ground water and water quality.
- **Water supply**: Upper Basin transmountain diversions were about 170,000 acre-feet annually, with several hundred thousand acre-feet more expected from the Colorado–Big Thompson project.
- **Irrigation**: About 700,000 acres were irrigated in the Green River basin per the report, with substantial additional irrigable land.
- **Ground water**: Lower Basin ground-water pumping exceeded 2,000,000 acre-feet in 1945 (as stated), with some aquifers being depleted; valley vegetation consumed hundreds of thousands of acre-feet annually.
- **Reservoir and recreation proposals**: Many dam sites were evaluated for recreation value, including Glen Canyon, Bridge Canyon, Marble Gorge, Davis, Dewey, Split Mountain, Echo Park and Lake Mead-area proposals. The Park Service argued that Grand Canyon's scenic integrity should be protected and that dams would drown significant geological and archaeological features.
- **Fish and wildlife recommendations**: Reservoirs should include wildlife conservation pools, minimum releases to protect fish, screened diversions, fish ladders where feasible, and new hatchery capacity (including a trout-bass station at Page Springs, Arizona).
- **Grazing**: 60 grazing districts covered about 142 million acres of federal land, with over 7,000 permittees and substantial livestock use.
- **Indian projects**: Dozens of tribal irrigation projects were listed with present and ultimate irrigable acreages and diversions, notably the Gila River, Colorado River (Parker) and San Carlos projects; the Navajo development was described as urgent.
- **Hydrology**: Estimated virgin flow at Lee Ferry averaged about 16.1 million acre-feet annually (period 1897–1943), with Lake Mead losses and Colorado River flows at Laguna Dam estimated at about 16.5 million acre-feet annually.
- **Policy stance**: Planning should coordinate water, power, minerals, recreation and wildlife use, and stakeholders warned against reservoir developments that would destroy unique scenic or archaeological resources without prior survey.
