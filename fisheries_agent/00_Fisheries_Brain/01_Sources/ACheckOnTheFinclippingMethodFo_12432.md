# A Check on the Fin-Clipping Method for Estimating Fish Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1943 paper by [[Louis A Krumholz]], published by the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]] (Michigan Department of Conservation), tests a [[Fin Clipping]] [[Mark-Recapture]] method for estimating fish populations. The method, adapted from [[David H Thompson]]'s black crappie study, was checked by comparing a [[Schnabel Estimate]] from netting with a total count from [[Rotenone]] poisoning in the north basin of [[Twin Lake]], Michigan. The netting estimate was 9,698 fish and the poisoning count was 9,605, a difference of about 1 per cent. The author concludes that the agreement is encouraging but that further checks are needed.

## Background & Description

- **Historical marking:** [[Izaak Walton]]'s *The Compleat Angler* (1653) records [[Francis Bacon]] tying ribbons to young salmon to study age and homing. Calderwood (1902) reported that Fraser marked salmon by removing the adipose fin in 1829. Fin clipping had since been used widely on salmon, trout and other freshwater fish, mainly for migration, growth and survival studies.
- **Prior population work:** Thompson (Illinois Natural History Survey) estimated black crappie (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) numbers in Lake Senachwine, a backwater of the Illinois River, but his results and technique were unpublished. [[Underhill]] (1941) used the same formula to estimate a breeding population of [[Chub Sucker]]s, marking fish by removing scales instead of fins.
- **Study site:** Twin Lake, Oscoda County, Michigan, has two basins. The north basin (about 7.75 acres, maximum depth 35 ft) was studied. The south basin (10.1 acres, maximum depth 40 ft) is larger and has a more irregular shoreline. A barrier dam with an 8-ft central opening was built by Civilian Conservation Corps labor in March and April 1941. The opening was filled on August 28.
- **Species present:** The north basin held only four species: [[Largemouth Black Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]] and bluegill x pumpkinseed hybrids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** The shoreline is sandy. Deep bottoms are covered with pulpy peat. Fallen and waterlogged trees ("deadheads") are common along the shore. They restricted net placement and made [[Seining]] impracticable. Aquatic plants were abundant in shallow areas.
- **Fish behaviour and gear selectivity:** Small fish were mainly found along shore. The author cites [[Van Oosten]] (1935) to suggest that fish may avoid nets darkened by small mesh.
- **Water conditions:** The surface temperature was 70 °F when poisoning began at 8:45 a.m. on August 29, 1941.
- **Fish movement:** Observations showed fish passing both into and out of the north basin through the dam opening. The author assumed the movement was approximately equal and had little effect on the estimate.

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- **Marking:** The anal fin was clipped first. Later the left pectoral fin was removed. Cuts were made about one-eighth inch from the flesh. Marked fish were released at a central buoy to allow dispersal in any direction. Fish that died in nets were subtracted from the marked total. Dead fish found outside nets were subtracted without being counted as returns. The shoreline was searched twice daily. No fish were marked during the last ten days of netting.
- **Netting:** Four [[Fyke Net]]s (A, B, C, D) of different mesh sizes were set from July 29 to August 23, 1941, and reset several times. Net A was a small-mesh Wisconsin design. Nets B and C were tarred and larger-mesh. Net D was a small fyke with no lead. Only fish over 45 mm total length were included in the estimate.
- **Estimation:** A modified Schnabel formula was used, with A = fish examined on a given day, B = marked fish in the lake, and C = returns. The author notes that this formula gives high yields, but correcting for the bias was judged too laborious.
- **Creel census:** Anglers' catches were checked daily during the summer. No marked fish were recorded in angler catches, so no loss was attributed to anglers. No allowance was made for unmarked fish caught by anglers or found dead.
- **Poisoning:** On August 29, 1941, the north basin was treated with powdered derris root (5 per cent [[Rotenone]]). Two-thirds was applied to surface waters from an outboard motor's wake. One-third was delivered to deeper water by a new hose-and-funnel apparatus. Application ran from 8:45 a.m. to 12:45 p.m. Distressed fish were seen within 15 minutes. Dead fish were picked up for ten days.

**Results**

- **Netting estimate:** The total population of fish over 45 mm was estimated at 9,698 fish from the netting data (Table I, July 31 to August 23 sampling).
- **Poisoning count:** 9,605 fish over 45 mm were recovered from poisoning. Their total weight was 565.57 lb. A further 1,773 fry under 45 mm weighed 11.88 lb.
- **Agreement:** The netting estimate was about 1 per cent higher than the poisoning count.
- **Species estimates (Table IV):** Estimated populations were largemouth black bass 51, bluegill 16,832, pumpkinseed 1,360, hybrids 837, for a total of 19,080. Recovered counts were bass 159, bluegill 8,983, pumpkinseed 319, hybrids 144, for a total of 9,605. The author states that the species-level estimates do not match the poisoning counts, and that the total estimate is the only reliable result.
- **Recovery rate:** 86 per cent of marked fish assumed alive in the lake were recovered by poisoning.
- **Size structure:** Netting samples (n = 1,028) were mostly 101–200 mm. Poisoning samples (n = 1,927) had a wider size range, including more fish of 51–100 mm.

**Sources of error and limitations**

- Fish may die from marking, may be caught by anglers, or may escape through the dam opening before poisoning.
- Some dead fish may sink and be unrecovered. Others may be eaten by reptiles, birds or mammals.
- Gear selectivity: nets A and D caught many small fish but few large ones. Nets B and C caught about 80 per cent of all fish taken.
- The netting period was short for a species-level estimate.
- Schnabel estimates are biased high. A correction was not applied.

**Conclusions**

- The netting estimate closely matched the poisoning count, supporting the fin-clipping method in this first check.
- The author states that more checks are needed to prove the method's accuracy.
- Rotenone supplies were severely limited by the war, and no suitable substitute had been found. Further checks were planned when conditions allowed.
