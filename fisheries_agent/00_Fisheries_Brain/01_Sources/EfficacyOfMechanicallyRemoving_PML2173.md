# Efficacy of Mechanically Removing Nonnative Predators from a Desert Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #ictaluridae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This six-year study (2007–2012) tested whether annual mechanical removal of [[Nonnative Fishes]] by [[Electrofishing]] and seining could reduce nonnative predators and benefit native fishes in a 4.6-km reach of the West Fork [[Gila River]], southwest New Mexico. Results were mixed. Mass of [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] declined in the removal reach, but brown trout also declined at a reference site, so its decline could not be attributed to removal. [[Spikedace]] was the only native species that increased significantly. Nonnative mass changed little overall until a single 2012 catch of [[Flathead Catfish]] dramatically altered the trend. The authors concluded that moderate, sustained removal can benefit some natives, but immigration from surrounding reaches requires continued control. The effort cost about $17,700 per year.

## Background & Description

Native fish faunas across the American Southwest have declined sharply over the past century, mainly due to [[Habitat Alteration]] and introduced species. Desert streams are often seasonally dry, depend on reservoir releases that do not mirror pre-regulation flows, and are frequently disconnected from their floodplains. Piscicide eradication, as used at [[Fossil Creek]] in Arizona, was judged impractical on the upper Gila River because of high flow volume and environmental permitting requirements. The study therefore focused on non-chemical removal, specifically electrofishing and seining, with an effort the resource agencies could sustain every year.

The study area was a 4.6-km reach of lower West Fork Gila River within the [[Heart Bar Wildlife Management Area]]. Habitats included deep cottonwood-associated pools, cliff-face pools, runs, cobbled riffles and backwaters. Riparian vegetation was dominated by narrow-leaf cottonwood, coyote willow and Goodding willow. Early June conditions included water temperatures of about 22 °C, dissolved oxygen of about 8 mg/L, specific conductance of about 150 µS/cm and discharge below 1.0 m³/s. Two reference sites on the [[Middle Fork Gila River]] and West Fork were monitored for comparison. Their fish assemblages were similar, although West Fork water was usually about 5 °C cooler.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The native fish community in the removal reach comprised eight species: five cyprinids, two catostomids and one salmonid. Three species (spikedace, loach minnow and Gila trout) are federally protected, and headwater chub is state protected. Ten nonnative species were collected, including cyprinids, ictalurids, salmonids, a poeciliid and two centrarchids. Native fishes made up 81–98% of individuals in 2007 and at least 92% from 2008 to 2012 (Table II, with exact counts in the source). Sonora sucker dominated native mass in every year.

The authors linked the results to regional and extrinsic factors. Synchronous declines across sites in 2010, followed by a rebound in 2011, were associated with prolonged spring runoff and delayed spawning. The 2011 [[Miller Fire]] also produced ash-laden flows that killed fish before the 2012 sampling. Species mobility and immigration from adjacent reaches appear to have limited control of [[Smallmouth Bass]].

## Key Findings

- **Study design:** Two 5–6 person crews sampled the removal reach for 4–5 days each early June from 2007 to 2012. Reference sites were sampled by a 3-person crew within 10 days.
- **Capture efficiency:** Two-pass capture-recapture at the Middle Fork reference site indicated at least 30% of nonnative predator mass was captured each year. The authors estimated that 50% or more was removed from the reach.
- **Nonnative trends (removal reach):** Mass of yellow bullhead, rainbow trout and brown trout declined significantly. Smallmouth bass mass did not decline, likely because of immigration into the reach. Total nonnative mass did not change significantly. It declined only when the 2012 flathead catfish catch (11 large individuals, about 31 kg, 38% of total mass) was excluded as an outlier.
- **Native trends:** Spikedace was the only native species to increase significantly in the removal reach. Its response was consistent with its higher population turnover and its use of low- to moderate-velocity habitats shared with nonnative predators. Loach minnow, an obligate riffle-dweller, did not change.
- **Reference sites:** The only significant native trends were declines in desert sucker adults at West Fork and sub-adults at Middle Fork.
- **Costs:** Total field costs were about $106,320 over six years, or about $995/kg of nonnative fish removed. Annual costs were about $17,700.
- **Synthesis:** Mechanical removal can benefit some native species, but continued control is needed because nonnatives move in from surrounding reaches. The authors recommended continuing the effort, because options to conserve native fish faunas are limited and the native species' extant populations are essential.
