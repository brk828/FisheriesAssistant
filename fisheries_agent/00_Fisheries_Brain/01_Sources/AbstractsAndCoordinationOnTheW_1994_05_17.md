# Native Fish Assemblages of the Colorado River, Grand Canyon (Glen Canyon Dam Effects)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary

This 1994 American Fisheries Society (Western Division) special session compiles abstracts from the **Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES)** program, examining the status, ecology, and management of the native fish community of the **Colorado River through Grand Canyon National Park**. The overarching narrative documents the decline of the native ichthyofauna following closure of **[[Glen Canyon Dam]]** in 1963, which converted a warm, turbid, seasonally flooding river into a cold, clear, hydropeaking tailwater. Of eight historically native fish species, only **four to five remain** in the Grand Canyon reach, with the endangered **[[Humpback Chub]]** persisting as one of the last self-sustaining populations tied to the **[[Little Colorado River]] (LCR)** confluence. The collected papers span limnology/lower trophic levels, historical fish distribution, dam operation recommendations, life history/habitat use, early life-stage transport and mortality, and non-native fish interactions, culminating in a call for a coordinated **Native Fishes Recovery Plan**.

## Background & Description

- **[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)**: Evolutionarily specialized "big-river" cyprinid morphology adapted to swift, turbulent, historically warm-turbid river dynamics; transitions from shoreline-associated juvenile morphology/habitat use to offshore adult habitat use at **175–200 mm TL** (approx. 2–3 years of age).
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)** and **[[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*)**: Catostomid body plans suited to benthic feeding in swift habitats (eddy interfaces, runs, riffles); populations in the mainstem are adult-dominated, indicating low recruitment/reproductive success.
- **[[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)**: Small-bodied cyprinid, generalist habitat use, common throughout the study reach, associated with tributary inflows and tepid springs.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**: Endangered catostomid; distinctive dorsal keel morphology (not described in text but scientifically notable); **not captured** during the 1990–1993 GCES sampling effort, indicating functional extirpation from the mainstem study reach.
- Native fishes as a group developed "specific characteristics and morphology to match river system dynamics" of the pre-dam Colorado River — implying loss of selective pressures (turbidity, warmth, flood pulses) under regulated flow may decouple morphology from current environmental conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pre-dam conditions**: Flood-prone, turbid, seasonally warm river; native fish assemblage evolved under these dynamic hydrologic and thermal regimes.

**Post-dam (regulated) conditions** — driven by **[[Glen Canyon Dam]]** hypolimnetic releases:
- Daily "tidal" hydropeaking fluctuations replace seasonal flooding.
- Reduced mean and variance of water temperature (chronically cold, ~10°C vs. ~20°C in warm tributaries like the LCR).
- Reduced sediment transport, increased water clarity, exposure of hard-bottom substrata.

**Lower trophic level effects** ([[Limnology of Regulated Rivers]]):
- Clearwater reaches below the dam dominated by **[[Cladophora glomerata]]**, supporting epiphytic algae and grazers (Oligochaeta, *Physella* sp., Chironomidae, *Gammarus lacustris*).
- Turbid lower reaches dominated by rock-coating *Oscillatoria* spp. and filter-feeding **[[Simulium arcticum]]** (buffalo gnat).
- Near-absence of Ephemeroptera, Odonata, Hemiptera, Plecoptera, Coleoptera, Trichoptera — taxa common in tributaries/other SW rivers.
- Standing biomass of benthos shows "stairstep" decline downstream, tied to turbidity inputs from tributaries (notably the **Paria River**, 26 km below the dam) and light (PAR) availability.

**Reproductive/nursery habitat**: Successful native fish reproduction largely restricted to **warm tributaries** (especially the LCR) due to year-round cold mainstem temperatures; larvae drift or are flood-transported into the mainstem, where many suffer "cold coma" and elevated mortality. Laboratory trials showed significant growth depression in humpback chub at 10°C vs. 20°C.

**Adult habitat use**:
- Adult humpback chub: primarily large eddy complexes (85% of captures, 80% of radio-contacts), though these comprise only ~20% of available mainstem habitat.
- Subadults: talus/vegetated shorelines and eddy return channels.
- High site fidelity: 69 radiotagged adults moved an average of only 1.5 km over ~93 days, with major movements tied to LCR spawning migrations (Feb–May) and post-spawn dispersal (June–July).
- Diel habitat partitioning in LCR: adults use deeper (>1 m), high-cover habitat by day, broader habitat at night; YOY and speckled dace use shallow, open habitat, most active in daylight.

**Diet**: Adult humpback chub feed primarily on simuliids (62% by volume), *Gammarus lacustris* (24%), midges (13%), plus annelids, terrestrial insects, and algae (1%) — roughly proportional to drift composition.

**Non-native fish community** ([[Non-Native Fish Interactions]]):
- Pre-dam: warmwater exotics (channel catfish, carp) competed with/preyed on natives.
- Post-dam: cold-water stocked species (dominant: rainbow trout) replaced warmwater exotics.
- Documented predation on humpback chub by **brown trout**, **channel catfish**, and **rainbow trout**.
- Striped bass migrate seasonally from Lake Mead into western Grand Canyon during warmer periods.
- Turbidity pulses from LCR floods reduce rainbow trout abundance/condition below the confluence, potentially providing native fish with refuge from predation.

## Key Findings

- **Species status**: Of 8 native fish species historically present, only 4–5 remain in Grand Canyon: humpback chub, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, speckled dace (razorback sucker present in very low numbers/not captured). **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**, **[[Roundtail Chub]]**, and **[[Bonytail]]** are extirpated from this reach.
- **Sampling data (GCES, Oct 1990–Nov 1993)**: ~28,200 fish captured; 41% native, 59% non-native (12 introduced species present). Rainbow trout most abundant overall (39%), followed by humpback chub (22%) and flannelmouth sucker (10%). No razorback suckers captured.
- **Humpback chub population structure**: Self-sustaining population concentrated in lower 14 km of the LCR and a 30-km mainstem reach around the LCR inflow; 11 additional aggregations of pre-adults/adults found at warm tributary inflows, tepid springs, or suitable habitat elsewhere.
- **Recruitment bottleneck**: Cold mainstem temperatures and daily flow fluctuations (dewatering/submerging backwaters) impose severe physiological costs and elevated mortality on drifting larvae/post-larvae, effectively limiting reproduction to warm tributaries.
- **Management recommendations** (multiple papers converge on):
  1. Minimize daily flow fluctuations from Glen Canyon Dam.
  2. Adjust seasonal hydrology to mimic natural pre-dam patterns.
  3. Deliver controlled floods to favor natives over non-natives.
  4. Consider dam modifications for seasonal water temperature increases and sediment augmentation.
  5. Develop a formal **Native Fishes Recovery Plan** with adaptive management, long-term monitoring, and explicit targets — while accepting that full restoration of the pre-1850 assemblage is not feasible given the scale of anthropogenic ecosystem change.
- **Ecosystem framing**: The session was explicitly structured to build "from the general to the specific," moving from geomorphology/hydrology and limnology through historical fish distribution, dam operations, life history, early life-stage ecology, and non-native interactions, synthesized in a concluding paper by **[[Richard Valdez]]**.
- **Overall outlook**: Authors conclude that anthropogenic alteration (dam operations, tributary chemical/hydrologic changes, non-native invasions) is too extensive to permit recovery of the pre-dam ecosystem; management must instead target a new, balanced native/non-native equilibrium under current environmental conditions.
