## Executive Summary

This document is **not a primary field or laboratory study of fish species** but rather an internal university memorandum from Arizona State University's Department of Zoology (1994) addressing curriculum reform for a "Wildlife Conservation Biology" degree. It is included here because it explicitly codifies **federal and state professional qualification standards for Fishery Biologists and Wildlife Biologists**, and outlines an "Aquatic Emphasis" track including [[Ichthyology]], [[Limnology]], and aquatic entomology coursework. The memo proposes renaming the curriculum to "Conservation Biology," restructuring aquatic/terrestrial course requirements, and creating a fifth-year Master of Natural Science (MNS) program with defined aquatic science emphases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While no specific ecosystem or species niche is described, the memo defines the **academic and professional "niche" of the Fishery Biologist** role as recognized by U.S. federal hiring standards, which requires formal training spanning:

- **Zoology** (15 semester hours): invertebrate zoology, comparative anatomy, histology, physiology, embryology, advanced vertebrate zoology, genetics, entomology, parasitology.
- **Fishery Biology** (6 semester hours): fishery biology, [[Ichthyology]], [[Limnology]], oceanography, algology, planktonology, marine/freshwater ecology, invertebrate ecology, and fishery population dynamics.
- **Physical/Mathematical Sciences** (15 semester hours): chemistry, physics, mathematics, statistics.

This framework positions academic aquatic science training as directly linked to employment within agencies engaged in **freshwater and marine resource management**, connecting concepts such as [[Aquatic Ecosystem Management]] and [[Fishery Population Dynamics]].

## Key Findings

- **Federal Standards**: The U.S. federal "Fishery Biologist" classification requires ≥30 semester hours in biological/aquatic sciences and 15 hours in physical/mathematical sciences, explicitly including [[Ichthyology]] and [[Limnology]] coursework.
- **Curriculum Renaming**: Recommendation to rename "Wildlife Conservation Biology" to "[[Conservation Biology]]," dropping restrictive terminology ("wildlife," "fisheries") while retaining historically recognized courses like Mammalogy, Ornithology, Limnology, and Ichthyology to satisfy federal/state hiring qualifications.
- **Course Consolidation**: Proposes merging ZOL 411 (Terrestrial Wildlife) and ZOL 413 (Aquatic Resources) into a unified two-semester course, "Biology and Conservation of Natural Resources I & II," to eliminate the anachronistic terrestrial/aquatic divide and better reflect integrated [[Conservation Biology]] principles (conservation genetics, population viability analysis, protected area management, risk assessment).
- **Aquatic Emphasis Track** (proposed elective cluster for a 5th-year MNS degree) includes:
  - CEE 340 Hydraulics and Hydrology
  - ZOL 454 Aquatic Insects
  - ZOL 354 Entomology
  - **ZOL 473 [[Ichthyology]]**
  - **BIO 426 [[Limnology]]**
  - GPH 511 Fluvial Processes
  - BOT 450 Phycology
  - **BIO 529 Advanced [[Limnology]]**
- **Field Training Deficiency**: ZOL 410 ("Techniques in Wildlife Conservation Biology") is identified as a curricular bottleneck due to insufficient offering frequency, delaying student graduation; the memo recommends alternative field-methods courses (e.g., Field Zoology, Research Techniques in Animal Behavior) and mandatory field-station/summer session components.
- **Fifth-Year MNS Proposal**: A new 30-credit-hour Master of Natural Science degree is proposed to formalize advanced training in [[Conservation Biology]], including aquatic, terrestrial, environmental management, law enforcement, policy/planning, recreation, and research emphases — intended partly to serve re-entry students and international students (particularly from Mexico) seeking credentialed aquatic/wildlife resource training.
- **Institutional Constraint**: The memo closes with a warning that the curriculum (including its aquatic science components) risks "collapse" without additional faculty hires or increased faculty participation in team-taught courses.
