# Arizona Riparian Council Newsletter, Vol. 11 No. 3 (1998): Desert Pupfish Exhibit and Riparian Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1998 newsletter of the [[Arizona Riparian Council]] (ARC) covers riparian conservation in Arizona. Its lead story is a new [[Desert Pupfish]] exhibit at the [[Phoenix Zoo]], which is designed to teach the public about threatened [[Riparian Habitat]]. Other items cover the American Rivers list of the most endangered U.S. rivers, a species profile of [[Damselflies]], the effort to restore flow to [[Fossil Creek]], a [[Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals]] ruling on nonpoint-source pollution under the [[Clean Water Act]], annotated riparian research publications, a membership statement, and a meeting calendar.

## Background & Description

**Desert pupfish.** The [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*) was listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in March 1986. Historically it was abundant in southern Arizona, southeastern California, and northern Mexico, occupying much of the lower [[Gila River Basin]] and lower [[Colorado River]] system (Minckley 1973). It lived in cienegas, springs, streams, and lake and river margins. Its decline is attributed to habitat loss or modification, introduction of exotic fishes, and pollution (USFWS 1993).

**Exhibit design.** The exhibit was approved by the Zoo and funded by the Phoenix Area Office of the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation through Arizona State University. It is a replica of an Arizona cienegas-type spring system, built with indigenous materials such as granite boulders and a small seep. Cottonwood and willow line a stream beside the Arizona Trail. Planted native species include cattails (*Typha* sp.), horsetail (*Equisetum* sp.), yerba mansa (*Anemopsis californica*), Huachuca water umbel (*Lilaeopsis recurva*, itself endangered), sedges, Arizona ash, desert hackberry, blue palo verde, saguaro, and soaptree yucca.

**Damselflies.** Damselflies are slender relatives of dragonflies in the arthropod order Odonata, which dates from the Jurassic. The order has three suborders: Zygoptera (damselflies), Anisoptera (dragonflies), and Anisozygoptera (two Asian species). Nymphs live in water, and adults hunt insects such as mosquitoes. The species profile is by Nancy E. McIntyre of the Center for Environmental Studies at ASU.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats to rivers.** The American Rivers list of the most endangered U.S. rivers is cited as the first 10 of 20. The threats it names are agricultural development, dams, channelization, poultry and hog waste, small hydropower dams, mining, water overuse, urban pollution, and nuclear waste contamination.

**Fossil Creek.** About 14 miles of flow have been diverted since 1909 to the Childs and Irving hydroelectric plants, leaving as little as 0.2 cfs in the stream. The power generated accounts for 0.1% of Arizona Public Service's (APS) annual output. The draft Environmental Assessment prefers continued operation, which would keep diverting 89% of natural streamflow.

**Riparian loss.** ARC was formed in 1986 because of alarming losses of Arizona's riparian areas. An estimated <10% of the state's original riparian acreage remains in natural form, making these among Arizona's rarest natural communities.

**Damselfly ecology.** Damselflies occur on all six temperate continents, with about 640 species worldwide. Forty-nine species are recorded from Arizona, and eight of them are known from no other U.S. state. One California species is federally listed as endangered and two others are "at risk." Nymphs are known to be sensitive to water clarity, very low pH, and low oxygen. How large-scale changes such as damming, draining, and development affect adult colonization is unknown.

**Pupfish exhibit ecology.** Approximately 30 pupfish were moved in autumn 1997 from a stock held in the moat by the cheetah exhibit. The fish have spawned and juveniles are abundant. Native toad tadpoles and aquatic insects have also been observed. The exhibit's signs address dewatering, pollution, and exotic species, and it is intended as a "living laboratory" for topics such as succession and food chains.

## Key Findings

**1. Pupfish exhibit and reproduction.** The new Phoenix Zoo exhibit established a self-sustaining captive population of [[Desert Pupfish]]. Stocked fish have spawned, and juveniles are abundant.

**2. Desert pupfish range and status.** Natural populations now persist only at Quitobaquito Springs (Arizona), parts of the [[Salton Sea]] and its tributaries (California), and scattered localities along the Rio Sonoyta and in the Laguna Salada basin (Mexico) (USFWS 1993). Recovery is still needed.

**3. Most endangered rivers (first 10 of 20).** American Rivers named the [[Hanford Reach]] of the Columbia River, the [[Missouri River]], the Pocomoke River, the Kern River, the Blackfoot River, the [[Colorado River Delta]], the Chattahoochee River, the Lower Snake River, the Apple River, and Pinto Creek. Some Missouri River fish and wildlife species are at less than 10% of historical levels. The Pocomoke is affected by Pfiesteria, a toxic microbe linked to poultry waste that has been killing fish since 1996. The Apple River threat is two factory hog farms housing 12,000 hogs.

**4. Fossil Creek flow diversion.** Continued operation of the Childs-Irving Project diverts 89% of natural flow. [[American Rivers]] was granted intervenor status by the [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] (FERC) on May 21, 1998. It then moved for a new environmental review, citing four inadequacies in the draft EA:
- failure to fully evaluate impacts to endangered species;
- failure to fully assess impacts on travertine deposits;
- inappropriate use of the Instream Flow Incremental Methodology (IFIM);
- failure to adequately consider decommissioning as an alternative.

The U.S. Forest Service believes decommissioning would be best for Fossil Creek's resources (comments dated September 25, 1997). A final FERC decision was expected early the following year.

**5. Nonpoint-source pollution ruling.** In *Oregon Natural Desert Association v. Dombeck* (1998), the Ninth Circuit reversed a district court decision. The lower court had held that cattle grazing was a nonpoint-source "discharge" requiring Clean Water Act §401 certification. The appeals court held that §401 covers only point sources. Its reasoning rested on the 1972 CWA amendments and §208 management plans. The Warm Springs Tribes had intervened for the plaintiffs. Arizona has a legislative provision for automatic §401 certification of grazing that meets best management practices (BMPs), but ADEQ has not yet adopted those BMPs in rule (A.R.S. §49-201.01 and .02).

**6. Wetland landfill ruling.** In *Resource Investments, Inc. v. U.S. Army Corps of Engineers* (1998), the Ninth Circuit reversed a lower court ruling. The court held that solid waste is not dredged or fill material under CWA §404, and that responsibility for the program lies with EPA or the states under RCRA. The reasoning may matter where more than one regulatory program applies to a riparian area.

**7. Riparian restoration principles (Kauffman et al. 1997).** Restoration should aim to return riparian areas to their predisturbance condition, considering soil and geomorphology, hydrology, and biota. Areas needing little effort can be restored passively by removing pressure such as grazing or pumping. The authors suggest waiting about 10 years before intervening. Active restoration should use native species and avoid exotics and in-channel manipulation.

**8. Debris-flow succession (Bowers et al. 1997).** On Grand Canyon debris flows aged between 5 and 3,1000 years (as printed), short-lived plant species dominated the youngest surfaces, and long-lived species increased with surface age. Early debris flows lack cryptogamic crusts, which the authors think form after 55 years (as printed). These crusts may later support woody species.

**9. Breeding birds and willows (Sanders and Edge 1998).** In eastern Oregon (1993–1994), continuous mesic shrub communities had the highest total bird abundance, species richness, and riparian-associated bird diversity. Densities of [[Willow]] flycatchers, yellow warblers, and song sparrows tracked willow volume. These species were absent from communities lacking willows.

**10. Damselfly research gaps.** Habitat requirements for many damselfly species are poorly understood. Researchers do not yet know how far adults disperse, what degree of habitat change they will tolerate, or how to manage riparian areas to support both adult reproduction and nymphal development. The newsletter calls for more research and for minimizing large-scale disturbance in the meantime.

**11. Membership and calendar.** ARC membership is open to anyone interested in riparian systems. Annual dues are $15. Upcoming events include the Desert Fishes Council Annual Meeting (November 12–15, 1998, Wahweap Lodge, Page, Arizona) and the Sixth National Watershed Conference (May 16–19, 1999, Austin, Texas).
