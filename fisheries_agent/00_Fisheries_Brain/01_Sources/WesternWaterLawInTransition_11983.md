# Western Water Law in Transition

#water-law #western-water-policy #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This article, a condensed version of a law review piece by [[Charles F. Wilkinson]] (University of Colorado-Boulder), argues that western water law is being reevaluated after more than a century dominated by the [[Prior Appropriation Doctrine]]. Traditional priorities (state-based appropriation, stable priority for historic uses, private rights, consumptive commercial uses, and subsidized irrigation water) are being challenged by population growth, groundwater depletion, pollution concerns, environmental and public-trust values, and Indian water rights. The author predicts a growing federal role, a shift toward [[Water Conservation]] as the main source of new supply, and wider recognition of environmental and public uses. The piece is a policy and legal analysis rather than fisheries research. It contains no species-level data, though it refers to fish and wildlife in the context of instream flows and salmon and steelhead harvest.

## Background & Description

**Historical foundations.** The prior appropriation doctrine arose from [[California Gold Rush]] mining camps, whose customs gave the first user of water an absolute priority. The [[Supreme Court of California]] approved these rules in *Irwin v. Phillips*, and Congress and the Rocky Mountain states followed. The doctrine contrasts with the [[Riparian Doctrine]] of the eastern United States and England, which protected landowners adjoining a watercourse and discouraged exporting water from the watershed. Under prior appropriation, junior users are cut off in shortage, there is no sharing, and streams can be drained, a passive policy that leaves use decisions to private users.

**Settlement and subsidies.** Annual precipitation below about 20 inches (51 cm), the minimum for dry farming, made irrigation essential across the arid region west of the 100th meridian. Private cooperatives could not finance storage dams and canals, so the federal government intervened through the [[Reclamation Act of 1902]]. The reclamation program was heavily subsidized. The [[United States Bureau of Reclamation]] supplies about 15 percent of western water. Special water districts deliver about half of western water and use tax-exempt bonds and acreage-based voting ("one acre, one vote"). Irrigation consumes nearly 90 percent of western water.

**Indian water rights.** The 1908 [[Winters Doctrine]] held that Indian water rights were reserved by implication when reservations were created, regardless of state diversion requirements. Such rights are senior in most western drainage basins. A Western States Water Council report for the Western Governors Association estimated Indian water rights could total more than 45 million acre-feet per year, over three times the annual flow of the [[Colorado River]]. One acre-foot equals 325,000 gallons.

**The Iron Triangle.** The author describes a political alliance of private western development interests and state agencies, federal water development agencies, and western-dominated congressional subcommittees. This "[[Iron Triangle]]" combined federally funded projects with state-law distribution favoring consumptive users.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article treats environmental values as a major shift in western water law. Modern legislatures recognize recreation and wildlife as beneficial uses, and many western states have adopted **instream flow programs** that keep water in streams for fish, wildlife, recreation, and aesthetics. The author notes these programs are still weak but likely to expand. Federal requirements also affect ecosystems. The [[Endangered Species Act]] requires sufficient flows to protect listed species, and laws regulating [[Pacific Salmon and Steelhead]] harvest in the Northwest have affected water allocations.

Groundwater and surface water are hydrologically linked, so separate management fails to reflect hydrologic reality. The author calls for conjunctive management. Overpumping can mine aquifers "much like oil or coal deposits," and the article cites the [[Oglala Aquifer]] as an interstate example where shortages forced valuable farmland out of production.

The article also cites reduced inflows and water loss at specific sites:

- **[[Mono Lake]]:** Los Angeles diversions from its feeder streams were limited under the public trust doctrine.
- **[[Salton Sea]]:** Return flows from the [[Imperial Irrigation District]] reached the sea, a loss the state board found wasteful.

## Key Findings

**1. Federal role is growing.** The author expects an expanded federal presence driven less by subsidies (which will decline) than by interstate conflicts, river basins that cross state borders, and treaties with Indian tribes, Canada, and Mexico. Public lands make up 48 percent of land in the 11 western states and shape water policy through erosion control, watershed protection, wild and scenic river designations, and reserved rights.

**2. Conservation is the primary new supply.** The article argues that [[Water Conservation]] of existing supplies will be the most important policy movement. The [[United States Soil Conservation Service]] estimates irrigation waste at 24 million acre-feet per year, almost double the Colorado River's annual flow and more than the total consumed by all municipalities and industries nationally. The author recommends tax incentives, low-interest loans, and pricing policy to phase out subsidies, with compulsion where incentives fail.

**3. Courts and legislatures are shifting toward public rights.** Key developments include:

- ***National Audubon Society v. Superior Court*** (Mono Lake case): The California Supreme Court held that the public trust doctrine modified long-standing diversion rights.
- **Imperial Irrigation District decision (1984):** The State Water Control Board found the district wasted water by letting more than 1 million acre-feet, a 38 percent loss, enter the Salton Sea. The district had diverted about 2.9 million acre-feet annually. Negotiations with the [[Metropolitan Water District of Southern California]] could fund conservation in exchange for salvaged water.
- **Montana public-interest legislation (1985):** Public-interest criteria apply to new permits and changes, with stricter standards for diversions above 4,000 acre-feet, plus a limited water-leasing program.
- **Fort Peck-Montana Compact (1985):** The Montana legislature approved a compact with the Assiniboine and Sioux tribes, produced by the Montana Reserved Water Rights Compact Commission.
- **Arizona Groundwater Management Act (1980):** The act aims to bring groundwater withdrawal down to recharge rates by 2025, with management plans, farm water duties, land retirement, new-acreage restrictions, metropolitan conservation, and groundwater withdrawal fees.

**4. Indian water rights are a central unresolved issue.** Some tribes oppose quantifying future reserved rights, while others prefer negotiated settlements to protracted litigation. The author argues western water planning cannot ignore these rights.

**5. Water budget and use data (1975 and 1978 U.S. Water Resources Council).** Average precipitation over the conterminous United States is about 4,200 billion gallons per day (bgd), or roughly 30 inches per year. About two-thirds evaporates immediately. The one-third that accumulates (1,450 bgd) is the only developable portion, and with existing facilities less than half, about 675 bgd, is available in most years. In 1975, total withdrawals were 398 bgd (under 60 percent of the developable potential, 85 percent fresh and 15 percent saline). Irrigation withdrew 159 bgd, and water consumed nationally was 107 bgd. Irrigation consumed 86 bgd, over 80 percent of all consumption.

**6. Withdrawal and consumption declined from 1980 to 1985** (U.S. Bureau of the Census, via *Statistical Abstracts*):

| Measure | 1980 | 1985 |
|---|---|---|
| Total withdrawals | 450 bgd | 400 bgd |
| Per capita withdrawal | 1,953 gpd | 1,650 gpd |
| Total consumption | 100 bgd | 95 bgd |
| Per capita consumption | 440 gpd | 380 gpd |

**7. Conceptual shift.** The author concludes that water law must recognize a wider set of "water users" and "water organizations," including recreational rafters, trout anglers, artists, the Wilderness Society, and the Navajo Tribe, not only farmers, power plants, and water districts.
