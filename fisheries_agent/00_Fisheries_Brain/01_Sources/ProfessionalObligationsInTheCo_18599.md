# Professional Obligations in the Conservation of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This invited editorial by [[Edwin P. Pister]], a retired state agency fishery biologist, argues that species and habitat endangerment has reached a point where complacency is unacceptable. The author calls for [[Scholarship of Application]] to receive at least equal consideration with [[Scholarship of Discovery]] in academic reward systems, for stronger communication between university researchers and agency biologists, and for changes to undergraduate curricula that would instill ethics and values. The paper draws on the author's career in desert fish conservation, the history of the [[Desert Fishes Council]], and reviews of University of California faculty reward policy. It is a position paper rather than a research study, and it reports no original data on fish populations.

## Background & Description

The essay is framed by a metaphor of six architects who ignore a collapsing house while some colleagues buy repair materials and others choose wallpaper. The author compares this to biologists, some focused on conservation and others on esoteric research with little concern for the habitats their study organisms depend on.

The author describes nearly 40 years of work "in the trenches" of environmental battles in the American Southwest. Key historical episodes include:

- **Green River project (1960s):** A consortium of agencies attempted to eradicate carp with rotenone before introducing rainbow trout into [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] and Fontenelle Reservoir (Utah and Wyoming). The detoxification treatment below the reservoirs failed, eliminating most aquatic life along 500 miles of a major [[Colorado River]] tributary. Most native fishes have since recovered to pre-treatment levels where suitable habitat remains.
- **Owens Pupfish:** On August 18, 1969, the author held the only remaining individuals of the [[Owens Pupfish]] in two buckets.
- **Genus Empetrichthys:** Shortly afterward, all remaining members of the genus were housed in a horse watering trough on the Desert National Wildlife Refuge north of Las Vegas, Nevada.
- **Devils Hole Pupfish:** The [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] was saved from extinction only after legal actions that reached the U.S. Supreme Court.

The essay also cites [[Robert Rush Miller]]'s 1961 paper *Man and the Changing Fish Fauna of the American Southwest* as an early warning, and [[Alfred Russel Wallace]]'s 1863 comparison of species extinction to lost letters in the historical record.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author identifies several pressures on aquatic biota:

- Growing human populations. The author notes the United States is now the world's third most populous nation.
- High resource consumption in Western nations, and rising standards of living in developing countries.
- Economic forces tied to natural-resource industries.
- Pollution, notably industrial pollution from Union Pacific Railroad yards at Green River City.
- Habitat change from dam construction, including Flaming Gorge (1962) and Fontenelle (1964).
- Continued efforts by some interests to introduce predatory non-native sport fishes throughout the Colorado system and elsewhere.

The author notes that extinction rates escalated during the twentieth century, citing [[Williams and Miller]] (1990).

International context: a symposium at the University of Lancaster, "The Biology and Conservation of Rare Fish" (Wheeler & Sutcliffe 1990), drew representatives of more than 30 nations. The author judged that it defined the problem but produced little practical solution. The same message was repeated at the World Fisheries Congress in Athens, Greece (Philipp et al. 1995).

## Key Findings

**Institutional and professional developments**

- The [[Desert Fishes Council]] was founded in 1969 at Death Valley National Monument. It has grown to more than 300 members, including university researchers, government scientists and managers, and private-sector members.
- Conservation sessions at the [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]] meetings drew strong interest. A noontime workshop planned for 50 people at the 1996 meeting drew 135, and others were turned away. The 1997 Seattle session on conservation issues drew an audience estimated at over 300.
- The [[Society for Conservation Biology]] is also involved in these concerns.

**University reward policy**

- A 1990 [[University of California]] task force, charged by Senior Vice President William Frazer at the direction of President David P. Gardner, reviewed faculty advancement criteria.
- The task force found that current practice gave neither sufficient encouragement nor reward for the broad range of university activities. It recommended that applied research receive commensurate reward, that publications be evaluated rather than merely enumerated, and that professional and community service be recognized.
- In the author's reading, the report called for scholarship of application to receive at least equal weight with scholarship of discovery in the faculty rewards system.

**Arguments and recommendations**

- Advocacy need not compromise scientific objectivity. The author argues that researchers' fears of damaging their reputations through advocacy are a major source of inertia.
- Better agency–university communication is needed, because agencies depend on university data for recovery and habitat programs.
- Undergraduate curricula should integrate ethics, values, and the humanities with the natural sciences. The author cites [[Starker Leopold]]'s broad curriculum at Berkeley as a model and suggests at least courses in environmental ethics.
- The author quotes [[David Orr]] on the "boxes" of specialized knowledge, and [[Aldo Leopold]]'s land ethic: "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the beauty, integrity, and stability of the biotic system."
- Einstein's warning against "drilling holes where drilling is easy" is applied to trivial research pursued for career advancement.

**Conclusion**

The author concludes that change will take generations rather than years, and that resistance from the status quo will be strong. Preserving aquatic biodiversity depends on conservation-oriented research combined with input from environmental philosophers, who must persuade the public of its moral obligation to protect all life forms.
