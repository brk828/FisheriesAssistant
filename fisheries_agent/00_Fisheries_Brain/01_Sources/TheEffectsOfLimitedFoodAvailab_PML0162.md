# The Effects of Limited Food Availability on the Striped Bass Fishery in Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This preprint, presented at a 1981 symposium on Colorado River aquatic resource management, examines how rapid growth of a [[Striped Bass]] population in [[Lake Mead]] reduced the abundance of its primary forage fish, [[Threadfin Shad]], and whether food limitation may now constrain the fishery. Striped bass angler catch rose sharply in the late 1970s, but by 1980 fish were emaciated, showed low condition factors and liver abnormalities, and threadfin shad had nearly disappeared from the limnetic zone. The authors attribute the decline to predation combined with low primary productivity following the formation of [[Lake Powell]], and they warn that future phosphorus reductions could further limit shad production.

## Background & Description

Striped bass are native to the Atlantic coast and were introduced to the lower [[Sacramento River]] in 1879 and later to reservoirs such as [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]] (South Carolina, 1954), Lake Havasu, Lake Mead and Lake Powell. They were stocked in Lake Mead in 1969, in response to declines in the [[Largemouth Bass]] fishery of the 1960s and in threadfin shad, and natural reproduction was documented by 1971.

A stocking program of [[Rainbow Trout]] and other salmonids was started to exploit surplus threadfin shad. Trout made up 13 to 19% of the total angler catch from 1970 to 1975, then fell to 1% in 1976 despite increased stocking. Rainbow trout occurred in a fraction of striped bass stomachs, and the decline was attributed mainly to striped bass predation. Threadfin shad made up 50% of striped bass diet.

Lake Mead was formed in 1935 by [[Hoover Dam]] and spans about 183 km of the Colorado River. It comprises the Gregg, Temple and Virgin basins (Upper Basin), the Boulder Basin (Lower Basin), and two embayments: the Overton Arm and [[Las Vegas Bay]], which receives secondary-treated effluent from the Las Vegas metropolitan area via the Las Vegas Wash. Trophic status ranges from oligotrophic (Upper Basin) and mesotrophic (Boulder Basin) to mesotrophic-eutrophic (Las Vegas Bay).

Key morphometric values (Table I) include a maximum operating level of 374 m, maximum depth of 180 m, mean depth of 55.4 m, surface area of 650 km², volume of 36.0 × 10⁹ m³, maximum length of 181 km, maximum width of 28 km, shoreline development of 9.7, discharge depth of 81 m, annual discharge of 9.5 × 10⁹ m³, and a storage ratio of 3.9 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Threadfin shad are planktivores whose production is closely tied to phytoplankton productivity. Phytoplankton productivity in Lake Mead declined considerably after Lake Powell was formed, and most of the reservoir is now oligotrophic to mesotrophic. Shad are therefore highly vulnerable to over-exploitation by striped bass.

Nutrient loading is spatially uneven. Roughly 50% of the total phosphorus load in 1977–78 came from the Las Vegas Wash inflow. An advanced wastewater treatment plant under construction is expected to reduce phosphorus loading to Las Vegas Bay and Boulder Basin, likely lowering phytoplankton growth there.

Chlorophyll-a averaged 1–7 mg/m³ in the Upper Basin and about 3 mg/m³ in Boulder Basin during 1977–78. Near the sewage inflow in Las Vegas Bay, chlorophyll-a averaged 7 mg/m³ and reached up to 25 mg/m³ during summer. Threadfin shad distribution was directly related to chlorophyll-a in Salt River reservoirs, and zooplankton standing crops in Lake Mead generally tracked chlorophyll-a, though seasonal and group-specific responses varied. Zooplankton abundance in Boulder Basin declined markedly after 1971. Summer 1981 experiments suggested chlorophyll-a was sufficient for Daphnia growth in Las Vegas Bay but not in Boulder Basin.

Lake temperatures rarely fall below 12 °C, well above the threadfin shad critical minimum of 9 °C, so winter kill does not explain the shad decline.

## Key Findings

- **Striped bass boom:** Angler catch rose from about 1,500 in 1977 to over 400,000 in 1979. Striped bass share of the total catch increased from 4.1% to 40.1% between 1978 and 1979, and angler effort rose from 15.5% to 51.5% of the total.
- **Condition decline:** In 1980 the overall condition factor dropped markedly, a decline not explained by a shift in size structure. Fishermen reported fewer large fish and many emaciated individuals.
- **Die-offs and pathology:** Annual spring die-offs of 56–71 cm fish, mostly males after spawning, occurred in 1976–79. Autopsies in July 1980 found thin fish with few parasites and few skeletal abnormalities, but over half had liver abnormalities. The authors concluded a nutritional problem likely existed and food may have become limiting.
- **Threadfin shad collapse:** Echo-sounding showed abundant shad schools in the early 1970s, but by 1980 they were nearly absent from limnetic waters. Scattered schools remained in littoral areas and in Las Vegas Bay near the sewage inflow.
- **Cause of decline:** Predation by striped bass is proposed as the main cause, with over-exploitation probably occurring between 1975 and 1979. Winter kill was ruled out.
- **Parallel with Santee-Cooper:** The Lake Mead pattern closely resembles the 1950s collapse at Santee-Cooper Reservoir, where shad fell 30 to 50% after striped bass numbers rose.
- **Future risk:** Reduced nutrient loading may further lower productivity, and the authors call for trophic studies to determine whether phytoplankton and shad production can sustain a productive striped bass fishery.

**Methods note:** Echo-sounding transects were run at about 5 mph for 5–15 min, at least monthly, in 1972, 1974–75 and 1980, using a Furuno recording echo-sounder at 50 kHz with a 28° beam angle. Striped bass data came from Nevada Department of Wildlife creel census reports. Fish trapping and mid-water trawling supported species identification.
