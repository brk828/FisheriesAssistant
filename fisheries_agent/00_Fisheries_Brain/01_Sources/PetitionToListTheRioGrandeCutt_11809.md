# Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout Endangered Species Petition (1998)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1998 petition, filed by the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]] and four other conservation groups (Petition #37), asks the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to list the [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]] as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and to designate [[Critical Habitat]] concurrently. The petitioners argue the subspecies has lost most of its historic range, survives in about 92 isolated populations, and faces habitat loss from [[Livestock Grazing]], [[Water Diversions]], [[Road Building and Logging]], and [[Hybridization]] with non-native trout. The document also reviews the subspecies' taxonomy, natural history, and two decades of inadequate federal and state management responses.

## Background & Description

**Identity and relatives.** The Rio Grande cutthroat is a strikingly coloured salmonid. It is closely related to the [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] (*O. c. stomias*), the [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] (*O. c. pleuriticus*), and the [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] (*O. c. bouvieri*). Two forms are recognised: a "Pecos form" with large spots (up to 30 mm) and about 175 lateral-line scales, and a "Rio Grande form" that more closely resembles *pleuriticus*. Both usually have 61 or 62 vertebrae and 30–50 pyloric caeca. Hyoid (basibranchial) teeth are weak or vestigial; slight hybridization with rainbow trout removes them in 50% or more of individuals.

**Taxonomy.** Girard described the subspecies as *Salar virginalis* in 1856 from specimens taken at the eastern end of the San Luis Valley. Cope later named *Salmo spilurus* from nearby specimens. Jordan's 1891 misplacement of the population in Utah shows the taxonomic confusion noted in the document. Shiozawa and Williams (1992) place the group in a closely linked genetic subgroup with the Yellowstone cutthroat.

**Historic distribution.** The first written account (1541, Coronado's expedition) describes a trout-rich stream, probably Glorieta Creek near present-day Santa Fe. Stumpff and Cooper (1996) suggest the subspecies may have occupied 40 hydrologic sub-basins in Colorado and New Mexico. The document argues that the historic range was larger than currently suitable trout water, citing lost reaches such as the [[Rio San Jose]] watershed.

**Taxonomic and regional histories.** Texas reports of trout in the [[Devils River]] and [[Limpia River]] are recorded but poorly documented. The only self-reproducing trout in Texas today is non-native rainbow trout in McKittrick Canyon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.** The subspecies needs high dissolved oxygen, low summer water temperatures, and clean gravel for spawning. It requires riffles for food production, and pools for overwintering and summer rest, in roughly equal numbers. Riparian shade and cover are essential. The document notes a positive correlation between roadless areas and healthy populations.

**Diet and life history.** It is insectivorous, feeding on invertebrate drift, with midge larvae, caddisflies, and mayflies prominent in the [[Rio Chiquito]]. Small fish eat zooplankton; terrestrial insects are taken in summer. Its food web is largely allochthonous, depending on leaf litter and riparian vegetation. Spawning occurs April to July during snowmelt, and some females spawn only every other year. Individuals live up to nine years (usually six), maturing at four years versus three for exotic trout. Females produce 200 to 4,000 eggs, depending on size.

**Competitors and predators.** The subspecies evolved with minnows such as the [[Rio Grande Chub]] and long-nose dace. Predators included garter snakes, herons, otters, and raccoons. Exotic [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], and [[Rainbow Trout]] compete directly and spawn earlier or in more stable conditions. The document argues that the timing of cutthroat spawning, during high spring flows, disadvantages the subspecies. Brown trout spawn earlier and their eggs are less exposed to flooding.

**Fragmentation and isolation.** Rinne (1995) describes gene flow among populations as "virtually non-existent." Stumpff and Cooper's data indicate that the remaining populations are scattered across [[Habitat Fragmentation]] in small headwater stream segments. The petition argues that small, isolated populations are vulnerable to inbreeding depression, [[Allee Effect]] dynamics, and [[Stochastic Events]] such as fire, flood, and drought.

## Key Findings

**Range and status**
- Stumpff and Cooper (1996) identified 92 extant populations, 53 in New Mexico and 39 in Colorado. Only 69 are native remnants.
- The subspecies is absent from up to 95% of its historic range (Stumpff and Cooper 1996). Its New Mexico range has declined by 91% on selected federal lands, a figure the petitioners argue understates the actual loss.
- Of 2,343 stream miles and 6,320 lake acres of suitable federal habitat in New Mexico, only 212 stream miles (9%) and no lake acres are occupied.
- Stumpff and Cooper (1996) report 4 populations as secure-expanding, 29 as secure/stable, 8 as declining, and 51 as unknown.
- Of 17 occupied sub-basins, 88.2% are classed "at risk," 5.8% "stable-secure," and 5.8% unknown.
- In Colorado, Harig and Fausch (1996) documented 68 stream and 25 lake populations. Of the stream populations, 18% (2) are extirpated and 43% (29) are insecure. Only 13% (12) of all historic and recent populations are known to be secure.

**Population fragmentation**
- Roughly 50 of 53 remaining New Mexico populations are physically isolated and cannot recruit new members.
- The subspecies now exists as roughly 80 discrete groups, each with a few hundred to a few thousand individuals (Stumpff, personal communication 1997).
- Catastrophic events such as a 1989 fire in Gila National Forest, which caused 100% mortality of [[Gila Trout]] in Main Diamond Creek, show how isolated populations can be lost without recolonisation.

**Threats (as ranked by the petition)**
- *Habitat loss* is the primary threat, with [[Livestock Grazing]] identified as the main degrading factor (Stumpff and Cooper 1996). Grazing reduces riparian cover, raises temperatures, and increases sedimentation. Apache trout were ten times more abundant on ungrazed streams (Parson and Wilson 1991).
- *Water diversions* have dried several miles of stream below storage dams and reservoirs, fragmenting the Ruidoso-Hondo-Bonito system.
- *Road building and logging* cause sedimentation and increase angler access. On a stream opened to a new road, the virginalis-to-brown-trout ratio fell from 98:2 to 10:90 (New Mexico Game and Fish 1973).
- *Hybridization and competition* with rainbow, brown, and brook trout are considered the second-greatest threat after habitat loss. Hybridization has replaced virginalis in hundreds of New Mexico waters, and the document notes that non-native trout occupy most habitat between remnant populations.
- *Angling* is a concern because the subspecies is easy to catch, and the petition notes that enforcement of the two-fish limit is difficult.
- *Whirling disease* from hatchery trout is noted as a regional threat, having eliminated 90% of wild rainbow trout in the Madison River.

**Management and regulatory history**
- The subspecies was listed as endangered in the 1967 *Red Data Book*, dropped in 1968, listed as rare in 1969, and described as "not viable" in the 1972 *Red Data Book*.
- It was listed as threatened in March 1973, but was omitted from the 1974 endangered list after the Endangered Species Act took effect. It became a candidate for listing in 1985.
- The [[U.S. Forest Service]] has no adopted conservation plan for the subspecies in any New Mexico or Colorado national forest. Several draft plans (1971, 1992, 1993, 1994) were never finalised.
- The 1996 Forest Plan amendments did not analyse the subspecies and contained no virginalis-specific policies.
- The New Mexico Department of Game and Fish has repeatedly recommended cattle reduction and exotic removal (Hubbard 1976; Sublette et al. 1990), but has limited authority over non-state lands.

**Petitioners' requests**
- List the Rio Grande cutthroat trout as endangered under the ESA.
- Designate critical habitat concurrently, covering all occupied areas and key unoccupied areas needed for restoration.
