# Differential Use of Stream Habitat by Spawning Catostomids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines spawning by sucker species (Catostomidae) in Deer Creek, a third-order tributary of the middle [[Wabash River]] in Carroll County, Indiana. Over spring 1977–1979 the authors combined adult sampling, blocknet movement traps, age-size analysis and spawning-site measurements. They report that species and size-age groups use the stream differently: white suckers dominate headwaters, redhorse are more abundant downstream, and older, larger golden and black redhorse spawn in the lower main stem. Spawning began only after water temperatures remained near or above 10 °C and followed discharge declines. Spawning-site depth and velocity separated species, which the authors interpret as a mechanism that reduces competition for spawning sites.

## Background & Description

Suckers are riverine fishes whose populations often depend on tributaries for spawning and nursery habitat. Before this study, the extent of upstream spring migration by adults, and the degree to which resident and migrant suckers compete for stream spawning sites, were unclear. Earlier work in Missouri, Ohio, Iowa and Illinois reported conflicting movement patterns for [[Black Redhorse]], [[Golden Redhorse]], [[Shorthead Redhorse]], [[Silver Redhorse]] and [[Quillback]].

The study system comprised Deer Creek (drainage area 709.7 km², mean discharge 6.71 m³/s), and two first-order tributaries, Bachelor Run (92.9 km²) and Paint Creek (40.9 km²). Twelve catostomid species occur in Deer Creek. Six 0.4–0.9 km stream sections in four zones (I–IV) were sampled. Adults were captured by hoopnets, [[Electrofishing]] and backpack shockers. Blocknets with paired hoopnets recorded movement direction. Ages were estimated from scales, verified with pectoral fin-ray sections, and compared using the Mann-Whitney test.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Physical conditions differed among streams in April–May 1978. Mean velocity was 0.9 m/s in Deer Creek and Bachelor Run and 0.6 m/s in Paint Creek. Mean temperatures were 13.2 °C, 13.5 °C and 12.2 °C respectively. Gradients were 9.6, 24 and 11 m/km (Table 1 values as reported by OCR; the gradient unit is ambiguous in the source).

Spawning was linked to hydrology and temperature. Spawning did not start until temperatures stayed at or above about 10 °C, after large decreases in discharge. Spring 1977 spawning occurred about one month earlier than in 1978 or 1979 because of an early warming trend. Water temperatures during spawning ranged from 10 to 22.5 °C.

Substrate used for spawning was cleaned by the fish themselves. Spawned gravel was coarser and freer of silt and sand than the original substrate, so the substrate at a site reflects the spawning activity as well as the original choice.

## Key Findings

**Species distribution and seasonal movement**
- Ten species were caught in zone IV, nine in zone III, seven in zone II and six in zone I.
- Of 491 adults captured in zone IV in spring, shorthead, golden and black redhorse plus northern hog sucker made up 84% of the catch.
- White suckers were almost all captured in Paint Creek (zone I), where creek chubsuckers were also restricted.
- Shorthead redhorse spawned only in the main stem (zones III and IV), never in Bachelor Run or Paint Creek, and were nearly absent in summer and autumn.
- Golden redhorse appeared to reside in the main stem year-round.
- Black redhorse occupied zones I–III for spring spawning and shifted toward zone IV in summer.
- White suckers, northern hog suckers and creek chubsuckers were the principal year-round residents of headwaters.
- Blocknet catches at zone II showed downstream movement after dark, with 95% moving downstream. At zone I, 75% moved downstream.

**Size and age segregation**
- Golden and black redhorse spawning in zone IV in 1977 and 1978 were significantly larger than those in zone II (P < 0.01).
- Golden and black redhorse in zone IV were significantly older than those in zone I (P < 0.01 and P < 0.05).
- Autumn golden redhorse in zone II were smaller and younger than spring fish from zones II or IV (P < 0.01), suggesting resident younger fish and migratory older adults.
- Northern hog suckers in zone I were smaller than those from downstream zones (P < 0.01), indicating that headwaters do not attract the largest adults.

**Spawning sequence and timing**
- The regular spawning order across three years was: white sucker, silver redhorse, northern hog sucker, creek chubsucker, shorthead redhorse, quillback, black redhorse, golden redhorse, river carpsucker.
- Spent female black redhorse were captured when golden redhorse were nearing spawning readiness, indicating black redhorse spawned earlier than golden redhorse.
- Blocknet catches of hog suckers, golden and black redhorse rose from 18 April to 10 May 1978. A major quillback downstream movement on 18 May was 93% female and mostly spent.

**Spawning-site characteristics**
- Golden redhorse, shorthead redhorse and northern hog suckers spawned over medium gravel in riffles 30–50 cm deep at 0.4–0.9 m/s.
- White suckers spawned over medium gravel at 20–25 cm depth and 0.50–0.59 m/s.
- Creek chubsuckers selected fine gravel in 50–75 cm depth at much slower velocity (0.10–0.24 m/s) and defended cleared depressions.
- Spawning did not begin until water levels fell to 20–60 cm over shoals.

**Interpretation**
- The authors propose that size, age, time and habitat segregation reduce competition for spawning sites. White suckers spawn early in headwaters, while golden redhorse extend further upstream than shorthead redhorse.
- Species–drainage area relationships may be used to estimate habitat available for spawning in tributaries.
- Stream alterations affecting temperature, flow, substrate or fish movement could change catostomid community composition.
- Age estimates for fish over age 5 may be unreliable from scales, so mean ages of spawning groups could be underestimated.
