# Sex Determination Mechanisms in the Platyfish, *Xiphophorus maculatus*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This conference paper, presented at the IX International Congress of Genetics (1954) by [[Gordon Myron]] of the Genetics Laboratory of the New York Zoological Society, reviews two opposing genetic mechanisms of sex determination found within the single species [[Xiphophorus Maculatus]] (platyfish). Wild populations from Mexican rivers show an XX female / XY male system, while the [[Belize River]] population and [[Domesticated Stocks]] show a WY female / YY male system. The author argues that the domesticated stocks probably descend from Belize River fish, which removes the need for an earlier hypothesis that female heterogamety arose through hybridization with the swordtail. Hybrid crosses are also analysed to explain sex ratios.

## Background & Description

*Xiphophorus maculatus* was formerly named *Platypoecilus maculatus*. It belongs to the family [[Poeciliidae]], a group in which both male and female heterogamety occur in closely related species, and in this species both occur within one taxon. Reviewers had speculated that female heterogamety evolved from male heterogamety. This idea was supported by [[Winge]]'s (1934) finding that in [[Lebistes Reticulatus]] (guppy), artificial selection can produce XX males and XY females. Gordon pointed out that this evidence is misleading: when rare XX males are mated with rare XY females, the usual XX female / XY male system reappears.

Because of the many, inconsistent terms for sex chromosomes in the literature, the author compiled a synonym table (Table 1). It lists the nomenclature of Bellamy, Kosswig, Breider, Castle and Gordon, with the stock and date for each system. The table shows that Gordon's "domesticated" and Belize River stocks share the same mechanism.

Historical background: the platyfish studied by European and American geneticists were derived from an importation made in 1907 to Germany for the aquarium trade, from an unspecified locality in Central America. The German aquarium-bred fish were later imported to the United States, and these American derivatives were the only ones available to geneticists until 1932.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Natural populations and sex-determining systems**

- **XX female / XY male (male heterogamety):** found in natural populations from the Rio Jamapa, Rio Papaloapan and Rio Coatzacoalcos (Figure 2 also lists the Rio Grijalva). Fish from the Rio Tonalá (OCR reading "Yonalé") and Rio Usumacinta had not been studied, because no live specimens were collected.
- **WY female / YY male (female heterogamety):** found in the natural population of the Belize River, British Honduras, and in domesticated aquarium stocks. Figure 3 notes that this mechanism had previously been found in aquarium platyfish worldwide.

**Figure 1** shows seven rivers in southern Mexico and western Central America from which large numbers of platyfish were collected.

**Gene frequency evidence:** the Belize River population contains every colour pattern represented in the 1907–1909 German importation. The Rio Jamapa and Rio Coatzacoalcos populations carry genes absent from the early imported fish, so those two rivers are unlikely sources of the domesticated stock.

**Sex reversal:** a partially functional XX male from a Rio Jamapa stock was reported in an earlier paper in the series. When mated to a normal XX female, all offspring were normal XX females. The author notes that sex-reversed individuals help establish the sex-determining system within a population, but say little about how the system evolved.

## Key Findings

1. **Two opposing mechanisms in one species.** Mexican rivers (Jamapa, Papaloapan, Coatzacoalcos) have XX = female and XY = male. The Belize River population and domesticated stocks have WY = female and YY = male.

2. **Domesticated stocks likely derive from the Belize River.** Shared sex-determining mechanisms, the colour-pattern match with the 1907–1909 importation, and the absence of Jamapa or Coatzacoalcos genes in the early fish all support this. The author states that this makes the earlier swordtail-hybridization hypothesis unnecessary.

3. **Genic balance theory.** The author uses the genic balance theory of sex determination, proposed by Bridges and extended to fishes by Winge, to explain sex reversals. Normally balanced sex genes in autosomes and sex chromosomes determine:
   - Females: XX in three Mexican rivers, WY in the Belize River, and WX in some intervarietal hybrids.
   - Males: XY in wild Mexican fish, and YY in British Honduras fish.

4. **Hybrid sex ratios.**
   - Crossing a WY female (domesticated or Belize) with a Mexican XY male produces WY and WX females and XY and YY males, in a 1:1 sex ratio.
   - Backcrossing an intervarietal WY hybrid female to a Mexican XY male keeps the 1:1 ratio.
   - Backcrossing an intervarietal WX hybrid female to a Mexican XY male gives a 3:1 female-to-male ratio (WY, WX, XX females : XY males).
   - The 3:1 ratio arises from orderly recombination of sex chromosomes, not from sex reversal.

5. **Histological confirmation.** Sections of developing gonads show normal sexual differentiation. WY, WX and XX fish develop as females and XY fish as males, as early as histology can detect.

6. **Source publication.** The report summarises Gordon's 1953 paper, *Sex determination in Xiphophorus (Platypoecilus) maculatus. III. Differentiation of gonads in platyfish from broods having a sex ratio of three females to one male* (Zoologica 38(2): 91–100). The paper includes a full bibliography. No sample sizes are given in this summary.
