# Comparison of Aging Structures and Life History of a Historical Roundtail Chub Population in the Yampa River, Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares three aging structures ([[Scale Aging]], [[Opercular Bone]], and [[Otolith]] analysis) for [[Roundtail Chub]] (*[[Gila robusta]]*) collected from the [[Yampa River]] in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] during 1998, 1999, 2001, and 2002. It also describes age structure, mortality, growth, body condition, and tuberculation relative to reproductive stages. Otoliths gave the most precise age estimates. Scales agreed with otoliths up to about 7–8 years, and opercles up to about 10–12 years, after which both underestimated age. The oldest fish was 22 years old, and the population showed an annual survival of about 85%. Both sexes reached first reproduction at about 5–6 years. The authors argue that this long-lived, large-bodied population, which declined sharply after 2002, is important for management in the [[Colorado River Basin]].

## Background & Description

The [[Gila robusta]] species complex includes three large cyprinids native to the Colorado River drainage: [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and Roundtail Chub. Humpback and Bonytail chubs have been listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Roundtail Chub is considered imperiled or vulnerable to extinction by state agencies across the Colorado River basin.

Taxonomy within the complex is unsettled. Genetic and morphological work by Carter et al. (2018) and Copus et al. (2018) recommended treating *G. robusta* as a single polymorphic species, with *G. intermedia* and *G. nigra* considered synonyms. In the upper basin, mitochondrial DNA of Roundtail Chub was replaced through hybridization with Humpback Chub mtDNA, although mtDNA does not track morphology.

Roundtail Chub remained relatively abundant in the Yampa River within Dinosaur National Monument at least through 2002. After that, predation by nonnative [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*) and [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) contributed to severe declines in the upper basin. Little has been published on the age, growth, and reproduction of this species. The only prior age and growth analysis from the upper basin was a scale study by Vanicek and Kramer (1969) on the Green River. Earlier reported maximum longevity was 5–7 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study reach:** Fish were collected from Deer Lodge to the confluence of the Yampa and [[Green River]] at Echo Park, within the Yampa Canyon.
- **Water temperatures during collections:** 20.5–22.5 °C (1998), 21–23 °C (1999), 19–23 °C (2001), and 18 °C (2002).
- **Collection dates:** Late June to mid-July each year (1998: 31 June–12 July; 1999: 1–4 July; 2001: 28 June–2 July; 2002: 11–12 June).
- **Collection methods:** Angling (1998) and [[Electrofishing]] (1999, 2001, 2002). The authors found no significant bias between gears for fish of the same age.
- **Life-history framework:** Using the [[Winemiller and Rose Life History Model]], the authors classify the historic Yampa population as a periodic species (larger body size, longer lifespan, longer generation time). Smaller tributary populations, such as those in the Gila River system, fall closer to opportunistic.
- **Habitat-size relationship:** Earlier work suggests that larger streams may support older fish, and that fish in smaller streams mature earlier. Comparable patterns were reported for [[Utah Chub]] in predator-rich lakes versus predator-free lakes.

## Key Findings

### Aging structure precision
- Interpretable annuli were obtained from otoliths for 109 fish (yearly counts: 24 in 1998, 21 in 1999, 35 in 2001, 29 in 2002), from opercles for 91 fish, and from scales for 111 fish.
- Otolith annuli showed the least measurement error.
- Scale annuli generally agreed with otolith annuli up to about 7–8 years, then consistently underestimated age.
- Opercular annuli agreed closely with otolith annuli up to about 10–12 years, then underestimated age.
- For juvenile fish under 150 mm SL, mean distances to the first, second, and third opercular annuli were 4.07 mm (n = 17), 6.07 mm (n = 16), and 7.8 mm (n = 15), respectively.
- Based on these results, the authors used otolith ages for all subsequent analyses, following [[Quist et al. Recommendations]].

### Age structure and mortality
- The oldest fish, a 350 mm TL male, was aged at 22 years.
- Seven fish exceeded 15 years of age.
- Lengths ranged from 63 to 446 mm TL (mean 262 mm TL). The largest fish was a 446 mm TL female (366 mm SL).
- A [[Catch-Curve Analysis]] for ages 6–14 yielded an instantaneous mortality rate (Z) of –0.158, equivalent to an annual mortality of about 15% and annual survivorship of about 85%.

### Growth
- Growth was modeled with the [[Von Bertalanffy Growth Model]]. Parameter estimates were L∞ = 472.2 mm TL, K = 0.102, and t0 = –1.08.
- As is typical for many species, growth was rapid early in life and slowed at older ages.
- The theoretical maximum length was similar to the 499.3 mm estimated for Verde River fish (Brouder 2005).

### Body condition
- The overall [[Length-Weight Relationship]] was log10(mass) = –5.046 + 2.963 log10(TL) (n = 200, R² = 0.987). Mass ranged from 6 to 766 g.
- The length–mass slopes differed between sexes. Females had slightly greater body mass per unit length than males for fish longer than 398 mm TL.
- Yampa fish showed a length–mass relationship essentially identical to that of Green River fish reported by Vanicek and Kramer (1969), indicating no meaningful change in condition over about 30 years.
- Verde River fish had greater body mass per unit length than Yampa fish.

### Reproduction and maturity
- Gonads were staged following Heins and Machado (1993) as modified by Albanese (2000).
- Both sexes reached first reproduction at about 5–6 years. Females showed early maturing gonads at 5 years, and later maturing or mature stages from 8 to 20 years.
- Males showed testicular development beginning at 6–7 years, with mature or running-ripe testes in fish older than 12 years.
- The authors did not collect females with ovaries in ripening or ripe stages, which may reflect sampling in late June to early July, before spawning.
- Older fish in mature stages likely spawned multiple times, so a reproductive lifespan of roughly 9–14 years is plausible.

### Tuberculation
- Tuberculation was scored in four stages, with stages 3 and 4 combined. Stage 1 is restricted to head and pectoral fin rays. Stage 4 covers the head, nape, dorsum, and pectoral and pelvic rays densely.
- Both sexes develop tubercles. Tubercle intensity is linked to gonadal stage.
- The greatest development occurred in males with maturing or running-ripe testes, and in females with maturing ovaries.

### Comparisons with other studies
- Scale and opercle ages matched otolith ages for young fish, but both underestimated age in older fish. Similar discrepancies were reported for [[Utah Chub]] and [[Cui-ui]].
- A Verde River study (Brouder 2005) reported a maximum age of 7 years and an oldest female of 427 mm TL. A Gila River study reported a maximum age of 7 years, though a preliminary opercle analysis suggested up to 13 years.
- Survival estimates of about 85% are comparable to adult survival of [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (65–82% for 2000–2003) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (about 66–87%, depending on study).

### Management implications
- Otoliths and opercles provide better precision for long-lived fishes, but both are lethal to the fish.
- Scales are suitable where the focus is on younger age groups.
- Nonlethal alternatives, such as sectioned pectoral fin rays, showed promise in other cyprinids and catostomids. They may be preferable for threatened or endangered species.
- The authors recommend that managers weigh the gains in precision against the cost of lethal sampling, especially for threatened fishes.
