## Executive Summary
This 1966–1967 completion report from the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] tracks the ongoing conflict between a proposed U.S. Army Corps of Engineers **channelization and vegetation clearing project** on the [[Gila River]] and [[Salt River]] and the preservation of riparian wildlife habitat. The Corps intended to clear a **2,000-foot-wide channel** from Granite Reef Dam to Gillespie Dam, which state and federal wildlife agencies opposed in favor of a reduced **500-foot channel**. Data show a fifth consecutive year of increases in dove hunter numbers and harvest in Maricopa County (1962–1966), underscoring the value of the [[Gila River]] bottomlands as a major dove-production area. The report also documents a mysterious waterfowl die-off (~200 ducks) potentially linked to effluent discharge into the Salt River, and notes continued deterioration of Gila River waterfowl habitat despite ongoing hunter use.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Riparian corridor habitat**: The 45-mile stretch of the [[Gila River]] between Phoenix and Gillespie Dam supports one of Arizona's major dove-producing habitats; the state's huntable white-winged dove population is considered dependent on reproduction in this corridor.
- **Quail nucleus habitat**: Thick riparian vegetation provides critical nesting, roosting, and escape cover for a large Gambel's quail population, increasingly important as urban Phoenix expansion (population growth from 663,510 in 1960 to 894,000 in 1966) consumes surrounding habitat.
- **Waterfowl habitat gradient**: Natural stream flow through the project area is rare (occurring roughly 1 in 100 years) due to upstream irrigation reservoir management on the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]]. Waterfowl habitat is instead sustained artificially by **effluent discharge** from wastewater treatment plants at 23rd and 91st Avenues, which flows through the Salt River, past the Buckeye diversion structure, and into the [[Hassayampa River]].
- **Effluent-linked mortality**: Approximately 200 dead ducks were found in the Salt River pothole area (91st Avenue vicinity) in January–February 1967, with cause of death undetermined but suspected to be linked to effluent ingestion — flagging a potential emerging contaminant/mortality pathway for waterfowl in altered riparian systems.
- **Habitat fragmentation risk**: Proposed channelization would eliminate large blocks of riparian vegetation, converting a primary game-bird production and hunting area into non-habitat, with cascading effects on regional hunting opportunity within 35 miles of half of Arizona's population.

## Key Findings
- **Dove harvest trends (Maricopa County, 1962–1966)**:

| Year | Hunters | Trips | Harvest |
|------|---------|--------|---------|
| 1962 | 17,431 | 51,300 | 298,074 |
| 1963 | 20,448 | 81,222 | 746,800 |
| 1964 | 22,385 | 114,995 | 974,985 |
| 1965 | 24,588 | 102,877 | 995,658 |
| 1966 | 27,186 | 109,748 | 1,151,292 |

- Dove harvest increased **15.6%** and hunter numbers increased **10.5%** from 1965 to 1966 — the fifth consecutive annual increase.
- The full 2,000-foot clearing proposal would destroy an estimated **8,485 acres** of habitat, causing an annual loss of **834,505 dove-use days** (Manes 1965).
- The alternative 500-foot channel recommended by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] and [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] would reduce habitat loss to **3,081 acres** and dove-use loss to **208,466**, cutting wildlife impacts by roughly three-fourths while still providing adequate flood protection.
- Political/regulatory timeline: the project was placed under **deferred status** by the Corps of Engineers in September 1965; Maricopa County voters rejected a **$22,679,000 flood control bond issue** (3:1) in March 1966; the Corps nonetheless reaffirmed intent to pursue the full 2,000-foot clearing as of November 1966, despite unanimous opposition from county, state, and federal agencies.
- Approximately 200 waterfowl deaths were documented near the effluent-influenced Salt River pothole habitat, suggesting effluent discharge may pose a **mortality risk to waterfowl** — an area flagged for further study.
- The report frames riparian vegetation retention as essential not only for game bird production but for maintaining accessible outdoor recreation opportunity near a rapidly urbanizing metropolitan population.
