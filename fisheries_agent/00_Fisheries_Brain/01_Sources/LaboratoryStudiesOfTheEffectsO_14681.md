# Laboratory Studies of the Effects of Alkyl Benzene Sulfonate (ABS) on Aquatic Invertebrates

#aquatic-invertebrates #ecotoxicology #ephemeroptera

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by [[Eugene W. Surber]] and Thomas O. Thatcher, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, reports laboratory experiments on the toxicity of [[Alkyl Benzene Sulfonate]] (ABS), the surfactant in a packaged household detergent, to stream-bottom invertebrates. The work used recirculating flow-through tanks that simulated stream currents. Mayfly nymphs were the most sensitive group, while caddisfly larvae of the family [[Hydropsychidae]] were the most resistant. Crayfish, freshwater shrimp, and sow bugs were seriously reduced at 10 ppm, and a snail (*Goniobasis*) was largely unaffected at that level. The authors describe the work as early results and call for further study of detergent toxicity in soft water under continuous exposure.

## Background & Description

**Problem.** The authors state there is "urgent need" for knowledge of detergent effects on aquatic life. Most commercial household detergents are anionic and biodegradable, except alkyl benzene sulfonate, the surfactant most commonly used in them. Surfactants make up about 18 to 30 percent of packaged detergents. The remainder is mostly [[Sodium Tripolyphosphate]] or [[Sodium Sulfate]] and other additives.

**Persistence.** Only about 50 percent of detergents are removed in sewage treatment plants. Detergents therefore occur in most lowland streams near population centers and in some drinking-water supplies drawn from surface streams.

**Test product.** The packaged detergent contained 27.0 percent ABS, 5 percent sodium tripolyphosphate, 10 percent sodium chloride, and 58 percent sodium sulfate. Concentrations were reported as active ingredient (ABS), not as product.

**Test organisms.** Most organisms were collected from the [[Little Miami River]] at Milford, Ohio. The authors chose them as the most common of the larger bottom organisms, to gauge the overall impact on the bottom fauna and to gain experience in handling them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Prior work cited:** [[Hepworth]] (1961) reported 96-hour LC50 values of 5.2, 4.7, and 4.9 ppm for mayfly, stonefly, and damselfly nymphs exposed to a nonionic detergent. Diptera larvae withstood 10 ppm. [[Wurtz and Bridges]] (1961) found 96-hour TL50 values of 10 to 24 ppm for sludge worms (*Limnodrilus hoffmeisteri*), depending on temperature and water hardness. [[Mann]] (1955) reported lethality at 10 to 25 ppm for anionic and nonionic detergents. [[Daphnia]] were first damaged at 5 ppm (Mann 1958), and adaptation occurred when concentrations were increased slowly.
- **Sodium sulfate (builder):** Four-day tests at 0, 56, 100, 180, and 320 ppm in soft water showed the principal builder to be nontoxic over 96 hours, though some emergence of mayflies occurred in all treatments, including the control.
- **Sodium chloride:** At the highest ABS concentration (32 ppm), calculated sodium chloride was 11.8 ppm. Literature cited by the authors indicates low toxicity from sodium chloride at these levels.
- **Sodium tripolyphosphate:** The authors knew of no toxicity data for this builder on aquatic invertebrates.
- **Fish context:** [[Henderson et al.]] (1959) found high toxicity to fathead minnows from a pure surfactant, with TL50 values as low as 3.5 ppm in hard water. [[Herbert et al.]] (1957) concluded that synthetic detergents were unlikely to be a serious danger to fish in British rivers, where concentrations were usually below 3 ppm.
- **Experimental conditions:** Flow velocities were about 0.5 to 1.0 ft/s. Temperatures were about 18 to 22 °C in the tanks after cooling, and the authors noted that higher temperatures increased emergence of insects.

## Key Findings

### Apparatus and methods
- Most experiments used 10 oval metal tanks, each about 3 feet long with 5-inch-deep channels, placed side by side. Paddle wheels on a common shaft simulated stream current.
- Each tank received 100 ml/min of water. ABS was delivered at nominal concentrations of 4, 8, 16, and 32 ppm, plus controls.
- Actual ABS concentrations were measured twice weekly with a modified [[Methylene Blue Method]]. Actual values were lower than nominal. For example, 10 ppm nominal gave 8.7 ppm actual, and 16 and 32 ppm gave 15.1 and 25.8 ppm actual in one test.
- Stock solution was 640 ppm ABS, prepared daily from glass carboy reservoirs. Flow calculations were checked against reservoir volumes, and the authors report good agreement.
- Temperatures above 25 to 30 °C caused insect emergence. A cooling unit was added to hold tank water at about 18 to 22 °C.
- Flat stones covered with algae (mostly *Cladophora*) and diatoms provided food and shelter. Lighting was changed to 12 hours on and 12 off, with little improvement in net-spinning behavior.

### Main results: ABS toxicity by group
- **Mayfly nymphs** (*Stenonema ares*, *S. heterotarsale*, *Isonychia bicolor*) were the most sensitive. Ten days at 16 ppm or more killed them, and 8 ppm reduced their numbers.
  - April 17–26 test: *Stenonema* and *Isonychia* were drastically reduced or eliminated at 8 ppm.
  - May 3–14 test: *Stenonema* survival at 8 ppm was 13.8 percent. None survived at 16 or 32 ppm.
  - May 19–29 test: no *Stenonema* survived at 16 or 32 ppm (actual 15.1 and 25.8 ppm).
  - July 20–30 and August 2–14 tests: mayflies failed to survive at 16 and 32 ppm (actual 15 and 28 ppm).
- **Caddisfly larvae** (Hydropsychidae, mostly *Cheumatopsyche*) were the most resistant. About 53 percent survived 8 ppm and 29 percent survived 32 ppm in the April test. Survival was similar across concentrations in the May 19–29 test.
- **Crayfish** (*Orconectes rusticus*):
  - 25-day test with 25 young crayfish per tank (26–32 mm): survival was 20 percent at 10 ppm versus 48 percent in the control.
  - 29-day test (June 6–July 5) in pond water with a nominal 10 ppm: 50 percent survived in the treatment versus 80 percent in the control.
- **Freshwater shrimp** (*Synurella* sp.) and **sow bugs** (*Lirceus* sp.):
  - 14-day test, 30 of each per tank, average 22 °C: survival was 36.7 percent (*Lirceus*) and 60.0 percent (*Synurella*) in controls, versus 6.7 percent and 0 percent at 10 ppm ABS.
  - Earlier 2-week tests reported serious reductions in *Lirceus* and 100 percent mortality of *Synurella* at 10 ppm.
- **Snails** (*Goniobasis* sp.):
  - Largely unaffected at 10 ppm in the June test (all survived in both control and treatment).
  - Eliminated at 32 ppm in the August 2–14 test, which included young snails of about 5–8 mm.

### Water quality and experimental complications
- Hard-water tests (Newtown spring water) ranged from 280 to 342 ppm total hardness, 209 to 282 ppm alkalinity, 5.7 to 7.49 ppm dissolved oxygen, pH 8.1 to 8.3, and 18.5 to 26.0 °C.
- The pond-water test had lower hardness (about 173 ppm) and higher conductivity in the treatment tank (432 versus 287 µmho/cm in the control).
- Insect emergence, especially of mayflies, confounded control survival. Handling and holding conditions also affected results, and the authors judged some May data "not very significant" because of small sample sizes and poor controls.
- Zoogloeal bacteria and *Sphaerotilus* caused two shutdowns of the battery when demineralized water was mixed with 5 percent spring water. Chlorine did not remove them. A 10 ppm concentration of aureomycin arrested the growth without harming insects or the algae.

### Authors' conclusions
- The authors describe their results as early and partial, with "little data" available for comparison.
- Test concentrations expressed as active ingredient may mean that other products are more toxic.
- Detergent toxicity to aquatic invertebrates in soft water under continuous exposure still needs to be determined.
