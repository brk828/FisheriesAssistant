# Summer Distribution and Reproductive Status of Fish of the Colorado River in Grand Canyon National Park and Vicinity, 1975

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This technical report, prepared by C. O. Minckley and Dean M. Blinn of [[Northern Arizona University]] for the [[National Park Service]] (Grand Canyon National Park, Colorado River Research Program Contribution No. 42), surveys the summer distribution and reproductive status of fish in the [[Colorado River]] and its major tributaries from Lee's Ferry to Diamond Creek. Sampling in July and August 1975 covered 22 areas and yielded ten species: four native and six exotic. Reproduction was verified in all native species and in three exotic species. Native fish, apart from the [[Bonytail Chub]], were more abundant and more widespread than exotics, and native populations appeared similar to 1968 levels. The report recommends further seasonal study, broodstock collection of the Bonytail Chub, and visitor education to protect rare fish.

## Background & Description

- **Prior work:** [[Miller]] (1944) produced the first checklist of Grand Canyon fishes, updated in 1975. Other sources cited include systematic studies by [[Holden and Stalnaker]] (1970, 1975) and life-history work by Minckley (1973).
- **Objectives:** Determine summer distribution and the general reproductive status of fish present in the Colorado River and its major tributaries.
- **Methods:**
  - Fish were collected by seining 22 areas with 6.5 m × 1.5 m × 6 mm or 3.0 mm × 1.0 m × 6 mm mesh seines.
  - Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin and later stored in 50% isopropanol.
  - Fish were measured to the nearest millimeter as total length and examined for ectoparasites.
- **Personnel:** Collections were made with the [[Museum of Northern Arizona]] Ecological Survey, with help from R. R. Miller, F. H. Miller, B. L. Minckley, and J. E. Bartoslek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report describes the native aquatic fauna of the American Southwest as becoming increasingly depauperate as river systems are developed for recreation and commerce. Grand Canyon National Park is presented as one of the few remaining refugia for several unique native fish. The four native species belong to two families: Cyprinidae (Speckled Dace and Bonytail Chub) and Catostomidae (Bluehead and Flannelmouth Suckers).

Ecological observations include:

- **Ectoparasites:** Fish were examined for ectoparasites, and none were found. The authors note that ectoparasites affect native fish elsewhere but do not affect native fish in Grand Canyon.
- **Competition:** Competitive interaction between exotic and native fish often leads to native extirpation or large declines. Little is known about such interactions in Grand Canyon, but native fish appear to be holding their own.
- **Little Colorado River:** This tributary appears to be a spawning area for Bonytail Chub. Its warm water and low turbulence may be suitable habitat, possibly the only suitable habitat left in the area.
- **Rainbow Trout stocking:** Trout are stocked both above and below the canyon by state fish and game departments, so collection numbers do not reflect population size.

## Key Findings

### Species Composition and Distribution

- **Total fish taken:** Ten species, comprising four native and six exotic (introduced) species. Native species make up about 40% of the ichthyofauna.
- **Percent occurrence (of 22 areas surveyed):**

| Species | Origin | Occurrence |
|---|---|---|
| [[Speckled Dace]] | Native | 86.5% |
| [[Bluehead Sucker]] | Native | 68.1% |
| [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] | Native | 45.0% |
| [[Bonytail Chub]] | Native | 4.5% |
| [[Fathead Minnow]] | Exotic | 40.9% |
| [[Rio Grande Killifish]] | Exotic | 31.8% |
| [[Rainbow Trout]] | Exotic | 23.6% |
| [[Common Carp]] | Exotic | 9.0% |
| [[Channel Catfish]] | Exotic | 4.5% |
| [[Black Bullhead]] | Exotic | 4.5% |

- **Abundance:** Native fish, except the Bonytail Chub, were more abundant in total numbers and in localities than exotics.
- **Population status:** Native populations appear similar to 1968 levels.

### Reproduction

- Reproduction was verified in all native species and in three exotic species (Fathead Minnow, Rio Grande Killifish, and Rainbow Trout), based on young-of-the-year collections.
- **Speckled Dace:** Slightly over 50% of summer specimens were juveniles (<60 mm). Mean length increased from July to August, indicating growth.
- **Bluehead Sucker:** Small individuals (<150 mm) were taken, indicating recent reproduction. Juveniles made up a high percentage of the sample, and mean length decreased from July to August.
- **Flannelmouth Sucker:** Mean length increased from July to August, and the percentage of juveniles stayed similar.
- **Fathead Minnow:** Fish in spawning condition were found at several sites. Young-of-the-year were collected at Carbon, Lava, Garden, Chuar, and Kanab Creeks and Parashont Wash. Mean length decreased slightly in August, consistent with recruitment.
- **Rio Grande Killifish:** Fish in reproductive condition were found at several sites, and juveniles were collected at Garden, Crystal, and Chuar Creeks and Parashont Wash. Mean length decreased from July to August, while the percentage of juveniles remained high.
- **Rainbow Trout:** Reproducing populations were considered likely in Tapeats Creek and Bright Angel Creek, based on juvenile collections. Spawning was noted at other tributaries but not verified.
- **Channel Catfish and Black Bullhead:** All specimens were adults capable of reproducing, but no reproductive activity was observed. Reproduction is considered likely.
- **Common Carp:** Only moderately sized individuals (about 0.5 kg) were taken, and no reproduction was observed, though it is expected.

### Bonytail Chub

- The Bonytail Chub was taken only at the Little Colorado River and is considered quite rare in Grand Canyon.
- Juveniles were present in July, and the estimated size of August specimens was 50–100 mm total length.
- The species is apparently declining, and the reasons are not clear from these samples.

### Health and Conservation Concerns

- All fish examined for ectoparasites were negative.
- The Humpback Chub, an endangered species, occurs in the region and hybridizes with the Bonytail Chub, which the report says should be checked before broodstock is taken.

### Recommendations

1. **Further research:** Conduct seasonal surveys (summer, fall, winter, spring) from Lee's Ferry to Pierce's Ferry, plus systematic surveys of selected tributaries, including near Phantom Ranch.
2. **Stomach and tissue sampling:** Collect stomach contents and tissue samples to support identification and to clarify why key tributaries are used.
3. **Bonytail Chub broodstock:** Collect 20 or more adults from the Little Colorado River for propagation at a national fish hatchery. Similar measures are recommended for the Humpback Chub if collected.
4. **Angler and visitor education:** Distribute a brochure on rare and endangered species to commercial and private boat trips and access points. Include a short description of endangered fish in National Park Service regulations at the rim. Require a 15-minute film strip for all river trips.
5. **Limit development:** Recreational and industrial use of the river system should not expand beyond its present level, so that the system's unique character is preserved.
