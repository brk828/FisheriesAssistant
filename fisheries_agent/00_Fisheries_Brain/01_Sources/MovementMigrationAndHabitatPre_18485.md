# Movement, Migration and Habitat Preference of Radiotelemetered Colorado Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 report from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] ([[Colorado River Fishery Project]]), prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], presents the first analysis of [[Radiotelemetry]] data for the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the [[Green River]], [[White River]] and [[Yampa River]]. A total of 31 fish were implanted with transmitters in 1980 and 1981. The study found two movement patterns, sedentary and highly mobile, which tracked sexual maturity. Mature fish made spawning migrations to a restricted spawning area in the [[Lower Yampa Canyon]] (lower 32 km). The authors report that these migrations were not captured by earlier studies and that they can bias abundance and distribution estimates.

## Background & Description

- **Study objectives:** Locate spawning sites and describe movement patterns of Colorado squawfish. Radiotelemetry continued in 1982-1984.
- **Study area:** Turbid mainstem Green River (Gray Canyon to [[Dinosaur National Monument]], about 340 km), the lower Yampa River (mouth to Juniper Springs, 160 km), and the White River (mouth to Rangely, Colorado, 140 km). The [[Duchesne River]] was also used by tagged fish.
- **Sampling:** Fish were collected by [[Electrofishing]], [[Trammel Net Sampling]] and seining in April-May 1980 (Green River) and April-May 1981 (Green, White and Yampa rivers).
- **Surgery:** Fish were anesthetized with tricaine methanesulfonate (200 mg/L) and implanted with AVM SM-1 "padlock" transmitters of about 11 g, with a theoretical battery life of about 215 days. Transmitters operated at 40.660-40.700 MHz with unique frequency-pulse signatures. Fish were held three to five days before release. No ill effects of surgery were noted.
- **Tracking:** Fish were located from boats, fixed-wing aircraft and helicopters. The helicopter was the most successful platform because airplanes cannot safely fly in most canyon areas.
- **Data handling:** Data were managed in the MANAGE system, with four data sets (PHYSICAL, CATCH, RARE, RADIO).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use**
- Fish were observed mainly in shoreline habitat over sand, though eddy, run, backwater and pool habitats and silt, boulder, rubble and gravel substrates were also used.
- Mean depths and velocities at radiotelemetry locations were:

| River | Mean depth (m) | Mean velocity (m/s) |
|---|---|---|
| Green | 1.44 (1980: 1.20; 1981: 1.68) | 0.20 (1980: 0.17; 1981: 0.20) |
| Yampa | 0.93 | 0.11 |
| White | 0.72 | 0.45 |

- Depths differed significantly between the Green River and both tributaries (p<.01). Velocities differed among river systems (p<.01).
- Radiotelemetry and conventional-gear habitat data showed close agreement, with minor departures. The authors considered telemetry more representative because it avoids disturbance and provides 24-hour data.

**Spawning habitat**
- The known spawning site is at RK 26.4 (RM 16.5) in the lower Yampa Canyon. The site is characterized by cobble/rubble substrate (mean diameter about 7.5 cm) and deep pool-riffle habitat.
- Water temperature was 22°C and conductivity was 340 µmhos/cm. Depths ranged from 0.9 to 2.1 m and velocities from 0 to 0.08 m/s (the summary gives 0-0.1 m/s).
- For comparison, [[Northern Squawfish]] spawned at about 10°C in water 4.8 m deep at 0.05 m/s, so habitat selection differs between the two species.

**Diel and environmental influences**
- Diel activity was not strongly rhythmic except in the postspawning period (after July 15), when movement peaked from 0800 to 1600. During the spawning period, Yampa fish were more active at night.
- Correlations between movement and environment were weak: flow r = 0.1 (linear model valid at p<.01), temperature r = 0.006 (p<.05), and flow and temperature on depth r = 0.17 (p<.01).

## Key Findings

**1. Two movement patterns, linked to sexual maturity**
- Sedentary fish (6 individuals) averaged 18 km of total movement. Mobile fish (10 individuals) averaged 245 km.
- Sedentary fish averaged 476.5 mm TL (4 fish, per Table 4), and mobile fish averaged 565.3 mm TL (11 fish). Mobile fish were larger and were more likely to be mature.
- Seethaler (1978) reported that 76% of males and 50% of females were mature by 503 mm TL.
- Fish No. 165, a mature female, moved about 161 km in 5 days and was possibly spawning when it reached the White River at RK 55.2 on July 16, 1980. Fish No. 064, an immature male, remained stationary.

**2. Spawning migration**
- Six radiotagged fish from the Yampa and Green rivers migrated downstream to the lower Yampa Canyon in June and July 1981. One fish migrated upstream from the Green River, for a total of eight fish. These migrations made up 96% of total movement during the study.
- Between 1 and 9 July 1981, 34-35 ripe or spent adults were captured in the lower Yampa Canyon (the report gives both figures). Ripe males predominated (25 males and 8 spent females in the 34-fish count).
- The authors estimate at least 43 adults were present in the lower Yampa Canyon between 5 June and 9 July 1981, including radiotagged fish.
- Larval fish (9-13 mm) were collected only in the lower 20 km of the Yampa River in 1981, and in the lower 16 km in 1980. This supports spawning being restricted to the lower 32 km.

**3. Homing behavior**
- Radiotagged fish returned to their former upstream or downstream locations after spawning, indicating a homing ability. The mechanism is unknown.
- Dangler-tag recaptures confirmed the spawning-ground pattern: four tagged fish were recaptured there, including two from the upper Yampa tagged in 1978 and 1980.

**4. Movement in the White and Green rivers**
- In the White River, fish No. 1601 (sedentary) moved 86 km downstream over 5 months. Fish No. 1624 (mobile) moved 611 km in 5 months, including 200 km downstream to Gray Canyon and a return to RK 80 on the White River.
- The authors suggest Gray Canyon may hold a second spawning ground.

**5. Methodological limitations**
- Of the 31 implanted fish, only 16 were tracked for more than 3 months.
- Contact was hampered by module failure, poor reception and high fish mobility. In the White River, radio interference from energy development further reduced contact.
- The authors note that habitat preference curves were not generated, since they would be preliminary.

**6. Implications for management**
- Movement and migration can bias capture-based estimates of habitat preference, recruitment and survivorship. Passive gear is especially prone to this bias.
- Ripe males captured in probable migration should not be taken as evidence of a spawning area without supporting evidence.
- Previous status assessments may have been misinterpreted because they did not account for movement. The authors also disagree with the hypothesis (Joseph et al. 1977) that Colorado squawfish spawn where they live year-round.

**7. Evolutionary and ecological context**
- Long-distance spawning migrations (over 100 km) are uncommon in North American freshwater fishes. Family [[Cyprinidae]] is the largest fish family in the world, with 275 genera and over 1600 species (Nelson 1976), but its riverine migrations are poorly studied.
- The authors argue that the link between upstream spawning sites and downstream nursery areas is key to Colorado squawfish survival. They conclude that suitable spawning sites are limited in the Green River basin, and that water development likely blocked access to spawning grounds and caused the loss of subpopulations.
