# Observations on Cleaner Shrimps of the Genus Periclimenes

#marine-biology #invertebrate-ecology #Palaemonidae #cleaning-symbiosis #coral-reef

## Executive Summary

This study documents the distribution, abundance, and cleaning behavior of commensal shrimps in the genus [[Periclimenes]] associated with the anemone [[Bartholomea annulata]] in [[Lameshur Bay]], [[Virgin Islands]]. Conducted during the [[Tektite I]] saturation diving mission (February 15–April 15, 1969), the research surveyed five distinct reef habitats and monitored 23 marked anemone stations over 25 days. [[Periclimenes pedersoni]] (Pederson cleaning shrimp) was the most abundant cleaner, followed by [[Periclimenes yucatanicus]] (spotted cleaner). Both species showed strong preference for [[Bartholomea annulata]] as host, with shrimp abundance varying significantly by habitat type. The unvegetated sand strip at the reef base supported the highest densities of P. pedersoni, correlating with high fish traffic and cleaning activity. Population monitoring revealed significant temporal variation in shrimp numbers per anemone, contradicting the notion of strict territoriality. The study provides insights into cleaning symbiosis, habitat preferences, and the evolutionary progression of cleaning behavior in [[Periclimenes]] species.

## Background & Description

[[Periclimenes pedersoni]] is a small shrimp (maximum carapace length ~6 cm) distributed in the Bahamas, Virgin Islands, Antigua, and Florida. It exhibits bright coloration making it conspicuous against the drab [[Bartholomea annulata]] anemone. The species is best termed an ectocommensal, deriving protection and a platform for attracting fish clients while providing no apparent benefit to the host anemone.

[[Periclimenes yucatanicus]] is smaller (<3 cm long) and widely distributed from Colombia to southern Florida. It associates with [[Bartholomea annulata]] and [[Condylactis gigantea]], and was observed on at least two other anemone species and the medusa [[Cassiopeia]]. This species demonstrates limited color-matching ability, assuming darker green shading when on green anemones.

[[Bartholomea annulata]] (corkscrew anemone) is the numerically dominant solitary anemone in [[Lameshur Bay]], occurring from just below low tide to at least 23 m depth. It typically attaches to hard substrate buried in mud or fine sand, often at the base of coral heads or on buried objects like dead coral or empty conch shells. The anemone hosts a complex community including at least four [[Periclimenes]] species, mysids ([[Heteromysis actinae]]), snapping shrimp ([[Alpheus armatus]]), hermit crabs, arrow crabs ([[Stenorynchus seticornis]]), and small fishes.

Other cleaner shrimps in the area included [[Stenopus hispidus]] (red banded coral shrimp) and [[Stenopus scutellatus]], though these were less important as cleaners than [[Periclimenes]] species. An unidentified species of [[Thor]] was regularly found on anemones, and [[Periclimenes americanus]] was occasionally observed near but not touching [[Bartholomea annulata]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area in [[Lameshur Bay]] encompassed five distinct physiognomic zones within swimming distance of the [[Tektite Habitat]]:

1. **Back reef** (13 m depth): 65 m transect with scattered coral boulders, rubble, profuse alcyonarians, carbonate sand, and diverse hard corals corresponding to Goreau's "cervicornis" zone
2. **Canyon** (re-entrant): North-south corridor housing the Habitat, steep-sided with moderate herbivorous fish grazing
3. **Reef front** (deep): Steep face typified by [[Montastrea annularis]], lacking intermediate buttress zone and shallow [[Acropora palmata]] due to low-energy conditions
4. **Unvegetated sand strip** (~10 m wide, 17 m depth): Bare zone at reef base maintained by herbivorous fish grazing, representing major corridor for fish movement
5. **Algal plain**: Extensive sand flats with [[Thalassia]], [[Udotea]], [[Syringodium]], [[Penicillus]], and [[Halimeda]], scattered boulders and sparse vegetation

The unvegetated sand strip is a persistent and important feature along Virgin Islands coastlines, delineated along more than half of St. John's 58-mile coastline. Its width corresponds to the maximum distance herbivorous reef fishes venture from shelter. The strip is most distinct where bordered by sea grasses and where the adjoining reef is well-developed, providing cover for more grazing fish.

[[Bartholomea annulata]] was approximately equally abundant in back reef, canyon, reef front, and sand strip (43–65% of all anemones in reef areas, 87% on sand strip, 74% on algal flat). The anemone's prevalence may explain its acceptance as the primary host for cleaner shrimp in the Caribbean.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and Abundance Patterns:**

- [[Periclimenes pedersoni]] was most abundant per unit area on the sand strip and reef front
- [[Periclimenes yucatanicus]] was most abundant on the algal plain
- Both species showed significantly higher abundance per anemone in sand strip and algal flat compared to reef areas (P = 0.05)
- On the sand strip, P. pedersoni averaged 3.30 shrimp per [[Bartholomea annulata]] versus 1.12–1.68 on reef areas
- P. yucatanicus averaged 0.72 per anemone on algal flat versus 0.00–0.20 on other areas

**Host Preference:**

- Both species showed strong but not exclusive preference for [[Bartholomea annulata]]
- P. pedersoni occurred on B. annulata 59–95% of the time depending on habitat (lowest on reef front where other anemones abundant, highest on sand strip where other anemones scarce)
- On sand strip, P. pedersoni occupied 83% of available B. annulata individuals
- P. yucatanicus occurred most often in habitats with isolated coral boulders on sand substrate
- No competitive exclusion between the two Periclimenes species; both frequently co-occurred on same anemones (chi-square test, P = 0.05)

**Population Dynamics:**

- Monitoring of 23 marked anemones over 25 days (seven surveys, March 2–26) revealed significant variation in shrimp populations between surveys (P = 0.05)
- Individual anemones with large populations (7–11 shrimp) consistently maintained high numbers; sparsely populated anemones (0–3) remained sparse
- No significant difference in individual shrimp length between surveys (P = 0.05)
- Repopulation experiments: after complete removal, anemones resettled at rate of ~1 shrimp per 3.5 days despite abundant shrimp on nearby anemones (within 6 m radius)
- Size-frequency analysis of 104 shrimp showed few juveniles in April sample
- Ovigerous females observed February–April (also reported April–August by Chace 1958), indicating lengthy reproductive period with slow continuous recruitment

**Cleaning Behavior:**

- P. pedersoni attracted fish by whipping antennae and swaying from prominent position on/near anemone
- Fishes often changed color, alighted on bottom, assumed submissive posture with mouth open and opercula flared
- Shrimp stopped antenna-whipping when fish close, then swam or walked to client
- Shrimp entered oral and gill cavities, sometimes exiting via gill opening
- If fish disturbed during cleaning, shrimp ejected from mouth before fish retreated
- Common fish clients: [[Nassau grouper]] ([[Epinephelus striatus]]), [[yellowtail snapper]] ([[Ocyurus chrysurus]]), [[mutton snapper]] ([[Lutjanus analis]]), [[dog snapper]] ([[Lutjanus jocu]]), [[spotted goatfish]] ([[Pseudupeneus maculatus]]), [[yellow goatfish]] ([[Mulloidichthys martinicus]]), [[gray angelfish]] ([[Pomacanthus arcuatus]]), [[bigeye]] ([[Priacanthus arenatus]]), [[sand diver]] ([[Synodus intermedius]])
- No predation on P. pedersoni or P. yucatanicus observed or reported in literature (though unidentified Periclimenes found in stomachs of [[yellowtail hamlet]], [[barred hamlet]], and [[high hat]])

**Supplementary Feeding:**

- Aquarium observations showed P. pedersoni captured and ate cyclopoid copepods from water column in absence of fish
- Mixed swarms of mysid [[Heteromysis actinae]] and unidentified brown copepod observed only at depopulated anemone stations, suggesting cleaners normally prey on small copepods
- Despite supplementary feeding capability, cleaning activity provides major food source

**Social Behavior:**

- Strong competition among individuals for prominent positions on anemone
- Size-dependent hierarchy: largest individuals stationed on tentacles or near oral disc; medium and small shrimp occupied positions on surrounding sand or vantage points (coral, sponges)
- Larger shrimp more active in soliciting fish, first to approach clients
- All individuals typically joined in cleaning once large shrimp initiated contact
- Peck order behavior observed in aquarium, with largest shrimp winning dominant positions

**Habitat-Behavior Relationships:**

- P. pedersoni abundance in sand strip attributed to high fish traffic rather than habitat preference per se
- Sand strip serves as natural corridor for fish movement along reef
- Dark [[Bartholomea annulata]] contrasts with light sandy substrate, providing good focal point for fish clients
- Visual cue enhanced by light-colored shrimp antennae against dark anemone background
- Reef stations attract smaller, more territorial fish species; less frequented by large fishes
- Midwater fishes ([[yellowtail snapper]], [[bar jack]]) rarely solicited cleaning from Periclimenes (single instance each), preferring cleaner fishes like [[Spanish hogfish]] ([[Bodianus rufus]]) and [[bluehead]] ([[Thalassoma bifasciatum]])
- Sessile shrimp cleaners tend to service fishes in open, accessible areas; cleaner fishes more successful soliciting clients in water column above main reef

**Evolutionary Speculations:**

- Proposed behavioral evolution sequence in [[Periclimenes]]:
  1. [[Periclimenes americanus]]: casual relationship with anemone, found around base beneath protective tentacles, no direct contact, no cleaning observed
  2. [[Periclimenes rathbunae]]: closer commensal relationship, dependent on anemone primarily for protection, hiding behavior and camouflage, no dancing or cleaning
  3. [[Periclimenes yucatanicus]]: danced and waved antennae but less active than P. pedersoni, attracted fish but never observed cleaning, never left anemone protection, bright coloration purpose unclear
  4. [[Periclimenes pedersoni]]: fully developed cleaning behavior, actively solicits and cleans fish clients
- P. yucatanicus suggested as intermediate stage: warning coloration may protect potential cleaner from predation prior to development of true cleaning behavior
- Mimicry hypothesis for P. yucatanicus not fully supported: color patterns dissimilar to P. pedersoni (longitudinal markings vs. saddles/spots), species co-occur randomly without competitive exclusion, suggesting niche separation in preferred food organisms
- P. yucatanicus cannot be induced to approach divers like P. pedersoni can

**Methodological Advantages:**

- Saturation diving from [[Tektite Habitat]] eliminated depth and time restraints of conventional SCUBA
- Extended observation periods allowed detailed behavioral studies and population monitoring
- Shrimp fearlessness permitted in situ counting and measuring with calipers without harm or removal
- Marked anemone stations (numbered styrofoam cubes on sandbags) enabled repeated surveys of same individuals
