# Experiments on Control of Phosphate Release from Lake Muds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

Hynes and Greib (1967, Proceedings of the Tenth Conference on Great Lakes Research) report laboratory experiments testing whether covering deoxygenated lake muds with polyethylene sheet or sand reduces phosphate release into overlying water. Across three experiments using muds from [[Sunfish Lake]] and deep water of [[Lake Erie]], covered muds released markedly less phosphate than bare controls, even when the polyethylene covered only part of the surface. The authors propose that weighted polyethylene sheeting could help restore small, valued lakes from [[Eutrophication]]. The study is explicitly preliminary, conducted in jars under artificial conditions. No fish or other animal taxa are studied; the work concerns sediment chemistry and water quality.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Phosphate is considered a key driver of [[Eutrophication]] in lakes. It is bound by ferric iron in benthic mud and released under deoxygenated conditions, a process described by [[Mortimer]] (1941).
- **Equilibrium concern:** Phosphate in water is in dynamic equilibrium with superficial deposits, so water concentrations alone may underestimate the reserve available. Organisms take up phosphate rapidly and recycle it, so small concentrations can support large biological production.
- **Rationale:** Preventing release, or restricting circulation, of phosphate from mud could control eutrophication in small lakes where high water quality is needed.
- **Material choice:** Polyethylene sheeting (density 0.93 to 0.95 g/cc, about 0.01 cm thick) was selected for its availability and impermeability to ions. Sand was tested as a second cover.

### Methods (summary)
- Mud collected with a grab sampler from depths below the [[Thermocline]], homogenised, and placed in 15 cm jars (about 1 litre of mud, 6 cm deep).
- Covers: polyethylene discs (complete, partial with 1/8 removed, or 7/8 cover), 1.5 cm acid-washed sand, or none (control). Water was layered with paraffin oil to seal the system.
- Oxygen measured by a scaled-down [[Winkler Method]]; phosphate by the Atkin modification of the Denigès method; conductivity in Experiment 3.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sunfish Lake** is meromictic, with black, azoic, sulphurous mud. Interstitial water contained 0.41 to 0.62 mg/l PO4, and the mud contained 0.9 mg PO4/g wet mud.
- **Deep Lake Erie mud** was grey, iron-rich, and contained 0.8 mg PO4/g wet weight.
- **Deoxygenation dynamics:** In Experiment 2, oxygen was nearly gone from all jars by day 32, when phosphate rose most steeply. In Experiment 3, controls became anoxic around day 100, while covered jars still had oxygen at day 201.
- **Microbial growth:** Visible bacterial growths developed on polyethylene and under the oil/water interface after about day 80 (Experiment 2), and in control jars from about day 46 (Experiment 3). The authors ended experiments partly because of these growths.
- **Temperature and pH:** Experiments ran at 10°C, a temperature higher than typical hypolimnetic stagnation. pH was not controlled; by day 56 of Experiment 3, controls reached pH 7.4 versus 6.7 to 6.9 under polyethylene.

## Key Findings

1. **Polyethylene reduces phosphate release.** In Experiment 1 (room temperature, Sunfish Lake mud, 6 covered and 4 control jars), the covered muds released far less phosphate. Ferric layers on the bare controls disappeared by day 6, whereas covered jars kept them until day 11. Controls also developed hydrogen sulphide odour by day 7.
2. **Partial covers are effective.** In Experiment 2 (4 sets of 6 jars, 10°C), sand and polyethylene covers significantly slowed phosphate release from days 20 to 30 onward. Partly covered polyethylene (7/8 cover, or 1/8 removed) was usually significantly different from controls, even though buckling exposed roughly half the mud surface. The authors concluded both sand and partial polyethylene significantly reduced release rate.
3. **Long-term persistence.** The four Experiment 2 treatment sets maintained their relative positions for over 120 days.
4. **Iron-phosphate dynamics.** In Experiment 3 (Lake Erie mud, 2 sets of 6 jars), control phosphate rose in two phases, attributed to oxidation of ferric hydroxide and then reduction of ferric phosphate. Covered jars had significantly lower phosphate from day 1. Phosphate in controls levelled off at about 1.0 mg/l after day 168. Iron in overlying water on day 36 was below 0.043 mg/l in all covered jars, versus only one such value among controls.
5. **Effects on algal growth (mixed).**
   - *[[Chlorella Vulgaris]]* (day 34): sand-treated water gave significantly lower dry weight than controls (0.00864 g vs 0.03092 g, P < 0.01). Polyethylene water (0.03661 g) did not differ significantly from controls. The authors traced the sand inhibition to an unidentified residual factor.
   - *[[Microcystis Aeruginosa]]* (day 110): polyethylene-treated water gave significantly lower dry weight than controls (0.03382 g vs 0.05274 g, P < 0.01) and sand (0.04834 g, P < 0.05). Phosphate was not the only limiting factor, and nitrate may also have been involved.
6. **Practical implication.** The authors suggest that a weighted, non-continuous polyethylene layer, laid when the lake is in full circulation and deposits hold the most phosphate, could help rescue small lakes from eutrophy. Gas release from deposits may make a fully continuous sheet inadvisable.
7. **Limitations acknowledged.** Mud was disturbed, so the natural mud-water interface was not preserved. Field hypolimnetic currents and dilution were not represented. Refined experiments with undisturbed interfaces were in progress.
