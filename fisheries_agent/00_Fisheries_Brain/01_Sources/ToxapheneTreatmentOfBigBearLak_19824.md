# Toxaphene Treatment of Big Bear Lake, California

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #pesticide-toxicology

## Executive Summary

[[Big Bear Lake]] in [[San Bernardino County]], California was treated with toxaphene in September 1960 to eradicate an abundant population of [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) and the ectoparasitic copepod *[[Lernaea carassii]]*. The treatment followed two unsuccessful rotenone applications. Despite multiple applications at concentrations ranging from 0.03 to 0.10 ppm, the toxaphene treatment failed to completely eliminate goldfish, though their numbers were drastically reduced. The lake remained toxic for 10 months, requiring extensive bioassay monitoring with [[Rainbow Trout]] before restocking could occur. The study concluded that toxaphene detoxifies slowly, contaminates lake biota for extended periods, and should not be used as a fish toxicant in California.

## Background & Description

[[Big Bear Lake]] is a reservoir located approximately 100 miles east of Los Angeles at 6,670 feet elevation in the [[San Bernardino National Forest]]. At the time of treatment, the reservoir had a surface area of 1,200 acres and volume of 4,800 acre-feet (reduced from full capacity of 2,600 acres and 72,400 acre-feet due to drought). Maximum depth was 32 feet at the dam. The reservoir is wasp-shaped with an upper shallow area (mean depth 2.5 feet) separated from a deeper lower area (mean depth 5 feet) by "the narrows."

The fish population consisted of approximately 90% [[Goldfish]], with small percentages of [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus nebulosus*), and a few hatchery-reared [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). Goldfish competed with trout for food and were a nuisance to anglers. The ectoparasitic copepod *[[Lernaea carassii]]* was endemic in the goldfish population but became problematic after the 1959 rotenone treatment failed, allowing the goldfish population to explode and Lernaea to spread rapidly through the trout population in epizootic proportions.

Water chemistry analysis (September 6, 1960) showed: pH 9.2, conductivity 223 micromhos/cm at 23°C, total dissolved solids 104 ppm (hard water series), total hardness 104 ppm. Major ionic constituents included calcium (30 ppm), carbonate (45 ppm), bicarbonate (37 ppm), and sulphate (30 ppm).

Toxaphene is an insecticide patented by Hercules Powder Company. Two formulations were used: 60% and 80% toxaphene as active ingredient, with remainder consisting of inert ingredients and petroleum carrier. The [[California Department of Public Health]] required that public fishing not be allowed until 50% of trout held in live-cars survived at least 20 days.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Zooplankton was abundant during summer before treatment, consisting of 95-99% *[[Cyclops]]* and *[[Daphnia]]*. Other plankton constituents included green algae (*[[Ulothrix]]*), protozoans (*Monas*), and unidentified rotifers. Secchi disk visibility was 18 inches at time of treatment.

Surface water temperature during treatment period ranged from 52-60°F. The watershed totals 36 square miles with intermittent streams. Lake tributaries were dry at time of treatment. The 1960/61 winter was exceptionally dry with only 2.76 inches precipitation compared to normal 37.66 inches, preventing the anticipated dilution of toxaphene through winter runoff.

Starting in January 1961, large concentrations of [[Cladocera]] were observed in limnetic (open water) and windward littoral areas. These invertebrates appeared healthy while confined trout died, a phenomenon also observed in [[British Columbia]] studies.

## Key Findings

**Treatment Protocol and Timing:**
- An earth dike was placed across the narrows to separate upper and lower lake sections
- Upper lake treated by airplane: 0.05 ppm (September 7), 0.05 ppm (September 16), 0.10 ppm (September 26)
- Lower lake treated by airplane (September 13, 28) at 0.03 ppm, with additional boat application from pressurized 30-gallon drums at 0.10 ppm
- Ornamental fish ponds within one mile treated with liquid rotenone exceeding 10 ppm

**Fish Mortality Results:**
- Dead and dying fish observed 2 hours after first treatment of both lake sections
- Fish remained in distress 2-3 days following each treatment phase
- Estimated 95% of goldfish and all other fish species eliminated
- Live goldfish located in large numbers one week after first treatment
- Most surviving goldfish were gold-colored adults 3-7 inches long
- Gold-colored goldfish proved more hardy to toxaphene than green-colored ones (no explanation found)
- Receding water left beach 10-20 feet wide almost paved with dead goldfish

**Toxaphene Residue in Fish:**
- Brown bullhead collected October 4-5 (5 days post-treatment): 3-5 ppm in flesh, 20 ppm in fat and flesh combined
- Goldfish collected October 4-8: 3 ppm in flesh, 20 ppm in fat and flesh
- Goldfish November 21 (54 days post-treatment): 2-25 ppm
- Goldfish January 20 (114 days post-treatment): 45-208 ppm
- Goldfish March 9 (162 days post-treatment): 60-260 ppm
- Live goldfish appeared in good condition despite containing toxaphene concentrations previously thought lethal
- Trout (composite samples) March-April: 2-16 ppm after 4 days in live-cars

**Toxaphene Residue in Other Organisms:**
- Plankton samples ranged from trace to 97 ppm throughout 265-day monitoring period
- Sessile algae: 7-23 ppm (March-April 1961)
- Bottom mud: 16-18 ppm (April 1961)
- Apparent increases in toxaphene residue indicated natural cycling in ecosystem
- Analysis of lake water proved unreliable as index of toxicity to fish life

**Wildlife Impacts:**
- Shore birds and waterfowl, mostly [[Coots]] (*Fulica americana*), died during treatment
- Coots killed by eating toxaphene-killed fish or direct exposure to aerial spray
- Toxaphene concentrations in birds ranged from 10 to 1,200 ppm
- Toxaphene killed all insects feeding on putrefying fish, causing rapid sun-drying of carcasses

**Bioassay Program:**
- Intensive monitoring started March 1961 (6 months post-treatment)
- Live-car stations established at shore docks and in lake center at surface and below
- Trout died within 12 hours in November 1960 and January 1961 tests
- Live-cars covered with fish netting instead of wire to prevent mechanical damage
- Some cars made semi-buoyant to stay just below surface

**Detoxification Attempts:**
- Two copper sulphate treatments (April and June) applied to control plankton
- Assumption: reducing plankton would reduce lake toxicity
- Live-car observations indicated toxicity reduced after copper sulphate treatment
- Lake remained toxic 10 months after treatment

**Reopening Timeline:**
- Lake opened to fishing 10 months after treatment (July 1961)
- Detoxification proceeded sufficiently to allow trout restocking
- Control station at [[Green Valley Lake]] operated May 19-July 5, 1961 without significant mortalities

**Critical Failures:**
- Treatment failed because too small a quantity of toxaphene was used
- Late application date contributed to failure
- Lack of winter runoff (drought conditions) prevented anticipated dilution
- Lake remained toxic much longer than estimated

**Recommendations:**
- Toxaphene should be used to control rough fish only after safer chemicals and methods ruled out
- Detoxifies slowly and contaminates lake biota for unknown period
- Authors recommend against its use as fish toxicant anywhere in California
- Results consistent with similar work in [[Canada]] (Stringer and McMynn, 1960) and [[New Mexico]] (Kallman, Cope, and Navarre, 1962)
