# Riparian Inventory of the San Pedro River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-vegetation

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Land Management technical report (San Pedro Technical Report Number 4, 1988) inventories woody riparian vegetation along the San Pedro River in Arizona as part of the proposed [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]]. Fieldwork in 1986 sampled two Spanish land grants using a point-sample pace-transect method. The inventory was designed to assess current riparian vegetation status, establish baseline data, and provide a basis for detecting short- and long-term trends. The study finds that the south grant supports a healthier [[Broadleaf Riparian Community]], while the north grant is dominated by [[Riparian Scrub Community]] and appears to be in an early successional stage.

Note: this document concerns riparian vegetation rather than fish or aquatic fauna. No fish species, aquatic invertebrates or water chemistry data are reported.

## Background & Description

The inventory was prepared by Kim McReynolds of the [[San Pedro Riparian Management Area]], San Simon Resource Area, Safford District, [[Bureau of Land Management]]. The study area comprises the San Juan de las Boquillas y Nogales grant (north grant) and the San Rafael del Valle grant (south grant), with the transect running from Hereford northward.

The study defined riparian zones by bank position:
- **Lower bank:** from the river edge to the first abrupt step created by minor flooding, situated in the [[Flood Plain]].
- **Middle bank:** from the lower bank edge to the next abrupt elevational change.
- **Upper bank:** typically upland vegetation, used as the riparian zone boundary.

Woody species were grouped into two communities for classification:
- **Broadleaf riparian:** Arizona walnut, Fremont cottonwood, Goodding willow, velvet ash, burro brush, mesquite.
- **Riparian scrub:** seep willow, netleaf hackberry, desert willow, graythorn.

A total of 13 woody species were listed in Table 2, including [[Fremont Cottonwood]], [[Goodding Willow]], [[Seep Willow]], [[Velvet Ash]], [[Arizona Walnut]] and [[Mesquite]]. Plant names follow the Soil Conservation Service National Plant List (May 1980).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design**
- Point-sample pace-transect with a 20-pace interval (approximately 120 feet).
- Transects spaced 60 paces (approximately 360 feet) apart, running perpendicular to the river.
- No sample points were placed in the river itself.
- Total of 1,824 sample points.

**Vegetation and population measures**
- Woody species were recorded as the nearest woody plant within a 180-degree arc, with broadleaf species preferred to maximise broadleaf data.
- Tree diameters were estimated with a Biltmore Stick and sorted into size classes: seedling (<1 in), young (1–3 in), pole (4–12 in), tree (13–24 in), veteran (25+ in).
- Species densities and size classes were recorded within a 0.1 acre plot at each point.
- Ground cover was classed as gravel, rock, bare ground, litter, perennial grass or forb.
- Browsing by wildlife and livestock was recorded.

**Ground cover (Table 1)**

| Location | Bare ground (%) | Litter (%) | Bermuda grass (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| South grant, lower bank | 58 | 36 | 6 |
| South grant, middle bank | 35 | 63 | 2 |
| North grant, lower bank | 58 | 37 | 5 |
| North grant, middle bank | 27 | 72 | 1 |

Litter on the middle bank was roughly twice that of the lower bank. The authors suggest lower-bank litter is washed away during river rises. Bermuda grass (*Cynodon dactylon*) was more abundant on the lower bank, where shallow-rooted plants have more moisture.

**Successional dynamics**
- [[Seep Willow]] is a pioneer species in shallow groundwater sites. Its stands trap sediment, building stream terraces that eventually provide seedbeds for cottonwood and root space for Goodding willow (Brady et al. 1985).
- Cottonwood germination requires large flows that deposit alluvium, which may occur only once every 2 to 3 years. Seeds lose viability within 3 to 5 weeks (Fenner 1980).

**Land use**
- Cattle have historically grazed the grants from early winter through summer, so seedlings are browsed during their growing season.

## Key Findings

- **Community dominance:** Riparian scrub dominates the north grant and most of the middle bank along the entire reach. Broadleaf riparian is well represented on the lower bank of the south grant. The southernmost part of the south grant is the exception where broadleaf dominates.
- **Broadleaf occurrence:** Broadleaf riparian species occurred in 83% of plots on the south grant, compared with 63% on the north grant.
- **Ecological condition:** The south grant's broadleaf community is described as in good ecological condition. The north grant is in an early successional stage and may be moving toward a healthy broadleaf community. Broadleaf quality declines from Hereford toward the north grant boundary.
- **Size classes:** Cottonwood and willow show a large seedling class (80% of cottonwood and 89% of willow seedlings were browsed). Goodding willow approximates a bell-shaped curve, while cottonwood does not. Arizona walnut and velvet ash sample sizes were too small for conclusions.
- **Bank patterns:** Seedlings and young trees occur mainly on the lower bank, while mature and veteran trees are found on the middle bank, consistent with terrace formation through aggradation and flooding.
- **Recommendation against planting:** The San Pedro River study team recommended against artificial tree planting (USDI 1987). Planting would confound future assessments of natural succession and regeneration timing.
- **Recommendations for future work:**
  1. Repeat the point-sample pace-transect at least every 5 years.
  2. Correlate size class data with river flow data.
  3. Continue browse measurements, even without livestock, to track wildlife use.
