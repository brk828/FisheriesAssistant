# Bonita Creek Unique Waters Nomination: Graham County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This April 1989 nomination, prepared by the [[Bureau Of Land Management]] Safford District Office and the City of Safford and submitted to the [[Arizona Department Of Environmental Quality]], seeks Unique Waters designation for a 15-mile perennial segment of [[Bonita Creek]]. The segment is proposed for protection under the antidegradation provisions of the Arizona water quality standards (R18-11-202 C and D) and the Unique Waters criteria (R18-11-303). The creek is a municipal water source for roughly 15,000 people in the Gila Valley, supports one of the highest breeding bird diversities in the United States, holds one of the greatest recorded fish biomasses in a southwestern stream, and contains critical habitat for several threatened and endangered species. The authors state that no known conflicts with current or anticipated uses should result. Proposed standards appear in Tables 2 and 3.

## Background & Description

**Location and extent.** The nominated segment begins where the creek exits the [[San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation]] (Section 27, T. 4 S., R. 26 E.) and flows about 15 stream miles south-southeast to the [[Gila River]] near Safford. The total watershed covers about 236,000 acres (370 sq mi), of which 32,300 acres (50 sq mi) lie within the nominated segment. Land is 92% BLM-administered, 2% City of Safford, and 6% private.

**Physiography and soils.** The watershed is dissected volcanic highland with steep, twisting canyons. Elevations range from 4,976 ft at upper Bonita Creek to 3,200 ft at the Gila confluence. Shallow mountain soils (Limpia-Graham-Rock outcrop; Peloncillo-Orthents-Pinaleno) dominate, and Torrifluvents-Riverwash soils occur along the channel.

**Climate and hydrology.** Annual precipitation is 10 to 15 inches, mostly in late summer. Drought is most common April to July. Flow is perennial but disappears in some reaches during hot summers and droughts. The [[USGS Streamgage 09447800]] has operated since August 1, 1981. Measured flow ranged from 0.68 cfs (December 1982) to 19,400 cfs (October 2, 1983). The BLM holds an instream flow application for 5 cfs (filed November 1985).

**Water rights.** The City of Safford holds a right to 3,240,000 gallons per day (granted 1953, priority date October 18, 1950).

**Access.** Two roads serve the canyon bottom, including the Upper Lee Trail, which reaches the gaging station.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish and wildlife.** Lower Bonita Creek supports several protected species (Table 1). Federally listed or candidate species include the [[Gila Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] (introduced by the Arizona Game and Fish Department), [[Southern Bald Eagle]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], [[Black Hawk]], [[Northern Beardless Tyrannulet]], [[Yellow-Billed Cuckoo]], and [[Gila Monster]]. Five native fish species breed in the creek, and the reported fish biomass is 46.7 g/m². Breeding bird richness is 64 species, and more than 100 Migratory Bird Treaty Act birds have been recorded. The text also cites 58 mammal, 9 amphibian, and 55 reptile species, and notes over 230 fish and wildlife species overall.

**Vegetation.** The creek historically supported [[Fremont Cottonwood]], [[Arizona Sycamore]], Goodding willow, Arizona walnut, and white oak. Grazing, flooding, and homesteading degraded much of this community to mesquite bosque or riparian scrub. Uplands are desert grassland, with chaparral and juniper at higher elevations and creosote and Sonoran desertscrub in the south.

**Threats to water quality** (eight listed): uncontrolled cattle use, excessive grazing and erosion, off-road vehicle (ORV) damage, mining claims, woodcutting, major floods, brush invasion after fire control, and upstream development on the reservation, which lies outside Bureau or City control.

**Cultural resources.** The canyon holds eight known cliff dwellings (one NRHP-eligible in 1976), six rock art sites, the Bonita Creek Ceremonial Cave (evidence of a late 13th-century [[Anasazi]] migration route), and the Kearny Campsite and Trail (NRHP-listed 1974).

**Minerals and energy.** Gold placers near the Gila mouth have been worked intermittently. A 1980 geothermal leasing assessment imposed a "no surface occupancy" stipulation within one-half mile of perennial streams, including Bonita Creek.

## Key Findings

**Water quality**
- Dr. Sommerfeld (1977–78, four sites) found physical and chemical parameters generally good. Total coliforms ranged from under 500 to 25,000 and exceeded surface-water standards about one-third of the time. About 15% of contamination was attributed to human sources.
- BLM monitoring (1986–1988, three sites) found overall quality excellent. Exceedances were limited to iron, fecal coliform, and total coliform, with iron exceeding standards on August 5, 1987, a week after a major flood. In December 1988, copper and iron exceeded drinking water and aquatic standards at Lower Bonita.

**Designation basis**
- Criterion C (outstanding public resource) and Criterion D (critical habitat for threatened or endangered species) are both asserted.
- Secondary criteria are claimed for eight of ten categories (R18-11-303), including recreation, national land system inclusion (proposed ACEC; mouth within proposed Gila Box Wild and Scenic River; eastern watershed within the Turtle Mountain WSA), essential habitat, social and economic benefits, scientific value, vulnerability, ecological uniqueness, and meeting water quality standards.

**Management measures**
- The 1988 Western Division of the American Fisheries Society awarded the BLM Safford District its top national riparian management award.
- The 1987 BLM Riparian Management Policy and the 1986 BLM–City cooperative agreement guide management.
- The Geronimo MFP recommends ONA classification and a 3–5 year grazing deferral.
- The RMP preferred alternative recommends a 2,747-acre ACEC with limits on mining, ORVs, woodcutting, and recreation, and a management plan due by 1993.
- Tree planting and exclosures have been carried out. Three grazing allotments have been reduced, and a deferred-rotation system is in place.

**Proposed standards (Table 2 and Table 3)**
- Trace and nutrient standards are set at observed maxima or derived maxima with standard deviations, or kept at state values where those were already more stringent. Examples: arsenic 0.010 mg/L, lead 0.020 mg/L, nitrates 1.26 mg/L, total dissolved solids 309 mg/L, turbidity 2.74 NTU.
- Fecal coliform single-sample standard is 200 CFU/100 ml (versus 1000 state DWS/A&W, 200 FBC), and geometric mean is 20 (state 1000).
- Fecal streptococcus interim single-sample standard is 3034 CFU/100 ml, pending a final standard submitted by June 1990 after two-month sampling.
- Bacterial trends are attributed to lower cattle numbers (permit reduced from 287 to under 100 on one allotment) and to restored riparian filtration. The new owner is now stocking the full permit of 287, and the authors expect some increase in bacteria.
