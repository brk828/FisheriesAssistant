# Distribution and Life History Notes on the Taillight Shiner *Notropis maculatus* in Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #notropis

## Executive Summary

This 1975 reprint from the *Transactions of the Kentucky Academy of Science* by Brooks M. Burr and Lawrence M. Page, of the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]], reports [[Notropis Maculatus]] (Taillight Shiner) from [[Ohio River]] oxbow lakes in Ballard and McCracken counties, western Kentucky. These populations are the northernmost known for the species, which extends the range recorded by Sisk (1973). The authors compare the Kentucky life history with a [[Central Florida]] population described by Cowell and Barnett (1974). Kentucky fish spawned earlier and in a shorter window, had a different sex ratio, and appeared to live less than 2 years, as in Florida.

## Background & Description

The authors note that Sisk (1973) recorded the species from three localities in extreme southwestern Kentucky and considered them the northern range limit. Burr and Page show that the species also occurs, sometimes commonly, in a series of oxbow lakes lining the southern edge of the Ohio River. These oxbows are deep, have cypress swamp margins, and some are connected to the river during floods.

Specimens are deposited in the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]]. Representative collections:

- **Ballard County:** [[Mitchell Lake]] (6 and 1 specimens), [[Butler Lake]] (3), [[Fish Lake]] (2), [[Prairie Lake]] (20), and an unnamed slough near Gum Corners (43).
- **McCracken County:** [[Crawford Lake]] (4), [[Metropolis Lake]] (5, 10, 83, and 5 specimens across 1967, 1969, 1972, and 1975).

Thirty-one other fish species were collected with the shiner, including gars, bowfin, paddlefish, minnows, sunfishes, darters, and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). The list is dominated by [[Lepomis]] sunfishes and [[Etheostoma]] darters.

Collections were made in April (5 specimens), May (83), August (77), and September (21).

Breeding males from the Metropolis Lake spawning school showed a suffusion of red over much of the body and head, and dark markings on the caudal peduncle, dorsal fin, and midlateral band were prominent. Females lacked red pigment except for some gravid females with pale red snouts. Small white tubercles were present on males but absent in breeding females. The authors note that the red coloration matches the Louisiana description by Douglas (1974) more closely than the Florida description.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Burr and Page found the species mainly in marginal vegetation and in accumulations of sticks and debris in shallow water. At Metropolis Lake on 28 May 1972, a school was spawning beneath or next to a large log in water 15 to 30 cm deep.

Range context:
- The new localities appear to mark the northern range limit. Extensive searches on the Illinois side of the Ohio River failed to find the species.
- Pflieger (1971, 1974) considered the species probably extirpated from Missouri, where it had not been found in more than 30 years.
- A hiatus separates the Ohio River oxbow records from Mississippi River backwater records in southwestern Kentucky. The authors suggest poor collecting, but topographic maps show few oxbows, sloughs, or backwater ponds in that area, so the species may be absent.

Life history comparisons with [[Central Florida]] populations:
- **Spawning season:** Florida fish breed from March to early October, with ripe fish in every collection. Kentucky fish showed young of the year in April and spawning individuals in May, and none in breeding condition in August or September. The spawning period was at least March to May.
- **Fecundity:** Mature ova are those over 0.8 mm in diameter. In Florida, 47 females carried 72 to 408 ova (mean 163). In Kentucky, 21 females collected 28 May 1972 carried 25 to 431 ova (mean 246). The larger average in Kentucky probably reflects that 11 of the 21 females exceeded 53 mm total length.
- **Size and sex ratio:** Females averaged significantly larger than males in both regions. Florida mean total lengths were 44.4 mm (females) and 41.6 mm (males). Kentucky females averaged 52.7 mm TL (n = 22, range 42.1 to 60.6 mm) and males 48.6 mm TL (n = 61, range 40.9 to 60.3 mm; t = 4.16, p < .005).
- **Age:** No annuli were visible in Florida. In Kentucky, a weak annulus appeared on some fish, but aging was not feasible. Size-frequency data (Table 1) indicate a maximum lifespan under 2 years in both Kentucky and Florida.

## Key Findings

1. **Northern range extension:** [[Notropis Maculatus]] is established in Ohio River oxbows of Ballard and McCracken counties, Kentucky. These are the northernmost known populations, and the authors consider the Illinois side of the Ohio River unlikely to host the species.

2. **Habitat:** The species occupies deep, cypress-margined oxbows and sloughs, usually in shallow vegetated or debris-rich water.

3. **Spawning behavior:** A large school spawned in shallow water near a log at [[Metropolis Lake]] on 28 May 1972 (83 specimens collected). Breeding males were brightly colored, with red pigmentation and white tubercles.

4. **Sex ratio:** Overall, the 190 Kentucky specimens had a female-to-male ratio of 0.8:1 (χ² = 2.10, not significant). At Metropolis Lake, the ratio was 0.5:1 across 106 specimens (χ² = 9.66, p < .005). In the 28 May spawning school (83 specimens), it was 0.4:1 (χ² = 18.32, p < .005). Unlike in Florida, where females outnumbered males, all Kentucky specimens were taken near shore, where males were relatively more common.

5. **Reproduction:** Kentucky spawning appears restricted to roughly March through May, a shorter season than the March to early October breeding recorded in [[Central Florida]]. Mature females carried 25 to 431 ova, and fecundity rose with body size.

6. **Growth and longevity:** Mature Kentucky fish were apparently about 1 year old, and the species appears to live less than 2 years, consistent with Florida data.

7. **Range gaps:** The gap between Ohio River and Mississippi River records in southwestern Kentucky may reflect a true absence of suitable habitat rather than inadequate sampling.
