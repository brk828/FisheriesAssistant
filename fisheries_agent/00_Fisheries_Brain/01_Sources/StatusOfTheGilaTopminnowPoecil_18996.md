# Status of the Gila Topminnow in the United States (1987)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 special report by [[Lee H Simons]] of the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] assesses the status of the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) in the United States under Project E-1 of Title VI of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The subspecies was listed as endangered in 1967. Its decline is attributed to habitat loss and predation by the exotic [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). Six years of intensive recovery work under the 1983 [[Recovery Plan]] produced 35 surviving introduced populations within historic range, and the report concludes that the [[Downlisting]] criteria were met in 1985 and remained met in 1987. The author warns, however, that most populations are fragile, that genetic diversity has not been conserved, and that complete recovery will require more introductions and protection of natural populations.

## Background & Description

- **Subject species:** The Sonoran topminnow (*P. occidentalis*) has two U.S. subspecies: the Yaqui topminnow (*P. o. sonoriensis*) of the Yaqui River drainage and the Gila topminnow (*P. o. occidentalis*) of the southern Colorado River drainage. Mexican populations are excluded from the report.
- **Historical status:** The species was once widespread and abundant in southern Arizona (Hubbs and Miller 1941). It was listed as endangered in 1967.
- **Management framework:**
  - A September 1981 [[Memorandum Of Understanding]] among the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Forest Service]], and Arizona Game and Fish Department set guidelines for introductions on Forest Service lands.
  - An intensive introduction effort began in 1982.
  - The 1983 Recovery Plan set separate downlisting and delisting criteria for the Gila subspecies.
- **Downlisting criterion:** Twenty populations successfully reestablished in the wild within historic range and surviving at least 3 years.
- **Delisting criteria (post-1987):** 50 reestablished populations surviving at least 3 years, or 30 surviving at least 5 years.
- **Terminology problems:** The report notes that key terms in the Recovery Plan are undefined, including "successfully reestablished," "in the wild," and "population." Stock tanks, troughs, and downstream flood-dispersed groups all raise classification questions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats**

- **Mosquitofish predation and competition:** Mosquitofish dominated or replaced topminnow at several natural sites. Examples include Sheehy Spring (>99% in 1987), Bylas Spring (99%), and Sharp Spring (92%).
- **Habitat instability:** Floods and droughts repeatedly eliminated populations. A mild 1987 drought had serious effects on introduced populations.
- **Fragile artificial habitats:** Many introduced populations depend on pumped groundwater, impounded water, barriers, or troughs that will eventually fail. These include [[Cottonwood Artesian]], [[Artesian Well 3]], and [[Yellowstone Tank]].
- **Other exotics:** Green sunfish, red shiner, largemouth bass, sunfish, bluegill, goldfish, and fathead minnow occur at several sites and pose additional risks.
- **Development:** Interstate 10 construction and related development near [[Cienega Creek]] could deplete groundwater, pollute habitat, and facilitate exotic introductions.

**Coexistence with mosquitofish**

- At [[Sharp Spring]], floods appear to displace mosquitofish more than topminnow. This allows coexistence over longer periods than in unflooded areas.
- At [[Redrock Canyon]], mosquitofish dominated the stretch below stock tanks, while topminnow occupied the stretch below. The two species co-occurred over about 5 km.

**Genetic context**

- All but two introductions since 1981 (and most earlier ones) derive from a single lineage, the [[Monkey Spring]] population.
- Monkey Spring fish are among the least fecund studied and are genetically uniform (Vrijenhoek et al. 1985).
- The author argues that total genetic diversity is at its lowest point ever.

## Key Findings

**1. Introductions and survival**

- 191 introductions are known in total, with 99 made since 1982.
- 18% of the 191 known introductions and 28% of the 99 introductions since 1982 persisted in 1987.
- In Fall 1987, 35 introduced populations existed within historic range.
- 30 of these had persisted at least 3 years, and 23 had persisted at least 5 years.
- The oldest known introduced population is at [[Hidden Water Spring]] (stocked 1976).
- Five extirpations were documented between 1985 and 1986, and 10 between 1986 and 1987. Table 2 lists 15 failed sites, with dried habitat, low water, and unknown causes recorded as reasons.

**2. Classification of introduced populations (1987)**

| Category | Description | Count |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Moderate–high numbers in natural habitat expected to persist without support | 10 |
| 2 | Moderate–high numbers dependent on anthropogenic support | 17 |
| 3 | Low numbers or limited habitat; not expected to persist many years | 6 |
| 4 | Threatened by mosquitofish | 2 |

- Categories 1 and 2 total 27 populations free of mosquitofish and immediate threats.
- Excluding Categories 3 and 4, 22 populations have persisted at least 3 years and 17 at least 5 years. These exceed the downlisting requirement.
- Six dispersal-derived populations (five from introduction sites, one from an unknown source) were excluded from Recovery Plan totals because their legal and management status is unclear.

**3. Natural populations**

- 10 natural populations persist. Four are free of exotic fishes, and six are threatened by mosquitofish.
- An eleventh, at Salt Creek, was lost between 1980 and 1984 after mosquitofish invasion.
- [[Sheehy Spring]] and Sharp Spring were nearly extinct in 1987.
- [[Bylas Spring]] was dominated by mosquitofish about 1.5 years after reinvasion.
- Below [[Patagonia Lake]], topminnow persist in [[Sonoita Creek]] amid mosquitofish, with relative abundance most stable 1.6–2.0 km below the lake.
- At [[Santa Cruz River]], topminnow persisted despite mosquitofish dominance (81% in August 1987).
- No formal protection (such as conservation easements) had been secured for the seven natural populations on private land.

**4. Newly documented sites**

- Topminnow were found at four previously unreported, isolated sites within the Redrock Canyon drainage, all without mosquitofish in 1987.
- A population was discovered near Rincon in Saguaro National Monument East.
- Mosquitofish were not confirmed in lower Lampshire Canyon in 1987 despite an earlier report.

**5. Conclusions and recommendations**

- The author concludes that downlisting from endangered to threatened is supported by the 1985 and 1987 data, though many populations are not truly self-sustaining.
- Future introductions should not use Monkey Spring stock. Sharp Spring fish, now held as broodstock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], are a more genetically varied source.
- At least one population from each geographic area (Bylas, San Rafael Valley, Sonoita Creek, Cienega Creek) should be used for introductions within its own area.
- Mosquitofish-free natural systems should be sought for introductions. Without them, complete recovery is unlikely.
- Continued monitoring, renovation, mosquitofish control, and protection of private natural populations (through purchase or easements) are required.
- Downlisting should be publicized to build support for desert fishes and the Endangered Species Act, but recovery work must continue so that progress is not lost.
