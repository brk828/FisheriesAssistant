# Multiple Clutch Spawning in Notropis leedsi (Bannerfin Shiner)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[D. C. Heins]] and [[F. G. Rabito Jr.]] (Tulane University, published in the *Journal of Fish Biology*, 1986) provides direct evidence that females of the North American minnow [[Notropis Leedsi]] (bannerfin shiner) produce multiple clutches of eggs in a single reproductive season. Laboratory-reared pairs were observed in aquaria, and ovum diameter histograms were compared with wild-caught females from Florida and Georgia museum collections. The authors conclude that the pattern of ovum development in laboratory and wild fish is the same, so the laboratory observations likely reflect natural processes. The work also argues that the term "fractional spawner" is a poor descriptor of this reproductive mode.

## Background & Description

- **Reproductive effort** is defined as the total investment in reproduction, including energy and material allocated to reproductive tissues and the hazards of reproducing (after [[Pianka]], 1983). An organism that produces more clutches per season invests more effort (after [[Williams]], 1966).
- The **schedule of reproduction** is a key component of life history strategy and is needed for building life tables.
- Earlier authors on [[Notropis]] suspected multiple clutches from vitellogenic ova distributions and female reproductive condition, but direct evidence was lacking. Prior work by [[Gale and Gale]] (1977) and [[Gale and Buynak]] (1978) showed that total fecundity per season exceeds the number of mature ova present at one time, but did not link ovarian events to spawning.
- A **clutch** is defined as a group of propagules that develop synchronously and are oviposited within a relatively short period.
- The species is a **crevice spawner**, as established by [[Rabito and Heins]] (1985).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Laboratory conditions:** Six 38-L aquaria with under-gravel filtration, 16:8 h light:dark cycle, 24-25°C (occasionally 22-26°C), fed commercial flake food.
- **Spawning substrates:** Pairs of 15 × 15 × 2 cm clay tiles separated by a silicone bead to form a crevice, raised 7 cm above the gravel on glass jars.
- **Field material:** Preserved specimens from the Florida State Museum (University of Florida, Gainesville), collected from streams in Florida and Georgia.
- **Comparative context:** The authors note that similar debates about multiple clutches occurred in darters ([[Percidae]]: *Ammocrypta*, *Etheostoma*, *Percina*), with [[Hubbs]] and colleagues and [[Gale and Deutsch]] (1985) on one side and [[Page]] (1983) on the other.
- **Temperature effect:** Interspawning intervals were recorded at 22-26°C; the seasonal timing in nature was not measured directly.

## Key Findings

**1. Spawning performance in the laboratory**
- 193 spawning intervals were observed across 34 pairs.
- Interspawning intervals ranged from 3 to 10 days, with a mean of 4.6 days and a mode of 4 days.
- Clutch sizes ranged from 26 to 228 ova in females of 36.7-52.5 mm standard length.
- In one female, three successive clutches were spawned at 4-day intervals while unhatched eggs remained on the substrates.

**2. Ovarian development**
- Pre-spawning females (Fig. 2) showed two distinct vitellogenic oocyte groups: mature (large, opaque, cream to yellow) and maturing (smaller, translucent to opaque).
- Females killed during spawning (Fig. 3) showed three groups: spawned eggs, ripe ovulated intra-ovarian eggs, and maturing oocytes.
- Females killed shortly after spawning (Fig. 4) had one group of vitellogenic ova, showing that all mature ova in a mature female are spawned in one relatively short episode.
- A small number of mature or ripe ova were occasionally not ovulated and presumably underwent atresia.

**3. Reproductive condition classification**
- The authors developed a scheme with five stages: Latent (LA), Early Maturing (EM), Late Maturing (LM), Mature (MA), and Ripe (RE). Females classed MA or RE are considered actively reproductive. The EM/LM distinction is the most subjective step.
- The scheme may apply to other small cyprinids, and possibly darters, with modifications.

**4. Batch size versus final clutch**
- Across 20 females, the final clutch was significantly greater than the mean preliminary batch size (P < 0.05) in only three cases (females 1, 11 and 19 in Table II). All other comparisons were non-significant.
- Overall, the number of mature ova in a female killed before spawning predicted the size of the next spawn.

**5. Effect of removing eggs from substrates**
- Of six females tested, two did not complete the test (one egg-bound, one discarded for irregular results).
- In the remaining four, removing eggs had no detectable effect on interspawning intervals (Table III).

**6. Reproductive season in nature**
- Gross examination of museum collections (Table IV, females > 35 mm S.L., from multiple Florida and Georgia counties) showed a reproductive season lasting at least May to September.
- The authors suggest reproduction may begin in April, but data for some months were weak or absent.
- Male reproductive condition supported the female data.

**7. Wild versus laboratory comparison**
- Ovum diameter histograms for three wild-caught MA females (41.5-48.4 mm S.L.) showed the same two-group pattern as laboratory females killed before spawning.

**8. Conclusions on terminology and method**
- The authors do not favour the term "fractional spawner" ([[Nikolsky]], 1963), since it does not describe the ovarian events of multiple-clutch species.
- Recruitment of primary oocytes likely occurs during the reproductive season, so a single season's eggs do not represent a fixed fraction of the oocytes present at its start.
- Fecundity estimates should rely only on mature ova, not maturing ones. Field samples should be random or stratified random and drawn periodically across the season.
