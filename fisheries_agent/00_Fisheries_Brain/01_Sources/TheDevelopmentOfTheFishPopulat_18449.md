# Fish Population Development in Clearwater Lake, Missouri (1948–1954)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tracks the fish community of [[Clearwater Lake]], a 1,650-acre flood-control reservoir on the [[Black River]] in the [[Ozark Highland]] of Missouri, during its first seven years after impoundment (1948–1954). Sampling used nets, seines, rotenone and [[Creel Census]] data. The authors did not estimate total population size; they tracked relative abundance and growth. They conclude that the fish community was largely stabilized by 1954 and that carrying capacity was probably reached by 1952, five years after impoundment. The main changes were a shift from river species to a reservoir community dominated by sunfishes, crappie, gizzard shad and bullheads, with several predatory and river species failing to establish.

## Background & Description

- **Reservoir:** Clearwater Dam, completed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers in 1948, impounds Black River about 26 miles from its headwaters. The conservation pool covers 1,650 acres, with a total storage area of 10,350 acres. Most of the lake is shallower than 30 feet.
- **Water quality:** Methyl-orange alkalinity was 97–106 ppm, and turbidity was usually below 7 ppm. Thermal stratification occurred only in deep borrow pits near the dam. July 1952 surface temperature was 86.0°F, falling to 54.5°F at 48 feet.
- **River:** Upper Black River is a spring-fed, clear stream averaging under 5,000 second-feet. It is 291 miles long, 171 of them in Arkansas, where it joins the White River.
- **Methods:**
  - Gill, hoop, trap and trammel nets, with mesh and set details varying by year.
  - Periodic seining (1948–1952), then fixed-site seining (1950–1954) with a 25-foot, ¼-inch mesh bag seine.
  - Rotenone treatment of small coves (1.34–1.63 acres per year), with fish recovered for three days after each application.
  - An intensive [[Creel Census]] from 1949 onward.
  - Growth increments back-calculated from [[Growth Increment]] body-scale relationships.
- **Relative abundance:** The authors did not estimate total population size, but Petersen mark-recapture estimates were made for crappie.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pre-impoundment Black River community:** Minnows made up 77% of small-fish collections. Among them, [[Bleeding Shiner]], [[Bigeye Chub]], [[Whitetail Shiner]] and [[Bigeye Shiner]] were numerically important. [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Rock Bass]] were the dominant stream predators. Other predators included [[Largemouth Bass]], walleye, sauger, grass pickerel, longnose gar, both crappies and [[Flathead Catfish]].

**Habitat shift:** Most small fish in the reservoir were river backwater and pool species carried in during filling. [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[White Crappie]] and yellow bullhead were also river-backwater fish. Newly flooded terrain and a developing benthic fauna provided abundant food early on. [[Impoundment Effects]] of this kind drove the rapid early growth.

**Food competition:** The authors suggested that the sharp post-1948 decline in young-of-year crappie growth reflected competition with the large sunfish and crappie cohorts. Growth declined for most species after 1948, while smallmouth bass young-of-year growth remained good, possibly reflecting lower density in that species.

**Failures to establish:** Smallmouth bass, rock bass, grass pickerel, hog sucker, spotted sunfish, walleye, black buffalo, sauger and others did not become established. The authors attributed this to poor spawning or survival facilities, small brood stocks, and, for some species, inability to adjust to reservoir conditions.

## Key Findings

**Carrying capacity and stabilization**
- All species numerically important in 1954 had spawned successfully at least once by 1952. The authors therefore assumed carrying capacity was reached by 1952, five years after impoundment.
- By 1954 the population was fairly stable, although short-term fluctuations from variable recruitment were expected. Small-fish abundance was similarly established by 1950.

**Dominant species in 1954 (larger fishes):** [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Bluegill]], [[Longear Sunfish]], [[White Crappie]], bullheads and [[Golden Redhorse]].

**Crappie**
- [[Black Crappie]] was caught only until 1951; since then only [[White Crappie]] has been taken. This dominance matches other large Missouri reservoirs.
- Crappie became the most numerous angler catch by 1951.
- Petersen estimates of crappie 7 inches or longer were 132,000 on May 1, 1951, and 118,650 in 1954. The authors noted that catch increases did not necessarily mean population growth.
- Natural mortality among white crappie was high, since fewer than 10% of tagged fish were caught by anglers each year.
- Growth of young-of-year crappie dropped sharply after 1948 and stayed low through 1953.

**Smallmouth bass:** Recruitment was poor. About 2,500 were caught in 1949, but only about 200 per year after 1950. The 1948 year class supplied most of the catch from 1949 to 1952. Growth was excellent, especially in young-of-year and yearling fish.

**Largemouth bass**
- The 1948 year class, supplemented by 85,000 stocked fingerlings, provided most of the catch in the first three years. It still made up about 8% of the catch in 1954.
- Strong year classes were produced in 1950 and 1953, and the 1952 spawn largely failed. The authors linked poor survival to falling water levels during spawning.
- Catch per 100 hours of artificial-lure fishing fell from 19 (1949) to 17, 8, 10, 5 and 1 (1954). The authors blamed the failure of much of the large 1950 crop to reach catchable size.

**Bluegill**
- Bluegills spawned successfully every year and were the only other centrarchid to contribute consistently to the angler catch.
- Estimated annual hook-and-line catches (1949–1954): 11,025; 15,125; 24,350; 13,100; 18,500; 7,600.
- Pondweed beds that developed in 1949 are thought to have boosted survival.

**Other sunfishes**
- Sunfishes under 4 inches exceeded 7,000 per acre in every rotenone census, peaking at 12,714 per acre in 1954. Bluegills and longear sunfish dominated these counts.
- Rapid 1948 growth let longear and green sunfish reach catchable size in 1949 (more than 10,000 longear and green sunfish were caught that year), but later growth was slow.
- [[Rock Bass]] and spotted sunfish failed to spawn successfully. [[Warmouth]] had successful spawns in 1948, 1950 and 1953.

**Catfishes**
- Black bullheads quickly replaced yellow bullheads. In 1949, black bullheads outnumbered yellow by more than 12 to 1 in net catches.
- [[Channel Catfish]] did not spawn successfully until 1952, which the authors attributed to a small brood stock and late maturity. Channel catfish made up 6% of the 1954 net catch.
- Flathead catfish remained scarce.

**Carp:** More than 100 young-of-year carp were netted in August 1948, showing that carp spawned in the lake that year. The 1951 year class dominated later samples. A 1954 electrofishing sample of 40 hours yielded 70 carp averaging 5.9 pounds. The authors considered carp well established.

**Redhorse suckers:** Golden redhorse were the dominant redhorse in the lake. Gill-net catches per 24-hour set rose from 1.3 (1949) to 5.9 (1954). [[Black Redhorse]] catches fell from 1.9 (1949) to 0.0 (1954). Small black redhorse were abundant in 1952–1954 rotenone samples.

**Gizzard shad**
- Shad produced a large brood in 1950. The 1948 year class dominated gill-net catches from 1948 to 1952.
- Standing crop in rotenone coves (1950–1954) ranged from 46 to 158 pounds per acre, more than any other species.
- Shad grew faster than river shad as fingerlings, but slower in later age groups. Lake shad also lived longer, with six-year-old fish captured in 1954.

Fishing pressure was light, and the harvest was probably well below the available crop. The 1949 and 1950 figures cover only the May 30–November 30 bass season.

**Small fishes:** In July seine hauls (1952–1954), [[Brook Silverside]], [[Bluntnose Minnow]], [[Bigeye Shiner]] and [[Whitetail Shiner]] were the dominant species. Bigeye shiner numbers rose from 2.2 per haul in 1952 to 10.2 in 1954. The logperch was present in low numbers except in 1954 (3.6 per haul). The studfish declined steadily, and the common shiner slowly increased after 1950. The authors concluded that no major persistent changes occurred in small-fish populations after 1950.
