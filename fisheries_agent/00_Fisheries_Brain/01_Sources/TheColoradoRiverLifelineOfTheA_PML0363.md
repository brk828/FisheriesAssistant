# The Colorado River: Lifeline of the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This review by Carlson and Muth (1989), published in the *Proceedings of the International Large River Symposium*, treats the [[Colorado River]] as a single holistic ecosystem and summarizes its ecology and management. The river, once free-flowing and characterized by extreme flow and physicochemical variation, has been transformed by [[Dam Construction]] and diversion into a regulated system. The basin supplies more water for consumptive use than any other United States river despite being among the driest in the world, and roughly one third of its native fishes are threatened, endangered, or extinct. The authors note that the mainstream's productivity and fisheries have received little publication.

## Background & Description

The Colorado rises in Rocky Mountain National Park, Colorado, and flows about 2,320 km to the Gulf of California (the Green River adds further length, giving a total of 2,735 km from the Wind River Range origin of the Green). The basin covers 632,000 km² across the United States and Mexico, spans elevations from sea level to above 4,000 m, and is divided into [[Upper Basin]] and [[Lower Basin]] at [[Lee Ferry]] for water management.

Geologically the basin crosses the Rocky Mountains, Colorado Plateau, and Basin and Range provinces. Human occupation dates back more than 10,000 years, with later [[Hohokam Culture]] and other agricultural societies building irrigation systems. [[John Wesley Powell]] began scientific investigations in 1869.

The native fish fauna is highly distinctive because the drainage has had no major connections with neighbouring basins for millions of years, leading Molles (1980) to describe it as "an aquatic island in a terrestrial sea." Of the native species, 64% are endemic. Only eight species occurred in both upper and lower basins; the "big river" fishes (humpback chub, bonytail, Colorado squawfish, flannelmouth sucker, and razorback sucker) were largely restricted to large mainstream channels.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pre-1935 conditions.** Before [[Hoover Dam]] closed in 1935, the river ran essentially unchecked, with discharge ranging from 0 to 7,000 m³/s at Yuma, AZ, and sediment transport peaking above 25,000 mg/L in floods. Water temperatures ranged from 0–20 °C in headwaters to 5–30 °C in low-elevation desert streams, and TDS was above 250 mg/L in the lower mainstream. Riparian vegetation was dominated by willows and cottonwoods, and [[Tamarisk]] (salt cedar) had become established by 1935.

**Regulation effects.** Dams have reduced high spring flows, raised summer flows, lowered mainstream temperatures by 10–15 °C, and caused channel degradation and armoured bottoms in tailwaters. Evaporation from reservoirs and diversions removed about 12% and 3% of annual flow respectively. Salinity has risen, and by 1957 natural salt levels at Lee Ferry had doubled. Lake Powell construction reduced phosphorus delivery to [[Lake Mead]], contributing to a decline in its fishery. Below Glen Canyon Dam, the river may rise 1.8 m within minutes.

**Biota.** Downstream of dams, [[Cladophora]] forms dense mats that support introduced fishes but little native invertebrate diversity. Benthic productivity in Grand Canyon is low. Cottonwood-willow communities along the lower river have been reduced to about 1,130 ha, of which under 202 ha are pure cottonwood.

**Legal and management framework.** Key measures include the [[Colorado River Compact]], the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), the [[Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Act]] (1974), and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], which has pursued five phases of development.

## Key Findings

- The Colorado has been transformed into a regulated system; the first high dam (Hoover, 1935) ended its free-flowing state.
- Virgin flow at Lee Ferry averaged 20.72 km³ before the Compact and 17.52 km³ since 1922; the basin was overcommitted by the first apportionment.
- Some 117 reservoirs with individual capacities over 0.001 km³ exist in the upper basin, plus about 40 trans-mountain canals and tunnels.
- Less than 1% of the river's virgin flow now reaches its mouth.
- Native fishes: about one third are threatened, endangered, or extinct. The Table 2 checklist lists roughly 30 native taxa with federal status and historic distributions, including the Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, razorback sucker, and woundfin.
- Regulation has reduced backwater and marsh habitat, lessening oxygen depletions while introducing risk of gas supersaturation.
- Tamarisk has spread northward at roughly 10 km per year and displaced willows and cottonwoods.
- Future conflicts are expected among water uses, wilderness values, and economic equity; the authors judge conflicts between development and natural ecosystems "paramount."
- Recommended research priorities include population dynamics, homing mechanisms, native-non-native interactions, and habitat requirements at all life stages; monitoring of threatened fishes is also advised.
- Data on the mainstream's productivity and fisheries remain sparse, and research has been largely descriptive.
