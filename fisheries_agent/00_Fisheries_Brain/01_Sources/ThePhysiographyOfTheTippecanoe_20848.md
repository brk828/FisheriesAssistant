# The Physiography of the Tippecanoe River

#physiography #limnology #freshwater-geology #glacial-geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This paper reports the physiographic results of four summers of fieldwork on the [[Tippecanoe River]], a large northern tributary of the upper [[Wabash River]] in Indiana. The study was undertaken to explain the migration routes and present distribution of freshwater snails (gastropods), which are published separately (Wright, 1932). The stream's course, lake basins, swamps, and sand plains are shown to be controlled by late [[Wisconsin Glaciation]] moraines and outwash, and by bedrock where the river meets the [[Cincinnati Anticline]]. The document is a descriptive geomorphological survey with no fish, invertebrate, or water-chemistry data.

## Background & Description

- **Location:** Lies between about 41° and 42° N and 85° and 87° W. Partly or entirely drains 12 counties: Noble, Whitley, Kosciusko, Miami, Marshall, Fulton, Cass, Starke, Pulaski, White, Carroll, and Tippecanoe.
- **Size:** Drainage area of 1,890 square miles (Scott, 1916); total river length of about 166 miles.
- **Drainage type:** A consequent stream whose course follows a complex system of moraines assigned mainly to the [[Saginaw Lobe]] of the late Wisconsin substage.
- **Lakes:** The "ninety-five or more" lakes of the basin mostly occupy depressions in the [[Packerton Moraine]] system. Many swampy and marshy areas in the middle and upper course are remnants of former lakes, several extinct through [[Artificial Drainage]].
- **Zonation:** The author divides the river into three sections for description:
  - **Upper course:** From the headwaters at Crooked Lake to the Fulton–Marshall County line.
  - **Middle course:** From the Fulton–Marshall line to Norway in White County.
  - **Lower course:** From Norway to the mouth at the Wabash in northeastern Tippecanoe County.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Moraine and outwash controls.** The river and its lakes follow a sequence of moraines, including the [[Packerton Moraine]], [[Maxinkuckee Moraine]], [[Rochester Moraine]], [[Burket Moraine]], [[Bremen Moraine]], and [[New Paris Moraine]]. Lake basins are intramorainic depressions, and many adjacent flats are outwash deposits.

**Profile and bedrock.** The upper course has a low gradient. The steepest fall is near Monticello, where the river crosses the Cincinnati anticline at Norway and cuts into hard [[Niagara Limestone]]. Below Norway, rock exposures are frequent, and banks are high. Bluffs reach 67 feet at Monticello and 100 feet at Oakdale, which the author considers close to the maximum valley depth. Between Winamac and Monticello, the fall is "not less than 100 feet in thirty miles" (McBeth, 1909), and the bed is strewn with boulders.

**Sand plains and the Kankakee.** The middle and lower courses cross extensive sand plains, including the [[Mud Creek]] plain and the [[Sand Plain]] of Pulaski and White counties, some with dunes. Near Ora the river enters the [[Kankakee Marsh]] region, where glacial floodwaters may have spread drainage toward the Kankakee and Iroquois systems.

**Dams.** Two dams were built in the lower course:
- At Norway, with a head of 30 feet, impounding water upstream to Buffalo.
- At Oakdale, with a head of 40 feet, impounding the narrow valley to Monticello.

**Drainage history.** The lower course is considered younger than the middle course. Leverett's interpretation suggests earlier northwestward drainage toward the Kankakee and southwestward drainage toward the Iroquois.

## Key Findings

1. **Origin of the drainage pattern:** The Tippecanoe is a consequent stream shaped by late Wisconsin moraines. Lake basins sit in morainic depressions and outwash, and the river's path was set by the ice margins.
2. **Scale:** The basin covers 1,890 square miles across 12 counties, with about 166 miles of river and at least 95 lakes.
3. **Longitudinal profile:** The upper course has little fall. The steepest drop is around Monticello, and the lower course gains fall because the Wabash deepened its valley faster than the Tippecanoe could cut through the rock at Norway.
4. **Former lakes and drainage changes:** Many lakes (for example, Heriff, Goose, and Old and New lakes) are nearly extinct or have been drained or ditched. Dredged outlets and cutoffs such as the Moon Ditch have changed natural drainage.
5. **Sand and dune landscapes:** Extensive sand plains and dunes cover much of the middle and lower courses, mainly from glacial outwash.
6. **Valley depth:** Bluff heights of 67 to 100 feet and the Norway and Oakdale dams mark strong contrasts between the bedrock-controlled lower course and the flat upper course.
7. **Purpose and linkage:** The physiographic framework was built to explain the postglacial migration and present distribution of four snail species. Those biological results are reported separately in [[Aquatic Mollusca of the Tippecanoe River]] (Wright, 1932).
