# Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan (1977 Draft and Final Edition Review)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This recovery plan, prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] under sponsorship of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], addresses the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the largest North American minnow and the only native predator of its kind in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species was listed as endangered in 1967 and received full protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The plan's primary goal is to restore self-sustaining populations to non-endangered status, potentially through reclassification to threatened or delisting. It sets out a step-down outline of tasks in three main steps (habitat protection and studies, population recovery and reintroduction, and captive gene-pool maintenance), a narrative, a five-year budget, and an appendix of agency comment letters with the team's responses.

## Background & Description

The Colorado squawfish is the largest of three living species of *Ptychocheilus*, known from Pliocene fossils. It is pike-like, with a large near-horizontal mouth, a nine-rayed dorsal fin set well back, 80–95 lateral-line scales, and strong countershading. Maximum weight has been recorded above 80 lb (36 kg) and length near 6 ft (1.8 m), though specimens over 15 lb (7 kg) have been rare in the past decade. Its large size and upstream migrations earned it the name "white salmon of the Colorado."

Historically the species was abundant from the Green River, Wyoming, to Yuma, Arizona, including the [[Gila River Basin]]. It was valued as a food fish and sport fish, and a fishery operated on the lower Colorado until about 1910. Adults occupy eddies and pools near the main current; young occupy quieter water over silt or sand. Spawning is linked to runoff and water temperatures above 70°F (21°C); fry hatch in under four days and average 6.5 mm. Young feed on invertebrates and switch to fish at about 200 mm. Calculated mean total lengths from the Green River were 74 mm at age I, 400 mm at age VI, and 610 mm at age XI (Vanicek and Kramer 1969).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The plan describes the pre-dam Colorado River as turbid, silt-laden, saline and highly variable, with flows from about 3,000 to 380,000 cubic feet per second. Its native fauna, including the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]], evolved under these conditions. Dams, beginning with [[Hoover Dam]] in 1935 and followed by more than 20 others, converted much of the river into reservoirs and cold tailwater streams.

Stated causes of decline include:
- Impoundment: squawfish do not establish populations in reservoirs and few adults persist after impoundment.
- Introduced species: [[Red Shiner]], [[Redside Shiner]] and [[Green Sunfish]] occupy habitats once held by native fishes; channel catfish spines were reported lodged in dead squawfish.
- Altered flow, temperature, turbidity and salinity below dams, which reportedly stopped reproduction in the Green River for 105 km.
- Rotenone eradication programs in 1962 (Green River, with Flaming Gorge closure) and on the San Juan River (with Navajo Dam closure), which directly killed squawfish. The magnitude was not documented.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status**
- Present range is greatly reduced. Holden and Stalnaker classified the species as rare in much of the Upper Basin; the lower Yampa River held 261 of 300 adults (87%) taken in a four-year study.
- Squawfish are considered probably extinct in the Gila River basin and in Arizona's waters, and the 1958 Salt River record was attributed to misidentified roundtail chub.
- Recent collections occurred in the Duchesne, White and San Rafael rivers and Plateau Creek; significance was under evaluation.

**Recovery strategy (step-down plan)**
- Step 1: preserve existing populations and habitats; prevent adverse habitat modification; study habitat requirements (chemistry, physics, biology, historical conditions) over at least three years; run information and education programs; enforce protective laws; monitor habitat.
- Step 2: prevent decline from direct take; monitor populations annually in designated trend zones across Colorado, Utah and Arizona; improve habitat through management plans.
- Step 3: expand life-history knowledge; reintroduce squawfish to historic range using hatchery (Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery, propagation since 1973 from eight adults) and wild stock; establish a captive reserve gene pool.
- Step 4: review progress and recommend delisting or reclassification when the primary goal is met.

**Budget and priorities**
- The five-year budget is a guide for agency funding responsibilities and is shared among federal and state agencies. Priority 1 items include habitat requirement studies (USFWS, Bureau of Reclamation, BLM, USFS and others at $25,000 each per year for the USFWS and related agencies), enforcement (USFWS $50,000 per year) and propagation.
- Habitat management plan preparation was scheduled for 1979–1980 and implementation from 1980, but agency comments warned that budgets are contingent on congressional appropriations.

**Agency comments and revisions**
- Multiple agencies (BLM California, Nevada, Colorado, Utah, New Mexico and Arizona; Bureau of Reclamation; National Park Service; Bureau of Indian Affairs; Forest Service; Fish and Wildlife Service Director) reviewed the 1977 draft.
- Key revisions adopted: Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act applies only to federal agencies; the critical habitat appendix was removed; budget years were changed to "Year 1, Year 2"; the White River was added as a monitoring zone; and the reintroduction and restoration tasks were both made priority 3.
- The team noted that comments such as the requirement for more habitat data were addressed as "a firm data base is needed," and that the Bureau of Reclamation's funding request was reduced to habitat studies only.
- Arizona BLM and Nevada BLM reported no squawfish on public lands in their states, limiting their involvement.
