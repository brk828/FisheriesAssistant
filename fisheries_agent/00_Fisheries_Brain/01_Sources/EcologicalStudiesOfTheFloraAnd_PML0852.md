# Aquatic Survey of Green River and Tributaries within the Flaming Gorge Reservoir Basin (1959)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1959 aquatic survey, published as part of the University of Utah's *Anthropological Papers* (Upper Colorado Series No. 3), of the fishes, aquatic habitats and water chemistry of the [[Green River]] and its tributaries before the planned [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] inundated the canyon. It was a by-product of a cooperative agreement between the University of Utah and the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]], and it was carried out with the [[Utah State Department Of Fish And Game]]. The survey was one part of a larger flora and fauna study. It combined physical and chemical measurements, [[Rotenone Sampling]], [[Gill Net]] and [[Seine Netting]] collections, and fish condition, parasite and diet analyses. The study recorded native and introduced fishes in the [[Colorado River Basin]], including several imperiled native species, and documented hybridization among trout and suckers.

## Background & Description

The 1959 expedition was led by [[Angus M. Woodbury]], with a team of 16 scientists and two boatmen. The field work was carried out by two downstream expeditions, from the upper end of the anticipated reservoir site, and it was recorded in river miles. The survey covered 47 locations, of which 36 were on the main river and 11 were in tributaries or side canyons. Physical data recorded at each site included habitat type, current, bottom type, width, depth, velocity, turbidity, and air and water temperature. Chemical data included pH, dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbonates and bicarbonates, and alkalinity. Specimens were preserved, with insects kept in alcohol and fishes fixed in formalin.

The fish checklist includes trout, whitefish, sculpins, suckers, minnows and catfishes. [[Brown Trout]] (introduced), [[Colorado Cutthroat Trout]] (native), [[Rainbow Trout]] (introduced) and [[Mountain Whitefish]] represent the salmonids. The native chubs and suckers of greatest conservation interest are [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. Introduced species such as [[Common Carp]] and [[Channel Catfish]] were also recorded. The full checklist and annotated list are in the appendices and are summarized here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions.** Main-river water temperatures were in the 60s to upper 70s °F during the July and August sampling. Turbidity was highest after flood events, which the authors attributed to runoff, and it was highly variable over 24-hour runs. Dissolved oxygen varied with the diel cycle, and the lowest values were recorded at night and in the early morning. Bicarbonate and carbonate levels were measured in the main river and tributaries, and the study notes that some tributaries were strongly influenced by [[Sheep Creek]] and [[Carter Creek]] inflow. Sheep Creek was heavily silted and reddish brown, and it was not occupied by trout.

**Habitats.** The main channel in the canyon was deep, with rubble, silt and sand bottoms, while the littoral zone was dominated by [[Redside Shiner]] and sucker populations, with seining and wading as the usual collection methods. Island channels were shallow and sometimes sheltered from the main current. Tributaries above the reservoir were small, with clear, cold water in the canyon streams, and many lower tributary reaches were muddy or silted.

**Trout streams.** Trout occupied four clear, cold, permanent tributaries within the canyons. In these streams, temperatures were 60 °F or less, dissolved oxygen ranged from 7.2 to 8.9 ppm, and pH was 8.0 to 8.6. Brown trout were limited to the lower ends of the cooler, more permanent creeks and were more tolerant of silt than other trout.

**Diseases and parasites.** External parasites included leeches (Hirudinea), which were common on suckers, chubs, trout and whitefish. The internal parasite *Ligula intestinalis* was found protruding from the anus of a bluehead sucker. Open, red, ulcer-like lesions attributed to a [[Bacterial Ulcer]] were found on suckers, mainly bluehead and flannelmouth suckers, and were not detected on other species.

**Diet.** Stomach analysis of bonytail (17 stomachs) showed that the species feeds on almost any available food. Insects were the main animal food, with ants the most numerous terrestrial insect. A large quantity of plant matter, mostly woody fragments, was also consumed, and the authors noted that its nutritional value was highly questionable.

## Key Findings

- **Native fish conservation concern.** The study documents native species in decline. Adult [[Colorado Squawfish]] were reported as once abundant in the upper Green River, but the species was apparently uncommon by 1959. [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]] were present, while [[Razorback Sucker]] was apparently becoming increasingly rare in the upper Colorado system.
- **Hybridization among trout.** The native Colorado cutthroat population showed varying degrees of hybridization with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]]. Pure-looking specimens matching the native description were found only in some isolated tributaries. Whether Yellowstone cutthroat influence was present could not be determined.
- **Hybridization among suckers.** Six specimens of hybrid razorback × flannelmouth suckers were collected in the Green River below Hideout Forest Camp. A further hybrid specimen was later collected near the Utah–Colorado line.
- **Distribution by habitat.** [[Bluehead Sucker]] was common in the Green River and slower tributaries but absent from colder, faster streams that supported trout. It was found in 23 of 46 collections, with larger fish in deeper main-river sections and smaller fish in tributaries and island channels.
- **Trout populations.** Trout were recorded in four cold tributaries, and 46 trout were collected by angling at Carter Creek. A single brown trout of about 17 inches (429 mm) reported from Sheep Creek was the largest trout recorded by the survey.
- **Colorado mottled sculpin.** [[Colorado Mottled Sculpin]] was among the most widely distributed fishes in the study. It occurred in the main river and in most tributaries, and it was the species with the broadest ecological tolerance of those collected.
- **Condition.** Fulton-type condition factors (K) were computed for trout, whitefish, suckers and chubs from the Green River and Carter Creek, documented in the appendices.
- **Study limitations.** The authors noted that the expedition was an initial reconnaissance and that the reservoir would alter the river considerably. The survey provides a baseline for the pre-impoundment fauna of the Green River and its tributaries.
