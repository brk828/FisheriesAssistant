# Fishes of the Upper Colorado River System: Symposium Proceedings

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is the front matter and proceedings of a 1981 American Fisheries Society symposium, co-sponsored by the Western Division of the AFS, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) Region VI, and the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation Upper Colorado Region. It compiles papers on the rare native fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin, focusing on research after 1975 on intermediate and large-river habitat zones. Its four main objectives were to present basic fish information, identify recovery needs, emphasize life history and status of rare fishes, and assess past and future human impacts. The collection covers the basin's fish fauna, water and energy development, endangered-species law (ESA Section 7), water allocation, recovery planning, and life histories of the Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub and razorback sucker. The overall outlook is pessimistic: the four large-river endemics are declining, and the bonytail chub is considered near extinction.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River rises in the Rocky Mountains and drains about 283,600 km² of the Upper Basin (western Colorado, southwestern Wyoming, eastern Utah, northwestern New Mexico, and northeastern Arizona). The Upper Basin is divided at [[Lee Ferry]] into the Green, Upper Main-stem Colorado and San Juan-Colorado sub-basins. The basin's long isolation since the Pliocene produced high endemism. [[Miller]] (1959) ranked the Colorado system second of seven North American endemism centers, with 87% endemism among primary freshwater fishes. [[Hubbard]] (1980) reported 30 native freshwater species, with 73% of species endemic.

Fish checklists vary between sources: 13 native species (Joseph et al. 1977; Behnke and Benson 1980), 20 native species plus 4 subspecies (Wydoski 1980), and 55 fishes in total (Tyus et al.). The most authoritative treatment in the proceedings is the [[Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin]] distribution survey.

The four large-river endemics are the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The Colorado squawfish and humpback chub were listed as threatened in 1967 (the bonytail chub was listed as endangered in 1980; the razorback sucker was not listed).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Joseph et al. (1977) recognized three zones: a cold, clear upper (headwater) zone; an intermediate zone with warmer, turbid water and cyprinid and catostomid dominance; and a warm, turbid lower (large-river) zone where primary production is nearly absent and energy comes largely from allochthonous inputs.

Key stressors identified in the proceedings:

- **Flow regulation:** [[Colorado River Storage Project]] dams trap about 75–80% of the sediment that formerly reached [[Lake Mead]]. Flaming Gorge releases produce cold tailwaters, and Fontenelle and Flaming Gorge reservoirs have ended native spawning in their tailwater reaches.
- **Water depletion:** Predevelopment flows were about 12.2 million acre-feet (MAF) annually. By 1980, about 3.963 MAF had been depleted, and the 226 identified projects could deplete an additional 2.9 MAF. Virgin flow at Lee Ferry averaged about 18.3 billion m³ (1896–1980).
- **Introduced species:** Stocked sport fishes and forage species compete with and prey on natives. Fifteen exotic fishes were probably introduced through pond and reservoir stocking, and the proceedings report 42 exotic species in total across the basin.
- **Salinity and water quality:** Salinity rises downstream, and the U.S. agreed in 1974 to a salinity standard for water delivered to Mexico.
- **Energy and mineral development:** Coal, [[Oil Shale]], [[Tar Sands]], gold dredging and new roads are destroying riparian and stream-bottom habitat.

## Key Findings

**Fish fauna and status (Tyus et al.)**
- A total of 55 fishes (52 species and 3 subspecies) occur in the Upper Basin. Of these, 49 are primarily riverine, while 42 occur in reservoirs, 7 of them primarily restricted to reservoirs.
- Status breakdown: 42 exotic, 13 native, 8 endemic, 1 threatened and 5 endangered fishes. A further 10 hybrid combinations were reported, without validation.
- Remnant populations of the four endangered and threatened large-river fishes persist in mainstem rivers. Two other taxa occur in headwaters: the endangered Kendall Warm Springs dace and the threatened Colorado River cutthroat trout.
- The authors conclude that endangered populations continue to decline, with no sign of increase. They recommend federal protection for the razorback sucker as threatened or possibly endangered.

**Water development and law (Shields; Harris et al.; Lambertson)**
- The 1978 ESA amendments require "reasonable and prudent alternatives" when a jeopardy opinion is issued. The ESA prohibits jeopardy; traditional [[Mitigation]] is not recognized under Section 7.
- The Conservation Plan proposes that project sponsors fund fish recovery in proportion to water depleted. Assessments set so far include $550,000 for Windy Gap, $500,000 for Moon Lake and up to $180,000 for Cheyenne Phase I.
- The [[Colorado River Compact]] (1922) guarantees 7.5 MAF per year to the Lower Basin, and the Upper Basin states are apportioned consumptive-use shares. The Colorado River Storage Project Act (1956) created the Upper Basin storage units, including [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], which gives roughly 26 MAF of storage space.
- Natural flow at Lee Ferry averaged about 15 MAF annually, with about 25% now used. Projected average annual flow at Lee Ferry falls from 10.0 MAF (present) to 9.1 MAF (1990), 8.6 MAF (2000) and 8.2 MAF (2030).
- Projected depletions reach 4.878 MAF by 1990 and 5.362 MAF by 2000 (Bureau of Reclamation estimates).

**Mitigation and habitat modeling (Prewitt and Stalnaker)**
- The authors argue that fish-habitat losses from flow depletion are partly retrievable by timing storage releases, so the focus should shift from annual volume to flow regime.
- They propose a sequence of tools: [[PHABSIM]] habitat simulation, temperature and water-quality models, sediment transport models, and water-budget models.

**Recovery planning (Miller; Behnke)**
- Three recovery plans (Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub) share goals of protecting existing stocks and habitats, maintaining captive gene pools, and restoring former range. The humpback chub plan targets five self-sustaining populations by 1990.
- Behnke proposes an independent agency funded by a small tax on environmentally disruptive benefits, such as hydropower generation.

**Life history of the Colorado squawfish (Holden and Wick)**
- The Colorado squawfish is North America's largest native cyprinid, historically reaching 1.5–2.0 m and 35–45 kg. Most remaining reproduction occurs in the Green River system.
- Adults mature in 5–7 years. Spawning occurs at about 20 °C, usually in July and August. Reproduction failed in the Green River below Flaming Gorge from 1966 onward, probably because cold dam releases prevented egg maturation. Inlet modification of Flaming Gorge Dam (1980) restored young-of-the-year production.
- Dams are identified as the major cause of decline. Reduced spring flows on the Gunnison and Colorado rivers coincided with near-disappearance of young-of-the-year.

**Bonytail chub and razorback sucker (Valdez et al.; Wick et al.)**
- The bonytail chub is now the rarest endemic fish in the Upper Basin, with only occasional collections. The authors state that its decline "foretells extinction in the near future."
- The razorback sucker has few young recorded, with reproductive failure, predation by exotics and competition with exotics proposed as causes.

**Outlook (Concluding Remarks)**
- Panelists and symposium authors expressed little optimism for the squawfish, razorback sucker, humpback chub and especially the bonytail chub. Biological prospects for recovery in good habitat are described as bright, but the availability of adequate future flows is seen as the major obstacle.
