# Guidance for Evaluating and Recommending Temperature Regimes to Protect Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service guidance paper by Carl L. Armour (December 1991, Instream Flow Information Paper 25) provides procedures for judging whether an altered stream temperature regime is safe for fish. Although its worked examples concern [[Spring Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*), the author states that the principles apply to all fish species. The paper presents basic [[Temperature Tolerance Relationships]] and three options for comparing alternative temperature regimes: experimental tolerance results, suitability of a simulated regime for key life stages, and population statistics with predicted responses.

## Background & Description

Water temperature is one of the most important environmental factors affecting fish, influencing migration, egg maturation, spawning, incubation success, growth, competitive ability, and resistance to parasites, disease, and pollutants. Several factors act simultaneously, and some act synergistically, which can mask the effect of any single factor.

Fish are grouped into coldwater, coolwater, and warmwater categories (Table 1). Generalized spawning thresholds from [[Pipe Hatchery Management]] (Piper et al. 1982) are below 12.8°C for coldwater species, 4.4–15.6°C for coolwater species, and above 15.6°C for warmwater species. The author stresses that such generalities are too imprecise, so each species needs its own evaluation.

Recommendations apply to streams affected by channel modification, diversions, reservoir releases, or land-use changes such as vegetation removal.

**Key temperature definitions:**
- **Acclimation temperature:** exposure temperature held for several days before a tolerance test.
- **Upper Incipient Lethal Temperature (UILT):** temperature at which 50% mortality occurs for a given acclimation temperature.
- **Lower Incipient Lethal Temperature (LILT):** the lower equivalent.
- **Upper Ultimate Incipient Lethal Temperature (UUILT):** the highest temperature at which tolerance stops increasing with acclimation temperature.
- **Temperature of Instantaneous Death (TID)** and **Line of Equality (LE)**, the 45° line representing equal acclimation and response temperatures.
- **Acute Thermal Preferendum (ATP)** and **Final Preferendum (FP)**: initial and eventual temperature choices.
- **Growth Optimum (GO):** temperature at which growth is maximal.
- **Zero Net Growth (ZNG):** temperature at which instantaneous growth and mortality are equal; described as the most sensitive performance indicator.
- **Physiological Optimum (PO):** temperature approximating optimal growth, stamina, and heart performance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Temperature tolerance is shaped by fish size, season, day length, sex, water chemistry, disease, genetics, and life-cycle stage. Juvenile and adult requirements often differ considerably, which can lead different age groups to use different habitat types.

Key habitat-related examples in the document:
- Juvenile [[Chinook Salmon]] growth was recommended within a range reported by [[Wilson Et Al 1987]] (midpoint 10.8°C).
- Spawning run adults become susceptible to lethal disease at 16.0°C (Snyder and Blahm 1968).
- Juvenile fish cannot tolerate temperatures above 25.1°C for extended periods.
- Growth stress (growth rates 20% below optimum) occurs near 18.5°C (Brett et al. 1982).
- Low temperatures also matter: reaches below cold-water dam releases can see species replaced and others impaired.

Stream temperatures generally rise from headwaters to lower reaches (Hynes 1972), so discrete zones can support distinct fish communities.

## Key Findings

**Option 1: Experimental Temperature Tolerance Results**
- **[[Maximum Weekly Average Temperature]] (MWAT):** calculated as MWAT = (OT + UUILT)/2, where OT is an optimal temperature for the life stage. Using OT = 10.8°C and UUILT = 25.1°C gives a conservative MWAT of 15.6°C for juvenile spring chinook rearing. Using Brett's (1982) optimum of 14.8°C gives MWAT = 19.2°C, which exceeds the 16°C upper limit Reiser and Bjornn (1979) reported for Alaska fish.
- Example: simulated mean weekly maximum temperatures under alternative flows showed MWAT exceeded at 950 cfs (Table 3).
- **Short-term Maximum (STM):** log(time) = a − b·T, with a 2°C safety margin for 100% survival. For a 15°C acclimation, the STM for 100% survival is 19.8°C (Table 4 values; 17.8°C with margin, per the source table). Exposure lethality is assessed with equation (7); a 27°C exposure for 360 min gave a ratio of 46.3983, so it would probably be lethal.
- Survival time at 27°C for 50% mortality (15°C acclimation) was 91.7 min. With the 2°C margin, the estimate fell to 7.9 min.
- Under alternative flows, the STM criterion was exceeded at 23.6°C (Table 5).

**Option 2: Suitability of a Simulated Regime for Key Life Stages**
- Recommended and tolerance ranges were tabulated for adult migration, spawning, incubation, and juvenile rearing (Table 6).
- **Spawning migration blockage:** future run size is FRS = (RS)(BSR)(S)(NF)(F)(ES)(SRA). With 250 adults, 20% mortality, 80% spawning success, a 50:50 sex ratio, mean fecundity of 3,900 eggs, 75% egg-to-fry survival, 15% fry-to-smolt survival, and 17% smolt-to-adult return, the estimate is 312 adults (rounded from the reported result).
- **Emergence timing:** 850 Daily Temperature Units (DTUs) are needed from fertilization to hatching, plus 700 more to emergence (1,550 total). Late-April peak runoff could flush fry under regime B.
- **Juvenile growth:** estimated with Monthly Thermal Units (MTUs). Regime A produced fish about 14% longer than regime B by the end of April (6.74 cm vs. 5.42 cm). Biomass was about 25 kg for regime A and 13 kg for regime B.

**Option 3: Population Statistics and Predicted Responses**
- Temperature envelopes are built from historical data for a specific stream, such as the Upper Salmon River (Figure 6).
- Population success measures include adult size, growth rate, yield, population number, and net production (Ricker 1968). Special status (rarity, endangerment) is treated as a separate category.
- Example: new flows raised temperatures above reported tolerances for adult migration, spawning, incubation, and rearing from late June to late August, which could support negotiating flows.

**Concluding Guidance**
- Use expert consultation (universities, state agencies, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Cooperative Fishery Research Units, National Marine Fisheries Service) when information is scarce.
- Consider low temperatures as well as high ones; a predicted cooling is not automatic justification that harm will not occur.
- Calculated values are not absolute. Some hatcheries exceed the 15.6°C MWAT moderately while fish populations remain successful.
- Mean temperatures alone can hide risk; one week with a 24.0°C daily peak gave a lethality ratio of 1.3 under equation (7).
- Each stream reach may matter differently for different life stages.

**Comparison of the three options (Table 11):**
- Option 1 focuses on experimental tolerance results from literature.
- Option 2 focuses on compliance of simulated regimes with life-stage tolerances.
- Option 3 is suited to evaluating deviations from known population status and is the most data-intensive.
