# Razorback Sucker Five-Year Status Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This five-year status report on the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) recommends retaining its Endangered classification. The species has declined from abundance to extreme rarity in the [[Colorado River Basin]] during the past 100 years. Adults survive only in small, isolated areas, and juvenile records are scarce. The report attributes the decline to dam construction, water diversions, channelization, and the introduction of exotic species, particularly predatory [[Green Sunfish]]. Hybridization with catostomids such as the [[Longnose Sucker]] and [[White Sucker]] is an additional concern. A [[Management Plan For The Razorback Sucker]] in California covers 1986 to 1995, and the species was reintroduced into the lower Colorado River in 1986.

## Background & Description

The report was prepared by Susan R. Ellis, Fisheries Biologist, California Department of Fish and Game, Inland Fisheries Division (Endangered Species Project), dated March 10, 1987. Review copies were sent to regional and federal biologists, including staff of the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]].

The species is known as the razorback sucker and, because of its dorsal structure, as the humpback sucker. Key morphological features include:

- **Body form:** Elongated head and laterally compressed body with a relatively flattened belly. A sharp-edged nuchal hump or keel develops with age and size, beginning at about 2+ years (about 6 in standard length).
- **Size:** One of the largest suckers, reaching 10 to 15 lbs.
- **Counts:** 68 to 87 lateral line scales (sometimes obscured by the thickened epidermis), 13 to 16 dorsal fin rays, 7 anal fin rays, and 36 to 50 slender gill rakers that appear "fuzzy" due to fine filaments.
- **Coloration:** Adults are dusky brown to olivaceous on the back, yellowish on the belly, with a dark lateral stripe. Spawning males turn black on the back and sides, with an orange lateral stripe, a yellowish belly and pelvic fins, and large conical breeding tubercles on the head, fins, and peduncle. Female coloration stays drab, though tubercles sometimes appear on the anal fin and lower caudal lobe. Spawning females have a broader, fleshier urogenital region.
- **Larvae:** Difficult to distinguish from other [[Catostomidae]] larvae. Useful identifying characters are lip structure (deep median cleft), dorsal fin ray count, and melanophore distribution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic distribution:** Historic collections came from the mainstream Colorado River and major tributaries, from Wyoming to Sonora and Baja California del Norte, Mexico. The range extended into the [[Salton Sea]] after it filled in 1905–1906, but the species disappeared as salinity increased. The species was widespread in the upper basin but was never considered abundant there.

**Current upper basin (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming):** The species is considered extremely rare in the Green River (Utah–Colorado) system. Records are concentrated at only two sites, with 10 to 25 individuals each: the mouth of the [[Yampa River]] and a flooded gravel pit (Walker Wildlife Area) near Grand Junction, Colorado. Only one specimen has recently been reported from the Gunnison River. In Utah, an irrigation reservoir connected to the San Juan River was emptied, stranding 250 razorback suckers. The species has not been taken in Wyoming since the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962.

**Current lower basin (Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico, California):** Records are sporadic and isolated. Notable records include:

- Large individuals collected in 1973 by the Arizona Game and Fish Department from [[Imperial Reservoir]].
- Occasional California captures from Davis Dam south to Imperial Dam in the mid- and late 1970s. However, a 1981 netting survey from Parker Dam to the U.S.–Mexico border found none.
- Young captured in 1973 and 1974 from ponds filled from the East Highline Canal near Niland, California.
- Seven young collected in 1984 from lateral reservoirs near the [[Coachella Canal]].

**Remnant populations:** Populations still occur in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Mead]]. The Lake Mohave population is described as the largest known population in nature. A small remnant population also persists in [[Senator Wash Reservoir]], California.

**Threats:** Decline has been attributed to physical, chemical, and biological changes. More than 20 major dams have been built on the Colorado River and its tributaries, and each has significantly altered the riverine environment both upstream and downstream. The altered river offers few warm, shallow areas needed for spawning, nursery, resting, and feeding. Channelization and dredging have also contributed to the decline.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** Retain Endangered classification. Adults are now restricted to small, isolated areas, and juvenile records are rare.
- **Exotic predation:** Predation by exotic fishes on eggs, larvae, and fingerlings has been documented and may be a significant factor depressing lower basin populations. In Senator Wash Reservoir and Lake Mohave, larvae have been found, but no recruitment occurs into the adult population. Green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*) prey heavily on recruitment. The report warns that if this continues, mortality of senescent adults will likely lead to extinction of the Mohave and Senator Wash populations.
- **Hybridization:** Hybridization with other catostomids has increased over the same period as the decline, which the report considers detrimental. Hybridization is a risk because the razorback sucker can hybridize with the non-native Longnose Sucker (*Catostomus catostomus*) and White Sucker (*C. commersoni*), which are now scattered throughout much of the basin.
- **Historic abundance:** The species was once one of the most abundant large fishes and an important food of various Indian tribes. It was commercially harvested for food and fertilizer in southern Arizona and was collected by the wagonload from canals and the Salt River near Phoenix. Concentrations declined precipitously after the early 1950s.
- **Long-term decline:** Fishery surveys since the early 1970s have confirmed continued decline in the lower basin (Minckley 1973, 1981). Adults are still observed in Lake Mead, but the population is believed to be low, with no documented recruitment. The species was becoming scarce in the upper basin by the 1950s and was classified as rare in the Green River downstream to Dinosaur National Monument.
- **Reintroduction:** Razorback suckers were reintroduced into the lower Colorado River in 1986. The success of this program and its effect on population size cannot be determined at this time.
- **Habitat preferences:** In the upper basin, the species prefers stagnant backwaters or slow-gradient, sandy-bottomed areas of the mainstream. It is associated with warm tributaries (summer mean of 72 °F) that enter the colder main river (35–55 °F), such as the mouth of the Yampa River. In the lower basin, shallow pools and backwaters are of primary importance to juveniles. Adults have adapted to the colder waters of reservoirs such as Lake Mead, Lake Mohave, and Senator Wash Reservoir. Razorback suckers formerly used irrigation canals near Phoenix, where they often became trapped.
- **Management strategy:** Survival appears to depend on river reaches that remain relatively unmodified. Restoring the species to its original range and abundance is impractical. The report recommends protecting existing populations and reintroducing the species to unmodified areas of its historic range.
- **California plan:** The Department of Fish and Game's management plan (Ulmer 1985) aims to "improve" the species' status in California by securing and perpetuating existing populations and establishing additional self-sustaining populations within its historic range in the state. It covers the 10-year period 1986 through 1995, during which the species is to be stocked into the lower Colorado River. Fish are being propagated at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] to meet stocking requirements. The plan will be reviewed and modified as necessary in 1991.
