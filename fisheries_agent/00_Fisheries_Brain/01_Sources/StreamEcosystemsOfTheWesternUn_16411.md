# Stream Ecosystems of the Western United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by Stuart G. Fisher (Arizona State University) reviews stream ecosystems of the western United States, a region of strong physiographic and climatic contrast. It evaluates how well the [[River Continuum Concept]] (RCC) describes western streams, and compares four regional stream types: Pacific Northwest coniferous forest streams, Rocky Mountain streams, cool desert streams of eastern Washington and Idaho, and hot desert streams of Arizona and southeastern California. The chapter argues that riparian vegetation is a central component of stream ecosystems, that autotrophy and in-situ production are important in arid systems, and that flash flooding, drought and anthropogenic alteration (dams, grazing, logging, water diversion) strongly shape stream structure and function.

## Background & Description

The region is physically diverse, containing both the highest (Mt. Whitney, 4418 m) and lowest (Death Valley, 86 m below sea level) elevations of the conterminous USA. Most of the region is arid, with the exception of the wet Pacific Northwest. Annual precipitation ranges from 396 cm at Wynoochee, Washington, to 7 cm at Yuma, Arizona. Soils are often high in phosphorus-bearing minerals, so biological processes are commonly nitrogen-limited, in contrast to the northeastern US and northern Europe.

The chapter emphasizes smaller streams over large rivers, since only the Columbia among western rivers ranks among the world's 20 largest, and much of its watershed lies in Canada. Two of the four original RCC study systems (H.J. Andrews Forest in Oregon and the [[Salmon River]] in Idaho) are in the West. The author notes that geopolitical grouping is intellectually limiting, since western streams resemble streams on other continents (e.g., Arizona streams resemble those of northern Mexico, Australia and southern Spain).

Key study sites named in the text include [[Sycamore Creek]] (Arizona), [[Deep Creek]] (Idaho), [[Rattlesnake Springs]] and [[Snively Springs]] (eastern Washington), [[H.J. Andrews Forest]] (Oregon), the Colorado River and Tecopa Bore (California thermal stream).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian influence.** Litter entering streams is supplemented by material sliding or blowing from slopes; at Snively Springs nearly twice as much litter entered by downslope movement as by direct fall. Riparian vegetation is distinct from upland vegetation, especially in arid regions, and hosts roughly two to three times as many plant species as upland areas (Gregory et al. 1991). Litter input ranges from about 300 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ in Oregon coniferous forest streams to less than 6 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ at Deep Creek.

**Light and temperature.** A dense coniferous canopy in Oregon may reduce insolation to 5% of open-area levels, versus 15% under deciduous canopies. Clearcutting increases temperature and photosynthesis, with effects lasting ten years or more. Photosynthesis saturates at about 20% of full sunlight.

**Geomorphology and disturbance.** Riparian vegetation affects channel morphology through coarse woody debris dams. Snag removal from the Willamette, Gila and Colorado rivers altered braiding and sediment routing. Arroyo cutting in the late 1800s, driven by overgrazing and climate change, drained many cienegas. Flash floods can mobilize essentially all benthic organic matter in arid streams, and recovery of in-stream processes is rapid, while riparian vegetation lags far behind.

**Anthropogenic pressures.** The chapter identifies loss of riparian habitat, flow regulation, cattle grazing and timber harvest as dominant pressures. Nationally, nearly two-thirds of riparian vegetation has been lost in 100 years; in the West the loss is nearer 80%, and about 98% in the Sacramento drainage (largely to orchards; Swift 1984). Riparian areas make up 1–2% of grazing allotments yet supply nearly 80% of forage consumed by cattle (Gillis 1991). Tamarisk (salt cedar) has invaded most Southwestern streams over the past 100 years.

**Terrestrial links.** Over 60% of Southwestern vertebrate species are riparian obligates (Olendorf 1989). In southwestern deserts, 77% of 166 breeding bird species are partially and 51% totally dependent on riparian communities (Johnson et al. 1977). Aquatic insect production in desert streams supports flycatchers and bats.

## Key Findings

- **RCC applicability:** Western streams broadly fit RCC predictions in forested, low-order, mesic reaches (Pacific Northwest), but high-order reaches of arid regions (e.g., the Salmon River, Colorado River) depart from them, with in-situ primary production and collector-gatherer dominance replacing allochthonous-based patterns.
- **Riparian zone as integral component:** The riparian zone is treated as a central part of the running-water ecosystem, not merely an external energy source.
- **Primary production:** Oregon forest streams (Bott et al. 1985, H.J. Andrews) showed the lowest rates, never above about 0.5 g C m⁻² d⁻¹. Salmon River sites averaged under 0.5 g C m⁻² d⁻¹, with a maximum of 1.3 g C m⁻² d⁻¹. Rattlesnake Springs produced up to 7.4 g C m⁻² d⁻¹ with respiration averaging 6.2 g C m⁻² d⁻¹ (Cushing and Wolf 1984), the highest recorded for a western stream. Deep Creek, Idaho, had a P/R ratio above one, supporting Minshall's (1978) argument for caution in extrapolating mesic-stream paradigms.
- **Limiting factors:** Light is a major limiting factor. In many western streams nitrogen is the critical nutrient, while phosphorus limitation dominates in the East. Sycamore Creek showed nitrogen limitation (Grimm and Fisher 1986), and N:P ratios from 92 Southwestern streams indicated nitrogen is commonly limiting (Grimm et al. 1981).
- **Disturbance control:** In Sycamore Creek, flood history explained more day-to-day variation in chlorophyll a than nitrate, suggesting disturbance sets standing crop while nitrate governs recovery rate (Fisher and Grimm 1988).
- **Secondary production:** Sycamore Creek annual invertebrate production was about 120–121 g DW m⁻² yr⁻¹, among the highest reported for streams. Cool desert streams of eastern Washington produced 14.1–23 g m⁻² yr⁻¹, with collectors contributing 84–94% of production. Regulated Colorado River mayfly production exceeded 30 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ versus 10.3 g at the unregulated site. Corbicula fluminea in an Arizona canal exceeded 25 g m⁻² yr⁻¹, the highest reported for a single mollusc species.
- **Functional groups:** Collector-gatherers dominate many arid streams. Autochthonous detritus and algae are the main foods except immediately after floods. Gathering-collectors at Sycamore Creek ingested organic matter equal to total benthic organic matter about every 3 days under certain summer conditions, implying fecal reingestion and rapid microbial conditioning.
- **Organic matter:** In Oregon forest streams, about 90% of detritus in low-order reaches is wood. Organic matter turnover time in the Salmon River is 0.7–1.5 years, compared with 14 years in Oregon headwater streams. Arid-stream organic matter is largely in situ and fine-particulate, and transport is concentrated in brief flash-flood events.
- **Hyporheic zone:** A community of at least 56 invertebrate species was found in the hyporheos of Arizona hot desert streams but not in surface samples (Boulton et al. 1992), and hyporheic nitrogen processing is substantial. The author argues that stream ecosystem studies must be resolved in three spatial dimensions.
- **Research gaps:** Data are scarce for riparian nutrient retention in western streams, for California streams (no primary production data except Tecopa Bore), and for large western rivers, which are mostly dammed.
- **Conservation urgency:** Riparian vegetation is rapidly dwindling, and the chapter calls for scientific understanding and rational management strategies for riparian zones.
- **Overall conclusion:** No single controlling factor (biotic or abiotic, disturbance or nutrient) explains western stream productivity. The challenge is to characterize how these factors operate across scales of time and space.
