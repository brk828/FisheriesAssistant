# Effectiveness of Fish Barriers and Renovations for Native Southwestern Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae #Poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey report, prepared for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Arizona Ecological Services) under the Central Arizona Project Fund Transfer program (September 27, 2005), reviews [[Fish Barrier]] and [[Chemical Renovation]] projects used to protect native fishes from non-native invaders in the southwestern United States. The authors compiled data on 75 barrier and renovation projects, drawn from interviews with 28 agency and university contacts, a literature review, and internet databases.

Of 49 projects that could be assessed, about 39% (19 projects) were compromised by non-natives within 3 years, while 35% (17 projects) kept native populations free of non-natives for 10 years or more. The authors argue for an [[Adaptive Management]] approach that learns from both failed and successful projects, combines thoughtful pre-project planning with long-term monitoring, and recognizes that barriers and renovations are often the only feasible tool when non-natives pose an immediate extinction threat.

## Background & Description

Many federal recovery plans for listed fishes recommend considering barriers and piscicide application to protect existing populations. Renovation projects follow a simple sequence: isolate a reach with a barrier, remove undesirable fish, and restock natives. The report emphasizes that this simplicity conceals substantial technical and institutional difficulty.

**Scope and methods.** The geographic range covered the Lower Colorado River basin (including Gila, Little Colorado, Salt, Verde, Virgin, Santa Cruz, and San Pedro rivers), the Yaqui River basin, and selected Nevada spring systems. Projects had to be completed by 2003 and have yielded at least one preliminary effectiveness check. Purely mechanical removals were excluded. Common and scientific names follow [[Nelson Common and Scientific Names of Fishes]] (2004).

**Species of concern.** The compilation identified 31 native fish species with barrier or renovation projects tied to their recovery, about 80% of the native fishes in the region. Eighteen non-native species were targeted for removal, including [[Red Shiner]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Blue Tilapia]].

**Annotated bibliography.** Appendix A lists about 125 to 127 references. Appendix B abstracts Desert Fishes Council proceedings from 1984 to 2004, arranged chronologically.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mechanisms of loss.** Non-native fishes, habitat alteration, and hybridization drove most declines. Miller et al. (1989) cited introduced species in 68% of North American fish extinctions over the past century.
- **Stochastic risk.** Catastrophic fire affected Gila trout and Gila chub populations (e.g., Aspen Fire, 2003; Sabino Canyon). Small isolated populations face extinction from random events, so replicate populations across drainages matter.
- **Natural barriers.** Waterfalls and extended dry reaches can exclude non-natives, but dry reaches fail when hydrology changes; red shiner invaded Aravaipa Creek once flows connected it to the San Pedro River.
- **Piscicides.** Rotenone and antimycin remain the most effective removal tools, but they are constrained by water chemistry (high pH, cold temperatures, high gradients), public opposition, and regulatory disputes. Only a few chemicals are registered as piscicides, and none is selective for southwestern fish communities.
- **Public and legal context.** Local ordinances, NEPA review, unauthorized human transplants by anglers, and legal conflicts under the Clean Water Act and FIFRA affect project feasibility.

## Key Findings

**Project performance**
- Of 75 projects, 52 had an indicator of success or compromise. Three projects with under 3 years of data were excluded, leaving 49.
- About 39% of the 49 (19 projects) were compromised within 3 years; 35% (17 projects) stayed free of non-natives for 10 years or more.
- [[Meronek et al. 1996]] reviewed 250 control projects from 131 papers; about 43% were judged successful, 29% unsuccessful, and 28% had insufficient data. Rotenone-treated projects succeeded in 48% of cases, antimycin in 45%.
- [[Hang et al. 2000]] (greenback cutthroat trout) found that about half of 23 failed translocations involved reinvasion by non-native salmonids, usually from failed barriers or incomplete removal.

**Barrier design**
- Natural waterfalls are preferred, but a 1.5 m drop did not stop brook trout (Adams et al. 2000 reported ascent of 1.2 m falls).
- [[Gabion Barriers]] built perpendicular to flow are short-lived (about 10 years in one Oregon study), too porous, and vulnerable to erosion. Tappel (1986) advised against perpendicular installation where velocities exceed 5 ft/s. The authors recommend reconsidering gabions as cross-channel dams, or capping them with concrete.
- Multiple barriers allow early detection, isolate incursions to the reach between barriers, and enable stepwise removal. Multiple barriers in Utah streams were more effective than single-point structures.
- Electric barriers (e.g., [[Central Arizona Project]] canals) work when operating, but outages and low flows allow non-natives to pass. Swink (1999) found an electric barrier on the Jordan River, Michigan, blocked sea lamprey, with 24% recapture of upstream-released lamprey.

**Renovation practice**
- Single treatments rarely remove all target fish; Hepworth et al. (2001) found second treatments about one year later generally succeeded.
- Salvage of non-target native species (e.g., Sonora sucker, speckled dace) and benthic invertebrates is recommended.
- On-site bioassays confirm complete kills in complex habitats and check downstream detoxification.
- Timing matters: antimycin is less effective in cold water and high pH, and rotenone degrades faster in heat, high pH, and sunlight.

**Monitoring and follow-through**
- Consistent monitoring is the only way to detect non-native return early, and it is needed to judge project success.
- Response plans should specify who surveys, who acts, and what thresholds trigger action and repeat renovation.
- Recent projects frequently failed through weak follow-through; Sabino Creek non-natives reappeared three years after treatment.

**Adaptive management and conceptual points**
- Adaptive management should archive failures and analyze root causes. Early Gila trout renovations failed, but progress followed.
- Barriers are best viewed as temporary, delaying measures when short-term extinction risk exceeds long-term risk (Hilderbrand and Kershner 2000).
- Novinger and Rahel (2003) found isolation management with barriers in Wyoming gave only short-term benefits, with limited wild trout enhancement.

**Economics**
- Future operations and maintenance costs should be discounted to present value. The authors suggest optimization methods (after Farmer et al. 1988) and advise consulting a natural resource economist for discount rates.

**Data gaps and recommendations**
- Records often lacked design flow rates, design life, salvage details, and consistent monitoring information.
- The authors ask readers to supply missing data to the senior author (Jeanette Carpenter) and to contribute to the Montana State University Wild Fish Habitat Initiative barrier database, which held 34 barrier descriptions, only two within the study area (the Aravaipa Creek double barriers).

**Conclusion.** The authors conclude that barriers and renovations can protect threatened fish, including temporary protection of evolutionarily significant units, and that an adaptive approach will improve their effectiveness.
