# Chemical Reclamation of Ponds, Lakes, and Streams with Fish Toxicants: A Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Technical Paper (No. 100, Rome, December 1970), prepared by [[Robert E. Lennon]], [[Joseph B. Ellis]], [[Rosalie A. Schnick]] and [[Ralph M. Burress]] and reprinted by the U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, reviews the use of chemical fish toxicants to remove undesirable fishes from ponds, lakes, and streams. The review covers literature from North America, South America, Europe, Africa and Asia, and draws on a worldwide questionnaire sent to 84 countries and territories. Chemical reclamation is described as a common management practice, but the authors urge that toxicants be used only where practical alternatives are lacking, that they be fish-specific and safe for other wildlife, livestock and humans, and that environmental well-being take precedence over short-term economy.

## Background & Description

The document is organized in sections on fish production ponds (Section 2), farm and ranch ponds (Section 2.2), lakes (Section 3), streams (Section 4), chemicals employed as fish toxicants (Section 5), questionnaire results (Section 6), and recommendations (Section 7). Eradication of unwanted fishes from public waters began more than 60 years earlier but accelerated within the last two decades as fishery management needs grew and improved toxicants became available.

Chemical types discussed include:
- Organochlorines and organophosphates, which are being replaced by more selective controls.
- [[Rotenone]], the most widely used general toxicant in the United States.
- [[Antimycin]] (Fintrol), a selective toxicant for larval lampreys, and [[Trifluralin]]-related lampricide TFM (3-trifluoromethyl-4-nitrophenol), which is selective for sea lamprey larvae.
- [[Squoxin]], in advanced development as a selective toxicant for squawfishes in salmonid streams on the west coast of North America.

Approximately 27 to 28 countries reported use of fish toxicants or interest in them, including the United States, Canada, many European countries and several Asian and African nations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Key ecological and management issues include:

- **Production ponds:** Fish culture ponds are frequently contaminated with undesirable species such as [[Bullheads]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Carp]] and [[Goldfish]], as well as crayfish, aquatic insects and parasites. Diseases such as [[Furunculosis]] and parasitic infections occasionally require whole-population eradication.
- **Lakes:** Causes of undesirable fish populations include introduction of exotic species (carp, [[Northern Pike]] fry in trout waters), illegal stocking, overpopulation of [[Bluegill]] in ponds, and habitat changes such as residential development.
- **Streams:** Management problems arise from erosion, siltation, pollution, excessive rough fish, and dams that restrict or enlarge fish distributions. Barrier dams and natural falls can limit re-invasion by unwanted species.
- **Toxicant behavior:** Toxicant effectiveness depends on water hardness, pH, temperature, turbidity, alkalinity, and the presence of vegetation or dense beds that absorb the chemical. Many toxicants are persistent (for example, [[Toxaphene]] and [[Chlordane]] analogs), harmful to birds and mammals, or toxic to aquatic invertebrates.
- **Non-target impacts:** The document reports losses of plankton, frogs, invertebrates and waterfowl food organisms, and warns of residues in fish flesh.

## Key Findings

1. **Recommendations on toxicant use:** Toxicants should be used only where practical alternatives are lacking, should be fish-specific and non-persistent, and must be applied by competent, trained biologists. Toxicant use should be the last resort after alternatives have been considered.
2. **Need for research:** The authors call for research on selective toxicants, dispersal equipment, detoxification methods (for example, potassium permanganate for rotenone and antimycin), and pre- and post-treatment survey methods.
3. **Effectiveness of rotenone:** Rotenone is the most widely applied toxicant and is effective against many species, but success depends on water chemistry (for example, one study found success of about 28.5 percent in waters with pH greater than 8.0). Concentrations of 1.5 to 5.0 mg/l (ppm) were most commonly used in the United States.
4. **Green River reclamation (1962):** Approximately 720 km of the Green River and tributaries were treated with 81,681 l (21,495 gal) of 5-percent rotenone before closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] to suppress non-game fish for a 16,800-ha reservoir. Controversy arose over threats to endemic species such as [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]].
5. **Lake reclamation success:** Stroud and Martin (1968) reported that good fishing followed total reclamation of cold-water lakes for an average of 7 years (range 3 to 10) and of warm-water lakes for an average of 5 years (range 1 to 11). Partial treatments were less successful.
6. **Antimycin properties:** Antimycin is pH-sensitive and degrades within a few hours at pH 8.5 and above; it is deactivated by potassium permanganate. Its formulations (Fintrol, Fintrol-15, Fintrol-Concentrate) are dispersed as granular or liquid preparations.
7. **Sea lamprey control:** TFM, developed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, is a practical and safe control for sea lamprey in the upper Great Lakes. Its toxicity depends on water hardness and pH, and it is most effective in soft, acid water. Adding 2 percent Bayluscide (niclosamide) synergizes TFM and reduces treatment costs by about 50 percent.
8. **Global survey:** Of 84 countries contacted, 28 countries sent replies (35 replies in total, and 28 countries provided data on use). At least 27 countries used or were using fish toxicants in some capacity, and the authors identified a need for better information on safe and effective toxicants.
9. **Reclamation of farm and ranch ponds:** Many farm ponds (about 3.7 million ha estimated in the United States by 1958 per the cited projections) are managed with toxicants. Failure of pond reclamation is commonly due to poor pre-treatment surveys, inadequate pond draining, reinvasion, and poor follow-up management.
10. **Overall conclusion:** The authors conclude that reclamation is a useful fishery management tool, that effective toxicants must be selective and non-persistent, and that greater research and policy development are required before toxicant use becomes routine across countries.
