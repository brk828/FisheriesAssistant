# Leslie Creek Base Flow, Groundwater Depletion, and Alfalfa Farming Impacts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #yaqui-fishes

## Executive Summary

This technical memorandum, written by [[Paul Tashjian]] on 2/18/2000 and addressed to Steve Cullinan and Paul Cornes, assesses how upstream land use could reduce the base flows of [[Leslie Creek]] within the [[Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge]]. The refuge holds an [[In Stream Flow Water Right]] (obtained in 1993) that protects base flow for the endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]] and [[Yaqui Chub]]. The memo estimates that 40 acres of [[Alfalfa Farming]] proposed east of the refuge would consume between 128 and 230 acre-feet per year from the [[Groundwater Basin]] that feeds the creek. This could reduce surface flow by about 0.6 to 0.7 cfs during peak months, which is more than twice the refuge water right in those months and is significant during drought, when flows approach 0 cfs. The memo recommends that no farming be allowed within the groundwater basin supplying Leslie Creek's base flows.

## Background & Description

**Refuge and fish context**
- Leslie Canyon NWR is located in Southeastern Arizona, 17 miles north of Douglas, along the southwestern flank of the Chiricahua Mountains.
- The refuge is one of two remaining U.S. habitats (with San Bernardino NWR) for the nationally endangered Yaqui Topminnow and Yaqui Chub.
- These fishes once dominated streams and cienegas of the south-draining Yaqui Watershed, including Whitewater Draw and Black Draw. Their habitats have deteriorated across Arizona and Mexico due to agricultural and groundwater development.

**Water right**
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service requested the in-stream flow right for Leslie Creek in 1992 (Tashjian, 1992), and it was received in 1993.
- The right is derived from the mean of the monthly median flows for the period of record, 1970–1992, at the [[USGS Gage]] on the refuge. The gage operated 1970–1977 and 1982–1992.
- The total requested volume is 419.9 acre-feet per year.

**Hydrology and monitoring**
- Leslie Creek is a flashy desert fluvial system. Storm events produce very large flows, but base flow is very low.
- The USGS gage is 0.6 miles downstream of the [[Check Dam]]. A flow of 0 cfs at the gage does not mean the entire reach is dry; flows increase toward the check dam.
- Groundwater levels in the basin just upstream of the check dam were compared with creek flows (Figure 3).

**Analytical approach**
- The analysis used USGS stream flow records, refuge water level records, and crop coefficients for the area.
- A simplified [[Mass Balance]] compared 40 acres under fallow versus farmed conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drought and drying**
- Drought periods occurred 1975–1982 and 1989–1991. During these periods, flow at the USGS gage approached 0 cfs.
- Refuge biologist [[Nina King]] reported drying in spring/early summer 1999. By 4/28/99, creek channels connecting the large pools were already drying. Fish and tadpoles were moved between 4/28/99 and 6/29/99. By the end of June, all narrow connecting channels were completely dry, and only the pools at the dam and the large pools above and below the gauging station held water. Monsoonal rains began at the end of June and ended in September.

**Groundwater–surface water link**
- Higher groundwater levels in the basin produce higher creek flows through the refuge. Consumptive use in the basin lowers groundwater levels.
- The correlation between groundwater levels and creek flow is strong on visual inspection, with R² = 0.58.
- Measurement limits reduce the strength of this value. The USGS gage uses a pygmy meter, and accuracy in the 0 to 0.5 cfs range is 50% at best. Sediment and algal buildup can quickly alter the rating. Evapotranspiration in the riparian corridor and bank storage from rain events also skew the correlation.

**Water balance of alfalfa farming (Southeast Arizona)**
- Alfalfa consumes roughly 4 acre-feet of water per year, of which 0.8 feet comes from rainfall.
- Average annual consumptive depletion from 40 acres is 128 acre-feet.
- A fallow field with 25% [[Phreatophytes]] using 4.8 feet per year would deplete about 40 acre-feet. Because these plants already consume water, this is excluded, giving a net additional consumption of 88 acre-feet. With no phreatophytes, consumption would be 128 acre-feet.
- Evaporative irrigation losses increase crop consumption by a factor of 1.2 to 1.8, depending on method. The memo uses 1.4 for the low estimate and 1.7 for the high estimate.
- Resulting impacts: minimum 128 acre-feet per year and maximum 230 acre-feet per year.

**Seasonal distribution (Table 1)**
- Consumption runs March through October, peaking in June and July.
- Under the 128 acre-foot scenario, June consumption is 22.3 acre-feet (17.4%, 0.4 cfs). Under the 230 acre-foot scenario, June consumption is 40.1 acre-feet (17.4%).
- Monthly shares: March 9.3%, April 12.8%, May 15.1%, June 17.4%, July 14.0%, August 12.8%, September 10.5%, October 8.1%.

**Spatial proximity**
- The proposed 40 acres lie just east of the refuge, just east of the spring head at the check dam, close to the perennial reach of Leslie Creek.
- The memo states that withdrawing 1 part of groundwater would reduce surface flow by close to 1 part.

## Key Findings

1. **Flow impact:** Farming consumption could reduce Leslie Creek flows by 0.6 to 0.7 cfs during maximum consumption months. This is twice the refuge water right during those months.
2. **Consumption estimates:** Annual depletion ranges from 128 acre-feet (minimum) to 230 acre-feet (maximum) for 40 acres of alfalfa. Net additional consumption over fallow ground is 88 acre-feet at the low end, assuming 25% phreatophyte cover.
3. **Water right vulnerability:** Because the water right is based on median monthly flows (1970–1992), the stream would be affected in average and dry years. Only unusually wet periods would leave the right unimpacted.
4. **Drought sensitivity:** A reduction of even 0.1 cfs is a concern during drought, when flows approach 0 cfs.
5. **Cumulative risk:** The static analysis does not capture cumulative withdrawals. Persistent overdraft could deplete aquifer storage, potentially drying the aquifer entirely, with recovery extending well into wet climate cycles.
6. **Data limitations:** The memo notes no current information on the size and characteristics of the small basin east of the refuge. A coupled groundwater–surface water model was proposed to address this, with results not expected for a few years.
7. **Recommendation:** No farming should be allowed within the groundwater basin supplying Leslie Creek's base flows. Upstream farming would threaten the [[In Stream Flow Water Right]], the endangered Yaqui fishes of Leslie Canyon NWR, and the unique [[Riparian Aquifer]] of Leslie Creek.
