# Lake Mohave Backwater-Rearing Facility Management/Research Plan

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #catostomidae #endangered-species #razorback-sucker

## Executive Summary

This management/research plan addendum (1994) documents the [[Colorado River Native Fish Work Group]]'s efforts to evaluate on-site rearing and replacement of the [[Lake Mohave]] [[Razorback Sucker]] population. The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was federally listed as endangered in November 1991. The [[Lake Mohave]] population declined from approximately 60,000 individuals in 1988 to 25,000 in 1992, representing the largest remaining population of this species. The population consists entirely of old adults (25-45 years) with no natural recruitment due to predation by non-native fishes on larvae and juveniles. The NFWG established backwater-rearing facilities at [[Yuma Cove]] and [[Davis Cove]] to produce subadult razorbacks (>25 cm) large enough to survive predation when released into the reservoir. Initial goals of producing 50,000 subadults in 5 years proved unrealistic; only 1,700 juveniles were produced in the first 3 years. The plan outlines revised strategies including multiple larval sources (wild-collected, artificially spawned, hatchery-produced), expansion to ephemeral backwaters, improved culturing techniques, and off-site rearing alternatives.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system, historically ranging from Wyoming to Mexico. By 1990, the species was reduced to remnant populations in the [[Green River]] (Utah), upper [[Colorado River]] (Colorado-Utah), [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada), and scattered individuals elsewhere. The [[Lake Mohave]] population of approximately 60,000 individuals in 1988 represented the largest remaining concentration, but consisted entirely of aging adults with no recruitment to juvenile stages for decades.

The [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) was formed in 1989 by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[National Park Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Nevada Division of Wildlife]], and [[Arizona State University]]. The original 1990 Management/Research Plan proposed isolating backwaters from [[Lake Mohave]] to allow adult razorbacks to spawn in predator-free environments, rear juveniles to 25-30 cm total length (requiring 12-18 months), then release them into the reservoir at sizes less vulnerable to predation.

Early experimental work (1984-1986) at [[Yuma Cove]] demonstrated that razorbacks could successfully reproduce and recruit to juvenile stages in isolated backwaters. Larvae reached 16-22 mm and juveniles grew to 18.2-20.4 cm by December 1986. However, storm-generated wave action repeatedly breached the sand/gravel berm, allowing reinvasion by exotic predators (particularly [[Green Sunfish]]) that consumed larvae and juveniles.

The [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), listed as endangered in 1980, is in even greater peril and considered extirpated from the upper basin despite recovery efforts. The [[Colorado River Fishes Project]] began in 1979, and by 1994 the [[Recovery Program of the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River]] had spent nearly $16 million with projections of $50-100 million total. While [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] maintained small self-sustaining populations, razorback and bonytail declines continued unabated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Lake Mohave]] is a reservoir on the [[Colorado River]] created by [[Davis Dam]]. The lake experiences seasonal elevation fluctuations that affect backwater connectivity and depth. Normal high elevations occur around June (645 feet), with drawdowns to minimum levels in late summer and fall. This hydrological cycle influenced rearing facility design and fish collection timing.

The primary limiting factor for razorback recruitment is predation by non-native fishes. Stomach content analyses revealed that [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], and other exotic species consumed razorback larvae and juveniles. At [[Davis Cove]], 7% of largemouth bass and 17% of channel catfish captured contained razorback remains or PIT tags. Nearly 700 kg of exotic fish were removed from [[Davis Cove]], yet predation remained severe—less than 1% of 10,000 small (45-70 mm) razorbacks stocked in June 1992 were recovered.

Population monitoring by Dr. Paul Marsh ([[Arizona State University]]) documented the rapid decline: from 60,000 razorbacks in 1988 to approximately 25,000 in 1992. In the upper basin, [[Green River]] populations declined to fewer than 500 individuals by 1994. Only old adults were being collected with no evidence of natural recruitment. The genetic diversity represented by the [[Lake Mohave]] population was considered irreplaceable.

Backwater habitat characteristics significantly affected rearing success. [[Yuma Cove]]'s shallow, gravelly substrate proved ideal for spawning, and its location near natural spawning aggregations facilitated adult collection. However, aquatic vegetation (primarily pondweeds) became a major obstacle, completely choking surface areas by mid-summer and harboring predatory insects (odonates) that consumed larvae. Vegetation also severely hampered fish collection efforts, requiring manual removal and making seining nearly impossible.

Water quality issues included thermal stratification and oxygen depletion in hypolimnetic zones during mid to late summer. High biological oxygen demand from decaying organic sediments created marginal conditions, though no direct mortality was observed. Disruption of stratified layers during collection activities caused stress-related mortality.

## Key Findings

**Population Status and Decline:**
- [[Lake Mohave]] razorback population declined 60% from 60,000 (1988) to 25,000 (1992)
- Upper basin [[Green River]] population reduced to <500 individuals
- Only old adults (25-45 years) present; no natural recruitment observed since 1950s
- [[Bonytail Chub]] considered extirpated from upper basin despite recovery efforts
- Sixteen wild razorbacks collected for [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] brood stock; all but six subsequently died

**Rearing Facility Performance (1991-1993):**
- [[Yuma Cove]]: Natural spawning produced no juveniles in 1991, ~300 in 1992, >1,400 in 1993
- Total production from all backwaters: 1,700 juveniles over 3 years (versus 50,000 goal)
- Phenomenal growth rates demonstrated: naturally spawned juveniles reached nearly 40 cm in 12 months
- Fish held longer before stocking exhibited higher survival but smaller size
- Three ephemeral backwaters added to program in 1993

**Davis Cove Barrier Net Experiment:**
- Temporary barrier net functioned adequately except where drift sand buried portions during low reservoir levels
- Barrier compromised at least twice by sand burial and subsequent tearing
- Nearly 700 kg of exotic fish removed, but predation remained severe
- Of 10,000 small razorbacks (45-70 mm) stocked June 1992, <1% recovered
- Of 150 larger fish (180-250 mm) from [[Yuma Cove]], nearly 50% recovered
- 800 razorbacks (130-160 mm) PIT-tagged and released in 1993; 14 largemouth bass captured afterward, 7 containing sucker remains or PIT tags

**Stocking Success Indicators:**
- Size at release critical: larger fish (>25 cm) showed substantially better survival
- 153 razorbacks (>25 cm) released fall 1992; 5 recaptured 6 months later
- Four recaptures occurred at different locations up to 50 km from release point
- Results provided "guarded optimism" that concept could work

**Propagation Methods Tested:**
- Natural spawning by adults in backwaters: low success initially, improved with experience
- Collection of naturally produced larvae from reservoir: recommended as primary source
- Artificial spawning of wild adults: developed as supplemental/backup source
- Hatchery production at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]: third source option
- Holding larvae 2-3 weeks before stocking increased survival by establishing feeding
- Holding larvae 4-6 weeks sacrificed some growth but enhanced survival further

**Major Operational Challenges:**
- Aquatic vegetation (macrophytes) severely hampered collection efforts, requiring labor-intensive manual removal
- No efficient passive collection methods developed; trammel netting and electrofishing caused stress
- Predatory insects (odonates) associated with dense vegetation may reduce larval survival
- Current production rate (600 fish in 3 years) represents only 1% of 5-year goal
- Herbicide permit obtained but not yet tested; benthic liner installation ($6,000 for 0.5 acre) showed promise

**Revised Goals and Strategies (1994-1995):**
- Revised annual production target: 5,000-7,500 subadult fish (down from 10,000-50,000 total)
- Priority larval sources: (1) naturally produced reservoir larvae, (2) artificially spawned reservoir adults, (3) [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]
- Multiple larval holding sites planned to prevent catastrophic loss
- [[Davis Cove]] barrier net to be replaced with earthen berm (fall 1994) for better predator control
- Expansion to additional ephemeral backwaters with enhanced berms
- Off-site rearing alternatives pursued: [[Boulder City]] golf course ponds, [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], State/municipal ponds, private hatcheries
- Herbicide (sonar) testing planned for 1994 to control aquatic vegetation
- Development of standard operating protocols (SOPs) for backwater management

**Tagging and Monitoring:**
- PIT (Passive Integrated Transponder) tags selected as preferred method
- Tags cost ~$4.50 each; monitors $1,200; tagging 50,000 fish would cost $225,000
- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] testing showed 100% tag verification, 83% survival in razorbacks ≥120 mm
- Annual "Razorback Roundup" during March spawning season: 2,152 adults tagged 1988-1990, 2.8% return rate
- Bi-annual monitoring (March and November) planned to assess stocked fish recruitment

**Downstream Expansion:**
- [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] Parker office developed similar on-site rearing program
- Nearly 50,000 small razorback and bonytail stocked in isolated backwaters downstream from [[Lake Mohave]]
- [[Pittsburgh Point Backwater]]: 199 PIT-tagged razorbacks introduced June 1993, grew from 178 mm (68 g) to 245 mm (190 g) in 6 weeks
- [[Office Cove]] ([[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]]): 17,992 bonytail chubs stocked October 1993
- [[High Levee Pond]] ([[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]]): 14,006 razorbacks and 21,503 bonytails stocked October 1993

**Cost and Feasibility Considerations:**
- Upper basin recovery program spent $16 million by 1994, projected $50-100 million total
- On-site rearing evaluation scheduled for completion 1996
- Population replacement timeline extended from 5 years to 10+ years
- Additional grow-out coves critically needed but access and construction costs prohibitive
- Labor-intensive nature of current methods (vegetation control, fish collection) threatens long-term feasibility

**Research Needs Identified:**
- Optimal habitat conditions for early life stages (diet, thermal requirements)
- Habitat and cover preferences of juvenile and adult razorbacks in reservoir
- Predator-prey interactions between native and exotic fishes
- Improved stocking techniques and locations to reduce predation
- Telemetry studies to understand movement patterns and habitat use
- Development of effective passive trapping methods

**Management Agreements:**
- No formal agreement yet developed among participating agencies
- Designation of critical habitat may require formal management procedures
- All participation (labor, equipment, funding) currently voluntary
- Future MOU anticipated to formalize long-term facility management

The document concludes that while on-site rearing proved more challenging than initially anticipated, the concept remained viable with modifications. The rapid decline of the aging adult population created urgency: "We are running out of time; the relic population is rapidly dying." Success would require expanded facilities, optimized production methods, off-site rearing support, and sustained multi-agency cooperation over at least a decade.
