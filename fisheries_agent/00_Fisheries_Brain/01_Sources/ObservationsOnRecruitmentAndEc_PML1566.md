# Observations on Recruitment and Ecology of Razorback Sucker in the Lower Colorado River, Arizona-California-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 field report by [[Paul C. Marsh]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] documents wild [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endemic and imperiled catostomid, in the lower [[Colorado River]] system downstream from [[Lake Mohave]] for the period 1962–1988. Wild adults, juveniles and larvae are very rare. The authors separate naturally produced young from hatchery-reared fish stocked during [[Reintroduction Programs]], and they argue that small numbers of wild-hatched young, many found in [[Irrigation Canals]], indicate some recruitment, but that this is insufficient to sustain the adult population.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Razorback sucker, a once abundant endemic fish of the Colorado River basin, now restricted to remnant populations. Large upstream populations occur above the Grand Canyon, and the lower-basin populations are small and scattered.
- **Study area:** The lower Colorado River downstream from Davis Dam, including Lake Havasu, Lake Mohave, riverine reaches (e.g., [[Needles-Topock Gorge]], Blythe to Imperial Reservoir), [[Laughlin Lagoon]], [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] and the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) Granite Reef Aqueduct.
- **Reintroduction history:** Few experimental reintroductions occurred before 1980. Substantial stockings began in 1980 and continued through the 1980s, with the first major mainstream release in March 1980 of nearly 1.4 million larvae (figure as given in source OCR, unclear).
- **Funding and agencies:** Work was supported by USFWS, USBR and ASU. Agencies cited include AZGFD, ASU, CADFG, NVDOW, USBR and USFWS.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Larval habitat:** Larvae occupy the littoral zone of Lake Mohave and are seasonally abundant. They are attracted to light at night and rarely occur in open water.
- **Juveniles:** Gaps exist between larval and adult size classes in collections, despite intense sampling.
- **Canal ecology:** Irrigation canals are maintained annually, usually in December or January, with mechanical cleaning and dewatering that decimate fish. Razorback suckers spawn early (January through March), which may allow larvae and juveniles to colonize canals before maintenance. Canals offer few predators, but annual drainage makes young fish vulnerable to collectors.
- **Drains:** Drains have slow, warm, often vegetated water, which may support larval survival but are rarely dry and receive excess irrigation water and salts.
- **Predation:** Nonnative predators such as [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] are considered a major limit on recruitment.
- **Aging:** Individuals can live at least 44 years (McCarthy and Minckley 1987), so low adult mortality may explain stable adult numbers in the 1980s.

## Key Findings

- **Adults:** Only 42 adult razorback suckers were confirmed from the lower Colorado River mainstream and associated habitats downstream from Davis Dam from 1962 to 1988. Sixteen were from Lake Havasu proper, 23 from riverine reaches, and 3 from the CAP aqueduct. Adults made up an average of about 2% of total fish caught in trammel-net samples from 1975 to 1988.
- **Juveniles:** Four naturally spawned juveniles (33–34 mm TL) collected from Lake Mohave in July 1987 are the only ones recorded there since the 1950s. Twenty-four wild juveniles (15 to 37.1 cm TL) were reported from the river, Lake Havasu and canals. Between 1974 and 1988 at least 19 young, wild-hatched fish were captured.
- **Larvae:** Razorback suckers made up only 0.1% of 6,617 larval fish caught in tow nets in Lake Havasu and upstream riverine reaches in 1985 and 1986. No larvae were found in shoreline surveys in 1988. Two larvae under 15 mm TL were among 5,036 larval fish sampled from the CAP canal in 1987.
- **Recruitment:** Wild-hatched young show that some natural recruitment occurs, but numbers appear too low to maintain the adult population. Potential recruits may be lost to canal maintenance.
- **Senator Wash population:** An estimated 957 individuals (95% confidence interval 178–1,736 as given in source) averaging about 60 cm TL persists in Senator Wash Reservoir, which spawns and produces young.
- **Origins:** Most canal-caught juveniles probably came from natural spawning in the river or impoundments, though hatchery stockings cannot be fully excluded; the relative contributions of wild and hatchery recruitment remain unresolved.
- **Research needs:** The authors recommend marking hatchery fish (e.g., with oxytetracycline) to distinguish them from wild fish, assessing whether stocked and wild young behave similarly, and studying predation by [[Ictalurid Catfishes]] as a deterrent to re-establishment.
- **Management implication:** Protecting canals and drains from maintenance losses and reducing nonnative predation are key steps for the [[Recovery Program]].
