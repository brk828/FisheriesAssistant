# Hatchery Pond Design, Conifer Plantation Wildlife Use, and Black Bear Parasitology References

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document contains three unrelated pieces. The main fisheries content is a 1960 study of [[Trout Hatchery Pond Design]] in New York State, which tested [[Water Circulation]], [[Aeration Efficiency]] and [[Screen Area]] in rectangular rearing ponds. Its key results are that full-width inlets and outlets give the most even circulation, that modified dams aerate better than simple dams, and that one square foot of usable screen per 25 gallons per minute of flow is recommended. The second piece is a wildlife study of a 256-acre [[Conifer Plantation]] at the Tully Forest, focusing on [[Ruffed Grouse]] use of closed plantations. The third piece is a reference list of about twenty publications on [[Black Bear]] pathology, parasites and hematology. Only the hatchery study concerns fish directly, so this note centers on it.

## Background & Description

**Hatchery pond study (Haskell, Davies and Reckahn, 1960).** The authors were hatchery staff of the New York State Conservation Department. The work was a contribution of Federal Aid in Fish and Wildlife Restoration Project F-11-R, published in the New York Fish and Game Journal, Vol. 7, No. 2 (July 1960). The study examined three design features of rectangular rearing ponds. It relied partly on the views of O. R. Kingsbury, whose production rules are summarized under [[Kingsbury Production Rule]].

Four rearing pond types were in use at New York State hatcheries:

- **[[Bath Type Pond]]:** Two sizes. The small pond measured about 50 × 15 × 2 ft (1,250 cubic feet). The large pond measured 100 × 30 × 3 ft (7,100 cubic feet).
- **[[Rome Type Pond]]:** Same dimensions as the Bath type, but with inlet and outlet spillways spanning nearly the full pond width.
- **[[Dirt Pond]]:** About 60 × 12 × 15 inches, with sloping, dish-shaped sides and 3-ft-wide inlets and outlets.
- **Circular ponds:** Not included in the study.

**Conifer plantation study (Bailey and Alexander).** The authors were, respectively, a graduate assistant and an assistant professor in Forest Zoology at the State University College of Forestry at Syracuse University. The summer study surveyed birds and mammals in a closed plantation of red pine, white pine and Norway spruce. The authors note that conifer planting in New York was expected to reach about 60 million seedlings from state nurseries by 1961.

**Black bear references.** The reference list covers topics such as [[Trichinella Spiralis]] in bear muscle, eyeworm infection in deer, Diphyllobothrium in Yellowstone bears, Dermacentor ticks, Mallophaga, Anoplura, and bear blood profiles. These references come from the bibliography only; no bear data are reported in the document.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry in hatchery ponds.** Poor circulation was linked to two water-quality problems. First, metabolic products measured as [[Ammonia Accumulation]] were higher in spots called "hot spots" in each pond, which were attributed to uneven circulation. Second, [[Dissolved Oxygen]] was depleted unevenly. Oxygen differences above a dam reached a maximum of 0.7 p.p.m. Depletion was greater in the poorer-circulating parts of the pond. The authors suggested that these effects may limit growth and fish health.

**Oxygen limits in pond series.** In a series of reused ponds, oxygen depends on the supply, the oxygen removed by fish, and the aeration that occurs at each dam. Because supply oxygen is usually not controllable, dam aeration is the main way to keep lower ponds adequately oxygenated. Low oxygen in the Rome Hatchery's lower ponds was a problem in September, when temperatures were high and fish loads were heavy.

**Wildlife in conifer plantations.** Large dense plantations have been criticized as wildlife habitat, but this criticism was based largely on general observation. The plantation supported chickadees, robins, black-throated green warblers and towhees. It also supported deer and cottontail rabbits, and ruffed grouse were the principal game species.

## Key Findings

### Hatchery Pond Design

- **Production rules (Kingsbury):** Potential trout production may be estimated from spring water supply, at least 40 pounds per year per gallon per minute. Required rearing space should produce at least 2 pounds of trout per year per cubic foot. These data apply specifically to ponds 2 feet deep.
- **Uniform conditions:** Ponds of uniform depth with straight sides and even flow through each cross section are recommended. These conditions should give even fish use of the pond and even growth.
- **Circulation:** Using fluorescein dye, the [[Rome Type Pond]] came closest to even circulation. The authors concluded that for best circulation the inlet and outlet should be the full width of the pond. Historic raceways about 4 ft wide with inlets and outlets about 1 ft wide performed poorly for small fingerlings. Full-width inlets and outlets worked well.
- **Spillway symmetry:** An obstructing valve on a by-pass line shifted the flow to one side of a large Rome pond. Symmetrical, unobstructed spillways are required for even circulation. The Dirt pond's circulation was, if anything, poorer than the other types, but no photographs were usable.
- **Aeration with falls:** Aeration increases with fall height and is approximately proportional to it. Two 6-inch falls approximately equal one 12-inch fall.
- **Water depth below dam:** For flows of 25 to 100 gallons per minute per foot of dam and falls of 4 to 15 inches, the depth of water below the dam made little difference. When depth was zero, aeration was greatly reduced.
- **Flow per unit length:** Flows of 25, 50 and 100 gallons per minute per foot of dam produced only small differences. A flow of 50 gallons per minute per foot was somewhat less efficient, and the result was checked repeatedly.

### Aeration Efficiency of Dam Modifications

The tests used a standard condition of 50 gallons per minute flow, an 8.6-inch fall and 4 inches of water below the dam. Efficiency of the [[Simple Dam]] was 6.2 per cent. The authors concluded that any modification of a simple dam improved aeration.

- **Best device:** A flat rack with widely spaced slats (about 3/4-inch wide slats) was the most efficient device tested. It reached 15.8 per cent at 50 gallons per minute, 17.7 per cent at 25 gallons per minute and 14.4 per cent at 100 gallons per minute. Slats must be wide enough to keep water streams from recombining through adjacent slots.
- **Narrow board below dam:** 14.1 per cent.
- **Flat board with 16 one-inch holes on the dam:** 15.6 per cent, and nearly the same at 100 gallons per minute when the board and holes were enlarged.
- **Window screen:** 15.1 per cent. Flat screens (1/4-inch and 1/8-inch mesh) gave 12.9 and 13.7 per cent. Finer and partly clogged screens aerated better.
- **Vertical rack on dam:** 14.6 per cent. Vertical board with seven one-inch holes: 13.5 per cent. Vertical board with rectangular holes: 12.5 per cent. Vertical board with slots: 11.0 per cent.
- **Chute below dam:** 11.7 per cent. Chute on top of dam: 8.8 per cent (sheet metal on top of dam also 8.8 per cent).
- **Rockpile below dam:** 10.8 per cent, judged not particularly effective in the arrangements tested.
- **Narrow board just above water level:** Aeration was small (0.5 per cent) when sampled through the board. Samples taken below the board showed a marked rise. Up to 14 per cent aeration was produced when falling water left the board as a thin sheet with high horizontal velocity. The best height of the flat surface above the water was about a fraction of an inch.

### Screen Area

- Adequate screen area at the head of a pond series reduces cleaning labor and protects fish.
- The recommended ratio is one square foot of usable underwater screen for each 25 gallons per minute of flow.
- At 85 gallons per minute per square foot, the screen was quite inadequate and needed continuous maintenance except under very favorable conditions.
- At 60 gallons per minute per square foot, the screen was somewhat inadequate.
- Screen area can also be too large, because debris may not be drawn to the screen enough for easy removal.

### Conifer Plantation Wildlife Use

- The 256-acre closed plantation of red pine, white pine and Norway spruce was studied during one summer.
- Ruffed grouse used the outer 5 chains of the plantation where other good habitat adjoined it. Grouse were found near old hedgerows, scattered apple trees, hardwood clumps or brushy openings inside the plantation.
- Grouse used the plantation more at midday and at night, and moved to surrounding habitat in the morning and late afternoon.
- The authors concluded that large conifer plantations should be broken up with openings to provide interspersed brushy cover where game is important.

### Black Bear Reference List

- The bibliography contains about twenty references. Topics include parasites (Trichinella, Thelazia, Diphyllobothrium, Dermacentor, Mallophaga, Anoplura), hematology, and shock disease in captive wild mammals.
- The list contains no bear data, results or conclusions beyond the citations themselves.
