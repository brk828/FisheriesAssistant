## Executive Summary
This editorial discusses the management conflict surrounding the endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (historically called the "Colorado Squawfish") and the [[Humpback Chub]] in the upper Colorado River basin. The piece highlights a historical irony: federal wildlife managers once poisoned river reaches to eradicate these species as "trash fish," but under evolving conservation policy, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service now considers supporting additional dam construction to stabilize flows and protect the same species. The core scientific/management issue centers on flow variability in a critical 15-mile reach of the Colorado River and its detrimental impact on endangered fish habitat, weighed against competing water demands from irrigation and trout fisheries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Reach**: The critical habitat under discussion is a specific **15-mile stretch** of the Colorado River between **Palisade** and the confluence with the **Gunnison River** near Grand Junction, Colorado.
- **Flow Sensitivity**: This reach experiences highly **inconsistent/fluctuating flows**, ranging from **350 cubic feet per second (cfs)** to **6,000 cfs**. Such fluctuation is identified as harmful to fish species broadly, and particularly detrimental to the endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]].
- **Historical Context**: The species once occupied a niche perceived as competitive with sport fisheries; it was historically targeted with piscicide (poisoning) programs to eradicate it as a "[[Trash Fish]]" species in favor of more desirable game fish.
- **Water Management Conflict**: The species' habitat needs are entangled with regional water management, including:
  - Irrigation demands (heavy, especially in dry summers).
  - Competing ecological interests, such as the **[[Fryingpan River]]**, a "premier trout fishery" that could be harmed by proposed water releases meant to help the squawfish.
  - Proposed release of **10,000 acre-feet** of water from **[[Ruedi Reservoir]]** (above Basalt) between mid-August and mid-October to augment flows in the Colorado River — though this volume is described as insufficient to meaningfully raise flows to desired levels.

## Key Findings
- **Flow Fluctuation as Primary Threat**: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biologists determined that radically inconsistent flows (350–6,000 cfs) in the Palisade-to-Gunnison reach are actively harming endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] populations.
- **Dam Paradox**: The same agency that historically opposed dams due to their detrimental effects on endangered species is now considering supporting *additional* upstream dams as a tool to stabilize and even out seasonal flow variability — illustrating a shift in conservation strategy from opposition to infrastructure toward strategic use of infrastructure for species benefit.
- **Historical Eradication Efforts**: Wildlife managers previously conducted **poisoning programs** targeting the Colorado Squawfish and [[Humpback Chub]] as undesirable "trash fish," a policy now viewed as short-sighted given their current endangered status.
- **Water Release Trade-offs**: A proposed short-term fix — releasing water from Ruedi Reservoir — is flagged as problematic because it risks damaging the Fryingpan River trout fishery and the local economy of Basalt, while likely being an inadequate volume to sufficiently benefit the squawfish's habitat reach.
- **Management Recommendation**: The editorial concludes that the Bureau of Reclamation should reject the Ruedi Reservoir release request, advocating instead for longer-term infrastructure solutions (more dams higher in the drainage) that could reconcile the competing interests of agriculture, recreational trout fishing, and endangered fish conservation.
