#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a personal correspondence and field-photography report (E. S. Gustafson to Dr. W. L. Minckley, Arizona State University) documenting a photographic survey of spawning behavior in the **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) at Arizona Cove, **[[Lake Mohave]]** Reservoir, on the Arizona-Nevada border. Observations were made on March 10, 1989, between 9:45–11:30 am, using a 35mm SLR camera with a telephoto zoom lens and polarizing filter to photograph fish through shallow water from an elevated bluff vantage point. The notes describe spawning aggregation structure, mate-guarding/herding behavior, microhabitat selection, and notable interference by non-native **[[Common Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*) at spawning sites — data intended to support a forthcoming scientific publication on razorback sucker reproductive ecology by Minckley.

## Background & Description

- **Uro-genital papilla**: A large uro-genital papilla was observed on a light-colored female, used as a field cue for sex identification during herding events.
- **Coloration/size dimorphism**: Females described as "large" relative to flanking males; individual coloration ("light colored female") noted as distinguishing feature in photographs.
- **Tagging**: Multiple individuals bore orange external tags, indicating this population was part of an ongoing mark-recapture or telemetry study.
- Sex determination in the field relied on behavioral position (leading vs. flanking/herding) combined with body size and papilla morphology, with some uncertainty acknowledged (denoted by "(?)" in field notes for both sexes).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Shallow reservoir shoreline zones, depths ranging from 0.3–1.8 m, with actual spawning confined to depths of **less than 1 meter**.
- **Substrate selection**: Spawning aggregations consistently selected the **boundary between gravel-cobble and sand substrate**, described as "a typical selection for most of the spawning aggregations observed" — indicating a strong, repeatable microhabitat preference for egg deposition.
- **Spatial context**: Observation site located along loose conglomerate bluffs 130–250 m north of a sand-gravel berm separating the Arizona Cove backwater from the main reservoir body; fish congregated 5–9 m offshore over a gravel-cobble-to-sand gradient.
- **Co-occurring species / interspecific interaction**: **[[Common Carp]]** were repeatedly observed converging on and actively feeding at razorback spawning sites within seconds to minutes after spawning events (documented feeding activity persisting at least 10 minutes post-spawn), suggesting significant potential egg predation pressure from this invasive species.
- **Timing**: Spawning activity observed during mid-morning hours (9:45–11:30 am) in early March, consistent with known late-winter/early-spring spawning phenology of the species in Lake Mohave.

## Key Findings

1. **Spawning aggregation structure**: Aggregations formed around a single female "leading," actively herded by multiple males (observed group sizes of 3, 4, up to 9–10 fish per aggregation), with males positioned flanking and trailing the female — consistent with a polygamous/promiscuous group-spawning mating system typical of catostomids.
2. **Herding behavior**: A recurring triad pattern was documented — one female flanked by two males — with additional males trailing behind in some events, sometimes obscured within silt clouds.
3. **Silt clouds as spawning indicators**: Turbidity/silt plumes were used as a reliable visual proxy for identifying active spawning events, appearing during and immediately after gamete release.
4. **Site fidelity to substrate transition zones**: Nearly all observed spawning events occurred specifically at the sand-gravel substrate interface, suggesting this transition zone is ecologically significant for egg adhesion/survival or represents an evolved spawning cue.
5. **Post-spawn dynamics**: Sequential photographs (taken seconds to minutes apart) captured a rapid influx of carp to spawning sites, with carp still actively feeding on the substrate 10 minutes after spawning concluded — raising concern about carp as egg predators competing with or threatening razorback sucker reproductive success.
6. **Confirmed reproductive readiness**: A concurrent Fish and Wildlife Service beach-seining operation at Arizona Cove recovered a gravid female and a flowing (milt-releasing) male, with mature eggs and milt physically collected, corroborating that active spawning condition coincided with the photographic observations.
7. **Methodological note**: The use of a polarizing filter (including reorientation by 90°) and controlled aperture settings (F-11 to F-22) allowed clear underwater visibility of fish behavior from an elevated shoreline vantage, offering a replicable non-invasive photographic method for documenting **[[Razorback Sucker]]** spawning ecology in reservoir systems.
8. **Conservation context**: The correspondence references broader conservation efforts, including a proposed **[[Virgin River]]** survey and ongoing **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*) recovery operations, situating this observation within a larger regional program addressing endangered Colorado River Basin fishes.
