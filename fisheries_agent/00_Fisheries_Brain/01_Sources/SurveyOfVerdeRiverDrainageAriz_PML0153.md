# Survey of Verde River Drainage, Arizona, for Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, submitted to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] by Brian Bagley, documents a 2000–2002 fisheries survey of Verde River tributaries in central Arizona, targeting the federally threatened [[Loach Minnow]]. Fish collections were made at 130 locations on 44 streams. No loach minnow were captured. The survey recorded 23 fish species (8 native, 15 non-native) and confirmed a newly discovered population of [[Gila Chub]] in Wet Bottom Creek. The report also includes a cover letter recommending three candidate sites for native fish reintroduction.

## Background & Description

The [[Loach Minnow]] is endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of New Mexico, Arizona, and Sonora, Mexico. It was listed as threatened in 1986 because of habitat alteration and competition and predation by non-native fishes. The species has been reduced to about a dozen remaining populations. The last recorded Verde River collection dates to 1938.

The species is a small, stream-dwelling cyprinid reaching standard lengths of about 68 mm, with an elongated body, a small, terminal, highly oblique mouth, and an olivaceous, blotched back. Breeding males show bright orange coloration at the bases of the pelvic, pectoral, and caudal fins and on the mouth. Its preferred habitat is shallow, turbulent riffles over gravel-to-cobble substrates. The author notes that loach minnow can persist for extended periods in pools, as observed in Pace Creek, New Mexico.

The [[Verde River]] drains roughly 16,900 km² (about 6,500 mi²) and is one of the larger drainages in Arizona. Its headwaters lie near 2,135 m (7,000 ft), and the mainstem flows southeast for more than 301 km (187 mi) before joining the [[Salt River]]. The basin supports 12 native fish species, including roundtail chub, Gila chub, headwater chub, spikedace, longfin dace, speckled dace, Sonora sucker, desert sucker, and reestablished populations of Gila trout, Colorado pikeminnow, razorback sucker, and Gila topminnow. Twenty-eight non-native species have been recorded, including [[Green Sunfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Brown Trout]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Typical loach minnow riffles were sampled extensively at each site, and runs and pools were also sampled to capture all species present. Gear included backpack electrofishing ("shocker"), dip nets, block (kick) seines, and gill nets. Fish were counted as young-of-year or older than one year. Voucher specimens went to the Arizona State University Museum. Field work ran from August 2000 through July 2002.

**Habitat and threats.** Most surveyed sites were rated poor or fair for loach minnow. Common limiting factors were:
- Dominance of deep pools, which favor non-native fishes
- Intermittent flow and dry reaches, especially in lower-elevation tributaries
- Silt, sand, or large boulder substrates instead of classic riffles
- Heavy cattle use and human disturbance along banks
- Wildfire effects, including the 1990 Dude Fire and burns about 10 years before the 2000 survey
- Flood scour, with severe flooding noted in several steep canyons

**Historic loach minnow sites.** Historic records came from the Verde mainstem near Beaver Creek, Beaver Creek itself, and a tributary near Chino. Beaver Creek was treated with fish poison in 1962 and is now dominated by non-native species, and the Chino tributaries have also been invaded. Loach minnow was reported from Eagle Creek in the 1950s, then not found for about 40 years, which illustrates how easily the species is overlooked.

## Key Findings

- **No loach minnow were encountered** despite sampling 130 locations on 44 streams. The author argues the species may still persist in the drainage because it is cryptic and easily missed.
- **New Gila chub population:** a population was found in Wet Bottom Creek (Gila County). Five chubs were taken to ASU for genetic analysis. Gila chub are known from five other basin locations (Weedman et al. 1996). Chubs were also confirmed at Walker Creek, Spring Creek, and Williamson Valley Wash.
- **Roundtail chub** were collected at Deadman Creek, South Fork Deadman Creek, Weber Spring, and West Clear Creek. Headwater chub was included with roundtail chub because chub taxonomy remains unsettled.
- **Gila trout** reestablished in Dude Creek, where the 1990 fire had eliminated non-native trout. Fish measured 10–25 cm (4–10 in), and only part of the creek was occupied in autumn 2000.
- **Most common natives:** longfin dace (29 locations) and speckled dace (20 locations). Sonora and desert suckers were locally abundant.
- **Gila topminnow** reestablished populations (stocked 1982) remained large at Walnut Spring and Lime Creek. Walnut Spring alone held an estimated several thousand fish.
- **Most common non-native:** green sunfish, found at 28 locations and most abundant at lower elevations. Brown and rainbow trout, stocked since about 1942 and still augmented, were common at higher elevations.
- **Reintroduction candidates (cover letter):**
  - *Upper Lime Creek:* a strong topminnow population already exists in Lower Lime Creek, and the pools near Little Cougar Canyon (deeper than 2 m) may suit chub.
  - *Horse Creek:* historically supported longfin dace and a topminnow population that persisted about 10 years before flood losses in the early 1990s. Periodic restocking after floods is proposed.
  - *Apache Creek:* supports a large speckled dace population. Its middle pool habitat appears suitable for chub.
- Of more than 130 locations visited, only these three were judged appropriate for additional native fish reestablishment. The author stresses that wherever water appeared suitable, non-native fishes were present, which complicates native restoration.
- **Conservation conclusion:** the report calls for protecting portions of the Verde drainage from human and non-native impacts, since the basin holds a significant share of Arizona's remaining riparian community and is key to native fish survival.
