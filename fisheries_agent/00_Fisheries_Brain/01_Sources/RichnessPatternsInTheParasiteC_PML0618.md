# Parasite Communities of Poeciliid and Native Fishes in South-East Queensland

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares parasite communities of three exotic [[Poeciliidae]] species, [[Gambusia Holbrooki]], [[Xiphophorus Helleri]] and [[Xiphophorus Maculatus]], with those of 15 ecologically similar native freshwater fishes from Queensland, Australia. The central hypothesis was that poeciliids, as introduced hosts, would carry depauperate parasite communities. The data supported this: poeciliid infracommunities and regional faunas were significantly species-poorer than those of natives (P < 0.0001). Parasite communities of the two groups were also qualitatively distinct, and little parasite exchange has occurred between them. Fish origin, environmental disturbance and host sex all significantly structured parasite community richness. The author proposes that the lack of parasites may give poeciliids a competitive advantage over native fishes, although this was not tested directly.

## Background & Description

Australia has only about 180 native freshwater fish species, and its native fauna is distinctive and highly endemic. Its dominant native groups include [[Eleotridae]], [[Ambassidae]], [[Melanotaeniidae]] and [[Retropinnidae]]. Cyprinids, percids, cyprinodontids and cichlids do not occur naturally. The Poeciliidae is the most successful exotic fish family in Australia. The five established poeciliid species account for almost a quarter of the country's exotic fish fauna (McDowall, 1996). The study also mentions [[Poecilia Reticulata]] and [[Phalloceros Caudimaculatus]] among the established species, although only the three species above were sampled.

Previous work offers a framework for the expected outcome. Bauer (1991), expanded by [[Kennedy Introduction Model]] (1994), predicts that parasites with direct life-cycles and high host-specificity persist best after introduction, followed by those with complex life-cycles and low host-specificity. Exotic pathogens are most damaging when related native hosts are present. Research in Hawaii ([[Font and Tate]], 1994; Font, 1997) and in the British Isles (Kennedy, 1993) reported similar patterns. The Norwegian salmon epidemic caused by [[Gyrodactylus Salaris]] is cited as the extreme case of exotic parasite impact.

The study aimed to: identify parasites introduced with poeciliids; quantify parasite exchange between natives and poeciliids; compare qualitative and quantitative community differences; and identify the factors that structure these communities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fishes were collected by dip-netting at 19 sites in south-east Queensland, spanning five drainages: the [[Brisbane River]], [[Kedron Brook]], Downfall Creek, Cabbage Tree Creek and the [[Burnett River]]. Each site was scored for habitat disturbance on a 1 to 3 scale, based on riparian and in-stream vegetation, substrate type and visible pollution. The scale was 1 for pristine, 2 for partly disturbed and 3 for heavily disturbed. The same observer scored every site.

Fishes were held in aquaria at about 27 °C and dissected within five days. This limited the proliferation of parasites with direct life-cycles. Intensity data were not recorded. The authors judged that the time cost outweighed its value for these aims, because some parasites multiply asexually within the host. All parasite taxa were examined, not helminths alone. Identifications were often made to family or genus, with letter codes for unnamed types. Parasite origin (exotic or native) was judged against a literature database of poeciliid parasites from their native ranges.

Analyses used infracommunity richness, regional fauna richness and [[Cumulative Richness Curves]]. Group differences were tested with a [[Mann-Whitney U-Test]]. Structuring factors were examined using [[Logistic Ordinal Regression]]. Presence/absence similarity was assessed by [[Cluster Analysis]] with 10,000 resampling iterations.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and parasite diversity**
- A total of 330 fishes were dissected, representing 18 species. These yielded 109 putative parasite species.
- Sample sizes for the main hosts were: [[Gambusia Holbrooki]] (n = 144), [[Xiphophorus Helleri]] (n = 54), [[Xiphophorus Maculatus]] (n = 30), [[Ambassis Agassizi]] (n = 28), [[Hypseleotris Compressa]] (n = 17), [[Retropinna Semoni]] (n = 10), [[Melanotaenia Duboulayi]] (n = 13), [[Craterocephalus Stercusmuscarum]] (n = 9) and [[Pseudomugil Signifer]] (n = 9).

**Infracommunity richness**
- Mean infracommunity richness was 1.03 for exotic fishes and 3.6 for native fishes (overall 2.74).
- Mean percentage of hosts harbouring at least one parasite species was 60.7% for exotic fishes and 95.9% for natives (overall 78.3%).
- The Mann-Whitney U-test showed exotic infracommunities were significantly poorer (P < 0.0001).
- Native infracommunity richness was approximately normal. Poeciliid richness was strongly skewed, with a mode of zero.

**Regional faunas and parasite groups**
- Poeciliid regional faunas had lower total richness than native regional faunas, although sampling effort differed.
- [[Myxozoa]] contributed strongly to native richness but were entirely absent from poeciliids. Coccidia and parasitic copepods were also absent. The Results also list strigeid digeneans as absent from exotic fishes.
- The proportion of complex life-cycle parasites was lower in poeciliids than in natives.

**Community similarity**
- Cluster analysis separated the exotic fishes from all native fishes. The exotic fishes' parasite communities were more similar to each other than to any native community.
- The two [[Xiphophorus]] species showed very strong similarity, mainly because they shared [[Prototransversotrema Steeri]] and cf. Leptocleidus sp.

**Structuring factors (LORA)**
- Host origin was the dominant factor, with native fishes richer than exotic fishes. Its effect was about two orders of magnitude greater than any other variable.
- Environmental disturbance was significant (P = 0.000). Pristine sites supported richer communities.
- Host sex was significant (P = 0.023). Females were richer.
- Host size was narrowly non-significant (P = 0.053).

**Host specificity and parasite exchange**
- Parasite host-range was strongly over-dispersed. Most parasites infected only one host species, with a maximum of 11. The average was 1.7 host species per parasite.
- Parasite sharing between poeciliids and natives was low. Shared parasites moved from native to exotic hosts. The exceptions were [[Trichodina Heterodentata]] and [[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]], which were introduced but probably did not arrive with poeciliids.
- Only three introduced generalist parasites were found that did not arrive with poeciliids: [[Centrocestus Sp.]], T. heterodentata and I. multifiliis. Of these, only Centrocestus has a complex life-cycle.
- [[Monogenea]] dominated poeciliid parasite faunas. Three species were recovered, including [[Gyrodactylus Bullatarudis]] and cf. Leptocleidus sp., which may be a new genus and species.
- [[Bothriocephalus Acheilognathi]] was absent from poeciliids in this study, although it is present in Australian Gambusia where they co-occur with carp. Carp, not poeciliids, introduced this tapeworm to Australia.
- [[Cane Toad]] (Barton, 1997) provides a contrasting case. Its parasite fauna grew richer after introduction, unlike the fish pattern.

**Conclusions and implications**
- Poeciliid parasite faunas are significantly poorer and qualitatively different from those of native fishes.
- Disturbance reduces parasite richness, so parasite communities may serve as useful bio-indicators of aquatic ecosystem health.
- The author argues that the low parasite load of poeciliids may give them a competitive advantage over native fishes, for example in food competition with [[Melanotaenia Duboulayi]] (Arthington, 1991). This hypothesis requires field and laboratory testing.
- Caution is needed before blaming exotic parasites for native fish declines in the presence of poeciliids, because the study found no evidence that poeciliids introduced parasites capable of infecting natives.
