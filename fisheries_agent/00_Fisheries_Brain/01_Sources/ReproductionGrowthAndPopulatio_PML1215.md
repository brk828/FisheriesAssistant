# Threadfin Shad Reproduction, Growth, and Population Dynamics in Central Arizona Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the front matter and introduction of a 1969 doctoral dissertation by James Edward Johnson, submitted to [[Arizona State University]]. It sets out the rationale for studying the reproduction, growth, and population dynamics of [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) in central Arizona reservoirs. The introduction traces how the species was introduced across the western United States as a forage fish for [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]], how it spread and established itself, and why its biology in reservoirs needs to be understood. The text reports no original data; its results are limited to stated objectives.

## Background & Description

Threadfin shad belong to the herring family [[Clupeidae]]. They are small, silvery, pelagic, schooling fishes that feed on plankton. The species is currently placed in the genus *Dorosoma*, with the closely related [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*). Early taxonomic work placed the two in separate genera, and later authors merged them. Subspecific names have been withheld pending revision of the subgenus *Signaria*. The native range lies along the Gulf of Mexico, from northern Florida to Guatemala.

Threadfin shad are physiologically restricted to water warmer than 9.0°C (Strawn, 1963). Sudden drops in temperature cause massive mortality (Hubbs, Lagler, Parsons and Kimsey, 1941 cited in text). This cold limit constrains where the species can establish. The text also notes that the species tolerates a wide range of salinities and has been found in both fresh and salt water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Introduction history.** Large reservoirs built on western rivers were stocked with predatory game fish, but small forage species often failed to supply enough food for the larger fishes. In the mid-1950s, the California Fish and Game Department obtained threadfin shad from Norris Reservoir, Tennessee, and planted them in [[Lake Havasu]] on the [[Colorado River]] in 1954. Nevada personnel may have planted them earlier, in 1954, in [[Lake Mead]]. By 1956 the fish had spread downstream past Davis Dam to the Gulf of California, into the [[Salton Sea]], and into most canals connected to the Colorado mainstream. [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]] showed immediate increases in growth rates. Stocking then spread to more than a dozen states over the following decade.

**Overwintering and dispersal.** Northward expansion has been aided by overwintering in warm spring inflows and in the outflows of industrial cooling water. Dryer and Benson (1957) reported that threadfin shad in [[Kentucky Lake]] survived lethal temperatures by congregating near steam plants that discharge warm water. Only a small fraction of the population survived winter, but this was enough to produce population explosions each year. The species appears capable of long-distance migration and of passing through or over barriers, so fish may persist in a drainage even where stocking attempts failed.

**Arizona introductions.** In Arizona, threadfin shad were not intentionally stocked in Bartlett and Horseshoe reservoirs on the [[Verde River]]. They entered those lakes accidentally when holding ponds at Page Springs Fish Hatchery, about 90 km upstream, overflowed into Oak Creek, a tributary of the Verde.

**Invasive potential.** Clupeids generally colonize newly invaded waters quickly. The text cites threadfin and [[Gizzard Shad]] in the Salton Sea and Pacific and Gulf waters as examples. The species appears to fill an empty niche in southwestern reservoirs, where no native pelagic, plankton-feeding species existed. Its plankton-feeding diet is considered valuable because it shortens the food chain and can increase the efficiency of energy transfer to predators.

**Comparison with gizzard shad.** Gizzard shad are also widely introduced as forage. In deep, clear lakes their populations are often controlled by predation. In shallow, turbid reservoirs they become abundant and can make up a large share of the overall fish population. This is partly due to very high fecundity, reported as high as 500,000 ova per female, and rapid growth that quickly moves young fish out of the forage size range. Threadfin shad are not limited by high temperature tolerances in the same way, but they rarely grow beyond forage size, and the dissertation's preliminary observations indicate lower fecundity than gizzard shad. They can still reach high abundance within a year of introduction.

**Possible competition.** In smaller lakes during winter, threadfin shad may compete with smaller carnivorous species for plankton (Gerdes and McConnell, 1963; McConnell and Gerdes, 1964). Under such conditions their populations may exceed their forage role. Annual stocking of pre-spawning fish in cold-water areas has been proposed to supplement summer food for carnivores, but the text notes no evidence that benefits accrue from such short-term population establishment (Bruce, 1961). Similar experiments with alewives (Burnside, 1960) gave similar results.

## Key Findings

- **Introduction purpose:** Threadfin shad were introduced as forage to support larger game fish, because earlier forage species did not supply enough food for predators such as largemouth bass.
- **Rapid spread:** After the 1954 Lake Havasu stocking, the species had spread by 1956 across the lower Colorado River, into the Gulf of California, the Salton Sea, and connected canals.
- **Temperature limit:** Threadfin shad are physiologically restricted to water warmer than 9.0°C, and mass mortality follows abrupt cold snaps. This limits their northern establishment.
- **Overwintering near heated discharges:** Fish survive lethal winters by congregating at warm-water outflows from power plants. Survivors can rebuild populations quickly.
- **Accidental Arizona introduction:** Threadfin shad reached Bartlett and Horseshoe reservoirs on the Verde River by overflow from a hatchery holding pond.
- **Ecological role:** The species fills an empty pelagic, plankton-feeding niche and may shorten the food chain to predators.
- **Lower fecundity than gizzard shad:** Preliminary observations, as stated by the author, indicate lower fecundity than gizzard shad, and threadfin shad rarely grow beyond forage size.
- **Potential competition:** In small winter lakes, threadfin shad may compete with small carnivores for plankton and may exceed their forage role.
- **Study objectives:** The dissertation aims to review basic ecology, summarize observations on reproduction, reproductive potential, growth, and population dynamics of threadfin shad, and describe these for central Arizona reservoirs.
