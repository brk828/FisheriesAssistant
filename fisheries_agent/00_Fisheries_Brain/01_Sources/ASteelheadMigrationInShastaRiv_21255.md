# A Steelhead Migration in the Shasta River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #anadromous-migration

## Executive Summary

This 1932-1933 study documented the fall migration of [[Steelhead]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) from the [[Klamath River]] into [[Shasta River]], California. Using a counting rack with observation gates, researchers enumerated 8,525 steelhead between September 19 and December 10, 1932. The migration proceeded in irregular pulses rather than a steady wave, peaking in mid-November. Contrary to prevailing theories about temperature-driven tributary selection in [[Pacific Salmon]], water temperature did not determine the steelhead's choice of the Shasta River as their spawning destination. The study was conducted by the Bureau of Commercial Fisheries as part of broader salmon investigations, with Carleton Rogers performing the steelhead counts.

## Background & Description

The Bureau of Commercial Fisheries had been conducting annual censuses of salmon migrating into [[Shasta River]] from the [[Klamath River]] main channel under the direction of Merrill Brown. The primary purpose was evaluating natural propagation when adequately protected. In 1932, the observation period was extended to encompass the steelhead run, which begins as the salmon run declines.

A counting rack was constructed across [[Shasta River]] near its mouth to facilitate accurate enumeration. Two counting gates were built into this barrier, leading fish over a white apron. An observer seated above could easily distinguish steelheads and count them accurately. The clear water at the river mouth normally permitted viewing of passing fish, but the rack system was necessary during periods of high fish density.

Gates were closed at night after observers learned that few fish moved after darkness. Migration activity did not commence in earnest until after noon each day. The first fish was recorded on September 19, 1932, with only 15 observed before October 10.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Shasta River]] is one of several tributaries entering the [[Klamath River]], some larger than the Shasta itself. Migrating steelhead originating from the sea traveled considerable distances and passed multiple tributary mouths before selecting and entering the Shasta River specifically.

Water temperatures declined steadily throughout the observation period as winter approached. [[Klamath River]] afternoon temperatures fell from 72°F to 38°F, while [[Shasta River]] temperatures dropped from similar levels to 33°F. On November 6, temperatures equalized between the two water bodies without arresting migration. After this date, conditions sometimes reversed, with the Shasta becoming warmer than the Klamath.

Weather conditions varied considerably during the study period, ranging from clear to foggy, rainy, and eventually snowy. The observation period ended prematurely on December 10 due to ice formation during a severe cold snap.

[[Steelhead]] appeared in the Shasta River and began spawning operations earlier than in several other [[Klamath River]] tributaries entering both upstream and downstream of the Shasta confluence.

## Key Findings

**Migration Timing and Magnitude:**
- Total count: 8,525 steelhead between September 19 and December 10, 1932
- First fish recorded September 19; only 15 fish observed before October 10
- Migration peaked in mid-November with maximum daily counts:
  - November 18: 2,675 fish
  - November 28: 2,124 fish
  - November 17: 703 fish
  - November 14: 459 fish

**Migration Pattern:**
- Migration proceeded in irregular spurts rather than a steady increasing wave
- Pattern consisted of a series of lesser waves
- Mass movement was apparently complete by December 10
- This pulsed pattern is common to most animal migrations and similar to observations in other [[Klamath River]] tributaries

**Temperature Analysis:**
Of 8,513 migrating trout:
- 974 fish (11.4%) passed from warmer to cooler water
- 1,075 fish (12.6%) moved on even temperatures
- 6,464 fish (75.9%) moved from colder to warmer water

Temperature did not determine tributary selection. Fish continued migrating after November 6 when temperatures equalized between the Klamath and Shasta rivers, and even when the Shasta became warmer than the Klamath. This contradicts temperature-based theories of tributary selection proposed for certain Alaskan salmon populations.

**Weather Influence on Migration:**
- Clear days (24 total): 1,353 fish migrated
- Cloudy days (13 total): 2,966 fish migrated
- Foggy days (10 total): 3,076 fish migrated
- Rainy days (10 total): 1,118 fish migrated
- Dark days combined (33 total): 7,160 fish migrated

While weather character may have influenced daily movement rates, it had no apparent effect on tributary selection.

**Behavioral Observations:**
- Minimal nocturnal movement; gates closed at night
- Migration activity concentrated in afternoon hours
- Fish successfully distinguished from salmon during counting operations

**Comparative Ecology:**
- [[Steelhead]] spawning in [[Shasta River]] commenced earlier than in other [[Klamath River]] tributaries
- Migration patterns similar across tributaries: all proceed in series of lesser waves
- Contributing factors for pulsed migration waves remain unknown
- Observations of [[King Salmon]] at the same location do not support temperature-driven tributary selection for that species either
