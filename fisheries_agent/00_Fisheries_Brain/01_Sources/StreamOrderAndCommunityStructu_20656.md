# Stream Order and Community Structure of Benthic Macroinvertebrates and Fishes in an Intermittent Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This doctoral dissertation by Richard Cunningham Harrel (Oklahoma State University, Zoology, July 1966) tests whether [[Stream Order]] classification, following [[Horton Stream Order System]] and [[Strahler Modification]], can predict the morphometry, [[Physico-Chemical Conditions]] and biological community structure of a sixth order, intermittent system: [[Otter Creek Drainage Basin]] in Northcentral Oklahoma. Benthic macroinvertebrates and fishes were sampled from 21 stations (plus one fish station) in nine streams between June 1964 and September 1965. Community structure was evaluated with [[Species Diversity]] indices from information theory, [[Diversity Per Individual]], [[Redundancy]] and [[Index of Heterogeneity]]. The study concludes that stream order is closely correlated with physical, chemical and biological features, with higher-order streams showing greater stability and more uniform communities. Drought and oil field brine inputs disrupted this relationship at some sites.

## Background & Description

The thesis was submitted in partial fulfillment of the requirements for the PhD degree and was supported in part by the [[U.S. Public Health Service]], Division of Water Supply and Pollution Control Research Fellowship (5-F1-WP-23,074). The author holds a B.S. from East Central State College (1959) and an M.S.Ed. from the University of Georgia (1963). Advisers and taxonomic verifiers are acknowledged in the preface.

The introduction reviews stream classification systems, from [[Steinmann]] and [[Thienemann]] through [[Usinger]], and notes that such systems are often subjective and region-specific. Stream order offers an objective, dimensionless alternative. Gravelius's original system was inverted by Horton, and Strahler later made ordering uniform across a basin.

The study area is a sixth order, intermittent stream in the mixed-grass prairie of northcentral Oklahoma. Key characteristics:
- Basin: 302.1 km² area, 106.2 km perimeter, 36.4 km axial length; main channel 41.8 km long, elevation 363 m to 287 m.
- Geology: Permian red beds (Hennessey and Garber formations); the Garber sandstone aquifer is hard and high in sulfates and chlorides.
- Climate: mean annual temperature 16 °C; annual precipitation about 81 cm, with frequent severe droughts.
- Morphometry (Table I): bifurcation ratio 3.97; stream-length ratio 2.08; drainage density 2.04 (metric); stream frequency 3.25 (metric).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physico-chemical gradients.** Average drainage area roughly quadrupled with each order increase. Average gradient decreased. Turbidity and temperature decreased, while flow, alkalinity and conductivity increased with stream order. Physico-chemical fluctuations were greatest in third order streams and declined with order, indicating greater stability downstream. Water temperature ranged from 1 °C (January) to 39 °C (July, small pool). Dissolved oxygen ranged from 0.6 ppm to 17.5 ppm.

**Hydrology and disturbance.** Flow was intermittent in lower-order reaches. Summer 1965 was a severe drought with most third and fourth order stations dry by August. Floods in September and October caused scouring; over 16 cm of rain fell between August and September 1965. Oil field brines seeped from wells about 180 m from two fourth and fifth order stations (9(4) and 22(5)), raising sodium chloride and sulfate levels and conductivity.

**Benthic community.** Oligochaetes (9 species, 70% of pool fauna) and dipterans (46 species, 22%) formed 92% of the total pool fauna. Limnodrilus sp. alone formed 66% of numbers. Seasonal responses included replacement of Limnodrilus by predatory tendipedid larvae (for example, Pelopia sp. B) during drought, and recovery of communities after floods through upstream migration and resistant life stages.

**Fish community.** Two collections (10 June and 28 September 1965) found 18 and 16 species, respectively. The combined total for the study was 21 species. Repopulation after drought occurred within two to three days of renewed flow, apparently by migration from [[Skeleton Creek]] and adjacent farm ponds.

## Key Findings

- **Morphometry:** Stream numbers and average gradient decreased exponentially with order, while average drainage area and stream length increased. The bifurcation ratio (3.97) exceeded the theoretical value of two, attributed to adventitious streams and high dissection.
- **Richness:** 121 benthic invertebrate species were collected. Species numbers rose from 75 (third order pools) to 86 (fifth order pools) and fell to 68 in the sixth order stream, attributed to heavy siltation reducing microhabitats.
- **Abundance:** Individuals were most abundant in fourth order streams year-round; the sixth order stream had the fewest individuals in fall, winter and spring.
- **Diversity:** Annual species diversity (H) rose from third to fifth order and fell in the sixth order stream. Diversity per individual (H') increased and redundancy (R) decreased with stream order. Ranges of these measures narrowed with order, indicating more uniform communities in higher-order streams.
- **Statistical significance (annual):** Species diversity differed at the 75% confidence level, diversity per individual at 95%, and redundancy at 90%.
- **Heterogeneity:** Mean annual heterogeneity (IH) decreased with stream order, so faunal similarity between stations increased downstream. The adventitious third order station 3(3) resembled higher-order streams more than other third order stations.
- **Brine effects:** Low brine influx (station 9(4)) increased H' and decreased R, interpreted as improved conditions with higher productivity. Concentrated brine (station 22(5)) reduced H' and increased R, with Limnodrilus and some tendipedids becoming superabundant.
- **Fishes:** Species diversity (d) rose with order in June (0.90, 1.54, 2.08, 2.46 for orders 3 to 6) and September (0.87, 1.38, 1.93, 2.06). Diversity values from the two collections were remarkably similar.
- **Drought and flood:** Drought and brine input disrupted the stream order–community structure relationship. Tendipedid larvae replaced Limnodrilus during drought; Limnodrilus was more flood-resistant than tendipedids.
- **Taxonomic records:** The author reports Prostoma rubrum (Nemertea) as a first record for Oklahoma, and seven Naididae species as first known records for the state.
- **Conclusion:** Stream order provides an objective framework linking basin morphometry, physico-chemistry and community structure. Diversity per individual and redundancy better distinguished harmful from beneficial disturbances than species diversity alone.
