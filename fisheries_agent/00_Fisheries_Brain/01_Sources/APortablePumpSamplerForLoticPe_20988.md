# Portable Pump Sampler for Lotic Periphyton

#phycology #freshwater-biology #bacillariophyta

## Executive Summary

This methods note describes a simple, inexpensive device and procedure for quantitatively sampling [[Periphyton]] (attached algae and microorganisms) on intact, submerged [[Sedimentary Substrates]] in shallow flowing water. A [[Peristaltic Pump]] evacuates the periphyton mat from a sharpened pipe that isolates a fixed area of stream bed. The same procedure yields both standing crop and colonization rate. Because the sampling sites are permanent and sit directly on the substrate, the method avoids the human disturbance that affects [[Floating Artificial Samplers]] in high-recreation areas. The method was tested at Slide Rock on [[Oak Creek]], Arizona, from 1979 to 1980.

## Background & Description

**Terminology.** The heterogeneous assemblage of attached microorganisms on natural and artificial submerged surfaces is called periphyton (Aufwuchs). Its algal component is termed phycoperiphyton. [[Bacillariophyta]] (diatoms) dominate the group in flowing waters.

**Use as indicators.** Phycoperiphyton, especially diatoms, are widely used as biotic indices of water quality. They have primary trophic status, rapid turnover, large surface-area-to-volume ratios, and a fixed attachment to the substrate. They have also been used to study dieldrin accumulation, radionuclide adsorption, and responses to sewage effluent.

**Existing sampling approaches.** Earlier techniques for natural substrates include scrapers, open-ended polyethylene bottles, and coring devices, which require the substrate to be removed from the water. Devices that sample while the substrate stays intact were described by Douglas (1958) and Ertl (1971). Many studies have used [[Artificial Substrates]] for uniform, reproducible data. These include floating samplers modeled on the [[Catherwood Diatometer]] (Hohn 1954), which are common in water-quality programs but are vulnerable to disturbance in recreational areas.

**Artificial versus natural substrates.** Evidence is conflicting. Castenholz (1961) and Stockner & Armstrong (1971) found minimal differences in algal composition and density between artificial and natural substrates. Several other studies reported significant differences in species composition or density (Tippit 1970; Foerster & Schlichting 1965; Tuchman & Blinn 1979; Sladeckova 1962; Pieczynska & Spodniewska 1963; Brown 1976).

**Design rationale.** The authors designed the method for shallow streams of less than 30 cm depth. It offers three stated advantages:
1. Quantitative sampling of standing crop and colonization rate from intact, submerged sedimentary substrates. The authors note that sedimentary deposits cover an estimated 75% of exposed land area worldwide (Pettijohn 1957).
2. Establishment of permanent sampling sites for seasonal or annual monitoring.
3. An alternative to floating samplers in high-recreation areas that removes human-disturbance factors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Sampling was conducted at Slide Rock, a recreational area of about 0.4 km on [[Oak Creek]], a permanent stream in Oak Creek Canyon, Coconino County, Arizona. The stream bed was intact and unfractured, so any method that required removing the substrate was ruled out. Recreational use made floating artificial samplers unfeasible for colonization studies.

**Hydraulic conditions.** The colonization pads were placed in areas of similar light, water depth, and current regime. The current was 20–40 cm/sec. The pads withstood spring scouring events and high discharges.

**Monitoring relevance.** The authors frame the method as a tool for ongoing [[Water Quality Monitoring]] programs. Permanent sites with a known area allow repeated sampling of the same substrate over time.

## Key Findings

**Device and procedure**
- **Colonization pads:** Galvanized metal conduit, 2.05 cm in diameter, cut to 12 cm lengths with a beveled edge machined on a lathe. A 0.6 cm hole through the opposite end took a 16 cm bolt used as a twisting lever.
- **Pad formation:** A repetitive twisting motion cut a circular groove about 0.5 cm deep, enclosing **3.3 cm²**. Each [[Colonization Pads]] site was cleaned with a stiff nylon brush and left to recolonize for **21 days** before random selection for sampling.
- **Pump:** A portable peristaltic "utility pump" (Manostat Co.) fitted to a cordless 2-speed drill. Tubing was 1 m of 0.635 cm I.D. plastic. Delivery rate depends on motor speed. Samples went into plastic bags, and tubing was flushed to recover residual periphyton.
- **Sampling of natural substrates:** The pipe is driven into the substrate next to the pads to a depth where no water leaks out. The same procedure is then applied, so natural and artificial substrates can be compared under one technique.

**Results**
- Diatoms made up most of the periphyton on the pads. Mean diatom densities ranged from **1.9 × 10⁴ to 8.5 × 10⁵ cells/cm²** during the program.
- Removal efficiency from the substrate approached 90%. Based on sampled and resampled pads (n = 10), mean removal was **87.8%** (s = 7.5; coefficient of variation = 8.5%).
- The pads kept their integrity through the year with no observable loss of sampling efficiency.

**Timeline**
- Sampling began at Slide Rock in **May 1979**.
- It continued in **May 1980** using the same pads.

**Conclusion**
- The authors judge the pad-and-pump technique suitable for long-term water-quality monitoring of intact stream substrates.
