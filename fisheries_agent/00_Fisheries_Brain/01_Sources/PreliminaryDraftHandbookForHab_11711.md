# Preliminary Draft Handbook for Habitat Conservation Planning and Incidental Take Permit Processing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #habitat-conservation-planning

## Executive Summary

This preliminary draft handbook (dated September 15, 1994; running headers read 1996) is internal guidance from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) to its Regional and Field Offices on developing [[Habitat Conservation Plan]]s (HCPs) and processing [[Incidental Take Permit]] applications under section 10 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). It also notes that the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS) will use the procedures when circumstances change. The handbook is not a species study. It covers legal authority, the three-phase permit process, a tiered categorization of HCPs, roles of FWS offices, required plan elements, mitigation standards, NEPA compliance, issuance criteria and appeals.

The central aim is to reconcile non-Federal development with listed species conservation. The handbook sets target processing times, promotes regional planning, and introduces the "No Surprises" policy, which limits additional mitigation demands on permittees whose HCPs are functioning properly.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and legal basis.** The handbook has three stated goals: ensure ESA conservation planning goals are met, set consistent national standards for section 10, and keep enough flexibility for local conditions. Congress added the [[Section 10 Permit]] mechanism in the 1982 ESA amendments so non-Federal parties could lawfully take listed species incidental to otherwise lawful activities. Section 10(a)(2)(A) requires a conservation plan detailing likely impacts and the measures to minimize and mitigate them. The section 10 provisions were modeled on the [[San Bruno Mountain HCP]], which protected two federally listed butterfly species.

**Regulations.** FWS published section 10 regulations on September 30, 1985 (50 FR 39681). NMFS published its regulations on May 18, 1990 (55 FR 20603). FWS declined to issue "cookbook" regulations and instead promotes "flexibility and ingenuity."

**Program scale.** From 1982 to July 1994, FWS issued 33 incidental take permits and 12 amendments. About 130 HCPs and applications were in development, roughly four times the number processed in the program's first ten years. Authority to issue incidental take permits was delegated to Regional Directors effective February 12, 1992. NMFS processes its permits through the Office of Protected Resources. To date NMFS has issued section 10 permits only for activities such as State fish hatcheries.

**Three-phase process.**
1. *Pre-application coordination*: negotiation, mitigation design and HCP preparation, ending with a "complete application package" (form, fee, HCP, draft [[National Environmental Policy Act]] document, and [[Implementing Agreement]]).
2. *Formal application processing*: Regional Office completeness review, Federal Register notice, public comment, [[Section 7 Consultation]], and permit decision.
3. *Post-issuance*: public notice of decisions, NEPA notification, and compliance and progress monitoring.

**Activities covered.** Section 10(a)(1)(B) authorizes only inadvertent take. Take for scientific purposes or enhancement of propagation requires a separate section 10(a)(1)(A) permit, which can be issued jointly with the incidental take permit.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Three HCP categories.** The handbook sorts plans by effect, with a case-by-case judgment required:
- **High-effect (regional) HCPs** involve significant effects, such as a large share of a species' population or range, ecologically essential range, pervasive long-term adverse conditions, species with low numbers or reproduction, or large multi-jurisdictional or ecosystem-scale areas.
- **Medium-effect HCPs** involve moderate effects, such as limited range impacts, transitory conditions, stable populations, or medium-sized planning areas.
- **Low-effect HCPs** involve minor effects, typically a single small landowner and relatively few acres.

**Species-level considerations.** Biologists are advised to review recovery plans and consult recovery teams. They should use tools such as [[Population Viability Analysis]] and reserve design theory. Plans should address species likely to be listed during the permit's life. Under section 7, the jeopardy standard applies to federally listed plants as well as animals. Plants on private land are protected from take only under state law, but the FWS still recommends that HCPs address them.

**Take definitions.** Under the regulations, "harm" can include significant habitat modification that kills or injures wildlife by impairing breeding, feeding or sheltering. "Harassment" is an act that significantly disrupts normal behavior. Habitat modification can therefore constitute take and must be detailed in the HCP.

**Cumulative and regional concerns.** Projects may not be segmented to exploit low-effect processing. The handbook argues that regional HCPs distribute impacts, reduce piecemeal habitat loss, and provide planning assurances. Adjacent HCPs should avoid inconsistent mitigation for the same species. FWS should not issue two permits for the same land at the same time.

**Mitigation hierarchy.** Mitigation should follow this priority: avoid, minimize, rectify, reduce over time, then compensate. Compensation habitat should preferably lie in the same drainage basin as the impact, and preferably be acquired existing habitat. Credit systems and habitat banks are discussed as tools. Funds paid into compensation funds are generally not themselves mitigation for habitat loss.

**NEPA context.** Issuing a permit is a Federal action. NEPA can be satisfied by a categorical exclusion, an [[Environmental Assessment]] (EA) or an [[Environmental Impact Statement]] (EIS). A "mitigated EA" may be used when enforceable mitigation brings impacts below the significance threshold, and the [[Finding of No Significant Impact]] then requires 30 days of public review. Programmatic EAs suit similar, well-defined actions. An EIS is generally needed only when HCP implementation significantly affects resources other than listed species, such as water or air quality or land use. Examples cited include the Coachella Valley and Riverside County HCPs (EIS) and the Clark County HCP (EA).

## Key Findings

**Permit processing targets (from receipt of a complete package to issuance):**

| HCP category | Target |
|---|---|
| High-effect or regional | 10 months |
| Medium-effect | 5 months |
| Low-effect | 3 months |

Targets exclude pre-application time and apply unless controversy, workload or other legitimate reasons cause delay.

**Documentation by category.** High-effect plans require an EA or EIS, a Solicitor's review, formal section 7 consultation and a biological opinion, and an Environmental Action Memorandum. Medium-effect plans usually need an EA, with optional Solicitor review. Low-effect plans may use the standard Implementing Agreement template in Appendix 6, and are candidates for categorical exclusion from NEPA.

**Six issuance criteria** (section 10(a)(2)(B); 50 CFR 17.22(b)(2)):
1. Taking will be incidental.
2. The applicant will, to the maximum extent practicable, minimize and mitigate impacts.
3. Funding is assured and unforeseen circumstances are addressed.
4. Issuance will not appreciably reduce the likelihood of survival and recovery in the wild. This matches the section 7 jeopardy standard.
5. Other measures FWS requires will be provided.
6. Other assurances are provided that the HCP will be implemented.

**No Surprises policy.** Approved by the Secretaries of the Interior and Commerce on August 11, 1994, the policy states that FWS and NMFS will not seek additional property interests or financial compensation from a permittee whose HCP is functioning properly. Additional mitigation may be sought only under "extraordinary circumstances," where the agencies bear the burden of proof. Permittees who are fully compliant with their HCP are also protected from further mitigation requirements, and the policy applies where an HCP was designed to provide an overall net benefit with measurable success criteria. A separate rule says no HCP is legally required to provide a net benefit.

**Unlisted species.** Congress intended HCPs to cover unlisted species. Applicants are encouraged to address proposed species, Federal Category 1 and Category 2 candidates, and species likely to be listed during the permit's life. Covering these species in advance, through "advance HCPs," makes later permit amendments simpler.

**Implementing Agreement.** This is a legal contract binding all parties and is required for all HCPs regardless of size. It should be signed only after public comment and changes are complete, and before permit issuance.

**Funding and perpetual protection.** Compensation lands are generally protected "in perpetuity." Funding strategies for regional HCPs include per-acre development fees, tipping fees, water surcharges, state or federal funds and assessment districts. Failure to provide adequate funding was the basis for the permit denial letter in Appendix 5.

**Application and fees.** The application fee is $25 per new application, amendment or renewal, and is non-refundable once significant processing begins. State and local government agencies are fee exempt. Incomplete applications receive a 45-day deficiency notice, after which they may be deemed inactive.

**Public participation.** The Federal Register notice of receipt and the 30-day public comment period are required under section 10(c). Draft EIS notices require 60 days under Departmental procedures, and final EIS notices require 30 days. Notices of permit issuance, amendment or denial should be published at least quarterly. A 45-day waiting period is recommended before publishing denials.

**Permit decisions and appeals.** Denial letters must state reasons and the right to reconsideration. The reconsideration decision is due within 45 calendar days, and the Regional Director's decision on appeal is the final administrative action of the Department of the Interior.

**Permit duration and reporting.** The handbook allows flexible permit terms, and suggests 30 years or more for long-term commitments. Annual reports are required from each permittee, even when no activity occurred. Failure to file an adequate report can lead to suspension or revocation.

**Named examples.** The handbook cites the Kern County HCP (MOU in Appendix 3), the Metropolitan Bakersfield HCP, the Coachella Valley HCP, the Riverside County HCP and the Clark County HCP. It also cites the 1989 Delano State Prison project as an example of an applicant discovering an ESA problem only after completing project planning.

**Internal coordination.** The Field Office must certify the application package before the Regional Office begins formal processing. The Field Office may also draft the biological opinion, Set of Findings and FONSI during the comment period. Solicitor review is required for high-effect plans and may be waived by a Regional official. Permit applicants and their consultants may draft EAs, but the FWS remains responsible for NEPA content.
