# Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center Annual Report FY 1992

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center (Dexter NFH & TC), Dexter, New Mexico, covers fiscal year 1992. The station, run by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service), serves as a refuge, study site and production facility for imperiled native fishes of the American Southwest and northern Mexico. In FY 92 it held 17 species, including several federally listed as endangered or threatened. Major events were the loss of most upper basin [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] broodstock after a chlorinated-water accident, a long-running [[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]] ("Ich") problem in [[Woundfin]] that led to heat-treatment trials, and the loss of chemical treatments for parasites after FDA bans.

## Background & Description

The station lies in the Pecos River Valley, 20 miles southeast of Roswell and 200 miles southeast of Albuquerque. It occupies 540 acres in Section 15, T13S, R25E, Chaves County. The Federal Government acquired the land on August 31, 1931 for $93,255.90, and operations began in 1932 under the [[White Act]] of 1931, originally to supply warmwater game fish to the Southwest.

After the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, the station joined the endangered species program in 1978, with three goals: refuge, study and production. Seven station objectives were defined: maintain a viable, protected gene pool of imperiled fishes; develop culture techniques; study biological requirements; provide live and preserved specimens; exchange expertise; publish findings; and run a public information program.

Of the 600 freshwater fish species in the United States, 85 are listed as threatened or endangered. Seventeen of these are held at Dexter, nearly all from New Mexico or adjacent states (Arizona, Utah, Colorado, Texas, Chihuahua and Sonora). Dexter was designated a Technology Center in FY 91, one of only six in the Service.

Staff for FY 92 comprised seven permanent employees, including Center Director [[Buddy Jensen]], Assistant Director [[Roger Hamman]], and Fishery Biologists [[Troy Winham]] and [[Caryl Williams]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report describes how Southwestern waters have changed over the past century. Rivers have been dammed and diverted, many unique freshwater ecosystems altered or destroyed, and native fishes have declined, often without human awareness.

Key habitat and environmental issues include:

- **Water supply:** Dexter's shallow water rights date from 1931 to 1933. Pending lawsuits by the State of Texas and the Mescalero Apache Tribe could threaten junior rights dated after the mid-1940s. About $324,000 (about $210,000 from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]) was available for pond lining in FY 92.
- **Nitrogen supersaturation:** Periodic mortality in [[Colorado Squawfish]] raceways was linked to gas supersaturation.
- **Woundfin habitat:** The species is restricted to a short reach of the [[Virgin River]] system in Utah, Arizona and Nevada. Its decline is attributed to reduced flow, introduced exotics (especially the [[Red Shiner]]) and habitat destruction.
- **Clear Lake, California:** Low water and irrigation releases led the Service to issue a jeopardy opinion to the Bureau of Reclamation over two endangered suckers.
- **Lake Mohave:** Source of the Razorback Sucker broodstock and [[Bonytail Chub]] collected for captive use.

## Key Findings

### Station Operations and Stocking

- The station planned to supply eggs and fry to cooperators, spawn [[Chihuahua Chub]] for reintroduction, and continue [[Woundfin]] culture work.
- Stocking protocol was revised after a 1991 Service and Arizona Game and Fish Department meeting. Fish would be stocked larger and in smaller numbers: an average minimum of 15 inches for Razorback Sucker and 8 inches for Colorado Squawfish. Razorbacks were expected to reach 15 inches in about 30 months, and squawfish 8 inches in about 40 months. Target annual stocking in Arizona was 3,000 to 5,000 razorbacks and 10,000 to 12,000 squawfish.
- The 10-year cooperative agreement with Arizona expired in 1991, so all three year classes of produced fish were held pending new agreements.

### Razorback Sucker

- On February 27, 1992, 20 female F1 domestic razorback suckers (mean 5.52 lb) were spawned with chorionic gonadotropin. The mean egg take was 14,582 per pound and 77,557 per female.
- Swimup fry totaled 235,887, a survival of 15.838 (as reported) from egg to swimup, similar to FY 91. Fry averaged 183 per gram.
- Fry were distributed to Colorado State University, NFCRC Yankton and Columbia, Dexter production ponds, Page Springs SFH, and Lake Mohave. Dexter ponds produced 27,240 fingerlings (27.22% return) from one pond and 251,342 fish (92.9%) from a second.
- In March 1992 a chlorinated-water accident by a private contractor killed six of the upper basin wild razorbacks. The incident drew nationwide media attention.
- Dexter currently maintains about 270 F1 domestic lower basin broodfish from Lake Mohave stock.

### Imprinting Study

- One female razorback was spawned on March 25, 1992 to test imprinting agents, morpholine and phenethyl alcohol. Eggs were split into control and treatment lots, with agents metered into the water supply of two lots.

### Colorado Squawfish

- Six F1 domestic 1974 year class females were spawned on May 27, 1992 using carp pituitary. All spawned. The total was 360,107 eggs (range 29,540 to 108,080 per female; mean 72,121), with nine males used.
- Remaining eggs (254,823) were used in the second part of the imprinting study with morpholine and phenethyl alcohol.
- Upper basin wild fish spawned on June 11, 1991 produced 21 genetic crosses. Of 21 groups, 15 survived to spring 1992, but eight of ten fish were lost in the March 1992 accident.
- Intensive raceway rearing (two year classes) had repeated mortality related to nitrogen supersaturation. Packed columns for inflow water were built, but a further fish loss followed.

### Bonytail Chub

- Five females spawned May 4, 1992 produced 180,454 eggs for contaminant research. On May 5, 30,000 eggs each went to NFCRC Yankton and NFCRC Columbia.
- On June 11, 1991, three females produced fry for an otolith daily growth study by Dr. Dean Hendrickson (Texas Memorial Museum). Growth was greatly reduced at 60°F, but similar at 70°F and 80°F.
- Dexter maintained 466 domestic F1 1981 year class broodfish. Eight wild-origin fish were lost in the March 1992 accident.

### Woundfin

- Woundfin is a scaleless cyprinid, first described by Cope in 1874. The population has fluctuated greatly. It is the only one of 16 species at Dexter that is chronically affected by [[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]] ("Ich").
- The species is also infected with the Asian tapeworm *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*, introduced with grass carp and likely spread with red shiners.
- Malachite Green/Formalin baths had been used for 14 days. The FDA banned Malachite Green and most formalin uses in fall 1991, so non-chemical alternatives were needed.
- Heat treatment trials were conducted. Fish were acclimated at 23°C and temperatures raised to at least 31°C daily over five days. In run one, three of one tank's fish died at a peak of 34°C. In run two (100 fish), 10 died. In run three, no fish died (overall maximum 32°C).
- Across runs, 13 fish died. Their mean maximum temperature was 34°C, versus 31.5°C for survivors (t = -6.577, p < 0.01).
- No treated or control fish contracted Ich, so results were inconclusive. Continuous flushing may have reduced tomite contact with hosts.
- The treatment also caused tapeworms to be expelled; 13 adult tapeworms were removed.
- Woundfin spawned even under treatment (257 fry from treated fish). Only one fry was observed in the untreated raceway, where temperature never exceeded 29°C. The spawning season was at least 15 weeks, the longest on record.
- Fish sent to Dr. Steven Vives of Georgia Southern University (October 7): 71 adults, 92 juveniles and 173 young-of-year on hand.

### Chihuahua Chub

- 19 females were spawned May 8, 1991 to produce young for reintroduction in southwestern New Mexico. A total of 129,152 eggs was produced, and swimup fry were stocked in May 16, 1991.
- Reintroduction was delayed by paperwork from the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish until late October 1992 (FY 93).
- The captive population originated from ten fish 13 years earlier. Dexter recommended adding wild fish for genetic diversity.

### Pahranagat Roundtail Chub

- Held for Region 1, this species has a highly restricted habitat in Pahranagat Valley, Nevada.
- Of 30 injected females, only 10 ovulated (39,835 ova). On May 16, 2,529 swimup fry were stocked.
- A leak in the pond went unnoticed. Only 74 fish (52 adults, 12 young-of-year) were recovered.

### Virgin River Roundtail Chub and Guzman Beautiful Shiner

- Pond spawning produced 50,000 or more recruits. A total of 116 adults were moved to a new pond.
- Guzman Beautiful Shiner remained positive for Asian tapeworm, despite praziquantel treatments in 1988 to 1990. Trials of alcohol-dissolved drug are planned for FY 93.

### Yaqui Catfish

- Electrophoretic genetic study by Eastern New Mexico University found 33 hybrids, 11 channel catfish backcrosses and 178 apparently pure Yaqui catfish. An independent lab confirmed the results.
- 31 pure Yaqui catfish were transferred to the University of Monterrey, meeting a Mexican permit requiring return of ten percent of fish.

### Other Native Fishes

- **Lost River Sucker, Shortnose Sucker, Warner Sucker:** Collected in 1991 by Service, Nature Conservancy and BLM personnel. Anchor worm (*Lernaea* sp.) infestation was controlled with chemicals, causing toxicity and deaths, especially in shortnose suckers. Dexter maintained 25 shortnose, 16 Lost River and 55 Warner suckers at the end of FY 92.
- **Desert Pupfish and Leon Springs Pupfish:** Both maintained in good ponds with thousands of individuals. 50 desert pupfish went to Hogle Zoo, Salt Lake City; no Leon Springs pupfish were distributed.
- **Gila Topminnow:** Over 6,000 were held for reintroduction.
- **Big Bend Gambusia:** About 5,000 were moved to a new winter pond, and about 250 were overwintered indoors. 50 went to Dr. Clark Hubbs (University of Texas) and 50 to Staten Island Zoo.

### Fish Health and Cooperation

- Virgin River chub tested positive for tapeworm; treatment was planned for winter or early spring.
- Yaqui catfish, Lost River and Shortnose suckers carried yellow grub at low incidence.
- Anchor worm was a recurring problem from new fish. The report recommends reviewing all new introductions for health risks before approval.
- Cooperators included state agencies in Arizona, California, Colorado, New Mexico, Nevada and Oregon, several NFH and NFCRC facilities, universities, and Mexican institutions in Chihuahua, Sonora and Nuevo León.
- Staff participated in the FY 92 National Fishing Week events and presentations. Buddy Jensen spent three weeks in India as a consultant to the Indian Council of Agricultural Research.

### Facilities and Future Outlook

- Cyclical maintenance expenditures for FY 92 were itemized, including holding house and pond projects (about $73,482) and a new well ($25,700).
- Congress appropriated $1.428 million for a new office complex, wet lab, genetics lab and water quality lab in FY 93.
- Future priorities include a "Genetic Plan" for all hatchery operations, based on a report by Dr. Tony Echelle, and expanded staffing to match new facilities.
