# Critical Habitat Proposed for Loach Minnow and Spikedace

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #conservation #ESA

## Executive Summary
In December 1999, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) proposed a substantially expanded critical habitat designation for two federally threatened small-bodied freshwater fishes—the [[Loach Minnow]] and the [[Spikedace]]—native to the [[Gila River Basin]] of southeastern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico. The revised proposal increased protected river/stream mileage from an original 190 miles (1994 proposal) to approximately 890 miles, an addition of roughly 700 miles, including the [[San Pedro River]] as a candidate reintroduction site despite the species' current absence there. The action followed a September 1999 U.S. District Court order compelling USFWS to identify and designate all streams necessary for the species' survival and recovery, after the original 1994 designation was invalidated by the 9th Circuit Court of Appeals in February 1996 for failure to conduct a required environmental assessment under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]].

## Background & Description
- Both species are described as small-bodied cyprinid-type fishes, approximately **3 inches (~7.6 cm)** in length.
- No further morphological detail (fin counts, coloration, scale patterns) is provided in this source; the article is a policy/regulatory news report rather than a taxonomic or morphometric study.
- Cross-reference: [[Loach Minnow]] is a benthic riffle-dwelling species; [[Spikedace]] is adapted to swift, open-water riffle habitats — details consistent with reliance on flowing, well-oxygenated stream reaches (inferred from habitat associations discussed below, not explicitly detailed in source).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range:** [[Gila River Basin]], spanning southeastern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico.
- **Habitat requirements:** Rivers and streams with intact riparian corridors, particularly cottonwood- and willow-lined riparian areas, implying reliance on shaded, structurally complex stream margins and stable flow regimes.
- **Proposed critical habitat rivers:** [[Gila River]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Blue River]], [[Black River]], [[Verde River]], and [[San Pedro River]], plus associated tributaries.
- **Sympatric protected species:** Portions of the proposed habitat overlap with existing critical habitat for the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], the [[Razorback Sucker]], and the [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy Owl]], indicating a multi-taxon riparian/aquatic conservation corridor.
- **Threats identified:**
  - Habitat destruction (implicated activities: mining using river water, groundwater pumping near streams, cattle grazing along riparian zones).
  - Introduction of non-native sport fish, which act as predators on loach minnow and spikedace — described in the source as possibly the "biggest threat," according to a cattle industry representative (B. "Doc" Lane, Arizona Cattle Growers Association), who downplayed livestock impacts relative to piscivorous non-native fish.
- **Reintroduction potential:** The [[San Pedro River]] is highlighted by USFWS officials as potentially the best remaining river in the Southwest for reintroducing these species, despite their current absence from it.

## Key Findings
- USFWS proposed designating **894 miles** of rivers and streams in Arizona and New Mexico as critical habitat for the [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]], up from **190 miles** in the original 1994 proposal — an increase of roughly 700 miles.
- The proposal was mandated by a **September 1999 U.S. District Court order** requiring USFWS to identify, designate, and protect all streams necessary for the species' survival and recovery.
- The original 1994 critical habitat designation was **invalidated in February 1996** by the 9th Circuit Court of Appeals following a lawsuit by New Mexico counties, on grounds that USFWS failed to conduct an environmental assessment under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]].
- Critical habitat designation does not create a wildlife preserve but requires federal agencies to consider habitat needs of listed species when authorizing activities such as mining, grazing, groundwater pumping, and urban development within designated reaches (per USFWS spokesman Jeff Humphrey).
- Stakeholder reactions were mixed: conservation groups (e.g., [[Center for Biological Diversity]]) supported the expanded designation as necessary for species recovery, while local government and agricultural representatives (e.g., Sierra Vista City Councilman Arthur "Casey" Jones; Arizona Cattle Growers Association) expressed concern over the scientific basis for the fivefold mileage increase and potential economic/land-use restrictions.
- Public hearings on the proposal were scheduled in Silver City, NM, Thatcher, AZ, and Camp Verde, AZ.
