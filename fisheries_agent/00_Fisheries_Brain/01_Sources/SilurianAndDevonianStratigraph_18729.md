# Silurian and Devonian Stratigraphy of Southeastern Arizona and Southwestern New Mexico

#paleontology #stratigraphy #devonian

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by [[Willard D. Pye]] of the University of Arizona, reviews the Silurian and Devonian rock record of southeastern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico, with comparisons to northern Sonora and central Arizona. It is a geological and stratigraphic document, not an ichthyological one: it contains no fish records, and its biological content is limited to fossil-bearing marine carbonate and shale units. Its main conclusions are that no Silurian rocks are known in southeastern Arizona, that Silurian beds occur in southwestern New Mexico and thin westward, and that Devonian strata divide into a western carbonate facies and an eastern fine-clastic facies. The paper also documents the confused nomenclature of Devonian units and proposes simplifying it.

The text is the stratigraphy section of a field-trip guidebook and is followed by an extensive reference list (pp. 282–290) of Arizona, New Mexico and Sonora geology theses and reports.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses the Paleozoic marine succession that underlies much of southeastern Arizona and adjacent New Mexico. The author states that he relied heavily on an unpublished manuscript by D. V. LeMone for the Devonian material. The chapter is organized by system (Silurian, then Devonian) and then by region and named unit.

Key stratigraphic and nomenclatural points include:

- **Silurian:** No rocks of proved Silurian age are known in southeastern Arizona. In southwestern New Mexico, the Silurian is represented by the [[Fusselman Dolomite]] (originally the Fusselman limestone), which is Niagaran and Alexandrian in age. [[Kelley and Silver]] (1952) and Pray (1953) restricted the name to Silurian rocks and separated the lower, Upper Ordovician-bearing part as the [[Cutter Formation]].
- **Devonian distribution:** Devonian beds occur throughout Arizona, southwestern New Mexico and northern Sonora. Basal beds are generally Upper Devonian in southeastern Arizona, Middle Devonian in southwestern New Mexico and central Arizona, and Lower Devonian in northwestern Arizona.
- **Facies:** The Devonian divides into a western carbonate facies and an eastern fine clastic facies, with the boundary a short distance west of the Arizona–New Mexico state line.
- **Nomenclature:** Table 4 lists a large number of Devonian unit names, many now abandoned or recognized only as local faunal zones or facies. The author favors "Martin formation" over "Martin limestone" because the unit ranges from calcareous to calcarenaceous.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The sequence records marine depositional settings. The document does not describe living aquatic communities, but it characterizes the sediments and fossils that record ancient seas.

- **Depositional facies:** The western [[Martin Formation]] is a carbonate unit that becomes sandier and more dolomitic from southeastern to central Arizona. The eastern [[Percha Formation]] is predominantly shale, with minor limestone, calcarenite and siltstone. The [[Swisshelm Formation]] is a sandy transitional facies between them.
- **Environmental indicators:** The Devonian limestones are fine- to medium-grained, dark-gray to buff, and dolomitic or silty, with crinoid columnals small or absent. The overlying Mississippian limestones are coarsely crystalline and light gray to white, with abundant, large crinoid columnals. Chert zones in the dolomitic facies serve as correlation markers. Locally, sand grains are well rounded and frosted, and sandstone and calcarenite beds occur.
- **Fauna:** The paper reports Upper Devonian fossils as common in both facies of the Martin formation. The reference list also cites brachiopods of the Percha shale ([[Stainbrook]], 1947), a clymenoid ammonoid from New Mexico ([[Miller and Collinson]], 1951), and Cambrian trilobites from northwestern Sonora, but these are cited rather than analysed in the text.
- **Hiatuses and contacts:** Disconformities separate the Devonian from older rocks. The hiatus decreases eastward from central Arizona toward southwestern New Mexico. The Devonian–Mississippian contact is generally conformable and often covered by debris from Mississippian cliffs, with no evidence of a disconformity in many areas.
- **Topography:** The Martin formation forms inconspicuous slopes, whereas the Swisshelm formation forms a brown to yellowish slope set against cliffs of the overlying [[Escabrosa Limestone]].

## Key Findings

1. **No Silurian in southeastern Arizona.** Silurian rocks are absent there, but present in southwestern New Mexico as the Fusselman Dolomite, which thins westward and northward from Luna and Sierra Counties and probably wedges out before the state line.
2. **Silurian–Devonian relationship.** Devonian beds overlie the Silurian conformably despite a hiatus of some magnitude. No significant topographic relief developed on the Fusselman before Devonian deposition.
3. **Devonian ages vary by region.** Upper Devonian is the basal age in southeastern Arizona, Middle Devonian occurs in southwestern New Mexico and central Arizona, and Lower Devonian is found in northwestern Arizona.
4. **Martin Formation at the type locality.** At Mount Martin near Bisbee, the Martin is about 340 feet of calcareous beds, with the lower half shalier than the upper. Its calcareous facies extends south to the Devonian limestone at Cananea, Sonora.
5. **Dolomitic facies in the Dragoon Mountains.** The dolomitic facies typically contains 20 to 30 units of thin-bedded dolomitic limestone and dolomite.
6. **Abandoned or local names.** The Martin subdivisions Picacho de Calera (73 ft), the restricted Martin, and the Lower Ouray (150 ft) at Tucson are now considered faunal zones rather than mappable formations. Central Arizona names such as the Crook formation, Island Mesa beds and Jerome formation are likewise considered local faunal or facies zones. Huddle and Dobrovolny (1950) did not recognize them as formal subdivisions of the Martin.
7. **Swisshelm Formation.** Its type locality in the Swisshelm and Pedregosa Mountains is 615 feet thick. It is Upper Devonian (Senecan) in age, with a lower siltstone and sandstone half and an upper impure carbonate part.
8. **Portal Formation.** Sabins (1937) named 342 feet of calcareous and siliceous black shale with nodular limestone near Portal. Its lower Upper Devonian (Senecan or Chemung) age and lithology match the Percha. The author suggests it be merged into the Percha, with "Portal" retained for faunal or facies use.
9. **Morenci Shale.** It is 175 feet of Upper Devonian rock in the Clifton–Morenci area: about 100 feet of clay shale over about 75 feet of fine-grained argillaceous limestone (the Morenci limestone, which may be locally absent). It correlates with the Portal and Percha.
10. **Percha Shale in New Mexico.** Gordon (1907) named the Percha shale, about 200 feet at Percha Creek, Sierra County, with no type locality. Kelley and Silver (1952) proposed "Percha formation" because of its lithologic variation. Stevenson (1945) divided it into the upper Box Canyon member (about 50 feet of yellowish-green shale with lime nodules) and the lower Ready Pay member (about 140 feet of black, fissile, carbonaceous shale).
11. **Age disputes within the Percha.** The Box Canyon has been placed in latest Devonian age, while Ellison (1950) assigned the Ready Pay an Upper Devonian (?) and Kinderhookian age, and Stainbrook (1947) considered the upper Percha Mississippian.
12. **Eastern New Mexico subdivision.** In the mountains north of El Paso, the Percha was subdivided into the Onate (Middle Devonian), Sly Gap (Upper Devonian, or Middle Cassadogan) and Contadero (Upper Devonian, Chemung) formations. The Sly Gap and possibly the upper Onate correlate with the Ready Pay.
13. **Obsolete terms.** The Chloride formation, Perchian series and Bella shale are no longer used for southwestern New Mexico Devonian strata.
14. **Cananea, Sonora.** The Lower Crystalline limestone is 200 to 250 feet thick, rests disconformably on older limestone, and is equivalent to the Martin's calcareous facies.
15. **Caborca, Sonora.** A lower Upper Devonian sequence of more than 900 feet of limestone and dolomite, with neither base nor top exposed, may equal the Martin but may include younger and older beds.
16. **Central Arizona.** The Martin becomes sandier and more dolomitic eastward to westward, thins over Cambrian and Precambrian rocks, and locally lacks its lower part through nondeposition. Basal Devonian deposits fill channels. The Devonian there is Middle to Upper and may be entirely absent locally.
17. **Stratigraphic regularity.** Devonian strata are conformably overlain everywhere by the Lower Mississippian [[Escabrosa Limestone]] or [[Lake Valley Limestone]], both Kinderhookian in their lower parts.

The paper's central recommendation is to simplify the terminology: the Martin, Percha and Portal (retained as a facies name) should be the principal units, while many local names are treated as faunal zones. Because the document is a stratigraphic review, the "findings" are interpretive and summarize existing literature rather than report new field data.
