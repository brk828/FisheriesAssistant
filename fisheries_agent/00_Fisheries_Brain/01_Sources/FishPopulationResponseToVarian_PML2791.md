# Fish Population Response to Variance in Stream Discharge: Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona State University master's thesis (Anthony Lawrence Velasco, December 1997) tests whether the spatial distribution of native fishes in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a desert stream in southeastern Arizona, tracks variance in stream discharge. It combines a decades-long fish dataset ([[Minckley Long-Term Dataset]]) with a short-term monthly survey (March 1991 to February 1993). The author frames natural disturbance as a process that maintains ecosystem integrity and argues that communities adapted to disturbance should show resilience. The central hypothesis, that spatial variance in fish populations follows variance in discharge, is supported by the long-term data, and the short-term data suggest that recruitment of some species tracks discharge-related disturbance.

## Background & Description

- **Management problem:** Fishes in highly variable "boom or bust" desert streams can disappear for years or decades and reappear, which makes theory hard to apply to conservation management. Many western native fishes are listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973, as amended).
- **Variance concepts:** Temporal variance is the difference in abundance over time; spatial variance is the differential distribution of abundance among habitats. The two often interact.
- **Hypotheses:** H0, the community shows no pattern of spatial variance; H1, patterns of spatial variance, if present, follow patterns of variance in discharge or discharge-related phenomena.
- **Study system:** Aravaipa Creek is in Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona. The long-term fish data were collected from 1964, and discharge was obtained from the [[USGS Gaging Station 09473000]] on the downstream (western) reach.
- **Native fish assemblage:** Table 1 summarizes habitat use and reproduction for the adult native fishes, including [[Longfin Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Desert Sucker]] and [[Gila Topminnow]]. Several are federally listed species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Disturbance regime:** Modal discharge is about 0.34 m³/s. Runoff accumulates quickly and major floods are common. Discharge exceeded 60 m³/s five times at 5 to 8 year intervals between 1964 and 1993. A major flood (>85 m³/s) occurred in March 1991 during the short-term study.
- **Geomorphic structure:** High-gradient, constrained canyon reaches carry high sediment loads during floods and keep large debris and bedrock exposed. Wider, lower-gradient floodplains in the eastern and western reaches deposit finer sediments. Winter runoff largely sets the habitat pattern for the year. Deep pools and armored riffles form where turbulence from large obstructions scours the channel.
- **Reproductive cues:** Reproduction appears linked to floods and fresh-scoured substrate. Autumn spawning by [[Loach Minnow]] followed a monsoonal spate by twelve days (Vives and Minckley, 1990), and spawning by [[Speckled Dace]] has been linked to flooding and fresh substrate.
- **Human influence:** The author argues that water use has produced a chronic drought cycle for aquatic systems in the American Southwest. Channelization and bank stabilization homogenize habitat, fragmentation reduces connectivity, and exotic species establishment is favored in homogenized environments.

## Key Findings

- **Long-term result:** Regression of smoothed CV for fish against CV of discharge gave highly significant correlations for several species. Spatial variance rose with discharge variance until a "threshold" was reached, after which it declined, possibly indicating habitat change or flood displacement.
- **Species contrasts:** [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] responded similarly, with nearly identical threshold values and slopes. The two catostomids and [[Spikedace]] showed strong positive slopes with little or no threshold response. [[Longfin Dace]] showed an early threshold, and [[Roundtail Chub]] showed a strong response that differed from other species, suggesting unmeasured variables are involved.
- **Short-term result:** Range of variance was greater than in the long-term data. The short-term data reflected more temporal than spatial heterogeneity, which may reflect annual cycles, life-history traits and localized disturbance.
- **Life-stage lags:** Spatial variance in all juvenile fishes peaked after a three-month lag, when discharge variance began to decline. A two-month lag approximates the time newly recruited fishes take to become catchable by seine.
- **Immediate responses:** Adults of several species became more uniformly distributed within a month of increased discharge variance, while [[Spikedace]], [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Desert Sucker]] adults appeared unaffected or showed possible positive responses.
- **Fine-scale limits:** Responses on a day-to-week scale were not evident in the short-term component. The author suggests finer subdivision of juvenile age or size classes would have helped.
- **Conservation implications:** The author recommends that the long-term dataset be combined with human-action records to detect change over time. Reducing channelization and bank stabilization would increase habitat heterogeneity and resilience. Current degradation rates may prevent recovery of species needed for healthy ecosystems.
- **Methods:** Spatial variance was estimated with the coefficient of variation (CV), with the unbiased small-sample estimator applied. Curves were smoothed with Distance Weighted Least Squares, and second-order polynomial regression was used. Analyses used Systat for Windows 5.05 and SigmaPlot 2.01. Sampling used seines (1.8 × 1.2 m and 4.6 × 1.2 m) over at least 200 linear meters per site, with two sites per reach sampled monthly.
