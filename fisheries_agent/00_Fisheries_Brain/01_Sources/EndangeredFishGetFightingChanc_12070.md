# Razorback Sucker Reintroduction to Lake Havasu

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

A newspaper report describes the release of 903 juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]]s, an endangered native fish, into the [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]] on [[Lake Havasu]], Arizona. The fish were raised at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico and trucked to the release site. The release is described as a "holding action against extinction." Several agencies collaborated on the four-month project, and the fish were tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] so they can be tracked. The article also notes that predation by introduced game fish threatens the survival of young razorbacks.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] once thrived in the lower [[Colorado River]], but the wild river habitat where it flourished has been lost. The species is now listed as endangered. According to [[Chuck Minckley]], a [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] fisheries biologist at the Parker Fish Resource Office, the reintroduction gives the species "a fighting chance."

Key description points from the article:

- **Four native fish species:** Before European-era changes, the river supported only four native fish: the razorback sucker, [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (called "squawfish" in the article). All other fish now present were introduced.
- **Distinctive keel:** These four species are reported as the only fish in the world with a keel, or hump, behind the head. The article says this acts like a hydrofoil against river current.
- **Size:** The released fish were juveniles about 12 to 15 inches long. Adults can reach about one meter, and the article says the species can live about 40 years.

The accompanying page also includes unrelated local items, including a charity donation article about illegal dumping at a thrift store, a police and fire log, and a tax-service advertisement. These are not part of the fish study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat loss:** The wild Colorado River habitat used by razorback suckers has been greatly altered, so the release relies on managed refuge habitat.
- **Predation:** Game fish introduced into the Colorado River and the man-made lakes prey on the young of the razorback sucker and the other three native species. The article reports that available information suggests any spawn from the released fish will also be eaten by predators.
- **Planned habitat work:** The [[Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Program]] is expected to include similar projects. These would raise endangered fish in grow-out coves until they are large enough to survive in the lake.
- **Generational timeline:** Minckley said the long lifespan of the fish means the effort could take decades, and he suggested future technology may be needed to help the next generation.

## Key Findings

- **Release:** 903 razorback suckers were released at the Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge on Lake Havasu, described as a "holding action against extinction."
- **Age discrepancy:** The article first describes the fish as "three-year-old," but later describes them as juveniles 12 to 15 inches long. The article does not reconcile these descriptions.
- **Origin:** The fish came from the Dexter National Fish Hatchery, about 18 miles south of Roswell, New Mexico.
- **Partners:** The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (refuge and habitat programs), Arizona Game and Fish, the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, and the Colorado River Indian Tribes collaborated on the effort.
- **Timeline:** The project took four months to organize.
- **Tracking:** All fish were tagged with PIT tags. Each tag is a microchip with an antenna, carries a 10-digit number, and lasts 10 years. Tags cost $5 each and were provided by the Bureau of Reclamation.
- **Main threat:** Predation by introduced game fish on juvenile razorback suckers and other native species, which the article says limits the expected success of natural spawning.
- **Conclusion:** Minckley argued that the effort is worthwhile, noting that people often value a visible animal such as a bird more than fish, and that the goal is to focus on positive outcomes despite the difficulty of monitoring underwater species.
