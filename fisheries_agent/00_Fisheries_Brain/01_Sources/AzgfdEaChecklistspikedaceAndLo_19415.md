# Spikedace and Loach Minnow In-Stream Study: Environmental Assessment and Refuge Stocking Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of 1987–1988 correspondence and project materials from [[Arizona State University]] (ASU) and the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (AZGFD). It contains a cover letter, an AZGFD [[Environmental Assessment Checklist]] with attachments, a refuge stocking memo, and three location maps. The core proposal is a 24-month [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] Study that includes in-stream enclosure experiments testing how non-native [[Red Shiner]] affect native stream minnows. The attachments explain why the project needs a federal and state permit review, where the work would occur, and how escape of listed fish would be prevented. The memo reports growth and survival of [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] stocked at [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]].

## Background & Description

**Cover letter (2 February 1988).** Paul C. Marsh of ASU sent the completed EA Checklist to Dean A. Hendrickson of the AZGFD Nongame Branch. The letter stresses that the work must start in March to stay on schedule. It also states that contact with the [[Tonto National Forest]] (U.S. Forest Service) had presumably begun and that a memorandum of understanding was forthcoming.

**Project origin.** AZGFD, in cooperation with New Mexico, contracted with ASU in January 1987 for a 24-month study (AG G70001). The study had two elements:
1. Microhabitat data for spikedace, loach minnow, and red shiner in Arizona and New Mexico waters. Interim reports 1–4 are on file, and the document describes this element as successful.
2. Experimental in-stream studies of interactions among these species. These studies are described as essential to understanding why native stream minnows have declined or been extirpated.

**Experimental design.** The work would use up to 10 temporary enclosures, built with fence posts and inert screening. The document gives the enclosure size as 2 x 5 m in one version and 2 x 3 m in the other (see Key Findings). Stream length needed would not exceed 0.5 km. Fish (spikedace and/or red shiner) would be placed by a predesigned scheme and observed for 10 to 14 days. Fish would then be sacrificed or kept for further trials, with variables such as fish number, fish size, or added habitat structure changed between trials. Fish would not be released to the environment.

**Study site criteria.** An acceptable site had to:
- lie within the historic range of spikedace;
- be currently occupied by red shiner;
- be easily accessible, preferably near the ASU research center;
- have typical spikedace habitat;
- avoid prolonged springtime snowmelt flows; and
- have a recent record of biota, habitat, and hydrology data.

**Candidate sites (in order of preference):**
1. [[Sycamore Creek]], Maricopa County, Arizona. This site is on Tonto National Forest land, about 40 miles (1 hour) northeast of Phoenix off State Highway 87, near the Dos S Ranch. It was favored for its proximity to ASU and an excellent existing data base. Experimental fish would come from [[Aravaipa Creek]] (spikedace) and local sources (red shiner).
2. [[Upper Verde River]], Yavapai County, Arizona. This site is on Prescott National Forest land, about 125 miles (3 hours) north-northwest of Phoenix. It was rated suitable but less desirable because it is distant from ASU, may be affected by snowmelt runoff, and has few background data. Obtaining enough fish of both species could be difficult at times.
3. [[Gila River]] vicinity of the "lower" box, New Mexico. This was the least desirable option because it is far from ASU, is subject to snowmelt runoff, and has few background data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Conservation status.** Spikedace is federally listed as threatened and listed by Arizona as Group 3. Because of this, the checklist items on listed species, state-listed species, and mortality of fish all apply.

**Historic and current distribution.** Sycamore Creek lies within spikedace's historic range and probably supported the species in the past, but spikedace no longer occur there. The species still occurs in the mainstream Verde River upstream from Clarkdale, Arizona.

**Donor population.** The document states that Aravaipa Creek supports a "substantial, secure" spikedace population. Removing up to 500 fish from it was judged unlikely to have any discernible local effect.

**Escape precautions.** The document lists four safeguards against the release of fish into Sycamore Creek:
1. Continuous inspection of enclosures to confirm that fish are secure.
2. Up- and downstream blocking of the study area with nets.
3. Location of the study area in a reach separated from the Verde River by dry channel. The two streams connect only after severe flooding.
4. Complete removal of experimental animals if local weather suggests severe flooding is likely.

The document judges the environmental consequence of any accidental loss to be "nil."

**Stocking program at Buenos Aires NWR.** Refuge tanks and Mormon Lake hold stocked native fishes. Stocked fish were in good health, with no obvious parasites or disease. The sampling team recommended distributing Razorback Sucker from the November 1986 Mormon Lake cohort to wild reintroduction sites that autumn.

## Key Findings

**Permits and compliance**
- Principal investigators (Minckley and Marsh) hold joint federal permit FRT 676811 (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Region 2, Albuquerque, NM).
- They also hold Arizona Wildlife Holding Permit No. 0018 (Marsh) and Arizona Management Research Permit Nos. 00032 (Minckley) and 00045 (Marsh). These permits allow collection, transport, holding, and killing of spikedace.
- The Forest Service memorandum of understanding was still pending at the time of the letter.

**Project scope**
- Up to 10 enclosures, with stream use not exceeding 0.5 km.
- Observation periods of 10 to 14 days per trial.
- Fish sacrificed after trials, not released.
- Site selection favored Sycamore Creek, followed by Upper Verde River and the Gila River.

**Timeline discrepancy.** The two attachments give different dates. Attachment 1 states work would begin "not later than March 1986" and finish in "summer 1998." Attachment 2 states March 1988 and summer 1988. The 1986 and 1998 dates are probably typographical errors, and the document does not resolve this. The enclosure size also differs (2 x 5 m versus 2 x 3 m).

**Buenos Aires NWR stocking results (sampled 20–21 October 1987)**
- **Road Camp Tank:** 2,980 juvenile bonytail (mean 7.6 cm TL) stocked 4 November 1986. Twenty-two fish were recovered, averaging 18.8 cm TL (range 15.0–28.5 cm) and 47 g.
- **Razorback Sucker fry** (about 10 mm TL) stocked 31 March 1987 at three locations:
  - **Rock Tank:** the recovered count is illegible in the scan. The fish averaged 14.1 cm TL (range 11.1–19.2 cm) and 25 g.
  - **Tequila Tank:** 40 fish, 8.4–22.7 cm TL (mean 11.7 cm), averaging 16 g.
  - **Mormon Lake:** 71 fish, 17.8–22.3 cm TL (mean 19.5 cm), averaging 85 g.
- None of the fish stocked into Mormon Lake in November 1986 were recovered there in October. Several of that cohort were taken there in September 1987.
- The authors attribute differences in fish size among tanks most likely to sampling gear. They also mention density, habitat size, and food availability as possible factors, but these were not investigated.
- Overall growth of both species was described as good, with consistent gains in length and weight.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The in-stream experiments are considered essential for understanding how non-native red shiner interact with native minnows.
- Sycamore Creek is the preferred site, with escape safeguards in place.
- Refuge-reared Razorback Sucker are large enough for wild reintroduction, and distribution was recommended for autumn of 1987.
