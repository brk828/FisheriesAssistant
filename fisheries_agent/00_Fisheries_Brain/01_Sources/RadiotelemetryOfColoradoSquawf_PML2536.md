# Radiotelemetry of Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker, Green River System of Utah, 1980

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cypriniformes

## Executive Summary

This 1980 field report from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Vernal, Utah) describes the first season of a [[Radiotelemetry]] program tracking endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in the Green River, Utah. Six Colorado squawfish and one razorback sucker were captured by electrofishing, surgically fitted with radio transmitters, and released. Contact lasted from 14 days to about 4½ months. The study identified two distinct movement patterns in Colorado squawfish, which may be linked to sexual maturity, and found the razorback sucker to be relatively sedentary. The authors present the paper as a methods and preliminary movement report, not final results, and note that an expanded investigation was planned for 1981.

## Background & Description

**Study species**

- **Colorado squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): the primary focus. The study aimed to examine major movements, habitat preference, and spawning.
- **Razorback sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): a secondary observation subject, tracked for one fish.

The work was integrated into the ongoing [[Colorado River Fisheries Investigations]] (Miller et al. 1980). The document is a report and does not describe family-level taxonomy.

**Capture and tagging**

- Six Colorado squawfish (total length 508–707 mm) and one razorback sucker (total length 510 mm) were captured by [[Electrofishing]] near Ouray, Utah.
- Fish were anesthetized with MS-222 and surgically implanted with transmitters immediately after capture.
- The procedure followed [[Bidgood Surgical Procedure]] (1980) with modifications: individual sutures (usually five) instead of a continuous suture, and a lateral incision placed anterior and slightly dorsal to the insertion of the right pelvic fin. Suture knots were coated with a surgical adhesive.
- Transmitters were [[AVM Radio Transmitters]] encapsulated in melted beeswax, with a "paddlock" shape and a theoretical battery life of about 215 days. They operated in the 40.660–40.700 MHz range.

**Tracking equipment**

- Receivers: [[Smith-Root Receivers]] (RF-40 model, which identifies a particular frequency, and SR-40, a search-type receiver that picks up all frequencies in range).
- Antennas: omni-directional and pin-pointing antennas. A Larson-Kulrod whip antenna was used for implanted fish, mounted on a 20 cm loop about 4 m above the water, either on a boat or on an airplane wing strut. A loop antenna was used only to triangulate fish location, by boat or from shore.
- Aerial tracking: a Piper Supercub aircraft flew at about 150 m altitude and 115 kph to maintain contact with several fish at once.
- Water conductivity (µmho/cm) and temperature (°C) were measured with a Yellow Springs Model 33 meter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Movement patterns**

Colorado squawfish showed two patterns:

- *Movement group*: Fish No. 2 and No. 3 moved upstream in the Green River, reaching their upstream limit in late June to early July after travelling about 80 km. They then returned downstream past the release point. Fish No. 2 was last located in [[Labyrinth Canyon]], more than 300 km downstream. Fish No. 3 ascended the [[White River]] to 56 km above its mouth before returning to the Green–White confluence.
- *Stationary group*: Fish No. 4 stayed within 5 km of its release point. Fish No. 5 did not travel more than 16 km from release.

**Razorback sucker**

Fish No. 7 moved about 5 km downstream into the [[Duchesne River]] and stayed less than 1 km inside it until flooding in early June carried it into the Green River at the mixing zone of the two rivers. It then moved slowly upstream about 11 km. Contact was lost on August 13, and recapture attempts failed.

**Water chemistry**

- Conductivity in the Green River was lowest in June (range 175–291 µmho/cm) and highest in September (mean 809 µmho/cm, range 610–950 µmho/cm). See [[Water Conductivity]].
- The Duchesne River measured 1950 µmho/cm on April 18.
- High conductivity, combined with depths over 2 m, reduced the ability to locate fish. Because the study reach had few deep areas, the shallow channel partly offset this problem.
- The authors also noted that the failure of one fish's transmitter to work could explain some lost contact, specifically the largest Colorado squawfish (Fish No. 4 in the text, a point the authors left as a belief rather than a confirmed fault).

**Spawning season context**

The authors connected the movement patterns to [[Spawning Migration]] and maturity. The study was designed to examine habitat preference and spawning, and the tracking data offered early clues about these behaviors.

## Key Findings

1. **Movement is linked to maturity.** Colorado squawfish displayed two movement types. The recaptured mature female (Fish No. 3) moved considerably during the expected spawning season, while the immature male (Fish No. 4) showed little movement. The authors describe the significance of Fish No. 3 orienting to riffle areas in the Green River (July 2) and the White River (July 16) as unknown. Fish No. 3 moved about 161 km in 5 days.

2. **Long-distance travel.** Movement group fish covered distances up to 300+ km, reaching Labyrinth Canyon. Stationary fish stayed within 5–16 km.

3. **Surgical implant effects.** Dissection of two recaptured Colorado squawfish (Fish No. 4 on August 8 and Fish No. 3 on September 2) showed no obvious adverse effects. The module was walled off by adhesions and had to be freed for inspection, with no irritation seen in internal organs. The authors credit the small module size and smooth surface.

4. **Razorback sucker behavior.** This fish was relatively sedentary, linked to flood-driven movement and a slow upstream drift.

5. **Radio range and conductivity.** High conductivity and deep water (over 2 m) limited tracking. The shallow study reach compensated partly for this.

6. **Program status.** The study was a preliminary account of tracking success, with contact times ranging from 14 days to about 4½ months. The report explicitly does not claim final results. The authors planned an expanded 1981 investigation.
