# Some Evolutionary Trends in Plecoptera

#entomology #plecoptera #stoneflies

## Executive Summary

This 1950 paper by W. E. Ricker (Indiana University) reviews [[Plecoptera]] ([[Stoneflies]]) systematics and evolution. It proposes a family and subfamily classification, traces trends in nymphal mouthparts, [[Gills]], [[Cross-Veins]], wing anal areas, thoracic sternal ridges and the male [[Supra-Anal Process]], and offers a tentative phylogenetic tree (Figure 1). It also summarises zoogeographic patterns for the major groups. The paper is a literature-based synthesis, not a field study, and it gives no new sample counts or measurements.

## Background & Description

Ricker recognises two suborders:

- **Holognatha (Setipalpia):** nymphs have bulky mandibles, short thick palpi and labial paraglossae about equal in length to the glossae. Adult mandibles stay large and functional. Families include [[Eustheniidae]], [[Austroperlidae]], [[Leptoperlidae]], [[Peltoperlidae]], [[Nemouridae]] and [[Pteronarcidae]].
- **Systellognatha (Filipalpia):** nymphs have thin mandibles, slender palpi and paraglossae much longer than the glossae. Adult mandibles are usually reduced. Families include [[Perlodidae]], [[Chloroperlidae]] and [[Perlidae]].

Sixteen subfamilies are recognised, among them the new subfamilies Diamphipnoinae and Notonemourinae. The generic list is long and largely follows [[Claassen Catalogue]] (1940), with corrections. Ricker notes that many genera are poorly revised, especially in Perlidae, and that the list is "far from a definitive arrangement."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Morphological trends (as reported):**

- **Gills:** Lateral abdominal gills are primitive and persist in Eustheniidae, Protoperlaria and one isogenine (*Oroperla*). Gills have been lost or relocated in many lineages, for example to the 10th segment (Leptoperlidae), coxae (*Taeniopteryx*) and mentum (*Nemoura cataractae*). Chloroperlidae lack gills entirely. Perlidae have profusely branched lateral thoracic gills.
- **Cross-veins:** A conspicuous trend is toward reduction of wing cross-veins. Eustheniidae retain them almost throughout the wing, while many advanced groups keep only a few. Acroneuriinae often retain apical cross-veins; Perlinae usually lack them.
- **Anal area and body size:** Reduction of the hind-wing anal area is correlated with small body size. It is seen in Leuctrinae, Capniinae and Chloroperlinae.
- **Thoracic sternal "Y-ridge":** Its attachment point to the furcal pits (posterior or anterior) varies, and the shift appears to have occurred independently in *Arcynopteryx* and *Isogenus*.
- **Supra-anal process:** This is the most characteristic male genitalic structure. It is lost or reduced repeatedly across families. Ricker judges many accessory genital traits to have evolved independently and to be of limited value for tracing lineages.

**Zoogeography:**

- Eustheniidae, Leptoperlidae and Austroperlidae are ancient families now confined to the southern periphery of the Australasian region and southern South America, paralleling the marsupials. A wingless leptoperlid persists in Japan.
- Pteronarciidae are primarily North American and invaded eastern Siberia and Sakhalin in relatively recent times.
- Peltoperlidae are widely distributed in North America but species-poor. They occur in Japan, the China coast, India and Pacific islands.
- Holarctic families (Nemourinae, Capniinae, Taeniopteryginae, most Leuctrinae) extend south to the Himalayas in Asia and to Mexico City in America (*Nemoura venusta*).
- Perlidae divide into Perlinae (Old World tropics, temperate Eurasia, Africa, eastern North America) and Acroneuriinae (American tropics, temperate North America, eastern Asia).
- Three Oriental Perlinae genera invaded North America: *Neoperla* (1 species), *Neophasganophora* (1 or 2 species) and *Paragnetina* (5 species). None occurs west of the Great Plains, which argues against a very recent invasion.

## Key Findings

1. **Classification:** Two suborders and multiple families are proposed. Diamphipnoinae and Notonemourinae are erected as new, and Neoperlinae is merged into Perlinae.
2. **Ancestry:** The Permian Protoperlaria (Lemmatophoridae, per Tillyard) are the ancestral group. Lateral expansions of the prothorax and the posterior median wing vein were lost in the transition to modern stoneflies.
3. **Eustheniinae** are proposed as possible ancestors of all other living stoneflies. Leptoperlidae derive from them through simplified venation and loss of lateral gills.
4. **Nemouridae** may branch from a leptoperlid-like stem. The ventral lobe on the ninth sternite links Nemouridae with Peltoperlidae, which Ricker suggests may have diverged directly from Eustheniidae.
5. **Systellognatha** arise from a Pteronarciid-like stage (E) through a change from holognathous to systellognathous mouthparts, which implies a shift from herbivory to carnivory.
6. **Chloroperlidae** derive from Isogeninae. **Perlidae** diverge from the systellognathous ancestor shared with Perlodidae, and they split early into Perlinae and Acroneuriinae.
7. **Peltoperlidae** are described as the most puzzling group. Their cockroach-like body form is noted, and their double thoracic gills indicate an origin far back in stonefly history.
8. **Limits:** Ricker presents Figure 1 as a tentative, simplified phylogeny. He stresses that modern groups are not direct ancestors of one another, and that many genera need revision.
