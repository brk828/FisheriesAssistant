# Geographic Variation in Life History Traits of Gambusia Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1995 Desert Fishes Council contributed paper by [[Clark Hubbs]] (University of Texas at Austin) compares three life history traits across [[Gambusia]] populations: predation on newborn young, birth weight, and interbrood interval. The study uses 48 populations of [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*G. affinis*), 7 of [[Pecos Gambusia]] (*G. nobilis*), 10 of [[Big Bend Gambusia]]-relative *G. geiseri*, and one population each of six other species. The author concludes that variation among populations is genetic rather than environmental because the traits do not covary consistently across species or localities. Because intraspecific variation can reverse interspecific trends, the paper argues that both species and source population must be considered in research and in practical uses such as [[Mosquito Control]].

## Background & Description

- **Study taxa:** The genus *Gambusia* (Poeciliidae) includes live-bearing freshwater fishes. Six of the species studied (*G. nobilis*, *G. speciosa*, *G. longispinis*, *G. senilis*, *G. gaigei*, and *G.* sp.) are native to the Chihuahuan Desert; five are listed as endangered by the federal government, and *G.* sp. is extinct.
- **Comparison taxa:** *Poecilia latipinna* (sailfin molly) and *Poecilia formosa* (Amazon molly) were used as prey in predation tests.
- **Distribution:** *G. affinis* is widely distributed in south-central North America and is used by public health agencies as a [[Biological Control]] agent for mosquitoes. *G. nobilis* occurs in spring-fed waters of the Pecos valley in West Texas and eastern New Mexico. *G. geiseri* was native to [[Comal Springs]] and [[San Marcos Springs]] in Central Texas and was widely released around 1930.
- **Background literature:** The author notes that earlier authors widely reported adult *Gambusia* preying on their own young. Prior studies on intraspecific life history variation include [[Tinkle]] (1969) on lizards and [[Stearns]] (1983) and [[Stockwell]] (1995) on introduced *G. affinis* populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stocks and sampling:** Fish were collected from localities mainly in Arizona, New Mexico, Texas, and Arkansas, and held in the Austin laboratory on Tetra Min and *Drosophila* larvae and adults.
- **Predation method:** Three to twenty newborn young were isolated with one adult in aquaria with airstones and *Physa* snails, fed about 20% of total fish biomass daily, and counted after 31 days. Survival percentages were reported; predation is the inverse.
- **Birth weight method:** Females were isolated in floating breeding chambers checked daily. Young were dried at 40°C for at least two days and weighed. Within-brood variation was usually about 10%.
- **Interbrood interval method:** The interval was measured between birth dates of successive broods. A new brood was defined as occurring after more than three weeks. Intervals exceeding ten were trimmed at the extremes.
- **Environmental variables:** The author lists salinity and thermal regime (stenothermal or eurythermal) for some sites but finds no consistent association with predation, birth weight, or interbrood interval.
- **Introduced populations:** Introduced stocks in Nevada, Hawaii, and Texas provide natural experiments in which the author compares differences in life history evolution over roughly 50 to 70 years.

## Key Findings

1. **Predation by adults on young is mainly by females.** Young with male predators survived at 70% versus 30% with female predators. Of more than 800 individual contrasts, two were significantly in the opposite direction; 394 favored females as the more predaceous sex.
2. **Predation varies among *G. affinis* populations.** With female predators, seven populations (including Bitter #3, Falcon, El Tigre, Fairy, Junction, Middle Creek, and Uncertain) had survival below 10%, while Lost River and Big Bend had survival above two-thirds. Survival differed by 43% between Bitter Lakes populations (chi-square = 81).
3. **Predation by *G. geiseri* is less variable.** Female *G. geiseri* produced 7 to 28% survival of *G. affinis* young, except for East Sandia Spring, which had roughly twice the next highest survival.
4. **Predation by *G. nobilis* females ranged from 0 to 14% survival** for the five samples with more than 100 young, similar in range to *G. geiseri*.
5. **Birth weight varies widely in *G. affinis*.** Averages ranged from under 10 mg to over 20 mg, with Lost River (8.0 mg) and Bitter Lake #3 (6.0 mg) among the lightest. *G. geiseri* averaged 17.1 to 23.3 mg.
6. **Mean birth weights by species:** about 16 mg (*G. affinis*, *G. speciosa*), 20 mg (*G. geiseri*), 25 mg (*G. longispinis*), 30 mg (*Poecilia*, *G. gaigei*, *G. heterochir*), and 40 mg (*G. nobilis*).
7. **Interbrood intervals range from 27 to 41 days in *G. affinis*** and are about 40 days in *G. geiseri*. Mean species values were about 35 days (*G. affinis*), 40 days (*G. geiseri*), 44 days (*G. gaigei*), 47 days (*G. heterochir*), and 52 days (*G. nobilis*).
8. **Species-level trends reverse at the population level.** *G. nobilis* is the most predaceous species, with the heaviest young and longest intervals. Yet selected *G. affinis* populations can reverse these trends when they are used to represent the species.
9. **Predation on *Poecilia* young was highest of all.** Survival of *Poecilia* young exposed to *G. affinis* predators was the lowest among tests with more than 100 young, despite these young being relatively large.
10. **Conclusions:** Traits are not concordant among species or localities, which the author interprets as genetic variation among populations within a morphologically recognized species. The author recommends that research and practical uses, including mosquito control and hatchery stocking, consider the source population as well as the species. Future mosquito control trials are mandated.
