# Threadfin Shad Impact on Macrocrustacean Zooplankton in Two Arizona Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), stocked to feed [[Piscivores]], affected macrocrustacean zooplankton in two desert impoundments in Arizona: [[Alamo Lake]] and [[Patagonia Lake]]. Zooplankton data from 1974 to 1984 show that large shad populations were followed by sharp declines in zooplankton and, in both lakes, near-total loss of open-water zooplankton communities for extended periods. The authors conclude that shad caused these declines and that the long-term negative effects of stocking planktivores outweigh the short-term benefits to predatory sport fish.

## Background & Description

**Introduction and rationale.** Stocking threadfin shad as forage for piscivorous sport fish, such as [[Largemouth Bass]], has been a common management practice. Most reports supported its benefits, but a growing body of evidence showed that shad can harm other fish by competing for zooplankton. Zooplankton are a key food base for larval and juvenile fish and an important diet item for many adult fish. The authors note that earlier work had documented [[Planktivorous Fish]] shifting zooplankton species composition, but their field data showed the virtual elimination of open-water zooplankton communities.

**Study lakes.**
- **Alamo Lake:** A desert flood-control impoundment in west-central Arizona, in the Bill Williams River drainage, at 357 m elevation. Surface area during the study was about 1,500–1,700 ha. Water level fluctuates with releases and rainfall. The lake is monomictic, turning over in mid-November. Thermal stratification runs from May through October, with the thermocline at 4–5 m. The hypolimnion lacks dissolved oxygen from June through October at depths greater than 7–10 m.
- **Patagonia Lake:** A desert highland impoundment about 19 km northeast of Nogales, Arizona, at 1,220 m elevation. Its 107 ha area was developed for fishing and recreation. Water level is relatively stable, supported by inflow from Sonoita Creek. It is also monomictic, turning over in late October. Stratification is strong from April to September, with only the upper 4–5 m holding oxygen levels adequate for aquatic life.

**Methods.** Threadfin shad were introduced into Patagonia Lake in May 1979; the date of introduction into Alamo Lake is unknown. Shad distribution and abundance were assessed by transect surveys with a graphic echo-sounder, electrofishing, and visual observation. Zooplankton were sampled with a standard net (mesh 0.076 mm) by 5-minute oblique tows from 5–6 m toward the surface, avoiding oxygen-deficient water. Organisms were identified with standard keys. Abundance was expressed in ml/m³ for Alamo Lake and as numbers/m³ for Patagonia Lake.

**Sampling periods.**
- Alamo Lake: about monthly, March 1978 to March 1982.
- Patagonia Lake: July–August 1974; about monthly until July 1975; and March 1981 to December 1984.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Shad were first seen in Alamo Lake in 1979 and became abundant in open water and near shore, with dense schools recorded on sonar. The largest school was 450 m long and 225 m wide. In Patagonia Lake, shad spawned in spring and grew rapidly, becoming very abundant by 1980–1981 and remaining so when the study ended in December 1984.

Zooplankton were very abundant in Patagonia Lake in 1974–1975, ranging from 20,164 to 115,860/m³. Species included three [[Cladocera]] (*Daphnia galeata mendotae*, *Ceriodaphnia quadrangula*, *Diaphanosoma brachyurum*) and two [[Copepoda]] (*Diaptomus siciloides*, *Cyclops vernalis*). By 1981 the community had shifted to four cladocerans (including *Daphnia parvula* and *Bosmina longirostris*) and one copepod, and numbers declined sharply. By July 1981 most zooplankton were gone.

## Key Findings

- **Strong evidence of causation.** The close timing between the buildup of large shad populations in both lakes and subsequent zooplankton declines supports the conclusion that shad caused the decline.
- **Rapid recovery after a die-off.** In Alamo Lake, zooplankton recovered to almost 40% of pre-shad abundance immediately after a massive winter kill of shad.
- **Virtual elimination, not just reduction.** Shad did more than alter species composition or reduce numbers. Zooplankton communities were virtually eliminated for about 20 months in Alamo Lake and about 25 months in Patagonia Lake.
- **No sustained recovery.** Small zooplankton pulses disappeared quickly. Once populations were at low levels, reproduction could not keep pace with heavy predation by shad.
- **Patagonia Lake timeline.** Brief recovery occurred in June 1983 and lasted less than one month. Zooplankton returned briefly in March 1984, with a mean of 12,400/m³, but were nearly gone by April and remained scarce through December 1984.
- **Consistency with other studies.** Similar patterns were reported elsewhere. In Millerton Lake, California, [[Von Geldern and Mitchell]] (1975) found that zooplankton declined as shad increased, followed by a decline in zooplankton in the stomachs of young bass. [[Putnam]] (1983) found that both largemouth bass and [[Bluegill]] populations declined after [[Gizzard Shad]] were introduced into Iowa lakes. Ongoing work in Arizona reported that young largemouth bass from the 1984 year class were in poor condition, linked to the severe zooplankton decline.
- **Management conclusion.** Stocking planktivores for piscivores has produced some early success, but the authors argue that long-term negative effects typically outweigh short-term benefits. The study was a joint effort of the University of Arizona, the Arizona Game and Fish Department, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
