# Status of Spikedace in the Verde River, 1999: Implications for Management and Research

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The federally listed threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) was once widespread and locally abundant in the [[Gila River Basin]] but now occurs in four isolated systems in Arizona and New Mexico. Based on six years of study, the species has declined sharply in the [[Upper Verde River]], and it was not detected in samples from 1997 onward. Calculated extirpation probabilities suggest that there is an 8 to 9 in 10 chance the species is gone from the upper Verde. Two interacting factors, the [[River Hydrograph]] (especially the absence of flooding) and [[Nonnative Fishes]], are identified as the main drivers. Changes in [[Livestock Grazing]] along the river corridor complicate the analysis. The author recommends intensified monitoring, immediate suppression of nonnative fishes, identification of [[Refugia Streams]], development of [[Culture Techniques]], and a coordinated regional research program that compares the upper Verde with the [[Upper Gila River]].

## Background & Description

The spikedace is a slender, laterally compressed cyprinid. The dorsal surface is olive gray to brown and often mottled, the sides are silvery, and the ventral region is whitened. Breeding males turn golden on the dorsal and lateral surfaces. The species is diminutive and short-lived. Most life-history data come from [[Aravaipa Creek]] in Arizona and from the Gila-Cliff reach of the mainstream [[Upper Gila River]] in New Mexico. Much of the available information comes from distribution and abundance surveys rather than focused biological studies.

Spawning occurs from April to June and depends on streamflow and water temperature. Eggs are released into the water column, become adhesive after fertilization, and attach to substrates where spawning occurs. Reproductively ready fish have been collected over sand to gravel-pebble substrates.

Historically, the spikedace was probably widespread in low- to mid-elevation reaches of the San Pedro (the type locality), Salt, Verde, Agua Fria, San Francisco, and Gila rivers. Its current range is restricted to intermediate elevations (1070–1830 m) in the upper Gila River, Aravaipa Creek, [[Eagle Creek]], and the upper Verde River. The author notes that the historical record is too incomplete, in part because museum collections are gaps in quantified data, to define its former distribution precisely. Range and abundance fluctuate naturally in response to environmental and climatic conditions. A related rare cyprinid, the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], shows a similar pattern.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** The upper Verde River, upstream of Sycamore Creek, is free-flowing, has low baseflows (0.57 m³/sec), has only one perennial tributary (Granite Creek), and experiences periodic extreme floods. A major flood with a 75-year recurrence interval occurred in the winter of 1992–93. A smaller flood with a 10-year recurrence interval occurred in March 1995, and another pulse of flooding occurred in 1996. No significant flows exceeding bankfull occurred from 1996 to 1998.

**Nonnative fishes.** Introduced species include [[Red Shiner]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], and yellow bullhead. These species are thought to affect spikedace through competition and predation, which can displace or replace native fishes.

**Livestock grazing.** Grazing was reduced for a decade and fully removed from the Verde River corridor in spring 1997. Riparian vegetation diversity, density, and biomass then increased substantially. The author notes that no published studies document the effect of grazing on spikedace, so the link remains unconfirmed.

**Habitat preferences.** In larger rivers, spikedace are most common in riffles with moderate velocities (30–50 cm/sec) and gradients of 0.5–1.0%, over pebble-cobble and boulder substrates. Boulders create eddying currents, which appear to be preferred habitat. Shear zones where two riffles converge are also favored.

**Genetic isolation.** Upper Verde spikedace have been isolated from the Gila River and Aravaipa Creek populations since the Horseshoe and Bartlett dams were completed in the 1930s. Genetic analysis (Tibbets and Dowling 1996) indicates the upper Verde population is genetically distinct.

## Key Findings

**Population decline in the upper Verde River:**
- Spikedace were common in the mid-1980s, comprising up to 11% of the native fish fauna, according to U.S. Bureau of Reclamation sampling.
- Abundance in the seven-site monitoring series (*Table 1*) declined as follows: 428 individuals in 1994, 72 in 1995, 140 in 1996, and 0 in 1997, 1998, and 1999.
- Spring 1999 sampling in the upper reaches (Bear Siding upstream) also found no spikedace.
- The author describes the species as "very rare" in the upper Verde and "perilous at best."

**Extirpation probability:**
- Using the [[Grogan and Boreman Method]] with U.S. Forest Service data since 1994 (last collection 1997), the probability of extirpation is P = 0.873.
- Using Arizona State University data since 1980 (last collection 1997), the probability is P = 0.832.
- The author states this corresponds to 8 to 9 chances in 10 that the species is extirpated, but notes it is statistically nonsignificant.

**Community shifts:**
- Native fishes made up 82%, 86%, and 85% of the total fish community in 1994–1996, then fell to 19%, 22%, and 29% in 1997–1999.
- Total fish counts were 8,750 (1994), 1,274 (1995), 2,210 (1996), 2,288 (1997), 1,523 (1998), and 1,090 (1999).
- Red shiner numbers were 1,473 (1994), 2,238 (1997), and 545 (1999). Smallmouth bass rose from 14 (1994) to 104 (1999).
- Nonnative fishes now make up more than 70% of individuals in the upper Verde.

**Interacting drivers:**
- Native fishes, including spikedace, respond positively to flooding. Lack of flooding appears to favor vegetation and nonnative fishes over natives.
- A study that covered only 1994–1996 could have attributed the decline to flooding, while one covering only 1997–1999 could have attributed it to nonnative fishes. The author concludes that continual monitoring is needed.
- The decline coincided with increased baseflow over the past two decades, a period without floods, and grazing removal. Correlations among these factors have not been statistically tested.

**Management recommendations:**
- Intensify monitoring for spikedace.
- Begin suppressing nonnative fishes in the most upstream reaches. Mechanical suppression is considered more significant than grazing removal or sport-fish limits, and it is most effective when timed after a flood.
- Identify and evaluate refugia streams in the Verde Basin.
- Develop culture techniques for propagation before the next flood.
- Do not translocate spikedace from other systems unless extirpation from the upper Verde is unequivocally confirmed.

**Research opportunities:**
- Compare spikedace dynamics in the upper Verde with the [[Upper Gila River]], where spikedace were about 37% of the native fish community in spring 1999 after [[Hurricane Linda]] floods in September 1997. Nonnatives made up only about 10% of more than 6,000 fish collected from March to July 1999.
- Study the population dynamics of co-occurring natives, including [[Longfin Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Sonora Sucker]], and [[Roundtail Chub]].
- Pursue a coordinated regional management-research program with the U.S. Forest Service, state agencies, and other partners. The author argues that the problem of floods and fish community structure cannot be addressed by a single river system or jurisdiction.
