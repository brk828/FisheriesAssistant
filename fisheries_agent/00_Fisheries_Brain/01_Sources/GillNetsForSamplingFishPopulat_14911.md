# Gill Nets for Sampling Fish Populations in Minnesota Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by John B. Moyle of the [[Minnesota Department of Conservation]], assesses how reliable standard experimental [[Gill Net]] catches are for estimating the comparative size of [[Yellow Pikeperch]], [[Yellow Perch]], and [[Northern Pike]] populations in Minnesota lakes. It analyzes 37 series of summer gill-net catches from 14 lakes. The catch distributions are strongly skewed, and their error is wide. The author concludes that comparing catch means at a probability above 80 percent is not practically feasible. To help with this, he presents an empirical table of confidence limits ([[Table 2 Confidence Limits]]) and a formula for netting error. The paper is offered as a proximate approach, not a final solution.

*Note: the abstract refers to 31 series, while the body text and Table 1 describe 37 series. The 37-series figure is used here.*

## Background & Description

Gill nets have long been used in northern waters because more direct methods, such as [[Seining]], poisoning, and [[Mark-Recapture]], are often impractical in large natural lakes. Net catches have been expressed as catch per unit of effort, including the [[Gill-Net Index]] (catch per 100 ft of net per 24 hours) and catch per standard gang of nets per day. Workers such as [[Rawson]] (on [[Lake Athabaska]]) and [[Carlander]] (on [[Lake of the Woods]]) treated these catches as indexes of abundance rather than exact measures. Carlander concluded that gill-net data are "estimates of abundance" and are valuable when enough samples are taken.

**Gear and sampling protocol**

- Nets are "experimental" type, 250 ft long and 6 ft deep, made of five 50-ft sections with mesh sizes of 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, and 4 inches (stretch measure).
- Warm-water species are sampled with sets [[Above the Thermocline]]. Trout, [[Tullibee]], and other cold-water species are sampled with sets [[Below the Thermocline]].
- Nets are fished about 24 hours, then moved before being reset.
- The author argues that catch should now be recorded as catch per 250-ft set per day, which is more useful statistically than the older per-100-ft index.

**Standardized criteria for the 37 series (Table 1)**

1. All sets used the standard 250-ft experimental net.
2. Each set fished for 1 day, and each series was taken within a few days in one lake.
3. Sets were made mainly in July and August.
4. Sets were above the thermocline in the main body of the lake.
5. Each series was made specifically to sample the lake's fish population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lakes in Table 1 are all larger than 500 acres and are typical [[Pikeperch Waters]] in Minnesota. Pike catches tended to have the smallest means, yellow perch the largest, and pikeperch intermediate values. Overlap was considerable, and the distribution pattern was similar across the three species, so they were analyzed together.

The author stresses that gill nets are passive gear. Fish must "catch themselves," so catch depends on more than sampling randomness and population size. The main additional factors are:

1. **Movement of fish**, which influences mean catch.
2. **Shape and structure of fish**, which cause gear selectivity (treated as constant for each species across waters).
3. **Associative (grouping) patterns**, which show up in the distribution of catches. Irregular grouping produces irregular catches, most evident when means are small.

Because lake habitats are far from uniform, the author suggests that catches above and below the thermocline be analyzed separately. In large lakes, catches might be analyzed by locality, as [[Miller and Kennedy]] did for lake trout in [[Great Bear Lake]]. Selectivity and seasonal variation in catch are acknowledged but not analyzed in this paper.

## Key Findings

**1. Catch distributions are strongly skewed.**
- Sets per series ranged from 11 to 31, and series mean catches ranged from 0.9 to 96.6 fish.
- In 35 of 37 series, more than half the catches fell below the series mean.
- Across all catches, 61.8 percent were below their series mean, compared with the 50 percent expected for a normal distribution.
- Mean ± one standard deviation contained 81.5 percent of all sets, versus the expected 68 percent. The range exceeded 68 percent in all but five series.
- The ratio of standard deviation to mean increases as the mean increases in small-mean series, the standard deviation roughly equals the mean. Dispersion (mean-to-SD ratio) declines as mean catch grows.

**2. Comparing means at >80 percent probability is not practically feasible.**
The author concludes that the wide error of catch means makes comparisons at this probability level impractical. Because the skew does not seriously violate standard-error methods (standard errors from skewed populations tend toward normality), the author argues that conventional statistics can still be applied with caution.

**3. Two comparison approaches are used in Minnesota.**
- Comparing a series mean with the [[State-Wide Average Catch]] for the same species.
- Comparing a series mean with the same lake's mean in a different year, under similar conditions.

Both rely on assumptions of representative sampling and similar fish association and movement under similar conditions.

**4. Worked problems illustrate the statistical approach.**
- **Problem 1:** An average catch of 4.2 pikeperch in 11 sets versus a state-wide average of 2.0 per set. Using t = 2 with 10 degrees of freedom gives p ≈ 0.1, so the odds are about 9 to 1 that the lake holds a larger population.
- **Problem 2:** A mean of 15 perch in 9 sets (July 1948) versus 10 perch in 8 sets (July 1949) in the same lake. Using a standard-error-of-difference formula for small samples, the odds are about 3 out of 4 that the first population is larger.
- **Problem 3:** A mean of 4.0 pikeperch in 6 sets versus the state-wide average of 2.0. Table 2 gives a range of 2.5 to 7.0 at 80 percent confidence. Because 2.5 exceeds 2.0, the chance is "somewhat better than 80 percent" that the lake is larger.
- **Problem 4:** A mean of 5.0 pikeperch in 9 sets versus 4.0 in 7 sets. The ranges (3.5 to 8.0 and 2.5 to 7.0) overlap, so the chance is "less than 80 percent" that the populations differ.

**5. Empirical netting error formula and table.**
The author derives confidence limits for catch means at the 80 percent probability level, based on the catch data in Table 1:

> Netting Error = X ± (1.2 × X × R) / √N

Where X is the series mean catch, N is the number of sets, and R is the smoothed ratio of standard deviation to mean for a mean of that size. R values range from about 1.1 at a mean below 2 down to 0.62 at a mean of 50.

- Example from Table 2: a lake with a true mean of 2 perch per set would yield 10-set combination means between 1.2 and 2.8 fish at least 80 percent of the time.
- Worked example: a true mean of 4 fish in 10 sets gives a range of 2.5 to 5.5 fish.

**6. Limits of applicability.**
The author states that these limits are "only proximate." They apply only to summer gill-net catches of yellow perch, northern pike, and yellow pikeperch in Minnesota lakes (or similar waters) that show similar catch distributions, and only when sampling follows the same protocol. He also acknowledges that interpretation of gill-net catches is controversial and that the approach is his own.
