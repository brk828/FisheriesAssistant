# Interactions Between Endangered Fishes and Introduced Gamefishes in the Colorado River, Colorado, 1986-1991

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by [[Thomas P. Nesler]] for the [[Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]] (Project No. 91-29, Federal Aid Project SE-3, [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]]) evaluates how well non-native warmwater gamefish have established in the Colorado River and how strongly they threaten endangered native fishes through predation and competition. Data were drawn mainly from the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP) fall seine surveys of backwaters (1986-1991), supplemented by blocknet-electrofishing, night sampling and stomach-content analysis.

Nonnative gamefish made up roughly 1% of backwater fish collected by seine, while nonnative cyprinids dominated. Centrarchids were mostly young-of-year and juvenile, with low abundance and sporadic concentrations. Largemouth bass was the most piscivorous gamefish, but predation impact was judged small given low bass numbers and an abundant alternative prey base. The report judges [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] to pose a greater competitive threat to young [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] than gamefish predation. Recommended control focuses on small-bodied cyprinids, [[Common Carp]] and [[Channel Catfish]], and on limiting centrarchid escapement from ponds and reservoirs.

## Background & Description

The study addresses the decline of the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]], which the report attributes in part to introduced gamefish. Under the [[Strategic Plan for Non-Native Fish Control]] (Tyus and Saunders 1996), "control" is defined as reducing non-native species to levels below which they no longer impede endangered fish recovery.

Objectives were:
1. Determine trends in relative abundance, distribution, reproductive success and size structure of non-native, non-salmonid gamefish populations.
2. Determine the incidence of predation by these gamefish on endangered fishes.

The study reach ran from RM 185 near Palisade, Colorado, to RM 129 near the Colorado-Utah state line, including the Grand Valley and the 22 miles below Loma through Ruby and Horsethief canyons. The [[Gunnison River]] confluence lies at RM 170. Sampling used a 1.2 x 4.6 m beach seine (3.2 mm mesh) in the first and second backwaters of each five-mile segment, with a [[Blocknet Electrofishing]] method adopted in 1989-1991 for larger fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime:** Spring runoff in 1989 had three peaks (about 5,000-7,000 cfs at Cameo); in 1990 a single late, larger peak near 10,000 cfs occurred in early June. Flows then declined through July.
- **Temperature:** July water temperatures were 22-27 °C (1989) and 21-25 °C (1990) at Cameo and Stateline. Green sunfish optimal conditions (~28 °C) and largemouth bass growth optima (~31 °C) were rarely reached in the mainchannel.
- **Habitat suitability:** Models for [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] (Stuber et al. 1982a, b) indicate the riverine mainstem is suboptimal for both. Backwaters with low velocity and warmer water were judged more favorable for centrarchid reproduction, and floodplain ponds and reservoirs were identified as likely sources of centrarchid escapement.
- **Sampling constraints:** Only 11-20 backwaters were available per year. Seine area covered 1.1-5.3% of available backwater habitat, below the 25% protocol criterion.

## Key Findings

**Objective 1: Population trends and life history**

- Six nonnative gamefish species comprised about 1% of the 63,700 fish collected by seine (1986-1991). Red shiner and fathead minnow made up 69-97% of catches each year.
- Green sunfish was the most abundant gamefish, followed by black bullhead, largemouth bass and channel catfish. Bluegill and black crappie were incidental.
- Seine catch rates for gamefish showed no consistent correlation with area seined or backwater area available; black bullhead catch rates correlated positively with area seined.
- Adult channel catfish and common carp were the most abundant large-bodied fish in backwaters in April and July 1989-1990, together making up 85% of catch. Adult native fish were present in April but largely gone by July.
- Channel catfish showed robust weight gain with length (length-weight slope above 3.0), though less so than in the [[Yampa River]] population.
- Centrarchid recruitment was judged unlikely in the mainstem. Blocknet sampling produced 15 bass and 52 green sunfish across 44 backwaters, while later studies suggested the report under-represented centrarchid abundance.

**Objective 2: Predation**

- Largemouth bass was the most piscivorous gamefish; fish occurred in about 57% of stomachs examined (n = 39). Only 12% of bass stomachs were empty.
- Fish prey were found in only about 7.4% of green sunfish stomachs; insect larvae dominated.
- Black bullhead (n = 111) and channel catfish (n = 159) fed mainly on non-fish prey (about 55% and 63% of stomachs respectively).
- Over 80% of 14,482 red shiner, sand shiner and fathead minnow in 1990 ISMP seines were under 30 mm, making them vulnerable to centrarchid predation.
- Larval [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (8.2-9.9 mm) were captured in drift nets at RM 153.8 and 134.5 in July 1991; they comprised 11% of the drift population on those dates.
- Nighttime sampling in July 1991 captured as many predatory gamefish (51 fish in four backwaters over three nights) as daytime sampling of 40 backwaters in the previous two years (54 fish).
- Predation impact on native fish was judged small because of low gamefish numbers, low ingestion frequency and a large cyprinid prey base. Red shiner and fathead minnow were judged more significant competitors.
- Channel catfish predation on stocked juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (documented in lower Basin studies) may limit recruitment if adult catfish concentrate in summer backwaters.

**Management recommendations**

1. Focus control on small-bodied cyprinids (fathead minnow, red shiner, sand shiner), common carp and channel catfish, which dominate backwaters.
2. Evaluate mechanical removal by seining for short-term benefits to native fish.
3. Evaluate trapping or controlled commercial harvest of channel catfish and common carp in summer backwaters.
4. Limit centrarchid escapement from floodplain ponds and small reservoirs, consistent with the [[Nonnative Fish Stocking Procedures]] (1994).

**Uncertainties noted by the author:** Daytime stomach sampling may under-represent fish prey in crepuscular feeders; the origin (riverine versus pond) of backwater centrarchids remained untested pending stable isotope analysis; control effectiveness depends on the ongoing removal study.
