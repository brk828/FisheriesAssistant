# The Environmental Conditions of an Irrigation Impoundment as Related to the Maintenance of a Game Fish Population

#fisheries-management #reservoir-ecology #warm-water-fisheries #irrigation-reservoir

## Executive Summary

This report presents an eight-year investigation (July 1947 through July 1955) of [[Holbrook Lake Reservoir]] in [[Otero County]], [[Colorado]], examining the environmental factors limiting game fish production in an irrigation impoundment. The study combined chemical, physical, and biological assessments with extensive fish population manipulation efforts, including rough fish removal (581 pounds per surface acre) and corrective stocking (over 1,000 fingerlings per acre). Despite these intensive management efforts, the reservoir failed to sustain adequate game fish populations. The investigation revealed that undesirable fish species (carp, suckers, sunfish) comprising over 80% of the fish population, combined with drastic water level fluctuations and lack of aquatic vegetation, created environmental conditions unsuitable for young-of-the-year game fish survival. The study concludes that complete eradication of undesirable species and installation of fish-killing electric screens in inlet/outlet canals are necessary for successful rehabilitation.

## Background & Description

[[Holbrook Lake Reservoir]] is located approximately two miles northeast of Swink in [[Otero County]], [[Colorado]], in the [[Arkansas River Valley]] region. The impoundment occupies a natural basin with an earth fill along south and east shores, reaching a maximum height of 21 feet. At high water level, the reservoir has a total storage capacity of 7,472 acre-feet with 673 acres surface area and maximum depth of 23 feet. At conservation pool level, surface area reduces to less than 250 acres with approximately 7 feet maximum depth. Original maximum depth before siltation was 30 feet.

Water is diverted from the [[Arkansas River]] about ten miles west and delivered by canal for storage, used primarily for exchange purposes and discharged directly into the river via a 1.5-mile canal. The reservoir basin is relatively shallow with gently sloping bottom composed of muck and clay in deeper areas, silt and blow-sand on periphery. The impoundment is partially surrounded by shade trees including willows, tamarix, and cottonwoods.

Historical records indicate that prior to 1944, the fish population consisted of [[Black Bullhead]] catfish, various sunfish species, [[Carp]], and suckers. Initial game fish stocking occurred in 1944 and 1946 with [[Largemouth Black Bass]], [[Crappie]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Channel Catfish]] fingerlings, but survival rates were poor. A 1947 fish population inventory revealed: 53% carp, 16% sunfish, 13% suckers, 11% bullheads, 4% channel catfish, 2% black bass, and 1% crappie.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Chemical Characteristics:**
Dissolved oxygen ranged from 3.2 to 8.5 parts per million (ppm), with lower readings near bottom. Supersaturation occurred near surface under favorable conditions. Constant wind action maintained good aeration. Carbon dioxide levels ranged from 0.0 to 6.0 ppm. Methyl orange alkalinity averaged 110-125 ppm, hydrogen-ion concentration 7.5-8.5 ppm, and total dissolved solids 800-1,700 ppm. All chemical parameters were within ranges suitable for fish survival and reproduction.

**Physical Characteristics:**
Secchi disc readings averaged 18 inches during 1953-1954, with early spring clarity reaching 6-10 feet before turbidity increased. Turbidity sources included plankton, suspended soil particles, and rough fish feeding activity. Water level fluctuation was extremely irregular, particularly 1949-1952, with drastic rises and falls disrupting the limnetic zone. Surface temperatures ranged from 51.8°F (April) to 84.5°F (August); bottom temperatures 45.0°F to 76.5°F. Wind velocity equaled or exceeded 20 mph on approximately 200 days per year, frequently exceeding 40 mph, causing almost constant water circulation.

**Biological Features:**
Higher aquatic vegetation ([[Polygonacea]], [[Potamogetonaceae]]) was common 1946-1948 but disappeared by 1952-1953, attributed to drastic water level fluctuation and rough fish activity. Zooplankton samples revealed copepods, cladocerans, and rotifers in order of abundance, with only traces of phytoplankton. Average summer zooplankton standing crop was 42 organisms per liter (copepods), 18 per liter (cladocera), and 8 per liter (rotatoria).

Bottom fauna consisted primarily of [[Chironomus]] (midge fly) larvae (89.2% by number), with [[Oligochaeta]] (aquatic earthworms, 9.4%), Chironomus pupae (1.0%), and [[Tabanidae]] (horse fly larvae, 0.4%). Average density was 7.45 Chironomus larvae per 1/4 square foot sample. Bottom fauna production was significantly lower than comparable waters, with deep muck areas supporting more organisms than shallow sand bottoms.

## Key Findings

**Fish Population Manipulation Results:**
Over eight years (1947-1954), 581 pounds of rough fish per surface acre were removed using seines, traps, gill nets, and fish toxicants, averaging 600 man-hours annually. Concurrently, over 1,000 fingerling or larger game fish per surface acre were stocked. Despite these efforts, a 1954 fish population inventory (12,476 samples) showed undesirable species still comprised 73% of total population. The manipulation program failed because: (1) artificially planted game fish could not survive in adequate numbers; (2) adult game fish reproduction was sporadic and offspring faced same survival difficulties; (3) undesirable species spawned successfully almost annually and continued entering via inflow water.

**Game Fish Species Evaluations:**

*[[Largemouth Black Bass]]:* 160,279 fingerlings (1.5-3 inches) planted 1944-1951; survival rate less than 1%. Larger fish (966 at 7 inches in 1952; 139 adults at 12 inches in 1954) showed better survival. First successful spawn in ten years occurred 1955. Fingerling stage identified as critical period; acute shortage of bottom fauna and aquatic invertebrates limited survival. Once fish reached second year, survival and growth were satisfactory. Creel census: 1-2% of catch.

*[[Bluegill Sunfish]]:* 69,200 fingerlings planted 1944-1950 (100 per acre); survival rate nil. In 1953-1954, 26,976 fish averaging 5.5 inches planted with over 50% survival. By summer 1955, some fish reached 8.5+ inches. Hybridization with less desirable sunfishes complicated reproductive assessment.

*[[Yellow Perch]]:* 12,051 fish (4-8 inches) planted 1949-1954; estimated 25% survival. Growth rate excellent, reaching 10-12 inches by spring 1955. Reproductive success nil, attributed to unsuitable spawning conditions (water level typically low during spawning period, lacking submerged vegetation for egg deposition).

*[[Black Crappie]] and [[White Crappie]]:* 76,234 crappie (1-10 inches) planted 1944-1954. Fingerling survival less than 5%; larger fish (over 4.5 inches) survival greater than 50%. Of total planted, 34,909 averaged over 4.5 inches. Species showed best adaptation to existing conditions except catfish. Spawned successfully three times in ten years. Age groups II, III, IV averaged 8.5, 10.5, 11.5 inches respectively. Creel census: 8-60% of catch 1950-1955.

*[[Channel Catfish]]:* 23,496 fish (3-15 inches) planted 1945-1954; over 60% survival. Evidence of successful reproduction during study period. Provided "big fish" (2-10+ pounds) for anglers. Fingerlings survived better than other game species, apparently obtaining adequate food. Some migration loss during drawdown periods. Successful reproduction followed heavy late-June inflows. Over 200 nail kegs (both ends removed) placed as spawning structures in 1950.

*[[Black Bullhead Catfish]]:* Native species, never artificially introduced. Furnished mainstay of angler catch. Reproduced successfully almost annually but abundance limited by diseases and parasites. Population fluctuated; occasionally near over-population, other years less numerous. Average size taken seldom over 8 inches.

*[[Walleye]] and [[White Bass]]:* Planted as predatory species for rough fish control and sport fishing. 206,786 walleye fry and 1,762 marked fingerlings planted 1953-1954. Growth comparable to other Colorado waters: 1953 fry reached 14.5 inches by 1955; 1954 fry reached 8.0 inches. 206 white bass (7 inches) planted 1953 as potential brood stock.

*[[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]:* 9,000 brown trout (8-10 inches, 3,000 jaw-tagged) planted 1954; 4% tag return by year end. 25,000 rainbow trout (2-4 inches) planted April 1954; only larger fingerlings (near 4 inches) survived. Survivors averaged 8.5 inches within four months, 12.2 inches by spring 1955 (15 months post-stocking). Trout remained in deeper, cooler waters largely inaccessible to bank anglers. Caught primarily during fall/spring when in shallows.

**Creel Census Analysis:**
Periodic creel checks 1949-1955 revealed extremely poor fishing. Average catch-per-man-hour for entire period: 0.30 (over three hours required to catch one fish). Accepted criterion for good fishing: one fish per hour. Bullheads and sunfish comprised 70% of total catch (from self-sustained populations); other game fish 30% (artificially stocked). Annual stocking expenditure showed minimal return to anglers. To provide good fishing via put-and-take method would require initial plant of 300,000 desirable-size fish plus annual plant of 50,000, costing over $10,000 annually.

**Environmental Limiting Factors:**
Investigation identified multiple interacting factors limiting game fish production:

1. *Water Level Fluctuation:* Drastic, irregular fluctuations (especially 1949-1952) created "deserts" in productive limnetic zone, disrupted bottom fauna establishment (one month required for Chironomus recolonization after 14-foot rise), curtailed fish reproduction, and prevented aquatic vegetation establishment.

2. *Turbidity:* Excessive wave action, water inflow, plankton production, and rough fish feeding habits caused persistent turbidity. Poor light penetration limited aquatic vegetation growth. Clear water periods showed shift from Copepoda to Cladocera dominance in plankton.

3. *Lack of Aquatic Vegetation:* Absence of higher aquatic plants resulted in acute shortage of associated organisms (mayfly nymphs, dragonfly nymphs, crustaceans, molluscs). Rough fish (carp, suckers) roiled bottom materials, reduced light penetration, and uprooted vegetation more than all other factors combined.

4. *Food Production Deficiency:* Good plankton crop and excellent forage fish (minnows) available, but bottom organisms and other foods extremely limited. Young-of-the-year game fish obtained adequate plankton nutrition early in life cycle but faced inadequate larger food items (freshwater shrimp, insect larvae/pupae) during critical transition period. Fish entering winter in poor condition; few survived. Once fish attained size to feed on minnows, crayfish, adult insects before winter, survival was good.

5. *Competition from Undesirable Species:* Large populations of carp, suckers, sunfish competed directly for foods needed by game fish and damaged environment, preventing young-of-the-year game fish survival in adequate numbers.

**Habitat Improvement Experiments:**
Artificial shelter devices constructed 1950-1953 using logs, brush, rock, old auto tires arranged from high to low water lines, covering approximately ten surface acres. Total cost: $1,365 (labor, hauling, materials). Observations showed definite increase in bottom fauna and organisms near shelters; Chironomus larvae abundant on rocks and tires; constant swarms of midges and water boatmen. Red fin and fathead minnows spawned April-September around shelters. Large schools of minnows and young crappie concentrated near shelters. Anglers caught more game fish near shelters than elsewhere. However, devices covered insufficient area for significant environmental improvement. Estimated 25% of lake bottom (170 acres) coverage needed at cost exceeding $20,000. Abundant higher aquatic vegetation would provide more beneficial and less expensive environmental alteration.

**Management Recommendations:**
1. Install fish-killing electric screens in both inlet and outlet canals to prevent re-entry of undesirable species (estimated $3,000 per screen; development by [[Colorado Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]] showing encouraging results).

2. After screen installation and testing, remove game fish by seining, then eradicate remaining population by chemical means (estimated $3,500 for treatment at low water level).

3. Restock with walleye, black bass, channel catfish, crappie, bluegill, redear sunfish, red fin and fathead minnows (fry, fingerling, and adult sizes).

4. Educate public regarding management plan purposes and desired results; seek cooperation preventing re-infestation through live bait use (estimated 10+ years before serious re-infestation would occur).

5. Conduct annual fish population inventories and creel census to determine management plan success and need for adjustments.

Total rehabilitation cost estimated at $12,000; pro-rated over ten years equals less than current annual "put-and-take" stocking expenditure. Between 1950-1954, over 28,000 crappie (4.5+ inches) and 30,922 other game fish (6.0+ inches) were planted at total cost of $12,250 ($2,440 annually) with minimal angler return.

**Critical Conclusion:**
The study demonstrates that no single factor limits game fish production; rather, a combination of unstable water levels, climatic conditions, and over-populations of undesirable fish species combine to alter environmental conditions preventing game fish survival in adequate numbers. The failure of young-of-the-year game fish to survive beyond first year is the major problem. While water temperature, depth, and chemical conditions are suitable, the absence of higher aquatic vegetation and inadequate fish food organism production are definite limiting factors, exacerbated by large undesirable fish populations. Complete eradication of undesirable species would likely result in re-establishment of aquatic vegetation (as observed in other northern Colorado reservoirs), altering environmental conditions to support good game fish production. Half-way measures cannot produce desired results; comprehensive rehabilitation is necessary.
