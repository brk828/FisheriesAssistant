# Catfish By The Acre: Commercial Pond Culture in Louisiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 magazine feature, reprinted from the *Louisiana Conservationist* and issued as Wild Life Education Bulletin No. 95, describes the rapid growth of commercial catfish farming in Louisiana. The article presents the industry as a profitable alternative to cash crops, with an acre of catfish said to yield about $200 net profit. It draws on interviews with [[Jim Davis]], a fisheries research biologist for the [[Louisiana Wild Life and Fisheries Commission]], and on visits to several working farms. The piece covers stocking rates, feeding, spawning methods, holding and marketing, and the business requirements for success. It presents the figures as reported by the sources and does not report independent verification.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Farm-scale channel catfish culture in earthen ponds, with fingerling production, live-bait shiner sales, and bullfrog harvesting as side enterprises.
- **Target species:** [[Channel Catfish]] is the preferred species. [[Blue Catfish]] may make up about 10 per cent of stocking where available. The article also shows an Opelousas catfish in a photo caption, with no further detail.
- **Key people and sites:**
  - [[Jimmie Thompson]], Commission chairman, experienced in catfish production.
  - [[Cummings Fish Farm]], 15 acres of ponds near Monroe, managed by L. Ted Barnes.
  - [[Pamplin Catfish Farm]], about 130 acres at Collinston, using a deep well for water.
  - [[Delhi Fish Farm]], owned by L. B. Armstrong, described as probably the largest in the state.
  - A Lafayette operation run by the Landry brothers.
- **Infrastructure:** Open holding sheds with concrete bins, constant water circulation, rotary-blade incubators for eggs, heated tanks for early spawning, and "cage and drum" breeding units.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water source:** Deep wells and artesian springs were preferred. The author notes that river or stream water could carry bacteria or pollutants that might wipe out a crop of fingerlings or marketable fish.
- **Land:** Louisiana's flat land and deep wells were cited as major advantages. Ponds were typically dug to four feet deep and about one acre in size.
- **Pesticide research:** Biologist Janice S. Hughes was testing how much pesticide catfish could tolerate and which chemicals they could not tolerate at all.
- **Seasonality:** Catfish spawn in June or July. Under pond conditions, the article estimates about 10 months to reach market size.
- **Market seasonality:** Commercial pond-fish sales are generally poor from Thanksgiving to about February 1. June through September is generally good for fish of one-half to two pounds.

## Key Findings

**Market and industry**

- National catfish demand was far greater than supply. Only about 25 per cent of national demand could be met.
- Production was quoted at about 15,000,000 pounds annually, against an immediate demand of about 60,000,000 pounds. Davis predicted Louisiana could produce 30,000,000 pounds of marketable catfish within three years and become the leading producer in the country.
- Current prices were 50 cents per pound raw and 90 cents per pound dressed.
- Catfish farms were reported in Texas, Arkansas, Alabama, Missouri, Kansas, and Florida. Florida was a major buyer of fingerlings.
- No catfish processing plants existed in Louisiana at the time. The article suggests a large plant could open a new industry as the sector grows.

**Economics**

- Davis cited a net profit of about $200 per acre, "perhaps more," and said catfish could outperform cotton and soybeans.
- A commercial operation was described as requiring at least 100 acres and about $100,000 in capital. Smaller operators could start with less and reinvest profits.
- One Lafayette pond of roughly 5¼ acres (OCR reads "514-acre") produced about 12,000 pounds in November, worth more than $6,000 at 50 cents per pound.
- A worked example for 10 one-acre ponds stocked at 2,000 fingerlings per acre, fed pellets, gives about 20,000 marketable catfish and roughly $10,000 gross. Feed cost was about six cents per fish (the OCR-garbled figure in the text, read as roughly 6¼ cents, is also given), plus labor and other costs.
- Feeding seven days a week rather than six was reported to yield a 15 per cent weight gain. For 2,000 fish, that meant 2,300 pounds instead of 2,000, worth about $150 more per acre, or about $1,500 across 10 ponds.

**Stocking and feeding**

- Fed ponds: about 2,000 fingerlings per acre, producing about 2,000 marketable catfish per acre in one year.
- Unfed ponds: no more than 500 fingerlings per acre. Unfed fish reached roughly one pound in a year.
- Overstocking without feeding was said to stunt growth.
- Pelletized feed was recommended. The article states that about 1.25 pounds of feed (printed as "114," an OCR error) produced one pound of catfish.

**Spawning and survival**

- Controlled spawning used breeding pairs in numbered cages with drums, heated water to induce earlier spawning, and rotary-blade incubators to keep eggs free of debris.
- Controlled spawning lost about 10 per cent of eggs. Adding another 10 per cent for other causes gave a total egg-to-fish loss of about 20 per cent.
- Louisiana could produce fry and fingerlings several months ahead of mid-western natural spawning.
- Commission biologists were working on an accelerated spawning system to complete a crop within one year.

**Holding and transport**

- Fingerlings and marketable fish could be held in circulating-water sheds for about a week. Research aimed to extend this to two weeks.
- Out-of-state buyers preferred 10,000-pound truck loads. Loads of 6,000 pounds were possible but reduced profit because of trucking costs.

**Side enterprises**

- Live-bait shiners were spawned on moss-covered wire trays. The article says practically all spawn was collected and hatched.
- Tanker trucks came from across the South to buy shiners for retail to fishermen.
- Bullfrogs were harvested at a flat price of one dollar per frog, dressed.
- Fingerlings were sold for "put and take" fishing ponds, which had previously stocked trout.

**Business guidance**

- The article stresses that catfish farming is a business and requires steady supply, careful management, and capital planning.
- Davis's paper on methods can be requested from the Monroe District Office of the Commission.
