# Effects of Density and Feed on Bonytail (Gila elegans) Growth

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This draft briefing paper from the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (WBNFH), a unit of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], reports two hatchery feeding and density trials on [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), an imperiled freshwater fish. The work supports a [[Bonytail Recovery Program]] goal of building a genetically diverse captive broodstock for future reintroduction. Experiment 1 found that a krill or black worm diet produced faster growth than a trout pellet and flake diet, with mean weight about twice as high by the end of the trial. Experiment 2 found that catfish feed promoted faster growth than trout feed, with no growth difference between the densities tested. The authors caution that the second trial had too few replicates for firm conclusions.

## Background & Description

*Gila elegans* is described as one of the most imperiled freshwater fish species. Its surviving population is small and largely confined to the lower [[Colorado River]].

Recovery goals call for a genetically diverse captive population from which offspring can later be reintroduced to the wild. Key points from the background:

- **Original broodstock:** A single broodstock was started in 1981 at WBNFH. It now resides at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (DNFH&TC) with approximately 260 individuals.
- **New broodstock effort:** A multi-year program is building a new broodstock from remaining adults and potentially from additional bonytail collected in [[Lake Mohave]].
- **Family lots:** Thirty-four pairs were spawned at DNFH&TC in May 2000. Their offspring were kept as unique family lots at DNFH&TC, Mora NFH&TC, and WBNFH.
- **Culture gap:** WBNFH has developed warmwater fish culture techniques over the past five years, but no methods specifically address bonytail growth. The two studies were designed to identify factors affecting growth and survival.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Warmwater Hatchery System

WBNFH sits about 18 km downstream of [[Hoover Dam]]. Its main water supply is cold water (about 12–14 °C year round) drawn from the hypolimnion of [[Lake Mead]]. The raceways are fed from [[Lake Mohave]]. Twelve raceways were modified to rear warmwater endangered fish, including bonytail and [[Razorback Sucker]]. These units were isolated from the gravity-fed cold water system.

Key features of the warmwater system:

- **Water movement:** A pump moves water through a diatomaceous earth filter and an in-line ultraviolet sterilizer, then through [[Solar Heating Panels]] mounted over the raceway canopy. Water exchange is generally completed every 3 to 4 days (the source value is garbled in the scan).
- **Biofiltration:** Plastic biological filter media act as a bacterial substrate to convert ammonia to nitrate and nitrite. Solar blankets reduce heat loss and algae growth.
- **Temperature control:** Timers regulate flow through the solar panels. Temperature is held at or below a set ceiling, which the scan renders illegibly.
- **Aeration:** A regenerative blower supplies air through PVC tubing and air stones.
- **Monitoring:** Temperature and dissolved oxygen are measured twice daily.

### Aquaria System

Water for the aquaria is pumped from Lake Mohave (about 12–14 °C), sterilized with UV, and stored in a 3,000 L reservoir. Solar collectors on the building roof warm the water to roughly 24 °C. Two portable electric heaters maintain reservoir temperature overnight. The water is UV-treated again before entering the aquaria. Temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, nitrite, and ammonia are recorded, since subtle changes in these parameters can affect fish growth, quality, and disease resistance.

## Key Findings

### Experiment 1: Feed Type and Family Lot Effects

**Methods**

- Family lots were obtained from DNFH&TC on 10 May 2000 and placed in 34 L aquaria. Fish were fed live brine shrimp eight times daily for 30 days.
- On 31 May 2000, family lots were split into paired aquaria with 150 fish each. Four family lots had fewer than 150 individuals and were larger at the start, possibly due to lower density, so only 10 family lots were used.
- From 8 June 2000, aquaria 6–15 received frozen krill or live black worms, alternating daily, four times per day. Aquaria 20–29 received a mixture of trout pellet (#2) and flake food (plankton, krill, or spirulina), also four times daily.
- Tanks were cleaned daily and mortalities recorded. Fish received weekly prophylactic treatment with 25 ppm formalin and 0.05 ppm malachite green.
- Fifty fish were sampled from each family lot for total length and weight. Sampling occurred on 8 June (start), 11 July (day 33), 23 August (day 76), and at the end of the experiment in October 2000.

**Results**

- Mean starting weight was 0.20 g.
- No differences in growth over time were found among family lots, so family lots served as replicates.
- Fish on the krill or black worm diet grew faster than fish on the trout pellet and flake diet.
- By the end of the trial, mean weight on the krill or black worm diet was about twice that of the trout pellet and flake group.
- Survival exceeded 98% in all aquaria, so survival was not used as a covariate.

### Experiment 2: Feed Type and Stocking Density

**Methods**

- Offspring from 20 pairs, with equal contribution from each pair, were obtained from DNFH&TC.
- Fish were placed in two raceways (C9 and C10). Each raceway was divided into four equal sections (10 m long × 2.4 m wide × 1.2 m deep).
- Densities were low (about 300 fish in C9 lower and 2,500 fish in C10 middle) and high (3,500 fish each in C10 upper and C10 lower). The scan's density assignments are partly ambiguous, so they should be checked against the original.
- Catfish feed was given to C9 lower and C10 upper. Trout feed was given to C10 lower and C10 middle.
- Fish were grown for 155 days. Temperature and dissolved oxygen remained constant throughout.

**Results**

- Fish fed catfish feed grew faster and were larger at the end of the experiment than fish fed trout feed.
- No difference in weight change was found between the two densities studied.
- The authors caution that replicate numbers were small. Feed nutrient levels were not analyzed.
- Behavioral observations suggested fish took the catfish feed readily, possibly because of its palatability and size.

### Overall Conclusions

- Diet strongly influenced bonytail growth in both trials, with live or frozen invertebrate foods (Experiment 1) and catfish feed (Experiment 2) outperforming the trout-based diets.
- Density effects were not detected within the ranges tested in Experiment 2.
- These results are preliminary. They should guide further hatchery culture work rather than serve as final feeding protocols for [[Bonytail Recovery Program]] broodstock.
