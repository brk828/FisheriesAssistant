# A Simple Method for Assessing the Annual Production of Stream Benthos

#limnology #benthic-invertebrates #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

Hynes and Coleman (1968, *Limnology and Oceanography*, 13(4): 569-573) propose a simple way to estimate the annual production of stream benthic invertebrates from a series of quantitative samples taken at intervals through the year. They argue it is currently the only simple, direct method of estimating production. The authors correct a conceptual error in [[Hynes 1961]], rework that dataset from the [[Afon Hirnant]] and add a second year of data, and compare the results with a richer hard-water stream, the [[Speed River]]. The method is explicitly crude, but they consider it suitable for remote [[International Biological Program]] work by non-specialists. A later erratum notice corrects two errors in the calculations.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a practical gap in production ecology. Production is usually estimated from known ages and population structure, a method borrowed from fisheries work (Allen 1951) and applied to unionid mussels ([[Unionidae]]), whose annual rings allow ageing (Negus 1966). Extending that approach to a whole benthic fauna is impractical because most taxa are hard to identify and their life histories are poorly known. The authors cite [[Waters 1966]], who applied the [[Instantaneous Growth Rate Method]] to [[Baetis Vagans]] (turnover of about 3 per generation).

An alternative is back-calculation from fish consumption, as in Allen (1951) and [[Horton 1961]] (brown trout, Dartmoor). Those estimates implied turnover ratios of 8 to 26, which the authors regard as implausibly high. They attribute the discrepancy mainly to benthic sampling problems, since samplers with coarse mesh miss small animals, and not only to fish-consumption errors ([[Gerking 1962]]).

Key sampling context:
- Stream insects occur in considerable numbers to at least 30 cm depth in gravel (Angelier 1953; Schwoerbel 1961).
- In one Ontario stream, only about 20% of the fauna found to 30 cm depth was in the top 7.5 cm.
- Quantitative sampling therefore needs improvement, though the authors do not treat that problem in detail (see [[Albrecht 1959]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study streams**
- **[[Afon Hirnant]] (Wales):** described as a soft-water, unproductive stream. Data came from November 1955-1956 and 1959-1960.
- **[[Speed River]] (Ontario):** described as a richer, hard-water stream. Sampling there was considered better than in the Afon Hirnant.
- The Afon Hirnant production values are probably too low because of inadequate sampling in depth. The authors argue this should not much affect the turnover ratios.

**Biological context**
- Many dominant stream insects are univoltine (one generation per year). Others hatch over long periods, which lowers mean population size and complicates estimates ([[Macan 1958]]; [[Hynes 1961]]).
- Speed River production was said to be able to supply the measured drift rate from the streambed and the probable needs of fishes.

**Drift and fish links**
- The authors note that drift rates were supplied by J. Bishop for the Speed River.
- The paper frames benthic production as food for fish, so fish-based turnover estimates serve as a cross-check.

## Key Findings

### Method (proposed)
1. Take adequate quantitative samples at intervals throughout the year.
2. Sort all individuals into 1-mm size classes (or 0.5-mm for finer data).
3. Divide the total in each size class by the number of sampling dates. This gives the equivalent of one "ideal" sample.
4. Calculate loss at each size stage. Each size group is assumed to grow through all remaining categories, with the loss at each stage counted as production.
5. Convert numbers to biomass using conversion factors for volume (size-cubed scaling) and assumed body shape and density. Direct weighing or calorific equivalents are preferred where available.

The authors recognise the method's limitations:
- It assumes all species are univoltine, which they are not.
- It underestimates production by missing newly hatched animals that die between sampling dates.
- Long-lived large taxa (some Plecoptera, Odonata, Megaloptera) partly offset this.
- Assuming all species grow to the size of the largest ones probably over-compensates for those losses.
- Refinements suggested: treat bivoltine, univoltine and multi-year species separately; group species by adult size; compare seasons.

### Afon Hirnant, 1955-1956 (Table 1)
- 13 sample sets from 1,800 cm², covering 10 identifiable common species.
- These species made up about 70% (752/1,063) of the total fauna. The authors do not examine how that percentage was determined.
- Standing biomass: 4.76 g/m²; production: 12.17 g/m²; turnover ratio: 2.56.
- Totals in 1-mm units: standing biomass 238,827; production 610,746.
- Hynes's earlier estimate (2.995 g/m² production from 4.76 g biomass) gave a turnover of 0.629, which the authors judged too low. The error was that production was based only on the smallest size group.

### Afon Hirnant, 1959-1960 (Table 2)
- 11 sample sets from 6,300 cm², measured in 0.5-mm size classes.
- Severe floods had reduced the fauna.
- Standing biomass: 1.30 g/m²; production: 3.81 g/m²; turnover ratio: 2.94.
- Totals in 0.5-mm units: standing biomass 1,539,577; production 4,530,300.

### Speed River, Ontario
- Production: 620.2 g/m² from standing biomass of 195.5 g/m² (turnover ratio 3.17).

### Comparison and discussion
- The Afon Hirnant turnover ratios (2.56 and 2.94) are "more or less in line" with the approximately 3 per generation reported for Baetis by Waters (1966).
- Turnover ratios for Unionidae were given as about 0.13-0.20 per year, far lower than for Baetis.
- Back-calculated fish-based ratios (8-26) were judged implausibly high.
- The authors argue that applying the method across many streams would build data on true turnover ratios, which probably vary greatly by stream type. This would allow comparison of streams by standing biomass alone.

### Errata (1968 correction notice)
Two flaws were identified by readers and are to be corrected in *Limnology and Oceanography*:
1. Size at loss should be taken at a point halfway between size groups, so the conversion factor should be ((L₁+L₂)/2)³ rather than the value used.
2. The "number of times loss occurs" should be n, the number of size groups, in every case, not 1, 2, 3 … n as shown in the tables.

Readers should therefore treat the numerical production values in the original tables with caution.
