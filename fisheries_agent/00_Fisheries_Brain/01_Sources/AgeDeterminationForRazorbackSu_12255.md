# Age Determination for Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) from Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada, are long-lived, and evaluates which bony structures give reliable ages. Recruitment into the population is low or absent, and most recently captured fish are large adults. Of seven structures tested, only sectioned sagittal [[Otolith]]s gave dependable ages for large, wild-caught fish. Wild fish were aged at 24 to 44 years, and most hatched before or during the filling of the reservoir. The authors conclude that the stock is likely to disappear before the year 2000 unless recruitment resumes.

## Background & Description

The Razorback Sucker is a large catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of western North America. It was historically widespread, ranging from the Green River in Wyoming to the Colorado River delta in Sonora and Baja California. It was once abundant below Grand Canyon and was an important food fish for Indigenous peoples and early settlers. Modern records are infrequent and mostly involve large adults. Young fish are sporadic and geographically scattered.

Reservoirs on the lower basin's larger streams were initially colonized by substantial numbers of the species. It disappeared from [[Gila River]] impoundments in the 1950s, roughly 40 years after dams were built. It has become increasingly rare in mainstream Colorado River reservoirs of similar age. A large population persists only in Lake Mohave, the last major reservoir built on the lower river.

Earlier work by Minckley (1983) reported large body size in the Lake Mohave stock (females averaging 50 cm and males 45 cm standard length in 1981). No fish under 37 cm SL were caught in annual sampling from 1974 to 1981. Scales could not be used for ageing, so the stock was presumed old, with origins before or at the closure of [[Davis Dam]] (1942-1951) and the final filling of the reservoir in 1954.

The study had three aims: to assess body structures for ageing, to test the hypothesis of substantial age in Lake Mohave fish, and to describe individual growth. It was adapted from a Master of Science thesis at [[Arizona State University]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study material.** A total of 84 wild-caught fish was examined, collected from 1980 to 1984. Sources included:
- 55 specimens collected in May 1980 and January 1981
- 9 broodfish from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] that died in 1982 and 1984
- 4 fish provided by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] in 1982
- 14 fish provided by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] in 1983

Sex was unknown for four fish. These were used only to test structure reliability.

Forty-five known-age hatchery progeny of Lake Mohave broodstock were also examined. These comprised 4 fish from the 1974 year class, 6 from 1979, 5 from 1980, and 30 from 1984 (under one year old). Reference ages were available for these fish, so they were used to validate the ageing methods.

**Reproduction and recruitment.** Annual spawning and larval production occur in Lake Mohave, but young fish disappear before reaching 12 mm total length. No evidence of recruitment into the adult population was found. Larger individuals of the 1974 hatchery class reached sexual maturity at about six years. Under hatchery conditions at Dexter, males matured at two years and females at three.

**Feeding and habitat.** The species has a protrusible, nearly terminal mouth and closely spaced gill rakers, which suggest facultative [[Planktivory]]. The authors suggest the species may be better adapted to backwaters and oxbows of the unmodified river than to the main channel. Rapid growth after impoundment may reflect increased availability of planktonic food in [[Lentic Habitat]].

**Population outlook.** The study reports that the Lake Mohave population declined in a pattern similar to earlier reservoirs. Recruitment has been undetectable since 1958 and minimal since 1954. Hatchery propagation and reintroduction are underway, but the reasons for lack of natural recruitment remain unknown.

## Key Findings

**1. Structure reliability.** Seven structures were compared (scales, branchiostegal rays, pectoral fin rays, opercular bones, cleithra, vertebrae, and otoliths).
- All seven gave clear annuli in hatchery fish of known age.
- [[Scale Analysis]] of wild fish was unreliable. Scales were small, eroded, or regenerated, and circuli were too uniform to show annuli.
- Fin-ray sections had presumed annuli, but counts differed between lobes and did not match other structures. They were not considered further.
- Opercles, cleithra, branchiostegal rays, and vertebrae of large fish were too dense and thickened for accurate ageing. Thickening masked the focus and inner annuli and caused underestimation. Opercles and cleithra might be usable if sectioned, but this was not attempted.
- Unsectioned otoliths were also compromised in large wild fish.
- **Sectioned otoliths** were the only structure reliable for large, old fish.

**2. Otolith readability.** Sectioned otoliths gave clear annual growth for 70 of 84 fish (93.3%). The unreadable 14 fish were lost to:
- breakage during sectioning (2.4%)
- irregularly aberrant margins (10.7%)
- sectioning away from the structural focus (63.6%)

The figures for the unreadable group are reported as in the source, although the stated sub-percentages do not sum to the total. The readable sample is the basis for all later analyses.

**3. Age.** Intact wild-caught otoliths had 23 or more distinct marks, which were read as annuli. The greatest apparent age was 44 years, counting the margin as a final annulus.
- The Abstract gives a range of 24 to 44 years at capture (1980-1984).
- The Discussion gives 23 to 43 otolith annuli, with the youngest fish 24 years old at capture in 1980.
- If the ages are accurate, the fish hatched between 1937 and 1958.
- Of the 70 readable fish, 62 (88.6%) hatched before or during the construction of Davis Dam and filling of Lake Mohave.

**4. Growth and sexual dimorphism.** Back-calculation used a significant positive correlation between sagitta radius and SL at capture (r² = 0.97, log-transformed data).
- Females averaged 26.7 ± 0.8% of their capture SL in the first year. Fish reached 50% of capture SL by the fifth year.
- Mean SL at the first annulus was 140 ± 4 mm in females and 103 ± 4 mm in males.
- Mean SL at the fifth annulus was 274 ± 7 mm in females and 214 ± 8 mm in males. The male–female difference was significant (Student's t-test, p < 0.05).
- Average annual growth slowed abruptly to a few millimetres after the fifth or sixth year.

**5. Effect of impoundment.** Fish hatched before and after the closure of Davis Dam were compared (before 1951, after 1951).
- Growth to the first annulus did not differ. Males were 103 ± 4 mm (n = 11) and 104 ± 9 mm (n = 7). Females were 136 ± 5 mm (n = 33) and 148 ± 7 mm (n = 16).
- Growth to the fifth annulus was significantly greater after impoundment (p < 0.05). Males were 180 ± 7 mm (n = 5) before and 234 ± 11 mm (n = 7) after. Females were 256 ± 19 mm (n = 8) before and 298 ± 10 mm after.
- The authors suggest lentic conditions may have improved growth.

**6. Supporting evidence.** Two external records agree with the high ages:
- A 52.4 cm SL male from the lower Colorado River (1974) was 22 years old.
- A 60 cm unsexed fish (1971) had 17 annuli.
- In the upper basin, one tagged adult showed no detectable growth in 1.5 years, and another grew 8.0 mm in 3.5 years.
- The Green River (Utah) tagging study reported an average growth rate of 1.65 mm per year over 1 to 6 years.
- The ages and growth patterns resemble those of the [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*) of Pyramid Lake, Nevada, which was aged at 28 to 41 years.

**7. Size records.**
- The largest Lake Mohave female was 57.6 cm SL (73.6 cm TL), but unweighed and unaged.
- The heaviest Lake Mohave specimen was a 4.99 kg female.
- The reported record of 7.26 kg (16 lb) from Saguaro Lake was considered suspect.

**8. Longevity conclusion.** The data support Minckley's (1983) hypothesis that Lake Mohave razorback suckers are old. The authors also note that the pattern of extirpation 40 to 50 years after dam completion appears to be repeating. If this continues, the population may disappear before the year 2000. Research on predation, nutrition, movement, habitat selection, and early life history is ongoing to identify the causes of recruitment failure.
