# Long-Term Trends of Native and Non-Native Fish Faunas in the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Olden]] and [[Poff]] (2005) analyzes more than 150 years of fish occurrence records in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] to quantify how native fish distributions have contracted and how non-native species have expanded. Using the [[SONFISHES Database]] (about 38,800 records for 132 species), the authors compare distributional change with the conservation status assigned under the [[U.S. Endangered Species Act]]. They find that native species have largely declined, that the fastest-spreading invaders are also among the most damaging, and that long-term distribution trends correlate with local extirpation risk. The authors argue that drainage-scale historical data can serve as a proactive surrogate for identifying species needing management before they become extremely rare.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] is one of the most heavily controlled rivers on Earth, with hundreds of dams and diversions supplying irrigation water to more than 3.7 million acres of farmland and water and power to about 30 million people in the United States and Mexico. Construction of water development projects began in the early 1900s, and by the 1930s much of the mainstem had been converted into a system of dams and diversions. These changes degraded habitats to which native fishes had adapted and facilitated the spread of non-native species that prey on and compete with natives.

Of the 31 native fish species in the Lower Colorado Basin, 25 were reported as extirpated, extinct, or listed under the Endangered Species Act, or believed to have suffered significant declines in distribution. Non-native fishes were deliberately introduced beginning in the late 1800s, and more than 90 species have been introduced, with about half considered established. Previous evidence of native community replacement was largely anecdotal, and the study addresses this gap with quantitative data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** Roughly 362,250 km² across five U.S. states and northwestern Mexico, including about 16,000 km of streams and rivers from Glen Canyon Dam to the Gulf of California.
- **Data:** Approximately 28,255 locality records (1840–2000, excluding artificial translocations) for 28 native and 48 non-native species, georeferenced in ArcGIS to the U.S. Geological Survey Enhanced River Reach File 2.0.
- **Time periods:** Pre-1960, 1960–1969, 1970–1979, 1980–1989, and 1990–1999. Historical native records were those collected before 1990; modern (extant) records were those from 1980–1999.
- **Metrics:** Distribution was measured as the percentage of total river kilometres where each species was present, relative to total river kilometres where all species were present in each period. Decline was historical range minus extant range, divided by historical range. Spread rate was extant distribution divided by years since introduction (km/year).
- **Status data:** Listing status came from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Threatened and Endangered Species System. Introduction dates came from [[Mueller and Marsh]] (2002) and the first occurrence in the SONFISHES database.
- **Threat context:** The American Southwest is among the most threatened aquatic systems in North America, and the fish fauna is highly endemic.

## Key Findings

**Native fish declines**
- Native fishes have predominantly decreased in distribution. Changes ranged from 100% range reduction to a 1.4% range expansion.
- Of the native species, 23 decreased and 8 slightly increased in distribution.
- Only the Santa Cruz pupfish was reported as extinct; 19 species occupy extremely small distributions (under 5% of the basin), and several others (e.g., speckled dace, longfin dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker) retain relatively large distributions.
- Native species with the highest declines included big-river species such as the Colorado pikeminnow, razorback sucker, bonytail, and flannelmouth sucker, along with spring- and stream-dwelling species such as the Gila topminnow.

**Relationship between decline and extirpation risk**
- Greater distributional decline was significantly and positively related to local extirpation probability (P < 0.001).
- Species with smaller present-day distributions had higher estimated local extirpation risk (quadratic relationship, P < 0.001).
- Some exceptions, such as the desert pupfish, spikedace, and desert sucker, had lower extirpation risk than their declines predicted.

**Conservation status**
- Endangered species had significantly greater declines and smaller extant distributions than threatened species, which in turn showed greater declines and smaller distributions than unlisted species.
- Exceptions suggest candidates for future listing (e.g., three non-listed species identified as possible candidates) and species that may warrant reclassification.

**Non-native species**
- The fastest-expanding invaders were red shiner, fathead minnow, green sunfish, largemouth bass, western mosquitofish, and channel catfish; these are also among the most damaging to native fish communities.
- Fathead minnow, green sunfish, and red shiner showed spread rates exceeding 50 km/year.
- Species introduced between about 1920 and 1950 spread fastest and occupy the largest distributions; species introduced after 1950 generally spread more slowly.
- Blue tilapia and flathead catfish, the two most recent invaders, showed very high spread rates despite limited distributions, likely reflecting unauthorized or aquarium-trade introductions.

**Comparisons and conclusions**
- Comparisons with [[Great Plains Streams]] in Wyoming, Oklahoma, and Kansas show similar invasion patterns, but spread rates differ, likely because of differences in human introduction history.
- The authors recommend [[Drainage-Scale Monitoring]] as a coarse surrogate for identifying reach-scale extirpation risk, and suggest that management of declining species should occur before extreme rarity.
- They also recommend targeted education to limit future human-assisted introductions, and note that ESA sampling effort and definitions of rarity can bias listing decisions.
