# Organochlorine Insecticide Residues in Fish and Atmospheric Monitoring of Field-Applied Pesticides

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Pesticides Monitoring Journal issue (Vol. 3, No. 3, December 1969) contains two papers. The main paper reports the first year-and-a-half of the [[National Pesticide Monitoring Program]] fish survey, in which whole-fish composites from 50 U.S. stations were analyzed for 11 [[Organochlorine Insecticides]] in spring and fall 1967 and 1968. [[DDT]] and its metabolites were found in 584 of 590 composites, and [[Dieldrin]] in about 75%. The authors conclude that some waters consistently hold high residues, but that laboratory variability limits the precision of the data. The second paper describes a field system for measuring atmospheric pesticide concentrations and shows that [[Endrin]] volatilized substantially after application to sugar cane.

## Background & Description

**Fish monitoring program.** The survey was prompted by a 1963 President's Science Advisory Committee recommendation for a continuing pesticide residue monitoring network. Its stated objectives were (1) a nationwide assessment of general residue levels and (2) identification of problem areas. It was run by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], Division of Fishery Services.

**Sampling design.** Stations were located in the Great Lakes and major river systems, many near Bureau facilities and near water-monitoring sites of other participants. Field staff were to collect three composites per station, each of five uniform adult fish of one species, in spring (April–May) and fall (October–November). Preferred indicator species were carp, buffalo, black bass, channel catfish, green sunfish, yellow perch, rainbow trout and squawfish; suckers and other catfish were added when preferred species were hard to obtain. Chemical poisons were prohibited. Samples were frozen in foil, shipped on dry ice, and analyzed as wet-weight whole fish.

**Laboratories and methods.** Seven laboratories (A–G) were used across the study. Five were commercial laboratories and two were Bureau laboratories. Methods were adapted from the [[Food and Drug Administration]] Pesticide Analytical Manual and included acetonitrile partitioning, [[Florisil Cleanup]] and [[Gas Chromatography]] with electron capture detection. Reported detection sensitivities varied from 0.001 to 0.1 ppm. No laboratory corrected results for recovery except where a stated correction factor was applied.

**Air monitoring system.** The second paper describes a monitoring system built from a stainless steel boom with regularly spaced ports, a regulating valve, a vapor trap, a pressure-vacuum pump and a flow meter. It is designed for field measurement of [[Volatilization]] of pesticides and other agricultural chemicals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Residue patterns.** Some stations were consistently high or low regardless of laboratory or species, which suggests site-specific contamination. The authors link high-residue waters to agricultural drainages and to highly industrialized areas.
- **Edible versus whole-fish basis.** Residues were reported per whole fish. Residues in fat would be roughly tenfold higher, and much of the accumulated residue may lie in portions humans do not eat. These portions may still be hazardous to fish-eating birds and other wildlife.
- **Food-chain relevance.** The indicator species chosen are near the top of the food chain, so they integrate residues from lower trophic levels.
- **Atmospheric loss.** Volatilization is proposed as one explanation for the unaccounted-for pesticide losses in balance-sheet studies of pesticide persistence.
- **Regulatory benchmark.** The [[Food and Drug Administration]] interim guideline for total DDT in interstate fish shipments is 5 ppm.

## Key Findings

**Fish residues (590 composite samples, 50 stations):**
- DDT and metabolites were found in 584 composites (all but 6). Five of the six were from Station 50 in Alaska. Maximum total DDT was 45 ppm.
- DDT exceeded 1.0 ppm (station means 1.0–16.0 ppm) at Stations 3, 4, 18, 20, 21 and 39 in all sampling periods. Stations 14, 16 and 30 exceeded 5 ppm in the October 1968 data.
- Mean DDT in fall 1968 exceeded the 5 ppm FDA guideline at 8 of 50 stations.
- [[Dieldrin]] was found in about 75% of samples, with a maximum of 1.94 ppm. Station means above 0.1 ppm occurred at Stations 2, 4, 26 and 31 in three of four periods.
- [[Heptachlor]] and/or heptachlor epoxide were found in 32% of samples, with levels above 0.1 ppm at Stations 2, 3, 18 and 24.
- [[Chlordane]] was found in 22% of samples. Station 26 exceeded 0.1 ppm in three of four periods.
- [[Lindane]] was found in 16% of samples, usually below 0.1 ppm, and was not consistent at any station.
- [[Endrin]] was consistent only at Stations 15, 28 and 30, at levels below 0.1 ppm.
- [[Aldrin]] was not consistent at any station. Most values above 0.1 ppm came from one laboratory that had also reported high cross-check values.

**Collection performance:**
- Only 9 stations yielded the same three species in all four sampling periods. Forty-one of 50 stations yielded at least one species in every period.
- 62 species appeared in the 590 composites. Many were represented at only one to three stations.
- Carp (97 samples, 34 stations), catfish (105 samples, 31 stations), suckers (111 samples, 35 stations) and black bass (61 samples, 22 stations) were the most widely collected groups.
- Carp, channel catfish, white perch and lake trout were more often in the high-residue category. Bullheads and bluegills were more often low.

**Laboratory variability:**
- Fall 1967 cross-checks showed inconsistencies. Laboratory A reported low DDT complex values, and Laboratory B's abnormal DDT ratios made its results questionable.
- Spring 1968 cross-checks were more consistent but still showed differences. Laboratory E reported lower and Laboratory F higher DDT-complex results.
- The authors concluded that cross-check data did not justify a correction factor. They recommended one laboratory for trend analysis until standardization is achieved.
- One laboratory found [[Polychlorinated Biphenyls]] (PCBs), which it attributed to bottle-cap liners.

**Seasonal and temporal patterns:**
- Total DDT and heptachlor appeared higher in fall than spring, and 1968 levels appeared higher than 1967. Dieldrin appeared higher in spring. Given laboratory variability, the authors reached no definite seasonal or annual conclusion.
- Comparison with the [[Massachusetts Pesticide Monitoring Study]] suggests DDT increased over 1965–1967. The Wisconsin survey could not establish trends.

**Main conclusions:**
- Several waters consistently hold fish with high DDT, dieldrin and other organochlorine residues.
- Residue variation between samples is large and may reflect laboratory error or fish movement, diet, species, size, age and fat content.
- Future work should use one laboratory and the same indicator species at each station, measure lipid content, and add stations for wider coverage.
- Fish data may later be correlated with residues in water, soil and waterfowl.

**Air monitoring study:**
- Field use of the boom-based system showed that volatilization could be a significant factor in the net loss of endrin applied to sugar cane.
- Mean atmospheric endrin peaked at 540 ng/m³ during the 3 days after application. It declined asymptotically to 30 ng/m³ by 77 days later.
