# Pre-Impoundment Aquatic and Riparian Biota of the Central Arizona Project Reservoir Sites, c. 1800 to 1890

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #historical-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1972 prologue report, prepared by W. L. Minckley of [[Arizona State University]] for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], reconstructs the environmental conditions and biota of four proposed [[Central Arizona Project]] reservoir sites (Orme, Buttes, Charleston and Hooker) as they existed roughly between 1800 and 1890. The author argues that stream incision, habitat loss, and introduction of non-native organisms have changed these systems radically, so that impact reports based on present-day conditions establish a baseline only after substantial change has already occurred. The document is largely negative in tone, is qualitative rather than numerical, and serves as a historical framing for four more detailed reports requested from Arizona State University personnel.

## Background & Description

The report draws on diary accounts of the [[Army of the West]] (1846-47), the [[Mormon Battalion]], oral testimony from long-time residents, early photographs, and botanical and faunal literature. Its scope is intentionally broad. Fishes are the only group with a relatively complete historical record of population declines; vegetation is documented quantitatively in [[Hastings and Turner]] (1965); other groups are treated as assemblages inferred from dispersal patterns, habitat preferences and food plants. The author notes that the bibliography is extensive and that most references are not cited in the text.

The four reservoir sites differ in setting:

- **[[Orme Reservoir Site]]**: near the base level of the [[Verde River]] at its confluence with the [[Salt River]], about 15-20 miles upstream of the Salt-Gila confluence. Historically a mosaic of marshes (cienegas), backwaters, and beaver ponds.
- **[[Buttes Reservoir Site]]**: located in central Arizona canyons of the [[Gila River]], with sparse documentation. Accounts suggest mostly barren sand and gravel bars and little phreatophytic vegetation.
- **[[Charleston Reservoir Site]]**: on the [[San Pedro River]], with the most extensive photographic and written record. Grassland extended nearly to the river's edge, and beaver-dammed pools supported fish.
- **[[Hooker Reservoir Site]]**: in montane canyon environments at higher elevation, with a diverse gallery forest of walnut, alder, sycamore and mesquite, and the least human impact.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author identifies four major categories of change:

1. **Stream incision**: a brief period of arroyo cutting around 1890, attributed to a combination of drier, warmer climate, overgrazing, and a shift from frequent small rains to large storms. Incision lowered water tables and drained cienega habitats, confining cienega-adapted organisms to remote refugia.
2. **Replacement of vegetation**: cottonwood, mesquite and acacia gallery forests and terrace woodlands replaced sedge, cattail, reed and willow marsh communities.
3. **Introduction and invasion of non-native species**, including [[Salt Cedar]], [[Bermuda Grass]], the [[Asiatic Clam]], starlings, and European sparrows.
4. **Direct human alteration**, including impoundment, hydroelectric and irrigation regulation, mining, cattle ranching and recreation.

Cienega ecosystems supported marsh plants, invertebrates (rotifers, crustaceans, snails, mayflies, beetles, dipterans), waterfowl, furbearers (beaver, muskrat), and aquatic reptiles such as musk turtles and garter snakes. Documented malarial incidence in military installations along the San Pedro is cited as indirect evidence of extensive cienega habitat.

## Key Findings

- Environmental conditions before about 1890 were markedly different: water tables were higher and extensive marshlands existed along many reaches, supporting a far greater proportion of aquatic and mesic plants and animals than today.
- **Fish decline is the best-documented change.** The [[Razorback Sucker]] was abundant enough in the upper San Pedro to be commercially fished and sold in Tombstone before stream incision, and was still present in Peckrs Lake in the 1950s. The [[Giant Squawfish]] (reported to reach 5-6 feet and nearly 100 pounds) also occurred in the upper Verde River, evidenced by Indian-site remains and local testimony. Both are now extinct in central Arizona.
- Introduced fishes outnumber native species in Arizona by at least 2 to 1, and many pose management problems.
- The [[Tiger Salamander]] persisted in Arizona as distinct subspecies, but importation for bait has diluted native populations through genetic interchange.
- The softshell turtle (*Trionyx spiniferus*) is the only introduced aquatic turtle that has become widespread and abundant throughout the lower Colorado system.
- Each reservoir site has changed to varying degrees. Charleston has developed a new Chihuahuan Desert-type vegetation after intense 1800s ranching and mining. Hooker, draining wilderness, has felt the least direct human impact.
- The author concludes that specific impact assessments must account for this historical baseline, and that future reports should include positive as well as negative impacts.
