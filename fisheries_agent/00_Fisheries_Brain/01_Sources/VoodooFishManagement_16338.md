# Trout Unlimited Spring 1991 Newsletter: Salmon Decline, Habitat Policy and Chapter Action

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Spring 1991 issue of the [[Trout Unlimited]] newsletter is a mixed advocacy publication. Its lead article, "Voodoo Fish Management," argues that wild Columbia River salmon have declined by about 97 percent, that habitat destruction (dams, logging, livestock grazing and beaver loss) is the root cause, and that agencies rely on ineffective in-channel "stream improvement" instead of stopping abuse. Other articles cover proposed amendments to the [[Clean Water Act]], reform of the [[Mining Law of 1872]], protection under the [[Wild and Scenic Rivers Act]], wetlands policy, a hydropower licensing challenge, and beaver management. The issue also contains chapter project reports, donor lists, membership information and advertisements. No original research data are presented; figures are drawn from agency reports and organizational sources as cited in the text.

## Background & Description

The lead article opens with the observation of [[Lewis and Clark]] on the Columbia River, where salmon were abundant and large dried-fish harvests supported Indigenous peoples from across the continent. Wild salmon runs in the Columbia are described as having declined by about 97 percent.

In spring 1990 the American Fisheries Society, Oregon Trout and others petitioned the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] to list five salmon runs under the [[Endangered Species Act]]:
- Spring, summer and fall [[Chinook Salmon]] (king salmon)
- [[Snake River Sockeye]]
- Lower Columbia River [[Coho Salmon]]

On April 2 the agency proposed listing Snake River sockeye as endangered, with a decision on the other four runs due in June. The article notes that no fish species listed under the Act has ever been removed because of population recovery; the only delisting so far has been through extinction.

Key historical milestones cited:
- The record Chinook catch occurred in 1883; all salmon and [[Steelhead]] peaked early in the 20th century, and by 1920 all species were in steady decline.
- Major dams were built from 1933 onward (Rock Island, Bonneville in 1938, Grand Coulee in 1941).
- The [[Mitchell Act]] (1938) promised mitigation through [[Hatchery Production]] but received no funding until 1948.

The article characterizes the 1800s near-extermination of [[North American Beaver]] by Hudson's Bay Company trappers as a likely trigger of habitat loss, since beaver dams trapped sediment, held water on the land, raised water tables and created nursery pools.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dams and migration.** Fish bound for the Clearwater River in Idaho must climb 830 feet of fish ladders over four Columbia and three Snake River dams, passing about 400 miles of largely slack water. Seaward-bound smolts face disorientation, high predation by [[Northern Pikeminnow]] in reservoirs, and mortality from turbines and spillways.

**Livestock grazing.** Quoting [[Ray White]], the article calls overgrazing "North America's foremost trout habitat abuse." Grazing affects 525 million acres in the 11 western states (70 percent of total land area). The [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) and [[U.S. Forest Service]] (USFS) supervise grazing on 260 million acres of public wildlands in the West.

**Logging and streambed disturbance.** Clearcutting to the water's edge, heavy equipment in streambeds and dragging logs through streams are described as causing most damage. These practices increased erosion, buried spawning gravels in sediment, dropped water tables and reduced summer flows.

**Beaver.** Beaver are presented as essential habitat engineers. Their loss is linked to erosion and the drying of permanent streams.

**Regional stream conditions.** The text reports that 68 percent of BLM lands are in less than good condition, and an [[Environmental Protection Agency]] (EPA) report cited that riparian areas across much of the West were in "the worst condition in history" in the late 1980s.

**Water quality.** EPA identified 17,000 waterbodies as seriously polluted by annual inputs of billions of pounds of pollutants, many toxic. Hardrock mining has contaminated more than 10,000 miles of American rivers (per the Bureau of Mines), and the 100-mile Clark Fork River in Montana is the nation's largest [[Superfund]] hazardous-waste cleanup site.

## Key Findings

**1. Habitat abuse, not only harvest, drives salmon decline.** The article argues that the current "conventional wisdom" blaming overfishing and dams is incomplete. Logging, grazing and beaver loss are presented as equally important. Agencies have "chosen alternative actions...amounting to little more than voodoo fish management."

**2. Grazing is undervalued and underpriced.**
- In 1990 the [[Whitehorse Ranch]] (R.E. Nafzger, Vale BLM District) paid $1.81 per month per cow-calf pair, totaling $19,870.
- Of this, 12.5 percent went to local governments in lieu of taxes and 50 percent to local "range improvements," leaving $7,451 returned to the U.S. Treasury.
- This was for grazing 126,982 acres, less than 6 cents per acre per year.
- Federal agencies collect about 45 cents per month for grazing a mile of public stream in the interior West.
- Only 22,000 ranchers use western public lands, which supply about 2 percent of the nation's beef.

**3. The Willow-Whitehorse trout case illustrates failed in-channel projects.** BLM projects in the 1970s (willow planting, 49 trash-collector dams and fencing) largely failed: by 1980 nearly all willows were gone, flooding destroyed 60 percent of dams, and gates were left open. The trout population has fallen 80 percent, and the BLM proposed spending $400,000 on wells, pipelines and fencing. Critic [[George Wuerthner]] argued that ending grazing on the allotment would be cheaper and yield more public benefit.

**4. Camp Creek shows the same problem.** A USFS project on Camp Creek (Umatilla National Forest) built rock-log weirs and fencing, but a fisheries biologist said the creek "would be better off if it had simply been fenced and closed to cattle."

**5. Prevention is the priority.** Ray White's position is that prevention of abuse comes first; once abuse stops, nature usually repairs streams at no public cost. In-channel "enhancement" should follow only after full restoration, and enhancing instead of protecting streams is "ridiculous."

**6. Economic value of sport fishing.** The Sport Fishing Institute reports that western national forests alone provide nearly 12 million angler-days per year, with anglers spending more than $1.1 billion, supporting 32,654 jobs and placing a value of more than $1.34 billion on their trips. Between 50 and 70 percent of Columbia Basin salmon habitat is on Forest Service land. The article rebuts Senator Bob Packwood's suggestion that some salmon species may need to disappear.

**7. Clean Water Act amendments are pending.** Key issues identified by [[Charles F. Gauvin]] and others include:
- [[Nonpoint Source Pollution]]: EPA calls it among the nation's most significant water quality problems, and it is hard to regulate under traditional permits.
- [[Irrigation Return Flows]]: Currently exempt from Clean Water Act discharge requirements.
- [[Whole-Effluent Toxicity]] testing: EPA requires it, but municipal dischargers want enforceability weakened.
- [[Hydropower]] impacts: Dams can reduce dissolved oxygen and damage downstream habitat in low flows. Proposed legislation would require state certification before [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] licenses are issued.
- [[Wetlands Protection]]: Trout Unlimited opposes a proposed exemption of saturated wetlands and recent legislation favoring development. Its letter to President Bush warned against retreat from the "no net loss" pledge.

**8. Mining law reform.** Hardrock minerals (gold, silver, lead, uranium, copper) are governed by the 1872 law, which allows mining without royalties and without federal cleanup funds. Representative Nick Rahall's H.R. 918 would end patenting of public lands and allow agencies to deny mining applications. Senator Dale Bumpers is gathering cosponsors for a similar Senate bill that would also impose royalties and reclamation standards.

**9. Wild and Scenic Rivers protection must allow restoration.** TU's Bill Maxon argues that the 1968 Act's "permanent protection" language does not specifically permit habitat improvement, and that the Michigan Rivers legislation should explicitly allow existing and future repair projects.

**10. Beaver management is a recurring theme.**
- In Wisconsin, the Green Bay and Marinette County chapters funded removal of 417 beaver and 712 dams along about 200 miles of water from April 1 to mid-October 1990. The Nicolet National Forest was estimated to hold 3,284 beaver (one active colony per 1.8 miles).
- A [[Clemson University]] study, funded by a $10,000-per-year USFS challenge grant, will examine beaver effects on trout in southern Appalachian streams, where beaver can warm water and convert coldwater fisheries to warmwater ones.

**11. Litigation and administrative action.**
- In North Carolina, Judge Joseph R. John awarded $39,016 to the state for restoration of Laurel Branch, damaged by sediment from a golf-course development. TU helped secure consultant [[Jim Gracie]] (Brightwater Consulting), whose testimony on the Rosgen classification and USFS "silt suckers" persuaded the court.
- In Kentucky, a federal judge barred Jamestown from spending HUD funds on a pipeline carrying industrial waste into Lake Cumberland until a hearing or an Environmental Impact Statement. The Lake Cumberland Trust estimates the effort may cost $250,000.
- The [[Bull Shoals Dam]] (White River, Arkansas) released low-oxygen water in October 1990. Dissolved oxygen measured 2.7 ppm below the dam, while trout need at least 5.0 ppm and the state standard is 6.0 ppm. An estimated 1,200 to 1,500 dead trout were found in one mid-October survey. The state issued an emergency order, and the Corps agreed to reduce generation and vent turbines. The fishery is valued at $143 million per year.
- Deep Creek (Utah) hydropower: A coalition led by Utah TU challenged FERC's extension of preconstruction time for a project that would destroy about 10,000 feet of perennial stream and riparian wetlands. Birch and Trout creeks host the [[Bonneville Cutthroat]], under consideration for listing as threatened.

**12. Chapter and organizational activity.**
- The [[Embrace-A-Stream Program]] (established 1976) funded 15 projects in 1991, with recipients generally matching grants with cash, materials and volunteer labor. Examples include fish ladders in Wyoming, a Washington watershed park, and stream restoration in Colorado, Arizona and Missouri.
- The Goldsborough Creek Sustainable Watershed Park (Mason County Chapter) will use land leased for $1 for 10 years, with five acres of streamside property, an environmental learning center and educational programs for the CHOICE alternative high school.
- The Tulpehocken Chapter's "Water Works Project" aims to create fish habitat in the creek near the Western Berks Water Treatment Plant for $25,000, with about 75 percent in hand.
- The Catskill Waterkeeper position is estimated at $60,000 per year for three years.
- The 1990 National Conservation Banquet Program raised $412,654 from 92 chapters.
- The [[Living Brightwater Trust]] (established 1984) sets trustee levels from $2,500 (Associate) to $50,000 (Sustaining), with the endowed share rising from 10 percent in 1987-88 to 50 percent in 1991-92 and 100 percent by 1997.
- Seven new chapters were chartered in March 1991, including Indiana's first active chapter.
- The 32nd TU National Convention is scheduled for Portland, Maine, August 21-24, 1991.
- National Fishing Week runs June 3-9, 1991.
- Colorado TU is lobbying for a 15-year state fisheries plan emphasizing wild trout, stream classification, access, habitat and hatchery cost-effectiveness; CDOW will decide on final issues in July.
- The Second Western Regional Instream Flow Conference is planned for Jackson Hole, October 2-3, 1992, with $75,000 from the Bureau of Reclamation.
