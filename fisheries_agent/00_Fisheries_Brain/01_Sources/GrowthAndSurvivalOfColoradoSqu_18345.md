# Predation by Largemouth Bass on Stocked Colorado Squawfish in Riverside Gravel-Pit Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 Utah State University M.S. thesis by Douglas B. Osmundson examines how introduced [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) predation affects survival and growth of hatchery-reared [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) stocked into five flooded [[Gravel-Pit Ponds]] adjacent to the Colorado River near Grand Junction, Colorado. The study combined pond field work (stomach analysis, mark-recapture, removal seining, growth sampling) with laboratory prey-selection experiments. Bass switched rapidly to squawfish after stocking, and survival ranged from 0 to 52% depending on pond and stocking event. The author recommends a moratorium on further largemouth bass introductions and suggests that predator-free riverside ponds could serve as low-cost grow-out sites for squawfish.

## Background & Description

The Colorado Squawfish is a piscivorous cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River system, capable of exceeding 150 cm and the largest native minnow in North America. It was once abundant but is now rare in the upper basin and extinct in the lower basin. It was listed as endangered in 1967 and protected under the 1973 Endangered Species Act. Research by the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] indicated unusually high juvenile mortality, making early survival a priority.

Young squawfish (larvae 10-29 mm; young-of-the-year 30-64 mm) occupy shallow, warm backwaters. These habitats are also used by introduced centrarchids, including largemouth bass, green sunfish, bluegill and black crappie, which were not part of the species' evolutionary history. Largemouth bass are often listed as "abundant" or "rare" depending on classification criteria, a discrepancy the author attributes partly to unequal sampling of habitat types.

Gravel-pit ponds, created by floodplain gravel mining and filled by groundwater seepage and river flooding, were chosen because they provide a closed system in which mortality can be distinguished from emigration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area:** Five ponds (Terry, Island, Pabco, T & F, Fish Chalet) lying 155-166 river miles above the Colorado-Green confluence. Elevation 1356-1378 m; annual precipitation about 20.3 cm. Riparian vegetation is saltcedar and Russian-olive with a Fremont cottonwood overstory.

**Pond morphometry (August 1983):** Surface area ranged from 0.85 ha (Terry) to 3.16 ha (Island); mean depth from 1.7 m (Pabco) to 3.7 m (Island). Fish Chalet never flooded after 1983, retaining high conductivity (3,800-4,580 µmhos).

**Physical conditions:** Mid-depth temperatures peaked at 25-29 °C in summer; ponds froze from December 1983 to late March 1984. Dissolved oxygen fell to about 3 mg/L at times in some ponds. Maximum specific conductance in flooded ponds reached 1,540-2,270 µmhos; estimated TDS in Fish Chalet ranged 2,050-3,450 mg/L.

**Forage community:** Forage abundance varied up to 20-fold among ponds and was negatively related to bass density. Terry Pond had the fewest forage fish (about 25-27 per seine haul), while T & F had the most (563-1,345). Fathead minnows, red shiners, mosquitofish, green sunfish and young-of-year carp were the principal forage species.

**Predators:** Bass densities were highest at Terry Pond; T & F had few bass; Fish Chalet had no bass but about 50-60 channel catfish.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and design**
- Squawfish (40-145 mm TL) were stocked at 3,090/ha on 13 Oct 1983, 24 Jul 1984 and 31 Oct 1984 (about 2,625-9,750 fish per pond per event).
- Three laboratory prey-type experiments (fathead minnow, green sunfish, red shiner) and one prey-size experiment were run using three bass size-classes.

**Diet switching**
- Bass switched to an almost exclusive squawfish diet immediately after each stocking; for example, squawfish made up 100% of Pabco stomach contents one and three days after the July 1984 stocking.
- Squawfish disappeared from bass diets within roughly 2-5 weeks, except after the fall 1984 stocking at Terry Pond, where they persisted for about a month.
- One large bass had eaten 23 squawfish.

**Survival (removal seining)**
- Fall 1983 stocking: Terry 0.0%; Island 2.4%; Pabco 49-52% (recovered about 30 weeks later); T & F 0.04%; Fish Chalet 7-9%.
- Summer 1984 stocking: Terry 0.0%; Pabco 0.05%; T & F 0.3%; Fish Chalet 42-46% after 63 days.
- Fall 1984 stocking: Terry 0.0%; Pabco 0.3%; T & F 0.0%; Fish Chalet 0.2%.
- The author estimated that bass consumed 611-2,190 squawfish at Terry Pond (23-83% of stocked numbers) within about two weeks.

**Size-selective predation**
- Binomial chi-square tests rejected random predation in six of nine pooled cases (P < 0.05). Small squawfish were eaten more often than large in most cases.
- In the laboratory, small bass avoided large prey, while large bass took a larger share of prey by weight.
- In the field, size-related survival was not consistently detected; in several ponds both size-classes suffered near-total mortality.

**Laboratory prey-type preference (4 trials × 3 bass sizes, 7 days each)**
- Fathead minnow vs. squawfish: no significant preference (mean 53.3% squawfish by number).
- Green sunfish vs. squawfish: bass preferred squawfish (83.4% by number; 81.9% by weight).
- Red shiner vs. squawfish: bass preferred squawfish (71.7% by number and weight).
- Under pond conditions, fathead minnows did not buffer predation on squawfish.

**Prey-size selection (7 trials)**
- Small squawfish (50-65 mm) were preferred by number (66.1%), but larger prey contributed substantially by weight.
- Daily food intake by weight fell from 4.08% of body weight in small bass to 1.13% in large bass.

**Maximum prey size (Lawrence 1958 model)**
- Theoretically, bass can swallow squawfish of body depth equal to mouth width, which was judged not to be the limiting factor for large bass.
- The author concludes that squawfish of about 230-250 mm TL would be essentially invulnerable to largemouth bass predation; the largest prey recorded was a 93 mm squawfish eaten by a 131 mm bass.

**Growth**
- Fish Chalet squawfish stocked in fall 1983 grew to a mean 226 mm (gain of 154 mm in about five months). The author estimated this at more than twice the rate reported for river fish by [[Seethaler (1978)]].
- Growth began when mid-depth water temperature exceeded about 13 °C, which the author proposes as a zero-growth threshold.
- Pond growing season (176 days above 13 °C in 1984) exceeded the river (mean 155 days); accumulated degree-days were 1,566 in the pond versus 873 in the river.
- Summer-stocked squawfish grew slowly across all ponds.

**Other mortality factors**
- Three parasites were recorded: anchorworm (*Lernaea* sp.), Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus opsarichthydis*), a ciliated gill protozoan. The tapeworm was the first report in the upper Colorado River basin.
- Channel catfish, green sunfish and black crappie were also observed to prey on squawfish at low levels.
- Predation was judged the main mortality factor in high-bass ponds; disease, condition loss, and winter stress were implicated where bass were scarce.

**Management recommendations**
- A moratorium on further largemouth bass introductions to the river system.
- Discourage additional stockings of non-native predators; keep natural spring flooding; secure gravel-pit dikes to limit escapes.
- Riverside ponds may serve as low-maintenance grow-out sites if predator-free, seinable, and supplied with self-sustaining forage (e.g., common carp or fathead minnow).
- Stock fingerlings in early spring when water exceeds 13 °C and release at 230-250 mm, possibly in fall of the same year.
