# Revised Biological Opinion on CAP Water Transfer and Nonnative Aquatic Species in the Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum (dated April 17, 2001) is a revised [[Biological Opinion]] under section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It evaluates whether transporting and delivering [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water through the Gila River basin, excluding the Santa Cruz subbasin, is likely to introduce and spread nonnative aquatic species that threaten nine listed species. Reinitiation was requested by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] after a court found the 1994 opinion's amendments arbitrary and capricious, citing delays in barrier construction and take exceeding 1994 expectations for spikedace and loach minnow.

The Service concluded that CAP, with the proposed conservation measures, is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of the listed species, nor to destroy or adversely modify designated critical habitat. The opinion is a programmatic, 100-year analysis built on fish barriers, electrical barrier upgrades, monitoring, recovery and nonnative control funding, and public education.

## Background & Description

**Species considered.** Eight fish and one bird were addressed: [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] (threatened), [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]] (endangered), [[Razorback Sucker]] (threatened), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (endangered), [[Gila Trout]] (endangered), [[Apache Trout]] (endangered, reclassified threatened in 1975), and [[Bald Eagle]] (threatened). [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]], a proposed threatened species, was excluded and handled separately.

**Project scope.** CAP takes water from Lake Havasu on the [[Colorado River]] and conveys it 336 miles (540 km) across Arizona. The biological opinion covers the 249-mile (400 km) reach from the Lake Havasu Pumping Plant to the Casa Grande Extension turnout. The reach has 37 turnouts, five pumping plants, three tunnels and eight inverted siphons. Between 1994 and 2001, four new turnouts were added. Deliveries are expected to approach 1.5 million acre-feet per year and about 88 users receive water. The project life is 100 years.

**Consultation history.** Informal consultation on the Pima Lateral Feeder Canal began in 1986 and formal consultation started February 12, 1991. The 1994 opinion found jeopardy for spikedace, loach minnow, Gila topminnow and razorback sucker. Litigation followed (consolidated in 1997), and a September 22, 2000 court order upheld the 1994 reasonable and prudent alternative but found later amendments arbitrary and capricious. Formal reinitiation began January 3, 2001, and a draft opinion was issued April 3, 2001.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical change.** Before European arrival, the Gila basin's perennial flow connected its rivers, and extirpated populations were recolonized from nearby areas. Livestock grazing, croplands, roads, diversions and dams then fragmented the system and reduced perennial flow by the mid-1900s. Native fishes became isolated in small headwater areas. Beaver were nearly eliminated.

**Nonnative threat.** Since common carp were introduced in 1885, at least 50 nonnative fish species have been recorded in the Gila basin, with 10 to 15 more incidental records. Nonnative invertebrates, amphibians, reptiles, plants and disease organisms are also present. Mechanisms include sport stocking, bait-bucket releases, aquarium release, biocontrol and interbasin transfers. The opinion describes nonnative species as among the most serious and rapidly growing environmental problems, and states that control of nonnative warmwater species is "the single most important requirement" for native fish survival.

**CAP as a "highway."** The aqueduct reconnects fragmented habitats. The opinion finds this benefits nonnative species, not natives. CAP has already moved striped bass into the Gila basin, and Asian clam is likely in the Santa Cruz subbasin. Grass carp and mosquitofish were introduced into CAP-connected features for biological control. A proposal to introduce black carp was dropped after objections.

**Species status (range-wide and within the action area).**
- Spikedace: range reduced about 90%. Common only in Aravaipa Creek and parts of the upper Gila in New Mexico.
- Loach minnow: range reduced about 85%. Common only in Aravaipa Creek, the Blue River and parts of the San Francisco, upper Gila and Tularosa Rivers.
- Gila topminnow: 12 remnant populations range-wide. Within the action area, only three natural populations remain, at Bylas Springs, contaminated with mosquitofish.
- Razorback sucker: extirpated from the Gila basin by 1960. Reintroductions have had very low survival and no documented reproduction.
- Desert pupfish: extirpated from the Gila basin by the mid-1900s. Two stocked populations exist in the action area, neither considered secure.
- Colorado squawfish: extirpated from the basin by 1970. Experimental nonessential populations in the Salt and Verde Rivers have had very low survival.
- Gila trout: good and improving range-wide, following removal of rainbow trout.
- Apache trout: good and improving, with 12 natural and nine replicated populations in the action area.
- Bald eagle: about 42 territories in Arizona and about 4 pairs in New Mexico. The status has improved but still needs intensive management. Thirty-nine of the Arizona territories lie in the Gila basin.

**Giant salvinia.** This floating plant was introduced into the Colorado River and has a very high likelihood of entering the CAP aqueduct. It threatens the bald eagle's prey-capture habitat, including at Lake Pleasant.

## Key Findings

**Conservation measures (the reasonable and prudent alternative, reorganized into five elements):**

1. *Upstream barriers.* Paired drop barriers on Aravaipa Creek (completed by mid-April 2001). A single drop barrier on the San Pedro River, due by March 2006. Electrical barrier upgrades on SRP and Florence-Casa Grande canals, with completion dates from December 31, 2001 to August 31, 2002. A recovery barrier program with all barriers due by March 2016, at least two per five-year period. Sites include the Verde River, Fossil Creek, Bonita Creek, Hot Springs or Redfield Canyon, the Blue River and Tonto Creek.
2. *Monitoring.* Annual fish monitoring of the CAP aqueduct, SRP canals, Florence-Casa Grande Canal, and several Salt, Gila, San Pedro and Aravaipa reaches. New nonnative detections must be reported to the Service within 5 days.
3. *Conservation of native fishes funding.* $250,000 per year for 21 years, plus 0 to 20% Service overhead.
4. *Control and management against nonnatives.* Also $250,000 per year for 21 years, plus overhead. A portion is held for barrier design, and funds may be reallocated for emergencies.
5. *Information and education.* A 5-year program with AGFD, aimed at bait-bucket transfers and pet dumping.

**Effects.** The Service expects that spikedace, loach minnow, Gila topminnow, razorback sucker and bald eagle will be seriously affected by CAP-mediated nonnatives. Apache trout and Gila trout are not expected to suffer significant impacts because they are far from the aqueduct, above mainstem dams and have barriers. Desert pupfish risk is judged very small, though giant salvinia could reach its habitats. The conservation measures do not remove all threats: the middle Gila above Ashurst-Hayden Dam is directly connected to CAP, and monitoring and barriers address fish only.

**Delay finding.** Implementation delays from 1994 to 2000 may have modestly raised risk to listed species, but did not significantly reduce the alternative's capacity to remove jeopardy. Provided the new schedule holds, the measures are expected to suffice.

**Take.** Take cannot be quantified for nonnatives. Barrier construction take is indexed to the fish community. Take is considered exceeded if conservation measures are altered, more than 20 dead fish appear within 500 yards downstream during construction, a toxic spill occurs, or activities exceed the disturbance area. For bald eagles, triggers include barrier sites within 1 mile of active nests (January 1 to June 30) and helicopter use within 1 mile horizontal or 2,000 feet above nests.

**Conclusion.** The CAP, with conservation measures, is "not likely to jeopardize" the spikedace, loach minnow, Gila topminnow, razorback sucker, desert pupfish, Apache trout, Gila trout or bald eagle, and does not adversely modify their critical habitat. The Colorado squawfish experimental nonessential populations are also not likely to be jeopardized.

**Conservation recommendations.** The Service recommends additional barriers (for example on the East Fork White River, Babocomari River, Hassayampa River and Agua Fria River), annual dryup of CAP canals where feasible, opposition to new nonnative introductions into the Colorado basin, expanding the native fish fund to include bald eagle, a lower Verde River monitoring site, and monitoring of non-fish nonnatives.

**Reinitiation triggers.** Reinitiation is required if take is exceeded, new information reveals unforeseen effects, the action is modified, or a new species is listed or critical habitat designated.
