# Species of Concern in Pima County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1999 cover letter and draft discussion paper prepared by the [[Pima County Flood Control District]] and a University of Arizona researcher for the [[Sonoran Desert Conservation Plan]] (SDCP). The letter, from hydrologist David Scalero to [[Wendell Minckley]] (Arizona State University), asks him to check the fish content of the draft report "Species of Concern in Pima County, Arizona" and its revised species tables for accuracy.

The draft identifies species, plant communities and habitats for conservation beyond those already protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). Thirteen experts were interviewed across six taxa. Fish make up a small but important share of the lists, and freshwater habitat loss is identified as a primary driver of regional extinctions.

## Background & Description

The SDCP is a regional conservation effort with five stated goals, including promoting recovery of the [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy-Owl]] and obtaining a Section 10 permit for a multi-species conservation plan. Under the ESA, Section 9 prohibits the "take" of federally listed animals, and Section 10 allows incidental take permits when an adequate conservation plan exists.

Table 1 lists 18 federally listed threatened or endangered species in Pima County. It also lists four candidate species, two proposed species and one petitioned species. Fish on the federal lists are:

- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*): Endangered. Two subspecies are recognized: the Desert Pupfish and the Quitobaquito Pupfish.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*): Endangered. Known from Upper Cienega Creek and the Santa Cruz River near Tubac.
- **[[Gila Chub]]** (*Gila intermedia*): Candidate for listing. Found in scattered pools, springs, cienegas and streams, with multiple private landowners.

The SDCP is built around six elements: riparian restoration, ranch conservation, historical and cultural preservation, biological corridor conservation, mountain parks, and critical and sensitive habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Pima County spans two eco-regions. The central and western areas are low-elevation [[Sonoran Desert]] vegetation. The eastern area has high, forested [[Sky Islands]] surrounded by desert or grassland. The sky islands "nurture perennial streams with a unique fish fauna" and served as refuges during warm interglacial periods. Elevations range from 660 feet west of Ajo to 9,157 feet in the Santa Catalina Mountains.

Historically, many rivers flowed year-round. High water tables supported cottonwood, willow and mesquite forests along the [[Santa Cruz River]], Tanque Verde Creek, Agua Caliente Wash and Canada del Oro Wash. Groundwater pumping and floodplain development have since lowered these water tables. Several streams, notably the Santa Cruz River and Rillito Creek, now stop flowing for much of the year. Streams and springs have also been diverted or developed for human or livestock use in ways incompatible with native fish.

The draft states that aquatic and riparian habitats are "rapidly disappearing" and that diversion and desiccation have caused the extirpation of at least five fish species in Pima County. Primary threats are groundwater pumping and the establishment of exotic or "invader" species.

## Key Findings

**Methods and expert input**
- Thirteen experts were interviewed across six taxa: mammals, birds, herpetofauna, fish, invertebrates, and plants and plant communities.
- Fish expertise came from Dr. Wendell Minckley (materials faxed only, 3/24/99) and Mr. Jeff Simms of the Bureau of Land Management (interviewed 3/15/99).
- Interviews were held individually rather than in a workshop, because The Nature Conservancy had recently run an expert workshop for the same eco-region.
- Species were sorted into four status groups:
  - **Status 1:** in jeopardy in Pima County, with local habitat critical to overall survival.
  - **Status 2:** in jeopardy locally and generally declining throughout the range.
  - **Status 3:** in jeopardy locally but not at risk overall.
  - **Status 4:** not at risk locally but important for ecological or social reasons.

**Extirpated fish (Table 3)**
- Table 3 lists 13 species extirpated from Pima County. Three are fish: the [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignus*), the [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*) and the [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*).
- The suckers "may occur periodically" in the Santa Cruz River outside the county.
- The Speckled Dace entry carries a taxonomic caveat, and surveys are needed in Buehman Canyon.
- The report notes that a disproportionate share of extirpations came through loss of aquatic habitat.

**Fish of concern (Tables 4A–4D and Appendix B)**
- The [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*) appears in the Status 2 table, for species declining across their range.
- The [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) appears in Appendix B, a list of species with little conservation concern. It has a narrow distribution in the county and is reported to be doing well.
- No fish appear in the Status 1 or Status 4 tables.

**Exotic fish and aquatic fauna (Appendix C)**
- Exotic fish and aquatic species listed include the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), the [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*, Sabino Canyon), yellow and black bullheads, and the [[Crayfish]] (*Orconectes virilis* and others).
- The African clawed frog and bullfrog are also listed as aquatic exotics harmful to native fish and frogs.
- Exotic-species notes recommend efforts against bullfrogs and warn that the Tiger Salamander may damage pupfish and topminnow populations.

**Management issues**
- Taxonomic uncertainty is a problem. Snail records from the 1930s are considered poor, and some fish status assessments depend on further surveys.
- Under ESA Section 9, federal protection covers listed animals but not listed plants. The draft argues plants should therefore get high priority under the SDCP.
- The draft states that ESA regulations have hampered recovery of listed fish. It notes reintroduction of unlisted fish is easier than reintroduction of listed species, which are less common in the wild (Jeff Simms, personal communication). Pre-listing agreements and local or federal management are suggested as alternatives.
- Protecting habitat and the processes it depends on (such as floods and fire) is presented as more effective than species-by-species protection.

**Document status and next steps**
- The report is a draft dated April 29, 1999. The Science Technical Advisory Team (STAT) is to review and adopt a revised set of focal species, plant communities and habitats.
- Section 9.0, Recommendations, is marked "Reserved for the STAT" and is blank in this draft.
- The cover letter requested a review of fish species accuracy by Dr. Minckley, which suggests this note's fish content is provisional until that review is complete.
