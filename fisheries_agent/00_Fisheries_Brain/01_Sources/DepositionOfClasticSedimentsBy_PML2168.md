# Clam-Mediated Deposition of Clastic Sediments in the Delta-Mendota Canal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyrenidae

## Executive Summary

Prokopovich (1969) argues that filter-feeding bivalves, specifically the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*), contribute far more to sedimentation than their skeletal remains alone would suggest. Their "sticky" excreta and pseudofaeces bind suspended particles into deposits that accumulate as bars on the invert of the [[Delta-Mendota Canal]], a 113-mile [[Central Valley Project]] conveyance in California. The study used dewaterings, sediment analyses, and laboratory pumping-rate experiments to link clam abundance with sediment accumulation. The author notes that the fishery and vertebrate biota are not treated in this document; the focus is on benthic bivalves and sediment chemistry/texture.

## Background & Description

The Delta-Mendota Canal runs from the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] near Tracy to Mendota Pool on the San Joaquin River. Key physical characteristics:

- Length: 113 miles, of which 95 miles are concrete-lined and the downstream 18 miles earth-lined.
- Concrete section: about 100 ft wide at the top, average water depth 16 ft.
- Design capacity: about 2 million gpm; average water velocity 3.8 ft/s (1.2 m/s).
- Subdivided into pools by 21 check structures; placed in operation June 1951.
- Downstream reaches are affected by [[Deep Land Subsidence]], with total subsidence locally exceeding 5 ft from 1953 to 1966.

The canal's water is drawn from the Delta, which contains extensive peat and planktonic production, and is characterized by high turbidity. Canal biota are dominated by a few species present in very large populations, including amphipods, freshwater sponges, and clams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Benthic community:** Biological productivity and siltation far exceed those in other USBR canals. The most notable species is the accidentally introduced Asiatic clam, found on concrete surfaces, trashracks, screens, and most commonly in clam-bearing sediment deposits on the invert. Live clams occur only in the aerobic uppermost 1–2 inches of these deposits.
- **Abundance:** Live clam densities averaged on the order of 100 to 2000 per square foot in individual pools, varying across dewaterings.
- **Downstream gradient:** Clam infestation declined downstream, from about 1,100 (pool 1) to 2.5 (pool 18) clams per linear mile in 1965–66. Biomass and other filter-feeder indicators also decreased downstream, suggesting the Delta is the main food source for canal biota.
- **Sediment structure:** Bars ranged from a few tenths of a foot to over 3 ft thick and from under 100 ft to over 1000 ft long, often on the inside of canal turns. Aerobic surface layers (2–4 in) were light to moderate gray; deeper anaerobic layers were dark gray to olive black and contained dead shells.
- **Water quality:** Turbidity was generally higher at the upstream end than downstream, likely due to inflow of "dirty" water from numerous drains. Suspended sediment discharge reached roughly 10,000 tons per day in the 1959–60 season.

## Key Findings

- **Sediment composition:** Across some 200 samples from four dewaterings (1960–66), shells made up only about 20–30 percent of dry weight. Fine inorganic sand with shell fragments and peaty debris comprised 30–50 percent, and inorganic silt and clay 20–30 percent. Cumulative grain-size curves were consistent across samples.
- **Deposition estimate:** Deposits were described as 25–35 percent excreta-derived material, 30–50 percent sand, and about 30 percent clamshells.
- **Sedimentation rates:** Rates declined from roughly 1,000 yd³/mi/yr in pools 1–5 to about 9 yd³/mi/yr in pools 15–18 (figures as reported in the source). Rates were highest upstream and lowest in the subsiding reach, contradicting a simple gravity-settling expectation.
- **Correlation:** Clam population and biomass correlated roughly with sedimentation rates, which the author interprets as evidence that clams are a major factor in deposition.
- **Pumping rates:** Laboratory tests on Corbicula with a range of weights (approximately 0.9 to 23 g wet weight) gave clearance rates, with the most common size class (about 1 g) pumping roughly 2 cc/hr or about 0.5 liter/day, or about 180 liters/year. Pumping rates varied with clam size, sediment type, and food quality.
- **Excreta yield:** Using a conservative 30 ppm suspended load, a 1-gram clam was estimated to precipitate about 5.4 g of sediment per year. Pool 1 and 2 clams (1965) were estimated to produce about 3,350 tons of excreta per year (dry weight), and pool 18 about 92 tons per year. The author stresses these are order-of-magnitude estimates.
- **Implications:** The author proposes that biologically mediated deposition could be significant in natural streams, lakes, and basins with turbid water, and may have contributed to some shale deposits in the geologic past. The author states that present data are insufficient for a quantitative estimate, but the general trend appears well established.
