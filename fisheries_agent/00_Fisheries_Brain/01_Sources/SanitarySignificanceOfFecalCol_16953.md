# Sanitary Significance of Fecal Coliforms in the Environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #enterobacteriaceae

## Executive Summary

This Federal Water Pollution Control Administration report (Publication WP-20-3, November 1966), authored by E. E. Geldreich of the [[Cincinnati Water Research Laboratory]], compiles previously published research on the sanitary significance of [[Fecal Coliform]] bacteria. The work argues that the [[Elevated Temperature Test]] (44.5°C incubation in [[EC Medium]]) is a superior, simpler method for separating coliforms of warm-blooded-animal origin from non-fecal coliforms, compared with the traditional [[IMViC Classification]]. Fish are addressed in Chapter IV, which finds that coliform and fecal streptococcal flora in fish is transient and reflects food and water contamination rather than a permanent gut community.

## Background & Description

The science of sanitary bacteriology began when [[Escherich]] (1885) identified bacteria characteristic of human feces as indicators of fecal contamination. Over the following decades, researchers produced detailed but controversial classifications of the [[Coliform Group]], including sucrose, salicin and carbohydrate-based schemes. [[Eijkman]] proposed a 46°C fermentation test to isolate fecal types, and [[Parr]] later combined four biochemical reactions (indole, methyl red, Voges-Proskauer, citrate) into the IMViC system.

The report is organized into seven chapters:
- Chapter I: Background and the history of biochemical differentiation tests.
- Chapter II: Fecal coliform techniques, including the [[Boric Acid Lactose Broth]] (BALB), EC medium, and the [[M-FC Membrane Filter Medium]].
- Chapter III: Warm-blooded animals.
- Chapter IV: [[Fresh-Water Fish]].
- Chapter V: Vegetation and insects.
- Chapter VI: Soils.
- Chapter VII: Water, including drinking water, sewage stabilization ponds and storm water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Bacteria enter streams from many sources: soil and mineral runoff, mine drainage, plants, insects, cold- and warm-blooded animals, and domestic sewage and industrial wastes. The report stresses that most aquatic bacteria lack sanitary significance, so emphasis is placed on groups tied to public-health hazards, such as [[Salmonella]], [[Shigella]], [[Vibrio]], [[Leptospira]] and enteric viruses.

Soil is described as a substantial non-fecal reservoir. Of 251 soils from 26 states and three foreign countries, undisturbed samples generally contained fewer than 2 fecal coli-aerogenes per gram, while polluted soils contained 3,300 to 49,000 per gram (25th to 75th percentile). The IMViC intermediate type (-+-+) dominated unpolluted soils (48.1% of 2,348 strains).

Vegetation and insects contribute little fecal coliform material: 14.1% of 1,203 coliform strains from vegetation and 14.9% of 1,084 from insects were fecal. Streptococci, by contrast, were abundant on insects.

## Key Findings

**Methodology and classification**
- The IMViC system was judged superior to any single test but not definitive, because indole and Voges-Proskauer reactions can change in artificial environments and the test's assumptions were not fully verified.
- The Elevated Temperature Test at 44.5°C (±0.5) for 24 hours in EC medium was recommended as the preferred fecal coliform test: 96.3% of coliforms from warm-blooded animal feces were EC positive, against 9.2% for unpolluted soils (in the Table 2 data).
- The BALB test (43°C, 48 hours) was not recommended as a single specific test: it gave 93.3% positives for IMViC ++-- strains but 13.5% positives for the other types.
- The membrane-filter M-FC medium (aniline blue indicator, 44.5°C, 24 hours) recovered an average of 88% of fecal strains in pure cultures; 93.2% of blue colonies were verified fecal. Its ratios to MPN ranged from 0.42 to 2.52 for fecal counts.

**Warm-blooded animals (Chapter III)**
- 8,747 coliform strains were examined from 103 fecal samples (43 human, 32 livestock, 28 poultry).
- Human feces held 11 coliform types; livestock feces were almost exclusively ++-- type.
- Estimated daily coliform discharge per capita was highest for sheep (18,080 million) and pigs (8,910 million) and lowest for turkeys (130 million).

**Fresh-water fish (Chapter IV)**
- Coliforms, fecal coliforms and fecal streptococci were found in fish from the [[Little Miami River]], with the highest densities in catfish (upper quartile 7,900,000 coliforms and 1,090,000 fecal coliforms per gram of intestinal material) and lowest in bluegill.
- 3,877 coliform strains from 132 fish of 14 species were classified; the fecal coliform percentage (EC positive) was 30.8% for fish and 31.0% for river water at the same site.
- Fish do not maintain a permanent indigenous coliform flora; their intestinal bacteria come from contaminated food and water.
- Tracer organisms persisted in fish guts for 9 to 14 days, and fish could contaminate potable spring water in tank trials, serving as carriers of pollution for up to about 7 days.
- Growth of fecal coliforms in sterilized fish fecal material was negligible at 10°C and occurred at 20°C.
- Streptococci that peptonize litmus milk were 16.1% of fish isolates (137 of 850), similar to the 16.9% found in warm-blooded animal feces, suggesting an insect-diet source.

**Vegetation and insects (Chapter V)**
- 152 plant species and 40 insect samples were tested. No coliforms or fecal coliforms were found in indoor foliage or sphagnum moss.
- No IMViC type dominated vegetation or insects, and no inhibition of fecal coliforms was observed in disc tests.

**Soils (Chapter VI)**
- Completed-test coli-aerogenes densities in unpolluted soils were typically low; the Fecal Coliform (FC) test was negative in most unpolluted samples.
- The report concludes the IMViC intermediate type is predominantly a soil organism.

**Water and sewage (Chapter VII)**
- Waste stabilization pond on a state-prison dairy farm (Lebanon, Ohio): raw sewage fecal coliform levels were 790,000 to 920,000 per 100 ml for three seasons, rising to 2.65 million in autumn; reductions in fecal coliforms ranged from 87.9% to 98.3%, and fecal streptococci reductions from 97.0% to 99.8%.
- Fecal coliform to fecal streptococci ratios were used for source separation: ratios above 4:1 indicate domestic wastes, while ratios below 0.6 suggest livestock or poultry sources.
- Storm water from a 27-acre residential and suburban area averaged 11,000 fecal coliforms and 21,000 fecal streptococci per 100 ml; a combined sewer serving about 25% of Detroit averaged 2,800,000 fecal coliforms and 502,000 fecal streptococci per 100 ml.
- Presence of any coliform in treated drinking water indicates inadequate treatment or post-treatment contamination.

**Conclusions**
- Fecal coliforms are regarded as indicators of recent, dangerous fecal pollution. No method yet distinguishes human from other warm-blooded animal origin.
- The elevated temperature test combined with total coliform determination gives the best specific data on fecal pollution fractions.
- The report's authors expect the fecal coliform approach to gain legal and regulatory use, citing hearings at Sioux City, Iowa, and St. Joseph, Missouri (1959).
