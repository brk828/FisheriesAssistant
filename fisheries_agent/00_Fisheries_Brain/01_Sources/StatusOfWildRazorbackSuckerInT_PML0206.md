# Status of Wild Razorback Sucker in the Green River Basin, Utah and Colorado, Determined from Basinwide Monitoring and Other Sampling Programs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (October 2002, Project 22D of the [[Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]], Larval Fish Laboratory Contribution 126) assesses the status of endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] in the [[Green River]] Basin from 1996 to 1999, supplemented by data from 1980 onward. Wild adults in the middle Green River declined sharply: the most recent and reliable estimates (1998 and 1999) suggest only about 116 wild fish (SE = 29) remained, compared with roughly 456 to 524 animals estimated for 1985 to 1992. Adult survival was stable, pointing to recruitment failure at the larval or early juvenile stage as the likely cause. Larval captures also declined after about 1994. The authors warn that wild razorback suckers may soon disappear from the Green River system and recommend continued stocking of hatchery fish, a rigorous monitoring scheme, and research into recruitment limitation.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was once widespread and abundant across the Colorado River Basin but is now rare. Concentrations persist in [[Lake Mohave]] and Lake Mead and in the middle Green River, with scattered individuals elsewhere. Wild populations are dominated by large, old fish, and recruitment is considered low or absent throughout its range. Lake Mohave held about 4,000 wild individuals in 2001.

Historical declines have been attributed to altered physical habitat and introduced fishes. [[Mainstem Dams]] change flow, temperature and sediment loads and block upstream movement. Flow regulation, channelization and levees reduce floodplain inundation, removing warm, food-rich rearing habitat for larvae. Predation on early life stages, combined with slow growth, is also considered a primary recruitment constraint.

The Basinwide monitoring program began in 1996 to track status and trends of razorback sucker in the Green River. Its sampling was sparse, so the authors pooled data from other programs (for example the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] and the [[Levee Removal Project]]) to strengthen inferences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The main area ran from the Yampa River confluence downstream to the Colorado River confluence. Most wild fish now occur in low-gradient valley reaches between Jensen, Utah, and near the [[White River]] confluence. Canyon reaches have high gradient, coarse substrate and a confined floodplain; valley reaches have sandy substrate and broad floodplains.

**Sampling methods.** Adults were captured with fyke and trammel nets in low-velocity channel margins, eddies, backwaters, pools and flooded tributary mouths, rather than by electrofishing spawning adults, which was used earlier. Larvae were sampled with [[Light Traps]], which proved more effective than other gears for early life stages in low-velocity habitat. Each trap was set at dusk and emptied before dawn.

**Spawning environment.** In the middle Green River, spawning occurred from early May to early June, mostly when flows were rising or at peak and water temperatures were 10 to 17 °C. In the lower Green River, spawning was earlier (late March to early June) and over a longer period, possibly because the river warms earlier at lower elevation. The [[San Rafael River]] inflow area, with warmer water in early spring, appeared to be a concentration area for larvae.

**Population analysis.** Because the data spanned 20 years and the population was open, abundance was estimated using [[Lincoln-Petersen Estimator]]s on pairs of sequential years (program CAPTURE). Survival was estimated with recapture-only and [[Pradel Models]] in program MARK.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and sampling totals**
- 1996 to 1999 Basinwide and other sampling captured 118 unique wild fish, most in the middle Green River (Escalante reach 46%, Ouray reach 32%, Jensen reach 22%).
- 70% of middle Green captures came from three locations: Escalante Ranch spawning sites (RK 500.4), Ashley Creek (RK 481) and near Old Charlie Wash (RK 401.4 to 401.6).
- Adults were very rare elsewhere: two each in the lower Yampa and lower Green rivers.
- Basinwide sampling caught 83 adults in total; at least 25 were hatchery-origin. Of 33 fish captured in 1999, 19 (58%) were hatchery-reared, showing that stocked fish were surviving.

**Abundance**
- Estimates for 1985 to 1992 averaged 456 animals (SE = 185). Earlier estimates (Modde et al. 1996) averaged 524.
- Estimates for 1993, 1998 and 1999 averaged 163 animals; the 1998 and 1999 estimates were the most reliable, giving 116 (SE = 29) wild fish (95% CI 80 to 180 in the abstract).
- Regression of ln(abundance) on time showed significant declines, from about 500 animals in 1990 to about 100 in 1998 and 1999.

**Survival and population trend**
- Apparent survival was 0.728 (SE = 0.0287) for 1980 to 1992 and 0.762 (SE = 0.0475) for 1990 to 1999, with survival consistent across periods.
- Over 1980 to 1999, Pradel's survival estimate was 0.69 (SE = 0.021) and λ was 0.91 (SE = 0.014), implying an average decline of about 9% per year.
- Because adult survival remained steady, the authors conclude that recruitment failure, not adult mortality, drove the decline.

**Growth and length**
- Length of recaptured PIT-tagged fish changed little, consistent with slow growth. Changes were generally under 20 mm TL per interval.
- Stocked fish grew: 1996 stockings recaptured in 1997 and 1998 gained an average of 99 and 130 mm TL; 1998 stockings recaptured in 1999 gained 66 mm TL.

**Larval production**
- Middle Green River light-trap sampling (1996 to 1999) captured 20,844 fish, including 247 razorback sucker larvae (1.2% of samples). Razorback sucker larvae varied from 12 to 174 per year.
- Lower Green River sampling (1996 to 1999) captured 41,829 fish, including 304 razorback sucker larvae (4.4% of native catostomids). Most (74%) of 1997 to 1999 lower-river larvae came from the San Rafael River mouth.
- CPUE of razorback sucker larvae was highest in 1994 in both reaches and declined afterward, suggesting an overall decline in early life stage abundance.

**Reasons for decline**
- Recruitment failure is the leading explanation. Juveniles were rarely captured, and the few small fish were mostly from a managed wetland (Old Charlie Wash).
- Flow and floodplain inundation in 1995, 1997 and 1999 did not produce a detectable recruitment response, possibly because too few adults remained, or because reproduction and flows were poorly timed, or because non-native predators reduced survival.
- Tag loss from earlier carlin tags may have biased abundance estimates; PIT-tag detection accuracy is critical.

**Conclusions**
- The wild population, stable from about 1980 to 1992, has declined and may be extirpated in the near future.
- Reproduction in the lower Green River and the lower Yampa River is occurring, but adult numbers there are very low.
- Light-trap sampling can detect reproduction by small populations but cannot yet be relied on as an absolute abundance index.

**Recommendations**
- Continue stocking hatchery fish, and use stocked populations to identify mechanisms of recruitment failure.
- Identify nursery and spawning areas in the lower Green River, with emphasis on the San Rafael inflow.
- Develop a rigorous monitoring scheme for all life stages, with annual spring netting and electrofishing (one or two passes, two boats) in the middle Green River.
- Quantify light-trap detection probabilities, possibly with mark-recapture of released larvae.
- Improve quality assurance, tag detection and database integration, and measure individual PIT-tagged fish at stocking.
- Use data on flow and temperature to guide [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] releases in favor of larval habitat timing.
