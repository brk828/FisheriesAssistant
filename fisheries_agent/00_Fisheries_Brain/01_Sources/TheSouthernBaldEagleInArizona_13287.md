# Survey of the Southern Bald Eagle in Arizona (1972–1975)

#ornithology #conservation-biology #haliaeetus-leucocephalus-leucocephalus

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, prepared by the Division of Wildlife Services in Phoenix, documents a survey of the endangered [[Southern Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus leucocephalus*) in Arizona, conducted from July 1972 to June 30, 1975. Surveyors located nesting territories along the [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]] and adjacent drainages using ground and helicopter surveys, and tracked reproduction. The population is estimated at 30 to 34 individuals after the 1975 season, with more than 90% of birds believed observed. The authors conclude the population is critically low and that loss of [[Riparian Habitat]] through dam construction and land clearing is the greatest threat. Fish, mainly [[Common Carp]] and [[Channel Catfish]], form the majority of the documented diet.

## Background & Description

The survey was designed to determine the population status of the southern subspecies, which is federally listed as endangered; residency or nesting south of the 40th parallel was the main identification criterion. The report notes that little was known about the subspecies' habitat requirements or life history before this work.

Standardized terms were used to quantify nest status:

- **Active territory:** one or more nests with a pair of adults present.
- **Inactive territory:** nests with no pair present.
- **Historic territory:** an area where a nest once existed but has been destroyed.
- **Successful nest:** young fledged; **unsuccessful nest:** active nest where young did not fledge.

Methods combined contacting conservation agencies (2 years of records, unverified), ground surveys by hiking, raft, boat and horseback, and aerial surveys by a B-1 Bell helicopter with two observers. The helicopter was selected for its slow speed, visibility and maneuverability in canyons, compared with fixed-wing aircraft (Todd, 1971). Survey intensity was tied to known and historic territories and suitable habitat. Ground surveys in January 1974 covered about 50 miles of the Salt River, including four reservoirs, and about 75 miles of the Verde River, including two reservoirs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and distribution.** In 1975 the species occurred mainly on the main-stem Salt and Verde rivers, with a few nests on the lower Black River and along Eagle Creek, a tributary of the [[Gila River]]. A single nest was found on the [[Colorado River]] at the Havasu National Wildlife Refuge, where a pair built a nest in a marshy backwater but did not breed. No nests were found on the main Gila, Bill Williams, San Pedro or Little Colorado rivers. Most residents used riparian areas of the upper Sonoran Life Zone. Eagles seen on lakes in the Transition Zone were probably migratory northern birds wintering in Arizona (Snow, 1973).

**Nest sites.** Of 36 known nests, 64% were built on cliffs or rock pinnacles and 36% in trees, usually at forks in large branches. Cliff nests were judged more durable. Nest #9 at Mule Hoof Bend was first observed in 1935 and still existed in 1975. Nest #19 near [[Bartlett Dam]] is the largest known nest in Arizona, about eight feet wide and thirty feet deep.

**Foraging.** Eagles fished from perches or snags near the water, diving directly onto fish near the surface. They also caught stranded fish in shallow pools left by high runoff. No foraging was seen near large reservoirs, possibly because of inadequate perches, shallow-water areas, few surface fish, turbidity or boat disturbance.

**Disturbance.** Nearly all disturbances were human-caused. Hiking and climbing above nests caused the most serious effects, with nests #8, 14, 19 and 30 abandoned during incubation. Helicopter approaches within 100 yards did not cause incubating birds to leave. River floating caused little disturbance, and riders approached within 35 yards without causing flight.

## Key Findings

**Population status**
- The 1975 count found 18 adults on nine active territories, five young raised, and 23 known individuals by June 1975. Eleven subadults were also sighted.
- Estimated population after the 1975 season: 30 to 34 individuals. Observers believed they saw more than 90% of the population.
- Only 18 young were known to fledge over the four survey years, indicating a very small population.

**Breeding (1975 Ft. McDowell territory)**
- Nest rebuilding began January 1975; copulation was first observed January 29.
- Incubation was observed from February 15 to March 18 (31 days between first and last observations; actual period probably longer).
- The first eaglet was sighted March 24, a second on April 3, and the young fledged June 3.
- Nest-level timing for other active nests roughly coincided with this territory.

**Breeding season summary (Table 5)**
- Nine active territories were observed, with incubation seen at six nests in February. Nests #8 and #21 failed and did not renest.
- Five nests hatched eight young; five of the eight eaglets fledged.

**Diet**
- Prey remains came mostly from fish: [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), with suckers also present.
- Other prey included [[American Coot]] (*Fulica americana*), ground squirrels, cottontail rabbits, a soft-shelled turtle, and a snake, mostly from nest #19 and a 1964 record.

**Conservation conclusions and recommendations**
- The population is "down to a critically low level"; additional habitat loss is the greatest single threat.
- Dam construction, land clearing and human disturbance in riparian areas are the main drivers of decline.
- Recommended actions: habitat preservation with proper management, public education, and future survey methods designed to minimize disturbance to nesting birds.
