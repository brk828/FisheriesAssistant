# Relating Baseflow to Catchment Properties in South-Eastern Australia

#hydrology #catchment-hydrology #baseflow #geology-vegetation-groups

## Executive Summary

This study by Lacey and Grayson (1998, *Journal of Hydrology* 204, 231–250) examines what controls the [[Baseflow Index]] (baseflow divided by total streamflow) across 114 catchments in [[Victoria]], Australia, with areas from 0.05 to 192 km². The authors test whether 12 [[Geology-Vegetation Groups]], dimensionless topographic indices, a climatic index, forest cover and forest growth stage, and catchment size explain baseflow behaviour. The geology-vegetation group is the dominant control. Topographic indices show no discernible effect within groups, climate adds little once groups are known, and there is no detectable catchment-size effect up to 100 km². The group means provide a practical estimator for ungauged catchments.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Baseflow is a key low-flow characteristic relevant to water management, drought planning, aquatic ecosystems, salinity, water quality and algal blooms. Soil surveys in Australia are too coarse and agriculturally oriented to support a [[Soil Index]] approach like those used in Europe and the UK.
- **Approach:** The authors use a scaling framework based on dimensionless parameters, and an environmental indicator in which the indigenous [[Ecological Vegetation Classes]] combined with bedrock geology act as surrogates for soil and bedrock hydrological properties.
- **Baseflow separation:** Baseflow was derived from daily hydrographs using the digital filter of [[Nathan and McMahon Filter]]. The authors note a dispute with Chapman (1991) over the filter's constant-baseflow implication, but state that findings hold for any consistent separation method.
- **Data sources:** Melbourne Water, the Department of Natural Resources and Environment, and a database from Nathan (1995). Records span at least 15 years, usually 20 or more. The standard deviation of annual baseflow index estimates is about 0.07.
- **Variables:** Dimensionless drainage index (L/√A), slope index (H/√A), flat area ratio, rainfall/potential evapotranspiration, forest cover, forest growth stage, and the geology-vegetation group.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** Victoria is bisected by the Great Dividing Range (maximum altitude near 200 m). Geology is varied, including Silurian and Devonian granite, Devonian rhyodacite, Ordovician to Carboniferous sedimentary rock, and metamorphic rock. Alluvial plains of the [[Murray River]] lie to the north, and Cretaceous sedimentary hills occur to the south. Rainfall is higher south of the Dividing Range and in the Cretaceous hills. Most catchments are forested, from tall dense forest in wet areas to low open forest in dry rocky terrain.

**Hydrogeology.** Rock properties control groundwater storage and transmissivity. [[Specific Capacity]] values from borehole data (Heislers, 1993) reflect rock transmissivity:
- Tertiary basalt: highest mean (28 m²/day), owing to primary porosity and fracturing.
- Silurian/Devonian granite: 18 m²/day, with contribution to baseflow mainly from weathered soil.
- Upper Ordovician sedimentary rock: 18 m²/day, with deep [[Saprolite]] weathering (one profile to 60 m).
- Lower Ordovician and Silurian/Devonian sedimentary rock: about 6–9 m²/day.

**Soils and vegetation.** Few detailed soil surveys exist. Vegetation-soil examples show deeper soils under wet forest on rhyodacite (one site 15 m deep) than on sedimentary bedrock. Foothill Forest soils are shallower and more varied, and Dry Forest soils are shallow (up to about 0.6 m) and stony.

**Ecological vegetation classes** used in the study, from wettest to driest: Sub-alpine Woodland, Montane Forest, Wet Forest (dominated by [[Eucalyptus regnans]]), Damp Forest, Foothill Forest, and Dry Forest.

**Clearfelling case.** A 0.53 km² catchment on rhyodacite, mainly Wet Forest, was clearfelled between November 1971 and April 1972. Total flow rose sharply and peaked in 1974, then fell below pre-1972 levels after 1979, likely due to higher evapotranspiration in regrowth forest.

## Key Findings

1. **Geology-vegetation groups dominate.** The 12 groups explain most variation in baseflow index (ANOVA P < 0.0001). Group means were used as estimators. Groups were statistically consolidated into four sets: {A}; {B, C}; {D, E, G, I, J, L}; {F, H, K}.
2. **Highest baseflow:** Group A (Devonian rhyodacite) has the highest mean (0.794, range 0.67–0.91, n = 21), attributed to very deep permeable soils under Wet and Damp Forest. Group B (Tertiary basalt, n = 2, mean 0.660) and Group C (Devonian granite, moist ecosystems, n = 17, mean 0.702) follow.
3. **Lowest baseflow:** Group D (granite, drier forest, n = 10, mean 0.423) and Group L (Lower Cretaceous sedimentary, mixed Wet/Damp Forest, n = 15, mean 0.380, range 0.25–0.47) show the lowest values among the main groups.
4. **Overall:** Mean baseflow index across all 114 catchments is 0.545 (standard deviation 0.074).
5. **Topography has no discernible effect within groups.** Drainage index, slope index and flat area ratio show no trends, confirmed by regression. Flat area ratio ranged only 0–0.4 in the sample.
6. **Climate has little independent effect.** Rainfall/potential evapotranspiration shows a significant upward trend only in groups A and H (P < 0.01). Across all catchments, its R² against residual baseflow index was 0.04, so it was excluded from the estimation procedure.
7. **No catchment-size effect up to 100 km².** The evidence is inconclusive above that threshold. Two adjacent catchments (54 and 155 km²) in group F differed (0.54 vs 0.41), but the direction of the size effect was not consistent across groups.
8. **Forest growth stage shows no clear effect.** Baseflow index in the clearfelled catchment dropped after 1981 to 0.42 in 1983, but low rainfall in 1979 and 1982 was judged the likely cause. The long-term average was 0.75, and a nearby unlogged catchment showed a similar decline.
9. **Estimation method.** A linear regression of baseflow index against group means gave R² = 0.84, standard error 0.074 (about 14% of the mean). For mixed catchments, a weighted average of group means is recommended. A test catchment of 135 km² (groups D, E, F, G) gave an estimate of 0.47 against an observed 0.49.
10. **Comparison with [[Nathan et al. 1996]].** Their regression across 164 Victorian catchments (R² = 0.72, standard error 19%) identified rainfall, forest cover and elevation as positive factors, and sedimentary rock as negative, consistent with this study. Vogel and Kroll (1992) and Gustard et al. (1989, 1993) report related results for US and UK catchments using topographic and soil indices.
11. **Conclusion.** The geology-vegetation approach is recommended as a surrogate for soil and bedrock properties, especially where soil data are scarce. The group means in the study's Table 5 should not be used outside Victoria without retesting.
