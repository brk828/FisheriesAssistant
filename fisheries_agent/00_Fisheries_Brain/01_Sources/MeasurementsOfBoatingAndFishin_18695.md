# Boating and Fishing Pressure on Clear Lake, Summer 1968

#ichthyology #marine-biology #recreational-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This survey by [[Protschol and Carlander]] (Iowa State University) quantified boating and angling on [[Clear Lake]], a 3,643-acre lake in north central Iowa, during June 29 to July 24, 1968. Scheduled counts were made by boat circuit of the lake, twice daily on weekends and weekdays. The study estimated about 5,747 boat-hours per week and 8,121 angler man-hours per week, with a weekly catch of 3,135 fish. Catch rates (0.43 fish per hour) and total fishing effort were markedly lower than in earlier creel censuses, a decline linked to the apparent absence of [[Yellow Bass]] after a [[Furunculosis]] epizootic. The authors caution that the small sample limits the reliability of conclusions and comparisons.

## Background & Description

Clear Lake supports fishing, boating, water skiing, swimming and other outdoor recreation. Boats were classified as fishing craft, sailboats, power boats, power boats with skiers, and other (including canoes and pontoon boats). Anglers were recorded on shore, wading, on docks, or in boats, with docks and boats holding roughly equal numbers and 10 to 20% of anglers on shore or wading.

Survey design: counts followed a prearranged schedule, with each 2-hour daylight period sampled equally. Saturdays and Sundays yielded one count per 2-hour period over a 4-week schedule; weekday estimates averaged two counts per period. Estimates assumed a 15-hour day. The authors noted that the small sample size means results should be treated with caution, though a fairly uniform trend across the day suggested representative data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Boating patterns:** Weekday peak usage occurred at 5 P.M. Saturday peaks were similar but higher (124 boats versus 70 on weekdays). Sunday showed the greatest boating, with a peak of 258 boats (about 1 boat per 14 acres) between 2 and 3 P.M., and an earlier evening decline than on other days.
- **Weather effects:** Thunderstorms interrupted counts on one Saturday (6 P.M.) and one Sunday (4 P.M.), and rain affected several afternoon counts, so weekend usage was probably underestimated.
- **Water skiing:** Activity concentrated in mid-to-late afternoon and evening, with little before 11 A.M.
- **Fishing effort:** Angler effort was similar on weekdays and weekends, with the highest totals on Saturdays and Sundays.
- **Yellow bass:** [[Bulkley]] (1969) reported that furunculosis almost eliminated yellow bass from Clear Lake in May 1968. No yellow bass were reported by anglers in this study.

## Key Findings

**Boating (Table 1, weekly boat-hours):**
- Total: 5,747 boat-hours per week (about 1.6 hours per acre); about 35% on weekends.
- Power boats: about 2,416 hours (roughly 40–48% of usage depending on day type); fishing boats about 1,495 hours (about 25%); water skiers 967 hours; sailboats 403 hours; other 466 hours.
- Sunday usage was highest at 1,192 boat-hours per day.

**Angling effort (Table 2, weekly man-hours):**
- Total estimated effort: 8,121 man-hours per week (shore and wading 889; dock 3,475; boat 3,757).
- Historical comparison: 20,000 to 29,000 man-hours per week in 1953–1956 (summer-long estimates), 10,000 in June–July 1960, and 2,800 to 8,100 in 1963–1966.

**Catch rates (Table 3):**
- Overall catch: 359 fish from 828 angler-hours, or 0.43 fish per hour.
- Weekend success exceeded weekday success; no consistent morning-versus-evening difference.
- Earlier censuses reported more than one fish per hour, except 1956 (about 0.6 fish per hour).

**Weekly catch (Table 4):**
- Total estimated catch: 3,135 fish per week, including 1,932 bullheads, 491 walleyes, 282 yellow perch (recorded as "perch" in the source), 256 bluegills, 69 crappies, and 69 northern pike.
- Walleye catch (491 per week) exceeded values reported for 1953–1956 (46 to 120) and 1963–1966 (26 to 295). Most anglers considered 1968 a good walleye year, though the study began June 29 and missed the peak walleye season.
- Total catch was well below historical estimates (for example, 23,000 to 35,000 in 1953–1955 and 11,000 to 16,000 in 1960 and 1965).

**Conclusions:**
- Declines in total fishing effort and catch rate are attributed partly to the loss of yellow bass, and the authors suggest the catch per hour would have been similar to other years if yellow bass had been excluded from earlier counts.
- The authors advise caution in interpreting results and comparisons because of the small sample size and the midsummer timing of the survey.
