# Ecological Value of Floodplain Habitats to Razorback Suckers in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Recovery Program final report, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Program, reviews the literature on how [[Floodplain Habitats]] support the survival of endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) larvae and juveniles. The authors argue that declining recruitment is linked to the loss of productive floodplain nursery habitat, altered [[Streamflow Management]] regimes, and predation and competition by [[Nonnative Fishes]]. The report concludes that reconnecting floodplains with rivers is expected to benefit razorback suckers, and it recommends integrating streamflow management, habitat restoration, nonnative fish control, and [[Captive Propagation]] as a single program.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a long-lived, fecund catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its populations are endangered, and the report notes that recruitment is the central problem. Adults spawn annually on clean cobble and gravel in rivers, and also on wave-swept rubble in [[Lake Mohave]], but larvae face high mortality during a short [[Critical Period]] after swimup.

Historical changes shaped the present condition of the basin. Water development, [[Dam Construction]] (notably [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], completed in 1962 per the document, and Blue Mesa/Aspinall Unit), levee construction, and irrigation reduced peak spring flows and disconnected floodplains from rivers. Nonnative fishes were introduced, and by the report's account nonnative species now make up 76% (42 of 55) of fish species in the Upper Colorado River Basin. The fish fauna is highly endemic: 54% of native species are endemic to the Colorado River system (Miller 1961).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Primary production.** Phosphorus is described as the key limiting nutrient for phytoplankton in north temperate freshwaters. High turbidity from fine suspended sediment in Upper Basin rivers limits primary production in the channel, so backwaters, embayments and floodplain wetlands with warmer water and lower velocity offer the best conditions for phytoplankton and zooplankton production.

**Zooplankton and benthos.** Cladocerans and copepods are the most abundant zooplankton. Reported densities were 0.2 to 24,000 per liter in floodplain habitats worldwide. In the Upper Basin, main-channel densities averaged roughly 1.3 per liter, backwaters about 81.5 per liter, and floodplain habitats about 4.2 (Table 1 summary of cited studies). Benthic chironomids averaged 4,150 per m² in the main channel, 31,125 per m² in backwaters, and 23,055 per m² in floodplain habitats (Table 4).

**Stream channel structure.** The report applies the [[River Continuum Concept]] to upstream reaches and the [[Flood Pulse Concept]] to lowland floodplains. Floodplains transfer productivity to the main channel via drifting invertebrates, and this flood pulse is described as even more important under fragmented conditions today.

**Streamflow and fish use of floodplains.** Adult razorback suckers congregate in backwaters, off-channel ponds and floodplain wetlands during high spring runoff, and some spawn in floodplains. Floodplain depressions can support growth of captive-reared fish; the report cites growth of roughly 100 mm TL after the first season in floodplain ponds versus about 300 mm TL in off-channel habitats.

## Key Findings

1. **Larval starvation is a primary bottleneck.** Razorback larvae are about 7–9 mm TL at swimup. The "point of no return" for starvation occurred between 15 and 17 days, and larvae need roughly 2 months to reach about 25 mm TL. Survival and growth increase with food density (Figures 1–2, from Papoulias and Minckley studies).
2. **Food resources are most limited during the critical period.** Zooplankton densities in the main channel and backwaters are low during spring runoff, when larvae drift downstream, making starvation a probable major factor in recruitment failure.
3. **Predation by nonnative fishes is severe.** Red shiners and fathead minnows made up 90% or more of fish in Upper Basin backwaters. Of 149,489 fish collected from 1985–1994, red shiners and fathead minnows constituted 90.4%. Adult red shiners consumed all 100 larvae in a cage experiment.
4. **Competition with nonnative fishes is likely but hard to document.** Overlap in diets is common, and competition is strongest during low-zooplankton periods.
5. **Reconnected floodplains are quickly colonized by nonnatives.** A gravel-pit pond connected to the upper Colorado River was colonized within four months, and five nonnative species reproduced within eight months.
6. **Floodplain access appears essential.** The report recommends reconnecting floodplains, removing levees on the lowest terraces, excavating terraces, and timing dam releases so that spawning substrate at Razorback Bar stays clean while floodplains are inundated for larval access.
7. **Inundation frequency.** Long-lived fish with strong year-class strength may need floodplain inundation only once in 5 to 10 years to maintain self-sustaining populations, but the authors state that more frequent recruitment is desirable until recovery goals are met.
8. **Captive propagation guidance.** Razorback suckers should be reared for two growing seasons and stocked at about 300 mm TL or larger. Stocking is described as a mechanism to maintain adults, not as recovery.
9. **Nonnative fish control.** Large-scale control in large rivers is considered impractical based on the literature; the authors recommend focusing management on reaches adjacent to reconnected floodplains.
10. **Recommendations.** Continue acquisition and enhancement of floodplains, adopt adaptive management, pursue timed and magnitude-specific streamflow regimes, pair floodplain depression ponds with stocking, and conduct field experiments on nonnative control. Floodplain depressions may be the only option for maintaining stocks if flooding cannot be restored.
