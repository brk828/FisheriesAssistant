# Razorback Sucker Recovery and the Proposed Black Wash Reservoir: Arizona Conservation Voter, Summer 1995

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This Summer 1995 newsletter of the [[Arizona League of Conservation Voters]] covers a range of Arizona conservation issues. Two articles are central to this note. The first is a feature on the recovery of the [[Razorback Sucker]], a large, long-lived fish endemic to the [[Colorado River]] drainage that is on the brink of extinction. The second is a critique of the [[Black Wash Reservoir]] proposed by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] near Tucson. The fish article, by [[Paul C. Marsh]] and [[W. L. Minckley]], reports a conservation program that captures and protects larval razorbacks from non-native predators, and the reservoir article argues that the project would harm native species and waste public money. The newsletter also includes shorter items on Mexican wolf reintroduction, state land policy, grazing fees, [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]], and other topics, which are not summarized here.

## Background & Description

The newsletter opens with a quotation from [[U.S. Forest Service]] Chief Jack Ward Thomas opposing the transfer of public lands to private ownership, describing them as a "birthright" that must not be "traded away for pottage."

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) once occupied much of the [[Colorado River Basin]], from the river delta in Mexico north through the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[San Juan River]] basins, into Utah, and up to the headwaters in Wyoming and Colorado. Adults grow to about 3 feet and up to 13 pounds. The species is endemic to the Colorado River drainage and is found nowhere else. Native Americans used it as a food source, and early settlers reportedly ate it as well. Large spawning aggregations were once described in the Salt and Colorado rivers. In 1979, a commercial fisherman was still selling razorbacks from [[Saguaro Lake]] on the Salt River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life history.** Razorbacks grow quickly, often reaching about one foot in the first year, and can live more than 50 years. Fecundity is high, at about 5,000 eggs per inch of body length, or up to 120,000 eggs in a 24-inch female. In rivers, they live near the bottom in slow or still water, over silt, sand, and gravel in channel centers or along deeper banks, and eat drifting organic matter, plants, and bottom animals. In [[Lake Mohave]], adults feed near the bottom among rocks along wave-washed shores and also take plankton and small animals in midwater. Outside the breeding season they form loose groups, and in mid-winter males gather in compact aggregations over cobbles along shore. Breeding males develop dark backs, yellow bellies, white ventral fins, and reddish bands along the sides, along with nuptial tubercles that help them grip females during spawning.

**Spawning.** Spawning occurs from about January through early May. Females, accompanied by several males, dive to the bottom and create a shallow depression by undulating in the sediment. Males press alongside and release sperm as eggs are released. The adhesive, fertilized eggs settle into spaces among stones. A female may spawn more than once.

**Threats and habitat change.** The authors state that human alterations have caused most native Western North American fishes to decline, and that some are extinct. Water is diverted and depleted for drinking, crops, lawns, golf courses, industry, waste disposal, and recreation, and for non-native game fish. Razorbacks were far more abundant before river regulation. The last wild population of any size in the lower Colorado basin is restricted to Lake Mohave, near the Arizona-Nevada border.

**Recruitment failure.** Hundreds of thousands of razorback larvae hatch in Lake Mohave each year, but no evidence shows that any survive to maturity. The youngest adults in the population hatched around 1954, when the lake was filled, so they are over 40 years old. Without new recruits, the authors warn the species could go extinct. Larvae hatch within about a week, live first on yolk, and then begin feeding on tiny plants and insect larvae before moving to larger plankton. Larvae disappear from the lake each year before they reach 5/10 to 6/10 of an inch. Three hypotheses were tested: (1) turbine mortality at the dam, for which no evidence was found; (2) absence of a specific food in the reservoir, which has some support; and (3) predation by non-native fish, which the authors say accounts for most of the losses. Predators include [[Common Carp]], [[Catfish]], [[Sunfish]], and [[Black Bass]].

## Key Findings

**Razorback recovery program (Marsh and Minckley).**
- Non-native predators cannot be removed from Lake Mohave, so larvae are captured instead. Larvae are **phototactic** (drawn to light at night), so they are collected by shining light from a boat or using a light inside a trap container.
- Captured larvae are fed in large aquaria for a few weeks, then held in isolated bays cut off from the lake, where non-native predatory fish have been removed. They are released at juvenile size and tagged electronically for individual identification.
- The full process takes about six months.
- More than 20,000 larvae were caught in Lake Mohave in 1995. The goal for 1996 is 60,000 fish. Tagged fish from earlier efforts have entered the breeding aggregations, which the authors cite as evidence that the program is working.
- Since 1973, students, volunteers, university professors, and state and federal fisheries biologists have contributed to the effort.
- The authors say the program is not yet a success story, but that limited success is better than none, and that the razorback sucker "may not be doomed to extinction after all."

**Black Wash Reservoir (G. Nabors).**
- The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] released a Draft Environmental Impact Statement (DEIS) on July 14 for public comment. The proposed reservoir would be built in the Santa Cruz River Basin, south of Tucson, to store up to 15,000 (OCR-garbled unit, likely acre-feet) of [[Central Arizona Project]] water for emergencies.
- The author argues that the reservoir would harm the federally listed endangered [[Pima Pineapple Cactus]] and about 30 other rare plant species.
- Federal administrators have suggested stocking the reservoir with non-native game fish, which the author warns could wipe out native fish in the Santa Cruz River Basin.
- The DEIS includes three alternatives to the reservoir: groundwater recharge with CAP water, constructing "shallow" recharge basins, and no action. The author favors **Alternative C**, which would add extra discharge lines from the seven CAP water treatment plants between the Gila River and Tucson. With extra lines, a single line failure would not interrupt delivery, and the plants could deliver water during CAP outages without the reservoir.
- The author states that dropping the reservoir would save taxpayers a large sum, citing Bureau of Reclamation figures. The exact amount is unreadable in the scanned text. The author also notes the existing debt from CAP construction.
- The article concludes that the Black Wash Reservoir is "a hare-brained idea whose time should never come."
