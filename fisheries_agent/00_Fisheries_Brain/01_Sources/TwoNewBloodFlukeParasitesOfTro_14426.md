# Two New Blood Fluke Parasites of Trout

## Executive Summary
This 1958 paper by J. H. Wales (California Department of Fish and Game) describes two newly identified species of blood fluke (family [[Sanguinicolidae]]) responsible for severe mortality events in hatchery-reared trout in California and Oregon. A 1955 epizootic at Darrah Springs Hatchery killed approximately 300,000 [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]] and [[Steelhead Rainbow Trout]], caused by a previously undescribed blood fluke, later named *Sanguinicola davisi*. A second species, *Sanguinicola klamathensis*, was found killing [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] fingerlings at the Klamath Hatchery, Oregon, in 1957. Both parasites use freshwater snails as intermediate hosts (*Oxytrema* spp. and *Fluminicola seminalis*, respectively) and cause pathology primarily through egg accumulation and rupture in the gill capillaries, impairing respiration and causing blood loss.

## Background & Description
- **Taxonomic group:** Digenetic trematodes of the family [[Sanguinicolidae]], commonly called "blood flukes."
- **Size:** Small, approximately 1–3 mm in length.
- **Morphology:** Lack true suckers and a true pharynx; long slender esophagus terminating in a lobed, X- or H-shaped intestine ("caecum"); separate male and female genital pores located posteriorly; eggs lack an operculum and are tri-cornered, oval, or subspherical depending on species.
- **Life cycle:** Adults reside in the circulatory system of fish (definitive host). Eggs are deposited into the bloodstream and lodge in gill capillaries. Miracidia develop within the egg, rupture through the gill epithelium, and swim to seek an appropriate snail (intermediate host). Cercariae develop within the snail and re-infect fish directly (no second intermediate host required), penetrating fin tissue and migrating via the circulatory system to the gills/kidney to mature.

### Species Descriptions

#### *Sanguinicola davisi* n. sp.
- Named in honor of H. S. Davis, fish pathologist.
- Adult ~0.85 mm long, ~0.21 mm wide; spindle-shaped body with rows of spines along the edges; poorly defined spinous proboscis; fusiform, poorly developed pharynx; straight, narrow esophagus expanding into an X-shaped, four-lobed caecum.
- Only **one egg** present in the ootype at a time.
- Eggs: oval (except very early spherical stage), 16–17 µm initially, growing to 65–70 µm at full miracidial development; distinctive "eye spot" of melanin granules visible in developing miracidium.
- Cercariae: 0.23 × 0.056 mm body, 0.34 × 0.035 mm tail; forked tail tipped with delicate spheres; lacks a body keel (unlike *S. inermis*).
- **Site of infection:** Main gill capillary, parallel to the cartilaginous gill arch; eggs typically lodge in capillaries of the secondary lamellae.
- **Intermediate host:** Snails of genus *Oxytrema* (specifically *Oxytrema circumlineata* in northern California; presumed *Oxytrema silicula* in Oregon's Alsea River).

#### *Sanguinicola klamathensis* n. sp.
- Adults larger: four measured specimens ranged 2.45–3.15 mm long; one specimen 2.67 mm long was 0.91 mm broad.
- Spindle-shaped body with two longitudinal columns of heavy spines plus fine spines covering the body; moderately developed proboscis with ~8 whorls of small spines; no pharynx-like enlargement; straight slender esophagus ending in an unsymmetrical sac-like intestine.
- Ootype may contain **several eggs simultaneously** (unlike *S. davisi*).
- Eggs: spherical to subspherical at all observed stages (unique among known *Sanguinicola* species, most of which are tri-cornered).
- Cercariae: lophocercous furcocercous type, ~0.98 mm total length; head separated from body by ring-like constriction; ~6 concentric rows of heavy spines anteriorly; delicate body keel extending about half the head length; tail forks tipped with claw-like projections (vs. spheres in *S. davisi*).
- **Site of infection:** Primarily the efferent renal vein (occasionally the bulbus arteriosus); eggs lodge in the **main capillaries of gill filaments**, forming bead-like rows.
- **Intermediate host:** Presumed *Fluminicola seminalis* (a second snail, *Parapholyx effusa*, also co-occurs in the reservoir but is not implicated).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Geographic distribution:** Limited by the range of snail intermediate hosts.
  - *Oxytrema* (host for *S. davisi*) ranges from about San Francisco Bay northward through Washington and into British Columbia, primarily in coastal flowing waters; absent from interior drainages.
  - *Fluminicola seminalis* (host for *S. klamathensis*) is documented at a spring-fed reservoir at the Klamath Hatchery, Klamath County, Oregon.
- **Habitat correlations:** Snail (*Oxytrema*) abundance is associated with water chemistry (calcium presence, though not strictly limiting), aquatic vegetation (especially water cress, *Radicula nasturtium-aquaticum*), and stream physical geography. Livestock disturbance (grazing/trampling, turbidity) reduced snail populations and correspondingly reduced fluke infection rates in one hatchery stream at Darrah Springs.
- **Case study — Russian River drainage (Sonoma County, CA):** Steelhead in Warm Springs Creek (west slope, moderate *Oxytrema* abundance) showed high parasitization; steelhead in Maacama Creek (east slope, no *Oxytrema*) showed no infection — a clear correlational natural experiment supporting the snail-dependent transmission hypothesis.
- **Host susceptibility:** [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]] are susceptible; [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) are not known to be susceptible to these flukes.
- **Age/size susceptibility:** Eggs found in wild steelhead fingerlings newly emerged from gravel, and in rainbow trout up to 15 inches; heaviest infections occur in fish under 1 year old and under 8 inches in length.
- **Related prior records:** Blood flukes previously recorded in North American freshwater fish only twice before this study — from *Stizostedion vitreum* (Van Cleave & Mueller, 1932) and from *Huro salmoides*/*Micropterus dolomieu dolomieu* (Fischthal, 1949). H. S. Davis (1953) reported an undescribed blood fluke in rainbow and cutthroat trout fingerlings at an Alsea River, Oregon hatchery — the first salmonid record, of uncertain identity relative to *S. davisi*.

## Key Findings
1. **Mass mortality event:** ~300,000 Kamloops and steelhead rainbow trout died in a 1955 epizootic at Darrah Springs Hatchery (Tehama County, CA), attributed to blood fluke infection concentrated in the gills.
2. **Two new species described:** *Sanguinicola davisi* (named for H. S. Davis) and *Sanguinicola klamathensis* (named for the Klamath Hatchery locality).
3. **Pathology mechanism:** Fluke eggs accumulate in gill capillaries, potentially occupying up to half of total gill volume in advanced infections, causing pallor, flaccidity of filaments, and presumed respiratory impairment; miracidial emergence ruptures capillary walls, causing blood loss that may be lethal in heavy infections.
4. **Sublethal recovery:** Infected fish moved to uninfected water may recover as eggs mature and depart or degenerate, allowing gill tissue to heal.
5. **Kidney involvement:** Steelhead rainbow trout fingerlings at Mt. Shasta Hatchery showed fluke eggs in kidneys in addition to gills, expanding the known tissue range beyond gills alone.
6. **Snail-dependent geographic distribution:** Distribution of *S. davisi* infection in wild and hatchery trout closely tracks the natural range of *Oxytrema* snails, supporting an obligate snail-intermediate-host life cycle.
7. **Experimental transmission:** Cercariae presumed to be *S. davisi*, when exposed to rainbow trout fingerlings, penetrated fin tissue within minutes, dropped their tails, and became active within vascular spaces between fin rays — supporting the hypothesized migration route to the gills.
8. **Taxonomic placement:** Both new species are retained within genus *Sanguinicola* (following Yamaguti's broader family concept over Hyman's narrower one), despite morphological differences (egg shape, ootype egg number, intestine shape) that distinguish them from previously known congeners and from related genera *Deontacylix*, *Psettarium*, and *Paradeontacylix*.
9. **Control limitations:** No effective chemical snail-eradication treatment exists yet that spares hatchery fish; the only reliable control is removing either the snail intermediate host or infected/wild trout from the hatchery water supply.
10. **Species-specific ecology:** *S. davisi* is broadly distributed across three northern California hatcheries and in wild steelhead within *Oxytrema* range; *S. klamathensis* is, to date, known only from a single southern Oregon hatchery population.
