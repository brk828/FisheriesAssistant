# Talking Squawfish: Oral Histories of Endangered Upper Colorado Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This newspaper feature (Los Angeles Times, by Pete Thomas) reports on an oral-history report compiled by Fred Quartarone of the [[Colorado Division Of Wildlife]] with the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]]. The report gathered first-hand recollections from more than 100 people, average age 77, about four native fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. These species were listed as endangered in the 1970s. The accounts document the recreational and subsistence fishing of earlier decades and the causes of decline: dams, pollution, rotenone poisoning, and the stocking of [[Hatchery-Raised Trout]] and [[Channel Catfish]]. Recovery remains slow, and the largest squawfish now seldom exceed 30 inches.

## Background & Description

The report was prepared for the Information and Education Committee of the [[Recovery Program For Endangered Fish Of The Upper Colorado River Basin]]. Its purpose was to learn how people interacted with the fish, both for recreation and for food. The article draws mostly on stories about the Colorado squawfish, a minnow-family fish that grew to about 6 feet. Early settlers called it "white salmon," and anglers also knew it as the "whitefish."

The article describes the fish as a predator that took almost any bait. Anglers reported using:

- Mice, the most favored bait
- Baby swallows, a close second, which fell from canyon-wall nests into the river
- Chicken parts
- Whole cottontail rabbits
- Baby blackbirds

Fishing methods included hand-lines, rod-and-reel, spearing, seining, and fishing from horseback with a rope "dally" on the saddle horn. Recollected catches included fish of 60 to 80 pounds, along with a 4-foot fish caught on a blackbird and a fish that reportedly took about 200 feet of line.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Accounts describe native fish that migrated up and down large rivers in great numbers. The fish preferred fast-flowing, untamed water. Several changes contributed to their decline:

- **Dams:** Built in various rivers of the region "not long after the Depression," which the article says essentially doomed the Colorado squawfish and other native fish.
- **Pollution:** Cited, with dams and hatchery stocking, as having "forever changed the fisheries" of the basin.
- **Introduced species:** Growing demand for [[Hatchery-Raised Trout]] and [[Channel Catfish]] led communities to stock game fish to attract anglers. Trout fishermen pressured officials to act because squawfish ate trout "so fast."
- **Catfish barbs:** A Vernal, Utah, angler recalled that squawfish swallowed channel catfish, whose dorsal barbs lodged in their throats and killed them. The article presents this as recollection, not a documented study.
- **Rotenone poisoning:** Starting in the early 1960s, fisheries officials ran rotenone projects to control squawfish. One spread farther than planned, and the fish kill was said to have left banks "littered with native fish."
- **Direct removal:** Some anglers seined squawfish and left them to die on the banks or used them as fertilizer.

Human uses also shaped the fishery. Farmers in Loma, Colorado, held community fish fries each fall, netting hundreds of large fish between boats. The events ended when political crowds made them unworkable.

## Key Findings

- **Interview base:** Quartarone interviewed more than 100 people, average age 77, about four native fish species that had been placed on the endangered list in the 1970s.
- **Recovery status (as reported):**
  - [[Bonytail Chub]]: close to extinction.
  - [[Humpback Chub]]: fairly stable population in the Little Colorado River.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]]: small populations in various upper Colorado tributaries.
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]]: found mainly in parts of the Colorado, Green and Yampa rivers, with few individuals exceeding 30 inches.
- **Historical size:** Squawfish reached about 6 feet, and anglers recalled catches of 60 to 80 pounds.
- **Decline drivers:** Dams, pollution, the introduction of hatchery trout and catfish, rotenone poisoning, and direct killing by anglers.
- **Diet and bait:** Squawfish readily took mice, swallows, chicken, rabbits and blackbirds, and anglers reported large numbers of baby swallows in their stomachs.
- **Cultural context:** The fish supported community fish fries, subsistence fishing during the Depression, and recreational contests along rivers in the region.
- **Central message:** The article contrasts the historical abundance of these fish with their current scarcity, noting that recovery "has been a slow one."
