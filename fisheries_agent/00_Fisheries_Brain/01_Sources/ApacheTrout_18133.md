# Apache Trout: Taxonomy, Relationships, and Restoration

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a largely first-person account by a fisheries scientist, written from a graduate-research background on Great Basin cutthroat trout, that traces the taxonomic history, biology, and conservation of the [[Apache Trout]] and its close relative the [[Gila Trout]]. It explains how a museum mix-up and a misattributed 1870s specimen first complicated the identification of the species, how [[R. R. Miller]] formally described the two taxa, and how genetic and karyotypic evidence led the author to treat them as two subspecies of one species. The second half covers the decline of Apache trout through competition and hybridization with introduced trout, the 1964 management plan and its restoration measures, and the setbacks to Gila trout after a drought and forest fires in 1989. A short faxed cover sheet and a partially legible internal memo are also included, but they contain no substantive data.

## Background & Description

**Discovery and naming.** In the late 1950s the author, studying native trout of the [[Great Basin]], examined preserved museum specimens of cutthroat subspecies from the [[Lahontan Basin]] and [[Bonneville Basin]]. Three specimens labeled "Panguitch Lake, Utah" (a Bonneville lake) were collected in 1873 and appeared distinct from any known trout, prompting the author's M.S. thesis question. Investigation showed that collections from geological surveys and railroad expeditions were often mislabeled. One specimen (U.S. National Museum number 15999) was labeled as from the White River, Arizona, and was described by [[Edward Drinker Cope]] and Yarrow in 1875 as a variety of the Colorado River cutthroat trout. The early settlers of the Salt and White river region knew the fish as "yellow belly" trout.

**Taxonomic history.** In 1950, Miller described the [[Gila Trout]] from the upper Gila River basin as *Salmo gilae* (now *Oncorhynchus gilae*) and mentioned the native trout of the White Mountains, which had been regarded as a form of Gila trout. Following later comparative studies, Miller formally described the Apache trout as *Salmo apache* (now *Oncorhynchus apache*) in 1972.

**Morphology.** Apache trout have a deep body, long fins, and light yellow coloration on the ventral surface. The sides are yellowish-brownish-olive with purplish tints. Fin tips are cream to yellow-orange, and black pigment around the eye gives a mask-like appearance. Spots are relatively large, rounded, and sparsely distributed. The species resembles some interior cutthroat subspecies, including a yellowish "cutthroat" mark on the throat, which the author notes can be misleading.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** The original range of Apache trout consisted of about 30 miles of a few small streams at elevations between 6,000 and 9,000 feet in the White Mountains region, before the reintroductions described below.

**Evolutionary origin.** The author traces the origin of both Gila and Apache trout to a common ancestor that entered the Gila River basin from California, probably during the Pleistocene (perhaps around a million years ago). The lineage later separated into two groups: one in the upper Gila basin (Gila trout) and one in the Salt–White river segment (Apache trout). Warmer, drier climatic periods fragmented populations into isolated mountain refugia, a pattern shared with other southerly cold-adapted fishes. Related California-origin trout include the [[Mexican Golden Trout]].

**Relationships.** On a 1-to-10 scale of genetic distance (1 = closest, 10 = most distant), Gila and Apache trout are 1–2 apart, while the relationship between these two taxa and cutthroat trout is about 9–10. The author notes that the two are more closely related to rainbow trout than to cutthroat trout, and that cutthroat-like features reflect traits retained from a shared ancient ancestor.

**Karyotype.** Gila and Apache trout share a chromosome complement of 56, compared with 58–68 in [[Rainbow Trout]] and cutthroat trout. Their arm counts (106 vs. 104) indicate relatively more V-shaped than I-shaped chromosomes.

**Competition and hybridization.** Apache trout cannot coexist with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]], which hybridize readily, and the hybrid offspring are fertile, so reproductive barriers do not prevent genetic absorption of native fish. [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] compete with Apache trout for habitat and food. The species persists best in small streams where nonnative trout are absent.

**Life history.** Apache trout are a spring-spawning species. Spawning begins at about 45°F (about 7°C), and timing varies with elevation. In headwater streams, mature fish may be only about 5–6 inches long. They feed opportunistically on aquatic insects and terrestrial insects, and they readily take artificial flies, which the author says is "too readily for their own good" when fishing pressure is high.

## Key Findings

- **Identity confirmed as distinct:** The Apache trout was recognized as a distinct species by Miller in 1972, after a long history of misidentification and mislabeled specimens.
- **Two subspecies proposed:** Genetic distance (1–2 on the author's scale), shared karyotype features, and morphological similarity led the author to classify Gila and Apache trout as two subspecies of a single species, *Oncorhynchus gilae*/*apache*.
- **Historic range reduced:** Apache trout originally occupied about 30 miles of a few small streams before reintroduction efforts, and the White Mountain Apache tribe recognized that nonnative trout had displaced them from most reservation waters.
- **Fort Apache survey:** During electrofishing on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]], brook trout outnumbered Apache trout, although Apache trout were still caught by fly fishers in the same stream.
- **Management plan (1964):** The plan included chemical treatments of some streams to remove nonnative fish, followed by stocking of Apache trout and construction of barrier-protected lakes and reservoirs to create self-sustaining populations.
- **Hatchery program:** The most ambitious restoration effort operates at the [[Alchesay-Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service). Its stated goal is annual production of Apache trout, about half of them of catchable size, for stocking in reservation waters and for angling.
- **Gila trout setbacks:** A proposal to reclassify Gila trout from endangered to threatened was in progress, but a 1989 drought and forest fires in the upper Gila basin damaged small sanctuary streams and forced a new restoration strategy.
- **Conservation status:** The author argues that both species depend on protected sanctuary streams and that recovery requires active management, including protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] for the taxa's listing status.
