# Windy-Gap Colorado River Fishes Study: First Annual Report (1982)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This first annual report of the [[Windy-Gap Study]] was prepared by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] under a cooperative agreement with the Northern Colorado Water Conservancy District (Municipal Subdistrict). It is part of the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] (CRFP), which studies endangered fishes of the Colorado River. The 1982 work sought to locate spawning and rearing habitats for [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]], identify factors affecting first-year survival, and evaluate river backwaters and gravel pits as nursery habitat. Methods combined [[Radiotelemetry]] of adults, larval and young-of-the-year (YOY) surveys, and field experiments with microtagged hatchery fish in backwaters and ponds. The report also outlines 1983 work plans, including a proposed [[Spawning Pond Concept]].

## Background & Description

The Colorado River fish community was historically dominated by endemic species found nowhere else. Because their ranges are limited, these endemics are more vulnerable to habitat alteration than widespread species. The Colorado Squawfish, Humpback Chub and [[Bonytail]] had declined enough to be listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Their recovery requires information on distribution, abundance and habitat use, so that water development projects can avoid harming them.

The report notes that endangered Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub apparently suffer unusually high mortality during their first months of life. Identifying the causes of that early mortality was therefore a central goal. The five objectives of the 1982 work were to:

1. Locate and describe reproductive habitats for Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub in the Grand Junction area.
2. Locate and quantify rearing areas for young fish.
3. Identify the major factors affecting first-year survival.
4. Modify river backwaters and gravel pits between Debeque Canyon and the mouth of the Green River to enhance survival.
5. Evaluate natural and modified backwaters as habitat for young fish.
6. Determine how far adults move within the Colorado River and its tributaries.

Study reaches included the Colorado River between Palisade, CO and Hite, UT; the Gunnison River; the Green River from the Yampa confluence to Gray Canyon; the lower 20 miles of the Yampa River; and upper Lake Powell.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Adult movement and radiotelemetry.** Adult Colorado Squawfish over 500 mm total length were surgically implanted with radio transmitters (about 10-11 g in air, expected life about 6 months). Fish were tracked at least biweekly, by boat, airplane and helicopter. River position was recorded in river miles (RM), with the Green-Colorado confluence as RM 0.

**Spawning indicators.** Tracking was intensified in July and August, the suspected spawning season. Trammel nets were fished where multiple radio-tagged fish gathered. Larval fish were also sampled by hand net at 2-5 mile intervals.

**Backwaters and gravel pits.** Backwaters are naturally indented areas with little or no current. Gravel pits are man-made, sometimes connected to the river only during high flow. Gravel pits are common in the Grand Valley, so the authors considered it prudent to assess their value for spawning and rearing.

**Physicochemical conditions.** Surveys measured dissolved oxygen, salinity, conductivity, turbidity, water temperature, and the degree of connection to the river. River discharge and temperature were tracked at the State Line gauge; the spawning aggregation near Clifton occurred as flows were receding and water temperatures were near 20 °C.

**Predator and competitor fish.** Fish Pond and Labor Camp Pond held large populations of [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Black Crappie]]. These predators were a major concern because they could eat stocked squawfish.

## Key Findings

**Adult movement and spawning**

- Six Colorado Squawfish radio-tagged in upper Lake Powell near Gypsum Canyon in spring 1982 (April-May) showed mostly local movements. Most fish stayed in or near Gypsum Canyon. Contact with some fish was lost in the reservoir. One fish was relocated near Cliften, Colorado, about 40 miles upstream of the Gunnison area.
- Of 16 squawfish implanted in the Grand Junction area, most stayed close to their release sites. Movements over about 40 miles were observed for some fish, and no fish moved more than 50 miles. Contact was lost with most fish by late August.
- Nine radio-tagged fish were released inside gravel pits, and three outside. Most fish released in pits later moved into the Colorado River.
- Near Clifton, nine squawfish, including one radio-tagged fish, were collected from a small side-channel pool, and most of the collected fish were ripe males. A large eddy at RM 176 yielded a ripe male, and a radio-tagged fish recaptured nearby appeared to be a maturing female that had gained about 500 g in six weeks.
- The authors caution that a ripe-male aggregation does not prove spawning occurred nearby. Males ripen earlier than females and can travel considerable distances before spawning.
- Seven ripe squawfish were collected in Gray Canyon on the Green River between July 13 and 22. This is the first documentation of a spawning site there. One 810 mm fish (No. 3001) moved about 322 miles during the study.
- Results indicated spawning in Gray Canyon about three weeks earlier than in Yampa Canyon.

**Larval and YOY surveys**

- 148 larval Colorado Squawfish were collected throughout the study area. Of these, 107 were found, mostly in the reach below Westwater Canyon.
- Larval squawfish were first collected above Loma later in the season than below, which may indicate different spawning times in the two reaches.
- YOY squawfish (30-40 mm TL) were collected in the Colorado River only below Westwater Canyon. Abundance was correlated with earlier high relative abundance of larvae.
- In the Green River, the YOY catch per effort was about half that of previous years. The authors attribute this to very high water levels that eliminated shallow backwater habitat, and they do not consider it a poor spawning year.

**Backwater and gravel pit investigations**

- Adult Colorado Squawfish and [[Razorback Sucker]] were collected from every gravel pit investigated during high flows. Squawfish left the pits before the pits were isolated from the river, while razorback suckers continued to be collected from one pit after isolation.
- Only two larval squawfish were collected in the study, both from Island Backwater on August 11.
- In Fish Pond, the initial population included 155 largemouth bass, 247 green sunfish, 881 bluegill and 1,483 black crappie removed before stocking. Green sunfish remained common despite removal efforts.
- Largemouth bass under 350 mm were the predators that most often contained stocked squawfish. The frequency of squawfish in bass stomachs declined over the study, probably because squawfish were scarcer and water temperatures fell.
- In river backwaters, catch rates were highly variable and often near zero by early November. Some stocked fish were found leaving backwaters in late October, and some were caught about 9 miles downstream.
- Thirty thousand squawfish were microtagged in separate lots. Tags were detected by a magnetic detector, which also checked predator stomachs for tagged fish.

**Management implications**

- The authors conclude that new gravel pits resembling existing pits will likely be used by rare fishes. Use alone, however, does not show that the pits enhance populations.
- Future work will examine how fish find and leave pits, whether pit residence affects maturation or pathogen exposure, and the use of a permanent weir at the outlet of Walter Walker Wildlife Area.
- A proposed Spawning Pond, built in an existing or new gravel pit with controlled inlets, outlets and fish passage, would allow tests of spawning site selection, predator effects and larval drift.

**1983 work plans**

- Radiotelemetry and larval sampling will be split between the Colorado River reach from Palisade to Westwater Canyon and the reach from Westwater to Spanish Bottom, with equal effort in each.
- A continuous radio receiving station will be installed on the Gunnison River upstream of Redlands Diversion.
- Eleven squawfish will be implanted with 18-month transmitters. Up to ten Humpback Chub will be radio-tagged in the Black Rocks area, possibly with pressure-sensitive transmitters to measure depth.
- Up to 20 squawfish will be tagged between Gray Canyon and the Colorado confluence on the Green River.
- YOY monitoring will continue, and known spawning sites will be checked and correlated with 1983 river flows.
