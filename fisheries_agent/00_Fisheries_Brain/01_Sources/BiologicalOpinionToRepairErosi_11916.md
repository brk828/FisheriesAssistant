# Biological Opinion on Phelps-Dodge Eagle Creek Diversion Dam Spillway Repair

#ichthyology #endangered-species #native-fish-conservation #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This biological opinion, issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Arizona Ecological Services Field Office on July 22, 1996, addresses formal consultation under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] regarding proposed spillway repairs at the Phelps-Dodge Eagle Creek Diversion Dam in [[Greenlee County]], [[Arizona]]. The consultation focuses on potential effects to endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). The Service concluded that the proposed repair work would not jeopardize the continued existence of either species, but authorized incidental take of up to 100 spikedace and 20 razorback sucker during construction activities, with anticipated mortalities not exceeding 10 spikedace and 2 razorback sucker.

## Background & Description

### Project Description

The proposed project involves repairing erosion damage to the spillway of the existing Phelps-Dodge water diversion dam on lower [[Eagle Creek]], located in T.4S., R.28E., NW1/4 section 23. The dam is a concrete structure approximately 16 feet tall and 135 feet wide that provides water for Phelps Dodge copper mines and municipal use at [[Morenci]] and [[Clifton]]. A scour hole approximately 140 feet long, 180 feet wide, and 10 feet deep was eroded downstream of the existing apron during winter 1994-95.

The repair would involve pumping water from the scour hole and partially filling it with approximately 1,200 cubic yards of concrete to form an abutment at the toe of the existing spillway apron. The abutment would be about 260 feet wide and extend about 40 feet downstream from the existing apron with a 3:1 slope. A portable concrete batch plant would be set up downstream from the dam on the stream bank in a previously disturbed area.

### Species Accounts

**[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was listed as endangered on October 23, 1991. This species grows to over two feet in length and has a distinctive abrupt, sharp-edged dorsal ridge behind the head. Once common throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]], it now exists sporadically in only about 750 miles of the upper basin. In the lower basin, a substantial population exists only in [[Lake Mohave]]. The 1887 type locality for razorback sucker is the [[Gila River]] at [[Fort Thomas]], downstream from the mouth of Eagle Creek. Between 1981 and 1988, razorback sucker was reintroduced into Eagle Creek using hatchery stock originating from Lake Mohave. Recapture records are few: two were caught in the impoundment above the Phelps-Dodge diversion dam in 1986, 10 were caught downstream from [[Sheep Wash]] in 1987, 1 was caught below the gauging station in 1987, and one was caught near [[Hidden Tank Wash]] in 1988.

Adult razorback sucker inhabit a wide variety of riverine habitats including mainstream and backwater areas such as slow runs, deep eddies, pools, and sloughs, as well as reservoirs. Larval and juvenile habitat use is poorly understood but is generally thought to be primarily shallow, slow-moving areas, backwaters and littoral zones. Spawning occurs from January to May in shallow water over large gravel, cobble, or coarse sand with little or no fine sediment. The species lives up to about 50 years and feeds on plankton, algae and detritus in reservoirs, with riverine populations also consuming a large amount of benthic invertebrates.

**[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*) was listed as threatened on July 1, 1986. This small silvery fish has a well-developed spine in the dorsal fin. Historically occurring throughout mid-elevations of the [[Gila River Drainage]], it is currently known only from [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona), the upper Gila River (Grant and Catron Counties, New Mexico), the middle Gila River (Pinal County, Arizona), Eagle Creek (Greenlee County, Arizona), and the [[Verde River]] (Yavapai County, Arizona).

Spikedace lives in flowing water with slow to moderate water velocities over sand, gravel, and cobble substrate. Specific habitat consists of shear zones where rapid flow borders slower flow, areas of sheet flow at the upper ends of mid-channel sand/gravel bars, and eddies at downstream riffle edges. Spawning occurs from March through May over gravel and cobble where eggs adhere to the substrate. The species lives about two years with reproduction occurring primarily in one-year-old fish. It feeds primarily on aquatic and terrestrial insects.

Spikedace was first reported from Eagle Creek in 1985 when collected as larval fish from lower Eagle Creek. In 1987, an intensive survey found spikedace common in the stretch from near Sheep Wash downstream to below the diversion dam. No spikedace have been found in several sampling efforts since 1987. Recent taxonomic and genetic work indicates substantial differences in morphology and genetic makeup between remnant spikedace populations. Anderson and Hendrickson (1994) found that spikedace in the Verde River and Aravaipa Creek are morphologically distinct, with Eagle Creek and upper Gila River spikedace intermediate between the two extremes.

**[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*) is also mentioned in the consultation. The 1950 record of loach minnow from Eagle Creek, once thought to be an error, has been confirmed by the location of specimens at the [[University of Michigan]]. The Service believes the project may affect but is not likely to adversely affect loach minnow due to the extremely low probability of occurrence in the scour pool at the project site.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat and Hydrology

[[Eagle Creek]] is a tributary of the Gila River in Greenlee County, Arizona. It is a spatially and temporally intermittent stream but is perennial in the project area. In the project area, Eagle Creek flows through a deeply incised narrow valley. "Normal" flows (median of yearly mean) at the stream gage near the dam are 38 cubic feet per second (cfs). Historic maximum flow is 36,800 cfs and historic minimum flow is 2.9 cfs.

Riparian vegetation in the immediate project area is sparse with no significant riparian vegetation present near the diversion dam. The nearest riparian trees are two mesquite (*Prosopis juliflora*) and one cottonwood (*Populus fremontii*), more than 200 feet downstream from the dam apron. Upland vegetation is dominated by mesquite, juniper (*Juniperus monosperma*), shrub live oak (*Quercus turbinella*), catclaw acacia (*Acacia greggii*), yucca (*Yucca* sp.), prickly pear cactus (*Opuntia phaeacantha*), and lippia (*Aloysia wrightii*).

### Human Impacts and Environmental Baseline

Human perturbations to Eagle Creek have come primarily from four types of activities:

1. **Livestock Grazing**: The primary pervasive use of the Eagle Creek watershed for the past 150 years, with substantial alteration of watershed vegetation, soil, erosion, and hydrologic characteristics.

2. **Water Development and Interbasin Transfers**: In 1945, Phelps Dodge Corporation constructed a diversion from the [[Black River]] ([[Salt River Basin]]) into [[Willow Creek]], a tributary of middle Eagle Creek. This diversion augments flow in Eagle Creek below Willow Creek by about 27 percent. That water, plus an additional 9 percent, is removed about 15 miles downstream at the diversion dam and pumping station. Local residents pump groundwater from the basin for domestic and agricultural use, and Phelps Dodge pumps groundwater and places it into the stream channel for transport to the diversion dam.

3. **Mining**: Has affected Eagle Creek through watershed destruction.

4. **Development and Recreation**: Residential and ranch operations, irrigated croplands, roads along the Eagle Creek floodplain, and off-road vehicle use have had substantial impacts to the stream.

These human-caused impacts have altered hydrologic conditions within the Eagle Creek watershed, resulting in an unstable, braided stream channel throughout much of the upper, non-canyon reach. Destabilization has exacerbated flood damage with loss of riparian vegetation, unstable streambanks, and a wide, braided, cobble/gravel floodplain.

### Nonnative Species

Various nonnative aquatic species have been introduced into Eagle Creek and have adversely affected spikedace, loach minnow, razorback sucker, and other native fishes through predation and competition. Nonnative species reported from Eagle Creek include:

- [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*)
- [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*)
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*)
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*)
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*)
- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*)
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*)
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*)
- [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*)
- [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*)
- Crayfish (prob. *Orconectes virilis*)

Native species still form the majority of the fish community in Eagle Creek above the Phelps-Dodge diversion dam, but nonnatives predominate below the dam. The long-term trend in the native/nonnative species balance is toward more nonnatives and fewer natives. However, the presence of the diversion dam has deterred the upstream movement of many nonnatives.

### Previous Consultations

Three formal consultations/conferences have been completed addressing effects to spikedace and razorback sucker in Eagle Creek:

1. Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests Land Management Plan (May 1986) - concluded implementation would not jeopardize spikedace survival and recovery
2. Soil Conservation Service channel stabilization project at Fillman Ranch (January 1994) - concluded no jeopardy to spikedace
3. Baseline and Horse Springs livestock grazing allotments on Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests (July 1995) - concluded no jeopardy to razorback sucker or spikedace

## Key Findings

### Effects Analysis

**Direct and Indirect Effects**: Repair of the dam by adding the proposed concrete abutment in the existing scour hole is unlikely to have any long-term effects on the stream or the spikedace and razorback sucker. The hydrology and channel morphology of the project area and downstream are already highly modified, and the repair is unlikely to alter or add to that modification. Riparian vegetation in the area is sparse and substantially altered, and the proposed action is not expected to disturb any significant amount of riparian vegetation.

Although the scour pool within the work area is not likely to support spikedace in the long term, there is a possibility of spikedace being present in the pool as a temporary refuge during current sub-average rainfall and flow conditions. Razorback sucker may also possibly be found in the scour pool below the dam. If razorback sucker are present, the scour pool would be relatively suitable habitat for the fish and one of the most likely places for any razorback sucker present to be found.

Pumping the water from the pool and filling a portion of the pool with concrete would adversely affect both species, if present, by desiccation and death of individuals. During repair work, the potential exists for introduction of toxic substances into the stream, including petroleum products from project equipment, chemicals being used, or toxic leachate from raw cement coming in contact with stream water. This potential is expected to be minimal for the proposed project.

**Cumulative Effects**: Lower Eagle Creek is primarily privately owned. Ongoing activities on these private lands that would be cumulative to the proposed action include water diversion, recreation, roads, off-road vehicles, and livestock grazing. These activities likely contribute to the degraded condition of the stream channel and fish habitat in Eagle Creek and to the intermittency of stream flow. Ongoing water manipulation to supply the Phelps Dodge mines and the towns of Morenci and Clifton has substantial impacts that are cumulative to the proposed action.

### Biological Opinion Conclusion

After reviewing the current status of the spikedace and razorback sucker, the environmental baseline for the action area, the direct and indirect effects of the proposed action, and the cumulative effects, the Service's biological opinion is that **implementation of repair of the spillway at the Phelps-Dodge diversion dam is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of the spikedace or razorback sucker**. No critical habitat for either of these species is present in the action area.

### Incidental Take Statement

The Service anticipates that the proposed repair may result in incidental take of spikedace and razorback sucker. Removal of water from the scour pool and filling a portion of that pool with concrete would result in take of any listed species present in the pool. Take might occur in the form of death or harassment of individuals from desiccation, handling, encasement in concrete, or failure to find suitable habitat in the relocation area.

**Anticipated Level of Incidental Take**:
- Up to 100 spikedace captured and relocated
- Up to 20 razorback sucker captured and relocated
- Up to 10 spikedace mortalities
- Up to 2 razorback sucker mortalities

### Reasonable and Prudent Measures

The Service identified three reasonable and prudent measures necessary to minimize incidental taking:

1. Conduct all proposed actions in a manner that will minimize direct mortality of spikedace and razorback sucker
2. Monitor the fish community and habitat to document levels of incidental take
3. Maintain complete and accurate records of actions which may result in take of spikedace and razorback sucker and loss or modification of their habitat

### Terms and Conditions

Key terms and conditions for implementation include:

- Minimize use of heavy equipment within the wetted area of Eagle Creek outside the scour pool
- Ensure no pollutants enter surface waters during action implementation
- A fish biologist must be present at all times during emptying of the pool and transport/relocation of fish
- Fish shall be captured using nets or standard electrofishing gear during pumping
- Razorback sucker shall be moved above the dam into deepest pools available
- Spikedace shall be relocated below the dam in runs or riffles (not above dam due to potential for mistaken transport of [[Red Shiner]])
- Adequate holding facilities (minimum 100-gallon non-toxic tank with oxygen) must be available at project site
- Monitor for dead or dying fish within 500 yards downstream during project activities
- Submit report to Service within 60 days following project completion with complete information on species and numbers of all fish found, disposition of nonlisted fishes, and details on any listed fishes

### Additional Recommendations

The Service noted that effects to listed species from the diversion dam itself have not been analyzed through section 7 consultation. Although the dam was originally constructed in 1897, it has been substantially rebuilt several times, most recently following flooding in 1993. The dam has substantial effects, some adverse and some beneficial, on the native fish fauna of Eagle Creek. The Service recommended the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] enter consultation on past and future effects of the dam and periodic repair and reconstruction needs.

The Service also concurred with findings of no effect to [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*), [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy-Owl]] (*Glaucidium brasilianum cactorum*), [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*), and [[Mexican Spotted Owl]] (*Strix occidentalis lucida*), and concurred with a finding of no effect for [[Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus*) conditional on the proposed work not being conducted between March 1 and July 15.
