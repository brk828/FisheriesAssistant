# Effects of Toxaphene upon Plankton and Aquatic Invertebrates in North Dakota Lakes

#freshwater-ecology #zooplankton #aquatic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1962 Master of Science thesis by Robert G. Needham, submitted to [[Montana State College]], examines how low concentrations of the piscicide [[Toxaphene]] affect plankton and larger aquatic invertebrates. The work was tied to North Dakota's [[Rough-Fish Removal]] program. Five lakes and reservoirs were sampled from June through September 1960 and 1961, and the author supplemented field data with laboratory tolerance tests.

The main result is that the dominant zooplankton showed no marked reductions after treatment at 5 to 35 ppb. The most notable post-treatment change was an increase in the blue-green alga [[Aphanizomenon]]. Laboratory tests ranked the tolerance of zooplankton and invertebrate groups, and the author concluded that field effects were probably smaller than effects expected with longer exposure.

## Background & Description

- **Author and context:** Needham completed a B.S. in Fish and Wildlife Management at Montana State College in 1960 and was a graduate student there when the thesis was submitted. He was employed by the North Dakota State Game and Fish Department during summers. The study was funded through Dingell-Johnson Projects F-2-R-7,9, and the National Wildlife Federation provided a fellowship for the final year.
- **Rationale:** The author notes that toxicant effects on fish are well documented, but effects on fish-food organisms are much less known. Previous toxaphene studies cited include [[Hooper and Grzenda]] (1955, Michigan), [[Hoffman and Olive]] (1961, Colorado), [[Cushing and Olive]] (1957, Colorado), and [[Stringer and McMynn]] (1958, British Columbia).
- **Study sites:** Five waterbodies were studied. Two natural lakes were in north central ND. Three impoundments were in the southwest. Each site is summarized below.
  - [[Wolf Butte Reservoir]]: 24 acres, maximum depth 9 ft, muck bottom, runoff-fed.
  - [[Raleigh Reservoir]]: 15 acres, maximum depth 18 ft, muck with silt, runoff-fed.
  - [[South Lake Metigoshe]]: glacial lake in the Turtle Mountains, 915 surface acres, average depth 9 ft, peat and muck bottom.
  - [[North Lake Metigoshe]]: adjacent to South Lake Metigoshe and connected by a channel about 30 ft wide. It was untreated during the study and served as a control.
  - [[Odland Reservoir]]: 100 acres, maximum depth 16 ft, muck bottom.
- **Toxaphene formulation:** The product was Cooper-Tox, an emulsified concentrate with six pounds of technical toxaphene per gallon. It was diluted 10 to 15 times with water and sprayed from a boat.

### Sampling methods

- **Plankton:** Pumped samples were taken at 1.5 and 7.5 ft. Each sample was 40 gallons, concentrated to 200 cc with a No. 20 silk net. Counts were total enumeration of 1 cc, or differential counts of 20 to 80 fields for abundant phytoplankters.
- **Plant-inhabiting organisms:** A custom metal sampler enclosed one square foot to a height of 30 inches. It was used in water 2 ft deep or less. Yields were expressed per pound of plants. In 1961, sample size was reduced to about 12 oz because analyses showed this was adequate.
- **Bottom fauna:** Ekman dredge samples were taken at 4 to 10 ft, usually 3 or 4 sq ft per station, and were concentrated with 30-mesh screen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** Table 1 reports Secchi depth, total solids, total hardness, pH, alkalinity, chlorides, sulfates, and iron before and after treatment. Most waters were moderately alkaline, with pH between about 8.2 and 9.8. Bottom temperatures ran roughly 60 to 71°F. No marked thermal stratification was found in any waterbody.
- **Vegetation:** Aquatic plants were abundant in all sites. The dominant species included *Potamogeton pectinatus*, *P. richardsoni*, *Myriophyllum exalbescens*, and filamentous algae (*Rhizoclonium*). At South Lake Metigoshe, the dominant plants also included *Najas flexilis*, *Ceratophyllum demersum*, and *Scirpus* sp.
- **Fish communities:** The reservoirs held green sunfish, black bullheads, and rainbow trout (Wolf Butte), and golden shiners, green sunfish, and white and black crappies (Raleigh). South Lake Metigoshe held yellow perch, black bullheads, northern pike, and walleye. Odland had young-of-the-year bullheads, yellow perch, northern pike, white crappie, and orangespotted sunfish.
- **Treatment levels:** Concentrations and dates were as follows.
  - Wolf Butte: 35 ppb on August 8, 1960.
  - Raleigh: 25 ppb on August 4, 1960, followed by a second treatment at 90 ppb on September 26, 1960, because a complete kill was not achieved.
  - South Lake Metigoshe: 10 ppb on July 17, 1960, plus 5 ppb on July 19. The table headings list this as 15 ppb.
  - Odland: 5 ppb on August 11, 1960.
- **Experimental context:** Laboratory tests used battery jars of 8 liters of filtered lake water at about 68°F, and galvanized tanks of 20 gallons at 71°F for larger invertebrates. The water had a dissolved oxygen content of 9.8 ppm, total alkalinity of 341 ppm, and pH 8.4.

## Key Findings

### Fish response

- **Wolf Butte Reservoir:** Many green sunfish and black bullheads were found dead or dying after treatment. Test netting a week later with two 125-ft gill nets (24 hours) caught no fish. In August 1961, one gill net and one frame net caught about 475 black bullheads and 83 trout. Young-of-the-year green sunfish were also seen, and adult green sunfish were trapped later in the fall.
- **South Lake Metigoshe:** The effects were severe for young fish and for some adults. Adult yellow perch dropped from 900 per 100 net-hours before treatment to 36 at one week and none at 11 months. Young-of-the-year perch fell from 610 to six, then to zero. Young-of-the-year bullheads fell from 240 to nine, then to zero. The author suspected residual toxaphene effects for the low catches at 11 months.
- **Odland Reservoir:** Large numbers of young-of-the-year bullheads and yellow perch were found dead the day after treatment. Adults were not significantly reduced, since only a few were found dead and large numbers were caught in later netting.
- **Raleigh Reservoir:** Large numbers of golden shiners, green sunfish, and crappies died. The first pre-treatment frame nets held several thousand golden shiners, about 5,000 green sunfish, and 1,200 white crappies. A week after the first treatment, two gill nets caught only ten white suckers and five black bullheads.

### Zooplankton

- **Wolf Butte:** *Keratella* fell from 91 before treatment to 15 at one week and two at one month. *Daphnia* dropped from 244 to 18 at one week, then rose to 129 at one month. *Cyclops* fell from 46 to three at one week, then rose to 11 at one month.
- **Raleigh (first treatment, 25 ppb):** *Brachionus* fell from 114 to 15 by 33 days. *Daphnia* fell from 65 to nine by 371 days. *Bosmina* dropped from 314 to 50 by 33 days and was absent at 371 days.
- **Raleigh (second treatment, 90 ppb):** The author reported that a marked reduction of many plankters followed this treatment. At 371 days, water levels had dropped about 6 ft, which may have raised toxaphene concentrations.
- **South Lake Metigoshe vs. North Lake Metigoshe:** *Trichocerca* fell from 234 to 31 at 40 days, and *Bosmina* from 110 to eight. Because North Lake Metigoshe showed similar declines, the author judged the South Lake decreases not significant.
- **Odland:** *Bosmina* fell from 459 to 24 at 28 days, and *Cyclops* from 71 to 16. Both recovered to abundant levels by 362 days.

### Phytoplankton

- **Cyanophyta** were the most abundant algae in all lakes.
- **[[Aphanizomenon]]** increased in all lakes after treatment. At Wolf Butte it rose from 7,377 to 54,716 at one month. At Raleigh it rose from 6 to 81,902 at 33 days.
- **[[Synechocystis]]** at Wolf Butte rose from 90,067 to 589,405 at one week, then fell to 149,306 at one month.
- At South Lake Metigoshe, the author judged that the large increases of *Anabaena*, *Aphanizomenon*, and *Nodularia* might be treatment effects, since they did not increase at untreated North Lake Metigoshe.

### Plant-inhabiting organisms and bottom fauna

- **Overall:** The most common plant-associated and bottom organisms showed no marked, consistent changes. The author attributed the few decreases largely to normal fluctuation, emergence, or sampling variation.
- **Wolf Butte:** *Gammarus* remained abundant. *Callibaetis*, *Caenis*, and *Ischnura* decreased at one week and one month, then recovered by one year. [[Gastropoda]] (*Physa* and *Gyraulus*) rose from 771 to 1,558 per pound by one year.
- **Raleigh:** *Gammarus* increased from 31 to 569 per pound at 33 days. *Sigara* fell from 39 to none after treatment, which the author linked to toxaphene because it had low tolerance in experiments.
- **Odland:** *Caenis* and [[Tendipes]] decreased slightly. The author judged this a probable normal fluctuation.
- **South Lake Metigoshe:** No marked changes were found among 28 genera of plant-associated organisms. No pre-treatment bottom samples were taken.

### Laboratory tolerance experiments

- **Zooplankton:** Rotifera were the most tolerant group, followed by Cladocera and Copepoda. Marked reductions occurred in Rotifera at 500 ppb, Cladocera at 250 ppb, and Copepoda at 100 ppb.
- **Daphnia magna:** No effects were obvious from 50 to 400 ppb. Movements were retarded at 1000 ppb and had nearly ceased at 1500 ppb.
- **Larger invertebrates:** Tolerance (100 percent survival) ranked in decreasing order as Hirudinea, Hydracarina, Gastropoda, Trichoptera, Odonata, Hemiptera, Ephemeroptera, Amphipoda, and Coleoptera. Survival between 100 percent survival and 100 percent mortality showed an approximately straight-line relationship. Genera within a group varied in tolerance.
- **Temperature:** Lower temperatures increased tolerance. For *Lestes*, tolerance rose about 35 percent when the temperature was lowered ten degrees.
- **Control check:** [[Fathead Minnow]] at 10 ppb produced 100 percent mortality, while all experimental invertebrates survived. Washed tanks showed no fathead deaths, so the author judged washing adequate.

### Discussion and conclusions

- The author concluded that extensive fish removal can be done without seriously affecting plankton. Large plankton reductions were reported at about 100 ppb by other workers.
- Other studies (Stringer and McMynn 1958; Hooper and Grzenda 1955; Cushing and Olive 1957) reported stronger effects on Amphipoda, Ephemeroptera, Odonata, and Tendipedidae at 10 to 100 ppb. Gastropoda, Oligochaeta, Hirudinea, and Unionidae appeared more resistant.
- The author cautioned that lower tolerance levels probably exist under field conditions with longer exposure periods.
