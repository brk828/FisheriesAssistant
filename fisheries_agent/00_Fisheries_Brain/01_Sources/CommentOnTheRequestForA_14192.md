# Comment on the Request for a Ruling on the Stem of Family-Group Names Based on the Type Genus *Petromyzon*

## Executive Summary
This document is not a primary biological or ecological study but a **taxonomic nomenclature dispute** published in the *Bulletin of Zoological Nomenclature* (1975). It addresses a formal request submitted to the International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN) regarding the correct **stem and spelling** of family-group names derived from the type genus **[[Petromyzon]]** (lampreys). Two ichthyologists — Carl L. Hubbs and C. G. Gruchy — offer competing perspectives on whether the family name should be spelled **Petromyzonidae** (traditional, majority usage) or **Petromyzontidae** (adopted by the American Fisheries Society species lists of 1960 and 1970). The debate also touches on correct authorship attribution of the family-group name and the grammatical gender of the genus name.

## Background & Description
No morphological data on lamprey anatomy is presented in this text. The document is purely nomenclatural/etymological, concerning:
- The **grammatical gender** of the generic name *Petromyzon* (treated consistently as **masculine** since the post-Linnaean era).
- The **etymological derivation** of the stem "Petromyzon-" vs. "Petromyzont-", debated via Greek transliteration (μύξον/myxon vs. μύζον/myzon, referencing mucus/slime — a nod to the biologically relevant trait of lampreys' mucous-covered skin, though not elaborated further).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
No direct ecological data (habitat, diet, life history) is provided. However, contextual references identify **[[Petromyzon|Lampreys]]** (family [[Petromyzonidae]], order Petromyzontiformes) as the taxonomic group under discussion — a well-known jawless fish (Agnatha) lineage with anadromous and parasitic/freshwater life histories, as established in broader lamprey biology literature (e.g., *The Biology of Lampreys*, Hubbs & Potter, 1971).

## Key Findings
- **Genus placement**: Carl L. Hubbs recommends the ICZN formally enter **[[Petromyzon]]** in the Official List of Generic Names in Zoology, specifying it as **masculine gender**, based on consistent post-Linnaean usage.
- **Family name placement**: Hubbs recommends entering **Petromyzonidae** (not *Petromyzontidae*) in the Official List of Family-Group Names, citing:
  - Historical dominance of the "Petromyzon-" stem from Linnaeus through the mid-20th century.
  - Widespread adoption of "Petromyzonidae" in major reference works: *Webster's New International Dictionary* (1934), the *Zoological Record* (through 1973), *Encyclopedia Americana* (1969), and *New Encyclopaedia Britannica, Micropaedia* (1974).
  - A 1972 ICZN ruling (Monte Carlo meeting) stating that a pre-1961 family-group name based on an incorrectly formed stem is **not to be amended** if it is in general use — protecting "Petromyzonidae" even if its stem were technically incorrect.
- **Source of the "Petromyzontidae" spelling**: Traced to the 2nd (1960) and 3rd (1970) editions of the American Fisheries Society's *A List of Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada*, which — without explanation — used "Petromyzontiformes" and "Petromyzontidae." This spelling was reportedly imposed editorially on author V. D. Vladykov against his explicit objection.
- **Authorship dispute** (C. G. Gruchy's contribution):
  - Hubbs and Potter (1971) attributed the family-group name to **Bonaparte (1832/1831)**.
  - Gruchy argues authorship should instead go to **Risso (1826)**, who used "Petromyzides" as a supra-generic taxon (family) based on the genus *Petromyzon* — despite using an incorrect suffix, this satisfies ICZN Article 11(e) for establishing a family-group name.
  - Bonaparte (1831) was the first to use the *correctly suffixed* form "Petromyzonidae," but priority for family-group name usage is claimed for Risso.
- **Etymological critique**: Gruchy questions Gill's (1894) transliteration rationale for preferring the stem "PETROMYZON-" over "PETROMYZONT-," noting inconsistency in Gill's Greek-to-Latin derivation (μύξον/myxon vs. μύζον/myzon).
- **Practical/scientific impact of unstable nomenclature**: Frequent, unexplained name changes in systematic literature cause:
  - Confusion among students and professional biologists using taxonomic keys.
  - Disruption of phylogenetic interpretation when generic names are reassigned.
  - Serious difficulties for information storage, retrieval, and literature searches in bibliographic/abstracting systems.
