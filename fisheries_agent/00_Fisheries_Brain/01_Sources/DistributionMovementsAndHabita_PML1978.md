# Distribution, Movements, and Habitat Use of Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in a Lower Colorado River Reservoir, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study used sonic telemetry to track the distribution, movements, and habitat use of wild adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a mainstem [[Colorado River]] reservoir on the Arizona-Nevada border. Ten fish were tagged in November 1994 and tracked until July 1997; six females and four males were tracked for the full 14-month transmitter life and used in the core analysis. Movement rates and ranges were similar to those reported from riverine populations, fish returned to spawning sites used in earlier years, and females were more active than males during peak spawning. Post-spawning, fish moved toward the warm/cold water mixing zone, which the authors link to productive algal feeding habitat. The authors also argue that reservoirs offer a more observable setting for studying this endangered species than rivers.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is the largest catostomid in North America and is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Once common, the species is now restricted to relict and fragmented populations. Three of four known populations exceeding 100 fish are in reservoirs, the largest in Lake Mohave. The species was listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1991.

Telemetry studies have mostly focused on rivers, where adults use backwater and other lentic habitats. Riverine monitoring is hampered by remote, large systems, scarce fish, and a short (2-3 week) spawning season on the ascending limb of spring runoff. Lake Mohave was created by the closure of [[Davis Dam]] in 1954, the last mainstem reservoir built on the Lower Colorado River. The reservoir population is thought to have originated from high recruitment of individuals trapped upstream during dam closure.

**Methods (summary):**
- Ten wild adults were captured with a trammel net at Yuma and Tequila coves, measured, weighed, anesthetized with tricaine methanesulfate, and surgically implanted with sonic transmitters (6 g, 14-month life expectancy, about 1 km detection range, logged body temperature).
- Weekly boat surveys (later biweekly) used a [[GPS Survey Grid]] of 71 listening stations spaced at intervals of less than 1600 m, with hand-held directional hydrophones.
- Movement rates, distances from shore, and shoreline use were analysed in GIS. Spawning was defined as November-April and nonspawning as May-October. Active spawning was confirmed by observation and collection of larval suckers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mohave has no tributaries other than the Colorado River, which enters as cold hypolimnetic releases from [[Hoover Dam]]. The reservoir has a surface area of 11,655 ha at full pool, a maximum depth of 35 m at Davis Dam, and water temperatures of 12-16 °C in the cold inflow zone. Summer stratification produced a thermal gradient from about 30 °C at the surface to 15 °C at depth. The reservoir offers higher visibility (more than 10-15 m), flows of 0-2 m/s, and access to both littoral and pelagic habitats, which the authors contrast with the more variable physical conditions of natural rivers.

Fish distribution was strongly seasonal. [[Big Basin]] and [[Owl Point]] were used year-round, [[Tequila Cove]] and [[Yuma Cove]] were used seasonally, and the warm/cold mixing zone was used in summer. Fish were seldom detected in up- or down-reservoir canyon habitats.

## Key Findings

- **Home range and movement:** Individual linear ranges averaged 39 km (18-72 km). Females averaged 42 km (29-72 km) and males 35 km (18-51 km). Mean daily movement was 521 m/d (range 2-1,665 m/d in the reported summary), with females slightly more active (593 m/d) than males (446 m/d). The difference between sexes was not statistically significant for the full data set.
- **Spawning-period activity:** During active spawning (31 January-15 March 1995 and 1996), females were significantly more active than males (ANOVA F = 6.79, P = 0.0314; 180 vs. 87 m/d).
- **Spawning-site fidelity:** All study fish returned to spawning sites used in previous years, and most females also visited other spawning sites. In some cases spawning sites were more than 50 km apart. One of ten fish crossed the reservoir between spawning groups on both the Arizona and Nevada shorelines. Males typically remained at a single site.
- **Post-spawning movement:** Following spawning, 70% of study fish moved up-reservoir to the warm/cold water mixing zone, where nutrient-rich releases stimulate algal production. The authors interpret this as feeding on productive habitat, which may help fish regain body condition.
- **Shoreline use:** Fish used shoreline habitat significantly more than expected (chi-square, P < 0.001). Most detections were within 50 m of shore, and mean distance from shore during spawning was less than 30 m. Fish moved offshore during summer (July-November), possibly to avoid warmer water.
- **Depth and body temperature:** Average depths ranged from 3.1 to 16.9 m (range 0.2 to more than 30 m). Body temperatures ranged from 12 to 28 °C across the thermal gradient, but summer body temperatures were typically 18-22 °C, similar to thermal preferences reported by Bulkley and Pimentel (1983). Average depth changed seasonally, with fish nearer the surface in spring and autumn and deeper in midsummer, suggesting thermoregulation through the selection of preferred temperatures during stratification. Vertical movement showed no correlation with time of day.
- **Sample and data volume:** More than 1,000 contacts were recorded, including 580 daily sightings, 300 hourly sightings, 800 body temperatures, and 100 vertical temperature profiles. Study fish averaged 609 mm total length (555-680 mm) and 2.78 kg (1.75-4.21 kg).
- **Reproduction:** Larval collections confirmed reproduction in the reservoir, which the authors note is rarely documented in river studies. Reservoir spawning runs from January to March, earlier and typically two to three times longer than riverine spawning.
- **Conservation implication:** The authors argue that reservoir populations are important for the species' recovery, and that backwater and floodplain habitats are critical for restoration.
