# First Records from Texas of Hybopsis gracilis and Notropis girardi, with Comments on Geographic Variation of the Latter

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports the first Texas records of the [[Flathead Chub]] (*Hybopsis gracilis*) and the [[Arkansas River Shiner]] (*Notropis girardi*), both common in the mainstem of the [[Canadian River]] across the Texas Panhandle. The authors also present a morphometric comparison of *N. girardi* from three localities, finding significant geographic differences in eye and head proportions. They conclude that divergence has occurred, but they decline to assign subspecific names, citing continuous populations, clinal variation, and the species' intermittent, flood-dependent spawning ecology.

## Background & Description

- **Prior literature:** Three recent accounts of Texas fishes (Jurgens and Hubbs 1953; Knapp 1953; Baughman 1950) did not list either species, and the authors found no other Texas record.
- **Flathead chub:** Widespread in larger rivers of the Great Plains north to southern Canada. The Canadian River appears to mark its southern range limit. Panhandle specimens are referred to *H. g. communis*.
- **Arkansas River shiner:** Endemic to the [[Arkansas River System]], abundant in the sand-filled channels of the South Canadian, North Canadian, and Cimarron rivers in Oklahoma and the Arkansas River in Kansas.
- **Motivation:** Visual comparison of Texas and Kansas *N. girardi* suggested a difference in eye size, prompting a formal test of [[Geographic Variation]].

### Collection Localities (1954, Dalquest and Lewis)

All four sites were in the Canadian River mainstem:
1. Town of Canadian, Hemphill County
2. Highway 117 crossing north of Borger, Hutchinson County
3. Highway 287 crossing north of Amarillo, Potter County
4. Old Tascosa bridge crossing, Oldham County

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range and habitat.** The Canadian River is intermittent and often lacks surface flow over long distances. The authors judge the regional habitat "monotonous." Variables such as flow volume, stream bed, gradient, turbidity, and temperature change only gradually, both across and along channels.

**Spawning ecology (after [[Moore 1944]]).**
- *N. girardi* appears to spawn only in mainstreams during high water.
- Free-floating eggs and larvae drift downstream in fast currents.
- About three days pass from spawning until larvae can move horizontally, so young begin active life many miles below the parent site.
- Subsequent dispersal patterns are unknown.

**Dispersal and gene flow.** No apparent barriers separate populations, but the authors note that population interchange is small enough for racial differences to arise. Morphological expression may follow a [[Clinal Variation]] gradient, which the intermediate lower Canadian population supports.

## Key Findings

### Meristic characters
- **Anal rays:** Almost always 8 in all three series.
- **Lateral-line scales:** 32–37, most often 34 or 35. No appreciable difference among localities.

### Morphometric comparison of *N. girardi* (Table I)
Measurements are thousandths of the larger dimension. Each series contained 25 specimens, compared within similar size ranges to reduce ontogenetic effects.

| Measure | Texas, upper Canadian (KU 3245) | Oklahoma, lower Canadian/South Canadian (KU 2330) | Kansas, Arkansas River (KU 2651) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard length, mean (mm) | 34.4 (31.0–38.0) | 33.4 (30.5–40.2) | 34.3 (32.0–37.4) |
| Head length / SL, mean | 238 (227–252) | 244 (234–257) | 249 (237–259) |
| Orbit / SL, mean | 060 (055–064) | 065 (062–069) | 069 (062–075) |
| Orbit / head length, mean | 254 (241–275) | 266 (250–282) | 276 (258–291) |

- The lower Canadian population is intermediate between the upper Canadian and upper Arkansas populations in all three characters.
- Means differ significantly among the three localities in all three characters.
- Orbit/SL and orbit/head length separate upper Canadian and Arkansas specimens at about 75 per cent. Neither extreme can be separated from the lower Canadian population at that level.
- Additional specimens from Hemphill County, Texas (KU 3410) and Barton County, Kansas (KU 2662) agreed closely with their regional values, so the extremes are not aberrant.

### Conclusions
1. **Divergence:** Variation has progressed to the point that upper Canadian and upper Arkansas populations could be recognized as different subspecies. Lower Canadian specimens would be intergrades.
2. **No names assigned:** The authors withhold names until the species is studied across its full range, over several years and under varied environmental conditions. Their reasons include clinal gradients, habitat uniformity, intermittency, and the spawning and drift pattern described above.
3. **General significance:** Subspecies names would exaggerate the contrast between extremes and could obscure the nature of the variation. The authors argue that intraspecific variation is itself the key level for evolutionary study and that similar problems are common among plains fishes.
