# Morphology and Foods of Hybrid Catostomid Fishes from Southern Arizona

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #hybridization #feeding-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study examines putative F₁ hybrids between [[Catostomus insignis]] (Sonoran sucker) and [[Pantosteus clarki]] (desert sucker) from [[Aravaipa Creek]] and [[Bonita Creek]] in southeastern Arizona. Morphological analyses using hybrid index (HI), discriminant function analysis (DFA), and principal components analysis (PCA) demonstrated intermediacy in most characters distinguishing parental forms, though with tendencies toward C. insignis. Coefficients of variation were similar between hybrid and parental samples, suggesting lack of backcrossing. Food habits of hybrids averaged intermediate to parental forms but showed high variation. Hybrids utilized a broad food base spanning the feeding habits of both parents. Results suggest that additive inheritance of morphological and behavioral traits is influenced by dominance and/or interallelic suppression, producing deviations from often-assumed intermediacy in hybrid fishes.

## Background & Description

[[Pantosteus clarki]] (desert sucker) represents one of the most specialized products of Pantosteus evolution, having developed cartilaginous sheaths on the jaws as an adaptation for scraping diatoms and other materials from solid substrates. Associated specializations for herbivory include a black peritoneum and elongated digestive tract. In contrast, [[Catostomus insignis]] (Sonoran sucker) is a generalized carnivore foraging in pools and slower runs on aquatic invertebrates, lacking specialized jaws, darkened peritoneum, or markedly lengthened gut, though it facultatively ingests algae, mud, seeds, and plant material at certain times of year.

Natural intermediates between these broadly sympatric species provide one of the most clear-cut and commonly reported examples of apparent hybridization in North American freshwater fishes. Miscegenation is geographically widespread and not associated with any fixed zone. Hybridization most likely occurs by chance due to common times (winter, spring) and places (gravelly riffles) of reproduction.

Specimens were collected from [[Aravaipa Creek]] and [[Bonita Creek]], relatively small, gravel-bottomed tributaries of the [[Gila River]] system, between 1964 and 1982. Hybrids were identified through 9 morphometric and 7 meristic features: standard length (SL), head length (HL), snout length (SN), mouth length (ML), length of union of lower lips (LU), width of cartilaginous ridge of lower jaw (RW), isthmus width (IW), mouth width (MW), head depth (HD), caudal peduncle depth (CP), scales along lateral line (LL), scales below lateral line (BL), scales around caudal peduncle (AP), scales above lateral line (AL), rows of papillae crossing midline of lower lip (LP), dorsal fin rays (DR), and pelvic fin rays (PR).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Both study streams are tributaries of the [[Gila River]] system in southeastern Arizona. [[Aravaipa Creek]] supported 7 native fish species, while [[Bonita Creek]] had 5 native species plus considerable introduced fish fauna in lower reaches. The streams differ in physical and biological characteristics, with temporal variations in hydrology ranging from drought through normal to flood conditions during the study period.

Such variations in desert streams influence habitat diversity and abundance and availability of foods, which are reflected in feeding patterns of fishes. Five of seven Aravaipa fishes and 2 of 5 in Bonita Creek relied on ephemeropteran nymphs as a major food base. Relative abundance of parental forms differed between streams: in Bonita Creek, P. clarki comprised 35.5% and C. insignis only 2.7% of total fishes in late 1970s samples, while comparable estimates for Aravaipa Creek were 27.3% and 23.0% respectively.

Spatial partitioning of fishes in Aravaipa Creek is pronounced, with C. insignis typically holding in pools and P. clarki feeding in current on riffles. Fine sand accumulates in pools while coarser materials occur in current, though plants on riffles sieve fine sand and debris from the water column.

## Key Findings

**Morphological Analysis:**

Hybrid means were intermediate in all characters that broadly discriminated the presumed parental species. SN, DR, PR, and AL differed little among parentals and hybrids. Coefficients of variation were similar among groups for all characters (Table 1).

Hybrid index (HI) analysis placed Aravaipa Creek hybrids at grand mean HI of 0.23 (0.36 when four characters of limited parental discrimination excluded). All Bonita Creek hybrids were clearly intermediate (HI grand mean 0.33). Six of 16 characters per individual from Aravaipa Creek varied outside parental means, typically toward C. insignis.

In DFA, LP, LU, RW, and IW were weighted highest in formation of canonical variable I (CV-I) in both samples. Hybrids segregated from and were intermediate between parental samples in plots of CV-I and CV-II, positioned slightly nearer C. insignis. Five atypical specimens from Bonita Creek separated from typical P. clarki along CV-II.

PCA placed putative hybrids intermediate to parental forms, with only one presumed hybrid from Aravaipa Creek overlapping the range of scores of C. insignis along sheared component II (H2). Character loadings were similar to DFA results.

**Food Habits:**

Average stomach fullness (excluding parasites) ranged between 27% and 53% among fishes containing foods. [[Catostomus insignis]] consumed the greatest number of food categories in both streams, eating the most invertebrates (primarily ephemeropteran nymphs) by relative volume and frequency of occurrence. Invertebrates were far less important by volume than vegetative material in P. clarki (10.2% and 18.2% for Aravaipa and Bonita creeks respectively), despite being frequent in occurrence (51.6% and 88.9%). Hybrids ate the same foods as P. clarki and consumed volumes intermediate to those of C. insignis and P. clarki.

Plant detritus was conspicuous in diets of all suckers in Aravaipa Creek (18.8%, 6.8%, and 13.2% estimated volumes in C. insignis, hybrids, and P. clarki respectively), but occurred only as traces (<2.0%) in Bonita Creek fish. Diatoms and filamentous algae (mostly [[Cladophora glomerata]]) were significant by volume in hybrids and P. clarki in both streams, but mostly not used by C. insignis.

Sand ingestion patterns differed qualitatively and quantitatively: P. clarki ingested large amounts of fine sand (<0.2 mm diameter; 53.0% and 17.8% relative volumes for Aravaipa and Bonita creeks), while C. insignis contained smaller amounts (5.0% and 7.4%) of larger diameter (>0.5 mm) grains. Hybrids ingested fine particles typical of P. clarki at intermediate (Aravaipa) or lesser (Bonita) volumes.

DFA of food categories showed variation in CV-I of hybrids exceeding or attaining that of parental species, with intermediate means. In Aravaipa Creek, inorganic and animal categories contributed most to CV-I; in Bonita Creek, animal material and diatoms were best discriminators.

**Parasitism:**

An intestinal cestode tentatively identified as [[Isoglaridacris bulbocirrus]] was abundant in C. insignis and hybrids from Aravaipa Creek. Incidence was 26.3% in C. insignis (10 of 38 fish), averaging 20.9% of estimated total volume in infected fish. Infestation in hybrids was 37.5% (6 of 16 fish), with cestodes averaging 10.7% of volume. No P. clarki from Aravaipa supported parasites in the anterior digestive tract. In Bonita Creek, infestation of P. clarki was occasionally extreme (mean volume 67.7%, occurrence 53.8%), with many stomachs essentially filled (≥90.0%) by parasites. Cestodes accounted for about 23.4% of gut volume in C. insignis at 38.5% infestation. Only one of 11 Bonita Creek hybrids contained parasites.

**Genetic Interpretation:**

Similar coefficients of variation among parental forms and hybrids, along with compact clustering along CV-I in DFA, provide independent indications of lack of introgression and support identification as F₁ generation. Trends toward C. insignis in some characters (ML, RW, MW, HD, BL, AP in Aravaipa Creek; ML, LU, MW, HD, BL in Bonita Creek) support conclusions that genic dominance and/or interallelic suppression alters intermediate expressions of phenotypes in fish hybrids.

Previous blood protein analyses suggested introgressive hybridization between C. insignis and P. clarki in evolutionary time, with serum enzyme patterns of P. clarki exhibiting unique affinities with C. insignis and other Catostomus species.
