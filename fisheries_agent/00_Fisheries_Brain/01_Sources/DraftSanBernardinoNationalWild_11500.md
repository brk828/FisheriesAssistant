# San Bernardino and Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuges Comprehensive Management Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This Comprehensive Management Plan (CMP) covers the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge]] in Cochise County, Arizona, and sets a 20-year planning framework for managing both units as elements of a larger "area of ecological concern" centred on the San Bernardino Valley and the Rio San Bernardino watershed, which is a tributary of the [[Rio Yaqui]] basin. The refuges are presented as the last natural sanctuaries in the United States for four threatened and endangered fishes: the [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Yaqui Topminnow]], [[Yaqui Catfish]] and [[Beautiful Shiner]]. The plan integrates water rights, habitat restoration, endangered species recovery, public use, cultural resources and cross-border cooperation with Mexico, and it notes that recovery of the Yaqui fishes cannot be achieved within the United States alone.

## Background & Description

**Refuge establishment and extent**
- [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] was established in 1983 under the Fish and Wildlife Act of 1956 and the Endangered Species Act, with its purpose to "conserve fish or wildlife which are listed as endangered species or threatened species...or plants." The source also gives the establishment date as 1983 and states the refuge was purchased in 1982 from the [[Nature Conservancy]]; it consists of 2,309 acres.
- [[Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge]] was added to the refuge system as a unit of San Bernardino NWR on May 31, 1988, under the Endangered Species Act. The Nature Conservancy held the 1,240-acre parcel in trust until fee title purchase. It attained full refuge status in summer 1993 and is managed by San Bernardino staff.
- The historic Slaughter ranch headquarters (131 acres) was sold to the [[Johnson Historical Museum]]; the Service holds an easement for fisheries management in House Pond.

**Management units**
- Management Units are zones defined by dominant management activity, e.g., the Leslie Creek Endangered Fish Management Unit. Special Project areas (rehabilitation needs) and Special Protection Areas (sensitive sites with elevated public-use threats) were also mapped.

**Physical setting**
- The area lies where the Sonoran and Chihuahuan Deserts overlap and includes alluvial flood plains, low hills, mesas, the Swisshelm and Chiricahua Mountains, and deeply eroded arroyos.
- The San Bernardino Valley forms the headwaters of the Rio Yaqui, which flows to the Gulf of California and the Pacific Ocean.

**Land ownership and jurisdiction**
- The Service holds proprietary jurisdiction. Adjacent lands are private ranch land, [[Bureau of Land Management]] land, Resolution Trust Corporation land and State of Arizona land, mostly used for cattle grazing.

**Socioeconomic context**
- Cochise County economy was historically based on copper smelting in Bisbee; mining employment fell to 4.2 percent of the county workforce. Agriculture and ranching rank first in employment and product value.
- The source reports that the Maquiladora Program had grown to 1,925 plants employing 452,399 workers.
- Douglas population is about 19,000; Agua Prieta about 80,000; total market-area population about 180,000, projected at approximately 250,000 by 2010.
- 1992 traffic averages: 50,333 pedestrian and 357,398 vehicle, totalling 407,750.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and seismicity**
- The San Bernardino Valley formed during the middle to late Tertiary by normal faulting. The 1887 Sonoran earthquake, reported as 7.5 on the Richter scale, influenced about 720,000 square miles, produced a 35-mile scarp with about 14-foot offset, collapsed buildings at the ranch, dried springs and wells, and created new water sources. The Slaughter Ranch house collapsed.
- [[Leslie Canyon]] dissects deformed Paleozoic sediments and Tertiary rhyolite intrusions.

**Vegetation and habitat types**
Eight habitat types were identified by Lanning (1981): Desert Grassland, Chihuahuan Desert Scrub, Riparian Forest/Woodland, Mesquite Bosque, Riparian Scrub, Marshland, Aquatic, and Old field/Disturbed. Approximate extents reported in the analysis:
- Desert scrub: about 39% (900 acres)
- Desert grassland: about 577 acres (25%)
- Mesquite bosque: 508 acres (22%)
- Fallow fields: 11% (254 acres)
- Riparian areas: 2% (55 acres)

**Soils**
- Soil series include Karro Loam, Bonita Clay, Bonita Cobbly Clay, Riggs, Stronghold, Mabray, Lampshire-Ridgelite and Gadwell-Caralampi Complex. Soil suitability ratings for wildlife vegetation are tabulated in the source.

**Hydrology and water rights**
- Water rights are a major management concern. Springs and artesian wells sustain the riparian and cienega habitats.
- Water from artesian wells is reported at 83–90°F.
- A 1983 water quality analysis showed all artesian wells had similar chemistry, possibly indicating a single aquifer.
- USGS photos (1958 and 1979) document downcutting in cienega habitats. Historic artesian pressure lifted water 12 feet above ground; current maximum lift is four feet.
- Leslie Creek appropriative permit: priority date March 20, 1992 (application filed March 1992 with ADWR). USGS gauge No. 09537200 median monthly flows (cfs): January 62, February 54, March 53, April 52, May 45, June 35, July 34, August 59, September 63, October 74, November 81, December 70. Annual total: 411.45 acre-feet.
- San Bernardino holds sixteen active water permits (some with priority dates of January 1, 1882 and January 1, 1884). The Service holds rights to 2,756.51 acre-feet per year.

**Fauna (as recorded in the source)**
- Birds: approximately 250 species; over 90 neotropical species.
- Mammals: 42 species. Reptiles: 27. Fishes: 9. Amphibians: 10.
- Threatened and endangered species listed: Yaqui catfish, Yaqui topminnow, Yaqui chub, beautiful shiner, peregrine falcon, aplomado falcon (possible reintroduction site), lowland and Chiricahua leopard frogs, Mexican garter snake, San Bernardino spring snail, lesser long-nosed bat.

**Non-native species and threats**
- [[Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia), bullfrogs, bullheads, sunfish, [[Largemouth Bass]] and channel catfish. Mosquitofish were found in Black Draw.
- Bullfrogs prey on leopard frogs and garter snakes.
- Historic declines were caused by grazing, farming, groundwater pumping and introduced fishes.

## Key Findings

**Yaqui fish status (survey conclusions)**
- **Yaqui chub**: nine populations; reintroductions by [[W. L. Minckley]] and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] followed a 1969 drought that nearly extirpated the wild population. Can withstand flux but needs minimal velocity.
- **Yaqui topminnow**: 15 populations in ponds, well outflows and shallow waters; threatened by mosquitofish.
- **Yaqui catfish**: extirpated from the United States in the wild; the only population is held at DNFHTC. Hybridization with channel catfish is a documented cause.
- **Beautiful shiner**: three populations in good condition, shown by multiple age classes; stock is raised at DNFHTC.
- **Yaqui sucker, Mexican stoneroller, roundtail chub, longfin dace**: Category 2 species; mostly in Mexico, with longfin dace on the refuge.
- Four of eight native Rio Yaqui species are listed as threatened or endangered.

**Recovery requirements (Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan, draft 1993)**
- Downlisting of Yaqui chub and topminnow requires five years of compliance with: eradication of non-native fishes from critical habitat; securing the San Bernardino aquifer so artesian flows persist year-round; protecting Leslie Creek flows; and protecting critical habitat from mining, non-native introductions and diversion.

**Management strategies**
- Cooperation with Mexico (including SEDESOL) on a binational management plan, especially given NAFTA-related pressures.
- Instream flow rights for Leslie Creek (already acquired) and Black Draw (monitoring wells proposed for five years).
- Acquisition or easements on the Johnson Historical Museum, 99 Bar Ranch and a 1,200-acre Resolution Trust Corporation property.
- Genetic monitoring of captive and wild stock; pedigree charts for each species.
- Removal of woody invaders (mesquite, acacia) to increase infiltration; gabion structures and black willow plantings to halt downcutting.
- Maintenance of a fish barrier at Black Draw and creation of independently drainable ponds.

**Other species recovery**
- Chiricahua leopard frogs: found only at Leslie Creek on the refuge; four impoundments planned for reintroduction with bullfrog exclusion fencing.
- San Bernardino spring snail (Candidate 2): limited by shaded seep habitat.
- Huachuca water umbel (Candidate 2): found in Black Draw, Oasis Pond and Evil Twin pond; plug transplants had limited success.
- Aplomado falcon: historically occupied the valley; preliminary studies ruled out contaminants in prey species.

**Public use and administration**
- Both refuges are closed to the public; fishing is prohibited; hunting limited to cottontail, dove and quail on San Bernardino. Special use permits are issued for birdwatching.
- Visitation estimates: Johnson Museum about 3,000 annual visits, with 50 percent predicted to stop at the refuge; an estimated 90 percent of Guadalupe Canyon visitors may stop at the refuge.
- Staffing: three permanent full-time employees (Refuge Manager GS-11, Office Assistant GS-5, Maintenance Worker WG-8). The source states staffing and budgets are "less than adequate."

**Plan outcomes**
- The planning effort aims for nine outcomes, including compatibility determinations, budget basis, monitoring, public participation and continuity of management.
- The source states that without a comprehensive plan, refuge decisions could affect natural resources on and off refuge lands.
