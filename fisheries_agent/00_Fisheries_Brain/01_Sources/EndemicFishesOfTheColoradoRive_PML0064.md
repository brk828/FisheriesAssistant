# Endemic Fishes of the Colorado River System: A Status Report (1977)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 status report, prepared for the [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]] by its Endemic Species Committees, reviews the status of 34 native fish taxa of the Colorado River system across seven western states. Its stated purpose is educational: to make readers aware that many endemic species have been harmed by dam construction, river channelization and the spread of exotic species. The report lists each species' range and status by state, summarizes state authority and management policy for threatened and nongame species, and identifies ongoing research and recovery efforts. Several species are recorded as extinct or believed extinct in parts of their former range, and many others are restricted, uncommon or protected.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]] was formed as an interstate committee in 1956 and formalized on March 31, 1964. It includes fish and game representatives from seven states: Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming. Nonvoting members may include federal agencies such as the Bureau of Land Management, Forest Service, Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Reclamation, National Park Service, Coast Guard and Army Corps of Engineers.

The Council's goals are to:
- Foster sound management and use of fish and wildlife resources in the drainage.
- Review federal projects affecting the drainage and advise agencies.
- Advise on interstate and intrastate planning and coordination.
- Recommend coordinated laws and regulations.

A professional [[Technical Committee]] provides biological expertise. It is divided into Upper Basin (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming) and Lower Basin (Arizona, California, Nevada) subcommittees. The Prohibited and Protected Fishes, Amphibians and Reptiles Committee oversees the [[Endemic Species Committees]], which consolidate management programs, coordinate study groups and direct recovery teams.

The species list is organized by family. The largest family is Cyprinidae, with 17 taxa, followed by Catostomidae (six), Salmonidae (four), Cyprinodontidae (three), and one each in Cottidae, Elopidae, Mugilidae and Poeciliidae. Notable taxa include:
- [[Humpback Sucker]] (Razorback Sucker), [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]], long-lived large river fishes.
- [[Woundfin]], [[Moapa Dace]] and [[Desert Pupfish]], restricted-range or spring-dwelling fishes.
- [[Gila Trout]], [[Colorado Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Arizona Trout]], native salmonids.

The report notes that endemic fishes are valuable environmental indicators, since a decline in a native population signals a corresponding decline in its environment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report identifies three main pressures on endemic fishes:
- **Dam construction**, which has altered flows and habitat.
- **Channelization** of long stretches of river.
- **Introduction and dominance of exotic species.**

The report links habitat loss directly to status. For example, [[Colorado Squawfish]] is recorded as extinct in Wyoming "because of loss of habitat resulting from construction of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]." Several species are described as vulnerable to "major ecological disturbances," including the Virgin Spinedace and Little Colorado Spinedace. Other species, such as the [[White River Killifish]], survive in isolated springs of the relict White River system.

Ongoing monitoring included a turbidity, temperature and sediment program on the [[Yampa River]] near Maybell and at [[Echo Park]] on the [[Green River]]. The [[Virgin River]] system is being studied for fish distribution, community structure and phenology.

## Key Findings

**Status summary (from the state-by-state tables):**
- **Extinct or believed extinct in parts of range:** Flannelmouth Sucker (California, extinct); Humpback Sucker (Wyoming, extinct); Bonytail (Wyoming, extinct); Colorado Squawfish (Wyoming, extinct; Nevada, believed extinct); Pahranagat Spinedace (Nevada, believed extinct); Desert Pupfish (California, extinct; Arizona, believed extinct, with reintroduction initiated); Monkey Spring Pupfish (Arizona, believed extinct); Roundtail Chub (California, believed extinct).
- **Endangered or listed:** Humpback Chub (Colorado, endangered, fully protected); Colorado Squawfish (California and Colorado, endangered); Woundfin (Nevada, endangered); Moapa Dace (Nevada, endangered); Kendall Warmsprings Dace (Wyoming, restricted range, endangered).
- **Rare or declining:** Humpback Chub is "extremely unusual" in Colorado, with very few records in the past decade. Colorado Squawfish has declined sharply in Colorado over the past decade.
- **Common or abundant:** Desert Sucker, Sonora Sucker, Mottled Sculpin, Speckled Dace and Longnose Dace (Utah, abundant, restricted distribution).

**Recovery and research:**
- The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] is investigating the status and life history of the Humpback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish.
- The [[Woundfin Recovery Team]] is studying Woundfin status and life history.
- An official [[Gila Trout Recovery Team]] has been established.
- Gila Trout are found in seven New Mexico streams closed to fishing.
- Gila Topminnow may be extirpated from the New Mexico Gila drainage and may be reintroduced.
- The Kendall Warmsprings Dace is restricted to a small range; Wyoming has a study under way, and only hook-and-line take is legal.

**State management authority (summary):**
- **Arizona:** Two biologists are assigned to nongame species, with emphasis on federally listed species.
- **California:** Three full-time wildlife biologists and two full-time fishery biologists work on rare, endangered and nongame species, under a cooperative agreement with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].
- **Colorado:** The Division of Wildlife has statutory authority and a Nongame Staff of one supervisor, one secretary and five biologists. Emphasis is on captive breeding, translocation and reintroduction, funded partly through the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]]. A June 3, 1977 law created a state income tax check-off allowing donations of $1, $5 or $10 to the nongame budget.
- **Nevada:** Two nongame biologists; priority is given to birds and mammals.
- **New Mexico:** An Endangered Species Program established in 1974 with four biologists.
- **Utah:** Three nongame biologists, with emphasis on nonaquatic species.
- **Wyoming:** An unofficial list of rare and endangered species; all management units share responsibility for nongame and threatened species.

**Methods and references:** The status information relies on state data and a 1973 [[Utah State University]] study, "Distribution, Abundance and Life History of the Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin." Taxonomy follows the [[American Fisheries Society]] Special Publication No. 6 (1970) list of common and scientific fish names, which the report calls "tentatively" followed for continuity.

**Conclusion:** The report concludes that interstate cooperation has increased state efforts to prevent extinction of endangered species. It presents the status of endemic fishes as a reflection of the health of the Colorado River environment, and it recommends continued study and protection.
