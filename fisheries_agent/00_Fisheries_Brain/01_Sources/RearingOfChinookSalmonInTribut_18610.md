# Rearing of Chinook Salmon in Tributaries of the South Fork Salmon River, Idaho

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service research paper (Research Paper INT-205, May 1978, by [[William S. Platts]] and [[Fred E. Partridge]]) evaluates how important small tributaries are for rearing juvenile [[Summer Chinook Salmon]] in the [[South Fork Salmon River]] (SFSR), Idaho. The adult summer run had declined steadily since 1957 and was approaching "threatened" status, while the juvenile stage had received little study. Twenty-three tributaries (about 80 stream miles) were sampled in 1971, 1972 and 1974. Juvenile chinook were found in 12 of them, and most of the population rears in the first 400 m of stream next to the river. The authors conclude that SFSR tributaries are only marginal rearing habitat.

## Background & Description

The SFSR is a major tributary of the [[Salmon River]] and part of the [[Columbia River Basin]]. Its watershed of 1,270 mi² (3,290 km²) is representative of forested, mountainous central Idaho. The study area covered 397 mi² (1,028 km²) along the upper 52 miles (84 km) of river, with elevations from 9,000 ft (2,740 m) at the headwaters to 3,700 ft (1,130 m) at the confluence with the East Fork-South Fork. Slopes are mostly steeper than 40 percent. Water is low in mineral content (averaging 60 mg/L total dissolved solids) because of granitic bedrock.

The SFSR historically held Idaho's largest salmon run, made up entirely of summer chinook. Returning adults fell from more than 5,000 in the mid-1950s to about 700 in 1977. Most chinook spawn in the river itself, and the authors assumed that juveniles reared almost entirely in the main stem. The paper tests that assumption.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish community.** Rainbow trout dominated the study tributaries, followed by chinook salmon, [[Dolly Varden]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Sculpin]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Dace]] and [[Sucker]]. Chinook were reared with rainbow trout and sculpin throughout most of their tributary range, and occasionally with the other species. Cutthroat trout and chinook were never found together.

**Habitat preferences.** Chinook favoured larger, lower-gradient, grassy-banked streams with deep pools. Most rearing occurred in fluvial and depositional landtypes, mainly in alluvial and alluvial fan areas.

**Anthropogenic pressures.** The authors attribute the main decline to impoundments on the lower Snake and Columbia rivers. In the SFSR, erosion from past logging probably added to the decline by silting and filling streambeds. Two tributaries were blocked by manmade barriers: Tailholt Creek by a dam, and Cabin Creek's culverts, which were thought to be impassable but were used by chinook.

**Sampling approach.** Study plots were chosen randomly from aerial photographs (1:15,000) and sampled from mouth to headwaters until the stream dried. Fish were collected with explosives (prima cord, 4 miles used across 2.75 miles of stream at 291 stations) behind mesh nets, which the authors judged to capture close to 100 percent of fish in each sample area. At Lodgepole and Curtis creeks, a Smith-Root type V electrofisher was used with two-catch population estimates (Seber and Le Cren 1967).

## Key Findings

**Distribution across tributaries**
- Juvenile chinook were found in 69 percent of sampled tributaries (one secondary and 11 primary streams of 23 sampled; 7 secondary and 16 primary were sampled in total).
- Only three streams (Curtis, Buckhorn and Lick creeks) had chinook more than 1 mile (1.6 km) upstream of the mouth. These are major tributaries, averaging over 18 ft (5.5 m) wide and more than 6.5 miles (10.4 km) long.
- Juvenile chinook were not found in the main SFSR above the Vulcan Hot Springs Creek confluence, about 7 miles (11 km) from the headwaters.

**Reach use**
- 58 percent of tributary juvenile chinook reared within the first 440 yd (400 m) of the river, the most important rearing zone.
- In the larger streams (Tyndall, Trail, Dollar, Blackmare and Fitsum creeks), chinook used only the first 440 yd, although stream size would predict further upstream use.
- At Lodgepole Creek, 98 percent of chinook were in the first quarter mile in 1971, and 94 percent in 1974. At Curtis Creek, 6 percent were in the first quarter mile in 1971 by random sampling, versus 44 percent in 1974 by electrofishing; the authors note that the 1971 sampling covered only part of the range.

**Standing crop**
- Standing crops ranged from 0.001 chinook/ft² (0.011/m²) in Dollar Creek to 0.036/ft² (0.383/m²) in Lodgepole Creek.
- Six streams (Tyndall, Lodgepole, Curtis, Cabin, Blackmare and Fitsum) averaged above 0.019/ft² (0.202/m²).
- The overall tributary average was reported as 0.06/m² in the research summary (0.005/ft², 0.055/m², for the remaining streams).
- The full lower reach of Lodgepole Creek, electrofished in 1974, gave an estimate of 0.006/ft² (0.068/m²).
- Comparison values: Big Springs Creek (1975) 0.002/ft² (0.020/m²); Bear Valley Creek (1975, visual) under 0.004/ft² (0.04/m²); Middle Fork tributaries (1972–1973) about 0.034/ft² (0.368/m²).
- The authors conclude that SFSR tributaries are marginal for summer chinook rearing, with lower standing crops than prime rearing areas.

**Community interactions**
- Rainbow trout (possibly largely juvenile [[Steelhead Trout]]) occurred in all areas with chinook. Rainbow made up 44 percent of the tributary population and chinook 32 percent.
- Sculpin were found in all tributaries and in all but one area used by chinook. Brook trout and chinook shared three streams.
- The authors argue that anadromous fish strongly control resident fish densities; without chinook and steelhead, lower tributary reaches would likely be used more by resident trout.

**Habitat associations**
- 55 percent of juvenile chinook occurred in pools rated excellent. Pool condition explained 8 percent of the variation in observed numbers.
- 59 percent were in areas where pools made up less than 20 percent of the channel, because chinook favoured naturally low pool-to-riffle lower reaches.
- 71 percent were in reaches wider than 30 ft (9.1 m).
- Numbers rose as channel gradient increased from 2 to 4 percent, then fell. None were found in channels steeper than 10 percent.
- Chinook reared at elevations of 3,600–5,600 ft (1,100–1,710 m), with 52 percent between 4,800 and 5,200 ft (1,460–1,580 m).
- 61 percent occurred in channels with grassy banks. Streambank stability and substrate composition had no detectable influence on population means.
- Chinook occurred in third, fourth and fifth order streams, with numbers rising with stream order. Fifth-order streams held 76 percent of the fish collected.

**Geomorphic association**
- Chinook occurred in six landtypes within two of the four landtype associations (fluvial and depositional). No chinook were found in the cryic or glaciated associations.
- Chinook dominated populations in the alluvial and alluvial fan landtypes and also occurred in moraine, dissected mountain slope, terrace and valley train landtypes.
- Proximity to the river was the most important variable determining whether chinook occurred.

**Conclusions**
- Juvenile summer chinook use tributaries more than previously assumed, but mainly the lower reaches close to the river.
- The authors recommend that land managers identify streams critical to summer chinook survival, since degraded habitat can only improve if those streams are known.
