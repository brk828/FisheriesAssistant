# Effects of Flow Patterns Below Large Dams on Stream Benthos: A Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #benthos

## Executive Summary

This review by James V. Ward (Colorado State University), published in the Instream Flow Needs Symposium proceedings (1976), synthesizes research on how modified flow patterns below dams affect stream benthic macroinvertebrates and the ecological factors linked to them. The central conclusion is that [[Flow Regime]] is the dominant control on benthos below dams: diversity is generally reduced, while [[Benthic Standing Crop]] may rise or fall depending largely on whether the regime becomes more constant or more fluctuating. Seasonal flow constancy tends to enhance standing crop even with moderate daily fluctuations, whereas severe short-term fluctuation eliminates many taxa. The author argues that flow criteria must be set for each dam individually.

## Background & Description

Stream benthos are emphasized as central to stream function. Benthic macroinvertebrates are a major food source for stream fishes, are useful indicators of past and present ecological conditions, are less mobile than fishes, and are taxonomically more tractable than microorganisms. A diverse benthic community can process large amounts of organic matter. Earlier North American studies treated benthos below dams only in relation to fish; recent work has focused on the benthos itself.

The paper covers flow-related effects only. Thermal effects are treated elsewhere by the author, and organic pollution and reservoir toxicants are excluded. Coverage is restricted mainly to peer-reviewed journal literature, with selected technical reports included. Table 1 summarizes effects of dams on benthic diversity and standing crop at 13 North American locations, drawn from published and selected unpublished studies.

Key concepts discussed include:
- Rheostenic (current-dependent) invertebrates, whose respiration or feeding depends on water movement.
- The [[Boundary Layer]] (a thin "dead water" zone a few mm thick above solid surfaces) and other dead-water refuges.
- The mosaic distribution of benthos, produced by microdistribution of current, substrate, and food.
- The [[Hyporheic Zone]], the subsurface interstitial habitat important for benthos and salmonid eggs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Current and microhabitat.** Many invertebrates depend on current for oxygen uptake or feeding. Blackfly larvae cannot feed below certain current velocities, and net-spinning caddisflies will not build nets below threshold velocities. Morphological adaptations such as flattening and streamlining help invertebrates avoid current. Anything reducing habitat heterogeneity reduces the number of niches and therefore benthos.

**Dam characteristics.** Effects depend on dam type (storage, hydroelectric, etc.), the depth of water release, regional benthic fauna, and reservoir limnology (trophic status, depth, retention time, temperature profile, drawdown). In temperate regions, temperature and flow regimes are often the most important factors.

**Riparian and food inputs.** Seasonal flow constancy stabilizes banks and favors riparian vegetation, which supplies allochthonous litter and terrestrial insects. In one cited Oregon stream, Chapman estimated that 25% of annual net production of coho salmon came from aquatic insects using terrestrial energy inputs, and 33% indirectly from terrestrial plants. Excess litter can create oxygen demand, and riparian encroachment can modify channels.

**Algae and macrophytes.** Increased bed stability and reduced turbidity favor attached algae and macrophytes, which provide food and shelter. Dense *Cladophora* mats create a "false bottom" supporting large chironomid and amphipod populations. Too much epilithic algae can exclude clean-rock specialists and cause nighttime oxygen depletion.

**Siltation and hyporheic habitat.** Constant flow without periodic high discharge can lead to siltation, especially where unregulated tributaries enter below the dam or where diversion reduces flow. Silt-free interstices support the hyporheic zone, which serves as refuge from drought, floods, anchor ice, and high temperature, and as a reserve for repopulating surface communities.

## Key Findings

**Diversity and standing crop**
- Diversity was invariably reduced where indicated.
- Standing crop was enhanced where flow became more seasonally constant, even with daily fluctuations, provided these were not too severe. Hoffman and Kilambi reported enhanced density below Arkansas hydroelectric dams despite daily fluctuations.
- Standing crop was reduced by flow reductions (refs 19, 23) and by increased discharge (ref 20).
- Powell found aquatic insect densities 3.5 times higher and biomass 49 times higher above than below a hydroelectric reservoir.

**Taxonomic responses**
- Dipterans are generally favored. Blackflies and Chironomidae are often enhanced; Blepharoceridae may be reduced or absent.
- Oligochaetes, amphipods, and gastropods are often enhanced.
- Trichoptera show mixed responses, enhanced in some studies and reduced in others.
- Mayflies vary by taxon; a general reduction in mayfly importance is reported.
- Stoneflies (Plecoptera) are the most severely affected and are greatly reduced or absent immediately below reservoirs, though thermal effects can contribute.

**Flow regime categories**
The author considers four categories: seasonal flow constancy, reduced flow, increased flow, and short-term fluctuation. A stream may fall into more than one.

**Short-term fluctuation and stranding**
- Pearson et al. recorded flow falling from 70 to 10 m/sec within an hour below Flaming Gorge Dam, Utah. (Units as given in source.)
- Trotsky and Gregory found 19 aquatic insect genera where bottom current fluctuated 0.5–0.9 m/sec, but only 4 where fluctuation was 0.1–0.5 m/sec.
- Brusven et al. found mayflies especially susceptible to stranding; chironomids showed negligible mortality after 24 hours of exposure during cool spring air temperatures.
- Fisher and LaVoy found great reductions in diversity and standing crop where areas were exposed 70% and 40% of the time, but little difference between always-submerged areas and those exposed 13% of the time. Unionid mussels were especially intolerant of exposure.
- Below a Colorado power dam, discharge fluctuated as much as 52 m/sec in one minute; alternating submergence and exposure reduced 11 insect orders except Plecoptera. Aquatic insects classed as "quality trout food organisms" were over four times more abundant above the reservoir, and trout above the reservoir had nearly 15 times more food in their stomachs.

**Drift and reduced flow**
- Drift correlates with discharge and can increase with both rising and falling flows.
- Reduced flow at Granby Dam, Colorado, showed that the most productive benthic areas were exposed first. Quality habitat for fish and benthos was 59, 43, 31, and 18% of the stream bed at flows of 2.8, 2.1, 1.1, and 0.6 m/sec respectively (ref 23, as given).
- Reduced flow can also increase summer temperatures and form anchor ice in winter, harming benthos.

**Increased flow**
- Below an Alberta hydroelectric dam (Radford and Hartland-Rowe), high flows caused turbidity and scouring. Trichoptera made up less than 1% of benthos versus 15% in an unregulated stream, attributed to disrupted feeding under fluctuating velocities rather than high flow itself.

**Temperature and discharge**
- In a high-runoff year, summer temperatures in the first 12 km below Flaming Gorge Dam nearly eliminated the summer generation of a formerly dominant mayfly (ref 18).

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Flow criteria must be determined for each river, and ideally each section, since geology, channel form, and limnology differ.
- The best minimum flow criterion may be the lowest discharge that maintains a minimum current speed and a specified range of bottom velocities.
- Rates of change in discharge should be gradual to let benthos track receding water and to limit drift losses.
- Heterogeneous substrate with large rocks should be maintained. Channel straightening and rock removal below dams should be discouraged.
- A relatively natural seasonal flow regime, including large releases to flush sediment, should be maintained.
- Further research is needed on sublethal life-cycle effects, biotic interactions, current preferences of important fish food species, and long-term flood effects.
- Specific flow criteria should be set through cooperation among stream ecologists, fisheries biologists, and hydrologists.
