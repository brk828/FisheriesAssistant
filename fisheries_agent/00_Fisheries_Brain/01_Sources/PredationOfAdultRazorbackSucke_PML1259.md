# Predation of Adult Razorback Sucker and Bonytail by Striped Bass in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #moronidae

## Executive Summary

Two records from [[Sport Fishing|sport-fish anglers]] in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada, document endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] in the stomachs of large (>13 kg) nonnative [[Striped Bass]]. The authors suggest that striped bass, present since about 1981, may have contributed to the decline of both native species. They conclude that current [[Repatriation Stocking|repatriation stocking]] faces major obstacles because all life stages of stocked fish, including large adults, are vulnerable to predation.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Lake Mohave is a mainstem reservoir on the [[Lower Colorado River]] and critical habitat for both endangered native species. The reservoir was enclosed in 1951. No natural recruitment of either species has been documented since then, so their persistence depends on stocking.
- **Stocking history:** Early programs released hundreds of thousands of juvenile fish but did not stabilize declines caused by nonnative predators. Stocking strategy has since shifted toward larger [[Size-at-Release]] to reduce predation losses.
- **Striped bass introduction:** Striped bass first appeared in Lake Mohave in 1981. Numbers surged after 1983, when excess water was released from [[Lake Mead]] through the spillway tunnels of [[Hoover Dam]]. Over three decades the reservoir became a trophy fishery producing fish over 25 kg.
- **Telemetry study:** A multiyear [[Acoustic Telemetry]] study began in 2006 to test whether size-at-release affects post-stocking survival. Subadult and adult razorback suckers were implanted with transmitters, released, and tracked by passive and active methods. Immobile transmitters were recovered by scuba and underwater receiver.
- **Data collection:** Anglers were asked to report stomach contents of large striped bass. Outreach signs were posted around the reservoir and on internet fishing forums. The authors also worked with [[Nevada Division of Wildlife]] creel clerks to document predation events.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Striped bass are a large nonnative piscivore. Earlier work has linked them to declines of native fish in Lake Mohave, particularly repatriated razorback suckers.
- Early life stages of native fish are known to be vulnerable to predation, including by [[Catfish Predation|ictalurid catfishes]] (Marsh and Brooks 1989; Marsh and Langhorst 1988, as cited).
- The authors argue that striped bass also prey on the largest repatriates currently stocked, which previously were assumed to be safe from predation because of their size.
- Recovery goals, according to the authors, require creating and managing additional [[Off-Channel Backwater Habitat]] free of piscivorous nonnative fish (Minckley et al. 2003; Mueller 2006).

## Key Findings

1. **Razorback sucker predation:** On 12 November 2008, an angler harvested a 13.6-kg striped bass near Liberty Cove and found an acoustic transmitter in its stomach. The fish was caught about 4 km downriver from Fortune Cove, where a small group of transmitter-tagged subadult (mean total length 38 cm) and adult (mean TL 54 cm) razorback suckers had been released. A second striped bass, caught within 1.6 km of Willow Beach, contained a transmitter-tagged razorback sucker that had been tracked for a 6-day period before contact with the fish.
2. **Bonytail predation:** A striped bass harvested near Willow Beach contained numerous bonytail. A photograph dated 18 December 2007 shows three bonytail removed from the stomach of a fish over 13 kg. The authors consider it highly probable that these bonytail were recently stocked fish.
3. **Stocking size and vulnerability:** The authors note that repatriates of the recommended stocking size (≥50 cm TL) were thought to be safe from predators because of their gape limitation. The predation records show that even the largest razorback suckers currently released are vulnerable.
4. **Bonytail repatriation outcomes:** Since 1995, 23,770 subadult and adult PIT-tagged hatchery bonytail have been repatriated into Lake Mohave. Only 8 have been recaptured, the last in 2005. This low recapture rate is consistent with heavy losses.
5. **Management implications:** The authors conclude that the striped bass fishery "may be hindering native-fish recovery efforts." They recommend substantial investment in off-channel backwater habitats free of nonnative piscivores. They also note that quantifying adult razorback sucker survival will require several years of releases and mark-recapture data.
6. **Limitations:** The findings rest on two angler-reported records and do not provide a quantitative estimate of predation rates. The authors state that adult survival is difficult to quantify without multiyear release and recapture data.
