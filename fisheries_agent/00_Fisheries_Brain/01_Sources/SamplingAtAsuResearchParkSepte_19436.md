## Executive Summary

This report documents fish community monitoring conducted by Arizona State University at the **ASU Research Park (ASURP)** lakes in Tempe, Arizona, on 19–20 September 1989, using overnight trammel netting and shoreline seining. The study is part of an ongoing conservation monitoring effort focused on reintroduced populations of endangered Colorado River native fishes — **[[Bonytail]]**, **[[Razorback Sucker]]**, and **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** — alongside a diverse assemblage of non-native sport and forage fishes (tilapias, sunfishes, shad, and cyprinids). Trammel nets captured 106 individuals across 10 taxa, dominated by **[[Blue Tilapia]]** and **[[Redbelly Tilapia]]**. Seining yielded nearly 2,100 fish across 4 taxa, dominated by **[[Bluegill]]** and Redbelly Tilapia. Larval sampling from February–June 1989 detected abundant **[[Threadfin Shad]]** and cyprinid larvae (later identified as **[[Goldfish]]**, *Carassius auratus*), but no larval catostomids or bonytail-like cyprinid larvae were found. Notably, neither Colorado Squawfish nor Razorback Sucker were captured in this sampling event, despite anecdotal angler reports confirming continued survival of Colorado Squawfish at the Park.

## Background & Description

### Bonytail (*Gila elegans*)
- Sample size: n = 3 (all from Lake B)
- Total length (TL): 29.2, 30.4, 32.1 cm
- Standard length (SL): 22.8, 23.4, 24.4 cm
- Weight: 142, 112, 223 g, respectively
- Sex: largest individual (32.1 cm) was female; remaining two were male
- Coloration: one male exhibited bright orange coloring at paired fin bases with faint orange ventral coloration
- Health: one specimen was found dead in the net; two others released alive without apparent harm
- Parasitism: **[[Lernaea cyprinacea]]** (parasitic copepod) observed on orbits and buccal cavity of one live specimen; oral infestation considered heavy enough to interfere with normal feeding
- Historical size comparison:
  - April 1989 sample: mean 33.2 cm TL (n = 8, range 31.5–34.6 cm)
  - April 1988 sample: mean 26.8 cm TL (n = 11, range 21.1–30.8 cm)
  - Growth trends could not be reliably inferred from small autumn sample size

### Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- Only 8 individuals captured via trammel net since stocking in October 1987 (2 in April 1987, 6 in March 1988)
- None captured in September 1989 sampling
- Anglers reported at least a dozen hook-and-line captures, with estimated sizes up to 30 cm TL, indicating continued survival despite low net-capture rates

### Channel Catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*)
- First captured April 1988 (single specimen), attributed to immigration from upstream canal system
- Recent September 1989 captures (n = 3) presumed derived from an unknown number of fish stocked by Park personnel in spring 1989

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System type**: Artificial recreational/research lentic lakes (Lakes A, B, C) at ASU Research Park, hydrologically connected to the Salt River Project canal system, enabling occasional fish immigration (e.g., Channel Catfish).
- **Community composition** varied markedly among the three lakes:
  - **Lake A**: Dominated by [[Mosquitofish]] (44%) and [[Red Shiner]] (38%) in seine catches; Redbelly Tilapia most abundant in trammel net catch (47%).
  - **Lake B**: Dominated by juvenile [[Bluegill]] (90% of seine catch); Blue Tilapia predominant in trammel net catch (33%); sole site for captured Bonytail.
  - **Lake C**: Highest overall seine productivity (CPE 78.4 fish/haul); Bluegill and Redbelly Tilapia co-dominant (52% and 46%, respectively); Largemouth Bass predominant in trammel net catch (38%).
- **Trophic/competitive dynamics**: Non-native cichlids (tilapias) and centrarchids (bluegill, largemouth bass, green sunfish) numerically dominate both larval and adult fish assemblages, likely competing with or preying upon native cyprinids and catostomids targeted for conservation.
- **Seasonal/temporal variability**: Marked inter-annual and intra-annual shifts in catch composition (e.g., red shiner dominance dropped sharply in Lake A between spring and autumn 1989) attributed to recruitment timing, natural post-spawn mortality, and thermally-driven habitat shifts (fish moving to deeper, cooler water in autumn).
- **Parasite ecology**: Presence of *Lernaea cyprinacea* on Bonytail indicates ectoparasite pressure within the reintroduced population, with potential feeding impairment implications for fish health.
- **Larval fish habitat use**: Nocturnal light-based larval sampling along Lake B shoreline (Feb–Jun 1989) revealed a larval assemblage dominated by Threadfin Shad and cyprinids (subsequently identified as Goldfish), with rare Red Shiner, Bluegill, and Tilapia spp. larvae, and a conspicuous absence of catostomid or bonytail-like larvae — suggesting limited in-Park natural reproduction of targeted native species during this period.

## Key Findings

- **No Razorback Suckers or Colorado Squawfish were captured** during the September 1989 trammel net survey, continuing a documented downward trend in Razorback Sucker catch since April 1987 (30 fish) to April 1989 (9 fish); cause is uncertain (population decline, seasonal capture bias, or other factors).
- **Colorado Squawfish persistence confirmed indirectly** via angler-reported hook-and-line catches (up to ~30 cm TL), despite negligible netting success (only 8 individuals ever captured since 1987 stocking).
- **Bonytail population appears stable but small**, with continued presence of reproductive-age adults (both sexes) and evidence of parasitic copepod infestation that may affect fitness.
- **Non-native tilapias and centrarchids dominate** both trammel net and seine catches, raising ongoing concerns about competitive/predatory pressure on native target species.
- **Goldfish larvae misidentified as "undetermined cyprinidae"** in the field were confirmed via laboratory rearing (12 April sample) — despite adult goldfish being rare in Park catches (only 5 since April 1987), suggesting either a small but reproductively active goldfish population or an unsampled source population.
- **Substantial spatial and temporal heterogeneity** in catch per unit effort (CPE) and species composition among the three lakes suggests lake-specific management/monitoring needs rather than treating the Park as a homogeneous system.
- **Future monitoring planned**: Bi-weekly larval sampling beginning January 1990 specifically targeting young **[[Razorback Sucker]]**, informed by comparative spawning-time data from **[[Lake Mohave]]**, Arizona-Nevada; next full adult sampling scheduled for spring 1990.
