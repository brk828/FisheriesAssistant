# Feasibility Study for an Endangered Fish Hatchery and Recovery Facility on the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This September 1993 Final Report, prepared for the [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]] by URS Consultants, FishPro, Bio/West and Leonard Rice Consulting Water Engineers, assesses the feasibility of a propagation, broodstock/refugia and research facility for four endangered [[Upper Colorado River Basin Fishes]]: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The study covers only biological, technical and engineering design issues; economic, social and philosophical questions were excluded but are flagged for the final siting decision.

The study screened 33 candidate sites, carried 16 forward, scored them against a 100-point matrix, and developed conceptual designs for three water-supply types (warm groundwater, cold groundwater and surface water) at three production scenarios. Capital costs ranged from roughly $16.5 million to $43.7 million depending on scenario and water source.

## Background & Description

- **Authorization:** The 1989 Colorado legislature (Senate Bill 85) directed the [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]] (CWCB) to study endangered fish propagation in Colorado.
- **Species:** The four endangered species are the Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), humpback chub (*Gila cypha*), bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*) and razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*). All are warm-water fishes adapted to the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Study team:** A [[Technical Advisory Committee]] (CWCB, [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]) directed the work; consultants included URS (site evaluation and engineering), FishPro (hatchery design), Bio/West (biology and life history) and Leonard Rice (water supply).
- **Scope:** Six tasks: literature review, biological design and culture techniques, site identification and initial screening, site evaluation and final screening, recovery facility design and costs, and issues and permits.
- **Literature base:** About 100 sources were assembled into an annotated bibliography and a catalogued library. Interviews covered 33 key contacts out of 73 people contacted, and site visits included [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and the [[Logan Experimental Fish Hatchery]].
- **Post-study note:** After the feasibility study, CWCB and CDOW revised the concept toward a native Colorado aquatic species facility covering declining native species as well as the four endangered fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Culture history.** Culture began in 1974 when adult Colorado squawfish were transferred from the Yampa River to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Razorback suckers entered captivity in 1979, humpback chub in 1979–1980, and bonytail chub in 1979–1980 from Lake Mohave. By 1990, 58 adults had moved from Willow Beach to Dexter and 12 more wild squawfish had been added.

**Water and temperature.** All four species are warm-water fishes. Preferred gonadal maturation temperatures are 18–20°C, the highest hatching success occurs at 20–21°C, and preferred growth temperatures are 20–25°C. Groundwater is favored for disease control and stable temperature; surface water offers a more natural genetic match. Mortality has occurred at nitrogen supersaturation above 110%.

**Water quality tolerance.** All four species tolerate high total dissolved solids (avoidance levels for squawfish 4,400 mg/L, humpback chub 5,100 mg/L, bonytail 6,600 mg/L). Squawfish are sensitive to mercury and chlorinated hydrocarbons. Dissolved oxygen of at least 4 mg/L is the design minimum.

**Disease.** Ich, columnaris and the Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) were identified as the most harmful pathogens. Diseases have rarely caused problems in wild populations, but stress and crowding in culture often trigger epizootics.

**Genetic structure.** Up to six genetic stocks may exist for squawfish, six for razorback suckers and six for humpback chub, while bonytail chub has one stock from Lake Mohave, and up to 19 subpopulations are estimated across the four species. Segregation of stocks is therefore a major design constraint.

## Key Findings

**1. Site screening.** Of 33 candidate sites, 16 advanced to final screening after 15 others were dropped (no specific parcel, owner unwilling, or probable fatal flaw in water rights or ownership). Final ranking was based on a 100-point matrix covering water source (25 pts), water quality (20 pts), site physical factors, biological considerations, location and probable cost. Water source was the highest-weighted criterion.

**2. Highest-rated sites.** The three top sites in the first ranking were all in the San Luis Valley (Rio Grande Basin), outside the native habitat of the four fishes. Their advantage came from naturally warm groundwater (about 71–118°F). Because of this concentration, the scope was changed to design for warm groundwater, cold groundwater and surface water supplies.

**3. Production scenarios.** Three production levels were defined: 150,000, 300,000 and 600,000 fish per species per year. Scenario 2 (300,000) was the detailed design basis and scenarios 1 and 3 were scaled from it. Half of production is intensive and half extensive.

**4. Capital costs (core facility, including 25% contingency).**
- Scenario 1 (150,000): warm well $9.95M; cold well $13.75M (approx.); surface $14.39M
- Scenario 2 (300,000): warm well $15.57M; cold well $19.55M; surface $21.74M
- Scenario 3 (600,000): warm well $24.92M; cold well $33.04M; surface $37.95M

Total recovery facility costs (all components) were $16.49M, $18.15M and $19.84M for warm-well scenarios 1–3 respectively, and $21.18M, $25.23M and $27.95M for the 300,000 warm-well, cold-well and surface options; the 600,000 scenario totals were $31.08M (warm), $39.17M (cold) and $43.69M (surface).

**5. Facility components.** The design includes a hatchery building (incubation to first feeding), 47 rearing ponds plus augmentation ponds, broodstock and refugia ponds holding about 5,100 adults (with about 56% at state and federal facilities), a research building with genetics and pathology laboratories, a visitor center, quarantine facilities, and three residences.

**6. Biological priorities.** The TAC recommended that only half of broodstock and refugia stocks be held at this facility, with the rest held at other federal facilities. Intensive and extensive culture were balanced equally. Quarantine, disinfection stations, escapement screening and bird predation netting are required.

**7. Broodstock timeline.** Female Colorado squawfish mature at 6–7 years, males at 4–5. Razorback suckers mature in 2–3 years and bonytail and humpback chub at about 3 years.

**8. Open issues.** The study lists the following unresolved questions: the need for the facility, production levels, the role of refugia, subpopulation numbers, the intensive/extensive mix, water-source effects, location relative to native habitat, and responsibility for construction and operation.

**9. Permits.** Permits that may be required include NPDES and wastewater discharge permits, construction dewatering, a Corps of Engineers 404 permit, zoning and building permits, water rights, an Endangered Fish Permit, and an Environmental Assessment or Environmental Impact Statement. Interstate fish-transfer rules differ by state.
