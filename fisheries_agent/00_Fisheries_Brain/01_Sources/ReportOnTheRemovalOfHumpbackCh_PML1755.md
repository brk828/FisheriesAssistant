# Humpback Chub Removal and Population Studies, Little Colorado River (1979)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This pair of reports by C. O. Minckley of the [[Museum of Northern Arizona]] (December 1979) documents fish surveys of the [[Little Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. The first survey, in October 1979, collected [[Humpback Chub]] broodstock for propagation at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. The second, in July 1979, examined population structure, reproduction, relative abundance, tagging, and ectoparasites. Very few adult humpback chubs were found in October, which was attributed to low mainstream [[Colorado River]] releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. July data confirmed successful reproduction and showed a population with many age classes.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub (*Gila cypha*) is a native cyprinid of the Colorado River system. The Little Colorado River population was the focus of the studies. The reports state that information on the species had become increasingly available in recent years, but many life-history parameters were still poorly known.

The October survey aimed to remove broodstock from the Little Colorado River and transport them to Willow Beach for a rearing program. The July study was a broader population assessment. Its methods included:

- Trammel and gill nets, with [[Trammel Net]] sets proving unmanageable when drifted.
- [[Seining]] in upstream and downstream sections of the Little Colorado River.
- Individual tagging of humpback chubs over 15 cm total length using numbered fingerling tags.
- Recording of total length, sex, and weight, with relative density estimated from seined area.
- Examination of all fish for ectoparasites.

Fish were transported in styrofoam coolers with bags of water, pure oxygen, and supplemental oxygen as needed. They reached the hatchery raceway about five hours after leaving the river.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and water conditions.** In October 1979, minimal releases from Glen Canyon Dam (reported as 1026 cfs in the scan) left normally fished areas dry. The downstream seining area was either dry or held colder mainstream Colorado River water. Water temperature in the Little Colorado River was 21 °C. The author did not consider temperature to explain the scarcity of chubs. In April 1979, high flows (above the threshold garbled in the scan) were apparent, and the author suggested that post-flood waters enabled spawning in the confluence area.

**Community.** Few fish of any species were using the system in October. The [[Common Carp]] was described as ubiquitous but was present in low numbers. Upstream, a small backwater held humpback chubs, [[Fathead Minnow]]s, [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]s, [[Bluehead Mountain-Sucker]]s, [[Speckled Dace]], and carp. It was at 21 °C and heavily used by young-of-the-year and yearling fish.

**Parasites.** Anchorworm ([[Anchorworm]], *Lernaea*) ectoparasites were first noticed in October 1978 and were still present in July 1979 but in much lower numbers. The author noted that this exotic parasite had been shown to be harmful to other native fishes.

## Key Findings

**October 1979 removal survey**
- Gill nets (Swedish): 6 humpback chubs, 13 flannelmouth suckers, and 1 cutthroat trout.
- Trammel nets: flannelmouth suckers (16 in the scan), channel catfish, 8 bluehead mountain-suckers, and 2 carp.
- Seining: humpback chubs (count partly illegible in the scan), flannelmouth suckers, 5 channel catfish, 1 speckled dace, 1 bluehead mountain-sucker, and 40 fathead minnows.
- Six adult humpback chubs were captured. Two escaped and one died, so three adults were transported to Willow Beach.
- Three additional fish were placed at Willow Beach after the October collection at the confluence.
- Smaller humpback chubs were found upstream, mostly in a backwater, and were associated with several other species. The author suggested that low flows affected adult fish but not small ones.

**July 1979 population study (13–15 July)**
- **Reproduction:** Successful reproduction was confirmed by 12 young fish, mostly taken upstream, ranging from 25 mm total length (upper value garbled). The author noted that the Little Colorado River is an important spawning and nursery area for this species.
- **Relative abundance** (total of 184 fish):

| Rank | Species | Number | Percent |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Humpback Chub | 87 | 47.4 |
| 2 | Flannelmouth Sucker | 56 | 30.4 |
| 3 | Speckled Dace | 22 | 11.9 |
| 4 | Carp | 11 | 5.9 |
| 5 | Channel Catfish | 8 | 4.4 |

- **Age structure:** More than ten age groups were represented. Growth was approximately 60–92 mm in the first year, reaching about 350 mm by the mature stage. Older fish dominated, with a nine-year-old class most common. Four-year-old fish were second most common, followed by young-of-the-year.
- **Relative density:** Comparable to previous data but lower than in 1978 in upstream areas. Relative densities of larger fish could not be estimated readily.
- **Tagging:** 67 humpback chubs were tagged and released, bringing the total marked in the Grand Canyon region to 216. No recaptures had been recorded, so movement of tagged fish remained unknown. Downstream movement into the mainstream Colorado River was considered the strongest possibility.
- **Behavior:** Adults were active along the substrate, at edges, and in midwater, and were observed in schools of 15–20 individuals. Size groups were distinct (under 100 mm, 100–200 mm, 200–300 mm, and over 300 mm). This pattern may indicate size segregation during schooling, but it was considered preliminary.
- **Ectoparasites:** Anchorworm infestation was much lower than in October 1978, when the incidence was reported as 52%. In July 1979, 2.3% of humpback chubs were infested.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Low adult numbers in October were linked to low mainstream releases and dry habitat. The author stated that the causes of the population's habitat use still needed research.
- Small-fish and young-fish abundance suggested continued reproduction and recruitment in the Little Colorado River.
- Further study was recommended on habitat use, movement, and the factors controlling distribution in the Little Colorado River system.
