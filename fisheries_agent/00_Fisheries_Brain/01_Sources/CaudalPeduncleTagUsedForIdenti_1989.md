# Caudal Peduncle Tag Used for Identification of Individual Small Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cichlidae #fish-tagging-methodology

## Executive Summary
This technical note (Langley & Driscoll, 1989, *The Progressive Fish-Culturist*) compares two anatomical placements for individual identification tags on small fishes: the traditional **head/dorsal musculature tag** versus a novel **caudal peduncle tag**. Using [[Rainbow Cichlid]] (*Herotilapia multispinosa*) as the primary study species, the authors demonstrate that placing coded bead tags through the caudal peduncle—rather than through the head musculature—significantly reduces physical damage and improves tag retention over an 8-month study period. The method was also validated on [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), other *Lepomis* spp., and the marine damselfish [[Dascyllus albisella]], suggesting broad applicability across taxa for small-fish individual identification in laboratory settings.

## Background & Description
- **Tagging apparatus**: Hypodermic needle affixed to a syringe (plunger removed), monofilament line (2–4 kg breaking strength), and plastic colored beads.
- **Caudal peduncle tag placement**: Needle inserted through the caudal peduncle approximately one-third of the distance from the dorsal surface, avoiding proximity to the spinal column. Monofilament threaded through, beads attached, line tied and sealed with cyanoacrylate glue.
- **Head tag placement (comparison method)**: Based on [[Fryer And Iles Head Tag Method]] (1972), placed through head/dorsal musculature; required a larger loop to accommodate full extension of the dorsal fin.
- **Bead coding**: Combinations of 4–5 beads recommended (per [[Helfman 1983]]) for reliable visual distinction; green and blue beads are difficult to visually differentiate and should not be combined. Two-bead combinations were used on one side of the fish in this study (both sides recommended for natural/field populations).
- **Minimum fish size tagged**: As small as 40 mm standard length, without growth-related complications — a key morphological advantage since the caudal peduncle grows proportionately less than other body regions as the fish matures.
- **Anesthesia**: MS-222 (tricaine) was tested but judged more distressing to rainbow cichlids than tagging without anesthesia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- All fish were maintained in **laboratory aquaria**, never exposed to natural/field conditions, limiting ecological inference but controlling confounding variables (e.g., predation, environmental stress).
- **Social/behavioral interactions** proved central to tag performance: conspecifics were observed approaching and tugging on head-tagged individuals (possibly attracted to colored beads, per [[Helfman 1983]]), leading to tag loss. Fish approaching caudal peduncle-tagged individuals typically approached from behind, prompting the tagged fish to swim away — reducing tugging and improving retention.
- The method's cross-species applicability (*Herotilapia multispinosa*, *Lepomis* spp., *Dascyllus albisella*) spans freshwater cichlids, freshwater centrarchids, and reef-associated marine damselfish, indicating relevance to both [[Freshwater Fish Ecology]] and [[Coral Reef Fish Ecology]] research contexts requiring individual identification (e.g., behavioral, mark-recapture studies).

## Key Findings
- **Head tag trial** (n=30): 3 died (unrelated causes, tag intact); 8 lost tags during the 8-month study; 19 retained tags but all showed damage to anterior dorsal spines, ranging from monofilament cutting into tissue to complete surface removal of up to five spines. One fish developed infection from monofilament overgrowth cutting into head musculature.
- **Caudal peduncle tag trial** (n=27, including 19 retagged survivors + 8 other survivors): 3 died (unrelated causes, tag intact); **all remaining 24 fish (100%) retained the tag**. No damage to the caudal fin observed at 6-month check; minor scar tissue formed around the monofilament, with slight redness (but no infection) in 8 fish.
- Scar tissue was slightly *more* pronounced with the caudal peduncle tag than the head tag, attributed to greater monofilament movement from peduncle activity — however, scarring fully receded within 3 weeks of tag removal with no permanent damage.
- One fish exhibited transient swimming impairment immediately post-tagging (resolved within 10 minutes), attributed to possible researcher inexperience/needle placement too close to the spinal column — recommendation: hold tagged fish briefly to confirm normal swimming before release.
- Cross-species validation: All fish tagged with the caudal peduncle method retained tags for the study duration **except one bluegill** (*Lepomis macrochirus*).
- **Primary advantages of the caudal peduncle tag**: (1) reduced physical damage (no dorsal spine/fin damage), (2) substantially longer/more reliable tag retention, (3) suitability for smaller individuals (down to 40 mm SL) due to slower relative growth of the caudal peduncle region.
- Authors caution that retention times observed in controlled lab conditions may not generalize to field studies with uncontrolled confounding variables (e.g., predation, environmental damage).
- This work builds on and improves upon earlier tagging/marking reviews by [[Wydoski And Emery 1983]] and [[Stott 1968]], and directly modifies the head-tag technique of [[Fryer And Iles 1972]].
