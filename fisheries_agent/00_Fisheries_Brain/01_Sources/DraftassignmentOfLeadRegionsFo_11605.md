# Lead Region Assignment for Endangered and Threatened Species (1988 Draft)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 draft policy from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) sets rules for assigning a **lead Region** to manage endangered and threatened species whose range or management scope crosses regional boundaries. The lead Region coordinates candidate assessment, listing, recovery planning, consultation, and permit issuance. The draft also includes a 1988 attachment, the [[Lead Region Database]] format, which lists about 110 species shared among Regions. Those species include a set of listed fish, which are the focus of this note. The document is a management and administrative text, not a research study, and it contains no ecological measurements.

## Background & Description

The FWS was reorganized to improve field-level management of fish and wildlife. Regional Directors received added budgets, responsibilities, and authority. The draft identifies one remaining gap: which Region leads on species whose range is not confined to one Region. The [[Washington Office]] maintains the database of lead Regions by species, covering candidate assessment, listing, recovery, and permit issuance.

The attachment is a table titled "Endangered and threatened species for which Regions share responsibilities" (dated 12/19/88). It records each species' scientific or common name, status, distribution (states and countries), the Regions involved (by number), and the lead responsibilities (L = listing, R = recovery, P = permit issuance). Extirpated ranges are shown in parentheses. OCR errors in the table make some entries incomplete. The approximate breakdown is:

- **Mammals:** bats, bears, cougar, jaguarundi, ocelot, manatee, wolves, and marine mammals (monk seal, fur seal, sea otter, dugong).
- **Birds:** whooping crane, bald eagle, peregrine falcons, plovers, terns, vireos, woodpeckers, and others.
- **Reptiles and amphibians:** sea turtles (with [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS) involvement), desert tortoise, alligator, boa, rattlesnake, and Puerto Rican toad.
- **Freshwater fish:** about 16 species, summarized in the Key Findings.
- **Freshwater mussels:** roughly 20 pearlymussel and pigtoe species.
- **Plants:** cacti, milkvetch, globe-berry, and others, plus several forest and wetland plants such as [[Pondberry]] and [[Swamp Pink]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document contains no population sizes, habitat measurements, or water chemistry data. Its ecological relevance comes from the range information in the attachment. Several species are noted as wide-ranging, including the [[Bald Eagle]] and [[Desert Tortoise]], which the draft cites as examples where dividing lead responsibility may be needed. The draft also cites the [[Whooping Crane]] as an example of a Region-led recovery effort. Many fish entries are restricted to southwestern or river-basin Regions, which are part of the reason they are shared among Regions.

## Key Findings

**Policy rules**

1. **Single lead by default.** In most cases one Region leads on all activities for a species. Divided leads are allowed in exceptional cases, for example one Region for listing and another for recovery and permits. Each division must be recorded in the database.
2. **Multi-Region ranges.** Lead responsibility for candidate assessment, petition findings, listing, and recovery plans is set by agreement among the involved Regions. Dividing a species into geographic segments or functional roles is expected to be rare because it raises the cost of keeping treatment consistent across the range.
3. **Concurrence and communication.** A lead Region must circulate draft documents and obtain written concurrence from other involved Regions before significant actions (candidate assignment, listing, reclassification, recovery plan approval). Unresolved disagreements go to the Director.
4. **Funding and research coordination.** The recovery-lead Region coordinates funding, research, and management, even when another Region initiates the work. The draft's example is [[Whooping Crane]] recovery, in which Region 2 leads and other Regions may receive funds for a non-migratory flock.
5. **Permit issuance.** The recovery-lead Region normally issues permits. It may delegate this with agreement, and applicants do not need to know which office holds that authority.
6. **Consultation.** The lead for consultation is set separately from recovery leadership, usually the Region with the largest impact or the most affected species. The Regional Director who signs a [[Biological Opinion]] is fully responsible for its content, including any jeopardy finding. Agreements with agencies running broad programs may set a framework for assigning consultation leads.

**Listed fish named in the attachment (about 16 species, from partly OCR-damaged text)**

- **Chubs (6):** [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Chihuahua Chub]], [[Slender Chub]], [[Sonora Chub]], [[Spotfin Chub]], and [[Yaqui Chub]].
- **Other minnows, shiners, and daces:** [[Loach Minnow]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], and [[Spikedace]].
- **Darters and pupfish:** [[Leopard Darter]] and [[Desert Pupfish]].
- **Large river fish:** [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (listed as "Squawfish, Colorado River") and [[Shortnose Sturgeon]].
- **Catfish and cavefish:** [[Yaqui Catfish]] and [[Ozark Cavefish]].

Most fish entries list Regions 2 and 3 (southwestern and river-basin areas). Shortnose sturgeon and Colorado pikeminnow are listed with Regions in the Atlantic and Mountain-Prairie areas. Exact Region numbers should be checked against the original scan, because OCR corrupted several rows.
