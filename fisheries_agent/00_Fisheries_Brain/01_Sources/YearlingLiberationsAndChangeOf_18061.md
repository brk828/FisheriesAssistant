# Yearling Liberations and Change of Food as Affecting Rainbow Trout Yield in Paul Lake, British Columbia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by [[E. J. Crossman]] and [[P. A. Larkin]] examines how the introduction of the [[Redside Shiner]] into [[Paul Lake]], British Columbia, altered the diet, growth and yield of [[Rainbow Trout]] from 1952 to 1957. Trout diets shifted from invertebrates and [[Plankton]] toward shiners, with shiners making up over 90 percent of the summer diet of trout over 35 centimetres by 1956. Since 1953, stocking of [[Hatchery Yearling Trout]] has supported the fishery, and the authors conclude that trout populations recovered from the initial harmful effects of the shiner introduction through increased predation on shiners.

## Background & Description

Paul Lake was barren until stocked with 5000 rainbow trout fry in 1909. By 1920 it held a large unexploited population, but competition reduced trout size by 1925 and population depletion was evident by 1931, when [[Mottley]] (1932) attributed it partly to insufficient spawning-stream flow for fry production. The lake was then stocked annually with 200,000 fry, and by 1937 the catch had stabilized at 9,000 to 10,000 fish per year, averaging one pound, or about 10 pounds per acre. Catch was made up mainly of 1- to 3-year-old fish.

Redside shiners entered the lake between 1945 and 1950 from an upstream drainage where they had been introduced. Between 1948 and 1954 the annual catch declined from about 10,000 trout to fewer than 2,400. Since 1954 the lake has been stocked each spring with marked (fin-clipped) yearlings, about 40 to the pound. A single planting of 10,000 unmarked advanced fry (about 600 to the pound) was made in autumn 1953.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Competition and predation.** The authors describe three interactions between shiners and trout: competition for food, predation of trout on shiners, and predation of large shiners on trout fry. Competition reduced growth of small trout, but predation on shiners by larger trout largely offset these harmful effects across the population.

**Diet change.** Before shiners, trout over 20 centimetres fed on amphipods (Gammarus and Hyalella), bottom organisms and plankton, with no fish found in stomachs in 1946 to 1949. By 1952 amphipods were negligible and fish were the main food of trout over 30 centimetres from July to September. In 1955 and 1956, plankton declined almost proportionately as shiner consumption rose, and bottom fauna (mainly dragonfly nymphs, snails and chironomid pupae) dominated the middle size range. Trout under 15 centimetres did not eat small shiners or shiner fry as late as 1955 to 1956.

**Winter diet.** In winter 1955 to 1956, from 76 trout taken by gillnets under the ice, bottom fauna dominated all size groups (94 percent in the smallest, 93 percent in the middle group, 83 percent in the largest). Shiners were nearly absent.

**Size selection.** Large trout preyed mainly on large shiners. Of shiners eaten by trout over 45 centimetres, 79 percent exceeded 7.5 centimetres. Small shiners of 1 to 2.5 centimetres were largely unused.

**Shiner parasitism.** Shiners in Paul Lake were heavily parasitized by the cestode [[Ligula Intestinalis]], which did not infect the trout and allowed identification of shiner remains in stomachs.

## Key Findings

- **Diet shift:** By 1956, shiners made up over 90 percent of the summer diet of trout over 35 centimetres. Plankton in the diet decreased nearly proportionately as shiner consumption increased from 1946 to 1956.
- **Average stomach volume:** Trout over 35 centimetres averaged about 1.5 cc in 1946, 6.5 cc in 1952 and 7.9 cc in 1956.
- **Hatchery yearlings:** Liberations since 1953 formed up to 8 percent of the anglers' catch in the autumn of liberation and as high as 50 percent the following spring. Marked fish made up about 50 percent of catch early in the 1955 and 1956 seasons, falling to about 20 percent in midsummer.
- **Growth:** Hatchery fish of a given age were less variable in size and grew faster than wild fish. Wild fish beginning the year above 17 centimetres in 1955 and 1956 grew at rates equal to pre-shiner fish. Calculated Age I lengths were 11.0 cm for wild fish and 8.2 cm for hatchery fish.
- **Bait comparison:** Trout caught on shiner bait averaged 46.3 cm, versus 42.7 cm on artificial bait. Catch per man-hour in July and August was 0.9 fish with shiners versus 0.18 with artificial bait (a significant difference in shiner volume, p < .01).
- **Large fish:** Fish over 45 centimetres made up 4 percent of the catch in 1955, 9 percent in 1956 and 13 percent in 1957. Angler catches included fish up to 61 centimetres.
- **Recovery:** Angling catch in 1957 was roughly equal to the pre-shiner period, and average weight had gradually increased. The authors conclude that liberations allowed the natural population to recover as trout learned to exploit shiners.
- **Management options:** Eradicating shiners with fish toxicants could be considered. The authors frame the choice as the present catch of about 10,000 fish averaging over 1.25 pounds (some up to 6 pounds), versus a post-poisoning catch above 15,000 fish averaging about 0.75 pound (occasionally up to 4 pounds).
