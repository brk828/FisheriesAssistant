# Fish Radiotelemetry: Theory and Application for High Conductivity Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (FWS/OBS-82/38, 1982) by [[Harold M. Tyus]] evaluates [[Radiotelemetry]] as a method for tracking fish in turbulent, turbid rivers with high conductivity (over 600 µmho). It has two parts: a simplified theoretical section comparing radiotelemetry with [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]], and a field study of endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]] in the [[Green River]], Utah, conducted in 1980. The authors conclude that radiotelemetry is the more suitable method for rapidly flowing rivers, that it is marginal at high conductivity, and that field workers must weigh equipment limits against water physicochemistry before starting studies.

## Background & Description

The report was prepared for the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] Western Energy and Land Use Team. Its purpose was to support recovery planning and habitat-preference data for [[Colorado Squawfish]], an endangered species of the upper Colorado River system. The Service was assessing four endemic fishes:

- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), endangered under the Endangered Species Act of 1973
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), endangered
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), endangered
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), proposed as threatened

Because remote whitewater sections made direct study difficult, the Service tested radiotelemetry on the Colorado squawfish, chosen for its large size and availability in flat-water sections of the Green River. Earlier work included a 1978 spawning-requirements study (Holden and Shelby 1978) and a feasibility study by Sinning (1979), which concluded that radiotelemetry results would be marginal in the Green River.

The theoretical section covers:
- **Ultrasonics:** typically 30 to 200 kHz; hydrophones must be immersed, which precludes aerial tracking and limits use under ice; signals are strongly affected by temperature, turbulence and sediment.
- **Radio propagation:** frequencies of 10 kHz to 50 MHz have been tested in water, with 40 to 160 MHz usually used. Attenuation rises with frequency and falls with conductivity. Radio transmission in salt water is virtually impossible.
- **Antennas:** transmitting whip antennas are typically about 0.25 wavelength; in water, a 0.25-wavelength antenna at 15 to 50 MHz is 0.06 to 0.17 m. Receiving antennas include omnidirectional whip, [[Yagi Antenna]] and [[Loop Antenna]] designs.
- **Batteries:** mercury and lithium cells are tabulated (Tables 1 and 2) with rated capacity and theoretical life at 0.07, 0.16 and 0.28 mA drains. Battery size and weight scale with range and transmission life.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water conductivity was the controlling physicochemical variable. Conductivity in the Green River ranged from about 200 to 1600 µmho in 1980, and the study area was largely shallow, with most reaches under 2 m deep and some reaches to 3 m. The river has long whitewater sections in Desolation and Gray Canyons and many underwater obstructions. Monthly mean conductivity (corrected to 25 °C) in 1980 rose from 443 µmho in April to 895 µmho in August, then 809 µmho in September (Table 4).

Table 3 summarizes earlier radiotelemetry studies in waters of 600 µmho or less. The highest reported conductivity was 600 µmho, with a usable signal depth of about 2 m; no signal was detected at 6 m. Reported ranges varied widely with equipment, frequency and depth, and no predictive model for practical use had been developed.

## Key Findings

- **Method choice:** Radiotelemetry is more suitable than ultrasonics for turbulent, turbid rivers; ultrasonics remains better in quiet water and deep lakes.
- **Conductivity effect:** Radio signals are attenuated more rapidly as water conductivity increases, and transmission in salt water is virtually impossible.
- **Air–water interface:** Energy meeting the surface is reflected unless the incidence angle is below 6°; only a small fraction of radiated power reaches the receiver, but long-range reception in air remains possible.
- **Efficiency:** Transmitter efficiency is typically 65 to 70%; antenna efficiency ranges from under 1% to 25%, giving roughly under 1% to 18% of battery power overall.
- **Antenna length:** Water's dielectric constant (about 80) shortens the required antenna; the radio wave velocity in water is about 0.11 that of air.
- **Receiver choice:** Field studies should use both search and pinpointing receivers, since a search model can prevent loss of a fish whose transmitter frequency drifts.
- **Prior Green River attempt:** Holden and Shelby (1978) implanted radio transmitters in seven Colorado squawfish and located only one, which was lost after 1.5 months. Sinning (1979) attributed failure largely to antennaless, long-life (low-power) transmitters, estimating output at no more than 10% of commercial units.
- **1980 field study:** Begun in March 1980 with [[AVM Fish Modules]] (SM-1 type, 40.660 to 40.700 MHz, Hg-630 battery, theoretical life 215 days, about 11 g). Six Colorado squawfish (TL 508 to 707 mm) and one razorback sucker (TL 510 mm) were implanted in April and May and tracked until September.
- **Range results:** At about 500 µmho (March), a whip antenna with [[Rf-40 Receiver]] detected signals up to 200 m and a loop antenna about 50 m. At 812 µmho, loop range fell to about 30 m. A fish at 800 to 1000 µmho was detectable only to a depth of about 3 m.
- **Aircraft:** A Piper Supercub flying about 100 km/h at 105 m gave the best results; a faster aircraft at about 150 km/h performed poorly.
- **Depth and range:** Range decreased as depth and conductivity increased. A regression on Larimore et al. (1975) data was significant (r = 0.998, n = 3), and Figure 7 showed the whip with ground reference performing best at 2 m depth.
- **Fish behavior:** Released fish mostly stayed near release or capture sites, and tended to occupy shallow areas beside dropoffs, which aided relocation.
- **Equipment cautions:** Water, dirt or corrosion on coaxial connections reduces signal, and signal boosters can raise noise enough to impair reception.
- **Conclusion:** Radiotelemetry can follow fish movements even at relatively high conductivity; field sampling was more successful than expected because of shallow water and fish behavior. Investigators should carefully weigh equipment limits and water chemistry before starting studies.
