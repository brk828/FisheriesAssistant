# Status of the Sonoran Topminnow and Desert Pupfish in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department special report assesses the status of the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) using field surveys from June 1989 to April 1990 and a review of historic records. Both species were federally listed as endangered (topminnow 1967, pupfish 1986). Declines are attributed mainly to habitat loss and predation or competition from non-native fishes, especially [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). The authors conclude that downlisting the [[Gila Topminnow]] is premature, because current recovery criteria do not address long-term persistence or genetic diversity, and that protecting natural populations should be the main recovery priority.

## Background & Description

- **Species and subspecies:** Both species are represented in Arizona by two subspecies each. The topminnow subspecies are the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*P. o. occidentalis*) and the [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*P. o. sonoriensis*). The pupfish subspecies are the Lower Colorado River desert pupfish (*C. m. macularius*) and the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*C. m. eremus*).
- **Historic distribution:** Both fishes once shared a widespread distribution across southern Arizona and were among the most abundant fishes there. The Quitobaquito pupfish has only one historic locality in Arizona, Quitobaquito Springs.
- **Scope:** Only Arizona populations are considered. Populations in Mexico, New Mexico and California are excluded.
- **Recovery framework:** A 1981 Memorandum of Understanding between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[U.S. Forest Service]] and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] set guidelines for introductions. A 1983 [[Gila and Yaqui Topminnow Recovery Plan]] followed. The desert pupfish draft recovery plan was still under review.
- **Key definitions:**
  - A "captive" population is one at a site with a mailing address. All others are "in the wild."
  - An "introduced" population results from transplanting fish to a site with no record of natural populations.
  - A "natural" population is one that existed before human transplants and has not been artificially mixed with other populations.
  - Site quality uses a four-category system (see Ecological Context).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats**
- Predation and competition from mosquitofish, which were the most serious threat to native topminnows.
- Desiccation, the main cause of loss at introduced sites. Surveys were timed for June–September 1989 to capture this threat.
- Flooding, which has eliminated populations and removed fish barriers.
- Habitat degradation from cattle use, dense [[Typha]] (cattail) growth, and silting.
- Hybridization and unauthorized stocking, which threaten the genetic integrity of pupfish.

**Site categories (Simons 1987)**
- **Category 1:** moderate to high numbers in habitats with natural surface water expected to persist indefinitely.
- **Category 2:** moderate to high numbers in habitats that persist only with human support.
- **Category 3:** populations too small or habitat too limited to persist for many years.
- **Category 4:** populations coexisting with mosquitofish, regardless of size or habitat quality.

**Watershed groupings of natural Gila topminnow populations**
- Bylas Spring group: Bylas Spring, Middle Spring, North Fork of Ash Creek.
- San Rafael group: Sheehy Spring, Sharp Spring, Santa Cruz River.
- Sonoita Creek group: Cottonwood Spring, Monkey Spring, Sonoita Creek, Redrock Canyon.
- Cienega Creek group: Cienega Creek.

Genetic and fitness differences among drainages support managing these groups separately.

## Key Findings

**Introductions and population status**
- Gila topminnows have been introduced into 192 wild habitats in Arizona. Of these, 32 populations were successful as of September 1989, a success rate of about 17 percent. Introductions into captive sites number more than 102, with more than 340 total introductions.
- Fifteen introduced wild populations failed between 1987 and 1989. Most losses were due to desiccation, and the 1989 drought was identified as the main factor behind at least seven introduced and one natural failure.
- Of the 32 successful introduced populations, 7 are Category 1, 10 are Category 2, 9 are Category 3, and 6 are Category 4.
- Yaqui topminnows: 8 wild populations were successful in 1989 (5 Category 2, 2 Category 3, 1 Category 4). The Tule Pond natural population remains.
- Desert pupfish: 4 wild populations were successful in 1989 and 4 failed. Two successful populations (Bog Hole and Finley Tank) contain probable hybrids. These will be renovated, leaving only Howard Well (introduced) and Quitobaquito Springs (natural) as wild pupfish populations in Arizona.

**Natural populations**
- Of 11 natural Gila topminnow populations successful in 1987, 8 persisted in 1989. Three failed: Sheehy Spring, Middle Spring and North Fork of Ash Creek.
- Cienega Creek is the largest natural Gila topminnow population in Arizona, with no mosquitofish ever collected. Its acquisition by the [[Bureau of Land Management]] in 1989 is described as the most important conservation action since recovery began.
- Sonoita Creek below Patagonia Lake and Santa Cruz River have topminnows coexisting with mosquitofish.
- Bylas Spring was renovated several times and remains threatened by mosquitofish. In 1989 only one topminnow was collected. In 1990, about 60 fish were moved to Arizona State University as a refugium.
- The Quitobaquito pupfish population at Quitobaquito Springs (Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument) is the only natural pupfish population in Arizona and is secure in 1989.

**Yaqui topminnow**
- Three introduced populations were eliminated in 1989 by mosquitofish. The San Bernardino Ranch acquisition (USFWS National Wildlife Refuge) met one of three delisting criteria. The other two, mosquitofish removal and long-term persistence, have not been met.

**Authors' recommendations**
1. Secure natural populations through mosquitofish removal, fish barriers and habitat management. Site-specific recommendations are in Appendix C.
2. Conduct genetic studies of all natural populations to guide management.
3. Introduce fish only into high-quality habitats with long-term persistence potential, and renovate non-native-occupied sites where needed. Potential sites are listed in Appendix D.

**Argument against premature downlisting**
- Current criteria are inadequate because they require only three years of survival and ignore natural populations after 1987. Eleven introduced populations that had survived at least four years before 1987 were gone by 1989.

**Core conclusion**
- The authors argue that the recovery criteria have been met but the species are still declining, and that agency performance on these fishes will indicate their willingness to recover other endangered species.
