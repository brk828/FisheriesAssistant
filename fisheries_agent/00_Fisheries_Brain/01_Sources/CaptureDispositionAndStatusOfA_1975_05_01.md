# Capture, Disposition and Status of Adult Razorback Suckers from Lake Mohave, Arizona

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Catostomidae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This 1975 field report from the Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory (Arizona State University) documents an intensive March–April recovery effort targeting spawning aggregations of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona. The goal was to establish a captive brood stock at the Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery and to document population status, behavior, and health of the lake's razorback population. Over four weeks, 101 adult *Xyrauchen* were netted using trammel nets at two sites: a hot-spring-lined lotic shoreline near Hoover Dam, and a cove in Cottonwood Basin. Critically, the study documents a minimum 6% incidence of hybridization between *Xyrauchen texanus* and introduced/native *Catostomus* species, the presence of a previously unrecorded exotic sucker (likely *Catostomus commersoni*) in the lower Colorado basin, and catastrophic bacterial disease (*Pseudomonas* sp. and *Aeromonas* sp.) that killed the majority of captured brood fish at the hatchery. The report frames disease and hybridization as combined existential threats to the Lake Mohave razorback population.

## Background & Description

- **Secondary sexual characteristics**: All 101 captured *Xyrauchen* displayed spawning-typical secondary sex characteristics as previously described by [[Douglas 1952]] and [[Minckley 1973]].
- **Spawning posture**: Triadic mating behavior observed via SCUBA — a single female flanked and pressed by two males in convulsing lateral contact ("laid to her left side by two convulsing males pressed against either flank"), consistent with descriptions in [[Douglas 1952]].
- **Hybrid morphology**: *Xyrauchen-Catostomus* hybrids were identified and separated from pure-strain individuals during processing; five hybrids were sacrificed for study (1 from Hoover Dam site, 4 from Cottonwood Basin).
- **Gonadal anomalies**: A number of autopsied hatchery specimens exhibited rudimentary or entirely absent gonads — hypothesized to result either from pathogenic infection or from the fish being sterile F1 backcrosses of *Xyrauchen-Catostomus* hybrids with parental *X. texanus*.
- **Introduced Catostomus specimen**: One unidentified *Catostomus* individual was preliminarily identified as *C. commersoni* (common white sucker), a species native to the Eastern United States and previously known only from the upper Colorado River basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning habitat**: Razorback spawning aggregations concentrated along an essentially lotic shoreline approximately one mile south of Hoover Dam, associated with hot spring effusions lining both banks. Fish were observed both in shallows (1.0–2.5 m) and deep water (3.0–7.5 m), with actual spawning occurring among boulders near a hot spring outflow in ~1 m of water.
- **Secondary capture site**: A cove on the northeast perimeter of [[Cottonwood Basin]] (~20 miles north of Davis Dam), featuring a wave-washed reef and a shallow inland shoreline — both netted with roughly equal catch success.
- **Sympatric species**: Captures included *Xyrauchen*, *Xyrauchen-Catostomus* hybrids, an introduced *Catostomus* sp., and four gravid female [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) taken incidentally at the Cottonwood Basin cove point.
- **Hybridization dynamics**: Natural hybridization between *X. texanus* and native *Catostomus* species (*C. latipinnis*, *C. insignis*) is documented historically at low frequency without apparent detriment to parent stocks ([[Hubbs & Miller 1953]]). However, the newly detected exotic *C. commersoni* introduces uncertainty regarding long-term hybridization frequency and direct ecological competition within the lake-confined population.
- **Disease ecology**: Captured fish carried pre-existing systemic bacterial infections (*Pseudomonas* sp. and *Aeromonas* sp.) at lethal concentrations, indicating the wild population already carried significant pathogen loads prior to hatchery transfer.

## Key Findings

- **Total capture**: 101 adult *Xyrauchen texanus* netted over four weeks (March–April 1975) across two Lake Mohave sites.
  - Hoover Dam site: 15 razorbacks (7 male, 8 female) + 1 hybrid.
  - Cottonwood Basin cove: 91 suckers total; 33 males and 21 females retained/transported (54 total), 32 already-spawned individuals released back to the lake, plus 4 hybrids and 1 exotic *Catostomus*.
- **Hatchery brood stock**: 40 males and 29 females (69 total) transferred to Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery raceway.
- **Spawning status at capture**: ~90% of females and ~44.5% of males had already spawned prior to capture.
- **Reproductive intervention**: HCG (Human Chorionic Gonadotropin) injections administered to ripening females to induce ovulation; eggs successfully stripped from one female and fertilized with sperm from two males, yielding ~5,000 fertilized eggs on 29 April 1975.
- **Disease mortality**: Autopsies (performed by USFWS biologist Bruce Rosenlund) on nine dead suckers confirmed lethal *Pseudomonas* sp. and *Aeromonas* sp. infections. By report date, all but 18 of the hatchery-held *Xyrauchen* had died from these infections.
- **Hybridization rate**: At least 6% of the Lake Mohave razorback population sampled showed evidence of *Xyrauchen-Catostomus* hybridization.
- **New species record (tentative)**: First indication of *Catostomus commersoni* (white sucker) presence in the lower Colorado River basin — previously undocumented in this region.
- **Incidental bonytail capture**: Four gravid female *Gila elegans* captured; one died in the net, two died at the hatchery within a week, highlighting parallel vulnerability of this co-occurring endangered species.
- **Conservation concern**: The report explicitly identifies the combined pressures of disease and hybridization as a serious threat to maintaining a viable Lake Mohave razorback population, calling for expanded research into population size, hybridization dynamics, ecology, reproductive strategy, disease surveillance/treatment, and monitoring of introduced species interactions.
