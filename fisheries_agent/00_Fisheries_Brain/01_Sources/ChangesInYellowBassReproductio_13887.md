# Yellow Bass Reproduction and Environmental Change in Clear Lake, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 study by Ross V. Bulkley, published in the *Iowa State Journal of Science*, examined how food supply and water temperature shaped the reproductive cycle and fecundity of [[Yellow Bass]] (*Morone mississippiensis*) in [[Clear Lake]], Iowa, from April 1967 to September 1968. Sampling was interrupted when a mass mortality caused by *Aeromonas salmonicida* (the agent of [[Fish Furunculosis]]) struck the adult population in May 1968. The comparison of a normal year (1967) with a poor-condition year (1968) showed that reduced body condition lowered potential and effective fecundity by roughly 40 to 50 percent, and that spawning was triggered by a rapid spring temperature rise of 3 to 4°C as water approached about 15°C.

## Background & Description

Yellow bass were the dominant fish in Clear Lake, a natural eutrophic lake of 1,474 hectares in north-central Iowa that supports 23 warmwater fish species (Bailey and Harrison, 1945). The species competes for food with most other lake fishes and is a prey item for piscivores such as walleye and northern pike, so fluctuations in its abundance affect the wider fish community. Wide annual variation in young-of-the-year abundance suggested that reproductive success was set before or shortly after spawning.

Adult fish were collected with 125-foot experimental [[Gillnet Sampling]] using mesh sizes from 3/4 to 2 inches, supplemented by [[Seine Sampling]] of young-of-the-year. Fish were preserved in 10 percent formalin, measured, and weighed; gonads and mesenteric fat were weighed separately, and stomach contents were volumetrically analyzed. Ovarian fecundity was estimated volumetrically from four 1.5 cc aliquots per fish, and ovum diameters were measured by microprojection. Fecundity was distinguished as potential (ova in advanced secondary development) and effective (ova actually matured and shed). Ages were taken from earlier work by Atchison (1967): males of the sizes studied were at least 3 years old and females at least 4.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning and temperature.** In 1967 spawning began in mid-May, and in 1968 in late April, when water temperatures approached 15°C after a rise of 3.5 to 4.5°C over several days. Sex ratios showed males moving into the shallow [[Spawning Site]] area in April and May, about two weeks later in 1967 than in 1968, which was attributed to temperature. Water temperature was recorded at 3 m depth at the City of Clear Lake water-treatment plant, and surface temperatures over the spawning area averaged only 0.3°C warmer. Comparison with other Morone species (white perch, striped bass, white bass) placed spawning near 15°C.

**Food and body condition.** Stomach content volumes never exceeded 1 cc in any month. [[Chironomid]] larvae dominated spring diets in 1967, *Daphnia* in 1968, and young yellow bass became the major food from August to October, a pattern linked to gonadal development and a possible appetite increase. Body condition was reduced in 1968: a 200 mm female weighed about 137 g in 1967 but 115 g in 1968, a 16 percent decrease, and mean male spawning weight fell about 13 percent. Mesenteric fat fell from 358 mg in March to 6 mg by 14 May 1968, showing that energy for gonad growth came from body reserves when feeding was inadequate.

**Disease and population decline.** Fluctuating spring temperatures and poor condition appear to have allowed [[Aeromonas Salmonicida]] to reach epizootic levels from 20 to 29 May 1968. Catch per gillnet hour fell from 2.158 in mid-May to 0.025 in mid-June and zero by late July. Earlier mass mortalities of yellow bass in Clear Lake (including 1965) were also attributed to this pathogen, and similar die-offs had been reported at Lake Chautauqua, Illinois.

## Key Findings

- **Reproductive cycle:** Gonad weight was under 1 percent of body weight when immature or spent. Ovaries reached up to 16 percent of body weight and testes up to 8 percent at spawning. The ovarian maturity index peaked on 12 May 1967 (mean 11.76 percent) and on 13 May 1968 (mean 8.36 percent), about 30 percent lower.
- **Ovum size:** Fresh ovulated ova averaged 0.80 mm (SD 0.11, range 0.66 to 1.18 mm; n = 102 ova from 4 females). Preserved ova averaged 0.4 mm before ovulation; ovulation appeared imminent once mean ovum diameter exceeded 0.4 mm. The species was judged a single-batch spawner with continuous ovum growth, not a fractional spawner.
- **Spawning in 1967:** Major spawning occurred between 12 and 24 May, and was essentially over by 2 June. Of 57 females examined on that date, only 2 were still functionally mature.
- **Spawning in 1968:** Spawning ran from 30 April to 14 May and was very limited. Most fish retained their sex products and died before spawning was completed.
- **Fecundity 1967:** Potential fecundity averaged 203,800 ova for a 199 mm, 142 g female, equal to 1,023 ova per mm or 1,439 ova per gram. Range was 51,300 (182 mm, 101 g) to 282,400 (197 mm, 149 g). Post-spawning ova retention was about 34 percent (over 70,000 ova).
- **Fecundity 1968:** Potential fecundity fell to a range of 82,400 to 156,000 ova. The effective fecundity of the average female was 53,900 ova, or 276 ova per mm and 350 per gram, against 560 per mm and 835 per gram in 1967. Effective fecundity fell 40 to 50 percent.
- **Cause of fecundity loss:** Body-minus-ovary weight explained about 58 percent of fecundity variation across both years (r = 0.77). Body length alone explained only 50 percent in 1967 and 36 percent across both years. The author concluded that lower body weight, not disease, caused the reduced 1968 potential fecundity.
- **Atresia:** Developmental atresia was about 15 percent from December to March and an additional 5 percent from March to 30 April. The 1967 post-spawning atresia (about 20 percent over December to May) was attributed to poor nutrition.
- **Sex ratio:** Adult samples were balanced (1,093 bass in 1967, male:female 1:1.112; 631 fish in 1968, 1:0.997). A winter bait-fishing sample of 69 adults was 80 percent female, interpreted as differential feeding rather than a true bias.
- **Recruitment:** Seine catches of young-of-the-year were 7,015 in 1967 and 77 in 1968, about 1 percent of the 1967 level.
- **Population decline:** Summer catch per gillnet hour was 2.633 (1965), 1.032 (1966), 2.009 (1967) and 0.029 (1968). The 1967 summer catch was 703 fish; 1968 was 8.
- **Laboratory feeding trial:** Over 20 days at 16 ± 1°C, females fed to satiation had about 135 percent more mesenteric fat (0.53 g) than those fed 1 percent of body weight (0.24 g). Gonadal development stalled in captivity, however, so extra food did not increase ova maturation.
- **Conclusion:** Reproduction in 1968 was poor because of low nutrition, adverse water temperature fluctuations and disease. The author noted that further study is needed in populations where disease is absent.
