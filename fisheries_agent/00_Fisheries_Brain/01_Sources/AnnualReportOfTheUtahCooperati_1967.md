## Executive Summary

This document is the 1967 Annual Report of the Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit (Utah State University), summarizing personnel, funding, coursework, and — most substantively — five active graduate research projects concerning the ecological impacts of Flaming Gorge Dam on native Green River fishes and invertebrates, physiological "fitness" criteria for hatchery [[Rainbow Trout]], stream-drift ecology of aquatic insects, and morphological/serological taxonomy of the [[Gila Robusta]] species complex. The central scientific narrative is the transformation of the Green River from a warm, turbid stream to a cold, clear trout fishery following dam closure (1962), and the resulting decline of native Colorado River fishes in favor of introduced salmonids.

## Background & Description

- **[[Gila Robusta]] complex (Colorado chub):** Two recognized forms — the *roundtail* and *bonytail* — exhibit significantly different length-weight relationships. The bonytail form grows slightly faster than the roundtail form. Age/growth data derived from 333 scale samples showed both forms grew more slowly post-dam-closure (1963–1965) than pre-closure (1955–1962).
- **Taxonomic study (Holden thesis):** 3,097 *Gila* specimens from Wyoming, Utah, Colorado, New Mexico, and Arizona were examined using 28 recorded morphometric characters (19 used in final numerical taxonomy analysis via "Taximetrics") to resolve systematic relationships and intraspecific variation within the *Gila robusta* complex — a group noted for "high degree of morphological variability."
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius):** Age and growth determined from 167 scale samples; individuals over 200 mm total length showed a dietary/morphological ontogenetic shift correlating with piscivory (see Ecological Niche).
- **Serological/immunogenetic characterization:** Preliminary work (Campbell thesis) used immunodiffusion, immunoelectrophoresis, and erythrocyte agglutination with absorbed antisera to distinguish blood-type/serological differences among *Gila* forms from the Colorado and San Juan Rivers — intended to cross-validate morphometric species boundaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Post-impoundment habitat shift:** Following rotenone treatment (Sept. 1962) and Flaming Gorge Dam closure (Nov. 1962), the Green River converted from a warm, turbid, native-fish stream to a cold, clear trout fishery for ~45 miles below the dam.
- **Longitudinal community structure:** Above Carr Ranch (42.7 mi below dam), invertebrate species composition was much simpler (1964–65) than pre-impoundment records; below Carr Ranch, invertebrate composition changed little. Bottom-fauna densities peaked near the dam (max. 6,347 organisms/ft²) and declined downstream, approaching pre-impoundment densities below Carr Ranch.
- **Native fish displacement:** In the first 26 miles below the dam, altered flow and thermal regimes caused native fish populations to be "largely replaced" by introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]. Effects on native fish extended as far as the Yampa River mouth (65 mi below dam); populations below the Yampa confluence remained similar to pre-impoundment conditions.
- **Reproductive success and thermal constraints:** [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Gila Robusta]], and bluehead sucker (*Pantosteus delphinus*) reproduced successfully in Dinosaur National Monument every year post-impoundment, evidenced by length-frequency cohort analysis — except during high-summer-discharge years with lower water temperatures (1964, 1966), when no native reproduction was detected above the Yampa River mouth. No juvenile humpback suckers (*Xyrauchen texanus*) were collected during the entire study, raising concern about recruitment failure in this species.
- **Diet/trophic ecology:** Colorado squawfish >200 mm TL were entirely piscivorous; smaller individuals consumed microcrustaceans and aquatic insects. [[Gila Robusta]] diet consisted largely of aquatic and terrestrial insects.
- **Stream-drift dynamics:** Drift rates of *Baetis* nymphs and Simuliidae larvae were highest near the dam. Drift-net catches were significantly affected by illumination, density of co-occurring organisms, and water temperature; turbidity and water-level fluctuations mattered under specific conditions, while date, dissolved oxygen, and water depth had little measurable effect.
- **Thermal disruption of insect life cycles (Temple Fork/Green River drift study):** A three-fold increase in dam water releases suppressed river warming below the dam, preventing the Green River from reaching *Baetis* egg-developmental threshold temperatures over an 11-mile reach — the summer generation of *Baetis* failed to hatch, forcing relocation of the drift-ecology study to Temple Fork of the Logan River, where the caddisfly *[[Oligophlebodes sigma]]* (larvae drift diurnally; adults undertake upstream flight) became the primary study organism alongside *Baetis*.
- **Rainbow trout physiological ecology:** Scope for activity (active minus standard oxygen consumption) was measured across temperatures (5–25°C) for domestic and wild rainbow trout strains, and under varying starvation durations (1–9 days), to develop fitness criteria predicting post-stocking survival in different environments.

## Key Findings

- **Dam-driven faunal turnover:** Flaming Gorge Dam altered flow/temperature regimes sufficiently to convert the upper 65 miles of the Green River from a native-fish-dominated warm-water system to a trout-dominated coldwater fishery, with the most severe replacement occurring in the first 26 miles below the dam ([[Vanicek 1967]] Ph.D. thesis, based on 23,735 fish from 639 collections; 9 native + 11 exotic species recorded).
- **Growth suppression post-impoundment:** Both [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Gila Robusta]] (Colorado chub) exhibited slower growth rates in the years following dam closure compared to pre-dam years, suggesting a bioenergetic or thermal cost of the altered flow regime.
- **Conditional native reproduction:** Native species reproduction in Dinosaur National Monument was climate/discharge-dependent — successful in most years but absent above the Yampa River during high-discharge, cold-water years (1964, 1966), implicating dam operational regime (not just mean thermal shift) as a key driver of recruitment failure.
- **Humpback sucker recruitment concern:** Complete absence of juvenile humpback suckers across the study period (1964–1966) is an early documented warning sign consistent with this species' later listing concerns.
- **Invertebrate drift regulation:** Multiple environmental variables (light, temperature, density-dependent effects) regulate drift-net catch rates of key trout-food organisms (*Baetis*, Simuliidae), with implications for fishery management aiming to maximize angler yield through understanding of stream secondary production.
- **Exercise-conditioning link in hatchery trout:** [[Boario 1967]] M.S. thesis found exercised rainbow trout acquired conditioned avoidance responses faster than unexercised controls in 12 of 16 trials, supporting a behavioral-physiological fitness linkage relevant to hatchery-rearing protocols.
- **Numerical taxonomy applied to a variable species complex:** The Holden thesis pioneered use of computerized "Taximetrics" analysis on 19 morphometric characters across ~3,097 *Gila* specimens to resolve taxonomically ambiguous individuals within the [[Gila Robusta]] complex, complemented by parallel serological work (Campbell) using immunological techniques.
- **Institutional/programmatic findings:** The Unit's four core research thrusts — (1) reservoir impacts on the Colorado River system, (2) hatchery trout fitness via metabolic scope, (3) stream-drift invertebrate ecology, and (4) fish immunogenetics — collectively aimed to build an integrated ecological and genetic basis for reservoir management decisions balancing public benefit against biological/social costs.
