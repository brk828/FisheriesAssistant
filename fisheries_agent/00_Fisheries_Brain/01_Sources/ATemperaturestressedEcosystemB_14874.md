# Temperature-Stressed Stream Ecosystem Below a Hypolimnial Release Reservoir

#invertebrate-zoology #freshwater-ecology #benthic-macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This one-year study (June 1972 to May 1973) by [[James V. Ward]] examined the [[South Platte River]] below [[Cheesman Dam]] in Colorado. Water released from the [[Hypolimnial Release]] outlet of [[Cheesman Lake]] made the river warmer in winter, cooler in summer, less variable in temperature, and displaced in its seasonal temperature maximum. These effects declined downstream but were still measurable 8.5 km below the dam.

The macroinvertebrate community was low in species richness but high in density and biomass. Ward argues that the altered [[Temperature Regime]] is the main stress factor, and that the river is a [[Stressed Ecosystem]] in the sense used for organic pollution. Species richness rose downstream, and the author links this to the thermal regime. The study also documents high alkalinity, clear water, dense benthic algae, and unusually abundant rooted aquatic plants.

## Background & Description

Hypolimnial release reservoirs are described as "heat traps and nutrient exporters," because cold, nutrient-rich deep water is discharged into the stream (Odum 1971). Downstream temperatures in temperate and mountain regions can be colder in summer and warmer in winter than in unregulated streams. Cheesman Dam was completed in 1905. The river below it runs through a steep canyon and was considered a good site for examining reservoir effects on [[Macroinvertebrates]]. Most earlier studies of this kind had been done on streams with depauperate benthos caused by fluctuating discharge or low dissolved oxygen. Cheesman Lake, by contrast, buffers spring runoff and has air-drafted release tunnels that keep the released water well oxygenated.

**Study area:**
- [[Cheesman Lake]]: spillway elevation 2,087 m; maximum depth 65 m; maximum area 353 ha; capacity 97,514,653 m³.
- Water is released through a 198 cm opening at 60 m depth and a 107 cm opening at 46 m depth (personal communication).
- The canyon vegetation belongs to the Foothills Life Zone. Douglas fir dominates north-facing slopes and ponderosa pine dominates south-facing slopes.
- The river is 5 to 20 m wide in the study section. It has deep granite channels, many potholes, large boulders, and more than 50 m² of pools up to 2 m deep. Flow is smooth, with few rapids.

**Sampling sites** (all in rubble riffles, numbered downstream):
- Site 1: 0.25 km below the dam, in a narrow canyon section with bare rock banks.
- Site 2: 2.4 km below the dam, at the tail of a pool, with grassy banks.
- Site 3: 5 km below the dam, below a small rapid, with sandbar willows (*Salix exigua*) and grasses on the banks.
- Site 4: 8.5 km below the dam, at the tail of a long shallow pool, with grasses and sandbar willows on the banks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling and methods.** Sites were sampled about every 28 days, giving 13 sampling trips. Measurements covered:
- Chemistry: free and bound CO₂ by titration, pH, dissolved oxygen by the Winkler method, and total suspended and dissolved matter with loss on ignition.
- Temperature: mercury thermometer about 10 cm below the surface.
- Flow: daily discharge from Denver Water Board records. Mean current was estimated from maximum float velocity multiplied by 0.8.
- Biology: zooplankton collected by pouring 10 L of water through silk mesh; epilithon scraped from rocks; and three to six Surber samples (929 cm² each) for bottom macroinvertebrates. Biomass was calculated from volume, assuming a specific gravity of 1.0.

**Physicochemical conditions** ([[Water Chemistry]]):
- Free CO₂ was negative at all stations, indicating strong photosynthesis.
- Annual median pH was 8.2, 8.4, 8.4, and 8.6 at Sites 1 to 4. This is very high for a Colorado Front Range stream, which rarely falls below 6.8 or rises above 7.2.
- Bound CO₂ averaged 54.8 ppm, placing the river in the "hard" category.
- Mean flow was 206 m³/min (121 cfs). Flow fluctuated greatly from June 6 to October 18 (68 to 601 m³/min), then stabilized.
- Mean current was 2.2 km/h over the lowest 10 months, which is "slow" and unusual for Colorado foothills streams.
- Annual mean total suspended matter was 2.2 mg/L at Site 1 and 25.5 mg/L at Site 4, a nearly 12-fold difference. Excluding the May 1973 flood, the first 12 trips gave mean values of 2.1, 1.9, 2.0, and 2.6 mg/L. Clarity was therefore maintained downstream except at bankfull flow.
- Total dissolved matter was high, with annual means of 356.9 mg/L at Site 1 and 338.8 mg/L at Site 4.

**Temperature regime** ([[Temperature Regime]]):
- The annual mean temperature rose downstream, although winter temperatures were similar or slightly lower downstream.
- Site 1 ranged from 3 to 13 °C through the year and reflected reservoir hypolimnion temperatures.
- Water temperatures converged at all sites on 28 October, the autumn overturn of the reservoir.
- Mean deviations from the annual mean were 2.1, 2.3, 2.6, and 3.2 °C at Sites 1 to 4. Site 1 showed the largest single deviation (+7.2 °C), caused by the overturn.
- Site 1 maximum occurred in late October. Sites 2 and 3 had the same late-October maximum. Only Site 4 peaked in summer.
- Diurnal fluctuation was dampened at Site 1 (assumed under 1 °C). Morning and afternoon readings at Site 4 showed greater variation, except in mid-winter.
- The river remained ice-free for at least 9 km below the dam during the coldest weather, while stretches a few kilometers farther downstream froze over.

**Epilithon and algae** ([[Epilithon]]):
- Chlorophyta dominated at all sites, at about 72%, 50%, 38%, and 34% of epilithon at Sites 1 to 4. Dominant genera included *Cladophora* and *Ulothrix*.
- Bacillariophyceae (diatoms) rose from 11% at Site 1 to 21% at Site 4.
- Cyanophyta were the least abundant group, rising from 1% to 6%.
- Detritus rose from 12% at Site 1 to 32% at Site 4.
- [[Hydrurus]] became important during parts of the year. This chrysophyte is restricted to cold streams.
- Mean annual epilithon dry weight per 5-minute sample was 9.73, 7.51, 12.02, and 12.40 g at Sites 1 to 4. Loss on ignition was 2.02, 1.65, 1.86, and 2.14 g.
- Epilithon standing crop peaked in winter, which the author attributes to warmer water and the absence of surface ice.

**Zooplankton.** A total of 18 taxa of lentic zooplankton were found in the river. Numbers fell downstream, with 55% remaining at Site 2, 86% at Site 3, and 17% at Site 4, relative to Site 1.

**Rooted aquatic plants.** Three species were present: *Potamogeton filiformis*, *P. crispus*, and *Ranunculus aquatilis*. They were absent from the first kilometer below the dam. Beds reached up to 1 m high and 1 m long. They appeared in August, peaked in late October, and persisted until mid-March. *P. filiformis* is characteristic of hard water.

**Macroinvertebrates** ([[Macroinvertebrates]]):
- A total of 39 species were recorded across the four sites, of which 17 were considered common.
- Species richness rose downstream from 19 species at Site 1 to 33 at Site 4. At Site 1, 10 of the 19 species were common.
- Insects made up nearly three-quarters of the species. Diptera, Ephemeroptera, and Trichoptera made up 84% of insect species.
- Diptera had the highest diversity, but it did not show a clear downstream increase. Diptera were first or second in abundance at all sites. [[Simulium Arcticum]] was very abundant at Site 4 and common at Site 1, but scarce at the middle sites.
- Ephemeroptera included [[Ephemerella Grandis]], *Baetis* sp., and *Rhithrogena* sp. *Ephemerella grandis* was uncommon near the dam but abundant downstream. It reached its upstream limit at Site 4.
- Only one stonefly, *Isoperla patricia*, was present, and it was absent from Site 1.
- The earthworm [[Eiseniella Tetraedra]] was important only at Site 4, where it was present throughout the year.
- The amphipod [[Gammarus Lacustris]] was abundant at Site 1 and only sparse at Site 4. It was absent in 9 of the 13 trips at Site 4. The author notes that this species is normally restricted to cold, high-elevation lakes and does not occur in foothills streams.
- Gastropods were limited to the middle and lower sites. *Lymnaea auricularia* was restricted to Site 3, and *Physa anatina* was most abundant there.
- *Dugesia tigrina* was confined to the lower two sites.
- Summary densities were 8,253 individuals/m² and 30.9 g/m² (summary section). The results section gives 3,253 organisms/m², so the density figure is inconsistent within the source. Maxima at one site were 22,526 organisms/m² and 157.3 g/m².
- Diptera and Ephemeroptera made up 92.7% of individuals and 82.8% of biomass.
- Seasonal biomass ranged from 9.9 to 116.2 g/m², with peaks in August, December, and April. Density closely tracked biomass.

**Why the community is stressed.** The author lists 13 characteristics that should favor a rich and productive benthic community: medium width, good pool-riffle structure, moderate current, rubble substrate, low turbidity, high dissolved salts, hard water, high pH, dense benthic algae, rooted plants, lentic plankton as food, high winter temperatures, and ice-free conditions, along with dampened spates. Despite these, the community has low diversity and high biomass. This matches [[Thienemann's Principle]], in which low species diversity with large numbers of individuals is typical of extreme conditions.

The author concludes that the temperature regime is the most likely limiting factor. Its relative constancy, low and displaced summer maximum, and high winter minimum may prevent the developmental triggers many species require:
- Diapause breaking by freezing or near-freezing temperatures.
- Hatching after cold followed by warming.
- A minimum temperature sustained for a set duration to trigger nymph maturation.
- A required number of degree-days before emergence.

Warm winter water may also trigger premature emergence (a rise of only about 1 °C has been shown to do this in some caddisflies). Other factors, including alkalinity, hardness, and dissolved salts, were judged unlikely to be major limits because they varied little within the study reach while richness increased sharply.

The author also notes that low diversity probably reduces community stability, so a relatively small biotic or abiotic change could produce large shifts in community structure.

## Key Findings

- The hypolimnial release from [[Cheesman Dam]] changed the river's temperature regime. Winters were warmer and summers cooler, with dampened daily and seasonal fluctuation and a displaced maximum. These effects declined downstream but persisted 8.5 km below the dam.
- Macroinvertebrate species richness was low near the dam (19 species at Site 1) and increased downstream (33 at Site 4). The total was 39 species, of which 17 were common.
- Macroinvertebrate density and biomass were high (biomass 30.9 g/m² on average; maxima of 157.3 g/m² at one site). Diptera and Ephemeroptera dominated.
- Water chemistry was alkaline and hard. Mean pH was 8.2 to 8.6, bound CO₂ was 54.8 ppm, and total dissolved matter was about 340 to 360 mg/L. Clarity was high except at bankfull flow.
- Epilithon was dominated by filamentous green algae. Standing crop peaked in winter, which the author attributes to warmer water and ice-free conditions.
- Three rooted aquatic plant species occurred in the lower study section, and the author considers them unusual for a granitic foothills stream.
- Some taxa were concentrated in the reach below the dam, including [[Gammarus Lacustris]] and *Lymnaea auricularia*. Others were more abundant downstream, such as *Ephemerella grandis*. The author interprets this as evidence of a thermal filter.
- The author's central conclusion is that the river is a temperature-stressed ecosystem. Low diversity alongside high standing crop reflects the reduced predation and competition that come with a species-poor community. The author expects this community to be unstable and vulnerable to small environmental changes.
