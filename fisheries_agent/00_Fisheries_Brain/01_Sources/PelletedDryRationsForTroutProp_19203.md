# Pelleted Dry Rations for Trout Propagation in Michigan Hatcheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether [[Pelleted Dry Diet]] formulations could replace the raw-meat rations long used in Michigan trout hatcheries. Pelleted dry-meal diets were compared with [[Raw Beef Liver]] and chopped raw-meat diets, using [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] as test animals. Growth, mortality, and feed cost were the main criteria. Pellets caused an initial weight loss, but fish recovered within two to four weeks. Pelleted diets supplemented with raw liver about once every three weeks supported growth at roughly half to five-eighths of the raw-meat feeding rate. The author reports that adopting pellets as the standard ration across all Michigan state hatcheries raised trout production by 60 percent and cut fish food expenditures by 40 percent.

## Background & Description

Before 1951, nearly all hatchery trout diets were raw meat or raw meat mixed with dry meals. These diets had practical drawbacks: particles separated in water, food was lost, and labor, refrigeration, and grinding equipment were costly. Michigan investigations into dry pelleted feeds began in 1952, and larger trials started in January 1953. The study had two goals: (1) develop a practical, economical pelleted dry-meal diet that produced healthy, legal-sized trout quickly, and (2) eliminate the need for raw meat.

Ingredients were chosen for availability, cost, nutrient quality, and bulk. The main candidates were [[Fish Meal]], [[Cottonseed Meal]], [[Soybean Oil Meal]], wheat flour middlings, skim milk, [[Brewers Yeast]], [[Torula Yeast]], and condensed fish solubles. Of 20 tested combinations, the one with the best physical properties was 38 parts fish meal, 5 skim milk, 23 wheat middlings, 5 torula yeast, 25 cottonseed meal, 3 condensed fish solubles, and 2 iodized salt. This mixture was designated Diet 1. Pellets remained intact in water for up to 10 minutes. Tablets were pressed with a hand-operated "Eureka" tablet machine, since small pelleting machines were not available. Commercial steam-extrusion was not practical with this equipment, so a small amount of water was added to the meal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The main study site was the [[Wolf Lake State Fish Hatchery]], 10 miles west of Kalamazoo, Michigan. The hatchery drew clear spring water through underground pipes. Recorded conditions were:

- Water temperature: 42°F to 58°F over the year
- Dissolved oxygen: 6.0 to 9.0 ppm
- Free carbon dioxide: 2.2 ppm average
- pH: 7.5 to 8.1
- Methyl orange alkalinity: 160 ppm

Experiments used 12 concrete tanks (each split into two compartments) and 16 wooden troughs. Trials moved through three phases: small troughs for screening, large indoor tanks, and outdoor gravel-bottom raceways at both the [[Wolf Lake State Fish Hatchery]] (southernmost Michigan trout hatchery) and the [[Marquette Hatchery]] (northernmost). Results then went to full-scale use across all Michigan trout hatcheries. Water was not filtered for small organisms. Because the tanks were indoors, predator losses were negligible apart from trout themselves.

## Key Findings

**Early pellet trials (1952–1953)**
- In a [[Hastings State Fish Hatchery]] trial, 4.5-inch rainbow trout (average 18 g) were fed three pellet sizes or raw pork liver for eight weeks. All pellet sizes supported survival and growth.
- Fish fed 3/32-inch pellets recovered weight loss in two weeks; those on 1/8-inch and 3/16-inch pellets needed four weeks. The author recommended a 2- to 4-week adjustment period when switching from meat to pellets.
- At the [[Oden State Fish Hatchery]], 6-inch brook trout fed pellets (four days a week, raw meat one day a week, meat at double the daily pellet allowance) grew more than those on the standard ration of 50 parts pork spleen and 50 parts commercial chick starter over 20 and 28 weeks. The author attributed this to the pellets' slower disintegration in water, compared with ground raw meat, which separates in water.

**Seven-diet evaluation with rainbow trout (1953–1954)**
- Setup: 12,000 seven-month-old rainbow trout (2.9 to 5.9 inches, 4 to 20 g), 1,000 per tank, observed in 14 two-week periods from October 1, 1953, to April 28, 1954.
- Diets 1 to 7 were dry pellet mixtures built on a fish meal and wheat middlings base. Diet 8 was the control, [[Raw Beef Liver]].
- Diets 2 and 6 produced the greatest gains. Diets 3, 7, and 4 followed. Diets 1, 4, and 5 grew more slowly than the control.
- Diet 2 (brewers yeast) outperformed Diet 1 (torula yeast) with the same formulation otherwise. The difference was highly significant (t = 2.96, p < 0.01, 26 df).
- Diet 2 versus the control was near significance (calculated t = 2.01; the tabular t at 0.05 is listed as 2.056 in Table 4). All other pairwise comparisons were not significant.
- Diet 6 (gelatin) gave a steadier gain than Diet 7 (stabilized fat).
- Reported percentage gains over 28 weeks at about 47°F were 266.0 and 267.4 for Diet 1 duplicates, and 311.0 and 346.7 for Diet 2 duplicates. Conversion ratios for Diet 2 were about 1.4, against 2.4 for Diet 1.
- Diets 6, 8, 1, and 2 produced the highest percentages of legal-sized fish (seven inches and over). Diets 3 and 4 produced the lowest. The control's trout were about one inch shorter at six months, but nearly equal in legal-size percentage by seven months.
- Feeding levels were tentative. Pellet groups started at 3.0 percent of body weight per day, and the control at 6.0 percent. Near the end of the sixth month, at 42°F, about 1.5 percent of body weight per day was fed (average 6.4 inches, range 3.0 to 10.1; average 2.3 ounces).

**Mortality and cannibalism**
- Recorded losses came from fish jumping out of tanks or handling injuries. No deaths were attributed to malnutrition.
- Unaccountable losses were high and variable. The author concluded that cannibalism was the likely cause, linked to variation in fish size. Grading fish to uniform size at the start would likely have reduced it.
- The data did not clearly identify one dietary component as the cause, though the author suspected one or more diet factors. The author believed a dietary solution could be found.

**Brook trout production trial (Marquette Hatchery)**
- Four groups of 4,000 young-of-the-year brook trout were held in gravel-bottom raceways for 28 weeks.
- Group A: pellets four days a week plus raw liver one day a week.
- Group B: pellets nine days, then raw liver one day, every two weeks.
- Group C: pellets fourteen days, then raw liver one day, every three weeks.
- Control: 8 percent torula yeast and 92 percent beef liver (the hatchery's usual mixture).
- Control fish received 3.6 percent of body weight per day, the pellet groups 1.7 percent.
- Cost per pound of trout was $0.19 to $0.23 for pellet groups and $0.50 for the control. Conversion was about 2.3 to 2.7 for pellets and 5.0 for the control. Food cost was $7.00 per 100 lb of pellets versus $9.90 for the control.
- Pellets fed for three weeks without raw meat did not reduce growth.

**Adoption and economic outcome**
- Diet 1 was adopted as the standard production diet by the Michigan Department of Conservation after a large-scale 1953 pelleting run. Hatcheries were slow to start, but all were using pellets by the time of writing.

- Indirect benefits included larger average fish, more trout reaching planting size in their second year, and less pond space occupied by overwintered fish.
- Pelleted feed could be stored without refrigeration, required little preparation, and was consumed more completely, which reduced pond pollution and cleaning labor.
