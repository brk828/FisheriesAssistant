# Distribution and Synonymy in the Pacific Ocean, and Variation, of the Greenland Halibut

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pleuronectidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 study by [[Hubbs]] and Wilimovsky, published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, reviews the Pacific distribution and taxonomic status of the [[Greenland Halibut]] (*Reinhardtius hippoglossoides*). The species is a commercially significant flatfish in the northwestern Pacific and [[Bering Sea]], and the authors document new records from the eastern Bering Sea, south of the [[Alaska Peninsula]], northern and central California, and northern Baja California. Morphometric and meristic analyses find no basis for recognizing Pacific and Atlantic populations as separate subspecies or species. The authors conclude the species is an undifferentiated [[Amphiboreal Distribution]] taxon, with southern records representing non-breeding stragglers from northern populations.

## Background & Description

The Greenland halibut is an elongate pleuronectine flatfish with a straight lateral line, a very large mouth, and strong non-depressible teeth set in a single row in the lower jaw. It is often confused with the [[Arrowtooth Flounder]] (*Atheresthes*), which has biserial, partly depressible teeth, many more vertebrae, and much larger scales. Other diagnostic features of *Reinhardtius* include parallel olfactory laminae lacking a rachis, massive short gill-rakers, and an extremely thin otolith (sagitta). The species is very dark, almost black on the eyed side. Russian and Japanese names translate as "black paltus" and "black flounder." The taxon has a long synonymy in the Pacific, with names such as *Reinhardtius matsuurae* and *R. hippoglossoides matsuurae*, and the authors note that *R. oleosus* was in fact a synonym of *Atheresthes evermanni*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pacific range:** Primarily the seas off eastern Honshu north of Kinkazan, Hokkaido, Sakhalin, the Kuril Islands, Kamchatka, and the Okhotsk and Bering seas. Records from central Honshu, California, and Baja California are treated as stragglers. The status between the Alaska Peninsula and northern California remained undetermined.
- **Depth:** Adults in the north generally inhabit deep water beyond the continental shelf. Russian data give 70–700 m for Asian waters. Shallow records include young at 18–40 m in the Gulf of Anadir, adults at 52–56 m in commercial catches, and a Baja specimen at 14.5–16.5 m.
- **Dispersal:** Sporadic southward occurrence is attributed to free-swimming adults and a prolonged pelagic larval stage, which retains bilateral symmetry to 54–57 mm. The authors suggest the [[Oyashio Current]], [[California Current]], and [[Labrador Current]] transport individuals southward, and that the [[North Pacific Drift]] carries young eastward.
- **Environmental correlates:** Abundance in the subarctic Pacific fits colder surface and near-surface temperature patterns and the location of the Alaskan Gyre and Western Subarctic domains, as described by Dodimead et al. (1963).
- **Cold-water events:** The Sagami Bay specimen (April 8, 1963, bottom temperature 12.7 °C) and the Gulf of Maine specimens (February 1963, water 1.5–2.5 °C colder than usual) were taken during unusually cold periods.
- **Reproduction:** Very young fish are abundant only in the far north. The authors suggest the species reproduces mainly in deep northern waters, and that southern populations are not established.

## Key Findings

1. **New Pacific records:** Six adults were taken off northern and central California (1962–1963), one adult from Baja California (May 9, 1962, 638 mm), and about 500 young were found in one northern fur seal's stomach near St. Paul Island (August 24, 1963). The fur-seal record indicates abundant reproduction in the eastern Bering Sea.
2. **No subspecific or specific separation:** Vernidub and Panin (1937) had proposed *R. h. matsuurae* on two morphometric ratios with 36 Pacific and 8 Atlantic specimens. The present data show overlapping values for all four body dimensions (head length, upper-jaw length, caudal peduncle length and depth), so the authors reject that separation.
3. **Vertebral difference:** Vertebral counts differ significantly between oceans (t = 2.5 for precaudal, 11.3 for caudal, and 12.9 for total counts). The authors regard the difference as probably genetic but below the racial level and short of subspecies criteria.
4. **Fin ray counts:** Dorsal and anal ray counts show no consistent ocean difference. Dorsal rays range from 83 to 108 and anal rays from 62 to 79. Pectoral rays range from 11 to 15, with modes of 13 or 14 and no taxonomic value. Pelvic rays are normally 6 per side. Caudal rays are typically 9 + 8 = 17 total.
5. **Meristic correlations:** Dorsal and anal ray numbers are strongly positively correlated (r = +0.58 ± 0.07). Vertebrae correlate only weakly with dorsal rays (r = +0.18 ± 0.11) and anal rays (r = +0.12 ± 0.10). Precaudal and caudal vertebrae are negatively correlated (r = −0.49 ± 0.08).
6. **Asymmetry:** Pectoral ray counts are nearly symmetrical (Pacific slight right-side excess, not significant). Pelvic and caudal counts show little asymmetry, which the authors link to the species' limited general asymmetry.
7. **Reversed specimens:** No left-eyed (reversed) specimens were found in the Pacific. Two reversed specimens from Greenland are recorded in the Copenhagen museum (Lichtenau Fjord, 1914; Sydprøven, 1921).
8. **Amphiboreal conclusion:** The authors describe the species as a uniquely undifferentiated amphiboreal taxon, with range expansion attributed mainly to changes in fishing and research effort rather than true expansion.
9. **Material base:** The analysis draws on specimens from Japan, Okhotsk, Kamchatka, Bering Sea, California, Greenland, Labrador, Newfoundland, Georges Bank, Iceland, and Europe. The appendix lists more than 60 lots, which are summarized here rather than reproduced.
