# Status of Leopard Frogs (Rana pipiens Complex) in Arizona and Southeastern California

#herpetology #conservation-biology #ranidae

## Executive Summary

This status survey, conducted from 1983 to 1987 in Arizona and Imperial Valley, California, assessed native [[Leopard Frogs]] (the *Rana pipiens* complex) against literature and museum records. The authors found populations of most native species greatly reduced. [[Rana Chiricahuensis]] was located at only 2 of 36 previously known localities, and [[Rana Pipiens]] was absent from 13 of 28 literature localities. [[Rana Yavapaiensis]] could not be found in Imperial Valley or the lower [[Colorado River]] and [[Gila River]], where the introduced [[Rana Berlandieri]] has displaced it. The authors conclude that native leopard frog populations have declined from historic levels, possibly through a combination of introduced predators and competitors, habitat loss, and possibly acid precipitation. They call for immediate research.

## Background & Description

Ranid frog declines across the United States have been documented for the past century. Proposed causes include habitat change, predation or competition from the introduced [[American Bullfrog]] and exotic fishes, random fluctuation of small populations, exploitation, toxicants, acid rain, pathogens, and catastrophic weather events. Specific causes have rarely been established.

The native ranids of Arizona and adjacent areas include:
- [[Rana Onca]] (considered extinct; formerly of the Colorado and Virgin river drainages)
- [[Rana Tarahumarae]] (recently extirpated from its US range in Arizona headwaters, and from some northern Sonoran localities)
- *Rana pipiens*, *Rana blairi*, *Rana chiricahuensis*, and *Rana yavapaiensis*

The study began as an attempt to collect *R. chiricahuensis* and *R. yavapaiensis* for electrophoretic comparison with a leopard frog found near Yuma in 1981. That specimen proved to be *R. berlandieri*, an apparent introduction from New Mexico. Repeated failures to find native frogs at former collection sites prompted a broader survey of leopard frog distribution across Arizona and Imperial Valley.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and methods.** Field surveys ran from March to November, 1983 to 1987. Sites were drawn from prior literature (e.g., [[Platz and Mecham]] 1979; [[Frost and Bagnara]] 1977; [[Storer]] 1925; [[Ruibal]] 1959) and from museum collections, including Arizona State University and the Museum of Northern Arizona. The team also surveyed about 65 km of the Colorado River between the US–Mexico border and Laguna Dam, northeast of Yuma. Localities were visited one to three times in favorable weather. Sites were searched mainly at night with flashlights, supplemented by daytime searches under rocks and logs. Each survey walked at least 0.5 km of shoreline (a boat was used on the Colorado River). Larvae were reared through metamorphosis for identification, and voucher specimens went to the Arizona State University Herpetology Collections. The authors state that the survey aimed for a geographically representative sample rather than complete coverage.

**Distribution and co-occurring species.**
- *R. chiricahuensis* was found at 2 of 36 localities that supported it in the 1960s and 1970s, which represents 55% of Arizona literature records. Sites 19 and 29 were the only surviving occurrences, and the team added two new localities: [[Ramsey Canyon]] and [[Rucker Lake]]. Of 21 sites of sympatry with other leopard frogs, only one (site 19, possibly site 20) still held other species. [[Rana Catesbeiana]] was present at six former *R. chiricahuensis* sites.
- *R. pipiens* was absent from 13 of 28 literature localities in Arizona. One small, previously unreported population was found at about 2,620 m on the [[San Francisco Peaks]].
- *R. blairi* was found at 2 of 6 literature localities surveyed, and a new population was reported. The species' Arizona range lies in [[Sulphur Springs Valley]], disjunct from its main range in the Great Plains. Some previously reported sites were dry, and most surveyed sites were intermittent stock tanks.
- *R. yavapaiensis* persisted in interior Arizona, where three new localities were found. It was not located in Imperial Valley, the lower Colorado River, or the lower Gila River. Its last records there date from the early 1960s.
- *R. berlandieri* was found at numerous sites along the Colorado River from the international boundary to the mouth of the Gila River, and upstream along the Gila to near Gila Bend, Arizona.

**Introduced species.** The bullfrog was present at all but one site in southwestern Arizona and Imperial Valley. It was absent from all interior Arizona sites where *R. yavapaiensis* persisted. Its distribution appears limited to low- to mid-elevation, low-gradient streams, permanent ponds, and agricultural areas, since wetlands above 2,100 m and steep streams did not hold it.

**Notable population dynamics: [[Aravaipa Creek]].** Annual counts of *R. chiricahuensis* and *R. yavapaiensis* along a 28-km reach (late September to early October) show marked fluctuation:
- Density peaked at 84.6 frogs/km in 1978 and fell to 0.1 frog/km in 1983, following a severe flood just before that census.
- A major crash occurred between 1980 and 1981, when mean density dropped from 57.7 to 3.9 frogs/km.
- Since the 1983 flood, densities have not exceeded 13.9 frogs/km.

## Key Findings

1. **Severe range contraction of native leopard frogs.** *R. chiricahuensis* persists at only two of its 36 historic localities, and *R. pipiens* is absent from almost half of its literature sites. The authors conclude that native populations have declined from historic levels.

2. **Displacement by introduced species.** *R. yavapaiensis* has been replaced by the introduced *R. berlandieri* along the Colorado and Gila rivers and is absent from Imperial Valley. The authors note that the bullfrog's distribution coincides with native losses, though coincidence alone does not establish causation.

3. **Rapid, widespread loss.** Declines of *R. chiricahuensis* and *R. pipiens* in Arizona occurred in less than about ten years, which the authors describe as especially alarming.

4. **Multiple hypothesized mechanisms, none confirmed.**
   - *Predation and competition:* bullfrogs and exotic predatory fishes, though rarely present at most *R. chiricahuensis* and *R. pipiens* localities.
   - *Habitat alteration:* overgrazing of riparian vegetation, wetland drainage, agricultural clearing, groundwater pumping, and altered water flow.
   - *Acid precipitation:* depressed rainfall pH may leach heavy metals from bedrock, with possible synergistic toxicity. This hypothesis needs further testing. Leopard frogs are relatively acid-intolerant compared with other amphibians (Pierce 1985).
   - *Pathogens and toxicants:* histopathology of five moribund *R. tarahumarae* specimens from northern Mexico showed no supporting evidence.
   - *Climate:* the authors rejected drought as a cause because conditions had become wetter since 1978.
   - *Point-source pollution:* never evident at surveyed sites.

5. **Population fluctuation may confound status assessment.** The Aravaipa Creek data show that leopard frog numbers can swing sharply with environmental stimuli. Frogs reappeared at sites 19 and 29 in 1987, suggesting that the survey may have coincided with a period of constricted distribution.

6. **Limitations of absence data.** Microsite differences, seasonal activity (*R. pipiens* forages far from water and is harder to detect), survey timing in March and April before emergence from hibernation, and possible random local extinction all limit the conclusions. The authors judge these factors to account for only a small share of the absence records. Many Imperial Valley sites could not be relocated because of habitat change since earlier collections.

7. **Recommendations.** The authors urge immediate research into causes of leopard frog decline, coordinated with work on *R. tarahumarae*. They also recommend that conservation concern extend to southwestern riparian ecosystems, which have lost habitat and suffered extirpations of fishes and obligate riparian species.
