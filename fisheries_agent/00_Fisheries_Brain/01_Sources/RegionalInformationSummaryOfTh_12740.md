# Threatened Wildlife of the Lower Colorado Region: Regional Information Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Cyprinodontidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation booklet (prepared by Wayne O. Deason, Environmental Office, Lower Colorado Region) reproduces, often word for word, sheets from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service "Red Book" (*Threatened Wildlife of the United States*, 1973 edition). Its purpose is to help Reclamation staff evaluate project impacts on threatened wildlife in the region. It covers fishes, reptiles and amphibians, birds and mammals, with one sheet per species or subspecies and a general-location map for most. Fishes are the largest section: 18 full species or subspecies accounts, 2 peripheral fishes and 8 status-undetermined fishes. Appendices list endangered species for Arizona, California and Nevada and the nationwide official list (as amended May 19, 1972).

## Background & Description

Each sheet gives:
- distinguishing characteristics
- present and former distribution
- status and estimated numbers
- fecundity
- reasons for decline
- protective measures taken and proposed
- captivity numbers and culture potential
- references

The categories are "threatened," "peripheral" (at the edge of the range in the U.S. but not range-wide) and "status-undetermined." The fishes are arranged taxonomically following the American Fisheries Society's 1970 list of names (Special Publication No. 3). Data came largely from specialists such as [[Robert R. Miller]], [[James E. Deacon]] and [[Robert J. Behnke]].

Fish sheets by group:

- **Livebearer and stickleback:** [[Gila Topminnow]] (Poeciliidae), [[Unarmored Threespine Stickleback]] (Gasterosteidae)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most listed fishes are relicts confined to isolated springs, spring outflows, headwater streams or short river reaches in the Colorado, Gila, Death Valley/Amargosa, Owens, Mohave and Little Colorado systems. Recurrent causes of decline across the fish sheets:
- **Exotic species:** mosquitofish ([[Gambusia affinis]]), exotic trout, goldfish, largemouth bass, bullfrogs, crayfish, black mollies and arroyo chub (hybridization with the Mohave chub).
- **Hybridization:** rainbow trout with Paiute cutthroat; arroyo chub with Mohave chub.
- **Water development:** irrigation, ditching of spring outflows, aquifer pumping, reservoirs and dams, and channelization.
- **Forestry:** forest removal altering trout streams.
- **Pollution and chemical rehabilitation** of habitat (for example, the spinedace).

Other taxa in the booklet face parallel threats:
- Amphibians: habitat restriction and irrigation practices.
- Birds: pesticide-induced eggshell thinning (brown pelican, peregrine falcon, bald eagle) and marsh loss (clapper and black rails).
- Mammals: hunting, predator control, grazing competition and habitat change.

## Key Findings

**Trouts**
- Paiute cutthroat trout: about 500 adults; threatened by overfishing, hybridization with rainbow trout and limited range. Hybrids were removed from Silver King Creek in 1965.
- Gila trout: about 4,300 in Diamond Creek; fecundity 200–600 eggs per female; restricted to Diamond, McKenna and Spruce Creeks, New Mexico.
- Arizona (Apache) trout: fecundity 200–500 eggs per female; restricted to two small streams and two lakes; several thousand held at a state hatchery.

**Minnows and sucker**
- Pahranagat bonytail: restricted to outflow of Crystal and Ash Springs, Nevada; numbers unknown; no protection yet.
- Little Colorado spinedace: 2–4 inch minnow; egg counts for 12 females ranged from 650 to 5,600; a refuge exists on East Clear Creek.
- Mohave chub: pure population only at Lake Tuendae (several thousand); transplanted to 3 other areas in 1969–1970.
- Moapa dace: estimated 500 to 1,000; threatened by spring alteration, gambusia and bullfrogs, and a proposed irrigation and dam project.
- Woundfin: now virtually restricted to the Virgin River below Hurricane, Utah; a proposed dam above St. George is a concern.
- Colorado squawfish: natural reproduction known only in the Yampa River and the Green River below the Yampa; it will not reproduce in cold tailwaters or reservoirs; the sheet recommends against temperature-lowering developments on the Yampa.
- White River sucker: decline suspected, not documented; situation reported as improving in 1972.

**Pupfishes and allies**
- Devils Hole pupfish: single spring-fed pool; 300 to 800 individuals, at times as few as 125 breeding adults; water-level decline possibly from aquifer pumping; a spawning platform was added in 1970.
- Tecopa pupfish: virtually extinct by 1966 (2 individuals seen), then 2 additional populations found.
- Nevada pupfish: 50 to 2,000 in each of ten Ash Meadows springs.
- Warm Springs pupfish: School and Scruggs Springs only; School Spring fenced in 1969 and a supplemental well drilled in 1970.
- Owens pupfish: thought extinct since 1939, rediscovered July 1964; refuge population established in 1970.
- Pahrump killifish: about 100 breeding adults, total about 1,300 in July 1967; a 1970 transplant was made to the lower Colorado River.
- Gila topminnow: only two U.S. spring areas remain, in Arizona; 40 to 200 held at Arizona State University.
- Unarmored threespine stickleback: extinct on the Los Angeles Plain between 1942 and 1961; remaining stocks in Santa Clara River headwaters.

**Other fish lists**
- Peripheral fishes: Mexican stoneroller and Sonora chub.
- Eight status-undetermined fishes, including Utah cutthroat trout, Yaqui chub, White River spinedace, spikedace, shortnose sucker, humpback sucker, Shoshone pupfish and Sacramento perch.

**Non-fish taxa (summary)**
- Amphibians: desert slender salamander (fewer than 500, about an acre of habitat), black toad (probably about 10,000 adults), Vegas Valley leopard frog (possibly extinct; last specimens 1942).
- Birds: 11 full accounts, with notable pesticide effects on California brown pelican (declining 14–18% per year in California), peregrine falcon and bald eagle. Yuma clapper rail estimated at 500 to 1,000.
- Mammals: 5 full accounts, including Kaibab squirrel (about 4,000 in 1968), Mexican wolf, tule elk, Sonoran pronghorn (about 60 on Cabeza Prieta and 15 on Organ Pipe in 1968) and peninsular bighorn (971 in 1971).

**Appendices:** Appendix A lists endangered wildlife by state for Arizona, California and Nevada. Appendix B reproduces the nationwide official endangered list (fishes, reptiles and amphibians, birds, mammals).
