# Food Habits of the Amargosa Pupfish in a Thermal Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined the diet of an [[Amargosa Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis amargosae*) population in the thermal outflow of [[Tecopa Bore]], California, using 218 specimens collected monthly from January 1972 to April 1973. The diet was dominated by an [[Algae-Detritus Mixture]] of blue-green algae and detritus, which made up at least 80% of gut volume on nearly all sampling dates. Invertebrates were a minor food source because they were scarce in the stream. Small fish ate some invertebrates, but this habit declined as the [[Gut Length Ratio]] increased with growth. The work is presented as the first systematic year-long feeding study of a pupfish.

## Background & Description

The [[Amargosa Pupfish]] is a small cyprinodontid fish endemic to the desert springs and streams of the American Southwest. The author notes that native desert fishes face threats from non-native species introduction and habitat alteration, and that several native species have become extinct in recent decades. Basic ecological requirements of these fishes were poorly known at the time, which motivated this study as part of a broader [[Bioenergetics]] investigation of the same population.

Sampling used a 0.25 m² wooden box pressed into the substrate immediately after sunset, when fish lie motionless on the bottom. Fish were preserved in 10% formalin and later transferred to 70% ethanol. Each month, about 15 fish were examined, five from each of three size classes: 5–15.0 mm, 15.1–30.0 mm, and 30.1–45.0 mm standard length (SL). Gut contents were analyzed because the species has no distinct stomach.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** [[Tecopa Bore]] is a thermal artesian well located about 13 km east of [[Death Valley National Monument]] in the [[Mojave Desert]]. Water emerges at 47.5 °C and flows about 300 m to a marsh. The stream is 3–6 m wide and less than 10 cm deep, with discharge ranging from 2.3 to 6.7 m³/min over the year. Mainstream water cools by 8–12 °C before reaching the marsh. Cooler margins and side pockets (30–35 °C) above the marsh form the main study area, where the pupfish population is dense.

**Primary production and fauna.** Algal mats cover much of the study area. They consist of autotrophic blue-green algae (*Oscillatoria*, *Spirulina*, and *Anabaena*, with some diatoms), a heterotrophic community of bacteria and fungi, and detritus. The aquatic fauna is dominated by an undescribed ostracod (*Potamocypris* sp.), an undescribed harpacticoid copepod (*Cletocamptus* sp.), the cyclopoid copepod *Eucyclops agilis*, minute moss beetles (*Ochthebius* sp.), and chironomid and *Tabanus* larvae. None of these were abundant, and they were restricted to cooler areas.

**Other fish.** The [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) made up less than 5% of the fish population and was the only other fish species present.

**Analytical methods.** Gut volume proportions were estimated with the [[Points-Volume Method]] (Hynes 1950; Naiman 1974), in which food categories were scored on a 0–10 scale and converted to percentages. Animal prey were also quantified by the [[Number Method]], which expresses each taxon as a percentage of all organisms eaten. Gut length was measured from esophagus to anus on a relaxed alimentary canal.

## Key Findings

- **Dominant diet:** The [[Algae-Detritus Mixture]] comprised at least 80% of gut volume on all dates except a short period in January and February. It was the principal food throughout the year.
- **Limited invertebrate intake:** Invertebrates and [[Ruppia]] (ditch grass) were important for only a few weeks per year. Traces appeared at other times. Gut-volume means were reported as 22% for animals and 5% for *Ruppia*, though the table values are partly garbled in the scan and should be verified against the original.
- **Prey composition (1,171 animal items):** [[Chironomid Larvae]] made up 36% of animals eaten and occurred in 11% of fish. [[Copepods]] made up 40% of animals eaten but occurred in only 3% of fish, reflecting cluster feeding by a few individuals. [[Ostracods]] made up 15% of animals eaten (2% occurrence). *Ochthebius* made up 4% (2% occurrence). Cyprinodon eggs and invertebrate eggs each appeared in under 1% of animals.
- **Ontogenetic shift:** Fish under 15 mm often ate some invertebrates, whereas larger fish rarely did. This change was slight at this site and coincided with a shift in habitat.
- **Gut length increases with size:**

| 20 | 1.9 ± 0.50 | 0.08 | 85 |
| 30 | 2.8 ± 0.63 | 0.21 | 85 |
| 40 | 2.9 ± 0.50 | 0.39 | 18 |

  Gut volume was highly correlated with fish size (r = 0.85). Gut length also increased with relative gut volume and degree of intestinal coiling.
- **Interpretation:** The author links the increasing gut length to better digestion and use of the algal-detritus diet as fish grow. A similar ontogenetic increase in GL/SL was reported for [[Cyprinodon Bifasciatus]] (Arnold 1972).
- **Scarcity-driven feeding:** In outdoor pools, the Amargosa pupfish actively selected mosquito larvae and other small invertebrates. When these became scarce, it switched to extensive bottom foraging, similar to what occurred at Tecopa Bore.
- **Comparison with the mainstream:** Fish from the [[Amargosa River]] mainstream near Tecopa, sampled in autumn 1970 (n = 60), had a diet of 78% algae-detritus with many diatoms. Invertebrates were scarce and insignificant at that time of year, so the diets were similar.
- **Conservation relevance:** The author argues that understanding the basic ecology of desert fishes is needed to support conservation of endemic species threatened by introduced fish and habitat alteration. Dietary plasticity and the algal-based diet of this population are relevant to that goal.
