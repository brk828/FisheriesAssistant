# Monitoring Native Fish Populations on the Coronado Forest and Nature Conservancy Preserves in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This May 1993 report by David F. Gori of [[The Nature Conservancy]] (Arizona chapter), submitted to the [[Coronado National Forest]], describes a seine-based survey and the establishment of permanent monitoring stations for native fishes in Arizona's southeastern desert streams. Its goals were to determine the status of fish populations in target drainages, establish long-term monitoring stations, survey native fishes in the upper [[San Pedro River]] of Sonora, and summarize other Coronado Forest sites. Native fishes in Arizona have declined sharply: of 31 original native species, one is extinct and four extirpated, and most remaining species are listed or candidates for federal listing. The report frames monitoring as an "early-warning" system for threats from habitat loss and [[Exotic Fish Species]].

## Background & Description

Native fish in Arizona were historically diverse (31 species at European settlement). Since the early 1900s, one species has gone extinct and four have been extirpated. Of the 27 remaining species, 14 are listed as endangered or threatened by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and 7 are federal candidates. Declines stem from two main drivers: loss of aquatic habitat through surface flow diversion, impoundment and groundwater pumping, and introduction of exotic fishes. Exotics compete with natives for food and nesting space and prey on young natives, which are poor at avoiding predation. Native communities occasionally resist invasion, but this is rare.

The study area covers target streams owned by the USFS or TNC, or with managed watershed portions: Bass, Hot Springs and Double R creeks in the Santa Rita Mountains; Turkey and O'Donnell creeks in the Canelo Hills; Redfield Canyon; and Sonoita Creek, a preserve stream in the Patagonia Mountains. Double R Creek is on TNC land but its watershed is managed by the [[Bureau of Land Management]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sampling followed a standardized protocol. Each monitoring transect spanned up to 100 m of stream habitat and was classified into cascade, rapid, riffle, run, glide, pool and backwater types, based on current velocity, disturbance and substrate, using a modified [[USFS Habitat Classification]]. Pools were lumped into one category. Fish were captured with a 4 x 10 ft seine (1/8-inch mesh), which excluded fry. Adults and juveniles were distinguished by size thresholds from [[Sexual Maturity Size]] data.

At Sonoita Creek, the authors used [[Depletion Sampling]] (repeated seining in 10 m segments until catch fell to 10% or less of the initial haul), a method that yields better abundance estimates but is more time-consuming. Shallow, rough, or cobble-filled habitats could not be seined, which may bias relative frequency estimates. Habitat data (length, width, depth, photographs) were collected to support future modeling of fish abundance against habitat variables.

Key site observations:
- **Bass Creek:** Perennial for about 40 m above the Hot Springs confluence; the upper stretch was dry in September 1991. Dominated by runs, glides and pools.
- **Double R Creek:** Perennial reach under 2 miles, with only two species.
- **Hot Springs Creek:** Perennial throughout the surveyed stretch, with native fish widely distributed.
- **Redfield Creek:** Perennial flow begins near Sycamore Canyon. A boulder barrier immediately above station 1 blocks fish movement.
- **Turkey Creek:** Water quality was poor in 1991 after several years of drought, with dried pools and an apparent oil slick on larger pools.
- **Sonoita Creek:** Dominated by riffles and runs, with pool habitat limited to station 3 in the preserve.

## Key Findings

**Arizona-wide context**
- 31 native species historically; 1 extinct, 4 extirpated, 27 remaining.
- 14 species federally listed as endangered or threatened; 7 candidates.

**Bass Creek (1991)**
- [[Longfin Dace]] was most abundant, making up 45% to 85% of captures across the five stations.
- [[Gila Chub]] was second most common, though its abundance was probably overestimated by sampling bias toward pools.
- [[Desert Sucker]] made up 15% of catches and [[Sonora Sucker]] about 3%.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] was taken from pools in 1989 but was not recorded in 1991. It is established in Redus tank in the west fork and may invade during high flows, but no evidence of breeding was found. The authors call it one of only a handful of Arizona sites without established exotics.

**Double R Creek (1991)**
- Two species: longfin dace (63%) and speckled dace (47%) of total captures, with longfin dace increasing upstream.
- Chub and adult suckers were absent, likely due to a lack of deep glide and pool habitat.

**Hot Springs Creek**
- Four native species were common. Gila chub was extremely rare (juveniles only, station 2). The authors attribute this to fewer pools and different riparian conditions.
- Longfin dace was most common (49% to 75%), and speckled dace reached 12% to 28%.
- The desert sucker juvenile-to-adult ratio was 17:1, compared with 2:1 in Bass Creek.

**Redfield Creek**
- Gila chub was most abundant, with a relative frequency of roughly 43% to 60%, depending on the station.
- A barrier-isolated, perennial 0.5-mile reach holds no fish. The authors recommend transplanting Gila chub there to create a refuge from exotics.
- [[Green Sunfish]] occurred here, but scouring floods keep its frequency low.

**Turkey Creek**
- Only a single longfin dace was found in 1991–1992 despite abundant Gila chub in 1989. Adult and juvenile chub were caught in 1991 on private land, but none were found in 1992. The authors suggest the species may be extirpated.

**O'Donnell Creek**
- Green sunfish is as abundant as Gila chub here, a serious concern.

**Sonoita Creek**
- Longfin dace (27%) and speckled dace (24%) were most common, with desert sucker at 18%.
- [[Red Shiner]] (the only exotic fish) occurred only in the pool at station 3, likely introduced as bait fish. Floods are expected to limit its spread.

**Sonora Survey (San Pedro watershed, 1990–1992)**
- Nine sites surveyed; 6 supported native fish. Five sites were dominated by natives, while four downstream sites were dominated by exotics.
- Gila chub was rediscovered at two spring-fed cienegas, [[Los Fresnos]] and [[La Cienegita]], although it was thought extirpated from Mexico. Los Fresnos supported a healthy chub population with multiple age classes and no exotics. La Cienegita had only three adult chub, and was dominated by [[Mosquitofish]] and green sunfish.
- Desert sucker was recorded from the San Pedro watershed in Mexico for the first time. Sonora sucker and loach minnow, previously reported, were not found.

**Other Coronado Forest Sites**
- Native fish populations at Bear Creek, Sabino Creek and Sycamore Canyon appeared healthy and showed recent reproduction. No recent data existed for Cave Creek.

**Recommendations and Planned Actions**
- Transplant native fish, especially Gila chub, into the fishless Redfield Creek reach.
- Continue monitoring at all native-fish sites, with emphasis on Redfield and O'Donnell creeks, Sabino Creek, and La Cienegita.
- The Arizona Game and Fish Department will survey Turkey Creek for remnant chub and assess habitat for reintroduction, and will monitor Sabino and Sycamore creeks.
- TNC will monitor Cave and Bear creeks in 1993 and begin pool-habitat monitoring at Sycamore Canyon to assess sediment effects.
- Long-term data will be used to build a model predicting fish abundance from habitat characteristics, to distinguish natural changes from human-caused or exotic-driven ones.
