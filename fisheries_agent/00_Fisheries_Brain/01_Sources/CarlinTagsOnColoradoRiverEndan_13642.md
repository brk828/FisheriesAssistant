# Carlin Tags on Colorado River Endangered Fishes

## Executive Summary
This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, part of the [[Colorado River Fisheries Project]], documents the use of **Carlin tags** to mark 881 individuals of four endangered/threatened fish species — [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — in the Upper Colorado River Basin during 1979 and 1980. The study evaluated Carlin tags as a low-injury alternative to Floy anchor tags for tracking movement patterns in these species across the Upper Colorado, Green, Yampa, White, Gunnison, Lower Colorado, and Little Colorado Rivers. Of 881 tagged fish, 38 were recaptured, providing valuable movement data, including one Colorado squawfish that traveled 147 km between rivers in one year. The tags demonstrated minimal tissue inflammation and necrosis compared to conventional injection tags, establishing Carlin tags as the standard marking method for Colorado River endangered fishes.

## Background & Description
This paper is primarily methodological rather than morphological, but it includes species size ranges relevant to tag application:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): 300–900 mm total length (TL); tagged with larger "dangler" tags due to size.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) and **Gila sp.** (including [[Bonytail Chub]]): 150–400 mm TL.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): 500–600 mm TL.

**Tag specifications:**
- **Dangler tag**: 16 mm × 6 mm, used on fish >150 mm TL.
- **Fingerling tag**: 8 mm × 3 mm, used on smaller fish.
- Both tags are oval-shaped, plastic, ~0.8 mm thick, color-coded by river/tributary, and serially numbered with a printed return address on the reverse.

**Tagging method:**
- Tags anchored to a doubled 40–45 cm polyethylene thread via a friction hitch, with a figure-eight knot tied 1–2 cm from the tag to prevent chafing.
- Two Sharps no. 6 sewing needles attached to thread ends and passed parallel through the dorsal musculature at the base of the dorsal fin, spanning two interneural rays, 1–2 cm apart and 1–2 cm below the dorsal fin base.
- Secured with a surgeon's knot pulled snug enough to slightly pucker the skin; thread ends trimmed to within 2 mm of the knot.
- Application time: 1–3 minutes per fish using a two-person technique (one restraining the fish in a wet towel/water tub, one applying the tag).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The four species discussed inhabit the mainstem channels and major tributaries of the [[Colorado River Basin]], a large, historically turbid, and hydrologically dynamic river system in the southwestern United States. Key ecological/habitat notes drawn from the study:

- Study rivers included the **Upper Colorado, Green, Yampa, White, Gunnison, Lower Colorado, and Little Colorado Rivers** — spanning multiple sub-basins and demonstrating the wide-ranging habitat use of these species.
- Movement data recovered from recaptures reveal contrasting mobility patterns among species:
  - **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** exhibited substantial long-distance movement, including a 147 km inter-river migration (Green to Yampa River) within one year, and a radio-tagged individual moving 570 km in 4.5 months.
  - **[[Humpback Chub]]** showed strong site fidelity, with all eight recaptured individuals remaining within 2 km of their release site even up to 9 months post-tagging — consistent with known canyon-dwelling, sedentary behavior of this species.
- The [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] served as a supplemental source of hatchery-reared *P. lucius* (~1500 individuals) tagged and released into the wild, indicating early conservation aquaculture efforts for this endangered cyprinid.
- The paper implicitly underscores the endangered status of all four species within the basin, reflecting broader concerns about habitat alteration (e.g., damming, flow regulation) affecting native Colorado River fish populations.

## Key Findings
- **881 total fish tagged** in 1979–1980: 143 *P. lucius* (Colorado squawfish), 695 *G. cypha* and *Gila* sp. (humpback/bonytail chub), and 43 *X. texanus* (razorback sucker).
- **38 fish recaptured**, most showing little movement shortly after release, but several yielding significant long-distance movement data.
- Notable recapture movements:
  - One *P. lucius*: 147 km (Green River → Yampa River) after 1 year.
  - One radio-tagged *P. lucius*: 570 km in 4.5 months in the Green River; later recaptured near release site after 3 months.
  - One *P. lucius* on the Upper Colorado River: 32 km upstream after 6.5 months.
  - Two *P. lucius* near Potash, Utah: recaptured by anglers 25 km upstream and released alive.
  - All 8 recaptured *G. cypha*: within 2 km of release site, up to 9 months later — indicating limited home range.
- **Tag performance**: Carlin tags caused minimal inflammation/necrosis compared to Floy FD-67 tags, which are prone to causing open wounds, secondary infection, slowed growth (Carline & Brynildson 1972), and excessive tag loss (Sackett & Hein 1979).
- **Healing observation**: After 1–2 months, the tag attachment site was overgrown by epidermis, forming a closed, pimple-like protuberance rather than an open wound.
- **Method refinements** identified over the two-year study:
  - Use of double independent loops to prevent thread wear and tag loss (one *P. lucius* tag was lost after a year due to thread wearing thin).
  - Recommendation to use a square knot with glued, closely-clipped thread ends to prevent irritation from excessively long thread ends.
  - Critical importance of needle placement well below the skin line and dorsal fin base to prevent tearing and tag loss.
  - Some recaptured fish showed algal growth or mineral deposition on tags, but this was not considered detrimental.
- **Adoption**: Carlin tags became the standard tagging method for endangered/threatened Colorado River fishes, used by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]], [[Museum of Northern Arizona]], [[BIO/WEST Inc]], and [[Northwest Fisheries Inc]].
- **Historical context**: Carlin tags were originally developed for salmon smolts ([[Carlin 1955]]) and previously applied to [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Coho Salmon]], and [[Cutthroat Trout]], establishing precedent for their low-injury profile prior to this application on endangered Colorado River fishes.
