# Hydrographic History and Relict Fishes of the North-Central Great Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Memoir of the [[California Academy of Sciences]] (Volume VII, published February 8, 1974) by Carl L. Hubbs, Robert Rush Miller, and Laura C. Hubbs reconstructs the Quaternary hydrography of 21 enclosed (endorheic) pluvial-lake basins in the north-central [[Great Basin]] of Nevada and one basin in Utah, and documents the native fishes that survive in their isolated remnant waters. The authors estimate that the lakes covered a combined 10,690 or 9,976 sq. km at maximum level (19.8 or 18.5 percent of the 54,028 sq. km drainage area), and that only four native fish species survive: two widespread minnows (*Rhinichthys osculus* and *Gila bicolor*) and two endemic relicts (*Relictus solitarius* and *Crenichthys nevadae*). The report pairs field reconnaissance, interviews with early settlers, topographic and altimeter analysis, and detailed meristic and morphometric studies of the surviving populations.

## Background & Description

The study area covers the contiguous drainage basins of 20 pluvial lakes in central and eastern Nevada plus [[Lake Pine]] (Wah Wah) in Utah, which is surrounded by the [[Bonneville Basin]]. The region was disrupted by [[Basin and Range]] faulting during late Quaternary time. The authors had earlier summarized the regional hydrography in Hubbs and Miller (1948b), and this report offers a more intensively documented treatment. Field work spanned 1915 to 1972, with major trips in 1934, 1938, 1942, and 1965, and the authors used 1:250,000 and 15-minute USGS topographic maps, Paulin precision altimeters, and planimeter measurements.

The paper provides detailed accounts of each basin's shorelines, outlet passes, depth, area, and native fish. It also treats systematics: size, coloration, scale structure, morphometry, sexual dimorphism, and meristics (fin rays, vertebrae, scale rows, gill rakers, pharyngeal teeth, and lateral-line pores). Most of the content is hydrographic and systematic detail; the document also includes a historical quotation from Angel (1881) about "bottomless fountains" near Wells that hold small fish found nowhere else on the continent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Pluvial lakes in the region reached depths of roughly 23 to 115 m, and total water storage was estimated at more than 100 times the present volume. The authors argue that cooler temperatures and reduced evaporation alone cannot explain the Pleistocene lakes, and that increased precipitation must also have been involved. Citing Snyder and Langbein (1962), they note that an increase in precipitation from 12 to 20 inches combined with a reduction in evaporation from 44 to 31 inches would restore a Pleistocene lake.

Native fishes persist almost exclusively in valley-floor springs, usually along fault lines, while mountain springs and canyon streams are fishless because rare torrential floods wash fish onto the playas. Several springs are hot (Potts Ranch at 32 to 34 °C, Dianas Punch Bowl at 37 to 39 °C), and the dace in these springs are dwarfed. The authors attribute the extreme depauperation of the fauna to increasing aridity, the isolation of basins, and flash-flood extirpation, and they note that salmonids, catostomids, cottids and other families that likely occurred in pluvial times are now extirpated.

Introduced species are widespread, including rainbow and brook trout, carp, goldfish, [[Largemouth Bass]], Sacramento perch, and the [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*), which has displaced *Relictus* at some sites.

## Key Findings

- **Hydrography:** Lake Gilbert, Lake Diamond, Lake Newark, Lake Clover, and Lake Railroad had Lahontan or Colorado connections at various pluvial stages. The authors conclude that Lake Newark likely discharged into the Humboldt system, based on fish evidence and a wave-cut terrace near Beck Pass. They reject the hypothesis that Lake Franklin overflowed into Lake Clover, citing a divide 13.4 m above the highest beach gravel.
- **"Hot Creek Lake" and related lakes:** The authors question the existence of Hot Creek Lake and other Snyder et al. (1964) lakes, and they reduce the Lake Steptoe estimate from a claimed 110 km length to about 42 km.
- **Species count:** Of the 21 basins, 11 are fishless, 7 contain one native species, and only 3 (Diamond, Clover, and Railroad) contain two. Two species are relicts: *Relictus solitarius* (the relict dace, in the [[Franklin-Gale]] and [[Waring-Steptoe]] systems, covering 14,682 sq. km or 27 percent of the study area) and *Crenichthys nevadae* (in the Railroad basin).
- **Endemic subspecies:** Four endemic speckled dace subspecies were described or formalized, including *R. o. reliquus* (Grass Valley), *R. o. oligoporus* and *R. o. lethoporus* (Clover basin), and the Lahontan form *R. o. robustus*. Endemic tui chubs include *G. b. newarkensis*, *G. b. euchila*, and *G. b. isolata*.
- **Extinction:** *R. o. reliquus* was almost certainly extirpated from Grass Valley, based on a 1969 search and testimony from Ira La Rivers, so the subspecies was described posthumously.
- **Barbel and morphology:** The barbel is absent in isolated spring forms (*R. o. reliquus*, *oligoporus*, *lethoporus*) and present in the Humboldt and Diamond populations. Spring fishes show reduced lateral-line pores, small rounded fins, and oblique terminal mouths suited to midwater feeding.
- **Meristics:** Dorsal rays are modally 8 in *Rhinichthys* and *Gila*, anal rays 7 in *Rhinichthys* and *Relictus*, and caudal rays 19 in all three genera. Pharyngeal tooth formulas are 1, 4–4, 1 for *R. osculus*, 5–4 for *G. b. obesa* (versus 5–5 in lacustrine *G. b. pectinifer*), and 4–4 for *Relictus*. Temperature shows no evident correlation with vertebral or ray counts.
- **Sex ratios:** In the Diamond system, the male-to-female ratio ranges from 25 per 100 females (Big Shipley Spring) to 124 per 100 (Potts Ranch).
- **Limited sympatry:** Only three isolated springs contain two native species together, including Birch Ranch (Diamond) and Warm Springs (Independence Valley, Clover basin).
- **Area-richness correlation:** The coefficient of correlation between drainage-basin area and number of species is 0.56 ± 0.10, consistent with island biogeography. The authors note that the 38 fishless basins omitted from the regional table are all in the arid southern Great Basin.
- **Introduced fishes:** A 1951 introduction of *R. o. robustus* from Diamond Valley into Ruby Marsh, and a 1950 stocking from Tea Creek, are documented, and the authors suspect that many "native" populations reported by locals were introductions.
- **Research recommendations:** The authors call for electrophoresis, karyotype analysis, breeding and transfer experiments, and water-chemistry analysis. They note that no Cenozoic fossil fishes have been found in the 21 basins.
