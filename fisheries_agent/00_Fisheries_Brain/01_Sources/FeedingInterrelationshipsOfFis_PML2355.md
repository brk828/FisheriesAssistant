# Feeding Interrelationships of Fishes of Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 Master of Science thesis by Donald Clifford Schreiber, from Arizona State University, examines the food habits of seven native fish species in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, sampled over a one-year period (1975–76). Food availability was estimated from benthic invertebrate samples and stream drift, and stomach contents were analysed by volumetric point estimation. The study found that [[Ephemeroptera]] (mayfly) nymphs were the predominant food of the carnivorous fishes and the dominant invertebrate in both benthos and drift. When mayfly abundance dropped in July, three fish species shifted to alternate prey, while three did not visibly respond. The author concluded that interspecific feeding competition is probably minimal because invertebrate food is abundant and fishes are spatially segregated.

## Background & Description

The thesis addresses the decline of native fishes in arid western North America, where habitat alteration threatens many species. Aravaipa Creek was notable because seven native species still coexisted there, with only one non-native species ([[Green Sunfish]] is named in the text as *Lepomis cyanellus*, a small established population) present. Earlier work on the system included [[Barber and Minckley]] (1966), who described the ichthyofauna, and studies of [[Spikedace]] biology and [[Longfin Dace]] ecology.

The seven native species studied were:
- [[Colorado River Chub]] (*Gila robusta*): too rare for adequate sampling; diet data came from museum specimens collected 1968–1978 and provided by James Burton.
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*)
- [[Gila Coarse-Scaled Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)
- [[Gila Mountain-Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*)

Of these, the [[Gila Mountain-Sucker]] was the only herbivore, and the [[Longfin Dace]] was a facultative omnivore. The remaining five species were carnivorous.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study reach and habitat.** The creek rises between the Pinaleño and Galiuro mountains and flows more than 100 km, mostly dry, before entering the [[San Pedro River]] at about 550 m elevation. The channel consists of riffles (about 10–15 cm deep, 1.5–4 m wide) separated by broad, shallow, braided reaches over sand and gravel. Pools (0.5–1.5 m deep) form only where canyon walls or obstacles deflect currents. Mean discharge from January 1975 to January 1976 was 0.33 m³/second (range 0.05–3.1), during a drought. The study site near the inflow of Turkey Creek was a riffle about 60 m long with a pool at a canyon wall.

**Vegetation and climate.** Riparian vegetation includes [[Fremont Cottonwood]], [[Arizona Sycamore]], and willows, with [[Mesquite]] on upper terraces and saguaro, cholla and palo verde in the Lower Sonoran Desert downstream. [[Watercress]] was the only important aquatic vascular plant. Air temperatures range from below 5 °C to above 40 °C; precipitation ranged from 19.4 to 54.9 cm at Klondyke, with most rain in July and August.

**Sampling methods.** Fishes were collected with 9.5 m seines (6.4 mm mesh) and electrofishing gear over five two-day periods (April 1975, July 1975, October 1975, January 1976, plus a February sampling). Benthos was sampled with a Surber sampler (0.09 m², two samples per period, gravel and cobble). Drift was collected with a 500 µm net sampled for 15 minutes beside a 28-hour fish collection period. Stomach contents were scored by [[Hynes Point Method]] volumetric estimation, with each stomach totalling a maximum of 20 points (10% per point).

## Key Findings

- **Food inventory:** 75 potential or actual food items were recorded across benthos, drift, and stomachs; 69 (92%) were invertebrates. Vertebrates (three species) were eaten only by the Colorado River chub.
- **Trophic patterns:** Three trophic groups were identified: herbivory ([[Gila Mountain-Sucker]]), omnivory ([[Longfin Dace]]), and carnivory (remaining five species).
- **Dominant prey:** Ephemeropteran nymphs comprised 57% or more of stomach contents in five of the six species analysed quantitatively. Baetid mayflies (*Baetis*, *Centroptilum*, and related genera) were the most abundant and most important group.
- **Benthos–drift relationship:** Mayfly drift and benthos numbers were loosely related, but biomass in drift and benthos showed no clear relationship. About 65% of drift (excluding non-animal material) came from the benthos.
- **Diet overlap:** The Colorado River chub had the most unique diet, with 28.6% of its items found in no other fish species. Overlap between most other species ranged from roughly 12–80%.
- **Seasonal shift:** In July, when mayfly biomass fell and average nymph size dropped, [[Spikedace]] increased use of drifting material, terrestrial and winged insects; [[Loach Minnow]] switched to rheophilic trichopterans; and [[Gila Coarse-Scaled Sucker]] increased turbellarian consumption. [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], and [[Gila Mountain-Sucker]] showed little change. Total stomach volumes fell in all species in July and remained low until October.
- **Conclusion:** Interspecific competition for food is probably minimal because of abundant invertebrate food and spatial segregation of habitat. Fishes showed opportunistic feeding and spatial and behavioural partitioning, which allowed exploitation of the food base without strong competition.
