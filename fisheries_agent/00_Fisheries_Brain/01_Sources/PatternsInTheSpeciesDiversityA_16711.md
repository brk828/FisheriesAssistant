# Fish Community Diversity and Composition in Tropical and Restored River Systems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document contains three items: a fragment of a reference list (Hawaiian goby and Guam streamfish literature), the full research article on the fish community of the [[Lacanja River]] in Chiapas, Mexico, and the opening (abstract and introduction) of a study on fish diversity after flooding in a reclaimed [[Upper Mississippi River]] wetland.

The main study sampled 12 sites along the 120 km Lacanja River over eight visits in 1996–1997 (four dry-season, four wet-season). It collected 44 species (41 native) and 5,490 fish. Fish composition divided the river into lower, middle and upper zones, separated by waterfalls. Diversity rose downstream, and each downstream zone gained species without losing any upstream species. Seasonal change was surprisingly small.

The Upper Mississippi study reports that flooding increased fish species richness in a newly created wetland.

## Background & Description

**Lacanja River (Chiapas, Mexico).** The river is about 120 km long, drains approximately 800 km², and falls from about 450 m at the headwaters to 100 m at the mouth. It forms the boundary between the [[Montes Azules Biosphere Reserve]] and Lacantun reserve, which protects it from major human impacts. The climate is warm and humid, with 2000–3000 mm mean annual rainfall and mean annual temperatures of 23–27 °C. The rainy season runs from May to January. Riparian vegetation is mainly tropical forested wetland with some upland [[Tropical Rain Forest River]] woodland.

**Study design.** Twelve sites (A–L) covered headwaters, the main channel, the river mouth, major and small tributaries, [[Floodplain Lake]] types (clearwater and blackwater), and riparian wetlands. Sampling used baited hooks, dip nets, beach seines, cast nets and gill nets (25–76 mm mesh), and continued at each site until no new species appeared. Specimens were deposited in the ECOSUR ichthyological collection. Presence–absence data from 24 pooled site samples were clustered using the [[Jaccard Coefficient]] and [[UPGMA Clustering]].

**Upper Mississippi River restoration.** The [[Riverlands Environmental Demonstration Area]] is a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers wetland project designed to offset habitat loss from navigation development. A new dam raised water about 4 m across a 3.2 km reach, creating a contiguous backwater and a wetland mosaic on 486 ha of former farmland. Fish surveys began in March 1993 but were halted by flooding in May 1993.

**Reference list fragment.** The references include a study of the native goby [[Lentipes Concolor]] in Hawaiian streams (Nishimoto and Kuamoo 1991), a thesis on habitat use by a tropical oceanic streamfish assemblage on Guam (Parham 1995), an ecosystem classification of tropical Pacific inland waters (Polhemus et al. 1992), and a Guam soil survey (Young 1988). ## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Lacanja River is described as one of the few relatively undegraded rivers in southern Mexico. The introduction notes that many rivers in the region face deforestation, oil and mineral extraction, hydroelectric development, pollution, overfishing and exotic species. The authors argue that baseline data are urgently needed for impact assessment and conservation planning.

Longitudinal patterns in temperate North American rivers are commonly driven by river size, habitat heterogeneity and barriers to movement. In tropical rivers, seasonal shifts in precipitation and flow are the main drivers of change, often through changes in water chemistry, food supply and predation.

Several 8–12 m waterfalls impede fish movement within the Lacanja system. Natural dams of bedrock or fallen trees trapping sand and limestone support dense fish aggregations and wetland vegetation. The mouth is structurally complex, with islands, sinuous channels, riffles, runs, pools and dams.

The Upper Mississippi wetland is a managed system. Water levels are controlled by ditches, and the adjoining land was converted from agriculture to wetland ponds and wet-mesic prairie.

## Key Findings

**Lacanja River: fauna and composition**
- 44 species (41 native) from 17 families and 5,490 fish were collected. Compared with earlier work, a 1996 preliminary survey recorded 29 species in nine families, and a literature review had listed only 10 species in three families.
- Cichlidae was the most species-rich family (18 species, 2,327 fish). Characidae contributed 2,117 fish, Poeciliidae 279 fish (five native species) and Ariidae 270 fish (three native species). The remaining 13 families had one or two species each and made up less than 10% of catches.
- The three most abundant and widespread taxa were [[Brycon Guatemalensis]] (1,212 fish, 22.1%), [[Astyanax Sp]] (Characidae) and [[Cichlasoma Intermedium]] (583 fish, 10.6%). Together they made up 49% of the catch. Fourteen species occurred at more than 50% of sites, 12 at 33–50%, and 18 at less than 25%.
- Non-native species include [[Oreochromis Niloticus]] (African, likely from aquaculture), [[Ctenopharyngodon Idella]] (Asian) and Cichlasoma urophthalmus, which is outside its natural range. The authors identify at least three non-native species.

**Longitudinal zonation**
- Fish composition divided the river into three zones:
  - **Lower zone (sites A–E):** about 38 species, 19 of them found nowhere else in the river. It was separated from upstream by the 15 m [[El Hippy Waterfall]], a major barrier.
  - **Middle zone (sites F, G, H):** 24 species. Three were unique to this zone (Atherinella sp., Cichlasoma pasionis, C. urophthalmus), all rare.
  - **Upper zone (sites I, J, K):** 16 species. Only Xiphophorus helleri was unique to the zone.
- Diversity was lowest in the upper zone and highest in the lower zone. All upper-zone species occurred downstream, and nearly all middle-zone species also occurred in the lower zone.
- Community change from upstream to downstream was driven entirely by species addition, with no replacement. The authors propose that waterfalls act as "filters" that allow only part of the fauna to pass upstream, so that less than half of the river's fish fauna reached the upper zone. This pattern is unusual: previous Middle American studies covering shorter reaches found substantial turnover, and temperate studies report additive patterns only over reaches under about 25 km.

**Seasonal patterns**
- Seasonal differences were surprisingly small compared with other neotropical rivers. Sites B, C and D showed the largest shifts.
- Species diversity was slightly higher in the dry season, and total catch was substantially higher. [[Brycon Guatemalensis]] was more dominant during the wet season, but most species varied little in relative abundance.
- Several species appeared in only one season, but none was common. The authors could not explain the stability between seasons.

**Upper Mississippi: flood effects on fish diversity**
- Before the 1993 flood, wetland habitats yielded 8 fish species (June 1993). In August 1993, after the flood crest, the same sites yielded 26 species.
- In June 1994, 33 species were collected. By August 1994 the number had declined to 16.
- The authors conclude that flooding caused at least a temporary increase in diversity.
