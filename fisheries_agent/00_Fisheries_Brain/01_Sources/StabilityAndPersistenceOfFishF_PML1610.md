# Stability and persistence of fish faunas and assemblages

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests the long-term stability and persistence of summer fish assemblages in three freshwater streams of the [[White River]] and [[Red River]] drainages: [[Piney Creek]] (Arkansas), [[Brier Creek]] (Oklahoma), and the [[Kiamichi River]] (Oklahoma). The authors distinguish *persistence* (qualitative presence or absence of species) from *stability* (quantitative change in abundance), following [[Connell and Sousa 1983]]. Collections span 14 yr and six surveys for Piney Creek, 17 yr and five surveys for Brier Creek, and three surveys from 1981 to 1986 for the Kiamichi River.

The authors conclude that whole-stream faunas were persistent and stable in all three streams. Stability at individual locations was variable. The more benign Piney Creek and Kiamichi River had more stable faunas than the physically harsh Brier Creek. The work extends [[Ross Et Al 1985]] with additional years of data.

## Background & Description

**Study systems**

- **Piney Creek** is an upland Ozark tributary of the White River. It has permanent spring-fed base flow, a rubble-gravel-sand substratum, generally clear water, and a known fauna of 50 species.
- **Brier Creek** is a prairie-margin tributary of Lake Texoma and the Red River. It lacks permanent flow in its headwaters and has a known fauna of 30 species. (The OCR text reads "360," which is corrected here to match the later statement "30 known species.")
- **Kiamichi River** is a medium-sized upland river in the Ouachita Mountains with a known fauna of 98 species, many of them rare.

**Sampling**

Fish were collected by [[Seine Sampling]] over 200–400 m reaches for 1.0–1.5 h per station. Net mesh and seine size varied among streams and years. The same reaches were sampled each year, with one exception: Brier Creek Station 5 was shifted about 200 m downstream in 1985.

**Terminology**

- *Assemblages* are local, potentially interacting groups of fish at a single station.
- *Faunas* are pooled data for all stations in a stream at one time, representing the watershed-level fish community.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Harshness gradient.** Piney Creek and the Kiamichi River are classed as benign. Piney Creek's lowest recorded dissolved oxygen was 7 ppm, and its temperature extremes were 6 and 31 °C. In the Kiamichi River, dissolved oxygen never fell below 6.4 ppm and the highest recorded temperature was 28.5 °C, although the most extreme headwater station had zero flow on an average of 32 d per year. Brier Creek is physically harsher and fluctuating. Its headwater stations (2 and 3) are dry in low-rainfall summers, while its lower mainstream (5 and 6) retains pools.

**Disturbances during the study period.**

- Brier Creek experienced a severe drought in 1980 and an Oct. 1981 flood that deposited large trees at Station 5.
- Piney Creek experienced a catastrophic flood in Dec. 1982, after which its fish community recovered rapidly.
- Both creeks had major floods during the study period.

**Theoretical relevance.** If stream fish assemblages are unstable or driven mainly by stochastic events, [[Equilibrium-Based Community Theory]] may not apply to them ([[Grossman Et Al 1982]]). The authors suggest that the majority of assemblages here show enough stability that equilibrium models might be applied to typical stream reaches, with caution.

## Key Findings

**1. Whole-stream faunas were persistent and stable**

- Abundant species did not disappear from any stream, and no originally absent or rare species became abundant, with two exceptions described below.
- Pooled-fauna rank concordance of the 10 most common species (Kendall's W, [[Kendall's W]]) was significant in all three streams:

| Stream | Years | W | χ² | P |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brier Creek | 1969–86 | 0.56 | 25.92 | <0.01 |
| Piney Creek | 1972–86 | 0.752 | 40.58 | <0.001 |
| Kiamichi River | 1981–86 | 0.859 | 23.19 | <0.01 |

**2. Faunal similarity was highest in the benign streams**

- For Piney Creek, [[Morisita's Index]] ranged 0.72–0.99 between consecutive surveys, and [[Percent Similarity Index]] (PSI) ranged 0.60–0.86.
- For Brier Creek, consecutive-interval Morisita values ranged 0.57–0.83 and PSI values 0.51–0.70. Four of 10 possible Brier Creek comparisons fell below the lowest Piney Creek value (0.66).
- Analysis of variance showed significantly higher Morisita (F = 7.69, P < 0.05) and PSI (F = 10.91, P < 0.01) values in Piney Creek than in Brier Creek.
- The Kiamichi River's three comparisons were similar to Piney Creek's.

**3. Species-level changes**

- *Notropis pilsbryi* was the most abundant (or tied for most abundant) species in every Piney Creek survey. *N. telescopus* was second in three surveys and never lower than fourth.
- No Brier Creek species was consistently first or second. *Notropis boops* was most abundant in three of five surveys but fell to fourth in 1981.
- [[Fathead Minnow]] was the seventh most abundant species in the 1969 survey. It was absent from two of the four later surveys and represented by only 11 specimens in the two years it was collected.
- [[Spotted Bass]] declined sharply, from 136 specimens in 1969 to one individual across the other four surveys.
- Of 50 known Piney Creek species, only nine were absent in two or more of five surveys, and six of those cases involved seven specimens in total. The largest change was [[Golden Redhorse]], absent in the first two surveys and well represented later, a pattern consistent with its upstream spawning migrations.
- In Brier Creek, 11 species produced no specimens in two or more of five surveys. Eight records of those species totaled 29 specimens.
- In the Kiamichi River, 24 species were absent in one of three surveys and 12 were absent in two of three. All non-persistent species were rare when collected.

**4. Individual assemblages were variable**

- Assemblage similarity was assessed using Morisita's and PSI indices across consecutive surveys.
- Mean values across all intervals were:

| Stream | Mean Morisita | Mean PSI |
|---|---|---|
| Piney Creek | 0.74 | 0.60 |
| Kiamichi River | 0.78 | 0.62 |
| Brier Creek | 0.65 | 0.53 |

- Differences between Piney Creek and Brier Creek means were not significant (F = 1.55 for Morisita, F = 1.77 for PSI).
- **Rank concordance by station (Kendall's W):**
  - Piney Creek: all five stations significant (P < 0.01 to P < 0.001).
  - Brier Creek: four of five stations significant (P < 0.05).
  - Kiamichi River: three of six stations significant (Stations 2, 6, and 7). Station 3 was excluded because too few individuals were caught. There was no clear pattern related to longitudinal position or stream size.
- The Discussion reports significant concordance at 9 of 10 individual Piney and Brier stations, versus 5 of 16 in Ross et al. (1985). Part of this difference may reflect the switch from two-sample Spearman tests to a multiple-sample Kendall's W.

**5. Combined similarity summary (59 comparisons at individual stations)**

- Using Morisita's index when ≥0.92 and PSI otherwise, 46% of comparisons had similarity >0.60, 42% fell between 0.41 and 0.60, and 12% were ≤0.40.
- The authors judge that most midwestern stream assemblages show moderate to high stability at the 200–400 m reach scale.

**6. Low-similarity stations had identifiable causes**

- Station 3 on Brier Creek is an ephemeral tributary that often lacks water. Its assemblage depends on recolonization from downstream permanent pools.
- Station 6 on Brier Creek is downstream, near [[Lake Texoma]], and receives fish movement from the reservoir.
- Piney Creek and Brier Creek stations with low similarity generally coincided with known disturbances, though many causes remain speculative.

**7. Methodological and conceptual conclusions**

- Rank-concordance and similarity results from one station can be misleading if the sampling period is short. Multiple surveys across years are needed to estimate community variance and reduce the risk of drawing conclusions from one unusual year.
- Generalizations that North American stream fish assemblages are typically stable or typically unstable are not warranted. Long-term data from additional streams are still needed.
- Future work should investigate compensating mechanisms, including migration, altered fecundity, and altered recruitment following disturbance.
