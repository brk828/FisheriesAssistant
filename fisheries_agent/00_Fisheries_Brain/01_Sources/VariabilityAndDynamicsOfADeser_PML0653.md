# Variability and Dynamics of a Desert Stream Fish Community

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study analyzes a 30-year spring-sampling database (1963 onward, with analyses restricted to 1970/1975–2000) of the native fish assemblage of [[Aravaipa Creek]], a Sonoran Desert stream in Arizona. The authors asked whether long-term directional change occurred in community composition, whether exotic species were related to flow characteristics, and whether natural versus anthropogenic drivers could be separated. Community change was detected, but it was not driven by flash floods. Instead, it was linked to a rise in base flow and flow stability after 1978, and to the arrival and spread of [[Exotic Fish Species]] after the early 1980s. The authors conclude that isolation from large rivers and maintenance of natural flow regimes are important for conserving remnant native fish communities.

## Background & Description

Desert streams are highly variable systems. Flash floods scour channels and change temperature and turbidity, while droughts can raise water temperatures above 40°C and leave isolated, algae-choked pools. Native southwestern fishes have declined mainly because of habitat destruction, flow alteration, and invasion by exotic species (Minckley and Douglas 1991). Because short studies can mistake natural fluctuation for lasting change, the authors argue that long-term data are needed.

[[Aravaipa Creek]] (drainage area of about 1500 km²) is divided into upper, canyon, and lower reaches. Its lower reach is ephemeral for the lowermost 1 km at modal discharge and joins the [[San Pedro River]] at about 650 m elevation. Modal discharge is about 3.4 m³/s, and major floods are common. The historically native assemblage is species poor, rarely exceeding five species per locality. Seven native fishes remain, including two federally listed threatened species, [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), and five candidate species.

Sampling was conducted by [[W. L. Minckley]] and colleagues using 1.8 × 1.2 m (3.2 mm mesh) and 4.6 × 1.2 m (6.4 mm mesh) double-weighted seines. About 200 m of stream was seined per site, and collections were pooled within reaches. Spring (March–April) samples were used to reduce the influence of autumn/summer recruitment pulses.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime.** Mean daily discharge spanned more than four orders of magnitude over 34 years. Mean minimum flow rose nearly fourfold, from 0.08 m³/s (1965–1977) to 0.30 m³/s (1978–1999), and the seasonal cycle was dampened. The 1983 flood raised daily mean flow from 1.9 m³/s to 453.4 m³/s within two days, and flows returned to normal by the fifth day. Increased winter precipitation after 1978 and reduced farming and groundwater-dependent irrigation were proposed as causes.

**Exotic species.** Eight exotic species have been recorded in the lower reach, where exotics have been present since the early 1960s. Their share of the community has ranged from 0% to 30% depending on year. Species recorded in the 1980s include [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Common Carp]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]. The [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) was first found in 1990 in the lower reach and is implicated in declines of native southwestern fishes.

**Connectivity.** The downstream connection between Aravaipa Creek and the San Pedro River, which previously occurred only in high-runoff years, has become common in winter. This connectivity increases the chance of exotic invasion.

## Key Findings

- **Persistence and concordance:** Persistence indices were all above 0.9, indicating high persistence. Kendall's W was significant in all reaches but low in the upper reach (W = 0.48), compared with above 0.70 in the canyon and lower reaches. The authors judge these coarse measures of limited use for conservation.
- **Time lag regression:** Directional change was detected in the upper and canyon reaches but not in the lower reach.
- **Multivariate ordination (DCA):** The upper and canyon reaches shifted directionally, with [[Agosia chrysogaster]] dominant in the 1970s, more even composition in the 1980s, and a resurgence of *A. chrysogaster* and *M. fulgida* in the 1990s. The lower reach returned to a 1970s-like state, showing no net directional change.
- **Flow predictability:** Predictability of maximum and minimum flow rose from 0.48 and 0.60 (pre-1982) to 0.50 and 0.68 (post-1981). The authors describe these increases as small and ecologically equivocal.
- **Flow and exotics:** Exotic proportion and richness were higher after years of lower flow variability, but these relationships were not significant. Results were strongly influenced by two high-variability years.
- **Cluster and tree analyses:** In the upper reach, year (1982) was the best predictor of clusters. In the lower reach, the proportion of exotics and first occurrence of *C. lutrensis* explained all variance in the final model.
- **Drivers of change:** Early-1980s changes appear to be system-wide and linked to base flow. Later changes, especially in the lower reach, are linked to exotic species and the rarity of *M. fulgida* there, which is a conservation concern.
- **Flood effects:** No direct effect of the large flash floods (1978, 1983, 1993) on the native assemblage was detected, consistent with the resilience of flood-adapted fish communities.
- **Conservation conclusion:** Retaining high flow variability and isolation from large rivers may be essential for protecting remnant native fish assemblages threatened by exotic species.
