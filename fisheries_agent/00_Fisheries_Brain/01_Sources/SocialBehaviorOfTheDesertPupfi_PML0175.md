# Social Behavior of the Desert Pupfish, Cyprinodon macularius, in the Field

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This ethological study describes the motor patterns, reproductive sequence, aggressive behavior, territoriality, and schooling of the [[Desert Pupfish]] ([[Cyprinodon Macularius]]), a small toothcarp of the [[Cyprinodontidae]]. Observations came from shore pools of the [[Salton Sea]] in California, supplemented by observations at nearby Fish Springs and by aquarium pairings. The author defines a glossary of roughly two dozen motor patterns and a reproductive chain that runs from approach to jerking. Males hold breeding territories, females and juveniles school and forage, and spawning is brief and tightly sequenced. The paper also compares these behaviors with those of other cyprinodontids and argues that the desert pupfish has among the simplest courtship and color systems in the group. The author presents the work as an introduction to the ethology of the species rather than a complete account.

## Background & Description

The study addresses a gap in the ethology of toothcarps (Cyprinodontidae). Earlier accounts covered [[Oryzias Latipes]], [[Fundulus Notatus]], [[Fundulus Diaphanus]], [[Plancterus Kansae]], [[Jordanella Floridae]], and [[Cyprinodon Variegatus]], but most were brief. The author frames the work within comparative ethology, citing the approaches of [[Konrad Lorenz]] and [[Niko Tinbergen]].

**Morphology and sexual dimorphism**
- Adults commonly reach a standard length of 45 mm. One exceptionally large female measured 72 mm.
- Males can mature at about 15 mm standard length, and the author presumes the same for females.
- Breeding males are compressed and arched at the nape, with dark metallic blue bodies and dorsal and anal fins. Their caudal peduncle, caudal fin, and pectoral fins are yellow-orange, and their eyes are black. Males are visible at up to 10 m in clear water.
- Females and juveniles are yellowish brown with irregular darker blotches. Their coloration matches the algal mats on pool bottoms, which makes them inconspicuous. Females have a small dark ocellus at the posterior base of the dorsal fin.

**Distribution and study sites**
- The species is widespread in desert springs and drainages of the lower [[Colorado River Basin]].
- Most field observations were made in shallow shore pools along the [[Salton Sea]]. The pools were visited at about monthly intervals from 1954 to 1956, mostly in the forenoon.
- Supplementary observations came from Fish Springs.

**Aquarium methods**
- Fish were kept in a 68-liter planted freshwater aquarium.
- Isolated males and females were paired in 45-liter tanks without plants and with pine gravel bottoms. The pairing experiment was repeated four times.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and thermal tolerance**
- Breeding males occupy shallow water, typically 5 to 10 cm deep along drop-offs into deeper water. Territories are never found in deeper parts (about 1 m or more).
- Territories center on topographic irregularities, such as submerged bushes, pieces of wood, shoreline coves, and bottom features. Where the bottom is flat, nearly every piece of debris is patrolled.
- In a 5 by 10 m pool holding about 150 fish per square meter at 38.3°C, all fish were actively foraging. The author notes that in larger pools fish avoid temperatures above 36 to 37°C.
- At night most territorial males sleep in their territories, while non-territorial fish sleep in the shallowest water. At sunrise, all fish gather in the deepest parts of the pools.

**Seasonality**
- Territorial holding in the shore pools runs consistently from mid-April or May to October.
- During warm, sunny winter weather, males sometimes return to nuptial coloration and take up territories. One such case was recorded on 11 February 1956.

**Substrate and pit building**
- Most shore-pool bottoms are covered by blue-green algae, which limits digging. Pits were built only where bare sand was available.

## Key Findings

**1. Motor pattern repertoire.** The author names and describes roughly two dozen motor patterns, including meandering, nuzzling, contacting, tilting, nipping, halting, sidling, S-shaping, wrapping, jerking, patrolling, facing, eyeing, arching, tail-beating, charging, circling, fleeing, escaping, digging, and plowing. Patrolling is treated as a composite pattern, while wrapping is a single unit.

**2. Reproductive sequence.** Spawning follows a stereotyped chain: approach, nipping by the female, tilting, sidling by the male, S-shaping, wrapping, and jerking. Jerking releases an egg from the female, and the male presumably releases sperm at the same time.
- In aquaria, the female usually nips first. Sidling then releases her S-shaping, and wrapping follows at about the third to fifth nip.
- Spawning lasts 30 minutes to 2 hours, with larger females spawning longer. Up to five eggs may be released in quick succession.
- At the peak of spawning, about one in four contacts proceeds to jerking.

**3. Variation in sequence.** The sequence varies but not at random. Omitted or repeated elements depend on the female's readiness. The author links this variation to differing spawning thresholds in the two fish.

**4. Aggression.** Males fight head on, eye each other, arch, give tail-beats, charge, and circle head to tail. The size of the opponent determines the first response. A larger male charges a smaller one, which usually flees. Equal or larger opponents progress to the full fight sequence. Males fight more than females, and males sometimes attack females that are not ready to spawn.

**5. Territoriality.**
- Males hold territories. When a new aquarium is set up, they fight until boundaries are established, after which fighting is restricted to the boundaries.
- In the 30 by 60 cm aquarium bottom, the maximum number of territorial males was four, each 25 to 30 mm long. Two males could not be kept in a territorial arrangement; one always dominated the whole bottom.
- Female territoriality is rare and sporadic. Females defended territories only when few fish were present.
- Territory size scales with male size and population density. On 4 June 1955, territories reached 5 to 6 square meters, compared with a customary 1 to 2 square meters, probably because few aggressive males were defending large areas during peak breeding.

**6. Subterritories and pits.**
- Sub-territories held by smaller males sometimes occur within the territory of a larger male. On 14 May 1955, a 45 mm male's territory of about 70 by 50 cm held four smaller males of 20 to 25 mm.
- Males in the small sand patch dug small pits about 2.5 cm deep and 6.5 cm in diameter, with raised lips. Pits were never seen where bottoms were covered by algae.

**7. Schooling.**
- Schooling was not seen in aquaria. Field schools are spread horizontally rather than vertically.
- Females and juveniles school year-round, and males join schools outside the breeding season. In winter, schools may become barely recognizable.
- Mixed-size schools sort by size, because larger fish swim faster and smaller fish fall behind and stay in shallower water.
- Juvenile schools were estimated to exceed 10,000 individuals. A mixed school of about 300 females and juveniles circled in a whirling disc when in a channel and showed sudden darting and temporary settling onto the algal mat.

**8. Light response.** A school avoided a shadow cast by a small pier and stayed in sunlit water. The author notes that this contrasts with laboratory results for [[Cyprinodon Baconi]], suggesting that environment and emotional state can alter light responses.

**9. Color and display.** The orange caudal fin contrasts sharply with the blue body, and the flickering of orange pectoral fins during patrolling makes the male conspicuous. The author proposes that the black eyes and caudal fin color may guide the head-to-tail fighting orientation. Among cyprinodontids, colorful pectoral fins are widespread and rare in other free-swimming fishes.

**10. Comparative and evolutionary notes.**
- The author compares the reproductive sequence with the mating ethogram of [[Oryzias Latipes]] (Ono and Uematsu, 1957), noting the closest correspondence in sidling, S-shaping, and jerking. The match for nuzzling is less certain.
- The spawning posture is viewed as a preadaptation to viviparity. Viviparity has arisen independently within the Cyprinodontiformes at least five times.
- The desert pupfish's color and courtship system appears to be among the simplest in toothcarps, and the author suggests that substrate limitations in desert pools may have selected against pit building.

**11. Feeding and courtship.** The author argues that nipping is an exaggerated and stereotyped version of foraging. The female's nipping movement is distinct enough from normal eating for the male to recognize a receptive female among foraging fish.
