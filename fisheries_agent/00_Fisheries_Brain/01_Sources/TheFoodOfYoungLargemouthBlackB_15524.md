# The Food of Young Largemouth Black Bass (Micropterus salmoides) in Clear Lake, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #feeding-ecology #largemouth-bass

## Executive Summary

This 1948 study examined the mid-summer feeding habits of young-of-year [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) in [[Clear Lake]], [[Lake County]], [[California]]. Analysis of 264 specimens collected August 10-12, 1948 revealed a clear ontogenetic diet shift: fish below 1.8 inches fed primarily on plankton and small insects, those 1.8-2.8 inches consumed mainly insects, and specimens over 2.8 inches fed almost exclusively on forage fish. [[Greaser Blackfish]] (*Orthodon microlepidotus*) emerged as the most important forage fish, while [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), despite abundance, were relatively unimportant in bass diets. No evidence of interyear class predation was detected.

## Background & Description

The study was conducted by Garth I. Murphy of the Bureau of Fish Conservation, California Division of Fish and Game, to determine: (1) feeding habits with increasing size of young bass, (2) most utilized forage species, and (3) extent of cannibalism within the year class.

Collections were seined from a five-mile stretch of shoreline between Lakeport and Rodman Slough on the northwestern end of [[Clear Lake]] using a 40-foot by 6-foot, four-to-the-inch mesh seine. All 264 whole specimens were preserved in formalin for laboratory examination.

Stomachs were removed, contents sorted, and volumetric determinations made by water displacement in graduated centrifuge tubes. Data were recorded on "Speed Sort" cards—a mechanical sorting system using marginal holes and wedge cuts that allowed rapid sorting by any recorded attribute. This system proved efficient for handling multiple data entries per specimen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area fish community was dominated by [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), sculpins (*Cottus* sp.), and [[Greaser Blackfish]] (*Orthodon microlepidotus*). Seine haul averages: bass of the year 29 per haul, bluegill (all size classes) 77 per haul, blackfish approximately 8 per haul, and sculpins approximately 8 per haul. Large numbers of bluegill too small for seine capture were present. Sculpin abundance was likely underestimated due to their benthic habits and seine avoidance.

Young-of-year blackfish distribution centered farther offshore (over 40 feet) than the seining area, suggesting actual abundance exceeded seine estimates.

Insect foods, particularly larval midges and corixids (all instars), and zooplankton were abundant based on gross observations. The food situation presented an abundance of all three food types (fish, insects, plankton), presumably allowing young bass to exercise dietary choice rather than being constrained by food availability.

Spawning phenology was significant: bluegill spawned 1 to 1.5 months later than bass due to higher temperature requirements, while blackfish and other cyprinids spawned earlier than or concurrent with bass. In 1947, cyprinid spawning virtually failed, and bass fingerling abundance was only one-eighth that of 1948, suggesting cyprinid forage fish importance for bass survival.

## Key Findings

**Size Distribution**: Bass specimens ranged from approximately 1.4 to 4.7 inches fork length. The sample was deliberately structured to obtain adequate representation across size classes rather than reflecting natural population structure.

**Ontogenetic Diet Shift**: A clear transition in feeding habits occurred with increasing size:
- Below 1.8 inches: Plankton (primarily [[Cladocera]]) and small insects were chief foods
- 1.8 to 2.8 inches: Insects were the most important dietary item
- Above 3.0 inches: Fish were almost the exclusive food

This transition is regarded as a natural change driven by individual fish choice rather than food supply limitations. Specimens larger than 3 inches that consumed non-fish items typically also had fish in their stomachs, indicating such items were incidental.

**Food Classes Consumed** (264 specimens examined, 10 empty stomachs):

*Fishes*:
- [[Greaser Blackfish]] (*Orthodon microlepidotus*): 28 occurrences, 37.8 cc total volume—most important forage fish
- Sculpins (*Cottus* sp.): 15 occurrences, 3.8 cc
- [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*): 6 occurrences, 3.1 cc
- [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*): 1 occurrence, 1.0 cc
- Unidentified fish: 24 occurrences, 7.2 cc

*Crustacea*:
- Water fleas ([[Cladocera]]): 538 occurrences, 1.32 cc
- Scuds (Gammarids): 35 occurrences, 0.44 cc

*Insecta*:
- Water boatmen ([[Corixidae]]): 142 occurrences, 6.04 cc—most important insect food
- Damsel flies (Odonata): 32 occurrences, 1.38 cc
- Midge pupae (Chironomidae): 27 occurrences, 1.87 cc
- Fly larvae (Diptera): 38 occurrences, 0.40 cc
- May fly (Ephemeridae): 8 occurrences, 0.08 cc
- Unidentified insects: 6 occurrences, 0.15 cc

*Miscellaneous invertebrates*: 8 occurrences, 0.14 cc

**Forage Fish Utilization**: Despite bluegill being the most abundant available forage fish based on seine catches, they were relatively unimportant in bass diets. [[Greaser Blackfish]] dominated, likely due to greater availability (inhabiting littoral zone without close cover association) and possibly preference (slender, fine-scaled, smooth body versus deep-bodied, prickly bluegill). A 2.4-inch partially digested blackfish was found in a 4.1-inch bass, illustrating consumability. Spot checks on bass stomachs from other lake sections confirmed blackfish as the most frequently taken forage fish during late summer.

**Sculpin Importance**: The frequency of sculpins in bass diets (15 occurrences) was unexpected given their benthic habits and seine avoidance. This indicates sculpins play an important role in the littoral economy of [[Clear Lake]].

**Absence of Cannibalism**: No evidence of interyear class predation was detected, despite some seine hauls capturing over 70 bass where smaller bass were the most abundant food available to larger bass. Availability and desirability factors that limited bluegill utilization likely operated similarly for smaller bass.

**Comparison to Other Studies**: The feeding pattern observed matches that described by Cooper (1936) and Ohio Division of Conservation (1925) for young bass, indicating the [[Clear Lake]] pattern is normal under environmental conditions favorable to feeding choice. Cooper's work in artificial rearing ponds suggested some foods were forced on fish by environmental limitations.

**Management Implications**: The study provides critical information for bass production in nature and rearing ponds. The most important factor appears to be provision of suitable-sized forage fish when fingerlings reach 2.5 to 3 inches in length. [[Greaser Blackfish]], as lake-spawning plankton feeders, may prove extremely valuable as forage fish in fluctuating reservoirs whose economy depends primarily on plankton, analogous to gizzard shad in midwestern and southern reservoirs.

The later spawning of bluegill (1-1.5 months after bass) means young bass and sculpins must bear predation pressure from older fish until bluegill fry appear. This temporal mismatch may result in lower bass survival to fingerling stage when cyprinid spawning fails, as demonstrated by the 1947 cyprinid spawn failure and corresponding 87.5% reduction in bass fingerling abundance.
