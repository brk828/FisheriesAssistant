## Background & Description

- **Landsat [[Thematic Mapper]] (TM)** data, used for regional and reconnaissance work.
- **Hard-copy imagery and aerial photographs**, which are simpler to interpret for users without remote-sensing training.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Wetland mapping is framed around water regime. Many [[Australian Wetlands]] fill only seasonally or intermittently, so the timing and duration of inundation matter for both mapping and [[Waterbird Monitoring]]. The chapter treats vegetation productivity as a second key variable. Vigour is estimated from the NIR/red vegetation index, which tracks green biomass and moisture status.

Two physical limits on mapping are noted:

- Features whose widths approach the sensor's spatial resolution are poorly defined.
- Shallow, intermittently inundated vegetated wetlands are hard to map because vegetation obscures the water surface. These wetlands are also difficult to survey by conventional field methods.

The chapter also draws on a US comparison. In a study of the [[Savannah River]] wetlands, [[Jensen Et Al 1986]] assigned different data sources to different tasks: [[Landsat Mss]] for regional distribution, airborne multispectral scanner data for detailed vegetation, and multi-date aerial photographs for historical change. The authors cite this as evidence that data resolution should be matched to the task.

## Key Findings

- **Matching data to task.** Remote sensing is most useful for regional reconnaissance and long-term monitoring. Detailed vegetation and habitat mapping is a weaker application (Jensen et al. 1986).
- **Geometric advantage of satellite imagery.** Geographically rectified TM imagery is suitable for mapping at **1:50,000** and can be overlaid directly on standard map sheets in a [[Geographic Information System]]. Aerial photography may carry considerable geometric distortion.
- **Hard-copy imagery for broad-scale work.** For users without remote-sensing training, hard-copy images are recommended. Basic interpretation can be taught quickly and costs much less. A Victorian forest-type study ([[Alexander Et Al 1990]]) combined visual analysis with manual digitizing and was judged simpler, cheaper and more efficient for broad-scale mapping (Woodgate 1990).
- **Preliminary classification without ground data.** Unsupervised classification and multiple density slicing based on vegetation index and water regime produced classes that reflected the study area's land-cover variability. The authors suggest these could serve as a base map for initial field surveys.
- **Wetland extent mapping.** Density slicing of MIR (M5) data is described as a simple, rapid method for mapping wetland extent and location. Image date is critical and the optimum date depends on the dominant wetland type. Using TM, wetlands down to **1 ha** can be adequately discriminated.
- **Seasonal classification.** Multi-temporal images allow simple classifications based on seasonal changes in water regime and vegetation vigour. The classes relate directly to physical parameters. Two or more images capture seasonal variability, which the authors describe as especially valuable for Australian wetlands.
- **Limits on vegetation-type mapping.** Classification is unlikely to reproduce field-mapped dominant-species distributions accurately. Image classes track vegetation density, vigour and moisture status more closely than species identity. The confusion-matrix results (Table 2) are the chapter's test of this limit, though the values are not legible in the source.
