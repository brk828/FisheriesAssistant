# Imperial Native Fish Habitat Reconstruction: Design Workshop Draft Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Design Workshop Draft Report sets out a redesign of the [[Du2 Ponds]] complex on the [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] (INWR), about 30 miles north of Yuma, Arizona. The site was built under the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (LCR MSCP) to serve as a [[Native Fish Sanctuary]] for [[Razorback Sucker]]. The original four ponds failed because of hypoxia, high water temperatures, and a persistent [[Non-Native Fish]] invasion by [[Warmouth Sunfish]]. The workshop, run by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], proposes six hydrologically isolated ponds with deeper contours, groundwater-fed inflows, fish salvage kettles, hummocks, and gravel spawning beds. The estimated construction cost is $3,106,000, which the report says should not be used for budgeting.

## Background & Description

- **Origin:** The site was built in 1997–2002 to meet the [[1997 Biological Opinion]] requirement that Reclamation construct or restore at least 300 acres of native fish habitat in the Lower Colorado River floodplain. Construction was completed in autumn 2002.
- **Site extent:** The complex covers 96 acres. The original design had four cells with about 35 acres of water, ranging from 4 to 14 acres, with a maximum depth of 6 feet.
- **Stocking history:** The ponds were treated with [[Rotenone]] in October and December 2002. About 658 fingerling razorback suckers were stocked in March 2003. Warmouth were still found in small numbers after multiple treatments.
- **Population outcome:** By 2003 the razorback sucker population had fallen to an estimated 25 fish, while warmouth rose to an estimated 17,000.
- **Riparian component:** Planted cottonwood, willow, and mesquite largely failed, attributed to high soil salinity. Soil electrical conductivity ranged from 29 to 40 mmhos/cm, far above the roughly 3 mmhos/cm at which few cottonwoods or willows survive.
- **Reference site:** [[High Levee Pond]] on [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] is the model. Since 1993 it has been the only successful sanctuary for LCR native fish, supporting all life stages of [[Bonytail]] and razorback sucker.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of failure identified:**
- **Hypoxia:** Dissolved oxygen was inadequate. Dense submerged aquatic vegetation (Najas, Myriophyllum, Potamogeton) consumed oxygen through nighttime respiration.
- **Shallow depths:** Shallow ponds left fish exposed to large numbers of avian predators.
- **Hydrologic connection:** Ponds were kept at lower levels to avoid flooding adjacent farm fields, which allowed vegetation to take over.
- **Single inflow and outflow:** One inlet and one outlet per system prevented adequate water-quality management and isolation from invaders.
- **Temperature:** Pond 1 survived only after a timer added water between midnight and 6 a.m. during hot periods. The other ponds had fish kills.

**Hydrology and water source:**
- Groundwater was chosen over a passive subsurface link. The stated reasons are the lower risk of introducing non-native fish, eggs, and larvae, and the possibility that alluvial substrate filters [[Selenium]] from Colorado River water.
- The design also notes that an irrigation pump could be fitted with fish screens as a lower-cost alternative. That technology was still being evaluated at another site.

**Geology and groundwater:** Groundwater did not intrude during 1997–98 construction, and the local hydrology is poorly understood. Test wells are proposed to find the water table depth, any clay lens, salt movement, and the degree of connection to the river.

## Key Findings

**Design goals (prioritized in the workshop):**
1. *Highest:* Reconstruct ponds as backwaters with the physical, chemical, and biological conditions needed for healthy native LCR fishes.
2. *Medium:* Expand surface area and/or marsh habitat within the 96 acres, and build features that are easy to maintain with a design life of at least 50 years.
3. *Lower:* Increase knowledge of managed habitat for LCR fishes.

**Proposed design:**
- **Ponds:** The total rises from four to six ponds, adding about 40–50 acres of backwater. The six ponds range from about 9 to 17 acres. Depth targets: 20% under 5 ft, 60% at 5–10 ft, and 20% at 10–12 ft or deeper.
- **Isolation:** Every pond gets its own inlet and outlet, which severs hydrologic links and lets each pond be filled, drained, and managed on its own.
- **Drainage:** An integrated drainage system on the east side drains each pond to limit salt buildup and diverts farm runoff.
- **Fish kettles:** Deep sumps (about 10 × 10 ft) at the outflows concentrate fish for salvage and reduce the volume of water treated with rotenone, which the report says would give large cost savings.
- **Inflow placement:** Inlets sit at depths over 10 ft, with aerated injection to raise dissolved oxygen. Cooler groundwater forms a thermal refuge.
- **Hummocks:** 18 hummocks (about 20 × 100 ft, tops within 12 inches of the surface) are planted with [[California Bulrush]] for shade and cover. They also support [[Western Least Bittern]] and possibly [[Yuma Clapper Rail]].
- **Spawning beds:** Flat, lined gravel-to-cobble beds (3/8 to 3 inches) cover about 5% of each pond, at depths of 2–6 ft.
- **Boat ramps and staging areas:** One per pond, gravel-surfaced, near the outflows and kettles.
- **Riprap:** Used on steep edges to limit emergent plants and provide escape cover for fish.

**Monitoring program:**
- *Water quality:* Dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity, temperature, depth, and ORP at a minimum of three permanent sites per pond (inflow, mid-lake, outflow). Turbidity is measured by Secchi disc or NTU. Sampling is quarterly in autumn, winter, and spring, and twice monthly in summer, when temperatures exceed 30°C.
- *Fish:* Larval collections, a one-hour summer sampling, and at least one cooler-season sampling per year to assess health and population size. Any non-native detection triggers renovation by draining, salvaging, and treating with rotenone in the kettle, or letting the pond dry.

**Soil and vegetation management:**
- Excavated material, which is likely highly saline, is to be reused for roads, berms, hummocks, and spawning beds, rather than hauled off the refuge at high cost.
- Salt cedar is to be removed by root plowing, chipping, and hauling off site. Follow-up herbicide treatment may be needed because of root resprouting.

**Cost and operations:**
- Total construction cost estimate: **$3,106,000**, excluding unlisted items, contingencies, design, contracting, project management, and permitting.
- Operating costs include pumping for two wells (about 75 HP continuous) at an estimated $5,000–$10,000 per year, pump and motor replacement every 25 years, and rotenone at about $100 per gallon.
- Environmental compliance (NEPA, the Archaeological Resources Protection Act, ESA section 7, and Clean Water Act sections 401 and 404) is the responsibility of INWR.

**Stakeholders:** The workshop included experts from Arizona State University, Reclamation, USFWS, and USGS. Contact for project questions: [[Nathan Lenon]], Reclamation Multi-Species Conservation Program Office.
