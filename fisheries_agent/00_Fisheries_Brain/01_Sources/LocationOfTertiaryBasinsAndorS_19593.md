# Tertiary Sedimentary Units and Paleoecology of Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This review synthesizes the Tertiary (Paleocene to Pliocene) sedimentary units of Arizona, cataloguing 41 numbered stratigraphic units by age and location. It links basin formation to three major tectonic events, the [[Laramide Orogeny]], the [[Mid-Tertiary Orogeny]], and the [[Basin and Range Disturbance]], and uses fossil floras, aquatic faunas, and isotopic dates to reconstruct paleoclimate, land elevation, and drainage history. Fish are addressed only indirectly, as aquatic faunal evidence for former interbasin connections, but the paper is primarily a stratigraphic and paleoecological synthesis rather than a fish study.

## Background & Description

The paper is a stratigraphic summary prepared by Northern Arizona University geologists. Its central tool is a pair of companion figures: Figure 2 maps the locations of the 41 units, and Figure 3 charts their chronology. Table 1 lists the isotopic and paleontologic age references for each unit. Only the assigned numbers (1 to 41) are cited in the text. Isotopic dating is the primary basis for correlation, and fossil mammal ages (North American Land-Mammal Ages) are used only where isotopic dates are absent.

The paper states that very few studies exist on Arizona's Tertiary plants and invertebrates. Its authors argue that such fossils have considerable untapped potential for interpreting paleogeography, paleoecology, and biostratigraphy in nonmarine basins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tectonic framework.** The Tertiary history of Arizona was shaped by three events:
- **Laramide Orogeny (late Cretaceous to Eocene):** broad epeirogenic uplift of the state. The [[Colorado Plateau]] became an eroded pediment, and the dominant deposits were stream gravels.
- **Mid-Tertiary Orogeny (35 to 17 m.y.):** crustal heating, plutonism, and volcanism that formed localized coarse-clastic basins, mainly in southern Arizona.
- **Basin and Range Disturbance (15 to 1 m.y.):** high-angle normal faulting and basaltic volcanism that created grabens and temporary internal-drainage basins filled with fine sediments and evaporites. Through-flowing drainage was established by the end of the Pliocene.

**Stratigraphic sequence by epoch.**
- **Paleocene (65 to 53.5 m.y.):** Southern and central Arizona was mountainous and volcanic. Northern Arizona has no defined Paleocene sedimentary units. Paleodrainages flowed into Paleocene lakes in Utah and New Mexico.
- **Eocene (53.5 to 37 m.y.):** The best documented units are in the [[Baca Basin]] and the Mogollon Rim gravels. Late Eocene volcanic rocks interbedded with sediments occur in southern basins.
- **Oligocene (37 to 24 m.y.):** Coarse clastic units were deposited in southern Arizona. The Cave Creek area yielded a mid-Oligocene oreodont. The Chuska Sandstone of the northeast is only tentatively dated.
- **Miocene (24 to 5 m.y.):** Many graben basins developed, with thick fluvial, volcanic, lacustrine, and evaporite sequences. The [[Gulf of California]] began forming in the proto-Gulf by about 20 m.y. ago, with its marine sediments at the [[Yuma Basin]]. The [[Bouse Formation]] records the northward expansion to Lake Mohave in the latest Miocene to Pliocene.
- **Pliocene (5.0 to 1.8 m.y.):** Several southeastern basins contain Blancan faunas. The [[Verde Formation]] also has a well-known Blancan fauna.

**Muddy Creek Formation and the Colorado River.** The [[Muddy Creek Formation]] was deposited in a lacustrine setting until 5.9 m.y. ago, before the [[Colorado River]] established its present course. By 3.8 m.y. ago the river was downcutting through the formation. Authors disagree on whether it had a marine or estuarine origin. Cornell (1979) and Lucchitta (1979) favored a nonmarine origin.

**Paleoclimate and elevation (floral evidence).**
- Oligocene floras show a shift toward a modern composition. Leaf-margin analysis indicates the Oligocene was cooler, drier, and more variable than the Eocene. Climate remained cool and dry through the Neogene.
- Late Tertiary [[Pollen Analysis]] from Arizona records at least four plant communities: pond and marsh assemblages, deciduous riparian gallery forest, oak-pine-juniper woodland or savanna, and pine forest.
- Verde basin pollen shows increasing aridity but no Pliocene cooling. The authors attribute this to tectonic subsidence rather than climate.
- High-elevation conifers (Picea, Abies, Pseudotsuga) are absent from all Tertiary samples in northern Arizona. This suggests that Miocene topography rarely exceeded about 2000 m within tens of kilometers of the Verde Valley.

## Key Findings

- **Stratigraphic inventory:** 41 numbered Tertiary sedimentary units are mapped and dated. Ages come mainly from isotopic dating, with paleontologic and paleomagnetic correlations used where isotopic dates are lacking.
- **Tectonic control:** Basin formation and drainage evolution are linked to the Laramide, Mid-Tertiary, and Basin and Range events. Drainage was internal during much of the Miocene and integrated only by the end of the Pliocene.
- **Colorado River history:** The Muddy Creek Formation records lacustrine conditions until about 5.9 m.y. ago. The river had established its course by 3.8 m.y. ago.
- **Gulf of California:** A proto-Gulf existed by about 20 m.y. ago and expanded northward, leaving marine and estuarine deposits in the Yuma area and the Bouse Formation.
- **Elevation change:** Pollen data suggest the [[Colorado Plateau]] rose about 200 to 300 m since the late Miocene. The western margin of the Verde basin subsided 600 to 800 m over the same period. The authors describe these opposing trends as tectonic in origin.
- **Paleoclimate:** Floras show Oligocene cooling and drying, followed by continued cool, dry conditions through the Neogene. The Verde basin shows no Pliocene cooling, which the authors attribute to basin subsidence.
- **Aquatic fauna and drainage:** Fish and other aquatic faunas are used to infer former drainage connections:
  - Uyeno and Miller (1965) identified an ancestral fish fauna linking the [[Bidahochi Formation]] to the Colorado River, consistent with middle Pliocene cyprinid fishes from that formation.
  - Smith (1966) used modern catostomid distributions to infer faunal connections between the Colorado River and Rio Grande drainages.
  - Hendrickson and others (1981) concluded that the Mexican Rio Yaqui drainage once included many southwestern Arizona drainages.
- **Verde Formation habitats:** Fossil aquatic communities indicate temporary and permanent ponds, marshes, cienegas, spring runs, and streams. The basin changed from a through-flowing system to an internal-drainage basin during the Miocene, and the modern Verde River drainage had formed by the Pliocene.
- **Research gaps:** The authors call for more paleoecological work on the Bidahochi Formation under detailed stratigraphic control. They also recommend aquatic faunal studies (fish, mollusks, and other invertebrates) to reconstruct interbasin connections among the Gila, Rio Grande, Colorado, Verde, Virgin, and Yaqui drainages.
