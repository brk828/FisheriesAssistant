# Colorado Squawfish Reintroduction into the Lower Colorado River (1990 Section 7 Consultation)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This memorandum and attached biological opinion, issued by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in June 1990, evaluates a proposal to reintroduce juvenile [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a federally listed endangered species, into the [[Colorado River]] mainstream between [[Headgate Rock Dam]] and [[Imperial Dam]] in Arizona and California. The action would be carried out as an experimental nonessential population under Section 10(j) of the Endangered Species Act, in cooperation with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. The Service concluded that the proposed stocking is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of the species and is expected to promote its conservation. Formal consultation and incidental take conditions were specified.

## Background & Description

The Colorado squawfish is the largest cyprinid in North America and the largest native predator of the Colorado River system. Maximum weights above 36 kg and lengths near 1.8 m have been recorded, although fish above 2 kg have become rare in recent times. Early literature called it "white salmon," "Colorado salmon," or "salmon." The species was listed as endangered in 1973 under the [[Endangered Species Act]], and no critical habitat had been designated at the time of the opinion.

Its evolutionary history involved repeated shifts between pluvial and arid habitats during the Miocene, Pliocene, and early Pleistocene. Adaptations such as large size, mobility, and spawning migrations suited drier seasons but may now contribute to its decline. Juveniles are initially invertebrate and zooplankton feeders but become piscivorous at roughly 60 mm or larger, and by about 200 mm the diet is almost entirely fish. Adults are opportunistic and eat a wide range of organisms, including terrestrial insects such as [[Mormon Crickets]]. Individuals have been recorded at more than 60 years of age, and captive broodstock at Dexter National Fish Hatchery are up to 10 years old.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Dam construction, beginning with more than 20 dams built on the mainstream and tributaries since 1913, drove much of the species' decline. Documented changes include:

- Colder water temperatures below reservoirs, linked to declines of native fishes.
- Reduced nutrients, sediment, and turbidity downstream, with channel bottoms armoring to cobble and boulders.
- Loss of backwaters and sloughs used as nursery and resting areas through channelization.
- Replacement of natural flood and drought cycles with regulated discharge.

Introduced nonnative fishes compounded these effects. In Arizona, at least 60 introduced species have joined about 31 native species since the turn of the century. Predation and competition by nonnatives, together with habitat modification, are considered major factors in the species' extirpation from the lower basin. Choking mortality has been documented when squawfish prey on channel catfish.

Current natural populations occur in three upper basin locations: the Green/Yampa rivers, the upper Colorado mainstream, and the San Juan River. A refugium and broodstock population is maintained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Water quality is considered unlikely to limit survival in the lower basin, and the species has survived in water with a wide range of chemical parameters. The proposed reach is a relatively free-flowing section of the mainstream, with [[Parker Dam]] (Palo Verde Diversion Dam) restricting upstream movement but not downstream movement. Downstream summer temperatures near Yuma commonly approach or exceed 35°C.

## Key Findings

**Biological opinion**
- The stocking is "not likely to jeopardize" the continued existence of the Colorado squawfish and will promote conservation of the species.
- Reintroduction of the species into a modified environment has never been tested before, so successful reproduction cannot be predicted with certainty.

**Project design**
- Minimum of 150,000 fry per year, supplied from Dexter NFH broodstock (more than 400 fish). Fry are reared to up to 1,000,000 fingerlings annually by the AGFD.
- Project duration: 10 years, with adjustments if successful reproduction occurs.
- Goal: establish a trophy sport fishery, with severely limited angler harvest.
- Complements 1986 approvals of experimental nonessential introductions in the Salt and Verde rivers.
- The 1988 egg take at Dexter yielded more than 2 million eggs from 22 fish, producing more than 199,000 fry. No wild stock will be removed.

**Impacts and take**
- Approximately 70 percent of yearly reintroduced fingerlings are expected to be lost within a year of release, mainly to predatory fish and some fish-eating birds.
- Mortality is also expected during transport from Dexter NFH and during rearing and stocking.
- Take is covered by existing permits and an incidental take statement.
- Reasonable and prudent measures include delaying authorized take until fish are sexually mature, an angler education program, and habitat monitoring.
- Reinitiation of formal consultation is required if new information reveals unanticipated effects, if the action is modified, or if a new species is listed.

**Other federally listed species**
- Three listed species occur near the area: [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Bald Eagle]], and [[Sonoran Pronghorn]]. The Service concluded that none would be affected.

**Recovery context**
- The project supports all objectives in the species recovery plan, including research on life history, interactions, aging, nonnative impacts, relocation sites, and stocking, and assessment of sport fishery potential.
