# Petrography and Origin of the 111 Ranch Cherts, Graham County, Arizona

#diatoms #paleolimnology #chert

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Philip Seff]], a research geologist with the Southwest Watershed Research Station, examines the chert deposits of the [[111 Ranch Beds]], a sequence of middle Pleistocene flood-plain, paludal, and lacustrine sediments in Graham County, Arizona. The author concludes that the chert is of secondary origin, formed through diagenesis of diatomaceous beds. The silica was supplied by alteration of tuffaceous (volcanic ash) sediments and by chemical weathering of the nearby [[Whitlock Hills]] volcanics, and was concentrated by the biochemical action of [[Diatoms]]. The study is a petrographic and stratigraphic investigation of a classic geological problem: the source of silica in chert and the timing of its formation.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The 111 Ranch Beds are exposed in badland topography about 14 miles southeast of Safford, Arizona, just north of the volcanic Whitlock Hills, near the intersection of the Gila and San Simon Valleys (T. 8 S., R. 28 E.).
- **Section:** The exposed section is about 200 feet thick. Clay marl, silt, and tuffaceous silt dominate. Fossil content and fine, well-sorted sediments suggest deposition in a [[Paludal Flood-Plain]] environment.
- **Key units:**
  - [[Lake Nancy Limestone]] (unit 6): the most extensive and continuous deposit in the area; a key stratigraphic marker.
  - [[Ed's Limestone]] (unit 3): a lacustrine limestone of minor consequence, with limited exposures.
  - [[Capybara Limestone]] (unit 10): variable lithology, mostly limestone, grading into massive diatomite near the Whitlock Hills. Thick beds of green chert are interbedded with the diatomite, usually near the unit's center.
  - [[Kiln Diatomite]] (unit 1): the oldest exposed unit, found only in the southwestern part of the mapped area.
- **Chert varieties:** Both bedded and nodular chert occur. Massive-bedded chert is confined to the upper section and is generally interbedded with thick diatomite.
- **Chert petrography:** Chert is divided into two end members (after [[Folk and Weaver]], 1952): microcrystalline quartz (grains averaging 3 to 5 microns) and chalcedonic quartz (fibrous sheaflike bundles). Microcrystalline quartz forms the bulk of most cherts. Opal occurs only in minor amounts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The sediments record a lake with a strong biogenic silica signal:

- **Diatom abundance:** Thin-section work by [[Mathias]] (1959) indicates diatomites with 75 to 90 percent diatom content, the purest being the Kiln unit.
- **Water chemistry:** The author proposes that interior lake waters were alkaline enough to precipitate CaCO3, while shoreline areas had neutral to slightly alkaline pH favorable to diatom growth. Diatoms extracted silica from the host waters, which the author states could have exhausted the supply locally.
- **Lake extent:** The extreme southeast corner, abutting the Whitlock Hills, became nearly pure diatomite, which the author interprets as an area of permanent or near-permanent water. The massive [[Capybara Chert]] is thickest there.
- **Volcanic influence:** Diatomite occurs only in contact with the Whitlock volcanic rocks, implying a genetic link between volcanism and diatom accumulation. [[Bentonite]] alteration of ash released silica into the lake waters. Differential thermal analysis of the clays found [[Montmorillonite]] to be the sole clay present.
- **Tuff input:** Pyroclastic material peaks in unit 8, which is rich in shardy material. Some ash was reworked by fluvial and aeolian processes.

## Key Findings

1. **Origin:** The chert is of secondary origin and was derived mainly through diagenesis of the diatomaceous beds. Close association of chert with diatomite, continuous fine laminations across contacts, and similar detrital content support this.
2. **Silica sources:** Two possible sources are proposed: (1) alteration of tuffaceous sediments and (2) chemical weathering of the Whitlock Hills volcanics. The author states that the lack of coarse detritus in diatomite lapping against the volcanics favors weathering as a major source.
3. **Biological concentration:** Silica was concentrated by the biochemical action of thriving diatoms. Living diatoms protected their skeletons with organic films. After death, silica dissolved and reprecipitated in denser beds during compaction.
4. **Limestone cherts:** Chert in limestone bedding planes and fissures is interpreted as secondary. Low permeability at the diatomite-limestone contact impeded silica-saturated waters. Decay of plant stems in the diatomite may have lowered pH, dissolving CaCO3 along cracks and bedding planes. Silica then replaced the limestone. Relict calcite in the chert and silicified fossils support this.
5. **Timing:** Chert formed early in the compaction stage. Chert is deformed as much as the host rock by small upwarps and downwarps, implying it hardened before intense compaction. Limestone cherts probably formed shortly after or contemporaneously with the massive diatomite chert.
6. **Dimensions:** The thickest diatomite is about 20 feet, of which 15 feet is cherty. Chert beds in the lower diatomites average 3 to 6 inches thick. The thickest Kiln chert does not exceed 2 feet.
