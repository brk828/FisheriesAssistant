# Physical Habitat Use by Spikedace (Meda fulgida) in Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines a blank manuscript review form (journal form RM 1630-6, July 1979) with a research manuscript by John N. Rinne (USDA Forest Service, Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station) and Erich Kroeger (Arizona State University). The review form is largely illegible and records no recommendation or reviewer comments that can be read reliably. The manuscript quantifies the physical habitat used by the [[Spikedace]], [[Meda Fulgida]], a threatened cyprinid of the [[Gila River Basin]], in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona. Spikedace occupied shallow, moderately flowing water over gravel-pebble substrate. Larger aggregations of fish used deeper, slower microhabitats near eddies, boulders and canyon walls.

## Background & Description

*Meda fulgida* is a small cyprinid (under 100 mm) endemic to the Gila River basin of Arizona and New Mexico. It is listed as threatened by the Arizona Game and Fish Commission and by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USDI 1986). New Mexico lists it as endangered. Published information on the species came mainly from distribution and taxonomy studies, and before this work, the ecological literature on its habitat came almost entirely from Aravaipa Creek.

The authors undertook the study because habitat requirements were poorly known. The paper reports only the Aravaipa Creek results. A comparative study across several desert streams in Arizona and New Mexico was planned for later publication.

The study area is a low-gradient, small (about 30 m wide), often braided and hydrologically stable stream with mainly gravel-pebble substrate (3 to 64 mm). Modal discharge ranges from 0.5 to 1.0 m³/sec, with the highest flows in winter. Late-summer storms can cause sudden, sometimes violent floods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Aravaipa Creek was sampled six times across the seasons: winter (Dec to Feb), spring (Apr to May) and summer (Aug to Sep), from 1982 to 1985. Sites were first chosen from prior collection experience. Once spikedace were captured by [[Electrofishing]] and a block net, at least two further transects were sampled 5 m upstream and downstream. A maximum of nine additional transects were usually added. Sites without spikedace were abandoned for other reaches.

**Measurements.** Physical habitat was measured with the [[Meter Transect Method]] and a habitat sampler. Each transect had 21 depth and velocity readings at 5 cm intervals, which were averaged before analysis. Velocity was measured 5 cm above the substrate. Substrate was classed visually, initially into five categories (sand, gravel, pebble, cobble, boulder). Two intermediate classes were added (sand-gravel and gravel-pebble), giving seven classes in total. Substrate was analysed as modal class values.

**Statistics.** Depth and velocity at occupied and unoccupied transects were compared with unpaired t-tests. Substrate was analysed with [[Chi-Square Analysis]]. Habitat use among sampling dates was tested with [[Analysis of Variance]] (ANOVA). Where variances were unequal, [[Welch's Test]] was applied. Post hoc comparisons used [[Tukey's Test]] or Dunnett's T3.

**Habitat associations.** The deeper, slower areas used by larger schools were eddying, and were associated with linear zones, instream boulders and canyon walls. The authors relate these areas to boulders that produced scour pools and to eddies where currents converged.

## Key Findings

- **Overall habitat use.** Spikedace used the habitat roughly in proportion to its availability, with a mean occupied depth of about 20 cm. The authors describe moderate velocities over gravel-pebble substrate.
- **Depth by date.** Depth varied significantly among sample dates (P < 0.001). Occupied depths were shallower (about 14 to 16 cm) in December, February and August, and deeper (23 to 27 cm) in April, May and September. Depths were significantly greater where spikedace were present only in December 1982 and April 1983, and significantly lower only in August 1983.
- **Velocity by date.** Velocities also varied among dates (P < 0.001), but the Tukey grouping showed no clear seasonal pattern.
- **Substrate.** Spikedace occupied gravel-pebble substrate that was significantly smaller in size than where they were absent, but only in December 1982.
- **Aggregation and microhabitat.** Based on relative abundance at transects, spikedace preferred deeper, slower water. Transects with 10 or more fish averaged 22.8 and 27.3 cm in depth. Transects with a single fish averaged 15.7 cm. Velocities at the larger-aggregation transects averaged 25.7 and 22.7 cm/sec, compared with 96.6 cm/sec at single-fish transects. (The abstract and results values are partly garbled in the scan, so the pairing of the two 10-plus values is approximate.)
- **Ontogenetic differences.** Juveniles (28 to 37 mm total length) made up 60% of fish on 5 of the 10 transects with more than ten fish, which suggests size-related habitat preferences.
- **Low-velocity use.** About 20% (19 of 91) of transects where spikedace occurred had velocities below 18 cm/sec.
- **Comparison with earlier reports.** Reported depths and velocities vary widely across studies:
  - [[Miller and Hubbs 1960]] described deep waters, swift pools and riffle mouths.
  - [[Anderson 1978]] reported moderate to swift riffles less than 30 cm deep over gravel-rubble.
  - Turner and Tafanelli (1983, as cited) reported 18 to 37 cm depths and 39 to 82 cm/sec velocities in Aravaipa Creek.
  - Propst et al. (1986) reported depths under 32 cm and velocities under 15 cm/sec in the upper Gila River.
  The authors attribute much of this variation to ontogenetic differences, habitat availability and differences in where velocity was measured.
- **Management implications.** The authors state that these results should not be extrapolated to other streams without comparable data. They recommend further work on habitat by life stage, spawning habitat, and interactions with competitors such as the [[Red Shiner]]. They also note the [[Loach Minnow]] as a related native species in the same system. The paper frames the work as a basis for recovery planning for this rare southwestern cyprinid.
