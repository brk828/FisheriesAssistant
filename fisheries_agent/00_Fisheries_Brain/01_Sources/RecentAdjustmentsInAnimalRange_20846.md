# Recent Adjustments in Animal Ranges

#ichthyology #marine-biology #vertebrate-biogeography

## Executive Summary

This chapter reviews how the geographic ranges of North American vertebrates adjusted over the last ~5,000 years (from 5,000 B.P. to the present). The author treats three periods: the [[Xerothermic Interval]] and early post-Xerothermic period (6,000–3,000 B.P.), the Prehistoric Period (3,000–200 B.P.), and the Historic Period (200 B.P. to present). Evidence comes from [[Palaeoecology]] for the first period, [[Archaeozoology]] for the second, and the historical record for the third.

The core argument is that warm-dry climate fragmented mesic forest ranges, leaving [[Relict Distribution]] populations, and that the cool-moist return reversed some of these patterns. The author concludes that the Historic Period, driven mainly by habitat destruction, introductions and transplantation, has produced the most pronounced changes. Fishes are singled out for their ability to bypass natural barriers and to be widely transplanted. The author explicitly describes the [[Xerothermic Interval]] as a hypothesis rather than settled fact.

## Background & Description

The chapter sets a 5,000 B.P. starting point and deliberately avoids controversial issues of late-Wisconsin chronology, periglacial climate, and glacial-maximum displacement. The warm period is referred to by several names, including [[Hypsithermal]], Altithermal, Xerothermic Interval, Climatic Optimum and Thermal Maximum. The author follows Smith (1957) in using:

- **Climatic Optimum:** warm-moist phase, ca. 8,000–6,000 B.P.
- **Xerothermic Interval:** warm-dry phase, ca. 6,000–4,000 B.P.

The discussion starts at the Xerothermic maximum. By then the ice had retreated to the polar region and topography and drainage were essentially modern. Late-Pleistocene megafauna were extinct, and the remaining biota was modern but probably distributed differently. The author notes that human-driven fire in grassland and savanna may have altered climax vegetation, though the effect is uncertain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Xerothermic and Post-Xerothermic Period (6,000–3,000 B.P.)

- Eastern North America was probably mesic during the Climatic Optimum. Some southern components extended farther north than at present.
- During the Xerothermic Interval, mesophytic forest was fragmented and xeric vegetation spread. Opinion on the magnitude of this aridity varies widely, from semi-desert conditions to no warming at all.
- Western evidence is thinner. The Xerothermic has been challenged for the Southwest (Martin, 1963).
- Forest-animal disjunctions and relict populations are explained by the premise that biotas occupied higher latitudes and altitudes during the Climatic Optimum. Forest animals later retreated and expanded westward along [[Gallery Forest]] corridors.
- Biogeographic examples include:
  - Disjunct butterflies, amphibians, reptiles and white-footed mice in peninsular Nova Scotia. These require a northward transposition of about 300 km, with entry via the Isthmus of Chignecto.
  - Relict amphibians and reptiles in Mississippi floodplain forests and swamps, requiring a 150–300 km northern displacement.
  - Snake intergradation patterns explained by the southern subspecies occurring about 300 km farther north.
  - Prairie snake relicts, requiring a 150–450 km shift in isotherms.
- Fishes and amphibians are disjunct between the [[Interior Low Plateaus]] and [[Ozark Plateaus]], and are absent from the lower Mississippi embayment and central Illinois prairie. The author notes that the list of fishes with this hiatus is larger than previously believed.
- The [[Black Hills]] of South Dakota hold forest-animal pockets surrounded by prairie.
- The author calls the Xerothermic hypothesis strongly supported but still a hypothesis, with growing evidence that it may not have been any drier than the present.

### Prehistoric Period (3,000–200 B.P.)

- Information comes from midden and cave deposits. These are biased toward animals humans used, are often undated, and can be contaminated by burrowing animals.
- At the [[Cahokia Site]], Parmalee (1958) found 325 specimens of over 20 marine mollusk species mixed with native shells.
- Recorded taxa include bison, elk, gray wolf, jaguar, passenger pigeon and many others. Some, like the bison, had brief tenures. The bison did not appear in numbers east of the Mississippi until about AD 1600 and was eliminated in the East by about AD 1800.
- In central Florida, four sites dated ca. 5,000–3,500 B.P. contained a fauna virtually identical to today's.
- In the West, change is more localized. Longnose gar and blue sucker are known from archaeological sites in northern New Mexico, where neither genus occurs today.
- Climatic extremes (11th and 16th centuries) were discussed at a 1962 Aspen conference. The symposium concluded that faunal remains have serious limitations for climatic inference.
- Eastern and central faunal records show the Xerothermic sequence poorly. The author cites only a few eastward prairie-related records (spotted skunk, plains pocket gopher, eastern mole) as support.
- The marsh rice rat's range shrinkage suggests gradual cooling over the past 1,000 years. Its northward spread with maize agriculture occurred 4,000–5,000 years after the Climatic Optimum.

### Historic Period (200 B.P. to Present)

- European settlement produced the most conspicuous effects: habitat destruction, accelerating range fragmentation, and extinctions.
- Extirpated species include the harelip sucker, great auk, Labrador duck, passenger pigeon, Carolina parakeet and coastal mink. Endangered species include the Colorado squawfish and some southwestern fishes.
- Naturalized exotic fishes include the [[Carp]] and [[Goldfish]], and the brown trout is among the exotic fish species. Exotics have not, as far as is known, completely eliminated any native species through direct competition or predation.
- The transplanting of fishes is the most extensive among vertebrates. Families including Salmonidae, Esocidae, Centrarchidae and Percidae have been stocked repeatedly. Minnow-bucket releases add to the number.
- The author notes that the effect of stocking can't be judged apart from habitat alteration.

## Key Findings

**1. Xerothermic-era range disjunctions.** Warm-dry conditions fragmented deciduous forest and left forest-animal relicts. The Xerothermic is the best available explanation for many biogeographic patterns, but the author holds it as a hypothesis.

**2. Eastward and westward range movements.** Grassland, forest-edge and aridity-tolerant species moved eastward during the warm-dry phase and retreated westward as the climate cooled. Some relicts persist in suitable local habitats.

**3. Archaeozoological records are weak for climate inference.** Midden and cave remains are seldom well dated, are biased, and rarely correlate with the palaeoecological climatic sequence. Large, wide-ranging birds and mammals are poor ecological indicators compared with amphibians, reptiles and small mammals.

**4. Ranges were largely stable before European contact.** Archaeozoological data suggest biotas had adjusted to local conditions by roughly 3,000 years ago, and ranges were probably similar to those when Europeans arrived.

**5. Fish dispersal depends on habitat.** Except in arid regions, fishes spread when habitat alteration makes the new water suitable. Eastern forms have shrunk, and tolerant prairie and plains species have spread eastward through deforestation.

**6. Canals and introductions drove fish invasions.** The [[Welland Canal]] (1824) allowed the [[Sea Lamprey]] and [[Alewife]] to bypass the [[Niagara Escarpment]], but their explosive spread into the western Great Lakes came only in recent decades. The author found no comparable dispersals through the other navigation canals.

**7. Saltatorial colonization by introduced fishes.** Species such as the [[Mosquitofish]], [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[American Smelt]] have appeared suddenly and explosively in waters where they were never introduced.

**8. Documented fish losses in the Southwest and Illinois.** Miller (1961) reported that almost 20% of native southwestern species were endangered or extinct in the past 100 years, and that 36 exotic species had been introduced. In contrast, Larimore and Smith (1963) found a smaller effect on the native fishes of Champaign County, Illinois, despite 60 years of environmental change and more than half a dozen exotics.

**9. Amphibians and reptiles changed mostly through range shrinkage.** Because of low vagility and narrow temperature tolerance, these groups changed their ranges slowly. Most documented changes reflect shifts in abundance rather than range. Artificial ponds and ditches may have aided some grassland species.

**10. Birds and mammals show mainly northward and eastward shifts.** Birds such as the cardinal, mockingbird and cattle egret spread northward. Eastern expansion of prairie species such as the horned lark and western meadowlark was also observed. Human modification of habitats is judged more responsible for these expansions than recent climate change.

**11. Relict distribution is the dominant trend.** The author concludes that fragmentation is increasing and that almost all vertebrate populations in many regions are now relicts, separated by barriers ranging from a paved highway to hundreds of square miles of unsuitable habitat.

**12. Data gaps remain.** Range responses are poorly documented for the West and for marine and estuarine habitats. The author notes that the loss of estuarine fisheries is well known, but the responses of individual species within those habitats are almost unknown.
