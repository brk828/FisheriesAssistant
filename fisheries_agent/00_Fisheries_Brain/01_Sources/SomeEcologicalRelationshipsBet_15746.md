# Some Ecological Relationships Between Yellow Perch and Cutthroat Trout in Thompson Lakes, Montana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Percidae #Salmonidae #FisheriesManagement

## Executive Summary
This 1954 study by John B. Echo examines the competitive and predatory dynamics between introduced [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) and native [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*) in the Thompson Lakes chain, Montana. Following the introduction of yellow perch into historically trout-only waters, researchers documented a classic case of ecological interference: a dense, stunted perch population dominating the littoral (shoal) zones during critical spring periods, while salmonids retreated to deep water. The study found minimal direct competition for spawning substrate but significant predation by trout on perch fry, alongside evidence that the perch population's stunting was likely due to overcrowding and intraspecific competition rather than trout competition. The paper concludes with experimental rotenone-based "spot poisoning" techniques aimed at selectively reducing perch density to benefit the sport fishery.

## Background & Description
Data focused on age, growth, and length metrics rather than detailed morphology.

### Yellow Perch (*Perca flavescens*)
- **Growth Pattern:** Severely stunted population compared to other studied lakes (see [[Eschmeyer 1938]], [[Hile 1942]]).
- **Calculated Mean Total Length by Age Class (in):**
  - Age I: 1.9
  - Age II: 3.4
  - Age III: 4.4
  - Age IV: 5.6
  - Age V: 6.6
- **Hook-and-line average (n=900):** 6.2 in.
- **Grand average (n=3,200, all capture methods):** 6.4 in. Only 1% exceeded 7.5 in.
- **Larval size:** ~0.3 in TL at hatching; ~0.6 in when schooling near surface begins.

### Cutthroat Trout (*Salmo clarki*)
- **Growth Pattern:** "About average" growth rate, comparable to or exceeding other lakes in the drainage (cf. [[Calhoun 1944]] Blue Lake, California data).
- **Calculated Mean Total Length by Age Class (in):**
  - Age I: 5.1
  - Age II: 7.8
  - Age III: 10.3
  - Age IV: 12.5
- **Maximum recorded length:** 23.7 in.
- **Average of all trolled specimens (n=41):** 10.6 in.
- **Spawning adults:** Averaged ~13 in TL (range with a ripe male at 13.7 in, ripe female at 13.2 in).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat & Physical Setting
- **System:** Three interconnected lakes (Upper, Middle, Lower Thompson) forming headwaters of the [[Thompson River]].
  - Upper Thompson: 375 acres, max depth 50 ft (~90% <10 ft deep).
  - Middle Thompson: 730 acres, max depth 160 ft.
  - Lower Thompson: 240 acres, max depth 150 ft (~25% <10 ft deep).
- **Thermal Stratification:** Lower and Middle lakes stratified in both study years; surface temps reached 75°F in July.
- **Water Chemistry:** High alkalinity (methyl orange ~220-236 p.p.m.), pH ~8.3-8.4, indicating a hardwater, productive system typical of Rocky Mountain valley lakes.
- **Dissolved Oxygen:** Showed clinograde decline with depth in summer, dropping to 1.8-2.5 p.p.m. at 35 ft by September — a factor restricting deepwater habitability for coldwater species during late summer stratification.

### Spatial Distribution & Niche Partitioning
- **Yellow Perch:** Strongly littoral. Concentrated in shoal (5-10 ft) and weed bed (10-15 ft) zones from late April through summer. Formed massive fry schools (e.g., one band ~4 ft wide extending nearly 1 mile along Middle Thompson Lake margin).
- **Cutthroat Trout & Kokanee ([[Oncorhynchus nerka kennerlyi]]):** Predominantly deepwater (35-70 ft) during summer; surface/trolling catches of both species declined sharply by mid-June, essentially vacating surface waters by July-August.
- **Niche Overlap:** Minimal spatial overlap during peak summer, reducing direct competitive interaction but creating strong **predator-prey dynamics** when trout entered perch nursery zones.

### Spawning Ecology
- **Yellow Perch:**
  - Spawned early May; first ripe fish caught late April 1953.
  - Eggs deposited in gelatinous ribbons on submerged substrates from surface to ~5 ft depth (commonly ~3 ft).
  - **Substrate Preference (experimental):** Conifers (19 egg masses) >> Birch (2) >> Chicken wire (0), confirming strong preference for coniferous woody debris over other structures.
  - Egg deposition also on birch, bulrush ([[Scirpus validus]]), and stonewort ([[Chara]]).
  - Hatching occurred within ~1-3 weeks (embryos visible May 10; hatched by May 29).
- **Cutthroat Trout:**
  - Spawned late May in tributary streams (e.g., [[Davis Creek]], [[Boiling Springs Creek]]), not in the lakes themselves.
  - No trout fry or young-of-year ever found in lake habitat itself — obligate stream-rearing for juveniles.
- **Interspecific Competition for Spawning Sites:** None observed among perch, trout, suckers, or squawfish despite spatial/temporal overlap in nearshore areas.

### Trophic Relationships / Diet
| Food Item | % Yellow Perch Stomachs (n=900) | % Cutthroat Trout Stomachs (n=83) |
|---|---|---|
| Any food present | 90% | 98% |
| Small fish (mostly perch fry) | 2% | 40% |
| Immature aquatic insects | 28% | 24% |
| Adult aquatic insects | 4% | 43% |
| Crustacea (*Daphnia*, *Leptodora*, *Gammarus*) | 82% | 23% |
| Snails | 2% | 0% |

- **Key Predation Finding:** Cutthroat trout were significant predators of yellow perch fry — up to 21 fry per stomach (avg. 7.5). Smallest trout with perch fry in stomach: 6.5 in TL.
- Unlike patterns described by [[Moffett and Hunt 1943]] and [[Allen 1935]] for perch elsewhere (dietary shift to fish >5 in body length), Thompson Lakes perch showed **no significant shift to piscivory**, remaining primarily planktivorous/insectivorous even at larger sizes — likely contributing to the stunted growth via resource limitation.
- [[Mountain Whitefish]] and Kokanee fed exclusively on plankton.
- Suckers examined had empty stomachs or only detritus.

## Key Findings
1. **Habitat Segregation Reduces Direct Competition:** Yellow perch dominate littoral/shoal zones while salmonids (cutthroat trout, kokanee) occupy deep water during the critical spring-summer period, minimizing spatial competitive overlap but not eliminating trophic interaction.
2. **No Spawning Site Competition:** Despite spatial proximity of spawning perch, suckers, and squawfish, no interspecific aggression or competition for spawning substrate was documented; cutthroat trout spawn exclusively in tributary streams, spatially decoupled from lake-spawning perch.
3. **Asymmetric Predation:** Cutthroat trout substantially prey upon yellow perch fry (40% of trout stomachs containing fish, mostly perch fry), while perch predation on fish (including potentially trout fry) was negligible (2%) — suggesting trout benefit nutritionally from perch presence, but this does not seem to control perch population size.
4. **Stunted Perch Population:** The yellow perch population exhibited unusually poor, stunted growth (avg. 6.4 in) compared to other published populations, likely due to intraspecific overcrowding rather than trout competition, since diet remained planktivorous/insectivorous rather than shifting to cannibalism/piscivory as seen in other systems.
5. **Effective Control Method Identified:** Targeted rotenone/Fish-Tox "spot poisoning" of perch fry schools and baited adult concentrations achieved near-complete localized kill, offering an economically feasible alternative to whole-lake treatment for selective population reduction.
6. **Management Recommendations:**
   - Partial/spot poisoning of yellow perch concentrations in littoral zones beginning ~June 20.
   - Post-treatment stocking of cutthroat trout fry (300+/acre) in treated littoral zones.
   - Heavy stocking of larger (7+ in) cutthroat trout in Middle Thompson Lake to exploit their demonstrated predation on perch fry.
   - Installation of a screen barrier in the channel connecting Middle and Lower Thompson Lakes to prevent fish movement/reinvasion.
   - Encourage angler harvest of both yellow perch and underutilized [[Mountain Whitefish]] to reduce standing biomass through fishing pressure.
7. **Broader Context:** Findings align with European studies ([[Swynnerton and Worthington 1940]]; [[Worthington 1949]]) suggesting perch introductions into trout waters generally necessitate perch population control to sustain trout fisheries, representing one of the first such documented cases in the United States.
