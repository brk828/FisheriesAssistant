# Basic Survey of Waters in District IV - Lower Colorado River Backwater Lakes

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #centrarchidae #warmwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This completion report (Project F-7-R-4, Work Plan 4, Job A-1) documents a comprehensive fisheries investigation of backwater lakes along the [[Lower Colorado River]] within the [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]], Arizona, conducted from January 16 to October 15, 1961. The study aimed to determine which backwater lake possesses optimal physical, limnological, and biological conditions for desirable fisheries production. Historical analysis revealed a significant decline in [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) condition since 1951, with age-growth analysis indicating slower growth rates and reduced average fish size. A preliminary creel census contacted 421 fishermen who expended 1,913 hours to harvest 578 fish, with 74.2% of anglers fishing backwater areas that yielded 84.1% of bass, 51.0% of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and 82.9% of [[Bluegill Sunfish]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*). The average catch rate was 0.30 fish per man-hour over 4.5-hour trips. Among study lakes, [[No Name Lake Number 2]] exhibited the most favorable conditions for fish growth, followed by [[Martinez Lake]], while [[Mittry Lake]] showed better bass condition despite extreme limnological conditions.

## Background & Description

The [[Lower Colorado River]] within [[District IV]] is controlled by irrigation demands, with flow reaching peak during summer months and declining September through February. Mean annual flows declined steadily from approximately 20,000 cubic feet per second (c.f.s.) in 1952 to 9,818 c.f.s. in 1960 (Table I). This 19-year investigation combined data from 1951-52 California Department of Fish and Game creel census with 1960-61 field sampling to assess changes in fisheries condition.

Primary study areas included [[Martinez Lake]], [[Mittry Lake]], [[No Name Lake Number 1]], and [[No Name Lake Number 2]]. These backwater lakes are characterized by prodigious growths of rooted aquatic vegetation including cattails (*Typha angustifolia*, *T. domingensis*), marsh cane (*Phragmites communis*), bulrush (*Scirpus californicus*, *S. acutus*, *S. olneyi*), with summer-fall submergent vegetation of sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*) and spiny naiad (*Najas marina*).

Historical fish introductions included [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) in 1954, [[Redear Sunfish]] (*Lepomis macrolophus*) date undetermined, [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*) in 1959 and 1961, with [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) proposed. Spawning seasons varied by species: largemouth bass April-September, channel catfish June-August, black crappie March-April, bluegill and redear sunfish May-September, threadfin shad March-September, and [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) February-October.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water Flow and Level Fluctuations**: Mean annual flows declined from 20,320 c.f.s. (1942) to 9,818 c.f.s. (1960), with annual variations up to 10,000 c.f.s. between years. Sluicing operations at [[Imperial Dam]] began in 1947 (weekly Thursday operations for 1-2 hours) to remove silt, declining after 1956 as excess water availability diminished. These operations potentially affected egg survival through spawning ground exposure, though no apparent detrimental effects were documented.

**Water Chemistry**: Surface water temperatures ranged 49-90°F across study lakes, with shallow [[Mittry Lake]] (average depth 4 feet) experiencing greatest daily fluctuation and midday temperatures exceeding 90°F. Stratification was inhibited by shallow depths and wind-driven circulation. pH remained constant at 8.0-8.3 (average 8.2) across all study lakes. Total alkalinity averaged 136 ppm ([[No Name Lake Number 1]]), 132 ppm ([[No Name Lake Number 2]]), 129 ppm ([[Martinez Lake]]), and 356 ppm ([[Mittry Lake]]). [[Mittry Lake]] exhibited extreme limnological conditions with turbidity averaging 56.6 ppm (range 22-90 ppm) compared to 9.6 ppm, 3.1 ppm, and 8.2 ppm for the other study lakes respectively. Total solids, chlorides, hardness, and sulfates were considerably elevated in [[Mittry Lake]] (2,614, 727, 609, and 649 ppm respectively) compared to river-associated lakes.

**Habitat Structure**: Emergent marsh areas of canes and cattails increased with declining water levels, providing cover for small fish fry and reducing predation rates. Plant encroachment was especially noticeable in [[Mittry Lake]]. Bottom fauna included aquatic insect nymphs (dragonflies, damselflies, caddisflies) and abundant midge larvae in vegetated areas, with limited life in sand-silt bottoms. Small freshwater clams were abundant in main channel and side lakes, with young providing food for redear sunfish during spring.

**Plankton Production**: Plankton production was generally low across study areas, supporting a large population of the almost exclusively planktivorous threadfin shad. Chlorophyll content ranked: [[Mittry Lake]] highest (37.1-61.6 mg/m³), [[Martinez Lake]] second (8.4-15.1 mg/m³), [[No Name Lake Number 2]] third, [[No Name Lake Number 1]] fourth, and main river channel lowest (4.8 mg/m³). Phosphate levels averaged 0.28 ppm ([[No Name Lake Number 1]]), 0.34 ppm ([[No Name Lake Number 2]]), 0.37 ppm ([[Martinez Lake]]), and 0.81 ppm ([[Mittry Lake]]).

**Fishing Pressure**: Angler use increased from 1.8 anglers/surface acre/year (1954-55) to 6.7 anglers/surface acre/year (1960), though current use remained a fraction of potential. Of total anglers, 74.2% fished backwater areas versus main channel (20.9%), secondary channel (4.9%), or potholes (1.6%).

## Key Findings

**Largemouth Bass Population Decline**: Age-growth analysis revealed significant decline in largemouth bass condition since 1951. Average calculated total length at end of each year of growth showed consistent reduction: Age 1 declined from 7.3 inches (1951-52) to 5.8 inches (1960-61), Age 2 from 10.3 to 9.6 inches, Age 3 from 12.4 to 11.4 inches, Age 4 from 14.0 to 12.6 inches—representing an average loss of 1.2 inches growth through age four (Table V). Growth rates were determined from 36 scale samples (1951-52) and 46 samples (1960-61).

**Condition Factor Analysis**: Approximately 66% of 1960-61 bass population sampled fell in poor condition range (C=44 for river study area, C=47 for [[Mittry Lake]]) compared to C=57 in 1951-52. Poor condition defined as C=35-45, average C=46-55, good C=56-65. Length-weight relationships showed 1951 bass consistently heavier than 1960-61 bass at equivalent lengths (Figure 1). At 12.5 inches: 1951 bass weighed 1.09 pounds versus 0.90 pounds in 1960-61; at 16.0 inches: 2.51 pounds versus 2.03 pounds.

**Creel Census Results**: Preliminary census (April-October 1961, 19 weekend check days) documented 421 fishermen, 1,913 man-hours, 578 total fish harvested. Average angler trip 4.5 hours with catch rate 0.30 fish per man-hour. Species-specific catch rates: bass 0.142/hour, channel catfish 0.073/hour, bluegill sunfish 0.074/hour, black crappie 0.0026/hour. Average harvested sizes: largemouth bass 12.4 inches (range 7.0-21.0), channel catfish 14.4 inches (range 7.0-30.0), bluegill sunfish 6.5 inches (range 6.0-7.0). Comparison with 1951 census showed decreased bass catch rate (0.304 to 0.135 per man-hour) and reduced average length (14.0 to 12.4 inches). Angler residence: 38.5% Arizona, 61.5% out-of-state (mostly California).

**Backwater Lake Productivity Rankings**: [[No Name Lake Number 2]] exhibited most favorable conditions for fish growth among river-associated lakes, with lowest turbidity (3.1 ppm average), moderate alkalinity (132 ppm), and stable phosphate levels (0.34 ppm). [[Martinez Lake]] ranked second, [[No Name Lake Number 1]] last among river lakes. [[Mittry Lake]], despite extreme limnological conditions (highest turbidity 56.6 ppm, alkalinity 356 ppm, total solids 2,614 ppm), showed better bass condition (C=47) than river study lakes (C=44), attributed to limited fry survival maintaining population balance and favorable threadfin shad size distribution.

**Threadfin Shad Population Structure**: Length-frequency distributions revealed larger shad dominated river study lakes, creating unfavorable predator-forage relationships (Figure 2). [[Mittry Lake]] showed more balanced size distribution providing desirable-sized forage for growing bass. Sample sizes: [[No Name Lake Number 2]] 104 fish, [[Martinez Lake]] 121 fish, [[No Name Lake Number 1]] 49 fish, [[Mittry Lake]] 59 fish. Shad condition factors: river area C=30, [[Mittry Lake]] C=34.

**Other Species Conditions**: [[Channel Catfish]] condition rated fair (C=30, average standard C=27), averaging one pound at 15 inches. Length-weight relationship: Log W = -2.76733 + 3.37942 Log L. [[Black Crappie]] condition fair (C=45-57), averaging one-half pound at 10 inches. [[Bluegill Sunfish]] condition: river area C=54, [[Mittry Lake]] C=47 (poor under 50, average 51-80, good over 80). [[Carp]] condition: river area C=45, [[Mittry Lake]] C=46 (average standard C=48).

**Population Dynamics Theory**: Investigators theorized that threadfin shad introduction (1954) increased largemouth bass fry survival through: (1) availability of shad larvae during bass spawning providing good initial start, (2) abundant small shad reducing predation on bass fry, (3) large numbers of small shad decreasing adult predation on small bass. Combined with increased emergent vegetation cover and reduced water fertility, this created inter-class competition where excessive small fish overtaxed food supply. Few individuals surpassed critical size barrier, proportion of stunted fish increased, and number reaching acceptable angler size decreased—a universal tendency in warmwater lakes.

**Management Recommendations**: (1) Continue study of fish population conditions and limnological factors affecting backwater lake productivity; (2) Include new study lakes to obtain complete evaluation of ecological types providing most suitable fisheries production; (3) Determine existing relationships between threadfin shad and primary predator species; (4) Consider regulating to increase or remove bag and possession limits on largemouth bass and channel catfish; (5) Initiate increased public education program on management principles and population balance, emphasizing catching and keeping all fish sizes; (6) Develop water level manipulation plan for population control after detailed study completion, though irrigation demands make this unlikely or impossible.
