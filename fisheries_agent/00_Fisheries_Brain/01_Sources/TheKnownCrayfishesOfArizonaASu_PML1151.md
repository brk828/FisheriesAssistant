# The Known Crayfishes of Arizona: A Summary Report

#crustacea #freshwater-ecology #crayfish

## Executive Summary

This introductory report, prepared by [[Terry Inman]] for [[Paul C. Marsh]] of the [[Arizona State University Center for Environmental Studies]] (Bio.310), describes the first systematic effort to document the crayfishes of Arizona. The state is unusual because it has no native crayfish species, even though most other U.S. states have at least one. Crayfishes have appeared in Arizona waters within roughly the last 30 years, mainly through stocking as forage for game fish and through probable bait-bucket introductions by anglers. The study aims to collect specimens from across the state, identify them to species where possible, catalog each collection, and produce a distribution map and summary report. The document covers the background, collection methods, preservation, and identification approach. Species-level results, site data, and appendices are not included in this excerpt.

## Background & Description

Crayfishes are the most diverse group of their kind in the world, and North America holds the greatest number of species. The document cites [[Taylor et al. 1996]] for 338 recognized taxa in the United States and Canada, comprising 308 species and 30 subspecies. Arizona has no native species, and its earliest records show no crayfish.

Introduction history is documented as follows:

- The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AZGFD) stocking records span 1931 to 1991. Crayfish were stocked as forage for [[Trout]] and [[Largemouth Bass]].
- The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] also stocked crayfish, though the full extent of its introductions is unknown.
- AZGFD reported three introduction sites, all lakes: one in 1971 and two in 1991.
- [[Bait Bucket Introduction]] by sport fishermen is considered the most likely explanation for their widespread presence, though no direct evidence has been recorded.

Crayfish have received little attention from federal, state, or scientific bodies. Their distribution, abundance, and species composition in Arizona had not been inventoried or studied before this project.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Collection methods.** Crayfish can be captured by [[Seining]] (fine mesh), [[Minnow Traps]] baited with meat, [[Dip Netting]], and [[Electrofishing]]. Dip netting, electrofishing, and seining are active methods. Baited minnow traps are passive and may need an overnight set to produce good catches. Bait choice strongly affects results. Fish pieces are highly attractive, and oily fish such as smelt or anchovies are preferred over non-oily fish such as cod or pollock. This preference is drawn from the author's experience and from [[Kutka et al. 1992]].

**Preservation.** Specimens are placed in 6 percent neutral [[Formalin Preservation]] for 12 hours up to one week, depending on size ([[Hobbs 1972]]). They are then rinsed in running water for several hours and transferred to 70 percent ethyl alcohol. Alternatively, 20 to 30 percent isopropyl alcohol may be used.

**Sex-form dimorphism and identification.** Accurate species identification depends on Form I males. Form I and Form II males occur in the subfamilies [[Cambarinae]] and [[Cambarellinae]], which undergo [[Cyclic Dimorphism]] linked to the reproductive cycle:

- In northern climates, breeding males molt at the end of the first breeding season into a juvenile-like Form II. A semiannual molt then returns them to Form I. Northern populations typically contain only Form I or Form II adult males.
- In lower-latitude species without a defined breeding season, Form I and Form II males can coexist in the same population.
- Form I males are identified by corneous (horny) terminal projections on the distal ends of the first [[Pleopods]].
- Body size does not indicate form. Molting can regress a male to Form II, so a Form II male may be larger than a Form I male.
- Most identification keys are based on Form I males because their pleopods are more distinct from those of Form II males and of closely related species' juveniles.
- The Form I approach applies to all North American crayfish except the genus [[Pacifastacus]], which lacks cyclic dimorphism.

## Key Findings

- **Global and regional diversity:** The U.S. and Canada have 338 recognized crayfish taxa (308 species, 30 subspecies). Arizona is the only contiguous state with no native crayfish species.
- **Introduction pathways:** Crayfish arrived in Arizona in recent decades, mainly through AZGFD and USFWS stocking (records 1931 to 1991) and likely through bait-bucket releases.
- **Documented introduction sites:** AZGFD reports three introduction sites, all lakes (one in 1971, two in 1991). The full USFWS introduction record is unknown.
- **Knowledge gap:** Arizona crayfish distribution, abundance, and species composition were unknown and uninventoried before this study.
- **Methodological guidance:** Active methods (dip netting, electrofishing, seining) are effective. Baited minnow traps require overnight deployment. Oily fish are the preferred bait.
- **Preservation protocol:** Fix in 6 percent neutral formalin (12 hours to one week), rinse in running water, then store in 70 percent ethanol or 20 to 30 percent isopropanol.
- **Identification rule:** Species identification relies on Form I males, identified by corneous terminal projections on the first pleopods. Size is not a reliable indicator of form. The exception is Pacifastacus, which has no cyclic dimorphism.
- **Project scope:** The study will collect and catalog Arizona crayfish, identify them to species where possible, map distributions, and produce a summary report. Appendices A, B, and C cover anatomy, identification, and specimen catalogs, but they are not included in this excerpt.
