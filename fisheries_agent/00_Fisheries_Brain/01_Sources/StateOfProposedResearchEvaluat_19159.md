# Evaluation of Recovery Models for Desert Pupfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This unpublished, preliminary research statement by [[Jason B. Dunham]] proposes a framework for evaluating a [[Hierarchical Recovery Model]] for the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*). The species once ranged across much of the American Southwest and northwest Mexico, but habitat destruction and introduced exotic species have sharply reduced its populations. The proposal argues that reintroductions and transplants, supported by protected [[Artificial Refugia]], will be needed for long-term survival. It outlines three research components: an [[Allozyme Survey]] of single-locus variation, a study of [[Quantitative Genetic Variation]] under laboratory conditions, and [[Computer Simulation]] of the recovery model. The document is explicitly preliminary and contains no literature citations.

## Background & Description

The introduction covers the genetic consequences of habitat fragmentation. When a formerly continuous population is subdivided, [[Genetic Drift]] can produce differences in allele frequencies among subpopulations, and loss of gene flow may lead to fixation of alternate alleles. Loss of genetic variation depends on the number of generations since subdivision and the founding population size (Nei et al. 1975). Rare alleles are especially vulnerable in small founder populations, which reduces a species' capacity to evolve or persist in changing environments.

The desert pupfish was listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1986 (Schoenherr 1988). Named threats include habitat destruction and exotic species such as *Oreochromis* spp., *Micropterus*, and [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). A recovery plan was being drafted at the time (P.C. Marsh, personal communication).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The desert pupfish naturally inhabits shallow, marginal areas of desert streams. Alternating wet and dry cycles strongly affect river flows, and the author proposes that pupfish track these fluctuations. In dry periods, populations contract to the most perennial stream sections or headsprings. In wet periods, they expand rapidly into the larger available habitat. The filling of the Salton Sea in California connected previously isolated populations, and before exotic species became established, pupfish became very numerous (Schoenherr 1988). The author describes pupfish populations as highly dynamic, with large fluctuations in size and attendant genetic consequences. The historic magnitude and frequency of these changes is unknown and may only be approximated from past climatic cycles.

## Key Findings

**Proposed recovery model (Figure 1):**
- Each remaining wild population would be used to establish three primary populations in suitable natural habitats with high persistence probability.
- Each primary population would establish three secondary populations in less optimal, quasi-natural habitats, which may need periodic restocking from primary populations.
- Limited gene flow would be allowed between secondary and primary populations, with somewhat higher gene flow between secondary populations.

**Four key uncertainties:**
1. What population sizes are required to maintain genetic integrity?
2. What migration or gene flow levels balance inbreeding and outbreeding?
3. How many secondary and primary populations should be established from each wild source?
4. How many hierarchical levels are needed, and are secondary populations necessary?

**Proposed research components:**
- **Single-locus variation:** Prior allozyme work by [[Turner]] (1983, 1984) on natural and refugium populations (sample sizes 26 to 46 individuals) found few overall genetic differences, but some allele frequency differences. The author proposes resurveying polymorphic loci with about 100 individuals per population to detect rare alleles (q < 0.01). The study would assess founding size, migration rates between refugia, and length of captivity.
- **Quantitative characters:** Growing fish from eggs collected across several locations under identical laboratory conditions would separate genetic from environmental variation. The author notes that additive genetic variation is thought to arise faster than single-locus variation (Lande 1987), so quantitative traits may reveal greater differences.
- **Computer simulation:** The hierarchical model would be implemented computationally, adding founding size, generation number, selection coefficients, habitat persistence, and bottleneck frequency. The idea was inspired by [[Gilpin PVA Model]].

**Methods and open problems:**
- Refugium fish would be collected only when populations are large enough to remove 100 or more individuals without major population reduction.
- Eggs would be obtained from several sites per refugium to avoid sampling from few parents.
- Unresolved issues include "temporal pseudoreplication" from different hatching times, group size for rearing, choice of characters and enzymes, and what counts as "acceptable" genetic variation.

**Planned tasks (approximately 13 items):** These include a priori statistical planning, literature review of enzymes and characters, coordination with refugia caretakers, reading Falconer (1989) and Hartl (1988), a fall 1990 evolutionary techniques course, funding searches, lab preparation, recording habitat and founding data for each refugium, and building a simulation program in True Basic.
