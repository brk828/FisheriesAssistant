# Home Range and Seasonal Movements of Muskellunge as Determined by Radiotelemetry

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 study by [[John D. Minor]] and [[E. J. Crossman]] used [[Radiotelemetry]] to test whether adult [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy*) establish home ranges and which environmental factors shape their use of habitat. Sixteen adults were tracked from May 1975 to October 1976 in a small, shallow, heavily vegetated lake ([[Nogies Creek]]) and a large, deeper lake ([[Stony Lake]]) in the [[Kawartha Lakes]] region of Ontario. All fish established summer and winter home ranges, and the timing of home range establishment and breakdown was linked mainly to water temperature and spawning.

## Background & Description

Habitat use is central to esocid management, but prior work relied mainly on mark-and-recapture, which gives few repeat locations, or on [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]], which is strongly attenuated by dense vegetation. Earlier telemetry studies of [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) reported transient movements with little long-term localization, and [[Crossman]] (1977) suggested restricted summer movements in muskellunge. The authors argued that radio signals are not attenuated by vegetation and do not require a line of sight, making them better suited to vegetated waters and meandering rivers.

Fish were captured by trap net during the post-spawn period (May–June), anaesthetized with tricaine methanesulfonate (100 mg/L), surgically implanted with transmitters in the body cavity, and released at their capture sites. Transmitters operated at 50 MHz, were padlock-shaped (60 × 25 × 12 mm), and weighed 14 g or 21 g in air depending on expected lifetime. Fish were located by triangulation four to six times daily, and home range was calculated with the grid-square method of [[Rongstad and Tester]] (1969).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nogies Creek** is a 35-ha, shallow lake (mean depth 1.8 m, maximum 5.0 m) fed by 6.4 km of meandering stream, with 47 ha of littoral zone. It is heavily vegetated, with submergent cover dominated by [[Potamogeton]] spp.

**Stony Lake** is a eutrophic lake of about 3,725 ha with a mean depth of 12.5 m and a maximum of 32 m in channels. About 1,800 ha are shallower than 3 m, with roughly 50% submergent vegetation cover (Potamogeton and Myriophyllum spp.). Thermal stratification occurs near 15 m in August and September, when the hypolimnion becomes anaerobic.

Water temperature was the main seasonal driver: fish established winter home ranges below 5 °C, and most were absent from home ranges in spring and fall at 8–15 °C.

## Key Findings

- **Sample:** 14 fish at Nogies Creek (5 males, 9 females, 700–920 mm fork length) and 2 large fish at Stony Lake (a 960 mm male and a 1,005 mm female), tracked May 1975–October 1976.
- **Home ranges established by all fish:** Winter home ranges (0.5–2.0 °C, January–March) averaged about 0.32 ha (SD = 0.05) and did not overlap at Nogies Creek. Summer home ranges were 0.6–1.1 ha for Nogies Creek fish and the Stony Lake male, in water under 2.0 m deep. The Stony Lake female used 7.2 ha over a sunken island in 20 m channel water.
- **Overlap:** Where home ranges overlapped, never more than one fish occupied the overlap area at a time.
- **Spring breakdown:** Fish left winter ranges by mid-April at 8.0–10.5 °C. Spawning occurred in daylight at 10.5–15.5 °C, in areas under 1.0 m deep (mean 0.65 m).
- **Male behaviour:** Males returned to the lake after spawning and set up summer home ranges at about 16 °C; they remained sedentary, and the Stony Lake male used shallow water (under 1.5 m) with rocky shelves and under 30% vegetation.
- **Female behaviour at Nogies Creek:** Females wandered the creek at 14.0–23.5 °C, travelling significantly farther than males (mean 121 m/day versus 32 m/day), and reached the lake by mid-August. They then held one or two smaller home ranges in deeper water (mean 2.8 m) with about 75% dense vegetation.
- **Stony Lake female:** Travelled up to 1.2 km/day after spawning before settling on a summer range, with an inverse relationship between contact frequency and distance from the island. Her summer mean temperature was 18.5 °C and daily travel was 150 m. In late October at 14.5 °C she left her range and travelled 2.2 km, after which signals were lost, possibly from transmitter failure.
- **Fall breakdown:** Males abandoned summer ranges earlier than females and reestablished winter ranges in November. Females did not settle until mid-December at 3.5 °C.
- **Size relationship:** Home range size increased linearly with fork length (r = 0.86, N = 15; Y = 619 + 273.1X, with X in hectares).
- **Movement peaks:** Maximum daily travel occurred between 10 and 15 °C during fall breakdown. Maximum movement reached 2.3 km/day, with a mean of means of 530 m/day.
- **Tag effects:** Surgically implanted tags did not appear to affect growth or survival. Fish recaptured 15 months after implantation showed normal length and weight gain. Incisions healed in 7–12 weeks.
- **Conclusion:** Muskellunge establish home ranges in both summer and winter. In deeper waters, males and females follow the same seasonal sequence, but males use shallower and more heavily vegetated areas than females. The authors also suggest the transient movements of Nogies Creek females may reflect a search for deeper, cooler water.
