# Transplantations of Native Arizona Fishes: Records Through 1980

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University) and James E. Brooks ([[Arizona Game And Fish Department]]), compiles published and unpublished records of nearly 200 intentional and presumably accidental transplantations of 26 species and subspecies of native Arizona fishes through 1980. It records localities of origin, introduction sites, and the current status of introduced populations. It is described as the first in a projected series of documentations for transplants made under recovery programs of the [[Endangered Species Act]] and related agreements with the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]].

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Transplants range from accidental releases (bait or aquarium escapes) that can cause management problems, to carefully planned stockings meant to create new populations or prevent extinction. Recovery-driven transplants are being intensified, and public records are needed because unrecorded stockings can obscure or falsify natural distributions.
- **Historical milestones:** Propagation of [[Apache Trout]] in the 1960s prompted widespread stocking. In the 1970s, [[Sonoran Topminnow]] was propagated and stocked for mosquito control.
- **Methods:** Early records came from [[Minckley 1973]] (*Fishes of Arizona*). New data came from state and federal stocking files, field notes (cited as "original data"), agency reports and the literature.
- **Locality coding:** Localities use the Arizona Resources Inventory System digital hierarchy (Silvey et al. 1984). Aquatic habitats begin with 600. The [[Colorado River]] watershed is 610, with major tributaries numbered from downstream to upstream, such as the [[Gila River]] (611) and the San Juan River (615). Subbasins are numbered after the decimal (Table 1 and Figure 1).
- **Inclusion criteria:** Introductions are recorded whether a species is established, only reported, or apparently extirpated. Transfers of wild fish to hatcheries and their progeny's return to the wild are included. Hatchery fish sent out of state for research are excluded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water development and non-native species** have depleted and modified Arizona waters, leaving natural aquatic habitats scarce.
- **Thermal limits:** Coldwater trouts are confined to high elevations by temperature requirements. Warmwater species spread readily through lowland habitats and are very hard to eradicate once established.
- **Competition:** Most Sonoran Topminnow stockings in its native lowland range, and many outside it, failed because of interactions with the non-native [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) (Meffe et al. 1983).
- **Climate limits:** The genus *Poeciliopsis* is tropical in origin, and winter water temperatures above 1,500 m almost certainly doomed introductions at such elevations.
- **Decline:** The state and regional ichthyofauna is declining toward extinction, which is the main reason so many species have been transplanted repeatedly.
- **Native-range policy:** Most transplants other than those to hatcheries were kept within native distributions. Transfers outside natural ranges are considered likely to cause unmanageable problems.

## Key Findings

- **Scope:** Records are documented for 26 species and subspecies, which comprise more than 75% of the native freshwater fish taxa known for Arizona. Table 2 gives origin, transplant site, dates, status and authority for each record.
- **Baitfish transfers:** Six taxa (including [[Sonoran Chub]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Little Colorado River Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Desert Mountain-Sucker]]) were probably moved by anglers as bait before legislation banned that use.
- **Aquarium release:** The occurrence of [[Beautiful Shiner]] in Phoenix canals is presumed to be an aquarium release.
- **Biological control:** The 1976 transfer of Flannelmouth Sucker to the Colorado River below Davis Dam aimed to control pestiferous dipterans (AGF files).
- **Sport fisheries:** Apache trout was introduced partly to create a unique sport fishery. Similar sport potential was noted for [[Gila Trout]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Yaqui Catfish]], if populations can be rebuilt.
- **Threat status:** Nineteen of the 26 fishes are listed or proposed for listing as threatened or endangered by state or federal agencies.
- **Lost populations:** Naturally reproducing populations of eight species have disappeared from Arizona. These are Gila trout, bonytail, Colorado squawfish, beautiful shiner, [[Razorback Sucker]], Yaqui sucker, Yaqui catfish and [[Desert Pupfish]] (lower Colorado River form). The undescribed [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]] is extinct. It was extirpated in nature by introduced [[Largemouth Bass]] and then lost from artificial holding.
- **Propagation:** Sixteen of the 26 taxa have been studied for propagation or are under hatchery production. The program serves four functions: reducing the need for future listings, providing broodstock for new or reintroduced populations, insuring against catastrophic loss, and supplying rare experimental animals for research. Key facilities include [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- **Reintroductions:** Eleven fishes have been reintroduced to suitable habitat in their native ranges, and some stocks are considered established. These include Apache trout, Gila trout, bonytail, humpback chub, [[Yaqui Chub]], Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, both desert pupfish subspecies, and both Sonoran topminnow subspecies. The rest await legislation, broodstock, or habitat.
- **Genetic note:** Hybrids among *Gila elegans*, *G. cypha* and *G. robusta* were produced at Willow Beach NFH in 1980 and later sacrificed for research.
- **Taxonomic note:** Desert pupfish populations are differentiated. Santa Clara Slough material is referred to the typical subspecies, *Cyprinodon macularius macularius*, while the Quitobaquito form has no subspecific name.
- **Future value:** New or reestablished populations can test genetic integrity of source populations and the ability of isolated endemics to adapt to new conditions.
