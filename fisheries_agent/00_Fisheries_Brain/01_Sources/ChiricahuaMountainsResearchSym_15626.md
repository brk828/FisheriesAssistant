# Chiricahua Mountains Research Symposium Proceedings (1992)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #terrestrial-ecology #sky-islands

## Executive Summary

These proceedings compile papers from the Chiricahua Mountains Research Symposium, held 16–17 March 1992 at Pine Canyon United Methodist Camp, Cochise County, Arizona, and published in December 1992 by the Southwest Parks and Monuments Association. The volume is a multidisciplinary survey of the [[Chiricahua Mountains]], a "sky island" range in the [[Madrean Archipelago]]. Its 26 contributions span geology, geophysics, plant ecology, fungi, vertebrate ecology, conservation and modelling. The primary aim was information exchange among researchers and land managers. Papers are research summaries, not peer-reviewed in the symposium itself.

The document contains no fish research. Its aquatic content is limited to a passing mention of native fishes in Cave Creek riparian systems and to the riparian and stream habitats that many taxa depend on. Taxon coverage is therefore terrestrial and avian.

## Background & Description

The symposium was sponsored by the [[National Park Service]], the [[USDA Forest Service]], the [[Southwestern Research Station]] of the American Museum of Natural History, and [[The Nature Conservancy]]. Organizers included Richard L. Anderson, Andrew M. Barton, J. Carrie Brown, Sarah A. Sloane, Wade Sherbrooke and Jeanne Wade. The evening keynote was Jerram Brown's study of the [[Mexican Jay]]. Proceedings are organized into themed sections:

- **Physical environment:** geology of the [[Turkey Creek Caldera]] and geophysical structure of the range (2 papers plus a figure).
- **Forests and plants:** pine distribution, plant richness, sensitive plants, a mycoflora survey, fire and flood history, and a lily management case (9 papers).
- **Animals:** birds, herpetofauna, owls, lizards, turkeys, bushtits, pikas, thick-billed parrots, accipiters and deer (about 14 papers).
- **Other applications:** cave-creek politics, GIS and remote sensing, partnerships, lichen air-quality monitoring, and island biogeography models (5 papers).

The document also records round-table topics: a planned international Sierra Madre–Sky Island conference, recreation planning and research promotion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Setting.** The Chiricahuas rise to 2975 m (9759 ft) and lie just south of the Madrean Line, a sharp biogeographic boundary that coincides with I-10 at that latitude. The range is an island of forest and woodland surrounded by grassland and desert. Its varied topography and isolation support high diversity. Bowers and McLaughlin's (1982) work, cited by [[Bennett and Kunzmann]], notes that the range holds about 30% of the Arizona flora and nearly 50% of North American bird species north of Mexico.

**Geology.** The [[Turkey Creek Caldera]] is a 20-km-diameter collapse structure formed about 26.9 Ma, from which 500–1000 km³ of rhyolite ash erupted as the [[Rhyolite Canyon Tuff]]. Gravity and magnetic data suggest the range is a north–south, west-dipping fault block bounded by the [[Apache Pass Fault Zone]].

**Fire and climate.** Pre-1900 low-intensity fires recurred every one to two decades. Fire suppression and grazing later altered this regime. Arid-humid boundary studies show that the elevation of woodland limits declines by about 200–283 m per degree of latitude to the south.

**Biodiversity and threats.** The [[Coronado National Forest]] has about 60 rare or sensitive plant species. Threats include fire suppression, grazing, floods and recreation. Several animals, including the [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] and [[Thick-billed Parrot]], are threatened or extirpated. The Portal Preserve on Cave Creek supports peripheral populations of native fish (*Rhinichthys osculus*), Sanborn's long-nosed bat and the Chiricahua leopard frog.

## Key Findings

**Mexican Jay social system (Brown & Brown).** Over 25 years of color-banding more than 1,400 jays, the authors found:
- Groups of 5–15 birds are nearly constant in size and composition, and defend group territories.
- Of six groups studied from 1969–70, five remained on their territories in 1992.
- Some first-winter birds rank at the top of dominance hierarchies, and some females outrank males.
- Three or four females may breed with several males in one territory.
- About 5% of breeding pairs are related. Adults survive at about 0.82 per year after age one, and the oldest birds reached 19 years.
- Breeding is keyed to precipitation, and drought reduces both egg-laying and nestling survival.

**Geology and caldera evolution (du Bray & Pallister).** Five stages are described. They include a stratified magma chamber, eruption of the Rhyolite Canyon Tuff, dacite intrusion as a laccolith more than 1 km thick, a 135 km³ moat rhyolite eruption, and radiometric ages of 26.9 ± 0.1 Ma.

**Geophysical structure (Gettings).** Gravity and aeromagnetic data suggest a tilted fault block dipping west beneath Sulphur Springs Valley. The Turkey Creek intrusion is probably a sill of 0.5–1 km thickness, not a batholith top.

**Pine distribution controls (Barton).** Three pine species (*Pinus discolor*, *P. leiophylla*, *P. engelmannii*) were studied. Results:
- Lower limits are set by dry-season water stress, which kills young seedlings.
- Upper limits of *P. leiophylla* are set by light competition and deep litter.
- Upper limits of *P. discolor* are set by fire. Juveniles are invading higher sites because fire has been suppressed for about 75 years.

**Plant richness (Bennett & Kunzmann).** The Chiricahua flora has 1188 species, the largest among the seven ranges compared. Madrean-element percentages are 18.0 for the Chiricahua and 19.8 for the Animas. Madrean richness correlates with a roughness index (R² = 0.897). The Sierra Ancha has the lowest roughness index (0.144).

**Sensitive plants (Gori; Malusa).** Four species were studied: *Astragalus cobrensis* var. *maguirei*, *Draba standleyi*, *Rumex orthoneurus* and *Polemonium pauciflorum* var. *hinckleyi*. A new West Turkey Creek population of *A. cobrensis* var. *maguirei* numbered over 500 plants. Four new *D. standleyi* sites were located. *R. orthoneurus* is a Category 1 candidate with transplant populations of 24–34 plants at several sites and over 200 at Barfoot Park.

**Mycoflora (Nishida et al.).** A total of 362 macrofungal species were recorded from 43 sites: 243 agarics and boletes, 41 aphyllophorales, 28 ascomycetes and 50 others. Abundance and diversity rise with elevation. Most common species are mycorrhizal (72% of the commonest agarics/boletes). Among the commonest genera, 80% are mycorrhizal. Inocybe and Amanita have the most species.

**Fire and flood history (Swetnam et al.).** Rhyolite Canyon had a 50-year fire-free gap (1801–1851), unprecedented among more than 30 regional fire histories. Flood dates identified include 1866, 1881, 1888, 1937 and 1954. The cause of the gap is unresolved. Candidate explanations include debris flows, climate and livestock grazing.

**Lemon Lily (Wood).** The population at Ramsey Canyon Preserve fell from 83 plants in 1980 to 28 in 1987. Downcutting after a 1983 flood, caused by fire in Pat Scott Canyon after 40+ years of fire exclusion, was identified as the main cause. In 1991 the population reached 94 plants, plus 101 in the National Forest.

**Herpetofauna and birds (Block & Severson; Lowe & Zweifel).**
- Point counts recorded 117 bird species across five ranges.
- Time-constrained searches recorded 444 herps, 23 species, of which *Sceloporus jarrovi* was most common.
- The Chiricahua checklist includes 72 amphibian and reptile taxa (12 amphibians, 60 reptiles).

**Mexican Spotted Owl (Duncan & Taiz).** At least 19 territories are documented in the Chiricahuas. Nests are at 1615–2621 m. Woodrats and white-footed mice are the main prey.

**Horned lizards (Marlor; Sherbrooke).** Four horned lizard species occur near the Chiricahuas. *P. douglassii* is viviparous, while the other three are oviparous. Sherbrooke reports rain-harvesting, blood squirting and blood plasma detoxification of harvester ant venom.

**Yarrow's spiny lizard (Middendorf & Frankel).** Muscle esterase phenotypes separated four zones in 25 localities. Chiricahua and Huachuca populations show affinity, as do Santa Rita and Pinaleno populations. A metapopulation model predicts gene flow along ridges and canyons.

**Gould's turkey (Schemnitz).** Population grew from 12 birds in 1983 to 75 in 1988 in the Peloncillo Mountains. Mountain lion predation, hybridization with domestic turkeys and riparian degradation are the main concerns. Management recommendations include removing hybrid flocks, building rock-header dams and reducing livestock use.

**Bushtits (Sloane).** About one-third of 200 nests had supernumeraries. The incidence fell from 57% in 1987 to 15% in 1989. A "thermoregulatory constraint" model is proposed to explain the social system. Bushtit numbers declined sharply in 1992.

**Pikas (Smith).** In 1991 almost all talus patches in the southern Bodie study area were unoccupied. This suggests correlated extinction, which is a warning for fragmented populations in the Chiricahuas.

**Thick-billed parrot (Snyder & Johnson).** The species was probably extirpated by shooting, with the last US record in 1938. Wild-caught birds were the most successful reintroduction source. Hand-reared birds showed major behavioral deficits. Goshawk predation and food shortages remain the main obstacles.

**Accipiters (Snyder & Snyder).** Cooper's hawk numbers in the Chiricahuas fell by more than half after 1986. Sharp-shinned hawks nest in dense microhabitats next to goshawks, which may reduce food competition.

**Coues white-tailed deer (Welch).** Movements were short-distance, driven by food, water and cover. Densities peaked at 37 deer per square mile at 6,000–7,000 ft.

**Modelling and applications.** Papers on lichens as air-quality biomonitors, GIS and airborne video, and "Partners for Wildlife" were presented. Atmar's island biogeography models were also included.

**Conclusions.** The symposium identified research needs across the range. These include long-term monitoring, fire-management integration with rare species, metapopulation-aware management, and an international Sierra Madre–Sky Island research network.
