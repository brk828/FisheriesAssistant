# Inland Fisheries of Europe: Country Summaries (EIFAC Supplement, 1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This FAO/EIFAC supplement (EIFAC Technical Paper No. 52 Suppl., by William A. Dill, 1993) gives standardized country summaries of inland fisheries for nine European countries. It completes the first volume, which covered other European states. The text provided here covers Albania, Bulgaria, France, Greece, Portugal, Romania and Spain in full, and opens the Sweden chapter, which is cut off during its physical geography section. The United Kingdom chapter listed in the contents is not present in the source text.

A recurring theme is that official statistics are unreliable. Catch figures often mix wild capture with [[Aquaculture]] output, areas of lagoons and lakes vary widely between sources, and some totals appear to be inflated or incorrect. Across the countries, inland fisheries are constrained by flow variability, water pollution, irrigation and hydropower demand, and land drainage. Growth is most evident in [[Aquaculture]], especially [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Common Carp]] culture, and in the stocking of [[Reservoirs]].

## Background & Description

Each country chapter follows the same format: area, population, physical geography, climate, hydrography, land and water use, fish fauna, fisheries, ownership and administration, state of the fishery, and prospects.

- **Albania:** 28 748 km², 3 388 000 inhabitants (1990). Mostly mountainous, with a narrow Mediterranean coastal plain. The fishery centres on three shared lakes, [[Skadar Lake]], [[Lake Ohrid]] and [[Lake Prespa]], and on coastal lagoons.
- **Bulgaria:** 110 912 km², 9 246 000 inhabitants (1990). Water-deficient, with large irrigation demands. Inland fisheries are split between reservoirs, a few high-altitude lakes, Black Sea coastal lakes (limans) and the [[Danube River]].
- **France:** 547 026 km², 55 470 000 inhabitants (1990). Rich in streams but short of large natural lakes. Sport fishing far outweighs commercial fishing. Trout culture is highly developed.
- **Greece:** 131 957 km², 10 085 000 inhabitants (1990). Rivers are short and torrential. The fishery depends on natural lakes and extensive [[Lagoon Fisheries]].
- **Portugal:** 91 985 km² (continental 88 944 km²), 10 304 700 inhabitants (1990). Among the poorest countries in Europe for water resources. Few natural lakes, many polluted rivers, and an inland fishery of minor importance.
- **Romania:** 237 500 km², 23 816 000 inhabitants (1990). The [[Danube Delta]] and its floodplain are the core of the inland fishery.
- **Spain:** 504 750 km² (continental 492 463 km²), 39 748 000 inhabitants (1990). Semi-arid, with few natural lakes but many reservoirs.
- **Sweden (partial):** 486 661 km², 8 305 000 inhabitants (1990). Inland waters cover almost 9% of the country and include almost 100 000 lakes. The source states the area figure differs from other references because of a 1979 change to territorial waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrography and flow.** Most countries have highly irregular river regimes. Portugal reports minimum-to-maximum discharge ratios of 1:8 to 1:10 in northern rivers and 1:40 in southern and eastern rivers. Albanian rivers flood in winter and can nearly dry up in summer. Spain's Ebro shows a January-to-August flow ratio of about 45:1. Many rivers in Greece, Portugal and Spain are reduced to pools or dry in summer.

**Lakes.** Albania's three large lakes are shared with neighbouring states. [[Skadar Lake]] has a total area of 391 km², of which 147.9 km² is in Albania. [[Lake Ohrid]] has a total area of 348.8 km², with 118.9 km² in Albania. Greece's [[Lake Prespa]] (Limni Megali Prespa) has a total area of 274 km². Bulgaria's high mountain lakes are mostly glacial, oligotrophic and small. Portugal has very few natural lakes.

**Lagoons.** Lagoons are central to the Greek, French, Spanish and Albanian fisheries. Greek lagoon area estimates range from 24 500 to 60 000 ha, and the author judges around 40 000 ha reasonable. Spain's [[Mar Menor]] covers 17 000 ha. France's [[Etang de Berre]] covers 15 000 ha. Yields vary widely, from about 25 kg/ha/year in Spain's Mar Menor to 471 kg/ha/year in Spain's 179-ha Tancada lagoon.

**Reservoirs.** Reservoir construction has expanded fishing waters in all countries. Spain has about 700 reservoirs holding 42 800 million m³ (1988). Romania had 1 192 artificial lakes (1968), covering 47 548 ha. Bulgaria reports conflicting totals, from 43 reservoirs (320 km²) to 2 015 dams (45 380 ha).

**Pollution and water demand.** Pollution is a major constraint. Portugal's major rivers are polluted by domestic and industrial wastes, and some were sterilized by mining waste. In Spain, about 37% of river length was affected around 1970. A later ICONA survey classed 8.3% of permanent streams as very contaminated, 15.3% as moderately contaminated and 76.4% as little or not contaminated. Bulgaria reports 46% of river length polluted (1972). Runoff per capita is low in Bulgaria, Portugal and Spain, which limits effluent dilution. Irrigation takes about 50% of water in Spain and about 20% in Romania (irrigation share of consumption, c. 1968).

## Key Findings

**Albania**
- Inland waters cover 1 350 km² (4.7% of the country) according to one source, though the author suspects lagoons are included.
- [[Skadar Lake]] hosts 15 fish families and 37 species, with cyprinids about 96% of fish biomass. [[Lake Ohrid]] has 17 species, 10 of them endemic.
- Recorded total catch including aquaculture was 12 468 t in 1987. Early FAO yearbook figures for Albanian inland fisheries (under 0.5 t) are judged "manifestly absurd" by the author.
- Albanian population is among the least urbanized in Europe. Sport fishing is not statistically documented.

**Bulgaria**
- 65 freshwater and 36 diadromous species are reported (Popov, 1981). The Bulgarian Danube alone has 60 species in 15 families.
- The estimated inland catch rose from 3 900 t (1965) to 12 860 t (1987). The author warns that the figures are unreliable and that aquaculture cannot be separated from capture.
- Aquaculture production was 11 164 t in 1989. Planned production targets were not met (for example, a 1982 freshwater target of 30 000 t).
- Sport fishermen rose from 48 000 (1960) to 155 000 (1980).

**France**
- About 65 species in 23 families are considered present in fresh waters ("legal viewpoint").
- Estimated fluvial waters total 270 650 km, of which 258 850 km are private. Static waters are estimated at 183 000 to 211 700 ha, depending on source.
- The 1980 estimate for average yield of trout streams and ponds ranges widely by water type (for example, 120 kg/ha/year for ponds).
- Commercial inland fishing is declining. About 486 professional inland fishermen were counted around 1990.
- Around 2.36 million anglers paid the fishing tax in 1980, and the author estimates about 4 million amateur anglers in total.
- Trout production reached about 32 000 t in 1989. The author argues that a 20 000 t "catch" of rainbow trout in the FAO record was aquacultural output.
- The Atlantic salmon catch in the Loire Basin fell from about 40 000 fish (1891) to under 2 000 (1970s).

**Greece**
- Greek inland fisheries are concentrated in lakes and lagoons. Total inland production in 1978 was about 9 000 t, of which about 2 500 t (28%) came from aquaculture, mainly trout.
- Lake yield estimates range from 30 to 125 kg/ha/year depending on the source. Crivelli (1990) reports a mean total yield of 1 790 t/yr over the last ten years of 17 lakes, down from 3 698 t/yr for 1928–1935.
- Ananiadis (1984) lists 61 lagoons totalling 43 448 ha, producing 2 052 t (about 47 kg/ha/year).
- Lakes and lagoons are mostly State property, leased to cooperatives or individuals. Cooperatives pay 25% of the gross value of the catch in one lagoon (Messolongion-Etolico).
- Greek lagoon culture is a capture fishery of fish that enter from the sea, not true aquaculture (see [[Lagoon Fisheries]]).

**Portugal**
- Estimates of fish species range from 33 (Almaca, 1965) to about 43 (Portugal/EIFAC, 1991).
- No valid commercial inland catch statistics exist. About 3 000 commercial inland licences and about 80 000 sport licences were issued around 1986.
- Trout farming produced about 1 000 t in 1985. A new Atlantic salmon farm was expected to yield about 625 t annually.
- Portugal is expected to more than double its reservoir capacity by 2000 (ECE, 1978).

**Romania**
- Romania has about 77 fresh and brackish species (Banarescu, 1967), with 76 found in the Romanian Danube and its lagoons.
- The FAO-recorded inland "catch" (including culture) rose from 21 200 t (1965) to 66 865 t (1987). Common carp and goldfish made up about 75% of the 1988 harvest.
- In 1986, 32 685 ha of Delta ponds produced 11 038 t (338 kg/ha). The author notes this is about ten times the capture fishery yield of the open Delta.
- Sport anglers exceeded 200 000 in 1990 (about 1% of the population).
- Experimental fed polycultures produced 1 500–3 000 kg/ha/year (Negoescu, 1982).

**Spain**
- Spain has no sizeable natural lakes. De Rada (1954) estimates about 4 000 lakes and lagoons, with 18 000 ha of fresh water and 65 000 ha of brackish water.
- The Atlantic salmon catch was halved between 1965 and 1976. Spain banned netting in rivers in 1942, making it the only European country to turn its rivers entirely over to angling.
- Registered anglers rose from 224 005 (1966) to 706 545 (1976). The 1980 figure was 709 534.
- Trout farm production reached about 11 000 t by 1982 (169 farms).
- Reservoir capacity rose from 17 314 million m³ (1960) to 36 628 million m³ (1970). Margalef et al. (1976) classify about 15% of reservoirs as little eutrophic, 50% moderately and 35% considerably.

**Sweden (partial text)**
- Recreational fishing is described as having already surpassed commercial inland fishing in importance. Sweden has one of the highest proportions of sport fishermen in Europe.
- Hydroelectric development has severely affected anadromous salmonid fisheries, which now depend heavily on stocking.
- Acid rain is a growing threat, as about three-quarters of Sweden lies on Precambrian granite and gneiss shield.

**Cross-cutting conclusions**
- Statistics across all countries are inconsistent, and the author repeatedly warns against using them for trends.
- Pollution, flow variability, irrigation, drainage and hydropower are the common constraints on capture fisheries.
- The prospects for capture fisheries are generally weak. Future growth is expected mainly in [[Aquaculture]], [[Sport Fishing]] and reservoir fisheries.
