> **Note on scope:** This source document is a **Fiscal Year 1996 Transition Monitoring Proposal** focused almost entirely on **wetland and riparian vegetation** along the Colorado River corridor in Glen Canyon and Grand Canyon, prepared for the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) program. It does **not** present direct fish population, physiology, or species-specific ichthyological data. Fish/fisheries are referenced only tangentially as a downstream management concern tied to dam operations (interim flow levels). This note extracts and organizes the aquatic/riparian ecosystem science that is actually present, with fish-relevant context flagged where applicable.

## Executive Summary

This proposal, submitted by the National Park Service (Grand Canyon National Park and Glen Canyon National Recreation Area) in collaboration with the National Biological Service, outlines a **FY1996-97 riparian and wetland vegetation monitoring program** for the Colorado River corridor between **Glen Canyon Dam (River Mile -15)** and **Pearce Ferry (River Mile 280)**. The study aims to track the response of riparian plant communities to **Glen Canyon Dam discharge regimes**, particularly the "interim flow" regime, using **aerial photography** for broad vegetation cover mapping and **ground-based marsh transect surveys** for detailed species composition, cover, density, and frequency data. While the study is botanically focused, it operates within the same regulated-river ecosystem framework that governs **native fish habitat** in the Colorado River, and dam-flow management is explicitly linked to fisheries considerations in the justification section.

## Background & Description

- **System altered by Glen Canyon Dam (1963):** Flow regulation has altered sediment, nutrient, energy, and biotic transport, fundamentally changing riparian vegetation dynamics — indirectly the same flow regime affects aquatic/fish habitat structure (backwaters, return channels, marshes).
- **Riparian zonation** described along a moisture/inundation gradient:
  - **Old high water zone**: western honey mesquite, catclaw acacia (historic, pre-dam high flow line, ~125,000 cfs stage)
  - **New high water zone**: tamarisk, coyote willow (post-dam regulated stage)
  - **New dry zone**: created by interim (reduced) flows, allowing further vegetation expansion downslope
- **Depositional environments** relevant to aquatic habitat: reattachment bars, return channel backwaters, separation (camping) beaches, channel margins ([[Colorado River]] geomorphology per Schmidt and Graf 1988).
- **Six marshes proposed for resurvey**, which represent key **wetland/backwater habitats** potentially significant to native fish and aquatic invertebrates:
  - Glen Canyon (RM -6.5)
  - Anasazi Bridge (RM 43.1)
  - Kwagunt Marsh (RM 55.5)
  - Cardenas (RM 71.5)
  - Forster (RM 122.8)
  - Hualapai Acres (RM 194.1)
- Additional marsh site recommended below **Diamond Creek (RM 225)** to extend coverage into the lower river reach.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regulation and native fisheries linkage:** The justification section explicitly states that historical vegetation and flow management decisions have been driven by dam operational priorities including **"management of native fisheries through the establishment of interim flow levels."** This is the only direct fish-related statement in the document, indicating that **interim flow regimes** set for hydropower and sediment conservation are co-managed with native fish conservation goals in the [[Colorado River]] system.
- **Hydrological disturbance dynamics:** A cited study ([[Cluer 1995]]) using time-lapse photography demonstrated that fluvial sand deposits — which shape backwater and marsh habitats potentially used by fish and aquatic invertebrates — can erode within **less than one day** and redeposit within weeks to months, creating high temporal variability relevant to aquatic habitat monitoring design.
- **Marsh/wetland habitat as aquatic-terrestrial interface:** Marsh transects capture emergent and obligate riparian vegetation (rushes *Juncus* spp., sedges *Carex*, *Eleocharis*, *Scirpus*, cattail/bulrush stands) that structure shoreline and backwater habitat complexity, which is ecologically significant for fish nursery/refuge habitat even though not directly sampled here.
- **Exotic species invasion pressure:** Tamarisk, Ravenna grass, camelthorn, Russian olive, and weeping lovegrass are noted as colonizing riparian corridors, with potential downstream effects on bank stability, shading, and organic input to the river — factors indirectly influencing aquatic productivity and fish habitat quality.
- **Terrestrial fauna linkage:** The document notes that "the response of terrestrial fauna (birds, mammals, reptiles, amphibians, and insects) to dam regulation is also interconnected with their use of vegetation," implicitly extending to aquatic-riparian food web connections relevant to fish prey availability (e.g., insect drift into the river).

## Key Findings

- **No direct fish population, abundance, or species-specific data are reported in this document.** The proposal is a vegetation/wetland monitoring plan, not a fisheries study.
- **Interim flow levels** (reduced flows from Glen Canyon Dam, initiated ~1991) are identified as a management tool balancing sediment conservation, riparian vegetation response, and **native fish conservation**, but no quantitative fish metrics are given.
- **Marsh habitats** proposed for vegetation resurvey (six sites plus one below Diamond Creek) represent locations of likely ecological relevance to backwater-dependent native fish species (e.g., in the broader [[Grand Canyon]] context, species such as [[Humpback Chub]] and other native Colorado River fish are known to use similar backwater/marsh habitats), though this document does not name or study these species directly.
- **Sampling design critique:** The proposal identifies serious methodological flaws in the prior GCES Phase II/Interim Flows Monitoring vegetation protocol (inadequate plot size/number, autocorrelation issues, poor site selection, lack of quality assurance), which — while framed for vegetation — underscores broader concerns about **monitoring rigor in the Glen Canyon Dam adaptive management program** that also governs fish-related interim flow decisions.
- **Recommended methodological improvements**: standardized cover/density/frequency protocols (Daubenmire 1968; Mueller-Dombois and Ellenberg 1974; Bonham 1989), randomized subplot sampling, TWINSPAN/DECORANA multivariate classification (Cornell Ecology Package), and coordination with GCES topographic surveying — establishing a more rigorous baseline that could support future integrated riparian-aquatic ecosystem assessments.
- **Time-lapse camera monitoring** (Pentax IQ Zoom 105, daily interval) proposed at marsh sites to document rapid fluvial erosion/deposition cycles, an approach with direct relevance to understanding physical habitat turnover in backwaters used by aquatic organisms.
