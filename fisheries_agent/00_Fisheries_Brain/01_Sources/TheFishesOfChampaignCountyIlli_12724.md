# The Fishes of Champaign County, Illinois, as Affected by 60 Years of Stream Changes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (March 1963, Vol. 28, Art. 2) by R. Weldon Larimore and Philip W. Smith compares three fish surveys of [[Champaign County]], Illinois, made in 1899 ([[Forbes and Richardson Survey]]), 1928 ([[Thompson and Hunt Survey]]), and 1959 ([[Larimore and Smith Survey]]). The authors document how agricultural drainage, dredging, urban growth, and pollution reshaped the county's streams over roughly 60 years, and how fish distribution and abundance responded. The 1959 survey recorded 90 species in the annotated list, of which 74 were taken in 1959 or subsequently, and 7 were introductions.

## Background & Description

The study sits at the intersection of two long-running research efforts. Forbes and Richardson's 1908 work on [[Illinois Fishes]] produced the first extensive county collections, and Thompson and Hunt's 1930 study applied standardized seining and quantitative sampling. Larimore and Smith aimed to replicate Thompson and Hunt's approach with more efficient methods, adopting a standard procedure of blocking off about 150-foot stream sections and electrofishing them, followed by supplemental "cruising" seine hauls. The 1959 field work produced 77 quantitative samples, all taken in July and August 1959, and 152 localities were sampled in total.

The county covers 988 square miles of flat to gently rolling, glaciated terrain with no rock outcrops, overlain by glacial till and loess. Dark upland prairie soils dominate, and the county is one of the most productive grain areas in the world. Four major stream systems originate within it: the Sangamon, Salt Fork, Middle Fork, Embarrass, Kaskaskia, and Little Vermilion drainages, each with characteristic habitats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Land use and hydrology.** Drainage improvements covered 36 percent of the county by 1900 and 82 percent (520,100 acres) by 1959. Dredging, straightening, and tiling reduced water storage, raised flood peaks, lowered drought flows, and made many small intermittent streams permanent only in name. Average stream gradient was low, typically 3 to 4 feet of fall per mile. Sangamon River discharge fell below 200 cubic feet per second eight times in the three decades after 1928, compared with once in the two decades before.

**Population and urbanization.** The population rose from 47,622 in 1900 to 132,436 in 1960, while the urban share increased from 31.1 to 75.6 percent. Champaign-Urbana, [[Chanute Air Force Base]], and the [[University of Illinois]] campus removed farmland from production.

**Habitat types.** Rivulets and small creeks (189 miles, 10 square miles of drainage on average) were mostly ditches; large creeks (176 miles) and small rivers (58 miles) contained alternating riffles and pools. Each habitat hosted characteristic species assemblages.

**Water chemistry and temperature.** Turbidity during fieldwork ranged from 15 to 150 ppm. Hardness ranged from 264 to 436 ppm as CaCO3, and pH mostly fell between 7.2 and 7.8. Summer temperatures in shallow pools could approach 100 degrees F, and an 8-hour swing of up to 20 degrees F was possible.

**Pollution sources.** Seven areas of chronic pollution were identified: the [[Boneyard Creek]], the West Branch, the East Branch, the lower Salt Fork, [[Copper Slough]] and Phinney Branch, the upper Sangamon, and the upper Kaskaskia.

## Key Findings

1. **Species richness and survey comparison.** Forbes and Richardson's collections (48 localities) contained about 65 species; Thompson and Hunt (126 stations) obtained 74; Larimore and Smith (152 localities) obtained 74 in their own survey, with 64 previously recorded and 10 newly recorded. The cumulative county list reached 90 species.

2. **Introductions and natives.** Seven of the 90 species were introduced ([[Bowfin]], [[Goldfish]], [[Brown Bullhead]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Yellow Bass]], [[Warmouth]], [[Redear Sunfish]]). The remaining 83 are native.

3. **Changes in occurrence.** Sixteen species increased in occurrence within the county, including [[Notropis Rubellus]], [[Notropis Lutrensis]], [[Micropterus Dolomieu]], [[Hypentelium Nigricans]], and [[Cyprinus Carpio]]. Fifteen species decreased, including [[Esox Americanus]], [[Minytrema Melanops]], and [[Lepomis Humilis]]. Many species showed little change.

4. **Extirpations and new arrivals.** Ictiobus bubalus, Hybopsis aestivalis, and Notropis heterolepis disappeared before 1928. [[Notropis Lutrensis]] (red shiner) appeared between 1928 and 1959 and dispersed naturally from the west.

5. **Changes in drainage richness.** Forbes and Richardson found the Salt Fork richest; by 1928 the Sangamon was richest, and in 1959 the Sangamon held the most drainage-restricted species (13 by 1928 counts). The Embarrass had no species exclusive to it in 1959.

6. **Magnitude of change.** Most species changes occurred in the first 30 years of the century (129 changes in distribution through 1928 versus 86 from 1928 to 1959). Middle Fork and Sangamon changes dominated the earlier period; Kaskaskia and Salt Fork dominated the later one.

7. **Associations.** Significant correlations were found between [[Notropis Dorsalis]] and [[Ericymba Buccata]] (ecological equivalents that co-occurred in the Sangamon), [[Notropis Chrysocephalus]] and [[Erimyzon Oblongus]], and [[Catostomus Commersoni]] and [[Phenacobius Mirabilis]]. The authors concluded that shared ecological preferences, not direct interdependence, explain most associations.

8. **Abundance and yield.** Species per station averaged 13.2 in 1928 and 19.0 in 1959 (ratio 1:1.4). Fish per 100 square yards averaged 387 in 1928 and 457 in 1959. In 1959 the county averaged 2.6 pounds per 100 square yards (about 124.4 pounds per acre), compared with the 150 pounds per acre estimated by Thompson and Hunt for 1928. Electrofishing success in an adjoining stream (Jordan Creek) suggested the true standing crop may have been near 250 pounds per acre.

9. **Stream size relationships.** Consistent with Thompson and Hunt, species per station increased downstream (r = 0.64), and fish numbers per unit area decreased downstream. Total fish weight per unit area was roughly constant across stream sizes. Thompson and Hunt's claim of a consistent per-species size-frequency pattern was not supported in 1959.

10. **Pollution effects.** The West Branch below the disposal plant was drastically reduced; 1959 sampling near the plant found only three species in the initial visit, with 12 more added in later revisits. Fish anomalies (small, upturned mouths, missing fins) were noted in the upper West Branch, especially in creek chub, bluntnose minnow, and common shiner. Recurrent fish kills occurred in the upper Sangamon and lower Salt Fork.

11. **Enrichment benefits.** Domestic sewage from Rantoul and Chanute enriched fish populations several miles downstream of toxic reaches, at 1,337 fish weighing over 9 pounds per 100 square yards, three to four times the county average. The authors noted that enrichment can rapidly turn toxic.

12. **Fisheries.** About 20 of the 90 species are commonly angled. Seven streams and about 125 ponds and small lakes provide fishing, with fee-fishing ponds stocked from outside sources.

13. **Conclusions.** Drainage, dredging, urbanization, and pollution changed Champaign County streams, yet many species persisted. Future management was recommended to balance drainage with water supply needs and to control effluents, since chronic pollution and enrichment strongly affect fish distribution.
