# Survival Rate Estimation and Movement of Hatchery-Reared Razorback Suckers *Xyrauchen texanus* in the Upper Colorado River Basin, Utah and Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Colorado River Implementation Program Project 128, Larval Fish Laboratory Contribution 159, April 2009) estimates post-stocking survival, recapture probability, and movement of hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] stocked into the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The species is federally endangered. Recovery requires two self-sustaining adult populations, each exceeding 5,800 individuals, before downlisting or delisting. Because wild populations are depleted, the recovery strategy depends on [[Hatchery Stocking]].

The [[Integrated Stocking Plan]] (initiated 2004) called for 9,930 age-2+ fish (>300 mm TL) to be stocked in each of the middle [[Green River]] and upper [[Colorado River]] subbasins for six consecutive years. The plan assumed annual survival of 50% for age-2 fish, 60% for age-3 fish, and 70% for adults. The study used [[Mark-Recapture Analysis]] of [[PIT Tagging]] data in [[Program MARK]], with model selection by [[Akaike's Information Criterion]].

Survival of fish stocked at the average length was very low in the first interval in the river. Survival after that interval was much higher and close to the planned adult rate. Recapture probabilities were low, and movement distances varied widely.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (family [[Catostomidae]]) is a large, long-lived, potamodromous catostomid of the Colorado River system. Recovery goals specify genetically and demographically viable, self-sustaining adult populations. Demographic rates such as survival and recapture probability are therefore central to management decisions.

Stocking began in 2004 under the Integrated Stocking Plan. The stocking strategy assumed that hatchery fish would survive and eventually reproduce, forming the presumptive recovery populations.

The study examined the following covariates for survival and recapture:
- River and river reach
- Year
- Season of stocking
- Individual [[Total Length]] at stocking
- First year in the river (ry1) versus subsequent years (post-ry1)
- Annual sampling effort (recapture probability only)

Rearing environment (tanks versus grow-out ponds) could not be analyzed because of data imbalance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study covered seven reaches in the Green and Colorado River subbasins, delineated by river gradient, geomorphology, or diversions:

- **CO1:** Colorado River from the Green River confluence to upstream end of [[Westwater Canyon]] (RK 0.0–200.0)
- **CO2:** Colorado River from upstream of Westwater Canyon to Price-Stubb diversion (RK 200.1–303.2), plus the lower [[Gunnison River]] (RK 0.0–4.9)
- **CO3:** Colorado River from Price-Stubb diversion upstream to Rifle (RK 303.3–390.0)
- **GU2:** Gunnison River upstream of Redlands diversion (>RK 4.9)
- **GR1:** Green River from the Colorado River to just downstream of [[Desolation-Gray Canyon]] (RK 0.0–206.0)
- **GR2:** Green River, Desolation-Gray Canyon (RK 206.1–347.7)
- **GR3:** Green River from upstream end of Desolation-Gray Canyon to the mouth of Whirlpool Canyon (RK 347.8–540.0)

Only CO2, CO3, GU2, GR1, and GR3 received stocked fish during the study period. Reaches differ in river gradient, canyon geomorphology, and diversion structures, which affects how fish move and where they are recaptured.

## Key Findings

**Study scope and data**
- 119,129 individually PIT-tagged razorback suckers were stocked into the Colorado, Gunnison, and Green rivers from 1995 through 2005.
- Recaptures ranged from 0 to 4 times per fish.
- Analysis used apparent survival (φ) and recapture probability (p) with maximum likelihood estimates.

**First-interval (ry1) survival**
- Total length at stocking had a large positive effect on ry1 survival. Fish stocked at <200 mm TL approached zero survival. Fish stocked at >500 mm TL reached 0.75 or higher survival, although only a few fish were this large.
- At the average stocking length of 252.5 mm TL, ry1 survival was only **0.05** (95% CI: 0.042–0.071).
- Season of stocking also had a large effect. For average-length fish, predicted ry1 survival was:
  - Summer: <0.02 (95% CI: 0.012–0.022)
  - Spring: 0.07 (95% CI: 0.044–0.094)
  - Autumn: 0.08 (95% CI: 0.057–0.100)
  - Winter: 0.08 (95% CI: 0.057–0.118)
- The seasonal pattern held for fish stocked at 300 mm and 400 mm TL, which indicates a season effect regardless of size.

**Post-ry1 survival**
- Survival in any interval after the first was **0.75** (95% CI: 0.699–0.801).
- Post-ry1 survival did not depend on stocking length or season.
- This rate is similar to the 0.70 adult survival assumed in the Integrated Stocking Plan. It also agrees with estimates for wild adult razorback suckers in the middle Green River before 2000.

**Recapture probability**
- Recapture probabilities were low, ranging from 0.002 to 0.128 for average-length fish.
- Reach GR1 produced the highest recapture probabilities, followed by CO2, GR3, CO3, and GU2, in descending order.
- First-occasion (ry1) recapture probabilities were higher than subsequent-occasion estimates, but the differences were not significant based on overlapping 95% CIs.
- The total length parameter for recapture probability was 0.0106 (95% CI: −0.0023 to 0.0297), a small positive effect.

**Movement**
- Movement was measured from stocking or a recapture event to the next recapture event.
- Mean minimum distance traveled per leg was **54.7 RK** (range: 0–514.9 RK).
- Mean rate of travel was **0.97 RK/d** (range: 0–65.37 RK/d).
- Distance and rate were highest on initial legs (stocking to first capture) compared with subsequent legs.
- Winter-stocked fish moved the longest average distance (86.7 RK).
- Summer-stocked fish moved at the highest average rate (1.34 RK/d).
- No consistent pattern was found in mean minimum distance by reach or direction.

**Management implications**
- Survival of hatchery fish in the first interval is the main bottleneck. Stocking larger fish and choosing a favorable season may improve early survival.
- Once established, fish survive at rates consistent with the adult survival assumed in recovery planning.
- Low recapture probabilities mean that population estimates depend heavily on model assumptions and sampling effort.
