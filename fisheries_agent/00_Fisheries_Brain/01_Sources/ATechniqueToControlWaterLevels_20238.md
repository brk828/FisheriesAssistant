# A Technique to Control Water Levels in Beaver Impoundments

#wildlife-management #beaver #waterfowl-habitat #Castoridae

## Executive Summary

This 1948-1949 progress report documents experimental techniques developed by the [[New Hampshire Fish and Game Department]] to control water levels in [[American Beaver]] impoundments using perforated drain pipes. The technique aimed to preserve valuable [[Waterfowl]] breeding and resting habitat while preventing damage to roads, cultivated lands, and timber caused by excessive water levels. Initial experiments with four-inch fibre pipe revealed design inadequacies, but subsequent modifications—including suspended pipe installations, wooden plugs, iron stakes, and barbed-wire entanglements—proved successful in maintaining desired water levels without beaver interference.

## Background & Description

The [[American Beaver]] (*Castor canadensis*) population increased significantly across all regions of [[New Hampshire]] during the decade preceding this report. Beaver impoundments consistently produced [[Black Ducks]] and [[Wood Ducks]] according to summer brood censuses conducted by department biologists. However, high water levels maintained by beaver dams frequently caused damage to infrastructure and property, leading to beaver removal and subsequent deterioration of valuable waterfowl habitat.

The experimental technique involved installing perforated "Bermico" fibre pipe through beaver dams. Initial designs used two eight-foot sections of four-inch pipe joined by flexible fibre couplings, with three-eighths inch holes drilled in the bottom 60° of the pipe. The upstream end was positioned to maintain desired water levels, typically lowering impoundments by six to sixteen inches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Beaver impoundments served dual ecological functions in [[New Hampshire]]: providing valuable waterfowl breeding and resting areas while generating trapping revenue as regulations relaxed. Summer brood censuses consistently demonstrated that beaver flowages were productive habitats for [[Black Ducks]] (*Anas rubripes*) and [[Wood Ducks]] (*Aix sponsa*).

The traditional management response to beaver-caused flooding—removal of beaver through killing or live-trapping, followed by dam destruction via tearing or dynamite blasting—resulted in loss of waterfowl habitat. The perforated pipe technique offered an alternative that preserved both beaver populations and the aquatic habitats they created while mitigating human-wildlife conflicts.

## Key Findings

**Initial Experimental Installation (October 1948):**
- Two eight-foot sections of four-inch fibre pipe with three-eighths inch perforations installed through beaver dam
- Water level successfully lowered approximately six inches by mid-November
- Design failures identified: unsupported upstream end broke at coupling under beaver weight and debris accumulation; unplugged upstream end allowed beaver to insert woody material; pipe inadequate for more than minimum runoff

**Second Installation (June 1949):**
- Ten-acre impoundment in drainage ditch
- Initial design: sixteen feet of pipe (ten feet upstream, six feet through dam) suspended sixteen inches from bottom using dried oak posts secured with baling wire; upstream end plugged with four-inch wooden disc
- Failure: beaver gnawed through oak posts, causing pipe separation at coupling and subsequent blockage with sticks and mud
- Successful redesign: three eight-foot sections with both three-eighths inch holes and six one-inch holes on underside of upstream section; suspended by iron stakes; fibre coupling reinforced with nails and cleats; upstream end plugged
- Beaver attempted to compensate by repairing dam gaps and extending dam wings but failed to raise water level above desired point

**Design Improvements:**
- Installations designed to handle at least a portion of maximum expected runoff based on preliminary reconnaissance measuring current flow volume and field data
- Tables developed for quick determination of required pipe size, number, and perforation specifications
- Barbed-wire entanglements placed around installations extending through road culverts prevented complete burial by beaver; pipes remained operational despite beaver depositing sticks, brush, and mud atop wire

**Management Implications:**
- Technique provides simple, successful method for controlling water levels in beaver impoundments
- Preserves valuable waterfowl habitat while preventing infrastructure damage
- Eliminates need for beaver removal and dam destruction
- Limited experimentation demonstrated potential value; further testing of modifications scheduled
