# Land, Water, Wildlife and Flood Control in the Mississippi Valley

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This 1956 essay by [[Gordon Gunter]] of the [[Gulf Coast Research Laboratory]] argues that land, water, wildlife, and flood control in the [[Mississippi River]] valley form one inseparable system that has been managed piecemeal. Gunter traces the river's history and the growth of the [[Mississippi River Levee System]], and contends that flood control based on confinement ("canalization") has wasted water, destroyed aquatic life and floodplain wildlife, and removed sediment that once built Louisiana's land. He proposes a shift from fighting the river toward a broader program of "River Use" that restores overflow basins and treats the river as a land builder, water supplier, and wildlife resource. The paper is a broad review rather than a data study; it contains no new biological sampling.

## Background & Description

The paper is a reprint from the *Proceedings of the Louisiana Academy of Sciences* (Vol. XIX, pp. 5–11, November 1956), based on a talk given at Tulane University on April 14, 1956.

- **Geographic setting:** Where the central [[Gulf Coastal Plain]] is crossed by the river, the [[Mississippi Embayment]] extends about 600 miles inland from the Gulf to the Missouri–Illinois region. Much of it is covered by the [[Mississippi Alluvial Valley]].
- **Geologic history:** The present river is a newcomer, diverted southward by the North American Ice Cap. Sea level was at least 400 feet lower during the Wisconsin ice sheet, and Pleistocene sea-level oscillations alternately incised and alluviated the valley. Russell and Howe (1935) called the infilling "alluvial drowning."
- **Floodplain:** Natural levees slope away from the channel at about seven feet per mile, forming flood basins cut off from the river at low stage. The floodplain covers 35,000 square miles.
- **Watershed:** The basin covers 1,244,000 square miles (about one-third of the United States plus 13,000 square miles of Canada), exceeded in size only by the [[Amazon River]] and [[Congo River]]. Average rainfall is about 30 inches per year, roughly one-quarter of which reaches the sea by the river.
- **Discharge and sediment:** Annual discharge at the mouth is estimated at 785,000,000,000 cubic yards. Sediment load is about two million tons per day, or 730,000,000 tons per year.
- **Historical sources:** Information draws mainly on Russell (1936) and Fisk (1944). The French explorer [[Le Page du Pratz]] (1758) first described natural levee formation and called lower Louisiana land "gathered from the sea."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Natural flood regime.** The river is low in late summer and fall, with a large annual rise following spring thaw. Crests usually fall between March and May, and sometimes January. Major floods recur about every four years. Elliott (1932) listed 58 recorded major floods between 1717 and 1929. Before levees, the river overflowed annually and spread across flood basins for many miles.

**Aquatic life.** Gunter states that the southern river was once rich in aquatic life in the main channel and in nearby bayous, lakes, and swamps. Most species spawned in spring and lived in quiet backwaters rather than the swift, muddy main stream. No comprehensive biological survey of the river had been made, so current populations were unknown.

**Swamps and wetlands.** Overflow swamps supported attendant wildlife. Gunter argues that draining swamps for living space is not justified, and that the only good reason for draining is controlling falciparum malaria, a severe disease still present in some Louisiana swamps.

**Sediment and coastal land.** Roughly 730,000,000 tons of fine soil reach the Gulf each year, equivalent to about 38,000 acres three feet deep. Historically this sediment built swamps and delta marshes. Under levees, jetties, and cut distributaries, it now moves beyond the bays toward deep water, and the building of lower Louisiana marshes has ceased. The unleveed [[Atchafalaya Basin]] is a partial exception, where sedimentation is rapid.

**Water use.** The United States already uses about half or more of its total daily water supply. Future transfers of Mississippi water elsewhere would reduce flood peaks and could help raise falling water tables.

## Key Findings

- **Flood heights have risen sharply.** Gunter estimates present flood heights are "certainly seven and possibly ten times higher" than before leveeing. Carrollton (New Orleans) gage readings were 14.95 ft (1882), 16 ft (1890), and 21 ft (1912). The 1927 flood was the greatest, though crevasses made heights unusable for comparison.
- **The levee system has grown to over 2,500 miles** and is up to 35 feet high in places. Gunter describes the river as "more and more like a ditch with walls."
- **Flood control has focused on canalization.** Its aim is to move water out of the river quickly and resist its force with walls. Gunter argues it protects houses but wastes water, destroys aquatic life and overflow wildlife, and wastes land.
- **The river has deepened, not risen.** Gunter rejects the idea that the bed has risen, arguing the channel has incised because of faster flow and straighter channels.
- **Sediment loss has reversed coastal building.** Louisiana is "no longer being gathered from the sea." The [[Louisiana Coastal Marsh]] has stopped building up.
- **Atchafalaya diversion threat.** The Atchafalaya distributary has grown into a major river. Newspaper writers in 1882, quoted by [[Mark Twain]], warned it could capture the Mississippi. Engineers now work to prevent this.
- **Recommended philosophy: River Use.** Gunter proposes moving from flood control alone to a program that returns overflow basins to the river, uses it as a land builder, water supplier, and wildlife/fishery resource, and integrates new works into the existing system.
- **Call for study.** Gunter urges biological studies of the river and adjacent areas, with evaluation of potential benefits once River Use is in operation.
- **Earlier proposal.** Viosca (1927) had argued for living with the river, for example by building houses on stilts. Gunter notes this is no longer practical, but says compromise with the river is still possible.
