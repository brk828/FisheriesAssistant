# Perpetual Succession of Stream-Channel Vegetation in a Semiarid Region

#botany #riparian-ecology #arid-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Campbell]] and [[Green]] examines the composition, distribution, and community structure of stream-channel vegetation along [[Sycamore Creek]], a 42-mile, semiarid stream in central [[Arizona]] that drains the [[Mazatzal Mountains]] and joins the [[Verde River]]. Using 402 randomly located 0.05-acre quadrats for shrubs and trees and 150 permanent 9.6-square-foot quadrats for forbs and grasses, the authors compared vegetation in the lower and upper halves of the reach. They introduce the term "pseudoriparian" for woody plants that grow on adjacent xeric slopes and reach their largest size in channels when extra subsurface moisture is available. The central conclusion is that channel vegetation is shaped by recurring floods, erosion, and deposition, so it consists of a mosaic of seral stages and never reaches a climax community.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a gap in knowledge. Flood-plain vegetation of large Southwestern rivers, such as the Colorado, Salt, Gila, Rio Grande, and Pecos, had been surveyed and mapped, but channel vegetation along arroyos, streams, and rivers in the foothills and mountains had not. No reliable estimates of its area, species composition, or density existed. Management concerns motivating such work included exotic species such as [[Tamarix Pentandra]] and [[Elaeagnus Angustifolia]], potential water savings from vegetation eradication, and the economic value of reclaimed flood-plain land.

The authors build on earlier work, including [[Lowe]]'s (1964) definition of a "riparian association" as vegetation in or adjacent to drainageways that differs in species or life forms from the surrounding non-riparian climax. The authors separate obligate riparian species from facultative ones. Pseudoriparian species can complete their life cycle on xeric or mesic sites but reach maximum size and density when subsurface moisture is available. Examples from Sycamore Creek include [[Prosopis Juliflora]], [[Cercidium Microphyllum]], [[Acacia Greggii]], [[Quercus Turbinella]], [[Juniperus Osteosperma]], and [[Pinus Edulis]]. These species most likely establish in canyon bottoms from seed dispersed by wind, gravity, or animals from adjacent slopes. Plant names follow [[Kearney and Peebles]] (1960).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The watershed covers approximately 121,000 acres, with a flood plain of about 3,000 acres excluding tributaries. The creek heads near Mt. Peele at about 5,500 feet. Channel gradient averages about 40 feet per mile on the lower 21 miles, about 155 feet per mile on the upper 21 miles, and about 235 feet per mile on the upper 7 miles of that sector. The upper sector is mountainous, with granite, schist, and volcanic material. The lower sector has gentle slopes of consolidated alluvium overlying unconsolidated gravel, sand, silt, and clay.

**Hydrology.** The upper sector has intermittent flow or stagnant pools in spring and summer and continuous flow in fall and winter. On the lower sector, flow in constricted reaches may occur only at night in summer, probably because [[Evapotranspiration]] losses reduce daytime flow. In the deep alluvium of the lower 8 miles, surface flow occurs mainly at flood peaks and seldom reaches the Verde River.

**Climate.** Average annual precipitation ranges from 9 inches at the Verde–Salt River confluence to 22 inches at Mt. Ord (7,000 feet). The authors estimate actual precipitation at Mt. Ord at 25 to 30 inches, because the exposed site is affected by high-velocity southwesterly winds. The 13-year average at Sunflower (3,500 feet) is about 20 inches. The summer-to-winter precipitation ratio is about 40:60. Late-summer convectional storms cause periodic flooding and high sediment movement, while winter storms are low-intensity and long-duration.

**Vegetation context.** A vegetation-type map shows a marked change in upland vegetation at about 3,000 feet, where upland slopes shift from desert grassland or desert shrub to [[Chaparral]]. The stream channel crosses this boundary. Shrub and tree species on the lower sector are largely desert species, while the upper sector has chaparral and montane species, including [[Pinus Ponderosa]] and [[Pseudotsuga Menziesii]].

**Temperature and microclimate.** High summer temperatures produce high potential evapotranspiration, but [[Cold-Air Drainage]] buffers channel temperatures. This buffering may extend the elevational range of some riparian species. Trees of the Transition Zone reached elevations as low as 4,500 feet within the channel. Lower-elevation trees such as [[Platanus Wrightii]] and Juglans major occur below 3,000 feet, where summer day temperatures often exceed 100°F. Along the Salt River flood plain below 1,300 feet, maximum summer temperatures often exceed 110°F.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and species richness**
- A total of 94 woody species were recorded in the 402 shrub and tree quadrats: 65 shrub species and 29 tree species.
- The 42-mile reach was divided into six 7-mile sectors. Sector elevations ranged from 1,400 to 5,500 feet.
- Forbs and grasses: the upper 21-mile sector had 95 forb and grass species, while the lower sector had 32. Of the lower-sector species, 62.5 percent (20) also occurred on the upper sector. About 21 percent of upper-sector species were also found below.

**Lower versus upper sector differences**
- Trees: 3 species occurred only in the lower 21 miles, 14 only in the upper 21 miles, and 12 in both. A chi-square test indicated that these differences could arise by chance, although the authors concluded that some species do genuinely occur in only one sector.
- Shrubs: 8 species occurred only in the lower 21 miles, 44 only in the upper 21 miles, and 17 in both. Shrub co-occurrence between sectors was less frequent than expected by chance (χ² = 15.33**).
- Forb and grass composition differed significantly between sectors (χ² = 36.14**).

**Density and frequency**
- Average shrub density was 6.1 per quadrat on the lower sector and 1.5 on the upper sector.
- Tree density was similar in both sectors (1.4 and 1.7 per quadrat).
- Average shrub frequency was 41.7 percent on the lower sector and 29.5 percent on the upper. Tree frequency was 32.5 and 35.6 percent. Trees were therefore more evenly distributed along the reach than shrubs.
- Percent crown cover across the entire reach was 50.0 percent.

**Riparian versus pseudoriparian**
- About 95 percent of shrub species occur on both mesic and xeric sites and were classified as pseudoriparian. Only about 52 percent of tree species were classified this way.
- The authors conclude that pseudoriparian species do not migrate beyond the vegetation types of adjacent slopes. Downstream migration is limited because transpiration exceeds the maximum rate at which roots can absorb water. Up-channel migration appears limited by lower winter temperatures at higher elevations.
- Riparian shrubs such as [[Baccharis Glutinosa]], [[Pluchea Sericea]], and [[Salix Exigua]] were found only on wet sites. Below 1,300 feet in the Salt River Valley, they also occurred on rivers and unlined canal banks.
- Riparian zonation is diffuse. Only pseudoriparian shrubs show a distinct zone near 3,000 feet.

**Species associations (association indexes, Dice 1945)**
- On the lower sector, [[Lycium Andersonii]] was strongly associated with [[Prosopis Juliflora]] (88 percent) and with [[Hymenoclea Monogyra]] (69 percent). Lycium occurred only beneath shade in the lower sector and became more open-site tolerant at higher elevations.
- Hymenoclea and Prosopis were associated on 71 percent of plots where Hymenoclea occurred. Prosopis occurred with Hymenoclea 77 percent of the time.
- [[Larrea Tridentata]] and [[Franseria Deltoidea]] were associated at 50 percent, more than expected by chance.
- In the second sector, Acacia and Hymenoclea were associated at 73 percent.
- In the third and fourth sectors, Acacia occurred on 100 percent of Prosopis plots, but Prosopis occupied only 36 percent of Acacia plots. Acacia was therefore limited to a narrower range of conditions, probably by available soil moisture.
- Among riparian and pseudoriparian species, Fraxinus pennsylvanica and Acacia greggii had an association of only 22 percent, which was significantly lower than expected. Two pseudoriparian species, Prosopis and Acacia, had an association of 45 percent, which was significantly higher than expected.

**Key ecological processes**
- Prosopis developed best on the first terrace, probably because its deep taproot reaches groundwater. Leaf drop has been observed when the water table was more than 45 feet below the surface.
- Hymenoclea was most common within the channel itself, where it propagates from buried stems and rootstock.
- Baccharis glutinosa was restricted to sites within about 6 feet of the surface or near surface water.
- Establishment depends on ecological amplitude, flood timing, stem-sprouting ability, and seed deposition. Competitive interaction for light, water, or nutrients does not cause major shifts in composition. Recovery from torrential flows, which damage stems, bury plants in sediment, or uproot them, is more important for survival.
- Riparian trees were taller and denser than adjacent desert shrubs, so they formed a conspicuous vegetation type.

**Succession and climax conclusion**
- Floods, erosion, and deposition produce a mosaic of seral stages. Each stage has a different combination of dominant species.
- The authors conclude that the riparian association, including pseudoriparian species, probably never reaches a climax community. They therefore reject the idea of a stable climax, following [[Lowe]]'s view that riparian communities may be climax communities.

**Management and methodological notes**
- Species with a frequency below about 20 percent were probably undersampled.
- The authors note that the sample sizes of forb and grass plots were not statistically tested for adequacy.
- Results on Sycamore Creek are described as localized. Similar conclusions were drawn for the [[Rio Grande]] in New Mexico (Campbell and Dick-Peddie, 1964).
