# Status Review of Gila Chub (*Gila intermedia*) in the United States and Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1996 draft technical report from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) reviews the status of the [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), a small, deep-bodied minnow endemic to the Gila River basin of the Colorado River system. It was funded by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to assess the species' range-wide status to determine whether protection is needed. The report compiles historical museum records, published literature, agency databases and new 1994–1995 field surveys.

The species is now known from 23 isolated streams or cienegas in Arizona plus a population in Sonora, Mexico, and it is considered extirpated from New Mexico. Of 23 discrete populations, seven have unknown status, seven are Unstable-Threatened, eight are Stable-Threatened and one is Stable-Secure. The main threats are [[Nonnative Fishes]], especially [[Green Sunfish]], along with water diversion, groundwater pumping, dam construction and sedimentation.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The species has a complicated nomenclatural history, with more than a dozen generic and specific names applied since the original 1854 description (first described as *Gila gibbosa*; the officially recognized name is *Tigoma intermedia* Girard 1856). It was treated as a subspecies of [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) for about 25 years before being re-elevated to full species status in 1970. The authors cite morphological differences and independent, contiguous evolutionary lineages as the basis for species status. Populations with phenotypic intermediacy between the two species, historically called "grahami," are treated in this report as roundtail chub, with the caveat that identification of individual specimens remains problematic.

**Morphology.** Gila chub are small-finned, deep-bodied and chubby, with darkly colored bodies and usually no barbels. Adult males average about 150 mm total length and females can exceed 200 mm. Key diagnostic features include:
- Lateral-line scales usually more than 61 and fewer than 80 (roundtail chub usually more than 80)
- Usually eight dorsal and anal fin rays
- Head length divided by caudal peduncle depth of 3.0 or less (roundtail chub 3.3–4.3)
- Total vertebrae 38–45 (usually fewer than 42)

**Life history.** Spawning occurs from late spring into summer, though it may be sporadic and, in one constant-temperature spring, continuous through the year. Breeding males show red or orange coloration and yellow eyes. Most individuals mature in their second or third year. Adults are carnivorous, feeding on aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, while young are more omnivorous and feed on algae and diatoms. Gila chub are highly secretive, preferring deep pools and undercut banks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Gila chub occupy pools in smaller streams, cienegas (spring-fed marshes) and artificial impoundments. They associate with riparian trees such as willows, cottonwoods and ash, and with aquatic plants such as watercress and horsetail. Young-of-the-year use shallow vegetated margins, while adults use deeper pools and eddies below swift runs.

**Associated fauna.** Native companions include [[Longfin Dace]], [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]] and [[Gila Topminnow]]. Nonnative fishes recorded within the historic range include channel and flathead catfish, red shiner, fathead minnow, green sunfish, [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Mosquitofish]], rainbow trout and carp.

**Historic distribution.** The species was historically present in about 30 rivers, streams and spring-fed tributaries across the Gila basin, including the Gila, Salt, Verde, Santa Cruz, San Pedro, San Simon, San Francisco and Agua Fria drainages. Its decline is closely tied to the loss of cienega habitat following arroyo cutting in the late 1800s and early 1900s.

**Survey methods.** Surveys used backpack electrofishing or 1/8-inch mesh seines. Sample effort was set at a minimum of 35 to 60 times the average stream width. Physical measurements included temperature, pH, conductivity and dissolved oxygen. Museum specimens from stream reaches known to contain Gila chub were accepted as *G. intermedia* when identification could not be verified.

**Population status categories.** Populations were ranked as Stable-Secure, Stable-Threatened, Unstable-Threatened, Extirpated or Unknown, using qualitative criteria because quantitative data are scarce.

## Key Findings

**Present distribution (summary of Table 16):**
- Current populations in Arizona include T-4 Spring (Babocomari River), Bass, Hot Springs and Double R canyons, Blue River, [[Bonita Creek]], [[Cienega Creek]], [[Eagle Creek]], Indian Creek, [[O'Donnell Canyon]], [[Redfield Canyon]], Red Tank Draw, [[Sabino Canyon]], San Carlos River, [[Sheehy Spring]], [[Silver Creek]], [[Spring Creek]], [[Sycamore Creek]], [[Walker Creek]], [[Harden Cienega Creek]] and Dix Creek, plus Little Sycamore Creek and the stocked Larry and Lousy creeks.
- In Mexico, the only known population is at Cienega los Fresnos, a tributary of the [[San Pedro River]], with a second population at Cienega la Cienegita reported in 1990–1991.
- The species is considered extirpated in New Mexico.

**Population status:**
- **Stable-Secure:** Cienega Creek, due to its protected Empire-Cienega Resource Conservation Area.
- **Stable-Threatened:** Silver Creek, Sycamore Creek, Spring Creek, Walker Creek, Bass Canyon, Redfield Canyon, Bonita Creek and others. Redfield Canyon holds about seven miles of perennial stream and Gila chub were the most abundant species in 1991 (72%) and 1992 (42%) TNC monitoring.
- **Unstable-Threatened:** Sabino Canyon, Sheehy Spring, O'Donnell Canyon, Eagle Creek, Little Sycamore Creek, Indian Creek and Babocomari River T-4 Spring, among others.
- **Extirpated:** Cave Creek/Seven Springs, Fish Creek, Monkey Spring/Sonoita Creek, Santa Cruz River mainstem, Queen and Arnette creeks, Post Canyon, Turkey Creek (last seen 1991, extirpated 1991–1992), San Simon River and the San Pedro River in the U.S.

**Key threats:**
- Predation and competition from green sunfish. In Sabino Canyon, Gila chub declined where green sunfish were present, and green sunfish as small as 51 mm preyed on young chub. Green sunfish were identified as the most likely suppressor of recruitment in O'Donnell Canyon.
- Largemouth bass caused the extirpation of Monkey Spring chub between 1969 and 1973, following a 1968 introduction.
- Water withdrawals, especially by the city of Safford on Bonita Creek, where a BLM reserved water right may be jeopardized and lower-reach chub show heavy *Lernaea* parasitism.
- Mining pollution. Spinal deformities were observed in Blue River chub in 1995, possibly linked to abandoned asbestos mines.

**Significant management recommendations:**
- Protect and monitor all populations, preferably twice yearly in spring and late autumn.
- Remove nonnative fishes such as green sunfish from Sabino Canyon and O'Donnell Canyon, and from Sheehy Spring if feasible.
- Protect cienegas, headwaters and spring-fed habitats.
- Restore flows and avoid stocking nonnative fishes.
- Protect the entire Gila fish complex, because gene flow among *G. intermedia*, *G. robusta* and intermediate populations may have evolutionary significance.

**Knowledge gaps:**
- Population density estimates exist only for Redfield Canyon and Sabino Canyon.
- Taxonomic identity remains uncertain for Red Tank Draw, Rye Creek and Eagle Creek chub.
- The authors call for intensive surveys to confirm suspected extirpations (Turkey Creek, Babocomari River, San Pedro River in the U.S., Santa Cruz mainstem).

**Stocking:** In 1995, AGFD and BLM translocated 615 young chub into Lousy Canyon and 423 into Larry Creek from Silver Creek. In 1988, 150 chub were stocked into Garden Canyon, which was found without fish in 1995.
