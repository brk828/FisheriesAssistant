# Evaluation of Three Types of Fish Rearing Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (Research Report 39, by [[Roger E. Burrows]] and [[Harry H. Chenoweth]]) evaluates three hatchery rearing-pond designs for salmon and trout: the [[Foster-Lucas Pond]], the [[Circular Pond]] and the [[Raceway Pond]]. The authors test whether the hydraulic behaviour of each pond can be predicted from [[Scale Model Studies]] built at 1:10 scale, then relate that hydraulic behaviour to carrying capacity, disease inhibition, food distribution and cleaning efficiency. Their central conclusion is that the Foster-Lucas pond is inferior to both the raceway and circular ponds on every criterion, and that no single design is best under all conditions.

## Background & Description

Artificial propagation exposes salmonids to abnormal conditions. Confining large numbers of fingerlings in small water volumes can impair growth and development. The report is the first phase of a wider study, and it concentrates on developing ways to evaluate ponds from their hydraulic characteristics. The authors hold that biological and physical conditions depend on hydraulics, and that hydraulics can be predicted with models.

The three pond types were chosen because they had the most operating data and were typical of rearing ponds in general use, not because they were judged best or worst. Each was a recently built modification:

- **[[Foster-Lucas Pond]]:** roughly oval, 76 by 17 ft, mean depth 8 ft, capacity 28,000 gal, tested at 202 gpm. It had a centre partition wall and 36 influent jets from two headers.
- **[[Circular Pond]]:** 25 ft inside diameter, flat bottom, about 31¼ in deep, capacity 9,630 gal, fed at 105 gpm by one tangential nozzle angled 35° downward.
- **[[Raceway Pond]]:** 8 ft wide, 80 ft long, averaging 2 ft deep, capacity 9,650 gal, fed at 490 gpm through slots along an elevated flume. The tested unit was one of a battery of 20 ponds, and its flow was not typical of the others.

Biological and physical data came from experiments at the Salmon-Cultural Laboratory (Entiat, Wash.) and from published work by other investigators.

### Model Theory

Pond flow is governed mainly by inertial forces, so models should follow [[Froude's Law]]. At a 1:10 scale, velocities are 0.316 times prototype velocities and flow is 0.00316 times prototype flow. Viscous drag cannot be scaled exactly, so smaller models distort the flow more. The 1:10 scale was chosen as the most practicable compromise, and the authors accepted that some distortion would occur.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water movement in a rearing pond shapes several biological conditions:

- **Dissolved oxygen:** Cited limits are 5 ppm as adequate for fish and 3 ppm as lethal, following Ellis et al. (1946). Oxygen supply depends on inflow, flow path and fish distribution.
- **Carbon dioxide and metabolic wastes:** Rising [[Carbon Dioxide]] reduces oxygen uptake (the Bohr effect), and [[Metabolic Waste]] such as ammonia lowers growth and disease resistance. Dead zones and eddies are where these build up.
- **Disease:** Bacterial gill disease was endemic at the Leavenworth Station water supply, and routine prophylaxis was needed in Foster-Lucas ponds.
- **Species context:** Experiments used [[Blueback Salmon]], [[Chinook Salmon]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]. The authors note that chinook growth is not inhibited in circular tanks, citing Johnson and Gastineau (1952) and Palmer et al. (1952).

## Key Findings

### Hydraulic Behaviour and Model Fidelity

- In models run at the prototype Froude number, the same general flow patterns appeared. The largest differences were in low-velocity regions, where models were more sluggish because viscosity damped eddies.
- The Foster-Lucas pond circulated at a maximum of about 0.8 ft/s. It showed a large eddy behind the partition wall, a roll at each end, and strong [[Short Circuiting]].
- The circular pond had a high-velocity peripheral mixing zone and a torus-shaped [[Dead Zones]] region about one-third of the radius out from the centre. A slow inward bottom current carried water to the outlet screen.
- The raceway pond had no recirculation and a mean current below 0.1 ft/s. Its unsymmetrical intake (one slot delivering about twice the flow of the other) produced an uneven flow pattern. Inlet jets struck the surface at about 15 ft/s, which caused turbulence. The authors state that a baffled intake fixes this.
- Models reproduced prototype behaviour but showed more short circuiting and larger dead-space fractions. Predictions for a proposed prototype should therefore be shifted slightly and reduced to about 90% of the 1:10 model ordinates.

### Short Circuiting and Dye-Tracer Results

[[Dye Tracer Test]] methods used gentian-violet dye injected at the influent, with effluent concentration measured by photocolorimeter. Model injections lasted 30 seconds. Prototype injections lasted 5 to 9 minutes.

### Carrying Capacity

- Earlier authors (Cobb and Titcomb 1930, Surber 1936, Prévost 1941, Davis 1946) held that circular ponds beat raceways. The authors' tests cast doubt on this. The circular pond carries more fish per cubic foot per gallon of inflow, but the raceway looks superior if inflow is ignored.
- Blueback salmon in deep troughs exceeded 5 lb per cubic foot before the experiment was abandoned because screen and drain capacity was overtaxed. Davis (1946) reported normal rainbow trout growth above 5 lb per cubic foot in shallow troughs.
- Chinook salmon in the circular pond reached slightly over 1 lb per cubic foot, with capacity a function of weight and volume rather than fish size. Disease limited Foster-Lucas ponds to about one-third lb per cubic foot.
- Under optimum water supply the raceway was rated superior to the circular pond, which in turn beat the Foster-Lucas pond. With limited water used at maximum efficiency, the circular pond was best. The Foster-Lucas pond was not suited to water reuse.

### Disease Inhibition

- Raceway ponds inhibited disease better than circular pools, and circular pools better than Foster-Lucas ponds.
- At about 60 °F and one-third lb per cubic foot, blueback salmon developed epidemic gill disease within 2 weeks in the Foster-Lucas setting. At about 1 lb per cubic foot in an 18-ft circular pool, an epidemic took 3 weeks. In an improvised raceway, with adequate inflow and loads no higher than 1 lb per cubic foot, the disease never reached epidemic levels.
- Isolation of infected fish and fast removal of pathogens are the main protective factors. Raceway fish separate naturally, with healthy fish near the inflow and sick fish near the outlet.

### Food Distribution

- Food distribution is mainly a function of current velocity. A current of 0.8 to 1 ft/s spreads most sinking feeds around the circular pond and the narrow section of the Foster-Lucas pond. The raceway current, below 0.1 ft/s, forces artificial feeding along the pond length.
- In the circular pond a floating food ring takes about 8 minutes per revolution, and most of the food is eaten during that time.
- The circular pond ranks best for food distribution, then the Foster-Lucas pond, then the raceway.

### Cleaning Efficiency

- Currents of 0.8 to 1 ft/s carry excrement and most debris. Heavier material settles at about 0.1 ft/s.
- The circular pond was self-cleaning, with debris swept from the torus eddy to the screens. The Foster-Lucas pond deposited debris in its large eddy, and the sediment was recirculated when drawn down. The raceway had little self-cleaning action, but material could be brushed to the screens during drawdown. The raceway was better than the Foster-Lucas pond on this criterion.

### Conclusions

- Hydraulic characteristics of rearing ponds can be studied satisfactorily with 1:10 scale models, after correction for viscosity.
- None of the three designs approaches perfection, and none is superior under all conditions. The Foster-Lucas pond is inferior to both the raceway and circular pond on all four criteria.
- A raceway with a baffled intake might justify its low initial cost where water is plentiful. Its velocities rarely suffice for self-cleaning.
- Circular ponds with recirculation cannot easily escape short circuiting and mixing, given the straight-line path of moving water and the retarding effect of fixed boundaries.
- The authors recommend that future pond designs be assessed through hydraulic modelling, with criteria for carrying capacity, disease, feeding and cleaning, before improvements are attempted.
