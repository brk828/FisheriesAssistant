# Colorado River Basin Gila Taxonomy Project Phase I Report

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #conservation-biology #taxonomy

## Executive Summary

This Phase I report (Parts 2 and 3) outlines management concerns, priority research needs, and a comprehensive research plan for resolving taxonomic confusion within the [[Gila Robusta Complex]] of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The project addresses critical uncertainties regarding three endangered cyprinid species—[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*)—whose taxonomic status has confounded recovery efforts under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Prepared by Wayne C. Starnes (Smithsonian Institution) for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service under Cooperative Agreement No. 14-16-0006-89-913, the document proposes integrated morphological and genetic investigations (protein electrophoresis, DNA analysis, cytogenetics) across the entire basin, including Mexican populations. The study emphasizes development of non-lethal sampling techniques to minimize impacts on imperiled populations while obtaining data necessary to identify "management units of concern" and resolve questions about hybridization, introgression, and species boundaries.

## Background & Description

The cyprinid genus *Gila* contains large western North American minnows distributed from Oregon's [[Klamath River]] to northwestern Mexico. The [[Gila Robusta Complex]] historically occurred throughout the [[Colorado River]] drainage except at high elevations, but many populations have been extirpated since the 1800s due to habitat alterations. Three nominal species have been recognized in mainstem rivers: *G. cypha* (described 1946), confined mostly to canyon reaches; *G. elegans* (described 1853), the bonytail; and *G. robusta* (described 1853), the roundtail inhabiting both large and small streams.

Taxonomic confusion has persisted for decades. Miller (1945) initially reduced *G. elegans* to subspecies status under *G. robusta*, a treatment maintained until Holden and Stalnaker (1970) restored species status to all three taxa. Various concepts have been invoked: "ecotypes" (La Rivers 1962), products of incomplete speciation (Holden and Stalnaker 1970), and a "superspecies" concept (Smith et al. 1979). Recent sampling has yielded numerous specimens with morphology seemingly intermediate between humpback and bonytail or roundtail forms, leading to conflicting opinions about whether these represent intraspecific variation, products of hybridization, or evidence of "coalescing" species due to habitat alterations.

Current distributions are severely restricted. Bonytails are extirpated from the [[Gila River]] system and lower Colorado except [[Lake Mohave]] and possibly [[Lake Havasu]], with only sporadic upper basin records. Humpback chubs occur primarily in [[Grand Canyon]] (especially near the [[Little Colorado River]]), [[Cataract Canyon]], [[Westwater Canyon]]-Black Rocks area, [[Desolation Canyon]]-Gray Canyon, and the [[Yampa River]]-[[Green River]] confluence.

Tributary populations present additional taxonomic challenges. The [[Virgin River]] chub (*G. robusta seminuda*), now federally endangered, shows variable morphology ranging between *robusta*, *elegans*, and *cypha*; Miller (1946) and Rosenfeld and Wilkinson (1989) hypothesized hybrid origin. The [[White River]] system harbors spring-associated populations described as *G. jordani* but later relegated to subspecific status. In the [[Gila River Basin]], populations have received varied treatments, with debate over the status of *G. intermedia* (species versus subspecies) and *G. grahami*. Mexican populations in [[Rio Yaqui]] and other Gulf of California tributaries (formerly *G. minacae*) are now considered synonymous with *G. robusta*, but definitive study is lacking.

Artificial propagation programs have successfully bred *G. elegans* (Lake Mohave broodstock), *G. cypha* (Little Colorado River, Black Rocks), *G. r. seminuda*, and *G. r. jordani*. Hybrid combinations and backcrosses have been artificially produced, demonstrating interfertility.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The taxonomic confusion has severe management implications. Habitat alterations—particularly impoundments like [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]], and [[Lake Powell]]—have been hypothesized to facilitate hybridization or select for different morphological variants. Holden and Stalnaker (1970) noted numerous "intergrades" in the Lake Powell area potentially resulting from impoundment, though similar problematic specimens collected pre-impoundment from the upper [[Green River]] could not be explained by this mechanism.

The inability to distinguish protected endangered forms from sympatric non-listed forms has frustrated recovery planning. Biologists studying populations in the [[Yampa River]], [[Desolation Canyon]], [[Westwater Canyon]], and [[Debeque Canyon]] have reached contradictory conclusions about population composition. Rosenfeld and Wilkinson (1989) found biochemical evidence of extensive hybridization between *cypha* and *robusta* in Desolation and Westwater canyons, resulting in genetic differences between these humpback-like populations and those from the Little Colorado River. However, other investigators believed these populations were largely separable morphologically.

The Grand Canyon population may represent relatively "pure" humpback chubs, but the historical absence (or extirpation) of roundtail and bonytail forms raises questions about gene subsumation. Throughout the basin, natural and artificial dewatering has isolated many tributary populations, creating additional management units of uncertain taxonomic status.

Mexican populations in eastern Gulf of California tributaries from [[Rio Yaqui]] to possibly [[Rio Culiacan]] remain poorly studied. Canyon reaches of Mexican rivers may harbor additional forms (e.g., humpback-like) analogous to Colorado Basin canyon specialists.

## Key Findings

**Management Concerns and Questions:**

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service posed five fundamental questions:
1. Are roundtail, bonytail, and humpback chubs three distinct species adequately supported by recent data?
2. Is genetic and morphological variation within these species adequately described?
3. Has there been identifiable change in genetic/morphological characteristics related to environmental perturbations?
4. What key characteristics separate these species? Can definitive criteria identify species and hybrids?
5. To what degree must genetic integrity be protected to preserve *Gila* spp. in the Colorado River?

**Priority Research Needs (in order):**

1. **Development of non-lethal techniques** for morphological and genetic studies to enable future population monitoring without sacrificing endangered specimens.

2. **Comprehensive genetic and morphological investigation of mainstem river populations** throughout upper basin and Grand Canyon to: determine variation within genetically identifiable units; search for hybrid indicators and introgression patterns; assess whether historical forms persist; document geographic population structure; provide baseline genetic variation data; enable comparisons with tributary forms.

3. **Comparative study of tributary populations** basin-wide to: determine taxonomic status relative to mainstem forms; characterize genetically distinctive populations; examine status of recognized taxa (*G. intermedia*, *G. r. jordani*, *G. r. grahami*, *G. r. seminuda*); search for introgression in [[Gila River Basin]]; provide baseline genetic data for endangered tributary populations.

4. **Study of larval and juvenile *Gila* spp.** using specimens genetically correlated with adult morphotypes to identify distinguishing characters for young stages and assess local assemblage composition.

5. **Comparative study of Mexican populations** in [[Rio Yaqui]], [[Rio Mayo]], [[Rio Fuerte]], and other Gulf tributaries to determine taxonomic status and explore possible canyon-adapted forms.

6. **Genetic study of artificially propagated stock** to compare hatchery stocks with wild populations and determine genetic variability relative to wild populations.

**Proposed Research Methods:**

- **Morphological investigations**: Univariate/multivariate analyses of preserved specimens and non-lethal imagery; correlation with genotypes; documentation of variation within and between forms; identification of distinguishing features; analysis of hybrid morphology; comparison of recent versus historical material.

- **Protein electrophoresis**: Survey of multiple tissues (muscle, liver, eye-brain, fin, blood) from representative populations to identify fixed differences and genetic markers; expanded analysis of larger samples focusing on marker loci; detection of hybrids and introgression through heterozygosity patterns; genetic distance analyses; estimation of genetic variability (heterozygosity).

- **DNA investigations**: Restriction enzyme studies or other methods to identify genetically distinctive populations; mitochondrial DNA analysis to test for hybrid origin, introgression patterns, and directionality of hybrid matings; estimation of genetic variability through sequence divergence or lineage diversity.

- **Cytogenetic investigations**: Application of advanced techniques (NOR and C-banding) on cultured fin tissue to identify distinctive chromosomal attributes for genetically distinct groups and detect hybrids.

- **Larval-juvenile studies**: Correlation of genetically identified juveniles with preserved developmental series; sampling of nursery areas with genetic identification of subsamples; artificial spawning of known genotypes for developmental documentation.

**Sampling Strategy:**

Extensive sampling proposed across 50+ localities from Wyoming to Mexico, with sample sizes varying by population status:
- **Standard preservation** (adults): 10-20 specimens per locale for non-endangered populations
- **Non-lethal sampling**: 50-150 specimens from populations containing endangered forms
- **Juveniles**: 10-100 frozen specimens, 20-100 preserved specimens per locale
- **Minimal sacrifice**: Maximum 5 specimens of endangered forms per population for comprehensive enzyme surveys

Sampling requires coordinated effort among U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, state agencies (Utah, Colorado, Wyoming, Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico), Bureau of Reclamation, National Park Service, academic researchers, and Mexican biologists. Non-lethal procedures include: anesthetization; video/photographic imagery; removal of small muscle and fin tissue samples (possibly blood/liver); wound treatment; rapid revival and release.

**Project Structure:**

Phase II (research phase) to commence upon approval, with Requests for Proposals derived from this plan. Phase III (synthesis) anticipated approximately three years hence, with collaborative preparation of final report by researchers and Project Manager under Review Panel oversight. Results to be published in primary scientific literature. Materials to be deposited in appropriate repositories (U.S. National Museum, National Ecology Center, researchers' institutions).
