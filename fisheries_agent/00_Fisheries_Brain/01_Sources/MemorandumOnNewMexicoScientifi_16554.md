## Executive Summary
This document is a scientific collecting permit memorandum issued by the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish (NMDGF) to Dr. Paul C. Marsh of Arizona State University, authorizing the capture and limited retention of native and non-native fish species within the [[Gila River Basin]]. The permit (Authorization No. 1818) governs sampling methodology, species-specific collection quotas, and mandatory coordination with the [[NMDGF Endangered Species Program]] for activities affecting sensitive taxa such as the [[Gila Trout]], [[Spikedace]], and [[Loach Minnow]]. While not a primary research paper, the memo provides indirect ecological and conservation-management data regarding protected fish taxa in southwestern warmwater and coldwater stream systems.

## Background & Description
No direct morphological data (e.g., meristics, body plan, coloration) are provided in this administrative document. However, the taxa referenced are well-characterized in ichthyological literature:

- **[[Gila Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus gilae*): A salmonid endemic to the upper [[Gila River Basin]], distinguished by golden-yellow coloration, small irregular black spots, and a lack of the pronounced red stripe seen in some related trout.
- **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*): A small-bodied cyprinid characterized by a laterally compressed body, a sharp, spine-like unbranched dorsal ray (giving rise to its common name), and adaptation to swift, shallow riffle habitats.
- **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): A small benthic cyprinid with a streamlined, subcylindrical body adapted for maintaining position in fast-flowing riffle substrates, with reduced swim bladder and flattened ventral surface.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- All target taxa occupy **low- to mid-elevation flowing-water reaches** of the [[Gila River Basin]], a system draining into the Colorado River Basin.
- The **[[Gila Trout]]** occupies cold, clear, high-gradient headwater streams, typically at higher elevations than the other listed species.
- **[[Spikedace]]** and **[[Loach Minnow]]** are riffle-obligate species requiring swift, shallow, gravel-cobble substrates in mid-elevation perennial streams — habitats highly sensitive to flow alteration, sedimentation, and non-native species introductions.
- The permit's differentiation between "native" and "non-native" species categories underscores the basin's status as an invaded ecosystem, where **non-native fish species outcompete or predate upon native fauna**, a major driver of the endangered status of [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]].
- Sampling is explicitly restricted to **wetted-water reaches**, reflecting the intermittent and drought-prone nature of Southwestern desert stream systems.

## Key Findings
- **Regulatory Framework**: NMDGF issues Scientific and Educational Permits/Authorizations for Protected Wildlife (SEPAP) governing legal collection of sensitive aquatic species; permits can be modified or suspended to protect wildlife populations.
- **Collection Quotas** (Authorization No. 1818, valid through March 1994):
  - Up to **250 salmonids** (explicitly *excluding* [[Gila Trout]])
  - Up to **50 [[Gila Trout]]**
  - Up to **60 combined [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]**
  - Up to **150 individuals of other native species**
  - Up to **300 individuals of non-native species**
- **Methodology**: Approved take methods are limited to **electroshocking** and **non-lethal netting**, minimizing lethal impact on protected populations.
- **Disposition**: Collected specimens/data are retained by Arizona State University (ASU).
- **Oversight Mechanism**: All sampling and associated activities must be coordinated with and approved by D.L. Propst of the NMDGF [[Endangered Species Program]], reflecting a cooperative management model between academic researchers and state wildlife authorities for [[Threatened and Endangered Fish Species]] in the [[Gila River Basin]].
- **Conservation Implication**: The tiered quota structure (very low caps on [[Gila Trout]], [[Spikedace]], and [[Loach Minnow]] versus higher caps on non-natives) reflects differential conservation priority, consistent with these species' listings under the U.S. Endangered Species Act.
