# Factors Affecting the Respiration of Aquatic Plants

#aquatic-botany #limnology #macrophytes

## Executive Summary

This 1964 study by M. Owens and P. J. Maris of the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] (Stevenage, England) measures how dissolved oxygen concentration and temperature affect the respiration of four common macrophytes from the [[River Ivel]], a small chalk stream in Bedfordshire. The authors tested excised shoots and, for some species, entire plants, under still and agitated water in darkness. Oxygen consumption rose with dissolved oxygen concentration, with the relationship fitted by R = aC^b. Water agitation increased respiration, and respiration rose with temperature. The work was motivated by the finding that aquatic plants may account for about 70 % of the summer oxygen consumption of the river community.

## Background & Description

The paper is a research article in *Hydrobiologia* (Vol. XXIII, Fasc. 3–4, 30 June 1964, Dr. W. Junk Publishers, The Hague). The journal page included editorial guidelines, which are not summarised here.

The introduction frames aquatic plant respiration as a principal driver of the oxygen balance in flowing and stagnant waters. The goal was to determine the oxygen consumption rates of the major species of the [[River Ivel]] plant community so that community oxygen consumption, and its effect on stream oxygen concentration, could be calculated. The study builds on earlier work by Edwards & Owens (1960) and Owens & Edwards (1961, 1962).

The four species studied were:

- *Ranunculus pseudofluitans* (Syme) Newbould
- *Hippuris vulgaris* Linnaeus
- *Callitriche obtusangula* Le Gall
- *Berula erecta* (Hudson) Coville

Plants were collected at regular intervals. Only healthy specimens were used.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The River Ivel is a chalk stream. Its macrophyte community consumes substantial oxygen, and the authors state that plants are probably responsible for about 70 % of the river community's oxygen consumption during summer. This makes plant [[Respiration]] central to the stream's [[Dissolved Oxygen]] budget, especially at low oxygen levels or high temperatures.

The authors discuss two types of oxygen-respiration relationship reported in earlier work. Marine algae, some freshwater algae, and *Posidonia* show respiration that falls in proportion to oxygen content. Higher freshwater plants and some freshwater algae show respiration that decreases logarithmically. The four species here belong to the second type.

Stirring the water increased respiration, which suggests that diffusion of oxygen to respiratory tissues may limit respiration under natural conditions. Gessner and Pannier (1958b) showed that very rapid shaking was needed to remove differences in respiration between samples at 100–400 % saturation, supporting this idea.

## Key Findings

### Methods

- **Excised shoots:** Apical shoot portions of about two inches were kept overnight in aerated water at the test temperature and in darkness. Shoots were acclimatized to the test dissolved-oxygen concentration for at least 1 hr.
- **Respiration chambers:** Shoots were sealed in closed tubes with about 65 ml of borehole water, saturated with a controlled gas mixture. Oxygen drop was measured over 1 hr at constant temperature. Some tubes were stirred by magnets, while others were kept quiescent.
- **Entire plants:** Whole plants, including roots, were placed in perspex tubes filled with air-saturated water. Oxygen was measured periodically with a dropping-mercury electrode, and the water was stirred between measurements. Acclimatization was not carried out, so oxygen was allowed to decline steadily.
- **Measurement:** Oxygen was determined polarographically with a wide-bore dropping-mercury electrode. Results are expressed as mg O₂ consumed per g dry weight of plant per hour, after drying at 105 °C to constant weight.
- **Statistics:** Regression analyses and significance tests followed Fisher (1954).

### Oxygen Concentration Effects (Excised Shoots, 20 °C)

- Oxygen consumption followed R = aC^b across the experimental range of about 1.2–17 ppm. Rates rose with dissolved oxygen up to roughly 200 % saturation, the maximum used.
- Agitated water produced higher respiration than quiescent water in all three species tested at 20 °C. Table I reports the following values:

| Plant | Agitated mean rate (mg O₂/g dry wt h) | Quiescent mean rate (mg O₂/g dry wt h) | Significance of regression coefficient difference | Significance of mean rate difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Callitriche* | 2.52 ± 0.04 | 2.08 ± 0.05 | P > 0.90 | P < 0.001 |
| *Hippuris* | 1.52 ± 0.02 | 1.37 ± 0.02 | P < 0.01 | P < 0.001 |
| *Ranunculus* | 1.51 ± 0.02 | 1.11 ± 0.02 | P < 0.01 | P < 0.001 |

- The summary reports rates at 20 °C in air-saturated water of 1.25 (*Berula erecta*), 2.8 (*Callitriche obtusangula*), 1.96 (*Hippuris vulgaris*), and 1.90 (*Ranunculus pseudofluitans*). These differ from the Table I means, and the basis for the difference is not stated in the text.

### Temperature Effects

- Respiration of *Ranunculus*, *Callitriche*, and *Hippuris* rose with temperature at all oxygen levels. Agitated plants respired faster than quiescent ones.
- Temperature coefficients (Q₁₀) were calculated over 10–15 °C, 15–20 °C, and 10–20 °C (Table III):

| Plant | Q₁₀ 10–15 °C (agitated / quiescent) | Q₁₀ 15–20 °C (agitated / quiescent) | Q₁₀ 10–20 °C (agitated / quiescent) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Callitriche* | 3.14 / 3.21 | 2.32 / 2.34 | 2.70 / 2.74 |
| *Hippuris* | 1.32 / 1.39 | 1.79 / 1.70 | 1.54 / 1.54 |
| *Ranunculus* | 3.48 / 3.36 | 2.12 / 2.15 | 2.72 / 2.65 |
| *Berula* | 2.42 (agitated only) | 1.93 (agitated only) | 2.18 (agitated only) |

- Q₁₀ values ranged from 1.32 to 3.48 over 10–20 °C, consistent with literature values cited (Platenius, 1942; James, 1953).
- Q₁₀ values were generally lower in the upper temperature range. The exception was *Hippuris*. Agitated and quiescent Q₁₀ values were similar.
- At low oxygen concentrations, temperature had a larger effect on Q₁₀ for most species. The effect was small for *Callitriche* and *Berula*.

### Entire Plants

- Entire plants responded to oxygen concentration in a similar way to excised shoots. The regression coefficients differed from those for shoots, although rates were comparable (Table IV):

| Plant | Regression equation (20 °C) | Mean rate (mg O₂/g dry wt h) | Mean oxygen (ppm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Callitriche* | R = 1.63C^0.25 | 2.29 ± 0.03 | 3.95 |
| *Hippuris* | R = 1.02C^0.31 | 1.58 ± 0.03 | 4.10 |
| *Ranunculus* | R = 0.84C^0.38 | 1.43 ± 0.02 | 4.12 |

- *Berula erecta* was tested as entire plants at 10, 15, and 20 °C. Its response to oxygen was similar to the other species, and its Q₁₀ decreased slightly with rising oxygen concentration.
- The authors note that different plant tissues respond differently to oxygen. Earlier work on *Arum maculatum* showed that spadix slices respired in proportion to oxygen across 2–100 %, while stalk slices were nearly constant across 5–100 %.

### Discussion Points

- Plant respiration rose with oxygen concentration. This is similar in form to equations for simultaneous diffusion and chemical reaction (Crank, 1957), which supports the diffusion-limitation interpretation.
- The lower Q₁₀ at higher temperatures agrees with Platenius (1942), who found this pattern in 7 of 10 vegetables. It reflects the general slowing of biological reaction rates near high temperatures.

### Conclusions

- The oxygen consumption of four macrophytes was measured across several oxygen concentrations and at three temperatures.
- Oxygen consumption and temperature both increased respiration. Stirring increased rates, consistent with oxygen diffusion as a limiting factor in nature.
- These rates are needed to estimate how the macrophyte community affects the oxygen balance of streams such as the [[River Ivel]].
