# Distribution, Abundance and Life History of the Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1973 Utah State University doctoral dissertation by Paul Bernard Holden documents the status of the fish fauna of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. Sampling ran from 1967 to 1972. The study found that introduced species outnumbered native species 19 to 10, and that four endemic large-river fishes ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Humpback Sucker]]) were rare. Reproduction of these forms was judged probable only in the lower [[Yampa River]], [[Desolation Canyon]] and the lower [[Green River]] in [[Canyonlands National Park]]. The main causes of decline were identified as habitat alteration by high dams and the introduction of exotic species.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin fish fauna is highly unusual. Miller (1959) calculated 74 percent endemism for native basin fishes, one of the highest values in North America, which the author attributes to a long period of geographic isolation. The river runs cold and clear in the Rocky Mountains, then flows warm, turbid and often violent through desert canyons.

The endemic fishes fall into two groups: small-stream forms, mostly in the lower basin, and large-river forms of the main stem and large tributaries. The large-river group includes:

- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), described as North America's largest minnow
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), both with humped backs thought to aid swift-water life
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), with a streamlined body
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), the most common endemic
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), not a true endemic but present as two body forms

Earlier surveys were sparse. Evermann and Rutter (1895) summarized pre-1895 collections. [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closed in November 1962, after a 1962 [[Fish Eradication Program]] removed most native fishes above and below the dam. [[Lake Powell]] formed behind [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in the 1960s.

Methods included gill nets, seines, electrofishing (from a raft in 1971), and fish tagging with spaghetti and dorsal-fin metal tags. No poisons or explosives were used because of the rare-species focus. Nomenclature followed Bailey et al. (1970), except for the humpback chub complex.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water conditions.** Turbidity was highest during spring runoff and after storms, and fell rapidly through June. Summer temperatures reached 21–23 °C in most of the study area, and winter lows were 1–3 °C. Lower reaches were warmer and more turbid because of larger desert watersheds.

**Habitat.** The study area covered about 20 sampled sections from Craig, Colorado, to the Green–Colorado confluence, plus Marble and Grand Canyons. Sand-bottomed reaches (Green River below Dinosaur National Monument, Green River near Green River, Utah, and the Colorado below Moab) contrasted with rocky, gravelly, fast-and-slow reaches. Rocky canyon reaches supported more species, higher fish numbers, and, as the author inferred, higher aquatic insect (secondary) production.

**Dam effects.** Cold tailwaters below high dams were judged unsuitable for native reproduction. Reservoirs were judged unable to support endemic reproduction, although fish could grow in them. In Marble and Grand Canyons, the main channel stayed cold for more than 250 miles and had few aquatic insects. The author called it "a very sterile environment," and daily water-level fluctuation prevented warm backwaters that juveniles need.

**Competition.** Introduced species, including [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Red Shiner]], may have increased competition for food and space, especially for juveniles.

## Key Findings

**Status and abundance**

- Twenty-nine species were collected: 19 introduced and 10 native.
- The four rare endemics were [[Colorado Squawfish]] (300 adults, 261 of them in the lower Yampa), [[Bonytail Chub]] (36), [[Humpback Chub]] complex (39) and [[Humpback Sucker]] (53).
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (375 adults) was the dominant native carnivore in the upper basin.
- Well over 7,500 adult fish were collected. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (over 6,000) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (over 1,000) were the most abundant species.
- Species richness was highest in the lower Yampa and Green (DNM), with 17 species each, and lowest in the Colorado (CNP), with 7.

**Reproduction and spawning**

- Flannelmouth suckers spawned at 12–15 °C, bluehead suckers at 15–18 °C, roundtail chubs at 18–21 °C, and Colorado squawfish at about 21 °C.
- Squawfish moved into the Yampa in July and early August as water warmed and cleared. More than 90 percent of those caught in the surge were ripe males.
- Bonytail reproduction appeared to have declined sharply after Flaming Gorge closure. Only three possible juvenile bonytails were found among several hundred young chubs.
- Humpback chub reproduction was probable in Desolation Canyon.

**Movement**

- A total of 3,322 fish were tagged, 2,549 of them flannelmouth suckers.
- Of 217 recaptures, 211 were flannelmouth suckers, bluehead suckers or squawfish, all taken within 10 miles of [[Echo Park]] in the Green and Yampa rivers.
- Most fish moved little. Downstream movement was more common. One group of flannelmouth suckers moved about 65 miles upstream to below Flaming Gorge Dam.

**Taxonomy of juvenile chubs**

- Seventy juvenile *Gila* (39.5–134.0 mm SL) were measured. Humped forms had a mean body-depth/head-depth ratio of 1.728 (range 1.538–2.000), against 1.490 (1.273–1.769) for non-humped forms.
- Juveniles could not be identified with adult characters. Non-humped juveniles were most likely *G. robusta* because of dorsal fin ray counts. Most humped juveniles were probably *G. cypha*, though some may be *G. elegans*.

**Hybridization**

- Three sucker hybrids were found: flannelmouth × humpback sucker (first collected in 1889, so habitat change was not the cause), bluehead × [[White Sucker]], and flannelmouth × white sucker.
- Bluehead × white hybrids were abundant in the upper Yampa, where both parents spawned in overlapping periods. The author attributed hybridization to introduced white suckers, which were abundant there.

**Marble and Grand Canyons**

- Gill nets caught 15 humpback chub complex fish in 1967 but only 1 in 1970.
- Native fish were limited to tributary-using species such as [[Speckled Dace]], bluehead suckers and flannelmouth suckers.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- The lower Yampa, middle Green (Desolation Canyon and Green DNM) and lower Green (CNP) should be kept natural. Any alteration of the Green below Flaming Gorge Dam was expected to harm the rare fishes.
- The Yampa may now be an important summer refuge because it warms the Green in summer.
- The author called the Colorado River system itself "Rare and Endangered" and warned that little additional alteration could push the remaining native species to extinction.
