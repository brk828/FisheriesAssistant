# Status of the Leopard Darter (Percina pantherina) in Oklahoma and Arkansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Endangered Species Report (1983, Report 12) presents a 2-year monitoring study of [[Leopard Darter]] populations in [[Glover Creek]], McCurtain County, Oklahoma, supplemented by a one-summer study on the [[Cossatot River]] in Arkansas and data from [[Orth Instream Flow Study]] (1980). The leopard darter was listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in January 1978, and Glover Creek holds the largest known populations of the species within the [[Little River System]].

A total of 139 leopard darters were collected in Glover Creek (August 1977 to July 1980) and 19 on the Cossatot River. Mean density was 3.64 fish per 100 m of stream on Glover Creek and 3.44 on the Cossatot. The estimated total population in Glover Creek was 2,827 individuals. Leopard darter densities were very low compared with other darter species, although all darter densities in Glover Creek were consistently low. Comparison with earlier surveys (1884 to 1972) suggested the Glover Creek population is stable. Leopard darters preferred pool habitat in all seasons, contrary to earlier descriptions of riffle habitat.

## Background & Description

The leopard darter (Percina pantherina) was described as Hadropterus pantherinus by [[Moore and Reeves 1955]]. On January 27, 1978, the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] determined it to be threatened, giving it full protection under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, and designated critical habitat within the Little River system. The Service, working with the [[Oklahoma Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] and Oklahoma State University, carried out monitoring from 1978 to 1980 to document status, biology and ecology.

The species is endemic to the Little River system of southeastern Oklahoma and southwestern Arkansas. Glover Creek, a tributary of the Little River that is the last unimpounded tributary of the system, was selected as the principal study area. Its drainage basin covers approximately 876 km², with the mainstem about 53 km long, and elevations ranging from 103 m at the mouth to 610 m in the upper reaches. The upper watershed is forested Ouachita Mountain terrain used for commercial timber harvest, while the lower reaches cross Gulf Coastal Plain flatlands used for livestock grazing. A proposed flood-control dam, Lukfata Lake, was not funded, and the critical habitat designation makes its construction unlikely.

Adult leopard darters were measured for total length from 45 to 92 mm, with a mean of 70.2 mm and a standard deviation of 9.0 mm. Specimens were assigned to age classes using scale annuli from 14 museum specimens.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat preference.** Leopard darters were captured at depths of 8 to 122 cm (most often 20 to 79 cm), at water velocities of 0 to 96 cm/s (most often 0 to 19 cm/s), and over substrates from gravel/rubble to bedrock (most often rubble/boulder). Of 139 captures, 108 were in pools, 19 in riffles and 11 in runs. Pools were the only habitat occupied during summer. Preference was consistent across seasons, with minor shifts in depth and substrate use.

**Sampling methods and their limits.** Seining was ineffective in pools where boulder substrate predominated and currents were weak. Underwater dipnet capture was limited to low, clear water. [[D.C. Electrofishing]] proved most broadly effective and was used for most of the study, though it was less efficient in slow pools, turbid water and deep, fast areas.

**Community context.** The Glover Creek darter community was dominated by the [[Orangebelly Darter]] (Etheostoma radiosum), which made up about 95% of the catch. Leopard darters, [[Logperch]] and [[Channel Darter]] contributed significantly, and the remaining species (Johnny Darter, Orangethroat Darter, Dusky Darter) were uncommon, largely confined to the lower Glover.

**Flow regime.** Glover Creek experiences extreme seasonal discharge, with high flows and occasional flooding in late fall and spring and low flows in summer and early fall. Periodic spring flooding may interfere with spawning, while low flow may limit habitat.

## Key Findings

**Population size and density**
- Glover Creek: 139 leopard darters collected (August 1977 to July 1980); mean density 3.64 per 100 m of stream; maximum 27 per 100 m (0.017/m²).
- Cossatot River: 19 leopard darters observed; mean 3.44 per 100 m; maximum 8 per 100 m.
- Estimated total in Glover Creek: 2,827 (East Fork 805, West Fork 1,254, mainstem 786).
- Of 56 population estimates, 25 (44.6%) exceeded zero; the maximum estimate at any site was 12.
- Seventy-one percent of captures occurred in four consecutive samples from August 1979 to March 1980. Catch then fell to earlier levels in July 1980, possibly due to drought and record high temperatures in May and June.

**Distribution**
- Leopard darters occurred across the entire designated critical habitat in Glover Creek and at several sites outside it, including 61200 m.s. on the West Fork, 51750 on Carter Creek, the area below the 3+7 bridge and 52500 on Pine Creek.
- Cedar Creek was the only sampled site without captures.
- Distribution was clumped rather than random (main sites: χ² = 11.95, P<.025; additional sites: χ² = 79.74, P<.0001).

**Age and growth**
- Age classes by total length: 0+ (<50 mm), 1+ (51 to 71 mm), 2+ (72 to 87 mm), 3+ (≥88 mm). Contribution to catch: 0+ 1.5%, 1+ 63.5%, 2+ 32%, 3+ 3.0%.
- The scarcity of 0+ fish was unexplained; the authors suggested sampling bias or use of inaccessible deep-pool habitat by juveniles.

**Habitat selection**
- Depth: preferred 20 to 79 cm (χ² = 37.00, P<.0001).
- Velocity: preferred 0 to 19 cm/s (χ² = 7.96, P<.10).
- Substrate: rubble, rubble/boulder and boulder preferred (χ² = 14.61, P<.10).
- Conclusion: leopard darters inhabit pools year-round, contradicting earlier qualitative descriptions of swift riffle habitat.

**Community and relative abundance**
- Main sites (Nov 1977 to Jul 1980): 4,614 orangebelly darters, 1 johnny darter, 73 logperch, 65 channel darters and 84 leopard darters.
- Additional sites (Nov 1978 to Jul 1980): 3,622 orangebelly, 13 johnny, 28 orangethroat, 21 logperch, 40 channel, 9 dusky and 52 leopard darters.
- Leopard darters, logperch and channel darters had similar relative abundance; the authors attributed this to shared habitat preferences.

**Sampling method conclusions**
- D.C. electrofishing was used exclusively on Glover Creek and combined with substrate disturbance; snorkeling and electrofishing were judged the most effective methods, with a combination likely optimal.

**Recommendations**
1. Extend critical habitat to the West Fork upstream to at least 61200 m.s., the mainstem from the State Highway 3+7 bridge downstream, Pine Creek upstream to 52500 and Carter Creek upstream to 51750, to protect all significant populations.
2. Monitor Glover Creek populations every other year.
3. Research water quality variables (temperature, oxygen, pH, alkalinity, turbidity), seasonal habitat use, reproduction and early life history, and interactions with other darters.

**Threats noted:** commercial timber harvest and associated road-building (sediment and flow changes), periodic gravel removal, and the pending Comprehensive Water Plan for Oklahoma.
