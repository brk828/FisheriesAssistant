# Recovery of a Desert River Fish: The Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Summer 1995 issue of the *Arizona Conservation Voter* newsletter covers several Arizona conservation topics. Its central feature, by [[Paul C. Marsh]] (Center for Environmental Studies, [[Arizona State University]]) and [[W. L. Minckley]] (Department of Zoology, Arizona State University), describes the decline of the endemic [[Razorback Sucker]] and a recovery program centered on [[Lake Mohave]]. A second article criticizes the proposed [[Black Wash Reservoir]] near Tucson and recommends a cheaper alternative. The remaining page listings (legislation, wolves, grazing fees, bighorn sheep and similar items) are not developed in the supplied text, so they are not summarized here.

## Background & Description

The front-page epigraph quotes [[U.S. Forest Service]] Chief Jack Ward Thomas opposing the transfer of public lands to the private sector, calling the public lands heritage "too precious and too unique in the world to be traded away for pottage."

The razorback sucker is described as one of the unusual, and unlucky, fishes of the large rivers that once flowed through the low deserts of Arizona:

- **Range:** Historically occupied much of the [[Colorado River]] system, from the delta in Mexico north to the Salton Sea, the Gila and San Juan basins, through Utah to the river's source in Wyoming and Colorado.
- **Size:** Grows to about 3 feet. The weight figure in the OCR text is unclear (reads as "up to :3 Ibs"), so it is not reported here.
- **Endemism:** Endemic to the Colorado River drainage and found nowhere else in the world.
- **Longevity and fecundity:** Lives over 50 years. Lays about 5,000 eggs per inch of body length, up to 120,000 eggs in a 24-inch female.
- **Historical use:** Native Americans and settlers used it as a food source. Settlers described large spawning concentrations in the [[Salt River]] and Colorado River in spring. As late as 1949, a commercial fisherman sold razorbacks from [[Saguaro Lake]] on the Salt River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and behavior.** In rivers, adults stay near the bottom in slow, strong current over silt, sand and gravel, in channel centers or along banks in deeper pools, and eat drifting organic matter and small bottom organisms. In Lake Mohave they feed near the bottom among rocks on wave-washed shorelines and take plankton in midwater of the deeper, clearer lake. In winter, males form compact aggregations over shoreline cobbles and develop breeding colors and tubercles.

**Spawning.** Females stay offshore and feed while their eggs mature, then move inshore. A female is flanked by males, creates a shallow depression by undulating over the bottom, and releases eggs and sperm. The adhesive eggs stick among stones. Spawning runs from January through early May.

**Reservoir-driven decline.** Dams and diversions have drained and altered the river system. The last wild population of any size in the Colorado basin is restricted to Lake Mohave. [[Lake Mohave]] was filled in 1954, and the youngest adults in the population are now over 40 years old. Hundreds of thousands of larvae hatch each year, but no evidence shows any surviving to maturity for many years. Without recruitment, the authors state, the species may be doomed to extinction.

**Larval mortality.** Larvae hatch in about a week, feed on yolk, and begin feeding on tiny plants and insect larvae within a week, then on larger plankton. All larvae disappear from the lake before reaching 5/10 to 6/10 inch. Three hypotheses were tested:

1. Larvae are drawn through dam turbines and killed. No evidence was found.
2. Larvae starve because a particular food is absent from the reservoir. This has some support.
3. Non-native fishes eat them. This hypothesis appears to account for most of the losses.

**Non-native predators.** Predatory [[Common Carp]], catfishes, sunfish and basses cannot be removed from Lake Mohave, which shaped the management approach.

## Key Findings

**Recovery method.** Because predators cannot be removed from the lake, managers capture larvae instead:

- Larvae are **phototactic** (drawn to light at night), so they are collected with bright lights from boats or with lights in containers that let them enter but not escape.
- Larvae are held in large aquaria and fed for a few weeks, then placed in isolated bays ("protective custody") that have been cleared of non-native predatory fishes.
- Once they reach about one foot long, they are injected with an electronic tag for individual identification and released into Lake Mohave.
- The full process takes about 6 months.

**Results and goals.**

- More than 20,000 larvae were caught in Lake Mohave in 1995.
- Earlier years produced fewer larvae, but methods have improved. The 1996 goal is 60,000 fish.
- Tagged fish from early efforts have entered the breeding aggregations, which the authors cite as evidence the program is working.
- Cooperative effort since 1973 has involved students, volunteers, university professors, and state and federal fisheries biologists.

**Conclusion.** The authors state that the effort "is not yet a success story," but that limited success is better than none, and the razorback sucker "may not be doomed to extinction after all."

**Black Wash Reservoir (related article).** The article reports that the U.S. [[Bureau of Reclamation]] closed public comment on its Draft Environmental Impact Statement (DEIS) for the proposed Black Wash Reservoir on July 14. Key points:

- The reservoir would be built near the [[Santa Cruz River Basin]] on the fringes of the Pascua Yaqui reservation south of Tucson.
- It would store up to 15,000 acre-feet of [[Central Arizona Project]] water as an emergency supply for Tucson.
- It would affect the federally listed endangered [[Pima Pineapple Cactus]] and nearly 30 other plant species.
- Federal administrators reportedly suggested stocking the reservoir with non-native game fish, which the article warns could wipe out native fish in the Santa Cruz basin if the reservoir failed and its water were released.
- The DEIS lists three alternatives: recharging the groundwater with CAP flow and building shallow holding basins, doing nothing, and Alternative C.
- **Alternative C** would add a second discharge line to each of the seven existing pumping plants between the Gila River and Tucson's CAP water treatment. If one line failed, the other could keep water flowing during CAP outages.
- The Bureau reportedly estimates the reservoir could be avoided at a savings of $35 million, relevant given existing CAP construction debt.
