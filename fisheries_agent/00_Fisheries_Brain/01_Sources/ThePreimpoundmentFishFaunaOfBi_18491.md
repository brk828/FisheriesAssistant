# Pre-Impoundment Fish Fauna of Big River (Meramec Drainage, Missouri)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This survey documents the pre-impoundment fish community of [[Big River]], the largest tributary of the [[Meramec River]] in east-central Missouri. Field collections from 1974 to 1976 at 37 stations found 80 species, and literature records and a later addendum raised the known total for the river to 90. The fauna is described as essentially [[Ozarkian Fauna]]. The authors divided species into three distribution groups and flagged 12 new records for the river. They also assessed the likely effects of two proposed [[Flood Control Project|flood control dams]] on fish composition and distribution, drawing on published impoundment studies from several regions.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** Big River rises in northern Iron County and enters the Meramec near Eureka. Its headwaters drain igneous rocks of the [[St. Francis Mountains]], while most of the watershed lies on the [[Salem Plateau]], where Ordovician and younger limestones and dolomites predominate. The stream is about 235 km long and drains almost 1,600 km². The watershed is mainly oak-hickory forest used for grazing, timber, and small farms.

**Physical character.**
- Headwaters are clear, swift and "Ozarkian"; the lower river is sluggish and more turbid.
- Mean discharge was 4.8 m³/s at Station 5 (5-year average), 18.4 m³/s near Station 23 (22-year average), and over 22.6 m³/s near the mouth (49-year average).
- Elevation falls from above 232 m at the origin to about 165 m at Station 23, about 131 m at Station 35, and about 124 m at the Meramec confluence.
- The bed is mainly coarse gravel and small boulders, with patches of sand, mud and limestone bedrock.
- Five larger tributaries join the main stem: Mineral Fork Creek, Mill Creek, Cedar Creek, Flat River Creek and Terre Bleue Creek. Terre Bleue Creek resembles the lower mainstream.

**Human pressures.** The basin has sewage effluent from small towns, most of Missouri's tiff (barite) strip mines, and several inactive lead mines. Kramer and Wixson (1977) reported elevated zinc, lead, cadmium and copper in Flat River Creek from lead mine tailings. Siltation from barite mining is also evident. Five old mill dams create pools but are submerged during floods, so they are not significant barriers to fish movement.

**Proposed impoundments.** Congress approved a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers flood control project in 1974 that would affect the whole Meramec system. For Big River it proposed two dams: one about 83 km upstream from the mouth and one about 25 km downstream from the origin. Funding has since been withdrawn, although the project has not been abandoned.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Ninety collections were made at 37 stations (20 on the mainstream, 17 on tributaries) between spring 1974 and summer 1976. Specimens were taken almost entirely by seining with 1.8 × 1.2 m to 12.2 × 2.4 m nylon seines, preserved in 10% formalin and stored in 40% isopropanol. Vouchers are held at three institutions. Nomenclature follows [[Bailey et al. 1970]], the common and scientific name list for North American fishes.

**Habitat groups.** Species were assigned to three groups based on where they occurred most often:
1. **[[Headwaters and Tributary Species]]**: found in clear, cool, narrow streams (under 8 m wide), with riffles of 50 cm or less and pools of 1.5 m or less, over gravel or bedrock.
2. **[[Lower-River Species]]**: found where turbidity is usually under 25 JTU, widths reach 75 m, pools reach 5 m, and the bottom is sand, silt and detritus with some clean gravel in riffles.
3. **[[Widely Distributed Species]]**: found throughout the system, though some (for example *Hybopsis amblops*) need specific microhabitats.

Species marked with an asterisk were placed in groups from angler reports or personal observation, because the seine could not sample them.

**Taxonomic note.** Specimens of *Campostoma* were not separated to species, but both *C. anomalum* and *C. oligolepis* occurred at stations 1 and 37 and at randomly checked stations in between, so both are treated as widely distributed.

**Historic context.** The Ozark fauna developed in the Quaternary. Sixty-five species are centred on the Ozarks in Missouri, and fourteen are endemic (Pflieger 1975). Before this survey the Meramec system held 105 species (Pflieger 1971), making it third-richest in the state, behind [[St. Francis River]] with 109 species.

## Key Findings

**Species inventory**
- 76,313 specimens of 80 species were collected from the 37 stations.
- Eight more species are known from literature records (Pflieger 1975), giving 88.
- An addendum recording *Lepomis gulosus* (two adults) and *L. microlophus* (one juvenile) at station 3 on 11 Aug. 1977 brings the total to 90.
- The abstract gives 82 species collected, while the results section gives 80; the discrepancy is in the source.

**New and notable records**
- Of the 80 collected species, 17 were of special interest. Five had their persistence in the river confirmed: *Hybopsis amblops*, *Phenacobius mirabilis*, *Pimephales promelas*, *Carpiodes carpio* and *Noturus nocturnus*.
- Twelve species are new records for Big River:
  1. [[Shortnose Gar]] (*Lepisosteus platostomus*), stations 35 and 37
  2. [[Alabama Shad]] (*Alosa alabamae*), stations 36 and 37
  3. [[Speckled Chub]] (*Hybopsis aestivalis*), station 37
  4. [[Silver Chub]] (*Hybopsis storeriana*), station 37
  5. [[Emerald Shiner]] (*Notropis atherinoides*), station 37
  6. [[Bigmouth Shiner]] (*Notropis dorsalis*), station 37
  7. [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*), stations 9 and 12
  8. [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), stations 1, 20, 34 and 37
  9. [[Spotted Bass]] (*Micropterus punctulatus*), stations 11, 20 and 31
  10. [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), stations 35 and 37
  11. [[Western Sand Darter]] (*Ammocrypta clara*), stations 35 and 37
  12. [[River Darter]] (*Percina shumardi*), station 37
- The abstract reports 14 newly reported species and 5 not collected since before 1945. These figures do not match the 12 listed records in the text.
- Three of the new records (*H. aestivalis*, *G. affinis*, *M. punctulatus*) raise the system total to 108 in the discussion. That is one fewer than the 90 counted in the results, so the source uses more than one total.

**Distribution and drainage history**
- The drainage has been a land area since the end of the Paleozoic. Uplifts and erosion cycles shaped it, and the drainage pattern of the upper basin has changed little since the late Tertiary.
- Changes in Mississippi River levels during Pleistocene glaciation probably affected the lower Meramec, as they did other Ozark drainages.

**Predicted impacts of the proposed dams** (Fig. 1 shows dam sites between stations 4–5 and 29–30)
- The upstream reservoir would reach almost to Station 1, so most of the mainstream and lower tributaries would be inundated or altered in temperature, nutrient enrichment, sediment load and flow.
- Some species are expected to increase, others to decline, and a few may be eliminated. Headwater species will probably persist, though their ranges would shrink.
- Riffle-dwelling darters and minnows would likely give way to pelagic minnows, and game fish would shift toward a bluegill–largemouth bass–crappie community. Gizzard shad, carp, buffalo and drum would likely increase.
- *Noturus nocturnus* and *Ammocrypta clara* are flagged for monitoring because of their localized distribution and specific habitat needs.

**Comparative impoundment studies cited**
- Black River, Missouri (Clearwater Lake, 1948): Funk et al. (1953).
- Tennessee streams: Ruhr (1958) reported more rough fish; Fitz (1968) found rough fish and game fish both increased on the Clinch River, and rheophilic forms disappeared.
- Belwood Lake, Ontario: Spence and Hynes (1971a, b) found benthic and fish differences and a 4-week delay in spring warming, with summer temperatures 7 °C cooler below the lake.
- Sagehen Creek, California: suckers rose from 18% to 79% of the population (Erman 1973).
- Tenkiller Reservoir, Oklahoma: dissolved oxygen below the dam was too low for many species needing more than 1 mg/L (Summers 1954).
- Lake Francis Case, South Dakota: adult fish numbers declined over 16 years, except for a few stable or increasing species (Gasaway 1970).

**Conclusion.** The authors present the 1974–1976 survey as a pre-impoundment baseline. They recommend monitoring the fauna, and especially rare species, if the flood control project proceeds.
