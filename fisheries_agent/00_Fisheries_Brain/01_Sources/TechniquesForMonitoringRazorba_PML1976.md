# Techniques for Monitoring Razorback Sucker in the Lower Colorado River, Hoover to Parker Dams, 2006–2007

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Open File Report (2008-1245), prepared with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under the Multi-Species Conservation Program, evaluates methods for monitoring the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the riverine reach of the [[Colorado River]] near Needles, California. Trammel netting, long the standard in reservoirs, proved ineffective in fast river currents. The study tested [[Hoop Netting]], [[Electrofishing]], [[Boat Surveys]], and [[Aerial Photography]] against trammel netting for accuracy, cost, stress to target fish, and by-catch. Electrofishing and boat surveys were the most cost-effective and accurate methods, while hoop netting failed and aerial photography was costly and unreliable for species identification.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] was historically monitored with [[Trammel Netting]] (4- to 12-cm mesh) at [[Lake Mohave]], where techniques were developed in the late 1970s. Spawning occurs in late winter (January through April), when game fish are less active. Trammel netting from 1999 to 2005 in the river reach between Davis Dam and Lake Havasu averaged only about 43 razorback suckers per year, and nets tended to collapse or foul with debris in the current.

In 2005, a large mid-channel spawning congregation was discovered near Needles, California, by following radio-tagged fish. Larvae collected downstream confirmed active spawning, the first documentation of riverine spawning downstream of Lake Mohave since the early 1950s. The authors described it as probably the largest riverine spawning congregation in existence. Fish spawned in currents above 0.5 m/s, and the sites were near residential and vacation homes, making conventional netting impractical for both hydraulic and public-conflict reasons.

The study's objective was to identify and test techniques that could improve population-estimate accuracy while reducing by-catch, cost, and public conflict. The report explicitly was not designed to establish a monitoring program, and it defines key terms: relative abundance (catch per unit effort trends), adult population size, sample variance (confidence limits), and spawning population (fish counted in a specific spawning reach).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** A 12-km reach of the Colorado River upstream of Needles (River Mile 245 to 253), with spawning concentrated in laminar flow over clean cobble-gravel at 1–3 m depth and velocities near 0.5 m/s. The broader Needles spawning reach spans about 50 km downstream of Davis Dam. Lake Mohave sites included Yuma, Tequila, and Carp Coves.
- **Water visibility:** Generally 3–5 m; mainstem clarity often exceeded 5 m, but plankton productivity reduced clarity in backwaters and the Lake Havasu delta.
- **By-catch and non-target fauna:** Common carp, game fish (sunfish, catfish, bass, striped bass), rainbow trout, shorebirds, waterfowl, grebes, and muskrats. Trammel netting composed 97 percent non-target fish in earlier studies.
- **Flannelmouth Sucker comparison:** Flannelmouth suckers in the Laughlin area spawn in deeper water (>3 m), whereas razorback suckers spawn shallower (<2 m), making razorbacks more vulnerable to electrofishing and capture.
- **Human factors:** Netting attracts spectators and law-enforcement calls; night electrofishing and lighting raised public concern, and FAA rules restricted helicopter flights below 100 m over residential areas and power lines.

## Key Findings

**Trammel netting (baseline, 1999–2005)**
- Averaged 43 razorback suckers per year; 0.5 fish per staff day; about $1,108 per fish.
- Annual cost estimated at $47,650; 90 staff days per season.
- Population estimates for 2003–2005 were 2,910 (95% CL 1,306–8,925), 1,768 (CL 878–3,867), and 1,652 (CL 1,004–4,494 as reported), reflecting small sample sizes.

**Hoop netting (2006 only)**
- 36 overnight sets (12 with females); no razorback suckers captured.
- Anchors failed in strong current; nets dragged, tore, and required repairs. Entrances were likely too narrow.
- Dropped after the 2006 season.

**Electrofishing (2006–2007)**
- 263 (2006) and 299 (2007) razorback suckers captured.
- Capture rate 8.3 razorback suckers per staff day at about $62 per fish.
- Adult population estimated at 1,196 (95% CI 925–1,546); confidence limits narrowed from ±500 percent to ±30 percent.
- By-catch limited to two carp; no recreational game fish, waterfowl, or mammals handled.
- Concerns: spinal injury, egg and larval damage, and altered spawning behavior with unnecessary shocking. The authors recommended capping collections at 100 fish per trip and limiting effort to three trips per spawning season.

**Boat surveys (2006–2007)**
- 888 razorback suckers positively identified per year on average; 29.6 fish per staff day at under $18 per fish.
- Density 20.5 fish/ha (2006) and 9.6 fish/ha (2007); spawning population estimates 2,520 (2006, range 1,764–4,020) and 1,152 (2007, range 792–1,740).
- Razorback suckers outnumbered other fish 3:1 (range 2.20:1 to 3.03:1) in 2006; common carp was the most prevalent non-target species.
- The 2007 decline (~39 percent) was attributed to removing a transect bias toward known spawning locations.
- Minimal effect on spawning fish; no capture or handling.

**Aerial photography (helicopter, 2006–2007)**
- Annual average of 580 fish detected on imagery; about 435 estimated as razorback suckers using the 3:1 ratio.
- 8.4 razorback suckers per staff day at about $78 per fish; $30,800 total cost.
- Helicopter crew visual counts underestimated fish by roughly half compared with photographs.
- Wind, glare, dorsal-only views, and flight restrictions limited effectiveness; data did not support population estimates.

**Comparative ranking (Table 5)**
- Scale: <2.5 Bad; −2.5 to −1.6 Far worse; −1.5 to −0.6 Poorer; −0.5 to +0.5 Similar to trammel netting; +0.6 to +1.5 Better; +1.6 to +2.5 Far better; >2.5 Excellent.
- Boat surveys scored +2.2 (far better), with about one-third the funding of trammel netting.
- Electrofishing scored +1.8 (far better) and ranked highest for data type and accuracy; it cost under 30 percent of trammel netting and was about 204 times more cost-effective per fish.
- Aerial photography scored −0.5 (similar), at about 25 percent lower cost than trammel netting.

**Recommendations**
- Alternate electrofishing and boat surveys between years depending on information needs.
- Use random transect designs with defined, detectable zone boundaries (including night-visible markers) to avoid bias.
- Install 120-volt outlets on shocking boats to avoid extra generators.
- Consider shore-based vantage points for boat-avoidance observations.
- Further test hoop nets with wider entrances and better anchorage, since the pheromone-attraction concept remains unproven.
