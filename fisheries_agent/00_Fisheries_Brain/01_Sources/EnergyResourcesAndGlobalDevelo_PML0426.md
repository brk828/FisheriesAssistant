## Executive Summary

This review (a Science feature, vol. 302, 28 November 2003) examines the global energy system, its distribution across countries, its links to economic development, and its environmental consequences. Fossil fuels supply about 95% of primary energy use, and reserves of oil, coal, and gas remain large relative to consumption. Energy use per capita rises strongly with national income, yet reserves are concentrated in a few countries and do not guarantee development. The authors argue that fossil fuel combustion drives climate change and other environmental harm, and that a diversified portfolio of renewables, supported by R&D subsidies and carbon pricing, is needed to shift the system.

## Background & Description

The document addresses the availability, consumption, and environmental costs of [[Energy Resources]] worldwide. Its data draw on the [[World Bank]] income classification, [[Energy Information Administration]] statistics, and [[International Energy Agency]] end-use data.

- **Scope:** 211 countries, year 2000 data, classified as low-income (GNI/pop below US$745; 69 countries), middle-income (US$746 to US$9,265; 85 countries), or high-income (above US$9,265; 57 countries). About 75% of countries are developing.
- **Energy use:** Global consumption exceeds 370 exajoules (EJ) per year, or about 170 million barrels of oil per day.
- **Sources:** Roughly 95% fossil-derived (oil 35%, natural gas 24%, coal 25%), 2.5% hydroelectric, 2.4% nuclear, and 0.2% non-hydro renewables.
- **Reserves:** Economically recoverable reserves include nearly 1 trillion metric tons of coal, over 1 trillion barrels of oil, and over 150 trillion cubic meters of gas. Reserves have generally risen over time as technology expands recoverable amounts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document's environmental analysis concerns atmospheric and terrestrial impacts rather than aquatic ecosystems. Key points include:

- **Climate:** [[Carbon Dioxide Emissions]] from fossil fuels rose from 5 to 6.6 billion metric tons of carbon equivalent between 1980 and 2001. The [[Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change]] projects more extreme climate events, with consequences for ecosystems, agriculture, and human welfare.
- **Other pollutants:** Carbon monoxide, methane, volatile organic compounds, and nitrous oxides contribute to ozone formation. Sulfur dioxide and NOx cause acid rain, smog, and nitrogen loading.
- **Extraction impacts:** Seismic testing and road building fragment habitats. Mining and drilling release toxic byproducts such as arsenic, cadmium, and mercury into local environments, and oil spills damage local ecosystems.
- **Hydroelectric and nuclear costs:** Dams alter [[Riparian Ecosystems]] and flood settlements and terrestrial habitat. Nuclear waste disposal and weapons proliferation remain concerns.
- **Biomass:** Indoor smoke from traditional biomass causes health problems, and biomass fuel production burdens agricultural land and productivity.
- **Wind and geothermal:** Turbines can kill migratory birds and face aesthetic objections. Geothermal plants emit CO2 and hydrogen sulfide.

## Key Findings

1. **Reserves and access:** Fossil fuel reserves are concentrated in a few countries. Half of low-income countries and over a third of middle-income countries have no fossil fuel reserves. Possessing reserves alone does not guarantee development. Nigeria has substantial reserves yet remains among the poorest countries, while Japan has developed with limited reserves.
2. **Consumption and wealth:** Per-capita energy use rises with income. Residents of the poorest 10% of countries consume less than one barrel of oil per year per capita, while those in the richest 10% use over 60 times as much. The average person in developed countries consumes about 220 GJ per year, compared with about 34 GJ in developing countries (roughly 6 times less).
3. **Efficiency:** Low-income countries use more energy per dollar of GDP than high-income countries. Developing economies are adopting efficient technologies, such as high-voltage direct-current cables in China, India, Brazil, and the Philippines.
4. **Sectoral use:** Developing countries use most energy in the residential sector, while developed countries use most in transportation. Transportation in the poorest decile of countries uses less than 30% of the energy consumed by that sector in the richest countries.
5. **Fossil dependence:** Consumption of oil, coal, and gas grew by 22%, 27%, and 77% (as printed, "7 lo/o" in the OCR, likely 77%) respectively between 1980 and 2001. Institutional inertia and the cost of replacing capital-intensive infrastructure slow the shift to alternatives.
6. **Renewables:** Renewables remain the least consumed energy form across income groups. Competition from gas turbine combined cycle (GTCC) plants will continue to undercut renewables even as costs fall.
7. **Policy recommendations:** Governments should fund R&D subsidies to lower renewable prices and raise fossil fuel prices through carbon taxes or permits. Other tools include emission limits, diversified portfolio standards, and emissions trading.
8. **Outlook:** Fossil fuels are expected to dominate for the next 25 to 50 years because of low prices and entrenched infrastructure. Faster change would require rapid depletion, government intervention, or a shift in political will that weighs fossil fuel harms more heavily.
