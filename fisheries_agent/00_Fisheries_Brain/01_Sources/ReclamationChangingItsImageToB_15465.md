# Interagency Native Fish Rearing Pond Proposal for Lakes Mohave and Havasu

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This undated-day letter (dated 8/7/89) is an informal internal correspondence from a [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] staff member to two colleagues, Paul and Minck. It proposes a low-cost, cooperative interagency project to build native fish rearing ponds on [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]] in Arizona. The ponds would rear [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]] and possibly [[Colorado Squawfish]] for stocking into the lakes. The author argues that the effort would move beyond data collection to create a working facility and put fish into the water. The letter is a planning document, not a research report, and it contains no experimental results.

## Background & Description

- **Author's agency context:** Reclamation is seeking to be seen as a resource manager rather than a "construction agency." Budgeting is shifting toward visible environmental projects, which the author sees as an opening for cooperation.
- **Parallel planning effort:** For about 18 months the author had worked on the [[National Park Service]] initiative for a fisheries management plan for Lakes Mead and Mohave. The author had hoped to advance a "rearing pond" concept through that committee but expected little progress for a couple of years, if at all.
- **Target species:** The letter names razorback sucker, bonytail and possibly squawfish as the native fish to be reared and released.
- **Precedent:** The author cites two earlier [[Razorback Roundup]] events on Lake Mohave, which used volunteer labor, as a model for future work.
- **Academic partner:** The proposal assumes technical input from Arizona State University, although Reclamation cannot fund the university's work on the proposal in full.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The letter identifies specific bays and backwaters on Lakes Mohave and Havasu as candidate sites for rearing, but it does not name or describe them in detail.
- The author describes the species as threatened ("If these species could be saved by paper, they would be in great shape"), which implies population declines. No population counts, survival rates or habitat measurements are given.
- The stated goal is to "maintain existing or reestablish populations" through stocking of large native fish.
- The author states that the proposal "certainly would not replace the need for additional research," meaning that information gaps remain.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed facility:** Build native fish rearing ponds (a "working laboratory") at several candidate locations. The author expects a range of outcomes, possibly ending with only a small pilot facility depending on support.
- **Cooperation model:** Form an interagency work group to manage, fund and supply volunteer labor. The author would invite other agencies, including a unit of the Fish and Wildlife Service, to provide money or services.
- **Division of responsibilities (proposed):**
  - The academic partner would supply the technical facility description, management recommendations, proposed research and labor commitments.
  - Reclamation would develop engineering designs for barriers, cost estimates and maintenance commitments.
  - Reclamation would offer an initial contribution of about $10,000 to start cost-sharing, plus possibly a few thousand dollars per year for miscellaneous costs. Both are contingent on matching support from other agencies.
- **Outreach:** Produce an audiovisual "packaged pitch" showing the site, the fish, the problem, barrier designs and artist's conceptions. Distribute it to key decision-makers and present it at professional meetings, modeled on a presentation previously prepared for the Desert Fishes Council.
- **Timeline and risks:** The rearing pond concept was not expected to gain traction through the existing NPS committee for at least a couple of years. The author sees the proposal as a logical near-term step while research continues.
- **Status:** This is an early-stage idea, not an approved plan. No budgets have been finalized, no sites selected and no agencies formally committed. The author asks colleagues for feedback and their interest.
