# A Key to the British Freshwater Cyclopid and Calanoid Copepods

#ichthyology #marine-biology #copepoda

## Executive Summary

This Freshwater Biological Association Scientific Publication (No. 18, 1960), by J. P. Harding and W. A. Smith of the [[British Museum (Natural History)]], provides a non-specialist identification key to free-living fresh- and brackish-water copepods of Britain. The key covers calanoid copepods of both sexes and free-living adult female cyclopoids. Because many species are taxonomically close and variable, the authors used a tabular "formula" key rather than a dichotomous one. The document also gives ecological and distributional notes for each species, including records from the [[Lake District]] lakes and tarns, based on [[Gurney's Monograph]] (1933) and additional museum and Association collections.

The document is not a fish study. Copepods are small crustaceans that form a major part of freshwater zooplankton and are food for fish, so the key is relevant to aquatic ecology but not to ichthyology directly.

## Background & Description

**Scope and purpose.** The key lets non-specialists identify British species of free-living fresh- and brackish-water copepods. It follows the classification and nomenclature of Gurney (1933), tested against museum material, and reuses Gurney's figures with permission from the [[Ray Society]].

**Orders covered.** The dichotomous key to major groupings separates:
- Harpacticoida (not included in this key), with a body length of 1 mm or less.
- Calanoida: antennules with 17–25 segments; antennae biramous; single egg sac.
- Cyclopoida: antennules with 6–17 segments; antennae uniramous; paired egg sacs.

**Methods of identification.** The authors recommend:
- Mounting specimens in polyvinyl lactophenol, with lignin pink or chlorazol black as stain.
- Dissecting under a binocular microscope, using lactic acid and tungsten needles (made by electrolytic-style erosion in fused sodium nitrite).
- Starting with cyclopoids and adult females carrying two egg sacs, since female calanoids carry only one.
- Building a "formula" of characters and comparing it with Tables 1 and 2. Bold characters are the most reliable and are checked first.

**Key Tables.** Table 1 covers the genus [[Diaptomus]] (six species, with females and males scored on characters A–G). Table 2 covers the Cyclopoida (about 50 species across genera [[Cyclopina]], [[Halicyclops]] and [[Cyclops]]) on characters A–Y.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Calanoids.**
- *[[Centropages hamatus]]* is marine and penetrates brackish estuaries. It is found all round the British Isles, relatively rare in the south.
- *[[Limnocalanus macrurus]]* is a cold-water species of deep lakes. Gurney found it to be the most abundant plankton species in the deep eastern part of Ennerdale Water, but it could not be found in 1956 and may now be extinct in Britain.
- *[[Diaptomus]]* species occupy a range of habitats. *D. castor* is characteristic of ponds that dry up in summer and is most commonly found with *Daphnia pulex*. *D. gracilis* is characteristic of large and small lakes, showing a preference for open water. *D. wierzejskii* is confined to oligotrophic northern lakes.
- *[[Eurytemora]]* species are brackish-tolerant. *E. velox* tolerates a wide range of salinity. *E. affinis* is widely distributed in brackish waters. *E. americana* occurs in brackish pools in Sussex and the Isle of Wight.
- *[[Acartia]]* species are brackish or estuarine. The *A. clausi* small form is recorded from North Uist and Norfolk, and the large form is common around British coasts.

**Cyclopoids.**
- *[[Cyclopina norvegica]]* is a marine littoral species common in east coast estuaries.
- *[[Halicyclops]]* species occur in brackish coastal pools with little vegetation.
- Many *[[Cyclops]]* species are tied to particular habitats: *C. prasinus* prefers small ponds, *C. strenuus* typical form occurs in ponds that dry up, *C. vicinus* is the commonest planktonic *Cyclops*, *C. viridis* is common in weedy lake margins, and *C. venustus* is associated with Sphagnum. *C. unisetiger* and *C. demetiensis* are subterranean.

**Lake District records.** Records use the symbols of a [[Lake District Waterbody Code]] list covering about 40 tarns and lakes, including [[Windermere]], [[Esthwaite Water]], [[Grasmere]] and [[Wastwater]]. The most widespread species in these records are *Cyclops agilis* and *Cyclops albidus*, each recorded at many sites. *Cyclops strenuus abyssorum* is the common form of *C. strenuus* in Scotland and the Lake District.

**Seasonality and life cycle.** Several species have seasonal patterns. *Centropages hamatus* is most abundant June–September. *Cyclops leuckarti* overwinters in Windermere as a dormant fifth copepodid (Fryer & Smyly 1954). *C. strenuus* reproduces mainly in winter and early spring. *C. gigas* adults occur only in winter.

## Key Findings

- **Identification approach.** A tabular formula key was used because a dichotomous key proved impractical for cyclopids and diaptomids, which are closely related and variable.
- **Coverage.** The key covers about 60 species and subspecies in total, with Table 2 alone listing about 50 cyclopoid entries.
- **Diagnostic examples.** Several species are separated by single characters. *Centropages hamatus* has a ventral backwardly curved hook on the female genital segment. *Eurytemora velox* has a furcal ramus 6 or more times longer than broad, while *E. affinis* has long pointed wings on thoracic somite V. *Cyclopina norvegica* has a large, two-branched mandibular palp.
- **Range of Britain's copepod fauna.** Many species are widely distributed, but several are rare or of uncertain status. *Cyclops scutifer* has not been found in Britain. *Limnocalanus macrurus* may now be extinct in Britain. *Cyclops sensitivus* is known from one subterranean locality (Ringwood, Hants).
- **Taxonomic revisions noted.** Gurney's name *Halicyclops aequoreus* is not applied to either British species, because Fischer's specimens came from Madeira. *Eurytemora lacustris* is omitted: Brady's 1910 record from Lea Mills Pond could not be confirmed, and the pond no longer contained *Eurytemora*.
- **Related Association publications.** The series includes keys to Cladocera, Megaloptera, Simuliidae, Gastropoda, leeches, Ephemeroptera, water bugs and stoneflies. Its "Scientific Publications" list also includes [[Hartley]]'s coarse fish reports (*The Food of Coarse Fish*, 1940; *The Coarse Fishes of Britain*, 1947), which are the only fish-related items.
