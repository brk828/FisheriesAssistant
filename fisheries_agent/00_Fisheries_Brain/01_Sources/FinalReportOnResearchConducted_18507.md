# Research on Little Colorado River Population of Humpback Chub, May 1989

#ichthyology #endangered-species #population-monitoring #mark-recapture #Cyprinidae #Colorado-River

## Executive Summary

This report presents the third year of monitoring the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) population in the [[Little Colorado River]], Arizona, during May 1989. Research teams established two camps and deployed up to 26 nets over 30 days, sampling from the confluence with the [[Colorado River]] upstream to 15 km. The study documented reproduction, conducted mark-recapture population estimates, and assessed movement patterns. A total of 2,944 fish representing eight species were collected, with [[Humpback Chub]] comprising 62.2% (N=1,849). Native fish dominated collections at 97.4%. Population estimate for the entire study reach was 18,530 humpback chub. The study documented spawning occurring earlier than previous years (late April) and captured substantial numbers of young-of-the-year and yearling fish (100-150 mm), contrasting with 1987-1988 when older age classes dominated.

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] is an endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin. This monitoring project, conducted by Northern Arizona University in cooperation with [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], represents ongoing efforts to document the biology and population dynamics of the [[Little Colorado River]] population. Previous research years (1987-1988) established baseline data on spawning requirements, population structure, and movement patterns.

The study area extended from the confluence of the [[Little Colorado River]] with the mainstem [[Colorado River]] upstream 15 km to Atomizer Falls. Two base camps were established: one at the confluence and one 10 km upstream at the mouth of [[Salt Trail Canyon]]. Physical habitat varied from swift boulder-substrate rapids to deeper pools with sand substrate. Water depth ranged from 0.5 m in shallow areas to 3.5 m in pools. The [[Little Colorado River]] is characterized by fluctuating turbidity and temperature conditions, influenced by both local hydrology and mainstem [[Colorado River]] flows regulated by [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

Sampling employed multiple net types: 1-meter diameter hoop nets (2.5 m length, some with 6 m wings), trammel nets (91.5-102 m × 2.5 m), and seines. Thirteen nets were deployed in upstream reaches and up to 17 at the confluence area. Nets were checked twice daily (dawn and evening) for hoop nets, at six-hour intervals for trammel nets, and at three-day intervals for distant upstream sets.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Little Colorado River]] provides critical habitat for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], serving as a major staging area and spawning ground. The river's native fish assemblage remained dominant, with native species representing 97.4% of all fish collected. Associated native species included [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*). Exotic species comprised only 2.6% of collections, primarily [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), with occasional [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) and [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*). One hybrid (razorback × flannelmouth sucker) was documented.

The confluence area experiences "tidal zone" effects from fluctuating [[Glen Canyon Dam]] releases, with water depths and current patterns varying substantially based on mainstem flows. When mainstem flows dropped below 3,400 cfs, nets were partially exposed; higher flows (>15,000 cfs) inundated the lower river. These fluctuations likely influence larval fish survival, habitat availability, and predator-prey dynamics.

Spawning occurred earlier in 1989 than documented in previous years. Fry were observed at the upstream camp on 23 April, suggesting spawning in late April or earlier. At the confluence, young-of-the-year (23 mm total length) were captured by the second week of May. Large schools (60+ individuals) of mixed-species juveniles ([[Humpback Chub]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Bluehead Sucker]]) were observed in upstream areas by mid-month. The barrier falls at 15 km, previously thought impassable, may have been sedimented in, potentially allowing access to an additional 10 km of upstream habitat.

## Key Findings

**Population Structure and Size Distribution:**
- Total collection: 2,944 fish representing eight species
- [[Humpback Chub]]: 1,849 individuals (62.2% of total catch)
- Native fish: 97.4% of collections; exotic species: 2.6%
- Mean total length: 216 mm overall (200 mm upstream nets, 217 mm confluence nets)
- Size range: 34-563 mm total length
- Length-frequency histograms showed dominance of 100-150 mm size class (presumed 1988 year class)
- Young-of-the-year (<80 mm) well represented, contrasting with 1987-1988 when older age classes dominated

**Sexual Dimorphism and Reproductive Condition:**
- Females: mean 287 mm (range 102-445 mm); upstream mean 274 mm, confluence mean 301 mm
- Males: mean 289 mm (range 114-459 mm); upstream mean 294 mm, confluence mean 284 mm
- Percentage sexed (adults) varied weekly: 18.7-41.0% upstream, 12.3-36.3% at confluence
- Larger numbers of both sexes present during first two weeks of May, then decreased
- Sexing based on gamete expression and urogenital papillae morphology (males: papillae flips forward when pressed)

**Mark-Recapture and Movement:**
- Tagged fish: 711 total (42% of humpback chub catch)
  - PIT (Passively Integrated Transponder) tags: 470 fish (66%)
  - Carlin fingerling tags: 241 fish (34%)
- Recapture rates: 11% overall; 17% at confluence (lower than 1987: 82%, 1988: 40%)
- PIT tag recapture rate: 7.1% (N=34); Carlin tag recapture rate: 14.1% (N=34)
- Maximum days at large: 25 days (PIT), 11 days (Carlin); mean 6.6 days (PIT), 3.0 days (Carlin)
- Maximum upstream movement: 2.4 km after 5 days
- Downstream movement: two fish moved from [[Salt Trail Canyon]] (10 km) to confluence in 5 and 7 days
- Long-term recaptures documented growth:
  - 1980 fish: +23 mm
  - 1985 fish: +60 mm
  - 1986 fish: +1 to +54 mm
  - 1987 fish: 0 to +24 mm

**Catch Rates and Sampling Efficiency:**
- Total net days: 451 (213 upstream, 238 confluence)
- Overall catch rate: 3.9 fish/day, 0.16 fish/hour
- Upstream nets: 3.3 fish/day, 0.14 fish/hour
- Confluence nets: 4.6 fish/day, 0.19 fish/hour
- Trammel nets more productive: 4.9 fish/day, 0.21 fish/hour
- Hoop nets: 3.6 fish/day, 0.14 fish/hour
- Individual net catch rates ranged from 0 to 10.4 fish/day
- 1989 hourly catch rate (0.16 f/hr) higher than previous years (1987: 0.21 f/hr; 1988: 0.11 f/hr)

**Population Estimates:**
- Entire study reach (0-15 km): 18,530 humpback chub
- Confluence area only: 10,120 humpback chub
- Previous confluence estimates: 5,732 (1987), 7,882 (1988)
- Estimates include all size classes

**Parasitism and Health:**
- Less than 1% of confluence humpback chub infested with anchorworm parasites
- Low mortality rates observed

**Habitat Utilization:**
- Confluence area confirmed as major staging area
- Species utilizes entire accessible reach of [[Little Colorado River]]
- Barrier falls at 15 km may no longer be impassable due to sedimentation
- [[Rainbow Trout]] observed above 15 km falls, suggesting potential for humpback chub passage
- If passable, an additional 10 km of habitat potentially available

**Interannual Comparisons:**
- 1989 captured substantially more small fish (100-150 mm) than 1987-1988
- Spawning occurred earlier (late April) versus mid-May in previous years
- Lower recapture rates may reflect larger unmarked population in upper reaches
- Factors influencing small fish presence: turbidity, water temperature, flow rates, net efficiency, predation
