# Current and Predicted Future Status of the Spikedace and Loach Minnow in the Lower Gila River System of New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 report, written by James W. Hartshorne for the [[Gila Fish and Gun Club]] and the [[Conner Dam]] Association, assesses the status of the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) in the lower Gila River of New Mexico. It was prepared to support the Conner Dam proposal while the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] was preparing a [[Critical Habitat]] designation for the spikedace. The author concludes that additional habitat loss is not the main threat to the two fishes. He argues that the main threat is displacement by the non-native [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), and that both species will disappear from New Mexico regardless of whether the dam is built. The report recommends reclassifying the species as endangered, lifting the dam prohibition, and delaying the critical habitat deadline. The document is an advocacy piece based on a limited data set and a literature review, not a peer-reviewed study.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** The spikedace and loach minnow are small cyprinid fishes that the report describes as nearly endemic to the Gila River system in Arizona and New Mexico.
- **Regulatory status:** A [[Federal Register]] notice (51 FR 23769, July 1, 1986) listed the spikedace as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The report states that the loach minnow was assumed to share the same status and reasoning.
- **Current known range (per the 1986 USFWS letter):** Aravaipa Creek (Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona); the Gila River upstream of Red Rock (Grant and Catron Counties, New Mexico); a small section of Eagle Creek (Greenlee County, Arizona); and a portion of the [[Upper Verde River]] (Yavapai County, Arizona).
- **Motivation:** The author began the work after discussions with local water advocates about the stalled Conner Dam project. He found that opponents had relied on historical arguments and on questioning USFWS figures, and he set out to build a documented counter-argument.
- **Constraints:** The author states the work was limited by time and resources and was prepared ahead of a June 18, 1987 deadline for critical habitat designation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drivers of decline.** The report cites [[Propst 1986]], who linked the long-term decline of the spikedace to land and water use. The USFWS listing lists threats including habitat destruction from damming, channel alteration, riparian destruction, channel downcutting, water diversion, groundwater pumping, and introduced exotic predatory and competitive fishes.

**Red shiner invasion.** The central argument rests on the spread of the red shiner. [[Minckley 1973]] reported that where the red shiner is established, native minnows are essentially absent. The report states the red shiner:
- was introduced west of the Continental Divide in Arizona and by 1970 had spread to most lower-elevation waters, up to roughly 5,000 feet;
- had reached the Gila River in New Mexico by the late 1970s or early 1980s, in the [[Redrock and Virden Valleys]];
- had reached the [[Cliff-Gila Valley]] by the 1983–84 survey.

**Drought and flood effects.** The author dismisses drought as a cause because the native species can recolonize. He cites Minckley's (1973) observation that red shiners increase during low water and that predation declines at higher flows. The author also notes that the red shiner survived the extreme floods of the late 1970s.

**Bait minnow harvest.** The report states that trapping and seining of bait minnows was permitted in the Gila River system until about three years before 1987. The author suggests this may have been a major impact on native populations. A Silver City newspaper editor's note records that wildlife biologist Bruce Anderson of the Gila National Forest described bait minnows as a vector for exotic species that compete with natives.

**Habitat requirements.** Anderson, quoted in the same newspaper, said the species require riffles in moving streams and possibly periodic flooding. He doubted that an impounded pond would provide suitable habitat. The report uses this to argue that a dam would likely destroy habitat rather than create it.

## Key Findings

**Population data cited in the report:**

| Location and period | Reported figure |
|---|---|
| Spikedace, Redrock and Virden Valleys, pre-1970 | 13.65% relative abundance |
| Spikedace, Redrock and Virden Valleys, 1970–1979 | 24.8% relative abundance |
| Spikedace, Redrock and Virden Valleys, spring 1985 survey | Zero (none found) |
| Red shiner, Redrock and Virden Valleys, 1983–84 | About 47% relative abundance |
| Red shiner, Cliff-Gila Valley, 1961–1980 (Propst data) | None recorded |
| Red shiner, Cliff-Gila Valley, 1983–84 | About 2.2% abundance |
| Spikedace, Cliff-Gila Valley | 8.2% reduction in relative abundance from the prior period |
| Loach minnow, Cliff-Gila Valley | 1.5% increase (attributed by the author to statistical noise or predation by catfish) |

The author states the spikedace decline from 24.8% to zero occurred in about five years or less.

**Principal conclusions:**
1. Additional habitat loss is not the major threat to the spikedace and loach minnow in the lower Gila River in New Mexico.
2. Both species are destined for extinction whether or not the Conner Dam is built, because red shiner displacement is already under way.

**Recommendations:**
1. Declare the question of further habitat loss in the lower Gila River Valley moot.
2. Redefine the basis for designation from "threatened" to "endangered," citing imminent displacement by the red shiner in the species' "last stronghold."
3. Eliminate the prohibition on dam construction in the lower Gila River system.
4. Study how spikedace and loach minnow coexist with red shiners in Aravaipa Creek and the upper Verde River, for possible use in New Mexico.
5. Commit more effort and resources to protecting the two species in the Redrock area.
6. Extend the critical habitat designation deadline beyond June 1987.

**Requests to agencies.** The club asked USFWS and the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish to accept the report as a principal basis for the critical habitat decision. It also asked them to delay the final rule until the 1987 survey data from [[Propst 1986]]'s team in the Cliff-Gila Valley could be reported.

**Critiques of agency practice.** The report also questions:
- the basis for listing the [[Gila Snail]] and the spikedace and loach minnow, and whether earlier surveys existed;
- whether the species had been transplanted or had moved into the Gila River;
- why bait minnow seining was permitted for years;
- whether the listings were used to block the East Fork access road, a dispute the author describes as possible "environmental harassment."

**Limitations.** The report's population figures come from a small number of surveys by a single agency team. Several rely on relative abundance rather than absolute counts. The author notes that data on prior surveys were not available to him. The report does not present new field data of its own. Its treatment of the Conner Dam's ecological effects is qualitative. Readers should weigh these points alongside the advocacy context.
