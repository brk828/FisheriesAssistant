# Genetic Monitoring of Wild and Repatriated Razorback Sucker Populations in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report (31 March 2004), prepared by [[Thomas E. Dowling]], [[Paul C. Marsh]], Anne T. Kelsen and C. Alana Tibbets of Arizona State University for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], evaluates whether the [[Native Fishes Work Group]] (NFWG) larval-rearing and repatriation program for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]] transmits the population's genetic diversity from wild adults to larvae and into the repatriated population. Using mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variation in a fragment of the cytochrome b gene, the authors screened 2,958 individuals across three life-history stages. They found that the program has been effective at transmitting variation, although the authors caution that the reproductive success of repatriates has not yet been tested.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a critically endangered fish endemic to the modified main stem of the [[Colorado River]] system. The Lake Mohave population, the largest remaining, has had no documented natural recruitment since the 1950s. Estimated numbers declined from more than 60,000 to fewer than 2,000 in the past decade (Marsh et al. 2003). Despite this decline, genetic variation remains as high as in any vertebrate examined, suggesting the survivors derive from a very large historical population.

A repatriation program began in 1991 under the NFWG to prevent extirpation of the Lake Mohave stock. Wild larvae are captured, reared in protective custody until large enough to escape predation, and stocked back into the lake. Over the 10 years through 2003, nearly 500,000 larvae were harvested and more than 80,000 young fish were stocked. Repatriates, including sexually mature individuals, are regularly encountered during monitoring.

The report notes that the program's high reproductive capacity is both an advantage and a risk: a small number of parents could produce many repatriates, potentially causing loss of genetic variation and [[Inbreeding Depression]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Genetic theory framing:** The report links genetic variation to population size, inbreeding depression, and genetic drift, all of which threaten small populations and reduce adaptive potential in changing environments.
- **Population status:** Recruitment failure in Lake Mohave is attributed to heavy predation on larval razorback suckers.
- **Other populations:** Small populations occur in Lake Mead and the Green River, with scattered individuals elsewhere.
- **Sampling locations:** Larvae were collected in four areas of Lake Mohave: [[Nine Mile Area]], [[Tequila Cove]], [[Wrong Cove]], and [[Yuma Cove]]. Larvae were captured at night with dip nets after attraction to submerged lights during the January to April spawning period.
- **Repatriate sampling:** Adults and repatriates were captured with overnight trammel nets. Repatriates were identified by unique PIT tags.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Target of 25 larvae per area, collected four times a year, for about 400 larvae per year.
- A 364 bp fragment of cytochrome b was screened by [[Single-Strand Conformational Polymorphism]] (SSCP) and representative haplotypes were sequenced.
- Haplotypes were assigned with PAUP*, and diversity statistics were calculated with ARLEQUIN. Significance was assessed by bootstrap resampling (10,000 replicates) with [[Bonferroni Correction]] (P < 0.0005).
- Population differentiation was tested with [[Analysis of Molecular Variance]] (AMOVA).

**Wild adults**
- 272 adults yielded 19 haplotypes across late 1980s, early 1990s, and late 1990s collections.
- Temporal differences were not significant (F_ST = -0.003, P = 0.55), so years were pooled as a baseline.
- Gene diversity was moderately high in the pooled sample.

**Larvae**
- 2,430 larvae from 99 temporal collections (1997-2003) yielded 27 haplotypes.
- Gene diversity ranged from 0.00 to 0.82 (mean 0.55).
- Of 93 collections, 25 of 186 comparisons showed significantly reduced diversity or haplotype number at P < 0.05. After Bonferroni correction, only eight remained.
- Significant deviations were not associated with particular years or areas.
- Within-year AMOVA showed significant differences among temporal collections within areas, which the authors attribute to a subset of females spawning briefly at particular times and places. Pooling across the spawning season diluted these effects.

**Repatriates**
- 256 repatriates from 11 cohorts (1992-2002) yielded 14 haplotypes.
- Small cohorts were pooled (1992-1994 and 2000-2002).
- Diversity values fell within the range expected from random resampling of the adult population, so repatriate diversity does not differ from wild adults.
- Among-cohort divergence was low and not significant (F_ST = 0.008, P = 0.134).

**Transmission from adults to larvae to repatriates**
- AMOVA among adults, larvae, and repatriates showed a significant result (F_ST = 0.006, P < 0.001), but the authors deem this biologically negligible.
- The among-stage component was not significant (F_CT = -0.001, P = 0.763), indicating the program is effective at transmitting variation.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The program has maintained the genetic diversity of the Lake Mohave stock to date, and the risk of loss is judged low in the foreseeable future.
- Reproductive capacity of repatriates has not been assessed. Periodic monitoring of larval genetic variation is recommended as an early warning. If diversity declines, managers may need to intervene so that certain individuals are not over-represented among repatriates.
- Additional repatriate samples are needed to improve statistical power.
