# Procedures for Nongame Wildlife and Endangered Species Re-establishment Projects in Arizona

#wildlife-management #nongame-wildlife #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1987 procedures manual, prepared by the [[Nongame Branch]] of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), sets out how the Department plans and approves [[Re-establishment Projects]] for nongame wildlife and species listed as threatened or endangered in Arizona. It implements an [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]] policy approved in June 1987. The manual provides a staged approval process, a weighted [[Re-establishment Scorecard]] for ranking projects, a narrative guide to scoring, a proposal abstract format, a review checklist, and a standard proposal outline. The document names no specific species, so its content is procedural and applies to any candidate taxon.

## Background & Description

The manual governs active re-establishment of species on the Commission's list of Threatened Native Wildlife in Arizona. Two baseline rules apply:

1. Reintroductions are generally limited to the known or probable **historical (post-1800) range** of each species. Experimental releases outside historic range are allowed only under [[Experimental Population Guidelines]] of the [[Endangered Species Act]] and its implementing rules, and they require Commission approval.
2. Releases use captive-bred and/or wild-caught animals of genetic makeup as similar as possible to the native forms that once occurred in the area.

The procedure runs from project concept through approval, with responsibilities split between project originators and the Nongame Branch. The manual uses several standard forms: the proposal abstract, the review checklist, and the full proposal format.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The framework rests on habitat and population criteria rather than on any single taxon. Key considerations include:

- **Historic occurrence** in Arizona and in the specific release area, documented by reports and records from the 1800s through mid-1900s.
- **Major extinction factors**, such as extreme habitat conditions, predation, disease, chemical contamination, and uncontrolled exploitation. Factors that are unknown or still present and uncontrollable score poorly.
- **Remaining habitat**, including critical components (water, foraging, cover, topography), spatial arrangement, and whether habitat is restorable.
- **Competition and interaction** with exotic or native species, and the potential for expansion of the re-established population.
- **Land ownership and land use**, since public land generally offers more management potential than private land, and land uses that have eliminated habitat or are incompatible with re-establishment reduce scores.
- **Species mobility, sensitivity to disturbance, and public accessibility**, which affect monitoring feasibility and public response.

## Key Findings

**Procedural framework**
- The process has two parallel activity lists. Project originators assess species status and resources, determine feasibility, complete the scorecard, and submit it to the Nongame Branch. The Branch then prepares the abstract, circulates it within AGFD, briefs the Director, solicits outside comment, prepares the proposal, completes an AGFD Environmental Checklist, and drafts an Environmental Assessment or Impact Statement if needed.
- The final proposal goes to the AGFD Director for action. Decisions are then communicated to the Commission and the public.

**Scorecard for ranking projects**
- The scorecard rates about 16 factors on a 1 to 4 scale (4 is most favorable). Each rating is multiplied by a weight, which the scanned form shows as ranging from 1.0 to 2.0, and the weighted scores sum to a **cumulative score** used to rank projects.
- Factor groups include historic range and occurrence, status and priority, stock availability, extinction factors, habitat, competition, expansion potential, land ownership and use, logistics (coordination, access, equipment, site preparation), monitoring (duration, objectives, methods, mobility, access), sensitivity, and public acceptance.

**Status criteria**
- Species status is scored using the state classification (Wildlife of Special Concern categories) and federal Endangered Species Act listing. A state Category 1 or 2 species, or a proposed federal listing, scores higher than a species with no official classification.

**Review checklist (15 items)**
- Reviewers confirm the abstract has been reviewed, whether the project conflicts with other threatened or endangered species programs, conservation plans, or Federal Aid projects, whether an existing Environmental Assessment can be modified, and which state and federal permits are required. They also identify which agencies, landowners, and stakeholders must be informed early. The checklist further asks whether quarantine, temporary holding facilities, or data-collection and information-and-education support are needed, and whether hunting, fishing, trapping regulations, or closures would be affected.

**Standard proposal outline**
- Proposals follow a fixed structure: (I) Introduction covering need, objectives (including a self-sustaining population target), approach, location, expected results, duration, and funding; (II) Historic Status in the State; (III) Present Status; (IV) Methodology covering capture, transport, release, monitoring, data collection, schedule, and budget; (V) Coordination covering authority, agency roles, EA/EIS requirements, public involvement, and conflicts; (VI) Implementation schedule; (VII) Management Alternatives, including a no-action consequence analysis; and (VIII) Recommendations.

**Scope limits**
- The manual contains no species-specific data, sample sizes, or population figures. Its outputs are procedures, criteria, and forms rather than empirical results.
