# Dispersal of Larval Fishes in a Regulated River Tributary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Robinson]], [[Clarkson]], and [[Forrest]] (Arizona Game and Fish Department, *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, 1998) examines how larval native fishes disperse in the [[Little Colorado River]], a tributary of the regulated [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. The authors measured longitudinal distributions, nearshore movements, and drift of four native species to identify spawning sites, dispersal patterns, and how many larvae enter the mainstem. They conclude that the four species spawn along most of the terminal 14.2 km of the tributary and that larval movement is partly active rather than purely hydraulically driven. Roughly 376,000 larvae drifted into the Colorado River over a 46-day period in 1993, and the authors argue that dam-controlled flows make their survival in the mainstem poor.

## Background & Description

The four extant native species studied are:

- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), a federally listed species
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)

Native fish populations in the Grand Canyon reach are declining, and their population dynamics depend mainly on early life-stage survival (Minckley 1991). The authors state that [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations have contributed to the extirpation of four of the eight native species in this part of the Colorado River system and have negatively affected the remaining four. Unregulated tributaries, especially the Little Colorado River, are considered the primary sources of recruitment.

The authors tested two competing hypotheses. Under purely passive drift, larvae should be highly clumped along the river. Under dispersal with an active component, larvae should be more evenly spread or found in habitats other than depositional zones.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dam-altered flows:** Regulated discharge from Glen Canyon Dam has largely eliminated the spring-summer ponding of tributary mouths that once occurred when mainstem flows were high. As a result, drifting larvae pass directly into the Colorado River.
- **Cold water:** Hypolimnetic water released from [[Lake Powell]] (7–14 °C) is unfavorable for embryonic development and post-embryonic growth. The authors describe mainstem water as perennially cold.
- **Unstable nearshore habitat:** Daily discharge fluctuations create unstable nearshore habitats, which the authors describe as critical rearing areas for native fishes.
- **Spawning substrate:** Suckers and speckled dace typically spawn in swift current over gravel substrates.
- **Community context:** Native fishes made up more than 87% of the adult fish community in the study area during the study period (Arizona Game and Fish Department, unpublished data).

## Key Findings

**Spawning and distribution**
- Larval distributions and drift indicate that native fishes spawn throughout the terminal 14.2 km of the Little Colorado River, below [[Chute Falls]].
- The authors could not identify specific spawning sites or spawning-site fidelity.
- Spawning appears to occur mainly from March to June, with sporadic low-level spawning possible year-round.
- Larval distributions were not concordant among life stages or with nearshore, low-velocity habitat.

**Drift and dispersal**
- Drift of native larvae was greater near shore than at midchannel for all larval stages except speckled dace protolarvae.
- Only speckled dace larvae showed a diel pattern, drifting more at night. The authors attribute this most plausibly to disorientation.
- Drift densities of eggs and larvae were greater at 1.9 km above the mouth than at 10.5 km for most species, suggesting a possible drift distance of at least 8.6 km for speckled dace, bluehead sucker, and flannelmouth sucker.
- Humpback chub larvae may drift shorter distances than the other three species.
- Larvae were captured in traps oriented in all directions, indicating that movement was not entirely passive.

**Quantitative results**
- Over 1,454 drift samples were collected; 31% contained larval fish and 26% contained eggs.
- Of the larval fish collected, 81% were native, 9% were nonnative, and 10% were too damaged to identify. Native composition: humpback chub 20%, speckled dace 38%, bluehead sucker 18%, flannelmouth sucker 5%.
- Of 600 fish captured in larval traps, 59.8% were unidentified.
- From May 11 to June 26, 1993, 5,968 larval fish were captured in 412,272.3 m³ of water filtered at the confluence.
- The authors estimated that approximately 376,000 fish larvae drifted from the Little Colorado River into the Colorado River in this period. The abstract gives the figure as "over 370,000."
- Egg drift densities were positively correlated with river discharge (N = 60, r = 0.81). Daily mean larval drift densities were not significantly correlated with discharge.

**Flow and spawning cues**
- Egg drift declined with falling flows, suggesting spawning peaked on the descending limb of spring runoff.
- Native fishes spawned in years without spring runoff (e.g., 1990), so declining flow is not required to initiate spawning. Temperature and photoperiod are likely also important cues.
- The May–June 1992 flood coincided with the disappearance of larvae from the Little Colorado River, suggesting they were flushed out and possibly killed.

**Nonnative species**
- Nonnative larvae, including [[Common Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Channel Catfish]], were virtually absent from nearshore habitats and rare in drift. Sample sizes were too small for statistical analysis.

**Management implications**
- Larvae that enter the mainstem probably have poor survival because of cold temperatures and unstable nearshore habitat.
- The authors recommend modifying spring-summer [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations to reduce daily discharge variation. They argue this would stabilize and warm nearshore habitats and improve larval survival.

**Statistical methods**
- Distributions were compared with random expectations using [[Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test]]s.
- Spatial and temporal differences in drift were tested with repeated-measures ANOVA on log-transformed densities.
- Concordance among life stages was assessed with Kendall's coefficient of concordance.
- Significance levels were adjusted for multiple tests using the Holm procedure (1979).
