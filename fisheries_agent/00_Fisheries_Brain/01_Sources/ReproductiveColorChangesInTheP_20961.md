# Reproductive Color Changes in the Pearl Gourami, Trichogaster leeri

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anabantoidei

## Executive Summary

This *Bios* journal extract is a mixed document. Its main research content is a laboratory study of [[Pearl Gourami]] (*[[Trichogaster Leeri]]*) that documents breeding-season color and body-marking changes in males and females. The extract also includes a fragment of a non-research essay on biology careers and space-travel research, and the opening of an unrelated plant study on *[[Genipa Americana]]* (jagua) antimicrobial activity. The fish study is the only part with fish data, so it forms the core of this note. The author states that color changes are useful indicators of spawning readiness, but their functions remain largely unknown.

## Background & Description

The pearl gourami is a small tropical [[Anabantoid Fishes|anabantoid]] fish, up to 100 mm total length, native to Southeast Asia. Both sexes undergo marked morphological, color, and behavioral changes at the onset of the breeding season. The study covers only color and body-marking changes.

Earlier work the author cites examined reproductive color change in related species:
- [[Baerends and Baerends-van Roon]] (1950): cichlid fishes.
- [[Miller 1964|Miller (1964)]]: [[Blue Gourami]] (*Trichogaster trichopterus*) social behavior.
- [[Barlow 1962|Barlow (1962)]]: [[Badis Badis]] sexual behavior.
- [[Forselius 1957|Forselius (1957)]]: several anabantoid species.

The author notes that many of these color changes are poorly understood, and that the present study does little to clarify their function. Even so, the author argues that they are useful guides to the motivational state of partners and to spawning readiness, which is valuable to aquarists, ethologists, and ichthyologists working on propagation and study of the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study conditions** (laboratory, [[Oklahoma State University]], [[Stillwater Oklahoma]]):
- Temperature held at 24-27 °C in a Constant Temperature Room.
- Photoperiod of 12 hours (6 AM to 6 PM), controlled by electric timers, with overhead fluorescent and incandescent lighting.
- Diet: *Daphnia*, dried foods, and midge larvae.

**Husbandry and sampling:**
- At least six pairs were maintained throughout the study.
- Fish were of breeding or near-breeding age, measured 40-90 mm standard length, and were obtained from aquarium dealers in Stillwater and Oklahoma City.
- Pairs were matched for equal or near-equal size to limit aggression, infection, and mortality.
- Data came from daily or twice-daily 15-minute observations per aquarium over one year.

The author cautions that coloration in teleosts depends on diet, neural and hormonal factors, temperature, and genetic differences. The changes described may therefore not appear in all populations.

## Key Findings

**Breeding male changes** ([[Sexual Dimorphism]] and [[Spawning Readiness]]):
1. **Dorsal fin ocellus:** Some, but not all, breeding males develop a small orange spot in the posterior soft-rayed dorsal fin. Its function is unknown.
2. **Post-opercular ocellus:** A dark post-opercular ocellus is characteristic of breeding males. It is not a remnant of the horizontal band, as Forselius (1957) proposed, because the band persists and the ocellus is wider than it. It does not appear to be a target for female courtship butting.
3. **Eye color:** The eye becomes uniformly dark at spawning, in contrast to the gray eye with a dark horizontal band seen in non-breeding males. This is among the most reliable indicators of spawning readiness, though not present in all males.
4. **Ventral coloration:** Most breeding males develop deep yellow or orange coloration on the throat, breast, and pelvic fins, with variable intensity.
5. **Dorsal and anal fin extensions:** Filamentous extensions of the soft rays appear in breeding males and break off near the end of the spawning cycle. The author links their growth to increased gonadal hormone levels and mitosis (citing Bullough, 1961).
6. **Caudal ocellus:** The well-defined circular spot of non-breeding males merges with the caudal peduncle melanophore network and becomes diffuse.
7. **Horizontal band:** In breeding males the band joins the body melanophore reticulations, becomes diffuse and shorter (one-half to two-thirds of its original length), and excludes the lateral line except on the caudal peduncle.

**Female changes:**
- Non-breeding females resemble non-breeding males.
- Breeding females may show some ventral yellow but lack the intense gold and reddish-orange colors of males.
- The caudal ocellus is more diffuse in breeding females, often disappearing before or during spawning, then reappearing at the end of the sequence.
- The horizontal band usually vanishes during spawning and reappears afterward; body color becomes uniformly pale with faint reticulations.
- Eye color shows the clearest sex contrast: the female eye becomes uniformly light with no trace of the band, while the male eye becomes uniformly dark.

**Discussion points:**
- Picciolo (1964) concluded that the horizontal band and reddish-orange throat and abdomen were not cues for sex discrimination. The author questions this, noting those experiments used only males of uncertain reproductive condition, at most 30 trials, and approach frequency as the sole measure.
- The author's observations suggest that body markings, color, and body configuration are all important stimuli for sex recognition.
