# Subadult Humpback Chub in the Colorado River: Geomorphic Reach and Shoreline Associations with Flow Regulation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined the densities of subadult [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*, under 200 mm total length) across a 24-km reach of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], downstream of [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Using electrofishing catch rates, physical habitat measurements and [[Geomorphic Reaches]] mapping, the authors linked fish distribution to shoreline type, cover, and flow regime. Subadults were strongly associated with cover-rich shorelines (talus, debris fan and vegetated margins), and dam-driven changes in discharge appear to have reduced habitat quality over time.

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] is an endemic native fish of the Colorado River basin. The study area lies in Grand Canyon, a canyon formed by the uplift of the [[Colorado Plateau]] and downcutting of the river through more than 1500 m of sedimentary and metamorphic rock. The river drops over 500 m in 450 km, with about half the drop occurring in rapids that make up only 9% of the distance. Rapids are largely formed by debris fans at the mouths of ephemeral tributaries.

Historically, the river was highly turbid and fluctuating, with temperatures from near freezing to almost 30°C and annual flows from about 60 m³/s to over 3000 m³/s. Glen Canyon Dam, closed in 1963 (the text gives the closing period as 1962–1991 in one passage), eliminated the annual flood pulse, maintained high base flows, trapped sediment and altered water temperature. Water released from the dam is about 7–11°C.

The [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) is the largest tributary in Grand Canyon. It is spring-fed, calcium carbonate-rich, clear at base flow (base flow 8.5 m³/s; 10-year flood 425 m³/s), and turbid only during heavy storms.

The study area was divided into three geomorphic reaches based on geology and channel form:

- **Reach 1** (above Lava Chuar, RM 63 to 65.5): Tapeats Sandstone and Dox Formation strata forming a narrow, deep corridor.
- **Reach 2** (RM 65.5 to about 73): Palisades Fault displacement exposes erodible Dox members, producing a shallower, wider corridor.
- **Reach 3** (below Unkar Rapid to Hance Rapid, RM 76.5): Shinumo Quartzite with a narrow corridor.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Shorelines were classified into six types: [[Bedrock Shoreline]], [[Cobble Shoreline]], [[Debris Fan Shoreline]], [[Sand Shoreline]], [[Talus Shoreline]] and [[Vegetated Shoreline]] (tamarisk-stabilized sand). Vegetation is a non-native, post-dam feature. Tamarisk (*Tamarix chinensis*) established only after flows stabilized sand deposits, and before dam operations annual floods scoured it away.

Flow regulation changed the discharge regime. Under the 1991 interim flow rules, flows range from a minimum of 225 m³/s to a maximum of 550 m³/s, with ramp-up limited to 70 m³/s/h and ramp-down to 45 m³/s/h. Before dam closure, flows were below 140 m³/s about 20% of the time; afterwards they were below that level only about 3% of the time (flows below 225 m³/s: 50% pre-dam versus 12% post-dam).

Nonnative salmonids, catfish and other piscivores, which did not historically occur, add predation pressure, making cover increasingly important for small fish. Cover also shields fish from high velocities and light, to which native species, which evolved in turbid water, are negatively phototactic.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**
- Electrofishing catch per unit effort (fish per 10 h) estimated relative densities of subadults in all three reaches and six shoreline types.
- Habitat was measured at 10-m intervals along 69 longitudinal transects (40 in Reach 1, 29 in Reach 2) at 0.5, 1.5 and 2.5 m from shore, recording depth, velocity and cover (lateral, instream and overhead).
- Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), discriminant functions analysis, ANOVA and simple linear regression were used. Flow duration curves compared pre-dam (1922 and 1960) and post-dam (1965 onward) Lee's Ferry discharge.

**Physical differences among reaches and shorelines**
- Reaches 1 and 3 were geomorphically similar. Reach 2 had a width-to-depth ratio nearly twice that of Reach 1 and a riffle area three to five times greater than Reach 3 (6%).
- A significant reach × shoreline interaction (Wilks' λ = 0.554, p = 0.003) meant shoreline effects differed between reaches. In Reach 1, mean depth of bedrock shorelines (2.39 m) was far greater than other types (0.30–1.0 m).

**Subadult distribution**
- Estimated catch rates were highest in Reach 3 (82 fish per 10 h), intermediate in Reach 1 (50) and lowest in Reach 2 (33).
- Overall densities were highest on vegetated shorelines, followed by talus and debris fan. Bedrock, cobble and sand shorelines had low densities.
- Fish-occupied locations had significantly more cover (p < 0.001) and lower velocity (p = 0.10) but did not differ in depth (p = 0.84).
- Reach and shoreline geomorphology together explained only about 12% of the variation in fish densities, while cover was the main correlate of presence.

**Discharge effects on habitat**
- Mean depth and velocity increased with discharge, and mean cover decreased. The trend was not consistent across shoreline types (depth and velocity decreased on sand and talus).
- The authors concluded that habitat quality declined in the post-dam era because high base flows reduce cover, particularly on talus and debris fan shorelines.

**Interpretation and recommendations**
- Subadults may use naturalized vegetated shorelines because natural shoreline types have been modified by flow regulation, previously important types are no longer present, or vegetation provides better conditions.
- Elevated base flows, altered thermal regimes (about 7–11°C in summer versus historical 9–18°C), and changed food availability were judged likely to limit growth and survival of subadults.
- The authors argue that the effect of artificially elevated base flows has not been adequately considered in evaluating [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations.

**Limitations noted by authors**
- The 2.5 m boundary for habitat measurement may be too broad to detect fine-scale depth and velocity associations with fish. Analysis restricted to the channel margin used by subadults would be preferable.
- The alpha level was set at 0.10 because of low sample sizes and probable low statistical power.
- Reach 3 was added after habitat data collection, so fish data were available only for that reach.
