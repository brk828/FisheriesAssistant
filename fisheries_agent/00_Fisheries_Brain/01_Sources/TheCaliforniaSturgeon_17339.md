# The California Sturgeon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Acipenseridae #sturgeon #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1954 California Department of Fish and Game document announces the reopening of recreational sturgeon fishing after a 35-year closure. The white sturgeon (*Acipenser transmontanus*) and green sturgeon (*Acipenser medirostris*) populations had recovered sufficiently to allow limited harvest. Commercial fishing remained prohibited, with recreational limits set at one fish per day with a minimum size of 50 inches. The document provides natural history information, historical context on the commercial fishery collapse, and guidance for prospective anglers targeting North America's largest freshwater fish.

## Background & Description

California supports two sturgeon species: the [[White Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser transmontanus*) and [[Green Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser medirostris*). The white sturgeon is the larger species, preferring fresh or brackish water and only occasionally found in the ocean. The green sturgeon is primarily marine, distinguished by its olive-green coloration.

**Physical Characteristics:**
- White sturgeon can reach 6-12 feet in length and weigh hundreds of pounds
- Four long whiskers (barbels) near the end of the broad snout
- Sides armored with bony plates
- Gray back, white belly
- Record California specimen: 7+ feet, 160+ pounds ("Wilma" at Mt. Shasta hatchery)
- Columbia River record: 1,900 pounds
- Russian record (white sturgeon): 26 feet, 3,200 pounds

**Life History:**
- Slow growing, may live up to 100 years
- Females mature at 20-30 years, males somewhat earlier
- At maturity: 3-4 feet length, 25-30 pounds
- Large females (6-7 feet, ~50 years old) may contain up to 5,000,000 eggs
- Spawning occurs in spring; April optimal for caviar harvest

**Feeding Ecology:**
- Bottom feeders locating prey by touch (sensitive barbels) and possibly smell
- Diet includes crayfish, insect larvae, snails, worms
- Also consume [[Lamprey]] eels and small fish: sardines, smelt, suckers, minnows

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution:** White sturgeon found in inland bays, sloughs, larger rivers, and some reservoirs north of [[Monterey Bay]]. Key habitats include the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin River Delta]], [[Pit River]], [[McCloud River]], [[Eel River]], and [[Klamath River]].

**Migration Patterns:** Most sturgeon migrate toward the ocean in warmer seasons, returning to fresher water in winter months. However, not all individuals undertake these migrations.

**Historical Fishery Collapse:**
- 1870s: Fish 6-12 feet long flooding market, often dumped unused
- 1880s: Commercial catch exceeded 700,000 pounds
- Rapid decline as largest fish disappeared
- 1901-1909: First closure
- 1910: Limited reopening
- 1917: Commercial catch down to ~10,000 pounds; complete closure implemented
- 1954: Recreational season reopened after 35-year closure

The 50-inch minimum size limit was established to protect fish until they reach spawning age, ensuring population sustainability.

## Key Findings

**Population Recovery:** After 35+ years of complete closure (1917-1954), reliable evidence indicated California sturgeon populations had staged a sizable comeback, with numbers sufficient to support limited recreational harvest.

**Regulatory Framework (1954):**
- Recreational fishing permitted year-round
- Bag limit: one fish per day
- Minimum size: 50 inches (to protect pre-spawning fish)
- Commercial fishing prohibited

**Angling Challenges:** The document notes that "nobody knows for sure how to catch one" as recreational targeting techniques were undeveloped. Historical commercial fishery used lamprey eels and small fish on set lines. Occasional incidental catches by [[Striped Bass]] anglers using sardines and [[Steelhead]] anglers using salmon eggs. Artificial lures ineffective.

**Recommended Tackle:** Heavy rod, strong wire line, large hooks essential. Fish described as "fast as well as heavy," with hooking one compared to "throwing a hook into the side of a speeding locomotive." Historical commercial fishery sometimes required horses and rope to land large specimens.

**Utilization:** Flesh resembles veal, light-colored and somewhat coarse. Particularly valued as smoked meat. Eggs produce high-quality caviar when taken before spawning (April optimal). Russian preparation methods include unsalted, salt-cured, or cured in vinegar and boracic acid brine.

**Notable Specimens:**
- "Wilma": 7+ foot female stranded in Squaw Creek pond, [[Shasta County]], 1950; rescued and maintained at Mt. Shasta hatchery as tourist attraction
- 14-foot specimen reportedly seen near Fender Ferry Bridge, [[Pit River]], 1946
