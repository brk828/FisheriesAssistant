# Reversing Introduced Species Effects: Experimental Removal of Introduced Trout Leads to Rapid Recovery of a Declining Frog

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether introduced trout cause the decline of the [[Mountain Yellow-Legged Frog]] (*Rana muscosa*) in protected Sierra Nevada wilderness. Using an eight-year experiment (1996 to 2003) in the [[Sixty Lake Basin]], the author removed [[Introduced Trout]], mainly [[Rainbow Trout]] and one population of [[Brook Trout]], from five lakes and compared frog recovery with [[Fishless Control Lakes]] and [[Fish Control Lakes]]. A separate enclosure experiment showed that trout actively eat frog tadpoles. Frog populations recovered quickly after trout removal, and the author concludes that trout are the most likely cause of the frog's decline and that this impact is reversible.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** *Rana muscosa* is a montane frog found mostly on protected land in California. It occurs at high elevations (up to 3,700 m), has a larval stage lasting up to four years, and depends on permanent water for reproduction. The species is considered apparently ancient and comprises four genetically distant phylogeographic units, three of which are on the brink of extinction. Southern populations are federally listed as endangered, and Sierra Nevada populations were found to warrant listing.

**Introduced fishes.** Salmonids were introduced into Sierra Nevada fishless lakes beginning in the late 1800s. Early transport used milk jugs and mules, which limited introductions. By the 1950s and 1960s, hatcheries supplied hundreds of thousands of juvenile trout and aircraft dropped fingerlings into most Sierran lakes. The paper notes that trout now occupy up to 90% of the frog's habitat in Sierra Nevada protected areas, and that trout occupy up to 95% of larger, deeper mountain lakes in the western U.S.

**Historical data.** Museum records document the once-abundant frog's decline, which addresses criticism that amphibian decline studies lack historical baselines.

**Study design.** The author compared:
- 5 trout-removal lakes
- 8 fish control lakes (introduced trout, no removal)
- 8 fishless control lakes
- A caging experiment in three fishless lakes to test tadpole vulnerability

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mechanisms of amphibian decline.** The paper reviews several hypothesized causes of decline in protected areas, including [[Emerging Amphibian Diseases]], UV radiation and climate change, air pollution and pesticides, and [[Nonnative Predators]]. The direct effects of introduced predators have received less attention, partly because their effects were assumed to be local.

**Predation and food webs.** Predatory fishes are a major force structuring amphibian assemblages, especially in permanent water bodies. Trout are highly effective predators, readily establish self-sustaining populations, and strongly affect aquatic food webs. Introduced trout are a widespread threat in the western U.S. and other regions, including Costa Rica, Spain and Australia, where amphibian declines or reduced species richness have been linked to trout.

**Protected areas.** Many perplexing montane frog declines have occurred in protected habitats that were also targets for salmonid introductions. Amphibians that evolved without fish predators are especially at risk.

**Metapopulation dynamics.** Habitat loss and fragmentation can reduce landscape-scale metapopulation capacity and increase extinction risk, often with a time lag. The author suggests that trout eliminating frog habitat fits this pattern.

## Key Findings

### 1. Trout are associated with frog absence in the pre-manipulation survey (1996)
- Of 50 lakes surveyed, 20 contained introduced trout (19 with rainbow trout and 1 with brook trout in lake 5).
- Tadpoles were found in 30 lakes (22 fishless, 8 trout lakes).
- Postmetamorphic frogs (adults plus juveniles) occurred in 39 lakes (21 fishless, 18 trout lakes).
- Densities were significantly higher in fishless lakes for both tadpoles (F = 25.78, df = 1,49, P < 0.0001) and adults plus juveniles (F = 19.55, df = 1,49, P < 0.0001).
- Large tadpoles (above Gosner stage 36) found in fish lakes occurred only in lakes directly connected to upstream fishless, frog-containing lakes. Frogs overlapped with trout only in small numbers and near source populations.
- The same pattern held in every year analyzed.

### 2. Trout removal was effective and sustained
- Thirty-five hand-deployed gill nets were used to remove trout from five lakes, with removal starting between 1997 and 1999. Lakes 4 and 5 began in 2001.
- By the end of the study, trout were extirpated from lakes 1 to 3 and greatly reduced in lakes 4 and 5.
- Repeated gill-net sets in lakes 1 to 3 caught no trout by 2003.
- Brook trout in lake 5 were the hardest to remove, and young-of-year trout continued to be caught in lakes 4 and 5 in 2003.

### 3. Frog populations recovered rapidly after trout removal
- Before manipulation, removal and fish control lakes did not differ in postmetamorphic frog density (0.042 ± 0.037 vs. 0.09 ± 0.03 per 10 m; n = 5 and 8; P = 0.39) or tadpole density (P = 0.174).
- **One year after removal:** postmetamorphic frog density was significantly higher in removal lakes than in fish control lakes (P < 0.0001). Tadpole density also differed significantly (P = 0.0183).
- **Three years after removal:** frog density in removal lakes no longer differed from fishless control lakes (6.85 ± 1.46, n = 3, vs. 4.73 ± 0.87, n = 8 per 10 m; P = 0.24). Tadpole density did not differ either (P = 0.14).
- Frog eggs appeared in lakes 1 and 2 one year after removal began. About 500 newly hatched tadpoles were found in lake 3 two years after removal began.
- Egg masses appeared every year in all eight fishless control lakes but in none of the fish control lakes.

### 4. Trout actively consume tadpoles
- In the 1998 enclosure experiment (three fishless lakes, two rainbow trout per enclosure, June 15 to 29), trout ate tadpoles in all three enclosures.
- Seven egg masses (150 to 400 eggs each) were laid inside enclosures. Trout did not attack egg masses but struck and consumed tadpoles as they hatched. No newly hatched tadpoles survived inside enclosures.
- Egg masses laid just outside the enclosures hatched successfully.
- Trout made 65 recorded strikes on large tadpoles. In 63 strikes the tadpole did not survive, and in two it escaped by hiding in the mud. Carcasses of 20 large tadpoles (>5 cm) were recovered.
- One of six trout died 12 h after release. The other five survived the 14-day trial.

### 5. The slowest-responding lake was distinct
Lake 5 responded most slowly for three reasons: it contained brook trout (harder to eradicate), it was not stream-connected to a frog source population, and it showed no frog reproduction by the study's end. This supports the importance of connected source populations.

### 6. Conclusions and interpretation
- The author concludes that trout are the most likely mechanism for the frog's decline and that the negative effects are reversible.
- The delay of roughly 100 years between the first trout introductions and the frog's regional collapse is explained by the localized early introductions. Airplane-based stocking in the 1950s and 1960s is a likely trigger for the sudden decline.
- The paper proposes that the trout hypothesis has been undervalued in analyses of the frog's collapse.
- **Conservation implication:** preventing fish introductions into historically fishless areas is likely easier than removing established nonnative fish populations.

### 7. Limitations noted in the source
- Lakes for removal were not chosen at random. They were selected for having no upstream trout populations and a downstream barrier (a waterfall with no jump pool).
- Removal could not start in all experimental lakes in the same year because of the effort required.
- Tadpole counts were not conducted in 2002.
- The paper acknowledges that some frogs overlap with trout and that other hypotheses have been favored for the decline.
