# Toxicity of Cyanide to Brown Trout and Smallmouth Bass

#ichthyology #toxicology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #water-quality #pollution

## Executive Summary

This study establishes toxicity curves for cyanide exposure in [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*) under controlled laboratory conditions. Using a constant-flow, constant-temperature apparatus, researchers determined median lethal times across a range of cyanide concentrations at 60°F for brown trout and 70°F for smallmouth bass. The study compared toxicity in waters of different alkalinity, evaluated effects of reduced oxygen concentrations (approaching regulatory "standards" of 5.0 p.p.m. for trout and 4.0 p.p.m. for bass), and developed graphical methods to approximate threshold values. Results indicate that cyanide toxicity increases with reduced dissolved oxygen and that threshold concentrations vary with environmental conditions. The continuous-flow method proved superior to static replacement solutions for obtaining reproducible data.

## Background & Description

Industrial discharge of cyanide-bearing wastes into surface waters frequently causes fish kills. When exposure duration is insufficient to produce death, recovery is rapid and complete once fish are removed to clean water or discharge ceases. For alkali cyanides, recovery has occasionally occurred even after apparent death. This pattern suggests that curves relating exposure time to concentration for 50% mortality are more valuable for predicting discharge effects than single threshold values for long-term low-concentration exposure.

The study employed fingerling [[Brown Trout]] from the [[Rome Hatchery]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] from the South Otselic hatchery. Fish were acclimatized to experimental temperature and water for at least one week and unfed for 48 hours before testing. Two natural waters were used: [[Woodhull Creek]] water (acid, low alkalinity 6-12 p.p.m., pH below neutral) and [[Rome Hatchery]] springwater (alkalinity ~100 p.p.m., pH 7.5-8.28). Complete analyses of these waters were previously reported (Burdick et al., 1954, 1955).

The constant-flow apparatus used low pressure to force cyanide solutions from 45-liter pyrex bottles through calibrated flowmeters to experimental aquaria maintained at constant temperature (±0.5°F) in insulated, thermostatically controlled water baths. Flow rates of 10-20 liters per hour were used depending on container size and fish number. Temperature was recorded continuously using Negretti-Zambra or Taylor recording thermometers. Death was defined as cessation of respiratory movement and failure to respond to irritation, rather than the 5-second cessation of swimming movements used by British researchers, because fish at low cyanide concentrations sometimes exhibited physiological adjustment allowing recovery after prolonged turn-over.

Cyanide was determined using the Aldridge (1945) procedure or a more sensitive modification. Oxygen was measured by iodized alkaline-iodide or straight Winkler procedures. Time to death followed logarithmic distribution; logarithmic means were computed and plotted against cyanide concentration on logarithmic paper. Graphical solutions estimated theoretical threshold values and calculated curve equations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was motivated by New York's 1949 Water Pollution Control Law (Article 12 of Public Health Laws), which established minimum dissolved oxygen standards for classified waters: 4.0 p.p.m. for warm-water fisheries and 5.0 p.p.m. for trout waters. Fish might be required to withstand these oxygen levels while simultaneously exposed to toxic substances like cyanide. Understanding toxicity under reduced oxygen conditions was essential for evaluating industrial waste admission and enforcing standards based on species' physiological responses.

Previous work by Southgate, Pentelow and Bassindale (1932) on [[Rainbow Trout]] showed increased cyanide toxicity with decreased dissolved oxygen and that the reciprocal of time for 50% mortality was approximately proportional to water temperature (5-20°C). Downing (1954) confirmed these findings using continuous-flow procedures at three cyanide concentrations (0.105-0.155 p.p.m.). Herbert and Merkens (1952) developed a constant-flow device for rainbow trout at 17.5±1°C, presenting data suggesting no true threshold (toxicity down to zero concentration), though their lowest tested concentration was 0.07 p.p.m. (mean survival 4,441 minutes). Wuhrmann and Woker (1953) demonstrated increased toxicity with temperature increase (1.2-25.4°C) for several species including brown trout.

For [[Smallmouth Bass]], limited information existed. Renn (1955) conducted experiments at low oxygen (~4.0 p.p.m. at 22°C) where smallmouth bass appeared among the most sensitive fishes, with no 24-hour survival at concentrations down to 0.061 p.p.m.

Three methods for reducing oxygen were compared: nitrogen gas sweep-out (increasing nitrogen partial pressure), vacuum application (removing oxygen, CO₂, and nitrogen proportionally), and boiling followed by dilution with untreated water. Theoretical calculations indicated nitrogen sweep-out would increase nitrogen content less than the other methods. Experimental comparison at 0.31 p.p.m. cyanide showed no significant differences in brown trout mortality between methods (Table 5), confirming any method would be satisfactory for cyanide testing. Nitrogen sweep-out was chosen for ease, rapidity, and control accuracy.

## Key Findings

**Comparison of Static vs. Continuous Flow Methods:**
Initial work used static solutions with periodic replacement when cyanide concentration decreased significantly. Continuous flow at 10 liters/hour through 7.5-liter jars provided more critical data with narrower time-to-death ranges. At three cyanide concentrations (0.060-0.121 p.p.m.) in [[Woodhull Creek]] water at 60°F, continuous flow showed: no significant difference at highest concentration (shortest exposure time), significance between 5-1% levels at intermediate concentration, and significance below 1% level at lowest concentration (longest exposure). Results confirmed continuous flow gives most accurate and reproducible results (Table 1).

**Water Alkalinity and pH Effects:**
Comparison of [[Woodhull Creek]] water (alkalinity 6-12 p.p.m., acid pH) versus [[Rome Hatchery]] springwater (alkalinity ~100 p.p.m., pH 7.5-8.28) at 60°F showed no significant difference in cyanide toxicity to brown trout. Mean times to death for equivalent concentrations were superimposed on the same curve (Figure 2, Table 2), indicating further comparison was unnecessary.

**Brown Trout Toxicity at Near-Saturation Oxygen (60°F):**
Three years of data (1954, 1956, 1957) using fingerling brown trout at oxygen levels approximating saturation for 60°F revealed population differences in cyanide resistance between years. The 1956 and 1957 curves were similar but displaced from 1954. Using logarithmic midmeans (central 80% of data) to minimize effects of extreme individuals, a composite curve was constructed (Figure 4, Table 3). Graphical threshold approximation: **0.09 p.p.m. cyanide**. Curve equation: log(y-2.2) = 1.06725 - 1.38325 log(x-0.09), where y=time to death (minutes) and x=cyanide concentration (p.p.m.).

**Brown Trout at Reduced Oxygen (~5.0 p.p.m., 60°F):**
Using Rome Hatchery springwater with oxygen maintained at ~5.2 p.p.m. (slightly above the 5.0 p.p.m. regulatory standard), toxicity curves were constructed using logarithmic midmeans (Table 6, Figure 6). Graphical threshold approximation: **0.080 p.p.m. cyanide**. Curve equation: log(y-2.2) = 0.89354 - 0.77309 log(x-0.080). Comparison with near-saturation curve (Figure 2) illustrates reduced oxygen increases cyanide lethality.

**Smallmouth Bass at Near-Saturation Oxygen (70°F):**
Bass from South Otselic hatchery were tested in Rome Hatchery springwater at 70°F with oxygen near saturation (8.6-9.8 p.p.m. range, mostly 9.0 p.p.m.). Five to ten fish per concentration were tested at flow rate of 10 liters/hour to 20-liter jars (Table 4, Figure 5). Graphical threshold approximation: **0.104 p.p.m. cyanide**. Curve equation: log(y-1.9) = 1.12960 - 1.16047 log(x-0.104).

**Smallmouth Bass at Reduced Oxygen (~4.0 p.p.m., 70°F):**
Using remaining bass stock, oxygen was maintained at 4.1-4.2 p.p.m. (approaching the 4.0 p.p.m. warm-water standard). Five fish per concentration were tested except at lowest concentration (10 fish). Limited fish availability restricted statistical accuracy (Table 7, Figure 7). Graphical threshold approximation: **0.086 p.p.m. cyanide**. Curve equation: log(y-1.7) = 0.86543 - 0.92335 log(x-0.086).

**Oxygen Reduction Methods:**
Three methods (nitrogen sweep-out, vacuum, boiling/dilution) were compared at 0.31 p.p.m. cyanide with brown trout. Mean times to death: nitrogen sweep-out 29.8 min (range 23-38), vacuum 30.8 min (range 24-40), boiling/dilution 32.0 min (range 25-42). Student's t-test showed no significant differences between methods (Table 5), confirming theoretical predictions that method choice would not affect cyanide toxicity results.

**Analytical Methods:**
Potassium cyanide stock solutions were standardized by silver nitrate titration (Liebig method initially, later APHA 1955 standard method). Test solution concentrations were verified using Aldridge (1945) procedure or a more sensitive modification maintaining reagent proportions necessary for accurate cyanide estimation. Some evidence suggested thiocyanate presence after repeated solution use with multiple fish batches, though definitive establishment was lacking. The analytical procedure did not differentiate between cyanide and thiocyanate radicals.

**Statistical Treatment:**
Time to death (minutes) followed logarithmic distribution. Logarithmic means were computed and antilogarithms plotted against cyanide concentration on logarithmic paper, inscribing smooth curves. Standard deviation and standard error were expressed as combined plus/minus ranges (spread) since unequal results occur when expressed as antilogarithms. Student's t-test was used for comparisons. Graphical solutions (Burdick, 1957) estimated theoretical threshold values by assuming curvature resulted from displacement on graph axes. Midmeans (central 80% of data) were computed when non-proportional variation or potential outlier effects were suspected.

**Control Experiments:**
Controls were used frequently (generally daily) throughout all experimentation. No losses were recorded among control fish during periods exceeding experimental durations, confirming experimental mortality resulted from cyanide exposure rather than handling, confinement, or water quality factors.
