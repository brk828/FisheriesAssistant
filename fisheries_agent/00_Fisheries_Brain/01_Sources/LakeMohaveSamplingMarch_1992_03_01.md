## Executive Summary
This 1992 internal memorandum from Arizona State University's Center for Environmental Studies (P.C. Marsh and W.L. Minckley) outlines the operational plan for a large, multi-agency field sampling effort targeting two endangered Colorado River fishes — the [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — in [[Lake Mohave]] during 14–23 March 1992. The document is primarily a logistics and protocol memo rather than a data report, coordinating five base camps staffed by federal, state, tribal, and academic partners. It also establishes standardized data-collection protocols (measurement, PIT tagging, scanning) for the two focal species and a secondary sampling protocol for the non-native [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), reflecting concurrent management interest in sport fish population dynamics (age and growth via scale analysis). The effort represents a coordinated population assessment and broodstock collection campaign as part of long-term recovery monitoring for these imperiled Colorado River basin species.

## Background & Description
Although this memo is procedural rather than descriptive, it specifies morphometric and diagnostic criteria used in the field to sort and process individuals:

- **[[Bonytail]]**: Individuals are size-classed using a **35 cm total length (TL)** threshold.
  - Fish **>35 cm TL** and lacking hatchery markings (e.g., fin clips) are considered likely **wild-origin broodstock candidates** and are transported live to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].
  - Fish identifiable as hatchery-produced (via fin-clip or other marks) or below the size threshold are processed in the field: measured (presumably TL and/or standard length), weighed, examined, scanned for PIT tags, photographed, and released.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Processed individuals undergo measurement (length), weighing, and **sex determination**, alongside a general health/condition assessment — implying visual/manual criteria (e.g., papillae, body condition, external lesions) are used to sex and assess fish in hand.
- **[[Striped Bass]]**: Individuals are measured, weighed, sexed, and have **scale samples removed** for age-and-growth analysis, a standard hard-part aging technique reliant on annuli counts.

No quantitative morphometric data (length-frequency, weight distributions) are presented in this memo; it establishes only the *protocol* by which such data would be collected during the sampling event.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: [[Lake Mohave]] is a Colorado River impoundment straddling the Arizona-Nevada border, downstream of Hoover Dam and upstream of Davis Dam. The sampling design divides the reservoir into distinct habitat/geomorphic zones corresponding to base camps:
  - Arizona Bay / Little Basin / Uplake
  - Carp Cove / Upper Cottonwood Basin (East)
  - Six- and Nine-Mile Coves, Cottonwood Basin (West)
  - Three-Mile Area, Cottonwood Basin (East)
  - Arrowhead Cove / Lower Lake (with concurrent work extending into adjacent [[Lake Mead]], South Cove vicinity)
- **Species interactions and management context**: The lake supports a combination of critically endangered, endemic big-river fishes ([[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]]) alongside a non-native, actively managed sport fishery species ([[Striped Bass]]), reflecting the broader ecological conflict in Colorado River reservoirs between native fish conservation and introduced piscivorous/game species. Striped bass are known predators of larval and juvenile native fishes, making concurrent food-habits examination (per AZGFD's request) ecologically significant for understanding predation pressure on recovering native populations.
- **Population monitoring framework**: The use of **PIT (Passive Integrated Transponder) tagging** as the "central technique" for population assessment indicates a mark-recapture ecological monitoring design intended to estimate abundance, survival, growth, and movement patterns of razorback sucker (and to a lesser extent bonytail) within the reservoir over time, integrated into a pre-existing long-term ASU database.

## Key Findings
- The March 1992 effort was a **coordinated, multi-agency mark-recapture and broodstock-collection campaign** spanning five camps and involving FWS, NPS, BLM, Bureau of Reclamation, AZGFD, Nevada Department of Wildlife, Hualapai Nation, ASU, and Mohave Community College.
- **Bonytail >35 cm TL** without hatchery markings were prioritized for live transport to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] to serve as **broodstock**, highlighting the acute conservation status and captive-breeding reliance for this species circa 1992.
- **Razorback sucker** protocol emphasized comprehensive individual-level data (length, weight, sex, health/condition, PIT tag status) to support ongoing **population characteristic assessments** (e.g., survival, recruitment, growth) via recapture data.
- **PIT tagging** was formally established as the standardized, central methodology for long-term individual identification and population tracking of razorback sucker in Lake Mohave.
- Secondary data collection on **striped bass** (an introduced predator/sport species) included food-habits examination and scale-based age-and-growth analysis, coordinated separately with AZGFD Kingman for fisheries management purposes.
- ASU (Marsh and Minckley) served as the **central data repository and analytical hub**, integrating field data from all participating agencies into an existing long-term database for native fish recovery monitoring in the Lake Mohave/Colorado River system.
- The operation exemplifies interagency collaboration typical of Colorado River native fish recovery programs during the early 1990s, preceding formal listing-driven recovery plans for [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] under the Endangered Species Act.
