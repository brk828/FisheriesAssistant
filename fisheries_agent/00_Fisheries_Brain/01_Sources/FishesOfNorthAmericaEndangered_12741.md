# Fishes of North America: Endangered, Threatened, or of Special Concern (1979)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1979 special section, prepared by the [[American Fisheries Society]] (AFS) [[Endangered Species Committee]] (authors James E. Deacon, Gail Kobetich, James D. Williams, and Salvador Contreras), presents a revised status list of North American freshwater fishes. After three years of work, the list contains **251 taxa** from the United States, Canada, and northern Mexico, with 203 from the United States and Canada. Each taxon is assigned a status of Endangered (E), Threatened (T), or Special Concern (SC), along with threat codes and historical distribution. The authors conclude that habitat destruction and modification is the dominant threat and that habitat restoration and protection is the most pressing conservation need.

## Background & Description

**Development of the list.** The effort ran in three phases: compilation from the literature (1976), extensive committee review (1977), and review by state and provincial fish and game agencies (1978). Responses came from every state and province except New Hampshire. The northern Mexican portion was presented at the Segundo Congreso Nacional de Zoologia in Monterrey, Nuevo León, in December 1978. Canadian listings drew on a status review by McAllister and Gruchy (1976).

**Agency review results.** The initial list of 182 taxa was changed as follows:
- 117 taxa recommended for addition (46 as E, 36 as T, 26 as SC; 9 disputed between categories).
- 41 taxa recommended for deletion (10 E, 28 T, 3 SC).
- 14 taxa recommended for deletion by some jurisdictions and retention by others.
- 16 taxa recommended for a status change (11 to less critical, 5 to more critical).

Many addition requests reflected status only within state boundaries. The authors therefore assessed status across each taxon's full range, ignoring political boundaries.

**Status categories.** The definitions follow the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]]. The committee added a new Special Concern category for taxa that could become threatened or endangered through minor habitat disturbance or that need more information. The earlier "peripheral" category was dropped.

**Taxonomic scope.** Subspecies were included where possible, because the authors considered subspecific designations as important for non-game fishes as for game fishes. A separate section lists about 100 undescribed taxa that experts recognize as distinct but that lack formal descriptions. The authors listed them so they would receive protection before formal recognition.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threat analysis.** Each listed taxon was coded for threats (1 = habitat, 2 = overutilization, 3 = disease, 4 = other natural or manmade factors, 5 = restricted range). Results for the 251 taxa:

| Threat | Taxa affected | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Habitat modification or destruction | 246 | 98% |
| Other natural or manmade factors | 93 | 37% |
| Restricted range | 41 | 16% |
| Overexploitation | 8 | 3% |
| Disease | 5 | 2% |

**Overexploitation.** The eight taxa affected are six ciscoes (*Coregonus* spp.) and two catfishes. Their declines combined habitat alteration, commercial and sport overfishing, and competition from introduced species. The authors judged regulation of commercial and sport fishing to have been "almost totally effective" for other freshwater stocks.

**Collection permits.** The authors found no evidence that scientific or educational collecting had contributed to the decline of any North American taxon. They argued that restrictive [[Permit Systems]] for collecting were addressing "largely a phantom problem" while hindering the information flow needed for species management.

**Regional patterns.** Many listed taxa are desert or spring-dwelling fishes of the Great Basin, Mojave, and Chihuahuan regions, as well as endemic fishes of southeastern U.S. river systems such as the Tennessee, Cumberland, and Alabama drainages. Several Mexican taxa occur in the Cuatro Ciénegas basin of Coahuila.

## Key Findings

- **Status of the list:** 251 taxa in total, including 203 from the United States and Canada and the remainder from northern Mexico. The list is to be revised biennially because status is changing rapidly.
- **Habitat is the primary threat:** habitat modification affects 98% of listed taxa. The authors estimate that well over 90% could be removed from their categories through nationwide habitat restoration and protection.
- **Management lesson:** defining and developing optimal habitat is "infinitely more effective" than restrictive regulation for fish conservation.
- **Extinctions:** the [[Tecopa Pupfish]], Whiteline topminnow, and Banff longnose dace were removed through extinction.
- **Rediscovery:** a population of [[Big Spring Spinedace]] was found in Condor Canyon, Lincoln County, Nevada, after being considered extinct since 1959.
- **Range expansion:** the [[Sunapee Trout]] was found established in Idaho, far outside its native range.
- **Notable listed fishes:**
  - [[Snail Darter]] (*Percina tanasi*), E, Tennessee.
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), E, multiple Colorado River basin states.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), T, Colorado River system.
  - [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), Little Colorado River system.
  - [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*), E, Nevada.
  - [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*), T, Arizona and New Mexico.
  - Several [[Cutthroat Trout]] subspecies, mainly in the western United States.
- **Undescribed taxa:** the list includes about 100 taxa recognized by specialists but not yet formally described, such as the Alabama shovelnose sturgeon (*Scaphirhynchus* sp.), E, Alabama and Mississippi.
- **Agency role:** the authors argued that the AFS holds professional responsibility for assessing fish status, while acknowledging that some state and federal agencies disagreed.
