# La Piscicultura en China (Chinese Aquaculture Report, 1965)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 Mexican rural fish-farming bulletin contains a report by Biol. María Luisa Sevilla on a study visit to China, undertaken at the invitation of the Chinese Fisheries Association. The author argues that aquaculture can strengthen rural economies and protein supply in Mexico and that Chinese methods, particularly the polyculture of several cyprinid species and the organization of labour, could be adapted to Mexican conditions. The article describes the history and yields of [[Chinese Pond Culture]], the seven main cultured species, feeding strategies, pond management, production figures at several farms and lakes, [[Oyster Culture]] in Canton, grass carp biology, and fish diseases with their treatments. A second article listed in the index (on feeding trout in ponds) is not included in the supplied text.

## Background & Description

The author, a Mexican biologist, notes that aquaculture in Mexico had been treated as a secondary activity or a hobby for people with money. Since the second half of the nineteenth century, Mexico had introduced exotic fishes, including common carp, rainbow trout, brook trout, bluegill, [[Tilapia]] species, and bullfrog, and had extended the range of [[Largemouth Bass]]. Most of these supported sport or local fishing but did not significantly affect national freshwater catches.

The report states that China ranks third in world fisheries and that 60% of its catch comes from freshwater, much of it cultured. The Chinese tradition is said to date back thousands of years. A legendary figure, the general Fanli (575 B.C.), declined a ministerial post to devote himself to fish farming in Wushi, where a park with ancient ponds is preserved in his honour.

The seven principal cultured species are:

- Grass carp, *Ctenopharyngodon idellus* (herbivorous carp)
- Black carp, *Mylopharyngodon piceus* (called "arenque chino")
- Silver carp, *Hypophthalmichthys molitrix* (called "tenca" in the text)
- Bighead carp, *Aristichthys nobilis* (called "cabezón")
- Common carp, *Cyprinus carpio*
- Chinese goldfish, *Carassius auratus* (called "barbo chino")
- Chinese bream, *Parabramis pekinensis*
- Mud carp, *Cirrhina molitorella*

The text also mentions the Tilapia *Oreochromis mossambicus* and the bullfrog, both cultivated at Shanghai, and Israeli golden carp.

Two types of fish farms exist: communal farms, run by teams within the people's commune, and state farms, financed through bank loans. Earlier, only common carp and Chinese goldfish were pond-cultured, while species requiring running water to spawn were collected from large rivers, notably the Yangtze. Later, intensive work allowed the cultivation of all the main species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Feeding habits and polyculture.** Mixing species makes full use of the water column, from surface to bottom:

- Silver carp and bighead carp feed on plankton.
- Grass carp feeds on aquatic and terrestrial weeds.
- Black carp eats clams, snails and crustaceans.
- Common carp, bream and Chinese goldfish feed on bottom organisms and detritus.

Mixing is done with care to avoid excessive competition or predation. Black carp and grass carp should not be combined, and fish of differing growth rates should not be mixed. Fingerlings are stocked at 10–12.5 cm to reduce predation losses. A suggested stocking ratio is 3,000 grass carp with 7,000 silver and bighead carp per mu.

**Snail control.** At Wushi, where rice cultivation prevails, schistosomiasis (a urinary-tract disease caused by a worm whose intermediate host is a snail) was common among farmers. Because black carp eats the snail host, intensive culture of this species helped reduce disease incidence, though not eliminate it.

**Pond design and management.** Pond sizes range from 600 to 2,400 m², with one-third hectare regarded as ideal. Small ponds are used for fingerlings and larger ponds for market-size fish. Depth ranges from 2.5 to 3.0 m. Ponds are drained, limed and dried after harvest to prevent disease and fertilize the soil. Manure and green manures are added at 200–300 kg per mu, and fish are fed with silkworm waste and silkworms, pretreated pig-pen sludge, plants, snails and clams, and leaves of peanut, sugarcane and wild vegetables. One worker can look after up to one hectare of ponds.

**Water quality.** Grass carp prefers clear, well-oxygenated water. In a controlled experiment, fish held at 5.56 mg/l of dissolved oxygen grew faster than those at 2.73 mg/l. Feeding stops at critical oxygen levels; at 1 mg/l the fish gulp air at the surface, and at 2 mg/l with abundant feeding they die. Oxygen drops sharply at night in dense vegetation, so excessive feeding in the afternoon should be avoided. Optimal temperature is 20–30 °C, with spawning only above 26 °C.

**Oyster culture (Canton).** Kwangtung province has more than ten oyster species, of which only a few are suitable for culture, notably *Crassostrea rivularis* and *Lopha cucullata*. Work is organized into three zones: a reproduction zone near the river with low salinity (density about 1.005), a spat-collection zone, and a growth zone nearest the sea (density 1.010–1.023). Collectors (tiles, shells, cement blocks) are placed from May to September. Oysters are harvested after 2–3 years. Suspension culture raises productivity 4–5 times over bottom culture.

## Key Findings

**Production and yields**

- Pond yields before 1949 averaged 750 kg/ha/year; current yields reach up to 7.5 t/ha/year. Lake yields rose from 50 kg/ha to nearly 1 t/ha.
- Wushi commune team (68 families, 302 members) with 236 mu of ponds: yields rose from 200 kg/mu/year before 1949 to 504 kg/mu/year.
- Soochou (Wukang) state hatchery, established 1951 on 230 mu, with 795 mu of ponds by the visit. In 1959 it began artificial incubation; the year's output was 93,870,000 eggs with a 45.2% hatch rate. Pituitary injection of broodstock was used to accelerate maturation.
- Shanghai (Chimbii) state farm, established 1959 on 900 mu (600 excavated, 300 pre-existing). 100 mu produces market fish at an average of 325 kg/mu/year.
- Hangzhou West Lake: 8,000 mu, mean depth 2 m (range 0.7–3 m). Annual output 450,000 kg (55 kg/mu/year). Species composition: silver carp 60%, bighead 35%, bream, common carp and goldfish 5%. Grass carp was excluded because it destroys the lotus. Pond yields rose from 40.5 kg/mu before 1949 to 63.5 kg/mu in 1957 and 131.5 kg/mu in 1963. Harvest is seasonal, with 2.5–6 t per haul.
- Le-Lin commune (Canton): 70,000 mu, of which 38,000 are ponds; pond yield rose from 122 kg/mu (1957) to 138.5 (1963) and 155 kg/mu (1964).
- Canton oyster output in 1963 was 2,000 t, with *C. rivularis* making up 73.3% of the harvest. Yields were 1,000 kg/mu/year for *C. rivularis* and 125 kg/mu/year for *O. cucullata*.

**Grass carp biology and culture**

- Females mature at 4–5 years, males one year earlier. Fecundity is 400,000–600,000 eggs (up to 1,000,000 in a 15 kg female). Spawning occurs once a year, typically May–June in rivers flowing 0.33–0.9 m/s.
- Growth: about 600 g in 5 months, 1,500–2,000 g in the second year and 3,000 g in the third under suitable conditions. Conversion ratios: 15 kg of terrestrial plants or 40–60 kg of aquatic plants per kilogram of gain.
- Fingerlings are fed plankton first, then soy or peanut cake, *Wolffia* and *Lemna* at 10 cm, and weeds after 20 cm.
- Stocking densities of 10–15 broodstock females per mu are recommended. High mortality is attributed mainly to overfeeding and disease.

**Disease and treatments**

Symptoms of poor health include reduced appetite, lethargy, loss of balance, skin lesions, swollen gills, cysts, tumours, protruding eyes, fin tears and internal haemorrhage. Quarantine and disinfection of equipment are recommended.

- *Chondrococcus* sp. (bacterial): bath in copper sulphate at 1:2,000; sulfamerazine and sulfaguanidine in feed for bacteria of the genera *Aeromonas* and *Pseudomonas*.
- *Trichodina minuta* (ciliate): acetic acid at 1:500 or formalin bath at 1:4,000; copper sulphate at 0.7 ppm has also been effective.
- *Cryptobia branchialis* (flagellate, transmitted by leeches): causes anaemia and telescopic eyes; no fully satisfactory treatment is known.
- *Dactylogyrus lamellatus* (monogenean): treated with potassium permanganate at 1:100,000 or 1:50,000. It is larviparous and shows paedogenesis.
- *Bothriocephalus gowkongoensis* (tapeworm): transmitted by the copepod *Mesocyclops lenckarti*.
- *Lernaea* (anchor worm) and *Argulus japonicus* (fish louse): removed manually with a brush, then treated with methylene blue or copper sulphate at 1:2,000 and potassium permanganate at 1:50,000; repeated after 10 days. Drying and liming ponds for about a month kills *Argulus* eggs.
- *Sinergasilus major*: treated with copper sulphate at 0.5 ppm plus ferrous sulphate at 0.2 ppm.

The author cites Hickling (1962) for broodstock disinfection: 10 ppm potassium permanganate for 10–12 hours, then formalin at 15 ppm with acriflavine at 1 ppm for 4–12 hours.
