# A New Freshwater Species of *Dolops* (Crustacea: Branchiura) from Tasmania, with Comments on Southern Hemisphere Disjunct Distributions

#crustacea #branchiura #dolops

## Executive Summary

G. Fryer (1969, *Australian Journal of Zoology* 17: 49-64) describes [[Dolops Tasmanianus]], a new branchiuran (fish louse) parasitic on a [[Galaxias]] fish from [[Lake Surprise]], [[Tasmania]]. Prior to this, the genus [[Dolops]] was known only from freshwater in South America and Africa. The paper also corrects taxonomic errors involving a supposed Chinese member of the genus, re-defines *Dolops*, and reviews the biogeographic evidence for the disjunct Southern Hemisphere distribution of the genus and related freshwater invertebrates. Fryer concludes, tentatively and without dogmatic claims, that continental drift is the most plausible explanation for these disjunct patterns.

## Background & Description

The genus *Dolops* had been reported as parasitic on freshwater fishes, with at least nine species in South America and a single species in Africa. Five specimens of a new *Dolops* were collected by Dr. W. D. Williams in Tasmania. Fryer's description is based on these specimens and includes morphological details from the holotype and paratypes.

**Adult female (diagnostic features):**
- Length to at least 5.86 mm (OCR "5-86" in source).
- Squat, orbicular carapace, wider than long, reaching only to the third pair of legs; carapace comprises about 77% of total body length.
- Abdomen extremely short and wide, undivided into two lobes, a feature that separates it from all other members of the genus.
- Maxillule ends in a massive sclerotized hook and bears an unsclerotized, finger-like distal process, possibly homologous with a fleshy lobe in *D. reperta*.
- Swimming legs 1 and 2 bear a dorsal flagellum; leg 4 has a conspicuous natatory lobe.
- Preserved colour: pale olive-brown.

**Immature male:**
- Only immature males were available, so the description is provisional.
- Length to at least 3.16 mm; carapace broadly elliptical.
- Testes are ovoid, not trilobed or bilobed as is usual in the genus.
- Claspers absent at this stage.

**Type material:** Holotype (Reg. No. P.15792) and four paratypes (P.15793), deposited in the [[Australian Museum]], Sydney.

**Host:** A *Galaxias* sp. The host identification is uncertain. R. Frankenberg suggested the fishes "may represent juveniles of *G. affinis*," but the taxonomy of this species is poorly defined.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Locality:** [[Lake Surprise]] is a small, nearly circular lake, with its longest axis not much more than 0.5 km. It lies at about 650 m (2100 ft) in the Frankland Range, about 2-5 km south of [[Lake Pedder]], and drains southward to the sea. The region also hosts ancient survivors such as syncarid crustaceans.

**Genus distribution:** *Dolops* is exclusively freshwater, with no records from the sea or brackish water. Its range covers three southern continents: nine species in South America, one in Africa, and one in Tasmania. In South America and Africa, the genus is largely tropical and absent from the southern tips of those continents. This pattern differs from that of many cool-temperate southern taxa.

**Fish fauna constraints:** In South America, true freshwater fishes are unknown within about 200 km of Cape Horn. The dry southern part of Africa also has a depauperate fish fauna. Because *Dolops* depends on freshwater fishes, its distribution follows theirs.

**Host associations:** Host specificity is not well marked in the African and South American species, and Fryer suggests the galaxiid host in Tasmania may be a matter of necessity rather than a strict association.

**Dispersal hypotheses:** Fryer weighs three explanations for the distribution:
1. Continental drift or migration along former land bridges, implying an age of at least 60 million years.
2. Retreat from more northerly land masses, implying an age of at least 60 million years.
3. Transoceanic dispersal, possibly by galaxiid fishes, which occur in South America, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand (where *Dolops* is absent). Fryer considers dispersal by sea hard to accept without qualification.

**Geological timing:** Fryer cites evidence that the three southern areas have not been directly connected since the beginning of the Tertiary, about 60 million years ago. Australia and Antarctica may have separated in the mid-Cretaceous, perhaps 100 million years ago. A possible Antarctic route therefore could have been available for early faunal exchange.

## Key Findings

1. **New species described.** *Dolops tasmanianus* is distinguished from all other *Dolops* species by the undivided female abdomen. The maxillule process may also be diagnostic, though descriptions of other species are often inadequate for direct comparison.

2. **Chinese record rejected.** *Dolops sinensis* Yu, 1935, is not a *Dolops*. Its maxillary claws are described as barbed, whereas *Dolops* claws are always unbarbed. Fryer argues that the related "*Huargulus chinensis*" (Yu, 1938) is probably a juvenile *Argulus*, as Tokioka (1940) also concluded.

3. **Taxonomic errors identified.** Yamaguti (1963) recognized six living branchiuran genera. Fryer reduces this to four living genera (*Argulus*, *Dolops*, *Chonopeltis*, *Dipteropeltis*), with *Talaus* a synonym of *Dipteropeltis*. Yamaguti's family and subfamily divisions are judged invalid, and Fryer argues that *Chonopeltis* and *Dipteropeltis* are not distantly related.

4. **Genus re-defined.** Key diagnostic points include a squat, carapace-covered body; maxillules ending in strongly sclerotized hooks, never suckers; no pre-oral sting; a fully developed flagellum on swimming legs 1 and 2; and sperm transfer by a single spherical spermatophore. Spermatophores are now known in *D. ranarum*, *D. geayi*, and *D. discoidalis*, supporting the genus as natural. The genus is parasitic mainly on freshwater fishes.

5. **Mutelid bivalves reassigned.** Australasian freshwater bivalves formerly placed in the Mutelidae belong instead to the Hyriidae, which Fryer argues shows trans-Antarctic affinity with South American hyriids. African and South American muteloids (Mutelidae and Mycetopodidae) belong to the superfamily Muteloidea. Fossil hyriids are known from Triassic deposits in North America.

6. **Continental drift favored, tentatively.** Fryer concludes that a direct Africa-South America connection is distinctly possible and would explain the shared distribution of *Dolops*, mutelid bivalves, characid fishes, and other groups. He does not claim this dogmatically but finds opponents' explanations strained.

7. **Addendum on Triassic tetrapods.** After submission, a Triassic labyrinthodont amphibian was reported from Antarctica (Barrett, Baillie, and Colbert, 1968). Fryer considers this supportive of a possible land connection, though it does not prove that *Dolops* used such a route.
