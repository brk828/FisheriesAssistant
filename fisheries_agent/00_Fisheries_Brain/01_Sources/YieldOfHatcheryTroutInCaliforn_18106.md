# Yield of Hatchery Trout in California Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This synthesis by Brran Curtis (formerly of the [[Bureau of Fish Conservation]], [[California Division of Fish and Game]]) compiles roughly a decade (1940 to 1950) of hatchery trout yield experiments in nine California lakes. Six lakes are treated in detail and three more briefly. The paper measures **yield to the angler**, defined as the percentage of planted fish that were caught, which is not the same as true survival. Proposed planning values are **about 4 percent for fingerling plants** in lakes under 80 acres and **about 50 percent for catchable-size plants**. The author stresses that these values rest partly on subjective judgment and that readers may choose their own estimates.

## Background & Description

The study asks what happens to hatchery trout after release and how many are eventually caught. Most lakes studied had no or insufficient natural spawning, so each experiment planted a known number of trout and tracked catches through [[Creel Census]] records, catch sampling, or other methods.

Key methodological points:

- **Identification:** Trout were mainly marked by [[Fin Marking]] (removing one or more fins). The author kept the assumption that fin marking does not significantly impair trout viability in California lakes, although salmon work and two conflicting California trials raised doubt.
- **Yield versus survival:** Yield is always lower than true survival, because some planted fish remain in the water after the season's catch is counted.
- **Stocking and fishing pressure:** Yield depends on [[Stocking Intensity]] and [[Fishing Pressure]] as well as survival. Heavy stocking in a lightly fished lake gives low yield and high angling quality; light stocking in a heavily fished lake gives high yield and poor fishing. Optimum results need these two factors balanced.
- **Reliability:** Lakes differ in creel record completeness, from complete counts to estimates or "informed guesses."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lakes span a wide range of elevation, size and climate, from 4,500 to 7,900 feet and from 10 to 28,000 acres. Most have snow and ice for four to six months each winter, and the author notes that lakes and streams produce different results.

Species and community notes from the document:

- **[[Castle Lake]]** held a remnant [[Lake Trout]] population (introduced 1924), golden shiners and speckled dace.
- **[[Frog Lake]]** showed natural spawning of eastern brook trout, and browns were present from an unauthorized pre-1939 plant.
- **[[June Lake]]** had fishing that had declined before fall-spawning rainbow stock was planted.
- **[[Gull Lake]]** had a large population of rough fish (*Siphateles bicolor obesus*) removed by [[Rotenone Treatment]] in 1940.
- **[[Crystal Lake]]** had goldfish and chubs that had overrun the trout before a 1941 rotenone kill.
- **[[Lake Almanor]]** had excellent natural rainbow spawning, and largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*) had recently appeared.
- **[[Conn Valley Reservoir]]** was warm at the surface (up to 78°F), with low deep-water oxygen, green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*) and landlocked Pacific lampreys (*Entosphenus tridentatus*).
- **[[Hume Lake]]** was twice overrun by rough fish, and its fish were destroyed by chemical treatment in 1940 and 1947.
- **[[Crowley Lake]]** held a large water-supply reservoir (183,000 acre-feet) with some tributary spawning.

### Species-level notes

- **Eastern brook trout:** In Castle Lake, brook fingerlings gave their best fishing as yearlings during phase 1. After the 1946 chemical treatment, brook fingerlings in phase 2 gave 27.1% (1947 class) and 23.4% (1948 class), with a combined average of 25.8% at writing. In Frog Lake, about 800 naturally produced brook trout were removed, leaving 1,271 hatchery fish (4.1%) from 31,000 planted.
- **Rainbow trout:** Frog Lake rainbows did poorly (0.7%, 772 caught from 105,000 planted). The author suspects the fall-spawning stock used was poorly suited to fingerling planting in high mountain lakes.
- **Brown trout:** Frog Lake browns yielded 7.7% (1,545 caught from 21,000 planted). Browns peaked in the catch as two-year-olds and remained in the fishery for up to six years in Castle Lake. About 1,960 brown trout were counted after the Castle Lake chemical treatment.
- **Cutthroat trout:** At [[Crowley Lake]], all cutthroat catches in the 1947 and 1949 creel counts came from the 1946 fingerlings.

### Fishing success and pressure

- Castle Lake success was about 0.6 fish per hour in phase 1 and 1.6 in phase 2 (1,051 angler-days per year, 22 per acre).
- Frog Lake averaged about 220 angler-days per season (about seven per acre) with catches of 0.4 to 0.8 fish per hour.
- Crystal Lake had 6,822 angler-days (682 per acre), about one fish per angler-hour, and 28% of angler-days produced no trout.
- Hume Lake had about 7,000 angler-days (about 75 per acre) and catches of 0.13 to 0.75 per hour.

### Conclusions and proposed values

- The paper reports fingerling yields in large lakes (800 acres and up: Almanor, Conn Valley, Crowley) of about 1%, with a range of 0.2 to 1%.
- In small lakes (38 to 68 acres) the average fingerling yield was 8.7%, with a range of 2.7 to 25.3%. The 25.3% Castle Lake phase 2 value is seen as distorted by favorable conditions.
- Arithmetic averages over all experiments were 5.4% for fingerlings and 44.9% for catchable fish. The author regards these as misleading because the data vary in reliability.
- **Proposed planning values:** about **4%** yield from fingerling plants (lakes under 80 acres), and about **50%** from catchable-fish plants.
- The Castle Lake phase 1 results show that removing a three-species community and a resident lake trout population, leaving one species, produced an extraordinary increase in fingerling yield.
