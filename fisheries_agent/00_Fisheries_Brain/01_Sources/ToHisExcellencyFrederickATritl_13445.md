# Arizona Fish Commission Annual Report (Third Annual Report, c. 1884-1885)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This is the third annual report of the [[Arizona Fish Commission]], submitted by business manager J. H. Taggart to Governor [[Frederick A. Tritle]] of the Territory of Arizona. It covers the commission's efforts to obtain and distribute fish, chiefly [[Common Carp]], and its introduction of [[Shad]] into the [[Colorado River]]. A companion report from commissioner [[Richard Rule]] of Tombstone covering the southern Territory for 1883 and 1884 is enclosed.

The carp shipment promised by the [[U.S. Fish Commission]] largely failed: about 400 fish were allotted to Arizona, and only a little more than one hundred survived until distribution. The commission reported a shad plant of one million fry at The Needles in June, which it described as the largest single fish plant within the author's knowledge. The report concludes that the Territory should build its own breeding ponds and hatcheries, requests an additional $5000.00 in appropriations, and recommends game protection measures.

## Background & Description

- **Organization:** The Arizona Fish Commission had been established for nearly four years at the time of the Kinds of report. Its members included three commissioners, one of whom was [[Richard Rule]] of Tombstone.
- **Management:** J. H. Taggart served as business manager and wrote this report after a severe illness delayed its completion.
- **Federal partner:** Carp were supplied by the [[U.S. Fish Commission]] under [[Spencer F. Baird]]. Commissioner [[Com. Gosper]] was the contact who attempted to secure replacement fish.
- **Funding:** The commission's work was constrained by the appropriation process. The auditor would not advance funds unless money had already been spent or a contract existed, which made payment for travel and expenses difficult.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Colorado River conditions:** Before the shad plant, the commission collected information on [[Green River]] water (analysis at the U.P. crossing showed exceptional freedom from alkalines), a daily temperature record of the [[Colorado River]] at the [[Yuma Bridge]], temperature comparisons with the [[Gulf of California]], high and low water periods, and food supply for fish.
- **Suitability assessment:** The author judged the conditions favorable. The food supply for growing fish was described as ample, water temperature was not too high, and the distance from the spawning grounds to salt water was not too great.
- **Known risk:** The author noted that no unfavorable condition had been found after careful inquiry, though this could not be ruled out.
- **Depletion warning:** The report cited the [[Sacramento River]], whose salmon fisheries had recently been rich but were now almost worthless, as a case of depopulation requiring strict police regulation.
- **Wild game decline:** Quail had been driven from populated areas, and deer were becoming harder to find each year.

## Key Findings

**Carp distribution failure**
- The [[U.S. Fish Commission]] had promised a car-load of carp free for Colorado, Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. Only one car-load was sent to the Southwest, and the agent distributed at least fifty fish per applicant before reaching Arizona.
- Arizona was allotted only 400 fish, and the author had expected at least 1,000 fry for each of the three commissioners.
- The fish arrived short in number and in poor condition. Only a little more than one hundred remained alive until distribution, which took about three days.
- About forty fish went to first applicants at Tucson, and the rest went to older applicants at Phoenix.
- The commission had advertised that it could supply all applicants, so many were disappointed.

**Shad introduction into the Colorado River**
- The author had urged the [[Colorado River]] stocking with [[Salmon]] and [[Shad]] for some time. Efforts to interest the fish commissioners of California, Nevada, and Utah failed.
- Shad was decided upon after more than two years of consideration. The salmon question remained open.
- In June, the U.S. Commissioner sent a special car carrying one million shad fry to The Needles, where they were placed successfully in the river.
- The author described this as the largest single fish plant within his knowledge.

**Expected shad life history (author's projection)**
- Fish would move slowly upstream for about two years until they reached adequate size and strength.
- They would then migrate to the ocean, spend one more year there, and afterward make annual spawning migrations to the headwaters, going up in spring and returning in autumn, similar to salmon.
- The author noted a claim that female shad spawn only once, but stated there was no good reason to believe it.
- Open questions included whether shad would return to spawn after passing down to the Gulf and whether they would stay within the Gulf, with the Colorado as the only considerable river they might encounter.

**Carp growth results (reported generally, no detailed reports received)**
- Most culture efforts failed, due to lack of knowledge or carelessness and inattention, especially by employees.
- Where reasonable care was taken, fish grew from minnows to weights of four and five pounds in one season, and one case reached eight pounds in one season.
- In other cases, fish reached twelve to fifteen pounds in two seasons.
- The author stated that no failure was reported that could not have been avoided with better knowledge and care.

**Funding and administrative problems**
- The balance on hand was $166.19, with the warrant for the remainder of the appropriation arriving January 5th, 1885.
- The discount or cost of collecting warrants was about $360.00, and this had to be paid from the remaining balance, along with the cost of receiving and distributing the promised carp and printing the report.
- Mr. Clark indicated he would accept a certified estimate of expenses and issue a corresponding warrant, which the author expected would prevent past annoyances.

**Recommendations**
- Establish commission-owned breeding ponds and hatcheries to avoid reliance on federal supply. The estimated cost to build and equip them was $5000.00, and ongoing maintenance costs were expected to be small.
- Produce fish besides carp suited to Territorial waters, including [[Catfish]], [[Trout]], and [[Bass]], since small catfish plantings had shown promising results.
- Increase the appropriation by $5000.00, and give commissioners power to construct and maintain ponds, buildings, and related works.
- Require annual artificial additions of shad to the Colorado River, if the current plant succeeds, along with strict, rigorously enforced police regulations to prevent depopulation.
- Consider adding game protection to the Fish Commissioner's duties, since wild game such as quail and deer is declining. The author also suggested acclimating the [[Prairie Chicken]] of the Mississippi valley to the Territory.
