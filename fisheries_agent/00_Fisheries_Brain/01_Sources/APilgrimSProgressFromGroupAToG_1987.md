## Executive Summary

This essay is a first-person retrospective by fishery biologist **Edwin P. Pister**, tracing his professional evolution from a "Group A" (utilitarian, commodity-based) to a "Group B" (holistic, biota-based) conservation philosophy, as framed by Aldo Leopold's "Land Ethic." While primarily philosophical, the text is grounded in concrete case studies of desert fish conservation in California's **eastern Sierra Nevada** and **Death Valley hydrographic system**, including the near-extinction of native pupfishes and springfishes, the institutional neglect of non-game aquatic species by mid-20th-century fish and game agencies, and landmark instream-flow and habitat-protection actions. The paper functions as a historical case study in the origins of desert fish conservation biology and the shift from put-and-take hatchery management toward ecosystem- and species-integrity-based management.

## Background & Description

- **Author's role**: Fishery biologist with the California Department of Fish and Game (CDFG), stationed in the eastern Sierra–desert region beginning in the late 1950s.
- **Management jurisdiction**: Nearly 1,000 waters spanning from the summit of Mt. Whitney (4,418 m) to the floor of Death Valley (~100 m below sea level) — one of the most extreme elevational gradients in North American freshwater management.
- **Species under stewardship**: Fishes, amphibians, invertebrates, and reptiles, though in practice management focused almost exclusively on introduced/exotic sport fishes.
- **Native fish component**: Of the game fishes in the author's district, only the **[[Golden Trout]]** (*Salmo aguabonita*, now *Oncorhynchus mykiss aguabonita*) was native; nearly all other game species were introduced or exotic (per the fisheries-profession distinction: "introduced" = from another drainage; "exotic" = from another continent).
- **Key taxa discussed**:
  - **[[Golden Trout]]** (*Salmo aguabonita*) — the district's sole native game fish.
  - **[[Owens Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon radiosus*) — native to the Owens River system, Mono County, California; subject of early species-recovery efforts (Miller & Pister 1971).
  - **Genus [[Empetrichthys]]** (springfishes) — Death Valley system endemics; at least two species and possibly the entire genus were on the verge of extinction by the late 1960s.
  - **[[Brown Trout]]** (*Salmo trutta*) — introduced/exotic species central to the Rock Creek water-rights case; described as "part of the ecosystem, but by no means the most important part."
- **Geographic scope**: The Owens River system, Walker River system, Rock Creek, Crowley Lake, Owens River Gorge, and the Pleistocene-era Death Valley drainages spanning California and Nevada.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat degradation baseline**: By the mid-20th century, the Owens and Walker river systems had already undergone significant habitat alteration and loss of native species composition, primarily due to water diversion for hydroelectric power and municipal water supply (Los Angeles Department of Water and Power).
- **Quantified impact (1985 FERC assessment)**: 88% of stream mileage downstream of Forest Service wilderness boundaries had been impacted by diversions; approximately 25% of the historic "hydroscape" had been eliminated entirely.
- **Institutional blind spot**: Prior to the 1960s, no systematic biological surveys of native, non-game aquatic biota (fishes, invertebrates, amphibians) existed within the author's management area — species identity and status were essentially unknown.
- **Case study — Rock Creek (1959)**: Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power sought to divert several miles of Rock Creek into Crowley Lake for hydroelectric generation, threatening an established recreational trout fishery. CDFG's court challenge — using early precursors of **[[Instream Flow]]** methodology — resulted in the first water-rights case CDFG ever won against the City of Los Angeles.
- **Case study — Death Valley interagency meeting (April 1969)**: Convened to address the status of native fishes across the Death Valley hydrographic area; revealed that CDFG, Nevada Department of Fish and Game, National Park Service, Bureau of Land Management, and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service all faced parallel crises regarding undocumented, imperiled native fish faunas. Agencies effectively bypassed slow bureaucratic channels to prevent extinctions.
- **Case study — High Sierra alpine lakes (late 1970s)**: National Park Service determined trout are non-indigenous to high-Sierra lakes and ceased stocking within park boundaries, citing biotic-integrity policy — despite most ecological damage having already occurred decades earlier at initial introduction. A compromise was reached: no new stocking of historically barren lakes, but continuation of stocking in lakes with a prior stocking history, differentiated between National Park Service and Forest Service jurisdictions.
- **Broader policy context**: The narrative predates or coincides with major U.S. environmental legislation — the first (1966) Endangered Species Act, National Environmental Policy Act, Marine Mammals Protection Act, and Clean Water Act — situating the fish-conservation anecdotes within the birth of modern American environmental law.

## Key Findings

- **Near-extinction event avoided**: Independent, non-bureaucratic action by multiple agencies in the late 1960s prevented the extinction of at least two, and likely five, fish species across two genera in the Death Valley drainage system, including the entire genus **[[Empetrichthys]]**.
- **Systemic neglect of non-game species**: State fish and wildlife agencies of the era (CDFG and peers) allocated the overwhelming majority of fisheries budgets to put-and-take hatchery trout stocking and angler creel censuses, with negligible investment in native/non-game species inventory or protection — a direct consequence of license-revenue-driven funding structures favoring "Group A" (commodity) management.
- **Paradigm shift in management practice**: The author substituted species inventory programs for creel censuses within his district, leading to informal recovery plans for the **[[Owens Pupfish]]** and other Owens River system natives; initial surveys found three native Owens River fishes to be endangered or of indeterminate status.
- **A–B philosophical cleavage persists institutionally**: Leopold's Group A/B division remains embedded in fish and wildlife agencies — "B" (biota-integrated) perspectives concentrate in academia, while "A" (harvest-commodity) perspectives dominate agency practice, reinforced by politically appointed commissioners and license-buyer constituencies.
- **Legal/management precedent**: The 1959–1960s Rock Creek litigation represents one of the earliest applications of instream flow science in California water law and the first successful CDFG water-rights case against a major municipal utility.
- **Species-recovery citations**: Early technical foundations for desert fish conservation are documented in Pister (1974, *Desert Fishes and Their Habitats*) and Miller & Pister (1971, management of the **[[Owens Pupfish]]**), both foundational works in North American desert fish conservation biology.
- **Emergent institutional reform**: By the 1980s, California had begun developing a Natural Diversity Data Base (in cooperation with The Nature Conservancy) to inventory statewide flora and fauna, alongside efforts to secure non-license-based funding for non-game species management — evidence of incremental Group B institutionalization.
