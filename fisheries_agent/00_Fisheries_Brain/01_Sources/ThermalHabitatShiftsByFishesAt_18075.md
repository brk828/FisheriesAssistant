# Thermal Habitat Shifts by Fishes at the Thermocline in Lake Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This study used bottom trawling where the [[Thermocline]] met the lake bottom near Grand Haven, Michigan, to compare the thermal distribution of fishes in [[Lake Michigan]] between 1977 and 1979. Between the two surveys, the native [[Bloater]] (*Coregonus hoyi*) rose from less than 1% to about 42% of the trawl catch, and adult [[Alewife]] (*Alosa pseudoharengus*) shifted from 11–16°C to 4–8°C. The authors interpret the shift mainly as [[Exploitation Competition]] with bloaters for food and thermal habitat, with [[Salmonid Predation]] and [[Interference Competition]] considered as alternatives that could not be excluded.

## Background & Description

Fishes partition habitat along environmental gradients, and temperature is a key niche dimension because small temperature changes affect survival, growth, and reproduction (Crowder and Magnuson 1982). Earlier work in Lake Michigan showed that common species segregate by [[Thermal Preference]] and that their diets complement their thermal habitat use ([[Habitat Partitioning]]).

The study site is the bottom intersection of the thermocline, a sharp and dynamic gradient. Sampling used day and night [[Bottom Trawling]] along bottom isotherms, with a 60-m bathythermograph at each station. Two surveys were conducted:

- **1977:** September 7–13
- **1979:** August 27–September 1

Trawl temperature was steered by depth contours. In 1979, a thermistor malfunction meant tow temperatures were estimated from the mean of beginning and end bottom temperatures. For 48 tows (27 day, 21 night), this difference averaged 0.6°C (range 0.4–4°C). Because several species migrate vertically at night ([[Diel Vertical Migration]]), analysis focused on day catches.

Data were grouped into four thermal strata, each about 4°C wide, corresponding to the [[Hypolimnion]], [[Metalimnion]] (lower and upper), and [[Epilimnion]]. Geometric mean catch per unit effort (15-min bottom time) was compared among strata using [[Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA]] and Dunn's multiple comparison test (α = 0.1).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native and introduced community:** Bloaters are native ciscoes, while alewives and rainbow smelt are prominent forage fishes in the lake. Alewives and small bloaters feed mainly on zooplankton ([[Zooplankton]]), whereas large bloaters (146–300 mm FL) feed mainly on benthic prey.
- **Predation pressure:** Alewives are heavily preyed upon by [[Lake Trout]], [[Coho Salmon]], and [[Chinook Salmon]]. Laboratory preferred temperatures are about 13°C for coho and chinook salmon and 11.7°C for yearling lake trout.
- **Seasonal movement:** Alewives move offshore in late fall as stratification decays, a pattern that could partly mimic the observed shift ([[Seasonal Offshore Migration]]).
- **Foraging conditions:** Zooplankton within 1 m of the bottom at the trawl sites was relatively large, which may favor bloaters, as they capture near-bottom prey more efficiently than alewives.

## Key Findings

**1. Community composition changed sharply.**
- A total of 16,170 fish of 19 species were caught in 48 bottom trawls in 1979.
- Bloater rose from less than 1% of the catch in 1977 to 41.7% in 1979 (text: "nearly 42%").
- Adult alewife rose from 5.5% to 14.2% of the catch (text: 6% to 14%).
- Bloater, [[Spottail Shiner]], [[Rainbow Smelt]], and alewife made up 90% of the 1979 catch, compared with 77% in 1977.
- Bloater sizes were bimodal: small (80–145 mm FL, probably yearlings) and large (146–300 mm FL).

**2. Thermal distributions differed significantly among species.**
- Significant temperature partitioning (Kruskal-Wallis) was found for spottail shiner, adult rainbow smelt, trout-perch, small and large bloaters, adult alewife, slimy sculpin, and [[Yellow Perch]].
- Spottail shiners were most abundant in the epilimnion.
- Small bloaters peaked in the upper metalimnion, while large bloaters were less common in the epilimnion.
- Adult alewives were more abundant in the hypolimnion than in the epilimnion in 1979.
- [[Slimy Sculpin]] and [[Ninespine Stickleback]] were caught mainly at cold temperatures (4–6°C).

**3. The alewife thermal niche shifted toward cooler water.**
- In 1977, 93% of day-caught alewives were within their preferred temperature +5°C, and 52% were within 2°C of the laboratory preferred temperature of 16°C.
- In 1979, only 8% were within 5°C of the preferred temperature, and less than 3% were within 2°C.
- Significantly more alewives were caught in the hypolimnion in 1979 than in 1977 (Mann-Whitney U, P < 0.001).
- The shift appears sustained: alewives were again abundant in cold deep water in August 1980 (unpublished data by the authors).

**4. Rainbow smelt may also have shifted slightly warmer.**
- Smelt occupied slightly warmer water in 1979, with significant abundance only in the epilimnion and upper metalimnion.
- Data from Port Sheldon, about 16 km south, support this: adult smelt were most common at 10–16°C in 1979, compared with 6–14°C in 1977–78 (Jude et al. 1980).

**5. Competition with bloater is the most plausible explanation.**
- Large bloaters overlap alewives in habitat but differ in diet, so bloaters appear to exploit different food.
- Small bloaters occupied the thermal zone between rainbow smelt and alewife and may have displaced them through food competition.
- Laboratory results show bloaters were more aggressive than alewives and more successful at capturing surface-introduced prey, which supports [[Interference Competition]] as a possible factor, though evidence is limited.
- Alewife catch was not reduced in 1979 relative to 1977.

**6. Alternative hypotheses were weighed and judged less likely.**
- **Salmonid predation:** A shift would require predators to move 6–9°C cooler than their preferred temperature, which is probably infrequent and energetically costly. Predation intensity also did not change drastically between the two years.
- **Seasonal offshore migration:** In late August 1979, alewives were most abundant at depths of 40 m or more, whereas in the 1960s they occupied about 25 m on comparable dates. This could reflect early migration but seems unlikely.
- **Other species:** Other native species showed no thermal shifts, which the authors argue supports competition, since alewife and smelt diets resemble that of small bloaters.

**7. Caveat.** The authors state that observational niche-shift data cannot conclusively demonstrate competition, and that predation or between-year variation could also explain the pattern. The study is ongoing, with continuing field and laboratory work planned.
