## Executive Summary

This study (Lanigan & Tyus, 1988) evaluated the population status of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Green River basin, Utah and Colorado, using capture-recapture data collected from 1980 to 1988. Using the program CAPTURE and the model M_t (time-specific capture probability), the researchers estimated the upper Green River (river kilometer 282–555) population at **948 fish** (SE = 97; 95% CI = 758–1138), based on 410 unique fish captured (68 recaptures) — later corrected in results text to 423 total captures (69 recaptures). The lower Green River (RK 0–211) population was too sparse (13 fish captured, 1 recapture) to estimate reliably and is considered a relict population. A geological barrier — Gray and lower Desolation canyons (RK 211–282), reinforced by a low diversion dam at RK 206 — appears to isolate the two populations, with no habitat or fish movement detected in this reach. The Green River supports the **largest known population of razorback suckers in native riverine habitat**, but the population exhibits low density, an apparent total lack of juvenile recruitment, and significant habitat alteration, raising serious concerns about long-term viability and warranting Federal Endangered Species Act listing considerations.

## Background & Description

- **Total length range of captured adults:** 426–617 mm (~42.6–61.7 cm), consistent with size-frequency data in Figure 2 (range roughly 48–66 cm depicted).
- **No juveniles found:** Despite extensive seining effort (1979–1986) across backwater (167,921 m²), shoreline (231,185 m²), eddy (58,439 m²), and riffle (2,530 m²) habitats using mesh sizes from 0.5–6.35 mm, no razorback sucker juveniles (20–400 mm) were captured — strong evidence of recruitment failure.
- **Age structure:** Vertebral centra counts on two Green River specimens yielded 16 and 26 growth rings; previous work (Tyus 1987) reported ages of >19 years (otolith annuli) and 12 years (vertebral centra) for two other individuals. Per [[McCarthy And Minckley 1987]], vertebral aging underestimates true age relative to otolith aging by an average of 8.6 rings, suggesting these fish are likely older than counted.
- **Longevity comparison:** Lake Mohave (Lower Colorado River Basin) razorback suckers were aged 24–44 years (1980–1981 data); Green River fish, while younger by direct counts, may approach similar longevity (40+ years) once aging bias is corrected, implying an old, non-reproducing (in terms of surviving offspring) adult stock experiencing low mortality.
- **Tag retention:** Carlin dangler tags showed ~94.2% retention (only 4 lost tags identified out of recaptured fish with tag scars).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution:** Endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]; in riverine habitat restricted to the mainstream and lower tributary reaches of the [[Green River]] and upper [[Colorado River]]. Relict subpopulations of old individuals persist in mainstream reservoirs of the Lower Colorado River Basin below Lee Ferry, Arizona.
- **Study area boundaries:** Sampling spanned Green River RK 35–555, Yampa River RK 0–169, and White River RK 0–235.
- **Population segregation:** Two genetically/ecologically isolated Green River subpopulations identified:
  - **Upper Green River** (RK 282–555): higher density, CPUE = 0.49 fish/hour.
  - **Lower Green River** (RK 0–211): extremely rare, CPUE = 0.05 fish/hour, relict status.
  - **Gray and Lower Desolation Canyons** (RK 211–282): zero captures — rapids and a diversion dam at RK 206 form apparent barriers to movement; absence attributed to lack of suitable habitat rather than sampling gaps.
- **Tributary use:** Only one individual found >2.4 RK upstream from tributary mouths (White River, RK 29, 1987), despite substantial electrofishing effort in the [[Yampa River]] (112.8 hours) and [[White River]] (83.7 hours) — indicating razorback suckers rarely use tributary habitats beyond confluence zones.
- **Co-occurring species / threats:**
  - Sympatric with the federally endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*); razorback sucker catches were only 1.8% of total catch and 33% of spring electrofishing catch relative to squawfish (Tyus 1987), underscoring razorback sucker scarcity.
  - Introduced ictalurid predators (catfishes) are hypothesized to prey heavily on juvenile razorback suckers, potentially preventing recruitment (supported by [[Marsh And Brooks 1988]] observations of ictalurid predation on stocked juveniles).
  - Habitat alteration following impoundment of [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] (1962) is hypothesized to have adversely affected spawning/recruitment habitat.
- **Reproductive evidence:** Larval razorback suckers have been documented below suspected spawning sites in the upper Green River, and ripe adults captured at suspected spawning sites — confirming reproduction occurs, but with an apparent complete recruitment bottleneck between larval and juvenile life stages.

## Key Findings

1. **Population estimate (upper Green River):** N = 948 fish (SE = 97, 95% CI = 758–1138); coefficient of variation = 10.2%, indicating good statistical precision (below the 20% threshold recommended for reliable studies).
2. **Model selection:** Program CAPTURE selected model M_t (time-specific capture probability model, analogous to Petersen-Lincoln/Schnabel estimators) as most appropriate; yearly capture probability (p) ranged from 0.02–0.09.
3. **Lower Green River population:** Considered relict; too few fish (13 captured, 1 recaptured over 9 years) for statistical population estimation; density approximately one order of magnitude lower than the upper river.
4. **Geographic and demographic closure assumptions:** Supported by field data — no tagged fish moved between upper/lower Green River segments or into the Colorado River; upper boundary at RK 555 with zero captures beyond this point over 8 years.
5. **Recruitment failure:** No juveniles captured in 9 years of extensive seine sampling despite documented spawning and larval presence — strongly suggests high larval/juvenile mortality, likely from introduced predators and/or habitat alteration.
6. **Longevity and demographic implications:** Adult population likely composed of long-lived (40+ year), aging individuals with low ongoing mortality but no replacement, mirroring the pattern documented in Lower Colorado River Basin reservoirs ([[Minckley 1983]]) where large relict populations persisted for years before disappearing due to lack of recruitment.
7. **Conservation status:** At time of publication, the razorback sucker had no protection under the U.S. Endangered Species Act, despite representing the largest known extant riverine population. Authors explicitly recommend Federal listing and coordinated management action to prevent extirpation of the [[Green River Basin]] population.
8. **Significance:** This is the first published population size estimate for razorback sucker in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], establishing a critical baseline for future conservation and recovery program efforts (e.g., subsequent USFWS Recovery Implementation Program, 1987).
