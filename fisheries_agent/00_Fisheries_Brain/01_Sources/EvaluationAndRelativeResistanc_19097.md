# Evaluation and Relative Resistance of Sixteen Species of Fish as Test Animals in Toxicity Bioassays of Petroleum Refinery Effluents

#ichthyology #marine-biology #toxicology #environmental-science #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study evaluated sixteen freshwater fish species as toxicity indicators in bioassays using [[Petroleum Refinery Effluent]] as the toxicant. Conducted at [[Oklahoma State University]] during 1960-1961, the research established relative resistance rankings among species and assessed their suitability for laboratory bioassay work. The [[Guppy]] ([[Lebistes reticulatus]]) served as a reference species across all tests. Results showed [[Lebistes reticulatus]], [[Carassius auratus]] (goldfish), and [[Gambusia affinis]] (mosquito fish) as the most resistant species, while [[Etheostoma spectabile]] (orangethroat darter) was least resistant. The study provides critical comparative data for selecting appropriate test species in industrial effluent toxicity assessments and establishes standardized protocols for fish bioassay procedures.

## Background & Description

### Study Design and Methodology

The research employed a statistical design testing each species four times with different concentrations of different effluent samples. All results were compared against [[Lebistes reticulatus]] as a common reference species. The sixteen species tested represented diverse families:

**Cyprinidae (Minnows and Carps):**
- [[Carassius auratus]] (Goldfish)
- [[Cyprinus carpio]] (Carp)
- [[Hybognathus placitus]] (Plains Minnow)
- [[Notropis percobromus]] (Plains Shiner)
- [[Notropis zonatus]] (Bleeding Shiner)
- [[Pimephales promelas]] (Fathead Minnow)
- [[Semotilus atromaculatus]] (Creek Chub)

**Centrarchidae (Sunfishes and Basses):**
- [[Lepomis macrochirus]] (Bluegill)
- [[Micropterus dolomieui]] (Smallmouth Bass)

**Other Families:**
- [[Etheostoma spectabile]] (Orangethroat Darter, Percidae)
- [[Eucalia inconstans]] (Brook Stickleback, Gasterosteidae)
- [[Gambusia affinis]] (Mosquito Fish, Poeciliidae)
- [[Labidesthes sicculus]] (Brook Silverside, Atherinopsidae)
- [[Lebistes reticulatus]] (Guppy, Poeciliidae)
- [[Salmo gairdneri]] (Rainbow Trout, Salmonidae)
- [[Catostomus commersoni]] (White Sucker, Catostomidae)

### Fish Acquisition and Laboratory Maintenance

Fish specimens were obtained from multiple sources including federal and state hatcheries (Ozark Fish Hatchery, U.S. Fish Hatchery at Tishomingo, Oklahoma), commercial dealers, wild collections from Oklahoma rivers and streams, and laboratory breeding (guppy only). Transport methods included oxygen-supplied plastic bags for long distances and hauling tanks with aeration for shorter trips.

Laboratory holding facilities consisted of 30 dismantled refrigerator liners (30-60 gallon capacity) arranged on metal racks. Holding water was dechlorinated tap water stored and aerated in a 600-gallon redwood vat. Water quality parameters: pH 7.4-7.9, hardness 138-153 ppm, bicarbonate alkalinity 107-132 ppm. Prophylactic treatment with terramycin and acriflavine prevented fin rot and disease.

### Bioassay Protocol

**Effluent Collection:** Samples collected 10-15 hours before testing from a single petroleum refinery, either from a holding pond or discharge pipe. Effluent temperature 90-100°F, cooled to laboratory temperature (~74°F) before use.

**Test Containers:** Polyethylene rectangular containers (11.5 × 7.5 × 12 inches) holding 10 liters of test solution.

**Test Conditions:**
- 10 specimens per concentration (or 5 if specimens >2 grams but ≤4 grams)
- Oxygen supplied at 1 bubble/second to prevent oxygen deficiency
- Dissolved oxygen maintained >5.6 ppm initially, near saturation at test end
- pH values typically 8.6 initially, 7.8 at test end
- Temperature range: 71-77°F
- Observation intervals: 1, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 96 hours

**Exploratory Tests:** Conducted with wide concentration ranges to establish appropriate test concentrations for each species before formal bioassays.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Species-Specific Laboratory Performance

**Highly Adaptable Species:**

[[Carassius auratus]]: 500 specimens (1.75-2.25 inches) shipped from Missouri. Hardy, arrived in excellent condition, immediately accepted prepared food (poultry pellets and powdered egg). Not excitable, maintained excellent condition. First tested day 4, used through day 22.

[[Lebistes reticulatus]]: Reared in laboratory from brood stock. Young removed to holding tanks at 70-75°F, immediately ate prepared food. No disease observed. Used at 0.6-0.7 inches length before sexual dimorphism evident. Served as reference species in all 24 bioassays.

[[Gambusia affinis]]: Collected from farm pond 4 miles from laboratory. Transported in hauling tanks with oxygen. Vigorously ate within 12 hours (live daphnia, dried daphnia, mixed food). Calm, excellent condition throughout. Large gravid females excluded due to size differences. First tested day 5, used through day 28.

**Moderately Adaptable Species:**

[[Pimephales promelas]]: 600 specimens (1-3 inches) from local ponds. Required gradual temperature adjustment (15°F difference). Took food sparingly after 24 hours, readily after 48 hours. Few casualties after initial period. First tested day 8, used through day 30.

[[Lepomis macrochirus]]: 400 specimens (2-2.5 inches) from federal hatchery. Transported 165 miles in oxygen-supplied plastic bags. Not excitable, ate lightly after 12 hours, vigorously thereafter. Only incidental deaths. First tested day 5, used through day 37.

[[Semotilus atromaculatus]]: 500 specimens (2.25-2.5 inches) shipped from Missouri. Not excitable, ate within 24 hours. Accepted variety of foods readily. No casualties during holding. First tested day 4, used through day 22.

**Challenging Species:**

[[Notropis percobromus]]: Three collections of ~200 specimens each (1.5-2.5 inches). Delicate with easily loosened scales. Injuries common during seining. Required 96 hours before feeding readily. Deaths occurred within 48 hours of confinement. First tested day 6, used through day 20.

[[Hybognathus placitus]]: 700 specimens (1.5-2 inches). Delicate tissues, easily loosened scales. 10% dead or injured upon arrival. Quite excitable initially. First feeding observed 72 hours post-capture. Some tail and fin rot from collection injuries. First tested day 7, used through day 33.

[[Salmo gairdneri]]: Multiple shipments totaling ~700 specimens (1.5-3.5 inches). One shipment lost oxygen, specimens dead/dying. Not particularly excitable, immediately ate live food and pellets. Smaller specimens could not compete for food, became emaciated and excluded from tests. First tested day 3, some used through day 16.

[[Etheostoma spectabile]]: Two collections of ~300 specimens each (1.5-2 inches). Initially excitable, preferred darkened areas. Fed vigorously on live daphnia within 24 hours. Became tame, would meet person at tank edge anticipating food. First collection tested days 8 and 16; second collection days 4 and 6.

### Water Quality and Habitat Considerations

The study emphasized that fish condition greatly influences bioassay results. Factors affecting tolerance included:
- Individual differences between fish
- Acclimation period length
- Temperature
- pH
- Dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide concentrations
- Environmental changes producing different physical/chemical characteristics

Proper acclimation was defined as the time necessary to observe and remove unhealthy specimens, allow fish to become accustomed to laboratory surroundings, and establish ready feeding. Acclimation time varied by species based on initial condition and excitability.

### Industrial Pollution Context

Petroleum refinery effluents represent complex mixtures of toxic compounds. The study noted that effluent toxicity varied between samples, necessitating exploratory tests before each bioassay. Chemical composition was not determined, focusing instead on biological effects. The research acknowledged that natural waters have become increasingly polluted by industrial sources, with bioassay serving as a critical tool for measuring toxicant effects when chemical measurement of all components is impossible.

### Statistical Populations

**Duncan New Multiple Range Test** identified four statistical populations at 24 hours:

**Population I (Most Resistant):** L. reticulatus, C. auratus, G. affinis - significantly different from Populations III and IV.

**Population II (Overlapping):** Includes C. auratus and G. affinis from Population I, plus all Population III species except N. zonatus.

**Population III (Intermediate Resistance):** C. carpio, P. promelas, E. inconstans, N. percobromus, L. sicculus, M. dolomieui, L. macrochirus, H. placitus, S. atromaculatus, C. commersoni, S. gairdneri, N. zonatus.

**Population IV:** Similar to Population III but includes E. spectabile and excludes C. carpio.

Analysis of variance for 24-hour TL₅₀ values: F-ratio = 9.5 (15 df for species, 45 df for experimental error), indicating highly significant differences among species.

### Mortality Patterns

The greatest percentage of test deaths occurred within the first 24 hours of testing, with little change during the last 72 hours. Fish in higher toxicity solutions reacted with definite excitability: jumping, gasping, circular erratic swimming, followed by brief convulsive periods before death. The 1-hour and 6-hour intervals primarily served to remove specimens quickly killed in most toxic solutions.

### Species Evaluation for Bioassay Suitability

**Most Desirable Test Species:**

[[Lebistes reticulatus]]: Most convenient species. Accepted testing conditions calmly, ate readily, small and uniform size, grows to maturity quickly, free of common diseases, easily raised in laboratory, similar resistance to native fish.

[[Carassius auratus]] and [[Pimephales promelas]]: Easily raised in ponds in large numbers, exhibit most desirable traits, though disease sometimes problematic. Goldfish particularly favored due to extensive physiological and pharmacological literature, availability, and laboratory suitability.

[[Gambusia affinis]]: Does not exceed usable size, hardy, usually found in considerable numbers within distribution area.

**Moderately Suitable Species:**

[[Lepomis macrochirus]], [[Semotilus atromaculatus]], [[Catostomus commersoni]]: Young have lengthy period of usable size, satisfactory in most aspects. Centrarchids noted as satisfactory but may require lengthy acclimation.

**Less Desirable Species:**

Small minnows ([[Notropis percobromus]], [[Hybognathus placitus]], [[Notropis zonatus]], [[Labidesthes sicculus]]): Always desirable testing size but difficult to collect in sufficient numbers; delicate tissue made injury and scale loss problematic.

Species with rapid metabolic rates or specialized diets ([[Etheostoma spectabile]], [[Eucalia inconstans]]): Require more intensive care.

Young of larger, faster-growing species ([[Micropterus dolomieui]], [[Salmo gairdneri]], [[Cyprinus carpio]]): Usable only for short periods due to size requirements.

### Critical Evaluation Criteria

The study established that no single species possesses all favorable testing characteristics. Important traits for evaluation include:

**Obtainability:** Distribution, availability, ease of procurement, cost (including travel, transport, hatchery fees, shipping).

**Identification:** Must be easily identified by external characters without excess handling.

**Temperament:** Mild, stable temperament; not easily frightened; docile during capture and containment; ability to tame; compatible when aggregated; resistant to shock or muscular tetany.

**Dietary Requirements:** Accept prepared food; no special or difficult feeding habits; maintain good health when fed; withstand testing period without food.

**Disease/Injury Resistance:** High resistance to disease and injury; specimens that injure easily from seine abrasion or contact create infection sources.

**Relative Resistance:** Should be within narrow range, neither extremely resistant nor extremely susceptible to toxicants.

### Methodological Insights

**Acclimation Period:** Proper acclimation defined as time necessary to observe/remove unhealthy specimens, allow adjustment to laboratory surroundings, and establish ready feeding. Not constant across species; determined by initial condition and excitability. In most instances, non-injured, disease-free fish are in best condition for testing immediately after subsidence of initial capture excitement. Laboratory feeding may not improve nutritional condition; lengthy periods with unaccustomed prepared foods may weaken specimens.

**Test Validity Requirements:** Fish must be healthy when used. Weakened specimens succumb sooner and to lesser toxicant amounts than healthy specimens. Death during bioassay must be directly traceable to toxic components in test solution. Poor care (rough treatment, disease presence, crowding, temperature extremes, improper feeding) reduces test validity.

**Quality Control:** If 10% of any collection deemed unfit for testing due to disease, injury, or unknown causes, entire collection destroyed and new collection made (following Doudoroff et al., 1951 recommendations).

### Application and Limitations

**Direct Application:** Results directly applicable only to particular species tested, with particular effluent source, at particular time, under similar controlled testing conditions.

**Comparative Value:** Previous study (Douglas, 1961) using effluents from two different refineries showed same resistance order for four species as present study, suggesting results may be generalizable across petroleum refinery effluents.

**Practical Applications:**
- Comparing results from one species with known resistant values of other fishes aids in determining overall toxic effect of particular effluent
- Knowledge of toxic reactions of many species valuable for investigating fish kills
- If high mortality of particular species occurs in acutely polluted area, all species with lower resistances would likewise be affected

**Span of Resistance:** The span between most resistant and least resistant species was relatively short when using petroleum refinery effluent, suggesting that factors other than relative resistance (obtainability, holding requirements) may have greater value in species selection.
