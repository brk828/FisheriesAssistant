# Guide to the Use of the Mosquito Fish, *Gambusia affinis*, for Mosquito Control

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 U.S. Navy guide, prepared by LT L. Lance Sholdt, LT Douglas A. Ehrhardt and Andrew G. Michael of [[Navy Environmental and Preventive Medicine Unit No. 2]] (Norfolk, Virginia), is a practical reference on the biology, husbandry and deployment of the [[Mosquito Fish]] ([[Gambusia Affinis]]) as a supplementary biological agent against mosquitoes. The authors stress that the fish supplements but cannot replace chemical control, and that an improperly managed fish program can harm the aquatic environment as much as pesticide misuse. The guide covers history, taxonomy and distribution, habitat, feeding, reproduction, effectiveness, trapping, transport, stocking and integrated control.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *Gambusia affinis* (Baird and Girard) belongs to the Poeciliidae, one of roughly 180 species in the family. The name *affinis* means "neighboring" or "related," and the genus name is linked to a provincial word meaning "nothing."
- **Morphology:** Males show a modified anal fin, the gonopodium, used for internal fertilization. Females are larger and show a dark "gravid spot" as pregnancy advances. Figure 1 of the source contrasts the male and female body forms.
- **Coloration:** Body is pale grey to silver, with an olive-green or greenish tinge in some individuals.
- **Size:** Newborn fish are about 3/8 inch long at birth.
- **Lifespan:** Fish may live about 4 years in aquaria but seldom survive that long in the wild. Field observations recorded a female that lived 15 months after liberation.
- **Historical spread:** Introduced from the southeastern United States to Hawaii (around 1905), Asia, Europe (from Spain in 1922) and beyond, often through mosquito control programs and the [[Rockefeller Foundation]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preference:** The species tolerates a wide range of conditions, including shallow water, vegetated margins, polluted waters and high temperatures. It shows reduced activity in cold water and is hardy against disease.
- **Feeding:** It is a surface-feeding, predaceous species that takes mosquito larvae and pupae. Its upturned, flattened mouth supports surface feeding. It also eats other invertebrates.
- **Reproduction:** Gambusia is ovoviviparous (livebearing). Females store sperm and produce multiple broods after one mating. Gestation lasts 23 to 24 days. Broods average about 40 to 104 young, occasionally more than 300. Females may breed from April or May to September or October, depending on latitude.
- **Environmental risks:** The authors warn that indiscriminate stocking can displace native fauna and alter ecosystems, and that fish stocking must be coordinated with the local environmental and health authorities.
- **Oxygen and temperature:** Overcrowding causes mutual interference at the surface and rapid mortality, especially in hot weather when dissolved oxygen falls.

## Key Findings

- **Efficacy:** Gambusia can suppress mosquito populations and reduce pesticide use, but it works best as a supplement within integrated control, not as a standalone replacement for chemicals.
- **Mosquito-control value:** The fish were used against *Anopheles* and *Culex* in ponds, rice fields, swamps and sewage lagoons. Reported examples include a record-setting program at Honolulu and a California field trial with trap captures of up to about 100,000 fish in one day.
- **Trapping methods:**
  - Aquatic dip nets and seines, which are simple but labor-intensive and can injure fish.
  - Minnow traps made of wire mesh with funnel entrances.
  - Permanent and portable box traps. The portable design is collapsible, lightweight, assembled by one person, and detailed in Appendix A. Traps are best set parallel to shore, 2 to 4 feet out, and fished in early morning and late evening.
- **Transport:** Fish were moved in garbage cans, plastic bags, 30-gallon containers and bait-fish aeration systems. Reported loads include about 300 fish in polyethylene bags, 2,000 fish in 30-gallon containers and 10,000 to 15,000 fish in 15-gallon cans for short trips. Sealed plastic bags with oxygen are cheap, rust-free and portable. Water temperature should be kept within 4 to 5 degrees of the source water.
- **Husbandry:** Fish should not be fed artificially for one day before or during transport, and starvation lowers metabolic waste. Wait periods after pesticide application are required before stocking.
- **Environmental limits:** Vegetation management, such as reducing dense emergent plants, can improve fish access to larvae. Aquatic herbicides and insecticides can kill the fish, so they must be used selectively and not in the same water body at the same time.
- **Integrated control:** The guide recommends coordinating fish use with physical, chemical and habitat methods and with local health and environmental agencies.
- **Document scope:** The guide is a 1972 Navy technical publication. Its opinions are the authors' own, and mention of commercial products does not constitute endorsement. The bibliography lists roughly 80 references, including works by [[Gerberich and Laird]] (1968), [[Krumholz]] (1948), [[Stains]] (1970) and [[Bay]] (1967).
