# Integrated Fisheries Management Plan for the Little Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department Technical Report (NGTR 146, August 1999, edited by Kirk L. Young and E. Patricia Lopez) presents an integrated fisheries management plan for the [[Little Colorado River Basin]], a 27,089 mi² (70,160 km²) watershed on the [[Colorado Plateau]]. It is a pilot effort to manage native and sportfish together on a watershed-based scale rather than species by species. The report combines five chapters with a 251-unit management plan: a policy history (Chapter 1), basin background (Chapter 2), [[Geographic Information System]] habitat modeling (Chapter 3), empirical habitat analysis (Chapter 4), and the plan's process and prescriptions (Chapter 5). The project is explicitly not a watershed management plan, though it is intended to serve as the fish component of one.

## Background & Description

**Policy context.** The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) was responsible for wildlife management under Arizona Revised Statute Title 17. Its "Wildlife 2000" strategic plan directed watershed-based plans that minimize conflict between native and sportfish. Native fish management lagged sportfish management by about 80 years, and listings under the [[Endangered Species Act]] pushed the Department toward proactive planning.

**Basin description.** The basin spans northeast Arizona and northwest New Mexico. Elevation ranges from 3,850 m (Mt. Humphreys) to 830 m at the confluence with the [[Colorado River]]. Mean annual precipitation exceeds 75 cm at high elevations and falls to 15 cm or less on the plateau. Evaporation often exceeds 127 cm per year.

**Scope of the project.** Three goals were set: reduce conflict between native and non-native sportfish management; provide an integrated strategy for all basin fish activities; and take a proactive stance to improve native fish status, support delisting, and avoid future federal listings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish fauna.** The basin holds 9 native species, four of them federally listed (Table 1.2). The listed species are [[Humpback Chub]], [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Apache Trout]]. The Department recorded about 80 introduced species statewide, compared with 32 native species.

**Hydrology.** Four north-flowing subbasins produce most streamflow: the Upper LCR, Silver Creek, Chevelon Creek, and Clear Creek. Median gaged flow totals 195,224 ac-ft. Cultural depletions remove an average of 91,480 ac-ft per year, and natural losses add 50,720 ac-ft. Perennial flow now typically disappears below St. Johns.

**Land use and historical change.** Overgrazing followed Anglo and Mormon settlement. The [[Aztec Land and Cattle Company]] ran about 60,000 head, and an estimated 150,000 cattle and 120,000 sheep grazed the basin before a drought in the 1890s. Dams built from 1876 onward were repeatedly washed out. Early travel accounts describe a perennial, cottonwood-lined river that has since become a wide, sandy channel. Land ownership is 36.2% private, 36.7% federal (mostly USFS), and 18.2% state.

**Angling and conflict.** The basin supports about 850,000 angler days per year, worth more than $80 million. Stocked trout and their progeny prey on native fishes, and three reservoirs were flagged as problem areas for spinedace in a [[Section 7 Consultation]] with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

## Key Findings

**Chapter 1: Policy and history**
- Sportfish management began in 1881 with the Arizona Fish Commission. The first statewide strategic plans, in 1990, dealt with sportfish only.
- The coldwater plan identified 159 stream reaches totaling 1,470 miles and 64 lakes covering about 3,000 acres. Warmwater waters were grouped into 12 types.
- The Little Colorado River basin was chosen as the pilot because of its sportfishery value, its 9 native species, and its reservoirs flagged under the ESA.

**Chapter 2: Basin background**
- Subbasin flow and depletion data show that cultural depletions (91,480 ac-ft) exceed natural losses (50,720 ac-ft).
- Historical accounts from 1851 to 1878 describe a river with groves of cottonwood and abundant grass, altered severely by grazing and drought.
- Trout fishing in the White Mountains was once abundant. By the late 1920s, unrestricted harvest had depleted many populations, and stocking of non-native trout followed, beginning with brook trout in 1917.

**Chapter 3: GIS habitat modeling (James R. Hatten)**
- The four focus basins (Clear, Chevelon, Silver, Lyman) covered 8,203 km². The 804 sample sites were derived from 27,949 fish records.
- The 804 sites were not randomly located. Lyman (367 sites) and Clear (274) were heavily sampled, while Chevelon (93) and Silver (70) were under-sampled, and first- to third-order channels were largely missed in Silver and Chevelon.
- Habitat suitability profiles were built for 6 native and 19 non-native fishes. Cell-based models were built for CASP, LEVI, PADI, RHOS, and brown trout. Apache trout and roundtail chub lacked enough sites for models.
- Little Colorado spinedace (LEVI) and Little Colorado sucker (CASP) showed upper elevation limits of 2,338 m and 2,287 m, respectively. Neither species was observed above these elevations.
- Speckled dace (RHOS) was the most widespread native species, observed at 330 sites. Roundtail chub (GIRO, 8 sites) and Apache trout (ONAP, 26 sites, Lyman Basin only) were too rare to model.
- The author concluded that the models are "liberal internally, but conservative externally." They define potentially suitable habitat, not confirmed habitat.
- Major error sources include a hydrology cover with a 0.8 km minimum mapping unit, which misses interrupted perennial reaches, and DEM vertical errors of 1 to 3 m.

**Chapter 4: Empirical habitat analysis (Linda J. Allison)**
- The analysis drew on 436 site visits (1976 to 1998) in the Upper LCR, with 339 lotic sites classified for stream order and permanence. Records were non-random and were collected under varied protocols.
- Fish presence was linked to elevation, stream order, water permanence, link slope, habitat width, substrate, and microhabitat using logistic regression.
- Brook trout increased with elevation, while CASP, PADI, PIPR, and RHOS declined.
- Stream orders 5 to 7 were all perennial, and Apache trout was the only species occurring in intermittent or ephemeral reaches (alongside rainbow and brook trout).
- Pairwise co-occurrence tests found all basin-wide associations with Apache trout to be negative. This reflects stocking practice and non-overlapping elevation ranges, not necessarily antagonism.
- Rainbow trout (ONMY) showed significant negative associations with most native species at the basin scale.
- Depletion sampling showed that LEVI, PADI, and brook trout were not always detected on the first pass (for example, LEVI on the first pass in 85% of capture sites).

**Chapter 5: Management plan (David B. Dorum and E. Patricia Lopez)**
- The plan divides the basin into management units (streams, lakes, reservoirs, stock tanks). Each unit is assigned a management emphasis using separate Native and Sportfish flowcharts. The higher result sets the emphasis.
- Of 1,534.8 stream miles analyzed, 822.5 miles (53.6%) were unknown, 644.3 miles (42.0%) were native emphasis, and 67.2 miles (4.4%) were sportfish emphasis.
- Of 1,534.8 stream miles, 73.1% of the native-emphasis length (362.5 miles) was interrupted perennial, so the true habitable length is probably smaller.
- Lakes: 52 lake units (6,432 acres) are sportfish, 3 units (5 acres) are native, and 14 units (813 acres) are unknown.
- Native fish needs were set by a panel of experts, with a single population target per sub-basin (Clear Creek, Chevelon, Silver Creek, and the Upper LCR). The panel failed to define population size or to produce a range of targets.
- Recommendations include no-net-loss management for speckled dace, bluehead sucker, and LC sucker. They also include repatriating native fishes into Show Low Creek, a Recovery Plan-driven approach for spinedace, and Apache trout management tied to a separate implementation plan.
- Table 5.1 lists 17 units with potentially antagonistic pairs of desired species. Table 5.2 lists about 40 adjacent-unit pairs with such conflicts. Explanations 1 to 14 cover mitigating factors.
- Recommendations for future work include standardizing data, a five-year plan review, an interrupted-perennial hydrology cover, and angler needs assessment. The angler needs assessment was not completed for this iteration, and the plan relied on staff judgment that current demand is met.
