# Status Summary of the Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in Lake Mead, Nevada-Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by Jon C. Sjoberg of the [[Nevada Division Of Wildlife]] in March 1994 and circulated as a June 1994 memorandum to Paul Marsh, compiles historic and recent records to assess the status of the [[Razorback Sucker]] population in [[Lake Mead]]. The author concludes that the population is dominated by large, old adults, shows no evidence of recent recruitment, and appears to have declined substantially since the mid-1960s. Only a small number of adults have been captured since 1990, concentrated at [[Blackbird Point]] and [[Echo Bay]]. Recommendations call for expanded survey effort and assessment of rearing and stocking options.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and morphology:** The razorback sucker is one of four endangered native fish species endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], alongside the humpback chub, bonytail chub and Colorado pikeminnow. It is one of three catostomid species with an abruptly rising dorsal keel immediately behind the occiput.
- **Historic distribution:** Historically common and widespread in warm-water portions of the basin, from the upper basin (Green and Virgin rivers) to Mexico, including the Gila and Salt rivers in Arizona. It was probably most common in the lower basin and relatively rare in turbid canyon reaches.
- **Recruitment history:** Significant collections of juveniles or young-of-the-year in the lower basin have been extremely limited. [[Marsh And Minckley]] (1989) documented only 24 wild juveniles collected in the lower Colorado River basin from 1962 to 1988, nearly all below Hoover Dam and most in side channels or artificial waterways.
- **Age and growth:** Assessment is difficult because wild-caught sub-adults are absent and growth beyond about year five is extremely slow. Based on [[McCarthy And Minckley]] (1987), about 27% of standard length (SL) is reached in year one and about 50% by year five. Otolith sectioning was the most reliable (though destructive) aging method.
- **Genetics:** [[Mitochondrial Dna]] analysis (Dowling et al., 1993) found the [[Lake Mohave]] population has extremely high haplotype diversity, well above captive hatchery stocks. The Lake Mead population approaches Lake Mohave levels, while upper basin stocks show reduced variability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning ecology.** In remaining riverine habitats, spawning adults prefer mid-channel areas with gravel, sand or small cobble, and spawning has generally occurred during ascending and peak spring flows. In lower basin reservoirs, spawning occurs over gravel to cobble substrates on gently sloping offshore areas and sheltered coves. Spawning behavior is observed from November to June, with peak activity usually January to March at 10–12 °C or 10–15 °C during warming. Peak spawning in Lake Mead is probably two to four weeks later than in Lake Mohave, based on thermal regimes (Burke, pers. comm.).

**Larval survival and predation.** Larval fish were not recorded in Lake Mead, and larval razorbacks made up only 0.56% of total larval fish collections in Lake Havasu (37 individuals). Larvae in Lake Mohave can be found up to about 20 mm, but survival beyond that size in open-water reservoir habitat does not appear to occur. Predation by carp, catfish and odonate naiads is implicated. Carp were observed feeding on spawning aggregations in Lakes Mead and Mohave.

**Movement and habitat.** Adults show strong fidelity to specific spawning sites. Movement and habitat use in large reservoirs outside the November to June period are essentially unknown, though some historic observations suggest dispersal into pelagic areas.

**Hydrologic drivers.** Three mid-1960s events altered basin hydrology: closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963, which changed Colorado River flow and temperature regimes, the filling of [[Lake Powell]] (which lowered Lake Mead storage and exposed previously submerged spawning habitat), and permanent modification of seasonal flow patterns.

**Sampling bias.** Historic records are largely incidental to sport fish surveys. Gill nets are poorly suited to adult razorbacks, which are difficult to capture in static gear, and sampling in shallow littoral zones rarely overlapped the spring spawning period.

## Key Findings

**Historic distribution and collections (1952–1983)**
- Jonez and Sumner (1954) reported razorbacks as widespread and abundant in Lake Mead in the early 1950s, concentrated in inflow areas and along gravel shores during spawning. NDOW 1950s field notes mention sightings of "hundreds" between Las Vegas Wash and Black Island and between Overton Beach and the Muddy River mouth.
- NDOW field records document 44 adult razorbacks at 23 locations from 1952 to 1980 (mean TL about 569 mm, mean SL about 442 mm), with most collections in the Overton Arm and Cregg's Basin/Grand Wash.
- Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) records add 36 contacts (Table 2), including records from 1967 to 1979.
- In 1962, eight adults were reported killed by deepwater detonations in Boulder Basin.
- No records exist for 1980–1990, and no documented larval or juvenile fish were found between 1969 and 1989.

**Recent status (1990–1993)**
- In April 1990, NDOW observed an apparent spawning aggregation of about 15 adults at Blackbird Point, Las Vegas Wash. Three adults were electrofished, tagged and released.
- Intensive targeted sampling from 1990 yielded 50 adult captures (Table 3; mean TL 593 mm, range 467–765 mm; mean SL 465 mm, range 366–599 mm), with 38 captured at Blackbird Point and Echo Bay sites.
- 21 adults were captured in 1993 (11 at Blackbird Point, 10 at Echo Bay), all in trammel nets in February and April.
- Since 1992 adults have been marked with PIT tags. As of 1993, 44 adults had been tagged, and six recaptures were documented.
- Searches by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1991), U.S. Bureau of Reclamation helicopter overflights (1992) and AGFD surveys found no razorbacks. No juvenile or larval razorbacks were found through February 1994.

**Interpretation**
- The existing population appears to be composed almost entirely of large adults; estimated ages of captured fish suggest a probable age range of roughly 20–30 years at the time of impoundment for the original cohort.
- A general decline in anecdotal adult observations after the mid-1960s, combined with a lack of recruitment, indicates probable loss of the original cohort in the 1970s and 1980s.
- Infrequent but consistent adult captures from the late 1960s through 1993 suggest a second, younger cohort may have been present, though the author notes that a survival period over 55 years would be unrealistic. Reservoir recruitment or immigration from upstream riverine habitat remains possible.
- Current occupancy appears limited to a small area of the Overton Arm, possibly indicating restricted distribution.

**Recommendations**
- Expand survey and inventory work using trammel nets and electrofishing, particularly in areas where presence has not recently been documented.
- Continue efforts to validate spawning success and evaluate threats to larval survival.
- Monitor adult movement and habitat use outside littoral zones and outside the spawning period.
- Consider on-site supplemental rearing and stocking of survivable-sized, genetically suitable juveniles to augment the adult population.

**Limitations.** The report excludes spring 1994 survey data, and no formal statistical analysis of size-frequency trends was completed. Its author describes it primarily as an in-house document.
