# Oncolites Forming on Snails (Goniobasis)

#malacology #paleontology #pleuroceridae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Malcolm P. Weiss]] (Northern Illinois University) tests whether calcareous [[Algal Laminite]] crusts kill living freshwater snails of the genus *Goniobasis*. The author concludes they do not. Crusts are restricted mostly to the outer spire of living snails, and the snails continue to grow new shell over them. Thicker crusts that cover the whole shell and plug the aperture are interpreted as post-mortem accumulations. The study connects a modern stream occurrence in Kentucky with Tertiary lacustrine oncolites from Utah, which the author previously described.

## Background & Description

*Goniobasis* snails are freshwater gastropods of the family [[Pleuroceridae]]. Oncolites, which are concentric accumulations of algal carbonate around a nucleus, had previously been reported on shells of *Goniobasis tenera* in Tertiary lacustrine limestones of Utah (Weiss, 1969). Algal carbonate layers were also reported on living and dead modern *Goniobasis* in a Kentucky stream (Minckley, 1962, unpublished). Edwards & Heywood (1960) described algal carbonate crusts on the aquatic snail *Potamopyrgus*, but they did not address effects on the snails. Minckley (1962) considered such crusts lethal, and this paper was written to test that view.

Bickel (1968) identified the Kentucky material as *Goniobasis curreyana lyoni* and reached a conclusion similar to the author's about snail survival.

The Utah specimens differ from the modern ones. In Utah the oldest lamina always enveloped the whole shell, whereas in Kentucky laminae were often confined to the spire.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site:** Doe Run issues from a spring in Mississippian limestone in Meade County, Kentucky. Minckley (1962) studied its ecology in detail and reported algal marl crusts on *Goniobasis* at four of five study stations. Samples were collected June 10, 1967, at "Station III," a shallow riffle about 100 feet upstream of a highway culvert, with a second collection from an adjacent quiet pool 30–40 feet upstream.

**Riffle habitat:** Water was up to about one inch deep over a spongy gravel pack. Living *Goniobasis* numbered about 50 per square foot, each with a greenish-black velvety algal coat on the older whorls.

**Quiet-water habitat:** The reach above the riffle was a few inches to one foot deep, with a mud bottom rich in organic debris. Live snails with soft rusty algal coats numbered perhaps six per square foot and grazed mainly on rocks and sticks. Dead snails were very rare. Eighteen live specimens were collected here, and their carbonate coats were conspicuously thinner than those from the riffle.

**Algal communities:** The carbonate layers were formed by mats of *Phormidium* and *Gongrosira* at this locality. X-ray analysis showed calcite with traces of quartz.

## Key Findings

**Sediment composition of the riffle surface**
- Weight ratio of pebbles : granules : sand was 2.1 : 1 : 1.8.
- Of 592 pebbles, 15% were rock fragments without algal laminae, 13% were irregular algal carbonate clots, 9% were rock or organic detritus overlain by concentric laminae, and 63% were snails overlain wholly or partly by laminae.
- Thus about 72% of the gravel consisted of oncolites, although the carbonate was still light and porous.

**Snail nuclei**
- Of 371 specimens with snail nuclei, all but 14 (three other species) were *Goniobasis*.
- About 60% had a thin coat, typically one layer, that did not change the conical shell profile. Others were heavily coated, and some approached a cylindrical form.

**Distribution of laminae**
- Single laminae were confined mostly to older whorls. Multiple laminae could sheath shells nearly to the aperture, and apertural margins remained clean.
- Nearly all dead snails carried carbonate coats. Some were thin and others thick. Worn or corroded shells were uncoated.
- Dead-shell coats took three forms: caps on the spire only, coats on the lip and aperture, or full coverage that plugged the aperture.

**Evidence that snails survive crusting**
- Remnants of algal laminae are enclosed beneath younger whorls in both living and dead specimens (Text-Figs. 1, 2, 4–6). This shows the snails lived and deposited new shell over the crusts.
- Full-coat laminae that plug apertures overlap incomplete layers and have older remnants between whorls, which indicates post-mortem growth.
- Dead snails with little carbonate and living snails with thick coats coexist. Laminae can also be lost through abrasion, solution, or other snails.
- Living and dead snails share similar modal lengths and whorl numbers. This suggests that snails grow to a genetically controlled size and then die.
- Some snails of that modal stage have overgrown crusts. The author proposes that snails may avoid suffocation by nibbling algae at the shell margin or by an unspecified biochemical process, and this ability could operate at any life stage.

**Specimen preparation**
- Shells were stabilized with low-viscosity epoxy (Shell Epon 815) before median sectioning. Some bubbles and reflections resulted.
- Figured specimens are deposited at the Orton Museum, Department of Geology, Ohio State University (OSU 20593–20599, plus OSU 21184–21197 from Weiss, 1969).

**Conclusions**
- Algal carbonate accumulation on living snails is an early stage in some oncolite formation. The oldest lamina is always confined to the spire exterior in these cases.
- Algal crusts do not kill the snails. Coils of new shell may cover them.
- Continued accumulation after death can fill and cover the aperture.
- The earlier conclusion that Paleocene and Eocene Utah oncolites formed mainly after the death of the mollusk nuclei is supported.
- One problem remains open. Oncolites formed on dead shells in a Tertiary lake and on living and dead shells in a modern stream. The author expects living snails with laminae to be found in a lake, and suspects that the Utah examples were lacustrine rather than fluvial in origin.
