## Executive Summary
This short communication describes the origin and developmental pattern of scale formation in the **Flier** (*Centrarchus macropterus*), based on 181 specimens (13–43 mm total length) collected from Missouri wetlands in May 1965. The study found that scale development in the Flier follows a distinct pattern compared to other centrarchids: scales originate at the midlateral body region beneath the junction of the spiny and soft dorsal fin rays, rather than at the caudal peduncle as seen in crappies and bass. Mean length at scale initiation was 15.8 mm, and fish became fully scaled at an average length of 31 mm.

## Background & Description
The paper situates the Flier within a comparative context of scale development studies across the [[Centrarchidae]] family and the related [[Esocidae]] family:

- **[[White Crappie]]** (Siefert, 1965) and **[[Black Crappie]]** (Ward and Leonard, 1952): Scales originate along the lateral line at the **posterior end of the caudal peduncle**, then develop rapidly anteriorly toward the operculum, and more slowly dorsally/ventrally.
- **[[Smallmouth Bass]]** (Everhart, 1949): Follows the same caudal-peduncle-origin pattern as the crappies.
- **[[Bluegill]]** (Potter, 1924): Scales appear scattered over the anterior body region (origin pattern not fully described).
- **[[Northern Pike]]** (Franklin and Smith, 1960; family Esocidae): Scales originate near the midbody region **below** the lateral line, then above it, developing anteriorly and posteriorly in parallel rows.

The Flier's pattern is explicitly noted as "quite different" from these other centrarchids, originating not at the caudal peduncle but at a specific midlateral landmark tied to dorsal fin morphology.

### Methodology
- **Sample:** 181 fish collected May 1965 from [[Mingo National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Duck Creek Wildlife Area]] in Southeast Missouri.
- **Size range:** 13–43 mm total length.
- **Preparation:** Preserved in 5% formalin, cleared in 1% potassium hydroxide, and stained with alizarin red (standard clearing-and-staining technique for skeletal/scale visualization).
- **Sub-sample:** 46 fish specifically used to determine length at scale formation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- The species was sampled from freshwater wetland habitats characteristic of the Mississippi Alluvial Plain region (bottomland swamp/wildlife refuge systems in Southeast Missouri).
- This study is part of a broader research effort by the **Missouri Cooperative Fishery Unit**, involving collaboration between the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Missouri Department of Conservation, the Edward K. Love Foundation, and the University of Missouri, conducted at the **Gaylord Memorial Laboratory**.
- Comparative scale ontogeny research such as this contributes to understanding early life-history traits and can serve as an aid in age/growth back-calculation studies and larval-to-juvenile developmental staging in [[Centrarchidae]].

## Key Findings

### Length at Scale Formation
- Fish **<15 mm**: no scales present (7 fish examined).
- Fish **15 mm**: 6 of 7 without scales; 1 with scales (transitional size).
- Fish **16 mm**: 5 of 6 with scales; 1 without.
- Fish **>16 mm**: all fish had scales.
- **Mean length at scale formation: 15.8 mm** — slightly less than reported for other centrarchids.

### Pattern of Scale Development
1. **Origin point:** Scales first appear on the **midlateral region** of the body, along the **horizontal skeletogenous septum**, directly below the union of the spiny-rayed and soft-rayed portions of the dorsal fin.
2. **Initial spread:** A single row of scales develops both anteriorly and posteriorly from this origin point, progressing **more rapidly toward the tail**.
3. **At 17 mm:** The single scale row extends from just behind the operculum to the end of the caudal peduncle.
4. **After 18 mm:** Scales continue developing anteriorly; growth is **faster below the lateral line** (toward the breast) than above it (toward the nape).
5. **At 21 mm:** Only the nape region remains unscaled.
6. **At 23 mm:** Body is completely scaled (trunk region).
7. **Head/suborbital region:** Scales develop slowly here — this is the last region to be fully scaled.
8. **Completion of scalation:**
   - First fully-scaled fish observed at **30 mm**.
   - Largest incompletely-scaled fish observed at **33 mm**.
   - **Average length at complete scalation: 31 mm.**

### Comparative Significance
The Flier's scale development pattern — originating at a **middorsal/midlateral fin-associated landmark** rather than the caudal peduncle — distinguishes it from the [[White Crappie]], [[Black Crappie]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]], and also differs from the [[Northern Pike]] pattern of parallel-row development from a midbody sub-lateral-line origin. This suggests interspecific variability in squamation ontogeny even within the same family, highlighting the taxonomic and developmental diversity within [[Centrarchidae]].
