# Declining Southwestern Aquatic Habitats and Fishes: Are They Sustainable?

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #poeciliidae #habitat-restoration #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This paper examines the dramatic decline of aquatic habitats and native fishes in the arid American Southwest over the past half century. Physical habitat changes from dams, diversions, channelization, and stream drying, combined with extensive introduction of non-native fish species, have severely impacted native fish fauna through hybridization, competition, and predation. The author reviews conservation efforts and addresses whether aquatic habitats and native fishes can be sustained in the region. Approximately 60% of native southwestern fishes are listed as threatened, endangered, or of special concern. The paper argues that sustaining these resources requires securing habitats, changing species and land management strategies, and continued research on native species biology and habitat requirements.

## Background & Description

The native fish fauna of [[Arizona]] and the arid American Southwest is depauperate compared to eastern drainages, with only about 30 taxa recorded in Arizona waters since the late 1800s. Several species are now extinct. Endemism is high and specialization is the rule; in the [[Gila River]], almost one-third (5 of 17 species) of native species belong to monotypic genera. Since the turn of the century, Arizona's fish fauna has nearly tripled through widespread intentional and accidental introduction of non-native fishes, primarily for sport, bait, or biological control. At least 29 non-native recreational fish species were introduced between 1900 and 1971, with species like [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and various sunfishes becoming established.

The [[Salt River]] exemplifies habitat transformation. Roosevelt Dam, the first U.S. Bureau of Reclamation dam, was completed in 1911, followed by three more dams within 25 years, effectively drying the Salt River at Tempe. In the late 1800s, more than a dozen native fishes inhabited these waters; presently, only introduced species persist in artificial habitats created by gravel mining and sewage effluent. The [[Gila River]] was similarly dried following completion of Coolidge Dam. Historically navigable with extensive marshy areas and oxbow lakes, the Gila once supported abundant [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]], with [[Colorado Squawfish]] running upstream over 200 km to spawn.

The [[Colorado River]] underwent complete hydrologic transformation beginning with Boulder Dam closure in 1935, followed by successive dams (Parker, Davis, Glen Canyon) that controlled and altered habitat for four large native cypriniform fishes: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. By comparison, 80% of mainstream river habitats in Arizona have been physically and chemically altered or completely lost through drying.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Upper elevation forested areas of central Arizona and the White Mountains sustained extensive timber harvest and grazing. The 1960 Multiple Use and Sustained Yield Act and subsequent environmental legislation emphasized interconnected land uses across landscapes. The concept of watershed/ecosystem-based land management developed in the late 1980s, recognizing the intimate relationship between watershed use and riparian-stream system functioning. Extensive literature in the 1980s documented grazing effects on stream habitats and fishes, with the Clean Water Act placing increased scrutiny on water quality from watersheds supporting native fishes.

Streams like [[Aravaipa Creek]] in southeast Arizona that remain generally unmodified by dams or diversions effectively sustain native fish fauna. Although non-natives are accidentally introduced from stock tanks during storms, subsequent floods appear to remove introduced fishes while sustaining native species, demonstrating the importance of natural flow regimes.

The [[Desert Fishes Council]], founded in November 1969 with fewer than 40 individuals, now numbers over 300 members and addresses conservation of desert fishes and their habitats throughout the western United States and northern Mexico. This organization emerged from efforts to prevent loss of rare desert fish species due to aquifer pumping and spring drying in the Ash Meadows system.

## Key Findings

**Hybridization Impacts**: [[Apache Trout]] has been reduced to less than 5% of its former range, primarily through hybridization with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]]. Fewer than a dozen of over 40 streams examined in the White Mountains between 1977 and 1982 contained Apache trout populations that had not hybridized with other salmonids. Recent genetic analysis by Carmichael et al. (1993) presents a bleak picture for sustainability of this native trout. However, Dowling and Childs (1992) reported that hybridization may be regulated and dampened maternally by the native trout.

**Competition Effects**: In Ord Creek, Fort Apache Indian Reservation, [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] replaced native Apache trout despite attempted stream restorations with fish toxin in 1977 and 1981. Brook trout comprised 85% of total number and 78% of total biomass of adult trout in 1977. Laboratory experiments demonstrated that adult brook trout are more aggressive than Apache trout, potentially interfering with feeding and spawning success.

The [[Red Shiner]], accidentally introduced by the bait industry and intentionally as forage, has become widespread in southwestern aquatic habitats. This habitat generalist has an inverse distribution pattern with two federally threatened cyprinid species, [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. Rinne (1991) suggested that utilization of the same physical habitat in the Verde River by adult red shiner and juvenile spikedace may represent one mode of competition. The red shiner has also increased in lowermost reaches of the Virgin River while the endangered [[Woundfin]] has decreased.

**Predation Impacts**: Blinn et al. (1993) demonstrated that rainbow trout have significant impact on the native [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] through effective predation on small (< 50 mm) individuals. Inverse, linear, and site-specific distributional patterns in the field indicate negative impacts of introduced trout on native cyprinids.

[[Green Sunfish]] appears to contribute strongly to replacement of native [[Gila Chub]], presumably via predation. The sunfish is abundant in downstream reaches of Sycamore Creek, Prescott National Forest, where the chub is totally absent, while the chub is abundant in headwaters where sunfish are absent.

The drastic decline of [[Razorback Sucker]] in the lower Colorado River has been attributed partly to predation on ova, larvae, and fry by catfish. Marsh and Brooks (1989) estimated that up to 900 juvenile razorback suckers (45-168 mm SL) were eaten by flathead and channel catfish in a 2-day period in the Gila River. Over 10 million razorback suckers were stocked in Gila River Basin waters during the 1980s; fewer than 120 have been recaptured, mostly within two weeks of stocking.

The [[Mosquitofish]], introduced worldwide as biological control for mosquito larvae, has been implicated as responsible for drastic reduction of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]]. Studies by Shoenherr (1974) and Meffe (1985) demonstrated that mosquitofish effectively eliminates topminnow primarily through predation on fry and, secondarily, by reducing survivorship of adult females. Meffe (1984) demonstrated that coexistence may depend on habitat complexity, with flood disturbance permitting persistence of topminnow in presence of mosquitofish.

**Conservation Strategies**: Successful conservation requires: (1) securing habitats through federal, state, and private conservation lands and special management areas; (2) moving from single-species to ecosystem-based management approaches; and (3) adopting multi-agency, multi-disciplinary approaches that address total diversity rather than individual species.

Acquisition of large, important habitats such as the San Bernardino Ranch (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service), White Mountain Hereford Ranch (Arizona Game and Fish Department), and numerous riparian-stream areas (The Nature Conservancy) has been critical. Recent land exchanges by the U.S. Forest Service on Nutrioso Creek and purchase of private lands along the Little Colorado River represent prerequisite actions for sustaining native fishes.

**Management Challenges**: Past conservation efforts were often necessarily crisis-oriented and monospecific. Chemical renovation attempts have shown mixed success; renovation of Ord Creek to recover Apache trout failed, with brook trout continuing to dominate. A recent chemical renovation of the Virgin River to remove red shiner and restore woundfin resulted in treatment of excessive river reaches and negative impacts on other native species.

The U.S. Forest Service Southwestern Region (Arizona and New Mexico) lists more than 230 sensitive vertebrate species, 15 of which are fishes. Management must move from "flagship" species philosophy toward proactive conservation of candidate, vulnerable, restricted, sensitive, or special concern species before they require federal listing.

**Sustainability Assessment**: Great strides have been made in understanding conservation issues since enactment of the Endangered Species Act. Minimal success in conserving native fishes has been largely attained at species level; genetic and ecosystem levels are only now emerging as important components. A landscape biodiversity approach has the least probability of being realized because of the often disjunct and isolated nature of aquatic ecosystems interposed with continued development of arid regions.

The paper concludes that sustaining aquatic habitats and native fish biodiversity for perpetuity requires giving priority to overall aquatic and influencing resources in conservation efforts. Only through innovative approaches that move from "saving" to "recovering" and sustaining native fishes in their native habitats can this unique regional fauna be maintained.
