# Lower Colorado River Biological Assessment (1995 Update)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1995 public update and overview from the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]], Lower Colorado Region, describes a [[Biological Assessment]] of routine operations and maintenance on the mainstem [[Lower Colorado River]]. The assessment is required under [[Section 7 Consultation]] of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It was prepared at the suggestion of the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] after most of the lower river was designated [[Critical Habitat]] for the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]].

Reclamation held six meetings and workshops in Arizona, California and Nevada and received over one hundred oral comments plus written submissions. The document summarizes those comments by category and gives Reclamation's responses. It also outlines the baseline definition, the species list, the scope and geographic boundaries, the possible assessment outcomes, and the path to formal consultation.

## Background & Description

**Legal and institutional framework.** A biological assessment determines whether a proposed federal action is likely to adversely affect listed or proposed species or designated critical habitat. Its outcome decides whether formal consultation is needed. Reclamation acts as custodian of the lower basin for the Secretary of the Interior, who serves as water master. The operation of the river is governed by "[[Law Of The River]]," a set of laws, compacts, contracts and treaties (Table 1 of the source lists about two dozen instruments, from the 1899 River and Harbor Act to the 1970 Long Range Operating Criteria and International Boundary and Water Commission Minutes 218, 241 and 242).

**Basin and infrastructure.** The Colorado River drains approximately 250,000 square miles and has an average annual water supply of 14.5 million acre-feet. [[Lee Ferry]], 16 miles below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], marks the boundary between the upper and lower basins. The river below [[Davis Dam]] extends 260 miles to the Mexican border and is divided into 10 river divisions, with 125 miles of levees. Operations include bankline riprap (about 200,000 tons of rock and gravel annually), levee armoring, quarrying at 7 active sites, dredging, and channelization. Flows are monitored at 7 gaging stations.

**Species list.** Table 2 lists 8 endangered species (5 avian, 3 fish) and 27 Category 2 candidates (10 bats, 2 rodents, 6 birds, 1 amphibian, 3 reptiles, 2 plants, 2 insects, 1 mollusc).
- Endangered: [[American Peregrine Falcon]], [[Bald Eagle]], [[Brown Pelican]], [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], Razorback Sucker, Bonytail Chub, [[Colorado Squawfish]].
- Category 2 examples: [[Desert Tortoise]] (Sonoran population), Arizona toad, Yuma myotis, and California floater.
- The [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and some plants near quarry sites were added at stakeholder request.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The source describes a historical ecosystem driven by the seasonal hydrograph, now substantially altered.

**Floodplain communities.** The first terrace flooded seasonally, and vegetation trapped sediment. Seasonal flooding kept soil salinity in check and supported cottonwood-willow riparian communities. Today, flooding is of extended duration and destroys vegetation that cannot tolerate long inundation. Lack of seasonal flooding is cited as the main driver of change. [[Salt Cedar]] was introduced in the 1920s and now dominates many areas, and cottonwood-willow stands have shifted toward salt cedar and screwbean mesquite. Avian, mammal and herptile species tied to riparian habitat have declined.

**Marsh communities.** These are dominated by bulrush, cattails and cane. They have generally increased with silting of backwaters, sediment buildup behind dams and levee construction. Marsh-associated species such as the clapper rail have increased.

**Aquatic communities.** Historically, flows ranged from 12 to 250,000 cfs at Yuma. The main channel was turbid, with an unstable fine-sediment bed and mainly detrital food webs. Off-channel areas held most aquatic diversity and served as nursery habitat. Backwaters were often anoxic in summer, and water temperatures often exceeded 30°C. Historically the fish community was depauperate, with nine species. Today the main channel is more productive, the substrate is armored, and roughly 24 non-native fish species have been introduced. Dams have altered temperatures in several reaches, favoring salmonid fisheries over native fishes. [[Sport Fish]] and non-game exotic predators are considered a major threat to natives, and only a few native fishes remain, all endangered.

**Management and operations.** Daily and hourly release schedules are set through a 24-month study updated monthly, and power demand, downstream orders and target lake elevations are combined. Target elevations for [[Lake Mohave]] follow a priority order: flood control, razorback sucker requirement, conservation, irrigation and domestic use, recreation, then power generation. [[Hoover Dam]] absorbs most daily fluctuations because it releases directly into a lake, whereas [[Parker Dam]] releases are flatter. Increasing salinity from irrigation, reservoir evaporation and depletions is a concern.

## Key Findings

- **Baseline:** Reclamation will describe historical events, including exotic fish introductions and dam construction, but the assessment baseline is its ongoing, recurring operations and their effects on listed species and critical habitat. The Fish and Wildlife Service accepted this interpretation without objection at a meeting on February 6, 1995.
- **Scope:** The assessment covers the mainstem from Lake Mead to the Southerly International Boundary (extended from the Northerly Boundary after public input). It excludes the Colorado River through Mexico to the Sea of Cortez and other contiguous systems such as the lower Gila River. The analysis covers the current floodway, plus quarrying.
- **Yuma Desalting Plant:** Excluded because it is not operating. Separate compliance will be needed if it starts operating.
- **Data limits:** Existing and historical data are limited, especially on habitat below the main reservoirs, so the assessment may be more programmatic than site-specific. Reclamation asked reviewers to identify missing data.
- **Stressors:** The assessment will consider predation by introduced fish, birds (possibly enhanced by clearer water), water quality, temperature, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, and interactions among operations and native and non-native fish. Water quality and contaminants will be treated in relation to the species analyzed.
- **Possible outcomes:** The assessment may find no effect on the species or habitat, no likely adverse effect, or a possible effect. Any further study depends on this result. If an effect is found, formal consultation begins, and the Fish and Wildlife Service may recommend Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives (RPAs) to avoid jeopardy or adverse modification. The RPAs published with the proposed critical habitat are only possibilities.
- **Cost and economic concerns:** Reclamation says costs would be shared proportionally among stakeholders who benefit from operations. Riverside entities voiced concern about economic and property impacts. Some proposed RPAs, such as seasonal flood-plain flooding, were considered infeasible because they would require breaching or pumping behind levees.
- **Experimental population:** A proposal to introduce a non-essential, experimental [[Colorado Squawfish]] population above [[Imperial Dam]] was under review. Reclamation included the squawfish on the species list while the proposal's status was being checked.
- **Schedule:** The time frame is short. Reclamation will use available information and commits to periodic progress mailings and meetings with interested groups.
- **Conservation:** The document recommends species-specific and ecosystem-wide initiatives, avoidance of single-species focus, and cooperation among agencies and states.
