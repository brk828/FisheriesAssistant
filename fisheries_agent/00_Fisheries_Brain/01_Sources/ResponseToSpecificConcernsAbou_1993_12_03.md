# Response to Specific Concerns About Status of GCES Research Data

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #GrandCanyon #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary

This 1993 memorandum from Owen T. Gorman (FWS GCES Project Coordinator) to Dr. W.L. Minckley (GCES Science Advisor) reports on the status of fish and habitat research data collected under the **Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES)** program in the Grand Canyon ecosystem. Two primary data streams are described: (1) [[Arizona Fishery Resources Office]] (AZFRO) studies focused on the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), and (2) subcontracted [[Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit]] (ACFWRU) studies of smaller Grand Canyon tributaries (Paria River, Bright Angel, Deer, Tapeats, Shinumo, Kanab, and Havasu creeks). The document catalogs data entry/archival status, summarizes preliminary findings on habitat partitioning among native fishes — particularly the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] — and identifies critical knowledge gaps, especially regarding spawning habitat identification and the ecological impact of exotic trout species on native fish assemblages.

## Background & Description

The source is a data-status/administrative memo rather than a morphological study; no quantitative morphometric data are presented. However, taxonomic identifications relevant to habitat and ecological analyses include:

- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) — endangered native cyprinid; primary conservation focus of GCES.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*) — native cyprinid, exhibits distinct diel activity pattern (more active by day).
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** (*Catostomus latipinnis*) — native catostomid.
- **[[Bluehead Sucker]]** (*Catostomus discobolus*) — native catostomid.
- Exotic species documented in tributary assemblages: **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), **[[Brown Trout]]** (*Salmo trutta*), **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*), **[[Fathead Minnow]]** (*Pimephales promelas*).

Life-stage classes referenced in habitat-use analyses: young-of-year (YOY), juveniles, and adults — treated as ecologically distinct "pseudospecies" units for niche analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat partitioning and diel patterns (LCR, AZFRO studies):**
- Analyses of June 1992–1993 transect and hoopnet data show that [[Humpback Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]], and sucker species (plus their YOY/juvenile "pseudospecies") each exhibit **discrete microhabitat use patterns**, indicating a high degree of niche segregation among co-occurring native fishes.
- Chub and suckers show strong **day-night habitat shifts**: during daylight they occupy a narrow range of habitats with moderate-to-high cover; at night they expand into a wider array of low-to-moderate cover habitats.
- [[Speckled Dace]] are comparatively more active during the day, while [[Humpback Chub]] and suckers are more active at night — suggesting differing predator-avoidance or foraging strategies.
- Short-term local movement and homerange data were collected specifically for *Gila cypha*.

**Water chemistry and distributional limits — [[Atomizer Falls]] study (Mattes 1993):**
- Distribution of chub and suckers terminates at the upper end near **Atomizer Falls** (km 13.5), coincident with sharp changes in habitat structure and water chemistry.
- At base flow, dissolved CO₂ concentrations above the falls exceed **200 mg/L**, approaching or exceeding the physiological tolerance limit for many fish species — a likely mechanistic explanation for the upstream distributional boundary.

**Tributary fish assemblages (ACFWRU studies):**
- Native species characterized across Bright Angel, Shinumo, Deer, Tapeats, Kanab, Havasu creeks, and the Paria River: *Rhinichthys osculus*, *Catostomus latipinnis*, *Catostomus discobolus*.
- Co-occurring exotic species (trout, catfish, fathead minnow) raise unresolved questions about competitive/predatory impacts on natives.
- The [[Paria River]] study (Weiss 1993) specifically addressed population structure and movement of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]].
- The [[Shinumo Creek]] study (Allan 1993) addressed distribution and abundance of the tributary fish community.
- The Bright Angel/Kanab study (Otis 1993) addressed selected ecological aspects of native and introduced fishes.

**Key ecological data gaps identified:**
- Spawning habitat of *Gila cypha* remains unidentified in the LCR — attributed to access restrictions (spring 1992) and anomalously high, sustained spring flows (1993) that precluded standard grid-sampling and reduced catch rates/sample sizes.
- Feasibility of establishing additional humpback chub spawning aggregations in smaller tributaries (Conservation Measure #7) had not yet been analyzed as of the report date.
- Impact of exotic trout on native fishes in tributaries — unaddressed.
- Seasonal changes in tributary stream habitat — unaddressed.
- Life history of resident native and exotic tributary fishes — unaddressed.
- Use of tributary **confluence reaches** by native fishes — unaddressed.
- Havasu Creek data analysis was frozen due to a data-use dispute between the University of Arizona and the [[Havasupai Tribe]], posing a risk to conservation-measure evaluations.

## Key Findings

- **Niche segregation**: Native LCR fishes (chub, dace, two sucker species) and their ontogenetic stages occupy discrete, non-overlapping microhabitats, with pronounced day/night habitat shifts in chub and suckers.
- **Physiological/chemical barrier**: Elevated dissolved CO₂ (>200 mg/L at base flow) above [[Atomizer Falls]] likely constrains the upstream range limit of chub and sucker populations in the LCR.
- **Data infrastructure status**: Most AZFRO-LCR data (1991–1993) were being entered into DBASE files, with full entry expected by March 1994 and archival readiness by mid-1994. ACFWRU tributary data remained in non-standardized formats held by the research unit as of the report date, with transfer expected in early 1994.
- **Thesis-driven analyses**: Detailed tributary analyses were being conducted via four University of Arizona Master's theses (Mattes – LCR/Atomizer Falls; Allan – Shinumo Creek; Otis – Bright Angel/Kanab creeks; Weiss – Paria River flannelmouth sucker).
- **Critical unresolved conservation question**: Inability to locate humpback chub spawning habitat in the LCR under the 1992–1993 flow/access conditions represents the most significant scientific gap, directly impacting recovery planning (Conservation Measure #7 feasibility analysis was still pending).
- **Institutional/administrative risk**: A legal/data-access dispute involving the Havasupai Tribe froze analysis of Havasu Creek data, threatening timely completion of conservation-measure assessments.
- **Recommended remedy**: The report recommends an integrated sampling program between FWS and Arizona State University (ASU) to overcome sampling limitations imposed by variable spring flow conditions in the LCR.
