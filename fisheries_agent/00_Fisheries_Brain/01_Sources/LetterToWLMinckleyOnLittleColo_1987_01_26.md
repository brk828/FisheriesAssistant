## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary research paper but a set of administrative correspondence and a draft strategic planning document concerning the [[Little Colorado River Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*), a small cyprinid fish endemic to the Little Colorado River drainage in Arizona. The letter from Dean Hendrickson (Arizona Game & Fish Department, Nongame Fish Biologist) to Dr. W. L. Minckley, chairman of the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]], reports on a January 1987 meeting convened by the Coconino National Forest to establish a "Little Colorado River Spinedace Committee" and associated strategic plan. The plan aims to link available Threatened and Endangered (T/E) species funding with riparian and aquatic habitat improvement projects in the [[East Clear Creek]] system, raising concerns among fisheries scientists about premature habitat manipulation and fish translocation absent sufficient baseline biological data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** Riparian and aquatic stream habitat within the [[Little Colorado River]] watershed, specifically the [[East Clear Creek]] system and its tributaries on the [[Coconino National Forest]].
- **Distribution:** Endemic to portions of the Little Colorado River drainage in Arizona; occurs in some streams but is notably absent from others where habitat "improvement" structures had already been installed.
- **Co-occurring species/conflicts:** Some streams within the watershed currently harbor trout populations; a proposal was discussed to renovate trout-occupied streams for spinedace reintroduction, indicating potential interspecific competitive or displacement dynamics between spinedace and non-native/game trout species.
- **Habitat status concerns:** Concept of transplanting spinedace into currently fishless streams was raised, implying suitable unoccupied habitat may exist within the broader watershed.
- **Threats/management context:** Riparian degradation and stream habitat alteration are implicit drivers behind the Forest Service's interest in habitat improvement funding, though the letter notes that structural improvements had already been placed in streams lacking spinedace — raising questions about whether such projects were ecologically well-targeted.

## Key Findings

- The species was under consideration for federal listing as **Threatened** at the time of writing (1986–1987) and was already classified as **Group III** by Arizona state authorities and as a **Forest Service Sensitive Species**.
- The Coconino National Forest secured funding ($13,000 initial, with potential for an additional $10,000 in "challenge money" contingent on raising $5,000 in non-federal matching funds) for riparian/stream habitat work in FY87, with a much larger allocation ($31,000 + challenge funding) projected for the following year.
- A "Little Colorado River Spinedace Committee" was formed, comprising biologists and hydrologists from the [[Coconino National Forest]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and [[Northern Arizona University]].
- The committee's draft strategic plan proposed:
  - Inventorying existing riparian/aquatic habitat and spinedace distribution and life history.
  - Identifying unoccupied habitat suitable for potential spinedace introductions.
  - Establishing management goals tied to the Forest Plan and species recovery objectives.
  - Developing habitat guidelines and a limiting-factors analysis for the species.
  - Producing a prioritized survey, habitat improvement, and monitoring plan.
- Stream surveys were planned for spring 1987 using the Forest Service's **G.A.W.S. (Guidelines for Aquatic Wildlife Surveys)** methodology, with a request made to include shocking (electrofishing) and specimen preservation as part of the inventory protocol.
- Dean Hendrickson expressed professional reservations at the meeting, arguing that translocation and habitat "improvement" projects might be **premature** absent adequate baseline studies of spinedace habitat requirements and biology — advocating instead for research prior to intervention.
- The correspondence highlights a recurring tension in endangered species management between **funding-driven habitat action** (to meet T/E quotas and secure "challenge money") and the **scientific need for foundational ecological data** before manipulating habitat or fish distributions.
- The [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]], chaired by W. L. Minckley, was identified as the appropriate coordinating body for oversight of the species' management given its jurisdiction over desert fish recovery efforts, and Greg Goodwin (Coconino NF Wildlife Biologist) was named as the primary Forest Service contact for coordination.
