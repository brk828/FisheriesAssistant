# Aquatic Ecology Studies of Twin Lakes, Colorado (1971–86): Effects of a Pumped-Storage Hydroelectric Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation Engineering and Science Monograph (No. 43, May 1993) reports 15 years of cooperative limnology and fishery studies (1971–86) on [[Twin Lakes]], a pair of dimictic, oligotrophic, glacially formed montane lakes in central Colorado. The studies assessed the effects of the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]] and the new Twin Lakes Dam, completed in October 1983, which raised maximum water surface elevation by 2 m and converted two connected lakes into a single reservoir with two distinct basins. Work was led by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], with the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] studying the sport fishery and the [[Colorado Cooperative Fishery and Wildlife Research Unit]] at Colorado State University conducting specialized studies.

Key conclusions: raised lake levels altered morphometry and stratification; a trophic upsurge followed the lake-level rise; zooplankton and Mysis populations declined in the postoperational period; and the [[Lake Trout]] fishery, a primary management concern, cannot yet be assessed for chronic impacts because studies ended before a new equilibrium was reached.

## Background & Description

**Study setting.** Twin Lakes lie at about 39°05′ N, 106°20′ W, 24 km southwest of Leadville, in the Southern Rocky Mountains. The lakes were impounded by terminal and recessional moraines of the Lake Creek Glacier. The Lake Creek watershed covers 238 km², spanning elevations from the summit of [[Mt. Elbert]] (4399 m) to the confluence with the Arkansas River (2578 m). The climate is cool and semiarid; Leadville averages 469.4 mm precipitation and a mean annual temperature of 2.3 °C.

**Morphometry.** Table 1-2 contrasts preproject and present conditions. The upper lake's area rose from 263 ha to 381 ha, while its mean depth fell by 1.1 m because of inundated shallows. The lower lake area changed less than 1%, and its mean depth rose by 0.7 m. Maximum water surface elevation is 2804 m.

**Project features.** The [[Fryingpan-Arkansas Project]] diverts Western Slope water through the Boustead Tunnel and Turquoise Lake to the Mt. Elbert Forebay. The powerplant has two 100 MW pump-turbine units; unit 1 went on-line in September 1981 and unit 2 in August 1984. Operation pumps water from Twin Lakes at night and generates power during peak demand in the day.

**Historical context.** Early work by [[Hayden]] (1876), [[Jordan]] (1889), [[Powell]] (1889–91), and [[Juday]] (1902–03) documented depths, fish fauna and transparency. Juday recorded [[Mackinaw Trout]] (lake trout), which had been introduced after Jordan's 1889 visit, and noted that the yellow-fin trout had become extremely rare. [[Mysis Shrimp]] were introduced into the lower lake in 1957 and were established by 1969.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Mean annual discharge at the Lake Creek gauge was about 0.15 × 10⁹ m³ (121,700 acre-ft) over 1947–84, and additional Mt. Elbert Conduit diversions added 0.16 × 10⁹ m³ (131,000 acre-ft), so inflow could more than double. Transbasin diversions into the Upper Arkansas Basin rose sharply over time. Hydraulic residence time dropped from 1.22 years (1981) to 0.43 years (1982), then stayed near 0.45–0.53 years, as lake enlargement offset increased inflow.

**Currents.** Upper lake surface currents were driven mainly by wind, with a maximum of 840 cm/min. Under-ice gyres were observed in both lakes. Powerplant generation in February 1982 created a large counterclockwise gyre in the lower lake, with under-ice velocities as high as 174 cm/min. Circulation drew planktonic organisms toward the powerplant intake and increased their entrainment vulnerability.

**Water chemistry.** Twin Lakes waters are extremely dilute, dominated by calcium, bicarbonate and sulfate. Lake Creek is the main source of iron, copper and zinc, largely from natural mineral deposits (jarosite) in the South Fork watershed. Upper Twin Lakes experienced a severe winter anoxia event in 1975 that released heavy metals, killing benthos and plankton and delaying primary production recovery for about 2 years. Conductivity mostly stayed below 100 µS/cm.

**Thermal regime and oxygen.** Both lakes remain thermally stratified in summer, with turnover in early October. Powerplant operation weakened summer stratification in the lower lake and delayed spring stratification by 9 to 17 days. Dissolved oxygen generally stayed near saturation, but deep-water oxygen was higher after operation began. Lake trout and Mysis both require dissolved oxygen above about 4 mg/L, so the aeration effect could expand their habitat.

**Sediments.** Sedimentation rate in the lower lake was estimated at 0.63 mm/year (range 0.42–0.83 mm/year) from cesium-137 dating. Upper lake sediments show a sharp rise in the araphidineae/centric diatom ratio (2.5 to 5 above 100 mm depth), consistent with eutrophication linked to mining and other human activity beginning about 100–125 years ago.

**Biota.**
- *Primary production:* Chlorophyll a and carbon fixation were measured monthly; a postoperational decline in algal density was noted.
- *Zooplankton and benthos:* Zooplankton densities decreased significantly after operation began, while benthic biomass increased.
- *Mysis:* Populations decreased after operation and appear to have been harmed by entrainment and higher flushing rates.
- *Fish:* The sport fishery included lake trout, [[Rainbow Trout]] (stocked), and suckers ([[White Sucker]] and [[Longnose Sucker]]). Lake trout recruitment to the spawning population takes 6–8 years.

## Key Findings

- **Morphometry and stratification:** Raised lake levels changed the upper lake's morphometry, altering summer stratification there, while powerplant discharge affected stratification in the lower lake.
- **Trophic upsurge:** A trophic upsurge followed the rise in lake levels and may have masked or offset declines from increased turbidity and faster flushing of the lower lake.
- **Phytoplankton and algae:** Species composition shifted and algal density decreased in the postoperational period. Postoperational Secchi depths in Upper Twin Lakes fell from roughly 6 m (1978, 1980) to under 4 m (1982–86).
- **Zooplankton and benthos:** Zooplankton densities decreased significantly, while benthic biomass increased.
- **Mysis:** Populations decreased, and 1989 trawls suggested harm from powerplant entrainment and increased flushing.
- **Winterkill risk:** A greater risk of winterkill may occur if the powerplant does not operate during severe winter months.
- **Lake trout habitat:** Habitat availability may have shifted from the lower to the upper lake after operation began.
- **Water chemistry:** Lower-lake dilution from imported Turquoise Lake water reduced conductivity and hardness. Nitrate and total phosphorus rose in both lakes after inundation, though the lakes remained oligotrophic and phosphorus-limited (TP below 10 µg/L).
- **Metals:** Manganese bottom concentrations in Upper Twin Lakes decreased significantly after operation. Lake Creek showed evidence of chronic copper toxicity.
- **Management conclusion:** Studies ended before a new equilibrium was reached, so chronic impacts on lake trout are unknown. The lakes can still be managed as high-quality sport fisheries, but further study of longer-lived species is needed.
