# Environmental Assessment: Critical Habitat Designation for Spikedace and Loach Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #critical-habitat #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Environmental Assessment evaluates the designation of critical habitat for two threatened cyprinid fishes, [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), endemic to the [[Gila River]] basin of Arizona and New Mexico. The document was prepared by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in December 1999 following a federal court order requiring critical habitat designation by February 17, 2000. Previous critical habitat designations from 1994 were set aside due to failure to conduct NEPA analysis. The proposed action designates 1,443 km (894 miles) of stream channels organized into seven geographic complexes, including both occupied and unoccupied areas essential for species recovery. Both species have declined to 10-20% of their historic range due to habitat destruction, water diversion, and non-native species introductions. The assessment examines alternatives including no action, identical 1994 designation, additional areas, and deletions, ultimately recommending the proposed designation as the preferred alternative.

## Background & Description

### Spikedace Biology and Status

[[Spikedace]] is a small, stream-dwelling fish endemic to the [[Gila River]] system. This monotypic genus (sole member of *Meda*) inhabits flowing water with moderate to swift velocities over sand, gravel, and cobble substrates. Specific microhabitats include shear zones where rapid flow borders slower flow, sheet flow areas at upper ends of mid-channel bars, and eddies at downstream riffle edges. Spawning occurs March through May, with eggs adhering to gravel and cobble substrates. Individual lifespan is approximately two years, with reproduction primarily by one-year-old fish. Diet consists primarily of aquatic and terrestrial insects.

Historically, spikedace was widely distributed in the [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], and their major tributaries upstream of Phoenix, plus the [[Agua Fria River]], [[San Pedro River]], and [[San Francisco River]] systems. Current distribution is restricted to approximately 445 km (276 miles) in portions of the upper [[Gila River]] (New Mexico), middle Gila River (Arizona), lower San Pedro River, [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Eagle Creek]], and Verde River—only 10-15% of historic range. The species is common only in Aravaipa Creek and parts of the upper Gila River.

Recent taxonomic and genetic work reveals substantial morphological and genetic differences among remnant populations. [[Anderson and Hendrickson]] (1994) found spikedace from Aravaipa Creek morphologically distinguishable from Verde River populations, with upper Gila River and Eagle Creek populations showing intermediate characteristics. Mitochondrial DNA and allozyme analyses (Tibbets 1993) confirmed significant geographic variation. Although currently listed as threatened, a petition to reclassify as endangered was found warranted but precluded by higher priority actions.

### Loach Minnow Biology and Status

[[Loach Minnow]] is a small, secretive, bottom-dwelling fish endemic to the Gila River basin. This species inhabits shallow, swift water over gravel, cobble, and rubble substrates, using spaces between and in the lee of larger substrate for resting and spawning. It is rare or absent where fine sediments fill interstitial spaces. Some studies indicate filamentous algae may be an important habitat component. Lifespan is approximately 2 years. The species feeds exclusively on aquatic insects. Spawning occurs March through May, with some autumn spawning documented. Eggs are attached to the underside of rocks forming cavity roofs on the downstream side, with limited data suggesting male nest guarding.

Historically distributed in Mexico (Rio San Pedro, Sonora) and throughout moderate-sized streams in the Gila River system upstream of Phoenix, loach minnow is now restricted to approximately 645 km (400 miles)—only 15-20% of historic range. Current distribution includes upper Gila River, [[San Francisco River]], [[Tularosa River]] and tributaries [[Negrito Creek]] and [[Whitewater Creek]], [[Blue River]] and tributaries, Aravaipa Creek and tributaries, Eagle Creek, [[White River]], and [[Black River]]. The species is common only in Aravaipa Creek, Blue River, and limited portions of San Francisco, upper Gila, and Tularosa rivers.

Biochemical genetic work (Tibbets 1992, 1993) reveals substantial genetic differences among remnant populations, which occupy isolated fragments of the Gila basin. Tibbets recommended managing genetically distinctive units separately to preserve existing genetic variation. Like spikedace, loach minnow is declining rangewide, and a petition to reclassify from threatened to endangered was found warranted but precluded.

### Reasons for Decline

Distribution and abundance of both species have been dramatically reduced over the past century. While natural spatial and temporal environmental variations historically affected populations, current threatened status results directly or indirectly from human activities. Habitat destruction and alteration has occurred through livestock grazing, agriculture, timber harvest, mining, roads, urban/suburban development, irrigation, water diversion, impoundment, flood control, channelization, vegetation manipulation, groundwater pumping, gravel mining, fuelwood harvest, and recreation. These activities commonly resulted in erosion, sedimentation, channel downcutting, changes in channel morphology, channel instability, and loss of surface water.

In the [[San Pedro River]] and [[Agua Fria River]], plus major reaches of the Salt and Gila rivers, dewatering and drastic habitat modifications resulted in demise of most native fishes. Downstream reaches of Verde, Salt, and mainstem Gila rivers have been affected by impoundments and highly-altered flow regimes. Neither species persists in reservoirs or non-flowing waters.

Introduction of non-native aquatic species has adversely affected both species through predation, competition, habitat alteration, community disruption, and disease. Detrimental non-native fish include [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictus olivaris*), black and yellow bullhead (*Ameiurus melas* and *natalis*), [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Physical Environment

The [[Gila River]] basin encompasses approximately 160,000 square kilometers (60,000 square miles) in southwestern New Mexico and southern Arizona. The Gila River originates along the continental divide and flows southwesterly to enter the [[Colorado River]] near the U.S.-Mexico border. Major tributaries (subbasins) include the San Francisco, Salt, Verde, San Simon, San Pedro, Santa Cruz, Agua Fria, and Hassayampa. The basin spans from subalpine coniferous forests to Sonoran desert scrub. The area is generally hot and arid with biseasonal (winter-summer) precipitation. Stream flow is flashy, with two-year flood events typically exceeding base flow by more than an order of magnitude.

Geology and topography vary greatly along proposed critical habitat reaches, including canyon areas and valleys with broad floodplains. Unifying factors are low gradient and moderate-sized substrate. Stream discharge varies from very small in tributaries like [[Pace Creek]] to moderate in the middle Gila River (mean discharge 15 cubic meters per second).

### Biological Environment

Riparian vegetation varies by elevation: cottonwood (*Populus fremontii* and *angustifolia*) and willow (*Salix* sp.) dominate throughout; higher elevations include alder (*Alnus oblongifolia*) and boxelder (*Acer negundo*); middle elevations feature sycamore (*Platanus wrightii*), velvet ash (*Fraxinus pennsylvanica*), and walnut (*Juglans major*); lower elevations include mesquite (*Prosopis juliflora*), seepwillow (*Baccharis* sp.), and hackberry (*Celtis reticulata*).

The native fish community is critically important. While the Gila River basin originally included 17 native species, one is extinct and several extirpated. Remaining or reestablished native species in proposed critical habitat areas (2-8 species) include spikedace, loach minnow, [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), and [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*).

Other endangered and threatened species occur in proposed critical habitat areas. The endangered razorback sucker has been stocked into Gila Box, lower San Francisco River, Blue and Verde rivers, and Eagle and Bonita creeks. Critical habitat for razorback sucker includes the Gila River and 100-year floodplain from Arizona/New Mexico border to [[San Carlos Lake]], including Gila Box, and Verde River from Perkinsville to [[Horseshoe Reservoir]]. The endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) has been stocked into Verde River as an experimental nonessential population.

The endangered [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*) occurs in many proposed critical habitat areas, with designated critical habitat substantially overlapping on San Pedro River, Verde River, upper Gila River valleys, East and West Forks Gila River, and San Francisco and Tularosa rivers. The endangered [[Huachuca Water Umbel]] (*Lilaeopsis schaffneriana* var. *recurva*) occurs along upper San Pedro River with designated critical habitat from Hereford Bridge to Benson. The endangered [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy-Owl]] (*Glaucidium brasilianum cactorum*) inhabits riparian areas along San Pedro and middle Gila rivers, with critical habitat designated from Roble Canyon to Gila River confluence and on Gila River from San Pedro confluence to Florence.

The threatened [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*) nests along Verde River, [[Tonto Creek]], middle Gila River, and uppermost San Francisco River. Wintering bald eagles use most streams included in proposed critical habitat.

### Human Environment

Land ownership and uses vary widely. On upper Gila, Verde, Blue, San Francisco, Tularosa, and Black rivers and tributaries, plus Eagle and Tonto creeks, predominant ownership is [[National Forest]], with uses including timber harvest, grazing, recreation, roads, mining, and others. On San Pedro and middle Gila rivers, and Aravaipa and Bonita creeks, [[Bureau of Land Management]] is primary manager, with livestock grazing, recreation, roads, and mining as major uses. Special use areas offering stream protection include [[Gila Wilderness]], [[Aravaipa Wilderness]], [[Hellsgate Wilderness]], [[Blue Range Primitive Area]], [[Gila Box National Conservation Area]], and [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]].

Private lands are scattered throughout, with large concentrations in Cliff/Gila Valley, Verde Valley, and middle/lower San Pedro River. Uses are primarily agricultural: livestock grazing, pasture, and irrigated cropland, with numerous irrigation diversions. Verde Valley and upper San Pedro have extensive urban and suburban development. Large mining companies (Phelps-Dodge Corporation, ASARCO) own significant areas in Cliff-Gila Valley, lower San Francisco River, Eagle Creek, and Winkleman area. [[The Nature Conservancy]] owns significant areas on upper Gila River, Aravaipa Creek, San Pedro River, and middle Verde River, managed for natural values with secondary recreational use.

Tribal lands exist near but are excluded from proposed critical habitat per Secretarial Order 3206. [[White Mountain Apache Reservation]] and [[San Carlos Apache Reservation]] lie near Black River and Eagle Creek areas. [[Yavapai-Apache Indian Community]] has lands along Verde River. [[Gila River Indian Community]] is downstream from middle Gila River area. About 200 allottees hold small area on lower Aravaipa Creek.

Archaeological and historical resources occur throughout, including [[Gila Cliff Dwellings National Monument]] (West Fork Gila River), [[Tuzigoot National Monument]] (Verde River), and [[Montezuma's Castle National Monument]] (Beaver Creek), plus numerous small sites.

Water development and diversion occurs on all ownerships. Large development is unusual except middle Gila River below [[Coolidge Dam]] (San Carlos Lake), where flow is regulated but river retains spikedace due to ameliorating influence of unregulated San Pedro River hydrograph. Small impoundments include [[Sullivan Lake]] (upper Verde) and [[Wall Lake]] (upper East Fork Gila River). Irrigation diversions throughout proposed critical habitat often divert all flow during some seasons (Verde Valley, Phelps-Dodge diversion on Eagle Creek, City of Safford diversion on Bonita Creek, private hatchery diversion on upper Blue River, Pleasanton-Glenwood area diversions on San Francisco River). Groundwater pumping seriously threatens surface flow in San Pedro River (Sierra Vista area) and upper/middle Verde rivers.

## Key Findings

### Critical Habitat Definition and Elements

Critical habitat under section 3(5)(A) of the Endangered Species Act includes: (i) specific areas within geographical area occupied by species at listing containing physical or biological features essential to conservation that may require special management; and (ii) specific areas outside geographical area occupied at listing determined essential for conservation. "Conservation" means using all methods and procedures necessary to bring species to point where ESA measures are no longer necessary (i.e., recovery and delisting).

Physical and biological features (50 CFR 424.12) include: space for individual and population growth and normal behavior; food, water, or nutritional/physiological requirements; cover or shelter; breeding, reproduction, or rearing sites; and habitats protected from disturbances or representative of historic geographical and ecological distributions.

### Primary Constituent Elements - Spikedace

Based on habitat requirements and population biology studies (Barber et al. 1970; Minckley 1973; Anderson 1978; Barber and Minckley 1983; Propst et al. 1986; Douglas et al. 1994; Stefferud and Rinne 1996; Velasco 1997), primary constituent elements include:

- Permanent, flowing, unpolluted water
- Adult living areas: slow to swift flow velocities in shallow water with shear zones, sheet flow areas at upper ends of mid-channel sand/gravel bars, eddies at downstream riffle edges
- Juvenile living areas: slow to moderate velocities in shallow water with moderate instream cover
- Larval living areas: slow to moderate velocities in shallow water with abundant instream cover
- Sand, gravel, and cobble substrates with low to moderate fine sediment and substrate embeddedness
- Pool, riffle, run, and backwater components
- Low stream gradient
- Water temperatures approximately 1-30°C (35-85°F) with natural diurnal and seasonal variation
- Abundant aquatic insect food base
- Periodic natural flooding
- Natural, unregulated hydrograph, or if modified/regulated, hydrograph demonstrating ability to support native fish community
- Few or no predatory or competitive non-native species

### Primary Constituent Elements - Loach Minnow

Based on habitat requirements and population biology studies (Barber and Minckley 1966; Minckley 1973; Britt 1982; Rinne 1989; Propst and Bestgen 1991; Douglas et al. 1994; Velasco 1997), primary constituent elements include:

- Permanent flowing, unpolluted water
- Adult living areas: moderate to swift flow velocities in shallow water with gravel, cobble, and rubble substrates
- Juvenile living areas: moderate to swift flow velocities in shallow water with sand, gravel, cobble, and rubble substrates
- Larval living areas: slow to moderate velocities in shallow water with sand, gravel, and cobble substrates and abundant instream cover
- Spawning areas: slow to swift flow velocities in shallow water with uncemented cobble and rubble substrate
- Low amounts of fine sediment and substrate embeddedness
- Riffle, run, and backwater components
- Low to moderate stream gradient
- Water temperatures approximately 1-30°C (35-85°F) with natural diurnal and seasonal variation
- Abundant aquatic insect food base
- Periodic natural flooding
- Natural, unregulated hydrograph, or if modified/regulated, hydrograph demonstrating retained ability to support native fish community
- Few or no predatory or competitive non-native species

### Proposed Critical Habitat Designation

The proposed action designates 1,443 km (894 miles) of stream channels organized into seven geographic complexes for both species (with some variation). Complexes include presently occupied and unoccupied areas essential for reestablishing populations to achieve recovery.
