# Status Review of Roundtail Chub, Flannelmouth Sucker, and Bluehead Sucker in the Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Larval Fish Laboratory Contribution 118, September 2002) by [[Nick Bezzerides]] and [[Kevin Bestgen]] reviews the taxonomy, life history, distribution, population trends, and status of three native big-river fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. The work was funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and intended to provide baseline data for state conservation plans and the Colorado River Fish and Wildlife Council.

All three species have declined in range and abundance, similar to the four federally endangered big-river fishes. Their decline is attributed mainly to reservoir construction and operation, cold hypolimnial tailwaters, and non-native fishes, with hybridization and habitat degradation as secondary threats. The species persist partly because of adaptive life-history traits and use of smaller tributary systems. The authors conclude that the species are not in imminent danger of extinction, but that data gaps justify state concern.

## Background & Description

**Physical setting.** The basin covers about 632,000 km² across seven western states and Mexico. It is divided into upper (UCRB) and lower (LCRB) portions, with Glen Canyon Dam used as the practical boundary. The basin is arid and produces little water per unit area (about 30,000 m³/km² versus 386,000 m³/km² in the [[Columbia River Basin]]). Flows are snowmelt-driven, and historical temperatures ranged from 0 to 30 °C.

**Fish fauna.** The native fish fauna comprises 22 genera and at least 35 species, with about 27% of genera and 14% of species endemic. Native big-river fishes share streamlined bodies, humped or keeled dorsal surfaces, and large falcate fins, which are adaptations to turbulent flow. Roundtail chub, flannelmouth sucker, and bluehead sucker are classed with these fishes, though they also occur in smaller streams.

**Taxonomy.** Roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*) belongs to a complex of medium-sized chubs with a history of synonymy and taxonomic revision; its close relatives include *G. nigra* and *G. intermedia*. Flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*) has been relatively stable taxonomically since its 1853 description. Bluehead sucker (*C. discobolus*) is contested between *Catostomus* and *Pantosteus*; the authors followed Robins et al. (1991) and used *Catostomus*.

**Species traits.**
- Roundtail chub are omnivorous opportunists; adults favor slow, deep pools with cover. Maximum total length is commonly 200–300 mm, and they mature at ages 3–5.
- Flannelmouth sucker are benthic omnivores (algae, detritus, invertebrates) that are most abundant in pools and deeper runs. Adults commonly reach 400–500 mm TL and mature around age 4–6.
- Bluehead sucker are benthic algivores, using chisel-like lip ridges to scrape periphyton. They prefer moderate-to-fast current over rocky substrates, and adults generally reach 300–450 mm TL.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Human impacts.** At least 140 dams and diversion structures occur on major CRB rivers, and about 20% of historically lotic reaches have been converted to reservoirs (Bishop and Porcella 1980). Dams alter temperature, sediment, and flow timing. Hypolimnial releases produce cold tailwaters that slow larval and juvenile growth, reduce swimming performance, and can block reproduction. Dams and diversions also entrain drifting larvae and fragment habitat. At least 60 non-native fish species have been introduced to the CRB, and non-native fishes both prey on natives and compete with them. Exotic plants such as [[Saltcedar]] have also been implicated in habitat loss.

**Current status of the four endangered species.** [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Humpback Chub]] are federally listed. Recovery programs in the UCRB, LCRB, and San Juan River basin are coordinated and well funded, but state-level management of the three study species is largely incidental to that work.

**Study methods.** The team compiled roughly 25,000 collection records: about 15,000 from the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] database, about 2,300 from literature, and the remainder from museums and state agencies. Maps were built in ArcView, and records were split into all-records and voucher-specimen sets. Historical (up to 1979) and recent (after 1979) distributions were compared. Percent range decline was calculated from shaded-reach lengths.

## Key Findings

**Range reduction (percent of historical CRB habitat occupied):**
- [[Roundtail Chub]]: about 45% (roughly 30% of LCRB and 55% of UCRB habitat). Extirpated from about 77% of historical LCRB habitat and about 45% of UCRB habitat.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]: about 45% (roughly 25% of LCRB and 50% of UCRB habitat).
- [[Bluehead Sucker]]: about 50% (roughly 60% of LCRB and 45% of UCRB habitat).

**Population designations.** The authors identified 15 preliminary populations of roundtail chub, 14 of flannelmouth sucker, and 12 of bluehead sucker. Designations are expected to change with barrier removal, repatriation, and new data.

**Abundance data.** Estimates are sparse. Anderson (1997) counted about 2,500 roundtail chub over 150 mm in two short Colorado River reaches (1994 and 1995). Bryan and Robinson (2000) estimated more than 6,000 roundtail chub in the lower Verde River below Bartlett Dam.

**Causes of decline.**
- Dams and non-native fishes are the principal drivers, with rotenone poisoning for sport fisheries also important in some populations.
- Habitat degradation (arroyo cutting, mining, industrial discharges, spills) contributes locally.
- Hybridization with [[White Sucker]] threatens flannelmouth and bluehead suckers, especially in the Yampa and Green rivers.

**Why these species persist where endangered fishes do not.**
- Flannelmouth sucker eggs and larvae are the largest of the CRB catostomids, which may improve feeding, swimming, and cold tolerance.
- Flannelmouth sucker can spawn at water temperatures as low as 6 °C, allowing reproduction in cold tailwaters.
- The three species spawn from early spring to mid-fall, and they do not undertake long-distance spawning migrations that dams can block.
- Roundtail chub and flannelmouth sucker are opportunistic feeders, whereas razorback sucker and Colorado pikeminnow are more specialized.
- Tributary streams act as refuges, and historical persistence has been linked to tributary use.

**Conservation status by state.**
- Lower basin: roundtail chub is endangered in New Mexico and a species of special concern in Arizona. Flannelmouth sucker is a species of special concern in Arizona. No lower basin state lists bluehead sucker.
- Upper basin: roundtail chub is endangered in New Mexico and threatened in Utah. Flannelmouth sucker and bluehead sucker are species of special concern in Utah and Wyoming.
- None of the three is currently listed under the federal Endangered Species Act.

**Management recommendations.**
- *Research:* conduct population surveys in data-poor tributaries; identify tributaries that maintain resident and transient populations; quantify non-native fish impacts and control efficacy; describe ecology of key life stages; test flow and temperature re-regulation below dams; investigate genetic variability and hybridization.
- *Management:* protect tributary flows and habitat; limit non-native introductions; align state fishing regulations with conservation status; repatriate species into suitable historical habitat; include native fishes in research, management, and education efforts.

**Data quality limitations.** The authors note that methods reporting was often incomplete, and they recommend recording sampling effort, including zero-capture sites, and life-stage at capture. The distribution maps show presence only and cannot indicate abundance.
