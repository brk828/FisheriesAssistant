# Predation by Odonate Nymphs on Larval Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether dragonfly and damselfly nymphs, common in the Arizona-Nevada backwaters of [[Lake Mohave]], are a significant cause of high larval mortality in the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]]. Two laboratory aquarium experiments (1992 and 1993) showed that odonate nymphs can destroy substantial numbers of small larvae within days. Survival of small larvae was 24% with dragonfly nymphs and 79% with damselfly nymphs, versus 88% in controls. The authors conclude that odonates are a plausible contributor to poor recruitment, especially in isolated, vegetated backwaters, and that predator-exclusion stocking practices were improving survival.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Xyrauchen Texanus]], a large catostomid endemic to the Colorado River basin. Its largest remaining population occupies Lake Mohave, and the species is listed as endangered.
- **Problem:** Recruitment of wild larvae has been very low for decades. Earlier attempts at population augmentation were also undermined by losses in the early life stages.
- **Predators studied:** [[Odonata]] nymphs, specifically a [[Damselfly]] (*[[Enallagma]]* sp.) and a [[Dragonfly]] (*[[Tramea]]* sp.), which were the most common nymphs in Lake Mohave backwaters.
- **Stage of vulnerability:** Larvae are at risk from hatching until they reach about 50 mm TL, when they are too large for odonates to capture.
- **Knowledge gap:** Before this study, the authors had not tested whether odonates were a meaningful predator of larval razorback suckers. Odonate predation on native fish was largely unstudied, and the authors also note that most aquatic insects in the system are native and that the main threats to native fishes are introduced species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat structure:** Isolated backwaters retain water year-round, allowing odonate populations to build up over several seasons. Dense growths of [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*) and spiny naiad (*Najas marina*) provide perches and ambush sites for nymphs.
- **Hydrology:** Before dam construction, river levels fluctuated widely, drying or flushing backwaters and limiting both vegetation and predator densities. Regulation has made backwaters more stable.
- **Predator community:** Few fishes in the backwaters eat odonates, and the introduced [[Centrarchids]], catfishes and minnows now occupy much of the system. Historically, predatory fish were scarce, which provided shelter for young larvae.
- **Larval biology:** Newly hatched larvae lack a functional gas bladder. They cannot swim sustainedly and spend most of their time on the substrate, making them especially vulnerable.
- **Seasonal timing:** Odonate nymphs become abundant in spring, overlapping with the stocking period for larvae.

## Key Findings

**Experiment design**
- Two separate experiments were run in aquaria with odonate predators and controls without predators, at a controlled water temperature of roughly 21–22 °C (OCR-garbled value).
- Experiment 1 used larger aquaria with the same initial density as Experiment 2. Experiment 2 reduced water volume to increase predator encounter rate and density (about 29 fish/L).
- Larval razorback suckers were approximately 14–15 mm TL (range 11–17 mm) in Experiment 1 and 15–19 mm TL in Experiment 2.
- Experiment 1 ended after 7 days. Surviving larvae were counted and measured, and each aquarium held a set number of odonate nymphs.

**Survival results**
- **Small larvae with dragonfly nymphs:** 24% survival.
- **Small larvae with damselfly nymphs:** 79% survival.
- **Small larvae in controls:** 88% survival.
- **Large larvae with predators:** 47% survival, versus 82% in controls. Larger larvae survived better but were still heavily affected.
- **Statistics:** Survival differed significantly among treatments (ANOVA, p = 0.00-range value; the exact figure is garbled in the scan). No significant difference was found between damselfly and dragonfly treatments within either experiment. Experiments 1 and 2 were pooled because their results did not differ significantly.
- **Size:** Large larvae reacted faster to nymphs and escaped more often. Dragonfly nymphs captured larvae that rested on the bottom or swam within reach.

**Behavioural observations**
- Odonate nymphs were seldom seen swimming. Many climbed into vegetation and used an ambush strategy.
- Many nymphs disappeared through mortality, cannibalism, or emergence, and the authors could not determine which cause dominated.

**Management implications**
- Odonate predation is a plausible contributor to larval losses, but the authors did not quantify its share relative to other mortality sources.
- Extensive vegetation in isolated backwaters may allow odonate populations to become very large.
- In 1993, larvae stocked at 20 mm TL achieved about 25% survival, compared with 10% or less for eggs and newly hatched larvae. Larvae stocked at 30 mm TL showed more than 90% survival in one backwater.
- Ongoing work addresses the predator impact on other insects and fishes, and the authors suggest that vegetation control and predator-exclusion measures may help recovery.
