# Distribution, Abundance, and Habitat Use of Mountain and Tahoe Suckers in Sagehen Creek

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study documents the distribution, abundance, microhabitat use, and behavior of two morphologically similar catostomid fishes, the [[Mountain Sucker]] (*Catostomus platyrhynchus*) and the [[Tahoe Sucker]] (*Catostomus tahoensis*), in [[Sagehen Creek]], a tributary stream of the Little Truckee River in the Lahontan Basin of eastern California. Surveys in 1982 and 1983 were compared with historical records from the western Lahontan Basin. The work found that the Tahoe Sucker expanded its upstream range and became dominant, while the Mountain Sucker declined and became restricted to the lower reach. The author attributes these shifts largely to habitat modification from reservoir construction, notably [[Stampede Reservoir]], filled in 1969.

## Background & Description

The Mountain Sucker is a cool-water, rapid-current, rocky-substrate specialist with small terminal mouth, reduced scale size, and lip modifications for scraping diatoms and algae. The Tahoe Sucker is considered a generalist that reaches its greatest size and numbers in lakes and reservoirs. The two species are morphologically similar, and natural hybrids occur. Historically, the Mountain Sucker was considered as abundant as or more abundant than the Tahoe Sucker in Sagehen Creek (Flittner 1953; Card and Flittner 1974).

Sagehen Creek rises from springs at 2,956 m on the east side of the Sierra Nevada and historically meandered about 9.0 km through meadows before joining the Little Truckee River at 1,768 m. Stampede Reservoir inundates about 4.8 linear km, reducing the 12.0 km of original stream habitat in the area by 10.6 km. The 1,200 m study reach, in the lower meadow, was divided into 12 sections of 100 m each. Late-July discharge averaged 0.54 m³/s, mean depth was 0.35 m, and mean width was 5 m.

Other fish species present included [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Lahontan Redside]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Lahontan Speckled Dace]], and [[Paiute Sculpin]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Microhabitat use was assessed with snorkel surveys and measurements of depth, velocity, substrate, cover, and algae. Cover, specifically willow or tree root masses, was the most important microhabitat factor for both species: 74.8% of observations with fish were in or near cover. Both species were most often in pools and runs, with Mountain Suckers more often in riffles. Substrate use differed: Tahoe Suckers used gravel, while Mountain Suckers used rubble and cobble.

Reservoir operations strongly influenced fish distribution. Fish moved upstream as reservoir levels fell, and the study showed that population numbers, distribution, and group size all increased with fish abundance and reservoir decline. Peak storage occurred on 7 June 1983. Environmental factors such as livestock grazing, channelization, and past piscicide applications (Martis Creek in 1977; Prosser Creek in 1984) also affected the study streams.

## Key Findings

- **Relative abundance shift:** In 1951–52, Mountain Suckers made up 19% of the Sagehen Creek sucker population (3,300 individuals estimated, Flittner 1953). By 1983, the estimated maximum population was 980 suckers, with Mountain Suckers comprising 40% (adults only) and Tahoe Suckers 60% (155 adults and 75 juveniles).
- **Dominance in other streams:** In 1983, Tahoe Suckers made up 100% of the suckers in the Little Truckee River, 87% in Sagehen Creek, 100% in Prosser Creek, and 95% in Martis Creek. This reversed earlier patterns in which Mountain Suckers were 52–100% of the population.
- **Range expansion:** Tahoe Suckers extended upstream well beyond their 1952–1969 range, while Mountain Suckers stayed confined to the lower 1,100 m of the study reach, roughly 20% of their historical longitudinal distribution.
- **Spawning and recruitment:** Tahoe Sucker spawning was observed on 22 June 1983. No young-of-the-year suckers of either species were seen in 1983.
- **Behavior:** Both species were gregarious, mostly resting side by side under cover. Mountain Suckers were found in mixed-species groups with Tahoe Suckers 93% of the time. No aggressive encounters were observed. Feeding peaked just after dusk, and both species were largely inactive at night.
- **Hybridization:** Hybrids were common historically (Flittner 1953 reported 9% hybrids in Sagehen Creek). The author concludes that reproductive isolation is probably incomplete and that the Mountain Sucker may face risk of local extinction in the study streams.
- **Conservation implications:** Reservoir construction, habitat alteration, and interspecific competition are the proposed drivers of change. The author recommends that the status of Mountain Sucker populations be monitored in eastern California streams.
