# Potential Effects of Global Warming on Native Fishes of the Southern Great Plains and the Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review assesses how [[Global Warming]] could affect native fishes of river and stream systems in the [[Southern Great Plains]] and the Southwest. Prairie streams already reach some of the highest free-flowing water temperatures on Earth, with summer maxima of 38–40°C. The authors argue that these fishes cannot escape warming by moving north, because drainages run west to east and cut them off from cooler habitats. A warming of 3–4°C could drive at least 20 endemic species toward extinction unless they adapt genetically or behaviorally. Evidence on behavioral adjustment is weak, and the evidence on local genetic differences in thermal tolerance is contradictory. The authors conclude that the outlook for native fishes is bleak.

## Background & Description

Fishes of the Great Plains and Southwest live in harsh, fluctuating environments with variable temperature, flow, and physicochemical conditions. Compared with upland stream species, prairie-stream fishes tolerate higher temperatures. In a study by [[Matthews (1987)]], five prairie minnow species had higher thermal tolerance than six upland species.

The regional drainages run mainly west to east. Fish in the southern Great Plains are therefore effectively "locked in" to a narrow latitudinal range. Northward dispersal would require travel downstream to the [[Mississippi River]] or [[Missouri River]] and then up to a thousand kilometers east before turning north. In the Trinity, Brazos, Colorado, and Pecos systems, northward movement would require crossing the [[Gulf of Mexico]], so no such route exists. The [[Arkansas River]], [[Red River]], [[Cimarron River]], [[Canadian River]], and Washita systems face the most acute problem.

Two direct threats are identified. First, many species already reach their thermal limits in summer, and some die in heat-related events. Second, warming combined with desiccation would lengthen low-flow periods. Some species, such as the [[Arkansas River Shiner]] (*Notropis girardi*) and [[Plains Minnow]] (*Hybognathus placitus*), spawn successfully only at high flow, so reproductive failure is likely. The authors also note that species-specific genetic adaptability is uncertain, because some species may lack variation at the structural gene loci that encode metabolic enzymes. Changes in nutrient inputs and food supply are acknowledged but excluded from scope.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Existing thermal conditions.** Water quality records give historical maximum stream temperatures of 39.0°C in the North Canadian River, 38.0°C in the Washita and North Fork Red rivers, and 38.5°C in the Deep Fork River. Temperatures of 39°C have been recorded in several north Texas rivers, and 40.0°C was measured in the Salt Fork of the Red River near Wellington, Texas, on 20 July 1981. In August 1976, mainstream temperatures of 37°C were found at many points in the [[South Canadian River]].

**Thermal refugia.** During extremes, most fishes, mainly minnows, moved to cooler refugia such as shaded pools and creek mouths. Some individuals stayed in hot mainstream water, where they died quickly after capture by seining. In the South Canadian River, backwater pools and the lower creek measured 32–35°C, compared with 36–37°C in the mainstream. Laboratory gradient tests showed that [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) avoided water above 36°C and selected positions between 26°C and 35°C.

**Habitat structure.** Prairie rivers are typically 100 m or more wide but rarely exceed 1 m deep, and they have almost no canopy. Shading occurs mainly at channel edges, and small tributaries often provide cooler, more shaded water. Heat restricts fish to small portions of this microhabitat, which may reduce foraging, deplete food in refugia, increase exposure to parasites and bacterial infection, and intensify density-dependent stress.

**Upland comparison.** Fish from Ozark and Arbuckle uplands never occur in low-gradient prairie rivers. Both groups were tested in the laboratory at 21°C.

## Key Findings

- **Current thermal limits are close to lethal.** Most Great Plains fishes survive brief exposure to 36–38°C, but these temperatures are near their survival limits. The Brier Creek *Etheostoma spectabile* population was apparently killed by water at 38–39°C in summer 1980.
- **Critical thermal maxima (CTM) differ between species.** The prairie species *Hybognathus placitus*, *Cyprinella lutrensis*, and *Notropis girardi* had mean CTMs of 38.6–40.0°C, and none exceeded 40.0°C. *Lythrurus atherinoides* had a mean CTM of 37.7°C, with no individual above 38.1°C, and it disappears from the river in hot summers.
- **Prairie species are more heat-tolerant than upland species.** Mean CTM was 35.8°C for prairie species versus 34.5°C for upland species (acclimated at 21°C; P < 0.001). Behavioral selectivity, however, did not differ significantly between the two groups.
- **Warming of 3–4°C is likely to be lethal for many species.** At least 20 species east of the Continental Divide have ranges so restricted to the southern Great Plains that total extinction, not just local extirpation, could result. Examples include the [[Mexican Tetra]] (*Astyanax mexicanus*), the [[Proserpine Shiner]] (*Cyprinella proserpina*), the [[Devils River Minnow]] (*Dionda diaboli*), and the [[Rio Grande Darter]] (*Etheostoma grahami*). Species living in springs and spring-fed creeks may buffer warming, but they become vulnerable if groundwater temperatures also rise.
- **Behavioral adjustment is unlikely to be enough.** Thermal selectivity varied among the 11 tested species, but no benefit was seen in the prairie group. The authors conclude that innate heat tolerance is needed to survive in prairie mainstreams.
- **Genetic variation among populations is contradictory.** [[Feminella and Matthews (1984)]] found that populations of *Etheostoma spectabile* from fluctuating thermal environments were more tolerant than those from constant-temperature springs. In contrast, [[Matthews (1986)]] found no significant difference in mean CTM among 18 *Cyprinella lutrensis* populations from north Kansas to southern Texas, all near 36°C at 21°C acclimation. Some stream systems showed wider CTM ranges, which may offer raw material for selection.
- **Genetic adaptation can occur quickly in some cases.** A thermally stable form of malate dehydrogenase in largemouth bass changed in frequency within 15 years of thermal effluent exposure, then returned to its natural frequency within 10 years after stress was removed ([[Smith et al. (1983)]]). Temperature-related genetic change was documented in *Cyprinella lutrensis* over 35 years. About one third of population-level gene variance was attributed to thermal heterogeneity.
- **Genetic variation is linked to survival.** Low genetic variation is associated with benthic, headwater, and spring-fed species, as well as restricted distributions such as those of *Cyprinella venusta* and *Lythrurus umbratilis*, which show lower CTMs. In the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), the population with the most genetic variation had the highest fecundity, growth, developmental stability, and survival.
- **Mutation is too slow to rescue these populations.** The authors state that mutation is the only source of new genetic variation and is too slow and random to keep pace with rapid warming. Survival will depend on existing variation and on how fast warming occurs.
