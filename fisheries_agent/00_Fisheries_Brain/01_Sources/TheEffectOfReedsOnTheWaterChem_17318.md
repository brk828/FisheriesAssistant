# The Effect of Reeds on the Water Chemistry of Gnadensee (Bodensee)

#limnology #aquatic-botany #phragmites

## Executive Summary

This 1975 study by M. W. Banoub, published in *Archiv für Hydrobiologie*, examines how [[Phragmites Communis]] (common reed) beds alter the water chemistry of the [[Gnadensee]], a morphologically distinct arm of the [[Untersee]] in the [[Bodensee]] (Lake Constance). Water inside three reed stands was sampled during the 1970 submersion season and compared with open water toward the lake middle. Reed zones showed higher calcium, total phosphorus, ammonium, alkalinity, conductivity, oxidizability and UV absorption, and lower nitrate, nitrite and pH. The author attributes these gradients to decomposition of reed biomass, release from bottom muds, and [[Denitrification]] under [[Anaerobic Conditions]]. Mass-balance calculations provide order-of-magnitude estimates of material input from reeds to the lake, and the author stresses that these rest on fragmentary data.

## Background & Description

- **Organism:** [[Phragmites Communis]], a emergent aquatic macrophyte. Dead shoots persist until their basal parts decompose, and fallen stems accumulate within the reed community or are washed out to the lake.
- **Study area:** The Gnadensee is part of the Untersee, the lower and separate part of the Bodensee. The reed area of the Untersee is about 5 km², with roughly one fifth in the Gnadensee (after Schröder, as cited).
- **Study sites:** Three reed stands in the eastern arm of the Gnadensee were sampled. Stand A is about 200 m wide, and stand C is about 60 m wide at high water. Section B is large but contains open areas and is more exposed to wind.
- **Sampling design:** Water was collected from the surface and at about 1 m depth at high water. Samples were taken at 0, 10, 30, 60 and 90 m inside the reed edge, plus comparison points at 50 and 500 m toward the lake middle. Water was analysed within 2 hours, using [[Whatman GF/C Filters]] for filtered fractions.
- **Parameters:** pH, [[Alkalinity]], [[Conductivity]], chloride, calcium, magnesium, nitrate, nitrite, ammonium, reactive, total, dissolved organic and particulate phosphorus, COD (oxidizability) and UV absorption at 260 nm.
- **Study objective:** To quantify the dynamics of material flux between water and macrophytes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeochemical processes among reeds**

- Decomposition of organic matter produces varying degrees of oxygen depletion, and both aerobic and anaerobic processes may occur, depending on water stagnation.
- Reed stands present a large surface area of shoots and their epiphytic and epipelic biota, which may enhance surface reactions and nutrient uptake.
- Birds, insects and other fauna also occur in the reeds, but their effects on deposition, feeding and water disturbance were not quantified.
- Water movement is limited, but measured currents reached about 100 m inside the reeds on a calm day, with flow rates of 0–150 cm/min at the reed edge and 0–20 cm/min further in (Schröder 1973, as cited).
- Hydrogen sulphide odour was noted on calm, warm days, indicating stagnation with increasing distance from the open water.

**Shore and sediment formation**

- Shallow banks with dead reed accumulations occurred 10–30 m from the reed edge. Section B, the most wind-exposed, contained the most banks and spits.
- Sediments in the reeds had lower carbonate, phosphorus and organic matter than deeper lake sediments, possibly reflecting carbonate precipitation near the reeds and net sediment transport.

**Seasonal and spatial dynamics**

- Seasonal trends inside the reeds broadly followed those of surface Gnadensee water. Inside-reed values showed stronger seasonal amplitude changes toward the reed interior.
- In autumn, mineralization dominated inside the reeds (indicated by Cl, Ca, alkalinity and conductivity), and total phosphorus was higher inside the reeds except in autumn, when the lake overturn affected the water.

## Key Findings

**1. Gradients in water chemistry**
- Reed zones had higher Ca, alkalinity, conductivity, total P, NH₄-N, COD and UV absorption than open water.
- Nitrate, nitrite and pH were lower inside the reeds. Magnesium and chloride were only slightly higher.
- Concentrations generally changed more with deeper penetration into the reeds.
- Minimum nitrate concentrations of about 100 µg NO₃-N/l occurred in open water during phosphorus depletion; inside the reeds nitrate fell below this value.

**2. Correlation analysis**
- Alkalinity was directly related to conductivity (about 0.016 mval/µS), and calcium increased with conductivity (about 0.013 mval/µS). About 80% of alkalinity changes were attributed to calcium, indicating [[Calcium Carbonate]] changes.
- Chloride was directly related to conductivity only inside the reeds (about 0.003 mval/µS).
- Nitrate and nitrite decreased with conductivity, especially inside the reeds, which was interpreted as denitrification removing nitrogen while mineralization proceeded.
- Nitrate and ammonium were positively correlated inside the reeds, suggesting both derive from decomposition, with possible nitrogen gas loss during nitrate reduction.

**3. Mass-balance estimates (order of magnitude)**
- Reed standing crop: assuming 75 plants/m², 10 g dry weight per shoot and 1 km² area, the total reed biomass was about 750 t per season.
- Theoretical release from Gnadensee reeds: about 840 kg Ca, 350 kg P, 4,500–18,500 kg N and 345,000 kg C.
- Mud release (in situ, after Schröder): about 530 kg total P, 480 kg PO₄-P, 980 kg mineral N and 54 kg Ca over a 120-day submersion season.
- Estimated average standing stock among the reeds: about 6.6 kg total P, 50 kg Cl, 2,000 kg Ca, 150 kg Mg, a loss of 11.2 kg mineral N, and 1,690–1,800 kg organic C.
- Residence time of P was estimated at 0.9 days, implying an average flow through the reeds of 2.55 m/h (4.25 cm/min) if movement alone controlled it.
- Nitrogen deficit of 11.2 kg, relative to an expected mineral N input of about 9,966 kg, was attributed to [[Denitrification]].
- Calcium standing stock (2,000 kg) exceeded the expected release from reeds and mud (about 900 kg), approximately 2.4 times the plant-only contribution. The author attributes the excess to mud release driven by decaying reeds.

**4. Conclusions and caveats**
- Reeds may contribute to lake nutrient inputs through death, decay and their effect on mud release. The littoral flora may influence eutrophication rates (Wetzel and Allen 1972).
- Decomposition may release carbon and nitrogen to the atmosphere, and bacteria, insects, fish and birds may provide further pathways of unknown magnitude.
- The author states that the calculations are imperfect, depend on assumptions, and call for measurements of residence times, decomposition rates and atmospheric losses.
- Comparison with [[Planter]] (1970) indicated P leaching differences (0.7 mg P versus 4.65 mg total P per stem), possibly due to calcium carbonate precipitation, re-adsorption or seasonal and regional variation.
