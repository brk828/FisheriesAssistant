# Survival of Hatchery-Reared Trout in the Big Laramie River, Albany County, Wyoming (1953–1957)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Wyoming Game and Fish Commission Fisheries Technical Report (Number 8, April 1958, authored by Robert Millis and Jack Kinaly) evaluates how much of the [[Hatchery Stocking|hatchery-reared trout]] planted in the [[Big Laramie River]] survived to anglers' creels between 1953 and 1957. It combines [[Creel Census]] surveys, electrofishing [[Population Inventory]] shocking, tag-and-clip recovery, and a [[Unit-Cost Analysis]] of stocking returns. Stocking took place only in 1953–1955; 1956 and 1957 served as no-stocking comparison years. The report also examines fish movement, natural and fishing mortality, and angler residence.

## Background & Description

The study was prompted by the lack of comparable research in Wyoming despite large expenditures on a hatchery program. It was carried out on a ten-mile public fishing section divided into ten one-mile sections, in landowner-permitted reaches. Two species were compared: [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*, cited as *Salmo gairdneri*) and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*). Three size classes were planted: fingerling (2–6 in), catchable (7–9 in), and catchable (9–12 in), at two times of year: spring (before high water) and late summer or early fall (after high water).

Marking methods differed by size. Fingerlings were marked by removing one fin (fin clipping), while catchable fish received a circular Jan tag. Creel data were gathered via personal contact, post cards, follow-up letters, and car cards, and analysed with a stratified statistical method. Population inventories used electrofishing with a modified [[Delury Method]].

The river is about 25 feet wide in the study area, with pools (~30%), flatwater (~20%) and riffles (~50%). Flows range from about 25 c.f.s. in the 1954 drought low to 300–400 c.f.s. in normal high water, and up to 2,000+ c.f.s. in extreme runoff. Food is dominated by Ephemeroptera and Trichoptera, with Diptera, Plecoptera and Mollusca in smaller numbers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and timing:** Heaviest flow occurs in May and June. The report repeatedly links spring plantings made before high water to better survival, and notes that high water through July 1957 likely raised catch rates that year.
- **Natural reproduction:** Rainbow trout reproduction was judged negligible; no naturally spawned rainbow young were found. Brown trout reproduction was considered possible but limited because smaller browns were caught before they could spawn.
- **Movement:** Roughly 79% of recovered tagged fish were caught within one mile of their release site, 93% within three miles, and a slight tendency for downstream movement was observed.
- **Mortality:** Total mortality of wild brown trout was roughly 60% in both 1956 and 1957, divided into fishing and natural components (see Key Findings).
- **Angler residence:** Residents of Wyoming made up about 82% of anglers, with out-of-state anglers from 30 states comprising 17.5%.

## Key Findings

**Survival to the creel (five-year combined):**
- Spring-planted 9–12 in rainbow trout: 58.6% recovered; spring-planted 9–12 in brown trout: 60.9%.
- Spring-planted 7–9 in rainbow: 36.5%; brown: 22.6% (the browns' low value was attributed to a weak condition of the 1955 fish).
- Fall-planted fish recovered far less than spring-planted fish in every category. The lowest was fall-planted 2–6 in rainbow (0.5%).
- Fingerling survival was roughly equal: brown 2.2%, rainbow 2.6%.

**Harvest and stocking effect:**
- In 1957, an estimated 2,962 trout were harvested by 1,889 anglers (catch rate 0.50 fish/hour; 1.56 fish per angler-day; 3.13 hours per angler-day).
- Catch rate fell from 0.73 (1955) to 0.31 (1956, no stocking), then rose to 0.50 in 1957.
- Wild brown trout harvest rose sharply during no-stocking years (75.0% in 1956 and 98.0% in 1957).

**Population inventory:**
- Standing crop of trout four inches and longer ranged from 1,287 per 6,000 feet of stream in stocked years to 575 in 1957.
- Hatchery trout remaining at season end: 115 (1953), 127 (1954), 220 (1955), 10 (1956), 2 (1957).
- Hatchery fish were 20.0% of fish shocked in 1955 and 0.4% in 1957.

**Mortality (1956 and 1957, wild brown trout):** total 60% both years; fishing mortality 29% (1956) and 35% (1957); natural mortality 31% (1956) and 26% (1957). Earlier years were excluded because shocking error was suspected.

**Unit-cost results:**
- Cost per recreational unit was $1.57 (first year), $0.61 (second year), and $0.74 (final year).
- Lowest cost per unit ($0.35) came from 2–6 in spring-planted rainbow; highest ($4.41) from 2–6 in fall-planted rainbow.
- Lowest cost per fishing day ($1.78) came from 2–6 in spring-planted rainbow; highest ($8.40) from 7–9 in spring-planted brown.

**Recommendations:**
- Discontinue fall stocking of trout in all categories.
- Plant about 1,000 four-inch rainbow fingerlings per mile across the five-mile public fishing section, and move future plantings to after high water.
- Supplement with about 250 catchable (9–12 in) rainbow or brown trout per mile, split into a pre-season and a mid-season plant (1,250 each) at management discretion.
- Conduct spot creel checks and end-of-season population inventories.

**Answers to key study questions:**
- Brown trout were considered the best resident fish for the stream, and rainbow trout were not expected to add successful spawners.
- Natural mortality ranged from 31% to 26%, and fishing mortality from 29% to 35%.
- About 50% of the catch came from resident fish during the three-year stocking period, and resident trout alone were judged insufficient to maintain 0.75 fish per hour.
- Approximately 70–75% of catchable wild trout remaining at season end were expected to be available to anglers the following season.
