# Correlating Hydrographic Events and Divergence Times of Speckled Dace

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study uses mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) sequence divergence among populations of [[Speckled Dace]] (*[[Rhinichthys Osculus]]*) to estimate when paleodrainage connections formed in the [[Colorado River Drainage]]. Cytochrome b (cytb) and ND4L sequences were analyzed with maximum likelihood and parsimony methods. Branch lengths were calibrated to geological time using a corrected fossil age and adjusted for rate heterogeneity linked to body size and temperature, following the [[Metabolic Rate Theory]] of [[Gillooly et al.]] (2005). The authors estimate that *R. osculus* diverged from its sister species at 6.3 Ma, that speckled dace have been in the Colorado drainage for about 3.6 m.y., and that upper and lower Colorado lineages diverged roughly 1.3–1.9 Ma. The results link fish dispersal and isolation to specific Pliocene and Pleistocene hydrographic events, including stream captures, lake overflows, and drainage divides.

## Background & Description

The authors frame the work around a long-standing question in Great Basin biogeography: whether population relationships match drainage configuration. Morphology-based inference of evolutionary timing has been unreliable because divergence rates vary, so the study turns to [[Mitochondrial DNA]] sequences. These are assumed to accumulate substitutions at stochastically constant rates within a taxon and gene, although the authors acknowledge that rate heterogeneity remains a problem.

Speckled dace are the most abundant and widespread fish in the Colorado River drainage. Adults are 5–9 cm long and usually live in flowing water. Populations in swift currents show streamlined bodies and large, falcate fins, while slow-water forms are robust with smaller fins (Figure 4). Their known range extends from the [[Columbia River Basin]] in southern British Columbia to the southern Gila River near Nogales, Sonora. Speckled dace are absent only from the Mojave River among major western drainages.

The authors assume that fish distributions reflect present and former habitats rather than long-distance dispersal, because fish and fish eggs are unlikely to be carried across deserts or mountains by birds or air. Four modes of isolation are considered:

- **Vicariance:** emergence of a barrier such as a basalt flow or tectonic uplift.
- **[[Stream Capture]]:** headward erosion that redirects a waterbody into another drainage.
- **Lake spillover:** water and fish passing over a divide when a lake overflows.
- **Isolation by distance:** gradual separation across geographic space.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several hydrographic and climatic events are proposed as drivers of divergence:

- **[[Glenns Ferry Formation]] lake:** The Pliocene [[Glenns Ferry Lake]] on the [[Snake River Plain]] was captured by a headwater stream at the Oxbow, probably ca. 2.8–3.0 Ma. This event is linked to an early connection between northern Bonneville and northern California speckled dace.
- **[[Grand Canyon]] barrier:** Rapids, falls, and lava dams limited upstream dispersal, and the Grand Canyon appears to have acted as an effective barrier to upstream immigration.
- **[[Bonneville Basin]] connections:** Drainage patterns near the Virgin River headwaters and the Escalante arm of the Bonneville Basin appear to have permitted exchanges between the upper Colorado and southern Bonneville lineages.
- **[[Muddy Creek Formation]]:** Virgin-White River lineages conform closely to the extent of this formation, which dates from 4.5–6 Ma. The authors suggest fish dispersal was constrained by the same Basin and Range topography.

Temperature and body size also influence evolutionary rates. Smaller-bodied fish in warmer, lowland environments (such as the Los Angeles Basin lineage) show faster substitution rates. Fish from colder, higher-elevation upper Colorado lineages evolve more slowly.

## Key Findings

**Methods and data**

- Sequences: mtDNA cytb (1040 bp) plus ND4L (297 bp).
- Parsimony analysis (PAUP) with 1000 bootstrap replicates produced 1536 most parsimonious trees of 746 steps (consistency index 0.53; retention index 0.94).
- Maximum likelihood trees used the GTR + I + G model selected by the Akaike information criterion in ModelTest 3.06, with 100 bootstrap replicates.
- Substitution rates were calibrated against fossil ages. Rates were estimated by dividing branch lengths by age.

**Calibration**

- The oldest fossil *R. osculus* characters come from the Glenns Ferry Formation (ca. 4–5 Ma). Three time horizons contain the species.
- Applying the Marshall method at the 50% level gave an alpha of 1.8 m.y. and an estimated time of origin of 6.3 Ma.
- The authors' earlier estimates of a rate of about 1.1% per m.y. for cytb in western cyprinids were considered too slow. The revised *Rhinichthys* single-lineage rate is at least 1.3% per m.y.
- Single-lineage rates within *Rhinichthys* range from 1.2% per m.y. in the north to 2.5% per m.y. in the southwest, with a mean of 1.8% per m.y.

**Divergence times**

| Event | Estimate |
|---|---|
| *R. osculus* split from sister species | 6.3 Ma |
| Northwest vs. southeast (Colorado) clades | 3.6 Ma |
| Upper vs. lower Colorado and Little Colorado divergence | ca. 1.3–1.9 Ma |
| Upper Colorado vs. lower basins (node 87) | 1.34 Ma (SD 0.68) |
| Los Angeles Basin colonization | 1.9 Ma (SD 1.18) |
| Gila and Salt lineage divergence | ca. 742 ka and 728 ka |
| Southern Bonneville and Bill Williams split | 546 ka (SD 110) |
| Upper Colorado and southern Bonneville exchange | 487 ka (SD 287) |
| Virgin-White group divergence | 170–87 ka |

**Biogeographic results**

- The Colorado River drainage lineages form six major groups: Little Colorado; Gila and Salt; Los Angeles Basin; upper Colorado; Bill Williams with a sister Bonneville clade; and Virgin-White drainages.
- Lineages in the Los Angeles Basin are sister to Gila and Salt lineages, an unexpected relationship that the authors attribute to a complicated biogeographic history.
- Amargosa River populations are most closely related to Owens and Lahontan lineages, not to lower Colorado populations as expected from pupfish patterns.
- Lineages from the upper Colorado River reached as far south as the Virgin River only in the Pleistocene (ca. 105 ka and 45 ka).

**Rate heterogeneity**

- The authors found that estimated metabolic rate co-occurred with substitution rates (p = 0.011) across 54 cyprinid genera and species.
- Estabrook et al. (2007) reported a threefold difference in substitution rates between large northern and small southern cyprinids.
- Maximum likelihood branch lengths were significantly predicted by smaller body size and warmer temperature.

**Conclusions**

- *R. osculus* is estimated to have begun diverging from its sister species at ca. 6.3 Ma.
- Speckled dace have occupied the Colorado drainage for about 3.6 m.y.
- Divergence in the upper and lower Colorado basins occurred ca. 1.3–1.9 Ma.
- Genetic divergence among allopatric lineages in the lower Colorado drainage was accompanied by morphological adaptations to stream gradients. Small genetic distances, however, indicate recent gene flow and no reproductive isolation.
- Morphotypes appear to track habitat (swift versus slow water) rather than drainage. Swift-water forms are not monophyletic, which the authors read as evidence of repeated adaptation.
- Confidence intervals on fossil ages and rate heterogeneity remain wide relative to the precision of the sequence data. The authors state that future work must correct for uneven fossil sampling and historically unique geologic events.
