# Measurement of Some Factors Affecting the Catch in a Minnow Seine

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 methods paper by [[Andreas A. Paloumpis]], published in the *Proceedings of the Iowa Academy of Science*, evaluates the factors that influence catch per haul when sampling fish with a 20-foot minnow seine in [[Squaw Creek]], a small intermittent stream in Iowa. The seine was the main quantitative tool in a wider study of drought and flood effects on fishes. The author tested seine direction, consecutive hauls, water level, time of day, and sample size, and assessed selectivity by size and species. Downstream hauls, lower water levels, and nighttime sampling produced higher catches, while consecutive-haul order had no significant effect.

## Background & Description

The study was part of a broader project on fish responses to drought and flood in Squaw Creek, a tributary of the [[Skunk River]]. The stream is about 40 miles long, drains 210 square miles, and has a largely shifting sand bed with only small riffle areas. During drought it shrinks to isolated pools. At bank-full stage, near the Iowa State College campus, it is about seven feet deep and 30 feet wide.

Three sampling stations, roughly one mile apart, each contained a few riffles and deep, muddy-bottomed pools. Each station was seined monthly, with hauls made hourly over a 24-hour period. Other methods used in the wider study were [[Wire Traps]], an [[Electric Shocker]], and [[Rotenone Sampling]]. The seine was a 20-foot common-sense minnow seine.

The paper addresses four questions about the catch per effort (c/e):
1. Does haul direction matter?
2. Does the first haul in a section differ from subsequent hauls?
3. Does water level affect the average catch?
4. Does the time of day or month affect the average catch?

It also considers the sample size needed to detect population change and how well the method represents species and size groups.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Squaw Creek is an unstable, intermittent system. Water levels rise and fall rapidly, and drastic population changes can occur quickly. The paper treats the stream as a dynamic environment where habitat changes strongly affect fish concentration and capture efficiency. Catch efficiency depends on current speed, water depth and width, and the availability of fish in reduced pools.

The fish assemblage was dominated by small cyprinids. The [[Bigmouth Shiner]] was the most abundant species, followed by the [[Red Shiner]] and [[Bluntnose Minnow]]. Sunfishes, [[Johnny Darter]], [[Stonecat]], and [[Carp]] occurred in small numbers.

## Key Findings

**1. Haul direction significantly affects catch.**
- Downstream hauls produced a higher average catch and captured more species than upstream hauls.
- Each test was run over two days at Stations 3, 6, and 8, with 135 hauls in each direction.
- Downstream hauls yielded 6,446 fish; upstream hauls yielded 2,673.
- Bigmouth Shiner made up 80.87% of the downstream catch and 70.37% of the upstream catch. Bluntnose Minnow was relatively more common in upstream hauls (10.66% vs. 4.27%).
- [[Analysis Of Variance]] for direction gave F = 4.86, significant at the 99% level. Station and time-of-day effects were not significant at the 95% level.
- Upstream seining let the current bag the net and lift the lead line, so fish could escape beneath it. In strong current, upstream seining was impossible, while downstream seining remained possible.

**2. Consecutive hauls do not significantly change catch.**
- Each haul was 20 feet long, with 20 feet left between hauls.
- Mean fish per haul for downstream hauls in 15 series were 30.33 (1st), 33.87 (2nd), 49.47 (3rd), 35.80 (4th), and 38.00 (5th). Upstream means were 21.53, 15.87, 32.27, 23.40, and 13.27.
- Downstream ANOVA gave F = 0.242, and upstream gave F = 1.007. Neither was significant at the 95% level.
- The author suggests the central (third) haul may have fallen in the best habitat.

**3. Water level reduces catch.**
- Gauge readings of 19 inches meant no continuous flow. Depth equaled the gauge reading minus 19 inches.
- Seining was ineffective above 45.0 inches.
- Thirteen water levels were sampled between June and August 1955. [[Regression Analysis]] gave Y = 158 − 3.92X, where Y is fish per haul and X is the gauge reading in inches.
- Catch fell by an average of 3.92 fish per haul for each one-inch rise in water level.
- The correlation coefficient was 0.24 (significant at 99%), so water level explained only about 6% of variation in catch.
- Mean c/e ranged from 72.81 at 23.0 inches to 11.60 at 40.0 inches.
- The author proposes adjusting catches to a standard water level by adding or subtracting 3.9 fish per inch. Higher water widens and deepens the channel, letting fish escape around the seine. Lower water concentrates fish.

**4. Catch varies with time of day.**
- Hauls were made hourly across 24 hours.
- Catches were higher between 9:00 p.m. and 3:00 a.m. than during the day, for both the total catch and [[Bigmouth Shiner]]. The author notes the pattern may not hold for less common species.
- Sampling effort was uneven: low water levels persisted longer, so more samples were taken then.

**5. Sample size requirements depend on the change to be detected.**
- Based on 111 hauls at Stations 3, 6, and 8 (June 7–14, 1955), with water between 28.0 and 31.5 inches, 3,873 fish were collected. Mean per haul was 34.89 and variance was 1,915.71.
- The formula uses a 95% confidence normal deviate (1.96) and the squared coefficient of variation.
- Samples per sampling period required for each detectable change:

| Detectable change | Samples required |
|---|---|
| 10% | 1,118 |
| 20% | 279 |
| 30% | 124 |
| 40% | 70 |
| 50% | 45 |

- About 60 hauls per day were feasible, so detecting a 10% change was impractical.

**6. Seining is size-selective but relatively non-selective among common species.**
- The method was most effective for fish of 1.0 to 5.0 inches total length.
- Larger fish were under-represented because they inhabit areas that could not be effectively seined.
- Wire trap, electric shocker, and rotenone data indicated that fish over 6 inches were more abundant than seine catches suggested. However, they formed only a small part of the population.
- Among the more abundant species, seining appeared fairly non-selective.

**Overall conclusion:** Regular seining with a 20-foot minnow seine gave a reasonably satisfactory estimate of population change in [[Squaw Creek]]. Standardizing for direction, water level, and time of day improves comparability between samples.
