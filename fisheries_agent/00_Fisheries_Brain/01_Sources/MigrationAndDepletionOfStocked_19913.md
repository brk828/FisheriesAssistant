# Migration and Depletion of Stocked Brook Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1938 study, published in the [[Transactions of the American Fisheries Society]], examines how hatchery-stocked [[Brook Trout]] move, are caught, and become depleted in two heavily fished New Hampshire streams: [[Bear Brook]] in Suncook and [[Moose Brook]] in Gorham. Fieldwork during the 1936 fishing season combined an angler interview survey with [[Fish Tagging]] and catch records. The authors concluded that repeated open-season stocking, with fish distributed widely along the stream, gives better and longer-lasting angling than fall or early-spring plantings or concentrated releases.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Brook Trout]] (*[[Salvelinus Fontinalis]]*), a salmonid stocked to maintain put-and-take angling.
- **Authors:** E. E. Hoover (New Hampshire Fish and Game Department) and M. S. Johnson (National Park Service).
- **Methods:** [[Civilian Conservation Corps]] enrollees interviewed anglers as part of an [[Angler Creel Survey]], explaining that the data would improve stocking policy and fishing. More than 70 per cent of fishermen were contacted. Data were sufficient for general discussion on two streams only.
- **Study sites:**
  - *Bear Brook* was chosen as a typical heavily fished southern New Hampshire trout stream.
  - *Moose Brook* was chosen as a typical heavily fished northern New Hampshire trout stream.
- **Note on dating:** The source text gives the season as "1986," but the stocking dates (fall 1935, spring 1936) and the journal volume (1937) indicate the 1936 season.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking and fishing pressure at Bear Brook**

- Fall 1935: 2,000 legal brook trout stocked after the fishing season.
- Spring 1936: an additional 2,000 legal trout stocked before opening day, intended as a reserve of 4,000 fish.
- During May, fishing declined steadily to "poor" by the end of the month. Only 15 per cent of the 4,000 pre-season fish had been removed by then.
- First week of June: 2,000 legal fish stocked during the open season (fish were planted secretly). Fishing improved immediately, along with an increase in the number of anglers.
- The June stocking of 2,000 fish outperformed the 4,000 fall and early-spring fish.

**Catch and effort patterns**

- Day-to-day catch showed no definite or consistent relationship to the number of fishermen, hours fished, or miles of stream fished. Better fishing did occur when more fish were present.
- Fifty per cent of the season's total catch was taken on weekends: Saturday (20 per cent) and Sunday (30 per cent).
- Catch per angler-hour fell when more anglers were present. Total daily catch rose with the number of anglers, but catch per unit effort varied roughly inversely with angler numbers.

**Movement of stocked trout**

- Tagged fish were recovered by survey staff who were unaware of the stocking location, and their capture points were plotted on a large-scale map.
- Of 380 recaptured tagged trout, 68 per cent had sufficient data to be useful.
- Roughly 80 per cent moved downstream and about 20 per cent moved upstream during the 20 days the fish remained in the brook.
- Mean downstream movement over 20 days was 1,268 ft; mean upstream movement was 1,454 ft.
- Farthest upstream capture: 3,900 ft (day 20). Farthest downstream capture: 3,700 ft (day 8).
- Mean distance moved from the stocking point was about 1,300 ft.
- The authors offer no explanation for the predominant downstream movement and suggest it may differ at other times of year.

**Stocking method comparison**

- 500 tagged fish released in one hole were depleted in about three weeks.
- 1,500 untagged fish scattered along the stream sustained good fishing for about five weeks.
- The authors assumed the tagged and untagged depletion rates were comparable and concluded that scattered stocking outperforms concentrated release.

**Moose Brook**

- The results broadly matched Bear Brook, but fewer fish were tagged, so movement could not be interpreted.
- 500 legal trout stocked in fall 1935 produced clearly poorer fishing than 500 legal trout stocked during the 1936 open season, and open-season stocking sustained better fishing for longer.

## Key Findings

1. **Open-season stocking outperforms fall and early-spring stocking.** Repeated small plantings during the season gave better and longer-lasting fishing in both streams.
2. **Repeated stocking is needed for fishing to be maintained.** Fishing declined through May in both brooks and recovered only after June stocking.
3. **Fall 1935 stocking did not measurably increase the 1936 catch at Bear Brook.** The data show no such effect.
4. **Depletion is rapid.** Tagged fish were 70 per cent removed within three weeks of June stocking, with 45 per cent gone by the end of week one.
5. **Distributed stocking lasts longer than concentrated stocking.** Scattered fish supported fishing for about five weeks versus three weeks for fish released in one spot.
6. **Most recovered tagged trout moved downstream.** About 80 per cent moved downstream, with mean displacements of roughly 1,300 ft from the stocking point. A few were recaptured upstream.
7. **Angling luck remains important.** The authors note that "fisherman's luck" still strongly affects catch despite stocking.

**Management recommendation:** Distribute trout widely throughout the stream and make small, repeated open-season plantings rather than large fall or early-spring stockings.

**Limitations noted in the study:** Movement data were available only for Bear Brook, the stocking-method comparison relied on assumed equivalence between tagged and untagged fish, and downstream drift was not explained.
