# Using PIT Technology to Study the Fate of Hatchery-Reared YOY Northern Pike Released into Shallow Vegetated Areas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) technology can track the fate of hatchery-reared young-of-the-year (YOY) [[Northern Pike]] released into a naturally flooded grassland in the [[Brière Marsh]], France, during May–June 2005. Of 192 PIT-tagged fish released, 19.3% were recaptured in a [[Fyke Net]] before the grassland dried out, compared with 6.9% of untagged control fish. Small fish (<50 mm fork length at tagging) died at higher rates. Tag loss was not observed among survivors, and growth fell within published ranges. A [[Portable PIT Detector]] located 71.4% of tagged fish present. Over 30% of tagged fish disappeared from the study area, and bird predation was considered the most likely cause. The authors conclude that PIT methods are effective for this species and that early post-release mortality is a critical constraint on [[Stocking Programs]].

## Background & Description

Northern pike (*Esox lucius*) is a predatory keystone species of shallow freshwater systems. Temporary flooded, vegetated areas serve as critical spawning and nursery habitat, where larvae and juveniles gain protection from predators and access to food. Juveniles must emigrate to permanent habitats before these wetlands dry out. Earlier studies of YOY pike survival were largely experimental or semi-natural. Some wild studies used mass marking, such as [[Mass Marking]] with otoliths or strontium, but did not identify mortality causes.

PIT technology, first developed for fish tagging by Prentice et al. (1990), has been widely applied to salmonids. Stationary antennas track habitat use and migration, while portable detectors have been developed for shallow streams. To the authors' knowledge, PIT technology had never previously been used on wild YOY northern pike. The study's objectives were to assess:

- PIT tag retention in YOY pike
- Post-tagging survival
- The efficiency of a portable PIT detector for tracking hatchery fish after release

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The [[Brière Marsh]] covers about 7,000 ha and comprises permanently flooded ditches, ponds, reed beds, and grazed grasslands that flood in winter and dry out during spring and summer. The experimental site was a 0.47 ha flooded grassland, divided into 233 rectangular cells (4 × 5 m). Its substrate was compact peat, with a mean water depth of 15.1 cm (±5.6 SD; maximum 36 cm). Emergent (Poaceae, *Phragmites australis*) and submerged plants (*Ranunculus* spp., *Callitriche* spp.) covered 47.2% (±28.5 SD) of the area. The grassland connected to a temporarily flooded pond at a single point, where a fyke net with 5 mm mesh and 1.2 m wings trapped fish leaving the grassland. The water level fell steadily until the grassland dried out completely in late June.

**Fish origin and handling.** Hatchery-reared YOY pike were fed zooplankton. Fish were anaesthetized with eugenol, measured, weighed, and tagged. They recovered for 6–8 h, were transported by boat for 2 h in groups of 13–15, and were released at a density of 0.06 ind m⁻². This density matched wild YOY densities in the marsh and was lower than densities reported elsewhere, reflecting an effort to limit [[Cannibalism]].

**Invertebrate and vertebrate predators.** The invasive [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarkii*) was abundant. A total of 4,764 juveniles (40–70 mm) were caught in the fyke net. Birds also used the site: [[Grey Heron]], [[Sacred Ibis]], and [[Black Tern]] were observed fishing there, and an [[Eurasian Otter]] was seen in the area.

## Key Findings

**Tagging and stocking**

- 197 YOY pike were PIT-tagged. Mean fork length was 51.0 mm (±5.3 SD) and mean weight was 0.86 g (±0.3 SD).
- Tags were 11.5 mm long and 2.1 mm in diameter (ID 100), inserted into the peritoneal cavity. The left pelvic fin was clipped to assess tag loss.
- 74 control fish (mean fork length 54.9 mm ±4.2 SD) received the same procedure, with a right pelvic fin clip and no tag.
- Five tagged fish (2.5%) and two control fish (2.7%) died during the recovery period. No tags were rejected, and no cannibalism occurred before release.
- Mortality did not differ significantly between tagged and control fish before stocking ([[Fisher's Exact Test]], p > 0.99).

**Survival and emigration**

- Of the 192 tagged fish released, 37 were captured in the fyke net (group I), 28 tags were found inside or under the net (group II), 68 tags were recovered on the dry grassland (group III), and 59 tags disappeared from the study area (group IV).
- Recapture rate in the fyke net was 19.3% for tagged fish and 6.9% for control fish. The difference was significant ([[Fisher's Exact Test]], p = 0.014).
- Survival rates were 14.6–33.3% for fish larger than 50 mm, and survival in smaller size classes was lower.
- Fish that died (group III) were significantly smaller at tagging (49.4 mm ±4.2 SD) than fish in groups I, II, and IV ([[Kruskal-Wallis Test]], KW = 11.79, p = 0.0081).
- About 42.9% of tags from fish larger than 60 mm FL belonged to group IV, indicating disappearance rather than death in place.
- Of the group IV tags, 72% disappeared within 5 days of release.

**Tag retention and detection**

- All trapped fish with a clipped left pelvic fin still carried their PIT tag, indicating 100% retention among survivors.
- The [[Portable PIT Detector]] (an [[Eurasian Otter|LID 650 reader]] with an ANT 612 antenna) detected 25 of 35 recaptured fish during the first scan, an efficiency of 71.4%. Detection range was 30–36 cm, which exceeded water depth for most of the study.

**Growth**

- Tagged fish recaptured in the fyke net had a mean [[Specific Growth Rate]] of 11.3% day⁻¹ (range 8.15–14.7%) and a mean fork length increment of 1.92 mm day⁻¹ (±0.27 SD).
- Growth did not differ between fish smaller and larger than 50 mm (Kruskal-Wallis, p > 0.637).
- These values fell within ranges reported in the literature.
- Control fish were too few (n = 5) for growth comparisons.

**Mortality causes**

- Size-dependent tagging effects and [[Predation]] by larger conspecifics (cannibalism) were both considered. Skov et al. (2003) reported up to a 60% reduction in stocked pike density within 7 days of release.
- Crayfish predation in the fyke net was suspected, given the high crayfish numbers in a small water volume.
- Bird predation was considered the most likely cause of tag disappearance and mortality, given the birds observed fishing in the grassland.

**Comparison with literature**

- Reported YOY pike survival in other studies ranged from 2–12% (Gronkjaer et al., 2004), 5–17% (Lucchetta, 1983), and about 34% (Bry et al., 1995). Post-stocking survival depends on age at release, temperature, food availability, timing, and pike density.

**Conclusions**

- PIT technology is effective for tracking YOY pike in shallow, vegetated habitats, and 11.5 mm tags can be used on fish of this size.
- The portable detector reliably locates tagged fish when water depth remains below its reading range.
- Mortality is concentrated shortly after release, suggesting a critical vulnerable period that could severely limit [[Stocking Programs]].
- Controlled experiments are needed to separate tagging effects from predation and to confirm tag retention in YOY pike.
