## Executive Summary
This field memorandum documents a fish salvage, tagging, and translocation operation conducted by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Parker FRO) between 23–26 May 1995 at two isolated "growout" ponds on the Lower Colorado River: Farmers Pond ([[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]) and High Levee Pond ([[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]]). The primary objective was the collection, measurement, and release of endangered native fish — the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] — which had been reared in isolation from predatory introduced species. Native fish were PIT-tagged (if >250mm TL) or fin-clipped (if smaller), then transported to release sites including the old Colorado River channel at Prettywater and [[Lake Havasu]] via the [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]]. Incidental catches of introduced sportfish (largemouth bass, sunfish species) and other taxa (crayfish, freshwater shrimp, threadfin shad, bullfrog tadpoles) were also recorded, providing evidence of species composition and pond conditions post-management (e.g., a 1993 poisoning event at High Levee Pond).

## Background & Description
Specific biometric data was recorded for tagging and database purposes (forwarded to Dr. Paul Marsh, Arizona State University):

- **Razorback Sucker** ([[Xyrauchen texanus]]):
  - Sample recovered at Farmers Pond: 194 mm total length (TL), 100 g weight (released, deemed too small for expected net capture).
  - Cibola sample: Most individuals recovered exceeded 300 mm TL, meeting the >250 mm threshold required for PIT tagging.
- **Bonytail Chub** ([[Gila elegans]]):
  - Two size classes noted: "larger" bonytails (large enough for PIT tagging, n=5) and "smaller" bonytails (right pectoral fin-clipped as a mark, per standard native fish monitoring protocol).
- **Tagging/Marking Methodology**:
  - Fish >250 mm TL: PIT (Passive Integrated Transponder) tagged.
  - Smaller bonytail: Anesthetized with MS-222, measured, marked via right pectoral fin clip, and released post-recovery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Context**: Both Farmers Pond and High Levee Pond function as isolated "growout" facilities — off-channel ponds used to rear endangered native [[Colorado River Fish|Colorado River]] fishes (razorback sucker, bonytail chub) in the absence of predatory/competitive introduced species.
- **Introduced Species Threat**: Presence of [[Largemouth Bass]] (n=21), [[Redear Sunfish]] (n=2), [[Bluegill]], and [[Warmouth]] (n=1) at Farmers Pond indicates a breach of isolation or re-invasion, necessitating removal via trammel nets and minnow traps to protect native larval/juvenile survival.
- **Recovery Indicator**: The detection of [[Threadfin Shad]] and hundreds of shad fingerlings at High Levee Pond marked the *first* introduced fish recorded there since a rotenone poisoning treatment in 1993, signaling either an incomplete eradication or a new introduction event.
- **Translocation Strategy**: Native fish were moved to two distinct habitat types:
  1. **Riverine** — Razorback suckers released into the historic Colorado River channel at Prettywater (naturalistic riverine habitat restoration).
  2. **Reservoir/Lentic** — Five large bonytail chubs relocated to [[Lake Havasu]] via the Bill Williams River NWR, likely for broodstock or genetic diversity augmentation in a lentic system.
- **Associated Fauna**: Crayfish (unspecified genus), freshwater shrimp (*Palaemonetes*), and bullfrog tadpoles (*Rana catesbeiana*) co-occurred in these pond systems, indicating a broader invertebrate/amphibian community subject to incidental capture during netting operations.

## Key Findings
- **Farmers Pond (Imperial NWR)** yielded 1 razorback sucker (194mm/100g, released) alongside 24 introduced sportfish (largemouth bass, redear sunfish, bluegill, warmouth), confirming contamination of an isolated growout pond by non-native species.
- **High Levee Pond (Cibola NWR)** produced substantially larger numbers of native fish:
  - **145 razorback suckers** collected; **144 released** into the Colorado River old channel (1 mortality).
  - **14 larger bonytail chubs** collected; 5 PIT-tagged and transported live to Lake Havasu, remainder returned to High Levee Pond.
  - **168 smaller bonytail chubs** collected via minnow traps; marked via fin-clip and released. Two previously clipped bonytails were recaptured, indicating pond retention/survival of prior marking cohorts. Two mortalities recorded among this group.
- **Transport Protocols**: Successful live transport techniques were documented and validated:
  - Bonytail: Cooler lined with double garbage bags, water salinized to 0.5% w/w with rock salt, pre-oxygenated for 10 minutes; fish remained "vigorous" upon release at Lake Havasu.
  - Razorback: 100-gallon heavy-duty tub with fresh lake water, transported ~3 miles to Colorado River stocking site with minimal transit time to reduce stress/mortality.
- **Operational Lesson Learned**: Trammel net timing caused unexpected stress/mortality risk to razorback sucker due to smaller-than-anticipated individuals being caught; future protocol adjusted to run nets before midnight and at first light to minimize overnight mortality.
- **Data Management**: All tagging data (PIT tag numbers, lengths, weights) were forwarded to Dr. Paul Marsh (Arizona State University) for incorporation into the regional [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] database, with copies retained by refuge biologists Carmen Kennedy (Imperial) and Angel Montoya (Cibola).
- **Archival Note**: Three boxes of historical native fish files pertaining to the lower Colorado River were transferred from the California Department of Fish and Game (Blythe office) during this trip, suggesting consolidation of long-term monitoring records for the region.
