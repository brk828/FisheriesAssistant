# Antitropical Distribution of Fishes and Other Organisms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This 1952 paper, presented at the [[Seventh Pacific Science Congress]] by Carl L. [[Hubbs]] of the [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]], argues that the so-called "bipolar" distribution of organisms is better termed "antitropical," because most related forms occur in moderate rather than polar latitudes and are separated by the tropics. The author attributes most cases to [[Pleistocene Glaciation]]-driven faunal interchange across the Equator, with surface-bound fishes crossing during the last glacial period and deep-water or relict groups crossing or persisting earlier. Cooling estimates are derived mainly from the eastern Pacific.

## Background & Description

- **Terminology:** The author proposes replacing "bipolar distribution" with "antitropical distribution" for forms that avoid the tropics, and "antitropicality" for the pattern. The term covers polar, boreal, cool-temperate, warm-temperate and subtropical forms.
- **Competing hypotheses reviewed:**
  - Separate or independent adaptive creation
  - Relict origin from a warm, uniform early Tertiary biota (see [[Tertiary Relict Hypothesis]])
  - Isothermal crossing of the tropics via deep water and cold mountain heights
  - [[Continental Drift]]
  - Pleistocene transgression of tropical waters during global cooling (the author's preferred view, following [[Leo Berg]], 1933)
- **Scope:** The discussion focuses on marine fishes, with which the author is most familiar. Sources include [[Norman]] (1937, 1938) on Patagonian and Antarctic coast fishes, and [[Garman]] (1899) on the [[Albatross Expedition]] off Panama.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Bipolar and bitemperate fishes.** Few fishes are truly bipolar. In the Antarctic and subantarctic, a small number of Arctic-related families occur, including hagfish (Myxine), skates (Raja), hakes (Merluccius), cods, sculpins, lumpsuckers, snailfishes, seapoachers and eelpouts. Bitemperate types outnumber bipolar ones and generally show less differentiation, implying shorter isolation.

**Relict fishes.** Southern lampreys (Mordacia, Geotria) and primitive teleosts (Galaxiidae, Aplochitonidae, Retropinnidae, Prototroctidae) are interpreted as pre-Tertiary or early Tertiary relicts. The subantarctic herrings (Clupea) may be early Pleistocene relicts.

**Deep-water crossers.** Sharks (for example Hexanchus, Lamna, Somniosus) and other bitemperate groups likely crossed the tropics through deep water during the Pleistocene.

**Surface-bound crossers.** Shore-pelagic fishes, including sardines (Sardinops), anchovies (Engraulis) and jack and chub mackerels, are considered recent transgressors of the last glacial period. Other organisms with a surface connection include sea lions (Otariidae), right whales (Eubalaena) and giant kelp (Macrocystis).

**Glacial oceanography.** The author argues that glaciation was synchronous in both hemispheres, that isotherms shifted equatorward, and that equatorial winds intensified coastal upwelling.

**Eastern Pacific cooling.** Using monthly surface temperature averages from 5° squares, the author estimates winter cooling of about 8°C along the eastern Pacific coast, and about 3°C in the warmest month. This cooling is presented as sufficient to explain the loss of much of the Panamanian marine fish fauna, while the tropical fauna around Magdalena Bay persisted.

**Other regions.** Equatorial western Pacific waters are estimated to have cooled by 2° to 4°C. Western Atlantic cooling is considered slight, while the eastern Atlantic is considered strongly affected.

## Key Findings

- The author recommends abandoning "bipolar" in favor of "antitropical" because true bipolarity involves few fishes, and most related forms occupy moderate latitudes.
- Only a few marine fish groups are truly bipolar; many more are bitemperate or biboreal, and some are warm-temperate as well as cool-temperate.
- Pleistocene glacial cooling, not Tertiary relict survival alone, is the preferred explanation for most cases. Surface-bound fishes crossed the tropics during the last glaciation.
- The paucity of the Panamanian marine fish fauna, especially reef fishes, is attributed to cooling-driven extermination. Indo-Pacific fishes on American Pacific outlier islands are considered recent arrivals.
- The upper Gulf of California contains an isolated, cool-adapted fauna, interpreted as stranded during Pleistocene cooling. Galapagos and South African faunas show similar recent transfers.
- Estimated eastern Pacific winter cooling of about 8°C is proposed as sufficient to explain the loss of much of the Panamanian fauna, though not all of it.
- The paper provides a qualitative framework rather than a quantitative test. Cooling estimates rely on surface temperature averages and inferred breeding seasons.
- Discussion notes include the recent spread of the barnacle *Elminius modestus* into British docks as an example of temperate species crossing tropical regions, and the transport of barnacles to the Salt Lake of Utah.
