# Gila Topminnow Restoration at the Bylas Springs Complex

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a cover letter (dated February 10, 1999) and an agency article describing a completed [[Habitat Restoration]] and reintroduction project for the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*). The work took place at the [[Bylas Springs Complex]] on the [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]] in Arizona. The [[Arizona Fishery Resources Office]] of the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] led the effort under the [[Fish Habitat Restoration And Partnership Initiative]]. Its aim was to counter the displacement of the species by [[Mosquitofish]] and the water loss caused by [[Salt Cedar]]. In 1996 to 1998 the project removed invaders, rebuilt habitat and returned captive-bred fish to the wild. The article reports that the project raised the number of reintroduced Gila topminnow populations to 20.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow is a small live-bearing fish. Historically it occurred throughout the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico, and in the Concepción, Sonoran and Gila river basins of Sonora, Mexico. Its status is set by the [[Endangered Species Act]], which lists it as endangered. The article calls it one of the most endangered vertebrates in North America.

The Bylas Springs complex comprises three small springs next to the Gila River, labeled S1, S2 and S3 from west to east. All three historically held Gila topminnow. The complex was first surveyed in 1968. Key events in its history were:

- **1980:** A flood in the Gila River watershed was followed by mosquitofish invasion of S1 and S3.
- **1983–1984:** Concrete fish barriers and fences were built on all three springs to keep out mosquitofish and livestock.
- **1968–1989:** Surveys found Gila topminnow as the only fish in S2, which mosquitofish never invaded.
- **November 1987 and September 1988:** S2 stock was placed in the Upper and Lower Headquarters ponds of [[Roper Lake State Park]] as refugia.
- **1990:** Gila topminnow disappeared from S2 because surface water was depleted. [[Dr. W.L. Minckley]] and [[Dr. Paul Marsh]] of [[Arizona State University]] linked the drying to dense vegetation after livestock exclusion, sediment and plant debris. Salt cedar (*Tamarix chinensis*) also invaded and came to dominate the system, which the article identifies as a major cause of the S2 decline.

Earlier reintroduction attempts had poor success. The article notes that of 176 reintroduced populations, only 18 were successfully established since 1936.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Non-native species were the main threat. Mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) invaded S1 and S3 after the 1980 flood. Subsequent stream renovations and barrier modifications did not remove them permanently from those springs. Salt cedar, a non-native tree that uses large volumes of water each day, reduced spring flow. The article links the combined effects of invasive species and land and water use to the threat of environmental catastrophe on the reservation.

Native vegetation was part of the remedy. Replanting with native trees was expected to improve water quality and quantity over time. The project also described the restored riparian community as a Sonoran Desert habitat that supports other wildlife.

The partners were:

- [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]]: access, support and equipment
- [[Arizona State University]]: propagation of the Bylas Springs stock until release
- [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]]: technical expertise and survey assistance
- U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service: design, financing, coordination and supervision, drawing on its Hatcheries, Ecological Services and Fish & Wildlife Management Assistance divisions

The project was also set within the Service's Southwest Strategy and [[Secretarial Order 3206]].

## Key Findings

**Population status**
- Before the project, natural Gila topminnow populations existed at only 11 locations. S1 was one of these.
- Following the project, the article reports 20 reintroduced populations, up from 18.

**Restoration actions (1996 to 1998)**
- **Summer 1996:** The S2 lineage, held in captivity since the 1980s, was reintroduced after exotic mosquitofish and salt cedar were removed and fish barriers were built. This protected the species from extirpation in S2.
- **May 14, 1997:** 43 Gila topminnow were captured from S1 and moved to the ASU Fisheries Laboratory for holding and propagation.
- **April 1, 1998:** Approximately 440 progeny from the original S1 fish were held at ASU.
- **S3 work:** Over 30 working days in 100–115 °F heat, 720 meters of spring channel were cleared of salt cedar, mosquitofish were removed (the article reports thousands), and eight ponds and two fish barriers were built. More than 30 large piles of salt cedar were burned.
- **Habitat:** The eight ponds total 100 m² of surface area, providing preferred habitat.
- **Riparian planting:** By January 1, 1998, 100 black willows and 25 cottonwoods, all native, had been planted along S3.
- **Survival:** Spring 1998 surveys found high replant survival and extensive growth.
- **Release:** On April 29, 1998, Gila topminnow were transported from ASU and released into the restored S3 habitat. The number released is illegible in the scanned text.

**Expected benefits**
- The article states the project will benefit 15 bird species, six mammal species, four reptile species and two snail species, in addition to the Gila topminnow.
- Replacing salt cedar with native vegetation is expected to improve water quality and quantity over time.

**Conclusions**
- The S2 population, restored in 1996, was described as thriving.
- The project is presented as a success story. It was the first success of the Fish Habitat Restoration and Partnership Initiative.
- The article attributes the outcome to partnering and to the San Carlos Apache commitment to preserving their natural heritage. It states that the project brings the species "one step closer to recovery."
