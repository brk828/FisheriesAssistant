# Some Observations on the Oxygen Requirements of Fishes in the Illinois River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This 1925 bulletin by [[David H. Thompson]], published by the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]], examines how [[Dissolved Oxygen]] limits fish life in the [[Illinois River]]. The river froze over in December 1924 and thawed in early February 1925. During that period dissolved oxygen fell to very low levels, causing fish to crowd into spring holes, die under the ice, and lose palatability. The paper estimates minimum oxygen tolerances for common fishes, documents avoidance behaviour, and links long-term declines in [[Fish Yield]] to sewage-driven oxygen depletion.

## Background & Description

The study builds on joint biological and chemical surveys by the Natural History Survey and the [[State Water Survey]]. Dr. R. E. Greenfield and Mr. A. L. Sotier of the State Water Survey first recorded oxygen conditions in late January 1925, finding fish crowding into the "spring holes" of [[Peoria Lake]] because parts of the lake were frozen. Thompson then spent late January and all of February collecting fish data and oxygen measurements where fish were caught, dying, or absent.

Sampling relied mainly on [[Hoop-Net Sampling]] with fishermen, with samples usually taken near the bottom where fish spend most of their time. The author notes that hoop-nets fish a restricted area but are selective: sluggish fish are caught less often, carp avoid small-mesh nets, and channel cat and bullheads enter small-mesh nets and basket traps, probably for concealment during the day.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Ice and oxygen mechanism.** Ice does not remove oxygen directly; it blocks gas exchange with the air, so aeration stops. Oxygen is consumed by the oxidation of organic matter, which feeds bacteria and larger [[Plankton]]. Consumption is slow in ice-covered water. Deep lakes seldom show winter fish kills because of less organic matter and more oxygen at depth. Streams are more vulnerable because currents keep organic matter suspended, though swift streams may stay partly open.

**Illinois River conditions.** The river is rarely ice-covered long enough to cause distress. In 1924–25 it was fully covered except for small open patches in swift water, notably [[Peoria Narrows]] and below bridges. Fishermen considered this the longest freeze since 1917–1918. Historical data from [[Kofoid]] (winter 1894–95) describe a similar prolonged freeze with near-total loss of plankton and many dead fish, and the author notes that the river was already heavily polluted then.

**Seasonal pattern.** From late autumn to late spring, cold water slows oxygen consumption so that aeration balances or exceeds it. Oxygen is then sufficient, except under ice. In midsummer, lack of oxygen excludes fish from large feeding grounds down to Peoria and sometimes below, except during high water. Warm water cannot sustain low oxygen as long as cold water does.

**Sewage and other effects.** Sewage fuels the oxygen demand. The [[Chicago Drainage Canal]] opening is mentioned as a historical reference point. Sedentary animals suffer most: large numbers of dead snails washed ashore on lower Peoria Lake, and fishermen reported declining populations of hibernating bullfrogs, snapping turtles and soft-shelled turtles. Active turtles (painted, ridge-back, terrapin) remained common in the lower river, as they were caught in traps through winter.

## Key Findings

### Oxygen trends during the 1925 freeze

- **January 23–24:** About 1.7 ppm at [[Peoria Narrows]], Pekin and Havana, versus about 6 ppm at Henry and La Salle. Low oxygen was probably widespread from Peoria Narrows to Grafton.
- **February 2:** 0.4 ppm in [[Mud Lake]], partly fed by river water. Bullheads died in traps and seines in [[Treadway Lake]] and [[Coleman Lake]], indicating low oxygen in those backwaters too.
- **Quiver Lake (mid-lake):** 2.3 ppm, raised by highly oxygenated water from Quiver Creek.
- **February 4:** Over 5 ppm in Treadway and Coleman lakes, from dilution with [[Sangamon River]] water.
- **February 5 (Beardstown):** 3.3 and 3.7 ppm mid-channel; 4.6 and 4.1 ppm near the east bank.
- **February 6–7:** About 5 ppm at Henry and Chillicothe; about 3 ppm at Peoria Narrows and Pekin, roughly double the earlier value at Peoria Narrows. Water temperature was 0–4 °C.
- **February 10 (Browning):** 5.8 ppm; February 11 (Beardstown): 6.6 ppm.
- **February 17–19:** 7.6 ppm and 7.2 ppm at Peoria Narrows, then 8.9 ppm at Water-works Point.
- **Late February:** 8–10 ppm near Beardstown and Meredosia, with no ice and water near 4 °C.

The channel was ice-free by February 8. Once the ice went out of part of the channel, normal cold-water oxygen returned within about a week.

### Oxygen thresholds for fish

- Concentrations between **0 and 2 ppm** appear to kill all kinds of fish.
- [[Common Carp]] and [[Buffalo Fish]] were found living at **2.5 ppm**.
- A variety of fishes was usually found only at **4 ppm or more**.
- The greatest variety was taken at **9 ppm**, on February 26–27 in [[Vette Swale]]: at least 22, and perhaps 26, species of larger fishes in nine 2¼-inch-mesh, four-foot hoop-nets.
- In summer 1923 at Peoria Narrows, fish died overnight in water with less than 2 ppm.
- Among species, [[Dogfish]] were the most sensitive, often dying while others in the same net survived. Carp were the most resistant, caught farther out in the channel in oxygen-poor water. [[Gars]] were the most abundant species where oxygen was little or none, probably because they breathe at the surface.

### Behavioural avoidance

- When oxygen fell, fish retreated to backwater lakes, spring holes, tributary mouths and other areas without stagnation.
- At a spring-fed lagoon near [[Spring Bay]] on the east shore of upper Peoria Lake (about two acres), clear, well-aerated water attracted several carloads of carp and buffalo during the last two weeks of January 1925.
- A river rise diluted this water with oxygen-poor river water. Large numbers of minnows, black bass fingerlings, crappies and sunfishes died, and carp swam at the surface with their heads out. After ice left part of the channel, fish stopped returning.

### Physiological and palatability effects

- Carp and buffalo from oxygen-deficient water were pale and sluggish. Those from well-aerated water were darkly pigmented and active.
- Fishermen described "[[Gassy Taste]]" in fish flesh, resembling coal gas, kerosene or tar. Thompson found it resembled the odour of putrefying river-bottom mud. It was linked to prolonged ice and scanty oxygen. Carp, buffalo, channel cat and bullheads were most affected. Fish held in well-aerated water for a few days lost the taste.

### Fish yield and long-term decline

[[Table III]] compares yields across river sections:

- Yields peaked in **1908**: about 19.27 million lb (Illinois Fish Commission total) and almost 24 million lb (U.S. Bureau of Fisheries).
- Totals were 9.90 million lb (1897), 11.61 million lb (1899) and 11.52 million lb (1900).
- After 1908 yields fell: 4.0 million lb in 1921 (a poor year) and 10.6 million lb in 1922 (a favourable year).
- Factors behind the 1908 peak: introduction of [[German Carp]], more fishing effort, added [[Lake Michigan Water]] that enlarged the river, and sewage-derived food.
- Factors behind the decline: reclamation of bottomlands and lakes, exclusion of fish from large areas by excess sewage, and occasional extreme pollution that killed food organisms and fish.
- [[Alvord and Burdick]] (1915) showed that fish yield is closely correlated with water area.
- **Beardstown–Grafton:** no marked change, as yield gains were offset by reclamation.
- **Peoria–Browning:** a marked rise to 1908, then an equally marked fall. Reclamation of Thompson's Lake district came later here.
- **La Salle–Chillicothe:** 1921–22 yields were scarcely half their former value, attributed entirely to sewage-driven oxygen depletion.

The paper concludes that the main effect of excess sewage is to reduce dissolved oxygen until fish life is excluded from immense areas of water.
