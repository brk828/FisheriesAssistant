# Spatiotemporal Variation in Length-Weight Relationships of Endangered Humpback Chub: Implications for Conservation and Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This manuscript, a penultimate draft prepared for submission to *Transactions* (cover letter dated 15 December 1997), analyzes length-weight relationships of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] ([[Gila Cypha]]) across six populations in the [[Colorado River]] Basin. The authors report that [[Grand Canyon]] fish were consistently heavier at length than fish from the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) confluence, and that condition at the LCR confluence declined significantly from 1979 to 1996. Several candidate explanations are discussed, including altered spawning timing, thermal regime changes below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], parasitism by Asian tapeworm, observer variability, and natural variation. The authors conclude that the cause of the decline cannot be determined from available data and recommend expanded monitoring.

## Background & Description

**Species and status.** The humpback chub is one of four endangered big-river fish species endemic to the Colorado River Basin. It was first described in 1946 and listed as endangered in 1967.

**Population structure.** Humpback chub persist in six populations: five in the upper Colorado River Basin above [[Lake Powell]] and one, the largest, in Grand Canyon. The Grand Canyon population comprises nine mainstem aggregations and one aggregation in the LCR. Only the largest mainstem aggregation, located near the Colorado–LCR confluence, ascends the LCR to spawn. The degree of mixing between this mainstem aggregation and the apparently resident LCR aggregation is unclear.

**Study design.** Fish were grouped by site:
- **LCR:** Fish were split into a lower reach (Powell Camp, n = 766) and an upper reach (Salt Camp, n = 1159).
- **Mainstem Colorado River:** Data were limited to the largest aggregation (n = 945).
- **Upper basin populations:** [[Black Rocks]] (n = 417), [[Cataract Canyon]] (n = 20), [[Desolation Canyon]] (n = 99), [[Westwater Canyon]] (n = 137), and [[Yampa Canyon]] (n = 109).

**Methods.** Analyses were restricted to fish >200 mm total length (TL), approximating minimum adult size in Grand Canyon. All fish identified as females were excluded because spawning females showed highly variable weights. Relationships were modeled with [[Analysis of Covariance]] (ANCOVA) on log10-transformed length and weight, using SAS version 6.11. Temporal patterns were analyzed by month-year combination. Repeated-measures models were not used because recapture rates were low, so successive samples were nearly independent. To remove the confounding effect of weight, figures show predicted weight with 95% confidence intervals for a 300 mm TL fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime.** Since the closure of Glen Canyon Dam in 1963, mainstem temperatures near the LCR confluence have ranged from 8–12 °C, well below the 16–22 °C range considered suitable for humpback chub spawning and incubation. The LCR is the only site where these temperatures occur, and essentially all humpback chub recruitment results from LCR spawning. Suitable spawning temperatures first occur in the LCR in mid-April, whereas before dam construction the temperature range did not occur in Grand Canyon until late May.

**Food base.** The dam eliminated most native macroinvertebrates by trapping particulate organic matter in Lake Powell and creating cold, isothermal flows. Most instream production now occurs in the first 26 km below the dam and consists of the green alga [[Cladophora Glomerata]], the introduced amphipod [[Gammarus Lacustris]], and midges (Chironomidae). No predam diet data exist for humpback chub.

**Parasitism.** The Asian tapeworm [[Bothriocephalus Acheilognathi]] is an introduced parasite that has been linked to stress and adverse impacts on the baitfish industry. Its effect on humpback chub is unknown; levels of infection have not been measured because detection requires severe stress to the fish.

**Flow variability.** Over the period of record, yearly maximum flows in the system ranged from less than 700 m³/s to more than 56,600 m³/s. The authors argue that a long-lived species in such a variable environment need not reproduce successfully every year to maintain viable populations.

## Key Findings

**1. Spatial differences in condition.** Grand Canyon fish were heavier at length than LCR fish in all four comparisons, with significant differences in every case (e.g., F = 13.31, p = 0.0003 for the first comparison; F = 24.34, p < 0.0001 for the fourth). Magnitude of differences varied over time, and inter-site LCR differences were not consistent, so the LCR data were pooled for comparison with mainstem data.

**2. Monthly comparisons.** Grand Canyon fish were significantly heavier than LCR fish in:
- 10 of 12 months (upper LCR, comparison 1)
- 7 of 13 months (lower LCR, comparison 2)
- 20 of 22 months (upper LCR, comparison 3)
- 15 of 21 months (lower LCR, comparison 4)

No month showed LCR fish significantly heavier than mainstem fish. Mainstem fish were at least 10% heavier than LCR fish in all but 2 of 22 months for which comparisons were possible.

**3. Long-term decline.** Length-weight data from the LCR confluence (roughly the lower 1.2 km of the LCR), collected during most spawning seasons from 1979 to 1996, showed a significant linear decline in predicted weight at length for non-female humpback chub >200 mm TL (slope = −0.0037, df = 1477, p < 0.0001). Fish measured during 1990–1996, a seven-year period of relatively stable condition, averaged 9–10% (the source text is truncated at this figure).

**4. Possible explanations for the decline.** The authors evaluate several hypotheses without resolving them:
- **Spawning timing:** Fish sampled in May may have been in post-spawning condition, as radio telemetry from 1991 and 1992 indicated spawning peaked in March–April. However, data show no consistent or unusual late sampling across seasons.
- **Thermal regime:** Cold mainstem temperatures may slow growth and recovery after spawning, although historic winter temperatures were also below 8–12 °C.
- **Tapeworm parasitism:** The decline is consistent with a possible deleterious parasite effect, but it is unclear whether parasites deplete energy or artificially inflate weight through intestinal bolus.
- **Observer variability:** Each dataset was collected by a different group. The authors judge that personnel and equipment differences more likely add variation than introduce consistent bias through time.
- **Natural variation:** The depressed condition may lie within the natural range for a species that can live more than 20 years in a highly variable river.

**5. Management recommendation.** Because the cause of the trend cannot be determined, the authors recommend careful monitoring and an addition to current monitoring practices (the recommendation text is truncated in the source).

**6. Editorial notes.** The cover letter reports that the discussion section was reorganized, that the authors were short on data from a full May sampling set, and that the reviewers were expected to scrutinize the appendix and acknowledgments. These notes indicate the manuscript was still under revision.
