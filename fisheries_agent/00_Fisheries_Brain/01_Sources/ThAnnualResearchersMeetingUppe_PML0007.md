# Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program and San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program Workshop (January 13-14, 2015)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-fish

## Executive Summary

This document is the agenda and abstract compilation for a two-day January 2015 workshop of the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] and the [[San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]]. Presentations cover endangered fishes ([[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]]), nonnative predator control ([[Northern Pike]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Walleye]], [[Asian Carp]]), habitat and flow management, hatchery operations, and the use of [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) technology for monitoring and data management. The abstracts report field results, population estimates, and management recommendations, with a closing session on PIT data pathways and a centralized database under development.

## Background & Description

The workshop was organized around two days of technical sessions and a final half-day on PIT tag data. The program covered:

- **Day 1 (January 13):** Modeling of Colorado Pikeminnow backwater habitat, floodplain wetland connectivity, Stewart Lake nursery management, selenium uptake, walleye management, smallmouth bass removal, and an electrical barrier test for Asian carp.
- **Day 2 (January 14):** Northern Pike removals and population dynamics, native fish responses to nonnative removal, PIT antenna monitoring in Grand Canyon, Little Colorado River monitoring, a canal weir, a spawning bar detection study, and White River passive detection.
- **Final session:** PIT tag data acquisition, analysis models, and development of the STREAMS database.

Poster abstracts cover fish distribution in the Upper Colorado River Basin, and laboratory tests of environmental factors reducing predation on native fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Endangered fish status:** Colorado Pikeminnow, Razorback Sucker, Humpback Chub and Bonytail Chub are federally listed and depend on mainstem, backwater and tributary habitats. Recruitment is a recurring bottleneck across several studies.
- **Flow regulation:** Reduced overbank flooding since closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] has changed floodplain connectivity; the Larval Trigger Study Plan uses releases timed to larval drift.
- **Nonnative species:** Northern Pike, Smallmouth Bass, Walleye, Green Sunfish, and Asian carp threaten native fishes through predation and competition. Razorback Sucker larvae are consumed by nonnative fish in [[Lake Mohave]].
- **Water diversion:** Irrigation canals (Hogback Diversion Canal, Green River Canal) entrain endangered fish. The Hogback Diversion Canal diverts approximately 250 cfs May through October, which can exceed 50% of river flow at low flow.
- **Water contaminants:** Selenium is elevated in [[Stewart Lake]] sediment, water and biota.

## Key Findings

**Predator control and invasive species**
- [[Carbon Dioxide]] (200 ppm) removed invasive green sunfish and smallmouth bass from small ponds but was not effective on northern crayfish or in larger ponds. Liquid ammonia eradicated green sunfish in a 2,840,000 L pond at 0.25 mL/3.78 L dose, but not in a 2,100,000 L pond even at 1.5 mL/3.78 L.
- Northern Pike removals in the Yampa River: 368 (98b) and 437 (98a) removed by electrofishing in 2014, plus 596 in backwater netting. A study of 8,929 individual pike (2004-2010) found survival highest in the downstream MS reach (average 0.54) and abundance highest in the upstream HC buffer reach. Removal was judged inadequate to permanently reduce abundance, because recruitment and immigration exceeded removal.
- Walleye: 594 removed from the Green and Colorado rivers in 2014. No evidence of successful reproduction was found, and populations are assumed to be supported by emigration from reservoirs.
- Smallmouth bass removal in the Yampa River coincided with increased native fish frequency after 2008, though authors note synergistic effects with higher flows.
- Laboratory tests: turbidity above 500 NTU reduced predation by most predators on juvenile fish but increased predation by flathead catfish.
- A pulsed DC sweeping field moved 100% of bighead carp >45 cm and silver carp 13-26 cm out of a raceway in under 15 minutes. A barrier at 0.35 V/cm prevented all bighead carp >45 cm from passing.

**Population status and demography**
- Lake Havasu Razorback Sucker population was estimated at 4,456 (95% CI 4,089-4,855) in 2013; remote PIT scanning contacted 1,313 (29%) of these. Lake Mohave combined subpopulations were estimated at 3,284 (95% CI 3,067-3,516), with 3,137 (96%) contacted.
- Humpback Chub in Desolation and Gray Canyons: extrapolated estimate of 1,863 (variance not yet calculated), based on about 1/3 of 63 sites sampled in 2014. First-year adult proportion fell from an average of 13% (2003-2011) to 4% in 2014.
- Little Colorado River humpback chub (>150 mm): spring 2014 estimate 7,983 (SE 456), fall 2014 estimate 3,956 (SE 422).
- Grand Canyon larval survey 2014: 240 sites sampled; Razorback Sucker larvae documented spawning, expanding the known breeding range.
- Colorado Pikeminnow in the San Juan River showed a lower trophic position than predicted for piscivores, suggesting altered prey availability may limit recruitment.

**Habitat and flow**
- Middle Green River backwater habitat (1986-2013) showed total backwater area per river mile similar across periods, but fewer and larger backwaters in recent years.
- Backwaters are warmer than mainstem water from late spring through fall, promoting faster growth of young Colorado Pikeminnow.
- Stewart Lake was filled in June 2014 and drawn down in September; over 700 juvenile Razorback Sucker up to 168 mm were captured during draining.
- Selenium analysis showed riverine larval Razorback Sucker carry an inborn selenium load, and selenium uptake in Stewart Lake fish is rapid and prolonged.

**Hatchery, stocking and monitoring**
- [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] successfully raised [[Bonytail Chub]] to 250 mm within two years, with more recaptures reported after stocking in the Middle Green and Lower White rivers.
- Portable PIT antennas in Grand Canyon increased total unique contacts by 1.0% and humpback chub contacts by 27%.
- The Hogback Fish Weir (cost approximately $3.5 million in 2013) was tested in November 2014: of 161 detected fish, 143 were bypassed and 18 entrained, an entrainment rate of 11% (2% of total stocked).
- A passive array on the Green River Canal detected 81% Razorback Sucker in 2013 (499 individuals); peak entrainment reached 270 fish in one day.
- White River passive array (since September 25, 2012) recorded over 43,000 detections of about 4,400 unique fish.

**Genetics and modelling**
- Colorado Pikeminnow: 25 new microsatellite loci developed, with 4-25 alleles per locus; cross-amplified in eight other cyprinids.
- Humpback Chub refuge population at Dexter, NM: genetically diverse, with low inbreeding and high allelic diversity.
- San Juan River aquatic ecosystem population model (10 fish species, 11 reaches) can simulate management alternatives over up to 100 years.
- Irrigation review: on-farm conservation alone often increases net water use, while integrated infrastructure and operational changes, plus water markets, can reduce diversions.

**Data management**
- PIT tag data across the basins has grown; about 18 PIA or temporary antenna systems were deployed, with 8 (44%) for tributary use.
- A centralized, web-accessible database called STREAMS (Species Tagging Research and Monitoring System) is being developed by the Colorado Natural Heritage Program at Colorado State University.
