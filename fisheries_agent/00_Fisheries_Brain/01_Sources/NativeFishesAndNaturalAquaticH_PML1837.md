# Native Fishes of the American Southwest: Status and Recommendations for Perpetuation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #conservation

## Executive Summary

This 1985 report by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) reviews the population and habitat status of native freshwater fishes in the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Region II west of the Continental Divide (Arizona, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Texas), plus adjacent headwaters of Mexican drainages and parts of the Lower Colorado basin. It argues that extinction of the regional fauna can be halted through protection of remaining relatively natural habitats, acquisition and restoration of suitable habitat, and an intensive management and propagation program. The author stresses that, because research time is limited, many actions must proceed under incomplete information. The document combines a narrative of habitat change, species accounts, watershed reviews, and management recommendations.

## Background & Description

- **Scale of loss:** At least 25 North American freshwater fish species and subspecies went extinct between 1900 and 1981. Fifty-four U.S. fishes were listed as threatened or endangered by the Department of the Interior (1985), with about 150 more considered in jeopardy or candidates for listing. The report states that almost 40% of the nation's freshwater fish species have disappeared and another 30% have declined to populations threatened with extinction.
- **Legal context:** Following the Endangered Species Act of 1973, the USFWS identified about 40 Region II taxa (species, subspecies, populations) as candidates for listing.
- **Recovery efforts:** These include protection of more than a third of the most critically endangered species at Dexter National Fish Hatchery (New Mexico), propagation and reintroduction, and habitat acquisition such as springs and artesian wells in southeastern Arizona for the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]].
- **Scope:** The report covers the vast [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] plus headwaters of three Mexican drainages (Rio Yaqui, Rio Concepcion, Rio Sonoyta). Some areas outside the region (White River of eastern Nevada, Salton Sea) are included for geographic continuity.
- **Structure:** Section I (habitat and fish status and management), Section II (species accounts by faunal group), Section III (watershed habitat reviews).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic conditions.** Much of the region is desert with high evapotranspiration, so surface waters depended on springs, runoff from higher mesic terrain, and groundwater. Pre-development aquatic systems were diverse, including meandering marsh-lined rivers, cienegas, and springs. Fish faunas consisted of a few highly adapted species occupying discrete niches for millennia.

**Major disturbances.** Dams, diversions, channelization, groundwater pumping, grazing, mining, pollution, and arroyo cutting (incision in the late 1800s) transformed the system. Non-native species now number in the many dozens; the report notes that numbers of non-native fish species in many western U.S. waters have tripled through naturalization.

**Habitat condition.** Figure 3 in the report estimates the proportion of aquatic habitat categories at three quality levels (springs/cienegas, high-elevation creeks, intermediate-elevation streams, small streams, big rivers). Habitat categories I–VI are defined from relatively natural and protected sites (Category I) to artificial refugia (Category VI).

**Faunal groups.** Big-river fishes (e.g., Colorado squawfish, bonytail, razorback sucker, humpback chub, woundfin) are the most damaged. Spring- and cienega-dwelling fishes (e.g., desert pupfish, Sonoran topminnow, Moapa dace) are the most vulnerable to dewatering and introduced predators. Intermediate-elevation creek fishes (e.g., spikedace, loach minnow, speckled dace) are declining, and generalists such as longfin dace and roundtail chub are more widespread but still threatened.

**Key threats** include non-native predators and competitors (largemouth bass, channel catfish, mosquitofish, red shiner, green sunfish), hybridization (e.g., bonytail and roundtail chub; Apache and Gila trout with rainbow trout), habitat dewatering, and regulation of flows.

**Geographic overview.** The Mexican drainages are relatively intact but threatened by agricultural pumpage and mosquitofish spread. The Sonoran Desert basins (Quitobaquito, Sonoyta) are heavily impacted, and the Salton Sea has become too saline for most native species.

## Key Findings

**Species status and taxonomy (Table 2 and Table 3 summaries)**
- 37 species are known from the portion of Region II west of the Continental Divide, of which 24 of 37 Lower Colorado species are endemic at the species or generic level per the report's tabulation.
- Among 57 taxa (species and subspecies) and additional problematic populations, at least some have declined in range since settlement. Of these, the report notes that about 8 (of the species it lists as extinct, threatened, or endangered) are federally listed as endangered; 12 are proposed or candidates for listing.
- Big-river fishes: seven species, five listed as endangered (bonytail, Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, woundfin) and razorback sucker under management.
- Mexican watershed endemics include the Yaqui chub, Sonoran chub, beautiful shiner, and Yaqui sucker; Yaqui catfish was historically in the United States.

**Management principles**
- Species are grouped into three categories: (1) endangered species restricted to large habitats; (2) species isolated in small, special habitats that must be maintained in place; (3) habitat generalists with broad ranges.
- Priority is assigned to habitats in categories I and II (protected, natural, or reclaimable). Category I habitats on federal, state, or Indian lands are the first priority.
- Refugia (Category VI) such as ponds at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and semi-natural habitats on [[Lower Colorado River]] refuges are recommended as cost-effective backup.

**Specific recommendations**
- Rapid acquisition or easements for springs and cienegas (e.g., Canelo Cienega, Babocomari Cienega, Redfield Canyon, Aravaipa Creek).
- Control of [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] on sites where native fish persist (e.g., Quitobaquito Spring, Sheehy Spring).
- Restrict introductions of non-native fishes and bait fish (e.g., red shiner, bonytail stockings in the upper basin).
- Regulate or oppose additional mainstream impoundments on the Upper Gila River.
- Monitoring: semi-annual monitoring for short-lived species (e.g., spikedace, loach minnow) since failure of one or two year classes is catastrophic; long-lived species (razorback sucker, which may persist more than three decades) need less frequent monitoring.

**Research needs**
- Basic life-history data on reproduction, recruitment, species interactions, dispersal, and habitat use, especially for razorback sucker, bonytail, Colorado squawfish, and humpback chub.
- Taxonomic clarification of complexes including bluehead and desert mountain suckers, speckled dace, roundtail chub, and Little Colorado River sucker.

**Notable cases**
- Quitobaquito Spring (Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument): an unauthorized introduction of golden shiner forced removal of pupfish; longfin dace was lost there.
- Razorback sucker: reintroduction since 1981 by USFWS and Arizona Game and Fish; stocked fish showed substantial predation by non-native species.
- Bonytail: fish held at Dexter NFH; spawning and pond culture show more resilience than expected.

**Conclusion.** The author concludes that perpetuation of the regional fauna requires rapid protection and improvement of habitat to population levels allowing downlisting or delisting, plus protection of unlisted species through habitat association. Sufficient information must be assembled through intensive monitoring, research, and facility support, and operational plans should be coordinated across agencies and basins.
