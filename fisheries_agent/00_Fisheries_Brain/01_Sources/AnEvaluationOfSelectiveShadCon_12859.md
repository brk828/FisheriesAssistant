# An Evaluation of Selective Shad Control at Medina Lake, Texas

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #Clupeidae #reservoir-ecology #population-dynamics

## Executive Summary

This report evaluates a selective [[Gizzard Shad]] (Dorosoma cepedianum) control program at [[Medina Lake]], Texas, conducted from 1953-1959. Initial surveys (1953-1956) documented an out-of-balance fish population with gizzard shad comprising 62-68% of collections and poor angling success (0.30-0.39 fish per man hour). In November 1956, the lake was treated with 0.13 ppm rotenone to selectively remove gizzard shad. The treatment removed an estimated 27,500 pounds of gizzard shad and 10,000 pounds of [[Carp]] (68 pounds per acre), with less than 3% game fish mortality. Post-treatment monitoring showed initial improvements: shad abundance dropped to 33% (1956-57) and 15% (1957-58) of collections, while angling success peaked at 0.52 fish per man hour in 1957-58. However, by 1958-59, shad populations rebounded to 51% of collections and angling success declined to 0.45 fish per man hour. The study concluded that selective rotenone treatment can temporarily improve sport fishing but results are short-lived due to rapid shad population recovery.

## Background & Description

[[Medina Lake]] is located on the [[Medina River]] approximately 25 miles northwest of San Antonio, Texas, within the [[San Antonio River]] drainage system. Completed in December 1912, it is the second oldest major impoundment in Texas. At spillway level, the reservoir has a capacity of 254,000 acre-feet and covers 5,700 surface acres. The lake is owned by the Medina Irrigation Company and was originally designed to irrigate 150,000 acres, though only 34,500 acres were being irrigated by the study period.

The reservoir experiences significant water loss through the fractured Edwards Limestone near the Balcones Fault, where water enters substrata through openings and passageways. During the study period, the lake fluctuated dramatically from an extreme low of only 8 feet depth in January 1956 to above spillway level in March 1957—the first time it had completely filled since 1936.

**Water Quality Characteristics:**
- Surface temperature: 52-95°F
- Spring water temperature: 70-72°F (stable)
- Dissolved oxygen: 5.0-11.5 ppm
- Dissolved CO₂: 0-12.0 ppm (spring water: 19.0 ppm)
- pH: 7.6-9.1 (average 8.6)
- Total alkalinity: 138-270 ppm (typically 138-210 ppm)
- Thermal stratification: June-November, thermocline at 18-30 feet

The lake typically remains clear with dark greenish color due to phytoplankton. Limited submerged vegetation occurs due to fluctuating water levels and phytoplankton blooms. Musk grass (Chara sp.) and broad leaf pondweed (Potamogeton sp.) are present in spring areas and shallow zones. Blue-green and green algae, mostly Spirogyra sp., occur in heavy growths along margins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fish community at [[Medina Lake]] included 30 species documented during the study period. The assemblage was dominated by rough and forage fish species, particularly [[Gizzard Shad]], which comprised the majority of biomass. Game fish species present included [[Channel Catfish]] (Ictalurus punctatus), [[White Bass]] (Roccus chrysops), [[Largemouth Bass]] (Micropterus salmoides), [[White Crappie]] (Pomoxis annularis), [[Bluegill]] (Lepomis macrochirus), and various other sunfishes.

**Pre-Treatment Population Structure (1953-1956):**

In 1953-54, rough fish species comprised 79% by number and 74% by weight of gill net collections. [[Gizzard Shad]] alone accounted for 62% of specimens, with standard lengths ranging 120-300 mm (mean 160 mm), weights 25-395 grams (mean 86 g), and K-factors 1.15-3.00 (mean 1.91). Game fish comprised only 21% by number and 26% by weight.

In 1954-55, gizzard shad decreased slightly to 53% of collections (10% by weight), with mean standard length of 161 mm and mean K-factor of 1.81. Rough fish overall comprised 64% by number and 46% by weight, while game fish increased to 36% by number and 46% by weight.

June 1956 rotenone collections confirmed the imbalance, with gizzard shad comprising 68% of 13,244 specimens collected from four sampling areas.

**Angling Success Pre-Treatment:**
- 1953-54: 0.30 fish per man hour, 27,543 fish (12,331 pounds) harvested
- 1954-55: 0.39 fish per man hour, 38,075 fish (20,338 pounds) harvested

The poor angling success and dominance of gizzard shad indicated an out-of-balance fish population requiring management intervention.

## Key Findings

**Rotenone Treatment (November 1956):**

The lake was treated with synergized rotenone liquid (2.5% rotenone + 2.5% sulfoxide synergist) at 0.13 ppm when the lake covered approximately 550 acres (9.6% of capacity area) and contained 5,568 acre-feet (2% of capacity volume) with maximum depth of 27 feet. The lake was divided into three sections for treatment: the deep area near the dam received two-thirds of toxicant sprayed on surface and one-third pumped to 10-15 feet depth; the middle section received half the dosage initially and half two hours later; the shallow upper third received the entire dosage in one surface application.

**Treatment Results:**
- Estimated 27,500 pounds of gizzard shad removed
- Estimated 10,000 pounds of carp removed
- Total: 68 pounds of rough fish per acre
- Less than 3% of fish killed were game fish (mainly [[White Bass]] and larger adult [[Largemouth Bass]])
- Cost: $1,165.00 total ($0.176 per acre-foot for toxicant, $0.033 per acre-foot for application)

**Post-Treatment Population Dynamics:**

*November 1956-June 1957 (7 months):*
- Gizzard shad: 33% of collections (5% by weight), mean standard length increased to 180 mm, mean K-factor increased to 2.22
- Rough fish overall: 72% by number, 87% by weight (inflated by [[Longnose Gar]] predators)
- Game fish showed major increases in mean K-factors: channel catfish, white bass, largemouth bass, and bluegills all exhibited improved physical condition
- Angling success: 0.28 fish per man hour (decreased due to trotline fishing predominance and erroneous public belief that no fish remained)

*July 1957-June 1958:*
- Gizzard shad: 15% of collections (5% by weight), mean standard length 198 mm, mean K-factor 1.97
- Rough fish overall: 39% by number, 62% by weight
- [[White Bass]] became dominant species at 45% of collections (21% by weight), reflecting successful 1957 spawn
- Angling success: 0.52 fish per man hour (peak success)
- Fishermen interviewed increased 70% (1,687 to 2,834)
- Anglers using lake increased 40% (3,646 to 5,050 on census days)
- Estimated harvest: 50,130 fish (30,078 pounds)
- Two anglers caught 50 largemouth bass weighing 48 pounds in two hours (many young bass weighed 1-1.5 pounds)

*July 1958-June 1959:*
- Gizzard shad: 51% of collections (15% by weight), mean standard length decreased to 172 mm, mean K-factor decreased to 1.77
- Rough fish overall: 67% by number, 66% by weight
- Angling success: 0.45 fish per man hour (declined)
- Estimated harvest: 23,363 fish (10,280 pounds)—decreased >50% from previous year
- Fishermen decreased to 15,575 (from 22,480)

**Physical Condition Indices:**

The coefficient of physical condition (K-factor) showed significant patterns. Gizzard shad K-factors increased from 1.91 (1953-54) to 2.22 (1956-57) immediately post-treatment as smaller individuals were removed, then declined to 1.97 (1957-58) and 1.77 (1958-59) as the population rebounded with smaller fish. Game fish species showed improved K-factors in 1956-57, with mixed trends thereafter as competition increased.

**Special Circumstances:**

The treatment of [[Medina Lake]] represented a special case because heavy runoff shortly after treatment refilled the lake to capacity for the first time since 1936. This created conditions more similar to a newly filled reservoir with an exploding fish population than an old reservoir being chemically renovated. The refilling coincided with [[White Bass]] spawning season, and good runoff provided optimum spawning conditions in the river above the lake, ensuring an abundant predator population to help maintain reduced shad populations longer.

**Management Conclusions:**

1. Angling success varies inversely with gizzard shad abundance
2. Selective rotenone treatment can temporarily improve sport fishing and fish physical condition
3. Results are short-lived (2-3 years) due to high reproductive potential of gizzard shad
4. Treatment is expensive, especially for large reservoirs at capacity ($53,086 estimated cost if Medina Lake had been at full capacity vs. $1,165 actual cost at low water)
5. Frequent retreatment would be necessary at 3-4 year intervals for sustained benefits

**Recommendations:**

The technique of selective gizzard shad removal should be confined to:
- Smaller reservoirs
- Reservoirs greatly reduced in volume by drought or other causes
- Timing just prior to reservoir refilling when possible
- Followed by heavy plantings of predator species ([[White Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]])

The study demonstrated that while chemical manipulation of fish populations can provide temporary improvements in sport fishing, the technique has limited long-term effectiveness in large reservoirs due to rapid population recovery of undesirable species and high treatment costs.
