# Gray Wolf Restoration in the Northern Rocky Mountains (1995–1998)

#mammalogy #canidae #canis-lupus #wildlife-management

## Executive Summary

This paper reviews the status of [[Gray Wolf]] ([[Canis Lupus]]) restoration in Montana, Idaho, and Wyoming through 1 November 1998. Wolves were eliminated from the region by the 1930s and returned naturally from southwestern Canada. Following the [[Recovery Plan]] (1987), wolves were reintroduced to [[Central Idaho]] and [[Yellowstone National Park]] in 1995 and 1996 as nonessential [[Experimental Population]]s under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). By late 1998 the three recovery areas held an estimated 65+ wolves in [[Northwestern Montana]], 122+ in central Idaho, and 115 in the [[Greater Yellowstone Area]] (GYA). The authors report that recovery proceeded faster and more cheaply than predicted, while livestock losses stayed low and human-caused mortality remained the dominant threat. The paper also documents litigation, illegal killings, wolf-dog hybrid problems, and the politics of state involvement.

## Background & Description

- **Historical extirpation and return:** Wolves were eliminated from Montana, Idaho, Wyoming, and adjacent southwestern Canada by the 1930s. Canadian populations expanded southward after human-caused mortality was regulated in the 1950s. Dispersers reached the northern Rockies but had no legal protection there until 1974.
- **Natural recolonization:** In 1985 wolves from Canada raised pups in [[Glacier National Park]], Montana, and a small population became established.
- **Recovery Plan (1987):** Three primary recovery areas were identified: northwestern Montana (>50,000 km²), central Idaho (53,900 km²), and the GYA (about 64,000 km²). The biological goal was more than 10 breeding pairs in each area for 3 successive years.
- **Reintroduction:**
  - January 1995: 15 wolves from Alberta were released in central Idaho; January 1996: 20 more from British Columbia were added.
  - January 1995: 14 Alberta wolves (3 family groups) were placed in pens in Yellowstone; January 1996: 17 British Columbia wolves (4 family groups) followed.
  - Wolves were designated as nonessential experimental populations to increase management flexibility.
- **Prey and livestock context:** Each area supports an estimated 100,000–250,000 wild ungulates ([[Elk]], [[White-Tailed Deer]], [[Mule Deer]], [[Moose]], and bison in the GYA only). Domestic cattle and sheep are at least twice as numerous, even on public lands. Most wolf pack territories overlap livestock use, primarily cattle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and prey.** Recovery areas were chosen for large blocks of public land, abundant wild ungulates, and minimal livestock. Yellowstone and wilderness areas in central Idaho and northwestern Montana were the only livestock-free areas large enough to hold packs. In the GYA, wolves prey primarily on elk (more than 90% of kills), with kill rates reported at 1.2–7.5 ungulates/wolf/year, which is in line with or slightly above the 12 ungulates/wolf/year range predicted in the EIS [[Environmental Impact Statement]].

**Population growth and dispersal.**
- Northwestern Montana grew about 22% annually to about 88 wolves in 7 packs by 1993, then plateaued and apparently declined. The 1998 estimate is more than 65 wolves in 6 reproducing packs. The authors attribute part of the decline to reduced monitoring and to a severe 1995–1997 winter that cut [[White-Tailed Deer]] numbers.
- Central Idaho reached more than 122 wolves, including 10 packs that produced pups in 1998. The EIS had predicted 56 wolves in 6–9 packs.
- The GYA reached 115 wolves in 7 packs that produced 10 litters in autumn 1998.
- Dispersal from reintroduced packs was expected to expand distribution. Dispersers have moved into areas of private livestock production. No successful wolf movement between the GYA and the other areas had been documented by 1998, although movement among the three populations is expected.

**Reproduction and genetics.** Northwestern Montana litters averaged 5.3 pups (range 1–9). Central Idaho litter size (n = 19) averaged 5 pups (range 2–9). GYA litters averaged 4.6 pups (range 1–11), and five packs produced multiple litters over three breeding seasons. Genetic analysis (DNA microsatellites) found high genetic variation in all three populations. The mixed Canadian founder stock was thought to reduce concerns about genetic viability.

**Mortality.**
- Annual survival of non-neonatal wolves in northwestern Montana and adjacent Canada (1984–1995) was 0.80 (residents 0.84, dispersers 0.66). Of non-neonatal mortality, 84% was human-caused.
- Of 58 radiocollared wolves in northwestern Montana that died since 1987, 49 (84%) were killed by humans.
- Relocated depredating wolves had the highest human-caused mortality (96%). Only 2 of 28 relocated females survived to reproduce.
- In the GYA, humans caused 68% of radiocollared wolf deaths. Natural causes included other wolves, prey, avalanches, and old age.
- Overall, 45 of 135 radiocollared wolves (33%) had died, with 68% of deaths human-caused. Annual mortality stayed below the 20% level predicted in the EIS.

**Wolf-livestock conflicts.** Confirmed losses in northwestern Montana (1987–1998) averaged 5 cattle, 4 sheep, and fewer than 1 dog per year. Central Idaho losses over four grazing seasons were 15 cattle and 64 sheep (compensation over $13,800). GYA losses from 1995 to 1998 were 8 cattle, 84 sheep, and 4 dogs (compensation over $18,000). These losses were well below EIS predictions. Montana producers reported losing an average of 142,000 sheep and 96,000 cattle annually from all causes (1985–1991), an estimated $45,000,000 in losses.

## Key Findings

### Management and control
- **Wolf control** was the leading cause of death in northwestern Montana. Control options ranged from monitoring and aversive conditioning to relocation, lethal removal, and "hard" or "soft" release.
- The authors recommended that depredating wolves in northwestern Montana be killed rather than relocated. Of 28 relocated wolves, 25 (89%) either died or attacked livestock again before breeding, and relocated wolves accounted for 5 cattle (5%) and 4 sheep (10%) killed later.
- In central Idaho, wolves relocated after depredations did not attack livestock again, and only 2 wolves died from control actions.
- A private [[Compensation Program]] administered by [[Defenders of Wildlife]] paid ranchers in northwestern Montana about $35,100 since 1987.

### Cost and economics
- GYA and central Idaho restoration (1995–1998) was estimated at $5,480,000. Actual federal costs were $4,631,800 (less than 85% of the EIS prediction).
- Total federal recovery costs from 1973 to 1998 were $10,225,000. The estimate for 1973–2002, through de-listing, is about $15,000,000.
- The EIS estimated negative economic costs of $1,728,000–$3,930,000 per year from a recovered population, and possible tourism-related benefits of up to $23,000,000 per year in the GYA.
- Recovery costs fell because reintroduction goals were met in 2 years instead of the 3–5 years planned.

### Policy and staffing
- The governors of Montana, Idaho, and Wyoming signed a memorandum of understanding (19 August 1997). The states declined direct wolf management until the species was removed from ESA protection and agreed to develop a tri-state plan by 2000.
- The USFWS wolf recovery staff was cut from 5 to 2 in 1995. The Nez Perce Tribe began managing wolves in Idaho under a cooperative agreement in 1996. National Park Service and USDA Wildlife Services personnel assumed most GYA management.
- The USFWS planned to propose reclassification of gray wolves in the lower 48 states in 1999 (de-listing in much of the US, downlisting to threatened elsewhere), with de-listing expected around 2002.

### Wolf-dog hybrids
- Two female wolf-dog hybrids were found in the wild south of Yellowstone, and another was observed in Wyoming. DNA and physical evidence confirmed hybrid status. Hybrids are hard to tell apart from wild wolves, and unofficial estimates suggest about 400,000 hybrids are privately owned in the US.
- Hybrid cases complicated enforcement. A proposed 1997 rule to manage non-wild wolves was never finalized after a Wyoming court declared the special rules illegal.

### Litigation and enforcement
- A Wyoming federal court (12 December 1997) ruled that the experimental rules illegally reduced protection for dispersing endangered wolves, and ordered reintroduced wolves removed. The court later clarified that the wolves should not be killed, and the ruling was stayed pending appeal to the 10th Circuit.
- The 9th Circuit upheld a Montana conviction for illegally killing a reintroduced wolf (U.S. v. McKittrick, 1998) and found the experimental rule legal. The Supreme Court declined to review the case on 11 January 1999. The 9th Circuit decision is not binding on the 10th Circuit.
- A Diamond G Ranch owner sued in spring 1998 over calf losses and missing livestock, citing Fifth Amendment protections. Several uncollared wolves were later reported on the ranch.
- Twelve wolves were illegally killed in the experimental areas and 9 in northwestern Montana. Fines ranged from $500 to $1,500 when defendants cooperated; one defendant received a $10,000 restitution order and jail time.

### Conclusions and outlook
- Wolf populations in the GYA and central Idaho were established within 2 years rather than the 3–5 years planned. In the GYA, 133 pups were born in 29 litters within 4 years, and 95 (71%) were probably alive.
- The authors expect the three populations to function as a single metapopulation, but note that conflicts will increase as dispersers settle on private land.
- The wolf is "generally viewed as successful." The authors predict continued controversy, with the species' long-term persistence in the northern Rockies depending on public tolerance.
