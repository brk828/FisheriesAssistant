# Ecology of Natural Disturbance and Patch Dynamics

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by [[Robert C. Vrijenhoek]], appears in *The Ecology of Natural Disturbance and Patch Dynamics* (1985, edited by [[S. T. A. Pickett]] and [[P. S. White]]). It argues that natural disturbances such as fire, drought, flood, and population crashes reshape the genetic structure of patchily distributed animal populations. Through genetic drift, bottlenecks, and founder events, disturbance can reduce [[Heterozygosity]] and, through inbreeding depression, lower fitness in the short term. Over longer periods, the increased variance among local populations may accelerate adaptive evolution through [[Interdemic Selection]] within the [[Shifting Balance Theory]]. The argument is illustrated mainly with long-term work on the freshwater livebearer [[Poeciliopsis Monacha]] in the Mexican Sonoran desert, supplemented by a review of heterozygosity–fitness studies in oysters, salamanders, lizards, trout, and plants.

## Background & Description

The chapter is a theoretical and empirical synthesis rather than a single study. Its author has studied natural disturbance and genetic variation in small freshwater fish of the genus [[Poeciliopsis]] (Poeciliidae) for over 10 years. These fish live in partially isolated springs and temporary arroyos of the Sonoran desert that dry out in many years. The chapter explicitly does not aim to review the full theoretical literature on genetic preservation in heterogeneous environments; it emphasizes temporal variation in the size of patchy local populations and its consequences for heterozygosity, immediate fitness, and long-term adaptive potential.

Key concepts introduced include:
- **Effective population size (Ne):** Reduced below the census number of adults by inbreeding, unequal sex ratios, variance in reproductive output, and fluctuating population size. For a fluctuating population, Ne is the harmonic mean over generations. The example given: a population cycling from 1000 to 1 million once every 10 generations has a harmonic mean Ne of 1111, while one held at 1000 for 9 generations then dropping to 10 for 1 generation has Ne of 91.7, showing that crashes affect effective size far more than flushes.
- **Heterozygosity decay:** Loss per generation is 1/(2Ne), giving H_t = H_0(1 − 1/2Ne)^t.
- **Breeding systems:** Selfing and cloning differ in their effects on heterozygosity, though both preserve genotype fidelity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Natural disturbances create a mosaic of colonies that fluctuate with environmental conditions, and gaps created by extinction and recolonization allow genetic differences to accumulate among local populations. In the Poeciliopsis system, dry-season crowding leaves most residual pools with only a few hundred fish (mark-release-recapture data, [[Eisenbrey and Moore]] 1981). Streams desiccate in most years, causing local extinctions, and populations later disperse from permanent springs during the rainy season. The main factors shaping genetic structure are the number of dry-season refugia and the rates of extinction and migration among springs.

Comparative examples in the chapter include:
- **Oysters** (*Crassostrea virginica*): epidemic diseases such as [[Haplosporidium Nelsoni]] can leave patchy survivor populations.
- **Tiger salamanders** (*Ambystoma tigrinum*), side-blotched lizards (*Uta stansburiana*), killifish (*Fundulus heteroclitus*), monarch butterflies, and rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*) were used in heterozygosity–growth or heterozygosity–asymmetry studies.
- **Plants**: quaking aspen, pines, and *Liatris cylindracea* show heterozygosity–growth or heterozygosity–size relationships in some studies but not others.
- **Self-fertilizing fish** (*Rivulus marmoratus*) are isogenic across strains; a survey of 12 strains at 31 loci found no heterozygosity within strains.

## Key Findings

- **Disturbance and drift:** In small, isolated populations, genetic drift reduces variability, and relatively deleterious alleles can drift to appreciable frequencies, depressing fitness.
- **Frequent extinction–colonization** reduces total effective size of a metapopulation and causes loss of genetic diversity among local populations.
- **Linkage disequilibrium** created by bottlenecks and founder events can persist for long periods despite gene flow, forming the basis of Wright's shifting balance model.
- **Heterozygosity and fitness:** Inbreeding depression reflects the dominance theory (masking of recessive deleterious mutations) more than overdominance, which has little experimental support. Heterozygosity-fitness relations in natural populations are difficult to establish because of confounding by history, chromosomal effects, and environment.
- **Poeciliopsis monacha, Rio Fuerte:** Average heterozygosity was about 4.5% of 25 gene loci, versus 1.7% in the Rio Mayo and 0% in the Rio Sinaloa. In a 1975 tributary sample, 5 of 25 loci were polymorphic. The upper Arroyo de los Platanos population was extirpated by drought in spring 1976 and recolonized from downstream. Its heterozygosity fell to 0.1% (upper), versus 4.5% (downstream) and 7.0% (mainstream) in 1978.
- **Developmental instability:** Fluctuating asymmetry of all eight measured bilateral characters increased significantly in the homozygous founder population relative to downstream populations. A clonal control, P. monacha-lucida (H = 52.0%), showed no consistent upstream trend, suggesting that asymmetry reflects loss of homeostasis rather than environmental gradient.
- **Competition:** Since 1976 recolonization, P. monacha has rarely exceeded 10% of fish in the upper Platanos, and a triploid clone (Clone I) has nearly displaced it. Downstream numbers were stable.
- **Breeding systems:** Selfing and cloning provide reproductive assurance for colonists. Sexual outcrossers with a history of drift may be purged of mutational loads, giving them a temporary advantage over panmictic species in patchy environments. Inbred P. lucida lines from one isolated population showed no inbreeding depression in temperature tolerance, but other inbred lines were less robust.
- **Long-term outcome:** The shifting balance process is accelerated in populations of numerous small colonies with repeated extinction and colonization, but rapid patch turnover may keep most colonies in adaptive valleys. The author concludes that short-term local fitness may decline through drift, while long-term adaptive evolution may accelerate through interdemic selection, depending on disturbance timescales and the balance among drift, migration, and selection.
- **Future work:** Planned tests include transplanting about 30 highly heterozygous P. monacha into upper Platanos pools (March 1983) and laboratory studies of heterozygosity–growth relations.
