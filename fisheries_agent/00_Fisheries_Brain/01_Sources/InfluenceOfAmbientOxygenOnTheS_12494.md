# Influence of Ambient Oxygen on the Swimming Performance of Goldfish and Rainbow Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #carassius-auratus #salmo-gairdneri

## Executive Summary

This 1968 *Canadian Journal of Zoology* paper by M. Narayanan Kutty tests how ambient dissolved oxygen limits the sustained swimming effort of [[Goldfish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]. Fish were forced to swim against controlled currents in a [[Blazka Respirometer]] while oxygen was lowered until each fish could no longer hold position. Goldfish were acclimated to [[Air Saturation]] (95%) or to about 15% air saturation; rainbow trout were acclimated to air saturation only. The study finds that swimming effort falls sharply as oxygen drops, that low-oxygen acclimation does not shift this relationship in goldfish, and that rainbow trout need more oxygen than goldfish to sustain the same speed.

## Background & Description

Earlier work established that the active metabolism of fishes is limited by ambient oxygen, but few studies had linked sustained swimming speed to oxygen concentration. The author notes that this gap was pointed out by Doudoroff (1957) and Macan (1963). The study uses two species:

- **Goldfish** (*Carassius auratus*), tested at 20 °C. Fish were 17.6–19.0 cm long, weighing about 91–128 g.
- **Rainbow trout** (*Salmo gairdneri*), tested at 15 °C. The single fish was 1.5 years old, 20.2 cm long, and weighed 90 g.

Fish were starved for 36 hours before testing (following [[Beamish 1964]]) and rested at least 12 hours after handling, usually overnight in the apparatus. Oxygen was varied by bubbling nitrogen through tap water flowing down a glass-marble column into the respirometer. Oxygen was measured with a dropping mercury electrode. The [[Critical Oxygen Level]] for each speed was the mean of two readings: one when the fish first fell back onto the shocker grid and one when it was clearly unable to swim steadily.

Swimming effort is expressed in body lengths per second (L./sec). Speeds tested ranged from about 18.6 to 74.0 cm/sec, or roughly 1 to 3.7 L./sec.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper places its results within the wider literature on oxygen and fish activity:

- **[[Katz et al. 1959]]** reported that juvenile coho and chinook salmon swam for 24–28 hours at 24–36 cm/sec in water near 3 ppm oxygen at 20 °C, close to lethal levels. Katz et al. did not determine critical oxygen levels; the present study did, for four to five speeds per species.
- **[[Whitworth and Irwin 1964]]** tested survival of nine fish species at moderate speeds in low oxygen for 6 hours or less, but without recording actual swimming speeds, so direct comparison was not possible.
- **[[Graham 1949]]** found a steep decline in cruising speed of speckled trout below about 6 ppm at 8 °C.
- **[[Ferguson Study]]** (in Fry 1957) found sharp reductions in cruising speed of yellow perch (*Perca flavescens*) below about 3 ppm.
- **[[Davis et al. 1963]]** found that sustained swimming speeds of juvenile [[Coho Salmon]] and [[Chinook Salmon]] at 10–20 °C were reduced at all oxygen levels below air saturation.
- **[[Shepard 1955]]** examined lethal oxygen limits and low-oxygen acclimation in speckled trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*). Shepard and [[Prosser et al. 1957]] reported that low-oxygen acclimation raises blood oxygen capacity in fish.

The author notes that the present curves for goldfish and rainbow trout correspond to the steeper parts of these earlier curves, despite differences in methods.

## Key Findings

**Oxygen and swimming effort**

- **Goldfish (air saturation, 20 °C):** swimming effort rose from about 1 L./sec at about 1 ppm oxygen to about 3 L./sec at about 2 ppm. Table I lists 10 tests; critical oxygen values ranged from 0.70 to 1.84 ppm across speeds of 18.6 to 59.2 cm/sec.
- **Rainbow trout (air saturation, 15 °C):** the rise was much steeper. Effort increased from 1 L./sec at 2 ppm to 4 L./sec at 2.5 ppm. Table II lists 10 values, with critical oxygen between 1.94 and 2.54 ppm across speeds of 30.1 to 74.0 cm/sec.
- **Sensitivity:** a change of less than 0.5 ppm within the tested range limited trout effort about fourfold.
- **Species comparison:** goldfish critical levels were lower than trout levels at low efforts (about 1 L./sec). The difference shrank at higher efforts. The author links trout's higher oxygen requirement to its limited tolerance of oxygen deficiency, citing [[Parry and Holiday 1960]] and Kutty (in press).

**Effect of low-oxygen acclimation**

- Goldfish acclimated to about 15% air saturation (1.4 ± 0.5 ppm) showed no marked difference from goldfish acclimated to air saturation. Table III lists critical levels of 0.75 to 1.98 ppm across the groups.
- Two subgroups were tested: fish kept overnight in the chamber, and fish tested within 1–7 hours after handling.
- The author concludes that acclimation to low oxygen does not change the swimming effort–oxygen relationship. This suggests that the oxygen response is mediated by a behavioral rather than a respiratory physiological mechanism. Acclimation-driven gains in blood oxygen capacity therefore have no value for swimming effort.

**Handling effects**

- Because [[Black and Barrett 1957]] showed that handling raises muscle lactic acid, the author checked whether handled fish swam worse. Five of seven handled-fish points fell within the range of the other goldfish values. Two points, taken 100 and 285 minutes after handling, lay outside that range.
- A t-test at the same effort (1.6 L./sec) found no significant difference between handled and overnight-rested groups.

**Mechanism of swimming failure**

- Failure at the critical oxygen level is probably not due to [[Fatigue]], because fish resumed steady swimming immediately once oxygen rose.
- The author suggests an [[Oxygen-Sensing Mechanism]] may cause the fish to stop, but notes there is no direct evidence for one (Jones 1952). Observations by Whitmore et al. (1960) and McFarland and Moss (1967) hint at such a mechanism, but further study is needed.

**Limitations**

- Only one rainbow trout was tested, so trout results rest on a single individual.
- Tests at higher efforts were needed to fully define the relationship, according to the author.
