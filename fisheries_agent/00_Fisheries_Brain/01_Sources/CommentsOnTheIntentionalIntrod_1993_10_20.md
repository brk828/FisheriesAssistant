## Executive Summary

This document is a formal scientific commentary letter from the [[American Society Of Ichthyologists And Herpetologists]] (ASIH) Environmental Quality Committee and the [[Desert Fishes Council]], addressed to the [[Aquatic Nuisance Species Task Force]] (ANS Task Force) regarding their draft report to Congress on intentional introductions of nonindigenous aquatic species. Rather than presenting original morphological or ecological field data, the letter provides expert ichthyological critique of policy definitions, taxonomic terminology, and management recommendations concerning the introduction of [[Nonindigenous Fish Species]] into U.S. waters. Key scientific points raised include concerns over species/subspecies definitions, the scale of global fish biodiversity relative to enforcement feasibility, and case-specific examples such as [[Brown Trout]] establishment in the [[Madison River]], Montana.

## Background & Description

Not applicable in the traditional sense — this is a policy commentary document rather than a species description or morphological study. However, the letter references **taxonomic classification issues** directly relevant to ichthyological practice:

- **Species/Subspecies Definition Dispute**: The committee objects to the ANS Task Force's use of "populations or stocks" as taxonomic units, noting that in zoological taxonomy, only the **subspecies** category is formally recognized below species level — not "populations," "stocks," or "demes."
- Committee members acknowledge the Task Force's intent to protect **genetically distinct populations** (demes) but recommend removing the term "taxonomic" from the report to avoid conflating informal genetic units with formal [[Taxonomic Nomenclature]].
- Reference to **transgenic organisms** — the Task Force's inclusion of genetically engineered/transgenic organisms under the definition of "nonindigenous species" is explicitly endorsed by the committee.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The letter addresses ecosystem-level and biogeographic concerns central to invasion biology and native fish conservation:

- **Hawaii as a Nonindigenous Species Hotspot**: The letter emphasizes that [[Hawaii]] should be highlighted alongside Florida and California as a critical case, containing **34 established nonindigenous fish species** — more than any other U.S. state.
- **Ecosystem Definition Ambiguity**: Dr. W.L. Minckley's critique highlights that the Task Force fails to rigorously define "ecosystem," inconsistently applying the term to political units (e.g., "State of Washington"), habitat types (e.g., "Chesapeake Bay" estuary), and river systems (e.g., "[[Madison River]], Montana").
- **Case Study — Brown Trout in the Madison River**: Cited as an example of an already-established nonindigenous species ([[Brown Trout]], *Salmo trutta*) that would be exempt from new regulatory recommendations because it is not a "new" introduction — despite ongoing ecological impacts.
- **Interjurisdictional Watershed Dynamics**: Minckley stresses that nonindigenous species dispersal is rarely confined to single political jurisdictions due to natural hydrological connectivity and human-assisted dispersal (e.g., by sport fishermen), necessitating interjurisdictional management panels.
- **Desert Aquatic Ecosystems**: Implicit focus via the [[Desert Fishes Council]]'s co-authorship, reflecting concern for fragile, isolated desert fish habitats vulnerable to nonindigenous species introductions.

## Key Findings

- **Global Fish Species Diversity**: The best estimates place the number of living fish species between **22,000 and 28,000** — exceeding the combined total of all other vertebrate species. This immense diversity is cited as a core scientific argument against a national "clean list" (approved species list) approach, which the committee deems "clearly unenforceable."
- **Undescribed Species Prevalence**: In some freshwater regions, an estimated **>25% of fish species remain scientifically undescribed**, compounding the impracticality of static approved-species lists.
- **Support for Regional Flexibility**: The committee endorses the Task Force's rejection of a uniform national "clean list," agreeing that regional environmental variation makes a species appropriate in one region but potentially invasive in another.
- **Recommendation 3A Endorsement**: Strong support for subjecting introductions into interstate drainages — including introductions into "isolated" waters with potential for interstate spread — to [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) review.
- **Federal Funding Restrictions (Recommendation 3C)**: Committee members recommend prohibiting the use of Federal funds for nonindigenous species introductions altogether.
- **Weaknesses in AFS Protocol (Recommendation 5)**: The 1986 American Fisheries Society (AFS) introduction protocol (Kohler & Courtenay 1986) is criticized as weaker than the 1973 AFS policy, which included multi-stage review and a **veto mechanism** for proposed introductions — removed in the 1986 revision due to anticipated opposition.
- **Interjurisdictional Panels (Recommendations 6A–6C)**: Support for mandatory interjurisdictional review panels, with a call for inclusion of **independent, non-agency-affiliated professional biologists** to ensure objectivity — a specification absent from the draft recommendation.
- **Legacy Introductions Gap**: A major critique (Minckley) is that the report's recommendations apply only to *new* introductions, neglecting the ecological, ethical, and legal ("Endangered Species Act") complications posed by **already-established nonindigenous species**, particularly in the context of sportfish vs. native fish management conflicts.
- **Federal Import Permitting Concerns (Recommendation 4A)**: While supportive of a Federal import permitting system, the committee flags potential unintended burdens on scientific researchers importing live specimens for non-introduction (research) purposes.
