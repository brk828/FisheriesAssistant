# The Comparative Food Habits of *Nocomis biguttatus* and *Nocomis micropogon*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #food-habits

## Executive Summary

This 1950 study by Ernest A. Lachner compares the food habits of two closely related cyprinid fishes — the [[Hornyhead Chub]] (*Nocomis biguttatus*) and the [[River Chub]] (*Nocomis micropogon*) — collected over four months (June–September, spanning 1939–1940) from [[Sandy Creek]], a tributary in the Lake Ontario drainage, Monroe County, New York. Stomach contents from 607 specimens (470 containing food), ranging from young-of-year to 3-year-old adults, were analyzed across defined age classes. The study found that both species consume a broadly similar diet dominated by aquatic insect larvae (especially [[Chironomus]], [[Simulium]], and the caddisfly [[Helicopsyche]]), along with crayfish (*Cambarus propinquus*) and substantial amounts of filamentous algae (*Cladophora*) and vascular plant material. Diet composition shifted markedly with age/size: young fish relied heavily on Ostracoda, Cladocera, and small insect larvae, while older juveniles and adults consumed more crayfish and plant matter. *N. micropogon* consistently ingested more filamentous algae than *N. biguttatus* despite habitat preferences that would suggest the opposite. A pronounced increase in empty stomachs during midsummer was linked to an observed compact "schooling" behavior in both species.

## Background & Description

- **Body size range sampled:** 27–150 mm standard length (S.L.), spanning young-of-year to 3-year-old adults.
- **Age classes examined:** 0+ (young), 1+ (juveniles, second summer), and 2+ or older (adults), determined via scale analysis.
- **Intestinal morphology (relevant to diet):**
  - Length of distended intestine relative to standard length (5 adults examined):
    - *N. biguttatus*: range 1.2–1.4, average 1.3
    - *N. micropogon*: range 1.0–1.4, average 1.2
  - Both species possess short intestines and raptorial (hooked) pharyngeal teeth lacking grinding surfaces — morphology consistent with a primarily carnivorous/insectivorous diet rather than herbivory, despite substantial plant material found in stomachs (see [[Pharyngeal Teeth Morphology in Cyprinidae]]).
- **Size differential between species:** At comparable collection dates, *N. micropogon* specimens were consistently longer than co-occurring *N. biguttatus* (e.g., mean S.L. 4.4 mm greater in September young; 20.4 mm greater in June juveniles; adults averaged 122.6 mm vs. 99.8 mm respectively).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat (Sandy Creek):** Clear stream, 50–150 ft wide, flow 50–100 cfs, sandstone bedrock bottom with gravel/rubble riffles every 100–150 yards; pools up to 3 ft deep below riffles; water temperatures 70–80°F in summer. Dense filamentous algae (*Cladophora*) and *Potamogeton* beds are characteristic, more so than typical *micropogon* habitat elsewhere.
- **Co-occurring species:** *Notropis cornutus chrysocephalus* (Rafinesque) — the three species (with the two *Nocomis*) were the most abundant fish taken by seining, in roughly equal numbers.
- **Habitat partitioning:**
  - *N. biguttatus* young are more common in algal/vascular-plant beds of slower water.
  - *N. micropogon* young favor open, faster-flowing water and generally avoid heavy vegetation.
  - Across their range, *N. micropogon* typically occupies larger streams than *N. biguttatus* (e.g., in the Allegheny River, PA).
- **Seasonal behavior:**
  - June: dispersed in riffles, shallow pools, and algal growth; caught in small numbers.
  - Midsummer (July–August): observed forming compact, easily visible schools in pools below riffles — correlated with high rates of empty stomachs.
  - September: more solitary and secretive, especially larger adults, often found under slab rocks.
- **Trophic position:** Primarily benthic insectivore/omnivore; nearly all food items were benthic in origin except Ostracoda and Cladocera consumed by young-of-year. See [[Benthic Invertebrate Feeding Guild]].

## Key Findings

1. **Overall diet composition:** Diet dominated by aquatic Insecta, Crustacea, and Mollusca, with substantial filamentous algae (*Cladophora*) and vascular plant remains (*Anacharis*, *Vallisneria*, *Potamogeton*) also ingested — likely secondarily with animal prey.
2. **Most frequent prey items across both species:** [[Simulium]] (black-fly larvae), [[Chironomus]] (midge larvae), [[Helicopsyche]] (caddisfly larvae), various Ephemeroptera (mayfly nymphs), and *Cambarus propinquus* (crayfish).
3. **Taxonomic breadth:** Eight insect orders in *N. biguttatus* diet; ten in *N. micropogon*. Only Homoptera, Lepidoptera, and Hymenoptera were terrestrial in origin; remainder aquatic.
4. **Age-related dietary shift:**
   - Young-of-year (July): heavy reliance on Ostracoda (up to 66.7% of stomachs, 36.1% of volume in *biguttatus*) and Cladocera.
   - Juveniles (second summer): insects dominate (53–72% of volume), with *Helicopsyche*, Chironomus, and Simulium larvae most prominent.
   - Adults (2+ years): insect volume drops sharply (12.2% in *biguttatus*, 32.5% in *micropogon*); crayfish (*Cambarus*) and plant material become dominant (crayfish in 33.3% and 29.2% of stomachs respectively; plants comprising 55.6% and 44.1% of volume).
5. **Algae consumption differential:** *N. micropogon* consistently consumed markedly more filamentous algae than *N. biguttatus* across all comparable age groups (e.g., late-summer juveniles: 56.8% vs. 29.1% of volume) — a counterintuitive finding given *biguttatus*'s stronger association with algal-rich microhabitats.
6. **Midsummer schooling and reduced feeding:** High incidence of empty stomachs during July–August (as low as 25.6% with food in *biguttatus*) coincided with observed compact schooling behavior in pools, suggesting reduced food availability or competition within schools. This pattern was absent in June and September collections.
7. **Minimal interspecific dietary divergence:** Despite differing habitat preferences, the two species showed largely similar prey-type profiles; differences were mainly of degree (e.g., algae intake) rather than kind.
8. **Comparison to prior literature:** Findings align with [[Breder and Crawford 1922]] regarding minimal dietary specialization relative to gut morphology, and contrast with [[Forbes 1883]]'s hypothesis linking elongated intestines to increased plant consumption — this study found intestine lengths similar between species (~1.2–1.3× S.L.) despite differing algae intake.
