# Extinctions of North American Fishes During the Past Century

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Robert R. Miller]], [[James D. Williams]] and [[Jack E. Williams]] (*Fisheries*, Vol. 14, Nos. 5-6, 1989) compiles a comprehensive list of recently extinct North American fishes and gives a detailed account of each extinction. The authors document **40 taxa**, comprising **27 species, 13 subspecies and 3 genera**, lost during the past 100 years. Extinctions occurred in every area except northern Canada and Alaska. The greatest losses were in the [[Great Lakes]], [[Great Basin]], [[Parras Valley]], the [[Valley of Mexico]] and the [[Rio Grande]] basin.

The main drivers were habitat loss, introduced species, chemical pollution, hybridization and overharvesting. Because extinctions are hard to confirm, the authors caution that the true number is likely higher, and that the rate of loss is expected to increase.

## Background & Description

Freshwater fishes across North America have been fragmented, polluted or destroyed. These losses are reflected in growing lists of endangered fishes and ecosystems. The [[American Fisheries Society]] lists 103 fish taxa as endangered, 114 as threatened and 147 as of special concern (Williams et al. 1989).

Confirming extinction is difficult. A species may not be found in a single survey, and small or cryptic fishes that live in hard-to-sample habitats are especially hard to document. Several taxa were presumed extinct and later rediscovered:

- **[[Owens Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon radiosus*) was rediscovered in 1964 in Fish Slough, California.
- **[[Shoshone Pupfish]]** (*C. nevadensis shoshone*) was rediscovered in 1986 after surveys had failed to find a population reduced to very small numbers.

Rediscovery is becoming less likely for many taxa. Populations that persist at very low numbers may also lose much of their genetic variation.

The authors defined the scope of the review to include fishes presumed extinct on the basis of numerous negative surveys, and poorly known taxa whose extinction is presumed and whose rediscovery appears impossible. Extinct taxa are grouped by family. The minnow family Cyprinidae lost the most members (15 taxa). Salmonids and cyprinodontids each lost seven taxa.

Representative extinct taxa include:

- **[[Miller Lake Lamprey]]** (*Lampetra minima*): a dwarf parasitic lamprey from Oregon, last collected on 17 July 1952. Its decline was linked to poisoning with ichthyocides in the 1950s.
- **[[Blue Pike]]** (*Stizostedion vitreum glaucum*): once a major commercial fish of Lake Erie. Annual catches of 5 to 10 million kg were recorded until the mid-1950s, falling to 35,834 kg by 1959. The last confirmed specimen was taken in 1965.
- **[[Silver Trout]]** (*Salvelinus agassizi*): endemic to the Connecticut River drainage. The last record from Christine Lake was in 1926, and from Dublin Pond in 1930.
- **[[Tecopa Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon nevadensis calidae*): extinct by late 1970 after hot-spring habitats were modified and hybridized with the Amargosa pupfish. It was the first creature officially declared extinct under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973.
- **[[June Sucker]]** (*Chasmistes liorus liorus*): once so abundant in [[Utah Lake]] that it was called the "greatest sucker pond in the universe." The original form was lost during the mid-1930s drought, and hybridization had already affected it.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most extinctions were tied to changes in water and habitat. Common threats included:

- **Habitat alteration:** water diversion for irrigation, dam construction, groundwater pumping, spring drainage, channelization and dredging. Loss of springs was a central factor in the Mexican and Great Basin extinctions.
- **Introduced species:** predation and competition from largemouth bass, common carp, mosquitofish, bullfrogs, trout and other exotics. Introduced fishes were implicated in the decline of many taxa, including the [[Independence Valley Tui Chub]] and [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]].
- **Pollution and eutrophication:** sewage, sediments, fertilizers, industrial waste, and pesticides such as DDD, which affected [[Clear Lake]] and its splittail.
- **Hybridization:** introgression with related species, or with hatchery stock, eroded genetic purity in several taxa, including the [[Rio Grande Bluntnose Shiner]] and the [[Banff Longnose Dace]].
- **Overharvesting:** commercial fishing, particularly for deepwater ciscoes in the Great Lakes and for suckers during spawning runs.
- **Natural stress:** droughts, such as those that affected Utah Lake in the mid-1930s, and unstable flow regimes in the Rio Grande.

## Key Findings

- **Scale:** 40 taxa went extinct in North America over the past 100 years: 27 species, 13 subspecies and 3 genera.
- **Geography:** extinctions occurred in all areas except northern Canada and Alaska. The Great Lakes, Great Basin, Parras Valley, Valley of Mexico and Rio Grande lost the greatest share of their native fauna. Parras Valley lost three species, including one monotypic genus.
- **Family losses:** Cyprinidae lost 15 taxa, and Salmonidae and Cyprinodontidae lost seven taxa each.
- **Causes:** habitat loss was a contributing factor for at least 73% of the 40 taxa. Introduced species were cited for 69%. Hybridization was involved in 38%, and overharvesting in 15%. Most taxa had multiple causes.
- **Recent trend:** 19 of the 40 taxa disappeared after 1964, indicating an accelerating rate of loss.
- **Policy gap:** the authors judge that current laws and recovery programs are inadequate to halt the growing list of endangered fishes.
- **Mexico concern:** the authors are especially concerned for the Mexican ichthyofauna, where fishes are poorly known, environmental protection laws are weak and the human population is growing rapidly.
- **Erroneous extinctions:** an appendix lists 25 taxa previously reported as extinct that were later reclassified as endangered, threatened, of special concern, common, or invalid. Examples include the [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]], the [[Owens Pupfish]] and the [[Snail Darter]].
- **Data limitations:** extinction is hard to confirm for small, cryptic or remote-habitat fishes. Many records rely on single collections or museum specimens. The authors note that some extinctions probably went unnoticed, as with the Snake River sucker, which is known from a single specimen.
