# Establishing a Captive Broodstock for Bonytail Chub (Gila elegans)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study evaluates how a new [[Captive Broodstock]] of the federally endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*[[Gila elegans]]*) should be founded from the 1981 F1 progeny produced at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and from any wild fish captured in [[Lake Mohave]]. The authors find that the genetic base of the 1981 progeny is very small. The [[Effective Number of Founders]] is 8.48 under the hatchery-based "high" estimate and 3.48 under the genetics-based "low" estimate, compared with 10 actual founding parents. The authors recommend a finite broodstock of about 30 pairs of F1 parents and argue that any wild fish captured should be incorporated, because each adds disproportionately large genetic value.

## Background & Description

The bonytail chub is one of the most critically imperiled North American freshwater fishes. Its decline is attributed to habitat destruction and alteration from water development, and to introduced non-native fishes (Minckley et al., 1989; Williams et al., 1985). The only known wild individuals are an unknown number of large, old adults in Lake Mohave and scattered fish in the upper Colorado River basin. Little recruitment has occurred since the closure of [[Davis Dam]] in 1954 and the establishment of non-native sport fishes.

In 1981, the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) produced F1 progeny at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery from wild adults captured in Lake Mohave (Hamman, 1982). About 2,300 of these fish were retained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH), and approximately 350 still existed there in 1997. The USFWS plans to build a new "future captive broodstock" from these fish and any additional wild bonytail. The stated goal is the most complete and representative genetic sample possible.

Wild-caught parents and tissue samples were not retained, and F1 fish were not marked. Consequently, genetic data from random samples of F1 and F2 fish are the only means of estimating the actual contributions of the original matings.

**Reproduction from wild fish (1981):** Five adult males were used with six previously captured wild females. One female produced no eggs. The remaining five produced 5,850 to 37,700 eggs (mean 25,090), with fertilization of 0.91 to 0.98 (mean 0.95). The proportions of fertilized eggs contributed by each female ranged from 0.049 to 0.290 (mean 0.2). These are the "high" estimate, assumed to be an upper limit on retained variation. Each female was mated to one male, but one male was used with two females.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors relate the genetic patterns to the species' ecology and hatchery history:

- **Recruitment failure:** Natural recruitment in Lake Mohave was essentially absent after the 1954 closure of Davis Dam and the spread of non-native sport fishes.
- **Captive selection:** Selection in captivity can alter genetics and may leave hatchery-reared fish maladapted for the wild (Frankham et al., 1986; Ryman and Utter, 1987). Risk increases with multiple hatchery generations and unequal parental contributions.
- **Hatchery predation:** Adult bonytail were observed preying on their own progeny in holding ponds.
- **Mixed origin of wild-looking fish:** Lake Mohave contains three categories of bonytail (true wild, F1 released in 1981, and F2 released after 1981), which can be hard to distinguish.

## Key Findings

**1. Allozyme data indicate a single dominant mating.** The F1 sample (n = 30 from DNFH) was examined at three polymorphic loci, and a 1985 juvenile sample (n = 24) was examined at six loci (Minckley et al., 1989). Locus Pgm-A showed a significant excess of heterozygotes (P < 0.001), and Genotype 1 occurred in 22 of 30 fish. The authors calculate that a single mating was 3.02 times more likely than multiple matings to produce these fish. The remaining eight fish fell into five other genotypes and probably came from two or three additional matings.

**2. Mitochondrial DNA supports a narrow base.** All 19 F1 fish tested for cytochrome b had haplotype a. In 16 additional carcasses, 7 (0.438) had haplotype a and 9 (0.562) had haplotype b. Simulations showed that a monomorphic sample of 19 is far more likely under the "low" estimate. At a wild haplotype-a frequency of 0.9, the proportion of monomorphic samples was 0.751 (low) versus 0.620 (high).

**3. Effective founders are small.**
- "High" estimate: Ne,f = 8.48, which is 84.8% of the 10 actual founders (the maximum).
- "Low" estimate: Ne,f = 3.48, reflecting three effective matings with most progeny from one.

The hatchery-based value is therefore already reduced by unequal contributions. Using F1 fish to found the new broodstock reduces variation further.

**4. Finite parental number matters most at small sizes.** Using 30 pairs (60 fish) retains about 89.3% of the maximum effective founders under the "high" estimate and 97.1% under the "low" estimate. With 150 pairs, the figures rise to 97.5% and 99.4%. The authors therefore recommend about 30 pairs, since more than 30 adds little.

**5. Each wild fish adds disproportionate value.** One additional wild fish raises the "high" value from 8.48 to 9.58 (11.8%) and the "low" value from 3.48 to 4.48 (28.7%). Incorporation guidelines are that a new founder's representation should lie between 1/(8.48 + Nw) and 1/(3.48 + Nw), where Nw is the number of additional wild fish. Wild fish should be integrated while F1 parents remain available.

**6. Mating design is constrained by available fish.** Three mating types are possible: F1 × F1, F1 × wild, and wild × wild. Table 6 shows that with 30 F1 matings, one wild fish needs about nine F1 × wild matings under the "high" estimate. Under the "low" estimate, the number of F1 × wild matings becomes far higher. The authors advise avoiding wild × wild matings unless more than about five wild fish are captured.

**7. Parent selection should be random.** To limit differential reproductive selection in captivity, broodstock parents should be chosen at random, regardless of phenotype, unless a genetic or congenital problem is identified.

**8. Hatchery stocking history.** Between 1981 and 1996, the text reports 41,517 F1 and 161,371 F2 juveniles stocked into Lake Mohave. All F1 fish were marked with oxytetracycline, but only 8.6% of F2 fish were marked. Table 7 lists 202,688 total stocked, which does not match the sum of the text figures (202,888); the discrepancy is noted for verification. Marking methods included oxytetracycline, fin-clip, PIT tags, nose tags, and sonic tags.

**9. Certifying wild status.** The authors propose mating a candidate fish to five F1 fish, sacrificing it, and estimating its age by otolith analysis to confirm whether it predates the 1981 stocking. Marsh (1997) examined 17 Lake Mohave fish caught from 1989 to 1994 and estimated that "not less than 10% to perhaps as many as two-thirds" were wild.

**10. Recaptured hatchery fish have value.** Recaptured F2 fish, which survived both hatchery ponds and Lake Mohave predators, are considered more desirable than hatchery-only F1 fish, though each contributes about half as many genes as an F1 parent. Their value grows if no additional wild or F1 fish are captured.

**Overall conclusion:** The existing hatchery stock contains relatively low genetic variation, equivalent to 3.5 to 8.5 wild founders, so every additional wild fish and a controlled, random selection of about 30 F1 pairs are critical to preserving what remains of the species' genetic diversity.
