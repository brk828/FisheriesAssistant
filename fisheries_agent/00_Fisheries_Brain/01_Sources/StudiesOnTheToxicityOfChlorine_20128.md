# Toxicity of Chlorine and Chloramines to Rainbow Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 laboratory study by J. C. Merkens of the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] examined how [[Chlorine Toxicity]] to [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) changes when chlorine reacts with [[Ammonia]] in river water to form [[Chloramines]]. Experiments were run at 15°C, two pH values (6.3 and 7.0), a 2-hour reaction period, and 0.1 p.p.m. N ammonia. The study finds that toxicity tracks the total concentration of residual chlorine, that [[Free Chlorine]] is roughly three times more toxic than [[Monochloramine]], and that the toxicity of mixed residuals lies between these two. The author concludes that residual chlorine should be treated as free chlorine for pollution control, and that a safe stream concentration may be as low as about 0.004 p.p.m., although this extrapolation is unverified.

## Background & Description

Chlorine enters rivers from industrial wastes, including [[Paper Bleaching]] and [[Textile Bleaching]] effluents, and from cooling waters dosed to limit slime growth in cooling towers. Earlier work reviewed by Doudoroff and Katz indicated that chlorine kills trout within a few hours at about 0.2–0.3 p.p.m. and goldfish at about 1.0 p.p.m., but little was known about how river chemistry alters this toxicity.

Chlorine reacts with ammonia to form monochloramine (NH₂Cl), dichloramine (NHCl₂), and nitrogen trichloride (NCl₃). At initial Cl₂:NH₃ molar ratios below about 1, conversion to monochloramine is nearly quantitative. Higher ratios produce the [[Break-Point Curve]], in which available chlorine is lost before free chlorine reappears.

Test fish were rainbow trout 10–15 cm long, drawn at random from a single trout-farm pond and acclimatized to 15°C for three days. Ten fish were tested per condition in a continuous-flow apparatus described by Merkens. Median survival times were estimated graphically.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Ammonia is a common background constituent of rivers. Windle Taylor's survey of 42 British rivers and streams found concentrations below 0.05 p.p.m. N in 20 cases, 0.05–0.1 p.p.m. N in 11 cases, and above 0.1 p.p.m. N in 11 cases. The 0.1 p.p.m. N level was therefore chosen as the test background.

Fish excrete ammonia and other reactive substances. To keep this contribution negligible, flow was held at 3.4 litres per minute through test tanks of about 35 litres. Dissolved oxygen was held at roughly 10–11 p.p.m., and chlorine was added as sodium hypochlorite and allowed to react for two hours in delay tanks before reaching the fish.

Chloramine species were measured by amperometric titration and by the diethyl-p-phenylene diamine method.

## Key Findings

- **Dose-response shape:** At pH 7.0, residual chlorine rose with added chlorine following the typical break-point pattern, with the break point at 1.1 p.p.m. added chlorine. Log median survival time was approximately a mirror image of the residual chlorine curve.
- **Dominant mechanism:** Toxicity depends mainly on total available residual chlorine rather than on added chlorine, and the author suggests the toxicities of chloramines and free chlorine are of the same order.
- **Species toxicity ranking:** Free chlorine is about three times more toxic than monochloramine. Mixtures of mono- and dichloramine with free chlorine (added doses 0.5–1.1 p.p.m.) fall between these two values.
- **Effect of pH:** Break-point curves were similar at pH 6.3 and pH 7.0. The first limb (monochloramine-dominant) was nearly identical at both pH values. Final-limb residuals (free-chlorine-dominant) were less toxic at pH 6.3, probably because more of the residual was chloramine at that pH.
- **Lethal level for chloramine:** 0.08 p.p.m. residual chlorine, mostly as monochloramine, killed about half of a batch of trout in 7 days at near 100% air saturation of dissolved oxygen.
- **Estimated safe level:** Extrapolating the pH 7.0 free-chlorine line gives about 0.004 p.p.m. chlorine for a median survival of 1 year. The author notes the linear log–log relation is untested below 0.08 p.p.m. and that more data are needed before a safe concentration can be set with confidence.
- **Pollution-control rule:** Residual chlorine in rivers should be assumed to have the toxicity of free chlorine, which gives a safety margin because much of it is usually in the less toxic chloramine form.
