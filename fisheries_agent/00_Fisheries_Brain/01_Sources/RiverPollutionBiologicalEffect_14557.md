# River Pollution: Biological Effects

## Executive Summary
This classic freshwater ecology paper by H. B. N. Hynes examines how various types of pollution affect the biological communities of British rivers, with particular attention to running-water ecosystems rather than lakes. The paper argues that fish, while the most visible and economically important organisms, are poor indicators of pollution because they are mobile and can flee degraded conditions. Instead, plants and benthic invertebrates — being sessile or slow-moving — provide far more reliable, sensitive indicators of water quality, including chronic, intermittent, or historical pollution events. The work classifies effluents into five categories (poisons, inert suspended matter, organic residues, heated water, and vegetation stimulants) and details their differential impacts on [[Trout Streams]] versus lowland [[Coarse Fish Rivers]].

## Background & Description
Hynes distinguishes British running waters into three fundamental types based on geology and chemistry:

1. **Swift, soft-water streams** — poor in nutrient salts, sometimes acidic (draining shale/slate/peaty uplands); classic [[Trout Stream]] habitat.
2. **Swift, hard-water streams** — poor in nutrients but alkaline (draining calcareous rock); also trout and young [[Atlantic Salmon]] breeding habitat.
3. **Slow-flowing, hard, nutrient-rich, alkaline lowland rivers** — the domain of coarse fishing.

**Fish fauna by stream type:**
- **Soft-water swift streams:** dominated by young stoneflies, mayflies, caddisflies, midges, and the freshwater limpet (*Ancylastrum*); fish include [[Brown Trout]] and young [[Atlantic Salmon]].
- **Hard-water swift streams (>20 mg/l Ca):** greater abundance of limpets, snails (*Limnaea pereger*), and the freshwater shrimp *[[Gammarus Pulex]]*; fish fauna includes **bullhead, loach, minnows**, trout and salmon — generally larger, faster-growing fish due to higher productivity.
- **Lowland rivers:** stoneflies and stone-dwelling mayflies scarce; instead, plant-dwelling mayflies/caddisflies, tube-dwelling midges, water-louse (*Asellus*), snails, mussels, and worms dominate. Essentially represents most of Britain's freshwater fish fauna.

Substratum type is identified as the fundamental ecological driver: swift streams have rocky/gravel beds favoring current-adapted, holdfast-equipped organisms; slow streams accumulate silt/sand, favoring burrowers and supporting dense, varied vegetation (*Ranunculus*, *Elodea*, *Potamogeton*, *Sparganium*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Pollution Classification
Hynes proposes five overlapping categories of effluent:
1. **Poisons** (metal salts, acids, alkalis, phenolic wastes)
2. **Inert suspended matter** (wood-pulp, coal washings)
3. **Organic residues** (sewage, milk/sugar-beet waste, slaughterhouse drainage)
4. **Heated water** (cooling tower discharge)
5. **Vegetation stimulants** (purified sewage, certain chemical wastes)

### Effects on Habitat and Food Web
- **Poisons:** Eliminate susceptible species outright; effects may be indirect via food-chain disruption. A Welsh case study showed lead-mine drainage eliminated fish, flatworms, and molluscs but left insects, because algal food supply (not the metal itself) was removed for case-bearing caddis larvae. Similarly, phenolic effluent in Liverpool was ~10x more toxic to *[[Gammarus Pulex]]* than to trout — meaning trout populations could crash via food-supply loss without direct toxicity.
- **Inert suspended matter:** Smothers plants and algae, devastates bottom fauna; only burrowers (e.g., Tubificidae) persist. Rarely kills fish directly (gill clogging) but destroys their food base.
- **Organic pollution:** Produces the classic "oxygen sag." Sensitive stoneflies and mayflies disappear first; tolerant species like the mayfly *Baetis rhodani* persist. Sewage fungus (*Sphaerotilus natans* and associated protozoa like *Carchesium*) blankets substrates, degrading holdfast habitat for lithophile species (e.g., eliminating freshwater limpet and mayfly *Ecdyonurus*). Chironomid "bloodworms" (*Chironomus*) proliferate in enriched sediments.
- **Heated effluent:** Raises temperature, lowers oxygen solubility, accelerates organic decay; also introduces its own organic/bacterial load from dead entrained organisms, sometimes compounded by toxic chlorination byproducts (e.g., chlorinated gasworks liquor).
- **Vegetation stimulants:** Nutrient-enriched, mineralized sewage promotes nuisance algal blooms — notably [[Cladophora Glomerata]] (blanket-weed) — causing extreme diurnal swings in pH and dissolved oxygen (discussed further below).

### River Self-Purification
Pollution effects diminish with distance downstream as species progressively reappear according to their tolerance thresholds — a process termed self-purification. Full recovery may require many miles, and the post-recovery river state differs from baseline (higher nutrient/salt content, siltier bed favoring genera like *Tanytarsus*).

## Key Findings

- **Fish are poor pollution indicators** because of their mobility; they may be caught near polluted outfalls (feeding opportunistically) yet die during acute low-oxygen events when unable to escape (e.g., trapped by weirs), leading to misattributed "mystery" fish kills.
- **Invertebrates and plants are superior bioindicators**: sessile/slow-moving, long life cycles (often one generation/year), and able to reveal intermittent or historic pollution invisible to short-term chemical sampling (BOD, dissolved oxygen).
- **Species tolerance hierarchy**: Stoneflies are most pollution-sensitive; various mayflies show graded tolerance (*Baetis rhodani* relatively tolerant); water-louse (*Asellus aquaticus*), bloodworm (*Chironomus thummi*), and tubificid worms show increasing dominance with increasing pollution severity, in that order.
- **Cross-toxicity effect**: Species resistant to one stressor tend to be resistant to others broadly — pollution-tolerant taxa are consistently the last to disappear regardless of pollutant type.
- **Food-chain-mediated fish decline**: Fish populations can be devastated by effluents that are not directly toxic to fish but eliminate their invertebrate prey (e.g., *Gammarus pulex* being more phenol-sensitive than trout).
- **Thermal pollution compounds organic pollution**: Cooling water discharge, even without added chemicals, elevates BOD via killed entrained organisms and reduces oxygen solubility, worsening any pre-existing organic load; trout breeding requires temperatures below ~14.5°C despite adult tolerance up to >20°C.
- **Discussion highlights (River Tweed case, Mr. Waddington)**: Heavy *Cladophora glomerata* growth below sewage outfalls caused dramatic diurnal water chemistry swings — pH from 7.6 (early morning) to 10.5 (afternoon), dissolved oxygen from 50% to 180% saturation — with elevated unionized ammonia at high pH causing significant salmon and trout mortality. Nutrient (nitrate/phosphate) depletion by algae created patchy growth zones between outfalls, raising questions (unresolved at time of discussion) about a link to phosphate-based detergents.
- **Methodological recommendation**: Biological surveys (species abundance assessments above/below suspected outfalls) are more time-efficient and sensitive than extended chemical analysis series, and should be repeated at 6–12 month intervals for early pollution detection. Best combined with chemical analysis — biology localizes and characterizes contamination; chemistry can then target specific compounds.
- Physical stream conditions (turbulence, flow velocity) can modulate biological responses independent of BOD/permanganate values — swift, shallow riffles resist *Sphaerotilus* fouling and retain normal fauna even amid otherwise polluted reaches, per discussion with Dr. Philipson.
