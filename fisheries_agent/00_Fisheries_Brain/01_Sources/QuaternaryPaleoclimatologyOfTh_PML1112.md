# Postglacial and Pleistocene Climate Change on the Pacific Coast of North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #paleoclimatology

## Executive Summary

This document is a paleoclimatic essay by [[Carl Hubbs]], published in the *CalCOFI Reports* (Volume VII, 1 January 1958 to 30 June 1959), with an appended transcript of a group discussion. Hubbs reviews evidence for long-term and short-term climate change in western North America during the Tertiary, Pleistocene and Postglacial (Recent) periods. He argues that the long-term trend has been toward cooler temperatures and greater aridity, with increasing fluctuation at shorter timescales. Fish faunas, shell-bearing mollusks, archaeological middens, and lake-shore geology are used as proxies. The discussion covers ocean heat transport and whether the observed aridity and temperature changes could be explained by atmospheric and oceanic circulation.

## Background & Description

The paper is a review rather than a field study with a fixed sample design. Its main evidence base includes:

- **Fossil and faunal records:** small fossil mammals, bottom-sediment mollusks, and cold- and warm-water faunal assemblages from the Pleistocene.
- **Isotope paleothermometry:** oxygen-18 temperature estimates from shells of surf-zone mollusks in [[Indian Middens]] and deep-sea cores.
- **Radiocarbon dating:** carbon-14 ages from charcoal and shell at archaeological sites.
- **Historical records:** the 1853–57 Pacific Railroad Survey fish collections and San Diego and Monterey weather data beginning in 1849.
- **Geomorphology:** shore bars of [[Lake LeConte]], pimple-mound topography, and alluvial deposits in desert ranges.

Hubbs notes that the paper treats mainly Postglacial events and the last few thousand years, with the most detail for the past seven centuries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Long-term trends
- Across Tertiary and Quaternary time, the overall trend has been toward cooler temperatures and greater aridity. Hubbs considers the Pleistocene a series of four major glacial periods with interglacials between them.
- Uplift of the [[Sierra Nevada]], [[Rocky Mountains]], [[Colorado Plateau]] and [[Great Basin]] is seen as a major driver of western aridity, though Hubbs argues it was not the only factor.
- The Great Basin may have been part of a coastal plain at less than 1,000 feet elevation in the Pliocene, which Hubbs supports with the presence of coastal-plain fish types there.

### Wisconsin (last glacial) extremes
- The [[Wisconsin Glaciation]] was probably the coldest and driest of the glacial phases, though it was still wetter than the present in some regions. Evidence includes boreal forest reaching the Gulf of Mexico and cold-water mollusks occurring farther north than today.
- Hubbs estimates that a sea-surface drop of about 3 °C in winter would have allowed [[Giant Kelp]] and the [[Pacific Sardine]] to cross the then-warm tropical waters between Peru and Baja California.
- Pimple-mound topography is interpreted as evidence of periglacial freeze-thaw activity, with more southerly examples interpreted as seasonal freezing over hardpan.

### Postglacial temperature
- A warm-water fauna from [[Guadalupe Island]] (240 miles south of San Diego) includes reef coral and tropical mollusks, indicating a late Pleistocene warm period.
- Oxygen-18 estimates from northwestern [[Baja California]] middens show sea-surface temperatures slightly warmer than present at about 4,000, 2,500, and 300 years B.P., with a cooler interval in between.
- [[Red Abalone]] dominated the diet of [[Santa Rosa Island]] people in an earlier period, implying cold coastal water. Later, [[Black Abalone]] (warm-water) was more common. Hubbs notes a "trace of Glacial cold" persisting at that time.
- The giant chiton [[Giant Chiton]] (*Cryptochiton stelleri*) was heavily harvested in Baja California middens but is now absent from that coast, suggesting cooler conditions about 1,000 years ago.
- Hubbs proposes a negative correlation between coastal California and Greenland temperatures during the Medieval warm period.

### Recent warm and cool phases
- The 1853–57 Pacific Railroad Survey recorded a warmer-water fish fauna at San Diego and Monterey than present, including a [[Seahorse]] taken at San Diego but not recently.
- San Diego and Monterey weather data show a cooling trend through the second half of the 19th century, with a possible late rise. Hubbs attributes part of the rise to urbanization and the most marked rise to the move of the station to [[Lindbergh Field]] (about 1.0 °F warmer than the former Federal Building site).

### Postglacial moisture
- The [[Pluvial Period]], accompanying the last glacial, brought much higher rainfall and lower evaporation, with rivers flowing full and great lakes in basins.
- Mid-Holocene desiccation (the "xerothermic" or climatic optimum) was followed by a "little pluvial" period, and a severe drought occurred in the 13th century (about 700 years B.P.).
- Fish remains in middens from [[Fish Creek]] (Fish Creek Mountains, Southern California) show that fish now restricted to permanent water once lived in much wetter conditions. Hubbs estimates rainfall about equal to the present coast's annual rainfall (over 40 inches per year) versus under 5 inches in the same watershed today, based on a radiocarbon date of about 1,000 B.P. from hearth charcoal.
- Shore bars of Lake LeConte, dated from about 1,000 to 800 years ago, indicate a wet interval; recession lines at about 300 years ago are about five feet apart, comparable to present evaporation rates.
- Hubbs argues that desert landscapes in the Southwest developed rapidly within the last few centuries as drought and overgrazing by livestock removed ground cover.

### Circulation and heat transport
- In the discussion, [[Ocean Heat Transport]] is debated. Jung's estimate of oceanic transport at roughly 10% at midlatitudes is discussed, and participants agree that oceanic and atmospheric transport cannot yet be separated accurately.
- Fleming estimates that evaporation from the ocean is about one meter of water per year, and that Pleistocene ice accumulation would correspond to a change of about 1 mm per year. He notes this is only about 1/1000 of the annual precipitation balance.
- Marker horizons are proposed for dating: a band of non-calcareous sediment off Washington and the ash fall from [[Mount Mazama]] (about 6,000 years ago).

## Key Findings

1. **Long-term trend:** Western North America has trended toward cooler and drier conditions since the Tertiary, with superimposed fluctuations that have grown larger over time.
2. **Coupled regional pattern:** The north-south climate gradient along the Pacific Coast and in the Southwest persisted through all major fluctuations, although the intensity of temperature, precipitation and wind changed.
3. **Wisconsin extremes:** The last glacial was extremely cold and, despite greater moisture in some regions, its ice cap may have been limited by lower precipitation.
4. **Faunal proxies:** Changes in fish and mollusk distributions (kelp, sardine, abalone, chiton, freshwater fish) record past temperature and water availability.
5. **Negative correlation:** Coastal California and the far north (Greenland) appear to have had negatively correlated temperature regimes in the Postglacial period.
6. **Recent aridity:** Desert conditions in the Southwest are recent, and rainfall was much higher in the last few thousand years at many now-arid sites.
7. **Predictions:** Hubbs expects strong fluctuations to continue, with the long-term drying trend likely persisting and accelerating, and recommends planning for further aridity in Southern California and Baja California.
8. **Open questions:** The discussion concludes that the role of oceanic heat transport and wind-driven circulation remains unresolved and requires paleotemperature data from additional regions, including Japan.
