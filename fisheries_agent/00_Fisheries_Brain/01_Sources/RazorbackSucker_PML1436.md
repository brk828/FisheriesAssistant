The text also gives a global figure of about 240 million tons of paper produced worldwide. This is a production note, not ecological data.

**Species description.** The razorback sucker is named for a dorsal hump that appears directly behind the head. Its snout is blunt, and it has a sharp, thin-headed profile. Adults are dark on the back. The text states that adults of both sexes typically reach 18 cm (7 in) in standard length, though the scanned size figure is partly unreadable. A small freshwater amphipod (*Gammarus* sp.), shown at about life size, is pictured as a food item.

**Habitat preference.** The species is native to large rivers of the Colorado River basin. Adults are said to prefer slow-moving backwaters, eddies, and off-channel habitats, and the text notes the species seems to avoid strong currents. Young fish are associated with shallow, vegetated backwater areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life history and spawning.** Adults gather in groups, sometimes forming schools, and migrate to spawn in late winter and spring. Spawning occurs over gravel or coarse sand in riffles, where females release eggs that are fertilized by a group of males. Eggs and larvae are vulnerable to nonnative predatory fish. Reproduction is strongly affected by water temperature, and the species appears to favor warm water. The scan describes temperature ranges of roughly 16 to 21 °C, but the digits are too damaged to confirm.

**Dams and flow alteration.** Dams fragmented the river. The text states that 15 dams have been built in the Colorado River basin, which changed water temperature, flow, and sediment and disrupted the spawning migrations and rearing habitat of native fishes. Reservoirs favored [[Nonnative Sport Fishes]] such as bass and catfish, which compete with and prey on the razorback sucker.

**Nonnative fishes and chemical control.** Introduced predators and competitors were a major threat. The text describes a controversial program in which reservoirs and rivers were treated with the fish toxicant [[Rotenone]] to remove nonnative species. This also killed native razorback suckers. The scan mentions a case on the Green River in which razorback suckers were killed by the toxicant, and a reservoir where they died after treatment.

**Historical harvest and archaeology.** Archaeological sites in the basin contain suckers in prehistoric deposits, showing that Native Americans used the fish as food. Commercial harvest became significant in the early 1900s, and the species was once taken in large numbers from the Colorado River and its tributaries. Commercial fishing declined sharply as the fish became rare, and the text says the species now survives in only a few rivers and reservoirs.

**Range.** The fish once occurred widely, including the lower Colorado River, the Salton Sea area of Baja California and the Gulf of California drainage, and upper basin waters in Wyoming, Colorado, and Utah. Populations are now fragmented, and some are described as having no successful natural reproduction. Most surviving fish are older individuals, and the text notes that few recent recruits are found.

## Key Findings

- **Species status:** The razorback sucker was added to a state list of rare species in the early 1970s and reclassified as endangered in 1974 (exact year partly garbled in scan). It was placed on the federal list of endangered species in 1991, after biologists began documenting its decline in the 1970s. See [[Endangered Species Act]].
- **Cause of decline:** Main drivers include dam construction, flow and temperature alteration, habitat loss, nonnative predatory and competing fishes, toxicant treatment of waters, and historical overharvest.
- **Recovery and hatchery work:** Hatchery propagation and stocking are central to recovery. The text says hatchery-raised fish have been reintroduced into Arizona rivers by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]], and that this continues today.
- **Failed reintroduction:** A reintroduction program for the lower Colorado River initiated by California was abandoned for a variety of reasons, including budgetary constraints.
- **Reproductive problems:** Recent surveys found that razorback suckers caught were very old, and that the species had poor recruitment in the wild. Hatchery production is viewed as necessary to maintain populations.
- **Context within the volume:** The account is one of many in *Life On The Edge*, which documents endangered species across California, such as the [[Delta Green Ground Beetle]], [[Shasta Crayfish]], and several butterflies, to support public education and conservation.
