# Creel Census and Expenditure Study, Madison River, Montana, 1950–52

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service report (Special Scientific Report: Fisheries No. 126, April 1954) measures fishing pressure, catch, and angler spending on the [[Madison River]] in southwestern Montana. Field work covered the regular fishing season (May through November) in summers 1950–52, with a different 119-mile section studied each year. The study was designed to set a baseline before water-development projects for irrigation and power were considered. Its main results are an estimated 40,772 fisherman-days and 80,459 fish (82,682 pounds) per year, spending of roughly $412,500 per year, and a fishery dominated by [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]].

## Background & Description

- **River:** The Madison forms at the junction of the Firehole and Gibbon rivers in [[Yellowstone National Park]] and flows about 140 miles north to join the Gallatin and Jefferson rivers at Three Forks, forming the Missouri River. About 119 miles were studied, subject to possible development.
- **Study units:** The stream fishery covered 98 river miles. Two impoundments were included: [[Madison Reservoir]] (3,800 acres, built 1908, maximum depth 37 feet) and [[Hebgen Reservoir]] (13,400 acres, built 1915, maximum depth 70 feet).
- **Physical setting:** The river runs through [[Madison Canyon]], the upper Madison Valley (gradient about 29 feet per mile), [[Bear Trap Canyon]] (about 11 miles), and the lower valley (about 16 feet per mile). The drainage is about 2,500 square miles, and flows are largely controlled by the two reservoirs. Summer flows are about 2,000 second-feet.
- **Fish history:** Whitefish, grayling, a few cutthroat, and suckers dominated before 1900. Steelhead were first introduced shortly after 1900, [[Brown Trout]] about 1910, and rainbow and brook trout around 1920. Rainbows became the dominant species. The [[Utah Chub]] appeared in Hebgen in the mid-1930s, probably introduced by live-bait anglers.
- **Management:** Recent management consisted of stocking legal-sized rainbow and brown trout (mainly from the U.S. Fish Cultural Station near Ennis) and enforcing regulations. The creel limit for the river and Madison Reservoir was 15 fish, including no more than 5 under 7 inches or 10 pounds, plus 1 fish. The Hebgen limit was 5 fish, not exceeding 10 pounds, plus 1 fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water temperature:** Readings were taken daily at 8:00 a.m. The Lower Section (1950) averaged about 60°F, with a recorded range of 70°F maximum and 0°F minimum (as printed). The Middle Section (1951) averaged 51°F, with a maximum of 67°F. The Upper Section (1952) had a seasonal average of 55°F, and the mid-July to mid-September average was about 60°F. The authors considered these temperatures below optimum for rainbow and brown trout, although rainbow growth rates matched or exceeded most Montana streams. Cooler water below the canyon was attributed to springs and seeps.
- **Water chemistry:** Water was medium hard and slightly alkaline (see [[Appendix A Water Analyses]]). Domestic and industrial pollution was minor, and silt was negligible.
- **Habitat:** The rubble and boulder bed, gravel and sand spawning bars, riffles, and islands (especially between Madison Reservoir and Ennis) provide cover and spawning areas. Hebgen's gravel shoals along the north and west shores are used for spawning but are limited by water-level fluctuation. Hebgen's Grayling Arm was murky because of a former bog bottom, and Madison Reservoir turbidity came from wave action.
- **Tributaries:** Of 102 tributaries, most are short and many dry up in summer. Important nursery streams include [[North Meadow Creek]], Duck and Grayling creeks, and the South and West forks of the Madison. Irrigators dewater some of them. The West Fork has beaver dams that restrict fish movement.
- **Diversions and losses:** The [[West Madison Canal]] diverts about 150 second-feet, and its fish screen was not operating in 1951. After an inadvertent closure in September (reported as 1948), more than 14,000 trout were found in the first 3 miles of the canal.
- **Food base:** Bottom fauna was abundant, dominated by mayfly, midge, and stonefly nymphs. The [[Salmon Fly]] (*Pteronarcys californica*) was abundant and widely used as bait. Hebgen had abundant stonefly, mayfly, and dipteran larvae in weedy areas. Aquatic plants, including coontail, water milfoil, and pondweeds, are abundant in both reservoirs.
- **Climate and land use:** The climate is typical of the northern Rockies, with severe winters and a short growing season (88 days at Hebgen Dam to 112 days at Three Forks). Ennis averages 10.69 inches of precipitation. The economy is based on livestock grazing, wheat in the lower valley, and tourism. Forests in the watershed moderate snowmelt and flash floods, which supports stable flows.

## Key Findings

**Methods.** Crews of five to seven men used roving patrols and check stations, with off-season checks in autumn. Data came from interviews with anglers on fishing parties, home address, fishing type, time fished, and catch. Contact rates were 50% for the 1950 river, 69% for the 1951 river, and 48% for the 1952 river, plus 21% for Hebgen Reservoir. Uncontacted parties were estimated. Average weights were applied to estimated numbers of fish by species.

**Fishing pressure and yield (annual averages, 1950–52):**

| Fishery | Fisherman-days | Fish caught | Pounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stream (98 miles) | 22,660 | 52,424 | 48,385 |
| Reservoirs (Madison and Hebgen) | 18,112 | 28,035 | see note |
| Total | 40,772 | 80,459 | 82,682 |

- Note: the text prints the reservoir weight as 3,297 pounds, but the totals imply about 34,297 pounds (Madison 18,141 plus Hebgen 16,153, from Table 12).
- Stream pressure and yield per mile were 231 fisherman-days, 535 fish, and about 494 pounds.
- The Channel Area (5 miles, 539 fisherman-days per mile) was the most intensively fished. It also had one of the highest catch rates, 0.75 fish per hour in 1951.
- Upper Bear Trap Canyon (11 miles) had 52 fisherman-days per mile.
- Madison Reservoir yielded 4.8 pounds per surface acre, about 1.3 times the 3.6 pounds expected from [[Rounsefell Model]] estimates. Hebgen yielded 1.2 pounds, about half the expected 2.2.

**Fishing success.**
- Rate of catch on the river ranged from 0.37 to 0.82 fish per hour, and pounds per hour from 0.38 to 0.67.
- Reservoirs were similar: Madison 0.32 fish per hour, Hebgen 0.30 fish per hour, and 0.37–0.39 pounds per hour.
- Catch rates rose early in the season with morning water temperatures up to about 50°F, dipped slightly through mid-August, and rose again in autumn.
- Compared with other western streams, the Madison's catch in numbers is in the lower half, but its catch in pounds per hour is among the highest, surpassed by only two others. Reservoir catch rates are average for the Rocky Mountain region.

**Species composition of creel catch:**
- [[Rainbow Trout]] 65.4%
- [[Brown Trout]] 22.1%
- [[Mountain Whitefish]] 6.6%
- Suckers 4.5%
- [[Utah Chub]] 0.6%
- [[Grayling]] 0.5%
- [[Brook Trout]] 0.2%
- [[Cutthroat Trout]] 0.2%

Browns were more common in the lower river and less common upstream, except in the Varney area and Hebgen. Hybrid trout were classified as rainbows. In Hebgen, 21.3% of the catch was Utah chub. Gill-net work found about six chubs to one trout in shallow water. The study found no significant food competition, but chubs compete with trout for space.

**Angler behavior and size.**
- Anglers were 83% men, 12% women, and 5% children on the river.
- Nonresidents made up 6% of users in the Lower Section, 22% in the Middle, and 55% in the Upper.
- Average fishing time was 3.2–5.8 hours per day. Residents fished about 0.5 hours longer than nonresidents.
- The largest recorded rainbow was 32 inches and 5 pounds, and the largest brown was 24 inches and 5 pounds.
- In 1952, many Upper Section anglers released fish; the report notes this but does not count it in catch rates.

**Expenditures.**
- Average daily spending per person was $7.90 for the Lower Section and Madison Reservoir (1950), $7.95 for the Middle Section (1951), and $13.08 for the Upper Section and Hebgen (1952).
- Cost per pound of fish was $3.31 for the Lower river, $3.47 for Madison Reservoir, $3.39 for the Middle Section, $7.97 for the Upper river, and $8.21 for Hebgen.
- Total average annual angler expenditure was about $412,500 ($412,523.63 in Table 12), with annual and investment costs estimated from a Yellowstone County survey at $2.50 per person-day.
- Upper Section spending was higher mainly because of longer travel distances (about 97 round-trip miles per person-day) and a higher share of nonresidents.

**Conclusions and recommendations.**
- The authors judged the Madison "highly productive of fairly large fish" and able to support considerably more fishing without reduced productivity, given better access.
- They recommended maintaining the river "in a productive natural state" in view of increasing demand and growing water development.
