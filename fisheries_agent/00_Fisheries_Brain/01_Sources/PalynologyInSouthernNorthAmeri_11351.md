# Palynology in Southern North America: Pollen and Sediments of the Mexico City Lake Basin

#palynology #limnology #paleoclimatology

## Executive Summary

This multi-part series from the *Bulletin of the Geological Society of America* (1965) uses lake-sediment cores from the [[Mexico City Basin]] to reconstruct Pleistocene and Holocene climate, volcanism, and tectonics. Because the basin lay beyond the limit of continental glaciation, its sediments provide a continuous record that ice-covered regions cannot. The series combines [[Pollen Analysis]], petrography, and water-content measurements from two central-city cores, [[Bellas Artes Core]] and [[Madero Core]], and compares them with a New Mexico core and regional archaeological and glacial chronologies.

The introduction (Sears) sets the research context. Part II (Foreman) divides the cores into seven petrographic zones. Part III (Clisby and Sears) links pollen composition and density to those zones. Part IV (Sears and Clisby) interprets the record as a moist-dry oscillation series that can be correlated with Wisconsin glacial phases.

## Background & Description

**Origins of the project.** The work grew out of [[Sears 1925-1926 Vegetation Studies]] of Ohio, which found prairie enclaves in a forest region. This led to the xerothermic (warm-dry) post-glacial hypothesis, and then to attempts to date climatic phases with pollen. The authors note that "post-glacial" is a relative term, since the Wisconsin ice retreat was a series of retreats and readvances.

**Study sites.** The two central Mexico City cores lie roughly 250 to 300 m apart:
- **Bellas Artes (core 80):** 75 m deep, sampled at about one sample per meter (about 10% of the sequence), with about 30% sampling in the lower 63.40–74.90 m interval.
- **Madero (core 57):** 69 m deep, sampled at about two samples per meter (about 10% coverage).

A companion core from the San Augustin Plains, New Mexico (645 ft, with about 300 ft analyzed), extended the record into the last interglacial. Cores were supplied by [[L. Zeevaert]], who also logged them and measured water content.

**Pollen as a climate indicator.** Upland forest pollen is the main climate proxy. The authors use four genera:
- [[Oak Pollen]] and [[Alder Pollen]] indicate moisture and warmth.
- [[Fir Pollen]] indicates moisture at cooler elevations.
- [[Pine Pollen]] indicates dryness and cooler conditions.
- [[Spruce Pollen]] is sparse and sporadic, and suggests cooler temperatures than today.

Nonforest pollen (composites, amaranths, grasses, sedges, cypress) tracks disturbance or swamp conditions rather than climate directly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Basin setting.** The Mexico City basin lies on the southern Central Plateau at high altitude (about 2300 m and above in surrounding forest belts). Its rocks are almost entirely andesitic volcanics. Foreman reports that of 49 rock types listed in one 1914 survey, 36 were andesites and 13 basalts. Sediments are mostly pyroclastic (ash) derived from upland volcanoes, with additional erosional sand, clay, diatoms, ostracods, and carbonate.

**Lake history.** During the Pleistocene a lake covered about 630 square miles of the basin floor. Today seven shallow saline remnants cover only about 10% of the floor. As late as 1520 A.D., nearly all of the basin was shallow water. Foreman's sedimentation evidence (root holes, oolites, sand-dominated zones) indicates that the lake was mostly shallow to swampy.

**Climate today.** Mean annual precipitation is about 700 mm (27.6 in.) for the basin and 588 mm for the valley floor. About 75% falls June to September as afternoon convective storms. Mean temperature is 15.6°C, with May the warmest month (18.4°C) and January the coolest (12.3°C).

**Geological context.** Volcanic, tectonic, and erosional events are recorded in the sediments. Foreman interprets fresh ash layers as volcanic falls, and coarse clastic beds as tectonic-driven rapid deposition. Ash-fall crusting and wind transport controlled how ash reached the lake.

**Key comparative sites.** Surface pollen from the San Augustin Plains showed that pinon pine (over 90% of forest pollen) came from within about 6 miles, while spruce, about 20 miles away, was absent. This supports the idea of short pollen transport in intermontane basins.

## Key Findings

**Stratigraphy (Foreman, Part II).** Each core divides into seven zones, similarly numbered and nearly identical in thickness, mineralogy, fossils, and water-holding capacity:

| Zone | Bellas Artes depth | Madero depth | Dominant sediment | Inferred conditions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0–3.60 m | 0–3.10 m | Disturbed fill | Surface disturbance |
| II | 3.6–8 m | 3.1–8 m | Sandy weathered ash with carbonate, marl, oolites | Low lake level, rapid erosion, possibly dry |
| III | 8–32 m | 8–34 m | Weathered clayey ash with ostracods and diatoms | Stable, shallow to swampy lake |
| IV | 32–37 m | 34–37 m | Ashy sand, slightly weathered | Rapid deposition, drier climate |
| V | 37–45.3 m | 37–47.5 m | Weathered clayey ash | Stable, shallow to swampy |
| VI | 46–61 m | 47.7–61 m | Ashy sand, poorly sorted | Strong tectonic activity |
| VII | 61–75 m | (same) | Alternating fresh and weathered ash | Intermittent volcanism |

**Water content and weathering.** Water content is high in weathered, clayey ash and low in sands and fresh ash. In some wet samples water weighed two to three times the solids. Montmorillonite is the main weathering clay, but it rarely exceeds 10% and is usually below 5% of samples. Fresh ash contains no clay, so the clay formed on uplands before deposition.

**Dating and chronology.** Key anchors include:
- Archaic (2500–500 B.C.), Teotihuacan (500 B.C.–900 A.D.), and Nahua (900–1500 A.D.) cultures, with a dry interval around 500 B.C.
- Younger Becerra radiocarbon dating of 11,000 ± 500 to more than 16,000 years.
- A carbon-14 date above 32,000 years for material from 33 m.
- A spruce-forest thermal maximum in Alaska dated 3500 ± 250 years (Heusser, 1953).

**Pollen density (Clisby and Sears, Part III).** Pollen density is high where sedimentation is slow and water content is high, and sparse where sedimentation is rapid. Median forest pollen density is high in Zones III (1840 Madero, 2300 Bellas Artes) and V (1160 Madero, 1550 Bellas Artes), and very low in Zone IV (42 and 26) and Zone VI (3 and 6). Nonforest pollen rises after ash fall or tectonic disturbance, but it never replaces forest pollen.

**Maize and spruce.** Maize pollen appears throughout the archaeological record and at 9 m, predating agriculture in the basin. It was also identified at 70 m, which the authors take as evidence of the American origin of maize. Spruce pollen (68% of forest pollen in one shallow Mexico City drilling at 3 m) is interpreted as redeposition from upland lakes or tarns, not wind transport from far away.

**Climate record (Sears and Clisby, Part IV).** The authors identify moist-dry oscillations controlled mainly by temperature. Moist maxima are warm and tied to glacial nourishment, while dry phases are cooler and tied to ice wastage. Three gradients appear above 50 m:
- **Gradient A** (about 50 m to 35–40 m): a series of interrupted glacial advances, peaking near 35–36 m.
- **Gradient B** (about 35–40 m to 25 m): a retreat, followed by an abrupt shift to another maximum.
- **Gradient C** (about 23 m to the top): a prolonged and vacillating retreat, which the authors correlate with the Cary-Mankato-Latest sequence.

The authors suggest that the record extends back to the early Wisconsin. They also note that the Mexican cores do not clearly reach an interglacial period.

**Conclusions.**
- Changes in forest pollen percentages reflect changes in moisture. Changes in pollen density reflect changes in deposition and weathering.
- Volcanic and tectonic disturbance can be separated from climate only by combining pollen and sedimentary evidence.
- The sediments support the interdisciplinary case: sedimentology, petrography, and palynology together give a more complete picture than any one approach alone.
- The authors frame the work as relevant to future water planning in a warming, drying trend since about 1750 A.D.
