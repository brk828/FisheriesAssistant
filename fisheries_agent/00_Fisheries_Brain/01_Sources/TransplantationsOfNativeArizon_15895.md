# Transplantations of Native Arizona Fishes: Records Through 1980

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae #Poeciliidae #Cyprinodontidae #Catostomidae #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[W. L. Minckley]] and [[James E. Brooks]] (Arizona State University and the Arizona Game and Fish Department) compiles published and unpublished records of nearly 200 intentional and presumably accidental transplantations of 26 native Arizona fish species and subspecies through 1980. It lists origin and transplant localities, dates, status of introduced populations, and authorities. It is the first in a planned series documenting transplants tied to [[Endangered Species Act]] recovery efforts. The paper also explains the numeric drainage identification system used for localities and discusses the conservation rationale, hatchery role, and management problems of translocations.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** 26 species and subspecies, comprising more than 75% of the native freshwater fish taxa known from Arizona.
- **Data sources:** Unpublished stocking files of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], field notes ("original data"), agency reports, and the open literature. Early records were compiled by Minckley (1973) in *Fishes of Arizona*.
- **Locality coding:** A 600-series digital sequence denotes aquatic habitats, with 610 for the [[Colorado River]] watershed and tributaries numbered down- to upstream (e.g., 611 Gila River basin, 612 Bill Williams, 613 Virgin, 614 Little Colorado, 615 San Juan, 620 Rio Yaqui, 630 Sonoran drainages, 640 closed basins). Subbasins are further numbered after decimals. The system derives from the [[Arizona Resources Inventory System]] (Silvey et al. 1984).
- **Inclusion criteria:** Records were included whether a population was established, reported, or apparently extirpated, because temporarily lost stocks may reappear. Wild-caught fish transferred to hatcheries and their progeny returned to the wild were also included. Records mapped without documentation in [[Atlas of North American Freshwater Fishes]] (Lee et al. 1980) and hatchery fish shipped out of the region for research were excluded.
- **Status categories:** Terminology follows Shafland and Lewis (1984).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Drivers of decline:** Water developments and non-native introductions have depleted and modified Arizona waters. The state and regional ichthyofauna is described as declining toward extinction.
- **Threat status:** 19 of the 26 species are listed or proposed for listing as threatened or endangered by state or federal agencies.
- **Lost populations:** Naturally reproducing populations of eight species (Gila trout, bonytail, Colorado squawfish, beautiful shiner, razorback sucker, Yaqui sucker, Yaqui catfish, and desert [lower Colorado River] pupfish) have disappeared from Arizona. The Monkey Spring pupfish (*Cyprinodon* sp.) is extinct.
- **Competition and invasion:** Sonoran topminnow stockings within its native range often failed because of interactions with non-native [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). The genus *Poeciliopsis* is tropical in origin, and winter temperatures above 1,500 m likely doomed introductions there. Largemouth bass extirpated the Monkey Spring pupfish in nature.
- **Temperature limits:** Coldwater trouts are restricted to high elevations, whereas warmwater species spread readily through lowland habitats and can be nearly impossible to eradicate once established.
- **Biocontrol:** Sonoran topminnow was propagated and stocked for mosquito control in the 1970s, and most introductions from 1970 to 1975 served that aim. Other natives, including desert pupfish, Sonoran (Yaqui) topminnow, and small minnows, were noted as potential insect suppressors. A 1976 transfer of flannelmouth sucker to the Colorado River below Davis Dam was an attempt at biological control of pestiferous dipterans.
- **Baitfish legacy:** Several transfers (e.g., longfin dace, speckled dace, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead mountain-sucker, Sonoran chub) were likely made by anglers as baitfish before legislation banned such use of native fishes.

## Key Findings

- **Transplant volume:** About 200 transplant records cover 26 native taxa. Most intentional transfers aimed at conservation, and most stayed within native distributions.
- **Successful examples:** Apache trout (*Salmo apache*) was propagated and stocked widely from the 1960s, with several established populations. Gila trout and other recovery stocks were also documented. [[Apache Trout]] introductions aimed to create sport fisheries while maintaining the species.
- **Sport-fishery potential:** Gila trout, Colorado squawfish, humpback and roundtail chubs, bonytail chub, and Yaqui catfish offer possible sport fisheries if populations can be restored and public acceptance improved.
- **Hatchery role:** Hatchery stocks provide (1) early warning of declines, (2) founders for new or reintroduced populations, (3) insurance against catastrophic loss, and (4) research animals otherwise too rare to obtain. Sixteen of the 26 taxa have been or are under propagation study.
- **Genetic concerns:** Hatchery production carries potential genetic consequences, and unrecorded stockings can obscure or falsify natural distributions.
- **Reintroduction status:** Eleven fishes (Apache trout, Gila trout, bonytail, humpback chub, Yaqui chub, Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, both desert pupfish subspecies, and both Sonoran topminnow subspecies) have been reintroduced into suitable habitats in their native ranges or elsewhere. Some stocks are considered established. Others await legislation, brood stock, or habitat.
- **Key management message:** Natural populations should be protected before they become depleted, so the time, expense, and genetic risk of listing and recovery can be avoided.
- **Taxonomic note:** Desert pupfish comprises differentiated populations. Material from Santa Clara Slough, Sonora, most resembles the typical subspecies *C. m. macularius* from the San Pedro River, Arizona.
- **Erratum:** The published version's citation of a nomen nudum (*Cyprinodon macularius "sonoytae"*) attributed to Anonymous (1977) was a lapsus. The name appears only in an unpublished Arizona Game and Fish listing, and the authors take full responsibility.
