# Feasibility Study of Leslie Canyon Dam Site, Cochise County, Arizona

#hydrology #water-resources #endangered-species #Poeciliopsis-occidentalis #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

The [[Arizona Department of Water Resources]] contracted [[Earth Technology Corporation]] in 1984 to reassess the feasibility of constructing a permanent-pool recreational lake on [[Leslie Creek]] in [[Cochise County]], Arizona. The original 1960s-1970s feasibility studies estimated average annual watershed yield at 1,900 acre-feet, sufficient to support a 350-acre lake. However, streamflow records from 1970-1977 revealed actual average annual runoff of only 641 acre-feet. This hydrologic reanalysis, using linear correlation with long-term rainfall stations and water budget modeling over a 30-year critical low-flow period (1942-1971), determined a "best probable estimate" of 643 acre-feet per year average annual runoff. The study concluded that this yield is insufficient to sustain a viable recreational reservoir, particularly given downstream water rights claims exceeding 1,000 acre-feet annually and the presence of endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*) at the dam site.

## Background & Description

The proposed [[Leslie Canyon Dam]] site is located near the southern extremity of the [[Swisshelm Mountains]] in southeastern [[Cochise County]], approximately 18 miles north of Douglas, Arizona (Section 21, Township 21 South, Range 28 East). The headwaters of [[Leslie Creek]] flow southwest through Leslie Canyon before traversing the east side of [[Sulphur Springs Valley]] to join [[Whitewater Draw]].

Initial feasibility investigations in the late 1960s and early 1970s by the [[Arizona Department of Water Resources]] and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] included preliminary hydrologic investigations, foundation and materials investigations, and environmental impact assessments. The site geology consists of limestone, conglomerate, and quartz latite bedrock, with conglomerate underlying most of the proposed reservoir area. Recent alluvial deposits of sand and gravels occupy intermittent stream channels.

The original hydrologic analysis transposed runoff data from two watersheds located approximately 65 miles west of Leslie Canyon, yielding an estimated average annual flow of 1,900 acre-feet. However, a U.S. Geological Survey streamflow recording station installed in October 1969 at the approximate dam location recorded actual flows from 1970-1975 ranging from 121 to 1,620 acre-feet annually, with a six-year average of 641 acre-feet—substantially below the original estimate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Leslie Creek]] watershed supports significant ecological resources that complicate dam construction. The site contains populations of the federally-listed endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*), a subspecies endemic to the [[Rio Yaqui]] drainage with extremely limited distribution in the United States. According to 1977 correspondence from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], Leslie Creek also supports other rare native fishes including *Agosia chrysogaster sonoriensis* and *Campostoma ornatum pricei*, species listed by Arizona as endangered (Group II—in danger of elimination from Arizona).

The area was proposed by the [[Arizona Academy of Science]] as a Natural Area under the Arizona State Parks system based on the quality of its riparian ecosystem and unique aquatic fauna. Riparian habitat in southeastern Arizona is rare and nearly disappeared; historical attempts to reestablish riparian communities have been largely unsuccessful. The 1976 Environmental Assessment noted that dam construction would result in highly negative secondary impacts on wildlife habitat and loss of riparian habitat.

Construction of the dam would violate the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]] (Section 7), which prohibits federal agencies from authorizing, funding, or carrying out actions that jeopardize the continued existence of listed species or modify critical habitat. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service stated in 1977 correspondence that assuming Leslie Creek habitat is critical to the endangered species' continued existence, they would not support dam construction. Federal funds (including Dingell-Johnson funds) would be precluded from the project.

Archaeological sites were also documented within the proposed reservoir basin.

## Key Findings

**Hydrologic Analysis Results:**

- The hydrologic base period selected for analysis was 1951-1982, representing conditions approximating the long-term mean based on cumulative departure analysis of rainfall and runoff at regional stations ([[Rucker Canyon]], [[Stephens Ranch]], Douglas, Willcox, Tucson for rainfall; [[Santa Cruz River]] near Lochiel and Nogales for runoff).

- Linear correlation of Leslie Canyon runoff (1969-1977) with annual rainfall at [[Rucker Canyon]] produced the best correlation coefficient and yielded average annual runoff estimates ranging from 632 to 647 acre-feet across three candidate base periods.

- The "best probable estimate" of average annual runoff was determined to be **643 acre-feet per year**, representing the middle estimate for the 1951-1982 base period.

- This estimate is approximately one-third of the original 1,900 acre-feet estimate used in 1970s feasibility studies.

**Water Budget Analysis:**

- A 30-year water budget routing analysis (1942-1971) was conducted to test reservoir viability during the historic critical low-flow period, beginning with an initial surface area of 200 acres and storage of 3,400 acre-feet.

- Without considering sedimentation, the reservoir declined to an end-of-year low of 60 acres with storage capacity of 546 acre-feet.

- End-of-year storage fell below 1,000 acre-feet for seven years during the 30-year period.

- The reservoir never returned to its initial 200-acre surface area and 3,400 acre-feet storage capacity.

- Average end-of-year water surface area was approximately 125 acres with storage capacity of about 1,500 acre-feet.

- Preliminary estimates indicated 50-year sediment accumulation of approximately 814 acre-feet, representing a surface area of 85 acres at 40 feet depth at the dam.

- With sediment accumulation considered, the reservoir would have dried up entirely three times during the 30-year routing period.

**Water Rights Constraints:**

- Downstream water rights claims on [[Leslie Creek]] total approximately 1,007.76 acre-feet annually, including 1,001.46 acre-feet for irrigation and 6.3 acre-feet for non-irrigation uses (primarily stockponds).

- Reservoir storage rights claims total 5,400 acre-feet.

- Total downstream claims (exceeding 1,000 acre-feet/year) exceed the estimated average annual watershed yield by approximately 350 acre-feet per year.

- During the 30-year routing period analyzed, estimated annual inflow would have been insufficient to meet prior rights claims in 25 of 30 years, allowing surface inflow to replace evaporation losses in only five years.

**Environmental and Legal Constraints:**

- The presence of endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*) at the dam site creates insurmountable legal barriers under the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]].

- Federal law prohibits moving endangered species populations to permit habitat modification, even to potentially suitable alternative sites.

- State agencies constructing dams on non-federal lands with non-federal funds that eliminate endangered species populations would be subject to punitive action under the 1973 Act.

- No federal funds (including [[Dingell-Johnson]] funds) could be used for projects jeopardizing endangered species.

**Economic Considerations:**

- Original cost estimates for a 350-acre lake totaled approximately $1,100,000 ($700,000 dam construction, $225,000 road relocation, $175,000 land acquisition).

- Reducing lake size to 100 acres (supportable by actual watershed yield) would reduce dam construction costs by only $100,000 (to $600,000), with road and land costs remaining essentially unchanged—total cost approximately $1,000,000.

- The economic cost-benefit ratio for a significantly reduced lake size was deemed unfavorable.

**Final Conclusions:**

The December 4, 1984 memorandum from [[Ray O. Perkins Jr.]] (Engineering Branch Supervisor) to [[Robert D. Curtis]] (Special Services Division Chief) concluded: "Based upon results of the hydrologic reanalysis of Leslie Canyon watershed, it is our opinion that construction of the dam at the proposed site would not be feasible from a hydrologic standpoint. The watershed yield, in our opinion, will not support a viable, permanent pool for recreational purposes."

The project remained "in limbo" as of 1977, with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]] conducting reconnaissance for alternate sites on national forest lands with assistance from the Douglas Forest Ranger, while maintaining commitment to providing fishing lake opportunities in southeastern Arizona.
