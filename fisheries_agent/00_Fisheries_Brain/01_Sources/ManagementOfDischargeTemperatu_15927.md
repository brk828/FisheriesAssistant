# Management of Discharge, Temperature, and Sediment in Grand Canyon for Native Fishes

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #conservation #dam-operations #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1994 management proposal addresses the severe impacts of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on native fish populations in [[Grand Canyon]]. The 1963 dam closure fundamentally altered downstream flow regimes, thermal characteristics, and sediment transport dynamics, resulting in extirpation of three native species ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]]) and severe population declines in others ([[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]]). The authors contend that restoration requires returning environmental conditions to approximate those present during the evolutionary history of these fishes. They recommend flow modifications that minimize daily fluctuations, adjust seasonal hydrology to parallel natural discharge patterns, and deliver controlled floods. Additional elements include water temperature modification via multi-level intake structure (MLIS) and sediment augmentation. The proposal emphasizes that hydrology, temperature, and sediment were inextricably linked in the pre-dam system and must be integrated into future dam operations.

## Background & Description

**Pre-Dam Environment:**
The pre-dam [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]] exhibited a predictable unimodal annual hydrograph driven by snowmelt from the upper basin. Based on USGS records from Phantom Ranch (1942-1962), mean monthly flows ranged from 5,000-10,000 cfs September through February, rising to approximately 50,000 cfs in June, then receding abruptly to base levels by August. Mean suspended sediment discharge was 66.1 million tons per annum at Lee's Ferry, approaching 86 million tons at Phantom Ranch. The [[Paria River]] and [[Little Colorado River]] contributed disproportionately large sediment loads relative to their water discharge. Water temperatures varied seasonally from 3°C in January to near 25°C in July-August.

**Native Fish Adaptations:**
Native fishes evolved morphological specializations including large fusiform bodies, depressed crania, large predorsal humps, elongated caudal peduncles, large falcate fins, small eyes, small embedded scales, and thick leathery skin—all adaptations to turbulence, high current velocities, and suspended sediment. Physiological tolerances span approximately 0-30°C for adults, though embryos and larvae have more restricted ranges. Longevity allowed persistence through short-term climatic events unsuitable for reproduction. Reproductive timing coincided with descending limb of peak flows (May-August) when temperatures exceeded 16°C, except [[Razorback Sucker]] which spawns at lower temperatures.

**Post-Dam Impacts:**
Glen Canyon Dam eliminated the natural hydrograph, replacing it with daily discharge fluctuations approaching 30,000 cfs under routine powerplant operations. Maximum discharges typically did not exceed 31,500 cfs (pre-uprating powerplant capacity). Sediment input from the Upper Colorado River Basin was curtailed by approximately 77% (66 million tons/year), with remaining load from tributaries below the dam. Water temperatures became perennially cold (7-15°C), eliminating seasonal variation. These changes resulted in substrate armoring, altered trophic dynamics, disrupted reproduction, reduced growth rates, and decreased survival of early life stages.

**Species Status:**
Three species were extirpated within a decade of dam closure. [[Humpback Chub]] reproduces only in a single tributary (Little Colorado River) with contracted mainstem distribution. [[Razorback Sucker]] is extremely rare with no current evidence of reproduction. Successful mainstem reproduction by remaining native species was reduced or eliminated. At least 10 non-native fish species now inhabit the canyon, along with non-native parasites including *Lernaea cyprinacea* and *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flood Pulse Concept:**
The authors apply the flood pulse concept (Junk et al. 1989) to explain adaptations of native fishes, arguing these adaptations transcend geomorphic differences between classical floodplain rivers and canyon-bound systems. Natural hydrological patterns have been advocated for restoration of Missouri River and Green River fish communities.

**Habitat Formation:**
Pre-dam spring flows inundated canyon-bound tributary mouths, creating large ponded areas with low current velocities. Debris fans from tributary floods created steep gradient rapids with large upstream pools. Flow separation below constrictions formed eddy complexes and backwater habitats. The ascending limb scoured peripheral and midchannel habitats; sediments deposited on the descending limb. Late summer tributary floods transported fine sediments that aggraded on channel bottoms.

**Tributary-Mainstem Interactions:**
Tributary reproduction peaks April-June. High mainstem flows historically backed up tributary mouths, providing excellent rearing habitat for larvae drifting from upstream spawning sites. Young-of-year fishes encountered warmer, clearer water with slowing currents that deposited invertebrates and organic matter. By July when mainstem flows receded, juveniles were large enough to survive mainstem entry. This pattern was observed during high water years 1984-1986 when numerous young-of-year [[Humpback Chub]] were found in the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence zone.

**Trophic Dynamics:**
Pre-dam metabolism was driven by heterotrophic processes during high discharge/sediment periods, with vast volumes of allochthonous organic matter from upstream terrestrial sources. Autotrophic production increased as sediments settled during base flows. Post-dam, standing crops of attached algae increased in the stable, clear, armored tailwater. High densities but low diversity of invertebrates characterize the tailwater. Thermal constancy and low temperatures disrupt life cycle cues for many macroinvertebrates. Transitory nearshore habitats are unsuitable for colonization by desiccant-intolerant taxa.

## Key Findings

**Recommended Hydrology (Without Temperature Modification):**
1. Replace daily fluctuations with seasonal adjustments producing single peak hydrograph
2. Maintain base flows minimum 5,000 cfs autumn-winter (September-February)
3. Begin raising discharge in March, reaching peak near 30,000 cfs in May-June
4. Maintain high flows through native fish tributary reproductive period
5. Descend in monthly steps concentrated in several successive days, reaching base flows by September
6. In years with releases exceeding 8.23 million acre-feet and sufficient sediment, program additional water into spring releases as controlled floods to displace non-native fishes

**Benefits of Recommended Flows:**
- Provide environmental cue for gonadal maturation and tributary ascension
- Back up tributary waters forming ponded rearing habitats at mouths
- Reduce transport of early life stages from tributaries to mainstem
- Create and maintain nearshore rearing habitats
- Reduce non-native fish populations through displacement, reproductive disruption, and lowered early life stage survivorship
- Allow extended periods of stable flows for young native fish growth

**Temperature Modification Recommendations:**
Install multi-level intake structure (MLIS) to control water temperature:
- Gradual increase from hypolimnetic releases beginning in spring
- Achieve 15°C June-September/October using epilimnial releases
- Rapid return to hypolimnetic releases November-February
- Expected downstream warming: additional 2°C at Little Colorado River (122 km), nearly 7°C at Diamond Creek (386 km)
- Would provide 19-20°C optimal spawning/rearing temperatures beginning ~200 km below dam

**Modified Hydrology with Temperature Control:**
With MLIS operation allowing successful mainstem reproduction:
- Mainstem reproductive and rearing habitats increase in importance relative to tributaries
- Peak flows can be maintained for shorter period
- More rapid descent to 5,000-10,000 cfs range beginning early July to maximize nearshore rearing habitats
- Potential for diel winter flow fluctuations to destabilize nearshore habitats used by non-native fishes, particularly small-bodied species (fathead minnow, red shiner)

**Sediment Augmentation:**
- Apply during highest spring flows when sediment most easily mobilized and distributed
- Restore functional role in temporal and structural formation of instream habitats
- Maintain sediment balance in Grand Canyon
- Include silts and clays as well as sand (pre-dam: ~70% sand, 30% silt-clay)
- Fine-grained sediments provide turbidity cover for native fishes, limit sight-feeding predator effectiveness, and supply phosphorus for primary productivity

**Implementation Strategy:**
- Protracted period measured in generations of long-lived fishes necessary for restoration
- Emphasize dynamic flexibility and experimentation in all operational phases
- Systematic, programmed, and opportunistic monitoring of habitat and biota changes
- Initially fix discharge regimens to eliminate daily fluctuations and mimic natural seasonal hydrograph for period adequate to document enhancement
- Contemporaneous research on seasonal thermal modification via MLIS
- Add sediment augmentation to complete restoration steps
- Continuous status assessment with feedback loop to water managers
- Process may take decades to resolve optimal combination of hydrology, temperature, and sediment

**Potential MLIS Concerns:**
- Alteration of algal species composition and productivity
- Alteration of invertebrate species composition and productivity
- Possible invasion/enhancement of non-native fishes from Lake Mead, Lake Powell, or tributaries
- Increased incidence of fish diseases and parasites
- Authors note proposed maximum release temperatures only 5-6°C higher than present, with continued hypolimnial releases autumn-winter

**Legal and Historical Context:**
- Pre-construction evaluation limited to sport fisheries expectations; native fishes largely ignored
- [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] listed endangered March 1967
- [[Bonytail Chub]] listed endangered April 1980
- [[Razorback Sucker]] listed endangered October 1991
- USFWS rendered jeopardy opinion for humpback chub in 1978, recommended studies before temperature modification
- Glen Canyon Environmental Studies initiated December 1982
- Environmental Impact Statement scheduled 1989, to be completed in 24 months

**Conclusion:**
The authors express confidence that returning the Colorado River to semblance of natural state through creative changes in discharge, thermal regime, and sediment augmentation will ultimately enhance native fishes. They emphasize this is not trial-and-error but requires operational flexibility once capability is in place. Water management should be responsive to native ichthyofauna requirements. Year class strength was historically variable; operations need not optimize conditions in all years, allowing opportunities to suppress non-natives, benefit other resources, or serve other purposes. Goals and objectives are realistic and can be successfully attained, though time frame is protracted.
