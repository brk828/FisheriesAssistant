# The Column From California: Military Logistics and Aquatic Notes on the 1862 Arizona Campaign

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is a self-published military history by Homer D. Wilkes (Scottsdale, July 4, 1988) recounting the 1861–1862 march of the [[Column From California]], a roughly 2,000-man Union brigade under Colonel (later Brigadier General) [[James Henry Carleton]]. Its mission was to reopen the southern land route between California and the eastern states by retaking Arizona and New Mexico from Confederate forces. The column crossed about 354 miles of desert from [[Fort Yuma]] to Las Cruces, New Mexico, largely by six-mule freight wagon, during the driest season in 30 years and amid severe floods on the Colorado and Gila rivers. The narrative covers the Tonto expedition, the battle at [[Apache Pass]] (July 15, 1862), the intelligence exploits of [[John Jones]], and the Confederate retreat of [[Henry Hopkins Sibley]]. The document is largely narrative and biographical, with an appendix-style bibliography.

## Background & Description

- **Format:** Book-length typescript (about 118 pages) with chapter headings, extensive citations to the *War of the Rebellion* official records, and a bibliography.
- **Author's perspective:** The author, a former freighter and Army officer, adds personal freighting anecdotes and logistical commentary.
- **Command structure:** The brigade was formed from First California Volunteer Infantry and Cavalry units, a Regular Army artillery battery, and detachments of the Fifth and Second California Volunteers.
- **Key figures:** [[James Henry Carleton]], [[Edward E. Eyre]], [[Joseph R. West]], [[William McCleave]], [[Emil A. C. Fritz]], [[John C. Cremony]], [[Thomas L. Roberts]], [[John B. Shinn]].
- **Opposing forces:** [[Sherod Hunter]] (Company A, First Texas Cavalry, about 75–95 men at Tucson), [[John R. Baylor]] (self-declared Confederate governor of Arizona), and [[William Steele]] (Confederate regiment at Mesilla).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The text is primarily military, but several passages describe the aquatic and desert environment:

- **Water sources:** The Gila River supplied the route from Yuma to Gila Bend and from Maricopa Wells to Sacaton Station. East of there, the Picacho, Tucson, Cow Springs and Cooke's Spring were the main watering points. Between Sacaton and Tucson, troops relied on wells.
- **Hydrology:** Rains from November 1861 to early 1862 caused the Colorado and Gila to flood. Fort Yuma became an island, and water stood 15 feet deep across parts of the lowlands. Later floods on the Rio Grande made Mesilla an island in July 1862.
- **Drought:** Arizona and New Mexico were in the worst drought in 30 years, and near-famine conditions had existed among native peoples for two years.
- **Fish record:** On June 26, 1862, Eyre's troops caught fish at a large cienega on the dry San Simon River bed. The soldiers believed them to be trout. Dr. W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) stated there were never native trout in the San Simon and that the fish were likely the native minnow *Gila robusta* (Colorado River Chub, also called bonytail, roundtail, or Verde trout). The flesh is pink in the mating season, which explains the confusion.
- **Ecological notes on the Pimas:** Pima and Maricopa farming towns provided grain, hay, and produce to the troops.

## Key Findings

- **Mission and route:** The Column From California was authorized by General Orders No. 1 (May 15, 1862). The southern stage route through Tucson was chosen over the northern route, which was impassable for about four months each year.
- **Logistics:** Supply lines stretched about 300 miles from Yuma to Tucson and roughly 750 miles to the Rio Grande. Four freight trains of 50 six-mule teams each were used. Carleton's staff estimated 1,009,575 pounds of three-month supplies. Wagon rebuild shops at Tucson repaired about two vehicles per day.
- **Campaign milestones:**
  - Tucson was entered June 7, 1862 (occupied after Hunter withdrew).
  - The Battle of Apache Pass occurred July 15, 1862. Roberts' force of about 97 enlisted men and two officers, with artillery support, held the spring against Mangus Colorado and Cochise. Casualties were two Union dead (Pvt. John Barr and Lt. Thompson's artillerymen) and one wounded. The author estimates Apache losses were far higher.
  - Carleton's headquarters reached Las Cruces August 10, 1862.
- **Intelligence:** John Jones carried Carleton's dispatches to General Edward Canby, traveling about 300 miles through Apache country. His escort, Sergeant William Wheeling and guide Chavez, were killed. Jones was captured at Picacho, New Mexico, and his message reached Canby via a Union spy.
- **Confederate retreat:** Sibley's brigade had about 3,200 men at its peak. Losses were stated as about 500 from wounds and disease. Sibley's wagon train was burned at Johnson's Ranch (Pigeon's Ranch battle, March 28, 1862).
- **Biographical notes:** The author provides detailed career sketches of Carleton, Canby, West, Bowie, Eyre, and others, along with a critique of the brevet system.
- **Author's conclusions:** The author argues that Carleton's success came from his care for animals and logistics. He also contends that Davis, not Sibley, originated the New Mexico invasion plan.
- **Limitations:** The text is a secondary narrative compiled from official records and newspapers, not original field research. Some sources cited in the bibliography are OCR-garbled or incomplete.
