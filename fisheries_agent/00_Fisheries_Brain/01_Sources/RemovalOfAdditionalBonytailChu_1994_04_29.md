## Executive Summary

This memorandum documents a fish salvage and translocation operation conducted at Palm Lake, part of The Nature Conservancy's Hassayampa River Preserve in Arizona, between 26–28 April 1994. The operation targeted the recovery of the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, also known as Colorado Pikeminnow), using trammel nets and boat electrofishing. A total of 25 bonytail chubs and 3 Colorado squawfish were captured, PIT-tagged, and relocated to the Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge (Office Cove, Lake Havasu) to establish or supplement populations outside of isolated, at-risk habitat. Incidental catch included large numbers of non-native [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*), some of which were examined for parasites and disease. Two bonytail chub mortalities occurred during handling with no clear cause identified. The effort was a multi-agency collaboration between the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), The Nature Conservancy, and Arizona State University.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Palm Lake functions as an isolated lentic (still-water) refuge habitat within the Hassayampa River Preserve, historically used to hold or protect populations of endangered native [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes.
- **Native vs. Non-native Interactions**: The presence of a large non-native [[Yellow Bullhead]] population within the same water body as endangered natives highlights a recurring conservation issue in the Colorado River Basin — competition and/or predation pressure from introduced ictalurids on native cyprinids.
- **Translocation Strategy**: Captured bonytail chub and Colorado squawfish were moved to the Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge (Office Cove, Lake Havasu), a designated recovery habitat for these federally listed species, consistent with broader [[Colorado River Fish Recovery Program]] efforts to consolidate genetically valuable individuals into managed, monitored habitats.
- **Species examined for health**: Non-lethal sampling for protozoan and bacteriological pathogens was conducted on bonytail chub and Colorado squawfish, reflecting concern for disease transfer during translocation efforts.

## Key Findings

- 25 bonytail chub and 3 Colorado squawfish (both federally endangered species) were successfully captured via trammel netting (primary method) and boat electrofishing (supplementary, primarily yielding yellow bullhead).
- All captured endangered fish were PIT-tagged for future identification and monitoring before release.
- Female bonytail chub were larger on average than males (mean TL 407 mm vs. 374 mm; mean weight 513 g vs. 378 g).
- Two bonytail chub mortalities occurred during the operation, with no identifiable cause — flagged for further investigation given identical handling procedures.
- Extensive incidental non-native [[Yellow Bullhead]] catch (>50 individuals) suggests significant non-native fish presence in Palm Lake, a factor relevant to habitat management for native species.
- Fish health assessments (parasite and pathogen screening) were conducted in the field by Fish Health personnel from Pinetop; results were pending distribution at time of memo.
- The operation exemplifies successful interagency cooperation among the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[The Nature Conservancy]], and [[Arizona State University]] in endangered fish conservation and translocation efforts within the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]].
