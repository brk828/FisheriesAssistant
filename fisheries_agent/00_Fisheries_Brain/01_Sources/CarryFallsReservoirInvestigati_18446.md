# Carry Falls Reservoir Investigation (1951–1955)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #reservoir-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This report, by District Fisheries Manager Donald G. Pasko of the [[New York State Conservation Department]], compares the fish resource and angling of a 9-mile section of the [[Raquette River]] in St. Lawrence County before and after impoundment by the [[Carry Falls Reservoir]]. Surveys were run in July and August of 1951 (pre-impoundment) and 1955 (post-impoundment). The study was a cooperative effort with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under the [[Coordination Act]] framework, intended to predict and manage the effects of impoundments on fish and wildlife.

The 3,200-acre reservoir, built for [[Hydroelectric Power]] storage and regulation, increased total fishing effort and the overall catch, but the gain came almost entirely from a large increase in [[Common Bullhead]] numbers. Angling quality for the principal gamefish, [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Northern Pike]], remained mediocre. Natural reproduction was judged sufficient, so hatchery stocking was not needed. The author recommended no changes to fishing regulations or management methods.

## Background & Description

Large numbers of artificial reservoirs were being built or proposed in the United States, and the author argues that their fish and wildlife effects should be evaluated during planning. The [[Carry Falls Reservoir]] site was selected because a Federal Power Commission license (1951) allowed comparison of pre- and post-project conditions with minimal delay.

The study area was a 9-mile river section flowing south to north through Adirondack forest. Before impoundment, about 7.5 miles were stillwater divided by two rapids sections, with a 70-foot drop mostly in the 2 miles of rapids between [[Jamestown Falls]] and Carry Falls. Carry Falls and Jamestown Falls were natural barriers to upstream fish movement. Thirty tributaries entered the section, 13 of them brook trout streams. Only Cold Brook and the Jordan River had notable trout angling value.

The completed dam was 830 feet long and 70 feet high. Together with five earth dikes, it created a reservoir that, at full pool (elevation 1,385 feet), covered 3,200 acres, held about 5 billion cubic feet of water, reached over 60 feet in maximum depth, and had about 25 miles of shoreline. The dam was completed in 1952, and the reservoir was first filled in spring 1953. The field summary gives 1953 as the completion year, but the body text places dam completion in 1952.

Two methods dominated the work: a creel census of anglers (via car counts, interviews, and estimated scaling of effort) and fish collections by seining, gill netting, and trap netting. Scale samples were used for age determination.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions.** Before impoundment, the river was shallow and unpolluted, with dissolved oxygen of 6.4–8.4 ppm, CO₂ of 2 ppm or less, methyl-orange alkalinity averaging 12.3 ppm CaCO₃ (soft water), and pH of 6.1–6.8. Water temperatures in July and August averaged about 70°F, with no significant stratification. Pond weeds (*Potamogeton*, seven species recognized), water lilies, quillwort, bur reed, bulrush, and spike rush were common.

**After impoundment.** The reservoir was lake-like for most of the year and river-like in late winter. Its annual water-level fluctuation was about 40 feet, with maximum pool in June–July and minimum pool in March. Summer 1955 was abnormally hot and dry. Surface water temperatures averaged 79°F in July and 76°F in August. Bottom temperatures stayed near 55°F. Partial thermal stratification developed in deeper water, with a [[Thermocline]] below about 20–30 feet. A true cold-water hypolimnion never formed, possibly because of drawdown through deep-water penstocks. By late August, dissolved oxygen was depleted below 30 feet (only slight traces), and hydrogen sulfide odor was noted near the bottom. Rooted aquatic vegetation largely disappeared from the main body, persisting only in the Jamestown stillwater, which had less water-level fluctuation. The author judged that the sandy, gravelly bottom was basically infertile.

**Fish population.** Thirteen species were recorded. Two species were not found in 1955: brook trout (no reservoir catches) and blacknose shiner (none seined, although large schools were seen in 1953). [[Lake Herring]] was newly recorded in 1955 (two specimens), though it had long been present upstream in the Raquette River drainage. Brook trout were still reported from [[Cold Brook]], and no warm-water species were reported entering trout waters.

**Management context.** The fluctuating water regime probably reduced overall fish production, but stillwater areas concentrated fish and forage during drawdowns and may have acted as sanctuaries. Natural reproduction by most species appeared adequate. Northern pike were a possible exception because of poor spawning conditions and predation on eggs by bullheads.

## Key Findings

**Impoundment results**
- The reservoir increased utilization. Estimated total effort in July–August 1955 was about four times that of 1951, but 1951 pressure was only about half of normal, so 1955 effort was roughly double a normal pre-impoundment year.
- Of 656 estimated angler cars in July–August 1955, 372 (57%) were counted and 281 (43%) were checked, containing 666 anglers (about 2.4 per car).
- The total estimated catch increased by about 20 times in number and about 10 times in weight. The 1951 total weight was 281 lb and the 1955 total was 2,846 lb.

**Bullhead expansion**
- Bullheads made up about 88% of the 1955 angling catch, compared with about 3% in 1951.
- Time to catch a bullhead fell from 96 hours (1951) to about 0.75 hour (1955).
- The average bullhead size dropped from 10.2 inches to 7.6 inches, and almost none of large size were taken in 1955.
- Bullhead weight rose to 1,793 lb (63% of the 1955 catch weight).

**Angling quality**
- Average effort per angler was 3.2 hours with 1 fish caught in 1951. In 1955 it was 3.3 hours with 5 fish caught, though over 4 of those 5 were bullheads.
- Time to catch a [[Smallmouth Bass]] was about 11 hours both before and after impoundment.
- Time to catch a [[Northern Pike]] dropped slightly from 35 to 32 hours.
- Fishing for other species (rock bass, yellow perch, sunfish, fallfish) was judged poorer or unchanged, though the data were considered unreliable.
- The author judged gamefish angling quality "mediocre" both before and after impoundment.

**Fish populations and growth**
- Smallmouth bass were the principal gamefish, with a large population of 6–9 inch fish in the rapids. Growth was considered average: 4–5 years to reach the 10-inch legal length.
- Average bass length rose slightly, from 11.6 to 12.4 inches. The age-length data showed only slight growth gains.
- Legal-to-sub-legal bass ratio shifted from about 1:3 (1951) to about 1:1 (1955).
- About 75% of bass caught in 1951 were undersized and released. In 1955, about 40% were sub-legal, with about 70% sub-legal in the Jamestown section and about 20% in the main reservoir.
- Pikeperch were introduced around 1916. Nine collected in July 1955 were in their second or third year and averaged 12.0 inches in the third-year group.
- Northern pike were about 3% sub-legal in 1955 and no young-of-year were collected, suggesting poor reproduction.

**Reproduction and stocking**
- No hatchery plantings were made in the impoundment area between 1953 and 1955.
- Abundant natural reproduction by [[Smallmouth Bass]] was observed, with black fry guarded by males in June 1955 and young-of-the-year common in July seining.
- Young perch, bullheads, and fallfish were also abundant.
- Natural spawning was judged sufficient to maintain angling species without stocking.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- No damage to the original fish resource was identified.
- Evidence of warm-water species invading tributary trout waters was inconclusive, but penetration was considered unlikely.
- Annual water-level fluctuation was unavoidable under the reservoir's power purpose. It probably limited fish production and utilization, but the net effect was an overall gain.
- No changes to open seasons, size limits, or catch limits were recommended.
- Public access was provided by the power company, including roads and two parking lots with boat ramps, and a permanent easement was granted on the old Hollywood road.
