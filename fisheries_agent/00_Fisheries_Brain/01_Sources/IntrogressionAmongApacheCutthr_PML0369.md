# Introgression among Apache, Cutthroat and Rainbow Trout in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 study by [[Carmichael]] and colleagues assessed the extent and direction of interspecific hybridization between [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), and [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarki*) across the historical range of Apache trout in Arizona. Using 10 polymorphic [[Isozyme Electrophoresis]] loci on 645 individuals from 31 wild populations, the authors found that introgression is widespread and that pure Apache trout remain in only 11 potentially unhybridized populations. Rainbow trout alleles were documented in 19 of 31 populations, and backcrossing toward Apache trout was the dominant trend. The authors conclude that hybridization, habitat degradation and barriers to rainbow trout immigration will complicate recovery under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Apache trout was described by [[Robert Rush Miller]] in 1972 (*Salmo apache*, Miller 1972). It was originally distributed in the upper Salt River, San Francisco River and Little Colorado River headwaters in the White Mountains, Arizona.
- **Decline:** Habitat loss, overfishing, and competition with introduced exotics reduced the species. By 1950 the only known populations were on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]]. Fishing was closed in 1955 (White Mountain Apache Tribe) and statewide in 1974. The species was listed as endangered in 1973 and downgraded to threatened in 1974; it now occupies fewer than 30 stream systems.
- **Stocking history:** Cutthroat trout were stocked from about 1920 to 1942, and rainbow trout from 1934 to 1954, with some continuing in marginal habitat. Competition with brown and brook trout has also been identified as a major cause of decline.
- **Recovery efforts:** Since 1965, the [[Arizona Trout Recovery Team]] (including the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[U.S. Forest Service]] and the White Mountain Apache Tribe) has relied on stocking "pure" Apache trout into allopatric streams and on renovation with the piscicide [[Antimycin A]]. Hatchery sources included [[Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]] and the [[Sterling Springs Hatchery]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** Samples were collected from the Fort Apache Indian Reservation and the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]] by electrofishing and angling from fall 1987 through spring 1989.
- **Sample design:** 645 individuals from 31 wild populations. Four reference samples were used: 25 Apache trout (hatchery), 25 Eagle Lake rainbow trout, 28 Yellowstone cutthroat trout (Brigham Young University), and 29 Apache × rainbow F1 hybrids.
- **Barriers:** River and creek barriers may limit rainbow trout migration, but the authors note that these barriers may be transient and not fully effective.
- **Genetic bottlenecks:** Many Apache trout populations have undergone severe and repeated bottlenecks, reducing genetic variability.
- **Methods:** Loci were resolved by [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]] of muscle, liver and eye tissue. Hybrid classes (F1, F2, backcross) were assigned from Mendelian expectations at diagnostic loci, analysed with the BIOSYS-1 program.

## Key Findings

- **Pure stocks are scarce:** Only 11 potentially unhybridized Apache trout populations were found. No pure rainbow or cutthroat trout were sampled from the wild.
- **Rainbow trout introgression:** Documented in 19 of 31 populations, including at least two populations in which every sampled fish was a hybrid.
- **Cutthroat involvement:** Apache–cutthroat hybridization was detected in four introgressed populations. At two localities, alleles from all three species were sampled. Apache–cutthroat hybrids were also found at one site lacking rainbow trout alleles.
- **Backcrossing trend:** In 19 of the 20 hybridized populations, backcrossing toward Apache trout was evident.
- **Diagnostic loci:** Of 55 or so loci assessed, 10 were polymorphic. Five loci showed fixed differences between Apache and rainbow trout, and six showed fixed differences between Apache and cutthroat trout. Diagnostic power was limited, so hybrid generations beyond the second could not be estimated, and the hybrid rate is probably underestimated.
- **Limited genetic variability:** Only three loci showed detectable variation in Apache trout populations, so the genetic variability of the Apache genome could not be reliably estimated.
- **Allele origin:** Seven alleles at four loci were found only in hybridized populations. Only one (sMDH-B1,2*e) could be confidently assigned to rainbow trout origin, based on its frequency in hybrid populations.
- **Management implications:** Recovery will be complicated by variable introgression, habitat deterioration and transient barriers. The authors recommend maintaining parallel hatchery stocks, including those carrying rare alleles (e.g., CK-B*a from Coyote Creek, PGM*d from Deep Creek, PGK*a from Boggy Creek-1), to limit inbreeding depression. They argue that an effort comparable to past introductions will be needed to prevent extinction of Apache trout.
