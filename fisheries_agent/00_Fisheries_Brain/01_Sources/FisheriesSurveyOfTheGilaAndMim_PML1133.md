# Fisheries Survey of the Gila and Mimbres Rivers Drainages

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This Project Completion Report (July 1955, [[Federal Aid Project F-1-R]], prepared by Earl H. Huntington with Homer C. Pickens and Roy E. Barker) summarizes a 1952–1955 survey of the fish resources of the [[Gila River]] and [[Mimbres River]] drainages in southwestern New Mexico. The survey aimed to inventory fish stocks and develop a management plan for optimum sustained yield of game fishes. Using [[Electrofishing]], stomach and scale analyses, stream bottom sampling and water chemistry tests, the authors documented 22 fish species, widespread trout hybridization, abundant rough fish in warm lowland streams, and limited fishing pressure on remote waters. The report closes with twelve management recommendations.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** The surveyed waters lie in the Gila National Forest and adjacent areas. The Gila River is part of the Colorado River drainage, and the Mimbres River belongs to the Rio Grande drainage. Both rise in the Mogollon and Black Mountain ranges. Elevations range from 3,700 to 10,778 feet, spanning Lower Sonoran, Upper Sonoran, Transition and Canadian life zones, with vegetation from mesquite and creosote to spruce, fir and aspen.

**Survey scope.** The report summarizes rather than reproduces individual stream reports, which are on file with the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish. Surveyed waters included more than 40 streams (Table 1) and three lakes: [[Bear Canyon Reservoir]], [[Wall Lake]] and [[San Simon Lake]].

**Introduced and native fishes.** Since 1901, plantings included brook, rainbow, brown, Gila (listed as "native") and other trout, along with black bass, bullhead, catfish, sunfish and steelhead/salmon records. A trout hatchery on the West Fork of the Gila River, established in 1923, was abandoned in 1935 because of its inaccessibility. Local residents reported that trout once ranged as far downstream as Red Rock, New Mexico.

**Species inventory.** Table 2 lists 22 known species, including four trout, [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], green sunfish, [[Channel Catfish]], two bullheads, two suckers, [[Colorado River Chub]], several dace and minnows, and common gambusia.

**Methods.** A 110-volt AC portable electroshocker, packed on a single pack animal, sampled stream sections, with seines and dip nets used as blocking aids. Lake populations were sampled with experimental gill nets and seines. Trout stomachs were examined in the laboratory, scales were read for [[Age and Growth]] analysis, and [[Benthic Macroinvertebrates]] were sampled with a square-foot bottom sampler. Water samples were tested in the field and sent to the Soil Conservation Service laboratory.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and hydrology.** Long-term precipitation records are sparse. At Fort Bayard, near Silver City, average annual precipitation for 1949–1954 was 12.6 inches. At the Hedrick Ranch on the East Fork of the Gila River, the six-year average was 15.0 inches. Discharge of the Gila River near Gila, New Mexico (drainage area about 1,870 square miles, 1930–1950 record) showed peak years of 490 second-feet (1932), 220 (1937), 378 (1941) and 369 (1949). The Mimbres River near Bear Canyon averaged 11.6 cubic feet per second. Mean temperature at Silver City in 1954 was 57.4 °F, about 3.9 °F above the long-term average. The authors concluded that precipitation and runoff had gradually declined in recent years.

**Water chemistry.** The three lakes ranged from pH 8.0 to 10.5, dissolved oxygen from 4.7 to 12.0 ppm, and total hardness from 80 to 240 ppm; all were classed as hard-water lakes. Stream chemistry varied with elevation: high-elevation streams were medium-hard, while low-elevation streams were hard. Example: Gila River at about 5,000 ft (pH 8.0, hardness 110 ppm).

**Physical barriers.** Dry sections, isolated pools during drought, and waterfalls shaped fish distribution. A waterfall of about 20 feet on the West Fork of the Gila River blocked suckers, and a waterfall of about seven feet at the mouth of McKenna Creek confined *Salmo gilae* to the reaches above it.

**Habitat and fish assemblages.** The greatest species diversity and largest fish numbers occurred in warm, low-elevation streams. Few fishes other than trout and dace occurred above 7,000 feet. Lakes such as Wall Lake were marginal for trout because of high temperatures. [[Log Dams]] installed in trout streams in the early 1930s provided cover and holding pools.

**Human pressure.** Many of the best trout streams are reachable only by pack trip of 10–15 miles from roads; reaching them from Silver City requires a 60–90 mile drive. Bear Canyon Reservoir and Wall Lake are easily accessible and receive high fishing pressure. San Simon Lake's dense [[Aquatic Vegetation]] (Typha and submergent species) hampers shore angling.

## Key Findings

**Fish populations and distribution**
- *Salmo gairdneri* ([[Rainbow Trout]]) was found in 30 surveyed streams and sometimes made up nearly 100% of the fish population; it likely provides more angling than any other game species.
- *Salmo gilae* ([[Gila Trout]]), the only native trout, was found in 16 Gila drainage streams, usually above 7,000 feet. Its range has contracted, and it faces threats from angling and stocking of other species. Identification was credited to R. R. Miller (1950).
- *Salmo trutta* ([[Brown Trout]]) occurred along the entire Middle Fork of the Gila River and in most tributaries, maintained mainly by natural reproduction.
- *Salvelinus fontinalis* ([[Brook Trout]]) was found only in the South Fork of Whitewater Creek, where a large population persisted despite repeated plantings elsewhere.
- *Micropterus salmoides* and *M. dolomieu* were uncommon in rivers, but San Simon Lake had a very good largemouth population with excellent natural reproduction.
- Game fish per mile exceeded 1,600 in the upper Mimbres River, South Fork Whitewater Creek, Dry Creek and Iron Creek (the Summary gives "over 1500"), each dominated by a different trout species.
- Rough fish (suckers and chubs) were abundant in warm streams such as the Gila, San Francisco and Mimbres rivers.

**Hybridization**
- Extensive hybridization between *S. gilae* and *S. gairdneri* was evident in many trout streams, with some evidence of *S. clarki* influence.

**Diet and food supply**
- Trout diets consisted mainly of aquatic organisms, with terrestrial insects important in some streams. Caddisflies (Trichoptera) occurred in 100% of 24 representative bottom samples, averaging 40.9 organisms per square foot.
- Rainbow trout diets were dominated by Coleoptera and Trichoptera in both drainages (745 stomachs examined in the Gila drainage; 20 in the Mimbres). Brown trout relied most on Diptera, and brook trout on Diptera, Trichoptera and Coleoptera.
- Food supply was judged adequate to support good trout populations.

**Growth and age**
- Rainbow trout (Gila drainage, 37 scales): 3.75, 6.00 and 7.62 inches in years 1–3. Mimbres rainbow trout grew faster (4.25 and 7.75 inches in years 1–2).
- *S. gilae* (57 scales) was the slowest-growing trout except in its first year: 4.12, 5.50, 7.37 and 9.37 inches in years 1–4.
- *S. trutta* (24 scales) generally grew fastest: 5.87, 8.62, 9.62 and 11.75 inches in years 1–4.
- Brook trout (73 scales): 4.00, 6.62 and 7.75 inches in years 1–3.

**Size structure**
- Most trout populations were dominated by fish under 10 inches, with a typical decline in numbers with increasing size. Rough fish were mostly under 12 inches.

**Management recommendations (summary)**
1. Continue supplementary stocking of catchable rainbow trout in accessible waters.
2. Liberalize regulations and consider a higher trout possession limit in remote areas.
3. Repair and install channel improvement structures in Willow Creek, Gilita Creek and Black Canyon Creek.
4. Keep Bear Canyon Reservoir a bass–bluegill lake, with an experimental channel catfish planting in 1955.
5. Control aquatic vegetation in Wall Lake by drawing water down 4–5 feet each June or July (successful in 1954), and spray San Simon Lake periodically.
6. Experimentally plant smallmouth bass in seven warm-water streams and rivers, and plant more harvestable species (red-ear sunfish, channel catfish) in San Simon Lake.
7. Permit minnow taking by trap only in named waters, and experiment with minnow control in Wall Lake.
8. Conduct a detailed fishing-pressure study and continue periodic checks on major fishing waters.
