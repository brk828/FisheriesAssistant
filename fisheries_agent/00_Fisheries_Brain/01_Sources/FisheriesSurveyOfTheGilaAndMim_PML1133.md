# Fisheries Survey of the Gila and Mimbres River Drainages (1951–1955)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This project completion report, issued by the [[New Mexico Department Of Game And Fish]] (Federal Aid Project F-1-R), describes a multi-year fisheries survey of the [[Gila River Drainage]] and [[Mimbres River Drainage]] in southwestern New Mexico. The survey aimed to inventory fish resources in streams and lakes, quantify fish populations and growth, and develop a management plan to provide sustainable game fishing. Field work sampled streams and lakes across a wide elevation range, and the report concludes with management recommendations for stocking, habitat improvement, and access. The scan is partly degraded, and some numbers are garbled, so specific figures should be checked against the original.

## Background & Description

- **Project scope:** The survey was designed to inventory fish in the Gila and Mimbres drainages, which were considered poorly documented. It was intended to support a development and management plan for game fish.
- **Study area:** The drainages lie in the Gila National Forest region and the Mogollon Mountains. Elevations ranged from about 3,700 to 10,778 feet, with a wide range of climate and vegetation zones (from desert grassland and piñon-juniper to conifer forest).
- **Study waters:** The survey covered many streams and lakes, including the [[Gila River]], [[Mimbres River]], [[Whitewater Creek]], [[Sapillo Creek]], [[San Francisco River]], and lakes such as [[San Simon Lake]]. Many lakes were small, and some were reservoirs.
- **Methods:**
  - Electrofishing with a portable, gasoline-powered shocker (about 400 watts), with fish collected and processed at each station.
  - Population estimates made with the [[Electrofishing]] removal method; the report notes that estimates were not corrected for recovery error.
  - Lake sampling with gill nets, and water chemistry measured in the field and laboratory.
  - Age and growth studies based on scale reading, with length-at-age tables for each species and drainage.
  - Stomach content analysis of selected species.
- **Limitations:** Some streams were sampled only once. The report states that the sampling effort was adequate to indicate, but not to fully estimate, populations in many waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Trout occupied cool, high-elevation streams, while warmwater species (bass, catfish, bullheads, suckers, and roundtail chub) predominated in lower, warmer streams and reservoirs. Water temperatures recorded during the survey were high in some low-elevation sites, and the report notes thermal limits for trout.
- **Water quality and structure:** Pools, undercut banks, and aquatic vegetation were described as habitat features. Some lakes had heavy emergent and submerged vegetation that the report recommends controlling. Fluctuating water levels were noted in some reservoirs, and drought periods caused isolated pools in streams, which concentrated fish and created the risk of mortality.
- **Human pressure:** Water diversion for irrigation and municipal use reduced flows, and the report discusses effects of pumping and drought. Access by road and trail varied; many remote streams were reached only by pack trips, while roadside waters received heavy angling.
- **Species interactions:** Hybridization was observed among some native and introduced trout, and the report questions the status of native fish in streams where introduced species had become established.

## Key Findings

- **Fish community:** Native and introduced species were identified across the drainages. Trout species included [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], and [[Cutthroat Trout]] (noted in some high streams). Warmwater game fish included [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Bullhead Catfish]], and bluegill and sunfish. The native [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and several sucker species were common in larger rivers.
- **Distribution:** Trout were found in many streams in the Gila drainage, including high-elevation sites up to about 9,000 feet. Smallmouth bass were concentrated in the middle and lower Gila, and the report notes that they were widely distributed in lower, warmer waters.
- **Population structure:** Trout populations were typically dominated by small individuals in tributary streams, with larger fish more common in some main-stem reaches. The survey noted that many trout populations were in good condition, but some were thinning due to angling pressure and drought.
- **Growth:** Growth varied by species and location. Gila trout in some streams grew more slowly than other trout, and the report provides mean length at each annual age from scale analysis. Growth rates for trout in the Gila drainage were generally reported as acceptable but with variation among streams.
- **Diet:** Stomach analyses showed that trout fed mainly on aquatic insects (including mayflies, caddisflies, and stoneflies), with terrestrial insects and small fish important in larger individuals. Bass and catfish fed on crayfish, insects, and small fish.
- **Lakes:** The lakes were characterized as relatively productive, and many supported fish populations that could sustain fishing pressure. Some lakes had fish populations that were limited by low water or winter kill.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Continue stocking catchable-size trout in roadside and fishable waters, with fish stocked in the spring or early summer.
  - Repair and maintain dams and water-control structures that impound or divert flows, and build new barriers on selected streams to protect native trout from introduced species.
  - Control emergent and submerged aquatic vegetation in lakes, including manual or chemical methods, to maintain fishing access.
  - Manage access to remote streams by limiting angling and protecting Gila trout populations.
  - Review regulations for bass and catfish in warm waters, and evaluate stocking of selected warmwater species in reservoirs.
  - Conduct further detailed studies of fishing pressure and population dynamics on the most important waters.
- **Overall conclusion:** The Gila and Mimbres drainages supported important game and native fish resources. The survey concluded that fish management, including stocking, habitat work, and protection of native trout, could provide sustainable fishing for the region.
