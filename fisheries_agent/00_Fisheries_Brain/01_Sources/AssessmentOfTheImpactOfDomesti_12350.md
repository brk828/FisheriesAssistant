## Executive Summary

This 1973 report by McSorley & Minckley (Arizona State University) documents a field assessment of the ecological impacts of untreated domestic wastewater from the City of Patagonia on [[Sonoita Creek]], a small permanent tributary of the (largely dry) Santa Cruz River in southern Arizona. Two intensive surveys (March and May 1972) combined physico-chemical monitoring (dissolved oxygen, pH, nitrate-nitrogen, phosphate-phosphorus, temperature, discharge) with qualitative and quantitative biological sampling across three stream segments extending 2.9 km downstream from the sewage outfall. The study found severe organic pollution and near-total biotic depletion immediately below the outfall (Segment I), progressive recovery through Segments II and III, and a return to conditions comparable to unpolluted Arizona streams (e.g., [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Sycamore Creek]]) by the 2.9-km station. Native fishes — [[Longfin Dace]], [[Gila Mountain-sucker]], and [[Speckled Dace]] — were essentially absent near the pollution source and became progressively more abundant downstream, illustrating classic stream self-purification dynamics under drought-reduced flow conditions.

## Background & Description

The source text provides no morphometric, meristic, or anatomical descriptions of the fish species collected. Only taxonomic identifications and relative abundances are reported:

- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*) — first individuals caught at 0.38 km downstream of the outfall; five individuals collected between 0.38 and 1.15 km.
- **[[Gila Mountain-sucker]]** (*Pantosteus clarki*) — nine individuals taken, first appearing at ca. 0.6 km downstream.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*) — only one individual taken (at ca. 0.6 km), and only during the March sampling period.

All three species were noted as "rare, but present" in Segment II, and became "present and abundant" by Segment III (1.15–2.9 km downstream), the most physico-chemically recovered reach of the study area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat structure by segment:**
- **Segment I (0–0.3 km)**: Substrate of sand/gravel overlain by 6–8 cm of black, malodorous organic detritus; near-laminar, non-turbulent flow; near-total absence of macro-organisms. No fish recorded.
- **Segment II (0.3–1.15 km)**: Sand-gravel substrate with organic debris confined to backwaters and watercress beds; increasing [[Watercress]] (*Rorippa*) cover; invertebrate diversity rose to 11 genera (Tabanid larvae, Chironomidae, Baetidae mayflies dominant); fishes rare but first appeared here.
- **Segment III (1.15–2.9 km)**: Diversified substrate (sand-gravel to gravel-sand-rubble) with miniature pool-riffle sequences; watercress beds expanded to nearly complete stream cover by May; highest biotic diversity — ≥9 insect genera, crustaceans (Cladocera, Copepoda, Ostracoda), rotifers, protozoans, ≥10 green algal genera, 23 diatom genera; all three fish species present and abundant.

**Water chemistry gradient (key driver of habitat suitability):**
- Dissolved oxygen rose from severely hypoxic levels at the outfall (<2.0 mg/L in March, <0.3 mg/L at night in May) to near- or super-saturated levels at 2.9 km (up to 12.4 mg/L in March).
- Phosphate-phosphorus dropped sharply downstream (e.g., 7.4–8.2 mg/L at outfall vs. 0.2–1.9 mg/L diel range at 2.9 km), reflecting uptake by algae/bacteria.
- Nitrate-nitrogen and pH showed comparable moderation downstream, with a stable, weakly buffered circumneutral pH (7.3–7.75) at the 2.9-km station.
- Discharge was minimal at the source (near-zero to ~1.0 m³/min) and increased downstream via subsurface percolation reaching the surface, doubling in volume between 0.3 and 1.15 km.

**Trophic/community context:** Dominant primary producers shifted from pollution-tolerant bluegreen algae (*Oscillatoria*, >99% of floral biomass near the outfall) to diverse green algae (*Cladophora* and epiphytic diatoms) and vascular macrophytes (watercress, pondweed) downstream — a classic zonation associated with organic pollution gradients and self-purification (cf. [[Hynes 1960]]).

## Key Findings

- Domestic sewage effluent from the City of Patagonia caused severe, chronic organic pollution in the uppermost ~0.3–0.6 km of Sonoita Creek during both March and May 1972 sampling.
- Drought conditions and upstream aquifer pumping reduced natural stream flow to a level insufficient for effective self-cleansing near the pollution source.
- Chironomid midge larvae and the bluegreen alga *Oscillatoria* served as classic pollution-indicator organisms dominating Segment I, consistent with established indicator-species literature ([[Reid 1961]]; [[Pennak 1953]]; [[Bold 1957]]; [[Prescott 1954]]).
- Native fish fauna — [[Longfin Dace]], [[Gila Mountain-sucker]], and [[Speckled Dace]] — was essentially excluded from the polluted headwater reach and only recolonized/increased in abundance as physico-chemical conditions improved downstream.
- By 2.9 km downstream, physico-chemical parameters (DO, pH, nutrients) were comparable to unpolluted reference streams in Arizona ([[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Sycamore Creek]]), indicating substantial natural stream recovery ("self-cleansing") over this distance.
- Watercress (*Rorippa*) proliferation in the lower segments likely influenced dissolved oxygen dynamics by shading substrate and altering photosynthesis/respiration balance, contributing to lower daytime DO supersaturation in May versus March.
- The study underscores Sonoita Creek's status as one of the last streams in southern Arizona retaining a native, albeit depleted, fish fauna, and recommends further bacteriological and long-term chemical monitoring, particularly under peak summer conditions and higher human-use pressure.
- Findings support a broader ecological principle that organic effluent input suppresses biota locally at the discharge point but may enhance downstream productivity following oxidation/assimilation of pollutants ([[Hynes 1960]]).
