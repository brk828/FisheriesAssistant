# Boletín del Programa Nacional de Marcado de Tortugas Marinas, Números 1–6 (1966–1967)

#marine-biology #cheloniidae #sea-turtles

## Executive Summary

This series of six bulletins, issued by the Mexican [[Instituto Nacional De Investigaciones Biológico-Pesqueras]] under the [[Dirección General De Pesca]], reports preliminary results of Mexico's first national sea turtle tagging program, which began in 1966. The bulletins document tag-and-release records from the Gulf of Mexico (Tamaulipas and Quintana Roo), the Gulf of California, and the Pacific coast (Sinaloa, Colima, Jalisco, Michoacán), and they track the first recoveries of tagged [[Lepidochelys Kempii]] (Kemp's ridley) in 1966. The program also protected nests and relocated eggs at the Tamaulipas nesting beaches, a practice not previously in place.

## Background & Description

The program was run by the [[Estación De Biología Pesquera]] in Tampico and other fisheries stations, with cooperation from licensed fishers (*permisionarios*) and foreign researchers. Each bulletin lists tag serial numbers with carapace length, sex, date, and location of marking.

- **Bulletin 1 (October 1966):** Records 285 Kemp's ridleys (*lora*, called *caguama* locally) marked near [[Barra Calabazas]] and [[Barra Coma]], Tamaulipas. Carapace lengths range from 54.5 to 75 cm. All records are coded "H" (female). Marking took place between 21 April and 6 July 1966.
- **Bulletin 2 (November 1966):** Lists 20 turtles marked in the [[Gulf Of California]]: 10 obtained from fishers at Bahía de los Ángeles and Santa Rosalía, and 10 marked from the research yacht *R.V. Dorsal* (Mission Bay Research Foundation, California). Species identified include *Chelonia mydas* (*carrinegra* and *agassizii* forms) and [[Caretta Caretta]]. No nesting beach was found on the Baja California peninsula.
- **Bulletin 3 (November 1966):** Reports 11 turtles marked at [[Isla Mujeres]] and Cozumel, Quintana Roo, identified as *Chelonia mydas mydas* and *Caretta caretta*. Fishing was suspended after a price collapse in the US market, which reduced the number of animals marked.
- **Bulletin 4 (December 1966):** Covers Pacific Mexico (Jalisco, Colima, Michoacán). Most records are [[Lepidochelys Olivacea]] (olive ridley, *golfina*), with a few *Chelonia mydas agassizii*. Marking dates run from 28 January to 16 August 1966.
- **Bulletin 5 (December 1966):** Describes sea turtle fishing off [[Mazatlán]], Sinaloa. Catches reached 100–150 animals in May with three boats and three to four fishers per boat. The reproductive period of *L. olivacea* is given as approximately June to September.
- **Bulletin 6 (January 1967):** A preliminary note on tag recoveries, based on 285 Tampico animals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nesting and egg protection.** The Barra Calabazas to Barra Coma stretch of the Tamaulipas coast was a major nesting site for *L. kempii*. A camp was set up there on 15 April 1966 and operated for three months. Bulletin 6 reports that about 30,000 eggs were moved to a protected zone to secure their development. Dearl E. Adams, cited in the bulletin, reported about 20,000 eggs in 178 nests by mid-May. Before 1966, egg poaching was widespread in the region. Enforcement by fisheries inspectors and Mexican Navy personnel reduced it for the first time.

**Commercial exploitation.** Sea turtles were economically significant. Bulletin 3 notes that in some years the economy of production centers such as [[Isla Mujeres]] was affected by capture bans (*veda*). Until 1965, turtles were exported live to the United States. Bulletin 3 also describes the first attempt to rear green turtle hatchlings from eggs taken from slaughtered females at Progreso, Yucatán, which it calls a world first. That effort was interrupted when the market collapsed.

**Migration.** Recoveries from the Tamaulipas tagging demonstrate long-distance dispersal. Turtles were recaptured in the Texas and Louisiana shrimp grounds, near Campeche, and off Veracruz and Tabasco. This is discussed further under Key Findings.

**Research context.** The bulletins cite [[Archie Carr]] (University of Florida) and [[David K Caldwell]] (County Museum of Los Angeles). Both had advised that Mexican Pacific turtle populations were poorly understood and that the Tamaulipas nesting region needed conservation attention.

## Key Findings

1. **Tagging scale (Tamaulipas):** 285 Kemp's ridleys were tagged between 21 April and 6 July 1966 along the Barra Calabazas–Barra Coma coast. This is the first tagging of this species on record in the bulletins.

2. **Species composition by region:**
   - Gulf of Mexico (Tamaulipas): *L. kempii* (285 animals).
   - Gulf of California: 20 animals, including *Chelonia mydas* and *Caretta caretta*.
   - Caribbean (Quintana Roo): 11 animals, including *C. m. mydas* and *C. caretta*.
   - Pacific (Colima, Jalisco, Michoacán): mainly *L. olivacea*, with a few *C. m. agassizii*.

3. **Recoveries:** Ten tagged *L. kempii* had been recovered by the time of Bulletin 6 (January 1967). Recovery dates run from 2 July to 7 October 1966:
   - Tag 1180: off Freeport, Texas (2 July 1966), about 16 days after tagging at Barra Calabazas–Barra Coma. This was the first recovery.
   - Tag 1279: Isla Aguada to Ciudad del Carmen, Campeche (18 July 1966).
   - Tag 1212: near Cameron, Louisiana (26 July 1966).
   - Tag 1240: about 41 km southwest of Freeport, Texas (1 August 1966).
   - Tag 1133: southwest of Vermilion Bay, Louisiana (3 August 1966).
   - Tag 1049: Tabasco coast, off Dos Bocas (8 August 1966).
   - Tag 1158: Bay of Campeche (18 August 1966).
   - Tag 1209: near Alvarado, Veracruz (26 August 1966).
   - Tag 1214: west of the Isles Dernières, Louisiana (27 September 1966).
   - Tag 1253: near Empire, Louisiana (7 October 1966).

   The bulletin states these recoveries give information on migration routes, travel speed, distances covered, and concentration zones. It does not publish the derived calculations.

4. **Protection and egg relocation:** About 30,000 eggs were moved to a fenced protected zone, and a 20,000-egg figure appears in a cited account. Bulletin 6 credits the Secretaría de Marina and fisheries inspectors with ending the illegal egg trade.

5. **Reward strategy:** Following Dr. Caldwell's recommendation, the bulletin 4 editors note that a larger reward for returned tags is under study. Caldwell also suggested returning the tag together with the reward and information about when and where it was placed, because fishers had kept tags as keepsakes.

6. **Pacific reproduction:** Bulletin 5 identifies June–September as the reproductive period for *L. olivacea* off Mazatlán, and May as the peak month for capture. It recommends future marking during May.

7. **Research gaps:** The bulletins state that the Baja California peninsula had no confirmed nesting area as of late 1966, and that the subspecies nesting there was still to be identified.
