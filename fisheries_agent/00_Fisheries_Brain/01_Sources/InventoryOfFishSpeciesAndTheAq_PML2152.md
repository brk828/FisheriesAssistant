# Inventory of Fish Species and Aquatic Environment of Sixteen Backwaters of the Imperial Division of the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game administrative report (Administrative Report No. 75-3, December 1975, by Gary W. Pooley, Region 5, Inland Fisheries) inventories fish species and physical, chemical and biological conditions in sixteen [[Backwater Habitat]] areas of the [[Imperial Division]] of the [[Colorado River]]. The Imperial Division is a 63-km (39-mile) reach at the eastern edge of Imperial County, where backwaters formed largely after completion of [[Imperial Dam]] in 1938. The report documents severe degradation driven by upstream flow regulation, sedimentation, and streambed degradation, and it recommends management priorities for the backwaters, including lake deepening and long-term protection of inlet-outlet channels.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] drainage covers 631,960 km² (243,000 sq. miles) across parts of eight western states and Mexico. Dams built by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], including [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Powell]], converted a historically wild river with high winter-spring flows into a regulated system with high summer flows for irrigation, municipal use and hydroelectric power.

The Imperial Division is characterized as low desert: hot, dry summers, mild winters, and about 8.9 cm (3.5 in) of precipitation per year. Maximum summer air temperatures often exceed 43 °C (110 °F), and backwater surface temperatures can exceed 32 °C (90 °F) in July and August. The floodplain is narrow (under 1.6 km wide), bordered by the Chocolate and Trigo Mountains. The report notes that the Imperial Division, together with Topock Gorge, is among the few unaltered desert riparian sections of the lower river in California and Arizona.

Native fish are described as endemic species that required rapid, turbid spring flows for spawning and predator protection; their populations declined sharply in the California reach, and several species are listed or proposed for listing as state or federal endangered species. Introduced sport species such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Redear Sunfish]] benefited from the more uniform flows.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and flow.** At average flows of 283 m³/s (10,000 cfs), the Division contains about 2,631 ha (6,500 acres) of water, of which the main channel accounts for most. Daily fluctuations from hydroelectric and irrigation demand can cause water level changes of about 30 cm (1 ft) in shallow backwaters. The most serious problem occurs after thunderstorms, when irrigation demand drops abruptly and flows fall from 396 m³/s (14,000 cfs) to 42 m³/s (1,500 cfs), stranding fish in backwaters that may dry completely.

**Sedimentation and degradation.** Imperial Dam formed a 1.05 × 10⁸ m³ (85,000 acre-ft) silt-trap reservoir that has filled with sediment, leaving about 1 × 10⁶ m³ (1,000 acre-ft) of storage capacity. Upstream Palo Verde and Cibola divisions are zones of active streambed degradation, which lowers water tables and causes upper Imperial backwaters to deteriorate as emergent plants such as [[Cattail]] encroach on shallow water.

**Substrate and vegetation.** Main-channel substrate is mainly silt and sand. Backwater bottoms are largely silt and detritus, often 0.6 to 1 m (2 to 3 ft) deep. Submergent plants include [[Spiny Naiad]], [[Sago Pondweed]], [[Water Milfoil]] and [[Coontail]]. Emergent plants include bulrush, cattail, and [[Carrizo Cane]] (giant reed). Riparian areas are invaded by [[Salt Cedar]] and mesquite.

**Land use and access.** Nearly all of the Division is public land, mostly within [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]], and access is largely by boat. Non-consumptive recreation (birdwatching, canoeing, boating, rafting) is high.

**Regional comparison.** The [[Mohave Division]] has been channelized, straightened and riprapped, causing channel degradation, a falling water table and severe loss of warmwater fishery habitat, including the drying of backwaters used for spawning and nursery.

## Key Findings

**Methods.** Sixteen backwaters were selected if they held water all year and were reasonably accessible. Fish were sampled with gill nets (standard mesh sizes from 0.9 to 8.9 cm) and/or fyke nets, plus Ekman dredge samples for benthos and 20 m plankton tows. Physical data included depth transects and contour maps, Secchi disc turbidity, temperature, dissolved oxygen (Winkler/Alsterberg modification), total dissolved solids, chlorides, hardness and alkalinity. Aerial photographs from 1971 were used to measure backwater acreage.

**Backwater inventory.** The Division's sixteen backwaters range from under 1 ha to over 200 ha. Key sites include:
- **Draper Lake** (Backwater #1): about 10.5 ha (26 acres), average depth about 1 m (3 ft), surrounded by two cattail species; receding river levels have lowered lake levels.
- **Vellan Lake** (#2): about 6.9 ha (17 acres) by 1971 photos, shallow (about 0.6 m average), with only four [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia*) captured; poor water quality and high temperatures (27–32 °C) limit fish establishment. Dissolved oxygen was zero, indicating high biochemical oxygen demand.
- **Taylor Lake** (#3): about 50.8 ha (125 acres), maximum depth 1.8 m, with Spiny Naiad covering about half the bottom; fish catches dominated by redear sunfish and largemouth bass.
- **Devil Lake** (#7): about 21.2 ha (53 acres), average depth about 2.4 m (8 ft), maximum 3.4 m (11 ft).
- **Ferguson Lake** (#8): about 220.5 ha (545 acres), the largest backwater, maximum depth 5.5 m (18 ft), with good water exchange.
- **Senator Wash Reservoir** (#14): 143 ha (354 acres) at high water, average depth about 7.6 m (25 ft), maximum 11.6 m (38 ft); constructed by the Bureau of Reclamation in 1965 to deliver water to Mexico, with essentially all fish shelter removed. Striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*) larger than 40 cm were captured, indicating spawning success from the 1967 and 1968 year classes.
- **Squaw Lake** (#16): 35.2 ha (87 acres), maximum depth 4.9 m (16 ft), with both inlet and outlet and excellent water exchange.

**Water quality.** Surface temperatures in several backwaters reached 29–33 °C. Dissolved oxygen ranged from zero (Vellan Lake) to 11.3 mg/L (Backwater #11, with high supersaturation). Total dissolved solids ranged from about 880 to 1,630 mg/L. Secchi readings ranged from less than 0.3 m to 8.5 m (28 ft) in Senator Wash Reservoir, reflecting plankton blooms and turbidity.

**Biology.** Plankton tows were typically dominated by cladocerans or copepods. Ekman dredge samples often yielded only Asiatic clams (*Corbicula*) or no benthos at all. Fish catches were dominated by carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Threadfin Shad]], channel catfish, bluegill, redear sunfish and largemouth bass, depending on the backwater.

**Ranking and management.** The authors ranked backwaters by fish habitat value. Backwaters with the best fishery potential have a single inlet-outlet channel on the downstream side and deep holding areas. Examples include Senator Wash Reservoir, Squaw Lake and Backwater #10. The poorest, most in need of improvement, are the upper reach backwaters near Vellan and Draper lakes, where degradation has eliminated winter freshening flows.

**Recommendations.**
- Deepen backwaters with explosives rather than dredging, which would alter the wilderness character; earlier explosive deepening at Ivanpah-area potholes and Ford-Rawson ponds near Bishop was judged successful.
- Upper backwaters are important waterfowl resting areas, so improved water quality would benefit both birds and fish.
- Long-term projects should deepen backwater portions and maintain downstream inlet-outlet channels to prevent sediment deposition.
- The report is the first in a series documenting habitat and fishery resources across the river's California divisions.
