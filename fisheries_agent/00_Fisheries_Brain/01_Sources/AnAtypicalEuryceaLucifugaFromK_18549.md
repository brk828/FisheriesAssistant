## Executive Summary
This short herpetological note (Minckley, 1959/1960) documents an atypical color pattern in a specimen of the cave salamander *[[Eurycea lucifuga]]* collected on May 10, 1958, from the twilight zone of a cave in Cherokee County, Kansas. The specimen displayed large, black, interconnected blotches across the dorsum instead of the species' typical discrete spotting pattern, while retaining normal spotted coloration on the tail. This aberration is compared to similar cases previously reported from caves in Oklahoma and Missouri, all located within a 75-mile-diameter area on the western periphery of the [[Ozark Plateau]]. The author notes the geographic clustering of these anomalous specimens as ecologically significant.

## Background & Description
- **Typical species pattern**: *E. lucifuga* normally exhibits discrete, well-separated orange/yellow spots across the dorsum and tail.
- **Aberrant specimen (KU 48589)**:
  - Large, black, **interconnected blotches** on the dorsum (rather than isolated spots).
  - **Normal typical spotting retained on the tail**.
  - Predominant body color: **white**, with loss of typical orange pigmentation.
  - Only three small, irregular orange blotches remained on the back.
  - Leg coloration was noted but omitted from the published figure.
- **Comparative aberrant specimens**:
  - Reese & Smith (1951): Similar dark, interconnected blotch pattern in a specimen from a cave near Flint, Delaware Co., Oklahoma. Interpreted as **"neoteny of pattern development."**
  - Eigenmann & Kennedy (1903): Two specimens from Marble Cave (Stone Co., MO) and Rockhouse Cave (Barry Co., MO) showed the blotched pattern restricted to the **sides of the body**, with normal coloration on dorsum and tail.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Twilight zone of caves — the transitional lighted/semi-dark region near cave entrances, a hallmark microhabitat for *[[Eurycea lucifuga]]*, a species considered a facultative troglophile.
- **Biogeographic context**: All four documented aberrant specimens originate from cave systems distributed across a relatively small area (~75-mile diameter circle) on the **western periphery of the [[Ozark Plateau]]**, spanning three states (Kansas, Oklahoma, Missouri).
- **Possible significance**: The geographic clustering suggests a localized genetic or developmental basis for this pigmentation anomaly, potentially linked to a shared ancestral population or regional selective/environmental pressures associated with cave-adapted lineages.

## Key Findings
1. Documentation of a fourth known case of atypical (blotched, interconnected) dorsal pigmentation in *[[Eurycea lucifuga]]*, extending previously known aberrant records into Kansas.
2. All known aberrant color-pattern specimens are geographically restricted to the western Ozark Plateau margin, suggesting a possible localized developmental or genetic phenomenon rather than random individual variation.
3. The aberration is hypothesized to represent **neotenic retention of larval/juvenile pattern characteristics** into the adult phenotype (per Reese and Smith, 1951), though the tail pattern in the Kansas specimen remained typical, indicating incomplete or regionally variable expression of this trait.
4. Historical specimens (Eigenmann & Kennedy, 1903) show a different distribution of the aberrant pattern (lateral only), suggesting variable phenotypic expression of the same underlying anomaly across individuals and populations.
5. Specimen KU 48589 is archived at the University of Kansas Museum of Natural History, providing a voucher for future genetic or developmental follow-up study.
