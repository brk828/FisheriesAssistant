# Lahontan Cutthroat Trout: A Megafish for Megatrends

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This opinion piece by [[Robert J. Behnke]], published in *Trout* (Summer 1993), argues that fisheries and natural resource management need a [[Paradigm Shift]] from [[Population Genetics]] to [[Evolutionary Genetics]]. Behnke uses the [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarki henshawi*) as the case study. He contends that the original native population of [[Pyramid Lake]] was a unique "megatrout" whose genetics made it a [[Keystone Predator]], and that this genetic resource was lost through dams, pollution, and diversion. He also argues that the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) and other agencies have not met their obligations under the [[Endangered Species Act]], and he calls on [[Trout Unlimited]] to press for change in public land management and hatchery policy.

## Background & Description

The [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] is the largest form of cutthroat trout. The world record cutthroat is a 41-pound Lahontan caught in Pyramid Lake in 1921. Most prize-winning cutthroat over the past 40 years have come from Pyramid Lake, but these fish are descendants of stocked populations, not the original native line.

- **Original Pyramid Lake population:** Last spawning run in 1938 averaged 20 pounds. Recent catches of the introduced fish are typically 12 to 15 pounds.
- **Historical records:** A 62-pound specimen was reported by Fred Crosby, who testified he handled it in 1913. A tribal member told Behnke in 1958 that much larger trout had been caught.
- **Subspecies relationships:** The [[Paiute Cutthroat Trout]] (*O. c. seleniris*) is a Lahontan form that lost its spots after isolation above a falls in the Carson River drainage. Distinct forms also evolved in the [[Humboldt Cutthroat Trout]] drainage and in the Alvord and Whitehorse basins.
- **Other native fishes:** About 12 species of minnow, sucker, and sculpin, plus the [[Mountain Whitefish]]. Key species include the [[Cui Ui Sucker]] (*Chasmistes cujus*, endangered and found only in Pyramid Lake) and the [[Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor*), the main prey of the cutthroat, growing to 15 inches or more.

### Geologic and Evolutionary Setting

The Lahontan basin lies within the [[Great Basin]], an arid region with no rivers reaching the sea. It was isolated from surrounding drainages from about two million years ago. Four Pleistocene glacial epochs created large lakes, and the ancestral [[Lake Lahontan]] reached about five million surface acres roughly 13,000 years ago. The basin then dried to about its present configuration by about 10,000 years ago. Cutthroat-like fossils at least 600,000 years old are known from the basin. Pyramid Lake, the deepest remnant of ancient Lake Lahontan, was the only water body where native cutthroat coexisted with the full array of native prey fishes, which allowed the keystone predator form to develop.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Alkalinity tolerance.** In freshwater lakes, salinity is measured as total dissolved solids (TDS), which in alkaline lakes is dominated by carbonates, bicarbonates, and sulfates rather than the chlorides of seawater. Specific ion concentrations, not total salinity, usually limit aquatic life. Lahontan cutthroat evolved tolerance to these conditions through the cycles of dilution and concentration in glacial lakes. This trait has made them useful for stocking alkaline lakes in Washington's coulee country, such as Lenore, Grimes, and Omak.

**Keystone predator role.** The ancestral cutthroat was the only large predator in the basin. Behnke argues that strong natural selection in large lakes favored a body size and genetic programming capable of preying on large tui chub. Populations isolated for thousands of years lost this fine-tuning:

- Heenan Lake stock (derived from the Carson River) and Summit Lake stock (isolated for perhaps 25,000 years) are less likely to reach maximum size in Pyramid Lake.
- The original Pyramid Lake population was rediscovered and published on in 1979, but has not been restocked to test how much keystone predator genetics it retains after perhaps 100 years of isolation in a small headwater stream.

**Impacts on Pyramid Lake and the Truckee River.** The Truckee River was the only spawning drainage for the Pyramid Lake cutthroat, with no other perennial tributaries entering the lake. Key impacts included:

- **Sawdust pollution:** Lumber mills on the Truckee River; a massive 1869 kill of spawning trout was reported in a Reno newspaper.
- **Dams:** By 1875, dams near Reno blocked about 75 percent or more of potential spawning habitat.
- **Toxic waste:** A paper mill in Floriston, California, polluted the river for many years.
- **Unscreened irrigation ditches:** Killed large numbers of migrating young trout.
- **Derby Dam:** Closed in June 1905 by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], dewatering the Truckee below the dam. Commissioner Frederick Newell dismissed concerns by stating "fish have no rights in water law." After about 1920, diversion increased and spawning became sporadic until the final run in 1938.
- **Lake decline:** From 1920 to 1969, the lake dropped 85 feet and the overflow body Lake Winnemucca dried completely. Surface area fell from about 200,000 acres before 1900 to 110,000 acres, and salinity rose to 5,500 parts per million.

**Historical abundance.** When [[John Charles Fremont]] reached Pyramid Lake in January 1844, Paiute people served him trout described as of "extraordinary size," generally two to four feet long. In the 1880s, railroad shipments from Wadsworth alone ranged from 200,000 to 250,000 pounds. Behnke estimates that the total annual catch exceeded half a million pounds in the 1880s.

**Stocking and current status.** Introduced stocks from the 1950s on are not the original genetic line. The lake has no natural reproduction, and Behnke doubts the recent fishery has ever exceeded about 50,000 pounds per year despite massive stocking.

**Listing and decline elsewhere.** The subspecies henshawi was declared extinct in the 1950s literature, though Behnke's 1959 searches located remnant pure populations in Independence Lake, Summit Lake, and several headwater streams. The Lahontan cutthroat was listed under the Endangered Species Act in 1973, alongside the Humboldt form and the Alvord and Whitehorse trout. The last Alvord trout were lost to hybridization with rainbow trout by the late 1980s.

**Quinn River drainage.** By the early 1980s, ten very small remnant populations were known in the Quinn River drainage, mostly on BLM land under chronic livestock overgrazing and drought stress. By 1992, five of the ten populations had gone extinct. Riser Creek, a Quinn River tributary, was reported extinct at the 1992 Interagency Lahontan Cutthroat Trout Management Team meeting, even though exclosure fencing had been built and revised grazing objectives declared met.

## Key Findings

- **Paradigm shift proposed:** Behnke argues that hatchery management should move from [[Population Genetics]] (artificial selection for catchable trout and generic brood stocks) to [[Evolutionary Genetics]] (natural selection and local adaptation).
- **Genetic uniqueness of the original megatrout:** The native Pyramid Lake population (20-pound average in 1938) was genetically distinct from stocked fish (12 to 15 pounds recently). Behnke argues it should be regarded as irreplaceable.
- **Fisheries policy recommendation:** Shift emphasis from mass production of catchable trout for put-and-take fisheries to maximizing survival of fingerlings for put-grow-and-take fisheries. Survival in competitive, predator-rich waters depends on the evolutionary genetics of wild populations.
- **Loss of diversity:** Much native inland cutthroat and rainbow trout diversity has been lost, but enough remains to supply the raw material for this paradigm shift.
- **Critique of the BLM:** Behnke describes BLM as having "bureaucratic schizophrenia," pairing livestock support with programs such as "Fish and Wildlife 2000" and "Bring Back the Natives" (including Marys River restoration). He says the Quinn River losses show the agency violating its ESA obligations.
- **GAO findings (1988):** The Government Accounting Office reported that improvements to grazed streams were occurring but would be slow, especially on BLM land. Behnke notes the report did not recognize that its "bad examples" showed BLM violating the ESA.
- **Multiple use critique:** Behnke argues that when commodity production conflicts with environmental values, "multiple use becomes multiple abuse," and calls for a more holistic, ecosystem-based management megatrend.
- **Call to action:** Behnke urges [[Trout Unlimited]] to test its stated commitment to national environmental policy leadership by influencing multiple-use management of public lands and the future mission of state and federal hatcheries.
