## Executive Summary

This 1981 study by Minckley and Mihalick examines the non-target ecological effects of **Antimycin A** (Fintrol®), a piscicide applied to [[Ord Creek]] (Apache County, Arizona) in 1977–1978 to eradicate introduced salmonids and enable reintroduction of the native [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*). While the primary management goal was fish eradication (detailed in [[Rinne Et Al 1981]]), this paper quantifies collateral impacts on stream-dwelling invertebrate communities. Benthic and drift samples were collected immediately before and after treatment (August–September 1977) and again three years later (1980) to assess short- and long-term effects. Results showed a dramatic immediate crash in invertebrate abundance, biomass, and taxonomic diversity at the treatment site, with corresponding spikes in downstream drift as dead and dying organisms were transported. By 1980, overall numbers, biomass, and diversity had largely recovered to pre-treatment levels, though some taxonomic shifts—particularly possible loss of long-lived caddisfly (Trichoptera) taxa—were noted, potentially confounded by major regional flooding in 1978–79.

## Background & Description

This paper is not morphologically focused, but it does classify invertebrate taxa by **functional/behavioral morphology** relative to their vulnerability to chemical treatment:

- **Burrowers** (e.g., Chironomidae, Oligochaeta): possess life habits allowing them to persist within substrate, buffering direct toxicant exposure.
- **Interstitial dwellers** (e.g., Heptageniidae, Nemouridae, Dixidae, Tipulidae, Hydracarina): inhabit pore spaces within porous substrates, offering partial protection.
- **Heavy-cased taxa** (e.g., Limnephilidae/limnephilid Trichoptera): protected by robust larval cases.
- **Free-living/exposed taxa** (e.g., Baetidae, Chloroperlidae, Simuliidae, Hydropsychidae): fully exposed to the water column, and consequently suffered the greatest mortality and highest drift rates.

No morphological data on *Salmo apache* (Arizona trout) itself is presented; the paper references [[Arizona Trout]] only as the target beneficiary species of the renovation project.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: [[Ord Creek]] originates at Bull Cienega (2,650 m elevation) and flows north ~11 km to the North Fork of the White River within the [[Gila River Basin]]. The stream alternates between open riparian marshlands (cienegas) and spruce-fir forest, over gravel/boulder substrates.
- **Discharge**: Ranged from 0.6 m³/min at the headwaters (Bull Cienega) to 25 m³/min at the lower study reach (Smith Cienega) during the 1977 study period.
- **Sampling Reaches**: Two sites were compared — [[Bull Cienega]] (headwaters, first/second-order tributary) and [[Smith Cienega]] (lower end of the treated 11 km reach).
- **Community structure**: Pre-treatment benthos was dominated by Baetidae, Chironomidae, and Heptageniidae, with lesser contributions from Plecoptera (Chloroperlidae, Nemouridae), Trichoptera (Hydropsychidae, Limnephilidae), Simuliidae, and Hydracarina.
- **Trophic/functional dynamics**: The invertebrate drift community serves as a downstream transport mechanism; post-treatment drift became dominated by dead/moribund organisms flushed from upstream reaches, temporarily altering the taxonomic signature of downstream (Smith Cienega) samples to resemble upstream mortality patterns rather than local community composition.
- **Disturbance ecology**: The study situates chemical treatment within the broader context of catastrophic disturbance ecology in Sonoran Desert and montane Arizona streams, drawing comparisons to flood-driven recolonization dynamics described in related work on [[Aravaipa Creek]] and other desert streams (Gray 1980, 1981; Gray and Fisher 1981).

## Key Findings

- **Antimycin A application**: Distributed on 31 August 1977 at 0900 hrs to achieve a target concentration of ~10 µg/L throughout the treated reach.

- **Immediate benthic effects (Bull Cienega, headwaters)**:
  - Numbers reduced by a factor of ~5 (1,394/m² before → 298/m² after).
  - Biomass reduced by a factor of ~70 (1.219 g/m² → 0.018 g/m²).
  - Diversity fell from 10.0 to 6.3 taxa/sample.
  - Surviving organisms were almost exclusively burrowers, interstitial dwellers, or heavy-cased taxa; free-living taxa (baetids, chloroperlids, simuliids, hydropsychids) were nearly eliminated.
  - Essentially all remaining organisms were moribund or dead.

- **Immediate benthic effects (Smith Cienega, downstream)**:
  - Numbers *increased* 8.7× (1,510/m² → 13,094/m²) and biomass increased 5.7× (1.107 g/m² → 6.351 g/m²), reflecting downstream transport of dead/dying organisms rather than local production.
  - Diversity increased from 9.3 to 14.7 taxa/sample, again due to accumulation of drifted material.
  - Baetid mayflies made up >50% of collected animals; chironomids were relatively reduced.
  - Most animals appeared dead upon collection.

- **Drift responses**:
  - In Smith Cienega, post-treatment drift increased ~19× in numbers and ~11× in weight relative to pre-treatment afternoon drift, remaining elevated through the following night.
  - By 1 September (day after treatment), no living invertebrates were found in drift samples.
  - In Bull Cienega, drift was more variable but still elevated; all drifting animals were dead.
  - Burrowing/interstitial taxa (chironomids, etc.) remained in the benthos rather than entering drift, due to their protected microhabitats.

- **Long-term recovery (1980, three years post-treatment)**:
  - Smith Cienega supported 16 invertebrate taxa in 1980.
  - 6 taxa present in 1977 were absent in 1980; 7 "new" taxa were recorded in 1980 that were absent (or undetermined) in 1977.
  - Overall numbers in 1980 were lower than 1977 pre-treatment levels, though this discrepancy is partly attributed to differing net mesh sizes (10 threads/cm in 1980 vs. 30 threads/cm in 1977).
  - Notably, hydropsychid and limnephilid Trichoptera (long-lived caddisflies) were absent in 1980 samples — possibly indicating a genuine long-term treatment effect, since such taxa are known to recover slowly from catastrophic disturbances (citing [[Gray 1980]], [[Gray And Fisher 1981]], [[Minckley 1981]]).
  - Confounding factor: extremely high regional runoff/flooding in 1978–1979 may have independently affected invertebrate recolonization, limiting the ability to attribute 1980 differences solely to the Antimycin A treatment.

- **Overall conclusion**: Antimycin A treatment produced a severe but short-term catastrophic effect on stream invertebrates, functionally similar to a flood disturbance, with free-living/exposed taxa most heavily impacted and burrowers/interstitial/heavy-cased taxa relatively buffered. Community-level metrics (abundance, biomass, diversity) recovered within three years, though possible permanent loss of certain caddisfly taxa suggests potential long-term restructuring of invertebrate assemblages following chemical fish eradication programs such as this one supporting reintroduction of [[Arizona Trout]].
