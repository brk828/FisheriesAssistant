# Lake Mohave, Arizona Backwater Summary of Razorback Sucker Study

## Executive Summary
This study summarizes multi-year (1984–1987) experimental stocking and monitoring of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in an isolated backwater habitat adjacent to Arizona Bay on [[Lake Mohave]]. The backwater, a semi-permanent desert wash habitat separated from the main lake by a porous gravel berm, was treated with ichthyocide to remove exotic predatory fishes and subsequently stocked with wild-caught adult razorback suckers from lake spawning aggregations. Natural spawning occurred, producing abundant larvae that grew substantially larger than lake-caught larvae, demonstrating the backwater's value as a predator-free nursery habitat. However, breaching of the protective berm during high lake elevations and storm events repeatedly allowed exotic species (particularly [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Bluegill]]) to invade, resulting in rapid and near-total predation-driven collapse of larval and juvenile razorback sucker populations. The study underscores both the promise and vulnerability of isolated backwater habitats for razorback sucker conservation and highlights adult emigration behavior in response to rising water as an important management consideration.

## Background & Description
- **Larval size at first capture (1985 cohort):** Mean total length ~10 mm (n = 367), captured 7 March 1985, 5–8 weeks post-stocking.
- **Larval growth trajectory:** Increased to >16 mm by late April 1985; largest specimen recorded at 20.3 mm.
- **Comparison to lake-reared larvae:** Largest larvae collected from the open reservoir measured only 12.2 mm, estimated at just a few weeks old — considerably smaller than backwater-reared individuals, indicating enhanced survival to greater age/size in predator-free backwater conditions.
- **Juvenile growth (1984 cohort):** By 1 June 1984, a single juvenile measured 100 mm. By early December 1986, juveniles averaged 189 mm total length and 75 g body weight (n = 12).
- **External comparative growth record:** A specimen from a protected habitat at Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge grew from ~10 mm to over 300 mm in only 19 months, illustrating the growth potential of razorback sucker in exotic-free environments.
- **Adult emigrant example:** A juvenile razorback sucker (~175 mm) voluntarily emigrated from an isolated pond in Tempe, Arizona, during a thunderstorm-induced high-water event.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Semi-permanent to permanent desert wash backwaters along the [[Lake Mohave]] shoreline, separated from the main reservoir by coarse gravel berms that allow porous water exchange; some backwaters dry seasonally while others (e.g., Arizona Bay backwater, 2.1 ha at max elevation) retain water even at low lake levels.
- **Connectivity dynamics:** Confluence between backwater and lake occurs at high lake elevations (e.g., 647 ft / 197.2 m) or during storm-driven wave action, enabling bidirectional fish movement (immigration of exotics, emigration of adults).
- **Spawning ecology:** Arizona Bay is a key site of natural [[Razorback Sucker]] aggregation and spawning during winter–spring, with larvae becoming abundant in adjacent backwaters in late spring.
- **Predator-prey relationships:** Exotic species inhabiting the adjacent lake — [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Black Crappie]] — are implicated as significant predators on razorback sucker eggs and larvae. Nearly 40% of green sunfish sampled in April 1985 contained an average of four larval razorback suckers each, providing direct evidence of predation pressure.
- **Management-relevant behavior:** Adult razorback suckers exhibit rapid emigration responses to rising water levels, apparently using flood/storm events as cues to move from isolated habitats into larger lentic or riverine systems — a behavior with direct implications for reintroduction strategy.

## Key Findings
- **Ichthyocide treatment efficacy was temporary:** The Arizona Bay backwater was treated with ichthyocide in autumn 1984 to eliminate exotic fish populations prior to razorback sucker stocking, but berm breaches in April 1985 and again by April 1987 allowed rapid re-invasion by exotics.
- **1985 stocking and spawning success:** 150 male and 30 female adult razorback suckers stocked January–February 1985 spawned naturally; larvae were first captured 7 March 1985 and increased rapidly in abundance through April.
- **Predation-driven population collapse:** Following berm breach on 4 April 1985, exotic fishes (threadfin shad, carp, channel catfish, largemouth bass, green sunfish, bluegill) invaded within days; larval razorback sucker abundance declined sharply and no larvae were collectable by early May.
- **Second reclamation (October 1985):** Ichthyocide treatment recovered the same exotic species plus black crappie, but no razorback suckers (larval or adult) were found — larval loss attributed to predation, while adult loss attributed to emigration during the earlier confluence event.
- **1984 cohort trajectory:** 64 male and 25 female adults stocked January 1984 produced abundant larvae by late March (35 collected in a brief sampling effort); by June only a half-dozen adults and one 100 mm juvenile remained; by December 1986 juveniles averaged 189 mm/75 g, but carp had gained unauthorized access (likely via angler introduction).
- **1987 status:** March 1987 netting produced only a single adult razorback sucker; 18 males and 32 females were subsequently added. By April 1987, no razorback suckers were found despite presence of carp, threadfin shad, and largemouth bass; a berm breach coincided with lake elevation reaching 196.8 m (0.36 m below full pool).
- **Growth potential in protected habitats:** Backwater and other secure habitats (natural and man-made) support razorback sucker survival and growth to ≥200 mm, with exceptional growth (10 mm to >300 mm in 19 months) documented at Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge.
- **Management implications:** Findings support use of isolated, exotic-free backwaters for stocking and grow-out of razorback sucker, with potential for direct transfer of larger juveniles to adjacent natural habitats or hatchery-truck transport to distant reintroduction sites. Routine removal of exotic species is identified as essential for survival of young razorback suckers in rearing habitats. Adult emigration behavior during high-water events warrants further dedicated study for reintroduction planning.
