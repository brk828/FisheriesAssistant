# Translocation as a Tool in Conserving Imperiled Fishes in Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This review, by W. L. Minckley of the Department of Zoology and Center for Environmental Studies, Arizona State University, evaluates translocation (moving fishes between natural sites, or from nature to propagation facilities and back) as a conservation tool for imperiled freshwater fishes of the [[Southwestern United States]]. Most native fishes in the region are declining because of aridity intensified by [[Water Development]], and at least 20 fishes have gone extinct there in the past century. Of more than 50 fish taxa translocated for conservation, about 20% underwent captive breeding before transplantation. The paper addresses site selection, transplant quality, follow-up monitoring, legal constraints under the [[Endangered Species Act]], disease and genetic risk, and the poor documentation of outcomes. The author concludes that translocation is an emergency measure that should be paired with habitat-based management, and that results must be published in the peer-reviewed literature.

## Background & Description

- **Scale of decline:** About 120 of the remaining 200 native taxa are of special concern. Most belong to three groups: salmonoids (trouts and salmons), cyprinoids (minnows and suckers), and cyprinodontoids (killifishes, livebearers and allies).
- **Early actions:** Conservation began with protection and reintroduction of rare trouts (*Oncorhynchus*, formerly *Salmo*) in 1929 and 1937. Translocation of non-sport fishes for conservation expanded in the 1950s and 1960s. Of 22 refuges dedicated to imperiled fishes, 18 were founded in the last two decades.
- **Definitions:** Translocation and transplantation are synonyms. Reintroductions place fish inside a species' native range; introductions place them outside it. "Success" is distinguished from *preservation* (short-term prevention of extinction) and from *maintenance of evolutionary potential* over ecological time.
- **Life-history categories:** Short-lived taxa live under three years, stay small and mature by their second summer. Long-lived taxa live more than five years (some up to 50), grow large, and mature in their third or later summer.
- **Political setting:** Public land and senior water rights shaped where conservation was feasible. Federal dams (about 1,000 built between 1903 and 1980) and groundwater pumping drove habitat loss in places such as the Los Angeles basin, Phoenix, and Las Vegas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Threats:** Desiccation, water diversion, and [[Groundwater Mining]] isolated permanent refugia and transient habitat fragments. Non-native predators and competitors, including sport fishes and [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis* and *G. holbrooki*), caused declines through predation and hybridization.
- **Hybridization:** Hybridization between native and non-native taxa is a major conservation problem in western North America. Examples include sheepshead minnow with Pecos pupfish in New Mexico, and the cyprinids red shiner, plains silvery minnow and Pecos bluntnose shiner, whose spread coincided with declines of native minnows.
- **Arid-land dynamics:** Short-lived arid-zone fishes show rapid population booms and busts with large range shifts. Permanent springs act as refugia, while ephemeral marshes and streams allow periodic gene exchange and dispersal.
- **Habitat-site suitability:** [[Moyle and Sato Preserve Design Criteria]] require resources for persistence, self-sustaining populations, internal replication, protection from edge effects, and replication across distant protected areas. In practice only a few criteria are usually met.
- **Disease and collateral damage:** Parasites travel with hosts, so quarantine is advised. Habitat modification and chemical eradication before stocking have caused losses, including endemic hydrobiid snails in California springs and endangered chubs (*Gila seminuda*) after poisoning of an exotic invader.

## Key Findings

1. **Success is hard to define.** Persistence is the key measure, and reporting has been inconsistent. Hendrickson and Brooks (1991) could not quantify trends across more than 400 transplants of 40 short-lived taxa because data were not comparable.
2. **Overall success rates.** An over-all success rate of 26.3% was computed for about 107 sites. A single reintroduction program of [[Gila Topminnow]] at 208 sites achieved 18.3%. Excluding that program, 199 sites succeeded 34.7% of the time. Nine taxa failed at every site, 14 established at all localities, and 17 achieved intermediate success.
3. **Eight critical cases.** Of eight taxa near extinction, six survive, including Yaqui chub, Leon Springs pupfish, Devils Hole pupfish, Owens pupfish, Pahrump poolfish and Big Bend gambusia. Monkey Spring pupfish and Amistad gambusia were lost.
4. **Yaqui chub recovery.** About 200 chubs moved in 1969 from a drying spring to an isolated creek later proved to be a relict population. After 1980, Dexter NFH chubs were translocated in lots of about 50 to many thousands of individuals, and all translocations established populations.
5. **Genetic management.** Effective population size, not census number, governs genetic risk. Hundreds of razorback suckers may be needed, while a few pregnant mosquitofish may suffice. Mixing stocks can break co-adapted gene complexes, so the author recommends caution.
6. **Propagation facility.** The [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico housed 24 imperiled taxa between 1974 and 1989. Its 1992-93 budget was about $350,000, planned to rise to $570,000 by 1995-96. Long-lived broodstock were held at about 440 Colorado squawfish, 300 razorback suckers and 1,800 bonytail in 1989.
7. **Stock numbers.** Allendorf and Ryman (1987) considered 25 of each sex an "absolute minimum" for salmonids. About 30 to 200 individuals are typically used to establish populations of short-lived taxa at Dexter NFH.
8. **Stress and survival.** Fertilized eggs survive transport best, followed by larvae, juveniles and then adults. Transport of razorback sucker, Colorado squawfish and bonytail larvae and juveniles at 17-18 °C had mortality consistently less than 0.5%, with transport not exceeding 12 hours.
9. **Razorback sucker example.** Survival of only 0.1% of 15 million stocked fish would yield 15,000 fish spread over an estimated 20,550 ha, less than one fish per surface hectare, which is difficult to monitor.
10. **Legal framework.** The 1982 amendment to the ESA created "experimental essential" and "experimental non-essential" categories. The non-essential category left introduced populations and their habitats largely unprotected. Authorization can take two years, and stocking of desert pupfish and Gila topminnow on Arizona federal lands stalled for six years.
11. **Documentation gap.** About 50% of Hendrickson and Brooks' data came from personal communications, agency files and unpublished reports. The author argues that formal documentation must be planned from the outset and published in peer-reviewed or regional journals.
12. **Overall conclusion.** Translocation should be an emergency or stopgap measure. The long-term goal is habitat-based management and ecosystem conservation, with translocations supporting reintroduction of native elements as habitats become suitable.
