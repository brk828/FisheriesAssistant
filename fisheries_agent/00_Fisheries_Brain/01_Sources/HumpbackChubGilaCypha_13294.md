# Humpback Chub Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan, prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] (15 members from federal and state agencies, 1975–1978), sets out the goal of restoring and maintaining a minimum of five self-sustaining populations of the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the [[Colorado River Basin]] by 1990. The plan describes the species' morphology, historical and present distribution, poorly known life history, and the causes of decline, including [[Dam Impoundment]] effects, cold tailwaters, exotic species and hybridization. It then presents a step-down outline of recovery tasks, a five-year budget, and a multi-agency appendix of review letters with the team's responses.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is a cyprinid minnow endemic to the Colorado River basin, first described by Miller (1946) from a Grand Canyon specimen. Its diagnostic features include a prominent nuchal hump with lateral grooves, a flattened head with a fleshy snout and small eyes, a subterminal to inferior mouth, usually 9 dorsal and 10 or more anal fin rays, and large falcate fins. Maximum length is about 45 cm. It is distinguished from the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*), and the plan notes that many intermediate specimens blur the boundaries among these species. Juveniles are hard to separate from other young Gila, with fin ray counts and mouth position the most useful characters at about 70 mm total length.

Historically, the species was not reported until the 1940s, partly because it lived in inaccessible canyons. Remains from Indian sites near Hoover Dam suggest it was lost from the lower basin before it was documented. Surveys of the 1950s–1960s, tied to [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Glen Canyon Dam]], found relatively large numbers in the upper Green River and Colorado River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Generally associated with fast currents and/or deep channels. Young-of-the-year and juveniles preferred low-current, silt-substrate areas at 0.3–1.0 m depth; adults used a variety of areas, usually over sand substrate (Holden 1977, Desolation and Gray canyons).
- **Feeding:** The subterminal mouth suggests bottom feeding, but no stomach analyses had been done. Surface feeding was observed, and fish below Glen Canyon Dam fed mainly on planktonic crustaceans from Lake Powell.
- **Life history:** No direct research existed. Spawning was inferred from related species at about 18°C, probably May–June in the Green River and June–July in Grand Canyon. September young-of-the-year measured 30–70 mm and juveniles 70–150 mm in Desolation Canyon.
- **Present status:** Distribution is sporadic and restricted to small reaches. Known areas are the Green River (Desolation and Gray canyons, Dinosaur National Monument), the Yampa River (Dinosaur National Monument), the Colorado River from Palisade to Black Rocks, Colorado, the Colorado River from River Mile 27 to 108 in Marble and Grand canyons, and the Little Colorado River to 13 km upstream. The plan identifies Desolation-Gray canyons as likely the largest population, with the Little Colorado River serving as a refugium from cold, fluctuating mainstream flows.
- **Threats:** Flaming Gorge and Glen Canyon dams (impoundment, cold tailwaters, reduced flows), possible losses to Hoover Dam, the Green River fish eradication program, exotic species competition and predation, and hybridization with related Gila that may "swamp" genetic stock in small isolated populations.

## Key Findings

**Goal and structure**
- Goal: at least five self-sustaining populations by 1990.
- Seven objectives: habitat requirements and essential habitat (1); protection and management of populations and habitat (2); captive propagation (3); taxonomy (4); information and education (5), plus related actions on habitat protection and planning.
- The plan states that habitat protection and maintenance are the most vital efforts. Reintroduction is secondary.

**Propagation (Objective 3)**
- Proposed production targets are 10,000 each of humpback, roundtail and bonytail chubs per year at a new facility, and at least 20,000 humpback chubs per year through 1990 across two refugia facilities (Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery plus one new facility).
- Brood stocks: 10 pairs of humpback chubs at each facility, 10 pairs of roundtail chubs at each facility, 5 pairs of bonytail chubs at the new facility, and 10 pairs of bonytail chubs at Willow Beach.

**Five-year budget**
- The Utah Division of Wildlife Resources and the Navajo Area BIA letter both refer to a 5-year cost of $1,410,000 (BIA letter), and the Colorado Division of Wildlife letter notes a request of $50,000 annually for propagation.
- Tasks are prioritized; Task 24 (enforce existing laws) was raised to priority 1 after comments, and Task 23 (establish new populations) was given priority 3.

**Agency review (Appendix A, about 20 letters)**
- Most reviewing agencies (National Park Service, Bureau of Land Management, U.S. Forest Service, California and Nevada fish agencies) endorsed the plan. Many noted that funding depends on appropriations.
- Major concerns were raised: the goal of five populations versus the four known populations (Regional comments M-1 to M-4); the Bureau of Reclamation's objection to funding hatcheries; the BIA's view that the plan lacked a valuation of the chub and that costs were excessive; the Upper Colorado Region's call for a two-phase study and an environmental statement before implementation; and the National Park Service's warning that Task 24 could lead to litigation.
- The team's responses accepted several changes, including editorial corrections, reworded goals and a 5-year budget increase. It rejected the call to exempt land management agencies and the request to downgrade the goal of five populations, but said the goal would be revised if habitat surveys show only four suitable areas.

**Recommendations for implementation**
- Survey existing populations, especially in the Yampa, White and Cataract Canyon reaches, before designating critical habitat.
- Develop non-lethal sampling methods, since gill nets and trammel nets cause mortality and electrofishing is ineffective in deep, swift rivers.
- Resolve taxonomic uncertainty among the Gila complex through preserved specimens and hatchery-produced hybrids.
- Coordinate habitat management plans and funding among multiple federal and state agencies.
