# Nature of Variability in Blood Lactic Acid Data from Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This conference paper by [[Charles W. Caillouet Jr.]] (University of Southwestern Louisiana), presented at the 95th Annual Meeting of the [[American Fisheries Society]] in 1965 and drawn from his 1964 doctoral dissertation at Iowa State University, examines why blood [[Lactic Acid]] data from fishes are so variable. The author argues that **heterogeneity of variance** is nearly universal in these studies, that it arises partly from experimental procedures and partly from individual differences in [[Muscle Glycogen]] and fitness, and that it has practical consequences for statistical analysis. The paper closes with recommendations for designing future studies and choosing appropriate tests.

## Background & Description

Muscular contraction under anaerobic conditions depends on [[Glycolysis]], in which lactic acid forms from muscle glycogen. Lactic acid diffuses into the blood and is later oxidized, resynthesized to glucose or glycogen, or cleared mainly by extrahepatic tissues, particularly the heart. Research on blood lactic acid in fishes gained momentum largely through work by [[Edgar C. Black]] and colleagues at the University of British Columbia. A central motivation was the hypothesis that mortality in hyperactive fish may be linked to rising blood lactic acid, possibly through tissue hypoxia and lactic acidosis. For this reason, blood lactic acid has been used to assess the effects of capture, handling and other manipulation.

The paper reviews the scattered observations on variability made by earlier investigators, including Black, Parker and Black, Barrett and Connor, and Secondat and Diaz. Most noted large or erratic variation, and Secondat and Diaz recognized that variation was slight in unexercised fish but greater in exercised fish. The reference list is long, comprising roughly 60 entries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's data come mainly from salmonids (including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Kamloops Trout]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Sockeye Salmon]] and [[Chinook Salmon]]), with additional studies on [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], [[Bluegill]], [[Yellowfin Tuna]], [[Skipjack Tuna]], [[Brook Trout]] and [[Tautog]], and a mention of codfish exposed to air. Fish were studied after forced exercise, live transportation, handling, capture and tagging, and during recovery. Caillouet's own work on channel catfish and mature carp (sampled in winter and summer) informs the analysis, and the discussion is framed by physiological concerns about fishway performance and fish mortality.

The paper identifies environmental and physiological modifiers of the response: temperature (noted in the cited work on low-temperature effects), fitness acquired through training, previous environmental experience, and genotype.

## Key Findings

- **Heterogeneity of variance is pervasive.** Caillouet (1964a,b) found that variation among individual fish was low when the group mean of unexercised fish was low. In exercised fish, both the mean and the variation increased. During recovery, both decreased. The author states that this pattern occurs in nearly every study of blood lactic acid in fishes.
- **Single sampling contributes to variability.** Most investigators sampled each fish only once to avoid disturbance artifacts. When groups are sampled over time, lactic acid can keep changing during sampling. Exercising fish in groups also yields less uniform effort than individual exercise, and Black et al. (1960) attributed variability to incomplete responses to forced exercise.
- **Link to muscle glycogen.** Black et al. (1960) showed that in fish that did not respond uniformly to exercise, higher blood lactic acid was associated with lower muscle glycogen, and the relationship was exponential. The author hypothesizes that when fish are unexercised, high variation in muscle glycogen coincides with low variation in blood lactic acid. During exercise, this pattern reverses. The hypothesis is presented as requiring direct testing with paired measurements of both variables in individual fish.
- **Fitness as a source of variation.** Crescitelli and Taylor (1944) attributed variable lactate responses in humans to differences in fitness. Hochachka (1961b) reported that trained trout acquired an oxygen debt three times as great as that of untrained fish, indicating greater glycogen utilization. Greater fitness may also speed removal of accumulated lactic acid from the blood.
- **Statistical consequences.** Failing to account for heterogeneity may lead to erroneous conclusions. The author recommends:
  - For time-course studies of accumulation and removal, sample individual fish at intervals and plot log10 of blood lactic acid against time (or log10 of time), following the approach of Barrett and Connor (1962). Log transformation reduces heterogeneity and gives the rate of lactic acid removal.
  - Consider fish recovered when inter-individual variability reaches a low, constant level.
  - For comparing group means across treatments, use tests designed for heterogeneous variance, or run tests after transforming the data (e.g., logarithmic transformation).
