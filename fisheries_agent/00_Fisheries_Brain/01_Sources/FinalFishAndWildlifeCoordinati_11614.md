# Upper Gila Water Supply Study: Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, prepared under the [[Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act]] for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], assesses 100-year fish and wildlife impacts of four new or modified alternatives for the [[Upper Gila Water Supply Study]] in Grant County, New Mexico. The study would pump [[Gila River]] water to offstream storage to support municipal and industrial use, as part of an exchange tied to the [[Central Arizona Project]]. The Service concludes that the direct pumping alternatives would harm the Gila River fishery, particularly the federally threatened [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]], and that [[Schoolhouse Canyon Dam]] is the preferred offstream site. It recommends 13 measures, including alternative water sources, relocating the diversion, instream flow protections, and habitat mitigation. The Bureau has since deferred the study because costs outweigh benefits.

## Background & Description

The Central Arizona Project was authorized in 1968 under the Colorado River Basin Project Act (Public Law 90-537). New Mexico was allowed a consumptive use not exceeding 18,000 acre-feet annually (averaged over ten consecutive years), including reservoir evaporation, in exchange for supplying Central Arizona Project water to downstream Gila River users in Arizona.

Initially the study considered groundwater pumping, floodplain storage basins, and several dam options. Most were dropped for economic and feasibility reasons, and the Conner and Hooker Reservoir alternatives were later dropped for economic and environmental reasons. Seven construction alternatives remained, all involving direct pumping from the Gila River to an offstream reservoir at either [[Mangas Creek]] or [[Schoolhouse Canyon]]. An eighth option, [[No Federal Action]], would meet demand through local acquisition of existing water rights.

Two operating scenarios were analyzed:
- **Preferred Option:** diversion at any time, most likely during non-irrigation and flood periods.
- **Option 2:** diversion only during flood events.

Table 1 of the report lists pump capacities, conservation pool sizes, and net yields. Net yields ranged from 5,500 to 15,000 acre-feet, depending on design and operation. Mangas Creek Dam-1 would deplete an average of 18,000 acre-feet from the Gila River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Gila River flows about 34 miles through the Gila Wilderness canyon and then into the 22-mile Cliff-Gila Valley. Mangas Creek is a small perennial tributary with a drainage of about 200 square miles, and base flow below 5 cubic feet per second. Schoolhouse Canyon, an intermittent tributary, drains about 10 square miles. Riparian communities are dominated by cottonwood, sycamore, hackberry, walnut, boxelder and willow. Mining, ranching and dispersed recreation are the main land uses.

**Fishes.** The Gila River in New Mexico supports one of the largest native fish assemblages in the lower [[Colorado River]] basin, attributed to its relatively unmodified condition and uncontrolled flows. Of 14 native fish species that may have originally occurred in the New Mexico portion, only eight remain. Introduced species, including [[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]], are thought to harm natives through competition and predation. Spikedace and loach minnow occupy only 6 and 15 percent, respectively, of their historical range. Three native fishes are state-protected, and spikedace and loach minnow are federally listed as threatened with proposed critical habitat.

**Terrestrial resources.** The upper Gila Valley holds some of the best remaining riparian habitat in the lower Colorado River basin, with the greatest diversity of raptors and over 60 percent of New Mexico's documented bird species. Fourteen terrestrial species in the project area have state or federal protection, including the [[Bald Eagle]], [[Peregrine Falcon]] and [[Gila Monster]]. Ten wildlife vegetation types were mapped, and game species include [[Mule Deer]], [[Javelina]] and [[Gambel's Quail]].

## Key Findings

**Aquatic impacts**
- Direct pumping could harm the downstream fishery during construction and operation. The infiltration gallery would avoid entrainment and impingement of fish.
- Mangas Creek reservoir would inundate about four miles of perennial stream and eliminate most native riverine fishes there. Schoolhouse Canyon would inundate no significant aquatic habitat.
- Spikedace densities are highest below the Mangas Creek confluence, so the proposed gallery there would degrade a significant portion of their habitat through siltation and flow reduction.
- Under the Preferred Option, flows would drop 10 to 30 percent of the time at the diversion site. Zero or near-zero flows could occur, with serious effects on native and sport fisheries unless bypass flows are maintained. Under Option 2, the one-in-ten-year flood could be reduced by up to 22 percent.
- Inflows to San Carlos Reservoir could fall by up to 9 percent, and tailwater discharges by up to 11 percent. Additional studies are needed for downstream fishery and bald eagle impacts.

**Terrestrial impacts**

| Alternative | Reservoir inundation (acres) | Total construction-related loss (acres) | Habitat units lost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mangas Dam Size 1 | 1,136 | 1,536 | 314,900 |
| Mangas Dam Size 2 | 631 | 1,101 | 246,500 |
| Schoolhouse Dam | 447 | 712 | 156,600 |

- The Schoolhouse Dam alternative would avoid the more valuable cottonwood-willow, riparian and wetland types. The report finds it would have significantly less environmental impact than Mangas Dam.
- Lowering the Mangas pool to 4,700 feet would reduce inundation to 356 acres.

**Instream flow recommendations (IFIM)**
- The Mangas diversion site would require monthly flows of 45 to 250 cfs. Relocation to Ira Canyon would require 40 to 212 cfs, with peak flows of 212 cfs in spring months.
- Flushing flows are needed to condition substrates, including about the two-year flood. Control of introduced predators, such as red shiner, would require a one-in-nine-year event of about 14,000 cfs.

**Mitigation**
- Lost wetland, cottonwood-willow and riparian habitat should be mitigated on at least an acre-for-acre basis.
- Estimated mitigation costs in Appendix F total $5,937,920 (Mangas Dam Size 1), $5,217,360 (Mangas Dam Size 2) and $3,808,680 (Schoolhouse Dam). These figures are for comparison only.
- Mitigation purchases in [[Cliff-Gila Valley]] and [[Redrock Valley]] would require 189 to 498 acres, depending on the alternative and land mix. Land purchase would require eliminating livestock grazing.

**Recommendations (summary of 13)**
1. Consider alternatives: joint Gila River and groundwater use, the [[Mimbres Basin]] groundwater, [[Bill Evans Lake]], or relocating the diversion to Redrock Valley or Ira Canyon.
2. Prefer Schoolhouse Canyon Dam over Mangas Creek Dam.
3. Keep the Mangas pool below 4,700 feet and relocate the borrow area.
4. Share rights-of-way for transmission lines and access roads.
5. Follow the Appendix A instream flow tables during pumping, and stop diversions when flows fall below recommended minimums.
6. Restrict gallery construction to September through February to protect spawning and larval periods.
7. Establish minimum instream flows below San Carlos Reservoir and Mangas Creek Dam.
8. Design outlet works and spillways to prevent escape of introduced fish.
9. Establish a conservation pool and sport fishery, avoiding vegetation clearing at pool edges.
10. Purchase and manage mitigation lands, prioritizing aquatic, wetland and riparian habitat.
11. Create an interagency monitoring plan through construction and project life.
12. Reclaim the lower [[Turkey Creek]] for a native fishery as partial mitigation.
13. Purchase large blocks of floodplain adjacent to mitigation lands.
