# Habitat Use by Roach and Perch in Response to Cormorants and Artificial Refuges

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study, presented at the Fifth Conference on Fish Telemetry in Europe (Ustica, Italy, 2002; published by FAO/COISPA, 2005), examines whether [[Artificial Fish Refuges]] reduce the predation pressure exerted by [[Great Cormorant]] (*Phalacrocorax carbo*) on freshwater coarse fish in England. Using [[Acoustic Telemetry]] and a high-resolution positioning system in a small stillwater lake at [[Bawburgh Lakes Fishery]], the authors tracked [[Roach]] and [[European Perch]] over a 30-day trial split into three phases: no refuges, refuges present, and refuges with cormorants excluded. Both species showed clear diurnal habitat use, favouring open water at night and marginal vegetation by day. Perch used an artificial refuge more over time, but no fish showed meaningful refuge use overall, and cormorant foraging was not measurably influenced by the refuges. The authors conclude that the evidence is insufficient to judge the value of refuges at fisheries.

## Background & Description

Cormorant numbers wintering in Britain roughly quadrupled over 25 years, and the birds have extended from coastal areas to inland fisheries, creating conflict with angling and fishery management. Cormorants are protected under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, and lethal control is licensed only in limited circumstances, creating a need for non-lethal management options. [[Underwater Fish Refuges]] were proposed as a low-cost measure for small recreational (rod-and-line) coarse fisheries.

Roach (*Rutilus rutilus*) and perch (*Perca fluviatilis*) were selected because they were the species most commonly consumed by cormorants at inland sites in England and Wales. The authors argue that effective refuges must both attract fish (through structure and overhead cover) and exclude diving birds, for example by netting.

The study lake, Works Lake, covered about 11,000 m² with a maximum depth of 5 m. Submerged vegetation had died back at the time of the trial, leaving only a marginal emergent strip no wider than 1–2 m. About 20 cormorants used the lake complex for foraging and roosting.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Diel behaviour:** Both species moved into open water at night, a pattern consistent with earlier work on prey fish and predation risk.
- **Cover and predation:** Vegetation cover is known to reduce predatory success of piscivorous fish, but it also restricts feeding, creating a trade-off between predator avoidance and feeding opportunities.
- **Seasonal vulnerability:** Cormorant numbers peak on inland waters in winter, when natural cover is lowest and cold water reduces fish swimming speed, so refuges were considered most relevant at that time.
- **Cormorant foraging:** Cormorants forage mainly in the early morning, which coincides with peaks in prey fish activity. Foraging was directed towards the natural marginal vegetation rather than the refuges, and birds approached from open water, which may limit fish escape options.

## Key Findings

**Trial design**
- Three sequential 10-day phases (21 Jan–19 Feb 2002): Phase 1 no refuges (baseline); Phase 2 refuges installed; Phase 3 cormorants excluded by active scaring.
- Two refuge groups (about 7 m diameter, ~90 m³ each, under 1% of lake volume) were deployed: refuge 1 adjacent to the margin and refuge 2 in open water. Components included wire-mesh cages with shade netting and artificial weed, floating cages with emergent vegetation, pyramid-stacked drainage pipes, and floating black-tube covers.

**Telemetry sample**
- Eight fish were initially tagged (four roach, four perch) with Vemco V8SC transmitters, the maximum the system could track. Only three (two perch, one roach) were tracked for the full 30 days, so two further roach were tagged on 5 February. Analysis was based on four fish tracked across all phases (two roach, two perch), with about 20 fixes per hour per tag.
- Fish lengths: perch 17.9–19.7 cm fork length; roach 15.6–17.8 cm.

**Roach**
- Both tracked roach showed significantly more open-water use at night in all phases (p<0.001).
- Marginal use fell from an average of 9.6 fixes per hour (phases 1–2) to 4.9 per hour (phase 3). Open-water use rose significantly in phase 3 when cormorants were absent (p<0.001).
- Neither roach made positive use of the artificial refuges.

**Perch**
- Both tracked perch showed significantly more open-water use at night in all phases (p<0.05).
- Marginal use fell from 8.1 fixes per hour (phases 1–2) to 3.7 per hour (phase 3). Use of the margins declined significantly (p<0.001), and use of refuge 1 increased significantly (p<0.001) in phase 3.
- Open-water use did not change significantly in phase 3. None of the perch made more than passing use of refuge 2.

**Cormorant foraging**
- Observations covered 48 h 10 min (seven days) in phase 1 and 54 h 40 min (eight days) in phase 2. In total, 33 cormorants visited in phase 1 and 65 in phase 2. Focal observations were made on 23 birds in phase 1 and 39 in phase 2, of which seven were excluded because they fed on discarded mackerel.
- Daily visit numbers did not differ significantly between phases (Mann-Whitney U, p>0.05), although the total number of visits roughly doubled.
- Foraging bouts were significantly longer in phase 2 (p<0.05). No other foraging parameter differed significantly (see [[Mann-Whitney U Test]] results in the source's Table 3).
- Edge-zone dives and resurfaces were 4% and 19% in phase 1 (n=345), and 12% and 18% in phase 2 (n=534). Dive and capture changes near the refuges did not differ from non-refuge sectors, so cormorants were neither attracted to nor deterred by the refuges.

**Conclusions and limitations**
- The authors stress that the fish sample was very small and that some fish may have been removed by predators; cormorants were foraging on the lake when two fish were last recorded.
- They conclude there is no evidence that refuges changed cormorant foraging efficiency or location, and that perch can be attracted to artificial structures. They do not consider the findings sufficient to judge refuge utility, which likely depends on site size, existing cover and fish species.
