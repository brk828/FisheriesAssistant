# Recovery Program FY 1997 Work Plans: Fish Passage and Floodplain Habitat Restoration for Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the FY 1997 work plan package of the [[Colorado River Recovery Program]] (dated October 2, 1996), covering two linked efforts for the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]: (1) fish passage at instream barriers, and (2) the [[Floodplain Habitat Restoration Program]]. It compiles proposed scopes of work (SOWs), task schedules, deliverables, budgets, reviewer lists, a 1996 symposium invitation, and a floodplain protection issue paper. The central question is whether the new [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] fishway will pass endangered fishes, and whether fish passage and floodplain reconnection are viable non-flow tools for recovery.

The package requests roughly $364,800 for the Redlands fish passage study (FY 1997-2000) and about $1.92 million for FY 1997 floodplain work. Other passage proposals (Price-Stubb, Grand Valley Irrigation Company, Grand Valley Project, Hartland) are at early planning stages, and the Yampa barriers study is awaiting a revised SOW.

## Background & Description

The Upper Colorado River Basin is home to two endangered native fishes. Blockage of migration by dams and diversions is hypothesized to have contributed to their decline (Tyus 1984; Burdick and Kaeding 1990), but its actual role is unknown. The [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] on the Gunnison River, built in 1918 as a 12-foot-high irrigation structure about 2.3 river miles above the Colorado confluence, has restricted upstream movement of fishes into the upper Gunnison. The Bureau of Reclamation, with the Service, Colorado, and Redlands Water and Power Company, built a fishway there designed to pass both juvenile and adult squawfish and razorback sucker. The fishway includes a forebay trap facility, a sluiceway for returning unwanted fish, and removable baffles for flow experiments.

Razorback sucker is now extremely rare in the upper basin. The last documented wild capture upstream of Redlands was in 1981. Stocking has included 21 adults (April 1994), 4 adults (fall 1995), and 316 juveniles (October 1995), all in floodplain reaches near Delta, Colorado.

The floodplain program targets low-velocity floodplain habitats, which are thought to provide food, warmer water, cover, and nursery and spawning habitat. Many were hydrologically cut off by levees, flow depletion from upstream reservoirs, and channel change.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Upstream native community:** The Gunnison River upstream of Redlands is 79% native fish, mainly [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]], and [[Speckled Dace]] (Burdick 1995). These were historically preferred prey of squawfish, so they could support recolonizing predators.
- **Squawfish status:** A small enclave population of juvenile and adult squawfish exists above Redlands. Squawfish use the plunge pool and the downstream reach, based on capture and radiotelemetry data.
- **Flow dependence:** Fish passage success depends on river flow. Low flows in the Gunnison may keep fish from reaching the fishway. Flow thresholds for fish vacating the plunge pool are a key study question. Flows were also linked to the [[Aspinall Unit]] reservoirs upstream, which may have altered spawning conditions.
- **Floodplain condition:** The 100-year floodplain along the Colorado, Gunnison, Green, Yampa, White, and Duchesne rivers is designated critical habitat. Gravel pits, levees, riprap, and jetties continue to reduce floodplain habitat quality and quantity, largely without a federal nexus.
- **Contaminants:** Selenium is a documented concern at floodplain sites. Adobe Creek sediment and water samples and Walter Walker samples exceeded levels of concern (water 2-5 ppm; fish tissue 8 ppm), and Walter Walker water reached as high as 170 ppm.
- **Nonnative fishes:** Nonnative species (channel catfish, northern pike, green sunfish, smallmouth bass, fathead minnows, carp) are a major concern. Old Charlie Wash harvests were dominated by carp (77% by weight) and fathead minnows (71% by number).

## Key Findings

**Fish passage (Redlands Diversion Dam, Project CAP-4b)**
- Key hypotheses: Colorado squawfish may have spawned upstream of Redlands historically, and blockage may have depleted upstream stocks through loss of adults and recruitment failure.
- Five-year monitoring study goals include determining passage use by native and nonnative fishes, radiotelemetry movement tracking, minimum flows for squawfish to remain in the plunge pool and 2.3-mile reach, and development of criteria for judging passage success.
- Radiotelemetry plan: 20-30 juvenile and adult squawfish implanted initially; antennae at the fishway entrance, midway and top of the fishway, and river miles 2.4 and 1.4.
- Trap checks are planned for Monday through Friday, with reduced frequency in winter if use declines.
- Budget by fiscal year: FY 1997 $95,000; FY 1998 $104,000; FY 1999 $81,000; FY 2000 $84,800. Total $364,800 (with overhead $429,047).
- Passage success criteria remain undefined and must be developed.

**Other passage proposals (Capital funding)**
- **Price-Stubb Diversion (Colorado River):** Would be the last significant structure impeding passage above the 15-Mile Reach, once the Grand Valley Irrigation Company (GVIC) structure is addressed. Total estimated cost $2,100,000 (1996-1998).
- **GVIC Diversion (Cameo/Roller Dam area):** Constitutes a barrier at low flows. Total estimated cost $725,000 (1996-1997).
- **Hartland Diversion (Gunnison River, RM 59.9):** Based on Burdick (January 1996) recommendations. Total estimated cost $775,000 (1997-1998).
- **Grand Valley Project Diversion:** Total estimated cost $3,300,000 (1997-2000).
- **Yampa Passage Barriers:** $50K earmarked; awaiting revised SOW; to be integrated with Yampa low-flow studies.

**Floodplain protection issue paper**
- Phase I (biological merits, priority areas, gaps in the current program) is budgeted with a combined FY 1996/97 cap of $30,000 (about $20K Colorado, $10K Utah). Phase II (protection tools such as zoning, easements, and incentives) depends on Management Committee review.
- Deliverables: interim Phase I report (August 1996) and a final strategy paper and action plan (July 1997).

**Floodplain Habitat Restoration Program (Umbrella SOW)**
- Goal: restore or enhance natural floodplain functions supporting endangered fish recovery.
- Inventory (FY 1993, Irving and Burdick 1995) identified 293 sites totaling 34,628 acres along 871 river miles of the Green, Colorado, Gunnison, Yampa, and White rivers.
- Nine priority reaches were ranked. Highest priority: Green River RM 238-320 (Pariette to Dinosaur, about 11,222 acres of potential floodplain). Green River RM 0-238 and Gunnison RM 50-75 are also rated high biological importance.
- Green River RM 320-410 is currently low priority because thermal releases from Flaming Gorge create a barrier. Studies may change this.
- Levee Removal Strategy FY 1997 Green River sites include Bonanza Bridge, Horseshoe Bend, Stirrup, Baeser Bend, Johnson J-4, Leota L-7, and Old Charlie (diked). Application sites are depressions, with some built as terraces to limit nonnative fish buildup.

**Controlled facility and site evaluations**
- [[Old Charlie Wash]] (Green River RM 250): FY 1997 budget was cut from $50K to $15K, so the site will fill and drain naturally with no additional data collection. The 1995 harvest was 631,798 fish (15 species, 22.3 lb), including 28 young and 2 adult razorbacks and 7 young squawfish.
- [[Walter Walker]] (Colorado River RM 164) and [[Adobe Creek]] (Colorado River RM 160): NBS contaminant studies to determine whether selenium affects razorback reproduction and survival, and whether freshening flows reduce selenium. FY 1997 costs $50,500 (Walter Walker) and $63,000 (Adobe Creek).
- [[Gravel Pit at 29 5/8 Road]] (Colorado River RM 174): Final report on whether a gravel pit can act as a surrogate floodplain, due May 1997; cost $8,250 (plus overhead).

**Land acquisition**
- Target: easement agreements on 1,000 acres of Green River floodplain and 200 acres along the Gunnison and Colorado rivers in FY 1997, rising to 800 and 400 acres in FY 1998.
- Acquisition options favored include a contracted local individual and Partners for Wildlife agreements. Fee-title acquisition is less preferred because it removes land from production and tax rolls.
- Budget: FY 1997 $750K; FY 1998 $1.15M; total $1.9M.
- The Escalante Ranch deal (Green River RM 305) stalled due to selenium contamination.

**Levee removal and floodplain connectivity (CAP-6-LR)**
- Tests whether reconnected floodplains increase fish production and whether gains for natives are offset by nonnative predators.
- Sampling plan: 96 site visits in the 1996-1997 field season. FY 1997 budget $309,300; FY 1998 $305,100; FY 1999 $320,400; total $934,800 (about $934.8K across three years).

**Budget and administration**
- FY 1997 Habitat Restoration Program total: about $1.92 million, excluding out-year budgets for FY 1998 onward, which are unknown.
- Reviewers include Keith Rose, Terry Ireland, Bob Norman, Frank Pfeifer, and Brent Uilenberg.
- The 1996 Third Annual Habitat Restoration Program Symposium (November 13-14, 1996, Grand Junction) was announced to give status updates and gather issues on Program scope.
