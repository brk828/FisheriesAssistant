# Biological Opinion on Rainbow Trout and Channel Catfish Stocking in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #endangered-species #conservation-management #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This biological opinion (2-21-94-F-244) evaluates the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's program to stock catchable-size (8-10 inch) [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Channel Catfish]] into the lower [[Colorado River]] from [[Hoover Dam]] to the International Border. The consultation addresses potential impacts to two endangered species: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and their designated critical habitat in [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Havasu]], and the Colorado River from Parker Dam to Imperial Dam. The opinion concludes that the proposed stocking program will not jeopardize the continued existence of either species nor adversely modify critical habitat, provided that specific conservation measures and monitoring protocols are implemented during 1995-1999.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] are endemic fish species of the Colorado River basin that have experienced dramatic population declines. The razorback sucker was listed as endangered in 1991, and the bonytail chub in 1980. [[Lake Mohave]] contains the largest remaining population of razorback suckers (estimated at 20,000 adults, down from 60,000 in the late 1980s), consisting primarily of aging adults with no documented natural recruitment. The bonytail chub population in Lake Mohave is extremely small, with only 57 individuals captured between 1974-1991.

The Service proposes to stock 560,500 rainbow trout annually into various locations: Lake Mohave (areas 1-7), mainstem Colorado River below Davis Dam, the [[Colorado River Indian Reservation]] (four sites), and the [[Fort Yuma Indian Reservation]] (two canals). Stocking occurs on varying schedules: year-round in upper Lake Mohave (areas 6-7), November through May in mid-lake areas (3-5), November through March in lower areas (1-2), and winter-only at reservation sites. [[Channel Catfish]] stocking is limited to isolated waters with no direct connection to the mainstem river.

The lower Colorado River has been profoundly altered by dam construction, water diversions, channelization, and introduction of over 70 nonnative fish species. [[Lake Mohave]], created by Davis Dam in the early 1950s, is 108 km long with a hydraulic retention time of 0.24 years, making it more riverine in character. Water released from [[Hoover Dam]] is drawn from the hypolimnion at 11-12.5°C year-round, creating a cold/warm water interface typically near [[Eldorado Canyon]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Razorback Sucker]] spawning occurs from December to June (peak January-March) in shallow (0.5-5.0 m) areas with gravel or cobble substrates at water temperatures of 10-21°C. Known spawning concentrations occur around [[Cottonwood Cove]], [[Arizona Bay]], [[Six Mile Cove]], and Eldorado Canyon. Larvae are found in quiet backwater areas and cove margins in shallow water (<2 m) at 21.7-24.4°C. Adult razorbacks utilize both backwaters and river channels, avoiding riffles.

[[Bonytail Chub]] spawning has been observed in May over gravel bars in water up to 10 m deep, with the spawning season extending from late spring to early summer. Adult bonytails occupy pools, eddies, and open water areas of reservoirs. Very cold water from Hoover Dam may restrict their use of upper Lake Mohave. Little is known about larval and juvenile habitat selection.

The introduction of nonnative fish species has been a major factor in native fish decline. [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) are significant predators on stocked rainbow trout, with 95% of bass stomachs containing trout in the Hoover Dam tailrace. Rainbow trout were directly observed preying on razorback sucker larvae in Lake Mohave in 1992. Trout have also been implicated as predators on young [[Humpback Chub]] in the Grand Canyon and on [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*).

The environmental baseline reveals that the lower Colorado River has experienced approximately 100 years of Federal, State, and private impacts, with the greatest changes occurring in the last 60 years following large dam construction. Physical habitat modifications, altered flow regimes, water quality changes, and nonnative species introductions have severely impaired native fish populations. Only 36 fish species are native to the Colorado River Basin, yet over 70 species have been introduced.

## Key Findings

**Population Status and Trends:**
- Lake Mohave razorback sucker population declined from ~60,000 to ~20,000 individuals, with continued decline expected as aging adults die
- No documented natural recruitment of razorback suckers anywhere in the Colorado River basin
- Bonytail chub population in Lake Mohave extremely small (57 captured 1974-1991); may be the most endangered large fish in the basin
- One naturally recruited young bonytail in Lake Mohave indicates minimal successful recruitment in late 1970s

**Predation Effects:**
- Rainbow trout observed preying on razorback sucker larvae in Lake Mohave (1992)
- Predation occurs during swimup period when trout and larvae co-occur
- No direct evidence of trout predation on bonytail chub
- Striped bass consume considerable quantities of stocked rainbow trout (95% of bass stomachs in Hoover Dam tailrace)

**Stocking Program Details:**
- Total annual stocking: 560,500 rainbow trout (8-10 inch)
- Lake Mohave receives 393,000 trout distributed across seven areas
- Davis Dam to Needles: 146,000 trout (August-May, none June-July)
- Colorado River Indian Reservation: 13,000 trout (winter only, November-March)
- Fort Yuma Indian Reservation: 8,500 trout (November and January)
- Channel catfish stocking limited to isolated waters

**Conservation Measures (1995-1999):**
- Annual census of razorback sucker population in Lake Mohave
- Pre-stocking surveys in areas 1-5 (November-April); no trout stocking if razorback larvae present
- Production of 10,000 advanced razorback sucker fingerlings (300 mm) annually for Lake Mohave
- Production of 25,000 advanced bonytail chub fingerlings (250-300 mm) annually for Lake Mohave (beginning 1996)
- All stocked endangered fish to be marked or tagged before release
- Efforts to locate bonytail chub spawning areas
- Development of Lake Mohave fisheries management plan

**Biological Opinion Conclusions:**
- Proposed action will NOT jeopardize continued existence of razorback sucker or bonytail chub
- Will NOT adversely modify designated critical habitat
- Incidental take of razorback sucker and bonytail chub larvae by stocked rainbow trout cannot be quantified
- Take of razorback larvae believed minimal; take of bonytail larvae not documented
- Rainbow trout do not reproduce in reservoir; action is reversible if future investigations show adverse impacts
- Reintroduction of large numbers of advanced fingerlings will have positive impact on recruitment

**Critical Habitat Assessment:**
- Lake Mohave and lower Colorado River (Parker Dam to Imperial Dam) designated as critical habitat for razorback sucker
- Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu designated as critical habitat for bonytail chub
- Primary constituent element (biological environment including food supply, predation, competition) significantly degraded by nonnative fish
- Continued trout stocking not shown to adversely affect adult populations of either species
- No adverse modification to critical habitat anticipated

**Incidental Take Statement:**
- Level of take anticipated but cannot be quantified
- Take considered exceeded if: (1) reasonable and prudent measures not implemented, or (2) Service-stocked rainbow trout shown to limit recruitment or adversely modify critical habitat
- Procedures in place to minimize contact between stocked trout and endangered fish

**Historical Context:**
- Rainbow trout first introduced to lower Colorado River in 1922
- Lake Mohave was premier trout fishery in western U.S. through 1980s
- By 1990, significant decreases in trout catch (up to 40% decline)
- Nevada stocking of 200,000 trout in Cottonwood Basin (1993) had <1% return to creel
- Decline attributed to striped bass predation and other factors

**Reintroduction Efforts:**
- Initial stocking of larvae/fry (1981-1991) showed high predation and low survival
- Shift to stocking larger fish (>250 mm) shows greater promise
- Cove-rearing program initiated 1992: 129 advanced fingerlings released from Yuma Cove
- 1993: 487 advanced fingerlings released from ephemeral ponds
- Survival verified: 5 fish from 1992 recaptured in 1993; 10 recaptured in 1994
- One 300 mm stocked fish grew to ripe male adult, found in spawning aggregation (1994)
- Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Project goal: 30,000 razorback suckers and equal number of bonytail chubs over 10 years

**Conservation Recommendations:**
- Include Lake Mohave razorback sucker and bonytail chub conservation as main element in future aquatic ecosystem management plan
- Incorporate Lake Mohave conservation elements into Lower Colorado River Basin Native Fish Management Plan
- Establish location and management protocols for spawning areas, backwaters, and oxbows as partnership options

The consultation period began March 9, 1994, with formal opinion issued July 1994. Reinitiation required if incidental take exceeded, new information reveals unanticipated effects, action modified, or new species listed. Consultation number: 2-21-94-F-244.
