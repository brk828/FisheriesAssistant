# Competition between Kokanees and Utah Chub in Flaming Gorge Reservoir, Utah-Wyoming

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study experimentally tested whether [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*) compete with [[Kokanee]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*, lacustrine sockeye salmon) for zooplankton resources in [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] (Wyoming-Utah). Using six 80-m³ in-lake enclosures stocked with three kokanees and varying densities of Utah chub (0, 3, 6, 12, or 24 individuals), researchers measured kokanee growth over four 21-day trials (May and June, 1991 and 1992). Results showed that competitive effects were **not constant through time**: in May 1992, no significant competition was detected despite high chub densities, while in June 1992, kokanee growth and zooplankton biomass declined sharply with increasing Utah chub density. The key driver of kokanee growth was food availability — specifically **Daphnia pulex density**, which explained 76% of variance in kokanee growth — rather than Utah chub abundance per se. Water temperature strongly mediated the strength of exploitative competition, with chub foraging activity and competitive impact increasing markedly above ~18°C. The authors conclude that monitoring zooplankton forage resources provides a better predictive framework for assessing competition than simply monitoring nongame fish densities.

## Background & Description

- **Kokanee (juveniles used in trials):** Mean initial weight 3.3 g (SD 0.7 g); size-matched to age-1 kokanees observed in the reservoir during spring.
- **Utah Chub (juveniles used in trials):** Mean initial weight 1.0 g (SD 0.4 g); size-matched to age-1 Utah chub in spring.
- Fish were weighed to the nearest 0.01 g both at stocking and at recovery.
- Handling/mortality notes: In 1991, Utah chub experienced high handling mortality (87%) when acclimated in net pens; in 1992, chub were introduced directly into enclosures, reducing mortality to 9%. Kokanee mortality was low in both years (13%).
- Zooplankton prey were sized and converted to biomass using taxon-specific length-weight regressions (McCauley 1984) for *Daphnia*, *Ceriodaphnia*, cyclopoid copepods, and *Diaptomus*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Both [[Kokanee]] and [[Utah Chub]] are **zooplanktivorous pelagic fishes** occupying overlapping trophic niches in [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], with prior work (Schneidervin 1985) reporting **95% diet overlap**, and both species preferentially consuming *Daphnia pulex*.
- The reservoir exhibits a trophic gradient: oligotrophic near the dam (study site), mesoeutrophic in mid-reaches, and eutrophic near tributary confluences (Blacks Fork and Green rivers).
- **Vertical habitat use differed by season/temperature:**
  - In May, kokanees foraged within 2 m of the surface (cooler epilimnion).
  - In June, as temperatures rose, kokanees were rarely observed near the surface, likely retreating to cooler, deeper water.
  - Utah chub remained within 5 m of the surface in all four trials regardless of season.
  - No direct antagonistic behavior between species was observed even when they shared epilimnetic space, supporting the conclusion that **exploitative competition** (for shared food, i.e., zooplankton), not **interference competition**, drives the interaction.
- Management context: [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] was historically managed for rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*); Utah chub populations expanded post-impoundment (1962). Piscivores brown trout (*Salmo trutta*) and smallmouth bass (*Micropterus dolomieu*) were introduced to control chub, and lake trout (*Salvelinus namaycush*, self-recruited from upstream) also prey heavily on Utah chub ([[Yule and Luecke 1993]]). Kokanees were stocked in the 1970s explicitly to both support sport fishing and compete with Utah chub for zooplankton.

## Key Findings

- **Seasonal variability in competition:** No significant relationship between Utah chub density and kokanee growth in May 1992 (F = 2.05, df = 3, P = 0.289, r² = 0.51). In June 1992, the relationship was strong and significant (F = 33.5, df = 4, P = 0.010, r² = 0.92), with kokanee weight gain ranging from **+64% (0 chub) to −2% (24 chub)**.
- **Zooplankton biomass decline:** Mean zooplankton biomass was stable across chub densities in May 1992 (62–77 µg/L; not significant, F = 1.53, P = 0.341). In June 1992, biomass declined significantly with increasing chub density (F = 11.0, df = 4, P = 0.045, r² = 0.79); at the highest density (24 chub), biomass dropped 98% within just 7 days.
- **Daphnia pulex as key indicator:** *D. pulex* density was the single best predictor of kokanee growth, explaining **76% of variance** (r² = 0.76, P < 0.001) — outperforming total zooplankton biomass (r² = 0.52, P = 0.001) and phytoplankton (chlorophyll-a) measures, which were poor predictors of kokanee growth in both trials.
- **Cladoceran suppression:** *Daphnia pulex* and other large Cladocera (*Ceriodaphnia quadrangula*, *Diaphanosoma birgei*) dominated zooplankton biomass initially but were selectively depleted in high-chub enclosures, shifting community composition toward copepods; this shift was most pronounced at high Utah chub densities.
- **Temperature-dependent foraging:** Utah chub foraging intensity, and thus competitive impact, increased with temperature. Surface temperatures never exceeded 15.7°C in May (no competitive effect), but reached 18.8°C in June (strong competitive effect) — consistent with prior findings on cyprinid-salmonid competition (e.g., [[Reeves et al. 1987]]; [[Coghill 1992]] on redside shiners and steelhead).
- **Interference competition ruled out:** Because kokanee growth declined only when zooplankton forage also declined (not uniformly across chub densities), the authors concluded that **exploitative competition for food**, not **direct interference/antagonism**, was the operative mechanism.
- **Growth threshold:** Kokanees exhibited positive growth in all enclosures where *D. pulex* density exceeded 3 organisms/L and total zooplankton biomass exceeded 50 µg/L.
- **Management implication:** The study suggests that fisheries managers should monitor **forage/zooplankton resource levels** rather than simply nongame fish (Utah chub) densities to assess and predict competitive impacts on salmonids like kokanee, since competitive strength varies with environmental conditions (notably temperature) rather than being a constant function of competitor abundance.
