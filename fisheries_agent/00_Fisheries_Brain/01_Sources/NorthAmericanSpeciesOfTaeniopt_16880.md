# North American Species of Taeniopteryx (Plecoptera, Insecta)

#plecoptera #taeniopteryx #entomology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic revision by W. E. Ricker (Fisheries Research Board of Canada, Nanaimo) and H. H. Ross (Illinois Natural History Survey) covers the eight American stonefly species of the genus [[Taeniopteryx]]. Four of these, [[Taeniopteryx Burksi]], [[Taeniopteryx Lonicera]], [[Taeniopteryx Metequi]] and [[Taeniopteryx Ugola]], are described as new. The paper provides a dichotomous key for males and females, synonymies, diagnoses, and distribution records by state and province. Its main purpose is to resolve long-standing confusion among the four previously described species. The document is an insect taxonomy paper, not a fish study, and its aquatic relevance lies in the freshwater stream habitats these winter-active insects occupy.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses the distribution of the "winter" stoneflies of eastern North America. The authors found that four undescribed species existed within the genus and that the four described species had been variously confused. Published records were reassigned to the revised scheme wherever specimens could be re-checked. Holotypes and allotypes of the new species are deposited in the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] collection.

Key taxonomic history includes:

- **[[Taeniopteryx Maura]]** (Pictet, 1841, as *Nemoura maura*): original types lost. The authors follow [[Needham and Claassen]] (1925), who associated the name with specimens bearing strong femoral spurs.
- **[[Taeniopteryx Nivalis]]** (Fitch, 1847, as *Nemoura nivalis*): Fitch's specimens are gone, but his description allowed the authors to identify the species.
- **[[Taeniopteryx Parvula]]** (Banks, 1918): described from Virginia and the District of Columbia.
- **[[Taeniopteryx Lita]]** (Frison, 1942): previously confused with parvula in Frison's 1929 and 1935 work.
- **[[Frison]] (1929, 1935)** illustrated what is now *burksi* under the name *nivalis*.

Diagnostic features used throughout include hind femoral spurs, the vesicle (ventral lobe) on the 9th sternite of males, the shape of the subanal lobes and cerci, the aedeagus sclerotization, and the notch of the 8th sternite in females. The key separates the species on these characters, and the authors note that female identification is harder than male identification for several pairs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The species are winter- and early-spring-active stoneflies that inhabit streams and rivers. Collection localities include creeks, the [[Ohio River]], the Potomac River, the Neuse River, the Eno River and many smaller tributaries. Emergence timing is a key ecological feature:

- [[Taeniopteryx Nivalis]] tends to emerge earlier in the season than burksi, though with much overlap. Fitch noted it emerges in the latter half of winter, shortly after the Small Snowfly ([[Allocapnia]]), and occurs with that genus on the snow.
- [[Taeniopteryx Maura]] tends to emerge later than burksi where both occur, and does not range as far north.
- [[Taeniopteryx Lonicera]] is associated with the region of the [[Appalachian Mountains]] and southeastern forests, and its range overlaps that of lita into Tennessee.
- [[Taeniopteryx Metequi]] coincides with the great broad-leaf hardwood forest of eastern North America.
- [[Taeniopteryx Ugola]] takes its name from the coal measures underlying its home area in West Virginia.

Biogeographic patterns noted in the paper:

- **Burksi** has the broadest range, across the eastern United States and southern Canada.
- **Maura** is more restricted, with records in Cape Breton Island, eastern New Brunswick, the northeastern United States, and southward to Georgia and Alabama.
- **Nivalis** is northern in the east (Labrador through Quebec and Ontario to Minnesota), and occurs in the Cordillera from northern California to Idaho, where it is uncommon.
- **Parvula** is centred on the [[Great Lakes Region]], extending east to Cape Breton, north to Hudson Bay, and west to Manitoba, with a gap in the [[Mississippi Valley]] between the Ohio River and the Ozark region.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic outcomes**

- Eight American species are recognized in *Taeniopteryx*. Four are new: burksi, lonicera, metequi and ugola.
- Burksi was previously confused with maura and nivalis. It is distinguished mainly by smaller size and darker colour than maura, the absence of strong spurs, and male 9th sternite characters.
- Lita and lonicera were mixed up in earlier records. Lonicera is characterized by a twisted, deeply excavated 9th sternite and a bent-outward subanal lobe tip, while lita has a swollen, light, hairy posteroventral projection.
- Metequi is distinguished from parvula by its lack of a ventral lobe and a mesal subacute process on the subanal lobes.
- Ugola is most similar to parvula in males (differing in the absence of a ventral lobe in parvula) and to burksi in females (differing in broad median plate and extra sclerotization).

**Species descriptions and types**

- **[[Taeniopteryx Burksi]]**: holotype from Urbana, Champaign Co., Illinois, collected 14 January 1928 by T. H. Frison. Named for Dr B. D. Burks of the United States National Museum.
- **[[Taeniopteryx Lonicera]]**: holotype male from Laurel Fork, south of Blountsville, Blount Co., Alabama, collected 26 January 1958 by H. H. Ross and J. A. Ross. Male body length 5.5-7 mm; female body length 7.5-8.5 mm.
- **[[Taeniopteryx Metequi]]**: holotype male from Hayes Creek, Glendale, Illinois, collected 25 March 1958 by H. H. Ross and L. J. Stannard. Male body 6-7.5 mm; female body 8.5-10 mm. Name derives from an Algonkian word for the broad-leaf forest.
- **[[Taeniopteryx Ugola]]**: holotype male from Fellowsville, Preston Co., West Virginia, collected 4 March 1959 by H. H. Ross and J. A. Ross. Male body length 8 mm; female body length 10 mm.

**Distribution records**

- The document lists collection records by state and province for each species, giving counties and number of sites, with date ranges. Full site-level detail is held in the American Documentation Institute's Auxiliary Publication Service.
- Burksi has the most extensive list, covering many US states and Canadian provinces, with collection dates from December to April.
- Lita records are concentrated in Illinois, Indiana, Arkansas and Tennessee, with a few specimens from Alabama, North Carolina, Kentucky and Mississippi.
- Maura is widespread in the southern Appalachian region and is common from Georgia to Alabama.
- Nivalis is strongly northern, with the Quebec, Ontario and Pennsylvania records most numerous.

**Notable conclusions**

- No overlap in spur size was observed where burksi and maura occur together (for example, in the Potomac River collections).
- Maura and nivalis show no evidence of intergradation in either sex.
- Frison's illustration of the female 8th sternite for parvula does not show the inward-pointing horns of the central plate that the authors found in specimens.
- Several Needham and Claassen records, including those attributed to parvula from Maryland and New Jersey, could not be re-checked. Needham and Claassen also cited Plummer's Island, Maryland, as the type locality of parvula, but Banks's description mentions no such locality.
- The name burksi and the species concept for nivalis were chosen to match earlier published illustrations and original descriptions, establishing a consistent basis for future North American stonefly work.
