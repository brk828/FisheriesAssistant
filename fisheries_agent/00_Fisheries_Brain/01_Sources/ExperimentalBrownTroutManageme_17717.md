# Experimental Brown Trout Management in Lower Sardine Lake, California

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #Salmonidae #trout-stocking

## Executive Summary

This seven-year experimental study (1954-1960) evaluated various sizes and strains of [[Brown Trout]] (Salmo trutta) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (Salmo gairdnerii) stocking in [[Lower Sardine Lake]], a high-elevation (5,968 feet), oligotrophic lake in [[Sierra County]], California. The research compared fingerling versus catchable-sized fish, wild versus domesticated strains, and seasonal stocking timing. Key findings demonstrated that fingerling plants produced very poor returns (<3%), while catchable-sized domesticated brown trout equaled or exceeded rainbow trout performance (75-81% returns). Early season stocking consistently outperformed fall stocking. Growth was severely limited in this oligotrophic system, with few fish exceeding 11 inches even after 4+ years. The study revealed significant predation by large brown trout and competition from abundant [[Tahoe Sucker]] populations, both likely contributing to poor fingerling survival.

## Background & Description

[[Lower Sardine Lake]] is located in the Lakes Basin Recreation Area of [[Sierra County]], California, in a glaciated granitic basin. Physical characteristics include:

- **Surface area**: 48 acres
- **Volume**: 1,390 acre-feet
- **Maximum depth**: 76 feet
- **Mean depth**: 38 feet (note: text states "88 feet" but this appears to be an OCR error given volume/area ratio)
- **Elevation**: 5,968 feet
- **Summer surface temperature**: 72°F
- **Ice cover**: December through May
- **Secchi disk readings**: Average 38 feet during plankton maxima
- **Total hardness**: 14 ppm
- **Aquatic vegetation**: Scarce higher aquatic plants

The lake features one small tributary where [[Rainbow Trout]] spawn with limited success and [[Brown Trout]] spawn over the stream's submerged alluvial fan. Meager fall flows prevent brown trout spawning in the stream proper. [[Tahoe Sucker]] (Catostomus tahoensis) are the only rough fish present.

Historical stocking included brown trout introduction before 1927, with annual stocking of rainbows or [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (Salvelinus fontinalis) from 1930-1948. Approximately 25,000 fingerling brook trout were stocked annually for six years preceding the study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The oligotrophic nature of [[Lower Sardine Lake]] severely limited fish growth and productivity. Natural reproduction provided a yield of approximately 3 pounds of 8- to 12-inch trout per surface acre. In 1954, naturally-produced rainbows and browns comprised 44% and 47% respectively of the unmarked trout catch (412 fish total).

**Competition and Predation Dynamics:**

The lake supported abundant [[Tahoe Sucker]] populations, with suckers accounting for over 70% of fish recovered during a 1963 rotenone treatment (considered minimal given many suckers remained in deep water). Diet overlap between suckers and trout was documented:

- Juvenile suckers (1-2 inches): Consumed microcrustaceans, rotifers, insects, and annelids
- Adult suckers (4-9 inches): Primarily aquatic insects with microcrustaceans, algae, and detritus

Large brown trout (18-27 inches) exhibited piscivorous behavior. Six large browns caught shortly after catchable stocking contained 12 trout, half of which were newly stocked. Four browns (6.2-10.5 inches) from a nearby pond each contained small Tahoe suckers.

**Growth Limitations:**

Growth was severely constrained by food availability. Mean length progression for Mt. Whitney strain browns stocked as fingerlings in 1955: 6.6 inches (1956), 8.7 inches (1957), 9.6 inches (1958), 9.9 inches (1959), 9.9 inches (1960). Of 10,024 marked brown trout measured, only 447 exceeded 10.9 inches, 52 exceeded 11.9 inches, and 2 reached 19.0-20.0 inches.

Condition factor declined with size, falling from 40 at 5.0-5.9 inches to 30-34 for 11.0-11.9 inch fish, reflecting approach to the growth ceiling imposed by food availability. Rare fish transitioning to piscivory exhibited renewed growth; trout weighing 5.0-7.5 pounds were recovered from the 1959 Massachusetts strain plant by 1961.

## Key Findings

**Fingerling Stocking Performance:**

Three brown trout strains were tested as fingerlings (10,000 each, stocked June-August):
- Wild [[Convict Lake]] stock (BN-C, 3.2 per ounce): 1.6% return
- One generation domesticated Convict Lake (BN-W, 9.4 per ounce): 1.2% return
- Long-domesticated Massachusetts strain (BN-Mass., 10.1 per ounce): 2.7% return

[[Eastern Brook Trout]] fingerlings performed even worse: only 38 caught in 1954 despite six previous annual plants of 25,000 fish (<0.2% return). The authors calculated that annually for 25 years prior to the study, more than $500 worth of fingerling trout were stocked with minimal return.

**Catchable-Sized Trout Performance:**

Catchable-sized trout consistently produced high returns (Table 1 data):

**Brown Trout Strains (cumulative returns and annual survival):**
- New Jersey domesticated (BN-N.J.): 81% return, 0.33 annual survival
- Massachusetts domesticated (BN-Mass.): 75% return, 0.36 annual survival
- Mt. Whitney (BN-W, 1-2 generations from wild): 61% return, 0.48 annual survival
- Wild Convict Lake (BN-C): 54% return, 0.61 annual survival

**Rainbow Trout Performance:**
- Mt. Whitney spring plant (May 7, 1959): 75% return, 0.03 annual survival
- Mt. Whitney fall plant (November 4, 1959, 10 per pound, 18% planting mortality): 36% return
- Mt. Shasta fall-spawning strain (September 1, 1960): 50% return

**Key Comparative Findings:**

1. **Domesticated brown trout equaled best domesticated rainbow returns** (75-81%) but with slower rate of return
2. **Early season stocking superior to fall stocking** for both species
3. **Annual survival dramatically higher for browns** (0.33-0.80) versus rainbows (0.03)
4. **Approximately 25% of brown trout plants caught as carryovers** (2+ years after stocking)
5. **Domesticated strains more vulnerable to angling** than wild strains, providing higher first-year returns

**Size-Selective Vulnerability:**

Tagged fish experiment (1957 Mt. Whitney browns) demonstrated size-dependent vulnerability:
- 8.5-9.5 inch fish: 29% caught in 4 months
- 6.5-7.5 inch fish: 13% caught in 4 months

Authors recommend trout stocked for put-and-take angling should be at least 8 inches long to maximize angling benefits per pound stocked.

**Hatchery Performance Trade-offs:**

Mt. Whitney and Massachusetts strains marked as 12-per-ounce fingerlings and reared together showed differential survival:
- Mt. Whitney: 29% hatchery survival, 6.9 per pound at planting
- Massachusetts: 56% hatchery survival, 3.2 per pound at planting (twice as large)

"Hatchery trout survived best in the hatchery but wild trout survived best in the wild."

**Angling Effort and Success:**

Despite eight-fold increase in catch (1,400 trout in 1957 to 10,500 in 1960), angling effort only doubled to 10,500 hours. Catch per hour increased from 0.28 to 1.00 trout. This weak response indicated relatively low demand for catchable trout at this water. Authors recommended limiting total catch to approximately 3,000 trout to support about 2,000 angling days per year, with excess fish better allocated to high-demand waters.

**Management Implications:**

1. Fingerling stocking in oligotrophic high-elevation lakes is economically inefficient
2. Domesticated brown trout strains offer advantages over rainbow trout in slower rate of return and higher annual survival
3. Potential exists for dual-purpose stocking (immediate put-and-take plus carryover fish) in more fertile waters
4. Competition from [[Tahoe Sucker]] populations and predation by large brown trout likely contribute to poor fingerling survival
5. Growth limitations in oligotrophic systems prevent realization of weight gains from carryover fish
