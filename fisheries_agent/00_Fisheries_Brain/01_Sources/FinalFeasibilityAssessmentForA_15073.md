# Endangered Fishes, Dam Operations, and Water Transfers in the Colorado and Gila River Basins

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of appendices to a December 1994 feasibility assessment for a multi-species management program on the Lower Colorado River, prepared by SWCA, Inc. for the Six-Agency Committee. It contains three groups of material: (A) the text of the Endangered Species Act of 1973 as amended; (B) three federal fish and wildlife documents, namely a draft biological opinion on [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations (October 1993), a summary biological opinion on delivering [[Central Arizona Project]] water to the [[Gila River Basin]] (April 1994), and the Upper Colorado River [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (1987); and (C) appendix material from the Glen Canyon opinion. The core scientific content concerns the decline of endangered fishes, chiefly the [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and the threats posed by flow regimes, cold-water releases, and non-native species.

## Background & Description

**Legal framework.** The [[Endangered Species Act]] defines "take," "species," and "threatened species," and sets out listing criteria (habitat loss, overutilization, disease or predation, inadequate regulation, other factors). Section 7 requires federal agencies to ensure actions are not likely to jeopardize listed species. Consultation outcomes take the form of a [[Biological Opinion]] with a [[Reasonable and Prudent Alternative]] (RPA), reasonable and prudent measures, and an incidental take statement.

**Focal species.** The documents treat the following taxa:

- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): endangered; canyon-bound, associated with swift water and boulders. The Grand Canyon population depends heavily on the [[Little Colorado River]] for spawning and rearing.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): listed endangered throughout its range on October 23, 1991; reproduction in natural riverine habitat is described as virtually nonexistent.
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): endangered; considered extirpated from Grand Canyon.
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*): endangered; considered extirpated from Grand Canyon.
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*): threatened; short-lived (about two years), so vulnerable to short, intense impacts.
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*): endangered.
- [[Bald Eagle]] and [[Peregrine Falcon]]: raptors considered in the Glen Canyon opinion and, for the eagle, in the CAP opinion.
- [[Kanab Ambersnail]] (*Oxyloma haydeni kanabensis*): a terrestrial mollusk, not a fish, known from one natural population in Grand Canyon.

**Water and dam infrastructure.** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed in 1963 as part of the Colorado River Storage Project. Minimum releases to the lower basin are 8.23 maf, including the Mexican treaty obligation. The [[Central Arizona Project]] aqueduct was declared complete in October 1993, with an expected life of 100 years, and draws from Lake Havasu.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic and current fish fauna.** The basin's fish fauna includes 32 species, 75% endemic, dominated historically by cyprinids and catostomids. Of the eight native Grand Canyon fishes, three are extirpated and four have reproducing populations; the razorback sucker occurs without reproduction. The humpback chub's known range has contracted since the dam: its last collection above Lee's Ferry was in 1967, at RM 17.8 in 1980, and at RM 29.9 in 1993.

**Flow and temperature.** Pre-dam flows were highly variable, with a spring peak. Post-dam operations deliver cold water from the reservoir hypolimnion (Lee's Ferry temperatures of 7 to 12 °C), suppress mainstem spawning, and impose daily fluctuations. Historically, daily fluctuations exceeded 12,000 cfs on more than 58% of days. Backwaters, which serve as nursery habitat, are reduced by fluctuating flows and by low, cold, or variable releases.

**Non-native species.** Introductions are repeatedly identified as a major cause of native fish decline. In Grand Canyon, brown trout remain in sizeable numbers, mainly near Bright Angel Creek, while rainbow trout stocked above the canyon are a management concern. In the Gila basin, the CAP opinion identifies striped bass, white bass, blue tilapia, rainbow smelt, and triploid grass carp as likely introductions, and lists dozens of non-native species already present in CAP and Salt River Project canals. Bait-bucket releases and dumped aquarium fish are cited as continuing sources.

**Habitat transitions.** Juvenile humpback chub shift from shallow, protected backwaters and stable shorelines toward moderate-depth, moderate-velocity areas with uneven substrate as they grow. Razorback suckers historically used eddies and backwaters, while modern radio-tracking records adult use of mid-channel sand runs.

## Key Findings

**Glen Canyon Dam draft biological opinion (October 13, 1993)**

- The Modified Low Fluctuating Flow alternative was judged *likely to jeopardize* the continued existence of the humpback chub and razorback sucker.
- It was judged *not likely to jeopardize* the bald eagle, Kanab ambersnail, or peregrine falcon. No incidental take was authorized for the eagle, peregrine, or razorback sucker.
- Incidental take was anticipated for humpback chub (young transported downstream by habitat maintenance or building flows) and for Kanab ambersnail (flood losses). Take was described as difficult to quantify.
- Population evidence is limited and uncertain. Estimates of humpback chub in the Little Colorado River range from 7,000 to 8,000 (Kaeding and Zimmerman, >200 mm) to 2,407 (Valdez et al. 1992a, >175 mm, 95% interval 1,102 to 6,564). Razorback suckers in Lake Mohave were estimated at about 60,000, and ages from otoliths range from 24 to 44 years.
- Grand Canyon hosts the largest known breeding population of peregrine falcons in the contiguous United States, with 71 breeding areas identified in 1988 to 1990 and an extrapolated 96 pairs.
- Recommended RPA elements include experimental flows (high spring flows, low summer and fall flows during low-release years of about 8.23 maf), a Little Colorado River management plan with a draft due within two years, a research program on temperature and biotic interactions, a razorback sucker workshop within one year, a second humpback chub spawning aggregation, and a [[Selective Withdrawal]] feasibility study. A multi-level intake structure was considered potentially essential for warming mainstem releases, but the opinion cautions it is not sufficient on its own.
- The September 7, 1994 RPA update gives a schedule: experimental flows initiated in fiscal year 1997, with design completed in fiscal year 1996, and a fallback to SASF flows from April to October if progress is insufficient.

**Central Arizona Project summary biological opinion (April 15, 1994)**

- Delivery of CAP water to the Gila basin was judged *likely to jeopardize* spikedace, loach minnow, Gila topminnow, and razorback sucker, and likely to adversely modify their critical habitat. It was judged *not likely to jeopardize* the desert pupfish, Colorado squawfish, or bald eagle.
- The opinion concluded that over the 100-year project life "the probability is high that one or more non-native fish species will use the CAP" to colonize listed-species habitat.
- The RPA has five elements: (1) four drop-structure barriers (two on Aravaipa Creek, two on the San Pedro River, with completion deadlines of three and one-half and six years); (2) continued operation of existing electrical barriers, with monitoring of non-native fish; (3) $250,000 annually for 25 years to the Fish and Wildlife Service for recovery and protection; (4) a further $250,000 annually for 25 years for non-native fish research and control; and (5) an education program on bait-bucket transfers, with at least five years of implementation.
- Take was judged unquantifiable and assumed exceeded if the project is altered or not carried out.
- Rejected alternatives (Appendix) include screening CAP turnouts (technically infeasible), ozone treatment (economically infeasible), and additional electrical barriers (lack of effectiveness data).

**Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program (1987)**

- The program covers three endangered species (Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub) and manages the razorback sucker as a candidate, over a 15-year initial timeframe.
- Five elements: habitat management, habitat development and maintenance, native fish stocking, non-native species and sportfishing management, and research/monitoring/data management.
- Annual operating cost is estimated at $2.3 million. A minimum of $15 million from Congress is needed ($10 million for water rights and $5 million for capital expenditures).
- New water project proponents contribute a one-time charge of $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion, adjusted for inflation. Existing Reclamation projects are exempt from this charge.
- Specific reach protections include a 5,000 acre-foot withheld reserve at Ruedi Reservoir and an additional 5,000 acre-feet in July through September on an average of 4 of 5 years.

**Overall implication.** The documents consistently tie fish recovery to flow management and non-native control, with explicit cost-sharing mechanisms. The recovery goals are described as general because the parameters of self-sustaining populations are not yet defined.
