# Effects of Las Vegas Wash Effluent on Game Fish Reproduction and Food Habits in Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, submitted to the [[Nevada Department of Fish and Game]] by the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, examines how effluent from the [[Las Vegas Wash]] affects the reproduction, growth and feeding of [[Largemouth Bass]] and other game and forage fishes in [[Las Vegas Bay]], Lake Mead. The work combined reproductive observations by diving, electrofishing and gill netting, stomach content analysis, age and growth studies, and a dedicated investigation of [[Threadfin Shad]] diet and reproduction. Bass nest density in outer Las Vegas Bay was comparable to [[Swallow Cove]], while the inner bay appeared to have poor egg survival, attributed mainly to turbidity and sedimentation. Shad and chironomids dominated bass diets, and debris from [[Tamarisk]] was a major shad food item.

## Background & Description

Lake Mead, impounded in 1935, is a major recreational fishery in the southwestern United States, with fishing showing an increasing trend. Las Vegas Bay receives industrial and domestic effluent from the Las Vegas Valley, which is known to degrade water quality. The study was initiated to determine whether these conditions affect the biology of largemouth bass and other species.

Methods included:
- **Collection:** Night electrofishing from a 20-foot barge rigged with a 2500-watt generator and copper-rod electrodes, plus one night of gill netting (30.7 m nets, mesh sizes 7.6 to 1.5 cm) in Las Vegas Bay. Stomachs from creel census fish were also supplied by Nevada Fish and Game.
- **Stomach analysis:** Contents were expressed as percentage frequency of occurrence. For shad, a gizzard-weight method estimated prey biomass, and filtered slide counts of pharyngeal pouch, foregut and gizzard contents were used. Counts were scaled with the formula N = 255 × 1.85 × C.
- **Age and growth:** Scales were read on a projection reader.
- **Visual surveys:** Shoreline and underwater diving transects counted nests, fry and adults.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Las Vegas Bay has high turbidity in spring (Secchi depth 1.5 m) and sedimentation in the inner bay. [[Black Island]] transects (Secchi 2.6 to 4 m) had rubble substrate and dense [[Tamarisk]] cover, with less [[Ruppia]] and [[Typha]]. [[Swallow Cove]] (Secchi 4.0 to 4.5 m) had rubble substrate and abundant [[Spiny Naiad]] alongside tamarisk, giving heavy fry cover. Water temperatures during bass spawning were a factor, with spawning starting near 16°C. [[Carp]] spawned actively in April and May, overlapping the bass spawning period, and could disturb nests. Recorded wind, abrupt temperature drops and lake-level lowering were noted as sources of nest and egg mortality.

## Key Findings

**Largemouth bass reproduction**
- Nest density in the main study area was low. Six nests were found in Las Vegas Bay in March to May, none in the transect area. Black Island transects had 26 nests (20 in May, 6 in June). No nests were seen at Swallow Cove, where spawning had finished before diving began.
- Fry production at Black Island was estimated at 1600, 1400 and 1900 fry per mile in May, June and July. UNLV divers counted 94 fry at Swallow Cove in August.
- Adult density at Black Island was 21.5, 17 and 32 per mile in June, July and August. Swallow Cove had 5 and 32 per mile in July and August.
- Egg counts were not made. Visual estimates of three nests gave 500 to 1500 eggs per nest.
- Conclusion: reproductive success in outer Las Vegas Bay appeared comparable to Swallow Cove. Egg survival in the inner bay was judged poor.

**Food habits**
- 13 food items were recorded in bass stomachs, including threadfin shad, chironomids, odonates, ephemeropterans and detritus.
- Shad dominated in fall and summer, and chironomids dominated in winter.
- Bass fry (mean standard length 20 mm, mean weight 0.22 g) fed almost exclusively on copepods and chironomids.
- Many stomachs were empty, partly because gill-netted fish regurgitate contents.

**Growth and condition**
- Bass in Las Vegas Bay grew faster than in earlier Lake Mead studies. Fish spawned in 1970 reached a mean total length of 201 mm by August 1971. The oldest fish aged was three years.
- Condition factor was low (mean 0.98), indicating relatively non-robust fish despite fast growth.

**Bluegill, rainbow trout and other species**
- Bluegill: 16 food types were identified, with Daphnia the most heavily used and chironomids the most frequent. Bluegill were described as omnivores with possible effects on bass egg mortality.
- Rainbow trout: 80 fish were received. Chironomids and threadfin shad were the main winter and spring foods.
- Carp: reproduction and large size made them a possible target for exploitation.
- Channel catfish: piscivorous, with detritus in about 50% of stomachs.
- Coho salmon, green sunfish, black crappie, striped bass and golden shiner were taken in small numbers, mainly as incidental catches.

**Threadfin shad**
- Diet analysis covered 51 fish from February to August 1971. Plant debris, probably tamarisk fragments, was the main food in all months, reaching 76.1% in April.
- Shad appear to select plant debris to meet energy needs for growth and reproduction. Debris enters the bay from wind, shoreline vegetation and the Las Vegas Wash effluent.
- Three year classes were identified. Year class II (1969 spawn) spawned earliest, in May to June. Year class I spawned in July, but its late spawn largely failed, possibly from competition with year class II larvae and high summer temperatures (75.0°F).
- Gonad regression showed a strong correlation between body and gonad weight from March to June.

**Management recommendations**
- Encouraging spiny naiad or otherwise increasing cover in spawning areas should improve fry survival.
- Carp populations could be profitably exploited.
- Management of the game fishery requires further study of shad population regulation and predation.
