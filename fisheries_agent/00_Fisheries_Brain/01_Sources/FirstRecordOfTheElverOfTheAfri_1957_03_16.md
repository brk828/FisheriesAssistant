# First Record of the Elver of the African Eel *Anguilla nebulosa labiata* Peters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anguillidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 *Nature* correspondence by Winifred E. Frost reports the **first documented elvers of the African long-finned eel [[Anguilla nebulosa labiata]]**, a species previously known only from adult specimens on the African mainland. Thirteen elvers were collected from the **Mzinga River/Mzinga Creek** near Dar-es-Salaam, Tanganyika Territory, in January and February 1956. Using morphometric and meristic characters developed by [[Vilhelm Ege]] (anal–dorsal distance ratios and vertebral counts), Frost and Ege identified **two specimens as *A. nebulosa labiata*** and the remaining **eleven as *[[Anguilla bicolor bicolor]]***, the latter marking the **first mainland African record of *A. bicolor bicolor* elvers** and only the second record of this species' elvers from African freshwaters overall (following prior Madagascar records).

## Background & Description

- **Size range of elvers:** 50.0–61.5 mm total length.
- **Preservation:** Specimens preserved in alcohol; first sample (January) retained shape and pigmentation intact, while the second sample (February) suffered damage during postal transit, degrading pigmentation/shape data.
- **Diagnostic taxonomic character (Ege's method):** The **"anal–dorsal distance"** — the distance between verticals through the anus and the origin of the dorsal fin, expressed as a percentage of total length — used to distinguish species and confirmed via **vertebral counts**.
- **Fin morphology distinction:** *A. nebulosa labiata* and *A. marmorata* and *A. mossambica* are **"long-finned" eels**, whereas ***A. bicolor bicolor* is the only "short-finned" eel species in Africa**, making dorsal fin length a key field character alongside the anal-dorsal ratio.
- Additional characters used by Ege to separate adult eels (and, by extension, elvers) include **colour** and **dentition**, along with tail pigmentation patterns in juveniles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat context:** Elvers were found in the **Mzinga River**, which flows into a tidal estuarine system (**Mzinga Creek**), approximately 200 yards above the high-water mark — situated just below a municipal water-supply intake for Dar-es-Salaam.
- This locality places the elvers at the **freshwater–estuarine transition zone**, consistent with the catadromous life-history strategy typical of [[Anguilla]] eels, wherein juveniles migrate from marine spawning grounds into estuaries and rivers to mature.
- **Biogeographic distribution:** Four *Anguilla* species are recorded from African mainland freshwaters and Madagascar drainages into the Indian Ocean:
  - *[[Anguilla mossambica]]* — mainland and Madagascar (elvers known from Natal, Breede River in Cape Province, and Madagascar)
  - *[[Anguilla bicolor bicolor]]* — mainland and Madagascar (elvers previously known only from Madagascar prior to this record)
  - *Anguilla nebulosa labiata* — mainland Africa only (elvers unknown prior to this study)
  - *[[Anguilla marmorata]]* — Madagascar only (elvers known only from Madagascar)
- The paper underscores that **elver records are exceptionally rare** for African *Anguilla* species, limiting understanding of their life-history and distributional ecology in African freshwater systems.

## Key Findings

1. **First-ever elver record for *A. nebulosa labiata***, collected from the Mzinga River/Creek near Dar-es-Salaam — filling a critical gap in life-history knowledge for this Africa-endemic long-finned eel.
2. **First mainland African record of *A. bicolor bicolor* elvers**, and only the second record of this species' elvers from African freshwaters overall (previously known only from Madagascar).
3. Species identification relied on a combination of **morphometric ratios (anal–dorsal distance), vertebral counts, and dorsal fin length**, following the methodology established by [[Vilhelm Ege]] in his 1939 Dana Report monograph on *Anguilla* systematics.
4. Collection circumstances (near a water-supply intake in a tidal creek) illustrate the **incidental nature of elver discovery**, highlighting how routine hydrological or municipal water monitoring can yield significant ichthyological records.
5. The findings collectively **extend known distributional and early-life-stage data for Anguillidae in the Indian Ocean African region**, supporting broader research into catadromous eel migration and spawning biology in African river systems.
