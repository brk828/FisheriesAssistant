# Trophic Changes in a Desert Fish Community Following River Regulation and Species Turnover: Implications for an Endangered Top Predator

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This retrospective study uses stable isotopes (δ13C and δ15N) from museum specimens (1959–1961) and contemporary collections (2003–2014) to assess whether changes in the food web of the [[San Juan River]] limit the reestablishment of the endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], a top predator nearly extirpated by the 1990s. Following [[River Regulation]] by [[Navajo Dam]], four of five community-wide trophic metrics declined, and species turnover had a limited effect per se. Shared trophic resources among core species generally increased (mean niche overlap +35.7%). Colorado Pikeminnow, however, showed a 72% decrease in niche breadth, and mixing models indicated a shift from piscivory toward insectivory. The authors suggest that increased reliance on similar resources may limit fish prey for Colorado Pikeminnow, contributing to slow reestablishment despite [[Hatchery Augmentation]].

## Background & Description

The Colorado Pikeminnow (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) was the historical top predator of the [[Colorado River Basin]] and is federally endangered. Hatchery augmentation, mimicked flow regimes, and intensive removal of large nonnative fishes over about 20 years stocked more than 5 million juveniles, yet the adult population remains small because age-specific survival of stocked fish is low (Clark et al., 2018).

The native community of the San Juan River comprises five large-bodied endemic species: [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Razorback Sucker]] (endangered), [[Roundtail Chub]], and Colorado Pikeminnow, plus two small-bodied natives, [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Mottled Sculpin]]. Nonnative species include [[Channel Catfish]] (the second most abundant large-bodied fish), [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Red Shiner]]. Roundtail Chub is functionally extirpated, and Colorado Pikeminnow and Razorback Sucker persist only through hatchery augmentation.

The study used [[Stable Isotope Analysis]] and [[Bayesian Modeling]] with museum specimens housed at the Museum of Southwestern Biology (University of New Mexico) to detect departures from historical conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The San Juan River, the second largest tributary of the Colorado River, is about 360 km of riverine habitat. [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (1963) inundated its lower reaches under [[Lake Powell]], and Navajo Dam (completed 1962) fragmented the upper river, limited spring peak flows, and elevated summer base flows. Continued deviations from the natural hydrograph, nonnative vegetation, and channel simplification further precluded floodplain inundation. Increased aridity has also hampered flow mimicry efforts.

Reduced basal resource diversity was attributed to loss of upstream inputs, limited floodplain exchange, and habitat simplification. Invertebrate declines after dam construction and reduced densities of invertivorous natives since the 2000s were also noted. Competition, prey limitation, and the absence of Roundtail Chub may compound stressors on the predator.

## Key Findings

- **Community-wide metrics:** Of five metrics, four declined after regulation. For all species, δ13C range fell 32.9%, δ15N range fell 27.2%(core species), centroid distance (CD) fell 26.2%, and nearest neighbor distance (NND) fell 27.9% in the all-species analysis. Standard deviation of NND (SDNND) did not change.
- **Species turnover:** Its effect was limited; the difference between all-species and core-species results for CD and NND was about 4–15%. Most of the reduction was attributed to factors other than turnover (probability 0.92). [[Red Shiner]] establishment likely helped retain food chain length, as it assimilated the lowest δ15N values.
- **Niche overlap:** Of 20 species pairs, 16 showed a probability ≥0.89 of increased overlap. Mean overlap rose 35.7%. Largest increases were for Channel Catfish (+81.1%), Colorado Pikeminnow (+49.5%), and Speckled Dace (+46.3%); Fathead Minnow (+25.4%) and Flannelmouth Sucker (+6.1%) increased less.
- **Niche breadth (SEAc):** Colorado Pikeminnow declined 72% (historical 7.59, 95% CI 3.29–17.04; contemporary 2.12). Speckled Dace declined 33%. Flannelmouth Sucker increased 165% and Fathead Minnow 726% (probability ≥0.99). Historical Colorado Pikeminnow had the largest niche breadth, although its sample size (n = 6) makes estimates potentially low.
- **Diet mixing model:** Invertebrate consumption by Colorado Pikeminnow rose (probability 0.94) from a historical median of 14.7% to a contemporary median of 24.9%. Red Shiner (median 28.4%) replaced Fathead Minnow (29.0%) as the most consumed fish prey in the contemporary period. Roundtail Chub was historically predicted to contribute 17.9% of the diet.
- **Basal resources:** Results suggest an overall reduction in basal resource availability after regulation, consistent with other regulated-river studies.
- **Limitations:** Historical invertebrate isotopes were estimated by assuming a mean isotopic shift, and similar prey signatures produced high model uncertainty. The authors recommend stomach content analysis and bioenergetics modeling.
- **Management implications:** Assessing whether contemporary prey abundance limits Colorado Pikeminnow establishment is an important next step. A shift toward insectivory may reduce growth and survival of stocked fish, and managers should consider how nonnative prey species such as Fathead Minnow and Red Shiner affect resource availability.
