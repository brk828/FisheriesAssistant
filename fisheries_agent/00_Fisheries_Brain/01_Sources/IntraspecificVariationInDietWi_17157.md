# Intraspecific Variation in Diet Within and Between Trophic Morphs in Larval Tiger Salamanders

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ambystomatidae

## Executive Summary

Collins and Holomuzki (1984, *Canadian Journal of Zoology* 62: 168-174) examined diet variation among larval [[Tiger Salamander]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum nebulosum*) trophic morphs collected from four habitats in Arizona. The dimorphism comprises a "broad-headed" (cannibalistic) morph with a wider head and enlarged vomerine teeth, and a "typical" morph. Broad-headed larvae consumed mostly other salamander larvae (mean 84% of diet), whereas typical larvae rarely ate conspecifics (1 of 170 larvae analyzed). Both morphs ate invertebrates. The authors conclude that "cannibalistic" versus "typical" describes a predilection rather than an absolute category. Diet of typical larvae also shifted with age, with older larvae consuming a wider range of macroinvertebrates.

## Background & Description

- **Morphological polymorphism:** Broad-headed morphs have wider heads and enlarged vomerine teeth relative to typical morphs. The polymorphism was first described by [[Powers 1907]] in Nebraska and has since been reported from Oklahoma, Texas, Colorado, and Arizona.
- **Conceptual framing:** The authors position this trophic polymorphism, which is independent of sex, as a useful system for studying how intraspecific ecological variation may be converted into interspecific isolation, a key process in [[Speciation]].
- **Sex and cannibal morphs:** Powers (1907) reported cannibals as almost exclusively male. In Cottonwood Tank, the authors collected eight broad-headed branchiate larvae: six females, one male, and one juvenile, showing that both sexes can develop the cannibalistic morph.
- **Study design:** Larvae were seined from five Arizona populations (Baker Lake, Snow Tank, Big Meadows Tank No. 1, Limestone Tank, Lower Cottonwood Tank). Stomach contents were analyzed for selected individuals, with prey grouped into six categories (salamander larvae, Crustacea, Mollusca, aquatic insects, terrestrial insects, plant matter). Volumes were estimated using the [[Hynes Point System]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitats:** Sites were natural sinkholes, marshes, and artificial tanks, mostly surrounded by [[Ponderosa Pine]] forest, at elevations of roughly 1860 to 2774 m. Macrophytes varied from none (Limestone Tank) to dense stands (Baker Lake, Snow Tank). Populations were described as fishless, with salamander larvae and dytiscid beetles as top predators.
- **Trophic shifts with ontogeny:** Larvae under about 20 mm SVL are primarily planktivores (cladocerans and copepods). Between roughly 35 and 50 mm SVL, a subset of larvae develops the broad-headed form and shifts toward predation on salamanders and benthic insects.
- **Density effects:** Density of conspecifics appears to influence the ratio of typical to cannibalistic morphs. Density of cannibals mainly affects conspecific larvae, while typical morph density affects benthic and pelagic invertebrates and the composition of pelagic crustacean communities.
- **Literature context:** Typical-morph diets in other studies included items not recorded here (planaria, oligochaetes, anuran tadpoles, amphibian eggs). Several studies reported cannibalism by typical larvae, mainly in laboratory settings, and the authors note that conditions favoring field cannibalism by typical morphs may exist.

## Key Findings

1. **Cannibal diet:** Broad-headed larvae consumed mostly salamanders (mean 84% of diet, per the abstract). Remaining prey were invertebrates, especially aquatic insects.
2. **Typical-morph diet:** Typical larvae ate macroinvertebrates and plankton. Salamanders occurred in only 1 of 170 instances. Aquatic insects, planktonic crustaceans, and molluscs were consistently eaten across all four sites.
3. **Within-habitat contrast:** At Snow Tank (site 2) and Limestone Tank (site 4), cannibals ate a large percentage of salamanders, while typical morphs ate only invertebrates. Overlap in mean prey use was small (for example, 20% vs. 1% for molluscs and 58% vs. 5% for aquatic insects at site 2).
4. **Between-habitat cannibal variation:** Cannibals at Snow Tank ate proportionally more salamanders and fewer aquatic insects than those at Limestone Tank. Differences were mostly quantitative. The authors suggest cannibal size as a possible explanation, since site 2 cannibals were larger.
5. **Typical-morph taxa:** Aquatic Diptera were eaten more than any other insect order. Trichoptera were eaten only at Big Meadows No. 1 (site 3), where larvae used a specialized feeding behavior ([[Holomuzki 1983]]).
6. **Age-related diet (site 3):** In Big Meadows No. 1, first-year larvae ate significantly more Cladocera than second-year larvae in June (t = 5.09, 48 df, P < 0.001) but not in July. Older larvae ate a significantly higher proportion of aquatic insects in June (t = 4.01, P < 0.001) and fed on twice as many taxa (14 vs. 7). By July, the two age-classes did not differ significantly in aquatic insect proportion.
7. **Salamander cannibalism in typical morphs:** Only one instance was recorded across 4 months at site 3. The authors conclude that typical morphs rarely prey on larval salamanders in the field, in agreement with [[Dodson and Dodson 1971]].
8. **Conclusion:** The terms "cannibalistic" and "typical" describe dietary predilections within a polymorphic population, not fixed diet categories.
