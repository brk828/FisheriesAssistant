# Relationship Between Yampa River Flows and Rare Fish Reproductive Success in the Green River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 BIO/WEST report, prepared for the [[National Park Service]] to support its reserved water-rights claim in Dinosaur National Monument, tests whether flow from the [[Yampa River]] governs reproductive success of [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the upper [[Green River]]. Using year-class catch data from 1975–1979 and from Vanicek (1967) for 1962–1966, the authors classify each year as good or poor reproduction and compare these outcomes with hydrographs. They conclude that normal spring Yampa flows are strongly correlated with successful squawfish spawning, that reproduction essentially collapsed around 1967–1968 after [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure and cold releases, and that regulation of the Yampa would be seriously damaging to listed large-river fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Listed and proposed species:** The [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] are listed as endangered. The [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] are proposed for listing.
- **Colorado squawfish biology:** Described as America's largest minnow, reportedly reaching about 6 feet and 80–100 pounds. Adults mature at about 7 years and spawn in summer at water temperatures near 70 °F. Young feed first on small crustaceans in quiet backwaters, then shift to insects and, at about 8 inches, almost entirely to fish.
- **Aging method:** Young were aged by length-frequency analysis, mainly for the first three to four age groups (young-of-the-year, yearlings, age II, age III).
- **Study rationale:** Flow reduction from river regulation is hypothesized to alter instream habitat and to impair natural-flow-dependent reproduction. Losses are most documented in the lower Colorado basin, with recent concern about the upper basin.
- **Data sources:** BIO/WEST sampling (1977–1979, Jensen to Ouray) and Vanicek's (1967) study in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (1964–1966) were the only long-term reproductive records (at least five years).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow gauges:** The Jensen gauge measures flow from the Yampa mouth to Ouray; the Greendale gauge measures Green River flow below Flaming Gorge Dam. The Yampa is the only significant inflow between them, so the Jensen–Greendale difference estimates its input.
- **Temperature:** Cold releases from Flaming Gorge Dam chilled the Green River for roughly 100 miles below the dam. The Yampa's natural, warmer water ameliorated this cooling in Dinosaur National Monument, but only until about 1967–1968.
- **Habitat dependence:** Bonytail and razorback sucker were far more abundant in the mainstem Green and Colorado than in tributaries, suggesting that large-river flows maintain habitat for these fishes.
- **Regulatory pressure:** Dam operations and water-use proposals in the basin, including a proposed power plant and Flaming Gorge inlet modifications, form the policy setting.

## Key Findings

- **Year-class success:** BIO/WEST classified year classes 1975, 1976, 1978 and 1979 as good. The 1977 year class was poor; only one possible 1977-class fish (5 inches, May 1979) was found, with 1977 effort high (106 seine hauls in August).
- **Table 1 effort:** 1977 effort was high, 1978 low (28 seine hauls at Jensen), and 1979 very high (163 seine hauls over monthly samples).
- **1977 hydrograph:** The only abnormal year among 1975–1979. A high spring peak was lost because Yampa runoff was very low; Green River water supplied most of the Jensen flow except during May–June.
- **Vanicek data (1964–1966):** Good success in 1962, 1963, 1964 and 1966; poor in 1965, linked to abnormally cold spring temperatures. Revised analysis suggested 1962 was also good, and the 1962 class was reclassified after potential age misassignment.
- **Temperature check:** Days above 60 °F by June 30 were lowest in 1965 (7). In 1977, 15 °C days were intermediate (35), so temperature was judged not the cause of 1977 failure.
- **Post-dam collapse:** Squawfish reproduction essentially collapsed around 1967–1968. Subsequent surveys (Holden 1973; Seethaler et al. 1976; Holden and Crist 1978, 1980) found only a few juveniles or yearlings, and none in some years.
- **Parallel trout decline:** The trophy trout fishery below Flaming Gorge Dam also collapsed around 1967–1968. Holden and Crist (1978) linked both declines to increased Flaming Gorge releases after 1967.
- **Bonytail decline:** Only 36 adults were collected by Holden in four years, and only three possible juveniles from Vanicek's 1964–1966 collections; the species appears to have disappeared from the Green around the early 1970s, with loss of reproduction a probable cause.
- **Dam effects on flow:** Mean monthly flows and temperatures at Greendale for 1951–1962, 1963–1966, and 1967–1977 show that Flaming Gorge Dam greatly altered natural patterns, with 1963–1966 releases relatively low.
- **Conclusion on regulation:** The Yampa is the main flow source to the Green below Echo Park, and tributaries such as the [[Duchesne River]] and White River also help maintain large-river conditions. The report argues that regulating the Yampa would seriously harm squawfish reproduction and would likely negate the power-generating potential of the system.
- **Management implication:** Recent inlet modifications at Flaming Gorge are expected to improve squawfish spawning in Dinosaur National Monument, but Yampa regulation would likely cancel that benefit.
