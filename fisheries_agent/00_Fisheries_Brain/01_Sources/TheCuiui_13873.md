# The Cui-ui: Natural History, Water Conflict, and Recovery of a Endangered Lake Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service account by [[Chester C. Buchanan]] and [[Mark E. Coleman]] (published in the Audubon Wildlife Report, 1987) describes the [[Cui-Ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*), a large, lake-dwelling sucker endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. The cui-ui must enter the [[Truckee River]] to spawn, so its survival depends on river flow, lake level, and water quality. Decades of diversion for the [[Newlands Project]] and upstream users reduced flows, lowered the lake, and formed a delta that blocked spawning access. The species is listed as endangered by the federal government (1967) and by Nevada. The authors conclude that recovery depends on secure water supplies, improved water-delivery efficiency, and a basin-wide water-management plan.

## Background & Description

- **Morphology:** Dorsal side blackish-brown, ventral side creamy-white; plump, coarsely scaled body; may reach 27 inches.
- **Longevity and growth:** Females live up to 42 years; males rarely reach their late twenties. Both sexes grow similarly until maturity at about six to 12 years. Fish are about four inches at age one and 12 inches at age five. After maturity, males grow about a quarter inch per year and females about a half inch per year.
- **Diet:** Larvae and adults feed on [[Zooplankton]] and benthic invertebrates; the species is described as omnivorous.
- **Spawning:** Adults gather near the Truckee River mouth and run upstream from April or May for four to eight weeks, with about 80 percent entering over seven to 14 days. Spawners usually stay in the lower three to six miles (occasionally about 12 miles), spending up to 16 days in the river. Eggs are deposited over gravel in 10 inches to four feet of water with velocities of one to two feet per second. Most fish spawn only once every three or four years.
- **Fecundity:** Roughly 20,000 eggs for a 16-inch female and 105,000 for a 26-inch female. Egg viability declines sharply near 30 years of age.
- **Early life:** Eggs hatch in one to two weeks depending on temperature. Yolk-sac larvae remain in gravel five to 10 days, then drift to the lake, where they stay in the littoral zone before moving to deeper water in late summer. Adults are often found near the bottom at 50 to 100 feet.
- **Taxonomic significance:** The cui-ui is described as the last pure member of the genus *Chasmistes*. Related species, the June sucker (*C. liorus*) and shortnose sucker (*C. brevirostus*), have hybridized with other suckers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Cultural history.** Archaeological evidence indicates use of the lake's fishes for at least 4,000 years. The [[Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe]] (the Kuyuidokado band, whose name means "cui-ui eaters") fished the Truckee River mouth and fed on cui-ui and [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] during spawning runs. Subsistence fishing continued after reservation restrictions in the 1850s until population decline ended it.

**Lake history.** Pyramid Lake (about 180 square miles) is a remnant of [[Lake Lahontan]], which covered about 8,700 square miles from roughly 9,000 to 40,000 years ago. Before regulation, average annual inflow was about 600,000 acre-feet, plus 50,000 acre-feet of precipitation. Current flow is about halved. Pyramid Lake fell 85 feet in 60 years, reaching its lowest level in nearly 4,000 years, and [[Winnemucca Lake]] dried by 1938.

**River and delta changes.** By 1926, a delta across the Truckee mouth stranded fish in shallow channels. Historically, cui-ui ascended nearly 25 miles in the 1800s; today they commonly go no further than six to 12 miles. Historic run sizes are unknown, though early reports suggest hundreds of thousands of fish.

**Water management.** The Orr Ditch Decree (1944), the [[Newlands Project]] (diversions since 1907 from Derby Dam), upstream diversions, and drought together drove the decline. Truckee Meadows districts over-diverted in normal years, degrading water quality and timing. The [[Stampede Reservoir]] on the Little Truckee River (operational 1970) became the cui-ui's only assured supply, supported by court rulings in 1982 and by the [[Marble Bluff Fish Facility]] (1976), which provides fish passage around the delta.

**Water quality.** Clean, cool water is essential. Nutrients from urban growth accumulate in Pyramid Lake and may alter conditions in harmful ways. Maximum daily temperatures above 63 degrees Fahrenheit cause high embryo mortality.

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** From 1950 to 1980 only two major year-classes (1950 and 1969) were produced. The 1969 class (about 100,000 individuals) dominates the adult population, while 1950-class fish are reproductively impotent.
- **Recent recruitment:** Six successful spawning runs occurred after 1980, averaging 13,600 fish (range 5,000 to 36,300). Hundreds of millions of larvae were produced, but a lack of statistics prevents assessing whether these year-classes can sustain the population.
- **Water storage gap:** Stampede Reservoir has 226,000 acre-feet of capacity, but average usable storage is about 50,000 acre-feet per year. Maintaining Pyramid Lake requires about 400,000 acre-feet, including at least 200,000 acre-feet in spring.
- **Delivery efficiency:** Newlands Project efficiency averaged 55 percent in 1985. The Bureau of Reclamation targets 63 percent by 1991, but the authors state that 80 to 85 percent would be needed to benefit the fishery.
- **Hatchery support:** The [[David Koch Cui-ui Hatchery]], turned over to the tribe in 1978, released 7.7 to 11.7 million larvae per year from 1982 to 1986.
- **Competing demands:** Sierra Pacific Power Company proposed using about 50 percent of Stampede storage; Washoe County applied for 9,000 cubic feet per second, far above normal-year flows. The Reno/Sparks area may face drought shortages by 1992.
- **Legal rights:** The tribe seeks a [[Winters Doctrine]] reserved right to remaining unappropriated Truckee water. A U.S. Supreme Court ruling barred reopening the Orr Ditch case.
- **Management priorities:** The federal recovery plan (approved 1978) aims for a self-sustaining population. Actions include improved delivery efficiency, operating criteria favoring cui-ui, securing unappropriated water and Stampede storage, retiring marginal Newlands farmland, and modifying Floriston rates.
- **Conclusion:** Recovery depends on a consistent clean water supply, and the authors call for a sound basin-wide water-management plan that distributes benefits equitably.
