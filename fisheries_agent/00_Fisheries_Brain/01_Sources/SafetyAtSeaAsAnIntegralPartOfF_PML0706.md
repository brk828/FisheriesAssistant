# Safety at Sea as an Integral Part of Fisheries Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Circular (No. 966, Rome, 2001, 39 p.) by [[Gudrun Petursdottir]], [[Olafur Hannibalsson]] and [[Jeremy Turner]] argues that fishermen's safety should be built into the general management of fisheries in every country rather than treated as a separate, largely voluntary maritime matter. The authors document that fishing remains among the most dangerous occupations, that injuries are under-reported, and that most international safety instruments exclude small fishing vessels. They conclude that linking safety requirements to fishing authorizations, backed by obligatory training and enforcement, offers the most realistic route to improvement, and they propose that [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] (FAO) adopt a holistic management approach for developing countries.

## Background & Description

The report was commissioned by the Fishery Industries Division of FAO to give a global review of fishermen's safety and to identify opportunities, constraints and priorities for FAO and national administrations. The authors note that fisheries managers focus on long-term conservation of stocks, while maritime administrations prioritize seafarers' safety, and that fishing vessels are excluded from most provisions of international shipping conventions. The text is organized into five main parts: dimensions of the problem; international conventions and guidelines (see [[International Maritime Organization]], [[International Labour Organization]] and [[Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries]]); safety training; fisheries management and safety; and safety in developing countries.

### Fleet and workforce scale

- About 15 million fishers work aboard decked or undecked vessels in marine capture fisheries, most on vessels under 24 m; including part-time workers, freshwater fishers and aquaculture, the total rises to about 36 million.
- The world fleet numbered about 3.8 million vessels in 1995. About one-third were decked; the remaining two-thirds were undecked, generally under 10 m.
- Decked vessels rose from about 600,000 in 1970 to 1,260,000 in 1995.
- Artisanal craft are probably over 2.5 million in number and are estimated to take about half of yearly capture fisheries for human consumption.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame fisheries as an interconnected global biosystem. Overfishing can collapse linked species, destruction of breeding grounds affects distant fisheries, and disruption of migratory routes has effects across coastal zones. The sea is described as a sensitive, limited resource requiring administration by all who exploit it. The [[UN Convention on the Law of the Sea]] gives coastal states rights and responsibilities over their [[Exclusive Economic Zone]]s, which the Conclusions state embrace some 90% of the world's marine fisheries. Many developing countries have fisheries that operate with few harbours, dispersed landings and beach launches, which complicates both resource and safety control.

## Key Findings

### 1. Fishing is among the most dangerous occupations
- Australia (fatality rate 18 times the national average; 143 vs. 8.1 per 100,000 person-years).
- Denmark (1989–1996): 25–30 times the land-based rate.
- United States (1996): about eight times the rate for motor vehicle operators and 1.6 times that of fire-fighting and police work.
- 1997 comparative multiples of national occupational fatality rates: Republic of Korea 15×, Italy 21×, Estonia 11×, Lithuania 11×, Poland 9×, Spain 6×, Canada 3.5×.
- The [[International Labour Organization]] estimates about 24,000 fishing fatalities worldwide per year.
- Nordic countries: 90–150 fatalities per 100,000 despite some of the best prevention and rescue services.
- Developing countries report much higher rates: about 500 per 100,000 in Guinea canoe fishing (1991–94); 300–1,000 per 100,000 for West African artisanal canoes; 585 per 100,000 in South Africa.

### 2. Injuries are grossly under-reported
- An Icelandic pilot study found that 80% of fishermen had either been injured or witnessed a shipmate being injured.
- Records are inconsistent across countries; the [[International Maritime Organization]] acknowledged in 1999 very limited response to its call for annual casualty statistics.
- Fishing is often lumped with agriculture, hunting and forestry in official statistics.

### 3. Accidents arise from layered causes
- The most common fatal events are vessel loss, man overboard and being crushed by heavy objects.
- Non-fatal injuries most often involve fractures, head and neck injuries, and amputations.
- [[Reason's Model]] (Swiss-cheese model) shows that accidents occur when latent defects across regulators, owners, line management and crews align. The report's example, a trawler accident involving an untrained new entrant, illustrates the point.
- In Iceland, fatal accidents fell over time, but non-fatal injuries did not; fishermen with more than ten years on the job faced threefold fatal-accident risk.

### 4. Existing international instruments leave most vessels uncovered
- [[SOLAS Convention]] (first adopted 1914; amended 1929 and 1948; revised 1960) excludes fishing vessels apart from Chapter V, wooden ships and non-mechanically propelled vessels.
- [[Torremolinos Convention]] (1977) was never brought into force; a 1993 Protocol raised the vessel threshold from 24 m to 45 m. It required ratification by at least 15 states whose 24 m-plus fishing fleets total 14,000 vessels; only six states had ratified it.
- [[STCW-F Convention]] (1995) covered vessels of 24 m and over; two countries had ratified it by May 2000.
- Voluntary guidelines (1980) cover vessels between 12 m and 24 m.
- The [[ILO]] has seven fisher-specific instruments (five conventions and two recommendations).
- The report concludes that regulations are not the main gap: "what is missing is their effective enforcement at national level".

### 5. Voluntary safety training has failed
- Fishermen's reluctance to attend courses is a serious concern; in a Canadian study, fear of injury ranked near the bottom of offshore fishermen's worries, behind fish stock depletion and loss of work.
- Speakers at the 1989 Rimouski symposium agreed that voluntary courses would be futile unless fishermen were compelled.
- Countries that made training obligatory typically require courses of about 20 to 40 hours, using hands-on drills on training vessels.
- In Iceland, the [[Icelandic Association for Search and Rescue]] runs the official obligatory 40-hour course for vessels over 12 m.

### 6. Effect of obligatory training
- Norway and Iceland recorded roughly half as many fishermen fatalities in 1990s than in the preceding five-year period; Denmark showed a similar but less marked trend.
- The authors caution that causation cannot be proven: the study periods are short, man-hour data are unavailable, and other factors such as better technology and rescue services also reduce losses.
- Cost-benefit: the 1997 cost of accidents at sea in Iceland was estimated at US$45–60 million (0.6–0.8% of GDP), while safety training for 1,112 fishermen cost about US$650,000, roughly 1% of accident costs (figures as scanned).

### 7. Fisheries management systems affect safety
- Open access encourages speed and overcapacity, pushing crews toward risk.
- Limits on vessel size prompt owners to alter designs, sometimes reducing stability and seaworthiness; smaller vessels pushed further offshore face higher danger.
- A [[Total Allowable Catch]] without further controls creates "derby" or "Olympic" fisheries.
- Restricted days at sea can stop safe planning around weather.
- [[Individual Quotas]] can reduce time pressure, but [[Individual Transferable Quotas]] reintroduce performance pressure and raise financial pressure on owners.
- Catch-share remuneration spreads risk but encourages long hours, fatigue, sailing in poor weather and overloading. Crews often cannot effectively refuse unsafe work.

### 8. Namibia as a management success
- Namibian hake stocks were severely overfished before independence in 1990.
- Five years after taking control, Namibia had created 6,000 new jobs, doubled wage employment in the fishery, tripled foreign-exchange earnings, and generated tax revenue three times the Fisheries Ministry budget (see [[Namibian Hake Fishery]]).

### 9. Developing-country challenges
- Small, mostly unmotorized craft (about 80% of African undecked vessels are unpowered) operate from beaches, far from administrations.
- Insurers and banks are reluctant to cover or lend against fishing vessels; mutual insurance through cooperatives is proposed.
- Motorization has enabled distant trips in vessels designed for inshore use; in 1998 about 1,100 small decked boats (9–13 m) ventured as far as Somalia's coast for up to a month.
- Basic equipment is often missing: VHF radios need battery power that outboards may not generate; GMDSS shifts distress alerting to satellite systems that small fishermen cannot afford.

### 10. Recommended integrated approach
- Link safety requirements to fishing authorizations so that "to fish legally will be to fish safely."
- Integrate safety into fisheries management from the start in new management regimes; retrofit existing regimes in others.
- Use the [[Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries]] as a framework: it addresses safety in at least eight paragraphs (6.17, 8.1.5–8.1.8, 8.2.5, 8.3.2, 8.4.1), and 60% compliance was reported in 2000.
- Combine [[Monitoring, Control and Surveillance]], [[Search and Rescue]] and safety at sea into shared services. A West African proposal (Box 6) links artisanal radio-boats, shore radar, and joint naval and fisheries patrols, where artisanal fatality rates are about ten times those of developed countries.
- Use volunteer organizations, such as the Norwegian Society for Sea Rescue (served 30,000 fishermen; reportedly saves 30–40 lives per year), to lower SAR costs.
- Maintain vessel registries with marked hulls to support licensing, inspection, statistics and SAR.
