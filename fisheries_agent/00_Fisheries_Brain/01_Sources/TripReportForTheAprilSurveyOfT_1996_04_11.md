## Executive Summary

This trip report documents a fish community survey conducted on 4–5 April 1996 at the Arizona State University (ASU) Research Park in Tempe, Arizona — a series of artificial ponds functioning as a refuge population site for native and endangered Colorado River basin fishes, notably the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Using seven entanglement nets (experimental gill nets and trammel nets) deployed across three ponds (A, B, C), researchers captured 780 individuals representing 7 species. The catch was dominated by non-native [[Threadfin Shad]] (86%) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (7%), while target endangered natives were captured in low numbers, with only a single razorback sucker recorded and no bonytail chub or Colorado squawfish detected. All captured fish were released alive.

## Background & Description

- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): One individual captured — a **tuberculate male**, total length (TL) = 530 mm. The specimen appeared healthy, with no external signs of fungus, scarring, or tumors. The presence of nuptial tubercles indicates the fish was in reproductive condition at time of capture.
- No morphological data were reported for other species; catch composition was recorded by species identity and relative abundance only.
- Net types used: 125 ft experimental gill nets and 150–300 ft trammel nets, varying in configuration between ponds (Pond C received two 300 ft trammel nets plus a 125 ft experimental gill net, indicating heavier gear deployment there).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The ASU Research Park ponds function as an **ex-situ refuge habitat** for imperiled Colorado River basin fishes, allowing persistence of species such as the razorback sucker and bonytail chub outside their native riverine range.
- The pond system supports a mixed assemblage of **native endangered catostomids/cyprinids** alongside **naturalized non-native species** (threadfin shad, largemouth bass, grass carp, bluegill, green sunfish, blue tilapia), reflecting a common conservation trade-off in refuge ponds where invasive/game species can dominate community biomass.
- [[Threadfin Shad]] overwhelmingly dominated the catch (86%), suggesting a forage-fish-rich environment likely supporting predatory species like largemouth bass.
- The apparent **decline or absence of [[Colorado Squawfish]]** since March 1988 (with only anecdotal angler catches through 1992) suggests this species may have been extirpated from the pond system, possibly due to competition, predation, or lack of recruitment in the artificial habitat.
- Reduced razorback sucker catch this trip was attributed partly to reduced sampling effort in Pond C, where a net had been illegally pulled by an unauthorized person shortly after deployment — highlighting a human interference pressure on monitoring efficacy.

## Key Findings

- **Total catch:** 780 individuals across 7 species (all previously recorded in past park surveys).
- **Dominant species:** Threadfin shad (86%) and largemouth bass (7%).
- **Minor contributors (≤3% each):** Grass carp, razorback sucker, bluegill, green sunfish, blue tilapia.
- **Razorback sucker:** Only 1 individual captured (vs. typical 4–6 in recent surveys); healthy tuberculate male, TL = 530 mm.
- **Bonytail chub:** Zero captured; typically 1–2 individuals expected per spring survey — notable absence.
- **Colorado squawfish:** Zero captured; species has been absent from collections since March 1988, with only sporadic angler-reported catches (hook-and-line) between 1989–1992, suggesting long-term decline or loss from the system.
- **Human interference:** An unauthorized individual disturbed a net set in Pond C, reducing effective sampling effort and likely catch of target species there.
- **Disposition:** All captured fish were released back into the ponds.
- **Next steps:** A follow-up survey was tentatively planned for autumn 1996, continuing the long-term monitoring effort led by [[Paul Marsh]] and the ASU Center for Environmental Studies.
