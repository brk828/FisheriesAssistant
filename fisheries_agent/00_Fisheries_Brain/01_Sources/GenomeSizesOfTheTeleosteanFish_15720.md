## Executive Summary

This 1971 NOAA technical report by Wesley J. Ebel documents dissolved nitrogen gas supersaturation in the Columbia and Snake Rivers during 1970 and its lethal and sublethal effects on juvenile and adult [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) and [[Steelhead Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). Supersaturation was driven primarily by spillway operations at hydroelectric dams, particularly [[Little Goose Dam]], which entrains atmospheric gas into water plunging into tailraces at depth, elevating dissolved nitrogen beyond equilibrium saturation. While Columbia River nitrogen levels were generally lower in 1970 than in 1968–69, the Snake River experienced acute supersaturation events (up to 146% saturation) linked directly to spill volume. These conditions produced widespread [[Gas Bubble Disease]] and substantial mortality in caged and free-migrating juvenile salmonids, with independent population studies estimating up to 70–90% losses of migrating juveniles between sampling points, and up to 45% mortality in pre-spawning adult chinook.

## Background & Description

This report is physiological/pathological rather than morphometric, focusing on external and internal symptoms of **[[Gas Bubble Disease]]** in salmonids caused by dissolved nitrogen supersaturation:

- **Gas Bubble Disease (GBD) symptoms**: External bubble formation (emphysema) observed on fish examined from gatewell bypass traps and hatchery holding facilities; symptoms assessed visually on live and dead specimens.
- **Species examined**:
  - Juvenile and adult **[[Chinook Salmon]]** — wild (native) spring and fall runs, and hatchery-origin fall chinook (Wahkeena Pond stock).
  - Juvenile and adult **[[Steelhead Trout]]** — Dworshak Hatchery origin.
- **Symptom prevalence** (from cage trials at Ice Harbor Dam forebay):
  - Surface-held fish: up to 100% mortality within 7 days; up to 100% of survivors exhibiting GBD symptoms.
  - Deep-cage (3.0–4.0 m) "control" fish: mortality reduced to 2–38%, reflecting partial compensation by hydrostatic pressure.
  - Free-swimming (0–4.5 m) cages, considered most representative of natural migrant behavior: 45–68% mortality from late May to early July.
- **Gatewell-trapped migrants** at Ice Harbor Dam: 25–45% of chinook salmon and 30–58% of steelhead trout showed external GBD symptoms.
- **Adult chinook** at Rapid River Hatchery: ~30% of fish arriving after 6 July showed external GBD symptoms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Anadromous salmonids migrating through a heavily regulated river system with multiple mainstem hydroelectric dams (Little Goose, Lower Monumental, Ice Harbor, McNary, John Day, The Dalles, Bonneville) along the [[Columbia River]] and [[Snake River]].
- **Study extent**: Lower 200 km of the Snake River and lower 640 km of the Columbia River, sampled semimonthly (7 April–18 August 1970) at 21 stations spanning dam forebays and tailraces to Astoria, Oregon.
- **Environmental driver**: Water spilled over dam spillways plunges to depth in tailraces, dissolving atmospheric gases (primarily nitrogen) at pressures exceeding surface atmospheric pressure, creating supersaturation that persists downstream. This is exacerbated during high-flow periods requiring spill (e.g., ~1,981 cms at Little Goose Dam on 7 July 1970).
- **Exposure pathway**: Juvenile salmonids migrating downstream through dam forebays/tailraces and adults migrating upstream through the same supersaturated reaches are both exposed, with cumulative/delayed physiological effects (embolism, tissue damage) contributing to pre-spawning mortality in adults.
- **Interaction with [[Dissolved Oxygen]] and water temperature**: Nitrogen supersaturation was tracked alongside dissolved oxygen percent saturation and water temperature at each station, indicating a coupled water-chemistry monitoring approach relevant to the broader **[[River Water Chemistry]]** context of dam-regulated systems.

## Key Findings

- **Regional contrast**: Columbia River dissolved nitrogen levels in 1970 were generally *lower* than in 1968–69 (e.g., McNary–Bonneville reach in early May: 96–107% in 1970 vs. 100–183% in 1968 and 128–144% in 1969), except during peak flow in early June.
- **Snake River hotspot**: Supersaturation was already at 129% below Little Goose Dam on the first survey (7 April 1970) and rose to a peak of 146% of saturation in the Lower Monumental Dam forebay by 8 June 1970, closely tracking spill volume at Little Goose Dam.
- **Spill reduction effect**: Reducing spill at Little Goose Dam from ~1,981 cms (70,000 cfs) on 7 July to 368 cms (18,000 cfs) on 21 July produced a marked drop in nitrogen supersaturation; by 18 August levels had returned to near 100% (normal).
- **Cage mortality experiments** (Ice Harbor Dam forebay, May–July 1970) consistently showed:
  - Surface-held juvenile chinook: 82–100% mortality within 24–48 hours under ~127–134% nitrogen saturation.
  - Deep "control" cages (3.0–4.0 m): substantially reduced mortality (2–45%), demonstrating hydrostatic compensation.
  - Free-ranging depth cages (0–4.5 m), the best proxy for wild migrant behavior: 45–68% mortality.
- **Population-level estimates** (Raymond 1971, unpublished): ~70% loss of migrating juvenile chinook salmon between the Salmon River (Whitebird, Idaho) and Ice Harbor Dam; steelhead from Dworshak Hatchery showed ~15% loss to Ice Harbor Dam and ~90% loss by McNary Dam.
- **Adult mortality**: 27 dead adult chinook salmon counted between Little Goose and Lower Monumental Dams on 7 July 1970, with GBD symptoms confirmed on surviving decomposed specimens; a companion study (Mallet et al. 1971) estimated 45% of adult spring chinook in the Snake River died before spawning due to delayed effects of nitrogen supersaturation.
- **Causal link established**: The correspondence between spill-driven nitrogen supersaturation and both immediate (juvenile) and delayed (adult) salmonid mortality provided key empirical support for U.S. Army Corps of Engineers efforts to redesign spillways and modify spill operations to mitigate **[[Gas Bubble Disease]]** impacts on Columbia–Snake River salmonid populations.
