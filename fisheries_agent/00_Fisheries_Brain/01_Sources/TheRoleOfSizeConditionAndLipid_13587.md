# Size, Condition and Lipid Content in the Overwinter Survival of Age-0 Colorado Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado State University master's thesis (Jay M. Thompson, Fall 1989, Department of Fishery and Wildlife Biology) tests how body size, condition factor and lipid reserves affect first-winter survival of hatchery-reared age-0 [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), an endangered [[Cyprinidae]] of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Three size classes were held for 210 days at simulated winter temperatures (3-4 °C) under three feeding regimes. Survival of starved small and medium fish was significantly lower than that of fed fish, and dying fish had lipid levels of only 3-6% dry weight. The author concludes that overwinter survival depends on both size and feeding, and that wild fish probably feed during winter.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is a large piscivorous cyprinid native to the Colorado River Basin. It was listed as endangered in 1973 and now survives only in the northern part of its historical range.
- **Declines:** Causes are attributed to dam construction, introduction of non-native species and poor land-use practices.
- **Early-life problem:** Small age-0 fish entering winter may limit recruitment to the adult stock. Late spawning (triggered at 20-22 °C) leaves age-0 fish a short growing season, and growth stops below about 13 °C.
- **Prior knowledge gap:** Studies of overwinter survival of age-0 Colorado squawfish were described as "conspicuously lacking." [[Osmundson (1987)]] found no size-selective mortality, but his fish were already about 54 mm TL at winter onset, larger than typical wild fish.
- **Preliminary study:** In 1988 the author held 331 hatchery-reared fish (39-87 mm TL) for 150 days and recorded 100% survival, but most fish were too large to be representative.

### Study objectives
1. Determine the influence of size, condition factor and lipid content on overwinter survival over 210 days.
2. Compare size, condition and lipid content of wild and hatchery-reared fish before and after winter.
3. Compare condition, lipid content and survival across feeding regimes (dry trout pellets, brine shrimp, starved).
4. Estimate the lipid level fish must maintain to survive overwinter.

### Methods overview
- Fish hatched June 1988 at Dexter NFH (New Mexico), reared at Willow Beach NFH (Arizona). Mean arrival lengths: small 30.1 mm, medium 35.7 mm, large 44.0 mm.
- Eighteen aquaria in an environmental chamber, held in a closed recirculating system with a shared [[Biological Filter]] and UV sterilizer to avoid treatment effects.
- Water temperature maintained at 3.5 ± 1.5 °C; photoperiod 10L:14D.
- Feeding: pellets twice weekly at 2% of biomass (9 aquaria); starved (6 aquaria); live [[Artemia]] ad libitum daily (3 aquaria).
- Condition factor K = (W × 10^5)/L^3 (weight in g, length in mm), following [[Weatherley and Gill (1987)]].
- Lipid extraction by [[Soxhlet Extraction]] with diethyl ether; fish were pooled (about 11 small, 8 medium or 4 large per sample).
- Statistics: one-way ANOVA with [[Tukey HSD Test]] for comparisons over time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Latitude gradient:** Winters lengthen and growing seasons shorten moving north, so northern temperate populations often suffer high age-0 overwinter mortality.
- **Mortality mechanisms cited:** hypothermia, predation, flood displacement, lipid depletion leading to starvation, and ice damage (anchor and frazil ice).
- **Size and energetics:** Weight-specific metabolism rises as fish get smaller, so small fish burn energy faster. Positive size-lipid relations have been shown in yellow perch, smallmouth bass and sand smelt.
- **Feeding:** Many temperate warmwater fishes restrict feeding at low temperatures. Smallmouth young stop feeding below 10 °C, but captive Colorado squawfish fed readily at 4 °C.
- **Water quality in aquaria:** Nitrite spiked to 6.1 mg/l on 23 August 1988 after biological filter failure, causing high medium-fish mortality. Salt used to treat [[Ichthyobodo]] raised sodium levels (382 mg/l in January 1989).
- **Wild comparison fish:** Wild fish from the Colorado River near Moab and Grand Junction were used for condition and lipid comparisons.

## Key Findings

- **Growth:** Fish grew little at winter temperatures. Length changes were slight, although some were statistically significant.
- **Condition:** Condition factor fell significantly for small and large fish between day 0 and day 210. It declined faster in starved fish than in fed fish. Mean condition of dying fish was 0.36.
- **Wild fish condition:** Wild fish condition fell from 0.75 ± 0.15 (October 1988, n = 30) to 0.60 ± 0.07 (March 1989, n = 30), still well above 0.36.
- **Lipid depletion:** Lipid content was inversely related to winter duration in all size classes and feeding regimes. Starved small and medium fish lost lipid faster than fed counterparts.
- **Lethal threshold:** Dying fish had lipid levels of 3-6% dry weight, significantly lower than surviving fish. This suggests death results from energy exhaustion.
- **Survival by feeding regime:**
  - Small starved fish: 2 of 61 survived to day 210 (14 removed for lipid analysis on day 70).
  - Small pellet-fed fish: 5 of 103 had died by day 210.
  - Small brine-shrimp-fed fish: 33 of 40 survived to day 210.
  - Medium starved fish: 6 of 90 survived to day 210.
  - Medium pellet-fed fish: 2 of 125 died.
  - Medium brine-shrimp-fed fish: 31 of 32 survived.
  - All large pellet-fed and starved fish survived to day 210. Large brine-shrimp fish suffered Ichthyobodo mortality in December.
- **Lipid levels:** Hatchery fish averaged 28.9% lipid dry weight before winter (26-59 mm TL, n = 180). Wild fish had 14.9% in October 1988 (n = 30) and 13.6% in March 1989 (n = 30). The lipid difference between wild and hatchery fish was not significant over winter in wild fish.
- **Feeding behaviour:** Feeding was inversely related to fish size. All size classes fed after an initial 2-3 week acclimation period. Wild stomach analysis showed 83% (15 of 18) of November 1987 [[Green River]] age-0 fish with food.
- **Body water:** Lipid content = 220.79 − 2.54 (body water %), r² = 0.92. Lipid fell as body water rose.
- **Length and survival:** Length was positively but weakly correlated with time to starvation (r² = 0.40). Piscivory is likely to begin at about 30-50 mm TL.
- **Conceptual model (Figure 5):** Four hypothetical size-starvation curves were presented (lines A, B, C and curve D). The author believes curve D, non-linear with a piscivory threshold near 50 mm, is the most likely relation in wild populations.
- **Latitude effect:** Green and Lower Colorado River fish are larger, so they may follow curve D or line B. Upper Colorado River fish are smaller and may follow lines A or C.
- **Live cage trials:** The 1988 river cages silted up and all fish died. In 1988-89 river cages again lost all fish. Pond cages retained 15 large fish, whose mean condition fell from 0.72 to 0.62 with no change in length.
- **Recommendations:** Simulate gradual autumn and spring temperature changes, analyse protein and ash (complete proximate analysis), collect wild fish in December and March across the Upper Basin, use floating or mid-water live cages, and use a lipid extraction method that needs less tissue.
