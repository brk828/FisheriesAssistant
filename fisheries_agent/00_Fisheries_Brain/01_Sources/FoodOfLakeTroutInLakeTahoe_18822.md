# Food of Lake Trout in Lake Tahoe

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 study by [[Ted C. Frantz]] and [[Almo J. Cordone]] (Nevada and California Departments of Fish and Game) describes the diet of [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*) in [[Lake Tahoe]]. Stomachs from 1,389 fish were examined, 1,097 of which contained food. Diet shifted from invertebrates to fish as trout grew. The authors concluded that trout under about 15 inches lacked suitable forage, and that the population in this size range was more planktivorous than piscivorous. This finding motivated the introduction of [[Mysis Relicta]] and [[Bonneville Cisco]] to supply summer forage in the deep, cold water that trout occupy.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Lake Trout]], locally called "mackinaw," was first confirmed in Lake Tahoe in 1889 by the Nevada Fish Commission. Earlier plants may have occurred from 1886, and by 1923 the species was considered plentiful. The Tahoe sport fishery is now dominated by lake trout.
- **Study context:** The work was part of the cooperative [[Lake Tahoe Fisheries Study]] (begun 1960) under Dingell-Johnson Projects Nevada F-15-R and California F-21-R. Its purpose was to determine whether new forage organisms were needed to supplement lake trout diet.
- **Prior work:** [[Miller 1951]] and [[Corlett and Wood 1958]] provided earlier food habit data.
- **Sampling (January 1962 to September 1964):**
  - Angler-caught fish (52%), mainly from a lakewide boat creel census and the Cave Rock Public Boat Landing.
  - Bottom gill nets set at 25 to 900 ft (44%).
  - Bottom trawls (4%).
- **Methods:** Stomachs were preserved in 10% formalin. Prey were counted and weighed. Unidentified fish were identified from vertebrae stained with Alizarin Red. Lake trout ranged from 1.5 to 33.2 inches FL.
- **Size classes:** under 5.0, 5.0–9.9, 10.0–14.9, 15.0–19.9, and over 19.9 inches FL. Seasons were winter (Jan–Mar), spring (Apr–Jun), summer (Jul–Sep), and autumn (Oct–Dec).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Empty stomachs:** The proportion of empty stomachs rose with length, from 6.7% (under 5.0 inches) to 30.0% (over 19.9 inches). Overall, 78.0% of stomachs contained food.
- **Vertical distribution:** In summer most lake trout occupy the hypolimnion near the bottom, while most potential forage fishes live in the littoral epilimnion. Overlap exists, since some trout are taken in shallow water, and sculpins are abundant at 200 ft and common at 400 ft.
- **Growth:** Lake Tahoe lake trout grow relatively slowly (Hanson and Cordone, 1967).
- **Comparative evidence:** Studies elsewhere link forage fish to trout performance. In Lake Superior, juvenile lake trout fed mainly on [[Mysis Relicta]]. In a Wisconsin lake, survival of stocked young trout tripled in an abundant Mysis year. Lake Minnewanka suffered poor growth and survival where forage fish were scarce. In Algonquin Park lakes, planktivorous trout grew more slowly and matured smaller than piscivorous trout.

## Key Findings

**Diet by size class**

- **Under 5.0 inches:** Cladocerans (mainly *Daphnia pulex*) and copepods (mostly *Epischura nevadensis*) made up over 90% of stomach contents by weight and frequency. No fish were eaten.
- **5.0–9.9 inches:** Fish were in 28.9% of stomachs and gave 56.0% of weight. Only [[Piute Sculpin]] was eaten. Cladocerans were most frequent (52.6%).
- **10.0–14.9 inches:** Cladocerans occurred in 66% of stomachs and fish in 43%, with fish providing more weight. Sculpins dominated, with up to nine per stomach. Crayfish appeared for the first time.
- **15.0–19.9 inches:** Fish occurred in 65.1% of stomachs and made up 84.7% of weight. Sculpins were most important (mean 2.9 per stomach containing them; one stomach held 40). Suckers and chubs were prominent, and crayfish were the third most important food.
- **Over 19.9 inches:** Fish exceeded 90% frequency. [[Tahoe Sucker]] was the major food item, and one 23.7-inch trout had eaten 31 sculpins. Planted trout were also eaten heavily, including 74 cutthroat from eight stomachs.

**Seasonal patterns**

- Fish feeding peaked in spring, when sculpins were most important across all sizes, and was lowest in summer.
- Mountain whitefish were most prevalent in autumn and winter, which may reflect availability linked to November–December spawning.
- Crayfish peaked in summer (21.3% of stomachs in the 15.0–19.9-inch group).

**Size of prey**

- Larger trout ate larger prey. Suckers and whitefish 9 to 11 inches long were found in 20- to 25-inch trout.
- The smallest trout with fish was 5.2 inches, and it had eaten a 1.3-inch sculpin.

**Comparison with earlier studies**

- In all three studies (present, [[Corlett and Wood 1958]], [[Miller 1951]]), fish were the dominant food, with sculpins the most common fish in the present study.
- Differences in rank order were not fully explained. Possible causes include sampling design, stomach-analysis methods, and annual variation in prey abundance.

**Conclusions and management implications**

- Trout under about 15 inches lack suitable forage and are more planktivorous than piscivorous.
- The authors recommended augmenting the summer forage supply in deep, cold water. This led to the introduction of [[Mysis Relicta]] and [[Bonneville Cisco]]. Mysis was established in Lake Tahoe, but its use by lake trout had not yet been measured, and the cisco's status was unknown.
