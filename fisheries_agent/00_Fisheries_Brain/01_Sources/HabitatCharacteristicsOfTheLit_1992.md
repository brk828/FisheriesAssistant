## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) technical report evaluates whether two designated study areas on the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) — the **Powell Camp reach** (river km 2.5–3.5) and the **Salt Camp reach** (river km 10.5–11.5) — are representative of overall in-stream habitat conditions along the perennial reach of the LCR (confluence to Blue Springs, km 0–21). Using systematic transect-based habitat mapping (depth, current, substrate) and Schoener's percent similarity (PS) index, the authors found that both study areas closely resemble adjacent river segments and the LCR as a whole (mean combined similarity ~0.73–0.74, within 1 SD of the river-wide mean of 0.75). The confluence zone (km 0), backwatered by the mainstem [[Colorado River]], and the "Atomizer reach" (km 13–16) emerged as habitat outliers. The study concludes that the USFWS study areas are adequate for characterizing microhabitat use patterns of [[Humpback Chub]] and other native fishes, and no relocation of study boundaries is warranted.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose**: Support the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) Phase II Aquatic Coordination Team by validating USFWS study area selection for a broader research plan (Gorman et al. 1992) assessing microhabitat use by [[Humpback Chub]] and other native fishes in the LCR.
- **Study Area**: The Little Colorado River, a major tributary of the Colorado River in the Grand Canyon region, sampled from its confluence with the Colorado River (km 0) upstream to **Blue Springs** (km 21), the point source of perennial base flow for the lower LCR.
- **Target Species**: Primarily [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), a federally protected native cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], along with other unspecified native fish species inhabiting the LCR.
- **Sampling Design**:
  - Conducted July–August 1991.
  - Transects spaced every 100 m along the river channel.
  - Habitat point-sampling every 1 m along each transect (depth, current, substrate).
  - Total: **6,809 points sampled across 201 transects**.
  - Methodology followed [[Gorman & Karr 1978]] and [[Gorman 1988]] habitat classification schemes.
- **Study Area Sample Sizes**:
  - Powell Camp reach (km 2.5–3.5): 347 points
  - Salt Camp reach (km 10.5–11.5): 319 points
  - Confluence (km 0): 110 points
  - Blue Springs (km 21): 101 points
  - Interior km segments (km 1–20): 235–407 points each

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat Dimensions Measured**:
  - **Depth**: 21 categories in 10 cm increments (0–200 cm, plus >200 cm).
  - **Current**: 6 categories from 0 (no current) to 5 (torrent, >1.2 m/s); modal current category = 3 (moderate, 0.3–0.7 m/s).
  - **Substrate**: 11-category alluvial series from 0 (silt) to 10 (bedrock/travertine); dominant substrates were **sand (category 2)** and **travertine (category 10)**.
- **Confluence Zone (km 0) Anomaly**: Strongly influenced by backwatering from the mainstem [[Colorado River]], producing:
  - Slower current
  - Greater average depth
  - Finer substrates (sand, gravel dominance)
  - Lowest similarity scores of any segment (combined PS = 0.59), excluded from the pooled dataset.
- **Atomizer Reach (km 13–16; Triple Drop to above Chute Falls)**: A second zone of distinct habitat structure:
  - Mean depth decreases by ~50% between km 13 and km 16.
  - Mean current decreases by ~20%.
  - Sand becomes the dominant substrate above Chute Falls.
- **Segment-Level Variability**:
  - Segments 5, 6, 9, 13, 16, 20 had elevated sand substrate proportions.
  - Segments 2 and 4 had slightly higher current.
  - Segments 13 and 15 had slightly lower current.
  - Segments 3, 7, 15, 16, 20, 21 had slightly shallower mean depth.
  - Segments 12 and 14 had greater mean depth.
  - Km 1–2 (USGS gauge to AGF camp) also identified as a habitat-distinct section.
- **Substrate Heterogeneity**: The greatest source of inter-segment dissimilarity was variable proportions of sand, travertine, and large boulder substrates (categories 2, 8–9, 10), reflected in high standard errors for mean substrate size.

## Key Findings

- **Overall River Homogeneity**: Excluding the confluence, LCR segments km 1–21 were highly similar in habitat structure (mean combined PS = **0.75**, SD = 0.024), supporting the use of any representative subsection for research extrapolation.
- **Habitat Dimension Similarity Ranking**: Current and depth distributions were more consistent among segments than substrate distributions, which showed the greatest variability river-wide.
- **Study Area Representativeness**:
  | Study Area | Depth PS | Current PS | Substrate PS | Combined PS |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Powell Camp (km 2.5–3.5) | 0.80 | 0.77 | 0.64 | 0.73 |
  | Salt Camp (km 10.5–11.5) | 0.79 | 0.80 | 0.64 | 0.74 |
  | LCR river-wide mean (km 1–21) | 0.79 | 0.79 | 0.66 | 0.75 |
  | Confluence (km 0, outlier) | 0.64 | 0.46 | 0.65 | 0.59 |

- **Nearest-Neighbor Similarity**:
  - Powell Camp study area most closely resembled segments 3 and 4 (combined similarity 0.93 and 0.87, respectively).
  - Salt Camp study area most closely resembled segments 10, 11, and 12 (combined similarity 0.82, 0.96, and 0.85, respectively).
- **Depth & Current Patterns in Study Areas**:
  - Both study areas showed depth distributions similar to the overall LCR, though the **Salt Camp** area contained a greater proportion of deeper-water habitat.
  - Both study areas exhibited more equitable current distributions than the river average, i.e., an increased proportion of faster-current habitat — potentially relevant to microhabitat partitioning by [[Humpback Chub]] and other native cyprinids.
- **Substrate Complexity**: Study areas showed decreased dominance of sand and increased dominance of travertine and large substrate particles compared to the LCR as a whole, suggesting **greater habitat structural complexity** within the study reaches — a possible driver of locally elevated native fish abundance.
- **Management/Research Implication**: Because the purpose of USFWS sampling is to characterize *relative* microhabitat use patterns (not absolute habitat availability), high similarity between study areas and the broader LCR validates extrapolation of microhabitat use findings to other river sections, even where relative habitat availabilities differ.
- **Conclusion**: No adjustment to USFWS study area boundaries (Powell Camp or Salt Camp reaches) was justified; both sites adequately represent the array of microhabitats present throughout the perennial LCR (km 0–21), excluding the confluence and Atomizer reach anomalies.
