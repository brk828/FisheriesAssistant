# Travels of an Ancient Reef

#marine-biology #paleontology #reef-corals

## Executive Summary

This Natural History essay by geologist George D. Stanley, Jr. describes a fossil [[Triassic Period]] coral reef found at Summit Point in the [[Wallowa Mountains]] of northeastern Oregon, roughly 6,000 feet above sea level. The reef's fossil fauna matches reef limestones of the Alps (Dachstein-type reefs) almost exactly, even though the two sites lie more than 13,000 miles apart. The author argues that the reef originated as a tropical reef on a volcanic island in the ancient Pacific and was carried to North America by [[Plate Tectonics]], which moves [[Displaced Terranes]] across ocean basins. The essay links the Wallowa find to broader questions about the Triassic reef record, mass extinction and recovery, and continental drift.

The article is a popular science narrative rather than a formal study. It reports observations and interpretive reasoning, with few new quantitative data beyond the fossil counts and geologic dates cited.

## Background & Description

- **Discovery:** In 1985 Stanley and German paleontologist Baba Senowbari-Daryan (Erlangen-Nürnberg University) excavated fossils from a large limestone outcrop in the Wallowa Mountains, Wallowa-Whitman National Forest. Senowbari-Daryan recognized familiar Alpine forms, including sponges, algae, mollusks, sea urchins, and corals.
- **Fossil content:** About twenty coral species, sponges, and other invertebrates had been identified at the time of writing. All were identical to specimens from alpine reef limestone. Rock features (color, bedding, cavities, cements, reddish infilling sediment) also paralleled the Alps.
- **Age:** The Wallowa reef grew about 218 to 210 million years ago (late Triassic). It is described as the first coral-dominated reef of this age found in North America or the eastern Pacific.
- **Triassic context:** The Triassic lasted from about 250 to 205 million years ago. The end-Permian mass extinction removed most genera and many families, and reefs were especially hard hit. Reefs and most reef organisms were absent for five to ten million years before reappearing in the mid-Triassic.
- **Coral evolution:** [[Hexacorals]], the ancestors of modern reef-building corals, appeared as a new group during the post-extinction radiation. Around 220 million years ago corals began increasing in abundance and diversity. They gradually replaced calcareous sponges and algae as the main reef builders. During the Jurassic (about 205 to 144 million years ago) corals became dominant on reefs, a position they have largely kept since.
- **Alpine reference:** Much of the Triassic reef knowledge comes from the Alps. Reefs there rimmed the [[Tethys Sea]], an east-west near-tropical seaway ancestral to the Mediterranean, which opened eastward to the [[Panthalassa]]. The [[Dachstein Reef Limestone]] of Austria and Germany includes reef complexes more than 4,000 feet thick. The author spent a year as a Fulbright scholar studying these reefs in 1981.
- **Regional distribution:** Dachstein-type Triassic reefs also occur on western Pacific islands from Japan south to the Moluccas and Timor. Remnant reef corals have been reported from the Philippines. No other Triassic examples are known farther east in the Pacific.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Latitude limit:** Reef corals and their associates require warm, uniform temperatures and well-lit water. They are restricted to about 30° N to 30° S. Reeflike limestones at high latitudes in Alaska and the Yukon therefore suggested that the rocks had traveled.
- **Dispersal limits:** Reef organisms (sponges, corals, clams) have free-swimming microscopic larvae, which limits their dispersal distance. Adults are sessile, shallow-water, bottom-dwelling invertebrates. Because of these limits, shelly groups can help distinguish terranes, especially those that were once isolated from one another.
- **Reef dynamics:** Wallowa reef facies grade from shallow-water reef deposits upward into deeper-water calcareous mudstones and slates. Reef growth kept pace with rising sea level until earthquake-triggered debris flows carried broken reef rock and fossils down slopes into the adjoining [[Intra-Arc Basin]]. Later, drowning and burial of the complex is recorded in Jurassic rocks. The author notes that this sequence resembles sunken atolls and subsea volcanic plateaus in the modern Pacific.
- **Volcanic setting:** Reef growth began on a subsiding volcanic island after violent eruptions. The Wallowa basalts and volcanic rocks differ in composition from those under [[Wrangellia]], suggesting a different setting. Wrangellia is a major terrane in Alaska and Canada with volcanic rock piles up to five miles thick, capped by shallow-water limy sediments of late Triassic age.
- **Ocean currents:** Global current patterns do not allow reefs to flourish on western continental margins. The Wallowa reef likely grew on the eastern side of an island arc, exposed to southwest-blowing trade winds.

## Key Findings

1. **Matching reef faunas across the Pacific:** The Wallowa Triassic fauna (about twenty coral species plus sponges and invertebrates) is identical to Alpine Dachstein-type fauna. This is the most Tethyan-type coral reef found in North America to date.
2. **Terrane origin:** The Wallowa terrane was an exotic volcanic island that formed in tropical latitudes of the ancient Pacific. It was not part of western North America during reef growth.
3. **Travel history:**
   - Reef growth began about 218 million years ago.
   - The Wallowa terrane collided with a second Oregon volcanic island terrane during 166 to 159 million years ago.
   - The combined terranes amalgamated in the late Jurassic and docked with North America about 100 million years ago, dated by intrusion of deep molten magmas.
   - The interval between reef formation and docking is about 118 million years.
4. **Movement estimate:** Using a conservative sea-floor spreading rate of 3 inches per year (the modern Pacific range is 1.5 to 7 inches per year), the author estimates 4,656 miles of movement. This is enough to carry the reef from Japan to Oregon.
5. **Displaced terranes:** Displaced terranes make up more than 25 percent of North America. Western North America is described as a conglomeration of these slivers. Most of their Triassic fossil reef localities are in terranes thought to have originated independently of North America. Except for Wallowa, these localities lack dominant corals or massive Tethyan-style reef complexes.
6. **Noah's Ark dispersal model:** Paleontologist Malcolm McKenna's term "Noah's Ark" describes the wholesale transport of island faunas by sea-floor spreading. The author applies it to the Wallowa reef, which accounts for both the faunal similarities and the Tethyan rock signatures. A similar signature occurs in a deposit in Japan, roughly midway between Europe and Oregon.
7. **Fate of other islands:** Most roving Triassic Pacific volcanic islands were probably destroyed by subduction or obduction at the North American margin. Remnants may survive in deformed and faulted terrane rocks along the continent. The author notes that finding any trace of these islands is fortunate.
8. **Beached Viking funeral ships:** Paleontologists use this metaphor for terranes carrying fossilized reef cargo that drifted ashore after crossing oceans.
9. **Broader significance:** The essay argues that plate tectonics and paleontology combine to explain puzzling reef distributions, including the apparent absence of Triassic reefs from the eastern Pacific and the presence of identical reefs on opposite sides of the world.
