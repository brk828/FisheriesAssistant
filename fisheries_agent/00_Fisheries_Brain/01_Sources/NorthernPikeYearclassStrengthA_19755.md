# Northern Pike Year-Class Strength and Spring Water Levels

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper by Fritz H. Johnson of the [[Minnesota Department of Conservation]] tests whether spring water levels affect the strength of [[Northern Pike]] year classes in [[Ball Club Lake]], Minnesota, for the seven years 1945 to 1952. Years were ranked by a combined [[Water Level Index]] for spawning and egg incubation, and separately by a [[Year-Class Strength]] index derived from [[Darkhouse Spearing]] catches. A [[Rank Difference Correlation]] between the two rankings gave r = 0.61, a moderate correlation. The author concludes that high spring water levels during spawning, combined with a small decline during egg incubation, favor strong pike year classes.

## Background & Description

**Study species and community.** The [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) is an important sport fish in Minnesota, taken by summer angling and winter darkhouse spearing. Pike are most abundant in lakes larger than 300 acres with moderate to high chemical fertility, extensive littoral area, and adjacent or connected flowages or marshes. Spawning typically occurs between March 15 and May 1. Understanding the factors that drive pike abundance is also relevant to managing associated species, especially [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) and [[Yellow Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*).

**Study site.** Ball Club Lake (Itasca County) is about 5 miles long, 1.5 miles wide, and covers 5,111 acres, with a regular shoreline. The southern third is shallow (60% under 20 feet), while the northern two-thirds is deeper (30% under 20 feet). The water is clear, free of noticeable algal blooms, and hard, with total alkalinity of 138 p.p.m. Marshy areas and adjacent flowages serve as spawning grounds that flood during high spring water and may dry in late summer. The lake is part of a Mississippi River headwater reservoir regulated by the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]].

**Fish community.** Test netting in 1952 and 1954 (experimental gillnets of five mesh sizes from 0.75 to 2.0 inches bar measure, plus trapnets) showed a game fish population dominated by northern pike and yellow walleye. [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) occurred in small numbers. Other species included [[Tullibee]] (*Leucichthys artedi*), [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*), [[Lake Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis*), [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus nebulosus*), and [[Northern Redhorse]] (*Moxostoma aureolum*). Yellow perch, tullibee, and white suckers were the most abundant non-game species.

**Sampling method.** Pike used for aging came from winter darkhouse spearing, in which spearers sit in a heated, windowless fish house and lure fish within range using a wooden minnow jigged through a large ice hole. The Minnesota season runs from December 1 to February 15, with actual dates varying somewhat across years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reproductive timing.** In 1952 the pike spawning run at Ball Club Lake began April 17, about seven days before the walleye run at the nearby [[Cutfoot Sioux Hatchery]], and lasted about 10 days. Incubation in Minnesota hatcheries is usually about two weeks, varying with water temperature. Comparable studies were cited:
- [[Rawson 1932]] at [[Waskeiu Lake]], Saskatchewan: most spawning in about 12 days (May 1 to 12), with eggs hatching in about 12 days.
- [[Carbine 1941]] at [[Houghton Lake]], Michigan: spawning runs of 15 to 24 days in April.

**Reconstructing reproductive periods.** Because the 1952 field data alone could not cover seven years, the author estimated each year's run as starting 7 days before the walleye run and lasting 10 days. Incubation was conservatively set at 18 days, giving a 28-day reproductive window. Table 1 lists these windows; for example, 1945 spawning was April 7–17 with incubation April 17–May 4, and 1950 spawning was April 27–May 7 with incubation May 7–24.

**Water level dynamics.** Lake levels are governed by (1) the lake level before spring breakup, (2) spring runoff and precipitation, and (3) regulation by control dams on the Mississippi and Leech Rivers. The Corps of Engineers records the level about weekly at the Ball Club River below the outlet, using a steel tape hung from a bridge girder set at zero. Because of this, high water gives small numbers and low water gives large numbers, the reverse of a standard staff gauge.

**Earlier evidence.** [[Carbine 1944]] linked the highest survival of pike eggs and young at Houghton Lake in 1942 (compared with 1939 and 1940) to higher and more constant water levels that year. Johnson notes that high spring water and flooded grassy sloughs have long been associated with good pike reproduction in Minnesota and elsewhere.

## Key Findings

**1. Year-class strength was estimated from spearing catches.** Scale samples were aged from darkhouse spearing catches in five winters: 238 fish (1950-51), 90 (1951-52), 196 (1952-53), 103 (1953-54), and 78 (1954-55). Age-group percentages were compared against test-net composition, and the author judged that fluctuations in the catch reflected real population abundance rather than fishing pressure. Each year class's percentages were summed across seasons (aggregate percentages), then divided by the five-year geometric mean for the same age groups to create an index, where 1.00 is average.

**2. Year-class strength ranking (high to low):** 1950 (index 1.69), 1947 (1.42), 1948 (1.16), 1945 (1.10), 1946 (1.04), 1951 (0.98), 1952 (0.70). The 1949 year class was excluded because spring water data were unavailable for that year.

**3. Water conditions were indexed by two components.**
- *Spawning-period level (Ms):* the mean spawning-period level for each year is divided by the seven-year mean of 3.15 feet. Because of the measurement method, lower values mean higher water.
- *Incubation drop (Md):* the maximum drop during incubation is divided by the seven-year mean decline of 1.03 feet.
- The overall water-condition index L is the sum of these two ratios. Each is given equal weight a priori. An average year scores 2.00, and lower L means higher, more stable water.

**4. Water-condition ranking (most to least favorable):** 1950 (L = 0.99), 1951 (1.81), 1947 (2.09), 1945 (2.11), 1948 (2.18), 1946 (2.30), 1952 (2.42). Example: 1945 spawning mean was 1.67 feet (index 0.53), and its incubation drop was 1.63 feet (index 1.58).

**5. Ranking correlation:** Applying the rank difference formula of [[Edwards 1946]] (ρ = 1 − 6ΣD²/[n(n²−1)]) to the two rankings gives ΣD² = 22 and ρ = 0.61. The author describes this as a moderate correlation. The shortfall from a closer fit is attributed to data uncertainty, the assumptions made in the calculation, and factors not modeled, such as a strong year class suppressing those that follow.

**6. Main conclusion:** A high spring water level during spawning and a small decline during egg incubation favor a strong northern pike year class. The 1950 year class fits this pattern best, with the highest water condition ranking and the highest year-class index. The 1952 year class is weakest on both measures.

**7. Caveats noted by the author.**
- The index values are for ranking only, not absolute measures.
- The 1949 year class could not be included.
- Reproductive dates were estimated rather than observed in most years.
- The weighting of the two water components was arbitrary.

**8. Data discrepancies in the source (OCR or reporting):**
- Table 5 lists "1954" in the first row, but the ranking text and the 1945 data indicate this is the 1945 year.
- The text gives the high year-class index as 1.68, while Table 3 gives 1.69.
- The Table 4 index for 1945 is shown as "B11." in the scan. Calculation from its components (0.53 + 1.58) gives 2.11.
