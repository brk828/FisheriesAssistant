## Executive Summary

This document is a peer-review correspondence letter from the [[American Fisheries Society]] Purposeful Introductions Committee, addressing a formal proposal to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] into [[Lake Powell]] (a reservoir in the Colorado River Basin). The letter, authored by Alexander V. Zale (Committee Chair), transmits independent reviews conducted by Zale and committee member Bob Howells regarding the ecological risks and merits of the proposed introduction. The primary management goal appears to be establishing a forage base to support piscivorous [[Striped Bass]] and [[Walleye]] populations in the reservoir. The reviewers concur that significant uncertainties exist regarding smelt thermal tolerance and forage-predator interactions, despite differing perspectives on the specific direction of risk.

## Background & Description

[[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) is being considered for purposeful introduction into [[Lake Powell]] as a potential forage fish species. The proposal was developed by an unspecified state or federal management agency and submitted for external scientific review to the [[Introduced Fishes Section]] of the [[American Fisheries Society]].

Two independent reviews were commissioned:
- **Alexander V. Zale** – Oklahoma Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit, Oklahoma State University
- **Bob Howells** – (affiliation not specified in text, likely Texas Parks & Wildlife based on historical context)

A third reviewer, Dennis Lee, was originally slated to contribute but instead incorporated his review into the State of California's official response to the proposal, reportedly to avoid any perceived conflict of interest, as neither Zale nor Howells had a "vested interest in the situation at Lake Powell."

The reviewers explicitly noted that the proposal itself was considered an "exemplary model" of the documentation and rigor that management agencies should employ when proposing new species introductions, though the letter's content focuses on committee critique and shared concerns rather than proposal specifics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The core ecological question underpinning this introduction proposal is the intended trophic interaction between introduced [[Rainbow Smelt]] and existing reservoir piscivores, specifically [[Striped Bass]] and [[Walleye]] in [[Lake Powell]]. The management rationale presumes that smelt would serve as an effective forage species to bolster these established (also non-native to this system) predator populations.

Key environmental context includes:
- **Reservoir thermal stratification**: Lake Powell, as a deep desert reservoir on the Colorado River, exhibits thermal gradients that are central to the debate over smelt viability.
- **Precedent gap**: The reviewers noted a critical knowledge gap — interactions between [[Striped Bass]] and smelt species in reservoir systems had not been "adequately evaluated elsewhere," making outcomes at Lake Powell difficult to predict from existing literature.
- **Non-native community assembly**: The proposal represents a case of introducing a forage species specifically to support other already-introduced piscivorous game fish, raising broader questions about compounding ecological risk in constructed/reservoir ecosystems.

## Key Findings

- **Divergent thermal tolerance concerns**: Reviewer Bob Howells expressed concern that rainbow smelt "may be more tolerant of high temperatures than field observations suggest," raising the possibility of unintended range/niche overlap or unpredicted persistence.
- **Opposing thermal concern from Zale**: Alexander Zale's concern ran in the opposite direction — that smelt would be too intolerant of warmer temperatures and would fail to occupy the same thermal strata as [[Striped Bass]] and [[Walleye]], undermining the forage-predator linkage needed for the introduction's intended effect.
- **Unverified trophic outcomes**: Both reviewers agreed that interactions between striped bass and smelt in reservoir environments were poorly documented elsewhere, creating substantial uncertainty as to whether the introduction would achieve its stated goal of enhancing piscivore populations.
- **Independent (non-synthesized) review approach**: The committee chair intentionally did not merge the two reviews into a single consensus document, citing the value of preserving two distinct, detailed, independent scientific perspectives for the benefit of the proposal's authors, rather than diluting critique into a single averaged report.
- **Positive procedural assessment**: Despite substantive ecological concerns, the proposal document itself was praised as a strong example of well-prepared introduction proposals, suggesting the review was procedurally rigorous even where scientific uncertainty remained.
- **No definitive recommendation captured**: The letter does not state whether the committee formally recommended for or against the introduction; it primarily transmits caveats and open scientific questions to the proposal's evaluators (implied to include California and other Colorado River Basin stakeholders).
