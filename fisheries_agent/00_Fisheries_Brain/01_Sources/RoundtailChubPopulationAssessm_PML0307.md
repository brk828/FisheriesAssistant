# Roundtail Chub Population Assessment in the Lower Salt and Verde Rivers, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 2003 monitoring report by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (AZGFD) assesses the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) population in the lower [[Salt River]] and lower [[Verde River]]. The study estimates population size, size structure, body condition, habitat use, and movement, and compares results with the 1999–2000 study ([[Bryan And Robinson 2000]]). It also tests [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT tags) retention.

The population estimate fell to 1,657 individuals (95% CI 1,097–2,742), a 74% decrease from the 2000 estimate of 6,424. The authors attribute the decline mainly to diminished recruitment, linked to the absence of significant flood spikes since 1998, and conclude that the population is declining rapidly. They recommend immediate management action.

## Background & Description

Roundtail chub are native to Arizona and are managed both as a sportfish and as a Species of Special Concern (AZGFD 1996). Although not federally listed, the species is declining throughout its range. Documented causes include alteration of the historic hydrograph from dam construction, habitat degradation, and predation by and competition with [[Nonnative Fishes]] (Minckley and Deacon 1991).

Before 1998, few records existed for chub below [[Bartlett Dam]] or below [[Stewart Mountain Dam]], which led biologists to assume the population was sparse. The 1999–2000 study showed chub were more abundant than expected. This report continues monitoring to evaluate population status, including the effects of prolonged drought.

**Primary objectives:**
- Determine current population size, size structure, and condition.
- Compare results with the 2000 study to identify trends.
- Determine PIT tag retention rates, since low recapture rates in 1999–2000 may have reflected tag loss.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The lower Salt River flows about 21.5 km between Stewart Mountain Dam and [[Granite Reef Dam]] through Sonoran desert scrub and tamarisk-mesquite flats. Winter flows are held at a minimum of 8 cfs, which restricts fish to shallow runs and deep pools. Summer flows rise to 800–1,200 cfs and create lateral scour pools, riffles, and swift runs. The lower Verde River flows about 41 km from Bartlett Dam to the Salt River. Its upper reach is a high-gradient canyon with pool-run complexes. Downstream it widens into desert flatlands. Winter flows typically range from 400 to 1,000 cfs, and summer flows are held between 125 and 250 cfs. Both rivers are regulated by [[Salt River Project]] for hydroelectric power and water supply.

**Habitat.** The upper 16 km of the Verde River provides preferred adult habitat (pool-run complexes adjacent to swift riffles) and shoreline eddies used for spawning. Abundance drops sharply about 16 km below Bartlett Dam, where the river is wider, shallower, and more moderately graded.

**Water chemistry.** Conductivity was about 1,255 µS/cm in the Salt River, which likely reduced [[Canoe Electrofishing]] effectiveness there. Conductivity in the Verde River was about 520 µS/cm, allowing more effective electrofishing.

**Flow history.** The authors note that roundtail chub have persisted for up to twenty years without a significant flood, so floods alone do not explain successful spawning. Appendix C shows regulated discharge histories below Bartlett and Stewart Mountain dams from the early twentieth century to 2004.

## Key Findings

### Methods

- **Sampling:** Fish were collected in spring and fall 2003 using [[Gill Netting]] (up to three experimental nets, 2.4 m × 45.5 m, mesh sizes 12.7–76.2 mm, fished no longer than 30 minutes) combined with canoe electrofishing (600 V, 6–8 A).
- **Sampling effort:** The Verde River was sampled twice in spring and twice in fall. The Salt River was sampled twice in spring and once in fall, because equipment failure prevented a second fall sample.
- **Marking:** Unmarked fish over 90 mm were injected with PIT tags in the abdominal cavity. Habitat type was recorded and locations were mapped by GPS.
- **Population estimate:** The joint hypergeometric maximum likelihood estimator, an adaptation of the [[Lincoln-Petersen Estimate]] for closed populations, was run in NOREMARK, with confidence intervals from the profile likelihood method. Salt and Verde fish were treated as one population because they can move between rivers.
- **Condition:** [[Relative Weight]] (Wr) was calculated using the standard weight equation of Didenko et al. (2004).
- **Statistics:** T-tests compared length and weight; chi-square tests compared habitat and distribution. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

### Population Characteristics

- **Captures:** 262 roundtail chub were marked in 2003. In the Verde River, 272 were captured (242 PIT tagged, 16 recaptures, 13 sacrificed for otolith aging, 6 escaped). In the Salt River, 25 were captured (20 PIT tagged, 5 recaptures).
- **Distribution:** The share of chub in the upper 16 km of the Verde River rose from 64% in 2000 to 88% in 2003 (chi-square, P = 0.000). In the Salt River, fish were sparse, found at only two sites 9.7 and 10.7 km below Stewart Mountain Dam.
- **Size structure:** The population is almost entirely large fish (> 35 cm), with few small fish (< 35 cm) and an apparent absence of juveniles (< 25 cm). No seasonal growth was detectable.
- **Size comparison:** Adult chub in the Salt River were heavier and longer than adults in the Verde River (weight t-test, df = 294, P = 0.000; length t-test, df = 229, P = 0.000). Chub in the upper Verde were smaller (mean TL 414 mm) than those in the lower reaches (mean TL 440 mm; P = 0.001).
- **Largest fish:** 552 mm and 1,826 g, caught in the lower Verde River.
- **Growth:** 2003 fish were longer than 2000 fish in both rivers (Salt, P = 0.000; Verde, P = 0.000).

### Body Condition

- Relative weight values ranged from 95 to 125, indicating very good condition.
- Salt River chub had Wr values of 125. Mean Wr in 2003 was higher than in 2000 in both rivers (Salt, P = 0.000; Verde, P = 0.000).

### Habitat Use

- In 2003, Salt River chub were mainly in lateral scour pools, whereas in 2000 they were mostly in mid-channel pools (chi-square, P = 0.000).
- In the Verde River, most 2003 fish were in lateral scour pools and runs, with few in low-gradient riffles. In 2000, fish were spread more evenly across habitat types (chi-square, P = 0.000).

### Movement

- Most recaptured fish showed [[Site Fidelity]]: 11 (68.8%) did not move, 3 (18.8%) moved upstream, and 2 (12.5%) moved downstream.
- Mean distance moved was 0.5 km (SE = 0.3). The furthest single movement was 2.8 km.
- Movement was detected only for fish first captured in early September and recaptured two weeks later, a period of low, steady flow.

### Population Estimate

- **2003:** 1,657 individuals (95% CI 1,097–2,742). 16 fish were recaptured.
- **2000:** 6,424 individuals (95% CI 5,048–8,397).
- **Change:** A 74% decrease over three years. The 2003 sample size was about 7% smaller than in 2000.

### PIT Tag Retention

- In February–March 2004, 29 fish received both PIT and floy tags. Only two were recaptured within the 90-day evaluation period. Both retained their PIT tags, and one lost a floy tag.
- Retention was 100%, but the sample was too small to calculate an accurate rate.
- Childs (2002) reported 96.6% PIT tag retention over three months in bonytail chub, which the authors used as a surrogate. They concluded that tag loss likely had little effect on the population estimate.

### Age and Life History

- Brouder (in prep.) reported a maximum age of seven years in the upper Verde River. Preliminary otolith ages suggest lower Verde chub may live up to 11 years.
- Fish that hatched in 1998 appeared as 90–120 mm one-year-olds in spring 1999. Their growth was tracked in 1999 and 2000 samples. The absence of juveniles in 2003 suggests the last significant spawn occurred in 1998.

### Conclusions and Management Recommendations

The authors conclude that the population is declining rapidly and faces possible extirpation. They attribute the decline mainly to diminished recruitment from a lack of significant flow spikes in the last five years. Good body condition suggests food is not limiting. The authors recommend:

1. **Immediate management action**, including careful evaluation or suspension of nonnative fish introductions, especially predatory species. The authors also recommend a thorough evaluation of [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking.
2. **Research** into the relationship between flood timing, duration, and magnitude and successful spawning and recruitment.
3. **Negotiation with Salt River Project** to allow periodic spike flows that could enhance reproduction.
4. **A [[Recovery Plan]]** covering watershed and flow protection, research on the causes of disappearance, and actions to reduce nonnative fish effects.
5. **A [[Brood Stock Program]]** for at-risk populations, including this one.
6. **Continued monitoring** of roundtail chub populations.
