# Planktonic Algae and the Ecology of Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This 1967 review by [[J. W. G. Lund]] of the Freshwater Biological Association argues that understanding the ecology of [[Planktonic Algae]] is essential to understanding the ecology of a lake as a whole. It covers algal productivity and biomass estimates, the transfer of energy from primary to secondary producers, the chemical and physical factors limiting algal growth, and the experimental methods used to study plankton in natural or semi-natural settings. It also addresses the practical management of algae in [[Fish Ponds]], [[Sewage Lagoons]] and [[Reservoirs]]. The document is a scientific review article rather than a proposal, and it contains no fish species data.

## Background & Description

The paper distinguishes the [[Phytoplankton]] (plant component of the plankton) from the [[Zooplankton]] (animal component), which also includes bacteria, rotifers, protozoa and fungi. Some algae, such as the dinoflagellate *Ceratium*, can swim actively, while small algae are largely moved by water currents. Macrophytes (stoneworts, mosses, pond weeds) and organic matter from the catchment can be more important carbon sources than algae in some lakes.

The review is framed around the [[International Biological Programme]] (I.B.P.), which uses the year as the standard unit for measuring production. Lund notes that seasonal patterns are often similar between years, even in tropical lakes, though their detailed course can vary markedly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biomass and light.** [[Chlorophyll A]] is preferred over particulate carbon or nitrogen as a measure of live algal matter. Steeman Nielsen estimated a possible maximum of 0.8 g/m² chlorophyll in the euphotic zone under self-shading, but reported lake maxima rarely exceed 0.2 g/m². Light is also absorbed by suspended particles, colloids and dissolved coloured substances.

**Production rates.** Steeman Nielsen estimated a representative maximum of 4.6 gC/m²/day for a temperate lake at 20°C, supported by a measured 3.89 gC/m²/day in a eutrophic Danish lake. Values of up to 10 gC/m²/day were recorded for [[Lake Mariut]], Egypt. Lund suggests culture-based maxima of about 25 g dry weight/m²/day (10–13 gC/m²/day).

**Energy transfer.** Herbivores are estimated to use at most one-third of plant production, and often only about one-tenth. Carnivorous fish production is therefore a very small fraction of primary production.

**Trophic status.** [[Eutrophic Lakes]] have high nutrient supply and dense algal populations, but their surface layers can shade out light, making the photic zone shallow. [[Oligotrophic Lakes]] have lower productivity per unit volume but may achieve similar rates per unit area because light penetrates deeper. Small naked flagellates, small diatoms and green algae are easily ingested by zooplankton, whereas colonial diatoms and most [[Blue-Green Algae]] are poorly grazed; water-blooms of blue-green algae are typical of eutrophic lakes.

**Light penetration in East African lakes.** Photosynthesis profiles from six East African lakes (Fig. 2) show similar total areas under the curves. Significant photosynthesis reached about 16 m in the open part of [[Lake Victoria]], but only about 2 m in [[Lake George]] and the [[Kazinga Channel]].

**Nutrients.** Phosphorus is most often limiting, followed by nitrogen. Silicate limits diatoms, which contain about 40% silica by dry weight. Potassium, calcium and sodium showed no consistent relationship with algal abundance. Very dense populations can deplete carbon dioxide and raise pH above 10, which inhibits growth. Thermal stratification blocks nutrient supply from the lower layers to the euphotic zone.

**Ice and light.** Algae can grow slowly under ice, and winter production depends on snow cover and ice clarity. Temperature sets the maximum growth rate and interacts with light-saturation of photosynthesis, as shown for [[Asterionella Formosa]] (Fig. 4).

## Key Findings

- **Productivity limits:** Realistic maximum primary production is around 4.6 gC/m²/day for temperate lakes, with cultured algae capable of about 25 g dry weight/m²/day.
- **Energy transfer:** Herbivores use at most about one-third, and often about one-tenth, of plant production, so carnivorous fish yield is a small fraction of primary output.
- **Grazing selectivity:** Zooplankton readily ingest small algae and avoid large colonial forms and most blue-green algae. In a Swedish eutrophic lake (Lake Erken), phytoplankton biomass was insufficient to support observed zooplankton production in summer, suggesting zooplankton fed substantially on bacteria and detritus (Nauwerck, 1963).
- **Experimental artefacts:** Small enclosed water samples change rapidly in chemistry and biology. Bacterial activity removes phosphate, and the effect is partly due to wall surfaces. Iodine-treated polyethylene bottles reduce this loss. Larger, longer tubes approximate lake conditions better but still change rapidly.
- **Large-scale enclosures:** The Freshwater Biological Association has used polyethylene tubes holding several cubic metres of water, and is testing plastic-screened "basins" to isolate large lake volumes. The minimum volume needed to keep lacustrine characteristics is unresolved.
- **Fish ponds:** Blooms of *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae* are encouraged in Eastern European carp ponds, with a reported correlation between algal biomass, photosynthesis and fish yield. Toxicity to fish is not well proven. Phosphate is often added to produce maximum fish growth.
- **Sewage lagoons:** Algae supply oxygen for bacterial oxidation of organic matter. Fishponds tend to be dominated by blue-green algae, whereas sewage ponds are dominated by green algae. The reason is unknown.
- **Eutrophication and management:** Widespread enrichment causes serious amenity problems. Whether reservoir phosphate can be reduced to prevent blooms remains an open question.
- **Research gaps:** Lund highlights the need to understand which factors control the quantity and quality of algal plankton. The chemistry of humus and extracellular organic compounds, including glycollic acid, amino acids and vitamins, is incompletely understood.
