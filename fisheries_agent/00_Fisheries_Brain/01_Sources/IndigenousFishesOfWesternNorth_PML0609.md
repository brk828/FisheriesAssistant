# Indigenous Fish Displacement: Red Shiner Impacts on Spikedace in Western North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 *Copeia* paper by [[Douglas]], [[Marsh]], and [[Minckley]] tests whether the decline of the threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) in the [[Gila River]] subbasin is caused by active competitive displacement by the nonnative [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), or by habitat degradation that favors invaders. Fish were seined from six stream reaches across [[Arizona]] and [[New Mexico]], and current velocity, substrate particle size, and water depth were measured at each site. Red shiners used similar microhabitat whether alone or with spikedace. Spikedace, however, shifted into significantly swifter currents when syntopic with red shiners, which the authors interpret as evidence of negative interspecific interaction consistent with displacement.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with the decline of native fishes in western North America, which the authors attribute largely to the introduction and establishment of alien species. Western fish faunas evolved in isolation within fragmented, arid aquatic systems shaped by [[Geologic and Climatic Events]], leaving them with reduced competitive abilities and predator defenses relative to fishes from more speciose regions.

Two competing hypotheses are outlined:

- **Replacement hypothesis:** environmental degradation, primarily human-induced, eliminates sensitive natives, and pioneering nonnative "colonizer" species occupy the vacated niches.
- **Displacement hypothesis:** indigenous species are actively displaced by invading nonnatives through competition, predation, or hybridization.

Spikedace is a small cyprinid of low-elevation streams with a restricted range; red shiner is a tolerant, aggressive invader from the Great Plains and Mississippi region. Their co-occurrence was documented in the Gila basin, where the invader spread through Arizona and New Mexico from the 1960s to the 1980s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study design:** Fish were collected by seining one to three sites in each of six stream reaches. Comparisons covered syntopy (same site), allotopy (different sites in one stream), and disjunct allopatry (separate streams). The authors used the terms of [[Remington]]-style spatial classification (allopatric, allotopic, syntopic).
- **Habitat variables:** Current velocity (cm/s), water depth (cm), and weighted substrate particle size were measured at five points per replicate area. Substrate was field-estimated as percentages and converted to particle size.
- **Statistics:** Data were analyzed with unweighted and weighted ANOVA (SAS, 1985), with Sidak's multiple comparison tests. Presence records were weighted by abundance to give more influence to sites where a species was most common.
- **Habitat character:** The study reaches, including [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Sycamore Creek]], and the [[Verde River]], were generally shallow, slow, and sandy or gravelly, with heterogeneous microhabitats used by both species.
- **Theoretical framing:** The authors draw on [[Competition Theory]] and [[Niche Partitioning]] literature, noting that competition is difficult to demonstrate in natural systems and that microhabitat segregation is a common outcome of competition in stream fishes.

## Key Findings

1. **Microhabitat overlap:** Red shiners occupied similar microhabitats whether allopatric, allotopic, or syntopic with spikedace.
2. **Spikedace fidelity:** Spikedace occupied the same microhabitat when alone or alongside red shiners in allotopic situations.
3. **Niche shift:** Spikedace syntopic with red shiners shifted into currents significantly swifter than those selected in isolation. Red shiners did not shift in the same way.
4. **Interpretation:** The authors conclude that the data support interspecific competition, specifically (1) similar niche attributes for both species in allopatry and allotopy, and (2) niche overlap and niche shift in sympatry. They argue that displacement of spikedace by red shiner suggests negative interactions potentially detrimental to the native fish.
5. **Mechanism uncertainty:** The authors acknowledge that the mechanism of segregation (aggression, competition for food, or predation avoidance) is not resolved. Possible causes include the more aggressive behavior of red shiners, a plausible hypothesis supported by studies of other fishes such as [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]].
6. **Recommended research:** Future studies should include direct observation of interactions between the species and experimental tests of decreased fitness in spikedace, such as metabolic measurement, plus experiments across seasons when habitat availability changes.
7. **Conservation context:** The paper was supported by [[USFWS]] Endangered Species Act Section 6 funds, and the spikedace was federally listed as threatened in 1986.
