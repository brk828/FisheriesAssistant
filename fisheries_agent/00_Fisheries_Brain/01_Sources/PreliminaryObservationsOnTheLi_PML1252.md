# Preliminary Observations on the Life History and Population Ecology of the Humpback Chub in the Little Colorado and Colorado Rivers, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report presents preliminary observations on the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*, Miller 1946) in the [[Colorado River]] system of [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. The species was historically distributed throughout much of the Colorado and [[Green River]] and their major tributaries, but contemporary populations are known only from relatively unaltered portions of its former range. Its survival was uncertain under continuing alteration of the river ecosystem, which led to its designation as an endangered species in 1967.

A stratified systematic sampling program from [[Fall]] 1980 to [[November]] 1981 focused on the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence region, where the largest and most reproductively active population was found. Findings address age and growth, sexual maturity, seasonal gonadal development, parasitism, and movement. The study was funded mainly by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], with additional support from the [[National Park Service]] and the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]].

## Background & Description

The humpback chub was described by Miller in 1946 and was among the last large North American freshwater fishes to be described by science. Its life history and population ecology remained largely unknown when this study was undertaken. The species is believed to have been historically abundant throughout the Colorado and Green River systems, though perhaps not abundant at all times and places.

Earlier work established the context for this study:

- **[[Flaming Gorge Dam]], Green River, Utah:** Closure of the dam was followed by documented losses of chub habitat. The presumed effects of the altered temperature regime on reproduction eliminated several native fishes.
- **[[Glen Canyon Dam]]:** Closed in 1963, the dam was followed by qualitative studies of the Grand Canyon reach (about 450 river miles). These studies indicated effects on native fishes similar to those documented below Flaming Gorge Dam.
- **Little Colorado River:** A large, warm, highly mineralized tributary where the humpback chub persisted in low numbers, with the greatest abundance and apparent reproduction near its mouth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors attribute the decline of native fishes to physical and chemical changes in the river ecosystem caused by dam operation, which they describe as clear and deleterious consequences. Competition with [[Introduced Species]] may also be a significant factor.

Several environmental conditions were linked to the species' performance:

- **Growth:** Poor growth of small chub in the Colorado River was attributed to low water temperatures and frequent water level fluctuations. These conditions severely limit food organisms in littoral areas.
- **Temperature and reproduction:** Laboratory studies indicated extremely high mortality of embryonic and larval chub at the test temperatures.
- **Parasitism:** [[Lernaea cyprinacea]] was the most conspicuous metazoan parasite of the humpback chub. The water temperatures required for its development were not found in the Colorado River. Its presence in infected chub from the Little Colorado River suggests former occurrence of the host species there.

## Key Findings

### Distribution and Age
- Humpback chub were distributed throughout the lower 13 km of the Little Colorado River.
- Examination of scales validated [[Scale Annuli]] as indicators of age in Little Colorado River fish.
- Growth was rapid during the first three years of life. Older fish showed relatively little annual growth.

### Sexual Maturity and Reproduction
- Relationships between the [[Gonadosomatic Index]] (GSI), the mean oocyte diameter (MOD), and fish length indicated that sexual maturity in female chub begins at approximately 250–290 mm, or about 3 years of age, in the Little Colorado River population.
- Field observations indicated that males mature at smaller sizes.
- Seasonal GSI and MOD values for females showed gonadal development between December and February/April. Spawning was indicated by rapid declines in both indices between April and May.
- Preliminary histological analyses suggested that chub undergo normal seasonal gonadal development in the modified thermal regime of the Colorado River. These analyses were still ongoing.
- No chub under 145 mm were collected from the Colorado River mainstem. The authors state that this absence provides evidence that successful chub reproduction does not occur there. Young fish in the Little Colorado River indicate that it is the primary reproductive area.

### Parasites and Mortality
- The high incidence of Lernaea infection in small Little Colorado River chub in December 1980 may have been an appreciable source of mortality.

### Movement
- Of 720 chub tagged and released, 32 (4.4 percent) were subsequently recaptured.
- Some chub moved substantial distances. The authors suggest this movement is largely related to spawning, and that most movement probably occurs between river segments.

### Overall Conclusion
The Little Colorado River is the principal refuge and reproductive area for the humpback chub in the study reach. Conditions in the regulated Colorado River mainstem appear unsuitable for successful recruitment, largely due to temperature regime, water level fluctuations, and limited food production.
