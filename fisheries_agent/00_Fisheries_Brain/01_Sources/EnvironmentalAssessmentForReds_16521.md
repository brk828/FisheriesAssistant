# Red Shiner Eradication Project: Environmental Assessment for the Virgin River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft environmental assessment (EA), prepared by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Service) in August 1996, evaluates a project to eradicate the nonnative [[Red Shiner]] from the Virgin River in Utah. The red shiner competes with and may prey on native fishes, including the federally endangered [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin River Chub]], and the [[Virgin Spinedace]], which was proposed for listing. The preferred alternative, the Utah Treatment Alternative, uses [[Rotenone]] applied with [[Potassium Permanganate]] detoxification over about 33.6 km (21 mi) of river from Washington Fields Diversion to the Virgin River Gorge, plus associated diversions, marshes and washes. A buffer zone alternative treats only the reach between Washington Fields and Johnson Diversion dams. The no-action alternative would leave the red shiner unchecked. The document is a draft, and its impact analysis finds short-term adverse effects that are outweighed by long-term benefits to native fishes.

## Background & Description

The red shiner (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) is a small minnow native from the North Central United States to northeastern Mexico. It was introduced to the lower Colorado River Basin as bait in the early 1950s and was first found in the Virgin River system in 1961 downstream of Mesquite, Nevada. Until the 1980s it was confined to the lower 62.4 km (39 mi) of river below the Virgin River Gorge. In April 1984 one red shiner was found above the Gorge, and it later became dominant in the 33.6 km (21 mi) reach between the Gorge and Washington Fields Diversion.

The 1988 pilot eradication project, run by the Service with the [[Utah Division Of Wildlife Resources]], included fish salvage by [[Depletion Seining]], a barrier at the Gorge, and rotenone treatment. Later operations in 1988, 1989, 1991, March 1996 and August 1996 followed. Earlier red shiner eradication efforts had only partial success, with reinvasion each time. The Service and Bureau of Land Management (BLM) previously issued Findings of No Significant Impact (FONSI).

The Virgin River is a desert tributary of the [[Colorado River]], about 320 km (200 mi) long, flowing across the boundary of the [[Colorado Plateau]] and the [[Great Basin]], separated by the [[Hurricane Fault]]. The basin covers about 13,209 km² (5,100 mi²): 59% in Utah, 33% in Arizona and 8% in Nevada. Elevations range from 3,048 m (10,000 ft) to below 579 m (1,900 ft). The river has highly fluctuating flows, high salinity, temperature and turbidity, and is influenced by mineral springs such as [[La Verkin Springs]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fishes.** The system supports six native fish species: woundfin, Virgin River chub, Virgin spinedace, desert sucker, flannelmouth sucker and speckled dace. The woundfin and Virgin River chub are federally endangered. The Virgin spinedace was proposed for threatened listing in 1994, but that proposal was withdrawn on February 6, 1996, after a [[Conservation Agreement]] was signed. In Utah, the woundfin, Virgin River chub and Virgin spinedace are State Endangered species; the flannelmouth and desert suckers are State Sensitive.

**Nonnative fishes.** Fourteen introduced species occur, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Carp]] and mosquitofish. The red shiner and black bullhead are the most abundant. Some impacts of the red shiner are competition, predation on eggs and early life stages, and possible introduction of the [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*). A crayfish (Astacidae sp.) is also present and may prey on young fishes.

**Other fauna.** Several amphibians are of concern: the southwestern toad, lowland leopard frog and relict leopard frog are State Sensitive; the relict leopard frog is considered extirpated from the basin. Invertebrates are sparse. Snails occur in springs but not in the mainstem. Terrestrial species of concern include the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] and [[Peregrine Falcon]] (both federally endangered), the [[Desert Tortoise]] (threatened), the [[Mexican Spotted Owl]] (threatened) and the [[Bald Eagle]] (threatened, winter resident only).

**Land use.** About 87,740 acre-feet per year of Virgin River water was used for agricultural irrigation in 1990, and 19,040 acre-feet for municipal and industrial use. Washington County's population grew from 13,669 (1970) to 48,560 (1990), with growth above 6% per year. The river is used for recreation, and the Virgin River Gorge is a BLM Recreation Area and Designated Scenic Area.

## Key Findings

- **Reinvasion and spread.** Red shiners were not detected above Washington Fields Diversion before 1989 treatments, but they reinvaded the Johnson to Washington Fields reach in 1989 and again in March 1996. In October 1987 woundfin fell to 15% of fish in the Gorge to Washington Fields reach, while red shiners rose to 59%.
- **Preferred alternative.** Chemical treatment of about 33.6 km (21 mi) from Washington Fields Diversion (River Mile 118.8) to the Gorge barrier. The project is long-term, starting in late summer/fall 1996, and will be done in stages: diversions and Fort Pierce Wash in fall 1996, a second treatment in spring/summer 1997, mainstem and Seegmiller Marsh in late 1997, then a second marsh treatment in fall 1997 or spring 1998.
- **Treatment parameters.** Rotenone at 1.5 ppm, tagged with rhodamine dye. Potassium permanganate applied at least 2.0 ppm for each 1.0 ppm of rotenone. Four to six times the calculated permanganate is kept on hand in case of rain-driven flow increases.
- **Salvage.** Pre-treatment salvage is expected to capture about 40–60% of target species. Salvaged fish will be released downstream.
- **Toxicity findings.** Rotenone half-life in warm water is 0.94 days; water detoxifies naturally in under four days at 26.7 °C. The EPA reports no basis for concern about using treated water for irrigation. Estimated lethal oral dose for a 60 kg person is above 60,000 L of water.
- **Biological impacts.** Short-term adverse effects are expected on native fishes, amphibian larvae (possible loss of a year's recruitment), and invertebrates, with recolonization expected rapidly. Native fish are expected to reestablish within one year or one breeding season. Long-term effects on native fishes are expected to be beneficial.
- **Buffer zone.** The buffer zone alternative treats only Washington Fields to Johnson Diversion dam, the reach that protects the last red-shiner-free stretch above Washington Fields.
- **No-action consequences.** Red shiners would likely invade above Washington Fields and become very hard to remove, and native fish populations could be pushed toward extinction. This would also complicate water development in Washington County.
- **Genetic concern.** A DeMarais et al. (1993) study reported bottlenecking and reduced genetic viability in Virgin River chub after the 1988 effort. The document states that current salvage and detoxification precautions should avoid this.
- **Consultation.** An intra-Service section 7 consultation under the Endangered Species Act is required before implementation.
