# Intra- and Interspecific Differences in Rate of Oxygen Consumption in Gobiid Fishes of the Genus Gillichthys

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gobiidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by [[George W. Barlow]] (University of California, Los Angeles) tests whether [[Gillichthys Mirabilis]] populations from different latitudes differ physiologically in oxygen consumption, and how they compare with the related [[Gillichthys Seta]]. The author argues that earlier latitudinal studies confounded genetic divergence with [[Thermal Acclimation]], so all populations were acclimated to the same temperatures before testing. Oxygen uptake was measured in a [[Warburg Apparatus]] at several temperatures.

Results show that populations differed in their [[Allometric Scaling]] slopes and in their [[Temperature Coefficient (Q10)]] values, while mean rates converged at 24°C. The two species diverged at 24°C and 31°C in their size-metabolism relationship. The author interprets these patterns as likely heritable and as evidence of adaptive, but not easily predicted, divergence.

## Background & Description

- **Study organisms:** [[Gillichthys Mirabilis]] (longjaw mudsucker species, as named in the source only as *G. mirabilis* Cooper) and [[Gillichthys Seta]] (Ginsburg). Common names are not given in the document.
- **Relationship:** *G. seta* is described as the species most closely related to *G. mirabilis*. The two are morphologically hard to separate, and the author considers *G. seta* to represent an extension of the morphological cline within *G. mirabilis*.
- **Habitat of *G. mirabilis*:** Intertidal regions of coastal sloughs, typically muddy and exposed at low tide. Some fish remain in crab burrows and breathe air. *G. seta* is also capable of aerial respiration.
- **Habitat of *G. seta*:** Restricted to the northern Gulf of California, in the highest intertidal zone of exposed coast.
- **Distribution:** *G. mirabilis* occupies disjunct populations across three roughly parallel north-south coastlines spanning several faunal regions: a low-boreal fauna in central California, a tropical Panamic region in the southern Gulf, and a depauperate mixed fauna in the northern Gulf.
- **Introduced population:** In 1930, *G. mirabilis* was introduced to the [[Salton Sea]] from San Diego Bay. The fish are established there, though the author considers the Salton Sea a marginal habitat, judging from the number of aberrant specimens.
- **Climate gradient:** Temperatures generally warm to the south. In the Gulf of California, the annual range of mean temperatures increases from south to north, and the Salton Sea continues this trend of greater extremes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study populations of *G. mirabilis*** (north to south):

| Population | Setting |
|---|---|
| San Francisco Bay (Alviso Ponds) | Central California, ~37° N |
| Los Penasquitos | Southern California, ~33° N |
| Salton Sea | Inland, ~33° N, warmer climate |
| San Felipe ("El Marino" slough) | Northern Gulf of California, ~31° N |
| Puerto San Carlos | Central Gulf of California, near Guaymas (used only at 24°C and 31°C; small fish excluded from covariance analysis) |

**Husbandry and acclimation:**
- Fish were held in sea water in 20- and 40-gallon tanks, aerated and filtered, and fed exclusively on fresh squid at irregular intervals to limit cyclic behavior.
- Minimum acclimation was two weeks at 24° ± 0.5°C and four weeks at 10° ± 0.05°C.
- Only male or immature fish were used. Illumination was continuous, which the author later suggests may be an unnatural influence.
- Fish were tested at 10°C and 17°C (cold-acclimated) or at 24°C and 31°C (warm-acclimated).

**Methods highlights:**
- Oxygen uptake was measured in [[Warburg Apparatus]] chambers of 45, 85, and 250 cc, with an oxygen well to maintain tension.
- Regression lines were fitted by least squares to log-transformed data, using the model Rate = a·Wt^(-b).
- Statistical significance was set at the 5% level.

## Key Findings

**1. Stable rates within populations**
- Rates for a given population stayed consistent across months (e.g., Salton Sea fish tested in January, April, and May).
- Adjustment time was roughly 9–10 h at 10°C and 6–10 h at 24°C, with a steady rate over a 12-hour window thereafter.

**2. Size and metabolism**
- Smaller animals consumed more oxygen per unit weight. The slopes (b) were steeper than typical for fishes.
- At 10°C, slopes were nearly the same among *G. mirabilis* populations, with differences near the 5% significance level and of doubtful meaning.
- At 17°C, slopes differed significantly. They were low in San Francisco and Salton Sea fish and higher in San Felipe and Los Penasquitos fish. No latitudinal trend was evident.
- At 24°C, slopes were statistically uniform among the four main populations.
- At 31°C, slopes again differed among populations, but not in a consistent direction.
- Summary slopes reported by the author: b ≈ -0.14 and -0.35 at 10°C and 17°C; b ≈ -0.45 and -0.63 at 24°C and 31°C. Values for *G. seta* were about -0.29 and -0.26 at 24°C and 31°C, much shallower than for *G. mirabilis*.

**3. Mean rates of oxygen consumption**
- Mean rates differed among populations at 10°C, 17°C, and 31°C, but not at 24°C, which the author considers near the thermal optimum for the species.
- Mean rates for *G. seta* at 24°C were lower than for *G. mirabilis*, but the two species were about equal at 31°C.
- The direction of population departures was irregular and not clearly linked to latitude.

**4. Temperature coefficients (Q10)**
- Cold-acclimated fish had higher Q10 values (roughly 2.2–2.8 in summary) than warm-acclimated fish (roughly 1.4–1.6).
- Gulf of California populations (San Felipe and San Carlos) generally had lower Q10 values, and their pattern contrasts with the usual finding that southern forms show higher coefficients.
- At 10–17°C, Q10 values were 2.22–2.84 at 10 g for the four principal populations; at 24–31°C, they were about 1.43–1.59.
- *G. seta* had a warm-acclimated Q10 of about 2.0, higher than any *G. mirabilis* group under the same conditions.
- A test on three California populations (10–27.5°C) showed that "cold depression" did not explain the high chronic Q10 values.

**5. Size and temperature coefficients**
- Acute Q10 values did not depend on size.
- After acclimation, chronic Q10 values were strikingly higher in smaller fish. The author concludes that acclimation was more effective in larger fish.

**6. Species comparison and character displacement**
- Where ranges overlap, the species diverged physiologically in Q10 and in the regression slope b, which the author describes as showing marked [[Character Displacement]].
- Morphologically, *G. seta* appears to retain juvenile traits, which the author suggests may partly explain its shallow slopes.

**7. Interpretation and caveats**
- The author treats metabolic differences between fully acclimated populations as indicating heritable dissimilarities, while acknowledging that the role of the environment in reversibility is unknown.
- The author notes that the Q10 pattern does not match the [[Prosser Classification]] pattern or the expectations from [[Latitudinal Variation]] studies in other fishes.
- Constant temperature and continuous light are unnatural conditions. The author suggests that oscillating temperatures would better reflect natural conditions.
- Overall, the author judges the degree of intra- and interspecific divergence in oxygen consumption to be roughly comparable in magnitude to the morphological differences reported in a companion paper.
