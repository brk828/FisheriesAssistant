# Biogeography of Australian Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #biogeography #endemism

## Executive Summary

This study by Peter J. Unmack (Arizona State University, *Journal of Biogeography* 28: 1053–1089, 2001) examines the biogeographic patterns of Australia's obligate freshwater fishes. Using similarity coefficients, clustering, non-metric multidimensional scaling, parsimony analysis and drainage reconstructions for lowered sea levels, the author analyses 157 species across 31 drainage-based regions and proposes a series of [[Biogeographic Provinces]]. The most striking pattern is a strong east–west and north–south split in endemism: southern, central and western provinces hold many endemics, whereas northern and eastern provinces hold few. The author attributes western and southern endemism mainly to [[Aridity]] and [[Drainage Divides]], northern low endemism to drainage connectivity during lowered sea levels, and eastern low endemism to climate, since no distinct physical barriers are evident. Most patterns are judged to have been established in the distant past, possibly as early as the [[Miocene]], with Plio-Pleistocene events having seemingly minor influence.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Approximately 200 freshwater fishes occur in Australia. Marine and estuarine vagrants and species with marine phases were excluded, leaving 157 species (159 named species were used for nomenclature). Eight undescribed species were included.
- **Taxonomy:** Nomenclature follows [[Eschmeyer Catalog of Fishes]] (1998), with family designations following Allen (1989) and Kemp (1997b) for [[Lungfishes]] (Dipnoi).
- **Geographic framework:** Australia was divided into 31 regions derived from river basins (AWRC 1976 scheme), with the [[Murray-Darling Basin]] split into three. Presence/absence data were compiled largely from museum specimens (e.g., [[Australian Museum]], [[Queensland Museum]], [[Western Australian Museum]]) and from [[American Museum of Natural History]] and other US collections.
- **Key taxa mentioned:** Ancient lineages such as [[Neoceratodus forsteri]] (Australian lungfish, persisting at least 100 Myr), [[Osteoglossidae]] (bony tongues, *Scleropages*), [[Galaxiidae]] (galaxiids), [[Percichthyidae]] (including [[Murray Cod]] and [[Macquarie Perch]]), [[Atherinidae]] (*Craterocephalus*), [[Melanotaeniidae]] (rainbowfishes), [[Eleotridae]] (gudgeons), and [[Plotosidae]] (catfishes, *Tandanus*).
- **Analytical philosophy:** The author stresses concordance across methods, treats results as testable working hypotheses for future phylogenetic work, and acknowledges key assumptions (e.g., species are monophyletic, fish cannot "jump" drainages, and absence data are unreliable).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geological and climatic setting**
- Australia has very low relief; the highest point is Mt Kosciusko (2228 m), the lowest is Lake Eyre (16 m below sea level). Erosion rates are extremely low (often <10 m Myr⁻¹ for the Mesozoic–Cenozoic).
- The continent separated from Antarctica around 95 Ma and was fully severed by 37 Ma. Northward drift of about 30° since then moved it toward warmer latitudes.
- Most major river basins were established by the Palaeocene. The Eastern Highlands orogeny ended around 90 Ma.
- Volcanism ranged from late Mesozoic to Quaternary (last activity 13 ka in northern QLD and 4.6 ka in south-eastern SA). Most volcanism was before 30 Ma.

**Sea-level and marine transgressions**
- Mid-Cretaceous seas inundated much of the continent. Tertiary transgressions affected the Eocene southern and western coasts, the late Oligocene–early Miocene Murray and Eucla basins, and the late Miocene–early Pliocene Murray Basin.
- Global sea level dropped to around −100 m several times in the past 10 Myr. Lowered seas connected northern drainages (e.g., [[Gulf of Carpentaria]], [[Lake Carpentaria]]), [[Bass Strait]] and [[Torres Strait]].

**Climate and vegetation**
- Early Tertiary climate supported rainforest; open sclerophyll forests became common later, and the first grasses appeared in the Pliocene.
- Aridity increased over the last 15 Myr, with palaeodrainage cessation in mid-Miocene and gypsum precipitation from about 5 Ma. Aridity spread eastward from the west; dunes at Lake Amadeus date to about 0.9 Ma.
- Around 70% of Australia is arid. Lowest mean annual rainfall is 110 mm (Lake Eyre); highest is 7664 mm (Mt Bellenden Ker).
- Glacial cycles (about 100 kyr glacial maxima, 10 kyr interglacials) and [[Milankovitch Cycles]] drove short-term climate fluctuations.

**Barriers to fish movement**
- *Sea water:* Short-term sea-level changes of up to 100–150 m occur on 100–150 kyr cycles. The author notes that the Miocene is the likely time when faunas last exchanged between Australia and New Guinea by migration.
- *Drainage divides:* Divides are breached mainly by long-term tectonic drainage rearrangement, river capture, or fish swimming over low divides. Evidence for drainage change across the [[Eastern Highlands]] is contested.
- *Climate:* Aridity has isolated south-western WA, Pilbara, Western Plateau, Lake Eyre and Bulloo drainages. Temperature and rainfall are the principal climatic constraints.
- *Ecology:* Most species' tolerances are unknown but are assumed broad; narrow habitat specialists are restricted to permanent water.
- *Dispersal by other means:* "Rains of fish" are reported but are considered rare and poorly understood.

## Key Findings

1. **Endemism pattern:** 78 species (47% of the fauna) occur in only one region, 64 in two to seven regions, 16 in eight to fifteen, and 9 in 16–22 regions. Endemism is highest in western, central and southern regions; the "endemic line" effectively splits Australia in two.
2. **Richness gradient:** Richness declines with latitude (R² = 0.58, rising to 0.83 when seven arid regions are excluded; both significant at P < 0.05). Richness was not correlated with region size. Highest richness: CYP (45 species) and NEQ (39). Lowest: TORR (1 species) and WP (1 species).
3. **Clustering and ordination:** Four similarity coefficients (Dice, Jaccard, Ochiai, Kulczynski no. 2) gave similar UPGMA trees, with cophenetic correlations of 0.92–0.95. Non-metric MDS gave stress values of 0.12 (Jaccard) and 0.11 (Kulczynski), with cophenetic correlations of 0.90 and 0.96.
4. **Parsimony analysis:** Fifteen most parsimonious trees were found (length 214, CI 0.411, RI 0.753). Bootstrap support was low (only six branches above 50%) because of the small number of characters per OTU (average 2.8).
5. **Proposed provinces:** The author proposes the following:
   - **South-western Province (SWWA):** Fully isolated, with no species in common with other regions and endemic families (Lepidogalaxiidae).
   - **Pilbara Province (PILB):** 5 of 12 species endemic (42%).
   - **Kimberley Province (WKIM and EKIM):** 16 endemics out of 39 species (47%), with the Speciose Northern, Depauperate Northern and North-eastern subprovinces nested within the Northern Province.
   - **Central Australian Province (BARK, LEB, BULL, TORR):** LEB has 40% endemics.
   - **Bass Province (SWV and NTAS):** Combined endemism of 36%.
   - **Southern Tasmanian Province (STAS):** All eight species endemic.
   - **Murray-Darling Province (MDB and SAG):** 31% endemism, with nine endemics.
   - **Northern Province (VOR to NEQ):** 38 endemics out of 75 species (50%), with 25 shared with southern New Guinea.
   - **Eastern Province (BURD to SEV):** Gradational, with 31% endemism (15 of 48 species).
   - **Palaeo Province (WP):** Default category; one species with no clear relationships.
6. **Explanation for endemism:** High western, central and southern endemism results from isolation by aridity and drainage divides. Northern low endemism reflects drainage connectivity during lowered sea levels. Eastern low endemism is more difficult to explain. Drainages appear isolated even at low sea level, yet there are no distinct barriers, so climate is considered the most likely cause of distributional limits.
7. **Timing:** Most patterns were probably established in the distant past, perhaps as early as the Miocene. Persistence of some families and genera over at least 30–45 Myr is supported by fossils. Earlier hypotheses of Plio-Pleistocene radiation are judged unlikely, and Plio-Pleistocene events seem to have had little influence on broad distributions.
8. **Specific cases:**
   - The Burdekin Falls and climate hypotheses for low BURD richness are both supported, but climate is preferred as the most parsimonious explanation.
   - Connections between LEB and BULL, and between MDB and LEB, are plausible but undated.
   - The Lake Carpentaria and Fly River route between Australia and New Guinea is considered unlikely for freshwater fishes during the last sea-level rise.
9. **Methodological recommendation:** The author argues that combined clustering and ordination should be used, and that distributional and geological studies such as this one are a useful precursor to phylogenetic work, providing testable hypotheses.
