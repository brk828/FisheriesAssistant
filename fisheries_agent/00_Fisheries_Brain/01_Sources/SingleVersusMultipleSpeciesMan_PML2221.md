# Single Versus Multiple Species Management: Native Fishes in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #salmonidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a scientific review, published in a Forest Service format, that asks whether threatened and endangered native fishes in [[Arizona]] are better protected through [[Single Species Management]] or through [[Multiple Species Management]] at the river-basin or community level. The authors, John N. Rinne and Jerome A. Stefferud, argue that the single-species approach has been dominant and has been essential for keeping many taxa alive, but that it cannot by itself restore fish communities. They conclude that the approach will continue to be needed, and that conservation should be pursued wherever possible at the watershed or ecosystem scale, with the long-term, cooperative and vigilant effort the authors identify as the underpinning of success.

The paper is not a field study with new sample data. Its evidence comes from the published literature, agency records and reported population counts cited in the text.

## Background & Description

The review opens with the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, which the authors note added recovery to listing and protection as a central goal. Since 1979 almost 1,100 species have been listed as threatened or endangered in the United States, and the text states that no species had yet been de-listed as a result of recovery activities.

The authors frame the central question as whether management should proceed species by species or address groups of species and communities. They focus on native fishes of the southwestern United States, especially Arizona. The three objectives are to examine the factors that shape the choice of approach, review examples of each approach and their success, and recommend the approach most effective and feasible for sustaining native fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate, topography and hydrology.** Aquatic habitats in Arizona are disjunct and unconnected, a pattern attributed to interactions among climate, topography and hydrology. The [[Colorado River]] and its tributaries drain most of the state. Historically the system was continuous, running from headwater cienegas and montane creeks through canyon streams to large low-elevation rivers. Elevations range from near sea level to about 3,000 m and stream size from under 0.1 to about 5,000 m³/s. Before dam construction the mainstream rivers were highly turbid and swift during spring runoff, clear at base flow, and intermittent in lower desert reaches during drought. Isolated perennial springs were also present. This variability supported a highly specialized fish fauna.

Floods and droughts were normal parts of the hydrograph. Large floods rejuvenated systems and stimulated spawning or recruitment. Droughts caused mass mortality in lower desert reaches, with fish retreating to refuges, and recolonization depended on connectivity with those refuges. Uncontrolled wildfire in forested watersheds could alter hydrology and extirpate local populations of cool- and cold-water fishes, which were then reestablished from downstream reaches.

**Alteration of historic hydrology.** Damming and diversion during the first half of the twentieth century permanently disrupted river connectivity and caused local extirpations. Impoundments, starting with the 1902 Reclamation Act, confined free-flowing water mainly to upstream, high-elevation landscapes above about 760 m in elevation. Lower reaches of the Salt, Gila, Verde and Colorado rivers became modified, stabilized or dry. Groundwater mining lowered water tables and stopped springs from flowing.

**Nonnative species.** Almost 100 fish species have been introduced into Arizona in the past century, and almost half are established. Competition and predation by these fishes have markedly reduced the range and numbers of many native species.

**Fauna diversity.** The native fauna is dominated by cyprinids and catostomids, with a few or single species of salmonids, cyprinodontids, poeciliids and ictalurids. The authors state that about three dozen native species, including three marine representatives, have been recorded from Arizona. By comparison, a single creek or riffle in more mesic eastern drainages may hold that many species.

## Key Findings

- **Current status.** Nineteen native species in Arizona are listed as threatened or endangered under the Endangered Species Act, and eight more are considered species of special concern.
- **Core conclusion.** Single-species management has been critical for survival, and some taxa were still lost. The authors judge that its success was largely dictated by habitat distribution and the need to respond to the situation at the time.
- **Future direction.** The authors argue that conservation must seek opportunities to manage fish communities. Riparian areas and streams should be managed for habitat integrity, and conservation should be organized by river basin or watershed.
- **Preserves.** Preserves may be crucial, but the probability of achieving them falls as the required area grows. Preserves for isolated populations and single species are easier to establish than those for whole communities.
- **Realistic outlook.** Because water is highly valued in the arid West, and because land ownership is fragmented, the authors expect single-species management to remain the most workable option, while still aiming for multi-species goals.

**Gila Topminnow (endangered).** Before 1940 this was the most common fish in the lower Colorado basin. It was listed as endangered in 1967 after declining to fewer than a dozen natural populations. Western mosquitofish, groundwater mining and livestock desiccation were the main threats. More than 200 localities were stocked, but fewer than 10 percent of sites succeeded for longer than a few years. The authors argue that this low rate reflects the species' natural history in unstable habitats and should not be treated as a failure of the recovery effort. True recovery in its native habitat is no longer possible, but isolated populations can persist through long-term reintroduction.

**Gila Trout and Apache Trout.** Gila trout (endangered) and Apache trout (threatened) are managed as single species. Both occupy disjunct headwater tributaries and have remained in the same listing status for more than two decades. Wildfire in 1989 destroyed the largest Gila trout population, and two more were lost later. Gila trout is now extant in 11 streams and may be proposed for down-listing again. The Apache trout is nearing the goal of 30 streams for de-listing, but genetic contamination by rainbow trout and invasion by brown trout have slowed progress. The authors consider biological recovery reasonable here, since habitat is available or can be restored by removing nonnative trouts.

**River basin and multiple-species efforts.** Apart from one exception, Arizona has had no formal basin-level effort to manage a native fish community. The Desert Fishes and Colorado Fishes recovery teams addressed groups of species, but in practice their work was largely the sum of single-species efforts. The Colorado Fishes program has supported [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] since the mid-1970s. In the lower basin, squawfish no longer have an extant population, so recovery for these fishes has followed their current distributions.

**Refuges.** The San Bernardino and Leslie Canyon national wildlife refuges were established to conserve four listed fishes of the Rio Yaqui, including Yaqui chub, Yaqui catfish and Yaqui topminnow. Habitats have been restored on the refuge, but the larger goal of conserving the whole Rio Yaqui fauna is limited because most of the watershed lies in Mexico, where groundwater mining continues.

**Limitations.** The authors give no quantitative sample sizes of their own. Several cited figures, such as the approximate number of introduced species and the share of stocking sites that succeeded, come from earlier studies and agency reports.
