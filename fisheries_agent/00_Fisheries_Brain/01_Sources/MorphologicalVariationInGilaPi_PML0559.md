# Morphological Variation in Gila (Pisces: Cyprinidae) and Geologic History: Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 Arizona State University Master of Science thesis by Bruce Douglas DeMarais examines morphological and meristic variation among chubs of the genus [[Gila]] (subgenus Gila) in the lower [[Colorado River]] basin. Many populations show intermediate characters between [[Gila Robusta]] (roundtail chub) and [[Gila Intermedia]] (Gila intermedia), and a named form, *G. grahami*, has been treated as a separate taxon. The author supports specific status for *G. intermedia* and argues that intermediate populations, including *grahami*-type fishes, are best explained as hybrid populations of varying age rather than ecophenotypic or ecotypic responses. The conclusions are tied to a Tertiary geologic history of endorheic basins, drainage reversals and captures that shaped the present fish distribution.

## Background & Description

The genus *Gila* is a group of cyprinids with a long, confused taxonomic history in western North America, where distinctive phenotypes were historically described as many taxa and more recently treated as variants within polytypic species. The thesis focuses on:

- **[[Gila Elegans]]**: streamlined, large-river form.
- **[[Gila Robusta]]**: intermediate-sized streams; the principal subject of the study.
- **[[Gila Intermedia]]**: thick-bodied fish of creeks and marshes.
- **[[Gila Cypha]]**: highly modified (humpback) species, considered the most specialized member of the genus, and lying outside the main morphological trends.

Five *Gila* taxa of subgenus Gila were recognized from the lower Colorado basin, three of which (*G. robusta*, *G. grahami*, *G. intermedia*) were studied in detail. Ten additional taxa (seven *Gila*, three *Temeculina*) from adjacent drainages were examined for comparison.

Methods included 35 counts and measurements per specimen, osteology from cleared-and-stained and dry skeletons, and multivariate analyses (principal components analysis on the correlation matrix and canonical discriminant analysis). Only specimens above 50 mm SL were used for statistics to limit allometric effects. Populations were grouped geographically into seven drainage categories: Santa Cruz, upper San Pedro, lower San Pedro, Gila, Verde, Salt, and Agua Fria.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study region spans the Colorado Plateau (elevated about 1,700 m above mean sea level, drained by the Little Colorado River) and the Basin and Range Province, separated by a highly variable Transition Zone. Most of the Basin and Range and Transition Zone drain to the Gila River watershed.

Historical geology is central to the interpretation:

- Structural features and drainage history relevant to fish evolution date to at least the late Oligocene. Miocene fossils referable to *Gila* are known from Nevada, Idaho and Arizona.
- Extensional tectonism and block faulting in the Miocene created northwest-trending basins. Faulting peaked about 15–10 MYA and largely ended by 6–5 MYA.
- Closed (endorheic) Miocene–Pliocene basins, including the [[Verde Formation]] (Perkinsville-Verde), Payson-Tonto, and Safford-San Carlos basins, were formed along the [[Colorado Plateau Margin]]. Drainage was intermittently connected and reversed as the plateau escarpment retreated.
- The [[Colorado River]] integrated the upper and lower basins in the late Miocene to early Pliocene, and entered the Gulf of California after the Bouse Embayment retreated, sometime after 5.5 MYA. The lower Gila was captured by the Colorado River Delta in the Pliocene, opening a route for cyprinids and catostomids to invade the Gila basin.
- Renewed Pleistocene faulting breached volcanic-tectonic dams and integrated the upper Gila River through the Salt River.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic status**
- *G. intermedia* is treated as a separate species: (1) phenotypic extremes (*G. intermedia* and *G. robusta*) are so divergent that, without phenotypic intermediates, specific status would be unquestionable; (2) *G. intermedia* and *G. robusta* have a mosaic distribution that places them in geographic sympatry, which argues against subspecies rank; and (3) the species shows characteristics typical of distinct species under any species concept.
- Populations of *G. intermedia* from the Santa Cruz and upper San Pedro drainages are morphologically homogeneous and are interpreted as genetically "pure" stocks.

**Morphological and meristic patterns**
- A graded series of intermediate forms links *G. robusta* and *G. intermedia*. Canonical discriminant analysis shows a continuum with *G. robusta* at one extreme and *G. intermedia* at the other, though no single population contains the full range of variation.
- Caudal skeleton features are informative. In *G. robusta*, the last 5–6 neural and hemal spines contact procurrent caudal rays, producing a flared caudal fin base; in *G. intermedia* only 4–5 spines are involved. Phenotypic intermediates show intermediate or asymmetric conditions.
- Intermediate populations fall into three situations: (1) populations resembling *G. intermedia*, mainly in Agua Fria, upper Verde (Walker, Oak creeks) and the upper Santa Cruz-San Pedro basins; (2) populations intermediate between *G. robusta* and *G. intermedia* in essentially all characters, including Fossil Springs, upper West Clear Creek, East Verde River and Tonto Creek, linked to the ancestral Verde-Payson-Tonto system; and (3) populations of *G. robusta* showing minor introgression, including Aravaipa, Burro, and parts of the upper Gila.

**Alternative explanations for phenotypic intermediates**
- The author considered four options: ecophenotypic response, ecotypic response, secondary contact and hybridization, and *grahami* as a monophyletic taxon. The thesis favors hybridization as the explanation for *grahami*, interpreting it as "a series of hybrid populations of variable ages and histories rather than a single recognizable subspecies."
- Ecophenotypy is rejected because intrademic temporal stability is documented, stream size correlates poorly with morphology, and the intermediate forms are restricted to the Gila basin, absent from the upper Colorado and Mexican drainages with *G. robusta*.
- Ecotypy is rejected because the strong correlations among polygenic characters would not be expected in independently evolved allopatric populations, and fishes in the Santa Cruz and upper San Pedro show phenotypic uniformity inconsistent with ecotypic plasticity.
- Hybridization is considered plausible. Natural hybridization among fishes is common, and geologic and climatic changes could have brought formerly allopatric stocks into contact.

**Phylogenetic and biogeographic notes**
- Uyeno (1961) found no significant osteological differences between *G. intermedia* and related species, and concluded these all shared a common ancestor.
- *G. grahami* is considered by the author to be of hybrid origin, not a monophyletic taxon. Robert R. Miller examined syntypes of *G. grahami* and found them not to differ from typical *G. robusta*.
- The author notes that the origin of the Gila River fishes is tied to vicariance, drainage capture and endorheic isolation, and that fossil *G. robusta* or a very similar form from the Miocene Bidahochi Formation predates integration of the upper and lower Colorado River.

**Limitations noted by the author**
- Not all populations were examined osteologically, and sufficient material for quantification of osteological characters was not available.
- The fourth explanation for *grahami* (a monophyletic taxon) was explored only speculatively and lies outside the scope of the study.
