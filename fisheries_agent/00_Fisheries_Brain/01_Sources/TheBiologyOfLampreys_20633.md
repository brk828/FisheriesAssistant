# The Biology of Lampreys, Volume 1: Distribution, Phylogeny and Taxonomy

#ichthyology #marine-biology #petromyzonidae

## Executive Summary

This note summarises the opening chapter of *The Biology of Lampreys*, Volume 1 (edited by M. W. Hardisty and I. C. Potter, Academic Press, 1971), with its foreword and editorial material. The chapter, by [[C. L. Hubbs]] and I. C. Potter, reviews the distribution, phylogeny and taxonomy of living lampreys, the jawless vertebrates (Agnatha) that together with [[Hagfish]] form the cyclostomes. The authors recognise three families (Petromyzonidae, Geotriidae, Mordaciidae), 9 genera and 31 species in the systematic list, propose a phylogeny based mainly on oral disc dentition, and argue that nonparasitism has evolved repeatedly from parasitic ancestors. The foreword by J. Z. Young adds a broader evolutionary framing.

## Background & Description

Lampreys and hagfishes are the only living representatives of the most primitive vertebrates. Both lack jaws, internal bone, scales and paired fins, and share an eel-like body, a single nostril and a proterocercal tail. Lampreys differ in having a well-developed oral disc with teeth and a larval (ammocoete) stage that undergoes metamorphosis in fresh water. Adults of parasitic species attach to fishes and feed on blood and tissue, while hagfishes are mainly scavengers.

Nonparasitic "brook lampreys" are small adults, typically 100–180 mm long, that do not feed after metamorphosis. Parasitic species usually reach 180–800 mm. Taxonomic identification relies on oral disc dentition, myomere counts and proportional body measurements. Larval identification is harder because ammocoetes overlap in size, so oocyte counts in older female larvae are used instead.

The fossil record begins with [[Mayomyzon]] from the Pennsylvanian of Illinois (c. 280 million years ago), which was placed in its own family, Mayomyzonidae. Its close resemblance to living forms suggests a slow rate of evolution since the Pennsylvanian.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and temperature.** Lampreys have an antitropical distribution. Lanzing (1957) found they lie broadly north or south of the 20 °C annual isotherm in their respective hemispheres. The main exception is the paired species Tetrapleurodon spadiceus and T. geminis, which persist as relicts in high-altitude streams in Mexico.

**Salinity.** Lampreys occur from fresh water to full-strength sea water (about 35‰), in the western Mediterranean (37–39‰), and in the Caspian Sea (about 150‰ of normal salinity).

**Marine range.** Larger anadromous species range farther offshore and are more widely distributed. [[Petromyzon Marinus]] has been recorded from the Grand Banks, and Geotria australis has been found at South Georgia.

**Freshwater parasitic species** have restricted ranges, usually within one large drainage basin (for example, Eudontomyzon danfordi in the Danube system). This may reflect the need for a large host population.

**Glacial history.** Distributions of [[Ichthyomyzon]] species and of the Lampetra subgenera are interpreted in terms of Wisconsin glaciation and postglacial dispersal (Hubbs & Trautman, 1937). Relict southern populations are common across the genera.

**Southern Hemisphere.** Only four species in two genera occur there: [[Geotria]] and [[Mordacia]]. Geotria ranges to about 33°S in South America and 32°S in Australia. The Northern Hemisphere has 27 species in seven genera. The authors attribute the southern paucity to few suitable river systems, except the Murray–Darling in Australia.

**Black Sea and Caspian history.** The Eudontomyzon species of the Black Sea drainage and the Caspiomyzon wagneri of the Caspian Sea are interpreted as products of past connections and isolations between these basins.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomic structure:** Three families are recognised: Petromyzonidae (Northern Hemisphere), Geotriidae (Geotria) and Mordaciidae (Mordacia). Mordacia and Geotria were previously placed at subfamily or family rank by different authors. Within Petromyzonidae, Lampetra is treated as a genus with three subgenera (Entosphenus, Lethenteron, Lampetra), and Eudontomyzon, Tetrapleurodon, Okkelbergia, Petromyzon, Caspiomyzon and Ichthyomyzon are kept as separate genera.
- **Dentition as the key character:** Oral disc and lingual lamina teeth provide most generic characters. The authors introduce standard terminology for supraoral, infraoral, circumoral, marginal and field teeth, and give an analytical key to the Petromyzonidae.
- **Phylogeny:** Ichthyomyzon has the simplest dentition and is treated as the most primitive genus. Petromyzon is close to the ancestral stock of the Eudontomyzon and Lampetra lineages. Geotria and Mordacia are considered distinct and more specialised.
- **Repeated loss of feeding:** Nonparasitism has evolved at least 15 times in the Northern Hemisphere, and at least once in the Southern Hemisphere (Mordacia praecox). Nonparasitic forms show reduced dentition, though some show increased tooth counts before later degeneration.
- **Chromosomes and haemoglobin:** Northern species have very high diploid numbers, typically 140–170. Mordacia mordax and M. praecox have 76, and Geotria's karyotype is reported as preliminarily similar to the Northern forms. Haemoglobin electrophoresis supports the separation of Geotria and Mordacia.
- **Spawning run length:** Mordacia mordax may have a spawning run of about 12 months. Northern Hemisphere species generally have runs about half that length.
- **Nomenclature:** The editors' notes assign fluviatilis and planeri to Lampetra and treat Lampetra lamottenii as the eastern North American nonparasitic derivative of L. japonica. The authors use "Okkelbergia aepyptera" for a highly degenerate brook lamprey.
- **Foreword and wider framing:** J. Z. Young notes that lampreys retain many primitive features, that they have been present since the Pennsylvanian, and that they remain abundant, sometimes outnumbering fishes in British river systems.
