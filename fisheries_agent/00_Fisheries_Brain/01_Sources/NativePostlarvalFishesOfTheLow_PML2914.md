# Native postlarval fishes of the lower Colorado basin, with a key to their identification

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Howard Elliott Winn]] and [[Robert Rush Miller]] (Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan) provides a developmental framework and an artificial identification key for the postlarval stages of native minnows (Cyprinidae) and suckers (Catostomidae) of the [[Lower Colorado River]] basin. The material came from an ichthyological survey in spring 1950, in which early stages of 12 species were collected, mainly as preserved specimens. The authors adopt and modify the stage-based terminology of [[Balinsky Developmental Stages]] and define the postlarval period as the interval from yolk absorption to a juvenile-like form, corresponding to developmental stages 33 to 40.

## Background & Description

Little was known about the early life history of North American freshwater fishes from a systematic standpoint. Identification of eggs and young is needed for locating spawning sites, studying larval distribution, conserving populations, and interpreting year-class strength and species relationships. Previous larval-figure studies cited include [[Fish 1929-1932 Lake Erie Studies]], [[Fry 1936]], [[Kopec 1949]], [[Weisel and Newman 1951]], [[Douglas 1952]], and [[Foerster and Pritchard 1935]] on Pacific salmon.

Field collections used fine-meshed dip nets, wire strainers and night light sampling. The lower Colorado River was defined as the river and tributaries below [[Lake Mead]] (Hoover Dam), including the entire [[Gila River]] system. Specimens were preserved in 10 percent formalin; because larvae were not reared, their ages are unknown.

Two families were treated: Catostomidae (suckers) and Cyprinidae (minnows), described as among the most difficult freshwater fishes to identify. Six minnow species and six sucker species were keyed. Other native families present in the basin include Elopidae, Salmonidae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, Mugilidae and Eleotridae. Thirteen measurements on postlarvae failed to separate Cyprinidae from Catostomidae diagnostically within each family, but some features did separate the two families.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors note that postlarval stages often differ from adults in microhabitat, feeding, sensitivity to environmental fluctuations and behaviour, making this period ecologically distinct. The distribution of several taxa is tied to river type: [[Ptychocheilus]] occurs mainly in main, swift-flowing rivers such as the Colorado and Salt rivers, while [[Catostomus latipinnis]] and Ptychocheilus have been rarely recorded in the Gila drainage in recent decades. [[Plagopterus argentissimus]] was probably extinct in the Gila River, with no specimens reported since 1898. [[Agosia chrysogaster]] prolarvae were collected in gravel nests of adults. [[Gillichthys detrusus]] does not penetrate far beyond tidal influence. [[Pantosteus delphinus]] was recorded for the first time from the lower Colorado River system (Burro Creek, tributary to Big Sandy River, Arizona) and was otherwise known from one record in the Williams River drainage, where it is scarce.

## Key Findings

- **Stage definitions:** Balinsky's cyprinid stages were condensed into a table. Stage 1 is the unfertilized egg; stages 2-27 are embryonic (hatching at about stage 27); stages 28-32 are the nonfeeding prolarva with yolk sac; stages 33-40 are the feeding postlarva; stages 41-46 cover finfold loss, scale and lateral-line development.
- **Postlarval definition:** The postlarva ends when a continuous finfold is lost and pelvic fin rays ossify, criteria the authors judged suitable for suckers, minnows and most egg-laying North American freshwater fishes.
- **Developmental timing:** The first intestinal loop in suckers forms at stage 37, the mouth reaches a ventral position between stages 39 and 40, and the operculum fully covers the gill arches only at stages 37-39 in suckers.
- **Pigment terminology:** Four horizontal chromatophore series (dorsal, lateral, ventro-visceral, unpaired midventral) were used, with dorsal head pigment subdivided into occipital, interorbital, narial and upper lip pigment.
- **Sample sizes:** Over 100 specimens were available for each species, except 17 of Tiaroga cobitis. Few juvenile Catostomus latipinnis were obtained, so a Green River, Wyoming collection (UMMZ 92248) was used.
- **Key structure:** The artificial key begins by separating minnows from suckers using the ratio of anus-to-caudal-fin distance to head-to-anus distance (less than or more than two times). Subsequent couplets separate species by pigment on the nares, mouth position, horizontal myoseptum pigment, a basal oval internal spot (Gila robusta and Ptychocheilus lucius), and chromatophore size and distribution.
- **Problematic taxa:** Species of Pantosteus could not be separated; Catostomus insignis and C. latipinnis are weakly separated (C. latipinnis generally has less pigment). Suckers from small streams keying to C. latipinnis may provisionally be called C. insignis.
- **Plates and table:** Plates 1-4 illustrate lateral and dorsal views of postlarvae (e.g., Agosia chrysogaster 10.1 mm; Xyrauchen texanus 12.6 mm). Table 1 gives the total length at which key characters develop in minnows and suckers (e.g., Gila robusta spinal upturn at 9.0-9.5 mm; Catostomus insignis at 10.0-12.0 mm for certain traits).
