# Maturation of an off-channel habitat concept to conserve native fishes in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered_species #conservation_management

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*, Cyprinidae) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, Catostomidae) are endemic large-river fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Razorback sucker can live >40 years; a single female produces >200,000 ova per season. Bonytail matures at younger ages than razorback sucker. Both species exhibit high fecundity, no parental care, and historically supported large populations. Wild bonytail populations are functionally extinct; no self-sustaining razorback sucker populations exist despite reproduction occurring. The [[Lake Mohave]] razorback sucker population numbered ~50,000 wild adults in the late 1980s but has declined to ~5,000 repatriates by 2022. A "wild" population of uncertain origin persists in [[Lake Mead]], showing evidence of natural recruitment otherwise unknown for the species. Bonytail and razorback sucker are among 12 native warm-water species historically in the Lower Colorado River; half are federally endangered and five are extirpated.

**Habitat Associations**: Historically occupied mainstem Colorado River and large tributaries. Bonytail and razorback sucker utilized backwater and off-channel habitats. Current persistence depends on isolated off-channel habitats (OCHs) free of nonnative predators. Successful OCH examples include [[Cibola High Levee Pond]] (4.9 acres, cutoff oxbow), [[Yuma Cove Backwater]] (2.6 acres, natural lakeside cove on Lake Mohave), and [[Imperial Ponds]] (74.2 acres total across 6 artificial ponds). Razorback sucker prefers clean substrate (large sand to small cobble) for spawning. Bonytail uses riprap crevices for cover. OCH water supply via passive percolation through porous berms or active well pumping; passive flow risks nonnative invasion, active pumping allows temperature control.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Not extensively reported for current populations. Historical metapopulation structure implied connectivity across large river reaches. Current management involves periodic transfer of adults among OCHs and between OCHs and mainstem to maintain genetic diversity and prevent local differentiation. Bonytail tracked repeatedly to specific riprap crevices at Cibola High Levee Pond, indicating site fidelity. Remote PIT-tag scanning in Yuma Cove backwater contacted 90%–100% of fish later harvested, demonstrating effective individual tracking.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Predation by nonnative fishes (especially on larvae) is the primary cause of recruitment failure in both species. [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) preys on larval razorback sucker; overall impact unknown. Competitive and predatory nonnatives (e.g., ictalurid catfishes) are established throughout the system. Bonytail and razorback sucker do not persist in open waters populated by nonnative fishes. Nutrient limitation examined but deemed insignificant compared to predation impacts. OCHs must remain nonnative-free; chemical renovations and wedge-wire screens attempted with mixed success.

**Environmental Drivers**: Water physicochemistry critical in OCHs. Thresholds established: pH <9.0, temperature <33.3°C, dissolved oxygen >4.0 mg/L. Exceedance triggers mitigation (additional water inflow). Die-offs at Imperial Ponds (2020, 2022) resulted from high temperature, low dissolved oxygen, or human error/pump failure. Seasonal and annual trajectories of water chemistry, primary/secondary production highly variable and unpredictable. Flash flooding at lakeside sites flushed native fish into reservoirs and allowed nonnative invasion. Hypolimnetic releases from large dams are perennially cold and clear; hydrograph highly modified for electrical, agricultural, urban demands. Backwater and off-channel connectives largely eliminated by channelization and levee construction downstream of Blythe, California.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Bonytail listed endangered 1980; functionally extinct in wild by 1980s. Between 1991 and end of 2022, 483,224 bonytails stocked (98,172 into Lake Mohave, 284,392 into Lake Havasu, 99,591 into Lower Colorado River mainstem downstream of Lake Havasu, 1,069 into inland impoundments). Stockings into open waters show no long-term survival, reproduction, or recruitment; stockings into nonnative-free OCHs show survival, reproduction, recruitment. Razorback sucker listed endangered 1991. Between 1991 and end of 2022, 725,837 razorback suckers stocked (296,875 into Lake Mohave, 191,370 into Lake Havasu, 228,154 into Lower Colorado River mainstem, 9,438 into inland impoundments). Lake Mohave population maintained at ~10% of 50,000-adult goal through intensive larval collection, hatchery rearing, and repatriation. Yuma Cove backwater stocked with 200 razorback suckers in 2013; population reached >400 adults and hundreds to thousands of young-of-year within a few years, suggesting resource limitation. Apparent overabundance led to removal of 80 adults and 2,877 young-of-year in autumn 2019. No population of either species meets recovery criterion of being genetically and demographically viable and self-sustaining.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Habitat loss and modification plus nonnative species establishment are primary threats. Competitive and predatory nonnatives are single most important factor causing decline. No mitigation of these threats in mainstem habitats. Climate change, continued water development, and potential for new nonnative introductions compound threats. Stochastic events (flash floods, equipment failures, water quality excursions) can extirpate OCH populations. Variance in OCH productivity (spatial and temporal) increases extirpation risk. High variance in reproductive success (VRS) among adults reduces effective population size. Razorback sucker exhibits sexually dimorphic survival (males lower than females) post-stocking. Cannibalism of eggs/larvae documented in bonytail. Density-dependent effects in confined habitats not fully understood. Neither species likely to meet current quantitative recovery criteria; both destined to remain conservation-reliant.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Implement revised off-channel habitat (OCH) concept: establish self-sustaining populations in multiple predator-free habitats; exchange individuals between OCHs and (where feasible) mainstem to maintain genetic diversity and prevent divergence. OCH size: 2–5 surface acres optimal (large enough for viable population, small enough to manage). Larger ponds (>5 acres) more difficult to manage; no self-sustaining population established in OCH >5 acres to date. Ensure OCHs remain nonnative-free through chemical renovation, desiccation (if drainable), or secure water sources (well water preferred over passive flow or filtered river water). Maintain water physicochemistry within tolerable limits (pH <9.0, temperature <33.3°C, DO >4.0 mg/L); automate monitoring and mitigation systems to prevent human error. Provide habitat features: riprap/boulders for bonytail cover, gravel-cobble spawning substrate for razorback sucker. Transfer adults among OCHs when N<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>c</sub> (bonytail) or N<sub>e</sub>/N<sub>c</sub> (razorback sucker) <0.2, indicating high genetic risk. Transfer more females if sex ratio strongly female-biased. Preferentially transfer dominant cohorts to maintain age structure. Spread risk across multiple OCHs distributed throughout Lower Colorado River corridor to reflect inherent environmental variability. Remove consistently poor-performing OCHs from program; add consistently productive OCHs. For razorback sucker, use Lake Mohave larvae (genetically diverse) in lieu of hatchery production where possible. For bonytail, develop novel strategies for genetic management given absence of mainstem survival. LCR MSCP goal: create/protect 360 acres OCH for bonytail and razorback sucker; 158 acres completed by end of 2022, with 185 additional acres in development through 2026. Adaptive management essential: regular demographic and genetic monitoring to guide transfer schedules and management actions.

**Biological Reference Points**: Razorback sucker Lake Mohave population goal: 50,000 adults (currently ~5,000). Genetic effective size (N<sub>e</sub>) to census size (N<sub>c</sub>) ratio: N<sub>e</sub>/N<sub>c</sub> ≈ 0.1–1.0 for Lake Mohave razorback sucker (increased over 15-year time series with transition from wild to repatriated fish). Annual effective number of breeders (N<sub>b</sub>) to N<sub>c</sub> ratio: N<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>c</sub> = 0.72–0.86 for bonytail in ephemeral ponds (5 of 6 cases >85% of adults contributed offspring); N<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>c</sub> = 0.09–0.68 for razorback sucker in ephemeral ponds (13%–70% of females contributed offspring; reproduction failed in 2 of 5 years in one pond). Long-term historical female N<sub>e</sub> estimated ~90,000 for bonytail, ~700,000 for razorback sucker (based on coalescence time). Variance in reproductive success (VRS) inversely proportional to N<sub>e</sub>/N<sub>c</sub> or N<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>c</sub>; low VRS (near Poisson minimum, mean = 1 offspring per parent per generation) yields N<sub>e</sub>/N<sub>c</sub> ≈ 1. Bonytail exhibits lower VRS and higher N<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>c</sub> than razorback sucker. Management action threshold: N<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>c</sub> or N<sub>e</sub>/N<sub>c</sub> <0.2 indicates high genetic risk, triggering fish transfers or other interventions. Survival thresholds in ephemeral ponds: razorback sucker male harvest 6%–68% (mean 31%–35%), female harvest 16.7%–84% (mean 53%–59%) across 9 iterations. Yuma Cove backwater: 21 of 100 males, 78 of 100 females survived first year post-stocking (2013); population stabilized at ~400 adults by 2016. Carrying capacity not quantitatively determined for either species in OCHs; resource limitation suspected when populations rapidly overpopulate (1–2 years post-establishment in small ponds).
