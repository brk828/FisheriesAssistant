# Fishes and Climates

#ichthyology #marine-biology #paleoichthyology

## Executive Summary

This 1964 *Natural History* article by C. Lavett Smith argues that fossil fishes can serve as indicators of past climate. Because fishes have specific habitat requirements, an ichthyologist who can identify the fishes living in a region during a given geologic interval can infer the environmental conditions of that time. The method depends on linking fossils to living species, on sampling several coexisting species rather than one, and on careful stratigraphy. The main case study is the [[Berends Local Fauna]] from the Oklahoma panhandle, which points to a Pleistocene climate in the [[Illinoian Glaciation]] that was roughly like modern southern Wisconsin and much wetter than the present semiarid plains.

## Background & Description

Fish culture shows that species differ in habitat needs. Trout and salmon require cold, clear water. Pike and [[Largemouth Bass]] need weedy shallows. Catfishes and plains minnows favor sluggish, muddy rivers. This ecological specificity makes fossil fishes useful as climate proxies.

Several conditions limit the method:

- **Living counterparts:** A fossil must match, or be closely related to, a living species. Without this link, its habits cannot be inferred.
- **Preservation of older fishes:** Fish skeletons are fragile and perishable, so the record is sparse before the end of the [[Miocene]] (about 13 million years ago). Post-Miocene fishes are assumed to share the life requirements of their living relatives.
- **Sample size:** Conclusions require a large sample of the aquatic fauna, ideally five, ten or more coexisting species, possibly with terrestrial animal fossils. A single fish may be atypical, but a large assemblage is unlikely to be composed entirely of exceptions.
- **Identification:** Specialists sort fossils into groups (fish, mollusks, mammals) and identify them by comparison with living or other fossil material. Some fragments are diagnostic, while whole series of bones may lack diagnostic features.
- **Naming:** Fossils are not renamed simply because they are older. Unless skeletal evidence shows genetic difference, a fossil is assigned to an appropriate living species. Extinct forms are accepted only after [[Statistical Analysis]] shows that the differences exceed normal individual variation.

Fossil preservation on the High Plains is favorable. Many specimens are replaced by dense mineral substances over long periods, which can preserve fine details, and in some cases internal microscopic structure. Stout or dense bones are more likely to survive than thin, delicate ones.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study region.** Over the previous three decades, field crews from the Universities of Kansas and Michigan, directed by [[Claude W. Hibbard]] of the University of Michigan Museum of Paleontology, collected faunas from the [[High Plains]]. Significant faunas are known from Kansas, Nebraska, Oklahoma, Texas and other plains states. Mollusks, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals have been studied alongside fishes.

**Berends Local Fauna.** This Pleistocene fauna was quarried in 1953 from land owned by an Oklahoma rancher, in the [[Oklahoma Panhandle]]. It contains about twelve species, nine identified with reasonable certainty:

- [[Yellow Perch]]
- Gar (later noted as [[Alligator Gar]] in the comparative discussion)
- [[Common Sucker]]
- [[Black Bullhead]]
- [[Channel Catfish]]
- [[Muskellunge]]
- [[Green Sunfish]]
- Two kinds of minnow

The fishes were found in beds dating from Illinoian times, the third glacial age. Nearly all are close to, or identical with, living forms elsewhere on the continent. Only the black bullhead and green sunfish still live in that part of the High Plains.

**Present-day conditions.** Southwestern Kansas and the Oklahoma panhandle are semiarid, with a mean annual rainfall of less than twenty inches and marked wet and dry years. Streams are few and far between and may dry up completely. Artesian springs feed some streams but support only a few small fish species. Gars, muskellunge and channel catfish have no suitable habitat there apart from artificial ponds, and the common sucker is absent, possibly because of a lack of gravel spawning beds.

**Other evidence.** Spruce, fir and pine pollen occur in the same deposits as the fish, as do remains of the meadow vole and northern shrew. Collections from comparable-age beds at other localities support the Berends interpretation.

**Stratigraphy.** Reconstructing the sequence is difficult because no single site exposes all fossil-bearing strata, and erosion and terrain collapse have complicated the layers. Workers therefore assemble the chronology piece by piece. [[Pearlette Ash]], a volcanic ash layer that fell near the end of the [[Kansan Glaciation]], serves as a reference horizon. Beds below it are older than late Kansan times, and beds above it are younger.

## Key Findings

1. **Climate inference from the Berends fauna.** The presence of fossilized large river fishes (gar, muskellunge, channel catfish) indicates that southern Kansas and the Oklahoma panhandle were much more humid during Illinoian times. This could mean higher annual rainfall, or that the sparse rainfall was more effective because evaporation was slower under glacial temperatures.

2. **Overlap with modern northern ranges.** Muskellunge and yellow perch are northern fishes, and yellow perch do not range farther south than central Ohio. Overlaying the living counterparts' ranges shows overlap only in the southern Great Lakes region. The author concludes that the climate during the Berends fauna was much like that of present-day southern Wisconsin.

3. **Glacial and interglacial fluctuation.** A cooling accompanying the advance of the Illinoian ice sheet would have allowed cold-water perch and muskellunge to extend their ranges southward. As the ice receded (generally four major glacial advances are recognized), cool-climate forms would have been replaced by warm-water southern species such as [[Alligator Gar]] and [[Buffalo Fish]].

4. **Fishes are scarce in interglacial deposits.** Because the region became warmer and drier between glaciations, fishes are seldom prominent in interglacial fossil faunas. The Illinoian fauna is currently the only significant fish fauna known from the area. Mammalian faunas, by contrast, document warm periods (zebras, camels, badgers, ground sloths) and cool periods (mammoths, beavers, northern shrews, northern grasshopper mice) across all major glacial advances and retreats.

5. **Pliocene and earlier records.** Fossil fishes also contribute to pre-Pleistocene climate history. Summary tables list faunas by epoch, including Pliocene taxa such as bullhead, white sucker, crappie and buffalo fish, alongside mammals and tortoises. The table is best consulted in the original article.

6. **Methodological caveats.** Fossil faunas remain hard to interpret because of patchy sites, complex stratigraphy and the need to confirm identifications statistically. The author stresses that conclusions become more reliable as the number of coexisting species grows.
