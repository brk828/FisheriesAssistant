# Survival Potential of Piranha in Southern California Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #characidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 California Department of Fish and Game administrative report, authored by [[James A. St. Amant]] of Region 5 (Inland Fisheries), assesses whether [[Piranha]] could survive and establish reproducing populations in southern California. It is a literature-based compendium supplemented by interviews, correspondence with researchers in the United States and South America, and aquarium observations; no formal biological experiments were conducted. The author concludes that piranhas pose a demonstrable threat to humans, fish and wildlife, and that an analysis of available temperature data indicates they could survive and become established in some southern California waters. The report recommends against introduction and outlines further studies.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** Piranhas belong to the family [[Characidae]] (characins), which contains about 1,000 described species divided into nine subfamilies; piranhas fall within the [[Serrasalminae]]. Taxonomy is described as confused: sources disagree on the number of genera (two to three, with Serrasalmus alone variously estimated at 17 to over 40 species in total across genera). The natural range is generally considered to lie east of the Andes through the [[Orinoco River]] and [[Amazon River]] basins to northeastern Argentina.

**Morphology.** Piranhas have a deep, compressed body, small scales (none on the head), a serrated ventral keel formed by overlapping abdominal scales, a long-based dorsal fin (14 or more rays), an anal fin of 29 to 37 rays, and a single row of close-set, shearing teeth (12 upper, usually 14 lower, per Gosline 1951). Similar genera, including [[Colossoma]], [[Mylossoma]], [[Myleus]], [[Metynnis]] and [[Piaractus]], may be distinguished by fin and dental characters; dental examination may be needed for small fish.

**Coloration.** Color varies widely; some species have a black edge on the tail fin (e.g., S. spilopleura).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and behavior.** Piranhas prefer quiet, clear backwaters and deep pools and avoid rapids. Spawning occurs in all months, with eggs laid on submerged vegetation or in shallow nests near banks. Eggs are adhesive and the young cling together.

**Thermal tolerance.** The central question is winter survival. Reported observations include:
- Piranhas found under ice (Axelrod, 1965).
- Fish occurring in waters with winter temperatures of 46 to 57 °F (Calhoun correspondence, 1955).
- A S. spilopleura held without heat through a winter at Marineland, kept in the 60s °F.
- Lab work by Braker: 3-month-old S. spilopleura became inactive at 55 °F and died after several days, with only one week of acclimatization.
- Brazilian reports of survival when water stayed near 4 to 8 °C during air frosts below 0 °C.

**Southern California reservoir temperatures.** Tables A-1 to A-11 provide surface water temperature data. Several reservoirs (e.g., Barrett, Sutherland, San Vicente, Lower Otay) seldom drop below about 50 °F, and some exceed 70 °F for one to seven months annually. Ramer Lake (Imperial County) recorded maxima of 98 °F. Colorado River minimum temperatures ranged from about 50 to 72 °F (1959–1963).

**Introduced species as analogues.** The report documents successful establishment of exotic fishes in California and elsewhere, including [[Common Carp]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Sailfin Molly]], [[Tilapia Mossambica]], [[Guppy]], [[Rainwater Fish]] and the [[Mexican Banded Tetra]]. Tilapia is established in a Salton Sea tributary, and the carp is cited as a major pest of nongame fish.

**Impacts on fisheries and wildlife.** Fontenele (1963) reported that in Brazil's Acarau Dam, after piranhas were eradicated, fishing licenses earned $29,465 and fish yield was 3,323 metric tons (May 1960–May 1961). The piranha-containing Estevam Morinho Dam produced only $12,860 in licenses and 1,336 tons. Piranhas also reportedly attacked cattle, ducks and geese in Brazil and Argentina.

## Key Findings

- **Threat to humans:** Multiple documented attacks are reported, including a Bolivian fatality, 21 guerrillas killed in the Arauca River (Venezuela, 1966), a 16-year-old boy wounded in Paraiba State, and bites to researchers and aquarists (e.g., a 2.5-inch S. spilopleura bite requiring treatment; a Serrasalmus bite needing 5 stitches). Accounts vary: some natives bathe safely, and behavior appears to depend on temperature, species and season.
- **Threat to fish and wildlife:** In aquarium trials, a 2.2-inch Serrasalmus destroyed seven red-ear sunfish (averaging 3 inches) in 15 days; a larger individual destroyed four in four days. Piranhas killed other fish, a soft-shelled turtle was bitten, and intraspecific cannibalism was observed.
- **Reproduction:** Captive spawning succeeded with S. spilopleura and S. niger. Reported clutches exceeded 1,000 eggs, with spawnings every 7 to 24 days and up to 5,000 adhesive eggs per spawning. Parental guarding was documented.
- **Growth and longevity:** Young S. spilopleura reached 1.75 inches in 3.5 months. Two S. niger lived more than 20 years at the Shedd Aquarium, reaching over 18 inches and 5.5 pounds. One S. spilopleura survived four months without food.
- **Survival potential:** The author concludes that piranhas could survive and establish in some waters, particularly certain Salton Sea tributaries, Imperial County waters, sections of the Colorado River, and low-elevation San Diego County reservoirs.
- **Conclusion and recommendation:** Piranhas would be "an undesirable addition" to California's fauna. Regulations preventing their introduction are "well justified." The author concurs with Calhoun that the risk is "out of all proportion to any benefits."
- **Recommended research:** (1) proper identification and collection of all piranha species as test fish; (2) an extensive limnological study of native waters, especially the La Plata area; (3) at least two years of study on temperature tolerance, acclimatization and natural selection.
- **Limited uses:** Piranhas have minor angling value, some food value for Amazonian Indians, and use as aquarium specimens only.
