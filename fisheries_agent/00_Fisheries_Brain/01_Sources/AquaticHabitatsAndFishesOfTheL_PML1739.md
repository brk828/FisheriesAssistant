# Aquatic Habitats and Fishes of the Lower Colorado River: Mohave Valley Division

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This appendix describes the aquatic habitats, fishes, benthic invertebrates, aquatic plants and algae, pigments, and physico-chemical conditions of the Mohave Valley Division of the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The reach runs about 67 km from the tailrace of [[Davis Dam]] (RM 422.0) to RM 464.0, and is the most upstream of the river segments studied. Sampling took place in spring 1974 and late summer to early autumn 1975, with additional 1975-76 netting and 1972 data cited for comparison.

The fish fauna is sparse. Of 19 species recorded from the division, only 13 were captured during sampling. Most sportfishing pressure is concentrated elsewhere, and the main channel yielded almost no fishes. The benthic community is diverse just below the dam and shifts to low-diversity oligochaete and chironomid assemblages downstream. Water temperature, dissolved oxygen, and conductance are strongly shaped by dam releases.

## Background & Description

- **Study reach:** The division begins at the base of Davis Dam and is heavily modified. About 65% of the shoreline has been physically altered by channelization, levees, or rock fill. Backwaters that open to the stream are small and shallow.
- **Lentic habitat:** Less than about 1% of the surface area at maximum discharge was estimated to be lentic habitat (value printed as "less than 1.02" in the source). [[Topock Marsh]], the largest backwater on the lower river, was excluded from this calculation. It is controlled by inflow and outflow canals, and only its lower portion is directly accessible from the river channel. No specific work was done there.
- **Substrate:** Channel bottoms range from boulder and gravel near the dam to sand-silt downstream. Backwater bottoms are variably organic, with more organic material in older habitats.
- **Flow regime:** Discharges serve hydroelectric generation and downstream irrigation and municipal deliveries. The 24-hour minimum discharge averages about 400 m³/s. Maximum releases are given in the source as a garbled figure (approximately "1,?75" m³/s). Flow may be stopped if necessary.
- **Depth:** Depths exceed 9.5 m in isolated parts of the channel, especially near the tailrace, and exceed 4.0 m elsewhere. Backwaters rarely exceed 2.5 m at highest water levels.
- **Study-period flows:** Discharges were low during the study periods, which made navigation difficult and dried many backwaters, at least at night.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fisheries and human use.** A substantial sport fishery targets [[Striped Bass]] seasonally and [[Rainbow Trout]] introduced into the Davis Dam tailrace. A large stretch of the main channel, roughly 2 to 50 km downstream, receives comparatively little fishing pressure. This pattern reflects the concentration of large fishes below the dam and of anglers at Bullhead City, Arizona, and Needles, California. [[Topock Marsh]] supports relatively large numbers of anglers, mainly for [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]], plus a few [[Black Crappie]].

**Benthic community.** Samples from coarse substrates above the dam-influenced reach showed high diversity. Taxa such as Elmidae (beetles), Simuliidae and Tipulidae (flies), mayflies, and Turbellaria are characteristic of cool, swiftly flowing water. Filter feeders such as hydropsychid caddisflies and [[Asiatic Clam]] beds depend on plankton exported from [[Lake Mohave]]. Diversity fell sharply about 10 km below the dam, where the sand-silt bottom became dominant. Oligochaetes and chironomids then dominated, and shifting sand-silt bottoms supported no organisms.

**Aquatic vegetation and algae.** Riparian and aquatic plants were not specifically studied. Emergent species recorded include cattail, diverse sedges, and sparse water-pennywort. Submergent species included sago pondweed, coontail, and a few small beds of hornwort and spiny naiad. Macroscopic algae in the channel included Cladophora, diatom films, and blue-green mats (chiefly Oscillatoria, Phormidium, and Spirulina). Drifting algae and higher plants were scarcely discernible, probably because of the early spring and autumn-winter sampling dates.

**Physico-chemical drivers.** Water released from the hypolimnion of [[Lake Mohave]] creates a constant thermal input that overrides local heating. Temperatures respond quickly to solar input, while [[Dissolved Oxygen]] changes more slowly and depends on mixing. Dissolved oxygen rises downstream as partly deoxygenated reservoir water gains oxygen. Daytime photosynthesis produced diel cycling at RM 428.3, but the pattern was inconsistent at RM 453.5. Backwaters showed a trend toward higher temperature, dissolved oxygen, and [[Specific Conductance]], with reduced phosphate and nitrate.

## Key Findings

### Fishes
- **Species richness:** 19 species were recorded from the division, and 13 were captured during the sampling periods.
- **Main channel:** Sampling produced essentially no fishes at six seined mainstream localities. [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Red Shiner]] were seen, and one [[Rainbow Trout]] was seen at RM 423.0, but none was caught.
- **Backwaters, 1974-75 seining:** Carp, largemouth bass, and black crappie were captured. Estimated density was 962 threadfin shad (18.9 kg/ha), 1,409 red shiners (15.5 kg/ha), and 91 young-of-the-year black crappie. The source's line ordering suggests these pairings, but the mapping is not fully explicit.
- **1972 backwater sampling:** Yields were 1,143 threadfin shad (25.6 kg/ha), 623 bluegill (14.7 kg/ha), 16 largemouth bass (23.9 kg/ha), and 4 black crappie (1.7 kg/ha), near the Topock Marsh inflow (RM 463.7). Channel catfish and bluegill were also reported at 2.1 kg/ha in 1972.
- **Gear results:**
  - 1972 hoop nets in the channel (11 net nights) caught carp, channel catfish, and largemouth bass.
  - 1975-76 hoop nets (12 net nights) and mainstream entanglement netting (10 nights) caught no fish.
  - 1974-75 entanglement netting in deeper backwaters (10 days, 11 nights) caught 14 carp weighing 17.2 kg in total (1.56 kg per night).
- **Diet of striped bass:** Threadfin shad made up 1,439 of 1,476 food items in 55 stomachs containing food. Rainbow trout, largemouth bass, green sunfish, and carp were also eaten. Edwards (1974) noted that many ingested shad were moribund or dead after passing through the penstocks of Davis Dam.
- **Native and sensitive fishes:** One native species, [[Razorback Sucker]], remains in the vicinity. [[Bonytail Chub]] persisted below Davis Dam at least until 1972 (Minckley et al., 1976).
- **Other species:** Black bullhead, yellow bullhead, mosquitofish, striped bass, and green sunfish were recorded from qualitative sampling or angler reports.

### Benthos
- **Surber samples (upper reach):** High diversity was found in coarse substrates. Surber samples in the first 13 km below the dam averaged about 310 invertebrates per m² (value printed as "31015" in the source).
- **Ekman dredge samples:** Four samples from the same stretch averaged about 1,131 individuals (value printed as "LrL31").
- **Downstream decline:** Numbers and diversity fell by about 10 km below the dam. Distant segments showed contrasting numbers, with the last segment yielding 242 organisms per sample set (no dredge samples taken).
- **Biomass:** [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula*) dominated weights, comprising 0.0 to 99.9% of total weight. Excluding it, biomass was 0.01 to 2.26 kg/ha (dry weight) from Surber samples and 0.03 to 4.56 kg/ha from Ekman dredge samples.
- **Backwater benthos:** Six Ekman samples yielded low-diversity assemblages dominated by chironomids and oligochaetes, plus snails, ostracods, and occasional turbellarians. Clams occurred in 5 of 6 samples at 0.4 to 294.0 kg/ha. Numbers ranged from 172 to 2,450 per m², with average biomass of 0.78 kg/ha.

### Pigments
- **Chlorophyll A:** Concentrations were low, with mean values within one order of magnitude at all sampling localities.
- **Chlorophylls B and C:** These varied widely, from non-detectable to magnitudes I and II.
- **Carotenoids:** Less variable, with no clear upstream-downstream trend.
- **Diatom index:** Remained at magnitude II at all points and dates. Hourly data showed little meaningful variation.
- **Drift interpretation:** Downstream chlorophyll A was about twice the upstream value in the 2-hour comparisons, but differences were not significant. The authors attributed this to rapid water transit through the division, which limits accumulation of drifting organisms.

### Physico-Chemical Conditions
- **Temperature:** Two 24-hour cycles at 10.0 km (RM 428.3) and 50.5 km (RM 453.6) showed diel heating and cooling. The downstream station peaked later in the day, which is typical of water gaining solar exposure through a channel.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Full saturation (100%) was generally reached about 12 to 15 km below the dam.
- **Conductance:** The main channel ranged from 900 to 1,000 µmhos/cm, rising to about 1,400 µmhos/cm directly below the Topock Marsh inflow (RM 463.7). That marsh effect was confined to the Arizona side and was diluted within 0.5 km.
- **pH:** Limited data indicated a range of about 7.7 to 8.0 from upstream to downstream.
- **Phosphate (PO₄-P):** Present in 49 of 53 mainstream samples. Forty-seven samples were below 0.10 mg/L, and only 3 exceeded 0.20 mg/L.
- **Nitrate (NO₃-N):** All 53 samples contained at least 0.01 mg/L. Twenty-eight ranged from 0.10 to 0.19 mg/L, and 23 from 0.20 to 0.26 mg/L (the highest value recorded).
- **Backwaters:** Data were sparse. Surface-to-bottom profiles showed little variation.
