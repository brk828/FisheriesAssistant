# Fishes of the Great Basin and the Colorado Plateau: Past and Present Forms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by John W. Sigler and William F. Sigler, is an annotated checklist of every fish species and subspecies known to occur naturally or through stocking in the [[Great Basin]] and [[Colorado Plateau]], excluding the Gila River Basin. It catalogues 190 taxa, of which 109 (57%) are native and 81 (43%) are introduced. Human modification of habitat and introduction of exotic species have caused major losses. The authors report that 44% of native fishes are legally protected, and they argue that no fish species should be allowed to go extinct.

## Background & Description

The chapter is part of the 1994 volume *Natural History of the Colorado Plateau and Great Basin*. It covers a region of hundreds of square miles of desert habitat, with fragile, geographically isolated ecosystems that each hold unique fishes.

The region contains two major drainages:

- **[[Lahontan Basin]]**, largely in Nevada, with five notable subdivisions.
- **[[Bonneville Basin]]**, primarily in Utah, extending into Idaho and Nevada.

The Colorado Plateau covers roughly the eastern half of Utah and parts of Colorado, New Mexico, and Arizona, with tributaries arising in Wyoming.

The Table 9.1 checklist is organized phylogenetically by order, family, genus, species and subspecies. Each entry gives native or introduced status, basin codes, legal status, authority and date. Sample annotations cover range, stocking history, habitat tolerance, threats and causes of failure.

Key taxonomic and status categories:

- **Native (\*)** and **introduced (#)** status, with basin codes (O, L, N, R, D, B, C).
- **Federal status:** threatened (T) or endangered (E) under the Endangered Species Act.
- **State and American Fisheries Society status:** listed separately.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Human-driven range change.** Fish movement across the landscape is almost entirely due to people. Natural dispersal events (stream capture, birds carrying eggs, hurricane-force winds) are estimated at fewer than one in 1,000 transport episodes. Major pathways include:

- Live-tank stocking of fishes seined from Midwest floodwaters, in the decades after the Civil War.
- Repeated stocking of [[Common Carp]] from 1831 until near the end of the century, promoted by federal and state agencies.
- Bait-bucket dumping, aquarium releases and deliberate "improvement" stockings by anglers and some agencies.

**Water development.** Reservoirs, ponds and irrigation canals were built on natural systems, directly harming native fishes and creating habitat for introduced species. The [[Colorado River]] has been dammed and diverted for water and power more than any river in North America except the [[Columbia River]]. Its fauna evolved under a fast, turbid, variable flow regime that no longer exists.

**Why stockings fail.** The authors identify several causes of failure:

1. Unsuitable habitat, the most common cause (spawning ground, cover, food, water quality or disease).
2. Stocked numbers too low for viable reproduction.
3. Handling and transport stress, with fish arriving unhealthy.
4. Predation on disoriented fish.
5. Inability to use food sources or compete with residents.

**Success of introduced species.** Common carp show what the authors call "physiological adaptability," ranging across temperature, water quality, altitude and interspecific interactions.

## Key Findings

**Diversity and status**

- 190 species and subspecies in total.
- Currently 100 native and 60 exotic taxa (160 present, 62% native).
- 21 stocked fishes (26% of introduced taxa) are no longer present.
- 9 native fishes (8%) are extinct.
- 33% of native fishes hold federal protection: 9 threatened and 24 endangered.
- An additional 11% are protected by states.
- 44 native fishes (44%) require special protection, and the American Fisheries Society recommends protection for two more.

**Notable species and places**

- **[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*):** a specialized minnow of the Colorado River, adapted to turbulent flows. Dams created deep, cold, stratified reservoirs. The chub is endangered and "may be doomed." [[Bonytail]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] face similar pressures.
- **[[Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor*):** a highly adaptable Lahontan minnow whose many populations were once described as separate species by J. O. Snyder. Subspecies differ in status, from the endangered Owens and Mojave tui chubs to the extinct Independence Valley form.
- **[[Pyramid Lake]] and [[Derby Dam]] (1905):** a transbasin diversion of the [[Truckee River]] to the Carson River Basin cut Pyramid Lake's inflow. Lake levels fell so far in the 1930s that the [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] could not spawn upstream, and most of the population was gone by 1938. The [[Cui-ui]] could not cross the resulting delta in drought years.
- **[[Bear Lake]] (Utah–Idaho):** four endemics (Bear Lake sculpin, Bear Lake whitefish, Bonneville whitefish, Bonneville cisco). The cisco is the most numerous fish in the lake.
- **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*):** endemic to a 23-square-yard habitat, the smallest known range of any vertebrate. It is endangered.
- **[[Salt Creek Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon salinus*):** lives in Death Valley waters reaching about 130°F, between 180 and 249 feet below sea level.
- **[[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*):** one of six New World minnows with spiny rays. It is endangered and now survives only in the Virgin River below LaVerkin, Utah.
- **Virgin River and Little Colorado spinedaces:** threatened by water demands for agriculture and by competition from the introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*).

**Introduced species of concern**

- Common carp: stocked in the United States from the 1800s, now present in 47 of the 48 contiguous states, with the authors calling them "at best a mixed blessing." The U.S. record weight is 56 pounds.
- Red shiner: abundant across much of the upper Colorado Basin, tolerant of turbidity and warm water, and competes with native minnows.
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: stocked widely; desert spring populations are mostly stunted, and the species may have caused the extinction of the Independence Valley tui chub.
- Gizzard shad: stocked as forage in 1990. Successful establishment in Willard Reservoir.
- Threadfin shad: stocked in Lake Mead in 1953 and Lake Powell in 1968, then reduced by introduced game fish predation.

**Stocking success stories**

- Striped bass: a few transplanted individuals on the West Coast produced a major fishery, and Lake Powell now supports a reproducing population.
- Rainbow trout: the most widely stocked trout in North America.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- Indiscriminate stocking is illegal in some situations and strongly discouraged by resource agencies and ichthyologists.
- Species diversity in the region is lower than in the Midwest's great rivers, owing to fewer nutrients and a narrower range of conditions.
- The authors' central recommendation is that "no fish species be allowed to go extinct."
- Many species, both native and exotic, have survived human modifications, and the authors see this as significant.
