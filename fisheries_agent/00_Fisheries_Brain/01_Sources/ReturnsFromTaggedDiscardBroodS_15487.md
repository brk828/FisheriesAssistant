## Executive Summary
This study by James W. Mullan (Massachusetts Division of Fisheries and Game) examines the fate of large, "discard" brood stock trout (12–28 inches, ages 3–7 years) stocked annually in Massachusetts streams and ponds between 1951 and 1954. Approximately 1,500–3,000 brood [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Rainbow Trout]] were released each year to evaluate angler claims of "carryover" trout and to assess the suitability of such fish for stocking marginal waters. Fish were marked using [[Cheek Tag|cheek tags]] (1951–1952) and jaw tags made of monel metal (1953–1954). Recovery rates ranged widely (3–49%), and the study found that discard brood trout were caught quickly after stocking or disappeared entirely, with virtually no evidence of overwinter survival ("carryover") into subsequent seasons.

## Background & Description
- **Species included:** *Salvelinus fontinalis* (brook trout), *Salmo trutta* (brown trout), *Salmo gairdnerii* (rainbow trout).
- **Size class:** Brood stock fish measured 12–28 inches in length — substantially larger than standard hatchery releases, reflecting their origin as retired breeding stock.
- **Age class:** 3–7 years old, aged out of the hatchery's [[Selective Breeding]] program.
- **Distinguishing note:** Superficial morphology of hatchery brood fish could not be reliably differentiated from wild trout, complicating angler-based claims of "carryover" survival.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** Released into a mix of Massachusetts streams and ponds/lakes, representing lotic and lentic freshwater systems.
- **Stocking timing:** Liberation generally occurred prior to the open fishing season, positioning fish for immediate angler exploitation rather than establishment.
- **Post-release behavior:** Discard brood trout, especially [[Brook Trout]], were characterized as "relatively easy to catch," consistent with a hatchery-reared history and reduced predator avoidance ("pampered existence").
- **Movement/dispersal:** One notable exception recorded — a rainbow trout stocked in the Westfield River (1951) was recovered nearly a year later in the Hammonasset River, Connecticut, a coastal stream reachable only via the Connecticut River and Long Island Sound (~120 miles), indicating occasional long-distance dispersal capability despite overall poor persistence.
- **Interaction with fishery:** Fish functioned largely as a short-term "put-and-take" resource rather than contributing to a self-sustaining or overwintering population in these marginal waters.

## Key Findings
- **Tagging methods used:**
  - 1951–1952: [[Cheek Tag|Cheek tags]] (Cable, 1950) — 5/16-inch rivets with 0.020-inch-thick red plastic discs (per [[Stroud 1953]]).
  - 1953–1954: Monel metal jaw tags.
  - Anesthesia: 0.5% urethane solution used to immobilize most trout during tagging.
- **Recovery rates:** Overall tag return ranged from 3% to 49% depending on year, species, and water type (stream vs. pond).
  - Aggregate totals (1951–54): Rainbow trout ~16.8% (streams) / 12.2% (ponds); Brook trout ~20.7% (streams) / 23.6% (ponds); Brown trout ~10.4% (streams) / 18.4% (ponds).
- **Tag performance issues:** Cheek tags used in 1951–1952 suffered significant unrecorded losses due to fouling in dip-net and seine twine during handling/distribution — a limitation previously noted by Stroud (1953), despite the cheek tag otherwise proving valid for large brood trout.
- **Angler non-response:** An unquantifiable but likely significant source of under-reporting affecting total harvest estimates.
- **Carryover findings:** Second-year recovery (fish stocked one year and caught in a later season) was "practically zero," with only a single documented exception (the Westfield River rainbow trout recovered in Connecticut). This strongly undermines angler claims that large brood stock trout contribute meaningfully to carryover populations in Massachusetts waters.
- **Management implication:** Large discard brood trout function primarily as immediate "put-and-take" fishery supplements rather than viable contributors to sustained wild or naturalized trout populations in marginal waters, due to rapid harvest or disappearance post-stocking.
- **External constraints:** Korean War-era metal shortages and limited time/manpower prevented complete tagging coverage of all brood stock in 1951 and 1953, introducing gaps in the dataset.
