# Growth Data on Sport Fishes in Twelve Kansas Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study reports age and growth data for nine species of [[Game Fish]] collected from 1952 to 1956 in twelve of Kansas's more important lakes and reservoirs. Ages were determined by [[Scale Reading]], and average total lengths at the end of each growing season are tabulated for each lake. The authors group the lakes into "clear" and "turbid" waters and into two large [[Flood-Control Reservoirs]], then compare abundance and growth among these habitat types. Growth comparisons between habitats were possible for only a few species, and the authors note that growth in at least one lake had changed by the time of publication.

## Background & Description

The fish were collected by personnel of the [[State Biological Survey]], mainly Mr. W. J. Davis, using:

- [[Gill Netting]] (3/4-inch to 2-inch bar mesh)
- Hoop and fyke nets (1-inch bar mesh)
- [[Rotenone Sampling]]

Most fish from [[Lone Star Lake]] were taken by hook-and-line by Mr. J. S. Carey.

**Study lakes (12):** Farlington (Crawford County State Lake No. 2), Gardner Lake, Lone Star Lake, Prairie Lake, Tonganoxie (Leavenworth County State Lake), Lake Wabaunsee, Wyandotte County Lake, Herington Lake, Nemaha County State Lake, Ottawa County State Lake, [[Cedar Bluff Reservoir]] and [[Kanopolis Reservoir]].

**Age and length methods:**
- Ages are the number of growing seasons completed, read from scales using a reader described by Van Oosten, Deason and Jobes (1934).
- Fish captured November 1 to June 1 were measured at capture total length and treated as being between growing seasons.
- Fish captured June 1 to November 1 had total length back-calculated to the last annulus by direct proportion, using scale measurements.

The numbers of fish in the growth table reflect the subset used for growth estimates, not relative abundance. Sampling effort differed among lakes, and many more fish were caught than were aged.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake types:**
- **Clear lakes (7):** Farlington, Gardner, Lone Star, Prairie, Tonganoxie, Wabaunsee and Wyandotte are comparatively deep and clear.
- **Turbid lakes (3):** Herington, Nemaha and Ottawa are shallow and usually turbid.
- **Flood-control reservoirs (2):** Cedar Bluff and Kanopolis lie on the [[Smoky Hill River]]. They are much deeper than the other lakes and have large shallow areas. Their turbidity varies.

**Dominant species by lake (as reported):**
- Farlington: bluegill, largemouth bass, white crappie, channel catfish
- Gardner: white crappie, bluegill, black bullhead, golden shiner
- Lone Star: bluegill, largemouth bass, white crappie
- Prairie: white crappie, channel catfish, bluegill
- Tonganoxie: white crappie, bluegill, largemouth bass, carp
- Wabaunsee: white crappie, drum, common sucker, bluegill, channel catfish
- Wyandotte: white crappie, black bullhead, bluegill
- Herington: carp
- Nemaha: river carpsucker, white crappie, channel catfish
- Ottawa: white crappie, channel catfish, carp

**Habitat-related abundance patterns:**
- [[Centrarchids]] (largemouth bass, bluegill, green sunfish, longear sunfish, [[Warmouth]], white and black crappie) were much more abundant in the seven clear lakes than in the turbid lakes or reservoirs.
- [[Carp]], [[River Carpsucker]] and [[Channel Catfish]] were more abundant in turbid lakes.
- Channel catfish were moderately common in Farlington, Prairie and Wabaunsee because of frequent stocking with adults.
- Lake Wabaunsee supports unusually large numbers of mayflies, fingernail clams and crayfish, and it had the largest drum sample.

## Key Findings

**Growth by habitat**
- [[Drum]] grew faster in clear lakes than in turbid lakes or reservoirs, and were most abundant in Lake Wabaunsee.
- [[White Crappie]] grew at nearly the same rate in clear and turbid waters, though they were more abundant in clear lakes.
- The fastest crappie growth recorded was in turbid Nemaha Lake in 1954, when crappie were scarce there. Later work by Ward found that crappie had increased greatly in Nemaha Lake and that growth had become much slower than the Table I values.
- Few species had enough fish from both habitat types to allow firm comparisons.

**Scope and limits**
- The paper contains growth tables for all nine species across the twelve lakes. Some entries are based on very small samples (n = 1 to 3).
- The table reports the number of fish at each age, not their relative abundance in each lake.
- The OCR text is imperfect in places, so a few values in the table may be misread.
