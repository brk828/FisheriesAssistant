# Distribution, Abundance and Habitat of the Little Colorado Spinedace in East Clear Creek

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1992 Arizona Game and Fish Department report assesses the distribution, abundance and habitat of the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] ([[Lepidomeda Vittata]]), a federally listed threatened cyprinid, across [[East Clear Creek]] and its tributaries in the [[Coconino National Forest]] and [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]]. Field surveys from May 1990 to November 1991 covered roughly 94.7 stream miles. Spinedace were absent from the 31 miles of designated [[Critical Habitat]] and from nearly all historic localities. The only surviving population found was confined to isolated pools in the [[Leonard Canyon]] system, centred on [[Dines Tank]]. The authors attribute the decline to habitat loss and drought-driven water scarcity, combined with predation and competition from [[Nonnative Fish Species]]. They recommend reevaluating the species' federal status, developing a recovery plan, protecting Dines Tank as a refugium, and pursuing genetic and life-history research.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] is a small cyprinid endemic to eastern Arizona. Its historic range included north-flowing tributaries of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and the upper LCR, and possibly the Zuni River in New Mexico, though no recent records exist there.
- **Historic distribution:** Miller (1963) found the species in East Clear Creek during 1959–1962 but not in other major north-flowing tributaries or the LCR. Minckley and Carufel (1967) documented apparent re-invasion between 1963 and 1966, and noted fish that disappeared from localities within hours to weeks of sampling.
- **Listing:** The species was listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1987). Critical Habitat covers 31 miles of East Clear Creek, from its headwaters to four miles below the 96 Road crossing, excluding [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]] and adjacent private lands.
- **Morphology and reproduction:** Smallest mature male recorded was 50 mm. Sexual dimorphism is minimal. Females can carry roughly 910 to 5,600 eggs, depending on size. Spawning peaks in spring, with additional developing ovaries noted in summer and autumn.
- **Diet:** Spinedace feed predominantly on aquatic arthropods, including insects such as Diptera, Ephemeroptera and Trichoptera. Blinn and Runck (1990) also reported amphipods, leeches, cladocerans, snails and crayfish in laboratory feeding trials. Stomach analysis of fish from Nutrioso and Chevelon creeks (May–June 1990) showed mainly insect larvae and nymphs, plus crustaceans and filamentous algae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preference:** Earlier studies describe the species in clear, flowing water over fine gravel, often beneath overhanging banks or large rocks. It has also been found in headwater streams with silt substrates and in stagnant pools, indicating a broad habitat tolerance. In East Clear Creek, the authors could not define preferences because all remaining fish occurred in one large, murky, isolated pool.
- **Water availability:** Surface flow in East Clear Creek was largely discontinuous or absent. Aerial surveys of 61 miles of Forest Service land (single-engine aircraft) found 16 miles with virtually no water and 45 miles with permanent water.
- **Disturbance:** Grazing, road access, and dam operations were recorded throughout the drainage. Heavy grazing was noted at Dines Tank, and erosion, silt and degraded water quality were observed at several sites. The authors also raised possible pollution impacts near Jones Crossing and 96 Crossing.
- **Predators and competitors:** Eight nonnative species were collected: [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Plains Killifish]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]]. Where rainbow trout were abundant, spinedace were absent. Blinn and Runck (1990) showed that rainbow trout and green sunfish readily preyed on spinedace in the laboratory.
- **Other fauna:** Native species that persisted included [[Speckled Dace]] and medium to large suckers. The authors noted that [[Roundtail Chub]] were collected at one reach in the lower drainage.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Approximately 94.7 stream miles in the LCR drainage were sampled on foot between May 1990 and November 1991.
- Sampling used timed [[Electrofishing]] with a Smith-Root BP4 backpack unit (approx. 0.35–0.5 amps), supplemented by seining in some sites.
- Field effort was about 88 person-days in 1990 and 44 person-days in 1991.
- Site-level locality and status data were entered into the Arizona Game and Fish Department's Heritage Data Management System for [[Section 7 Consultation]] purposes.

**Distribution and abundance**
- No spinedace were found in the 31 miles of Critical Habitat on East Clear Creek.
- All perennial tributaries of East Clear Creek were surveyed. Spinedace occurred only in isolated pools within a 2–3 mile segment of Leonard Canyon and West Leonard Canyon, upstream to Dines Tank.
- Sites with historic records, including Macks Crossing, Hamilton Crossing, 96 Crossing and Kinder Crossing, yielded no spinedace in 1990–1991.
- Dines Tank produced one adult and several fry in 1990. By November 1991, 25 individuals were recorded there, and the authors noted that numbers had apparently increased.

**Dines Tank**
- Dines Tank is a large pool, about 200 m long and 25 m wide, that holds permanent water during drought when the rest of the stream is dry.
- In 1983, spinedace made up 51 percent of the total catch. In 1990, they comprised under 10 percent.
- Fathead minnows rose from under 5 percent of the catch in 1983 to 12–70 percent in 1990. Authors caution that differing collection methods may bias these comparisons.

**Factors limiting recovery**
- Habitat degradation and water scarcity along upper East Clear Creek may have extirpated the species there. In 1983 spinedace were the second most common fish in that reach, but in 1990 no fish of any species were found.
- Exotic predators and competitors are abundant, and the fathead minnow now occupies the entire historic range of the species in the drainage. Its food habits are closer to those of spinedace, making it a potential major competitor.
- Dines Tank is vulnerable because it is isolated for much of the year and subject to grazing impacts.

**Recommendations**
1. Reevaluate the federal status of the Little Colorado spinedace in light of the marked range reduction.
2. Develop a recovery plan that includes stocking and reintroduction, with Macks Crossing identified as suitable former habitat once exotics are controlled.
3. Designate Dines Tank as a refugium. Protect its water quantity, quality and riparian conditions from human activity and livestock.
4. Survey Leonard Canyon below Dines Tank more thoroughly and conduct a genetic assessment of the remaining East Clear Creek population.
5. Investigate life history, large-scale movements, re-invasion after drought, refugia, and preferred habitat through more quantitative study.
