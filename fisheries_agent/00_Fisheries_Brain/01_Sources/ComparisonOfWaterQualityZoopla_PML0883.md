# Comparison of Water Quality, Zooplankton Density, and Cover in Razorback Sucker Spawning Areas of Lake Mead and Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 2001 annual report, prepared by [[Bio-West Inc]] for the [[Southern Nevada Water Authority]] (SNWA), compares water quality, nutrients, zooplankton density, and aquatic cover at spawning areas of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]]. Lake Mead's [[Las Vegas Bay]] and [[Echo Bay]] support small, self-sustaining razorback sucker populations with recruitment, whereas Lake Mohave sites show reproduction but no recruitment. The second year of sampling (March and May 2001) found that Las Vegas Bay had the highest nutrient levels and zooplankton densities, that cover was substantially reduced at Echo Bay and Las Vegas Bay compared with 2000, and that turbidity was highest at Las Vegas Bay and Echo Bay. The authors conclude that long-term lake-level fluctuations, which drive changes in cover and productivity, likely influence recruitment, and they recommend multi-year monitoring to identify the conditions that allow strong year classes.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, Abbott) is an endangered catostomid fish of the [[Lower Colorado River]] system. Lake Mohave holds a much larger population than Lake Mead, but it persists mainly through continuous rearing and stocking by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]]. The Lake Mead population was once thought extirpated and survives through limited natural recruitment in two bays.

Predation by [[Nonnative Fishes]] is widely regarded as the main reason razorback sucker fail to recruit in Lake Mohave. Predator abundance in Lake Mead is thought to be at least as high as in Lake Mohave, so the authors proposed that productivity and cover may determine recruitment success in Lake Mead.

Sampling targeted the littoral zone, where larval razorback sucker congregate, because earlier work (e.g., [[Paulson and Baker 1984]]) had focused on limnetic areas. Study locations were:
- Lake Mead: Las Vegas Bay (LVB), Echo Bay (EB), Trail Rapids Bay (TRB)
- Lake Mohave: Arizona Bay (AZB), Tequila Cove (TQC)

Sampling used a 200 m × 20 m site design, Hydrolab DataSonde 4a and surveyor 4a instruments, zooplankton tows with 80 µm mesh (30 × 90 cm net, about 6 m horizontal tows at 0–0.5 m depth), and video or camera mapping of cover.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Nutrients:** Las Vegas Bay is more productive than most of Lake Mead and Lake Mohave. Elsewhere in Lake Mead, nutrient and primary productivity values are similar to or lower than those in Lake Mohave. [[Las Vegas Wash]] is the largest nutrient input to Lake Mead, often carried into [[Boulder Basin]] by a density current, with flow potentially continuing downstream through [[Hoover Dam]] to Lake Mohave.
- **Zooplankton:** Densities at all locations were relatively low, especially for sizes preferred by larval razorback sucker. Historical work (e.g., [[Marsh and Langhorst 1988]]) reported extremely low zooplankton densities at some Lake Mohave spawning areas.
- **Cover and turbidity:** Cover (primarily dead or live vegetation) and turbidity both provide protection for larval and juvenile suckers from predation. Lake level dropped more than 24 feet between March 2000 and May 2001, leaving submerged vegetation on shore.
- **Algal bloom:** An extensive algal bloom occurred throughout the system from February to June 2001 and reduced visibility, especially in Las Vegas Bay.

## Key Findings

**Water quality and nutrients**
- Las Vegas Bay had significantly higher nutrient levels (nitrate, ammonium, total phosphorus) than all other locations in both years. Total P and NH4-N were higher in 2001 than in 2000, especially at Las Vegas Bay.
- Total dissolved solids were significantly higher at all Las Vegas Bay sites in March 2001 and at the Las Vegas Bay sites in May 2001.
- Turbidity was highest at Las Vegas Bay and Echo Bay sites. Secchi depths at Las Vegas Bay ranged from 0.5–1.9 m in May 2001, while Trail Rapids Bay ranged from 4.7–7.7 m.
- Dissolved oxygen varied widely in March 2001 but was relatively uniform in May. Echo Bay and Tequila Cove had significantly lower DO in May 2001.

**Zooplankton**
- In March 2001, mean zooplankton density at Las Vegas Bay and Tequila Cove was significantly higher than at Trail Rapids Bay and Echo Bay.
- In May 2001, Las Vegas Bay had the highest mean density; Tequila Cove, Arizona Bay, and Las Vegas Bay had higher densities than Echo Bay, Arizona Bay, and Trail Rapids Bay in the comparisons reported.
- Density was higher overall in 2001 than in 2000, but only Las Vegas Bay showed a significant within-location increase in March.
- Adult copepods and copepod nauplii dominated at all locations in 2001, in contrast to 2000 when Bosminidae cladocerans were prominent in Lake Mohave samples.
- Average small zooplankton density exceeded the 10 items/L threshold (Papoulias and Minckley 1990) at Las Vegas Bay, Tequila Cove, and Arizona Bay in March, and at Las Vegas Bay and Tequila Cove in May. At Echo Bay and Trail Rapids Bay, it stayed well below 10 items/L in both months.
- The 2001 mesh change (153 µm in 2000 to 80 µm in 2001) confounds year-to-year density comparisons.

**Cover**
- Cover dropped sharply at Echo Bay (over 42% of sampled area lost between March and May 2001) and at Las Vegas Bay. Average lake elevation fell from 1,194.68 ft (March 2001) to 1,187.32 ft (May 2001).
- By May 2001, no location differed significantly in percent cover.
- Paired tests showed no significant between-year differences in percent cover at each location, though reductions at Las Vegas Bay and Echo Bay were substantial.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Las Vegas Bay is the only Lake Mead location with consistently higher nutrients than Lake Mohave, and its higher zooplankton density suggests greater productivity there in some years.
- Echo Bay has low zooplankton density, indicating that food quantity alone does not explain recruitment there. Turbidity, linked to wind action and the silt bottom, appears to be the key differentiating factor between Echo Bay and Las Vegas Bay versus the Lake Mohave sites.
- Long-term lake-level fluctuations appear to drive cover and turbidity, and so may be central to recruitment success.
- The authors recommend continued multi-year sampling, correlating conditions across lake elevations with the presence of strong razorback sucker year classes, and sampling the Colorado River inflow area if a sucker population is confirmed there.
