# Morphology and Feeding Behavior of Hybrids Between Catostomus insignis and Pantosteus clarki (Catostomidae)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #catostomidae #hybridization #behavioral-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Master's thesis examines F₁ hybrids between [[Catostomus insignis]] (Sonora sucker) and [[Pantosteus clarki]] (desert mountain-sucker) from [[Aravaipa Creek]] and [[Bonita Creek]], Arizona. The study tests hypotheses of morphological and behavioral intermediacy in natural fish hybrids using multiple analytical methods: hybrid index, discriminant function analysis (DFA), and principle components analysis (PCA). Morphological analyses consistently supported hybrid intermediacy, though with a "dominance-like" effect favoring C. insignis morphology. Feeding behavior analyses yielded conflicting results between the two stream populations. Aravaipa Creek hybrids exhibited overall behavioral intermediacy, while Bonita Creek hybrids showed little intermediacy but definite "dominance" of C. insignis-like feeding behaviors. The study demonstrates that these hybrids are morphologically and behaviorally capable of utilizing both parental feeding bases.

## Background & Description

[[Catostomus insignis]] and [[Pantosteus clarki]] are widely sympatric catostomid fishes in the upper [[Gila River Basin]]. Hybridization between these species was first documented by Hubbs et al. (1943) as rare but geographically widespread, not associated with hybrid zones. [[Pantosteus clarki]] represents one of the most specialized products of Pantosteus evolution, featuring extreme development of a cartilaginous scraping sheath on the lower jaw adapted for removing algae and diatoms from solid substrates. Additional specializations include lengthened gut and darkened peritoneum associated with herbivory. In contrast, [[Catostomus insignis]] is more generalized, lacking specialized scraping mouthparts, lengthened gut, and darkened peritoneum.

Previous work by Schreiber (1978) and Schreiber and Minckley (1982) demonstrated almost mutually exclusive feeding habits between these species in Aravaipa Creek: P. clarki relied upon algae and diatoms while C. insignis was a generalized carnivore. This ecological separation makes their hybrids particularly valuable for examining behavioral intermediacy hypotheses.

**Study Sites:** [[Aravaipa Creek]] originates at 1310 m elevation, flows northwest for 75 linear km (~140 km by stream channel) to enter the San Pedro River at 660 m elevation. Modal discharge is approximately 0.85 m³/sec but subject to flashfloods reaching 570 m³/sec. The stream features steep-walled canyons with walls exceeding 250 m height, swift currents (>1 m/sec in riffles), and gradient averaging 8.2 m/km in the canyon. [[Bonita Creek]] ranges from 950 m at its Gila River confluence to 2,130 m at Turtle Mountain. Perennial flow begins around 1,300 m elevation. Modal flow varied from 0.17 m³/sec in narrow canyon sections to 0.06 m³/sec near the Gila confluence (1955-1956 data). Gradient is steeper (10.6 m/km) with lower discharge and less pool development than Aravaipa Creek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The two study streams differ substantially in physical and biological characteristics. [[Aravaipa Creek]] supports seven native fish species with minimal exotic species presence, representing one of the few remaining streams in the Gila Basin retaining a large native fish fauna. Five of seven Aravaipa species rely upon ephemeropteran nymphs as their major food base. [[Bonita Creek]] supports only five native species (four common in collection areas) with considerable introduced fauna in lower reaches. This difference in predator load on invertebrates may influence hybrid feeding behavior between streams.

Habitat preferences differ between parental species: [[Pantosteus clarki]] prefers swiftly-flowing riffles and pools (adults also use quiet pools), while [[Catostomus insignis]] congregates in relatively quiet, deep water for most of life, inhabiting swifter portions only as fingerlings. Grain size of inorganic material in digestive tracts initially seems contradictory to habitat preferences. Fine-grained sand in P. clarki stomachs likely results from efficient algae removal from stones in riffle areas, where epilithic algae filter and collect finely-divided materials. Large-grained sand in C. insignis indicates feeding in areas of greater current velocities and/or inadequate mouth structure for close-cropping.

Intestinal cestode parasite ([[Isoglaridacris bulbocirrus]]) occurrence differed dramatically between streams. In Aravaipa Creek, C. insignis showed 26% infestation (20.9% average stomach volume), hybrids 31% infestation (10.7% volume), and P. clarki 0% infestation in anterior digestive tract. In Bonita Creek, infestation was occasionally extreme in P. clarki (54% occurrence, 42% average stomach volume), lower in C. insignis (36% occurrence, 6% volume), and lowest in hybrids (9% occurrence, 5% volume).

## Key Findings

**Morphological Analyses:**

Nine morphometric measurements and seven meristic counts were analyzed on specimens from both streams (N=33 C. insignis, N=16 hybrids, N=24 P. clarki from Aravaipa; N=26 C. insignis, N=11 hybrids, N=27 P. clarki from Bonita). Five atypical P. clarki specimens from Bonita Creek were characterized by less laterally-flared, more posteriorly-directed lower lips than typical population members.

All three analytical methods (hybrid index, DFA, PCA) agreed in supporting hybrid intermediacy, though with consistent "dominance-like" effect of C. insignis morphology. Hybrid index values for separate characters fell within parental means (0.0-1.0) in majority of cases. Average individual hybrid indices were 0.364 (Aravaipa) and 0.332 (Bonita), approaching theoretical 0.5 for F₁ populations. Characters with little parental discrimination (snout length, dorsal and pelvic ray counts, scales above lateral line) frequently exceeded parental means.

Coefficients of variation were essentially equivalent among groups, indicating no introgression. DFA produced non-overlapping discriminant function scores between groups, further supporting absence of backcrossing. Number of papillae rows crossing lower lip midline, length of lower lip union, width of cartilaginous ridge of lower jaw, and width of isthmus received highest weightings in formation of canonical variate I in both samples.

PCA using log-transformed morphometric ratios (permillage standard length) provided greater objectivity than untransformed data. PC I represented 62-63% of variation and achieved complete separation among all groups. This method is recommended over entering morphometric data directly, where PC I primarily measures overall size and allometry rather than interspecific discrimination.

**Feeding Behavior Analyses:**

Stomach fullness averaged 29-53% among samples; 0-26% of specimens had empty stomachs. [[Catostomus insignis]] utilized the greatest number of identifiable food categories in both streams, consuming most invertebrates (chiefly ephemeropteran nymphs) by relative volume and occurrence. [[Pantosteus clarki]] showed frequent invertebrate occurrence (51.6% Aravaipa, 88.9% Bonita) but low relative volumes (10.2% and 18.2%), with vegetative materials far more important. Hybrids generally consumed intermediate volumes of animal materials.

**Aravaipa Creek Results:** Hybrids exhibited overall behavioral intermediacy. Average relative volumes were almost precisely intermediate in two well-distinguished categories (inorganic and animal materials). In categories with little parental differences, hybrids were intermediate in percentage occurrence (diatoms and vascular plant material). DFA supported behavioral intermediacy hypothesis (Fig. 5). Hybrids displayed most variation along canonical variate I, exceeding either parent. No apparent dominance of C. insignis feeding characteristics was evident.

**Bonita Creek Results:** Hybrids showed little intermediacy in average relative volumes, instead ingesting quantities typical of C. insignis (exception: algal component resembled P. clarki pattern). Little hybrid intermediacy occurred in percentage occurrence of items. DFA placed average hybrid feeding behavior between parents but very close to C. insignis. However, limits of CV I scores for hybrids were intermediate between parental limits, indicating capability to utilize both parental feeding bases. The small sample size (N=10) may have been insufficient for accurate representation.

**Discrepancies Between Streams:** Differences in feeding patterns likely reflect pronounced physical and biological differences between streams. Lesser discharge, lower habitat diversity, and fewer native fish species in Bonita Creek (4 vs. 6 common species) compared to Aravaipa Creek could account for differences. Relative absence of heavy predator load on invertebrates in Bonita Creek may allow hybrids to concentrate on that resource. Dominance by biomass of largely herbivorous P. clarki in Bonita Creek (41.4% of fauna, 22.4 g/m²) versus Aravaipa Creek (41.0% of fauna, 6.6 g/m²) may influence hybrid diet toward carnivory.

**Methodological Comparisons:** The hybrid index allows separate assessment of characters but requires a priori identification and discriminating character choice. DFA correctly classified specimens more definitively than hybrid index and allows visual assessment of relative variability within and between groups (potential introgression indicator), but requires a priori identifications and has complicated assumptions. PCA does not require a priori identifications but objective identification of F₁ hybrids (let alone backcrosses) appears exceedingly difficult and subject to researcher bias. Entering morphometric data as log-transformed permillage standard length rather than directly provides greater objectivity and interpretability.

**Genetic Expression:** Results support conclusions of Ross and Cavender (1981) that dominance and/or interallelic suppression may alter consistently intermediate additive phenotypic expression. The tendency for hybrids to resemble generalized C. insignis morphology may pervade entire genetic structure, realized in feeding behavior as well. Blood protein studies by Koehn (1967, 1969) suggested introgression between these species in evolutionary past: P. clarki serum enzyme patterns show unique affinities with C. insignis, and C. insignis exhibits cathodally-migrating electrophoretic hemoglobin element typical of P. clarki.

**Sample Sizes:** Morphology analysis used 33 C. insignis, 16 hybrids, 24 P. clarki from Aravaipa Creek; 26 C. insignis, 11 hybrids, 27 P. clarki from Bonita Creek. Feeding analysis excluded 5 C. insignis from Aravaipa Creek and 1 hybrid from Bonita Creek due to empty digestive tracts.
