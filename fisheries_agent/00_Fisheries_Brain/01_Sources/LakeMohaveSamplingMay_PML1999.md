# Lake Mohave Native Fish Monitoring and Recovery Coordination (1992–1998)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compiled set of fisheries records, memoranda, meeting minutes and planning notes concerning two endangered native fishes of [[Lake Mohave]]: the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The core material is a June 1995 survey by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] Native Fish Program, which captured adult bonytail and razorback suckers during May 30 to June 1, 1995, and PIT-tagged them. The remainder covers the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) and its Core Group coordination, hatchery production and stocking, telemetry movement studies, a proposed multi-agency monitoring program, a 1998 bonytail transport operation, and a 1995 rearing-pond summary. The records show a bonytail stock dominated by a few year classes, a razorback population believed to have declined from the 1990 era, and an ongoing effort to coordinate federal, state, university and tribal roles in recovery.

## Background & Description

**Focal species**

- **Bonytail chub** (*Gila elegans*, family Cyprinidae): a native Colorado River fish listed as endangered. Adults in the 1995 catch measured roughly 400 to 550 mm in total length and were described with breeding coloration and tubercles on the head.
- **Razorback sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*, family Catostomidae): also endangered. Both captured adults were tagged and released in 1995; the monitoring plan treats suckers under 450 mm separately.

**Non-target species in the 1995 catch** (Table 2 summary, Lake Mohave, May 30 to June 1, 1995): common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) was the most numerous, with 101 fish in the summary; the remaining taxa were channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*), largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*), striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*), bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*), green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and yellow bullhead (*Ameiurus natalis*), each in small numbers.

**Sampling method**

Gill and trammel nets were set in coves between Davis Dam and Arrowhead Cove at depths of 0.5 to 20 m and checked about every six hours. Catch was reported as fish per unit effort (one unit = one net-night, approximately 24 hours). Table 1 is a per-cove summary, including Arrowhead Point, Arrowhead Cove, Stopsign Cove, Sourdough Cove and Ski Pass, with sets from 30 May through 1 June.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Adult bonytail were captured at depths of about 2 to 10 m, mainly near Arrowhead Point and north of Arrowhead Cove. Fish were captured on the lake's reservoir bottom and along coves during the spawning-season window.
- **Movement:** Telemetry of stocked razorback suckers (1994 release, Gordon Muller, USFWS) indicated that adults have large home ranges, one adult travelled over 51 miles of the approximately 61-mile lake, and average range across tracked fish was near 30 miles. Juveniles moved rapidly after October stocking; several crossed Cottonwood Basin. Adults moved up to 30 miles during spawning, and at least one visited a separate spawning area near Tequila.
- **Water conditions:** Lake levels were reported higher than normal in 1995–1996, which affected the timing of fish harvest from ponds. Salinity and temperature were recorded during the 1998 transport (target hatchery water temperature 52°F).
- **Hatchery and rearing context:** Razorback larvae were collected from ephemeral ponds in early 1995 for rearing at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and the Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center (DNFH&TC). Rearing enclosures were placed downriver of Davis Dam and in shoreline backwaters.
- **Habitat restoration:** Davis Cove restoration was completed successfully in 1995; repair of the associated berm was still pending.
- **Conservation status:** Both species are federally listed; the Bureau of Reclamation (BR) holds Endangered Species Act (ESA) section 7 consultation responsibilities with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS).

## Key Findings

**1. 1995 Lake Mohave survey (AZGFD memorandum, June 22, 1995)**

- Eight adult bonytail were captured at depths of 2 to 10 m, mostly near Arrowhead Point and north of Arrowhead Cove, with three more from the same net and one additional fish captured between sets.
- Two razorback suckers were captured, tagged, and released. The memo states that both razorbacks and all bonytail swam away vigorously on release.
- All bonytail and razorbacks were weighed, measured and PIT-tagged ("microchip" and extended-range tags). Table 3 lists about 15 PIT-tagged fish, including about 12 bonytail and 3 razorbacks; several bonytail showed breeding coloration, tubercles, or side scars, and one razorback had a missing left pectoral fin and a caudal peduncle sore.

**2. Native Fish Work Group and Core Group coordination (1995–1996)**

- The NFWG is a group of professionals from participating agencies with prior success in recovering native fish in Lake Mohave. Funding and direction come from each agency; outside funding is routed to the affected agency.
- Action items included developing an information base on razorback and bonytail needs, a field schedule for 1996 (targets from January to September), a field-personnel plan for sampling and tagging, and a cost breakdown and monitoring proposal for submission to the Core Group.
- BR committed to funding its conservation program and indicated $1,000,000 for FY 1996 interim conservation measures.

**3. Hatchery production and stocking**

- Ten bonytail (5 males, 5 females) were spawned successfully at Willow Beach NFH; more than 100,000 fry were produced. Chemical analysis of progeny suggested that fewer than five females contributed to the resulting year class.
- About 90 bonytail have been captured since 1990, but the USFWS had not yet produced a new year class from these broodstock fish. The group strongly recommended collecting additional bonytail from Lake Mohave for a new broodstock.
- Over 20,000 razorback larvae were collected in early 1995. Burke expected more than 1,500 razorbacks and 500+ bonytail to be harvested and released after the fish removal.
- In 1995 the BR released 444 bonytail into Lake Mohave, as recorded in the stocking summary.

**4. Population estimates**

- Razorback sucker population: about 60,000 fish in the 1990 era, with broad confidence limits; the current estimate is about 25,000 fish, with narrow limits. The group concluded that the population has declined since the 1990 era but has been relatively stable over the last three years.

**5. Proposed monitoring program**

- The National Biological Service (NBS) would provide initial funding, personnel and coordination for an interagency monitoring program, with the Arizona State University (ASU) responsible for data processing and analysis.
- The initial effort would last about one month, beginning late in the spawning season, with passive and active sampling and a research component (telemetry) to clarify where and when fish can be collected.
- Field data to be recorded: PIT tag numbers, sex, length and weight for suckers under 450 mm, GPS coordinates, time, date, and sampling technique. Field notes are to be archived at the Boulder City office.

**6. Bonytail transport operation, May 18–21, 1998 (Parker FRO memorandum)**

- Three camps operated on Lake Mohave (Arrowhead Cove, Bill Gayes Cove and Carp Cove).
- Four adult bonytail were taken; two came from Arrowhead Cove, and the others from Half-Way Wash and the morning catch. Fish were held overnight in live cars, then transported to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in coolers with oxygen. Some fish had net marks, but none were considered life-threatening.

**7. Management recommendations and open issues**

- The 1995 minutes and memoranda identify ongoing needs for expanded BTC monitoring, a second bonytail year class from wild-collected broodstock, and interim conservation measures.
- Several items remained unresolved in the documents, including the schedule for the next NFWG meeting ("none set") and a plan to find alternate sources for proposed research funding.
