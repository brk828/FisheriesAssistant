## Executive Summary

This 1988 American Fisheries Society position paper, authored by Jack E. Williams, Donald W. Sada, Cynthia Deacon Williams, and the AFS Western Division Endangered Species Committee, presents the first standardized guidelines for translocating and introducing threatened and endangered fishes as part of recovery efforts under the U.S. Endangered Species Act of 1973. The authors reviewed 39 approved federal recovery plans and found that 82% (32 of 39) called for some form of introduction — whether to supplement existing populations, establish new populations, create genetic reserves, support artificial propagation, or serve educational/biological-control purposes. The paper is framed by the cautionary case of the [[Amistad Gambusia]] (*Gambusia amistadensis*), which went extinct in captivity after being genetically contaminated by invasive [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). The guidelines are organized into three sequential phases: (1) site selection, (2) conducting the introduction, and (3) post-introduction monitoring and reporting, all grounded in emerging principles of conservation genetics.

## Background & Description

This paper is primarily a policy/methodology document rather than a taxonomic description, but it references morphological and phenotypic changes documented in translocated populations as case evidence:

- **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon diabolis*): When translocated from its native limestone cavern (Devil's Hole) to a cement refuge below Hoover Dam with greater light exposure and primary productivity, the refuge population developed **larger, more brightly-colored bodies** and altered **mensural characteristics** compared to the source population (Williams 1977).
- **[[Threespine Stickleback]]** (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*): An unusual **melanistic population** from Holcomb Creek, California, was initially believed to represent a novel evolutionary form endemic to the area (Bell 1982). Later allozyme analysis revealed it was genetically identical to stickleback populations from the lower Santa Clara River, indicating the melanism arose as a **phenotypic response to introduction** (likely via inadvertent transport with hatchery trout) rather than local evolutionary divergence.
- **[[Guppy]]** (*Poecilia reticulata*): Cited as a model species demonstrating **rapid evolution of life-history traits** (e.g., age/size at maturity) in introduced populations facing novel predators or habitats (Reznick and Bryga 1987).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses habitat and ecological considerations essential to introduction site selection:

- **Historic/native range restriction**: Introductions should occur within a species' documented historic distribution, defined as known localities plus interconnected waters where occurrence would be reasonable. Examples span a gradient from wide-ranging species like the **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, historically spanning the Green River, Wyoming to the Gila River, Arizona) to narrow single-spring endemics like the **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]]**.
- **Habitat homogeneity vs. heterogeneity**: Species from homogeneous habitats (e.g., thermally constant springs) tend to exhibit lower genetic variability than those from heterogeneous environments (e.g., eurythermal streams) (Vrijenhoek et al. 1985), affecting both site selection and required founder population sizes.
- **Protected/secure sites**: Introduction sites must be secured from habitat destruction via management agreements or protective land designation.
- **Dispersal potential**: Site selection must weigh whether dispersal is desirable (range expansion within historic habitat) or undesirable (introductions outside historic range).
- **Life history requirements**: Sites must provide adequate food, spawning, and rearing habitat across all life stages, with water quality benchmarked against undisturbed natural habitat (per U.S. EPA 1976 criteria).
- **Hybridization risk**: Particular concern for isolated-drainage fishes with weak reproductive isolating mechanisms — notably suckers (family [[Catostomidae]]) and chubs of genus *Gila*, which readily hybridize with sympatric congeners.
- **Non-target species impacts**: A documented failure case is highlighted — introduction of the **[[Owens Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon radiosus*) into Fish Slough, California, involved habitat modification that eliminated an endemic hydrobiid snail population (Landye 1983), underscoring the need for pre-introduction surveys of co-occurring rare invertebrates.

## Key Findings

- **Prevalence of introductions in recovery plans**: 32 of 39 (82%) reviewed U.S. recovery plans for endangered/threatened fishes included provisions for introductions (Table 1), spanning taxa such as [[Apache Trout]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Woundfin]], [[Cui-ui]], and multiple pupfish and gambusia species.
- **Historical success rates are low**: Only 2 of 12 introduction attempts of the **[[Mohave Tui Chub]]** (*Gila bicolor mohavensis*) resulted in established populations (Hoover and St. Amant 1983). Minckley and Brooks (1985) documented an overall ~30% success rate for translocation attempts of rare Arizona fishes.
- **Extinction case study**: The **[[Amistad Gambusia]]** went extinct by 1979 after captive stocks were found to be entirely contaminated by *Gambusia affinis*, illustrating the catastrophic risk of inadequate taxonomic verification during propagation.
- **Five recognized purposes for introductions**: (1) information/education, (2) genetic reserve/artificial propagation, (3) biological control agent, (4) supplementing existing populations, (5) establishing new populations — with sport fishery creation noted as a secondary motive for species like [[Apache Trout]] and [[Gila Trout]].
- **Genetic considerations are central**: 
  - Mixing genetically isolated stocks is discouraged due to risk of breaking up coadapted gene complexes, causing outbreeding depression in later generations (documented in [[Atlantic Salmon]], Stahl 1981).
  - Recommended effective population size (Ne) of 200 for sustaining hatchery salmonid stocks (Allendorf and Ryman 1987); minimum of 25 males and 25 females suggested for establishing hatchery salmonid populations (Ryman and Stahl 1980).
  - No more than 10% of an available source stock should be used annually for introductions unless the source is imminently threatened.
  - Source stock selection should consider genetic purity, rarity, geographic proximity, or ecological similarity; centrally located populations may show higher fitness than edge populations (Meffe 1987), as demonstrated in **[[Sonoran Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) electrophoretic studies (Vrijenhoek et al. 1985).
- **Disease/pathogen screening**: A minimum sample of 60 fish is recommended to detect pathogen presence/absence in source populations (Ossiander and Wedemeyer 1973); two-week quarantine recommended where feasible (Hoffman and Schubert 1984).
- **Stress physiology**: Handling and transport stress can cause immune suppression, delayed mortality, and impaired learning/imprinting for weeks post-transport (Schreck 1981, 1982; Mazeaud et al. 1977), affecting migration and homing behavior.
- **Documentation gap**: The authors emphasize a systemic failure to publish introduction outcomes and causes of failure in the literature, hampering iterative improvement of recovery techniques — a core motivation for the guidelines.
- **Regulatory tool**: The 1984 "experimental population" designation (Federal Register, 27 August 1984) is identified as a mechanism to reduce local opposition to introductions by easing Section 7 consultation requirements, classifying such populations as "essential" or "nonessential" threatened populations.
- **Three-phase guideline structure**: 
  1. **Site selection** — historic range, protected status, dispersal control, life history suitability, adequate habitat for viable population size, hybridization avoidance, protection of non-target rare species.
  2. **Conducting the introduction** — source stock selection, taxonomic verification, pathogen screening, adequate founder numbers/sex ratio/age structure, low-stress transport, favorable release timing/conditions, thorough documentation.
  3. **Post-introduction activities** — systematic monitoring (quarterly in year one, then annual), conditional restocking, failure-cause analysis, and public documentation of findings.
