# Machias River Salmon Restoration

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmo-salar

## Executive Summary

This 1955 management report by fishery biologist James S. Fletcher, published by the [[Atlantic Salmon Commission]] of Maine, evaluates why the [[Atlantic Salmon]] run in the [[Machias River]] collapsed and what must be done to restore it. Studies began in June 1949 and covered dams, fishways, river habitat, water control, pollution, stocking, adult enumeration, regulations, and the sport fishery. The author concludes that obstructions, above all the [[Whitneyville Dam]], are the main barrier to recovery. The river holds very large spawning and nursery areas above that dam, so fixing passage there would unlock most of the potential. The report also warns that hatchery stocking has so far shown limited returns.

## Background & Description

The Machias River flows southeast from the Machias Lakes into Machias Bay on the Maine coast. Its drainage covers more than 450 square miles of forest. The river falls 375 feet over about 60 miles, and its main channel and more than 95 tributaries total over 400 linear miles. More than 25 lakes and ponds range from 20 to 2,850 acres. Major tributaries include the West Branch Machias River, Old Stream, and Mopang Stream.

The river has a long history of decline. Atkins (1874) reported that a man with a dip-net could take 60 salmon in one day. By contrast, the highest single-day trap total at the [[Lower Dam]] in 1954 was 33 adults. Dams built after the settlement of Machias in 1763 blocked both [[Atlantic Salmon]] and [[Alewife]] migrations. Early conservation rules, in force by 1780, kept some passage open. Once the [[Upper Dam]] (1841–42) and Whitneyville Dam (operating by 1842) were built with inadequate fishways, Atkins reported that salmon disappeared. A remnant population persisted and produced occasional "Big Run" years.

The 1954 migration of 544 adults showed that restoration was feasible. Most fish returning in 1954 were the progeny of the 1949 spawners.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life history.** Eggs are laid in late fall and hatch the following spring. Young fish spend about two summers as [[Parr]] in fresh water, then migrate to sea as [[Smolt]]s about 10 inches long. At sea they grow rapidly. Most of the run returns in its fifth year, weighing over eight pounds on average. The 1954 run included about 3 percent [[Grilse]] (1¼ to 6 pounds) and about 15 percent older fish weighing 12 to over 30 pounds. Most fish weighed 8 to 10 pounds.

**Physical habitat.** The [[River Inventory]] of 1951 found large spawning and nursery areas above Whitneyville Dam, totaling 845,922 square yards. Areas below the dam covered only 13,445 square yards, and freshets and river drives reduce their value. Water impoundments at Sabao Lake, Third and Fourth Machias Lakes, and First Chain Lake often leave downstream reaches nearly dry. These dams have no fish passage or minimum-flow provisions. Low flows concentrate fish, reduce food, and can produce lethal oxygen and temperature conditions in summer.

**Water quality.** Domestic sewage is minor and not harmful to aquatic life. Periodic oil discharges between the two lower dams, however, deter salmon from entering the [[Gorge]] and fishways for several days after each release. The report calls the Machias "one of the cleanest of our large rivers."

**Co-occurring species.** The report mentions [[Alewife]] runs, which the Gorge has blocked, and [[Brook Trout]], which is caught alongside young salmon and must be distinguished from parr and smolts.

## Key Findings

**Obstructions**

- **[[Gorge]]:** The natural rock gorge beneath the Route 1 bridge in Machias injured many ascending salmon, as shown by pathological examinations at the East Orland hatchery. In 1949 the Commission built four concrete weirs that diverted the flow to the west bank. The first day after the weirs went in, 27 salmon were counted above the Gorge. The report judges the Gorge passable at low water but not at high water. Further improvement or a bypass fishway is recommended.
- **[[Lower Dam]]:** Three dams sit on the falls above tidal water. The center section is a total barrier, and the right wing blocks passage at all water levels except higher-than-normal discharges. The fishway passes fish readily at low and normal water. Turbulence from overflows of two inches or more is harmful. Relocating the weir notches is recommended. The [[Bangor Hydro-Electric Company]] turbine uses up to 172 cubic feet per second (cfs), with about 155 cfs the normal operating level.
- **[[Upper Dam]]:** Water leaks through the structure. Records show at least 150 cfs passing under it while the crest stays dry, and the 1953 average was 285 cfs. During an 83-day low-water period in 1949, 120 salmon released at the Lower Dam were held below this dam and exposed to predation and pollution. An experimental five-pool [[Fishways|fishway]] built on the east bank in 1953–54 passed 432 of 484 released salmon (about 90 percent). Fish took about four days on average to move between the two dams.
- **[[Whitneyville Dam]]:** This is the most serious obstacle. Its 1947 pool-type fishway has ten pools and a restricted exit tunnel. A 1954 trap recaptured 65 of 544 salmon (12 percent), meaning about 88 percent did not get past. The 1948 Fishway Engineer report called it "probably the poorest ladder" visited on Maine rivers. Six modifications were requested: fencing, overflow water control, two extra pools, sluice regulation, ice and flow protection, and regular maintenance. The owner has already agreed to add one pool and blast a resting pool.

**Adult enumeration and stocking**

- The [[Counting Station]] at the Lower Dam is the only reliable measure of stocking success on a major Maine river. Annual counts record the total run, marked returns, size, and scales.
- Trap totals were 362 (1949), 154 (1950), 258 (1953), and 544 (1954).
- Records cover about 25 [[Stocking Experiments]] from December 1940 to January 1955 (Table 2). Most plants were unmarked or fin-clipped.
- Fall fingerlings returned about 4 adults per 10,000 stocked. Yearlings returned 8 adults per 20,000 stocked.
- In the 1949 brood year, 176 adults were removed for hatchery eggs. Only 15 mature marked adults returned in 1954, about 1 per 6 taken. The report concludes that hatchery stocking in rivers with natural runs has "limited value" and that removing brood stock may harm existing runs.
- At [[Hobart Stream]], survival to smolt was nil for fry planted before the end of their first summer. Fall fingerlings were the most cost-efficient age class.

**Regulations, sport fishery, and pollution**

- The bag limit of two salmon per day is probably adequate for now. The report suggests anglers consider reducing it until runs are re-established.
- "Fly fishing only" rules cannot be enforced because of other species present. Anglers should be free to use any legal gear.
- Anglers took about 3 percent of the 1954 spawning migration, which the report judges a minor effect.
- Posters and sportsmen's cooperation have prevented the need to close nursery areas.

**Recommendations**

1. Improve the Gorge further, or build a bypass, so fish pass at all water levels.
2. Make minor alterations to the Lower Dam fishway for spring run-off or abnormal summer flows.
3. Add a trash rack above the Upper Dam fishway exit.
4. Modify, operate, and maintain the Whitneyville fishway and regulate its sluices. This is the top priority.
5. Manage impoundments for minimum fluctuation and set minimum flow guarantees across the drainage.
6. Maintain the high water quality of the river.

The author states that failing to act will prevent full salmon restoration in the Machias River.
