## Executive Summary

This fragmentary document, a scanned excerpt from a paper attributed to Paul C. Marsh, reviews the distribution of native fishes in the [[San Pedro River]] Basin of southeastern [[Arizona]] since 1963. It documents a severe decline of the native ichthyofauna, with two key drivers: pollution from copper mining in the headwaters in [[Sonora Mexico]] (February 1978), and habitat destruction along the river. The text also describes the 1978 discovery and extirpation of a [[Gila Topminnow]] population at an [[Artesian Well]] near [[Mammoth Arizona]]. ## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes a basin in which native fish populations are already depleted and fragmented. Recolonization of the upper mainstream depends on tributaries, particularly the Babocomari River, where small populations of native species persist. The text emphasizes that habitat loss is widespread along much of the San Pedro. Several pressures are named:

- **Mining pollution:** In February 1978, pollutants from [[Copper Mining]] operations in the headwaters in Sonora apparently eliminated all fishes in the upper mainstream.
- **Riparian clearing:** [[Mesquite Clearing]] adjacent to aquatic habitat was under way at the site described below.
- **Groundwater diversion:** Artesian well flow was redirected to irrigation.
- **Sedimentation:** Clearing caused siltation of the outflow area, which reduced fish observations.

## Key Findings

1. **Severe native fish decline:** The native ichthyofauna of the San Pedro is described as "already depleted." Several species were lost in the early 1900s, and the table documents further local extirpations.

2. **Mainstream extirpation from pollution (February 1978):** Copper mining pollutants apparently eliminated all fishes in the upper mainstream. The author identifies tributaries as the only likely source for reestablishment.

3. **Gila Topminnow extirpation at Mammoth (1978):** A new locality for the [[Gila Topminnow]] was identified in July 1978 in an artesian well outflow south of Mammoth. Within about three months the site was lost:
   - July 1978: population discovered; clearing of adjacent mesquite under way.
   - September 1978: well piping partially capped; dense mesquite removed by bulldozer; outflow silted in, with few fish observed.
   - Early October 1978: well fully capped and connected to an irrigation system, drying the habitat and extirpating the population.

4. **Significance of the loss:** The well outflow was the only known site in the entire San Pedro Basin where the Gila Topminnow persisted. It was also one of only seven naturally occurring populations known in the United States. The author characterizes it as an "important endangered species population" discovered and extirpated within a single season.

5. **General habitat trend:** The Mammoth incident is presented as typical of what is "generally happening to fish and wildlife habitat along much of the San Pedro." The implied conclusion is that protection of remaining tributary populations and riparian habitat is urgent.
