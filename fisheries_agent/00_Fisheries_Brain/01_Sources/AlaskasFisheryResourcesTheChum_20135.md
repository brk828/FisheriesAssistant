# Alaska's Fishery Resources: The Chum Salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service / Bureau of Commercial Fisheries Fishery Leaflet (No. 632, by Theodore R. Merrell, Jr., Auke Bay Biological Laboratory) profiles the [[Chum Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus keta*), the most widely distributed and second most abundant of the five [[Pacific Salmon]] species. The leaflet covers geographic distribution, [[Life History]], [[Economic Importance]] to Alaska, and the state of research. Its central message is that chum salmon are commercially less valued than other Pacific salmon, yet economically important through abundance, and that the species is less understood biologically than any other Pacific salmon.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Member of the genus *Oncorhynchus* (meaning "hooked snout"), a name derived from the hooked jaw shared by all five Pacific salmon species. The other four are pink (*O. gorbuscha*), sockeye (*O. nerka*), chinook (*O. tshawytscha*), and coho (*O. kisutch*).
- **Appearance:** Ocean fish have rich red flesh and silvery sides. On approach to spawning, flesh pales to light pink, and vertical purple-black and dull red streaks appear on the sides, more pronounced in males. Males develop a hooked jaw and enlarged front teeth used in combat with rival males.
- **Size:** Mature fish typically 3 to 5 years old, 8 to 15 pounds, about 30 inches long; males usually larger than females.
- **Quality:** Pale flesh and lower palatability reduce the appeal of canned product; chum salmon are harder to catch and fetch lower prices per pound.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Anadromy and migration:** Adults return to freshwater to spawn after most of their life at sea, and die after spawning, as do other Pacific salmon. Spawning occurs from small coastal streams to headwater springs of large rivers. The longest known migration is by Yukon River chum salmon, which swim over 1,500 miles upstream from the Bering Sea.
- **Spawning behavior:** The female digs a series of pockets (a nest or *[[Redd]]*) by beating her tail on the gravel, releasing a few eggs at a time fertilized by the male's milt, then covers them and guards the site until she dies a few days later.
- **Egg and fry survival:** A female produces 1,000 to 7,000 eggs, but fewer than 10 percent of eggs produce fry that reach the ocean. Threats include superimposition by other spawners (especially later-spawning pink salmon), low oxygen from low flows and warm weather, floods, freezing, and predation by birds and fish.
- **Hatching and emergence:** Eggs hatch in midwinter and fry remain in the gravel, nourished by their yolk, until spring. At a constant 50 °F, eggs hatch in about 50 days. Fry have greenish backs, silvery sides and parr marks, which fade at sea.
- **Diet:** Fry generally do not feed during seaward migration. In their first ocean summer they eat barnacle nauplii, tunicate larvae and copepods; larger juveniles eat herring and euphausiids. Mature fish stop feeding on return and rely on stored oils.
- **Distribution:** In North America, from Oregon to the Alaskan Arctic coast; in Asia, from the Siberian Arctic coast to northern Japan. Alaska's densest runs are in the southeastern Panhandle, Cook Inlet, and large Arctic tributaries of the Bering and Chukchi Seas. Tagging through the [[International North Pacific Fisheries Commission]] showed Alaskan fish mingle with Asian stocks in the North Pacific and Bering Sea.
- **Scale comparison:** Asian streams produce about four times as many chum salmon annually as North American streams. The [[Amur River]] has the largest runs of any river in the world.

## Key Findings

- **Abundance and reach:** Chum salmon are the most widely distributed and second most abundant Pacific salmon.
- **Life history:** Young hatch in midwinter, emerge in spring, spend 2 to 4 years at sea, and return to their natal stream to spawn.
- **Fecundity versus survival:** Females produce 1,000 to 7,000 eggs, but fewer than 10 percent of eggs yield fry reaching the sea.
- **Comparative life history (Table 1):** Chum salmon spend 2 to 4 years at sea, average about 8 pounds at return, and about 3,000 eggs per female, the lowest egg count of the five species compared (pink, coho, sockeye and chinook all differ).
- **Commercial catch (Table 2, 1959 to 1968, Alaska):** Average of about 6 million fish, 45 million pounds, 525 thousand cases of canned product, and wholesale value averaging $12.9 million per year (not including subsistence catches). Annual catches ranged from 3 to 7 million fish.
- **Egg products:** In 1968, the value of salmon egg products across all species in Alaska was about $12 million, while canned chum salmon was worth $28 million. Chum eggs are larger and brighter than other salmon eggs, making them valued for caviar and bait.
- **Harvest methods:** Most commercial chum salmon are taken by purse seine, with some drift gillnets. The season generally runs July to October. Chum salmon dive under the lead line and their teeth snag netting, causing lost fishing time.
- **Subsistence:** Hundreds of thousands are taken each year for personal use by Alaska Natives along the Bering and Chukchi Sea rivers, mainly with set gillnets and, on the Yukon and Kuskokwim, fish wheels. Fish are dried for human and dog food. Use of motorized toboggans is reducing dog-team demand for fish.
- **Management:** The Alaska Department of Fish and Game regulates harvest to ensure enough spawners escape, using its own and Bureau of Commercial Fisheries research.
- **Research gap:** Little biological research has been done on chum salmon, so less is known about them than any other Pacific salmon. Key open questions include annual return forecasts, optimal spawner abundance, freshwater and early marine survival factors, and causes of large year-to-year variation in adult numbers.
