# Muleshoe Ecosystem Management Plan and Environmental Assessment (Draft, 1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This September 1996 draft from the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) Tucson Field Office presents the Muleshoe Ecosystem Management Plan (EMP) and an accompanying Environmental Assessment for roughly 57,500 acres in the Galiuro Mountains of southeastern Arizona. The planning area spans BLM, [[Forest Service]], [[The Nature Conservancy]] (TNC) and private and state lands within the Muleshoe Cooperative Management Area (CMA). The plan is an interdisciplinary, activity-level document that integrates wilderness, [[Hot Springs Watershed ACEC]], livestock grazing, habitat, recreation and cultural management, and it sets six resource objectives with monitoring schedules. Its central premise is that healthy riparian and aquatic habitats, which support five native fish species, depend on restoring upland watershed function through prescribed fire and grazing changes. The EA concludes that significant impacts are not expected and that an environmental impact statement is not required.

## Background & Description

The Muleshoe planning area lies within the [[Basin and Range Province]] and drains to the lower [[San Pedro River]] through three watersheds: Redfield Canyon, Hot Springs Canyon and Cherry Springs. The planning area includes about 26,500 acres of BLM land, 22,000 acres of Forest Service land, 6,000 acres of private land and 3,000 acres of Arizona state land (EA figures; the EMP text gives 22,000 acres of BLM land in its own tally, so figures should be checked against the source). Plan authorship involved an interdisciplinary team drawn from BLM, the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), Forest Service, TNC, the Soza Mesa Ranch, the Saguaro-Juniper Association, and Bayless-Berkalew Company. The plan replaces several separate activity plans, including the Redfield Canyon Wilderness plan and the Hot Springs Watershed ACEC plan.

Public participation included a scoping open house in Benson, Arizona in November 1990, a mailing to more than 500 recipients (later reduced to about 150), and two field trips in July and August 1994.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and geology.** Annual precipitation ranges from roughly 12 to 16 inches, falling in a bimodal pattern of summer thunderstorms and winter storms. The landscape is shaped by Tertiary volcanics and conglomerates. Canyon streams have cut into basin-fill sediments, in some places to depths of hundreds of feet.

**Watersheds and flows.** The planning area contains seven perennial streams totaling over 23 miles. Steep, shallow soils with rock cover shed water quickly, producing high storm peaks that scour channels. Watershed condition in the BLM-managed portions of Redfield and Hot Springs was rated fair (BLM 1989). Hot Springs Canyon contributes a significant share of base flow to the lower San Pedro River. Instream flow water rights have been filed for Hot Springs, Bass, Redfield, Swamp Springs and Wildcat canyons; a certificate was issued to TNC for lower Hot Springs Canyon in 1994.

**Vegetation.** Five major communities occur: Sonoran desert scrub, semi-desert grassland/shrubland, broadleaf deciduous riparian woodland, evergreen woodland/chaparral, and montane forest. Riparian canyon bottoms support velvet ash, sycamore, Arizona walnut, willows and Fremont cottonwood. Historic yearlong grazing and fire suppression have shifted much grassland toward shrub dominance. A five-state grassland state-transition model was used to set upland objectives.

**Fish and wildlife.** Riparian areas hold the highest wildlife diversity on the Muleshoe. Of 29 special-status species, five are fishes, four are reptiles, one is an amphibian, eight are birds, ten are mammals and one is a plant. The five native fish species are the Gila chub, longfin dace, speckled dace, Sonoran sucker and desert sucker, all identified as biologically imperiled. Gila chub is described as the most sensitive. The non-native green sunfish is a monitored exotic invader in Redfield Canyon. Federally listed species noted include the [[Mexican Spotted Owl]] (threatened) and the southwestern willow flycatcher (endangered; no individuals or breeding pairs found in recent surveys). Gould's turkey reintroduction on Forest Service lands was largely unsuccessful, with one female remaining as of June 1995.

**Cultural resources.** Human occupation may span about 12,000 years. Eleven prehistoric sites are documented, and the Hooker's Hot Springs ranch headquarters is listed on the National Register. The Western Apache (Aravaipa Band) identified medicinal and edible riparian plants they wish to see protected.

**Mineral potential.** Gold, silver and copper potential is low (USGS 1995). Redfield Canyon Wilderness is closed to new mineral entry.

## Key Findings

**Condition of aquatic habitat (1994 inventory).** Fish habitat was most diverse in Redfield Canyon, which had the most pools per mile, the most pools deeper than 2 feet, the most woody cover and the most undercut bank. Hot Springs Canyon fish habitat was judged well below potential. Bass Canyon was scoured by a 1993 flood, and its pool recovery is expected.

**Riparian condition.** Pre-1994 inventories rated Redfield, Hot Springs and Bass canyons as less than satisfactory. Since livestock grazing was suspended in 1982, riparian function, age-structure diversity and streambank stability have improved. Redfield and Swamp Springs canyons were judged in properly functioning condition in 1994.

**Riparian Objective (1A and 1B).** Achieve or maintain properly functioning condition and high seral states within five years of any major flood that reduces tree density by at least one-third. Key targets include a sapling-to-tree ratio of about 3:1 and specified densities per acre, for example 425 or more in Redfield Canyon (1994 density: 474, ratio 3.0). Keep ash, sycamore, cottonwood, alder and willow present at key sites.

**Aquatic Objective (1C).** Meet or exceed targets for pools per mile, percent pool habitat, woody cover, undercut bank, bank stability and minimum monthly flow by 1999. Redfield Canyon targets include 50 pools per mile and more than 1,000 ft of woody cover per mile. Gila chub targets are a 40% share of adult fish in Redfield Canyon, rising from about 1% to 30% in Bass Canyon, and rising from a trace to 10% in Hot Springs Canyon.

**Fish and wildlife (Objective 3).** Evaluate habitat for reintroduction, range extension or supplementation of seven species by 2000, including Gila chub, Gila topminnow, desert pupfish, loach minnow, spikedace, desert bighorn sheep and turkey. Inventory exotic fish and amphibians in stock tanks. Gila chub occur at only 13 locations worldwide.

**Upland Objective (2A, Muleshoe).** Within 30 years, place 80% of the Muleshoe portion in States I and II (64% in State I, 16% in State II). This requires restoring periodic fire (roughly every 3-10 years) and reducing grazing pressure.

**Upland Objective (2B, Soza Mesa).** Maintain potential natural community or high condition on 80% of the acreage. Improve 350 acres of low-condition loamy upland to good by 2000.

**Prescribed fire.** Fifteen fire units are designated, each treated on a 5-10 year cycle, with three to six units burned annually. No more than 20% of total burn-block acreage is to be treated annually during the first five years. Riparian buffers of 300 ft or more and avoidance of slopes above 80% are specified as mitigation.

**Livestock grazing.**
- Eliminate grazing in riparian areas.
- Shrink the Muleshoe Allotment (No. 4401) to the Pride Basin, with a preference of 346 AUMs (down from 3,204 AUMs). Grazing stays suspended until upland objectives are met.
- Soza Mesa Allotment (No. 4402) moves to a four-pasture deferred-rotation system at its existing preference of 502 AUMs.
- Limit utilization on key perennial grasses to 40%.

**Recreation and access.** Estimated visitation is 1,700-1,800 per year, based on sign-in stations. Bass Canyon becomes a day-use area. The Hot Springs Canyon riparian area (140 acres) is closed to off-highway vehicles. Jackson Cabin Road and Soza Mesa roads are maintained to a four-wheel-drive standard. Pullouts, kiosks and brochures are planned. Legal access acquisition is pursued for several routes.

**Wilderness.** Post signs, cap group size at 15 persons, allow only natural-ignition fires in wilderness, and continue pursuing inholding acquisition. Redfield Canyon Wilderness covers roughly 6,600 acres of public land (EA estimate; the text also cites 3,600 acres in one place, so the figure needs checking).

**Cumulative and mitigation points.** The EA reports that the San Pedro watershed has been degraded by water diversions, non-native fish introductions and grazing, and that the Muleshoe CMA is important for remaining native fishes. Mitigation requires cultural site inventories before burns, black-lining around sensitive sites, and avoidance of desert tortoise areas during fire activity. The EA concludes that no significant impacts are expected.

**Status.** The Executive Summary names a draft plan dated September 1996, with public comments due December 4, 1996. Section IX (implementation and cost estimates) is marked "to be developed in the final plan."
