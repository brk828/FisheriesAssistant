# Floodplain Fisheries Management in Regulated Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #floodplain-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by Robin L. Welcomme (Chapter 8, *Alternatives in Regulated River Management*) reviews how the flood regime structures floodplain fish production, how traditional and modern fisheries exploit it, how river regulation alters fish communities, and which management techniques are available for modified rivers. The chapter argues that the flood is the dominant factor conditioning fisheries in unmodified rivers, that regulation erodes fish diversity and productivity through additive and synergistic effects, and that management should aim at compromise and self-sustaining populations rather than full restoration or sterile channelization. Techniques discussed include channel redesign, fish passes, artificial spawning areas, managed flood releases, introduction and stocking, aquaculture, and reservoir fisheries.

## Background & Description

The chapter opens with a historical account of river modification, from early floodplain alteration along the Nile, Indus and Mesopotamian systems to large-scale dam building after the 1950s. It notes that river research was recent: of 680 references in Welcomme's review of river fisheries, 70% were published after 1970. Many traditional floodplain fishing and culture methods were developed spontaneously by local cultures and are considered proven within their contexts.

Fish are grouped into three ecological guilds:

- **White fish**: main-channel species that migrate long distances and are mainly lithophilic or semi-pelagic spawners.
- **Black fish**: floodplain-confined species with low-oxygen tolerance and complex breeding (nest building, mouthbrooding, live bearing), often dark in color.
- **Gray fish**: intermediate species inhabiting river fringes and backwaters, often phytophilic, that migrate laterally onto the floodplain during floods.

The same guild concept is applied to temperate systems such as the Danube, Rhone and Mississippi-Missouri.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flood pulse dynamics.** Tropical rivers usually have one flood per year and equatorial rivers may have two; temperate regimes show spring and autumn peaks. During the dry season, fish are quiescent. As water rises, fish breed, migrate and disperse onto the plain, where nutrient release and floating vegetation support rapid growth; up to 75% of a 1-year growth is said to occur during the rising flood. On the falling flood, concentration in shrinking refuges causes very high densities, heavy predation and mortality. Residual water is often 10 to 20% of peak flood area, sometimes as little as 3%. Year-class strength depends strongly on flooded area and residual water, and catches vary with past flood intensity and duration.

**Fisher typology.** Riverine fisheries are spatially diffuse, based in small settlements along channels. Fishers are classed as professional full-time migrants, part-time (combining farming and fishing), or occasional. Traditional fisheries use diverse gear because river communities contain many species and life stages.

**Changes after regulation.** The chapter describes conventional exploitative development, tabulated into four stages of basin modification (from unmodified to completely modified). Three key alterations are channel barring by dams and weirs, channelization, and reduced inundation through levees and bunding. Hydrological regimes are shifted toward steady or displaced flows. Long-distance white fish are typically lost first, floodplain-dependent black fish decline with filling and flood control, and gray fish fare better. A slower "fishing-up" process replaces large fish with smaller individuals and species, reducing biomass and diversity while sustaining yield until year-to-year variability destabilizes the fishery. ## Key Findings

**Capture fisheries on unmodified floodplains** are organized into components:
- Flood fisheries (rare, sporadic trap fishing).
- Falling-flood fisheries, the most intense, using barrage traps and cast-nets on white and gray fish.
- Low-water fisheries in floodplain lagoons (poisons, plunge-baskets, clap-nets) and in the main channel (trawls, gill-nets, scoop-nets, traps).
- Rising-water fisheries targeting migratory white fish ("subiendas" or "piracemas" in Latin America), analogous to salmonid and sturgeon runs.

**Culture fisheries** rely on natural repopulation and feeding, enhancing survival during low water:
- **Drain-in ponds (fish holes):** The Oueme "whedos" are channels 4 to 5 m wide and up to 1.5 m deep. Yields were 2.12 ± 1.18 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ (n = 34) for 1955 to 1958 and 1.57 ± 0.40 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ (n = 34) for 1968 to 1970, a decline attributed to basin-wide overfishing. Approximately 3% of the 1000 km² floodplain was in whedo form, and up to 13% at Dogba-He. Bangladesh has about 30,000 ponds. Rice-fish culture yields fell with double cropping and pesticide use: single-cropped sump ponds averaged about 200 kg ha⁻¹, double-cropped about 95.7 kg ha⁻¹.
- **Floodplain lagoons:** Impoundment prolongs retention. In the Niger, impounded lagoon yields of about 185 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ could be raised to 500 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ with stocking and feeding. Italian *vallicoltura* in the Po Delta yielded a mean of about 100 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ (range 30 to 690).
- **Brush parks** (acadjas, garse, samra and others): Lake Nokoue acadjas were recorded at up to 20 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, but the 1968 to 1971 average of 15 acadjas was 3.88 t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, mainly because fishing regimes were poor. Concerns include siltation, deforestation and overfishing.

**Channel management** options:
- Derestriction of floodplains (setting back levees), used in England and the Czechoslovakian Danube, though landowners often object.
- Channel control structures such as notched dykes, rootless and vane dykes, chute closures, low dams and cover structures. Evidence is strong for rhithronic trout streams. In the Swales and O'Hara study, biomass increased by 319% for *Leuciscus leuciscus* and 25% for *L. cephalus* within 1 year in a small British lowland river.

**Fish passes** have worked for salmonids in north temperate rivers. In Latin America, most ladders had design defects, though several migratory genera used them. Ladders on the Tigris and Euphrates were used successfully by migratory cyprinids. The Markala barrage ladder on the Niger failed, attributed to insufficient capacity.

**Artificial spawning areas** include substrate restoration for gravel spawners, the Volga sandy-substrate project for inconnu (*Stenodus leucichthys*), and the Volga delta "water splitter" weir flooding a 215,000 ha depression. Catches of semianadromous cyprinids rose by about 12,500 t yr⁻¹.

**Managed flow releases** stimulated breeding when timed to the normal flood period. Examples include the Pongolo floodplain (Africa), Shire River dam releases to Elephant and Ndinde marshes, and Kariba reservoir releases to the Mana floodplain of the Zambezi, where juveniles of at least nine species appeared in lagoons.

**Introduction and stocking:**
- Introductions carry ecological and disease risks, yet many aquaculture and capture fisheries rely on non-native species. Screening follows EIFAC procedures.
- Stocking, mainly with native species, maintains production, offsets basin impacts, or boosts extensive culture. Volga stocking includes about 90 million sturgeon, 6 million Kura salmon, 18 million white salmon and 3.6 billion food fish annually. In the Pearl River, release of 9.5 million Chinese carp into the Tanjiang each year doubled yield within 4 years.

**Aquaculture:** Hungarian backwaters and oxbows compared favorably with fish ponds (Table 3). Fish pond total yield was 883 kg ha⁻¹ against 813 kg ha⁻¹ for backwaters, with backwaters needing far less feed (1.3 vs 2.3 kg starch value per kg yield). Rumanian Danube delta floodplain depression lakes and ponds yield about 27,000 t yr⁻¹. Pangasius is cultured in floating cages in Thailand.

**Reservoir fisheries:** Reservoir yields are often comparable to or above the rivers they replace, especially in flat-terrain reservoirs such as Volta and Nyumba ya Mungu. Losses of downstream spawning grounds may offset gains, and in many cases downstream losses exceed the dam's fish potential. Migratory white fish disappear first, black fish adapt in littoral zones, and gray fish colonize open water.

**Management conclusions:** The chapter concludes that no overall river management policy exists in most regions and that the goal should be neither restoration to virgin condition nor sterile straight channels. Management must reconcile aesthetic, practical and ecological needs, accept compromise with power, transport and water supply users, and favor self-sustaining populations over palliative, continuously subsidized stocking. Reform sustainable redevelopment (RSD) is the proposed framework.
