# Tolerance of Grass Carp to Seawater

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 laboratory study by D. G. Cross of the [[Salmon And Freshwater Fisheries Laboratory]] tested how well older [[Grass Carp]] (*[[Ctenopharyngodon Idella]]*) tolerate diluted [[Seawater]]. The question matters for risk assessment: if grass carp were introduced to British waterways for [[Aquatic Weed Control]], could they migrate through [[Estuaries]] or [[Brackish Water]] into other river systems? The fish died within hours in 50% seawater and survived about 24 days in 30% seawater. The author concludes that migration through full-strength seawater is effectively impossible, but migration through brackish water connecting adjacent river systems is feasible.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Grass carp, *Ctenopharyngodon idella* Val. A herbivorous freshwater cyprinid, proposed for biological control of aquatic weeds in Britain.
- **Context:** Earlier work (Pentelow & Stott, 1965; Stott, 1967; Cross, 1969) considered grass carp for weed control. Cross (1969) judged that the species is unlikely to breed naturally in Britain, so migration would be a risk but not a route to a population explosion.
- **Prior salinity evidence:** [[Doroshev]] (1963) reported that grass carp fry, 3 to 4 weeks old, survived more than about 1.5 to 2 months at 9 and 12 g/l sodium chloride equivalents (Sea of Azov and Aral Sea waters) at 17 to 20.5 °C. The OCR text for the duration is garbled and is reported here as given in the source.
- **Knowledge gap:** The author found no comparative data on the salinity or toxin tolerance of fry versus older fish, which motivated testing older animals.
- **Water chemistry:** [[Aral Sea]] and [[Sea Of Azov]] waters contain more divalent magnesium and sulphate ions than oceanic water, with a smaller difference for the Sea of Azov (Zenkevitch, 1963). The seawater used here differs in ionic composition from those waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Test seawater.** Seawater was prepared by adding Tidman's Sea Salt, evaporated from Atlantic seawater off Spain, to Metropolitan Water Board tap water. 100% seawater was defined as equivalent to 35 g/l sodium chloride.

**Fish.** Test fish were about 2+ years old (age recorded as "2+" in the OCR text) and averaged 40.8 g. They were smaller than normal for their age because they had been kept on a restricted diet in aquaria. Fish from the same stock used in field trials averaged 200 g.

**Relevance to migration.** The [[Volga River]] to [[Ural River]] transfer, which crosses the northern [[Caspian Sea]] where salinities are lower than those tested, shows that grass carp can move through low-salinity brackish water (Pavlov & Nelovkin, 1963).

**Tidal constraint.** Where connecting water varies between river and seawater salinity with the tide, migration would be possible only near low water, and would require a strong directional stimulus to complete within the short window.

## Key Findings

**1. Experimental design**
- Fish were acclimatised to 15% seawater for 2 days, then to 30% seawater for 2 days.
- They were then held at constant salinities of 30, 34, 37, 40 and 50% seawater.
- Tests ran in 170 l aquaria with 5 fish per dilution, at 20.1 ± 0.4 °C, with gentle aeration.
- Five control fish held in freshwater remained in good condition throughout.
- Fish were moved to a clean tank of the same salinity and temperature if the water became turbid.

**2. Survival outcomes**

| Seawater concentration | Equivalent NaCl (g/l) | Maximum survival time |
|---|---|---|
| 30% | 10.5 | approximately 24 days |
| 50% | 17.5 | 5 h |

- The author used maximum rather than median survival times. Grass carp could breed or spread if a few fish migrated, so the most resistant individuals were the relevant measure.
- With only 5 fish per concentration, a full statistical analysis of deviations from the median was not possible, and the survival times of highly resistant fish could not be estimated reliably.
- Individual survival times were narrowly spread, so even highly resistant fish would probably not have survived much longer than the fifth fish at each concentration.
- A straight line fitted through the maximum survival points showed no threshold concentration within the tested range.

**3. Comparison with earlier work**
- Results were similar to Doroshev's (1963), suggesting little difference in seawater tolerance between fry and older grass carp.

**4. Conclusions on migration**
- Because fish survive only briefly in 50% seawater, migration between river systems *through the sea* is impossible.
- Migration between adjacent river systems connected by brackish water is feasible.
- A brackish connection may therefore not be an effective barrier to grass carp spreading into an unstocked river.
- Successful migration would most likely occur only around low tide, within a short window.

**5. Overall significance**
- Grass carp have limited salinity tolerance, which constrains coastal dispersal.
- Brackish estuarine water is the main pathway of concern for establishing grass carp in connected river systems, so risk assessment should focus on it rather than on open seawater.
