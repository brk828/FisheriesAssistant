# A Revised Key to the British Water Bugs (Hemiptera-Heteroptera) with Notes on Their Ecology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hemiptera #heteroptera #corixidae

## Executive Summary

This is the second edition (1965) of a taxonomic identification key to the aquatic and semi-aquatic true bugs of the British Isles, written by T. T. Macan as Freshwater Biological Association Scientific Publication No. 16. It covers the surface-dwelling families (Mesoveliidae, Hebridae, Hydrometridae, Veliidae, Gerridae) and the water-dwelling families (Nepidae, Naucoridae, Aphelocheiridae, Pleidae, Notonectidae, Corixidae). It provides dichotomous keys to families, genera and species, with structural notes, collecting and preparation methods, and brief ecological notes on each species' distribution and habitat in Britain, with particular reference to the Lake District.

The document is an identification tool and reference rather than a field study. It contains no original sampling data, but it does provide specimen counts for a few taxa and important taxonomic and ecological observations.

## Background & Description

**Scope and history.** The key revises earlier editions (Scientific Publications Nos. 1, 1939 and 4, 1941). Changes since the first edition include the recognition of two British *Velia* species, rather than one, and revised understanding of several corixid identifications.

**Specimen preparation.** The text recommends:
- Killing specimens by immersion in boiling water and mounting them immediately, or using a killing bottle of ethyl acetate over celluloid chips, which keeps specimens relaxed.
- Mounting on pins or on card or celluloid triangles with tragacanth mucilage, rather than fluid preservation, so that ventral structures remain visible.
- Dissecting a sample of each species and mounting parts in Canada balsam or polyvinyl lactophenol, especially genitalia.
- Noting that the black hemielytral markings are more developed in northern British specimens.

**Diagnostic structures.** The key relies on:
- Antennal segment count and proportions.
- Leg insertion and relative lengths.
- Ocelli presence and wing development (macropterous, brachypterous, apterous forms).
- Pronotal and hemielytral patterns.
- Male genitalia, including the aedeagus and its sclerites in *Gerris*, the parameres in *Notonecta*, and the forceps and pala (fore tarsus) in Corixidae.
- Metasternal xiphus shape in Corixidae.

**Morphological terms.** The text defines several structures used throughout, including the scutellum, connexivum, strigil, pala, metasternal xiphus and aedeagus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Surface versus water-dwelling forms.** Surface-dwelling forms have long, narrow bodies and antennae longer than the head. Water-dwelling forms are swimming or crawling bugs with antennae concealed in pits. Macan notes that the claws of *Velia*, *Microvelia* and *Gerris* sit just before a pad of bristles that keeps the limb from breaking the water surface, which reflects a more fully aquatic life on open water. The other surface genera are found on floating leaves (*Mesovelia*) or near emergent vegetation at the bank.

**Wing polymorphism.** Many species are dimorphic. *Mesovelia*, *Microvelia* and *Velia* are commonly wingless in Britain, while *Hydrometra* and *Hebrus ruficeps* have rudimentary hemielytra. Winged forms occur occasionally and have a larger prothorax. Among the *Gerris* species, *G. najas* is usually wingless, *G. costai* and *G. gibbifer* are winged, and the remaining species (excluding *G. rufoscutellatus*) have a winter generation of mainly complete forms and a summer generation with reduced or absent wings (citing Brinkhurst 1963).

**Habitat classes in Corixidae.** Macan (1954d) grouped corixids into three habitat assemblages: heath, moorland and mountain ponds and pools (base-deficient waters); rich ponds, including those draining agricultural land or polluted; and lakes and rivers. Most species of the first group are rarely found in the others, while rich-pond species often occur in lakes and rivers.

**Terminology used in the ecology notes.** "Widely distributed" refers to localities far apart, "common" or "rare" to the number of localities in a region, and "abundant" or "scarce" to numbers at a single locality. A "tarn" is a small upland lake, and a "pool" is a usually shallow peat pool.

**Other ecological observations cited:**
- *Corixa germari* (as *Arctocorisa germari*) in an upland reservoir: estimates of annual production (Crisp 1962a) and biology (Crisp 1962b).
- Popham (1964) on factors inducing flight in water-bugs.
- Scudder's lecture on salt requirements of brackish-water species.
- Zwart's finding that corixid nymphs could be reared successfully only with some animal food.

## Key Findings

**Structure and scope**
- The key covers 11 families, with about 62 species and named subspecies or varieties (approximate count from the key's entries).
- Families keyed in the surface-dwelling group: Mesoveliidae (*Mesovelia furcata*), Hebridae (*Hebrus ruficeps*, *H. pusillus*), Hydrometridae (*Hydrometra stagnorum*, *H. gracilenta*), Veliidae (*Velia caprai*, *V. saulii*, *Microvelia* spp.) and Gerridae (ten *Gerris* species).
- Families keyed in the water-dwelling group: Nepidae (*Ranatra linearis*, *Nepa cinerea*), Naucoridae (*Ilyocoris cimicoides*), Aphelocheiridae (*Aphelocheirus montandoni*), Pleidae (*Plea leachi*), Notonectidae (four *Notonecta* species) and Corixidae (about 33 species, with two named subspecies or varieties of *Callicorixa wollastoni*, one of them *var. caledonica*, and *Notonecta obliqua* var. *delcourti*).

**Taxonomic revisions**
- Brown (1951) showed that two *Velia* species occur in Britain, neither of them *V. currens*.
- *Gerris lateralis* has been confused with *G. asper* and *G. costai* in British literature; Wagner and Zimmermann (1955) showed that British material previously called *G. asper* is *G. lateralis*.
- *Sigara striata* was recently recorded in Kent and East Sussex (Waterston 1956).
- *Plea leachi* is used here, rather than the name *P. atomaria* from the Kloet and Hincks checklist, on the advice of Dr W. E. China.

**Diagnostic results from the text**
- *Sigara dorsalis* vs *S. striata*: in a sample of 155 *S. dorsalis*, one had five pale pronotal lines, 123 had six, three had seven, and 28 had one forked line (seven on one side, six on the other). Most *S. striata* had seven or more pale lines.
- *Microvelia* species are separated by pronotal and hemielytral markings in winged forms and by pronotal and abdominal characters in wingless forms.

**Distribution and rarity (selected)**
- *Gerris rufoscutellatus*: seventeen specimens recorded in 96 years. Leston (1956b) suggests these were transported from the continent and that the species has never established itself in Britain.
- *Notonecta maculata*: a Mediterranean species, fairly common and abundant in southern England, which attaches its eggs to hard substrata and is often found in concrete reservoirs.
- *Hydrometra gracilenta*: known only from a few localities in the Norfolk Broads and New Forest.
- *Aphelocheirus montandoni*: mostly southern, but recorded from the Tweed near Berwick and its tributary the Till, on stony river bottoms. In the Lake District it was taken in numbers on the stony shores of Bassenthwaite.
- *Sigara fallenoidea*: included in the key only as a note, because it is known only from western and central Ireland (the notes give "south and west of Ireland").
- *Micronecta minutissima*: rare in Britain.

**Lake District records (selected)**
- *Gerris najas* is restricted almost completely to dry-stone-wall boathouses on Windermere, possibly because wooden or cement boathouses lack overwintering crevices (Brinkhurst 1959a).
- *Gerris lateralis* is abundant in one locality, a pool in a Salix-Phragmites swamp (Congo Carr) beside Windermere.
- *Sigara falleni* is abundant in Esthwaite Lake, "the most productive of all the lakes", and fairly abundant in Blelham Tarn.
- *Sigara distincta* occurs in lake reed-beds where soil organic matter exceeds 10%.
- *Notonecta obliqua* var. *delcourti* occurs with the type in School Knotts Tarn and one other nearby locality, and is nearly as common as the type in Wiltshire chalk-downland dewponds.

**Taxonomic principle**
- The key uses square brackets to mark characters that are not exclusive, so a bracketed character may apply to several groups.
- Macan notes that species-level distinctions in *Corixidae* often depend on males, since many female pairs are difficult or impossible to separate.
