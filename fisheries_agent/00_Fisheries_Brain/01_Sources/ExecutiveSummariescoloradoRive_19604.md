# Colorado River Native Fish Research and Management Summary (1999)

#ichthyology #native-fish-recovery #catostomidae #cyprinidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document compiles executive summaries of multiple research and management projects focused on endangered and native fishes of the [[Colorado River]] Basin as of April 1999. The work encompasses monitoring, stocking, and recovery efforts for four federally endangered species: [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Projects span multiple agencies including NDOW, SNWA, USFWS, USGS, Arizona Game and Fish Department, and various contractors. Research activities include population surveys, mark-recapture studies, telemetry tracking, habitat assessment, stocking evaluations, disease monitoring, and experimental studies on growth and swimming performance. Geographic scope covers the [[Virgin River]], [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]], [[Grand Canyon]], and lower Colorado River reaches from Nevada through Arizona.

## Background & Description

The document presents coordinated recovery efforts for Colorado River native fishes conducted by multiple agencies under various funding mechanisms including Federal Aid Section 6, SNWA contracts, USGS appropriations, and stewardship funding. Key species addressed include:

**Endangered Species:**
- [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): Small cyprinid endemic to Virgin River system
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Large catostomid with distinctive dorsal keel
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): Endemic to Colorado River, primarily in Grand Canyon
- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila cypha*): Rare cyprinid, subject of intensive stocking programs

**Other Native Species:**
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*)

Research methods employed include electrofishing, trammel netting, mini-hoopnets, seining, PIT tagging, sonic telemetry, coded wire tags, and hand-injected cut wire tags. Studies address population status, distribution, habitat use, spawning activity, recruitment, movement patterns, disease prevalence, growth rates, and swimming performance under varying temperature and flow regimes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Native fish populations in the Colorado River Basin face multiple stressors including dam operations, flow regime alterations, thermal modifications, habitat degradation, competition and predation from non-native species, and disease. Key ecological contexts include:

**Habitat Types:**
- Mainstem river shorelines and eddy complexes
- Tributary streams (Paria, Little Colorado, Bright Angel, Shinumo, Kanab, Havasu, Beaver Dam Wash)
- Reservoir environments (Lake Mead, Lake Mohave, Lake Havasu)
- Backwaters and inflow areas
- Artificial rearing facilities (golf course ponds, refuge ponds)

**Flow and Temperature Regimes:**
Glen Canyon Dam operations create daily fluctuations of 50-100 cm in river elevation. Water temperatures in mainstem Colorado River are thermally modified by dam releases. Growth experiments at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] test temperatures of 12°C, 18°C, and 24°C to evaluate thermal requirements for [[Humpback Chub]] growth.

**Non-Native Species Interactions:**
Non-native species dominated mainstem catches in Grand Canyon (59% of 2,335 fish), with [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) most abundant (37%). Other non-natives include [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Red Shiner]], and [[Common Carp]]. Competition and predation from these species affect native fish recruitment and survival.

**Disease and Parasites:**
Fish health monitoring documented presence of anchorworms (*Lernaea cyprinacea*), Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), and protozoan *Ichthyobodo* (Costia). Parasite prevalence was lower in 1998 compared to previous years, possibly related to prolonged spring flooding. Condition factor and body fat were higher in mainstem fish compared to tributary fish.

## Key Findings

**Virgin River Woundfin Studies (NDOW/SNWA/Bio-West):**
- Between 1993-1998, 23,490 adult [[Woundfin]] and 1,095 [[Virgin River Chub]] from Dexter NFH were released in lower Virgin River at Mesquite Bridge and Riverside Bridge
- Hand-injected cut wire tags showed short-term loss rates of ±10% with retention in adult fish for several months
- Tagged fish could not be located beyond 6-9 months after release
- Study results (1993-1997) supported presence of small but self-recruiting [[Woundfin]] population in lower Virgin River, particularly at and below Riverside
- Former abundant populations (e.g., Beaver Dam, Arizona) now appeared absent
- Significant substrate changes observed in some areas compared to historic conditions

**Lake Mead Razorback Sucker Surveys (NDOW/SNWA/Bio-West):**
- Formal surveys initiated 1990; 92 net nights at 21 sites in four basins during 1990-1993
- Total of 50 adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (467-765 mm TL) captured 1990-1993
- Except for single fish at Bark Bay (1991), all captures from Echo Bay (Overton Arm) and Blackbird Point area (Boulder Basin near Las Vegas Wash)
- After February 1992, all captured fish (42) tagged with PIT tags
- Expanded study (October 1996-June 1997): 61 adult and ~5,000 larval [[Razorback Sucker]] collected
- 21 adults surgically implanted with sonic tags; Boulder Basin fish ranged up to 5 miles, Echo Bay fish up to 15 miles
- 1997 population estimate: 50-60 adults at Echo Bay, ~400 adults in Las Vegas Bay
- Revised 1998 estimate: 170-200 fish at Las Vegas Bay, <100 fish at Echo Bay
- Capture of 4 apparent juvenile fish (318-384 mm TL) at Echo Bay (November 1997-February 1998) documented recent recruitment in reservoir environment
- Of 21 sonic-tagged fish, only 7 remained active through June 1997
- Clear seasonal use patterns: fish moved away from immediate inflow areas June-November

**Lake Mohave Studies (USGS - Gordon Mueller):**
- Telemetry research examined fish passage at water control structures, habitat use, predation, and survival
- Post-stocking dispersal studies showed holding fish with blocking net for 3 days significantly reduced short-term dispersal rates
- Juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] typically nocturnal; moved at night, found secluded in backs of coves during day
- Fish actively hid in flooded vegetation or rock rubble
- Golf course pond rearing: fish grew at tremendous rates (>250 mm/year) without contracting internal or external parasites or diseases

**Grand Canyon Native Fish Monitoring (USFWS - Owen Gorman):**
- 120 days field work March 1998-January 1999; 4,771 fish captured in mainstem and tributaries
- 11,200 m shoreline habitat sampled at low and elevated flows
- Mainstem Colorado River: non-natives dominated (1,368 of 2,335 fish, 59%); [[Rainbow Trout]] most common (868, 37%)
- Native fish in mainstem: 967 captured; [[Speckled Dace]] (403, 17%) and [[Humpback Chub]] (391, 17%) most abundant
- Mini-hoopnets revealed relative abundance of [[Humpback Chub]] in 80-200 mm TL size class, not previously observed
- Smaller tributaries: natives dominated (739 of 847, 87%); [[Speckled Dace]] (370, 44%) and [[Humpback Chub]] (133, 16%) most abundant
- Little Colorado River: natives dominated (1,362 of 1,600, 85%); [[Humpback Chub]] (914, 57%) and [[Speckled Dace]] (308, 19%) most abundant
- Anchorworms relatively uncommon (0-5% occurrence, lowest in mainstem); Asian tapeworm relatively common (12-32% occurrence, lowest in mainstem)
- Both parasites lower in prevalence than previous years, possibly related to prolonged spring flooding
- High incidence of *Ichthyobodo* (Costia) infection in Kanab Creek fish

**Flannelmouth Sucker Reintroduction (Arizona Game and Fish):**
- March 29-31, 1976: 612 [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] collected from Paria River at Lee's Ferry
- Fish averaged 2.5-3 pounds; majority in spawning condition
- Stocked April 1, 1976 in Colorado River below Davis Dam at Bullhead City Community Park
- Water temperature: 16°C in tank, 15°C in river
- One fish dead at stocking; others appeared healthy and readily dispersed

**Verde and Salt Rivers Reintroduction (Arizona Game and Fish):**
- October 1994-1998: 13,250 [[Razorback Sucker]] released in Verde River (14 stockings); all but two stockings ≥300 mm TL
- December 1996: 2,046 [[Razorback Sucker]] (285 mm average) stocked in Salt River at Horseshoe Bend
- December 1995-1998: 8,537 [[Colorado Squawfish]] (≥300 mm) stocked in Verde River at Beasley Flat and Childs Powerplant
- [[Razorback Sucker]] recaptures increased steadily: 12 (1996), 29 (1997), 61 (1998)
- 1996: 67% recaptures <1 year in river; 1997: 72% <1 year; 1998: 47% <1 year
- Fish recaptured 1-3 years in river: 8% (1996), 3% (1997), 12% (1998)
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] recaptures: 27 (1996), 29 (1997), 30 (1998); all <1 year in river
- Two [[Colorado Squawfish]] collected >15 miles from Childs (1997, 1998); both >400 mm, good condition
- Three age classes of [[Razorback Sucker]] found within 1-2 miles of Childs during past 2 years
- Resident [[Razorback Sucker]] population established around Childs Powerplant area

**Lower Colorado River Electrofishing (Arizona Game and Fish - Yuma Region):**
- Fall surveys (1993-1998): [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) dominated most divisions
- Spring surveys: [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) dominated
- Mittry Lake: [[Largemouth Bass]] 80-96% of catch
- Imperial Division: [[Largemouth Bass]] 86-97% fall catch; [[Flathead Catfish]] 100% spring catch
- Parker Strip (1998 sample): 21% [[Largemouth Bass]], 23% [[Bluegill]], 17% [[Redear Sunfish]], 10% [[Green Sunfish]], 10% [[Threadfin Shad]]

**Grow-Out Facilities:**
- Emerald Canyon Golf Course: 82 [[Bonytail]] and 1,052 [[Razorback Sucker]] raised and released into Lake Havasu
- High Levee Pond (Cibola NWR): 344 [[Bonytail]] and 914 [[Razorback Sucker]] raised and released
- Achii Hanyo Project: 455 [[Bonytail]] and 201 [[Razorback Sucker]] repatriated into Lake Havasu
- Office Cove (Bill Williams River NWR): 196 [[Bonytail]] and 150 [[Razorback Sucker]] raised and released into Lake Havasu

**Experimental Studies:**
- Growth experiments at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] testing YOY [[Humpback Chub]] at 12°C, 18°C, and 24°C
- Swimming performance studies planned for YOY [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] to determine temperature and flow velocity ranges where juveniles are not displaced
- Food base studies in Little Colorado River examining linkage between primary production, benthos, and fish using stable isotope analysis
