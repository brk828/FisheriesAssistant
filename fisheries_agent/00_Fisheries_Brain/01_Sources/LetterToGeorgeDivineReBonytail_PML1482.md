# Bonytail Handling Protocol Comments (Lake Mohave, 1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This is a 13 June 1996 letter from [[Paul C. Marsh]] of the [[Arizona State University]] Center for Environmental Studies to [[George Divine]], Recovery Coordinator at the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service). It responds to a request for comments on a fish handling protocol for the critically imperiled [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*). The protocol was applied during fish collections at [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona and Nevada, from 12 to 19 May 1996. Marsh judges the protocol effective and recommends a few practical changes, chiefly in tag screening, chemical bath timing, boat equipment, and safety.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), described in the letter as a critically imperiled fish.
- **Protocol:** An informal handling protocol developed by the Service and used during the May 1996 collection effort.
- **Collection effort:** Netting and retrieval of target fish at Lake Mohave, with transport to the Service facility at [[Willow Beach Hatchery]], Arizona.
- **Author's role:** Marsh was a participant in the May 1996 trip and wrote on behalf of the participants. The letter is correspondence, not a research report, so it gives no sample sizes, counts, or statistics.
- **Copies:** C.O. Minckley and Parker M. (names as legible in the scan).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** The fish were captured in [[Lake Mohave]], a large western reservoir. The letter notes the hazards of small craft, wind, waves, and darkness on such reservoirs during field work.
- **Holding conditions:** Survival after capture depended largely on the availability of warm water at the hatchery, according to Marsh.
- **Condition on arrival:** Fish arrived at the Willow Beach facility "in good shape."
- **Tagging:** Captured fish may carry tags or other marks (PIT, sonic, or other), which the protocol needs to detect.

## Key Findings

1. **Protocol effectiveness:** The handling protocol "seemed to work well" and helped ensure fish arrived in good condition. Marsh considers it an enhancement of the program that should continue to benefit the fish.
2. **Causes of survival:** Post-capture survival is attributed more to (1) warm water and availability at the hatchery and (2) renewed efforts by Service and other personnel than to the protocol alone.
3. **Recommended adjustment: tag screening.** Captured fish should be checked for PIT, sonic, or other tags through a brief external inspection and scanning, done without removing fish from the water. Marsh calls this the most important change.
4. **Recommended adjustment: chemical bath timing.** Preparation of the chemical holding and transport bath should wait until fish are actually in hand, not start at the beginning of the effort when catch is uncertain. Marsh expects this to save considerable effort.
5. **Recommended adjustment: boat equipment.** Not every watercraft needs the full set of treatment chemicals, especially when a pickup craft is present. Net boats need only a salt solution to hold fish briefly before transfer.
6. **Safety priority:** Safety must come before fish welfare. Potential fish loss is never a reason to take unsafe action. Marsh notes that some less experienced workers may focus more on following the rules than on personal and crew safety, and asks the Service to make the protocol somewhat more flexible.
7. **Program outcomes:** The May 1996 trip was rewarding. The protocol was implemented and tested, several fish were transferred to the Service hatchery, [[Arizona State University]] began a new [[Telemetry Study]], and cooperation among participants built a framework for further investigation.
8. **Outlook:** Marsh foresees "only improvement and continued success" for the program.
