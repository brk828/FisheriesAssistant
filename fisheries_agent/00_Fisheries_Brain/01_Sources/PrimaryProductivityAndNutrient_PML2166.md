# Primary Productivity and Nutrient Balance in a Lower Colorado River Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This 1982 limnological study by John C. Priscu, Jacob Verduin and James E. Deacon, published in [[Archiv für Hydrobiologie]], examines phytoplankton primary productivity and nutrient cycling in [[Lake Mohave]], a 115.3 km² warm-monomictic reservoir on the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Sampling ran from October 1976 to September 1977 at five stations. The reservoir receives hypolimnetic water from [[Lake Mead]] via [[Hoover Dam]] and loses water through deep penstocks at [[Davis Dam]]. The authors conclude that discharge management at both dams is the dominant control on the reservoir's nutrient balance and productivity. The document contains no fish data; its scope is planktonic and chemical.

## Background & Description

Lake Mohave was built in 1953 chiefly as a buffer for highly variable discharges from Lake Mead, making it a natural setting to test how fluctuating deepwater discharge affects primary productivity and nutrient balance. Roughly 30 km of remnant river lies between Hoover Dam and the lentic reservoir. Inflow comes from about 61 m depth in Lake Mead, is high in dissolved nitrogen and phosphorus, nearly free of phytoplankton, and is thermally uniform at about 13 °C. Outflow at Davis Dam (about 40 m) fluctuates with hydroelectric demand. Local precipitation is below 13 cm per year, so edaphic nutrient inputs are small.

Methods included:
- **Productivity:** [[14C Method]] incubations at 0–7 m, with integral estimates.
- **Nutrients:** DIN, DP, TKN, TP and TON analyses, with [[Nutrient Loading and Retention]] calculated from discharge.
- **Phytoplankton:** chlorophyll-a and taxonomic enumeration.
- **Turbidity proxy:** [[Beam Attenuation]] (alpha) measured by transmissometer and used to map the river–lake interface.
- **Bioassays:** [[Nutrient Enrichment Bioassay]] experiments in 500 ml flasks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reservoir is strongly stratified in summer, and the position of the river–lake interface depends on density differences between cold, nutrient-rich lake water and warmer river water. Hoover Dam discharge controls this interface, which in turn shapes where nutrients are supplied and productivity peaks. The authors place the system within a broader theory that surface outflows tend to trap nutrients while deep outflows export them. They compare it with [[Konowskie Lake]] (Poland), where hypolimnetic withdrawal reduced eutrophication, and with the Madison River reservoir in Montana studied by Merrell and Ahlstrom (1978).

## Key Findings

- **Productivity:** Mean daily primary production was 1770 mg C m⁻² day⁻¹, which the authors describe as among the highest recorded for freshwater systems. Peak rates exceeded 5 g C m⁻² day⁻¹ near the plunge point, where turbulent mixing (eddy diffusion ≈ 38 cm² s⁻¹) supplied nutrients.
- **Seasonality:** Stations M1–M3 varied about 7-fold between winter and summer. Station M4 varied nearly 100-fold between winter and summer.
- **Interface control:** After stratification, a linear regression of interface location on Hoover Dam discharge was highly significant (r² = 0.69, n = 14, p < 0.01). Adding lake elevation raised the explained variance by only 19% (F = 1.56, p < 0.10, not significant).
- **Chlorophyll:** Chlorophyll-a at Station M4 correlated with prior-month Hoover discharge (r² = 0.61, n = 8, p < 0.05), consistent with dilution followed by nutrient-driven growth.
- **Phosphorus:** Dissolved phosphorus made up about 40% of total phosphorus, unusually high, possibly because of dissolved organic phosphorus from decaying phytoplankton and macrophytes.
- **Nitrogen:** Surface dissolved inorganic nitrogen (DIN) was depleted by spring diatom and bloom activity. Hypolimnetic nitrate reached 390 µg l⁻¹ in September near Davis Dam. Organic nitrogen did not correlate with phytoplankton crop.
- **Bioassays:** Phosphorus addition significantly increased growth in the May experiment at M4. Nitrogen alone did not, indicating phosphorus limitation in spring. In August, initial dilution by river water was followed by enhanced growth after 234 hours.
- **Nutrient limitation:** DIN:DP ratios below 10 in summer were associated with blue-green algal dominance, while ratios above 10 favored diatoms. The authors suggest blue-greens have a competitive advantage at low DIN.
- **Budget:** Phosphorus retention was low, with 139 kg day⁻¹ of dissolved P converted to particulate form and lost through Davis Dam. Net DIN gain (2.33 × 10³ kg day⁻¹) was nearly offset by TON loss (1.84 × 10³ kg day⁻¹). Normalizing to mean Hoover discharge (324 m³ s⁻¹) raised retention for all fractions.
- **Regression:** Only DIN was significantly explained by discharge variables, with Davis Dam discharge the strongest (standardized coefficient 0.97, p < 0.01 in the reported table). The authors conclude that Davis Dam discharge has the key role in regulating nutrient balance.
- **Management implication:** Flow management, including maintenance of reservoir elevation, strongly affects the nutrient balance. Biogeochemical knowledge could help manage multipurpose reservoirs for both productivity and water quality.
