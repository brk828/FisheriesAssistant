# A Non-Lethal Stomach Pumping Technique for Recovering Gut Contents of Roundtail Chub

## Executive Summary
This study (Wasowicz & Valdez, BIO/WEST Inc.) evaluates a modified, non-lethal stomach-pumping technique for recovering gut contents from Colorado River cyprinids, using the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) as a surrogate for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). Forty adult roundtail chub were captured via boat electrofishing from the mainstem [[Colorado River]] near the Colorado-Utah state line (August 1992); 20 were stomach-pumped (test group) and 20 served as unpumped controls. Necropsy confirmed the technique achieved 100% recovery efficiency of gut contents with no damage to the esophagus, intestinal lining, or pharyngeal teeth. Because *Gila* species possess a simple S-shaped intestine and lack a pyloric sphincter, gut contents flush out the anal vent rather than the buccal cavity — a key procedural distinction from techniques used on other fish families. The method is recommended as a safe, standardized protocol for non-lethal diet studies of endangered Colorado River Basin *Gila* species.

## Background & Description
- **Digestive tract anatomy**: Simple, S-shaped intestine; **absence of a pyloric sphincter**, distinguishing *Gila* spp. from fishes with highly convoluted guts and pyloric sphincters — this prevents backpressure buildup and dictates flushing direction (anal vent, not mouth).
- **Pharyngeal dentition**: Typical pharyngeal tooth formula of 2,5-4,2 (per Miller, cited from unpublished source), though highly variable due to the **deciduous (polyphyodont)** nature of pharyngeal teeth.
- **Bilateral asymmetry**: Greater number of teeth on the left pharyngeal arch — a trait common among North American [[Cyprinidae|cyprinids]] (Eastman and Underhill 1973).
- **Tooth replacement biology**: Teeth are continuously shed and replaced (polyphyodont). New "tooth buds" develop in the pharyngeal epithelium adjacent to existing teeth; growth of the new tooth constricts blood supply to the old tooth's pulp, causing atrophy and shedding, followed by ankylosis (anchoring) of the new tooth to the pharyngeal arch. Replacement rate is tied to wear, not synchronized across all teeth.
- **Size range of study specimens**:
  - Test group (pumped): mean 279 mm TL (range 220–404 mm TL), n=20
  - Control group (unpumped): mean 260 mm TL (range 219–347 mm TL), n=20
- Mucosal tissue surrounding the pharyngeal arch in euthanized specimens precluded row-by-row tooth counts; only total counts per side were recorded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Habitat: Mainstem [[Colorado River]], collected near the Colorado–Utah state line.
- Trophic ecology: Diet dominated by **aquatic/terrestrial insects**, frequently mixed with **algae** and unidentifiable organic debris — consistent with an insectivorous/omnivorous feeding strategy typical of large Colorado River cyprinids.
- The [[Roundtail Chub]] serves as an ecological and morphological surrogate for the endangered, endemic [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]], sharing similar gut configuration, mouth parts, and pharyngeal tooth structure and counts (Holden 1968).
- Conservation context: Endangerment status of *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* precludes lethal sampling, making non-lethal dietary sampling methods critical to understanding factors limiting survival of these Colorado River Basin endemics (Valdez and Clemmer 1982).

## Key Findings
- **100% recovery efficiency**: No measurable food remained in dissected guts following stomach pumping across all 20 test fish.
- **Feeding results**: 8 of 20 pumped fish yielded measurable food volumes; 8 yielded only algae/debris traces; 4 contained no food at all.
- **No pharyngeal or esophageal damage**: No broken/damaged pharyngeal teeth attributable to the procedure; loose teeth observed in both test (9) and control (7) fish were attributed to natural shedding/ankylosis, not the pumping technique. No scratches or marks were found on tubing after esophageal insertion.
- **Anesthesia unnecessary**: Roundtail chub remained docile during pumping without sedation (MS-222 was used only for post-test euthanasia), suggesting anesthesia may also be unnecessary for humpback chub.
- **Anatomical basis for anal-vent flushing**: Unlike most previously studied taxa (salmonids, centrarchids, ictalurids, percids, esocids) where content is flushed from the buccal cavity, *Gila*'s simple gut and lack of pyloric sphincter necessitate flushing through the anal vent with minimal water pressure, reducing intestinal stress.
- **Comparison to prior literature**: Consistent with Gengerke et al. (1973), who reported 100% efficiency and zero mortality across 864 pumped fish of nine species, and Meehan and Miller (1978), who recovered 93% (by number) and 85% (by weight) of prey items from 155 salmonids with no significant survival difference after 30 days.
- **Practical recommendation**: Technique requires two operators (one to secure the fish, one to operate the pump) and careful pressure control to avoid intestinal blockage from large food boluses (e.g., algae mats).
- **Apparatus design**: Modified [[Seaburg Stomach Sampler|Seaburg (1957)/Gengerke (1973) design]] — two 50-cm lengths of flexible Tygon tubing (5–10 mm diameter, adjustable for fish size) connected to a hand-held rubber bulb (gasoline primer bulb); clear outlet tube inserted past the esophagus into the buccal cavity, flushing contents out the anal vent into a collection jar.
- **Management implication**: This validated protocol is now recommended for direct application to endangered *Gila cypha* and *G. elegans* populations, enabling non-lethal food habits studies critical for their conservation management in the [[Colorado River Basin]].
