# First Fossil Record of Threadfin Shad (*Dorosoma petenense*) from the Plio-Pleistocene Gatuna Formation, Southeastern New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This short paleontological note reports the first fossil occurrence of the herring subfamily [[Dorosomatinae]] (family [[Clupeidae]]), represented by a nearly complete juvenile of [[Dorosoma Petenense]] (threadfin shad). The specimen comes from the type locality of the [[Gatuna Formation]], an alluvial deposit of uncertain Plio-Pleistocene age in the [[Pecos River]] valley of southeastern New Mexico. Because the living species is not known from the Pecos River in historic time, the author argues that the fossil points to a more equable, probably wetter and cooler, climate during Gatuna time than exists today.

## Background & Description

Gizzard shads (subfamily Dorosomatinae) are a specialized group of herrings. They have a naked midline on the back anterior to the dorsal fin, a muscular, thick-walled stomach like a fowl's gizzard, and very fine, numerous gill rakers. They feed mainly on diatoms, unicellular green algae, copepods, and cladocerans filtered from the water. The group is largely migratory and primarily marine, entering brackish or fresh water to spawn, while some species are restricted to fresh water or form landlocked populations. The author notes that no post-Miocene clupeid had previously been reported as a fossil from North American alluvial beds.

**Specimen and locality:** The single specimen is about 34 mm standard length (46 mm total length). It was collected in 1977 by J. Platt Bradbury, who donated it for study. It comes from about 11 km east and south of Loving, Eddy County, New Mexico (SE¼ SE¼ SE¼ Sec. 33, T23S, R29E), at an elevation of 930 m (3,050 ft).

**Morphology:** The fossil is an almost complete juvenile. Its head is longer than the greatest body depth, as in living juveniles of similar size. The upper jaw tip is missing, but the projecting mandible typical of the species is visible. Fin positions and body proportions closely match a 35 mm standard-length comparison specimen from Guatemala (UMMZ 190064). Remains of the dorsal filament, which extends from the last dorsal ray, and impressions of ventral scutes along the abdomen are preserved. The dorsal fin origin appears slightly more anterior in the fossil, which the author attributes possibly to preservational distortion of the vertebral column.

**Diagnostic differences from the closest relative:** The fossil differs from the [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) in having fewer anal rays (21 versus 25 to 36) and fewer vertebrae (about 42 versus 48 to 51).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Gatuna Formation age:** The Gatuna Formation is a gravel and sand fill of the Pecos Valley. It was once regarded as post-[[Ogallala Formation]] in its southern area, but deposits about 165 km north suggest it predates the Ogallala. It straddles the upper [[Blancan Land Mammal Age]] and lower [[Irvingtonian Land Mammal Age]], and its base could extend back 2 to 3 million years, into late Pliocene time. No other fossils are known from the formation. The author notes that assigning a Pliocene fossil to a living species is not unusual, since Smith (1981) recorded 15 Recent species in seven families of U.S. and Canadian freshwater fishes with Pliocene records. Identification with a living species would also fit a Pleistocene age.

**Life history and habitat of the living species:**
- Spawning begins in spring when water temperatures reach about 21°C, in open water near plants or other objects, involving large schools.
- It is a plankton feeder that lives in open water and forms schools of thousands; age groups usually school together.
- Along the Texas coast, it occurs in bays with salinities of 3.8 to 26.9 ppt, with most captures between 10 and 20 ppt.
- In fresh water it is attracted to strong currents, and large aggregations gather below dams in spring and fall.
- It is very sensitive to cold, with extensive mortality reported below about 9°C; sudden temperature changes often cause mass deaths.
- It is short-lived, with a maximum life span of perhaps 3 years, and is smaller than the gizzard shad.

**Paleoclimate inference:** The author reasons that the living species' sensitivity to cold and its habitat needs imply that the proto-Pecos River had a more equable climate than today. [[Bachman (1976)]] stated that Gatuna-time climate was much wetter than present and probably cooler.

**Distribution:**
- Threadfin shad range from the Ohio River of Kentucky and southern Indiana westward and southward to Oklahoma, Texas, and Florida, and along the Gulf of Mexico coast to northern Guatemala and Belize. Its Ohio River populations appear to be recent, probably since the 1940s.
- Gizzard shads historically occurred up the [[Pecos River]] to near Carlsbad and up the [[Rio Grande]] above Albuquerque, but threadfin shad are not recorded farther up the Rio Grande drainage than Webb County, Texas.
- In Mexico, the species occurs well up coastal streams and in estuaries (Tampico to Laguna Tamiahua, Tecolutla, and Laguna de Alvarado). It also occurs in landlocked lakes, including [[Laguna Catemaco]] in Veracruz and [[Laguna de Petén]] in Guatemala, the type locality. In Guatemala it is strictly freshwater.

## Key Findings

- **First record of subfamily Dorosomatinae in post-Miocene North American alluvium.** The single specimen is the first reported fossil of this group, and the first post-Miocene clupeid fossil from North American alluvial beds.
- **Identification:** A nearly complete juvenile (34 mm standard length, 46 mm total length) from the Gatuna Formation, Eddy County, New Mexico, closely matches *Dorosoma petenense* in fin positions, body proportions, and shape.
- **Diagnostic counts:** The fossil has 21 anal rays and about 42 vertebrae, compared with 25 to 36 anal rays and 48 to 51 vertebrae in the gizzard shad.
- **Age:** The Gatuna Formation straddles the upper Blancan and lower Irvingtonian land mammal ages, with a base possibly 2 to 3 million years old, extending into late Pliocene time. The author regards a Pleistocene age as also consistent with the identification.
- **Paleoclimate:** The species' habitat and cold sensitivity suggest a more equable climate for the proto-Pecos River than today. Bachman (1976) describes Gatuna-time climate as wetter and cooler.
- **Biogeographic implication:** The species is absent from the historic Pecos River, so the fossil documents a past presence in the region that has since disappeared.
