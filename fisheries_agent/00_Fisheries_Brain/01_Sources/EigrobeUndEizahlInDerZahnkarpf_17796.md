# Egg Size and Fecundity in the Toothcarp Genus Aphanius (Pisces, Cyprinodontidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1979 study by H. Grimm (Zoologisches Institut und Zoologisches Museum, Universität Hamburg), published in [[Meeresforschung]] (Reports on Marine Research), compares egg size (measured as yolk volume) and fecundity across seven [[Aphanius]] species, with a focus on nine populations of [[Aphanius Anatoliae]] from southwest and west Anatolia. Egg size varies by a factor of 4.4 across the genus and by 3.5 within *A. anatoliae*. The differences are described as genetically based. Egg size and fecundity are inversely related, and the author proposes that both traits reflect adaptation to the salinity of the habitat, with small eggs favoured in saline water and large eggs in fresh water.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses the causal link between egg size and fecundity, which earlier fisheries work had mostly treated only in terms of population dynamics. The introduction reviews theory and examples:

- [[Svärdson]] (1949) proposed that egg number and egg size are adaptations shaped by natural selection.
- [[Peters]] (1963) found that brood-caring cichlids (*Tilapia*) lay few, large eggs, while substrate spawners lay many small eggs.
- [[Hempel and Blaxter]] (1967) linked herring egg size to food availability during spawning.
- [[Solemdal]] (1967) attributed larger flounder eggs in the Baltic to lower osmotic concentration of ovarian fluid in brackish water.

The study group is the genus *Aphanius*, a group of small cyprinodont fishes. Specimens were collected on expeditions of the Hamburg institute and kept in artificial seawater at 5‰. The fish included:

- **Anatolian species:** *A. anatoliae* (nine populations, imported 1973–1974), *A. chantrei* (two populations: Karpuzatan, Soysal), and *A. (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus* (Hazar Gölü near Elazığ).
- **Non-Anatolian species:** *A. iberus* (saltworks, southern Spain), *A. (Tellia) apodus* (thermal waters, Algeria), *A. dispar* (Siwa Oasis, Egypt), and *A. sophiae* (Maharloo Lake, Shiraz, Iran).

Yolk diameter was measured with a calibrated ocular micrometer. Non-spherical yolks were converted to spherical equivalents. Statistics included means, standard deviations, 95% confidence intervals, and the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney U-test. Length–age and length–egg-size relationships were fitted with exponential functions, and growth curves with the von Bertalanffy function.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The *A. anatoliae* populations live in extreme, species-poor habitats that differ strongly in total salinity and ionic composition. The author describes the lakes and springs as follows:

- **Burdur Lake:** deep, with salinity rising steadily to about 19‰ after the post-Pleistocene drying of the region. Only the small-egg population survives there.
- **Acıgöl:** a shallow lake whose water level fluctuates strongly. Marginal springs (0.92–2.14‰) hold fish, which spawn mainly in spring and summer. Springs dry up within about 200 m of the lake edge.
- **Freshwater populations:** all produce large eggs.

Temperature is also discussed as an ecological factor. Smaller eggs have a higher surface-to-volume ratio, which may help them in low-oxygen, warm waters such as thermal springs. *A. apodus* and *A. iberus* live in waters up to 32 °C, but the Anatolian populations do not differ much in temperature, so this explanation is not tested for them.

Geology is linked to speciation. Freshwater conditions prevailed in the Pliocene and early Pleistocene. Increasing aridity then produced salinization and isolation of the populations (cf. [[Kosswig]] 1961, 1964; [[Bering]] 1971).

## Key Findings

- **Egg size range:** Mean yolk diameters across the genus span 16.17–26.51 ME (1.01–1.66 mm). The volume ratio is 4.4:1, between the large eggs of *A. dispar* and the small eggs of the Burdur population of *A. anatoliae*.
- **Intraspecific variation:** In *A. anatoliae*, the largest eggs (Düğer, 24.48 ME, 1.53 mm) and the smallest (Burdur, 16.17 ME, 1.01 mm) differ in volume by a factor of 3.5. Within-population variation is low (variation coefficients below 5.2% for most populations; 9% in *A. dispar*).
- **Genetic basis:** Egg size differences are described as genetically based. Rearing the Düğer population at different salinities for three generations showed no significant change in mean egg size, so modification by salinity is small.
- **Three egg-size groups in *A. anatoliae*:** (a) large eggs: Düğer, Eflatun, Ilıca; (b) medium eggs: Acıgöl populations (Gelif, Kokarsu, Tahtaköprü, Gemiç, Acıpınar); (c) small eggs: Burdur.
- **Egg-size and fecundity relationship:** Eggs per female per day are inversely proportional to egg size. Large-egg females produce about 4 eggs per day, medium-egg females 10–11 (Acıgöl Gemiç and Acıpınar), and small-egg Burdur females about 20 per day (about five times as many). *A. dispar* (large eggs) produced about 4 per day, while *A. chantrei* and *A. iberus* (medium) produced 8–11.
- **Daily yolk production:** Large-egg populations produce 22,600–32,000 (ME)³ per day. Medium- and small-egg populations produce 40,000–43,500 (ME)³, which is 33–50% more. Per gram of body weight, the Burdur and Acıgöl females produce almost twice the yolk mass of Düğer females (Table 3: Düğer 39.1, Gemiç 74.3, Burdur 73.5 (ME)³ per mg body weight).
- **Age and length relationships:** In the Düğer population, egg volume increases with age and total length, especially in young fish. In the Burdur population, egg volume decreases significantly with age (p = 0.01), and no correlation with length was found.
- **Salinity adaptation hypothesis:** All freshwater populations have large eggs. Medium-sized eggs occur at 0.91–2.14‰, and the smallest eggs at 19‰. The author suggests that small eggs withstand higher osmotic pressure better, assuming equal membrane thickness. Larval mortality in saline water may be offset by higher fecundity.
- **Acıgöl hybridization hypothesis:** The intermediate and uniform egg size of the Acıgöl populations may reflect adaptation to intermediate salinity. It may also reflect heterozygosity or hybridization origins, as the Bastard hypothesis of Axelrod (1952) and Kosswig (1953) would predict.
- **Methodological limit:** Because *Aphanius* spawns almost continuously in aquaria, total seasonal fecundity could not be measured. Daily egg counts were taken over 12–72 days instead, so a direct link between fecundity and age or size could not be established.
- **Conclusion:** Egg size and fecundity in *A. anatoliae* appear to be adaptations to salinity, with fecundity increased partly by greater yolk production, not only by reallocation of yolk to smaller eggs.
