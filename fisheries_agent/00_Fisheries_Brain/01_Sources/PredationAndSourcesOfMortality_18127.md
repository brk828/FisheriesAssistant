# Predation and Sources of Mortality of Gerreidae in Natal Estuaries with Special Reference to the Kosi System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Gerreidae #estuarine-ecology #predation-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study investigated sources of mortality for three species of [[Gerreidae]] ([[Gerres acinaces]], [[Gerres rappi]], and [[Gerres filamentosus]]) in Natal estuaries, South Africa, between 1978 and 1982, with primary focus on the [[Kosi System]]. Despite being abundant benthic feeders forming an important component of the fish fauna, Gerres exhibited remarkably low predation rates by piscivorous fish (<1%). The research examined three mortality categories: fish predation, avian predation, and human exploitation. Results indicate that indigenous human fishing activities, particularly fish kraals and spear fishing, likely have the greatest impact on Gerres populations, while predation by piscivorous fish remains surprisingly limited despite prey abundance.

## Background & Description

The [[Gerreidae]] are a tropical family with members of genus [[Gerres]] occurring along the east coast of Africa as far south as the [[Zwartkops River]] in the eastern Cape, though uncommon south of the [[Mtamvuna Estuary]] in Natal. The [[Kosi System]] in northern Natal (26°54'S, 32°53'E) served as the main study area, with additional data collected from [[Lake St Lucia]], [[Mlalazi]], [[Fafa]], and [[Mtamvuna]] estuaries.

The three focal species—[[Gerres acinaces]], [[Gerres rappi]], and [[Gerres filamentosus]]—form an important component of the [[Kosi System]] fish fauna and provide significant food resources for the indigenous population. These species are benthic feeders consuming mainly bivalve siphon tips and invertebrates, occurring abundantly throughout Kosi and other Natal estuaries.

**Otolith-Length Relationships**: Linear regressions established relationships between standard length (mm) and otolith length (mm) for prey size reconstruction:
- [[Gerres rappi]]: y = 1.60 + 0.03x (r = 0.97; n = 31)
- [[Gerres filamentosus]]: y = 0.16 + 0.05x (r = 0.99; n = 35)
- [[Gerres acinaces]]: y = 1.38 + 0.03x (r = 0.94; n = 37)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Kosi System]] provides clear water conditions with narrow shelf areas, contrasting with the turbid conditions of [[Lake St Lucia]]. Gerres are essentially clear water species, occurring in eastern areas of Lake St Lucia and throughout most areas of the Kosi system. The benthic feeding behavior of Gerres keeps them near the substrate except in shallow waters, influencing their vulnerability to different predator types.

**Shoaling Behavior**: A distinctive "loose association" shoaling pattern characterizes adult Gerres (>40 mm), contrasting with the tightly packed shoals of other common estuarine species like [[Ambassis natalensis]], [[Ambassis productus]], [[Gilchristella aestuarius]], and [[Sarotherodon mossambicus]]. Gerres fry (15-25 mm SL) form closely packed shoals but typically frequent shallow margins where large predators are absent. Pre-spawning [[Gerres rappi]] form compact shoals of large individuals (>150 mm) before leaving the system, primarily between January and March.

The study area supports approximately 90 fish kraals (maze-type traps) stretching from the northern shore of [[Lake Mpungwini]] to within 1.5 km of the mouth, representing significant fishing pressure by the indigenous population.

## Key Findings

**1. Piscivorous Fish Predation (Extremely Low)**

Stomach content analysis of 1,008 piscivorous fish representing 26 species from the [[Kosi System]] and 118 fish of 9 species from other estuaries revealed remarkably low predation on Gerres:
- Only 7 individuals of 5 predator species contained Gerres (1% predation rate)
- 10 Gerres prey items total, with all but one being [[Gerres acinaces]]

**Predator-Prey Records**:
- [[Elops machnata]] (670 mm): 1 [[Gerres rappi]] (90 mm)
- [[Scomberoides lysan]] (139 mm): 1 [[Gerres acinaces]] (13 mm)
- [[Scomberoides lysan]] (116 mm): 4 [[Gerres acinaces]] (11-13 mm)
- [[Sphyraena barracuda]] (94 mm): 1 [[Gerres acinaces]] (12 mm)
- [[Sphyraena barracuda]] (380 mm): 1 [[Gerres acinaces]] (115 mm)
- [[Sphyraena jello]] (320 mm): 1 [[Gerres acinaces]] (80 mm)
- [[Pomatomus saltatrix]] (225 mm): 1 [[Gerres acinaces]] (82 mm)

[[Terapon jarbua]] (100 mm SL) was observed darting toward shoals of Gerres fry (15-25 mm SL) in the channel between [[Makhawulani]] and [[Mpungwini]], though successful capture was not confirmed.

**Shoaling Hypothesis**: The low predation rate is attributed to the "loose association" shoaling behavior of Gerres. Unlike tightly packed shoals that confuse predators but allow isolation of individuals (as demonstrated by Major 1977, 1978), the widely spaced Gerres shoals may prevent effective prey isolation. When threatened, Gerres individuals escape in any direction rather than attempting to rejoin a compact shoal, potentially making capture more difficult for predators.

**2. Avian Predation (Moderate to Significant)**

The clear water and narrow shelf areas of Kosi do not favor most piscivorous birds compared to [[Lake St Lucia]]. Key avian predators include:

**Cormorants** (Likely Major Predators):
- [[White-breasted Cormorant]] ([[Phalacrocorax carbo]]): Common between mouth and [[Mpungwini]]; maximum count of 65 individuals in estuary area
- [[Reed Cormorant]] ([[Phalacrocorax africanus]]): Present in lower salinity areas (<14‰)
- White-breasted Cormorants observed catching 38 fish in 10 minutes in reef area
- [[Gerres acinaces]] recorded in diet at [[Lake St Lucia]]
- Diet determination from pellets ongoing

**Kingfishers**:
- [[Pied Kingfisher]] ([[Ceryle rudis]]): Most abundant kingfisher; highest count of 27 individuals (including flock of 17) in July 1980
- Four pellets collected September 1980 contained 7 otoliths from 4 fish of 3 species: 2 [[Pranesus pinguis]] (82 and 70 mm), 1 [[Kuhlia rupestris]], and 1 [[Gerres acinaces]] (65 mm)

**Other Raptors**:
- [[Fish Eagles]] ([[Haliaeetus vocifer]]): ~6 resident pairs; observed catching mullet only
- [[Ospreys]] ([[Pandion haliaetus]]): Occasional summer visitor; observed catching mullet
- Benthic feeding behavior of Gerres likely limits vulnerability to surface-feeding raptors

Wading piscivorous birds occur but not in significant numbers; no Gerres recorded in diet of four wading bird species at [[Lake St Lucia]].

**3. Human Predation (Greatest Impact)**

**Fish Kraals** (Most Significant):
- 90 fish traps in operation from northern shore of [[Lake Mpungwini]] to within 1.5 km of mouth
- April 1981-March 1982: 37,302 fish of 34 species caught
- Gerres comprised 9.0% (3,379 fish) of total catch
- [[Gerres rappi]] made up 97% of Gerres catch
- 80% of Gerres caught January-March during pre-spawning aggregations

**Spear Fishing** (Three Methods):
1. *Traditional method* (older men): Stand motionless in waist-deep water; spear thrust to head between eyes; targets large fish (up to 500 mm SL). Sample of 10 fish: 7 [[Mugil cephalus]], 2 [[Gerres rappi]] (140 and 180 mm), 1 [[Sphyraena jello]] (800 mm)

2. *Juvenile method* (teenagers): Rush through shallows throwing spears randomly at juvenile fish shoals; catch composition unknown

3. *Underwater spearing with masks* (extremely effective): Two divers caught 60 fish (average 160 mm SL) in one hour on 2 April 1979 in channel near mouth; 59 of 60 were [[Gerres rappi]]. Effort easier during summer/autumn when large pre-spawning shoals move seaward. Juvenile [[Gerres acinaces]] speared over narrow shelf area. Estuary reef fauna now less diverse than eight years prior.

**Rod and Line Fishing** (Three Categories):
1. *European fishermen*: Target larger species ([[Pomadasys commersonni]], [[Rhabdosargus sarba]], [[Acanthopagrus berda]], [[Caranx]] spp.); occasionally keep [[Gerres rappi]] for bait

2. *African rod-and-reel fishermen*: Fish areas where shelf is narrowest (particularly [[Mpungwini]] south); catch [[Rhabdosargus sarba]], [[Pomadasys commersonni]], and [[Gerres rappi]] in unknown numbers

3. *Young Africans* (shoreline fishing): Daily fishing on rising tide within 2 km of mouth using crab bait and small hooks; target juveniles <80 mm SL. Sample of 20 catches: [[Gerres acinaces]] 55%, [[Rhabdosargus]] spp. 15%, [[Terapon jarbua]] 8%. Average daily catch: 12 fish per person; minimum 15 people fishing daily.

**Illegal Netting**:
- No authorized netting except research purposes allowed in Natal estuaries
- Illegal netting well-organized in [[Kosi System]], particularly in [[Mtando]] channel
- Confiscated nets: small mesh (~6 mm bar size)
- Catches consist mainly of juvenile [[Gerres rappi]] and [[Gerres acinaces]]

**4. Comparative Context**

Low Gerreidae predation rates consistent with other studies:
- [[Lake St Lucia]]: Whitfield and Blaber (1978) examined 12 piscivorous fish species; no Gerres found
- Lagos harbour: Fagade and Olaniyan (1973) found only 2 of 7 piscivore species ([[Elops lacerta]] and [[Caranx hippos]]) fed on [[Gerres melanopterus]], a common species
- Mangrove habitats: Austin (1971) examined 109 piscivorous fish where gerrids [[Diapterus rhombeus]] and [[Eucinostoma argenteus]] were most common juveniles; predation rate <3% by [[Centropomus undecimalis]], [[Sphyraena barracuda]], and [[Scorpaena grandicornis]]

**Conclusion**: Although Gerres are abundant in the [[Kosi System]], they do not form a significant part of piscivorous fish diets (<1% predation rate). This is likely related to their distinctive "loose association" shoaling behavior. [[White-breasted Cormorant]] and [[Reed Cormorant]] are probably major avian predators, though quantitative diet data were not obtained. Indigenous human population activities—particularly fish kraals, underwater spear fishing with masks, and shoreline juvenile fishing—likely have the greatest influence on Gerres populations in the [[Kosi System]].
