# Detection of Larval Remains after Consumption by Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study tested whether small nonnative fishes that prey on native fish larvae leave identifiable remains in their guts. Five nonnative species from Arizona were each fed a single larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and dissected at 15, 30, 45, or 60 min after consumption. Identification of larval remains declined with time for [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Yellow Bullhead]], but no clear time relationship was found for [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]], which masticated their prey. The authors conclude that gut content analysis is an unreliable way to demonstrate predation on native larvae, so failure to find remains in the field should not be taken as evidence that predation is absent.

## Background & Description

Declines in native fish populations have been linked in part to predation by [[Nonnative Species]] on early life stages. In southwestern North America, native fishes can disappear quickly where their larvae share habitat with nonnative fishes. Field studies have documented native young being consumed by nonnative fishes, yet few direct observations show the broad recruitment losses that predation could cause. Most earlier studies reported difficulty identifying small larvae or eggs in predator guts because of rapid digestion.

The razorback sucker is a native catostomid with successful annual spawning but near-total recruitment failure. Field evidence points to consumption of larvae by nonnative fishes, particularly green sunfish, as a likely cause of population collapse. The authors proposed several explanations for the scarcity of gut-based evidence: specimen collection and preservation methods, timing of collection and analysis, predator digestion and evacuation rates, and the fragility of larval tissues.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study setting and organisms.** Predators were collected in February 2006 from the Salt River and Sycamore Creek in Maricopa County, Arizona, by backpack electrofishing. Razorback sucker larvae (subsample n = 26 of 300 collected; mean total length 11.4 mm, range 10.3–12.8 mm) were collected after dark from [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona–Nevada, with dip nets and submersible lights.

**Laboratory conditions.** Fish were held in 37.9-L aquaria at ambient room temperature of 20 °C in dechlorinated, aerated water. Predators were starved for 72 h before the experiment and were acclimated for 30 min in individual [[Isolation Chambers]] before a single larva was introduced. The 20 °C temperature fell within the spawning range reported for razorback suckers in the lower [[Colorado River]] (10.5–21 °C). The authors noted it was near the warm extreme, and that digestion would likely slow at lower temperatures.

**Digestion and handling.** Digestion is known to speed up with temperature. Formalin fixation caused regurgitation in a number of fish, and fish held in collection buckets also regurgitated readily. Fragile larval tissue can be lost or discarded when many specimens are preserved together.

## Key Findings

**Predation and recovery**
- Sixty-five percent of experimental predators consumed a larva.
- Of the consumed larvae, 54 of 72 (75%) were retrieved from the predator or preservation jar.
- 18 of 72 (25%) predators regurgitated the larva during the brief period before death.
- Where larvae were not regurgitated, remains were most often found in the gill basket or foregut.

**Digestive state and identification**
- Remains were graded into four digestive states: (1) fully intact, (2) partially digested with identifying characters, (3) extensively digested with no identifying characters, and (4) no prey material present. States 1–2 were pooled as "identifiable."
- Logistic regression modeled the probability that remains were identifiable as a function of time since consumption, using [[Logistic Regression]] in SAS version 9.1.

**Results by predator group**
- **Pooled sunfishes** ([[Green Sunfish]] and [[Bluegill]]): identification declined significantly with time (G² = 20.96, P < 0.001). All eight larvae were identifiable at 15 min and none at 60 min. The model predicts a probability of 50% or greater for identification up to about 35 min after consumption. These predators swallowed prey whole, and mastication was not observed.
- **Yellow bullhead** ([[Yellow Bullhead]]): the time relationship was marginal (G² = 3.31, P = 0.069). A 50% or greater probability of identification required analysis within about 38 min. Larvae were sometimes damaged, regurgitated, and reconsumed. Digestion was rapid (under 1 h).
- **Pooled minnows** ([[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]]): no significant relationship with time (G² = 0.2105, P = 0.646). Only 1 of 11 larvae was identifiable, and none was identifiable at 15 min. Mastication in the pharyngeal mill rendered most remains unrecognizable.

**Pooling justification**
- Log-linear tests showed no significant species effect within either pooled group: sunfishes (X² = 4.22, P ≈ 0.37) and minnows (X² = 1.25, P = 0.5358). This justified pooling to increase sample sizes for regression.

**Interpretation and recommendations**
- Under these favorable laboratory conditions (known feeding time, known prey species, no competing food), identifying razorback sucker larvae was difficult after about 30 min. The authors argued that field conditions would be less favorable, so these results may represent a best-case scenario.
- Ruppert et al. (1993), cited in the paper, found larvae in the foreguts of 15% of red shiners when fish were euthanized with tricaine methanesulfonate rather than formalin.
- Identifying larval prey requires (1) consumption shortly before capture, (2) an active capture method such as electrofishing, seining, or angling rather than passive gear, and (3) preservation that limits regurgitation.
- Even under these conditions, gut analyses are likely biased. The authors recommend verifying predation with alternative approaches such as larval tagging ([[Otolith Tagging]]), [[Bioenergetics Modeling]], or immunoassay.
- Predation should not be dismissed as a major cause of population decline simply because routine [[Gut Content Analysis]] fails to detect larval remains.
