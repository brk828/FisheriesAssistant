## Executive Summary

This 1978 study by Michael A. Bell provides the first comprehensive description of the fish fauna of the **Santa Clara River system** in southern California, based on 46 fish collections made primarily between September and November 1975. Fifteen fish species were documented, of which only **five are considered native**: [[Threespine Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*), [[Tidewater Goby]] (*Eucyclogobius newberryi*), [[Pacific Staghorn Sculpin]] (*Leptocottus armatus*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), and [[Pacific Lamprey]] (*Lampetra tridentata*). All native species are classified as peripheral freshwater or anadromous fishes, indicating no historical faunal exchange with adjacent river systems. The paper documents the presence of the endangered **unarmored threespine stickleback** (*Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni*), whose only known native population survives in this drainage. The study establishes a baseline for assessing urbanization impacts and identifies major threats including channelization, water importation, and species introductions.

## Background & Description

**Study System**: The [[Santa Clara River]] system drains a large area of southern California, bounded by the Santa Susana Mountains (southwest) and San Gabriel Mountains (southeast). Major tributaries studied include:
- [[Sespe Creek]] — large tributary with numerous waterfalls (a few cm to >3 m), flowing through deep rocky canyons; mostly within Los Padres National Forest (including Sespe Condor Sanctuary)
- [[Piru Creek]] — largest tributary; flow regulated by Santa Felicia Dam (Lake Piru) and receives imported water from Pyramid Reservoir
- [[Castaic Creek]] — dry, sandy valley; receives outflow from Castaic Lake (also imported-water reservoir)
- [[San Francisquito Canyon]] — three discrete flowing segments, including discharge from Drinkwater Reservoir
- [[Todd Barranca]], [[Santa Paula Creek]], [[Arrastre Canyon]] — smaller tributaries, variably studied

**Methodology**: Fish were collected using a 3.048 m (10 ft) knitted nylon seine (3.175 mm mesh). Sampling was biased toward slow-water habitats (backwaters, pools, margins) because the original objective was to study **stickleback phenotype distribution**, meaning species preferring fast currents may be under-sampled. Specimens were fixed in 10% formalin, stored in 50% isopropyl alcohol, and deposited at the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County (LACM) and California Academy of Sciences (CAS).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Gradients**: Physical station data recorded stream distance from mouth, gradient, water temperature, depth, width, velocity, bottom composition, plant cover, and water color across 46+ stations, ranging from near-sea-level tidal influence (station 1) to high-gradient headwater riffle/pool systems in upper Sespe Creek (stations 32–37).

**Distribution Patterns**:
- *Gasterosteus aculeatus* and *Gila orcutti* were the most widespread species, occurring at nearly all stations with flowing/pooled water; *Gila orcutti* penetrated further upstream, tolerating stronger currents.
- *Gambusia affinis* absence correlates with increased stream gradient — largely absent in steep headwater tributaries.
- *Catostomus santaanae* was absent from small tributaries (Todd Barranca, San Francisquito Canyon, Arrastre Canyon) and upper Sespe Creek above a waterfall barrier (station 32).
- *Pimephales promelas* was restricted to low-gradient river reaches and areas connected to reservoirs receiving imported water, suggesting introduction via water transfer infrastructure.
- Lake-associated species (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*, *Dorosoma petenense*, *Cottus asper*) were concentrated near [[Lake Piru]] and [[Castaic Lake]], both recipients of imported water from **Pyramid Reservoir** (sourced from Sacramento/San Joaquin drainages) — a probable vector for non-native introductions.
- *Salmo gairdneri* occurred in cooler, higher-gradient Sespe Creek reaches and near the Fillmore Fish Hatchery discharge.
- *Lampetra tridentata* was restricted to a unique riffle-pool habitat in Sespe Creek.
- *Eucyclogobius newberryi* and *Leptocottus armatus*, euryhaline marine-derived species, were found only at station 1 near the river mouth.

**Native vs. Introduced Determination**: The author used historical records (e.g., Girard 1854 describing *Gasterosteus williamsoni* from "Williamson's Pass," now Soledad Canyon), fossil evidence (Pliocene-age sticklebacks), habitat-suitability reasoning, and testimony from long-term residents/researchers to classify species origin. All five native species are either **anadromous** or **peripheral freshwater fishes** — species whose distribution results from marine dispersal — supporting the conclusion of **no historical connectivity** with neighboring drainages (e.g., Santa Ana River, Ventura River, Los Angeles River systems).

**Comparative Case Study — Los Angeles Basin**: The author draws a cautionary parallel with the Los Angeles Basin ichthyofauna, where *Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni* went extinct between 1929–1942 following urbanization, channelization, and *Gambusia affinis* introduction; *Lampetra tridentata* is also likely extinct there. This is used as a predictive model for the Santa Clara system's vulnerability.

## Key Findings

- **High diversity for the region**: The Santa Clara River system supports 15 fish species, described as unusually high diversity for a southern California coastal stream.
- **Endangered subspecies stronghold**: The system holds the only known native population of *Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni* ([[Unarmored Threespine Stickleback]]), protected under California Fish and Game Code (Sec. 2050–2055) and federal endangered species provisions.
- **Two stickleback subspecies + intergrades**: *G. a. williamsoni* (mostly upstream of Saugus) and *G. a. microcephalus* (Sespe Creek headwaters) hybridize across much of the intervening drainage.
- **Introduction vectors identified**:
  - Direct stocking (e.g., *Salmo gairdneri* via Fillmore Hatchery and California Department of Fish and Game plantings in Piru Creek since ≥1931).
  - Imported water transfer via Pyramid Reservoir (linked to *Cottus asper*, *Pimephales promelas*, and *Catostomus fumeiventris* introgression into *C. santaanae*).
  - Forage-fish stocking for sport bass fisheries (*Dorosoma petenense*, *Notemigonus crysoleucas* in Lake Piru).
  - General California-wide introductions (*Gambusia affinis* for mosquito control, 1922; *Ictalurus punctatus*, 1895; *Pimephales promelas*, ~1950).
- **Channelization threat**: A 1972 U.S. Army Corps of Engineers proposal to channelize ~46 km of the river (including 18 km between Saugus and Lang) was identified as a major threat — potentially enabling introgression of *G. a. williamsoni* by *G. a. microcephalus* and facilitating invasive species movement.
- **Habitat destruction case study**: Channelized Santa Paula Creek (comparable gradient to unchannelized Todd Barranca) supports **zero fish species**, versus 3–4 species (including *Gasterosteus aculeatus*) in the analogous unchannelized reach — direct empirical evidence of channelization's impact on fish persistence.
- **Emerging biological threats**: Introduction of the [[African Clawed Frog]] (*Xenopus laevis*) into the drainage, likely via pet release, was identified as a potential predator/competitor threat to native fishes.
- **Hybridization risks**: *Gasterosteus aculeatus*, *Gila orcutti*, and *Catostomus santaanae* are all known to hybridize with confamilial species, raising concern that new introductions (especially via imported water) could cause further genetic introgression.
- **Recommendation**: The paper explicitly frames its dataset as a **baseline** for future monitoring of urbanization impacts, given rapid development pressure near Saugus and the Oxnard-Saticoy area.
