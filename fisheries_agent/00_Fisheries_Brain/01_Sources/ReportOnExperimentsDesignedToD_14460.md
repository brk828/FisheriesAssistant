# Effects of Underwater Explosions on Fish Life

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sebastidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 report by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Andreas B. Rechnitzer]] (Scripps Institution of Oceanography) examines how underwater explosions used in [[Seismographic Exploration]] for offshore oil affect fish. Explosives of the [[Dynamite]] type produced heavy fish kills in [[Scripps Submarine Canyon]] (1950) and in controlled 1951 trials, even at small charge sizes. Charges of [[Black Powder]] were far less lethal, which allowed the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] to permit their use. The authors conclude that offshore exploration with black powder can continue without undue destruction of fish life, and that the reported 1952 survey data support this.

## Background & Description

The conflict arose when the oil industry sought seismic data from rock layers beneath coastal waters of Southern California. Seismic work needs a point source of low-frequency sound, and explosions are the most practical source. Standard 60 percent gelatine dynamite produces a sharp shock wave with an abrupt front. This wave gives good seismic data but is very injurious to fishes with an air bladder, which include most important food and game species.

Fish kills from seismic work in California (Aplin, 1947; Fitch and Young, 1948) and in [[Chesapeake Bay]] (Coker and Hollis, 1950) prompted the state to revoke submarine oil exploration permits in 1949. Permits were renewed in September 1951, after the experiments in this report suggested that black powder could yield usable data with far fewer fish deaths. The authors note that little published work existed on the biological effects of slow-burning explosives such as black powder.

**Methods (summary):**
- **Cages:** Fish were held in 18 x 36 x 36 inch galvanized hardware-cloth cages on steel frames, chosen to avoid air-containing materials that could absorb shock energy. Usually two cages were used (one surface, one bottom); a nine-cage unit was used in some trials.
- **Pressure measurement:** Peak pressure (psi) was recorded by piezoelectric gauges and oscilloscope records by the [[Union Oil Company]] contractor group (United Geophysical Company).
- **Charges:** Standard units were 5-pound 60% dynamite, with smaller charges cut from sticks. Black powder (Hercules FFG and FFFG) was packed in hose sections or metal containers, up to 90 pounds.
- **Damage criteria:** Deliberately rigid. Any injury not clearly due to handling counted as probable death, and internal injuries were checked by dissection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Submarine canyon kill and recovery (1950-1951).** On April 6-7, 1950, ten 50-pound dynamite charges were fired in Scripps Submarine Canyon to test for induced turbidity currents. The recorded kill was 1,431 fish, including 1,032 [[Blacksmith]] and 245 [[Rockfish]]. The May 23-24 shots killed 3,357 fish on the first day, followed by only 121 on the second day. Of those, 89 were silversides (Atherinidae), which were presumably unaffected by the previous day's shots. The authors estimated that most of the fish population was destroyed through perhaps 1,000 to 1,500 feet of the upper canyon.

By 1951, diver Conrad Limbaugh observed 31 fish species at depths of 35 to 185 feet. Blacksmith were again the most abundant. Fish appeared more numerous in and over the canyon than in comparable nearby areas, and the canyon's fish life, valued by local anglers, had apparently recovered.

**Physical context.** Explosion effects depended on bottom type and depth. Jetted charges created a broad conical crater, leaving a possible shadow zone near the bottom. A jetted black powder shot produced a gas boil 50 to 100 feet across, with mud and sand brought to the surface. Above-surface spouts from black powder reached 50 to 150 feet, but black powder made much less noise than dynamite or Hercomite.

## Key Findings

**Dynamite and related explosives were lethal at small sizes.**
- **10-pound 60% dynamite:** Fatal to caged fish whether surface-set or jetted 50 to 55 feet into sediment. A surface shot killed 8 saury.
- **5-pound jetted charges (19 experiments, Nos. 4-22):** Fatal at distances of about 65 feet. Large kills of free-swimming fish occurred only for shots jetted less than 30 feet deep. When jetted deeper than 35 feet, kills were usually small, though two deeper shots (45 and 65 feet) destroyed fish in both cages.
- **2.5-pound jetted charges (Exps. 23-25):** Caged fish were damaged in all three trials. Experiment 25 killed 44 jack mackerel, plus about 300 injured fish that probably would have been preyed upon.
- **1.25-pound jetted charges (Exps. 26-36):** Free-swimming fish were killed at 10 feet, and caged fish were killed in six of nine trials. Heavy to complete kills occurred when peak pressure exceeded 70 psi, and little or no kill when peak pressure was below 60 psi.
- **4-pound Hercomite (two trials, 20 and 30 feet deep):** All 36 caged fish were killed. Hercomite appeared to resemble dynamite in its lethal action.

**Black powder was relatively harmless.**
- In 17 experiments with 4 to 40 pounds jetted 5 to 20 feet into sediment, no fish deaths were recorded inside or outside cages.
- A 10-pound black powder shot placed 5 feet from cages (Exp. 54) left the cage damaged, but the two fish inside survived without signs of injury.
- Of 28 black powder experiments, only one free-swimming Pacific mackerel was killed outside a cage.
- Table 5 (September 4, 1951 to March 21, 1952, Point Conception to San Mateo Point) records 2,847 shots, 2,335 open and 512 jetted, with 432 fish killed. The average kill was 0.013 per open shot and 0.79 per jetted shot, and the overall average was 0.15 per shot.

**Physical mechanisms.**
- **Peak pressure:** Dynamite lethal thresholds were 40 to 70 psi. Black powder peaks of 124 to 160 psi did not kill fish in cages.
- **Pressure decay:** Peak pressure is proportional to the cube root of charge weight, so a 5-pound charge gives about one-third the pressure of a 125-pound charge. Near-bottom pressure from jetted 5-pound dynamite decayed as the 2.6 power of distance, compared with about the 1.15 power usually reported in open water.
- **Rise time:** Dynamite produces a near-instantaneous shock front. Black powder burns slowly and produces a pressure pulse of long duration and low amplitude, with little abrupt rise.
- **Rarefaction:** Surface reflection converts compression to negative pressure, which the authors consider the main cause of higher surface mortality. Preliminary tests showed marine fish with air bladders were killed by negative pressures of 20 to 30 inches of mercury, while those without air bladders survived.
- **Tolerance:** Fish tolerated hydrostatic pressures up to 1,000 psi and sustained positive pressures of 15 to 500 psi for 12 hours without apparent harm.

**Other observations.** Game fish were not deterred from feeding after black powder shots. Bottom cages often showed less damage than surface cages, suggesting that many bottom species lack air bladders.

**Conclusions and recommendations.**
- Black powder "strongly" appeared safe for large charges (to at least 45 pounds) in submarine seismic surveys.
- Reservations about fish size and air-bladder variation should be tested during operations. Later reports support use of black powder up to 90 pounds without excessive fish loss.
- The authors state the conflict between oil exploration and fisheries had been resolved, and that the state was not faced with "oil versus fish."
