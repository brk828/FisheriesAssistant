# Life History of the Carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) in the Des Moines River, Boone County, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 study of [[Cyprinus Carpio]] in a 7-mile reach of the [[Des Moines River]] (Boone County, Iowa) examined growth, condition, age structure, reproduction, diet, and parasites. Fish were collected from October 1957 to October 1958, mostly by [[Electrofishing]]. The author found that weight scaled with length at a power (3.02456) not significantly different from the cube law, that [[Age and Growth]] estimates from [[Scale Aging]] and [[Opercle Aging]] agreed for 84.5% of fish, and that growth was about average or somewhat below other published scale studies. Diet was dominated by plant material and seeds, and a tapeworm, tentatively *Caryophyllaeus laticeps*, was reported in Iowa carp for the first time.

## Background & Description

Carp are among the most important angling fish in the Des Moines and other Iowa rivers, yet their growth and life history in these waters were poorly documented before this work. The study was conducted as part of the [[Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]], sponsored by the Iowa State Conservation Commission and Iowa State University, with cooperation from the U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife.

Sampling covered the river from Fraser Dam to Boone Waterworks Dam. Total length (tip of snout to end of caudal fin) was the primary measure, with standard and fork lengths also recorded on most fish.

- **Sample:** 520 carp collected; sex determined for 500 fish.
- **Length conversions:** Based on 143 fish in 1-inch groups. Mean total-to-fork factor was 0.890 and total-to-standard factor was 0.798. Conversion factors increased slightly with fish length.

### Length-Weight Relationship

The fitted equation was:

- log W = −1.34370 + 3.02456 log L

Here W is weight in pounds and L is total length in inches. An [[Analysis of Covariance]] found no significant difference between male and female regression slopes or adjusted mean weights, so a single equation was used for both sexes. The author cautioned that using inch-class means underestimates within-sample variance, so the nonsignificant results should be interpreted with care. The 95% confidence interval for the slope was 2.96 to 3.08.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Condition Factor and Water Level

Because the slope did not differ significantly from 3.0, [[Condition Factor]] (C) was expected to show no trend with length, and none was found. Seasonal condition changed with river conditions:

- C rose rapidly in spring as growth resumed.
- It dropped in late spring and early summer, possibly linked to spawning.
- It peaked in midsummer (July mean 51.0, n = 169), a period of frequent rain, high turbid water, and flood-plain inundation that provided extra habitat.
- It declined through late summer and fall (October mean 45.7).

Combined mean C was 49.6 (n = 517). The author considered these values average compared with carp from other areas (Carlander, 1953).

### Water Levels and Year-Class Strength

The most abundant year class was 1956 (339 fish, the strongest in the sample), which was produced during a period of low, stable water levels. Table 6 compares monthly stages from 1955 to 1958 with the 15-year average (1933–47). The 1956 summer readings were consistently lower and less variable than in other years.

### Reproduction

- The first spring upstream movement, with fish jumping over low-head dams, was observed on May 14, after surface temperatures reached 66°F on May 13.
- Gonad condition of seven mature fish captured May 17 ranged from green to ripe.
- Capture became harder from May 17 to June 25, coinciding with rising water level, stream velocity, and turbidity.
- The first spent fish appeared June 25. Ripe, partially spent, and spent fish were present through the summer, suggesting erratic spawning and possibly a split season, with some fish failing to spawn.
- Ovaries with two distinct egg developmental stages supported the split-season idea.

### Food Habits

Carp were omnivorous. Plant material was the most consistent food item and the bulk of the diet. Seeds were most common during high water, likely washed in by rain. Insects were minor until August, when water levels were stable or falling. Chironomid midge larvae dominated the insect fraction.

- **Combined diet (summer 1958, by volume, n = 75):** plant material 54.8%, seeds 22.3%, insects 10.3%, miscellaneous 12.5%.
- **Flood conditions:** earthworms and terrestrial insects were important during floods, especially for fish in inundated flood plain water.
- **Comparison:** Harrison (1950) reported a Des Moines River diet of 53% insects and 32% plant material, showing a different balance.

### Parasites

- ***Caryophyllaeus laticeps*** (unsegmented tapeworm) was common in the upper third of the intestine, usually 2 to 6 per fish (maximum 8). It is a common carp parasite in Europe, and the author found no earlier American record.
- The fish louse *Argulus* sp. occurred on several fish in low numbers, possibly under-detected.
- The anchor parasite *Lernaea* sp. was found on fins and unscaled body areas in limited numbers.
- The author judged parasite burdens too small to affect the population.

## Key Findings

### Age, Growth, and Aging Methods

- Scales and opercles were both used. Final ages agreed for 376 of 445 fish aged by both methods, or 84.5%. Only one disagreement exceeded one year.
- Scales were unreadable for 52 fish (10%) due to abnormal or regenerated scales. Opercles were unreadable for 10 fish (1.9%), leaving 510 aged by opercle.
- Of 74 disagreeing sets, 57 were resolved by joint reading and 17 remained in disagreement.
- Opercle resumption of growth was evident earlier in spring than on scales. The first scale annuli were observed May 17.
- Body-opercle regression was more nearly linear than body-scale regression:
  - Body-scale: intercept −0.1712 in, slope 0.1038, r = 0.972.
  - Body-opercle: intercept −0.6980 in, slope 0.5705, r = 0.998.
- Opercle-based back-calculated lengths were somewhat higher than scale-based values. Part of the difference may come from the zero-intercept method and from annulus measurement bias.
- Age group II made up 82.6% of the sample and grew more slowly than other year classes.

### Growth Comparisons

- Scale-based growth was somewhat below the average of other scale studies (Carlander, 1953).
- Growth was similar to carp in Fort Randall Reservoir, South Dakota ([[Shields 1957]]).
- Compared with [[Salt River Missouri]] carp, Des Moines River carp grew more slowly in the first three years but outgrew them in later years.
- Growth was above average in 1954, 1956, and 1957, with 1956 also a good growth year for channel catfish and river carpsucker in the same reach.

### Maturity and Fecundity

- A few fish of each sex matured at age II. Of 11 age I fish, 6 were female and 5 were male.
- The largest immature female was 13.2 inches. Mature males were as small as 9.3 inches, and immature males were up to 12.3 inches.
- Fecundity increased with age and length ([[Fecundity]]):

### Sex Ratio

- Males made up 44.5% (224) and females 55.2% (276) of the 500 sexed fish.

### Management Implications

- The study points to water-level-dependent recruitment, with strong year classes after low, stable river conditions.
- Growth-rate comparisons and split-season spawning suggest that management could consider flow regimes that protect spawning and early-life habitat.
- The author noted that the Des Moines River carp is an important angling resource, and this baseline data supports future assessment.
