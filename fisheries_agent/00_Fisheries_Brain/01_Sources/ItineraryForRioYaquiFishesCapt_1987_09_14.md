# Itinerary for Rio Yaqui Fishes Capture/Transport Trip

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ictaluridae #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #ConservationTranslocation #RioYaqui

## Executive Summary
This 1987 Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) inter-office memo details the logistics for a binational field expedition to capture and legally import four native [[Rio Yaqui]] fish species from Sonora, Mexico, into the United States for conservation purposes (likely refugia stocking at facilities such as [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]). The document is not a scientific data paper per se, but a field operations plan that reveals important ecological, taxonomic, and conservation-status context for the target species, including collection permits, target quotas, priority ranking, source localities, and gear specifications. The trip involved coordination between AGFD, [[Centro Ecologico de Sonora]] (CES), Arizona State University, Dexter, and San Bernardino personnel, reflecting an interagency/binational effort typical of endangered desert fish conservation in the [[Rio Yaqui]] drainage.

## Background & Description
No direct morphological measurements or descriptions are provided in this document, as it is a logistical memo rather than a species account. However, taxonomic identity of the four target species can be inferred:

- **[[Ictalurus pricei]]** (Yaqui Catfish) — Family [[Ictaluridae]]; a native North American catfish endemic to the Rio Yaqui basin.
- **[[Notropis formosus]]** (referred to as a "shiner," likely synonymous with or closely related to the Yaqui/beautiful shiner) — Family [[Cyprinidae]]; small-bodied minnow, described in the text as being caught via seining, consistent with a schooling, stream-dwelling cyprinid.
- **[[Campostoma ornatum]]** (Mexican Stoneroller) — Family [[Cyprinidae]]; a grazing/algivorous minnow typical of clear Sonoran streams.
- **[[Catostomus bernardini]]** (Yaqui Sucker) — Family [[Catostomidae]]; benthic sucker species associated with the Cajon Bonito drainage.

Gear specifications described (mesh sizes) offer indirect clues to relative body sizes of target taxa:
- Hoop nets (~1/2" mesh, 1 m diameter, some with 30 ft leads) — sized for larger-bodied catfish and suckers.
- Bag seine (1/2" wings, 1/4" bag) and finer-mesh seines (1/8"–1/4") — intended for smaller-bodied cyprinids such as *Notropis* and *Campostoma*.
- Trammel nets (100 ft, 4–5) — likely for larger catfish/sucker capture in deeper pools.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat/Locality:** All four species are native to the **[[Rio Yaqui]]** basin in Sonora, Mexico, an isolated Pacific-slope drainage distinct from Colorado River basin ichthyofauna. Specific collection localities named include:
  - **[[Natora]]** — primary site for *Ictalurus pricei* (river habitat suitable for hoop-netting and trammel-netting, implying pooled/slow-moving river reaches).
  - **[[Cajon Bonito]]** — secondary site targeted for *Notropis formosus*, *Campostoma ornatum*, and *Catostomus bernardini*, a well-known spring-fed tributary noted for high water quality and native fish diversity.
- **Environmental context:** The region is characterized by steep, rocky, remote terrain (very steep, rocky roads, ~400 mile round trips), suggesting these fish inhabit remote, low-disturbance montane/foothill stream systems with mixed pool-riffle structure.
- **Community structure:** The co-occurrence of a catfish (*Ictalurus*), a sucker (*Catostomus*), and two minnows (*Notropis*, *Campostoma*) in the same drainage reflects a typical southwestern desert stream fish assemblage with trophic diversity — benthic omnivore/detritivore (sucker), algivore/grazer (stoneroller), invertivore/planktivore (shiner), and larger-bodied benthic predator/scavenger (catfish).
- **Conservation translocation purpose:** The expedition's goal — importing live specimens under permit to U.S. facilities ([[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]) — indicates these species were subjects of ex-situ conservation/refugia programs, consistent with imperiled status of Rio Yaqui native fishes due to habitat loss, non-native species introduction, and water use pressures in Sonora.

## Key Findings
- **Permitted collection quotas** (as of Sept 1987): *Ictalurus pricei* (150), *Notropis formosus* (200), *Campostoma ornatum* (200), *Catostomus bernardini* (150) — with potential for adjustment upward if permitted.
- **Priority species** for the mission were *[[Ictalurus pricei]]* and *[[Notropis formosus]]*, indicating these were of higher conservation concern or logistical priority (possibly due to rarity or receiving-facility readiness) compared to *Campostoma ornatum* and *Catostomus bernardini*, which were considered "easiest to get."
- **Two-phase collection strategy**: Phase 1 focused on *Ictalurus pricei* at Natora (Oct 1–4, holding fish in-river prior to transport); Phase 2 targeted *Notropis formosus*, *Campostoma ornatum*, and *Catostomus bernardini* at Cajon Bonito (Oct 6–8).
- **Transport logistics**: Live fish were held in the river at capture site, then transported by tank truck to Douglas, AZ, with catfish transferred to a separate truck destined for [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico — indicating specialized live-fish transport protocols for sensitive desert fish species.
- **Multi-agency, binational collaboration**: Participants included AGFD, [[Centro Ecologico de Sonora]] (CES), Arizona State University researchers (Mike Douglas), Dexter National Fish Hatchery staff, San Bernardino NWR staff (Ben Robertson), and potentially a SEDUE (Mexican federal environmental agency) representative — highlighting the regulatory complexity of transboundary endangered fish conservation work.
- **Field gear inventory** documented: ≥8 hoop nets (1 m diameter, 1/2" mesh, some 30 ft leads), 4–5 trammel nets (100 ft), multiple seines (30 ft 1/2"–1/4"; 30 ft 1/8"; 15 ft 1/4"; 4 ft 1/8"), backpack and bank electroshockers, indicating a multi-method sampling approach tailored to different species' size classes and habitat types.
- **Logistical constraints** (fuel availability, remote unpaved roads, ~800 mile round trip) underscore the difficulty and resource-intensity of conducting native fish conservation work in remote Sonoran drainages, a recurring theme in [[Rio Yaqui]] fish conservation literature.
