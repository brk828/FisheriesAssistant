# Growth and Survival of Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study estimated growth and adult survival of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the upper [[Colorado River]] using mark-recapture data from fish captured 1990–1995. It relied on recaptures of [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT tags) to minimize dependence on [[Scale Analysis]] for aging, which the authors argue is unreliable for long-lived individuals. Mean annual growth declined with size, and adult survival was estimated at 0.83–0.87 using a modified [[Chapman-Robson Method]] applied to length distributions of fish 550 mm total length (TL) and longer. The authors present these values as a baseline for future monitoring, since no comparable historical data exist.

## Background & Description

The Colorado squawfish is an endangered, long-lived cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River System]]. Previous age and growth studies relied on scales, which are known to fail in several ways: many first-year fish do not form an annulus, and outer annuli are compressed or lost in older fish. Scales have also underestimated age in long-lived western catostomids and cyprinids ([[Scoppettone]] 1988). Validation has been limited because known-age individuals are scarce, and checking against other bony structures requires sacrificing rare animals.

External dangler tags, such as [[Carlin Tags]], were previously suspected of reducing growth, which created a discrepancy between scale-based and recapture-based growth estimates. Internally implanted PIT tags were adopted to avoid this bias. Burdick and Hamman (1993) found no growth difference between PIT-tagged fish and controls, and tag retention was 98–100% after 20–24 months.

The study had four objectives: refine growth estimates using recaptured PIT-tagged fish, estimate adult survival, estimate age by size and potential longevity, and examine the disappearance of very large individuals reported historically.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** About 300 km of river occupied by the species, upstream of the Green River confluence (river kilometer 0.0). Dams at rk 298.1 (seasonal blockage) and rk 303.0 (year-round) restrict upstream movement, and a dam about 3.5 km above the mouth blocks access to the [[Gunnison River]].
- **Reach division:** Adults concentrate in the upper reach, while early life stages occur mainly downstream. [[Westwater Canyon]] (rk 181–200) served as the boundary and was not sampled.
- **Habitat use:** Radiotelemetry showed high seasonal use of low-velocity, off-channel backwaters as main-stem flows rose during spring runoff.
- **Thermal conditions:** Water reaches the 13 °C growth threshold in March or April. Historically, the lower basin provided 1.5–2.3 times the annual thermal units for growth compared with the upper basin.
- **Other species:** The authors discuss the roles of the extirpated [[Bonytail]], the introduced [[Channel Catfish]] (as a mortality source through choking), native suckers, non-native minnows as forage, and predation by great blue herons and bald eagles.

## Key Findings

- **Sampling:** Fish were captured in late April to mid-June 1991–1994 using trammel nets to block backwater mouths, and electrofishing along shorelines where backwaters were rare. Captured fish were PIT-tagged and measured.
- **Growth by age:** Mean TL was 7.7 mm at hatching and 71.2 mm at age 1 (n = 73 fish seined in 1989). Growth was rapid through about age 10, when fish averaged about 550 mm TL.
- **Growth by length:** Mean annual increments were 42.7 mm/year for fish 400–449 mm, declining to 19.8 mm/year for 500–549 mm and 9.5 mm/year for fish 550 mm and longer.
- **Age–length:** Estimated mean ages were 15 years at 600 mm, 25 years at 700 mm, and 32 years at 800 mm. Individuals of the same length varied widely in age (for example, 20–40 years at 800 mm).
- **Growth consistency:** Growth in one year was not related to growth in the following year for fish 400 mm and longer, and growth rates did not differ significantly among 1991–1995.
- **Survival:** Adult survival for fish 550 mm and longer was 0.83–0.87 for combined 1991–1994 data, with a best estimate of 0.85. All years combined gave a range of 0.82–0.87.
- **Population stability:** Length distributions did not differ significantly among years, and trammel-net catch rates were stable from 1991 to 1994 (Kruskal-Wallis, P = 0.590).
- **Tag effects:** Mean annual growth was 14.4 mm for fish 450 mm and longer (n = 121) and 11.9 mm for fish 500 mm and longer (n = 104). These values match earlier estimates from Carlin-tagged fish, so the authors found no evidence that dangler tags reduced growth.
- **Scale limits:** Scale-based growth estimates diverge from recapture data beyond about age 10. The authors agree with [[Hawkins]] (1992) that scales are unreliable beyond roughly age 10.
- **Maximum age:** The largest fish captured (960 mm, 1990) is likely very old. For 900 mm fish, a minimum age of 34 years is suggested, with an average of 47–55 years. Only 3 of about 1,000 adults and subadults captured in 1979–1995 exceeded 900 mm.
- **Historic giants:** Anecdotal accounts report fish of 1,200–1,800 mm TL in the upper basin. The authors propose a third hypothesis: large fish grew in the lower basin and later moved upstream, where blockage by dams may now prevent this.
- **Mortality sources:** Likely contributors include high-water accidents, spawning stress, bird predation, stranding in gravel pits, and angling and netting mortality, although some impacts are declining. Angler harm is reduced by penalties for harming protected species.
- **Method limits:** The survival method requires multi-year population structure data and unbiased capture. It is not a replacement for Jolly-Seber models, and the trammel-net bias could not be tested.
- **Comparison:** The survival estimate is higher than the Green River estimate of 0.81 ([[Gilpin]] 1993), and well above values for [[Walleye]] in a lightly harvested population (0.65 females, 0.80 males).
