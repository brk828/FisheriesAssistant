# Report of the Symposium on Inland Fisheries Management and the Aquatic Environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Report (No. 681, Supplement) records the proceedings of a symposium on [[Inland Fisheries Management]] and the aquatic environment, held in [[Windermere]], United Kingdom, from 12 to 15 June 2002. It ran alongside the Twenty-second Session of the [[European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission]] (EIFAC). The meeting drew 114 participants from 27 countries, with 29 experience papers and 29 posters.

The symposium examined biological, environmental, social and economic impacts of fisheries management on lakes and rivers. Its central conclusion is that fisheries management can deliver benefits to ecosystems and stakeholders beyond the fishery itself. It also found that traditional fisheries management is not always implemented successfully in European inland waters. The field is moving toward integrated ecosystem management, which calls for new participatory approaches.

## Background & Description

The report is a symposium proceedings document, not a single research study. It is organised into six thematic sessions, followed by conclusions and recommendations. Its appendices list participants and provide abstracts of submitted contributions.

The symposium had four stated objectives:

- Exchange knowledge among countries and appraise fisheries management activities and their environmental impacts.
- Identify constraints and gaps in knowledge.
- Recommend and promote action to improve inland fisheries management for the aquatic environment.
- Provide guidelines for policy, planning and evaluation.

The sessions covered:

1. Impact of stocking and introductions on the environment
2. [[Biomanipulation]] as an environmental management tool
3. Rehabilitation of inland fisheries
4. and 5. Impact of fish community management
6. Role of fish conservation in environmental management

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several recurring ecological themes run through the report.

**Non-native species and introductions.** Species-poor European freshwater fish communities are vulnerable to invaders. Introductions can cause competition, predation, hybridisation and parasite transfer. The Lake District studies examined roach, ruffe and zander. Anguillicola crassus, a parasite of eel, has spread widely across Europe. Genetic introgression from stocked trout has been recorded in Spanish rivers and Newfoundland salmonids. Introductions are not always harmful. Whitefish and Arctic charr became established in large alpine lakes without detectable harm.

**Eutrophication and trophic control.** Biomanipulation, which reduces planktivorous fish or boosts predators, was tested in German, Danish, Hungarian and Dutch-linked lake systems. Its effectiveness depends on water chemistry, especially phosphorus availability and sediment oxygen conditions.

**Oxygen and habitat.** Hypolimnetic oxygenation extended usable fish habitat in Swiss lakes. However, it did not restore coregonid egg survival on the sediment while nutrient loading continued. Borrow pits and cutaway bogs can provide nursery and spawning habitat when flood timing is managed.

**Catchment-scale drivers.** Arterial drainage, riparian clearance, grazing and urbanisation degrade fish habitat. Restoration works often fail when they ignore catchment-wide problems.

**Wetlands and predators.** Fish populations in UK reedbeds are a food resource for the [[Eurasian Bittern]], a red-listed species.

## Key Findings

### Session 1: Stocking and Introductions

- Stocking and introductions are widespread, driven by angling demand. Examples include trout stocking in Spain and large non-native fish stocking in the UK.
- Illegal introductions and discarded or escaped live bait are believed to have established some non-native populations.
- Monitoring networks for introductions are largely absent, so causal links are hard to separate from environmental correlation.
- Participants agreed on a [[Precautionary Approach]] to species introductions. The English Lake District adopted legislation banning live and dead freshwater fish, salmonids and eel as bait.
- The Environment Agency's national strategy has five elements: education, legislation, policy, enforcement and audit.

### Session 2: Biomanipulation

- The classic approach, using stocked predators to reduce planktivores, was judged ineffective in central Europe at low trout densities. The [[Saidenbach Reservoir]] case used lake trout under 1 kg at 15–20 ind./ha. Trout consumed only 2–3% of roach and perch biomass, and the trout diet was 11% fish.
- At [[Salford Quays]], the report concludes that fish stocks were not the main driver of algal blooms. De-stratification and oxygenation were more effective than fish manipulation.
- A German survey of carp anglers (n = 705) reported average annual catch of 332.7 kg per angler. Ground-baiting supplied about 1.018 kg gross P per angler per year. Carp angling can reduce carp stocks but may also add phosphorus through excessive baiting.
- The report recommends including experienced carp anglers where dense carp stocks drive eutrophication. Catch-and-release should be avoided in biomanipulation projects.
- A step-by-step guideline for lake restoration by biomanipulation was proposed. It integrates stakeholder interests and includes phosphorus thresholds and critical fish biomass values. It still needs field testing.
- Long-term fisher involvement is needed for projects that rely on continuous fishing pressure.

### Session 3: Rehabilitation

- Hypolimnetic oxygenation met the 4 mg O₂/l criterion in three Swiss lakes over more than ten years. It did not restore coregonid natural reproduction, because sediment decomposition continued to suffocate eggs.
- Two Irish cutaway-bog lakes (approx. 3.5 ha each, created in 1996) reached about 95% aquatic plant cover by summer 2000. Anglers' individual catches of up to 45 kg were recorded.
- Irish salmonid restoration emphasises catchment-level baseline studies and a control subcatchment for monitoring.
- For salmonid stocking, a net gain is possible where natural egg-to-smolt survival is about 1% or less. Atlantic salmon fry survival in English streams averaged 22% (7.8–41.3%) to the end of the first growing period.
- Managed flooding of Czech borrow pits raised species richness and provided nursery habitat, but early floodwater recession limited benefits.
- Participants noted that rehabilitation often fails because expectations are unrealistic and post-project monitoring is absent.

### Sessions 4 and 5: Fish Community Management

- Under the [[Water Framework Directive]], a French approach defined "contexts" as minimum units sustaining an indicator species life cycle. The analysis found that most French aquatic environments are disturbed.
- Ecohydrological modelling (Zalewski) concluded that restoration to pristine conditions is neither realistic nor necessary. The target should be intermediate disturbance, balancing biodiversity and productivity.
- On Lake Peipsi-Pihkva, shared between Estonia and Russia, larger mesh sizes and total allowable catch quotas raised catch potential.
- The Lower Danube sturgeon fishery has collapsed. Romanian annual sturgeon catches fell from 1,000 to 10 tonnes since the early 20th century. Fishing effort is 2–5 times historical levels, with about 2,500 fishing units and 5,000 fishermen by 1994. The authors call for entry limits, effort reduction and catch quotas.
- A French study of 20 natural lakes and 30 reservoirs found that non-native species are associated with lower native richness and reduced biodiversity.
- Finnish crayfish management shows that clear rules work. However, the [[Signal Crayfish]], which resists crayfish plague, creates incentives for local non-compliance.
- Hungarian experiments support the view that bottom-up forces matter more than top-down effects in shallow cyprinid-dominated lakes. Stocking multispecies piscivore communities (min. 25%) was suggested for water quality.
- Stakeholder attitudes shape management outcomes, as shown by Finnish [[Saimaa Ringed Seal]] conservation and Lithuanian poacher and "insider/outsider" studies.

### Session 6: Fish Conservation

- Freshwaters have been intensively altered over the past 100 years. Many fish species are now extinct, rare or endangered.
- Sturgeon conservation relies on gene banks and managed release strategies. The Kuban River hatcheries released 26–28 million juveniles annually (1996–2001) with no natural spawning. A federal Living Gene Bank in Krasnodar held over 8,000 sub-adults of seven species.
- Research in the Sea of Azov proposed a staggered release scheme for juvenile Russian and stellate sturgeon.
- At Minsmere (UK), roach sometimes became dominant after reedbed rehabilitation, which may reduce food availability for bitterns. Around 20 breeding male bitterns were reported in the UK.
- The conservation plan for [[Anaecypris Hispanica]] in Portugal was expected to benefit education, other endangered species, ecosystem health and social-economic outcomes.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- Improve communication and education programmes on fisheries regulation compliance, since public unwillingness to respect rules was noted.
- Develop new guidelines for biomanipulation, and update stocking and introduction guidelines into national and local policy.
- Develop improved stocking protocols for sturgeon.
- Include all stakeholders in consultative and decision-making processes, moving toward full participatory management.
- Fully evaluate catchment basins before restoration works, and set realistic restoration targets.
- Adopt post-project monitoring as a component of evaluation, with wide dissemination of results.
- Apply risk-based approaches to all fisheries management. Regulatory strength should match potential risk.
- Establish or reinforce mechanisms for managing international water bodies.
- Develop in vivo conservation mechanisms for endangered fish, with sturgeons as the priority.
- The implications for the [[Water Framework Directive]] and for global environmental change should be recognised in future management.
