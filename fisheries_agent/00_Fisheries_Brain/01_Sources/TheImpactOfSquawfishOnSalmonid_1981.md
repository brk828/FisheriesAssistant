# The Impact of Squawfish on Salmonid Populations: A Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1981 review by Brown and Moyle critically examines the literature underpinning the widespread belief that [[Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus* spp.) are significant predators and competitors of [[Salmonids]] (salmon and trout) in western North America. Despite this belief having driven numerous costly control programs (electrical barriers, dynamiting, rotenone treatment, and the piscicide **Squoxin**), the authors find the evidence is largely anecdotal, confounded, or context-dependent. Squawfish predation on salmonids is documented to be significant primarily in **lakes** (e.g., [[Lake Washington]]) and in **highly localized stream conditions** related to dam/diversion effects or poorly timed hatchery releases—not under natural stream conditions generally. Evidence for meaningful **interspecific competition** with trout is even weaker, with habitat partitioning studies suggesting spatial segregation minimizes overlap. The authors conclude that squawfish "control" programs are likely addressing symptoms of anthropogenic habitat disturbance rather than a genuine biological threat.

## Background & Description

- **Family:** [[Cyprinidae]] — squawfish are the largest native cyprinids in North America.
- **Four recognized species**, each associated with a major drainage basin:
  - **Colorado squawfish** (*P. lucius*) — largest species, individuals reported up to **36 kg**; range drastically reduced by damming/diversion of the Colorado River; federally and state listed as endangered ([[Colorado Squawfish]]).
  - **Sacramento squawfish** (*P. grandis*) — found in Sacramento–San Joaquin, Russian River, and Pajaro-Salinas drainages of central California ([[Sacramento Squawfish]]).
  - **Umpqua squawfish** (*P. umpquae*) — restricted to the Umpqua and Siuslaw rivers, Oregon ([[Umpqua Squawfish]]).
  - **Northern squawfish** (*P. oregonensis*) — widest distribution: Columbia River system, Harney-Malheur Basin, coastal Washington/British Columbia drainages north to the Nass River, and the Peace River (east of the Continental Divide) ([[Northern Squawfish]]).
- **Ontogenetic dietary shift** correlates with body size:
  - Small juveniles: aquatic insects and small invertebrates.
  - Transition size ~18–25 cm FL (fork length): fish and crayfish become dominant prey (>50% of diet in individuals ≥18 cm FL, per Falter 1969).
  - Larger adults (20–30+ cm): predominantly piscivorous/crayfish-feeding.
- **Growth rate:** notably slow, suggesting high metabolic demand, heavy reproductive investment, or infrequent feeding.
- **Thermal tolerance:** upper incipient lethal temperature ~29°C (Black 1953); preferred range 16–19°C but tolerant of warmer water; can survive dissolved oxygen as low as 0.8 mg/L (Dimick and Merryfield 1945) — markedly more tolerant of degraded conditions than [[Rainbow Trout]] (preferred range 13–21°C, avoiding >22°C; requires higher dissolved oxygen).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Feeding behavior:** Opportunistic generalist predators, feeding on whatever invertebrate/vertebrate prey is most abundant. Peak feeding around dusk/dawn, though full stomachs occur throughout day/night (Steigenberger and Larkin 1974).
- **Digestion rates:** Rapid and highly temperature-dependent — ~5%/hour at 4–6°C rising to 40–50%/hour at 24°C; ~14%/hour at 10–12°C with two-thirds of fish clearing guts within 24 hours. High frequency of empty stomachs in dietary studies may reflect regurgitation upon capture rather than infrequent feeding.
- **Habitat partitioning with salmonids:**
  - In streams, squawfish typically occupy large, slow pools while trout occupy areas of faster current (Falter 1969; [[St. Joe River]]).
  - Physical factors (stream velocity, temperature) — not biological interactions — best explain co-occurrence patterns (Dettman 1976, [[Deer Creek]]; Smith, in press).
  - In lakes, juvenile squawfish tend toward littoral zones while juvenile sockeye salmon are limnetic ([[Lake Washington]]; Eggers et al. 1978), indicating spatial segregation.
- **Predator-prey dynamics with salmon smolts:**
  - Squawfish predation on migrating smolts is concentrated below **dams and diversions**, where confused/injured fish concentrate (e.g., Columbia River tailraces; Red Bluff Diversion Dam on the Sacramento River).
  - Natural anti-predator behaviors in smolts — nocturnal mass migration, use of high flow/turbidity — reduce vulnerability (Ginetz and Larkin 1976; Foerster 1968).
  - Predator-avoidance behavior in juvenile salmonids is rapidly learned within days of predator exposure (Kanid'yev 1970; Patten 1977).
- **Sculpins (*Cottus* spp.)** frequently emerge as a dominant, non-salmonid prey item across multiple systems (Willamette River, St. Joe River), and are themselves considered potential salmonid competitors/predators ([[Sculpin]]).
- **Community role:** Positioned as a potential "keystone" predator in some lake systems (e.g., alongside arctic charr, cutthroat trout, and coho salmon in [[Cultus Lake]]), but comparative predation-efficiency studies (Foerster 1968) show other salmonid predators consumed sockeye at 3–19x the per-capita rate of squawfish.
- **Interaction with anthropogenic disturbance:** Channelization, riparian vegetation removal, and impoundment raise water temperature and lower dissolved oxygen — conditions favoring squawfish physiology over trout, potentially shifting community balance independent of direct predation/competition.

## Key Findings

1. **Streams:** Squawfish are **not significant predators** of salmon/trout under natural conditions. Elevated predation is restricted to highly localized, seasonal, or unusual circumstances — chiefly below dams/diversions or following poorly timed hatchery smolt releases (e.g., Thompson 1959 found 87% of countable prey in stomachs were salmon, but this correlated almost entirely with nearby hatchery releases; Buchanan et al. 1980/1981 found only 1.4–2% of Willamette River squawfish stomachs contained salmonids under natural migration conditions).

2. **Lakes:** Squawfish predation on juvenile sockeye salmon can be substantial in some systems (e.g., an estimated **3 million sockeye consumed** in [[Lake Washington]] from June 1972–May 1973), but sockeye still comprised only 10–30% of diet (prickly sculpin dominated at ~70%). Critically, **no study demonstrates that this juvenile predation translates into reduced adult salmon returns** — removal experiments at Cultus Lake showed a 3-fold increase in smolt production following predator reduction, but this could not be disentangled from control of other predators, and increased smolt output did not necessarily improve adult returns due to density-dependent effects (food competition, smaller smolt size, higher marine mortality) and a documented increase in competing stickleback populations.

3. **Competition:** Evidence for meaningful interspecific competition between squawfish and trout is **insufficient and inconclusive**. Diet-overlap studies are compromised by coarse (order-level) prey identification and lack of demonstrated food limitation. Habitat studies consistently show squawfish and trout are segregated by physical factors (velocity, temperature) rather than actively competing. Laboratory trials (Li 1975) showed rainbow trout dominate and are more aggressive than Sacramento squawfish, suggesting squawfish are unlikely to exclude trout from feeding territories.

4. **Confounding factors:** Apparent inverse correlations between squawfish and trout abundance can arise from non-competitive causes — e.g., overfishing of trout stocks or human-driven habitat degradation favoring warmwater-tolerant squawfish — rather than direct biological interaction (illustrated via a Lotka-Volterra competition model in Li 1975).

5. **Management implication:** The authors argue that squawfish "control" programs frequently rest on anecdotal justification rather than robust demonstration of impact, and that in disturbed systems, squawfish abundance is more likely a **symptom** of anthropogenic habitat alteration (impoundment, flow regulation, hatchery release timing) than a primary cause of salmonid decline. They call for future research on: energy budgets and consumption rates across temperatures, squawfish's potential "keystone" role in fish communities, comparative microhabitat/food-use studies under sympatry, and how dam/diversion operations amplify predation risk to inform better-timed hatchery releases.
