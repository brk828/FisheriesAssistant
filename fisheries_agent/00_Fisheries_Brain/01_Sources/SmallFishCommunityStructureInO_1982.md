# Small Fish Community Structure in Ozark Streams

## Executive Summary
This study by [[William J. Matthews]] re-examines abundance data for 13 common "minnow" species (Cyprinidae, Cyprinodontidae, Atherinidae) across eight Ozark watersheds of the White River system (Arkansas/Missouri) to test whether apparent nonrandom patterns of species co-occurrence reflect true biotic structuring (e.g., competition) or are statistical artifacts of random species assembly. Using null-model simulations (following [[Connor and Simberloff]] and [[Diamond's Assembly Rules]]), the paper finds that **species richness per watershed is nonrandomly distributed** (more "restricted" and more "widespread" species than expected by chance) and that **morphologically distinctive species tend to be widespread**, supporting a role for diffuse competition in limiting overall community size. However, **specific mutually exclusive species pairs occur no more often than expected by chance**, indicating that pairwise competitive "assembly rules" do not drive fine-scale community structure in these systems.

## Background & Description
- **Study system:** Eight watersheds of the White River drainage, Salem Plateau, Ozark Uplift (north Arkansas/south Missouri): [[Big Creek]], [[Piney Creek]], [[Sylamore Creek]], [[Strawberry River]], [[Jane's Creek]], [[Fourche River]], [[Eleven Point River]], and [[Cane Creek]].
- **Habitat type:** Clear, non-cold, free-flowing limestone streams with high-gradient upper reaches; minimal pollution; sparsely populated drainages. Six smaller watersheds (Big, Cane, Jane's, Fourche, Sylamore, Piney) were treated as the primary comparable dataset; the larger [[Strawberry River]] and [[Eleven Point River]] were used for comparative context only.
- **Taxa analyzed:** 13 small, free-swimming, pool/channel-dwelling "minnow" species common to the region, all partial insectivores, sharing similar food/habitat requirements (unlike *Campostoma anomalum*, *Phoxinus erythrogaster*, and *Semotilus atromaculatus*, which were excluded due to divergent ecology).
- **Taxonomic notes:** *Notropis pilsbryi* and *N. zonatus* were combined due to close morphological/ecological similarity and complementary (non-overlapping) geographic distribution. *Notropis chrysocephalus* treated as distinct per [[Gilbert 1964]]; *Dionda nubila* referred to *Notropis nubilis* per [[Robins et al. 1980]].
- **Data source:** Compiled from prior theses/publications by graduate students of [[G.L. Harp]] and [[J.K. Beadles]] (Arkansas State University), based on standardized seine sampling across all seasons and sites within each watershed (1969 onward).
- **Abundance scale:** 0 = absent, 1 = rare, 2 = occasional, 3 = common, 4 = abundant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Watersheds are physically/chemically similar (clear, uncontaminated, similar drainage area/mainstream length for the six small systems) yet show substantial variation in which "minnow" species are common/abundant — variation not explained by geography, drainage pattern (White vs. Black River tributaries), water quality, or predator fish community composition (predator richness was similar across watersheds).
- Large mainstream rivers (White, Black, Spring) act as poor conduits for small-stream fish dispersal, so each watershed's minnow fauna developed largely independently (per [[Black 1940]] and [[Pflieger 1971]]).
- Ecological traits assessed: **body width/depth ratio** (streamlining/habitat use, per [[Hubbs 1941]]) and **mouth gape width** (food use, per [[Keast and Webb 1966]]) — used as proxies for niche position in two-dimensional morphological space.
- Predation pressure was ruled out as an explanatory factor for differential minnow abundance, as predatory fish taxa/richness were comparable across all watersheds.
- The paper situates its findings within the broader null-model/neutral-model literature on community assembly (e.g., [[Caswell 1977]], [[Connor and Simberloff 1979]], [[Joern and Lawlor 1980]]), directly testing whether "assembly rules" (sensu [[Diamond 1975]]) apply to stream fish communities as they had been proposed for insular avifaunas.

## Key Findings

### 1. Species Distribution Patterns (Table 1)
- Only **[[Fundulus olivaceus]]** was common/abundant in all six small watersheds.
- Four species — **[[Fundulus catenatus]]**, **[[Pimephales notatus]]**, **[[Notropis telescopus]]**, and **[[Notropis nubilis]]** — were common/abundant in all but one watershed.
- The remaining eight species were common/abundant in no more than four of six watersheds.
- Notable local anomalies: *N. nubilis*, *N. pilsbryi/zonatus*, and *N. telescopus* were rare/absent in [[Big Creek]] despite being abundant elsewhere; conversely, *[[Labidesthes sicculus]]* and *[[Notropis boops]]* were abundant in Big Creek but rare in nearby [[Piney Creek]] and [[Sylamore Creek]].
- Minnow faunal similarity did not track geographic proximity (Big Creek fauna more similar to Cane/Jane's/Fourche than to geographically closer Piney/Sylamore creeks).

### 2. Restricted vs. Widespread Species (Nonrandom)
- Null model: 10 simulated matrices randomly distributed the 13 species among six watersheds, holding column totals (species richness per watershed) constant.
- **Result:** Actual distribution of species vs. number of watersheds occupied differed significantly from random expectation (**χ² = 40.599, df = 5, p < 0.005**).
- More species were highly restricted (common/abundant in only 1 watershed) and more were widespread (common/abundant in 5 watersheds) than chance predicted; no species was common/abundant in exactly 3 watersheds, though ~3 were expected by chance.
- **Conclusion:** Some nonrandom (likely biotic) factor governs how many watersheds a given species is abundant in.

### 3. Morphology vs. Distribution Breadth
- Species with morphology at the periphery of morphological space (large gape or extreme body roundness) — **F. olivaceus, F. catenatus, N. chrysocephalus, N. nubilis, P. notatus** — were common/abundant in 4–6 of six watersheds.
- Species near the morphological center (a cluster of 8 "near-average" species) showed the most variable occurrence; no more than 5 of these 8 were ever co-abundant in a single small watershed.
- Distance from morphological centroid (mean = 27.9 units) correlated with breadth of distribution: species farther than the mean distance were common/abundant in significantly more watersheds than species closer to the mean (**t-test, p < 0.05**).
- **Conclusion:** Ecologically/morphologically distinctive species achieve wider regional abundance, consistent with reduced competitive interference.

### 4. Mutually Exclusive Species Pairs
- Among the 8 morphologically "average" species (28 possible pairs), 9 pairs were never mutually common/abundant in the same small watershed.
- Two pairs had a plausible competitive/biological basis due to shared morphology (narrow gape, long lower jaw, V-shaped mouth corner, mid/upper water column use): **[[Labidesthes sicculus]]–[[Notropis rubellus]]** and **[[Notropis boops]]–[[Notropis rubellus]]**. Notably, these pairs do co-occur abundantly in the larger Strawberry and Eleven Point rivers.
- **Probability test:** Given actual per-species watershed occupancy rates, expected number of mutually exclusive pairs = **11.6**; actual observed = **9**.
- **Conclusion:** The observed number of exclusive pairs is consistent with chance; pairwise mutual exclusion does not significantly exceed random expectation.

### 5. Random vs. Nonrandom Assembly of Species Pairs
- Using modified simulations that fixed both row totals (watersheds occupied per species) and column totals (species per watershed) to match real data (per [[Connor and Simberloff 1979]] methodology), the observed distribution of species-pair co-occurrence across watersheds closely matched random expectation (**χ² = 2.047, df = 5, 0.5 < p < 0.9**).
- Simulations predicted ~7 mutually exclusive pairs; actual data showed 9 — a close match.
- **Conclusion:** Overall assembly of species pairs into Ozark "minnow" communities requires no nonrandom process or competitive "assembly rules" to explain observed co-occurrence patterns.

### Synthesis / Proposed Model
- A **core group** of morphologically distinctive species (*F. olivaceus, F. catenatus, P. notatus*) is common/abundant in nearly all small Ozark watersheds, forming a consistent baseline assemblage.
- A **variable group** of morphologically "near-average" species is added to local assemblages seemingly at random, up to a ceiling (typically 5 species) likely set by habitat complexity, productivity, and/or **diffuse competition** (sensu [[Pianka 1974]]) rather than pairwise species-specific competitive exclusion.
- Overall: biotic interactions (competition) may cap the *total number* of abundant "minnow" species per watershed and influence which species achieve widespread dominance, but do **not** dictate specific pairwise co-occurrence patterns — challenging strict competition-based "assembly rule" models for stream fish communities.
