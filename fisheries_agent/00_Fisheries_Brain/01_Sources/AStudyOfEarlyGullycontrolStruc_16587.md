# Early Gully-Control Structures in the Colorado Front Range

#freshwater-ecology #geomorphology #gully-control #erosion-control

## Executive Summary

This Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station report by [[Burchard H. Heede]] evaluates check dams and related structures installed in the 1930s to control gullies on the [[Pike and San Isabel National Forests]], 20 to 25 years after construction. The study covers four gully systems in the [[Colorado Front Range]] and finds that gully control succeeds only when [[Critical Locations]] are treated, structures are designed for the expected peak flow, and sound materials and bank protection are used. Over-spaced or undersized dams, failed wooden components, and willow plantings that obstructed flow were common causes of failure. The report concludes with principles and recommendations for future gully treatment.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The study had two aims: (1) to evaluate gully-control structures installed 20 to 25 years earlier on the Pike and San Isabel National Forests, and (2) to show the role of [[Check Dams]] in gully control.

**Rationale.** Gully control stabilizes valley bottoms at a set base level by promoting sediment accumulation in the gully channel. This reduces sediment deposition in river streambeds and improves water supply quality, benefiting both on-site and downstream users.

**Method.**
- A design storm of 2 inches of precipitation, derived from records at the [[Manitou Experimental Forest]], estimated stormflow.
- [[Infiltration Curves]] were established for each vegetation-soil complex (e.g., open timber on granitic soils, grass on granitic alluvium). Applying them to the storm's rainfall pattern gave rainfall excess (runoff).
- Peak flow per acre was computed for each vegetation-soil complex. Watershed areas were measured on aerial photographs with a radial-line plotter. Peak flow at a structure equaled the upstream segment acreage multiplied by per-acre peak flows.
- [[Discharge Rating Curves]] were built for 1-foot-long broad-crested weirs of various heights and breadths.
- Field measurements used a tape, survey rod, and Abney hand level. A standardized field-data form was developed (see Appendix).
- The 1930s photographs were the only record of the treatment, so structure intent was inferred from them and from early reports. Success was judged by sediment deposition behind a dam, with a structure counted as successful if it met its purpose during the life of its materials.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Setting and climate.** Study watersheds lie in [[Ponderosa Pine-Bunchgrass Zone]] terrain. Annual precipitation at Manitou is about 15 inches. Erosion is driven mainly by high-intensity, short-duration summer cloudbursts. Rain gauges at Trout Creek recorded 7.31 inches in 1958 and 15.00 inches in 1959. Long-term weather records were not available.

**Soils and vegetation.**
- Nursery gully soils derive mainly from [[Pikes Peak Granite]]; forest covers about 80% of the drainage.
- Upper Trout Creek soils derive from shale, siltstone and sandstone; forest covers about 70%.
- Small Tributary soils are shallow, low-fertility residuals of decomposed granite, mostly bunchgrass with some [[Pinyon Pine]].
- Reuter gully soils derive from granite, and the lower basin has lost nearly all its original surface layer.

**Hydraulic context.** The report draws on [[Leopold and Miller Gully Classification]] and on ephemeral-stream hydraulics. Ephemeral channels have unstable banks, and meandering flow widens them. Wood in such channels is submerged only briefly, so it rots quickly.

**Study systems.**

| System | Key dimensions reported |
|---|---|
| Nursery gully (Manitou) | 360 acres; 1.5 miles long; maximum width 46 ft, depth 6 ft; elevation about 8,000 to 7,500 ft |
| Upper Trout Creek gully | 800 acres; 1.5 miles long; maximum width 64 ft, depth 12 ft; elevation about 9,400 to 8,900 ft |
| Small Tributary gully | 2 acres; 377 ft long; channel gradient 31.5%; average depth 2.3 ft |
| Reuter gully | 75 acres; 0.5 mile long; surveyed 745 ft; maximum depth 5.5 ft, width 25.5 ft |

## Key Findings

### Gully classification and mechanics
- Two gully types were identified, matching the classification of [[Leopold and Miller]] (1956), developed from gullies near Santa Fe, New Mexico.
- **Discontinuous gullies** grow both upstream (headcutting) and downstream (gradient increase), until individual gullies coalesce into a **continuous gully**.
- In a continuous gully, the most active channel change occurs in the lowest segment above the gully mouth, where the difference between channel and valley gradient is greatest.
- A headcut can be buried by upstream sedimentation if deposition outpaces headcutting.
- The report does not determine whether gully formation differs between the two types. It calls this knowledge important for erosion control.

### Critical locations
- Treat critical locations first to achieve maximum erosion control at minimum cost.
- In discontinuous systems, these are the headcuts and the gully mouth.
- In continuous systems, the main critical location is the lowest segment near the mouth.
- Other critical locations include sharp meander curves, dense brush, tree growth, and channel constrictions.

### Hydraulic design
- Structures must pass the highest expected peak flow for the treatment period. Overdesign wastes money, and underdesign can damage every upstream installation.
- No structural failure occurred when flow was below 8 cfs, where hydrostatic and dynamic forces were too small to cause destruction.
- Small Tributary's expected peak at the gully mouth was about 1 cfs. No dam failed there, whether or not it passed the flow.
- Rectangular notches in log dams were ineffective when their capacity was smaller than total flow. At Upper Trout Creek dam No. 22 (15.5 ft long), the expected peak flow was 46 cfs but the notch capacity was about 1.5 cfs, so about 44.5 cfs spilled over the crest and threatened the unprotected banks.

### Structure design and materials
- Dams should be keyed about 2 feet into solid banks and about 1 foot into the channel bottom. Bank keys should extend above the crest.
- An apron is needed to dissipate the falling water's energy. Inadequate apron length or quality led to undermining.
- Weir geometry matters. Longer, shallower weirs allow greater dam height, and dam height controls the extent of sediment deposits upstream.
- Most successful materials were steel, masonry, and loose rock reinforced with galvanized woven wire. Gabion-type structures performed very well.
- Unpreserved wood was prone to rot. At Reuter gully, rotting willow cuttings embedded in dams caused the rim walls to break down, and otherwise successful structures lost sediment.
- The most solid structure belongs at the gully mouth. At the Nursery gully, a weaker mouth dam failed and triggered renewed erosion that lowered the base level 650 feet upstream and undermined four dams.

### Placement and spacing
- Dams were spaced by the **head-to-toe rule**, placing each crest level with the toe of the next upstream dam. This often required closely spaced structures (sometimes 15 feet apart, averaging 2 feet high).
- The rule can cause overdesign. Sediment deposits have a gradient above zero, so the upstream migration of deposits is stopped early.
- At Reuter gully, deposits were 1 to 2 feet deep at the lower dam toes, so the dams could have been spaced farther apart. Fewer dams would have stabilized the gully.
- Deposit gradient at Reuter gully (5 to 6.5%) was about half the original channel gradient (9.5%).
- Where runoff and sediment production are small, as in headwater reaches, numerous dams are not justified.

### Bank protection and vegetation
- Tributary junctions often create critical bank cutting. Revetments should deflect flow parallel to the channel centerline. Crib jetties at the Nursery confluence angled flow toward the opposite bank, so revetments placed parallel to the bank would have been more effective.
- Where mechanical shielding is infeasible, short-grass lining effectively controlled bank and bottom erosion (following Ree and Palmer, 1949).
- Steep banks should be graded to an angle of repose where channel depth permits. This succeeded at Upper Trout Creek, where banks were under 5 feet high.
- Willow plantings survived well but caused problems: floating cuttings rooted into dense stands that obstructed flow, undercut banks, and widened gully bottoms. [[Locust Plantings]] (New Mexican locust) survived poorly and did not spread.
- Woody vegetation should not be planted within the high-water channel. Species that will not spread to moist channel sites should be chosen.

### Performance of the four systems
- **Nursery gully:** Discontinuous upper segment (about one-quarter mile) requires headcut treatment. The continuous lower segment (3,600 ft) had check dams and bank revetments from the 1936–37 Civilian Conservation Corps work. Several structures failed, including Nos. 12 and 13, which lost 1 foot in bed level.
- **Upper Trout Creek gully:** The upper segment is well stabilized by vegetation. Dams in the 1930s-era segment (Nos. 1–28, per [[Table 1]]) were mostly successful, with failures at Nos. 27 and 28. The lower segment dams (Nos. 37–45) failed after a headcut migrated through them. Structures 37–45 failed largely because of undercutting from a headcut 2,000 feet away from Dam No. 45, and the headcut continued to threaten upstream masonry spillways.
- **Small Tributary gully:** Of 18 check dams, 15 restricted all flow, and none failed.
- **Reuter gully:** Loose-rock dams with wing walls on a 825-foot segment (28 dams, average spacing 29.4 ft) were mostly successful, though overspaced and weakened by rotting cuttings. Five masonry drop structures installed in 1937 on a segment bordering U.S. Highway 24 were all successful, but concrete deterioration now requires repair.

### Treatment and retreatment
- Gully control is a continuing process. Treatment begins with critical locations and critical banks, and periodic inspections determine further installations or repairs.
- Once dams fill with sediment, pools act as stilling basins, slowing flow and reducing erosive force until equilibrium forms a stabilized bed.
- The 20 to 26 years since treatment were insufficient to restore vegetation where the treatment consisted only of cattle removal or controlled grazing. Sheet erosion continued on the slopes, and gullying resumed where structures failed.
- Retreatment priorities: remove flow restrictions (failed dams, trees, brush); build a strong check dam at the gully mouth; add a new dam at the headcut (for example, at Nursery structure No. 10); protect banks with parallel revetments; and grade steep banks.

### Main recommendations
1. Determine whether the system is continuous or discontinuous, and identify critical locations.
2. Estimate the highest expected storm runoff and peak flow at all structural sites, and design structures to pass it.
3. In discontinuous systems, control headcuts and lower segments near the mouth. Install water spreaders below the system if the lowest mouth does not reach the main flood plain.
4. In continuous systems, start at the gully mouth with the strongest dam.
5. Install structures below channel-bottom headcuts and protect sharp curves and cutting banks with parallel revetments.
6. Remove boulders, trees, and other flow restrictions.
7. Defer bank sloping until the channel bottom has been raised by deposits.
8. Inspect periodically and maintain after treatment.
