# Sunburn in Farmed Brown Trout (Salmo trutta)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This short paper by J. C. Eiras of the [[Instituto De Zoologia Dr Augusto Nobre]], Faculdade de Ciências do Porto, reports the first occurrence in Portugal of sunburn-like lesions in farmed [[Brown Trout]] ([[Salmo Trutta]]). Affected one-year-old fish showed grey, rough, eroded skin on top of the head. Only about 0.5% of the sampled stock was affected. Histological examination showed epidermal spongiosis, necrosis, epidermal separation from the dermis, and, in the most severe cases, minor cartilage destruction. The author concludes that solar radiation is clearly involved but insufficient on its own, and that the aetiology is uncertain, with probable concurrent factors such as phototoxic agents or dietary deficiencies. Shade covers are recommended for trout farms.

## Background & Description

Skin abnormalities are a common feature of many wild and farmed trout diseases. "Sunburn" is one such condition. Its clinical signs are widely recognised, but its precise cause is not known (Roberts, 1979). The paper aims to describe the disease for Portuguese farmed Salmo trutta populations for the first time.

The affected fish came from two state-run trout cultures:

- [[Estação Aquícola De Vila Do Conde]]
- [[Posto Aquícola Do Marão]]

Sampling took place in February and April 1980 and April 1981. The fish were held in concrete raceways. Tissue was sectioned at 5 µm and stained with Mallory stain and haemalum-eosin (HE), using standard [[Histopathology]] methods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The disease is tied to exposure to solar radiation, but the paper argues that radiation alone does not explain it.

- **Light exposure:** Sunburn often occurs in hazy weather, when [[Ultraviolet Radiation]] levels are reduced (Roberts & Shepherd, 1979). All sampled fish were under the same radiation level, yet only a small proportion was affected.
- **Culture contrast:** At the sunnier [[Boticas Trout Culture]], no sunburn-like lesions were found. The [[Posto Aquícola Do Marão]] culture, where diseased fish were more numerous, is sited in a wood. Trees shade the tanks for several hours a day.
- **Photosensitisation:** Phototoxic agents can produce skin lesions when combined with light. [[Phenothiazine]] is a known photosensitising compound absorbing at 300–320 nm. Its role in this disease is discussed below.
- **Nutrition:** A [[Niacin Deficiency]] has been linked to sunburn-like lesions in experimental fish.
- **Age:** Sunburn lesions had not been described in adult fish at the time of writing, which suggests that age is an important risk factor.

Comparable lesions have been reported in other salmonids, including [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*).

## Key Findings

### Clinical observations
- Affected fish showed an area of grey, rough skin on top of the head. No lesions were found elsewhere on the body.
- Affected fish moved slowly near the water surface and were captured easily, with weaker escape reactions than unaffected fish.
- Prevalence was about **0.5%**.
- Among 12 examined specimens:
  - 9 had short opercles
  - 2 had lost one eyeball
  - 2 had small haemorrhages in the eyes and pectoral fins

### Histopathology
- The first sign of the lesion is an epidermal inflammatory response with intercellular oedema. Epidermal thickness is reduced to about half of normal.
- Malpighian cells are separated by elongated, filamentous empty spaces. Desmosomal attachments are stretched or ruptured.
- Most nuclei are pycnotic. Many cells are necrotic, with ruptured nuclear membranes and basophilic granules.
- Lesions progress to total epidermal loss in places. Basal cells elongate two to three times their normal length near the rupture zone, and they recover normal size with distance from it.
- Epidermis separates from the underlying dermis in all affected animals.
- The dermal stratum spongiosum is oedematous and thinned, and sometimes disappears completely. The stratum compactum is preserved, though thinner. Where the upper dermis is lost, the spongiotic epidermis contacts the stratum compactum directly, which suggests that upper dermal destruction can precede epidermal destruction.
- In the most affected fish, small areas of cartilage destruction occurred. These were not deep enough to cause brain lesions.

### Discussion and interpretation
- **Solar radiation alone is insufficient.** The author cites cases in which lesions appeared in other locations or caused other effects:
  - [[Allison Study]] (1960): "bald spots" in about 20% of lake trout, with no heavy mortality.
  - [[Delong Study]] et al. (1958): lesions anterior to the dorsal fin, causing 10–15% daily mortality.
  - [[Bullock And Roberts Study]] (1981): lesions on the pectoral and dorsal fins of rainbow trout fry.
- **Phototoxic mechanism:** [[Bullock And Roberts Study]] (1979) found that rainbow trout fed pellets coated with phenothiazine and exposed to short-wave UV (280–320 nm) developed dorsal skin lesions. The authors also suggested that UDN-like lesions may be caused by phototoxic agents.
- **Dietary factor:** Fish fed a niacin-deficient diet developed sunburn-like lesions (Delong et al., 1958). A niacin-fortified diet delayed bald spots in experimentally irradiated lake trout fingerlings (Allison, 1960).
- **Conclusion on aetiology:** The author describes the cause as uncertain, probably involving several concurrent factors.

### Management recommendations
- The disease can cause heavy mortality and significant economic loss.
- Trout farms should use covers that shield fish from direct sunlight, if necessary. Bullock and Roberts (1979) reported that this approach worked on several farms.
