# Changes in the Fish Fauna of the Vermilion and Embarras Rivers, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Freshwater-Fishes

## Executive Summary

This study compares fish collections made before 1901 with collections made from 1956 to 1966 in the [[Vermilion River]] and [[Embarras River]] basins of east-central Illinois, both tributaries of the [[Wabash River]]. One species is extirpated and 20 are decimated. The author attributes the losses mainly to physical habitat alteration (changed flow and bottom type), increased suspended silt with loss of aquatic vegetation, and reduced stream size. Dams and reservoirs had the most dramatic effects. Pollution is judged to have been less important than these factors for long-term population change. The paper also warns that proposed reservoirs threaten Illinois populations of several restricted darters.

## Background & Description

The Vermilion and Embarras rivers both rise in Champaign County, Illinois, and flow southeast to the Wabash, about 100 straight-line miles apart. They share many physical and faunal similarities. Historical collections were made by personnel of the Illinois State Laboratory of Natural History under [[Stephen A. Forbes]]. Between 1882 and 1901, 59 collections were made at 54 sites, mostly during 1899–1901. Between 1956 and 1966, the author and associates made more than 100 collections at 86 sites.

Nearly 100 fish species have been reported from the two basins. The assessment focuses on 21 species: one extirpated and 20 decimated. The extirpated species is the [[Blacknose Shiner]]. Decimated species are grouped by their habitat requirements:

- **Riffle and hard-bottomed pool specialists (9 species):** northern hog sucker, stoneroller, silverjaw minnow, suckermouth minnow, brindled madtom, [[Greenside Darter]], fantail darter, eastern sand darter, blackside darter.
- **Clear, vegetated pool specialists (5 species):** spotted sucker, bigeye chub, [[Bigeye Shiner]], brook silverside, black crappie.
- **Large-river species (3 species):** silver chub, [[Bullhead Minnow]], mud darter.
- **Silt- and pollution-tolerant soft-bottom species (3 species):** black bullhead, tadpole madtom, orangespotted sunfish. Their decline is unexplained, although the same species were also cited as decimated in Champaign County by Larimore & Smith.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Watershed change.** Both basins are primarily agricultural, growing corn and soybeans. Drainage districts were formed to drain marshes and speed runoff. Repeated [[Dredging]] and [[Channelization]] converted meandering, riffle-and-pool streams into straight, uniform canals with fewer habitats. Dredging was most intense in the Vermilion headwaters. More than 10 miles of the lower Embarras now run in a man-made channel. Lowered water tables made streams wider, shallower, and more variable in flow. Many former permanent tributaries now dry during droughts.

**Inputs.** Fertilizers, herbicides, and insecticides washed in with silt, and grazing livestock eroded banks and fouled water. Gravel washing added silt.

**Pollution.** Chronic [[Chronic Pollution]] sources included:
- Sewage from the Champaign-Urbana plant, which excluded fish from much of the West Branch of the Salt Fork.
- Sewage from the Danville plant, which sludged the Vermilion below the city.
- Severe discharges from Rantoul and the Chanute Air Force Base into the East Branch of the Salt Fork.
- Industrial effluent and oil-field pollution in the lower Embarras.

Strip mining was not found to have affected fish populations. Accidental releases of anhydrous ammonia and potassium cyanide caused [[Fish Kills]], but populations recovered quickly and no permanent damage was demonstrated.

**Dams and reservoirs.** Three main impoundments affected the basins:
- An 1894 wooden dam on the North Fork near Danville created no impoundment. A 1914 private-utility dam created a reservoir of several acres. A 1925 dam flooded the older structure to form [[Lake Vermilion]], which initially covered 775 acres and has silted to about 617 acres.
- A 1947 dam on the Embarras near Charleston impounded 400 acres to form [[Lake Charleston]], which is now appreciably shallower.
- Several smaller tributary dams had no demonstrable effect on parent-river fishes.

Impoundments drown riffles, cause siltation, and create mud bottoms and murky water, extending effects many miles upstream. Dams also act as barriers. Large-river species such as the bullhead minnow cannot recolonize upstream after the river temporarily regains large size. Reservoirs create large-lake niches. Typically lacustrine species are rare among native stream fishes, so these niches may be filled by introduced lake fishes or by native oxbow and backwater species.

## Key Findings

- **Losses:** one species extirpated (blacknose shiner) and 20 decimated across the two systems.
- **Principal causes:** (1) altered physical habitat (flow rate and bottom type), (2) increased silt with loss of aquatic vegetation, and (3) reduced stream size. The author judges these to have been more important than pollution per se for long-term change.
- **Pollution:** severe pollution produced local decimation, such as the West Branch of the Salt Fork, where Forbes & Richardson (1920) recorded at least 16 species that no longer occur. Recovery is rapid once pollution abates, because fishes recolonize from tributaries and downstream.
- **Dam effects:** dams on the lower North Fork and middle Embarras extirpated or sharply reduced riffle species (greenside darter, northern hog sucker, brindled madtom, blackside darter, fantail darter, bullhead minnow). The bullhead minnow remains abundant up to the Lake Charleston dam but cannot pass it.
- **Possible beneficiaries:** about 20 oxbow, backwater, and soft-bottom species (e.g., gizzard shad, golden shiner, fathead minnow, [[Largemouth Bass]], bluegill, pirate perch) may have increased. No data confirm this, and three of them were themselves listed as decimated.
- **Proposed threats (as of March 1967):**
  - [[Lincoln Reservoir]] on the Embarras at the Coles–Cumberland County line, authorized by Congress, with a seasonal pool of 6,760 acres extending almost 30 miles upstream. It would cause further habitat decimation but is not expected to extirpate any Illinois species.
  - A reservoir on the [[Middle Fork]] near Kickapoo State Park, recommended but not authorized, extending about 12 miles upstream. It would eliminate the Illinois population of the bluebreast darter.
  - Small conservation lakes on Salt Fork and Embarras tributaries, with little expected effect on overall distribution.
- **Restricted endangered fishes:**
  - [[Bluebreast Darter]]: found only in about a 12-mile stretch of the lower Middle Fork, in extremely fast riffles with boulders.
  - [[Harlequin Darter]]: found sparingly in roughly a 10-mile stretch of the middle Embarras. It has one Indiana record and this Illinois population is the only northern one extant. A dam 15 miles farther downstream would eliminate it.
  - [[Eastern Sand Darter]]: 12 of 13 Illinois records are from the Embarras and Middle Fork.
  - [[Dusky Darter]]: 23 of 26 Illinois records are from the Vermilion and Embarras basins.
- **Conclusion:** the number of endangered species is small, and only one faces extirpation if the contemplated reservoirs are built. However, decimation of riffle and clear-pool fishes in the Middle Fork and Embarras is considered inevitable with new dams. Reservoir construction on main streams is identified as the most avoidable and greatest threat to these fishes, and pollution and siltation will continue to intensify hazards for restricted species.
