# The Plankton Sampler: An Instrument for Quantitative Plankton Investigations

#marine-biology #limnology #zooplankton

## Executive Summary

This 1950 Special Publication of the [[American Society of Limnology and Oceanography]] describes the [[Clarke-Bumpus Plankton Sampler]], developed by G. L. Clarke and D. F. Bumpus of Harvard University and the [[Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution]]. The device is a closing plankton net fitted with a propeller-driven volume meter, so a catch can be taken from a chosen depth and its filtered water volume measured. The document covers the design, operating and setting procedures, flow calibration, care, and a comparison with other plankton samplers. The source is an instrument manual and methods paper, not a fish study.

## Background & Description

The sampler was built to meet a long-standing need for an easily handled device that collects plankton at any depth in the sea or in inland waters and reports the volume of water sampled. It was first designed to capture the [[Copepods]] of marine [[Zooplankton]], and the authors note it also works for larger forms (sagittae, [[Euphausiids]], amphipods), for smaller animals including eggs and larvae, for freshwater zooplankton, and for [[Phytoplankton]] retained by silk netting if clogging is limited.

Key physical specifications:
- **Tube:** brass, about 5 in. (about 12.7 cm) in diameter and about 6 in. long
- **Nets:** interchangeable, about 2 ft. long, in any desired mesh, attached by a bayonet-lock ring
- **Weight:** the sampler weighs about 10 lbs. and is rugged enough for rough weather
- **Supporting cable:** designed for a heavy wire of 5/32 in. diameter on a power winch, but usable on lighter rope from rowboats, outboard boats, or small power boats
- **Bottom weight:** 100 to 150 lbs. for large vessels, lighter for small boats

The tube pivots so that it stays horizontal when towed, whatever angle the cable makes with the vertical. Two angled planes at the rear help keep it level. The frame swivels around the cable, so the opening always faces forward.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The sampler is intended for horizontal or oblique towing, not vertical hauls, although the authors suggest it could be modified for vertical use. Its wide-area sampling makes catches more representative than trap or pump methods where plankton is unevenly distributed. Several samplers on one cable, released by messengers, can give the vertical distribution of plankton within an area. Oblique hauls, with the cable shortened periodically, can sample all depth levels and give a synoptic picture of a region.

Sampling reliability depends on flow and clogging. Silk netting as fine as No. 10 mesh (109 threads per inch) can be used, but finer mesh causes inaccurate metering when clogging is heavy. A draining cup with fine wire netting is recommended for washing the net tail.

## Key Findings

**Operation and mechanism**
- The shutter is a disc on vertical pivots, opened and closed by two messengers (about 1 lb. each) that trip a trigger. The volume meter (a cyclometer-type counter geared to a propeller) runs only while the shutter is open, so exact messenger timing is unnecessary.
- Multiple samplers can be attached to one cable and operated simultaneously by releasing a second set of messengers from hooks on each sampler.
- An "open" version without shutter or trigger, mounted on a light rectangular frame, is also described.

**Calibration**
- Five instruments calibrated at Woods Hole in flume tanks gave ratings of 3.8, 4.0, 4.0, 4.3 and 4.5 liters per revolution.
- Each rating varied by less than 5% for flow speeds corresponding to boat speeds from a fraction of a knot (the value is garbled in the scan) up to 4 knots, so boat speed need not be known precisely in that range.
- A rating of 4 liters per revolution was found satisfactory as an approximation for all instruments whose propeller spins freely with the shutter open.
- Clogging does not cause error as long as flow stays above 1/2 knot, because the meter records only water that actually passed through the net.
- Field calibration: tow the sampler without a net between fixed markers at about 1 to 2 knots (acceptable from roughly 1/4 to 3 knots), in still water, averaging runs in opposite directions if a current or wind is suspected. Each instrument's minimum safe revolutions per minute (measured at 1/2 knot with no net) should be recorded to check each tow.

**Validation**
- Nets 5 in. in diameter were compared statistically with 12 in. and 30 in. nets (Winsor and Clarke, 1940). No significant difference in catch variability was found, and species proportions did not indicate size-related selection.
- Hardy's [[Continuous Plankton Recorder]], with an opening of one square inch or less, gave consistent results, which supports the small opening.
- Preliminary comparison with the limnologist's [[Plankton Trap]] at [[Trout Lake]], Wisconsin, found good agreement between the average of eight sampler tows and 34 trap hauls in an area where plankton was relatively uniform.
- Further studies compared the sampler with the [[Juday Plankton Trap]] and other dip methods (Neess, 1949) and with the [[Plankton Pump]] in the [[Lofoten Area]], Norway (Wiborg, 1948).

**Maintenance**
- Keep moving parts lubricated. In sea water, nickel-plate all parts at construction, use skunk oil on exposed parts, and wash and oil the instrument after each use.

**Availability**
- Samplers were available from Mr. Fred Schueler of Waltham, Massachusetts, at the time of publication.
