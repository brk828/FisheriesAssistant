# Persistence and Stability of the Virgin River Fish Assemblage, 1977–1989

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether the [[Virgin River]] fish assemblage, a southwestern desert stream community, is structured mainly by biotic interactions or by abiotic disturbance. It draws on autumn seine data from five mainstream stations collected from 1977 to 1989. The period included a chemical eradication of fishes (October 1988) followed by a record flood (1 January 1989), and the invasion of the non-native [[Red Shiner]]. Persistence (low species turnover) was moderate to high at all stations. Concordance of rank abundance was statistically significant at all five stations, so the authors reject random or non-equilibrium control of community structure. Stability was lowest where red shiners rose to dominance and highest where native fishes remained dominant.

## Background & Description

The study applies [[Equilibrium Theory]] and the competing view that variable environments limit biotic interaction. Its premise is that stream systems in the arid southwest fluctuate enough to test these ideas, and that human disturbance offers a chance to observe both immediate and long-term responses of threatened assemblages.

The Virgin River originates in south-central Utah and flows about 320 km southwest through Arizona and Nevada to [[Lake Mead]]. It is a regulated desert stream with steep gradients, rapidly fluctuating discharge and temperature, seasonal intermittency, and an indigenous fish community.

The system supports six native fish taxa:
- **Listed endangered cyprinids:** [[Woundfin]] and [[Roundtail Chub]]. Both are restricted to the mainstream below [[Pah Tempe Springs]], where thermal, saline springs enter a narrow gorge at [[Timpoweap Canyon]]. The springs and high gradient form a physico-chemical barrier to upstream dispersal.
- **Widespread natives:** [[Virgin Spinedace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]], found throughout the river.
- **Introduced species:** [[Red Shiner]], now abundant in the lower reaches, particularly downstream of the Virgin River Gorge.

Recent disturbances gave the study its opportunity: a chemical eradication of fishes in a mainstream segment, followed about 10 weeks later by a flood from the collapse of a dike at [[Quail Creek Reservoir]]. An extensive fish dataset maintained since 1977 (Deacon 1988) allowed analysis of persistence and stability over time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study stations.** Five autumn-sampled stations were analysed: [[Ash Creek]] confluence, [[Hurricane Bridge]], [[Twin Bridges]], [[Beaver Dam Wash]] confluence, and [[Riverside]], Nevada. Only Beaver Dam Wash has a complete record for 1977–1989. The other four have one to five years missing, but each still spans several generation times.

**Methods.**
- Persistence was measured by [[Species Turnover]] rates, following Meffe and Minckley (1987), modified from Diamond and May (1977).
- Stability was measured by [[Kendall's W Concordance]] of ranked relative abundance across years.

**Environmental drivers.**
- Ash Creek is upstream of the diversions, the Washington Diversion area and red shiner invasion. Its instability was linked to saline discharge from Pah Tempe Springs and to the proportion of flow from Ash or La Verkin Creeks. Salinity and turbidity were judged most influential.
- Below Hurricane Bridge the flows ranged from the lowest on record (1977) to a January 1989 flood at least twice the previous record.
- Twin Bridges and Riverside lie below diversion structures that inhibit colonisation and reduce or interrupt summer flow. Irrigation returns add to rapid fluctuations.

## Key Findings

**1. Persistence is moderate to high across the system.** Fish diversity changed little over time at each station. The Ash Creek station had the highest persistence value, followed by Beaver Dam Wash. Below Hurricane Bridge also remained relatively high. [[Twin Bridges]] and [[Riverside]] had the lowest values.

**2. Specific persistence values reported:**
- Beaver Dam Wash, 1977–1987 before poisoning: 0.9472.
- Beaver Dam Wash, 1977–1984: 0.9337.
- Twin Bridges, 1977–1984 (before red shiner invasion): 0.7457.
- Twin Bridges, 1977–1987 (after red shiner added in 1985): 0.8103.

**3. Poisoning and flood effects were station-specific.**
- The October 1988 poisoning lowered persistence at some stations. At Twin Bridges, poisoning followed by flooding left no fish collected in 1989.
- Persistence at Twin Bridges changed little after poisoning, but adding one species in three of eleven years substantially raised the value.
- Persistence at Beaver Dam Wash was relatively unaffected by the red shiner increase.

**4. Stability is significant at all five stations.** Concordance was statistically significant at every station, so the null hypothesis of random or non-equilibrium control was rejected. The authors conclude that biotic factors influence community structure primarily.

**5. Stability differs by location.**
- *Relatively high:* below Hurricane Bridge (native-dominated) and near Riverside (red shiner-dominated).
- *Relatively low:* Ash Creek, Twin Bridges and Beaver Dam Wash.
- Rank order was most consistent at Riverside and least consistent at Beaver Dam Wash.

**6. Red shiner invasions drove major restructuring.**
- At Beaver Dam Wash, red shiner rose from about 6% to about 60% of the population between 1984 and 1985. The species had been a rare, permanent component for more than 20 years.
- At Twin Bridges, invasion and dominance occurred from 1984 to 1986.
- The authors suggest abiotic conditions near Twin Bridges enabled the invasion, after which biotic factors restructured the community at Beaver Dam Wash.

**7. Riverside is a low-diversity, red shiner-dominated system.** Native fishes arrive from upstream only occasionally and disappear under extreme abiotic conditions. High concordance there mainly reflects red shiner dominance, not a balanced native community. The authors describe the site as "not really a fish community."

**8. Conceptual conclusions.**
- Persistence and stability are both interpreted at the level of station-specific circumstances. The authors urge careful consideration of unique events at Ash Creek, Twin Bridges and Beaver Dam Wash.
- Only the Hurricane Bridge assemblage was judged both persistent and stable, and structured mainly by biotic factors.
- Dams and diversions block dispersal and alter discharge and water quality. Combined with red shiner presence, these produce a mix of abiotic and biotic influences.

**9. Management implication.** Because the assemblages respond unevenly to disturbance, the authors argue that a broader understanding of these underlying processes is needed to improve management of threatened southwestern fish assemblages.
