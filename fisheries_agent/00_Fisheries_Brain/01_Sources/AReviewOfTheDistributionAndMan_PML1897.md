# Review of the Distribution and Management of Bonytail in the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Charles O. Minckley]] and [[Mitch S. Thorson]] synthesizes roughly three decades of information on the [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), a cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Historically it inhabited the main stem and major tributaries from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] downstream into Mexico. Wild populations have collapsed, and the species now persists largely through hatchery-reared stocking. The paper covers broodstock management, grow-out ponds, native fish habitat development, and stocking into [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. The authors attribute the lack of recruitment chiefly to predation by introduced fishes, and report that small numbers of stocked adults are being recaptured, suggesting establishment is possible. A multi-agency management plan for the big-river fishes of the lower basin was completed in 2004.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The bonytail belongs to a group of western "big-river fishes" that includes the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]. Intermediate forms among these species have complicated taxonomy, though recent morphological and genetic work indicates the species remain distinct, with limited interbreeding when sympatric.
- **Morphology:** Adults reach about 60 cm and 1 kg, with a slender body, narrow caudal peduncle, and enlarged caudal fin, adaptations for swift, turbulent river habitats.
- **Conservation status:** The bonytail is listed as endangered. Earlier sources described it as "closer to extinction, if not extinct," in the upper basin, and the species persists in unknown numbers in some mainstem reservoirs.
- **Behavior:** Wild fish hold position in low-amplitude currents with the caudal fin beating slowly, moving from midwater to the surface and bottom to feed on drifting particles. In Lake Mohave and High Levee Pond, adults seek cover in deep water or along rip-rapped banks during the day and are active at night.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical habitat:** The species flourished in the meandering lower river, which formed runs, pools, braided channels, and backwaters that provided nursery areas for native fish. Historically it also occurred in impounded waters such as the [[Salton Sea]], where it was used as food by Native Americans.
- **Decline:** Collections from the lower river dropped sharply after 1950, and the decline was well underway by the mid-1930s. The principal cause is impact from introduced fishes through predation and competition. Dams and altered flow regimes also adversely affect bonytail and other native fishes.
- **Basin-wide consensus:** Most researchers agree that if introduced fishes were removed, viable populations of native fishes would likely persist in the lower basin.
- **Current refuge:** Lake Mohave is the only site where wild bonytail may still occur, based on [[Marsh 1997a]] age and otolith work.

## Key Findings

**Management actions:**

1. **Broodstock development:** Established at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in 1980–1981 from a small number of fish collected in Lake Mohave. Additional collections (1980–2002) were made to raise genetic diversity. The original broodstock is aging and is being replaced by a new broodstock developed at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center, using nonsibling matings.
2. **Stocking in Lake Mohave:** Stocking began in 1981. Between 1981 and 2002, 180,000 bonytail were stocked, and 86% (N = 155,005) of fish stocked between 1981 and 1995 were less than 10 cm. Current stocking uses fish greater than 25 cm, passive integrated transponder (PIT)-tagged. The goal is to establish a population of about 50,000 large individuals.
3. **Stocking in Lake Havasu:** A stocking program began in 1993. The goal is about 30,000 fish greater than 25 cm, PIT-tagged, to develop large self-sustaining populations. Hatchery production was supplemented by golf course ponds near Parker, Arizona.
4. **Isolated backwaters and ponds:** Constructed ponds such as High Levee Pond grow fish to greater than 35 cm before release and provide habitat for self-recruiting populations. High Levee Pond supported bonytail and razorback sucker from 1993 to April 2004, until largemouth bass introductions decimated native fish populations.

**Monitoring results:**

- **Lake Mohave:** Between 1980 and 2003, 113 bonytail were captured during spring trips aimed at collecting wild adults for broodstock. One recaptured fish had been recently stocked (under 2 months), and another PIT-tagged fish from a telemetry study was captured in 2003. Whether it was wild or hatchery-origin is unknown.
- **Lake Havasu:** Since monitoring began in 1996, 19 bonytail have been captured, 10 from Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge, 2 caught and released by anglers, and 7 by other methods. The authors consider this a small number but evidence that stocked fish can be recaptured.
- **Recapture concern:** Few returns from both lakes, particularly Lake Mohave, are a concern, though the authors note that recovering stocked fish so soon after introductions began is encouraging.

**Planning and conclusions:**

- The lack of recruitment is mainly due to predation by non-native fish, especially largemouth bass.
- A [[Management Plan for the Big-River Fishes of the Lower Colorado River Basin]] (USFWS 2004) was completed. It sets goals for broodstock, grow-out ponds, native fish habitats, and augmentation with larger fish, and complements the 2002 big-river fishes recovery goals.
- The authors state that the [[Multispecies Conservation Plan]] will support continued management actions.
- Native fish habitat that replicates historic backwater conditions is still needed so the species can persist outside hatchery environments.
