# Survey of Fishes, Mammals and Herpetofauna of the Colorado River and Adjacent Riparian Areas of Grand Canyon National Park

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1976 baseline survey, authored by [[Royal D. Suttkus]], [[Glenn H. Clemmer]], [[Clyde Jones]] and [[C. Robert Shoop]] and funded in part by the [[National Park Service]], documented fishes, mammals, reptiles, amphibians and plants along the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]] from September 1970 to June 1976. Collections came from float trips and terrestrial sampling at ten primary study sites. Nineteen fish species were recorded, four native and fifteen introduced. The authors attribute the main impact on native fishes to [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and the altered downstream conditions it produces, not to river-running parties.

## Background & Description

The survey aimed to establish "base-line" data on fishes, mammals, herpetofauna and riparian plants. The senior author made six float trips before the contract; four further trips were supported by the National Park Service in 1975, and four more occurred in the first half of 1976. Specimens are held at Tulane University, and fish specimens were catalogued in the Tulane University fish collection. Fish identifications follow [[Bailey et al. 1970]] and mammal names follow [[Hoffmeister 1981]].

Totals reported across the survey:
- Fish: 6,352 specimens from 35 sites (nearly all taken with a small-mesh minnow seine)
- Mammals: 795 dry study skins and skeletons plus 35 sets of embryos, representing 24 species
- Reptiles and amphibians: 285 specimens representing 19 forms, from 46 localities
- Plants: 45 collections from 31 localities, with roughly 2,400 herbarium sheets expected

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Colorado River in Grand Canyon historically held few native fish species, perhaps eight to ten. Most are endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Introduced species add predation and competition pressure. The river's native fauna is shaped by cold, regulated releases from Glen Canyon Dam, which keep water temperatures too low for spawning of several native species. Measured water temperatures ranged from 10°C at Lee's Ferry (May 30) to 13°C at Kanab Creek in the main river, while tributary and side-pool temperatures ranged from 12°C in Royal Arch Creek to 27.5°C in Spencer Creek.

The survey notes that the riparian zone along roughly 560 river miles is a mosaic of tributary deltas, canyon walls and sand bars. Native riparian vegetation, such as [[Fremont Cottonwood]] and [[Desert Willow]], is patchy, and the authors note that the presence of [[Tamarisk]] (saltcedar) is beyond question.

## Key Findings

**Fishes**
- Nineteen species plus one hybrid sucker combination and at least one hybrid trout combination were collected. Four are native: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]] and the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Fifteen are introduced.
- Three native suckers and dace persist by spawning in many small tributaries, which tolerate warmer temperatures than the main channel. Flannelmouth Sucker spawning was recorded at the Paria River mouth. Spawning was observed in Shinumo, Kanab and Havasu creeks for Bluehead Sucker.
- No [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was taken. Three hybrid specimens of Flannelmouth Sucker × Humpback Sucker were collected at River Mile 65.8 region. Dam-regulated cold water and the lack of small tributary spawning habitat are cited as reasons.
- Humpback Chub was documented near the Little Colorado River and Shinumo Creek. The authors conclude its range within Grand Canyon has been reduced to an area near and just below the Little Colorado River mouth, and that continued regulated releases threaten its survival.
- No [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) was found. The authors believe it was extirpated from the Canyon by impoundment and the conditions that followed.
- Carp was the most widely and continuously distributed exotic species. Young carp were found only at the Little Colorado River mouth and near Cardenas Creek and Hance Rapids, suggesting Lee's Ferry is not favorable spawning habitat.
- Other introduced species recorded include Rainbow Trout, Threadfin Shad, Channel Catfish, Red Shiner, Fathead Minnow, [[Rio Grande Killifish]] (well established in tributaries, abundant at Spencer Creek, with spawning groups observed June 8, 1976), Mosquitofish, Green Sunflish, Bluegill, Largemouth Bass, and a dead Striped Bass (about 700 mm) at River Mile 250. A single Coho Salmon was seined at River Mile 71.
- No evidence was found that river-running parties affected fish populations.

**Mammals**
- Twenty-four species were recorded. Several bat records, including Fringed Myotis at River Mile 19, extended known ranges. The Yuma Myotis was recorded above Phantom Ranch, and the Pallid Bat and Big-eared Bat were recorded at several sites.
- Deer mouse (*Peromyscus maniculatus*) records extended its range within the riparian zone, and Pinyon Mouse was collected at Lee's Ferry and Tuckup Canyon.
- Sightings of Coyote, Gray Fox, Mule Deer, Bighorn Sheep and feral burros were made. All burro sightings were on the right side of the river between River Mile 209 and 220.
- No adverse effects of river running on small mammal populations were found.

**Reptiles and Amphibians**
- Specimens were obtained from 46 localities, with 19 forms documented by specimen or photograph. Examples include Redspotted Toad, Woodhouse's Toad, Canyon Treefrog, Western Spadefoot, Banded Gecko, Western Whiptail, Chuckwalla, Desert Spiny Lizard and Western Rattlesnake.
- No amphibians were taken at Scorpion Island.

**Plants**
- Forty-five plant collections were made. The Lee's Ferry area was judged the most representative site. Two exotic plants (a small palm at the Little Colorado mouth and a garden tomato at Lower Bass Camp) were recorded.

**Conclusions and Recommendations**
- The authors conclude that river runners had no detectable impact on fishes, small mammals, reptiles, amphibians or plants.
- Major threats to native fishes are the Glen Canyon Dam and its downstream flow regime, along with exotic species competition and predation.
- Recommendations include: more basic data from the ten primary sites; regulation of detergents and soaps in the river; monitoring of native fish populations, spawning and recruitment; restoration of habitat; and an assessment of possible plans, including removal of Glen Canyon Dam, construction of a Bridge Canyon Dam, by-pass channels around Glen Canyon Dam, and a tunnel shunting surface water from Powell Reservoir into the Paria River.
