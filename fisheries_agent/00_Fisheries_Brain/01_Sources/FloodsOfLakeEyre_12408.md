# Floods of Lake Eyre: Hydrology, Inflows and Water Resources

#limnology #hydrology #ephemeral-lake

## Executive Summary

This study is the first systematic attempt to quantify how much water reaches [[Lake Eyre]] and how often. It combines historical records, satellite and aerial imagery, field reconnaissance and a [[RORB Model]] rainfall-runoff reconstruction covering 1885-1984. The [[Lake Eyre Basin]] covers 1 140 000 km² and is one of the largest endorheic drainage systems in the world. The authors conclude that inflows to [[Lake Eyre North]] are relatively frequent, that the basin's hydrological data network is inadequate, and that large-scale watering or filling schemes are impractical at present.

The document contains no fish, invertebrate or plankton data. Its aquatic content concerns water flow, salinity, flood timing and lake morphology. Biological references are limited to incidental observations, such as vegetation flourishing after floods and a seagull colony on [[Silcrete Island]].

## Background & Description

The basin is mostly flat, often less than 150 m above sea level. Its main drainage lines are the [[Cooper Creek]] and [[Diamantina River]] in the east, which feed through [[Goyder Lagoon]] and the [[Warburton Groove]]. Western inflows come from the [[Macumba River]], the [[Neales River]] and the [[Finke River]]. Southern inflows arrive via the [[Frome River]] and [[Warriner Creek]] and [[Margaret Creek]].

Lake Eyre itself is split into two basins:

| Parameter | Lake Eyre North | Lake Eyre South |
|---|---|---|
| Area | 8 430 km² | 1 260 km² |
| Volume at -9.5 m AHD | 27.7 km³ | 2.4 km³ |
| Mean depth | 3.3 m | 1.9 m |
| Deepest point | Below -15.2 m AHD (Belt Bay) | -13.2 m AHD |

The lake bed is very flat, and the two basins are linked by the 15 km long [[Goyder Channel]]. The Eromanga and [[Great Artesian Basin]] underlies most of the catchment. Groundwater discharges through [[Mound Springs]], with a median discharge of 69 m³/day across fifteen measured springs (range 1.7-337 m³/day).

The mean annual runoff of the basin is 2.8 mm over the catchment. The document reports that this is significantly below the 57 mm for all of Australia. Climate is arid to semi-arid, with mean annual rainfall close to 120 mm/year over the lake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Eyre is an [[Ephemeral Lake]] that is usually dry. The document describes it as a terminal point where most stream channels ultimately trace back to the lake bed. Key environmental controls identified include:

- **Extreme variability:** Runoff depends on tropical cyclonic and summer monsoonal rain. Long droughts alternate with major floods. The rainfall variability index exceeds 2.0 in the [[Simpson Desert]] and the northern lake area.
- **High evaporation:** Filled-lake evaporation is estimated at about 2 000 mm/year (Bonython, 1955; Tetzlaff and Bye, 1978). The document argues that evaporation alone does not explain the lake's dryness. [[Lake Titicaca]] has comparable area and higher evaporation, yet stays full.
- **Salinity:** Around 400 million tonnes of salt are stored in the lake. Floodwaters dissolve the [[Salt Crust]], which can reach 460 mm thick during dry periods. Salinity in [[Madigan Gulf]] rose to 57 g/kg in early 1976.
- **Wind effects:** Wind can move large areas of the lake bed. A moderate 30 km/h wind can produce tides of 0.6 m in Lake Eyre North. The document describes a possible "roving" lake in water depths below 0.5 m.
- **Climate drivers:** Flood occurrence is linked to the [[Southern Oscillation]], with major floods in strong positive SOI phases.
- **Ecology noted in passing:** Floods bring vegetation growth along watercourses and in desert areas. Pelicans and a [[Seagull Colony]] are mentioned. No fish or aquatic invertebrate surveys are reported.

## Key Findings

**Flood history**
- Earlier records are sparse. Before 1949, only Ross (1869) and Halligan (1922) were reported to have seen water in Lake Eyre North.
- The first well-documented flood was [[1949-50 Flood]]. The lake reached -10.30 m AHD at the end of September 1950. It was never fully dry in any calendar year for the following decade.
- The largest known flood was [[1974 Flood]]. Lake Eyre North reached its highest recorded level of -9.09 m AHD in May-June 1974. The equilibrium level between the two lakes of -9.5 m AHD was reached in October.
- In January 1984, Lake Eyre South overflowed into Lake Eyre North for the first time on record. The [[1984 Flood]] filled both lakes within days from local rainfall.

**Inflow modelling (1885-1984)**
- Daily rainfall from all basin stations was modelled with RORB Version 3. Of the 100 years, 60 were modelled and 40 drought years were excluded.
- Inflows to Lake Eyre North occurred 51 times, about every second year. Mean annual inflow was 3.8 km³ with a standard deviation of 6.2 km³.
- Mean annual inflows by source were Diamantina 2.40 km³, Cooper 0.63 km³ and other tributaries 0.72 km³ (per the conclusions).
- Flood frequency for annual inflow volumes: 1:5 year 6.5 km³; 1:10 year 11.3 km³; 1:100 year 35.5 km³; 1:1000 year 76.0 km³.
- Lake Eyre South inflows are estimated at roughly 0.15 km³/year, but the modelling was only approximate.

**Filling frequency (conclusions)**
- Lake Eyre North fills to the following levels, on average:
  - -11.7 m AHD, once in 10 years
  - -11.0 m AHD, once in 20 years
  - -10.4 m AHD, once in 50 years
  - -9.5 m AHD, once in 100 years
  - -7.5 m AHD, once in 500 years
  - -6.3 m AHD, once in 1000 years
- Old beach lines 0.7, 1.6 and 2.8 m above the 1974 level indicate unrecorded earlier major inflows. The document therefore cautions against calling the lake "full" in any absolute sense.

**Flow and transmission losses**
- The Diamantina at Birdsville has a catchment of 115 000 km². Its mean annual flow for 1950-83 was 1.42 km³.
- Cooper Creek at Innamincka has a catchment of 236 700 km². Its mean annual flow for 1973-83 was 2.06 km³.
- Transmission losses are large. The 1974 peak on Cooper Creek fell from 3 740 m³/s to 1 900 m³/s after about 6 km³ was retained.
- Flood-wave speeds differ: the Diamantina travels about four times faster than the Cooper (12.5 vs 3.1 km/day).

**Salt and water balance**
- Tetzlaff and Bye (1978) reported a water balance for January 1974 to June 1976. Total intake was 56.0 km³ and evaporation was 39.5 km³, leaving 16.5 km³ in June 1976.
- About 30 million tonnes of salt moved into Lake Eyre South in 1974. Allan et al. (1986) estimated that about 40% returned to Lake Eyre North in 1984.
- Maintaining the 1974 level would require a constant inflow of about 600 m³/s (20 km³/year), similar to the mean discharge of the Murray River.

**Economic impact of 1984 flood**
- Total damages and losses were AUD 2 459 000. The largest item was the Highways Department at $1 090 000.
- Lake Eyre South's share in refilling Lake Eyre North was estimated at 0.6 km³.

**Data gaps and recommendations**
- The hydrological data network is described as inadequate for the basin. Arid-zone gauging is sparse, with about one station per 350 000 km² in the arid zone.
- The WMO minimum for arid areas is one station per 5 000-20 000 km². Even the lowest density would require about 50 stations in South Australia's arid areas.
- The authors recommend that about 30% of arid-region stations monitor sediment discharge and 25% provide water quality data.
- Real-time telemetry, solid-state recorders and satellite transmission are recommended for remote sites. Seven potential gauging sites were selected on the Stevenson, Alberga, Hamilton, Macumba and Neales rivers.

**Conclusions and feasibility**
- Filling Lake Eyre with seawater is judged impractical. Evaporation would leave about 30 km³ of salt after 100 years.
- Diverting rivers or building large reservoirs is not justified before local water use is maximised.
- Filling the lake would not measurably increase regional rainfall.
- Water entering the lake is saline and not suitable for human consumption, although its use for other purposes is noted as possible.
- The overall conclusion is that watering the Lake Eyre Basin is impractical at present, though the situation may change as needs grow.
