# National Fish Hatchery Review Panel Report (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report, dated December 30, 1994, was produced by the National Fish Hatchery Review Panel for the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Service). The Panel was funded by the National Fish and Wildlife Foundation, contracted through The Conservation Fund, and co-chaired by Dr. Robert Putz and Dr. Fred Meyer. It was convened at the request of the Service Director to assess the future role of the [[National Fish Hatchery Program]] in [[Ecosystem Management]].

The Panel concludes that the continuing decline of aquatic habitat and fisheries demands fundamental redirection of hatchery programs, personnel, and facilities. Habitat alteration and destruction are judged the major causes of decline, and hatchery production alone is considered an ineffective substitute for habitat protection. The Panel recommends that hatcheries serve as a support function to ecosystem managers, that many of the 78 facilities be consolidated or divested, and that federal support for recreational stocking be sharply limited. The report also contains a [[Minority Report]] by Val S. Nash, representing state interests, which dissents on recreational stocking.

## Background & Description

**Introduction and causes of decline.** Nearly 400 taxa in the United States have or need special protection (Williams et al. 1989; Moyle and Leidy 1992). Twenty-seven species and thirteen subspecies of North American fish have gone extinct since 1900 (Miller et al. 1989; Minckley and Deacon 1991). Moyle and Leidy (1992) grouped the causes of fish decline into five categories: competition for water, habitat alteration, pollution, introduction of exotic species, and commercial exploitation. The report argues that hatcheries, once seen as compensation for development, can themselves be part of the problem.

**History.** Federal propagation began at Bucksport, Maine (1871) and McCloud River, California (1872). The number of federal hatcheries once reached 96. Since the 1980s, budget reductions and policy shifts led to the closure or divestiture to states of 17 facilities. Enactment of the White Act (1930), Coordination Act (1934), and Mitchell Act (1934) drove a construction period from 1934 to 1960.

**Current program (1994).**
- 78 National Fish Hatcheries (NFHs), 9 [[Fish Disease Control Centers]], and 6 Fish Technology Centers.
- About 220 million fish of 69 species produced annually for mitigation, restoration, threatened or endangered species preservation, and recreation.
- Production profile: 52 coldwater, 13 warmwater, and 13 mixed coldwater/warmwater hatcheries.
- 1993 budget of $54.1 million, of which $3.5 million was reimbursable.
- NFHs absorb about 80% of Fishery Resources (FR) dollars and personnel.
- Restoring all 78 facilities would cost about $270 million.

**Review process.** The Panel had 4 university members, 2 state representatives, and one representative each from several federal agencies and private groups. It met three times (April 9, June 8, and July 13, 1994), submitted draft recommendations on September 14, 1994, and visited all Service Regional Offices.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hatchery impacts on wild fish.** Hatchery fish are linked to poor survival, behavioral naivete, introduced parasites and diseases, genetic pollution, predation, and competition with wild stocks. Hatchery-produced non-native species and hybrids can compete with and prey on native fishes. Overharvest of wild fish can occur when fisheries cannot distinguish hatchery from wild stock. Domestication reduces growth and survival under natural conditions, and interbreeding can reduce the fitness of wild fish.

**Pacific Northwest and Atlantic salmon.** Runs of Pacific and Atlantic salmon continue to decline despite record hatchery releases. Hydropower dams injure migrating smolts and block adult passage. Reservoirs have warmed water, reduced flow, and favored predators such as squawfish and walleye. Clear-cut logging, road building, and livestock grazing have warmed spawning streams and increased siltation, which suffocates eggs and fry.

**Habitat as the key variable.** The Panel concluded that habitat alteration is the major cause of decline and that mitigation based solely on hatchery production is not working. In inland waters, stocking may offset lost spawning and nursery habitat, but it can alter gene pools and introduce harmful predators or competitors.

**Restoration limits.** Dams are usually permanent, so ecosystems cannot be fully restored to prior states. Coldwater discharges below dams can create trout tailwaters that lack spawning habitat, so these fisheries require perpetual "put, grow, and take" stocking. Reservoirs such as [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] illustrate the problem: stocked [[Striped Bass]] have preyed on stocked salmonids.

**Genetic and conservation considerations.** The National Marine Fisheries Service designated [[Evolutionarily Significant Units]] of Pacific salmon. The remnant [[Snake River Sockeye Salmon]] population at [[Redfish Lake]] is one example. In 1993, only a single male sockeye returned to its traditional spawning area at Redfish Lake. Captive populations face inbreeding risk when few breeders are available, and hatchery selection for survival fosters domestication. The Panel opposes introducing non-endemic salmon into the Snake River or other depleted systems.

## Key Findings

**Management philosophy**
- The Service has ample statutory authority to manage under an [[Ecosystem Management]] concept. No new legislation was judged necessary.
- A well-defined national fisheries program with explicit goals, objectives, and evaluation strategies does not yet exist.
- Federal policy shifts were made without consultation with states, which undermines state programs that take years to build.
- In the early 1970s, when the Division of Fisheries Management was abolished, hatchery production became the FR program rather than a part of it.

**Role of hatcheries (Panel's proposed functions, in priority order)**
1. [[Threatened And Endangered Species]]
2. [[Tribal And Treaty Obligations]]
3. Mandated mitigation
4. [[Sport Fish Restoration]]
5. Recreational fishing, only if consistent with national goals, state-funded, and compatible with ecosystem management

**Facilities and personnel**
- Not all 78 NFHs are needed. Excess facilities should be divested, leased, or consolidated.
- Federal hatchery staff are described as the largest assemblage of trained fish culturists in the world and can be reassigned to habitat work.
- Hatcheries should be evaluated against eight criteria, including ecosystem consistency, mitigation obligations, cost effectiveness, adaptability to threatened species work, and physical condition.

**Mitigation**
- 38 of the 78 hatcheries produce fish for mitigation, either statutory or de facto.
- Mitigation has not achieved its goals, particularly in the Pacific Northwest and New England.
- Hatchery production should be reduced, with savings directed to habitat restoration and fish passage research.
- A 1991 audit of two projects (four mitigation hatcheries) in the Pacific Northwest found the Service should be compensated by beneficiaries, involving about $1.5 million.

**Recreational stocking (majority view)**
- Federal tax dollars should not fund put-and-take fisheries or stocking of private waters.
- Federal obligations to stock federally funded waters should end once self-sustaining populations are established.
- States should assume costs and responsibility for trout tailwater fisheries.

**Minority view**
- Nash argues that federal hatcheries should continue stocking federal waters, since states rely on federal fish and have built tourism and access around them.
- Abrupt withdrawal could collapse recreational fisheries in Georgia, South Carolina, North Carolina, Tennessee, and West Virginia.
- A gradual phase-out over years, up to 10 years in some states, is recommended.

**Private aquaculture**
- Commercial strains are domesticated, uniform, and inbred, so they are unsuitable for ecosystem management or threatened species work.
- The Panel recommends transferring lead federal responsibility for aquaculture to USDA, with an interagency group to address feral and exotic impacts.
- Fish health services to non-federal clients should be postponed until state, private, or USDA capacity exists.

**Priority recommendations**
- Recommendation 1: the Service Director should declare that the primary mission of hatcheries is to support ecosystem management and habitat restoration.
- Recommendation 2: artificial propagation should not substitute for habitat restoration.
- Recommendation 5: stocking should occur only where suitable habitat, food base, and spawning areas are confirmed.
- Recommendation 6: fish from wild populations should be used as broodstock where possible.
- The full set contains 31 numbered recommendations covering budgeting, regional ecosystem management teams, public education, and staff morale.

**Partnerships and outreach**
- Interagency cooperation and coordination with states, tribes, and private landowners are weak.
- Visitors and politicians largely perceive hatcheries as fish-production facilities, and the Service needs to re-educate the public.
- Transition plans should be negotiated with states and tribes, with cost-sharing and gradual phase-out.
