# Evolution and Biogeography of Madrean-Tethyan Sclerophyll Vegetation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #plant-biogeography #sclerophyll

*Note on scope: this source is a botanical and paleobotanical review of terrestrial vegetation, not a fish or aquatic study. The tags above follow the house format; the content concerns plants and climate history.*

## Executive Summary

Axelrod's 1975 review (Annals of the Missouri Botanical Garden, vol. 62) argues that broadleaved evergreen sclerophyllous vegetation, the [[Chaparral and Macchia]] type, originated from older [[Laurophyllous Forest]] alliances as dry climate spread from the middle Eocene onward. The author holds that the structural adaptations of these taxa are ancient and not a response to [[Mediterranean Climate]] or fire. Current distributions are relict remnants of a once-continuous subhumid belt, and chaparral and macchia are largely seral or human-maintained rather than natural climax. The paper addresses three biogeographic problems: trans-Atlantic and Madrean-Tethyan links, the origin of Appalachian disjuncts in Mexican cloud forest, and the source of the [[Canarian Laurel Forest]].

## Background & Description

The paper is a long synthesis based on field work in North America and southern Eurasia, funded by a U.S. National Science Foundation grant (GB-37533) and by the University of California, Davis Committee on Research. The author, of the Department of Botany, University of California, Davis, frames the study around the "Madro-Tertiary Geoflora" (North American) and the "Tethyan-Tertiary Geoflora" (Eurasian), two parallel floristic provinces. Sites surveyed include closed-cone pine and chaparral stands in California, pinyon-juniper and oak woodlands in Arizona and New Mexico, the Sierra Madre Oriental of eastern Mexico, and macchia and Pinus brutia woodland in Spain, France, Morocco and Turkey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Climate contrasts:** Sclerophyllous vegetation now occurs in areas of summer-dry winter rain (California, Spain), summer and winter precipitation (Arizona-New Mexico, eastern Mediterranean), summer rain with winter drought (eastern Mexico, northwestern India), and well-distributed rainfall (northern Turkey). Walter's cited data indicate that summer water losses in wet habitats are 3 to 6 times greater than in dry ones.
- **Miocene conditions:** Miocene sclerophyllous woodland in the Mohave-Sonoran region implies 20-25 inches of precipitation distributed in summer and winter, with mild temperatures. The Vasquez and Mint Canyon floras, displaced 150-175 miles by San Andreas fault movement, indicate similar conditions.
- **Xerothermic period:** The author suggests the most favorable time for natural chaparral expansion was the Xerothermic (Hypsithermal) period, 8,000-4,000 BP.
- **Fire and grazing:** Chaparral is maintained by fire, and the paper cites historical records linking chaparral spread in San Diego County to heavy cattle and sheep grazing from the late 1700s and after the 1850s.
- **Aquatic context:** The paper touches on aquatic and marine evidence only to reconstruct climate. It cites planktonic foraminifera from the Pungo River and Yorktown formations of North Carolina, which record cool-water faunas and a warm-water shift near 13 m.y. The Florida herpetofauna, including the desert toad [[Scaphiopus]], is used as evidence of dry savanna in the middle Miocene.

## Key Findings

1. **Origin:** Sclerophyllous taxa first appear in the middle Eocene (North America) and were derived from [[Laurophyllous Forest]] alliances; in Eurasia, forerunners appear in the late Cretaceous and xerophyllous floras by the later Eocene and early Oligocene.
2. **Ancient structural traits:** Sclerophylly, deep roots, crown sprouting and thick epidermis occur repeatedly across unrelated families in diverse environments, including the Cretaceous Shasta series (about 127-128 m.y.). The author therefore treats broadleaved evergreens as "generalists" rather than Mediterranean-climate specialists.
3. **Functional adaptation:** Functional traits matched to [[Mediterranean Climate]] evolved more recently, through shifting flowering and germination to winter and spring. Wells (1969) reported that 59 of 75 *Arctostaphylos* species and 46 of 58 *Ceanothus* species in California have lost stump-sprouting.
4. **Chaparral as seral:** Chaparral and macchia were chiefly seral to oak-pine-laurel woodland during the Tertiary, and the author concludes that widespread shrublands are largely human-made. Pinus brutia reforestation in the Izmir region (rainfall 25-28 inches) is cited as evidence.
5. **Trans-Atlantic links:** Links resulted from east-west migration across a narrower Paleogene Atlantic with islands (Azores), a more easterly Appalachian axis with dry edaphic sites such as [[Granite-Gneiss Domes]] and shale barrens, and a broader subhumid belt. The author argues against a sunken land bridge given the density contrast between sialic and simatic crust.
6. **Appalachian disjuncts:** Deciduous hardwoods in Mexican and Central American cloud forest are relict, not migrants from the Appalachians. Dry climate and cold eliminated evergreen dicots in the east by the middle Oligocene, while temperate rainforest persisted in Mexico. The author argues the "migration problem" is "non-existent."
7. **Canarian laurel forest:** The laurel forest derives from a rich laurophyllous forest that covered North Africa into the middle Miocene, not from a Pliocene southward migration from southern Europe. The eastern Canaries were colonized by about 20 m.y. and the western volcanic islands from about 16 m.y. The Canaries moved from about 18°N in the early Eocene (50 m.y.) to 28°N by the middle Miocene.
8. **Summary of biogeographic conclusions:** Madrean-Tethyan floras are largely distinct today, with unique autochthonous taxa in each region and differences in the herbaceous and woody floras. The author concludes that past links were minor but important, and that the modern restricted distributions reflect Quaternary segregation under new dry climates.
