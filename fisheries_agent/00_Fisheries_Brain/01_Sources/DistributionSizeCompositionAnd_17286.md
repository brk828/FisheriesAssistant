# Distribution, Size Composition, and Relative Abundance of the Piute Sculpin in Lake Tahoe

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 study, part of the six-year [[Lake Tahoe Fisheries Study]] (begun 1960 by the California and Nevada Departments of Fish and Game), describes where [[Piute Sculpin]] live in [[Lake Tahoe]], how large they are, and how abundant they are relative to other fishes. Most fish were collected by [[Otter Trawling]] between 100 and 500 ft, supplemented by [[Rotenone Sampling]] and [[Sled Trawl]] work in shallow water. The authors found that the sculpin is one of the most abundant fishes in the lake, that densities peak at depths of 200 ft or less on rocky, steep substrate, and that the two trawling areas differ in size structure and year-class strength, suggesting limited movement between them.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and range:** The species was first described by [[Eigenmann and Eigenmann]] (1891) from Tahoe specimens. It is found throughout the [[Lahontan Basin]] of California and Nevada and in the Bonneville and Columbia River drainages (La Rivers, 1962).
- **Prior work:** [[Richard Miller]] (1951) studied the life history of Tahoe sculpins from shallow-water collections and stomach analyses of predatory fishes, including [[Mackinaw Trout]]. The present study confirms many of his findings. [[Ebert and Summerfelt]] (1969) report other aspects of its life history.
- **Study setting:** Lake Tahoe lies at 6,229 ft altitude on the California–Nevada border. It is a large, deep, extremely oligotrophic alpine lake with a surface area of 123,300 acres, maximum depth of 1,645 ft, and mean depth of 1,027 ft. Dissolved solids generally range from 60 to 65 ppm, and Secchi disk readings are usually 75 to 100 ft.
- **Funding:** The work was supported by Dingell-Johnson Projects California F-21-R and Nevada F-15-R through Federal Aid to Fish Restoration funds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling gear and effectiveness**

- Methods included otter, sled, and midwater trawling; bottom and open-water gill netting; minnow trapping; seining; and rotenone treatment.
- Of 7,390 sculpins collected, nearly 98% came from otter trawls, which were most effective at 100 to 500 ft.
- About 60 sculpins were taken by the [[Sled Trawl]] (described by Linn and Frantz, 1965), which was very effective in shallow water but was used little because its value became apparent late in the study.
- Virtually all remaining sculpins came from [[Rotenone Sampling]] in shoreline areas.
- Two sculpins were taken in bottom gill nets. Neither was actually gilled; both had been rolled up in the net.

**Trawling areas**

- **[[Agate Bay]]:** A large, gently sloping shelf between Flick and Stateline Points. The substrate graded from sand and rubble at 100 to 150 ft to mud and clay at 400 to 500 ft. Dense [[Deepwater Plant Beds]] (including Chara, filamentous algae, mosses, and liverworts) were trawled from 200 to 350 ft and not at all from 500 ft.
- **[[South Tahoe]]:** Trawling contours were staggered and farther apart. Bottom types were similar to Agate Bay except that only sand was found at 100 ft. Vegetation was less abundant and more uniformly distributed with depth. Topography was steeper and less uniform, making contour trawling more difficult.

**Night versus day trawling**

- Twenty-three matched pairs of [[Night Versus Day Trawling]] were made from September through December 1963 at 100 to 400 ft.
- Night trawls caught 95.8% of the paired sculpin catch (individual pairs ranged from 66.7% to 100%). The difference was highly significant (t = 10.49, 22 d.f.; t.01 = 2.82), using log (y + 1) transformation.
- Night trawls also took 94.5% of the [[Tui Chub]] catch (t = 6.10, 9 d.f.) and 96.7% of [[Tahoe Sucker]] catch (t = 8.58, 18 d.f.).
- These results led to the termination of day trawling in December 1963.

**Trawling schedule and operational problems**

- From September 1963 through September 1964, one series of night trawls was usually made each month in each area, at 100-ft intervals between 100 and 400 ft.
- Supplementary trawls were made at 150, 250, and 350 ft at Agate Bay, with 500 ft added from June through September 1964.
- Standard tows lasted 10 minutes and covered about 900 ft.
- Fouling on rocky obstructions forced the discontinuation of 100-ft trawls at Agate Bay in November, reduction of 150-ft tows to 5 minutes in April, and relocation of the South Tahoe 400-ft contour in June. Some heavily fouled trawls were discarded during analysis.

## Key Findings

### 1. Spatial distribution and depth

- Sculpins were widely distributed around the lake, taken from very shallow water (in some cases less than 5 ft) to 500 ft by trawl, and from 15 to 300 ft during exploratory trawling.
- Otter trawls at Agate Bay and South Tahoe, September 1963 to September 1964, captured 5,471 sculpins at 100 to 500 ft.
- A single specimen from a bottom gill net at 700 ft is the greatest recorded depth for the species.
- Numbers were heterogeneous among locations. Mean catch per trawl at 200 ft was 129.2 at Agate Bay and 27.5 at South Tahoe (F = 54.87**, F.01 = 9.65). At 400 ft the pattern reversed: 19.8 at Agate Bay and 51.5 at South Tahoe (F = 8.68**, F.01 = 4.84). At 300 ft the means were 12.2 and 12.4, which did not differ significantly (F = 0.52).
- Densities were generally highest at 200 ft or less. At South Tahoe, monthly catches at 100 ft ranged from 12 to 315 per trawl. At Agate Bay, 200-ft monthly catches ranged from 32 to 244. Partial trawls at 100 and 150 ft at Agate Bay indicated even higher densities, though these data were unreliable because of fouling.
- Only two sculpins were caught at 500 ft, and one at 700 ft. The authors conclude that numbers decline appreciably near 400 ft and that few invade the profundal zone.

### 2. Substrate and habitat preference

- The largest numbers were associated with rock and rubble, and possibly with steeper slopes, which could not be sampled directly.
- Sandy or muddy bottoms with little gradient usually yielded the fewest sculpins.
- The apparent low density at 300 ft in Agate Bay is attributed to dense plant beds, which probably reduced trawl efficiency. A mean of 35 lb of vegetation per 10-minute tow was taken at this depth.

### 3. Seasonal patterns

- No definite seasonal or spawning migrations were detected, and the changes that did occur were inconsistent between areas.
- Miller (1951) and Ebert and Summerfelt (1969) report spawning from early May to late August in shallow rocky areas. The authors found no evidence of spawning in the 100- to 500-ft trawling zone and no spring migration into shallow water.
- Large catches at 400 ft at South Tahoe in winter and early spring contrasted with uniformly low catches at Agate Bay. The authors suggest either a seasonal deep-water migration or real density differences between sites.

### 4. Size composition

- Sculpins ranged from 0.7 to 5.0 inches total length. Of 5,200 measured, only 3 were 4.0 inches or longer. The mean length was 2.37 inches.
- Mean length at 200, 300, and 400 ft was 2.33 inches at Agate Bay and 2.44 inches at South Tahoe (F = 160.16**, d.f. = 1, 2817).
- At South Tahoe, 13.5% of sculpins exceeded 3.0 inches (versus 1.3% at Agate Bay), and 22.4% were under 2.0 inches (versus 11.2% at Agate Bay). The variance difference was highly significant (F = 3.25**).
- Size distributions differed between areas, suggesting little intermingling of the two populations.
- Area differences are attributed to year-class strength. The 1963 and 1964 year classes were weak at Agate Bay relative to South Tahoe, while a strong 1962 year class was present at Agate Bay (Figure 5, November 1962 exploratory trawl).
- Small sculpins were proportionally more common in shallow water. Over 28% of the otter-trawl sample at South Tahoe at 100 ft was under 2.0 inches, and 39.2% of rotenone-collected fish were in that size range.
- Very small fish (0.3 to 1.1 inches) were taken by sled trawl at 10 to 50 ft in September 1963, July 1964, and August 1965, possibly representing young-of-the-year dispersing from nearshore rocky areas. Incubating eggs have been observed in less than 4 ft of water.

### 5. Relative abundance and community composition

- Otter trawling is selective and does not give a true measure of absolute abundance, but the authors consider the indices useful for detecting changes if the sampling is repeated.
- In the 100- to 500-ft zone, the [[Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor*) comprised about 53% of the catch, sculpins about 43%, and [[Tahoe Sucker]] about 3%. Other species, including [[Lahontan Speckled Dace]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], and [[Lake Trout]], each made up less than 0.3%.
- Sculpins outnumber chubs in water deeper than 300 ft, while chubs dominate at shallower depths.
- Minnow trapping and gill netting found no unusual concentrations of other species, supporting the dominance of chubs and sculpins.
- Sculpins are also important in the shallow littoral zone. Baker (1967) reported that they made up about 7% of fishes in shoreline rotenone collections and about 32% of sled trawl catches at 10 to 50 ft.

### 6. Conclusions

- The Piute sculpin is one of the most abundant fishes in Lake Tahoe, second only to the tui chub at depths less than 300 ft and most abundant at 300 ft and deeper.
- Highest densities occur on rocky and rubble substrates at depths of 200 ft or less.
- Significant year-class differences between areas indicate that the species is not far-ranging.
- The authors' statement that Miller's (1951) claim of abundance at "great depths" was not supported: the species is common in the littoral zone and upper slope, but sharply declines by 400 ft.
