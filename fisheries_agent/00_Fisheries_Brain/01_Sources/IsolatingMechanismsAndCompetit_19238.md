# Isolating Mechanisms and Competitive Interactions of Gambusiine Fishes from British Honduras

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1973 research proposal submitted to the [[National Science Foundation]] by David W. Greenfield of [[Northern Illinois University]]. It requests $35,382 over two years to study four livebearing species of the genus [[Gambusia]] in [[Belize]] (British Honduras) and adjacent Central America. The species are [[Gambusia Sexradiata]], [[Gambusia Yucatana]], [[Gambusia Luma]] and [[Gambusia Nicaraguensis]].

The central puzzle is distributional. [[Gambusia Sexradiata]] and [[Gambusia Yucatana]] live together in the north of the country, while [[Gambusia Luma]] is concentrated in the south, where the other two are absent. Many other freshwater fishes in the country range the full length of the country, so the author argues that no geographic barrier explains the pattern. He proposes that biological factors, including habitat preference, feeding niche, predation, and [[Isolating Mechanisms]], are responsible. The proposal frames these questions around the [[Competitive Exclusion Principle]].

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** British Honduras has seven poeciliid genera. Three of them are *Gambusia* species: *G. sexradiata* Hubbs, *G. yucatana* Regan and *G. luma* Rosen and Bailey. Authors disagree on species-group placements. [[Rosen and Bailey]] (1963) placed *G. sexradiata* and *G. luma* in the *punctata* group and *G. yucatana* in the *affinis* group. [[Rivas]] (1963) placed *G. sexradiata* in the *nobilis* group and *G. yucatana* in the *puncticulata* group. [[Fink]] (1971a) treated *G. yucatana* as a subspecies, *G. p. yucatana*. The author notes that *G. luma* appears most closely related to *G. sexradiata*.

**Morphology and identification.** *G. sexradiata* and *G. yucatana* are very similar externally and are hard to tell apart when alive. They are separated mainly by gonopodium structure. *G. luma* is easily recognized by its lack of body and fin pigment, although Rosen (personal communication) found some pigmentation in specimens from the Sarstoon River. *G. nicaraguensis* superficially resembles *G. p. yucatana* and *G. sexradiata*. All three have spotting in rows along the sides, and the females have darkly pigmented, falcate anal fins. Fink (1971b) interpreted this as convergence under similar selective pressures.

**Ranges (from the literature).**
- *G. yucatana*: Atlantic slope from the lower Rio Coatzacoalcos, Veracruz, south to northern British Honduras, including the Yucatan Peninsula.
- *G. sexradiata*: Atlantic slope from the Rio Nautla, Veracruz, south to northern Guatemala and northern British Honduras; mostly absent from the Yucatan Peninsula.
- *G. luma*: Atlantic slope of British Honduras and Guatemala (Belize River to Puerto Barrios).
- *G. nicaraguensis*: Atlantic slope from Lake Izabal, Guatemala, south to the Rio Chagres basin, Panama. Its presence in Panama may be the result of introduction.

**Author's field observations (1970–1972).** *G. luma* has its center of abundance in the southern half of the country, from Stann Creek southward. *G. yucatana* and *G. sexradiata* are absent there but abundant together north of Stann Creek. The author collected *G. luma* with the northern species only once, a single specimen from the Sibun River. That fish was taken mid-river, whereas the other two species usually occur along the edges. *G. luma* is sympatric with *G. nicaraguensis* around Puerto Barrios, Guatemala, but *G. nicaraguensis* does not extend into British Honduras.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** *G. sexradiata* and *G. yucatana* occur both in northern rivers and in temporary savannah pools near Belize. They can be taken in the same seine sweep, but their relative abundance differs greatly between localities. Some sites contain only one species, and others hold both in roughly equal numbers.

**Predators.** Preliminary collections showed that savannah pools vary in predator composition. Some held various [[Cichlasoma]] species, some held only [[Belonesox]], and others had no predatory fishes. The author sees these pools as a near-ideal natural experiment for testing how predators affect the ratio of the two species. Krumholz (1963) showed that predation affects fecundity, sex ratio and age structure in *Gambusia manni*. Hubbs (1971) showed that predators push *Gambusia* into offshore and deeper waters.

**Climate and tolerance.** Goodyear et al. (1972) reported that *G. affinis* tolerates temperatures from 25–36 °C and pH from 7.5 to 10.3. The author uses this to suggest that poeciliids may survive environmental disturbance better than many native species.

**Human context.** The proposal notes that tropical environments will change quickly with population growth and pollution. It also notes that *Gambusia affinis* has been used for mosquito control, often with harmful results when introduced outside its native range (Myers, 1965). Insecticide-resistant strains have been documented (Vinson et al., 1963; Culley and Ferguson, 1969).

**Theoretical framing.** The author notes debate over the [[Competitive Exclusion Principle]]. Hardin (1960) argued it cannot be tested by facts, while Birch and Ehrlich (1967) considered it untestable. MacArthur (1968) argued its consequences are worth studying anyway. Inger and Greenberg (1966) asked how much niche overlap permits coexistence, which is the question the proposal adopts. Zaret and Rand (1971) provided field support for the principle in Panamanian stream fishes.

## Key Findings

The proposal does not report new data. Its main points, plans and conclusions are:

1. **Problem statement.** The distribution of the four *Gambusia* species cannot be explained by geographic barriers. The author attributes it to biological factors, including habitat, feeding, predation and reproductive isolation.

2. **Premating isolation (planned).**
   - *Temporal isolation*: reproductive cycles and diel activity periods will be determined, using baited traps after Hubbs (1971). Complete seasonal isolation is considered unlikely.
   - *Habitat isolation*: seining, surface observation and [[SCUBA]] surveys will test whether *G. sexradiata* and *G. yucatana* occupy different microhabitats. The author also tests whether *G. luma* behaves differently where it is allopatric and where it is sympatric with *G. nicaraguensis*. Preliminary observations suggest *G. luma* is more schooling and stays near the surface.
   - *Ethological isolation*: courtship will be tested with the [[Hubbs and Delco]] (1960, 1962) methods. Males will be paired with conspecific and heterospecific females, and behaviors will be recorded on videotape. Allopatric and sympatric populations will be compared to test for [[Reinforcement]].
   - *Mechanical isolation*: genital anatomy and gonopodial thrusting will be compared across species, following [[Peden]]. Models of female genitalia will be made to record gonopodial imprints and thrust direction.

3. **Postmating isolation (planned).**
   - *Cytomorphology*: karyotypes will be compared, since earlier work reported chromosome numbers from 2n=42 to 2n=48, most species 2n=48.
   - *Laboratory crosses*: artificial crosses will test hybridization, gamete or zygote mortality, and possible F1 sterility through backcrossing. Laboratory stocks of *G. luma*, *G. yucatana* and *G. sexradiata* were already being maintained at Northern Illinois University.

4. **Predation.** Computer correlation of species occurrence with predator presence will use 1970–1972 collections. Predator stomach contents will be analyzed. If predation explains abundance differences, predators will be removed from isolated pools to track population responses. The proposal cites [[Levin]] (1970) for the prediction that the species with the higher predator threshold will replace the other.

5. **Feeding niche.** Stomach contents will be sampled through the year. Twenty-four-hour feeding studies will identify peak feeding periods. Food overlap will be measured with the [[Horn Overlap Coefficient]], which ranges from 0 (no shared food categories) to 1 (identical proportions), and compared with MacArthur and Levins' (1967) limiting-similarity criterion.

6. **Age, growth and fecundity.** These will be measured for all four species, with emphasis on the sympatric *G. sexradiata* and *G. yucatana*. Differences could decide competitive outcomes in savannah pools.

7. **Budget and duration.** The project runs for two years, with about 168 field days in total. The request is $35,382. Year 1 totals $17,826 and Year 2 totals $17,556. The largest items are personnel, travel between Chicago and Belize, and field vehicle rental. Indirect costs total $3,787. Northern Illinois University will cost-share according to NSF policy.

8. **Personnel and facilities.** The principal investigator is an associate professor in biological sciences. A research assistant, who would be a graduate student in ichthyology, would help with aquarium maintenance, breeding, chromosome analysis and summer fieldwork. The university's aquatic biology laboratories hold 5 100-gallon and 20 15-gallon aquaria, four SCUBA sets, video and photographic equipment, and a conductivity meter and oxygen meter.

9. **Expected outcome.** The study is intended to clarify how closely related species can coexist, and what isolating mechanisms and competitive pressures shape the distribution of four congeneric fishes in the freshwater fauna of Central America.
