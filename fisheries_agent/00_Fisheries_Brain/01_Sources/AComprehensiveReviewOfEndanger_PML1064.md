# Comprehensive Review of Endangered Species Act Recovery Plans

#endangered-species #recovery-plans #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This lead paper introduces a collaborative, systematic review of [[Recovery Plans]] prepared under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). The project was initiated in 1998 by [[Dee Boersma]], then president of the [[Society for Conservation Biology]] (SCB), and was carried out in partnership with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) and the [[National Center for Ecological Analysis and Synthesis]] (NCEAS). Its aims were to build a detailed database on the content of a representative sample of recovery plans, quantify patterns among them, and assess how useful plans are as conservation tools. The review sampled 181 species, produced a standardized [[Recovery Plan Data Collection Instrument]], and used a seminar-based team of researchers to code the plans. The paper also reports a consistency test of the method and outlines the project's benefits and limitations. The other papers in the special feature are products of this project.

## Background & Description

**Policy context.** The ESA of 1973 (16 U.S.C. §1531 et seq.) aims to recover threatened and endangered species so they no longer require protection. Recovery plans are the ESA's main tool for guiding that process. In the mid-1990s, several legislative proposals would have substantially restructured the ESA and expanded the role of recovery plans. Dr Boersma recognized that better recovery planning could greatly benefit listed species, but that those benefits depended on the effectiveness of the plans themselves. She sought SCB approval for a national study intended to inform public policy on species conservation. The USFWS and NCEAS provided funding, workshop facilities, and computing support.

**Study goals.** The project was designed to:
- Compile a detailed database on the characteristics and content of a large, representative sample of recovery plans.
- Quantify important patterns, trends, and differences among plans.
- Evaluate the usefulness of recovery plans as conservation tools.

**Background on the ESA population.** Through 1998, the USFWS had approved recovery plans for 931 listed species. Earlier reviews, such as those by [[Tear et al. (1993, 1995)]] and [[Clark (1994)]], were largely qualitative or based on case studies. The project therefore aimed to bring a quantitative, standardized approach to the question.

**Sampling design.** A post-stratified random sample of plans was drawn using three stratification variables:
- Taxon (plant, vertebrate, invertebrate)
- Scope (single-species or multi-species)
- Revision status (unrevised or revised)

Revised plans were limited in number, so all revised plans were included. The remaining cells were filled by random selection of unrevised plans. The original sample comprised 195 plans. Reviews for 14 plans were not finished in time, leaving a final set of 181 plans, which the authors judged statistically comparable to the original sample. Cell distributions are summarized in Table 1 of the source. Multi-species plans were included when they contained sampled species. In those cases, the authors counted the species of interest, not the plan, as the managed unit.

**Final sample composition (n = 181 species):**
- Taxon: 85 plants, 75 vertebrates, 21 invertebrates
- Scope: 100 single-species plans, 81 multi-species plans
- Revision status: 113 never revised, 68 revised one or more times (only the most recent revision was reviewed)
- Approval date: 65 approved in or before 1990, 116 approved after 1990

The year 1990 marks publication of USFWS policy and guidelines for recovery plan preparation, issued in response to the 1988 ESA amendments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The data-collection instrument covered the full range of factors that bear on species recovery. Its main topics were:

- **Plan administration:** authorship and institutional affiliations.
- **Descriptive statistics:** listing date, taxonomic group, and species distribution.
- **Species biology:** habitat requirements, population biology (size and demographic rates), genetics, and behavior.
- **Threats:** construction and urban development, agriculture and forestry, pollution, exotic species, species interactions, habitat dynamics, and other factors such as inbreeding and climate change.
- **Population management:** translocation, captive breeding, and predator control.
- **Habitat management:** restoration of habitat quality and habitat acquisition, plus incentive programs such as USDA wetland reserves.
- **Monitoring:** focal-species population metrics, associated species, and habitat quality and trends.
- **Public relations:** public awareness and enforcement of legal protections.
- **Recovery criteria:** population targets, the number of subpopulations, and how target values were set.

Reviewers recorded whether each topic was covered and how it was addressed. The authors note that many plans lacked information on some of these topics, and that the database captures those gaps through standardized negative response codes.

## Key Findings

**Methodology and instrument**
- The instrument used numeric and alphabetic codes to identify each question. Response codes were standardized to make data entry consistent.
- Negative codes recorded non-applicable items (-2), information not presented in the plan (-1), and reviewer failure to answer (-3). These codes were kept so analysts could tell why data were missing.
- Only information inside the recovery plan (or the original listing document) was used to answer questions. Exceptions were the recovery priority and latest status trend (from the USFWS 1999 report to Congress), and the extent of task implementation (from a USFWS-nominated expert).

**Data collection and coordination**
- Research groups were recruited from 19 universities across the United States. The count of participating researchers is partly illegible in the scan but appears to be about 125.
- Seminar groups were randomly assigned plans. Exceptions were made to avoid assigning plans to groups with conflicts of interest.
- Groups were trained through a December 1998 workshop in Washington, D.C., reading on recovery planning, and a calibration exercise on the endangered Hawaiian tree [[Kokia Drynarioides]]. Communication continued through a project website and email listserv.

**Consistency analysis (Kokia calibration)**
- Eighteen of the seminar groups submitted Kokia data. Two groups from the University of California, Davis, submitted one set of data, and one group did not submit.
- [[Concordance Analysis]]: 37% of questions had a concordance score above 0.94 (at least 17 of 18 responses identical). 68% of questions scored above 0.75, and only 7.4% scored below 0.50.
- Question-level discrepancy: the median absolute discrepancy was 1, and the maximum possible discrepancy on count questions was used for the largest errors. Of discordant responses, 41% were negative and 59% positive. Errors were therefore small and roughly balanced in direction.
- Collapsing the distribution-range question into three broader bins raised concordance to 0.83.
- The USFWS-related question (Q67 in the source) had an intermediate concordance of 0.67, yet inspection showed all seminars agreed on the answer. That result reflected the nature of the question, not inconsistency.
- The authors caution that the single-plan Kokia test cannot predict error rates for individual questions. It was used to show that the method, as a whole, was consistent.

**Usefulness and implications**
- Standardization allowed effective comparison across plans while preserving detail. The instrument also separated objective attributes from later judgments, which were left for subsequent statistical analysis.
- The database is intended to inform lawmakers, researchers, and the public, and to support future review of temporal trends in recovery planning.
- The project yielded several collateral benefits, including a reusable research method, cross-institutional training for students and faculty, and a shared understanding of the ESA planning process among participants and USFWS staff.

**Cost and funding**
- Budget information was highly variable across plans. Some plans estimated total costs, while others estimated only the first few years or only federal costs. Because of this inconsistency, many cost fields were excluded from the analyses.
- The authors stress that funding is critical to converting a recovery plan into action. Even a well-crafted plan will not succeed without adequate and sustained funding, and existing appropriations were described as often insufficient and misallocated.
- Critics argue that ESA costs outweigh benefits. The authors believe the costs of recovery planning can be justified and that the database offers a way to examine such claims.

**Conclusions**
- Recovery plans will be more effective if they are institutionally supported, implemented, and adequately funded.
- The analyses and recommendations arising from the project are intended to inform policy, professional practice, and academic research on endangered species recovery.
