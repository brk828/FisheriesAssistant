# Summary of FY-1999 Fish Surveys for a Long-Term Monitoring Plan in the Gila River Basin, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by [[Paul C. Marsh]] of Arizona State University for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (Phoenix Area Office), summarizes fish sampling carried out in fiscal year 1999 (October 1, 1998 to September 30, 1999) under a long-term monitoring plan for fish populations in selected waters of the [[Gila River Basin]], Arizona. Sampling was conducted by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Arizona State University]], and the Bureau of Reclamation between 29 September 1998 and 28 January 1999. Across seven monitored waters, 27 taxa (including Lepomis hybrids) were captured. Only four were native, and most of the catch was non-native. The report also lists recommendations for improving canal access and coordination with canal operators.

## Background & Description

The monitoring follows standard operating procedures described in [[Clarkson 1996]] (a–c), which cover the overall monitoring plan and field protocols for canals and rivers. Monitored waters (stations) were:

1. [[San Pedro River]], downstream from the U.S.–Mexico border
2. [[Gila River]], between Coolidge Dam and the Ashurst-Hayden Diversion
3. [[Salt River]], between Stewart Mountain Dam and the Granite Reef Diversion
4. [[Central Arizona Project Canal]] (CAP), at selected pump plants
5. [[Salt River Project]] (SRP) North Canal
6. SRP South Canal
7. [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]] (FCG)

Additional opportunistic, non-standard collections were made on the SRP North and South canals and on the SRP Consolidated and Tempe canals. The report explicitly does not compare FY1999 data with 1995–1996 monitoring results, which are covered in Clarkson (1998).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most sampled waters are artificial canals, and the community is dominated by non-native species. Native fish are largely confined to river reaches and a few canals. Several factors limit comparability among stations:

- Sampling effort and gear type differed among stations; canal samples from SRP North, SRP South and FCG were opportunistic and qualitative, except above the electric fish barriers on the SRP canals.
- Sampled area was variable and only roughly estimated, and electrofishing efficiency varied among crews.
- Canal operations (drawdowns, outages) and locked access gates sometimes compromised timing and access for sampling.

Electric fish barriers on canals (e.g., at China Wash on the FCG Canal) separate upstream and downstream fish communities, a key structuring factor in the data.

## Key Findings

**Overall richness and composition**
- 27 taxa were captured. Four were native: roundtail chub, longfin dace, Sonora sucker and desert sucker. The remaining 23 were non-native.
- Station richness ranged from 6 (FCG Canal) to 14 (SRP North Canal); no stream or canal had more than three natives, and none occurred in the CAP Canal.
- Native proportion of the total catch ranged from 0% (CAP Canal) to 74.7% (Gila River; see Table 3 values in source), while SRP North, SRP South and FCG canal samples were 12%, 25.8% and 11% natives respectively, per the source.

**Station-level results**
- **San Pedro River:** 12 species; longfin dace was the only fish present at all stations and was the most abundant native. Mosquitofish was the most abundant non-native (about 40% of catch), followed by red shiner (4.3%), black bullhead (4.1%) and green sunfish (2.2%). Natives comprised roughly 70% of individuals overall.
- **Salt River:** desert sucker dominated the catch, followed by largemouth bass; a list of 13 species was taken, with 10 non-native.
- **Gila River:** red shiner was predominant (followed by Sonora sucker). Native fish made up 17.1% of the catch above the barrier, with desert sucker (7.2%), Sonora sucker (5.9%) and roundtail chub (8.9%) important. Tilapia (undetermined species, mostly young of year, 56.9%) and red shiner (14.6%) were the most abundant overall.
- **CAP Canal:** entirely non-native; bluegill was most abundant, followed by carp, with hybrid sunfish dominant at the upper reach.
- **SRP South Canal:** tilapia (56.0%) dominated, followed by red shiner and channel catfish. Natives made up 25.8% of the catch.
- **FCG Canal:** eight species, including two natives. Red shiner was predominant (74.9% of catch), followed by mosquitofish (9.5%), channel catfish (8.3%) and yellow bullhead (3.4%). Desert sucker, once abundant in 1995, was largely absent.
- **SRP Consolidated Canal:** mosquitofish most abundant, followed by red shiner, redbelly tilapia and channel catfish; desert sucker ranked fourth (17 specimens). It was the only canal where redbelly and blue tilapia occurred together.
- **Tempe Canal:** five species observed visually; desert sucker and Sonora sucker considered abundant, channel catfish common.

**Recommendations**
- Obtain keys to locked gates along SRP canal roadways.
- Improve communication between canal operators (SRP, SCID) and monitoring staff so sampling can coincide with scheduled outages.
