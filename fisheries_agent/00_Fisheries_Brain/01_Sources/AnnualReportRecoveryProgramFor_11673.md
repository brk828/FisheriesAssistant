# 1995 Annual Report: Recovery Program for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species #habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

The 1995 annual report documents the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]]'s transition from research to direct recovery actions for four endangered fish species: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). The program achieved significant milestones including altered dam operations at [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Blue Mesa Reservoir]], construction of fish passage facilities, wetland restoration, and endangered fish stocking. Early signs indicate [[Colorado Squawfish]] populations in the [[Green River]] are increasing in response to modified dam operations. The program successfully balanced endangered species recovery with water development, issuing favorable biological opinions on more than 170 water projects since 1988 in compliance with the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Background & Description

The four target species are endemic to the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] and exist nowhere else on earth. [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] evolved more than 3 million years ago. These native fishes face multiple threats from human activities including construction of hundreds of dams and water diversions, stocking of non-native fish species, and wetland loss.

Only 14 species of fish are native to the upper [[Colorado River Basin]], while 40 or more non-native species have been introduced. In some locations, non-natives comprise as much as 95 percent of the biomass of existing fish populations. Wetlands have been reduced by 50 percent in [[Colorado]], 30 percent in [[Utah]], and 38 percent in [[Wyoming]] over the last 200 years.

The [[Recovery Program]] was established in 1988 after the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] determined that water depletions were a major barrier to endangered fish recovery, threatening to block all water development in the basin. The program aims to recover endangered fish while providing for future water development.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] encompasses the [[Colorado River]], [[Green River]], [[Gunnison River]], [[Yampa River]], and [[White River]] systems. Young endangered fish require wetland habitats for growth and survival, but these habitats have been severely degraded. River habitat has changed greatly from historical conditions, affecting survival of stocked fish.

Human impact has caused 40 species and subspecies of fish in North America to become extinct during the 20th century. In the upper [[Colorado River Basin]], the greatest impacts to native fish have come from non-native fish stocking and dam construction.

Modified dam operations now provide 2.5 million acre-feet per year of releases timed to improve endangered fish habitat through higher spring flows and lower, more stable flows the rest of the year. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] made 10,000 to 20,000 acre-feet of water available annually from [[Ruedi Reservoir]].

## Key Findings

**Population Response:**
- Early signs indicate [[Colorado Squawfish]] populations in the [[Green River]] are increasing in response to changes in [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations

**Stocking Activities:**
- In fall 1995, approximately 900 5-inch [[Razorback Sucker]]s were stocked in the [[Green River]] in [[Utah]]
- 300 4- to 8-inch [[Razorback Sucker]]s were stocked in the [[Gunnison River]] in [[Colorado]]
- Nearly 200,000 endangered fish have been stocked in the upper [[Colorado River Basin]] over the past 15 years

**Infrastructure Development:**
- A total of 34 ponds have been built to hold and raise endangered fish: 6 at [[Horsethief Canyon State Wildlife Area]] near Fruita, Colorado; 22 at [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] in [[Utah]]; and 6 at [[Wahweap State Fish Hatchery]] in Big Water, [[Utah]]
- Construction of a fish ladder at the [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] was begun in 1995 for completion in 1996

**Habitat Restoration:**
- Four wetlands have been restored to raise young endangered fish: three on the [[Colorado River]] near Grand Junction, one at [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] on the [[Green River]]
- Young endangered fish grow significantly faster in riverside wetlands where water is warmer and food is more available
- Biologists completed an inventory of riverside wetlands covering 870 miles of river systems

**Water Management:**
- The [[Utah State Engineer]] set a water rights policy to protect [[Green River]] flows between [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and the [[Duchesne River]]
- The [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]] filed four new water rights applications for in-stream flows for endangered fish in the [[Yampa River]] and [[Colorado River]]

**Regulatory Success:**
- Since 1988, the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] has issued favorable biological opinions on more than 170 water projects in [[Colorado]], [[Utah]], and [[Wyoming]]
- 70 percent of approved projects were in [[Colorado]], with 15 percent each in [[Utah]] and [[Wyoming]]
- New projects account for 75 percent of the total; more than 40 pre-existing projects also received favorable opinions

**Program Budget (1995):**
- Total funding: $8,628,700
- Largest funding source: [[Bureau of Reclamation]] capital funds ($4,733,000, 54.8%)
- Largest expenditure categories: In-stream flow identification and protection ($2,654,900, 30.8%); Habitat restoration ($2,573,300, 29.8%); Endangered fish stocking ($1,547,000, 17.9%)
