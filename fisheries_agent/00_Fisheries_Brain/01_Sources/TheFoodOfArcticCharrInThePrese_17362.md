# The Food of Arctic Charr in the Presence and Absence of Brown Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Ronald N. B. Campbell]] (University College, Cork; published in *Glasgow Naturalist*, 1982) compares the diet of [[Arctic Charr]] living alone with that of Charr living alongside [[Brown Trout]]. The allopatric (alone) Charr of [[Loch Meallt]], Skye, were compared by stomach analysis with sympatric (co-existing) Charr and Trout of [[Loch Fada]], North Uist. Allopatric Charr and Trout fed mainly on the bottom, while sympatric Charr relied mainly on [[Zooplankton]]. The author interprets this as competition with Trout pushing Charr into a zooplankton niche, a pattern previously described in Scandinavian lakes.

## Background & Description

- **Study question:** Charr in the British Isles nearly always live with other salmonids, so earlier work mostly concerned competition. Charr living alone had not been studied in Britain.
- **Historical diet records:** Early accounts (Jardine 1834, Baird 1857, Maitland 1884, Frost 1951) listed "minute entomostracea" and "aquatic insects." Charr in Loch Borralie were reported to eat [[Three-Spined Stickleback]], corixids and insect larvae.
- **Taxonomy:** British charr taxonomy has received more attention than diet (Friend 1956, 1959).
- **Context:** The Loch Meallt population was discovered in 1974. Other Charr-only lochs have since been found (Maitland et al. 1982, in press).
- **Origin of data:** The work was first presented as an honours thesis at the University of Aberdeen (1976).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study lochs**

| Feature | [[Loch Meallt]] | [[Loch Fada]] |
|---|---|---|
| Trophic status | Eutrophic | Oligotrophic (moorland) |
| Area | 8.2 ha (20.3 acres) | 44.25 ha (109.3 acres) |
| Maximum depth | 3 m | 30 m |
| Altitude | 51 m, on an exposed sea cliff | 10 m, on Lewisian gneiss |
| Fish present | Charr only | Charr and Brown Trout |
| Migratory access | No (outflow falls over sea cliff after 15 m) | Said to be accessible |

- Loch Meallt has three small inflows and one outflow.
- Loch Fada's inflows and outflows have not been accurately mapped.

**Food availability**
- Benthos, zooplankton and fish samples were taken in spring and autumn from both lochs.
- The same broad food categories were available in both lochs.
- Loch Meallt is the richer loch, with a large supply of permanent bottom fauna such as [[Gammarus]] and molluscs.

**Climate and feeding**
- Scandinavian lakes freeze in winter. Spring bottom food accumulates under the ice, and fish feed less at low temperatures.
- Scottish lochs in the Hebrides seldom freeze, and fish feed through winter (Campbell 1954).
- Nilsson's (1960) explanation of competition therefore may not apply directly to Scottish lochs.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**
- Fish were caught with bottom-set monofilament "[[Tundhovn Net]]" gill nets, left overnight at bay entrances.
- Loch Meallt Charr: 6 (1 and 4 May 1975), 11 (26 August 1975), and 77 of 98 Charr caught on 21 May 1975 by the Freshwater Fisheries Laboratory, Pitlochry.
- Loch Fada: 17 Charr and 19 Brown Trout (2–3 April 1975); 8 Charr and 22 Brown Trout (28 August 1975).
- Diet was analysed using the [[Percentage Occurrence Method]], which records the percentage of stomachs containing each prey category. This method suits fish that eat both many small items and few large ones. Stomachs with several categories count toward each.
- Prey were grouped into three categories after [[Frost And Brown 1967]]:
  - **A. Benthos:** larval Chironomidae, Coleoptera, Ephemeroptera, Megaloptera, Plecoptera and Trichoptera; adult Gammarus, molluscs and Oligochaeta.
  - **B. Mid-water and surface:** adult insects, pupal Chironomidae, Hydracarina and sticklebacks.
  - **C. Zooplankton.**

**Results (Figure 1, a three-axis diagram; exact percentages are not given in the text)**
1. **Sympatric Loch Fada Charr** depended strongly on zooplankton in both seasons.
2. **Sympatric Loch Fada Trout and allopatric Loch Meallt Charr** concentrated on bottom feeding in spring.
3. **Spring-to-autumn shift:** both spring bottom-feeding groups moved toward surface and mid-water prey in autumn. This is probably linked to a decline in bottom fauna in late summer as insect larvae hatch. The shift was smaller in the richer Loch Meallt.

**Discussion and conclusions**
- Charr living with Trout were zooplankton feeders. Charr living alone were mainly bottom feeders, as Trout were.
- Competition with Trout seems severe enough to push Charr into zooplankton feeding, a niche they otherwise leave.
- Sympatric Scottish Charr are small and of little value, whereas Scandinavian Charr reach sporting and commercial size. [[Steinbock]] (1950) found that zooplankton-only feeding stunts growth. The author proposes that Scottish Charr may be restricted to zooplankton all year.
- The exception may be Loch Borralie, where Charr eat a varied, non-zooplankton diet alongside Trout. [[Campbell]] (1961) reported poor Trout spawning there, which would leave Trout sparse and let Charr keep to the littoral zone.
- The Loch Meallt results agree with Nilsson's (1955–1969) Scandinavian work and have no British comparison yet. The sympatric Loch Fada results agree with [[Frost]]'s (1977) study of [[Windermere]] Charr.
- Nilsson's Scandinavian studies found allopatric Charr were mainly bottom feeders. Sympatric Charr fed on the bottom only in spring and became increasingly zooplankton-dependent through the year.
