# Endangered Species of New Mexico: 1979 State Handbook

#ichthyology #marine-biology #vertebrates

## Executive Summary

This handbook, issued by the [[New Mexico Department Of Game And Fish]] in 1979 and written by John P. Hubbard, Marshall C. Conway, Howard Campbell, Gregory Schmitt and Michael D. Hatch, documents the 93 animal species listed as endangered in New Mexico under the [[Wildlife Conservation Act]] (Regulation No. 599, revised 25 May 1979). The list covers 14 mammals, 30 birds, 18 reptiles, 6 amphibians, 24 fishes and 1 crustacean. Of these, 2 mammals, 3 birds, 4 fishes, 1 reptile and the crustacean are also federally listed. The text is a species-by-species compendium of identification, distribution, biology, status, conservation needs and remarks, aimed mainly at land and water managers and biologists.

The document is a reference handbook rather than a research paper. Its full accounts cover mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians. The fish section and the crustacean account (the [[Socorro Isopod]]) appear only as checklists in the table of contents; the detailed fish accounts are not included in the supplied text.

## Background & Description

**Legal framework.** The state mandate was enacted on 26 February 1974 with the Wildlife Conservation Act (NMSA 1978, Secs. 17-2-37 to 17-2-46). By September 1974 the Endangered Species Program was staffed by four full-time biologists and a secretary. The first State list was issued on 24 January 1975 as Regulation No. 563, with revisions in 1975, 1976, 1978 and 1979.

**Definition of endangered.** New Mexico's definition is geopolitical. A species is endangered if its prospects of survival or recruitment *within the State* are in jeopardy. As a result, many non-endemic species that are rare, peripheral or declining in the state are listed. The handbook uses two informal categories:
- [[State Endangered Group I]]: prospects of survival or recruitment in the state are in jeopardy. These species have usually suffered notable declines in numbers or range.
- [[State Endangered Group II]]: survival may become in jeopardy in the foreseeable future. These species are more stable but could decline at any time.

**Protection and penalties.** Taking, possessing, transporting or selling listed species, or their parts, eggs or offspring, is illegal, except in emergencies involving immediate threats to human life or property. Penalties are:
- Category A (illegal acts that could have been done under permit): fines of $50 to $300, jail up to 90 days, or both.
- Category B (acts not justifiable even under permit): a set fine of $1,000 and 30 days to one year in jail, or both.

**Federal relationship.** The Department considers state listings binding on federal agencies and lands. Unlike the federal [[Endangered Species Act]], the state law has no [[Critical Habitat]] clause. The Department argues that informing federal agencies of occupied areas acts as a de facto equivalent.

**Maps and records.** Distribution maps use filled circles or squares for verified records and open symbols for unverified ones. Circles denote 1960 and later records, and squares denote pre-1960 records.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The handbook repeatedly links decline to habitat loss and degradation. Recurring threats include:
- **Grazing and range deterioration**: overgrazing of desert grassland, tundra and sagebrush, and drought effects, as with the [[White-Sided Jackrabbit]], [[Sage Grouse]] and [[White-Tailed Ptarmigan]].
- **Riparian woodland loss**: cottonwood and sycamore clearing affects many birds, including the [[Gila Woodpecker]], [[Bell's Vireo]] and several hummingbirds.
- **Water and stream change**: reservoir construction, dewatering and stream degradation affect fish, otters and bald eagles.
- **Persecution and collecting**: shooting, trapping, poisoning and over-collecting of reptiles and amphibians for the pet trade.
- **Pesticides**: hydrocarbon residues caused eggshell thinning in bald eagles and possibly affected peregrines.
- **Competition and parasitism**: introduced [[European Starling]]s compete for nest holes, and [[Brown-Headed Cowbird]]s parasitize vireo nests.
- **Prairie dog poisoning**: campaigns against prairie dogs reduced prey and shelter for [[Black-Footed Ferret]]s.

Many listed species are at the northern or southern edge of their ranges in New Mexico. Examples include the [[Coppery-Tailed Trogon]], [[Thick-Billed Kingbird]] and [[Gila Monster]]. The handbook notes that the Guadalupe Canyon area of Hidalgo County is a key refuge for numerous Mexican-derived birds and reptiles.

## Key Findings

**Mammals (14 species)**
- The [[Arizona Shrew]] is known from only 10 specimens in three Arizona mountain ranges and one New Mexico specimen drowned in the Animas Mountains.
- The [[Southern Yellow Bat]] has New Mexico records only from Guadalupe Canyon.
- The [[Gray Wolf]] is endangered throughout North America. Occasional *C. l. baileyi* wanderers cross from Mexico into Hidalgo County. Pack sizes seldom exceed eight.
- Up to 340 [[White-Sided Jackrabbit]]s were estimated in the Animas and Playas valleys in summer 1976. At least 33 were counted in November 1975.
- The [[Tularosa Black-Tailed Prairie Dog]] survives in only two colonies, in Lincoln and Sierra counties.
- The [[River Otter]] was last verified in New Mexico in 1953 and may be extirpated.
- The [[Marten]] is known only from the San Juan and Sangre de Cristo mountains. Its New Mexico population is at the southern US limit of the genus.

**Birds (30 species)**
- The [[Olivaceous Cormorant]] first bred at Elephant Butte Lake in 1972, the northernmost colony in the species' range. The 1975 maximum was seven nests, which fledged five young.
- The [[Bald Eagle]] no longer nests in New Mexico. Winter 1978–79 numbers were estimated at 200 to 300.
- Fewer than 20 active [[Peregrine Falcon]] eyries were thought to exist in New Mexico.
- The [[Whooping Crane]] has an experimental flock at Grays Lake, Idaho. Its young migrate to New Mexico's central Rio Grande Valley each autumn. Eight or nine birds were known to survive in winter 1977–78. The wild North American population was 60–70 birds.
- The [[Sharp-Tailed Grouse]] was apparently extirpated after 1952 reintroductions of 21 North Dakota birds in Colfax County.
- The [[Aplomado Falcon]] has been reliably recorded about once every five years since the 1940s.
- Several Mexican species, including the [[Violet-Crowned Hummingbird]] and [[Beardless Flycatcher]], are known from only a few records or pairs in Guadalupe Canyon.

**Reptiles (18 species)**
- The [[Texas Slider]] occurs only in the lower Pecos Valley.
- The [[Sand Dune Lizard]] is restricted to the Mescalero Sands and depends on active dunes that may be stabilizing.
- The [[Gila Monster]] has declined in recent years, largely through collecting for the pet trade.
- The [[Ridge-Nosed Rattlesnake]] is known only from a few high points of the Animas Mountains. Its habitat is entirely on private land, and the landowners have closed the range to unauthorized entry. A life-history study is under way under contract with the US Fish and Wildlife Service.

**Amphibians (6 species)**
- The [[Jemez Mountains Salamander]] is endemic to the Jemez Mountains and occurs at few localities above about 8,000 feet.
- The [[Sacramento Mountain Salamander]] is known from the Sacramento, White and Capitan mountains.
- The [[Boreal Toad]] is known from only three localities in the San Juan Mountains.

**Fishes (24 species) and crustacean (1 species)**
- The fish checklist includes the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Pecos Gambusia]], [[Silvery Minnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loachminnow]] and [[White Sands Pupfish]]. Four fishes are federally listed.
- The only crustacean is the [[Socorro Isopod]].
- Detailed fish and isopod accounts are not included in the supplied text.

**Policy conclusions**
- The Department's central tools are habitat protection, restriction of collecting, and surveys before control or development. Transplantation is recommended where feasible, as for the ferret, sage grouse and marten.
- The handbook treats listing as only a preliminary step. Its stated role for the Endangered Species Program covers surveys, research, habitat acquisition, information and law enforcement.
- New Mexico qualified as a cooperating state under the federal Act in mid-1976, with the agreement signed in that year.
