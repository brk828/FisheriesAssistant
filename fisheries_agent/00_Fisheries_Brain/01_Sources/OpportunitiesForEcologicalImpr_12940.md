# Opportunities for Ecological Improvement Along the Lower Colorado River and Delta

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-wetlands

## Executive Summary

This 1997 final report, prepared by [[Sonoran Institute]] researchers for [[Defenders of Wildlife]] and the [[National Park Service]] US/Mexico Affairs Office, assesses the ecological condition of the [[Lower Colorado River]] from Parker Dam to the [[Colorado River Delta]] and evaluates past recovery projects. Twelve sites were reviewed: nine recovery efforts, mostly revegetation, and three natural areas, with the [[El Doctor Wetlands]] treated as a fourth natural area in the recommendations.

The authors conclude that river impoundment, diversion, groundwater pumping, exotic plants and agriculture have devastated native riparian and wetland habitat, and that the delta is "not beyond recovery." Their central recommendation is that effluent and agricultural drainage water be allocated to wetlands rather than evaporative basins, alongside altered reservoir releases, more effective revegetation, and bi-national, community-based approaches.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] drains about 632,000 km² and flows roughly 2,240 km (1,400 miles) to the Sea of Cortez, including 5,200 km² in northern México. Historic accounts suggest that riparian ecosystems changed little from the 17th century until [[Hoover Dam]] was completed in 1935. Afterward, the river passed through 28 dams, irrigated over 1 million hectares (2.5 million acres) of farmland, and supplied water to more than 20 million people in the U.S. and México.

Hydrologic change has been a major driver of fish decline. Before dam construction the river was warm, muddy and highly seasonal; afterward it became clear, cold and relatively stable. The authors state that this favored non-native [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]] over native warm-water species, including razorback, bluehead sucker, flannelmouth, [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Apart from the bluehead sucker and speckled dace, all native fishes are endangered or under consideration for listing.

Two fish species receive particular attention:

- **[[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*)**: an endangered fish that inhabits the [[Cienaga de Santa Clara]].
- **[[Totoaba]] (*Cynoscion macdonaldi*)**: once common throughout much of the delta, now endangered. Its decline is linked to delta ecological decline, and it is culturally important to the [[Cocopah]].

The report also addresses the [[Vaquita]], a porpoise dependent on the delta's protected waters and nutrient supply, which is listed as endangered.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian and wetland loss.** Cottonwood-willow forests (*Populus fremontii/Salix gooddingii*) along the mainstem reportedly declined from over 2,000 hectares (5,000 acres) in the 1600s to less than 200 hectares (500 acres). [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix* spp.), introduced in the mid-1800s, now forms homogeneous stands along significant reaches. Impoundment also limited the creation of sandbars and channel islands that native riparian plants need for propagation.

**Salinity.** The [[Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Forum]] (1990) projects that salinity below Parker Dam will rise from 640 mg/l in 1990 to over 850 mg/l by 2010. The EPA attributes the increase from 1944 to 1988 principally to out-of-basin exports (3%), irrigation (37%) and reservoir evaporation (12%).

**Delta.** Before dam construction the delta covered over 780,000 hectares (almost 2 million acres) and was estimated to support 200 to 400 plant species, along with jaguar, beaver and abundant waterfowl. Reduced silt loads now favor erosion over accretion, so the authors expect the delta area to decrease. Under the [[1944 Water Treaty]], México is allocated 10% of the lower river's base flow in non-flood years, but most is diverted to the Canal Central, so mainstem water reaches delta wetlands mainly during high flows.

**Wetland birds.** Marsh habitat supports waterbirds, shorebirds and wading birds, including the endangered [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] (*Rallus longirostris yumanensis*).

**Principal delta wetlands:**
- [[Rio Hardy Wetlands]]: fed by the Rio Hardy River, agricultural runoff and high flows.
- [[Cienaga de Santa Clara]]: sustained by agricultural runoff from the [[Wellton-Mohawk Canal]].
- El Doctor wetlands: supported by artesian springs.

**Reserve status.** The Cienaga de Santa Clara lies within the [[Reserva de la Biosfera Alto Golfo y Delta del Rio Colorado]], established by the Mexican government in 1993 and recognized by UNESCO.

## Key Findings

### Site evaluations

Nine recovery efforts were reviewed. All but one, Three-Finger Lake, used revegetation as the main strategy. Sites are numbered upstream to downstream.

| Site | Project | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | [[Ahakhav Tribal Preserve]] (150-acre revegetation, 1996 onward) | About 90% of plants survived to date; many reached almost 6 m |
| 2 | [[No Name Lake]] (17 ha, completed 1987) | About 53% of planted stock survived (1990); only trees in a low-lying erosional depression survived among cottonwoods and willows |
| 3 | [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] saline site (1986) | Early germination was high, but mortality was high later; only 3–10 mesquite per plot remained in 1997 |
| 4 | Cibola Dredge Spoil Site (1977–78, first Bureau of Reclamation revegetation experiment) | Plants on the agricultural (west) side of the levee were healthier than on the east side |
| 5 | [[Farmer's Market Bridge]] (1986) | Cottonwoods and willows showed water stress; mesquite was healthier |
| 6 | [[Three-Finger Lake]] (50 ha, due for completion 1998) | Dredging began 1994; a native fish rearing pond was created |
| 7 | [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] cottonwood-willow site (1995) | All cottonwoods survived; about 90% of willows died, likely from localized salinity |
| 8 | [[Mittry Lake]] (23 ha, 1986) | Growth was slowed by water stress and rabbit and deer damage; plantings near a leaking canal grew best |
| 9 | [[Fortuna Fish Pond]] (3.2 ha, 1985) | About 30% of trees died within two years; many later exceeded 10 m |

Three natural areas were also assessed:

- **Site 10, [[Colorado River-Gila River Confluence]]:** over 80 hectares (200 acres) of natural riparian forest that regenerated after the 1983 and 1993 floods.
- **Site 11, Rio Hardy Wetlands:** wetland area fell from 63,000 ha (1983) to 1,175 ha (1988) after a natural dam was destroyed in 1983. Flows in 1992–93 raised the area to 24,000 ha, though the authors consider this gain likely temporary.
- **Site 12, Cienaga de Santa Clara:** about 20,000 hectares, with 4,500 hectares thickly vegetated. It grew from 200 ha (1973) to about 20,000 ha after the [[Wellton-Mohawk Canal]] was completed in 1978.

### Recommendations

1. **Improve recovery efforts.** Set clear, specific objectives; consider alternatives to revegetation, such as natural regeneration; base strategies on site conditions (depth to saturated soil, salinity, exotics, use intensity); plant species adapted to current hydrology; exploit agricultural runoff and mesic low-lying sites; and fund maintenance and monitoring for at least three years.
2. **Protect existing wetlands.** Treat the Cienaga de Santa Clara, the Rio Hardy marshes and the Gila-Colorado confluence forest as short-term priorities. Rebuild the earthen dam destroyed in 1983 at the Rio Hardy.
3. **Allocate water for restoration.** The 1922 Colorado River Compact and US-México treaties do not dedicate water to ecosystems, and the river is nearly fully allocated before reaching the delta.
4. **Expand refuge revegetation.** Imperial refuge uses less than half of its 27 million m³ (22,000 acre-feet) consumptive allocation.
5. **Pursue binational restoration.** The authors argue the U.S. should share delta restoration responsibility and aid México.
6. **Use community-based approaches.** Over 23 million people have a stake in the river. A regional [[Ecotourism]] initiative and guide training at the Cienaga de Santa Clara were highlighted.
7. **Create protection zones.** Restrict vehicle traffic in marshes, since unrestricted traffic reduces waterfowl use.
8. **Modify reservoir operations.** Test flows, such as the 1996 [[Glen Canyon Dam]] flow, could create depositional bars and backwater fish breeding areas. Lake Powell's near-capacity status may increase flood flows reaching the delta.
9. **Resolve other water issues.** Address groundwater over-pumping in Mexicali, Blythe and Yuma, plus pollution and conservation.
10. **Use wastewater effectively.** Agricultural drainage and municipal effluent are growing and could sustain wetlands. Officials at a binational meeting suggested improved wastewater management could restore up to 40,500 hectares (100,000 acres) of delta wetland.

### Overall conclusions

The authors describe the river's ecological decline as reaching a "critical juncture." They identify dam releases as offering the greatest opportunity for broad recovery, since river impoundment is a major cause of decline. They also stress that short-term gains can come from wastewater use, protecting remnant habitats, improved revegetation and community-based conservation.
