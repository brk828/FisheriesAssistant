# Historic Extinctions: A Rosetta Stone for Understanding Prehistoric Extinctions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #extinction-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[Jared Diamond]] uses witnessed extinctions of the recent past as a "Rosetta Stone" for interpreting prehistoric extinctions. Its central argument is that natural extinction risk is inversely related to population size, which is itself a function of habitat area and population density, at every time scale examined. Because large species generally have lower densities, megafauna are preferentially vulnerable. The chapter then evaluates climate-based and man-based explanations of prehistoric extinction, emphasising that man's impact operates through several distinct mechanisms (overkill, habitat destruction, introduced predators, competitors, diseases and trophic cascades) whose effects vary across islands and continents. The author concludes that the key unresolved problem is explaining why extinctions struck some taxa in some places but not others.

The chapter is largely concerned with terrestrial birds and mammals. It contains no fish-specific data; fish appear only as examples of freshwater and marine exterminations and as a noted gap in extinction records.

## Background & Description

The chapter opens with the detailed, well-documented extinction of the [[Great Auk]] (*Pinguinus impennis*), which was killed through exploitation at Funk Island, Iceland, and elsewhere, with the last two birds killed at Eldey Rock on 3 June 1844. Such documentation contrasts with the limited evidence available for late Pleistocene extinctions.

Diamond frames the analysis around five time scales and community types: near-equilibrium communities over a decade, the same over many decades, communities undergoing net species loss over many decades, communities losing species since the end of the Pleistocene, and communities over geological time. Key study systems include [[Bardsey Island]] (breeding land birds, 1954–69), the [[California Channel Islands]], [[Barro Colorado Island]], [[Bass Strait]] mammals, and [[New Guinea]] land-bridge islands.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Natural extinction patterns.** On Bardsey Island (1.8 km²), 37 breeding land bird species bred at least once in 1954–69, with 24–30 breeding in any one year. Four species became regular breeders, about two disappeared, and roughly fifteen flickered in and out. Across Britain (230,737 km²) over 1900–75, 157 land bird species bred at least once; 125 bred every year and 32 did not. Extinction risk on islands declined with island area, and within communities it declined with population density.

**Habitat fragmentation.** On Barro Colorado Island (16 km², created when Gatun Lake formed around 1914), about 47 of roughly 200 known land bird species had disappeared by 1981. In Brazilian forest patches, the 1,400-ha patch lost 14% of breeding bird species, the 250-ha patch 41%, and the 21-ha patch 62%.

**Land-bridge islands.** Rising post-glacial seas isolated islands such as Britain, Tasmania, Trinidad, Borneo and Japan. Small islands lost species faster and ended with more losses than large ones. Bird populations went extinct more than ten times faster than lizard populations of the same area on the same islands.

**Differential susceptibility.** On Bass Strait, large carnivores were more extinction-prone than small carnivores, carnivores more than similar-sized herbivores, and habitat specialists more than generalists. The thylacine and Tasmanian devil survived only on Tasmania (67,900 km²).

**Endemism.** Endemism in Pacific island avifaunas increases with island area and isolation; New Zealand had the highest value cited (69%).

**Climate.** Recent climate-linked changes in Iceland and Europe produced range shifts and local flickering rather than total extinction. Examples include Iceland's breeding species changes over 1870–1970 and the decline of British Whitethroat after the 1968 Sahel drought.

**Modern extinctions.** About 63 mammal species (plus 52 subspecies) and about 88 bird species (plus 83 subspecies) have become extinct since AD 1600. Of the bird extinctions, 155 of 171 total taxa were island populations. The chapter states that no comparable estimates exist for reptiles, amphibians and fish.

## Key Findings

- **Main pattern:** An inverse relation between extinction rate and population size (proportional to area and density) holds at every time scale, and megafauna are preferentially susceptible.
- **Climate challenge:** Modern climate effects produce mostly range shifts and local losses, not total extinctions; the late Pleistocene megafaunal waves are hard to explain by climate alone because plants were not lost, poikilotherms were not hit harder, and the timing does not match glacial cycles in North America, Eurasia or Australia.
- **Man-related mechanisms:** Overkill, habitat destruction, introduced grazers and browsers, introduced predators, introduced competitors, introduced diseases and trophic cascades. Overkill caused most modern large-mammal extinctions, while habitat destruction was judged the most important modern cause.
- **Introduced predators:** Thought responsible for about half of island bird extinctions and none of continental bird extinctions. Rats and cats are the most destructive. [[Atkinson Hypothesis]] proposes that birds without co-evolved rats or land crabs were most vulnerable.
- **Overkill is multi-motivational:** Meat was only one motive; plumes, cultural items, crop protection, pet capture and sport hunting together outweighed it.
- **Hunting timescales vary widely:** The [[Stephen Island Wren]] was lost within one year of discovery, while the Great Auk endured hunting for at least 20,000 years.
- **Trophic cascades:** Examples include the puffin starvation in the North Sea after sand eel shifts and extinctions of Hawaiian plants following pollinator loss.
- **Fossil-record biases:** Genus-level analysis may underestimate or misrepresent extinction, and the minifauna is poorly sampled, especially on Madagascar and in Hawaii.
- **Future research:** Study remote islands and little-explored Pacific, Indian Ocean and Melanesian archipelagos; compare the [[New Guinea]], Bismarck and Solomon land-bridge systems; and shift from genus-level to species-level analysis.
- **Fish gap:** The chapter notes that no comparable modern extinction estimates exist for fish, and that marine extinctions have been rare, possibly because of the difficulty of finding every last individual.
