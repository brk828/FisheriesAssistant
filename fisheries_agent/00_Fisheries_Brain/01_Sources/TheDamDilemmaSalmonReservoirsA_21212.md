# The Dam Dilemma: Salmon, Reservoirs and the Case for Removing Dams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Smithsonian magazine feature, compiled with unrelated advertisements and short arts and science pieces, examines whether American dams should be removed to restore migratory fish. Its central case study is the [[Elwha River]] in Washington, where [[Elwha Dam]] and [[Glines Canyon Dam]] have cut off anadromous salmon and steelhead from their upstream spawning grounds. The article contrasts this with the [[Edwards Dam]] removal on the Kennebec River, the stalled [[Ocklawaha River]] restoration in Florida, the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] debate on the Colorado River, and the salmon crisis in the [[Columbia River]] hydrosystem. Its conclusion is that dams can no longer be assumed permanent, though removal remains contested on economic, energy and political grounds.

## Background & Description

The Elwha Dam was finished in 1912, collapsed on Halloween night of that year, and was rebuilt by 1914. The article describes it as a structure that "looks much older and yet, somehow modern," with penstocks feeding a powerhouse. It was built without fish passage, contrary to the laws of its time. Local voices include Buck Adamire, a former president of Rescue Elwha Area Lakes (REAL) who has accepted removal, and Beatrice (Aunty Bea) Charles of the [[Klallam Tribe]], who describes salmon as "our food," "our culture" and "our life."

The feature also covers the broader debate on dams. Environmentalists argue that dams disrupt seasonal river flows, alter thermal regimes, trap sediment and shrink estuaries and wetlands. Defenders, including geomorphologist Will Graf, argue that dams supply about 10 percent of US electricity and about 50 percent of renewable energy. The article notes that a quarter of US dams are at least 50 years old and nearly one in ten is rated high-hazard (citing SMITHSONIAN, April 1978).

Two inserts are unrelated to the dam theme: a feature on Jackson Pollock's retrospective at the Museum of Modern Art and a feature on sargassum communities in the Sargasso Sea, where scavengers rely on "food falls" from the surface.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pacific Northwest.** The pre-dam Elwha hosted all ten native anadromous salmon and trout species of the Pacific Northwest, which spawn in fresh water but spend most of their lives at sea. The river also had legendary 100-pound chinook. Adult returns of about 380,000 salmon and steelhead have fallen below 3,000. At least two native Elwha runs are extinct. Pat Crain, fisheries manager for the Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe, reported only 50 pink salmon counted last year, compared with 50,000 in 1977. Across California, Oregon, Washington and Idaho, 106 native salmon and trout stocks are extinct and 214 more are at risk.

**Thermal and water-quality stress.** By early August the Elwha's lower river reached 60°F, warmed by the reservoirs. Officials feared a lethal gill parasite of the genus *Dermocystidium* would proliferate, so volunteers hauled fish from the river daily to a hatchery. The article calls this a stopgap measure. The dam also leaks up to 450 gallons of water per second through its foundation.

**Columbia and Lower Snake.** Eight federal dams sit on the route of Columbia and Snake River salmon. Juvenile fish face turbines and about 400 miles of predator-filled slack water. Barging and trucking smolts past Bonneville Dam has produced dismal returns. Snake River chinook and steelhead are listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]], and sockeye were listed as endangered in 1991. Only one sockeye returned to Redfish Lake in 1994 and none in 1995. Steve Pettit of the Idaho Department of Fish and Game argues that restoring the lower river is the surest route to the 2 to 6 percent return rate needed for recovery. Barging has yielded less than 1 percent, and as little as 0.2 percent.

**Kennebec River.** [[Edwards Dam]] is 160 years old and 30 feet tall. It generates only 0.1 percent of Maine's power and blocks 17 miles of spawning habitat. Ten migratory species are affected, including alewife, shad, Atlantic salmon and snub-nosed sturgeon.

**Florida.** The [[Ocklawaha River]] is a 78-mile tributary of the St. Johns River. The reservoir behind [[Rodman Dam]] covers about 9,000 acres and is described as "a weed-choked, declining ecosystem," though it supports bass fishing.

**Colorado River.** Dams and diversions at more than 30 points mean the river reaches the sea only in especially wet years. [[Lake Powell]] (186 miles long) loses roughly one million acre-feet a year to evaporation and seepage, and serves two water users in Arizona. Recreation brings nearly three million visitors a year.

## Key Findings

- **Elwha salmon decline:** Returns fell from an estimated 380,000 adults to under 3,000, and two native runs are extinct. The dam was built without fish passage.
- **Regional losses:** 106 native salmon and trout stocks in California, Oregon, Washington and Idaho are extinct, and 214 more are at risk.
- **Dam removal as precedent:** FERC denied the license for Edwards Dam and ordered its removal, the first time the commission had taken such a step. A settlement secured $37.5 million for demolition, with other industries funding the cost.
- **Stalled projects:** Elwha removal was endorsed by the Department of the Interior but had received no funding as of the article. Ocklawaha removal, approved unanimously by the Florida Cabinet in 1992, has no money, and its federal permit expires December 31.
- **Safety and aging infrastructure:** About a quarter of US dams are at least 50 years old, and nearly one in ten is high-hazard.
- **Cost debate:** Wisconsin found removal could cost one-third to one-fifth as much as repair. Unlike nuclear plants, dams carry no decommissioning funds, raising concerns about "deadbeat dams."
- **Energy trade-off:** Dams supply about 10 percent of US electricity and about 50 percent of renewable energy, and proponents call removal economically unrealistic for hydropower-dependent regions.
- **Political resistance:** Senator Slade Gorton conditioned support for Elwha removal on guarantees that no Snake or Columbia dams would be removed without congressional approval.
- **Restoration uncertainty:** Experts disagree on what "restoration" means. Dave Wegner argues for "long-term ecological sustainability" through decommissioning Glen Canyon Dam, while Larry Tarp of Friends of Lake Powell warns of economic and ecological disaster.
- **Long-term outlook:** Hydrologists note that reservoirs will eventually silt up and rivers will reclaim their channels. Secretary of the Interior Bruce Babbitt argued that dam owners must now demonstrate by "hard facts" that continued operation serves the public interest.
