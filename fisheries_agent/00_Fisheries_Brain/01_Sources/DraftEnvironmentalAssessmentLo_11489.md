# Lower Colorado River Imperial Division Restoration and Enhancement Environmental Assessment

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #habitat-restoration #endangered-species #wetland-management

## Executive Summary

This draft environmental assessment evaluates restoration and enhancement actions for the [[Lower Colorado River]] from [[Imperial Dam]] through the [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]], covering approximately 153 backwater lakes and 6,100 acres of riparian, wetland, and aquatic habitat in Arizona and California. The document was prepared by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] [[Yuma Area Office]] in coordination with multiple state and federal agencies. Approximately 50% of productive open water aquatic and wetland resources have been lost to accelerated ecological succession following the 1983-1984 floods, which deposited sediment and isolated water bodies from critical flow. The preferred alternative proposes hydraulic dredging and amphibious excavator work to restore channels, reconnect isolated backwaters, protect riparian tree stands from wildfire, and revegetate native species. The project aims to benefit both common sportfish ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Striped Bass]], catfish) and endangered species including [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], and [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]].

## Background & Description

The [[Imperial Division]] represents a unique reach of the [[Lower Colorado River]] containing extensive backwater lake complexes and associated wetland-riparian ecosystems. Management responsibility is shared among the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[California Department of Fish and Game]], [[U.S. Bureau of Land Management]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. The [[Lower Colorado River Management Work Group]] initiated inventory efforts in 1986, leading to formation of the [[Lower Colorado River Backwater Sub-Committee]] in 1987 to prioritize restoration needs.

The project area extends from River Mile 49 to 86, encompassing both Arizona and California sides of the river. In the mid-1980s, 69 of 153 backwater lakes were open to river interchange; 84 were closed. The 1983-1984 flood event caused extensive sediment deposition that filled wetlands, blocked distribution channels, and isolated water bodies from sustaining flows.

**Aquatic Habitat Characteristics:**
- Main river channel: deep, swift-flowing
- Side channels and backwater lakes: variable depth and flow
- Shallow marshes: dominated by cattail (*Typha* spp.) and giant bulrush (*Schoenoplectus californicus*)
- Submergent vegetation: spiny naiad (*Najas marina*), milfoil (*Myriophyllum* spp.), pondweed (*Potamogeton* spp.)
- Riparian zones: cottonwood (*Populus fremontii*), willow (*Salix* spp.), mesquite (*Prosopis* spp.)

**Fish Community:**
The reach supports an important warm-water fishery. Major sportfish include [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), sunfish (Centrarchidae), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*). Forage fish include shiners, minnows, carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), and [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Successional Dynamics:**
Approximately 50% of open water surface has been lost to undesirable ecological succession toward dry terrestrial communities dominated by *Phragmites australis*, salt cedar (*Tamarix* spp.), and arrowweed (*Pluchea sericea*). Without active management intervention, these resources face permanent and irretrievable loss.

**Water Quality:**
Water in this reach exhibits typical [[Lower Colorado River]] characteristics: high salt/mineral content, low heavy metals and trace minerals, normal pH, low turbidity, and low pesticide/herbicide contamination. Some backwaters are becoming eutrophic due to lack of flow; others exhibit good water quality depending on location and hydrology. A few become anoxic in summer depending on flows and water temperature.

**Threatened and Endangered Species:**
The project area provides habitat for multiple federally listed species:

*Fish:*
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) - Endangered, with designated Critical Habitat in project area

*Birds:*
- [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] (*Rallus longirostris yumanensis*) - Endangered
- [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*) - Endangered (listed February 27, 1995)
- [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*) - winter visitor, 20-30 birds estimated along lower Colorado River
- [[Brown Pelican]] (*Pelicanus occidentalis*) - occasional vagrant
- [[Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus*) - migration visitor

Additional candidate and state-listed species include [[California Black Rail]], [[Yellow-billed Cuckoo]], [[Arizona Bell's Vireo]], and numerous others.

**Wildlife Community:**
The area serves as a major flyway for migratory waterfowl, neotropical birds, shorebirds, and marsh birds. Resident species include white-winged dove, mourning dove, Gambel's quail, jackrabbit, cottontail rabbit, coyote, muskrat, and beaver.

**Wildfire Threat:**
Recurring wildfire routinely destroys valuable native riparian tree stands. Without active fire protection, struggling stands face elimination as integral ecosystem components.

## Key Findings

**Preferred Alternative Components:**

*California Side (Imperial Dam to Ferguson Lake):*
1. **California Channel Restoration:** Dredge 3.3 miles of channel approximately 30 feet wide and 10-12 feet deep, restoring 13 acres of open water aquatic habitat and facilitating revegetation of 8 acres of mixed cattail/bulrush wetland. This would enhance and protect approximately 170 acres of mixed riparian woodland and wetland marsh.

2. **Backwater Reconnection:** Restore inflow/outflow to 10 isolated backwater lakes using amphibious excavator to open inlets/outlets approximately 10-12 feet wide and 6-8 feet deep (total 950 feet combined length), preventing eutrophication and improving water quality.

3. **Fire Protection:** Dredge two open water channels (600 feet and 1,000 feet long, 30 feet wide) as firebreaks and access routes for suppression crews, protecting existing riparian stands of native cottonwood, willow, and mesquite.

4. **Wetland Restoration:** Restore approximately 50 acres of dried wetlands using amphibious excavator to develop finger-like channels approximately 10 feet wide and 6 feet deep (8,000 feet total), reestablishing water flow.

5. **Revegetation:** Plant 1,000 mixed native cottonwood, willow, and mesquite trees in suitable areas.

*Arizona Side (Imperial Dam to Martinez Lake):*
1. **Backwater Channel System:** Restore streamflow to 10 backwater lakes via 1.5-mile interconnected channel (10 feet wide, 4-6 feet deep) with 0.5-mile dredged inflow channel (30 feet wide, 10 feet deep), restoring flow to 100 surface acres of isolated lakes and creating 1.8 acres open water plus 1.2 acres cattail/bulrush wetland.

2. **New Distribution Channel:** Dredge 2.5-mile channel adjacent to Arizona Channel (30 feet wide, 10 feet deep), reestablishing 8 acres water surface and 5.4 acres cattail/bulrush wetland, enhancing approximately 225 acres of cattail marsh through renewed water flow.

3. **Riparian Fire Protection:** Create approximately 1,600 feet of water firebreaks around 50 acres of native tree stands at River Miles 54.9 and 55.2.

4. **Wetland Enhancement:** Restore approximately 50 acres of dried wetlands via small interconnecting channels (10 feet wide, 6 feet deep).

5. **Revegetation:** Plant 1,000 mixed native trees.

*Imperial National Wildlife Refuge:*
Multiple objectives targeting restoration and enhancement of approximately 2,000 acres of wetlands and backwaters through channel construction, backwater reconnection, and water control structures at specific locations including [[Island Lake Complex]], [[Martinez Lake]], [[Ferguson Lake Complex]], [[Norton Lake]], [[Adobe Lake]], and [[Draper Lake]]. Approximately 1,000 acres of undesirable vegetation (cattails, cane, *Phragmites*, salt cedar, arrowweed) would be removed.

**Alternative Analyses:**
- **Alternative One:** Focuses on restoring historical Arizona Channel alignment rather than creating new parallel channel; uses smaller openings for backwater connections
- **Alternative Two:** Completes all work with amphibious excavator only (10-12 feet wide, 8-10 feet deep channels); no hydraulic dredging
- **No Action Alternative:** Continued habitat degradation, restricted recreational access, no fire protection, continued wetland decline

**Environmental Consequences:**

*Positive Impacts:*
- Substantial improvement in water quality through restored circulation
- Prevention of eutrophication in isolated backwaters
- Enhanced habitat diversity for entire biotic community
- Significant benefit to endangered [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]
- Improved warm-water fishery for sportfish populations
- Enhanced recreational opportunities (boating, fishing, wildlife viewing)
- Protection of irreplaceable native riparian communities

*Negative Impacts:*
- Temporary turbidity increase during dredging operations (short-term, localized)
- Minor temporary disturbance/displacement of wildlife during construction
- Potential minor impacts to macro-invertebrates in localized dredge areas
- Increased recreational use may disturb some wildlife species (manageable through no-wake zones and enforcement)

**Project Implementation:**
Completion expected over 2-4 years depending on equipment performance, weather, river flows, and substrate conditions. Excavator work most efficient during low-water winter months; dredge operable at any normal water level. Bureau of Reclamation can fund 50% of project cost; additional 50% must be acquired from other sources. Arizona Water Protection Fund grant received for Arizona side work from Martinez Lake to Imperial Dam.

**Water Budget:**
Detailed water budget analysis provided in Appendix D (not included in document text).

**Cultural Resources:**
Cultural resources inventory completed (Appendix C). No land disturbance anticipated outside floodplain. Protocol established: if cultural resources discovered during implementation, all ground-disturbing activity stops until mitigation measures developed in consultation with State Historic Preservation Officer.

**Consultation:**
Extensive interagency coordination through [[Lower Colorado River Management Work Group]] and [[Lower Colorado River Backwater Sub-Committee]]. Public meetings held March 27, 1996 and June 5, 1996 in Yuma. Biological Assessments prepared for [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] with favorable biological opinions from [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]; additional Section 7 consultation required for modified Arizona work and refuge activities.

**Cumulative Impacts:**
No significant adverse cumulative impacts identified. Long-term benefits to fish, wildlife, water quality, and recreation far outweigh short-term construction disturbances. Project represents coordinated interagency effort to restore, enhance, and protect critical [[Lower Colorado River]] ecosystem components facing irreversible loss without intervention.
