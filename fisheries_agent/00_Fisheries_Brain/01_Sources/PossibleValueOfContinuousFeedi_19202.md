# Continuous Feeding of Medicated Dry Diets to Control Furunculosis in Hatchery Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper by [[Edward F. Grassl]] of the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]] (Michigan Department of Conservation) and [[Michigan State University]] tests whether continuous feeding of a mild medicament can control [[Furunculosis]], a bacterial disease caused by [[Aeromonas Salmonicida]] in hatchery trout. The hypothesis is that reducing low-pathogenicity intestinal organisms with a continuously fed drug could prevent serious outbreaks indirectly. The author tested [[Aureomycin]] (chlortetracycline) in a pelleted dry diet. In a single natural outbreak in 2-year-old [[Brook Trout]] at the [[Wolf Lake Hatchery]], medicated fish survived at markedly higher rates than untreated fish, and their eggs hatched at higher rates. The author describes the work as preliminary because it rests on one experiment and two groups.

## Background & Description

- **Disease problem:** Furunculosis has caused serious epidemics wherever trout are reared under hatchery conditions. [[McCraw]] (1952) described it as the disease most feared by hatcherymen.
- **Species susceptibility:** In Michigan, [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) are the most susceptible, followed by [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*). Many other fish species contract the disease, but it reaches epidemic status in them less often.
- **Conventional treatment:** Feeding a mixture of [[Sulfaguanidine]] and [[Sulfamerazine]] with the daily ration. Recurrent outbreaks often require successively higher drug levels, which may select for resistant pathogen strains and risk harming the host.
- **Proposed alternative:** Mild medicaments fed continuously might reduce the number of low-pathogenicity organisms in the intestinal tract, so that "cleaning up" the gut could be enough to prevent outbreaks.
- **Rationale for drug choice:** Work with warm-blooded animals showed that some [[Antibiotic Feeding|antibiotics]] can be fed continuously. They differ in structure and activity, vary in spectrum, mix compatibly with other diet ingredients, and are essentially nontoxic even in excess. Proposed benefits include suppressing subclinical disease organisms, reducing competition for dietary vitamins (Jukes 1955), and improving intestinal absorption.
- **Diet form:** Michigan had recently adopted a pelleted trout food made from animal and vegetable meals. Medicated pellets can be stored for several months at each hatchery, drugs mix more uniformly in dry diets, and daily mixing is eliminated, which gives better control of the amount of drug fed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pathogen persistence:** The author suspects *Aeromonas salmonicida* is constantly present in hatchery water supplies where repeated outbreaks occur, although trout can still be reared in such waters. Outbreaks may depend on fluctuating host resistance, environmental conditions, and intercurrent subclinical infections.
- **Natural versus artificial infection:** The author argues that injecting pathogens to create an epidemic gives less valid results than infection from endemic contamination, so the trial waited for a natural outbreak.
- **Rearing conditions:** The affected brook trout had been reared since hatching in concrete tanks on an all-dry-meal diet.
- **Spawning temperature:** Eggs were incubated in water at 42°F.

## Key Findings

**Prior feeding trials with antibiotics had mostly been negative:**
- A 1953 six-month trial of aureomycin at 200 g per ton (a 1% level of a supplement containing 10 g chlortetracycline per pound) in rainbow and brook trout fingerlings showed no attributable advantage or disadvantage.
- Schumacher (1955) found no statistically significant effect of aureomycin on the growth of brown trout fingerlings (chi-square test).
- Phillips et al. (1952) concluded that [[Aureomycin]], [[Terramycin]], [[Penicillin]], and [[Bacitracin]] had no beneficial effect on growth, conversion, or mortality in brown trout. Phillips also found that aureomycin depressed growth in rainbow trout in one test.
- Wagner (1954) reported that rainbow trout lost weight during the first 2 weeks of feeding penicillin or aureomycin.
- Snieszko (1952) found aureomycin ineffective against furunculosis in brook trout injected with *Aeromonas salmonicida*, although it inhibited the pathogen in vitro less strongly than [[Chloramphenicol]] or terramycin.

**Natural outbreak trial at Wolf Lake (September 1955 to March 1956):**
- On September 3, 1955, furunculosis was detected in a group of 2-year-old brook trout. The group was split: 106 fish stayed in the original tank and received the medicated diet, and the other half went to an adjacent tank on the nonmedicated diet.
- The medicated diet contained 200 g aureomycin per ton of feed. Both groups were fed at 1.7% of body weight per day.
- The medicated group had received aureomycin continuously for 2 months when spawning occurred.
- Survival in the medicated group was 83% on November 18, 1955, versus 58% in the nonmedicated group. On February 29, 1956, survival was 57% versus 21%.
- Egg hatch was 58% for eggs from medicated fish versus 23% for eggs from fish on the regular diet.

**Interpretation and limits stated by the author:**
- The only known dietary difference was aureomycin, so the author concludes that it likely aided recovery of already-infected fish and may have prevented infection in others.
- Some mortality was attributed to rough handling during spawning. Both groups were handled similarly, so the author judged this unlikely to explain the survival difference.
- Egg production and fertilization occurred in fish with furunculosis, and two months of medication before spawning did not impair fertilization or hatchability.
- The work is preliminary: it rests on one experiment and two groups. The author recommends feeding trials with other continuously fed antibiotics before making recommendations.
