# Razorback Sucker in the Upper Colorado River Basin, 1974–1976

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service technical paper by Charles W. McAda and Richard S. Wydoski reports a field study (August 1974 to November 1976) of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species has declined largely because of dam construction and other river alterations, and it was proposed for listing as "threatened." The study nonetheless found relatively large concentrations at two restricted sites: the mouth of the [[Yampa River]] and a flooded gravel pit on the [[Colorado River]] near Grand Junction, Colorado. The authors examined distribution, age and growth, fecundity, spawning, movement and food, and assessed the species' future. They conclude that the Yampa River is critical to the survival of the species and other native fishes, and that reproductive failure is a major factor in the decline.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The razorback sucker is one of four endemic Colorado River fishes of concern, along with the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] were listed as endangered in 1973. In 1978 the bonytail was proposed as endangered and the razorback sucker as threatened.
- **Endemism:** About 74% of the native fish species of the Colorado River basin are endemic (Miller 1958). Most native large-river fishes have been eliminated from the lower basin, but all endemic species of the upper and lower basin still occur in the upper basin.
- **Identification:** The last ray of the anal fin is curved in mature males and straight in mature females. Young razorback suckers closely resemble [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] juveniles, which complicates identification.
- **Study objectives:** The authors aimed to (1) determine distribution and relative abundance of the endangered species and others; (2) determine movements, especially spawning migrations from the upper Green River into the Yampa River; (3) delineate spawning areas and their ecological requirements; and (4) summarize the species' life history.
- **Sampling:** Fish were collected with trammel nets, seines, electrofishing and [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]]. Endangered or threatened fish were released after capture, except a few that died from injuries. Dead razorback suckers were frozen and sent to Utah State University for enzyme analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dam effects:** Dams for hydroelectric power and irrigation have changed the river environment in three main ways: seasonal streamflow fluctuations decreased, daily fluctuations increased, and summer water temperatures dropped while winter temperatures rose. Turbidity was also reduced. These changes are believed to be major factors in the species' decline. Water development in the upper basin is expected to increase with energy extraction (oil, oil shale, coal and uranium).
- **Exotic species:** Holden and Stalnaker (1975a) recorded 10 native and 19 introduced fish species in the main stem of the upper Colorado River. Competition for food and space with exotics may be a major factor in the decline of natives.
- **Study area:** The primary sites were [[Echo Park]] (station 3), at the Yampa–Green confluence, and the [[Walker Wildlife Area]] (station 8). The Yampa flows through narrow canyons with deep, fast sections. The Green River reach has low water velocity and shallow, sand- or silt-bottomed channels. Riparian vegetation includes box elder, cottonwoods, willows and tamarisk. The region is arid, with average annual precipitation of 25 cm. Water temperatures ranged from 0 °C in winter to 30 °C in late summer.
- **Walker Wildlife Area:** A flooded gravel pit of 25.6 ha, 0.5–2.0 m deep, connected to the Colorado River by a 6-m canal. It has no aquatic vegetation and a bottom of gravel under thick silt. The adjacent Colorado River is 80–120 m wide, and irrigation withdrawals substantially reduce summer flow there.

## Key Findings

### Distribution and Abundance
- The species' range has been markedly reduced. Early observers reported it as abundant in the lower basin, including the Salton Sea and Lake Mead.
- A commercial fishery in Arizona harvested about 5.4 metric tons from Sahuaro Lake in 1949. No razorback suckers were found when the lake was drained in 1966.
- The species has apparently disappeared from the Gila River arm of the lower basin. Lakes Havasu, Mead and Mohave still hold only mature fish, indicating that reproduction has been greatly curtailed or absent.
- In the upper basin the species is more widespread but has never been reported as abundant.
- Within the present study, the species was most common, though never numerous, at two locations: the Yampa mouth at station 3 in early spring, and the Colorado River at station 8 throughout the year. Occasional fish were also taken at stations 4 and 7.
- A total of 23 razorback suckers were collected at station 3 during a 3-week period in May 1975. In the spring 1975 trammel net catch, seven razorback suckers made up 4.3% of the catch (25 April to 22 May).
- No razorback suckers were collected in the Yampa upstream of station 3 except a single fish at station 1 in July 1979, reported by Colorado State University investigators.

### Age and Growth
- Scale analysis indicated that all collected fish were mature and had completed 4 to 9 growing seasons.
- Colorado River razorback suckers were significantly larger than Green and Yampa fish of the same estimated age. The authors attribute this probably to higher water temperatures in the Colorado.
- Among Yampa fish that had completed five growing seasons, females were significantly larger than males.
- In 1975, fish aged 4 and 6 years dominated samples in both rivers (n = 20 and n = 35, respectively).
- Ages are uncertain. The annuli were hard to read, the method was not validated for this species, and no small fish were available for length-frequency checks. One tagged fish grew little in 1.5 years, and another grew only 8 mm in 3.5 years, possibly because of tag effects. Two California fish estimated from otoliths were 22 and 17 years old.

### Length–Weight Relationship
- The relationship was described by a logarithmic equation (log W = b₁ log TL − b₀).
- Sexes were pooled because their length–weight relationships did not differ significantly. The two river systems also did not differ significantly, though Colorado fish tended to be longer and heavier than Yampa fish of the same estimated age.

### Reproduction and Fecundity
- Fecundity was estimated gravimetrically from 10 preserved specimens, mostly collected in 1964 in the Green River in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Sampling error was 3–4%.
- Estimated egg counts ranged from 27,614 to 76,576 per female. Two ovaries were actually counted: 24,490 eggs (fish 529 mm) and 44,624 eggs.
- Fecundity varied widely and was not significantly related to total length.
- Spawning occurred in April and May, at water temperatures of 6–15 °C in the study. Earlier reports cited water temperatures of 12–18 °C in [[Lake Mead]] and 14–18 °C in [[Lake Mohave]].
- Before the large dams, razorback suckers made extensive spring spawning migrations into tributaries. After impoundment, they spawned in reservoirs, where reproduction appeared poor.
- In the Yampa, a gravel bar about 400 m upstream of the mouth held 14 spawning fish (2 females and 12 males) captured over two weeks in May 1975. Water was about 1 m deep, with cobble substrate (20–50 mm) and a velocity of about 1 m/s.
- At the Walker pit, two ripe females and five ripe males were captured in one overnight set. Water was near 17 °C with about 100 JTU turbidity. No native fish reproduction was found there in 1975, possibly because of introduced predators such as [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]] and green sunfish.
- Reproductive failure is thought to be a major cause of decline. Subadults were rarely found: P. B. Holden collected a few, and two juvenile catostomids of uncertain identity were taken in 1977.

### Movement
- **Ultrasonic telemetry:** Transmitters (64 mm long, 16 mm diameter, 19.3 g) were implanted in one female and seven males. Tracking range was about 150 m in quiet water but only 10 m in turbulent water. Tracking of the 1975 Yampa fish largely failed due to equipment and turbulence problems.
- Of the Yampa-tagged fish, none moved more than 1 km upstream before returning to the mouth. Fish were generally found in quiet water, and those that moved were generally heading downstream when contact was lost.
- In the Walker pit, one tagged female stayed mostly in the 3-m deep end. Males and females moved independently.
- **Anchor tags:** 11 of 98 anchor-tagged fish were recaptured. A female tagged at station 4 in April 1975 was recaptured two weeks later 21 km upstream at the Yampa mouth, ripe and probably spawning. Another was recaptured 26 km upstream in a backwater flooded by irrigation flows. A third, tagged at station 7, was recaptured 3.6 years later about 130 km upstream.
- Vanicek (1967) recaptured 4 of 18 tagged fish, none more than 1.6 km from the release point, all moving downstream.
- The authors note that the Walker recaptures suggest fish remain in quiet water for extended periods.

### Food
- No quantitative food data were collected. Earlier studies reported midge larvae and algae (Jones and Sumner 1954), Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera and Diptera larvae with algae and plant debris (Banks 1964), and mud with chironomid larvae (Vanicek 1967).
- In lower-basin reservoirs, the species consumed large amounts of plankton.
- Long gill rakers suggest filter-feeding on plankton, consistent with the species' preference for quiet backwaters. No plankton data exist for the large upper-basin rivers.

### Hybridization
- Three catostomid hybrids were recently reported: flannelmouth sucker × razorback sucker, flannelmouth sucker × white sucker, and bluehead sucker × white sucker. An older bluehead sucker × flannelmouth sucker hybrid was also recorded.
- In this study, only the flannelmouth × razorback hybrid was found: three at station 8 (versus 66 razorback suckers) and five at station 3 (versus 29 razorback suckers).
- Holden and Stalnaker (1975b) reported hybrids at 7% of the razorback sucker count (as given in the OCR text). The authors suggest hybridization may be increasing in altered systems where the two species share spawning habitat, which they do.
- Hybridization of the other crosses has resulted directly from river alteration and the introduction of the white sucker.

### Future and Conservation Outlook
- The Yampa River is the largest free-flowing river remaining in the Colorado basin. It warms the cold water released from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], and its influence is considered vital to native fish downstream on the Green River.
- Roughly one-fourth of the basin's river length is inundated by reservoirs, and most remaining reaches are affected by reservoir releases.
- The Green River from the Yampa confluence to Canyonlands National Park holds most of the remaining large-river habitat in the basin. Changes in temperature or streamflow upstream could significantly reduce that habitat.
- The decline of the species is well correlated with basin development, but the specific causes are unclear. Sections of the Green and Yampa remain relatively unaltered yet hold few razorback suckers, while other natives are still abundant.
- The closely related [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], which appears to have similar spawning requirements, is still doing well. This makes the cause of reproductive failure in the razorback sucker difficult to determine.
- Modifications to the intake penstocks of Flaming Gorge Dam in June 1978 were made to provide warmer water for trout. The authors suggest that warmer water may also benefit the rare native fish.
