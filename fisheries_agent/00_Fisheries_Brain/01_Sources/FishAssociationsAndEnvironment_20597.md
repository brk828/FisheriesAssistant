# Thyroxine Concentration as an Indicator of the Imprinting Critical Period in Razorback Suckers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This Eastern Washington University technical report (Scholz, White, Horton and Koehler, July 1992) measured [[Thyroxine]] (T4) in [[Razorback Sucker]] eggs and larvae to test whether a thyroid hormone surge marks a [[Critical Period]] for [[Olfactory Imprinting]]. The work was funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service under Cooperative Agreement No. 2-FC-40-11830. Eggs were collected from the Green River, Utah, and reared at [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]]. T4 was measured in 550 eggs or larvae by [[Radioimmunoassay]] from fertilization through 45 days post-fertilization (dpf).

T4 peaked twice: at 19.9 ng/g body weight just before hatching (day 6) and at 32.5 ng/g at swim-up (day 11). The authors conclude that, if T4 is an accurate guide to imprinting timing, the critical period for this endangered species falls at hatching or swim-up, before larvae drift downstream from natal spawning sites. This timing has direct implications for [[Hatchery Supplementation]] and for the recovery of depleted [[Colorado River Basin]] stocks.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It has been extirpated from most of its original range and is now confined mainly to the upper basin above [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The species is listed as endangered by Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada and Utah, and under the U.S. Federal Endangered Species Act of 1973. Adults can live about 40 years (Minckley 1983).

Population declines have been attributed to predation and competition from introduced [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Northern Pike]], and to dam construction that destroyed habitat, blocked migrations, altered temperature and flow, and dewatered backwater nursery areas. Lanigan and Tyus (1989) estimated the upper Green and Yampa River population at 948 adults (95% CI 758 to 1,138). Few fish were captured in the lower Green River, so no reliable estimate was possible there. Juveniles (>20 to 40 mm) were not captured in the Green River despite sampling from 1979 to 1986, and most fish alive there were born before [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closed in 1962.

Razorback suckers show repeat homing to selected spawning sites and travel long distances to reach them. The authors propose that eggs or larvae imprint to site-specific odors and later use them to home, as shown in salmonids. Eggs are adhesive and broadcast over cobble, while swim-up larvae drift downstream within a few days of hatching. Imprinting, if it occurs, must therefore happen during the egg or early larval stages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning and nursery habitat.** Two spawning areas were identified on the Green and Yampa Rivers. Spawning occurs near the peak discharge of late May and June. Tyus (1985) hypothesized that groundwater seeps from sandstone and limestone formations supply the odor cues needed for [[Natal Homing]]. Spawning fish congregated at seep-associated sites and not at similar, seep-free sites.

**Habitat disruption.** Flaming Gorge Dam is thought to have lowered Green River water temperatures and caused backwater sloughs to dewater, both of which may reduce egg and larval survival. Exotic predators concentrate at spawning and rearing areas. Feeding analyses found no larval or juvenile razorback suckers in 575 channel catfish or 123 northern pike, so predation may be real but unproven as a recruitment bottleneck.

**Movement and fidelity.** A [[Mark-Recapture]] study by Tyus and Karp (1990) captured 230 fish from 1987 to 1989, 84% of them ripe. Of 39 fish recaptured in later years, 33 (85%) were found at their original marking site. Radiotracked fish moved 52.8 to 190.4 km to reach spawning sites, passing other suitable areas. One male captured in 1981 was recaptured at the same Yampa site in 1988 after a 52.8 km downstream movement.

**Hatchery role.** [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] and the Dexter, New Mexico hatchery propagate razorback suckers for stocking into [[Colorado River Basin]] tributaries. Hatchery-reared fish may be genetic clones of a few parents, so mixing them with wild fish could reduce genetic variability.

## Key Findings

**Methods.** Eggs were collected on May 27 or 28, 1991 (the abstract gives May 27 and the methods and results give May 28). Eggs and larvae were reared at a constant 21.1 ± 1.0°C in [[Heath Tray Incubator]] stacks, then moved to troughs at swim-up. Twenty-five fish were sampled daily from 1 to 18 dpf and then every 7 days to 45 dpf, between 0830 and 0930 h to control for diel T4 variation. Fish were weighed, frozen at -80°C, and extracted in ethanol with propylthiouracil. T4 was measured in duplicate using a commercial coated-tube RIA kit (Diagnostic Products, Inc.), with a standard curve of 0 to 24 ng/dl.

**T4 profile (mean ± SEM, ng/g body weight).**
- Early eggs (days 1 to 4): 4.4 ± 0.7 to 11.1 ± 2.5
- Hatch surge (day 6, just before hatch): peak 19.9 ± 7.1
- Post-hatch trough (day 7 larvae): 6.7 ± 1.6
- Swim-up surge (day 11, larvae): peak 32.5 ± 8.0
- Day 15 larvae: 23.0 ± 5.1
- Day 18 larvae: 11.2
- Days 24 to 45 larvae: 3.2 ± 0.5 to 8.8
- Unfertilized eggs (15 eggs, June 10, 1991): 1.5 ± 0.1

**Magnitude of surges.** The hatch surge was about 2 to 4 times (200 to 400%) the basal early-egg level. The swim-up surge was about 4 to 6 times (400 to 600%) the level in recently hatched larvae. The highest values occurred near complete yolk-sac absorption.

**Assay quality.** Blind quality-control samples at low, medium and high concentrations (actual 2.3, 7.3 and 11.5 ng/dl) measured 2.1 ± 0.1, 8.0 ± 0.6 and 12.5 ± 1.2 ng/dl, all within the manufacturer's acceptable ranges. Non-specific binding was 341 ± 22 cpm against 56,427 ± 306 cpm total counts. Mean percent error across 275 duplicate samples was 8.9 ± 1.4% (range 0 to 32.5%), consistent with the 8.8% pipetting error. The authors argue that the 200 to 600% surges far exceed assay error. Their suspected error sources were the viscosity of ethanol resuspension and pipetting. (Figure 3 reports 109 duplicate pairs, a different count from the 275 in the text.)

**Main conclusion.** Developing razorback suckers show thyroid surges at hatching and at swim-up. If T4 tracks the imprinting window, as it does in salmonids, the critical period is at hatching or swim-up. This is before larvae drift from the spawning site, and it argues that eggs fertilized and incubated in a hatchery could imprint to the hatchery water supply.

**Supporting literature reviewed.**
- In coho salmon and steelhead, exposure to morpholine or phenethyl alcohol during the smolt stage (after a thyroid surge) produced imprinting. Exposure at the basal pre-smolt stage did not. Injecting thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) in February advanced imprinting.
- Thyroid hormone binds neuronal nuclear receptors and is thought to drive [[Nerve Growth Factor]] expression, which builds the neural wiring that stores olfactory memory.
- Lanier (1987) found more neuronal proliferation during the steelhead [[Thyroid Hormone Surge]] than before it.
- Birds and rats show comparable thyroid-linked sensitive periods for neural development and learning.

**Recommended hatchery strategies.** The authors propose three options for combining hatchery culture with imprinting:
1. Fertilize and incubate eggs in situ at spawning sites through swim-up, then move them to a hatchery. Juveniles stocked nearby could relocate the site.
2. Transfer fertilized eggs to an "adopted" site with a distinctive water source (such as a seep or tributary), suitable spawning substrate and a downstream nursery. Incubate them there through swim-up, then release them or move them to a hatchery.
3. Expose hatchery-reared eggs to synthetic odorants from egg to swim-up, then stock them into a river site and meter the same chemicals into the water to establish a natural spawning run.

Options 2 and 3 could also reduce exotic predation and keep hatchery fish separate from wild stock.

**Caveats noted in the text.** The work is a single-season study (1991 annual report) with one egg collection, and it measures hormone levels rather than imprinting directly. The timing of imprinting is inferred from the T4 surge pattern and from salmonid studies. The Methods section gives 25 fish per lot, while the results and abstract describe 550 fish total, with some inconsistency in the collection date and swim-up timing (day 10 to 11 in the introduction, day 11 to 12 in the results).
