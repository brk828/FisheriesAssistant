# Fishes of the Upper Colorado River System: Present and Future

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This proceedings volume, published by the [[Western Division American Fisheries Society]] in December 1982, compiles papers from a 1981 symposium on the rare and endangered fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The contributions cover fish distribution and status, energy and water development impacts, mitigation under the [[Endangered Species Act]], recovery planning, and life histories of the four main endangered "large-river endemics": [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The overarching message is that water development is outpacing the knowledge and legal tools needed to protect these fishes, and that compromise, coordinated recovery and long-term commitment are essential.

## Background & Description

The volume was edited by William H. Miller, Harold M. Tyus and Clarence A. Carlson, and was prepared from a symposium held at the annual meeting of the American Fisheries Society in Albuquerque, New Mexico, on 18 September 1981. Sponsors included the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and the Western Division of the American Fisheries Society. Some 30 participants contributed, drawn from federal agencies, state wildlife departments and universities.

The Colorado River Basin is predominantly arid to semiarid. The Upper Basin is divided into the Green, Upper Main-stem and San Juan-Colorado sub-basins. The basin has about 16 contributing reservoirs above 1,200 surface hectares, and the Colorado River Storage Project created large reservoirs including [[Lake Powell]], [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] and [[Blue Mesa Reservoir]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish fauna (Tyus et al.):** A total of 66 fishes (63 species and 3 subspecies, per the abstract) are reported from the Upper Basin. Of these, 49 are primarily riverine and 6 are primarily reservoir-restricted. The abstract lists 42 exotic, 18 native, 9 endemic, 1 threatened and 5 endangered fishes. Common exotics include [[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] in reservoirs.
- **Habitat:** Large-river endemics prefer deep, swift runs over rock or gravel, backwaters and eddies. Juveniles use slow backwaters; adult squawfish move seasonally between runs, backwaters and deep holes.
- **Hydrology:** Natural flow at Lee Ferry averaged slightly under 15 million acre-feet (MAF) annually over 80 years, ranging from 6 to 24 MAF. Present use is about 25% of natural flow, with projections of about 40% depletion by 2000 under the Upper Basin development scenario.
- **Impacts:** Dams, flow regime alteration, temperature change, channelization, gravel mining, roads, power plants, oil shale, tar sands and synfuel plants are identified as major pressures. Basin population is projected to reach 3.5 million by 2020.
- **Water depletion:** About 3,963,000 acre-feet had been depleted by 1980 from 226 identified projects, with potential for a further 2,900,000 acre-feet.

## Key Findings

**Fish status and distribution**
- Four endangered endemics (Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub, razorback sucker) persist only as remnant populations in mainstem rivers. Endemic headwater taxa include the Kendall Warm Springs dace and Colorado River cutthroat trout.
- Endangered species appear to be declining further, and no sub-population is increasing. The razorback sucker was recommended for federal protection as threatened or endangered.
- Non-native fishes, flow reductions and habitat loss were the main factors in declines. Native fishes were once abundant in the Gunnison and San Juan rivers.

**Regulatory and mitigation approach**
- Section 7 of the ESA requires federal agencies to prevent jeopardy, not merely reduce harm. Traditional mitigation was judged inadequate for endangered species.
- A Conservation Plan proposed by FWS would allow projects to proceed in exchange for financial assessments based on water depleted. Examples include Windy Gap ($660,000), Moon Lake (up to $1,000,000 in one listing) and Cheyenne Water Project Phase II (up to $180,000).
- Prewitt and Stalnaker proposed a structured analytic approach, combining PHABSIM habitat simulation, temperature and water-quality modelling, sediment modelling, a macro water-budget model and legal review.

**Recovery and species accounts**
- Colorado Squawfish: recovery plan goals are self-sustaining populations sustained by instream flow. Historic declines followed dam closure and non-native fish competition. Adults use runs and backwaters; spawning migrations of up to about 146 km were recorded. Reproductive success appears linked to spring temperature and flow.
- Humpback Chub: four apparently self-sustaining populations were identified, in Black Rocks, Yampa Canyon, Westwater Canyon and Little Colorado River. The population in the Green River near Desolation Canyon had declined sharply.
- Bonytail Chub: low numbers, a trend toward extinction, and no evidence of natural recruitment in the Upper Basin. Hatchery propagation and reintroduction were recommended.
- Razorback Sucker: reproduction has failed for unknown reasons. Artificial propagation is feasible, and formation of a recovery team was urged.

**Policy conclusions**
- Recovery requires a coordinated long-term commitment. Recovery teams are constrained by budgets, political opposition and water-use priorities.
- Behnke proposed an independent agency, funded by a levy on benefits from environmental change such as hydropower, to oversee recovery and remain insulated from political cycles.
- Miller's closing remarks outlined four options for the Conservation Plan: optimum populations through flow requirements; concentrating on current strongholds; key-area habitat with hatchery stocking; and a full hatchery approach. He concluded that only the humpback chub could still maintain a viable population in the altered river.
