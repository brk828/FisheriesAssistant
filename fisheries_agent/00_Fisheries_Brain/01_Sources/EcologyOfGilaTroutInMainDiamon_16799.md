# Ecology of Gila Trout in Main Diamond Creek, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife Technical Paper (Technical Paper 5), by Danny M. Regan of the [[Colorado Cooperative Fishery Unit]], reports field and laboratory work on [[Gila Trout]] (*[[Salmo Gilae]]*) in a 2.5-mile section of [[Main Diamond Creek]] in New Mexico. Fieldwork ran from June to November 1962, with additional work in 1963. The study estimated a population of about 4,300 fish, documented growth, condition, reproduction, diet, and morphology, and assessed the stream's physical, chemical, and biological characters.

The species was once widespread in the [[Gila River Drainage]] but now survives as pure strains in only three isolated headwater streams. The author concluded that its survival in New Mexico is "at best precarious," citing slow growth, low fecundity, a marginal habitat, and a summer decline in condition. The paper was written to provide a baseline for management and preservation of the fish as a sport fishery resource and a unique species.

## Background & Description

Gila trout were described from specimens collected at Main Diamond Creek on July 18, 1939 (Miller, 1950). Their defining features are very fine, profuse spotting on the dorsal and caudal fins, spotting largely restricted above the lateral line, and an unusually large, well-spotted [[Adipose Fin]]. In life the body is deep golden yellow with a yellowish "cutthroat" mark. The species shares morphological resemblances with [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]], and the author suggested that hybridization between ancestral parents was possible.

Range contraction was attributed to overfishing, climate change, introduction of exotic fishes, and overgrazing, but before this study little was known about the species' ecology. Past artificial propagation attempts failed. The [[New Mexico Department Of Game And Fish]] was attempting to rear the fish at the [[Glenwood Hatchery]], with plans to rehabilitate former habitat and reestablish the species if successful.

**Study area.** Main Diamond Creek (T.11S., R.10W.) lies in the northwest part of Sierra County, in the [[Black Range]]. The stream is roughly 7 feet wide and 4 inches deep on average in the study section. [[Stream Improvement Structures]] installed in 1931 created pools where fish congregate. The stream is formed by permanent springs and seasonal runoff, rising near Diamond Peak at about 8,500 feet and descending to about 5,900 feet at its confluence with the East Fork of the Gila River. Under the [[Stream Classification]] system of Kuehne (1962), it is an order 1 stream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and climate.** Stream flow in the study section ranged from 175 to 423 gallons per minute. Flow fell from 225 gpm in June to 175 gpm in August, then reached its highest recorded value, 423 gpm, on October 30, 1962. Water temperature ranged from 43°F to 60°F. The stream goes underground about half a mile below the study area and has no surface flow, apart from occasional runoff, for roughly 12 miles. This dry reach blocks upstream migration of fish from lower reaches.

**Chemistry.** Dissolved oxygen ranged from 6.6 to 8.8 ppm, alkalinity from 26 to 31 ppm, and total hardness held constant at 34 ppm. No free carbon dioxide or phenolphthalein alkalinity was detected. The author found no chemical differences that would contribute to species survival. Values were comparable to other tributaries at the same altitude.

**Vegetation and land use.** Vegetation grades from high-elevation Douglas-fir, Engelmann spruce, and aspen to pinyon pine, juniper, and ponderosa pine at lower elevations. After stream closure to fishing and grazing reductions with fencing, the watershed is in good condition and stream-bank vegetation is dense.

**Biota.** Gila trout are the only fish in the upper study section. Below the natural barrier, the fauna includes rainbow trout, white sucker (*[[Catostomus Commersoni]]*), Gila sucker (*[[Pantosteus Clarki]]*), longfin dace (*[[Agosia Chrysogaster]]*), and speckled dace (*[[Rhinichthys Osculus]]*).

**Distribution.** Gila trout were once abundant in the Gila River system, with catches at about one fish per minute in Gilita and Willow creeks. Pure populations now persist in Diamond, McKenna, and Spruce creeks. Isolation by barriers and the New Mexico policy against stocking exotics in certain streams have helped survival. Mixed populations with rainbow and cutthroat trout occur in several other waters, and hybrids occur in Arizona streams.

## Key Findings

**Population and status**
- Estimated population: about 4,300 fish longer than 50 mm total length, based on sampling of 1,600 feet of stream, June 7 to 11, 1962. Young of the year were excluded.
- Age groups I through VI were represented.
- Electrofishing efficiency was high: at station 1, 95% of fish were caught on the first pass; at station 2, 96%.

**Growth and age**
- 525 scale samples were taken, and 337 (64%) yielded usable, non-regenerated scales.
- Of scale readings, 86% were verified by a second reading and 14% needed a third; 8 fish were rejected for lack of agreement.
- Males (50) and females (48) showed no significant difference in length-weight regression (95% confidence), so their data were combined.
- Mean length increases between collections: age group I, 23 mm; age group II, 7 mm; age group III, 5 mm. Age groups IV, V, and VI increased by 4, 6, and 2 mm, respectively, between June 7 and August 16.
- Growth was much slower than for three other trout species in the Gila River drainage. The author attributed this to environmental rather than genetic factors. After acclimatization at a hatchery, Gila trout grew at about the rate of rainbow trout.

**Length-weight and condition**
- Combined length-weight relation: log W = −5.27816 + 3.14009 log L (r = 0.98).
- Condition factor K(TL) declined steadily from 1.10 in June to 0.94 in November. The author linked this decline to competition for food and space during low flow.

**Body-scale relations**
- Analyses used 361 scale samples (June 7, 1962, to June 23, 1963).
- Linear and curvilinear models explained 64% to 79% of regression variance, which was not considered adequate for [[Back-Calculation]] of body length. None of the seven relations evaluated met this standard.
- High scale regeneration, likely from abrasion during low flow, and scale variability were the main obstacles.

**Reproduction**
- Fecundity was estimated from two wild females (ova counts of 196 and 96) and 13 hatchery fish (averaging 150 eggs per female), at total lengths of 185 to 270 mm.
- Average fecundity was fewer than 200 eggs per female. This compares with 200 to 600 eggs for Arizona native trout and roughly 2,400 (rainbow) and 1,577 (cutthroat) eggs for comparable-sized fish. The author suggested low fecundity may reflect hybrid ancestry.

**Diet and bottom fauna**
- 63 stomachs (20 in June, 20 in August, 13 in October and November) were analyzed. Fish measured 47 to 168 mm.
- June: [[Diptera]] (adults) were dominant, at 37% of number, 28% of volume, and 80% occurrence.
- August: [[Trichoptera]] (31% of number, 47% of volume) and [[Ephemeroptera]] (35% of number, 22% of volume) dominated.
- October and November: Trichoptera made up 48% of number, 76% of volume, and 92% occurrence.
- Overall, Trichoptera, Ephemeroptera, Diptera, and Coleoptera were the most important food items. Diet resembled that of other Gila River trout, implying possible interspecific competition.
- Bottom samples (Surber sampler) showed little variation in insect composition between June and August. Invertebrate richness was classed as average using Lagler's standards.

**Taxonomy and morphology**
- 25 specimens (98 to 157 mm, mean 116 mm) were measured for six meristic and 15 morphometric characters.
- Results were approximately the same as those for the 1939 paratypes, supporting the diagnostic characters defined by Miller (1950).

**Conservation conclusions**
- The population survives in marginal habitat, with low reproductive rate, slow growth, and summer condition decline.
- Opening the stream to angling is a concern: two anglers using barbless flies caught 130 fish in 5 hours. The author noted that Gila trout are vulnerable because they show little fear of humans and concentrate in pools.
- The paper's stated goal was a sound baseline for future management, preservation, and possible reintroduction through hatchery rearing.
