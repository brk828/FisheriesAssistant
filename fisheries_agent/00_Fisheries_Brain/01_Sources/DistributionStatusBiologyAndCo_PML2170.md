# Distribution, Status, Biology, and Conservation of the Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis) in New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] (Propst, Bestgen, and Painter) documents the distribution, status, life history, microhabitat use, and conservation needs of the [[Loach Minnow]], a small, cryptic, endemic cyprinid of the [[Gila-San Francisco Drainage]]. Fieldwork ran from December 1982 to September 1985 and combined a fish-community inventory of warmwater reaches with focused biological studies in the [[Cliff-Gila Valley]]. The species' occupied New Mexico habitat has fallen from an estimated historic ~600 stream km to ~330 stream km, and its range is now fragmented. The authors conclude that the species persists mainly in streams that remain relatively unregulated and that its survival depends on protecting critical habitat, controlling non-native fishes, and maintaining natural flows.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described by [[Girard]] (1856) from the San Pedro River, Arizona; the first New Mexico record was a 1938 collection from Cliff by [[Carl L. Hubbs]].
- **Morphology and behavior:** A reduced gas bladder and dense body allow the fish to hold position in fast riffles with minimal energy expenditure. It is cryptic, solitary, and sedentary, feeding mainly on benthic rheophilic macroinvertebrates.
- **Status:** Listed as endangered by the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish (1985) and proposed as threatened under the U.S. Endangered Species Act (1985/1986). It has been extirpated from parts of its Arizona range and greatly reduced there.
- **Study design:** Sampling covered all permanent and many intermittent warmwater streams of the basin, with sites generally 1–4 km apart. Methods included backpack electrofishing, seines, gill nets, drift nets, and rock-by-rock egg surveys. Five study areas were used: [[Tularosa River]] (Eagle Peak Road), [[San Francisco River]] (Glenwood–Pleasanton Reach), [[Gila River]] (Forks Area), and Gila River (Cliff-Gila Valley and Riverside).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas**
- **Tularosa River, Eagle Peak Road:** A small stream about 3 m wide, with riffles, runs, and pools. Livestock grazing caused bank loss, downcutting, and sediment increase.
- **San Francisco River, Glenwood–Pleasanton:** About 10 m wide, with mixed habitats and sparse riparian cover. Mid-day water temperatures ranged from 13.0 °C (winter) to 23.0 °C (summer).
- **Gila River, Forks Area:** Intermediate-sized (13 m West Fork, 12 m Middle Fork), with cold winter and warm summer temperatures.
- **Gila River, Cliff-Gila Valley:** The largest site (22–45 m wide), with runs, riffles, and sand substrate. Mean mid-day temperatures were 12.8 °C in winter and 22.1 °C in summer.

**Distribution**
- Historic New Mexico range: presumed ~600 stream km. Current occupied habitat: ~330 stream km.
- Arizona range: historically up to ~2000 stream km, now under 200 stream km. The species is locally common only in [[Aravaipa Creek]] and the [[Blue River]] (~120 stream km).
- Pre-1960 San Francisco River records: 11 collections.

**Community changes**
- Native species such as [[Gila Chub]], [[Spikedace]], and [[Roundtail Chub]] have been extirpated or sharply reduced in New Mexico, while about 15 non-native species (out of 20 reported) were found in this study.
- Non-native predatory and competing fishes, including [[Channel Catfish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Mosquitofish]], have increased.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction**
- Spawning occurred over a short period (about 2–3 weeks) when daytime water temperatures were around 16–20 °C, from April into early summer.
- Eggs were attached to the undersides of flattened, smooth rocks in shallow, slow water (<15 cm/s). Over 80 rocks with attached eggs were found among more than 1000 examined at two sites (17 and 23 April 1984).
- Clutch sizes ranged from 40 to more than 100 ova. Age-II females produced more ova (259 and 297 on 4 and 13 April) than Age-I females (142 and 145 on the same dates).
- Larvae emerged at about 5.4 mm TL. Growth was rapid through September/October, and the 1983 year-class averaged about 39 mm SL in September 1983.
- The largest fish was a 62 mm SL male. Maximum observed longevity was about 18 months.

**Diet and drift**
- Chironomids (midge larvae) and mayfly nymphs were the primary food for larvae and juveniles. Caddisfly larvae were important in adult diets during some seasons.
- The species is mostly absent from drift samples, which suggests low downstream displacement.

**Microhabitat**
- Mean water velocities used: eggs 31.1 cm/s; larvae 7.9 cm/s; juveniles 35.1 cm/s; adults 52.6 cm/s.
- Mean depths: eggs 15.5 cm; larvae 10.6 cm; juveniles 16.8 cm; adults 18.3 cm.
- Velocity use differed significantly among life stages. The authors describe a movement toward faster water as fish grow.

**Population dynamics**
- Floods (for example, October 1983) did not show a demonstrable negative effect on native fishes and may have enhanced loach minnow abundance through improved spawning and survivorship.

**Threats**
- Water diversion, impoundment, channelization, sedimentation, grazing, nutrient loading, elevated water temperatures, and non-native fish introductions.
- Isolation of populations limits recolonization after local losses.

**Recommendations (summary of 11 items)**
1. Designate critical habitat in the Cliff-Gila Valley, lower West and Middle forks, East Fork, lower Tularosa River, and San Francisco River.
2. Maintain threatened listing under the federal Endangered Species Act and endangered listing under New Mexico law.
3. Develop cooperative landowner agreements and a recovery plan.
4. Discontinue introductions of non-native warmwater fishes.
5. Evaluate reintroduction into historic range, possibly using stocks from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
6. Pass legislation to maintain permanent instream flows.
7. Establish guidelines to advise on instream projects that might harm habitat.
