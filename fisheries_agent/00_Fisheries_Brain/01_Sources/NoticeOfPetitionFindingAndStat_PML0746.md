# Petition Finding and Status Review for the Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy-Owl

#ornithology #conservation-biology #strigidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a U.S. [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) notice, with a cover memorandum dated March 17, 1993, announcing a 90-day finding on a petition to list the [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy-Owl]] (*[[Glaucidium Brasilianum Cactorum]]*) as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The petition, submitted by conservation groups and individuals, presented information that the Service judged to be substantial. The Service therefore initiated a status review and requested data from the public, agencies and the scientific community. The owl was already a Category 1 candidate species, meaning the Service had enough information to propose listing but had not yet done so because of higher-priority actions.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Federal Register notice (Vol. 58, Proposed Rules, Tuesday, March 9, 1993), with an accompanying Service memorandum from the [[Arizona Ecological Services Field Office]] in Phoenix.
- **Petition:** Submitted by the Greater Gila Biodiversity Project, Robin Silver and the Biodiversity Legal Foundation, with Peter Galvin also named as a petitioner in the Service's correspondence. The petitioners asked that the owl be listed as endangered and that critical habitat be designated concurrently. The Service accepted the petition on June 2, 1992.
- **Finding date:** The finding was made on February 26, 1993, and published March 9, 1993. Comments and information were requested by June 7, 1993.
- **Candidate status:** The owl was elevated to Category 1 candidate status on November 21, 1991 (56 FR 58804). Under the Act, a candidate species is one for which the Service has sufficient information to propose listing, but a proposed rule has not been issued because of higher listing priorities.
- **Taxonomy:** The cactus ferruginous pygmy-owl is one of three subspecies of the ferruginous pygmy-owl recognized in North America. Its range runs from central Arizona south through western Mexico, and from southern Texas south to Tamaulipas and eastern Mexico. The subspecies was recognized by Friedmann et al. (1950) and is accepted by most later authors. The American Ornithologists' Union (AOU, 1983) did not recognize it as a separate subspecies, which the petition and the Service addressed in their review.
- **Legal framework:** Under section 4(b)(3)(A) of the Act, the Service must decide within 90 days whether a petition presents substantial scientific or commercial information indicating that listing may be warranted. If it does, a status review is initiated and a 12-month finding is due. A "threatened" species is defined in terms of likelihood of becoming endangered in the foreseeable future.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and ecology**
- In the northern part of its range, the owl occurs in tropical and subtropical deciduous forest and woodlands, including riparian cottonwood, willow, mesquite, hackberry and oak stands along watercourses.
- It is also associated with desert communities dominated by mesquite, palo verde, acacia, ironwood and saguaro.
- The owl is believed to be closely tied to lowland watercourses and riparian corridors, and historically occurred in riparian woodlands in the Sonoran Desert.

**Threats identified in the petition and the Service's review**
- **Habitat loss and modification (Factor A):** The petition contended that riparian habitat loss in the Southwest has caused the owl's population and range to decline. The Service's information indicates that declines and habitat loss have been most severe in southern Texas and southern Arizona. Wetland-loss figures for several states were cited from Dahl (1990), but some percentages in the scan are partly illegible. Habitat loss and fragmentation were also linked to urban and agricultural conversion in the lower Rio Grande Valley and parts of Arizona.
- **Range reduction:** Historical records (Oberholser 1974, Phillips et al. 1964) show the owl was once more widespread in Texas and Arizona. Surveys by Hunter (1988) and others documented decreasing numbers. Oberholser (1974) and Texas sources considered this a significant decline.
- **Disease and predation (Factor C):** The petition presented no information that disease or predation constituted a significant threat. The Service will evaluate the potential for these factors as part of the status review.
- **Human disturbance:** The petition said owls can be disturbed by harassment from birdwatchers who concentrate at known owl locations.
- **Regulatory mechanisms (Factor D):** The petition argued that existing regulatory mechanisms are inadequate. It stated that no conservation plans or management guidelines exist for the owl or its habitat on lands managed by several state and federal agencies, and by Indian Nations and private parties. The Service noted that take of the owl and destruction of its habitat are often prohibited under other regulations, and that the owl is protected under the [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]].

## Key Findings

1. **Substantial information threshold met:** The Service concluded that the petition, along with the cited references and information in its files, presented substantial information indicating that listing the cactus ferruginous pygmy-owl may be warranted. This triggers a full [[Status Review]].
2. **Status review initiated:** The Service will evaluate the species' status, the adequacy of existing regulations, and the potential threats identified, and will issue a 12-month finding on whether listing is warranted.
3. **Candidate status maintained:** The owl was already a Category 1 candidate species (56 FR 58804, November 21, 1991). The Service noted that it has information on population declines and habitat loss, primarily in southern Texas and southern Arizona, and that information from Mexico was unavailable at the time.
4. **Information requested:** The Service asked for information on eight topics:
   - Distribution, abundance and population trends.
   - Habitat use, distribution and trends.
   - Present or threatened destruction, modification or curtailment of habitat or range.
   - Overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific or educational purposes.
   - Disease or predation.
   - Inadequacy of existing regulatory mechanisms.
   - Other natural or manmade factors affecting continued existence.
   - Beneficial management practices.
5. **Comment deadline:** Information, comments or questions were due by June 7, 1993. The petition, supporting data and comments were available for public inspection at the Phoenix office during normal business hours.
6. **Contacts:** Timothy Tibbitts was the primary author of the notice. Sam F. Spiller, Field Supervisor, was the contact for further information. Requests for the Service's [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy-Owl]] bibliography were also directed to the Phoenix office.
7. **Reference base:** The notice cites roughly 30 references, including the AOU checklist (1983), Arizona and Texas state bird and wildlife lists, Oberholser (1974), Phillips et al. (1964), Hunter (1988), Millsap and Johnson (1988), Johnson et al. (1988), Dahl (1990), and state riparian task force reports. Many are historical bird distribution works, which reflects the reliance on long-term survey and records data.
