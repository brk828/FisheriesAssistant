# A Method of Rearing *Simulium ornatum* var. *nitidifrons* (Diptera, Simuliidae) in the Laboratory

#simuliidae #freshwater-biology #aquatic-entomology

## Executive Summary

This 1963 methods paper, published in [[Hydrobiologia]] (Vol. XXII, Nos. 1–2) by R. E. Hatt and J. J. Harrop of Southampton University, describes a closed-circulation apparatus for rearing the larvae of [[Simulium Ornatum]] var. [[Simulium Nitidifrons|nitidifrons]], a black fly from the family [[Simuliidae]]. The larvae came from a small stream in the [[River Test]] valley. The system was designed to reproduce the current speeds of natural streams, supply abundant food, and avoid the high larval mortality reported in earlier attempts to breed simuliids. From about 1,000 first instars, 140 adults emerged (14%), and most losses occurred in the first larval instar. Development from hatching to adult took five to eight weeks at 17 °C.

## Background & Description

Simuliid larvae live in fast-flowing water, so rearing them in the laboratory requires control of current speed as well as food supply. Earlier breeding attempts, reviewed by one of the authors (Harrop, 1962), often produced heavy larval mortality. Those methods also could not provide high current speeds or measure them accurately. The authors needed an apparatus that:

- matched the current speed of the source stream;
- supplied abundant food;
- kept water in continuous, closed circulation, since removing water would lose food particles;
- worked with a limited volume of water, because local tap water had a high chloride content and natural stream water was used instead.

**Apparatus:**
- An upper tank of about 100 litres, with an adjustable tap feeding wide polythene tubing.
- A tin trough, 140 cm long, 10 cm wide and 15 cm deep, lacquered against rust. It had a large outlet at the lower end and a smaller side outlet about 50 cm back, both screened with fine mesh. The side outlet increased flow through the trough.
- A lower tank with copper cooling coils, through which tap water flowed slowly.
- An electrically driven pump, with a variable resistance controlling the rate at which water returned to the upper tank.

Balancing the tap against the pump kept flow and water depth constant at each setting. Flow could be varied from 20 to 80 cm/s. A depth of 4–5 cm at 50 cm/s was the standard condition. The lower tank level was kept below the trough outlets, so falling water aerated it. Tests showed the water was always supersaturated with oxygen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Source habitat:** The larvae came from a small stream in the River Test valley, Hampshire. The text describes it as a stream, and the Harrop (1962) thesis refers to a chalk stream. The rearing system was seeded with natural habitat water.
- **Water supply:** Filling used natural stream water. Losses from evaporation were made up with sand-filtered tap water, because tap water had high chloride levels.
- **Food:** Larvae were fed a mixed culture of diatoms and desmids.
- **Environmental settings:** Water temperature was held at 17 °C and flow at 50 cm/s during the main rearing run.
- **Adult containment:** Emerging adults were kept in a polythene and muslin cage placed over the trough.

## Key Findings

**Rearing method**
- About 3,000 eggs were placed in widebore glass tubing with muslin-covered ends and suspended in the trough. Enclosing the first instars was necessary because they were very small and hard to find in an open trough.
- The paper reports 1,001 larvae hatching from these eggs. Later text refers to "1000 first instars," a minor inconsistency in the source.
- Newly hatched larvae were split into five groups of 200 in muslin-covered tubes. Once they reached the second instar, the trough was divided into five screened sections, and each group was reared separately until death or pupation.

**Mortality and development** (from Table I)
- **First instar:** 527 of about 1,000 larvae died (52.7%), mainly during ecdysis. This was the greatest mortality of any stage.
- **Second instar:** 163 of the remaining 473 died (34.5%), leaving 310 third instars.
- **Third instar:** 91 died (29.4%), leaving 219 fourth instars.
- **Fourth instar:** 50 died (reported as 22%).
- **Fifth instar:** mortality 8.3%.
- **Sixth instar:** mortality 4.5%.
- **Pupae:** 145 pupae were obtained, with 3 larvae lost during the experiment. Five pupae died (3.5% of those reaching this stage).
- **Adults:** 140 emerged (14% of the total), comprising 79 females and 61 males.
- **Development time:** five to eight weeks from hatching to adult, at 17 °C.

Across the five groups, adult emergence ranged from 14 to 43 individuals per group. Mortality fell at each successive stage after the first instar, so the first instar was the main bottleneck.

**Conclusions**
- The apparatus gave a continuous, aerated, and controllable flow suited to larvae from fast-flowing waters.
- Survival improved sharply after the first instar, showing that early-stage handling and environment were the key constraints.
