# A Digitized Classification System for the Biotic Communities of North America

#plant-communities #biotic-communities #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This 1979 report by David E. Brown, [[Charles H. Lowe]], and Charles P. Pase extends the [[Southwest System]], a hierarchical, computer-compatible classification of natural ecosystems, from the North American Southwest to the whole continent. Digit levels 1–4 are expanded for North America, and levels 5 (series, or community) and 6 (association) are illustrated for biomes in the Southwest. The document is a classification framework and nomenclature, not a field study. It contains no fish data and does not treat fish or other fauna as classified units. Its aquatic content is limited to wetland and submergent plant communities.

## Background & Description

The Southwest System is described as evolutionary in basis and hierarchical in structure. The authors present it as a natural biological system rather than a geography-based one, so the resulting classifications are natural hierarchies. The system is "open-ended" and can add, transfer, or delete ecological taxa and quantitative parameters as knowledge grows. Digit levels 7 to n hold quantitative data, and levels 1–6 hold ecological taxa.

The authors state the following uses and context:

- **Mapping scales:** Levels have been mapped at 1:1,000,000, 1:500,000, 1:250,000, and 1:62,500, among others.
- **Operational use:** The system is used in the RUNWILD program (Region 3, [[Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station]], [[Patton 1978]]) and in the [[Arizona Resources Inventory System]] (ARIS). Industry and agencies use it for biological studies, resource inventories, and environmental analysis, including work required under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]].
- **Contributors:** Content draws on roughly one hundred investigators, mainly biogeographers, wildlife biologists, and ecologists.

The document also builds a "digitized hierarchy of the world's natural ecosystems." Its digit levels are:

1. **Biogeographic realm** (e.g., 1,000 = [[Nearctic Realm]]). Seven realms are adapted from [[Alfred Russel Wallace]] (1876) and related works.
2. **First digit (vegetation):** 1,100 upland and 1,200 wetland. Cultivated lands are 1,300 and 1,400.
3. **Second digit (formation-type):** Six upland formations (tundra, forest and woodland, scrubland, grassland, desertland, nonvegetated) and six wetland formations (wet tundra, swampforest/riparian forest, swampscrub/riparian scrub, marshland, strandland, submergent aquatic).
4. **Third digit (climatic zone):** Four zones: Arctic-Boreal, Cold Temperate, Warm Temperate, and Tropical-Subtropical. Minimum temperature is the main control.
5. **Fourth digit (biome):** A subcontinental major biotic community with a distinctive vegetation physiognomy. Biomes are disjunct, unlike biogeographic provinces, which are continuous.
6. **Fifth digit (series):** Communities grouped by shared climax dominants, such as the Pine Series (122.32, *Pinus ponderosa*).
7. **Sixth digit (association):** Local or regional plant associations, roughly equivalent to habitat types.
8. **Seventh digit and beyond:** Detailed quantitative structure, density, and composition.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Formation definitions depend on plant-available moisture and temperature. Key distinctions include:

- **Tundra:** Too cold for trees; vegetation is herbaceous, shrubby, lichen, and moss, with grasses poorly represented.
- **Forest:** Trees potentially over 15 m tall, with closed or multilayered canopies.
- **Woodland:** Trees under 15 m, usually with open canopies.
- **Scrubland:** Sclerophyll or microphyll shrubs, generally under 10 m.
- **Desertland:** Usually under 300 mm precipitation per year, with bare ground between plants.
- **Wetland:** Periodically, seasonally, or continually submerged ecosystems supporting life forms different from adjacent upland climax vegetation. Riparian forests are classed as wetlands.

The climatic-zone descriptions give growing seasons and freezing conditions. Arctic-Boreal growing seasons last 60–150 days. Cold Temperate seasons last 100–200 days. Tropical-Subtropical zones have infrequent or no freezing.

Aquatic and semi-aquatic formations include:

- **Marshland:** Herbaceous emergents with basal portions submerged.
- **Strandland:** Beach and river-channel communities subject to periodic submersion, waves, or spray. The authors treat it as the wetland equivalent of desertland.
- **Submergent aquatic:** Communities of plants that are mostly submerged, including Pondweed, Milfoil, Ruppia, Eelgrass (*Zostera marina*), and kelp series (e.g., *Macrocystis pyrifera*) in marine settings.

Biogeographic provinces are mapped separately. Figure 2 lists 22 provinces for Nearctic and adjacent Neotropical North America, from Polar and Alaskan to Sonoran, Sinaloan, and Caribbean. The authors note that distributions of dominant plants can span climatic zones. [[Mesquite]] (*Prosopis juliflora*) is a dominant in desertland, disclimax grassland, scrubland, woodland, and riparian forest, and it shows phenotypic differentiation along its gradient. The chromosome races of creosotebush (*Larrea divaricata*) are another example of within-species variation.

The authors acknowledge that the fifth and sixth levels will need revision as field data accumulate. Knowledge of some wetland and strand communities is described as incomplete, so representative series and association examples are not given for them.

## Key Findings

- **Classification structure:** The seven-level hierarchy (realm, vegetation, formation, climatic zone, biome, series, association) plus quantitative levels provides a single framework for mapping ecosystems. It serves wildlife habitat mapping, natural area delineation, and land-use planning.
- **Flexibility and scale:** The hierarchy can be mapped at any scale, and complex communities can be mapped at higher levels where detailed work is impractical.
- **Continental extension:** Nomenclature at digit levels 1–4 now covers North America. Tables 2–5 list formations and climatic zones, and Tables 6–7 give upland and wetland examples for the Nearctic and adjacent Neotropical regions.
- **Southwest examples:** Table 6 (upland) and Table 7 (wetland) provide the series and association examples. Upland examples include Pinyon-Juniper, Ponderosa Pine, Sonoran Desertscrub, and Chihuahuan Desertscrub. Wetland examples include Cottonwood-Willow riparian forests, Saltcedar disclimax scrub, and Californian coastal marine submergents.
- **Aquatic scope:** Aquatic content covers only plant communities (marshes, strands, riparian forests, and submergent vegetation). The document does not classify fish, invertebrates, or water chemistry.
- **Applications:** The authors note the system's use in the [[Biosphere Reserve Program]] (MAB) and in other biome-based mapping of states, regions, and continents.
- **Status:** The document is a working classification. The authors expect revisions, and the sixth-level association examples are illustrative rather than complete.
