# Declaration of W.L. Minckley on Grazing Impacts to Native Southwestern Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #conservation-biology #riparian-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This document is a sworn expert declaration by Dr. Wendell L. Minckley, a veteran ichthyologist and zoology professor at Arizona State University, submitted in support of litigation against the U.S. Bureau of Land Management's Safford District. The declaration argues that chronic livestock overgrazing has driven severe, cumulative watershed degradation in southwestern Arizona, which in turn constitutes indirect "take" of threatened and endangered native freshwater fishes under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Minckley uses [[Freshwater Fishes]] as bioindicators of ecosystem health, citing regional data showing that a majority of southwestern fish taxa are already extinct, listed, or imperiled. The declaration traces a causal chain from livestock grazing practices (particularly tank-based water dispersal) through soil compaction, ground-cover loss, arroyo cutting, and channel incision to degraded aquatic habitat and fish population decline. It closes with a call for BLM and other land-management agencies to adopt science-based grazing reform to protect watershed and riparian function.

## Background & Description

This declaration is not a morphological study; it is a policy/conservation-status document. It does not describe body plans, meristics, or anatomical traits of the listed species. Instead, it treats the listed taxa as **ecological indicator species**, grouped by the stream habitat type they characterize:

- **Upland, forested headwaters:** [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gila*), [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*), [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*)
- **Woodland/grassland streams at intermediate elevation:** [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Cyprinella formosa*), [[Sonora Chub]] (*Gila ditaenia*), [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*), [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*), [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*, *P. o. sonoriensis*)
- **"Big river" / lowland grass-desertland habitats:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), [[Virgin Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*, *C. m. eremus*)

Species marked with an asterisk in the original text occur or formerly occurred in or adjacent to the Safford District, tying this taxonomic list directly to the litigated management area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Minckley frames native southwestern fishes as **obligate aquatic residents** entirely dependent on continuous, functioning stream systems — they "are absolutely tied to the environment, unable to move overland in the absence of water." Key ecological points:

- **Stream connectivity dependence:** Because streams are cumulative collectors of watershed-wide perturbations, fishes are captive to all upstream impacts, including short-duration events (e.g., brief dewatering, toxic spills) that can be immediately terminal to local populations.
- **Isolation and extirpation risk:** Once a species is eliminated from a stream reach isolated by a dam, or a dry channel from diversion/pumping or natural drying, recolonization is impossible without human intervention — an especially acute risk in arid-land systems characterized by naturally fragmented, isolated habitats.
- **Habitat stratification:** The fish community is organized along an elevational/habitat gradient from cold, forested headwater streams (trout, spinedace, loach minnow) through mid-elevation wood/grassland streams (chub, shiner, topminnow, catfish) to lowland "big river" desert habitats (squawfish, bonytail, woundfin, razorback sucker, pupfish).
- **Trophic linkages:** Aquatic insect production (affected by turbidity, nutrient input, and light penetration) links fish populations to riparian consumers such as birds and bats, illustrating cross-ecosystem trophic connectivity affected by grazing-driven water quality changes.
- **Grazing-driven habitat degradation mechanism:** Cattle concentrate in riparian zones for water and shade despite dispersal efforts (tanks, salt licks, pasture rotation), causing ground-cover removal, soil compaction, and trail-concentrated runoff. This drives arroyo cutting, channel incision, floodplain bank-storage drainage, reduced riparian gallery forest viability, and diminished stream-flow permanence — collectively degrading the physical/chemical habitat matrix fish depend on.
- **Downstream propagation of impact:** Upstream perturbations (increased turbidity, altered nutrient loads, altered light penetration) propagate as a "progressive wave of change" affecting the entire ecosystem from headwaters to mouth.

## Key Findings

- Between **150 and 200** freshwater fish taxa occur west of the Continental Divide in the U.S.; by the late 1980s, an estimated **100–135** of these were considered imperiled (Minckley & Douglas 1991).
- **31 of 32** native Arizona fish taxa are extinct, federally listed as threatened/endangered, or designated species of special concern (Minckley, unpubl.).
- **Thirty fish taxa** have gone extinct in the USA over the past century (Miller et al. 1989).
- Minckley & Douglas (1991) note a shift in the scale of decline: historically, pervasive environmental stress eliminated individual species from small rivers/springs; under current regional-scale development stress, **entire faunas of major western river basins are now collapsing**.
- Livestock overgrazing is consistently identified across multiple independent regional studies (Hastings 1959; Cooke & Reeves 1976; Bahre 1991; Brown 1994; among others) as a major driver of watershed-scale degradation in the American Southwest, alongside land-use practices like clear-cutting.
- Construction of livestock-watering tanks paradoxically **increased** total watershed exposure to grazing (by allowing cattle to range farther from streams) while often simultaneously **increasing total stocking numbers**, undermining the intended dispersal benefit.
- Cattle preference for riparian shade partially negates dispersal management strategies, concentrating grazing impact precisely in the most ecologically sensitive riparian corridor zones.
- Documented physical impacts observed directly by Minckley in the Safford District include: ground-cover removal, soil compaction, trail-based flow concentration, arroyo cutting, flash flooding, channel incision, and reduced riparian gallery forest viability — all linked mechanistically to reduced native fish population viability.
- The declaration concludes that native fish decline serves as a **direct, measurable index of watershed ecosystem failure**, with implications not only for [[Endangered Species Act]] compliance but for long-term human water supply sustainability.
