# Fisheries Management of El Capitan Reservoir, San Diego County, California, 1960-1962

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #reservoir-management #warmwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This administrative report documents a three-year fisheries management program (1960-1962) at [[El Capitan Reservoir]], [[San Diego County]], [[California]], conducted by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]]. The reservoir exhibited a long-standing population imbalance characterized by overabundant, small [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) and limited [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) recruitment. Management interventions included stocking 219,775 largemouth bass, 177,890 [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and 412,500 [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*) to increase predation pressure on bluegill and diversify the fishery. Despite intensive stocking efforts and marking programs, the quality of bluegill and bass fishing declined over the study period. Mean bluegill fork length decreased from 6.1 inches (1960) to 5.4 inches (1962), and bluegill catch per angler hour dropped from 0.90 to 0.52. The report concludes that management attempts did not produce substantial improvements, though multiple confounding variables (water level decline, introduction of [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Carp]], variable angling pressure) made definitive evaluation impossible.

## Background & Description

[[El Capitan Reservoir]] is a warmwater impoundment owned and operated by the City of San Diego, completed in 1935 and filled by 1937. The reservoir is classified as an alkali lake with relatively fertile waters but steep sides and reduced littoral area. High turbidity and water level fluctuations discourage rooted aquatic plant growth. The reservoir operates on a "free-water" basis with water levels maintained low to reduce evaporation, resulting in substantial volume decline from 41,220 acre-feet (January 1959) to 8,915 acre-feet (December 1962).

The fishery was first opened in 1955 after a 20-year closure following impoundment. This extended closure allowed fish populations to reach a mature, unbalanced condition dominated by small bluegill. The prevailing hypothesis held that bluegill overpopulation resulted from predation on young largemouth bass, which limited bass recruitment and thereby reduced predation on bluegill themselves—a self-reinforcing cycle producing numerically large but stunted bluegill populations.

By 1962, the reservoir contained eleven fish species: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides floridanus*), [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*), [[Walleye]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus nebulosus*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), and [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). Threadfin shad and carp entered during 1958 via [[Colorado River]] water pumped from the aqueduct and became well established.

The reservoir was open to fishing on Thursdays, Saturdays, Sundays, and holidays, requiring $1 daily permits for anglers over 16 years of age.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The predator-prey dynamics at [[El Capitan Reservoir]] centered on the bluegill-largemouth bass relationship. The working hypothesis proposed that bluegill, through superior reproduction and survival, limited bass recruitment via predation on young-of-year bass. Adult largemouth bass represented the only significant predator on adult bluegill prior to 1962 walleye introductions. This imbalance created a population structure dominated by small bluegill with limited older/larger individuals.

However, growth analysis of 27 bluegill collected in 1962 revealed normal growth rates compared to 44 samples from [[Carlander]]'s reference data, suggesting the population was not stunted but rather composed of predominantly young, rapidly growing fish with high turnover rates. This implies differential mortality based on size, with younger fish experiencing lower mortality than older individuals—potentially due to combined angling and natural mortality pressures.

The introduction of [[Threadfin Shad]] in 1958 may have compounded management challenges by serving as a buffer forage species, further reducing predation pressure on small bluegill. Walleye stomach analyses indicated predominant feeding on threadfin shad rather than bluegill.

Environmental stressors included dramatic water volume decline (over 50% reduction from 1959 to 1962), which decreased littoral habitat area and potentially altered carrying capacity. The reservoir's steep topography and limited littoral zone constrained productive fish habitat. Water chemistry remained typical of southern California reservoirs with alkaline conditions.

[[White Crappie]] exhibited a clear population cycle during the study period, with peak abundance in 1961 (likely from an exceptional 1958 year class) and peak size in 1962 (mean fork length 12.0 inches).

## Key Findings

**Stocking Program Results:**
- 219,775 largemouth bass stocked (1960-1963), ranging from 100 fish per ounce to 1.5 pounds each
- Large bass (1.5 pounds) showed minimum 56% return to creel before end of second growing season following stocking
- Small bass showed very low creel return; stocking either very small or very large bass deemed uneconomical
- 163 tagged bass (mean 1.5 pounds, 13.7-13.8 inches fork length) released February-March 1960 showed 56% tag return by end of 1961 season, with over 50% returned during first season
- 177,890 channel catfish stocked (1961-1963); beginning to enter creel but evaluation incomplete
- 412,500 walleye stocked (1962-1963); showed exceptionally fast growth (up to 4 pounds in 3 years) and good survival but minimal harvest by anglers due to unfamiliarity and diel migration patterns not coinciding with legal fishing hours

**Bluegill Population Dynamics:**
- Mean fork length declined from 6.1 inches (1960) to 5.4 inches (1962)
- Catch per angler hour decreased from 0.90 (1960) to 0.52 (1962)
- Total harvest increased from 28,650 fish (60.7% of catch, 1960) to 36,618 fish (8.7% of catch, 1962)
- Growth study of 27 bluegill indicated normal growth rates, suggesting population composed of young fish with rapid turnover rather than stunted individuals
- Population structure suggests differential mortality based on size, with younger fish having lower mortality rates

**Largemouth Bass Population:**
- Mean fork length remained constant at approximately 14.0 inches throughout study period (1960-1962)
- Catch per angler hour declined slightly from 0.04 (1960) to 0.03 (1962)
- Total harvest increased from 1,393 fish (2.9% of catch, 1960) to 2,717 fish (6.0% of catch, 1962)
- Right pelvic-clipped bass from 1959 stocking contributed significantly: 4.6% (1960), 6.0% (1961), and 3.4% (1962) of bass catch

**White Crappie Cycle:**
- Definite population cycle evidenced over three-year period
- Mean fork length increased from 6.9 inches (1960) to 9.4 inches (1961) to 12.0 inches (1962)
- Peak abundance occurred in 1961, apparently from exceptional 1958 year class

**Brown Bullhead:**
- Mean fork length increased from 9.1 inches (1960) to 10.7 inches (1961)
- Data available for 1960-1961 only

**Creel Census Statistics:**
- 1960: 8,688 estimated anglers, 5.4 fish per angler, 1.5 fish per angler hour
- 1961: 17,660 estimated anglers, 7.8 fish per angler, 1.2 fish per angler hour
- 1962: 11,130 estimated anglers, 4.0 fish per angler, 0.9 fish per angler hour
- Best fishing occurred during first few months of each season
- Fishing pressure varied substantially year-to-year

**Chemical Treatment:**
- Original plan for extensive peripheral rotenone treatments and complete treatment of one reservoir arm largely abandoned
- One arm treated April 1960, followed by stocking 13,800 bass in treated area

**Management Evaluation:**
- Overall fishing quality declined during study period based on catch-per-unit-effort metrics
- Multiple confounding variables prevented definitive evaluation of management program effectiveness: water volume decline (>50%), species introductions (threadfin shad, carp, walleye, channel catfish), variable angling pressure, and changing season lengths
- Water volume decreased from 41,220 acre-feet (January 1959) to 8,915 acre-feet (December 1962)
- Decreased water levels reduced littoral area and potentially altered carrying capacity
- Report concludes that at present level of understanding, accurate evaluation of total management program effect is not possible

**Marking Program Insights:**
- Right pelvic-clipped bass (1959 stocking, 8-5 fish per pound) contributed 65 estimated fish to 1960 creel, 150 to 1961 creel, 50 to 1962 creel
- Left pelvic-clipped bass (1960 stocking, 11.6-5 fish per ounce) showed minimal contribution
- Anal-clipped bass stocked 1958-1961 at various sizes
- Florida bass (600 fish) stocked July 1961 with right pectoral clips

**Ecological Observations:**
- Threadfin shad may act as buffer forage, reducing predation on small bluegill
- Walleye feed predominately on threadfin shad rather than bluegill
- Carp increased rapidly and were numerous by 1963
- Reservoir classified as relatively fertile alkali lake with limited rooted vegetation due to turbidity and water level fluctuations
