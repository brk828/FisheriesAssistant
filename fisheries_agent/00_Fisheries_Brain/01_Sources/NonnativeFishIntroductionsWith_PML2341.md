# Non-Native Fish Introductions with Special Reference to Florida

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1979 *Fisheries* article by fisheries biologist Paul L. Shafland (Florida Game and Fresh Water Fish Commission) on the introduction of non-native fishes, with emphasis on Florida. Florida has more established non-native fish species than any other US state: 14 species, mostly cichlids, with eight more possibly established. The author attributes this to the state's subtropical climate, a depleted native freshwater fauna, and interconnected canals. Most releases are linked to the ornamental fish trade and unauthorized dumping. The paper argues that purposeful and accidental introductions should be curtailed, that federal regulation and an independent advisory board are needed, and that the consequences of past introductions, especially [[Blue Tilapia]], justify caution. A short second piece by E. A. Seaman describes a spring-fed Virginia pond used for observing [[Goldfish]] harvesting [[Pondweed]].

## Background & Description

The paper defines a non-native fish as one intentionally or accidentally moved by humans outside its natural range. Movements between countries are termed foreign, alien, or exotic introductions; movements within a country are termed transplants. An "established" species is one with stable or increasing populations. A "possibly established" species has reproducing but unstable populations, or evidence of a viable population of less than five years.

The author's background is noted: Shafland holds a B.A. in biology and an M.S. (1975) and worked on non-native fish research and the biology of Florida's exotic fishes, including temperature tolerance and interactions with native fishes. The article is Contribution Number 14 of the Non-Native Fish Research Laboratory.

Table 1 of the paper lists established and possibly established non-native fishes in Florida with their common names, scientific names, and families (summarized below).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Establishment drivers.** The paper cites the long-recognized impoverishment of Florida's native freshwater fish fauna, the tropical-subtropical climate, and recent channelization and water-flow changes. It notes that islands and lakes are especially vulnerable to introduced species, and that cichlids are among the most aggressive and reproductively successful exotic freshwater fishes.

**Vectors.** Most fishes reached Florida through the ornamental fish industry. Roughly 40% of live fish imports into the United States entered through the two authorized Florida ports, Miami and Tampa (October 1971 data). The ornamental industry was implicated in the unauthorized release of nearly 80% of established non-native fishes. Other vectors were:
- Dumping of a [[Pike Killifish]] into Dade County canals after a medical experiment.
- Carp invading northern Florida via interstate drainages from Alabama and Georgia.
- [[Green Swordtail]] introduced as a mosquito control agent.
- Research distribution of blue tilapia and other cichlids across the southeastern US in the early 1960s.

**Parasites and pathogens.** The paper warns that introduced fishes carry parasites and diseases. At least 48 associated parasites had accompanied transferred fishes worldwide (Hoffman 1970), and 25 foreign parasites had infected fish in North America by 1970. Examples include the agent of whirling disease, *Myxosoma cerebralis*. A tapeworm, *Bothriocephalus gowkongensis*, was introduced with grass carp, and golden shiners and fathead minnows from Kansas were also noted as vectors of concern.

**Non-fish introductions.** The non-native snail *Melanoides tuberculata* in south Florida mangroves is an intermediate host for two human trematodes (the Oriental lung fluke and the liver fluke). [[Hydrilla]], introduced in 1956, is described as one of Florida's most serious aquatic weeds.

**Other regions.** The paper states that at least 11 non-native fish species are established in California.

## Key Findings

- **Species count:** Florida has 14 established non-native fish species: 10 cichlids, 1 poeciliid, 1 clariid, 1 loricariid, and 1 cyprinid. Eight further species are possibly established, including a belontiid, an erythrinid (*Hoplias malabaricus*), and poeciliids. The paper also reports one reproducing piranha population (*Serrasalmus*) in an isolated pit, which had persisted 13 to 14 years before elimination.
- **Scale of trade:** In 1972, more than 100 million foreign fishes, involving some 1,000 or more species, entered the United States.
- **Blue tilapia impacts:** Blue tilapia are the most widespread non-native fish in Florida, found in at least 21 counties. In Lake Morton, blue tilapia ranked second by weight among 14 species three years after introduction. They made up 57% of fish biomass in Lake Parker after three years and 68% three years later. High densities can prevent successful recruitment of largemouth bass.
- **Walking catfish:** Walking catfish, introduced by a tropical fish farmer, became a major pest for fish farms. Some farmers built low fences around their ponds to exclude them.
- **Failed and pre-study claims:** Pre-study publicity claimed blue tilapia would be a good sport fish and control aquatic weeds. Those predictions were disproven, and attempts to eradicate illegal releases were unsuccessful.
- **Core argument:** Past failures should be an integral part of decision-making. Introductions should be approved only after an affirmative decision based on sound ecological principles. Evaluation should weigh harmful effects as heavily as benefits, and the "innocent-until-proven-guilty" standard is inappropriate because established populations cannot generally be eliminated.
- **Recommendations:**
  - Federal rules and enforcement prohibiting unauthorized release of foreign fishes.
  - An independent advisory board of biologists and state representatives to permit introductions and set evaluation guidelines.
  - Stronger public education and enforcement in Florida, where existing rules are adequate but poorly applied.
  - Restricting high-risk fishes such as piranhas to tightly controlled scientific use.
  - Controlling the transplanting of established species, as with illegal blue tilapia transfers and oscar and pike killifish problems in south Florida.
- **Policy context:** The paper quotes a 1977 executive order directing agencies to restrict the introduction of exotic species into US ecosystems.
- **Conclusion:** Foreign fishes could produce beneficial changes in ecosystem energy flow, but the information needed to introduce them safely is largely unavailable. The author concludes that it is "best to assume that no one can accurately forecast" the ultimate effects of introducing foreign species.

**Seaman note (Northern Virginia pond).** The second article describes a spring-fed pond of about one-third acre with an average depth of three feet, on the headwaters of the Colvin Run drainage. Spring water enters at 54°F year-round, and surface temperature can reach 78°F in August. The pond is shaded on two sides by an oak-hickory stand. Goldfish (*Carassius auratus*) and *Potamogeton foliosus* are the focus of observation. Other fauna include [[Crayfish]] (*Cambarus bartonii*), pickerel and green frog tadpoles, a snapping turtle, and a painted turtle. The text provides only the site description, with no harvesting results.
