# Maximum Sustained Yields from Fluctuating Environments and Mixed Stocks

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmon-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[W. E. Ricker]] uses numerical models to test how random environmental variability and common fisheries affect the long-term yield of fish stocks. Six single-age stocks, each with a different density-dependent [[Stock-Recruitment Relationship]], were simulated under two levels of environmental variability. The central results are that closely regulating fishing to the optimum escapement each year raises average catch, that this gain grows with environmental variability, and that it comes at the cost of greater year-to-year catch instability, sometimes requiring complete closures. When several stocks with different reproductive potentials are fished together, maximum yield can drive one stock to extinction, so separate management of each unit is always preferable where feasible.

## Background & Description

The study addresses two complications to the classical theory of exploiting homogeneous fish stocks, under the assumptions that fishing takes fish just before spawning, that the spawning stock has a single age, and that density does not affect adult size:

1. The effect of random, environmentally caused variability in reproduction on the best percentage harvest of a stock.
2. The effect of a common fishery harvesting two or more stocks that differ in reproductive potential.

Six reproduction curves (A, B, C, F, G, H) represent contrasting density effects on recruitment. Curves A to C follow a simple expression with parameter values 1, 2 and 2.678 (Ricker, 1958). Curve F rises rapidly to a stable maximum. Curve H follows a [[Beverton-Holt Model]] form, with parameter 0.9 chosen to fill a gap among the other curves. Curve G is designed so that low-density stocks are unproductive, mimicking a pattern attributed to [[Dr. F. Neave]] for pink and chum salmon.

Environmental variability was simulated with random multipliers and divisors drawn from a normal-distribution table ([[Ricker Random Factor Table]]). Two levels were used:

- **Level A:** one standard deviation equals unity; the total range of effects is about 7.1:1 (greatest multiplier 2.5, greatest divisor 2.8).
- **Level B:** four times the variance (twice the standard deviation); the range is 18.4:1.

Each simulation covered 28 generations, starting at replacement density (unity). The first four were discarded, so averages are based on 24 generations. Stock sizes and catches are expressed as fractions or multiples of replacement density.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Source of variability:** The asymmetric progeny distribution produced by the model resembles observed patterns in [[Pink Salmon]] and [[Chum Salmon]], where mediocre years are interrupted by occasional exceptionally good ones.
- **Empirical comparison:** [[Wickett]] (1958) reported that recruitment from pink and chum spawners on the central BC coast can vary by at least 24:1, which Level B (18:1 over 24 years) approximates. [[Sockeye Salmon]], [[Coho Salmon]] and [[Atlantic Salmon]] appear more stable, with the Level A range (7:1) covering most observations. Individual streams and nursery grounds vary more than large statistical areas.
- **Life-history limits:** The models apply directly to stocks vulnerable for only one year at one age, such as pink salmon, and approximately to coho and some sockeye. Fisheries on [[Mixed-Age Stocks]] (such as trout or flatfish) add the question of harvesting at optimal ages, balancing natural mortality and growth.
- **Predictability:** Weather affecting Pacific salmon reproduction has shown no recognizable, predictable trends, so a random-series model was judged the most suitable.

## Key Findings

### 1. Effects of Environmental Variability on Yield (Constant Harvest Rate)

- At Level A, mean catches from Curves A, B and C changed very little. Population F decreased slightly and Population H increased slightly, because high exploitation on F depresses the stock after poor years, whereas H avoids density-dependent losses after good years.
- Environmental variability mainly increases year-to-year catch differences rather than long-term means.
- The best constant harvest rate under fluctuating conditions is the same as, or very close to, the best rate under stable conditions.

### 2. Partial Stabilization of Escapement (Table IV)

Holding escapement to the optimum norm for each stock gave improvements over constant exploitation at Level A:

| Population | Improvement |
|---|---|
| A | +9% |
| B | +7% |
| C | +8% |
| F | +21% |
| H | +5% |

For Stock A, five years had no catch, and the smallest catches shrank for all stocks except F.

### 3. Optimum Stabilization of Escapement (Table V)

With the fishery taking everything above the escapement norm, averages rose by 36% for A, 22% for B, 20% for C, 30% for F and 12% for H, relative to Table III. Population A had no catch in 8 of 24 years. Overall, optimal regulation improves average take by 12% to 36% over the best constant percentage, with the largest benefit for low-potential stocks such as A.

### 4. Greater Variability (Level B, Tables VI–VII)

- With constant exploitation, averages at Level B were close to the no-variability values for A, B and C. H rose by 31%.
- With optimum escapement control, averages increased by 79% (A), 62% (B), 55% (C), 64% (F) and 26% (H) over the constant-exploitation values of Table VI.
- Gains from regulation are larger under greater variability, because environmental effects are multiplicative while catches are averaged arithmetically. Year-to-year variability is much larger, especially for A-type populations.

### 5. Type G Reproduction: Vulnerability at Low Stock Density

- Curve G has the same maximum equilibrium yield as Curve A (0.330 at 43.3% exploitation, spawning stock 0.433).
- Its critical threshold is 0.34 spawners: below this it cannot compensate for 43% exploitation and collapses.
- Under constant exploitation at either variability level, spawners fell below 1% of replacement and catch became negligible after 9 or 10 generations, i.e., extinction.
- Escapement-first regulation (Table VIII) prevented collapse, giving an average catch of about 0.36 (Level A) and 0.33 (Level B), with large year-to-year variation.
- Optimum regulation (Table IX) gave yields about 30% above the 0.33 obtained without environmental variation, though the Level B yield was 25% lower than that of Curve A.

### 6. Common Exploitation of Different Stocks

- Equilibrium maximum yields (Table II): A 0.330 at 43.3%; B 0.935 at 72.2%; C 1.656 at 84.1%; F 0.760 at 78.3%; H 0.520 at 68.4%.
- C's maximum sustained yield is five times that of A. Optimal exploitation rates differ strongly among stocks.
- When two stocks are fished together, the maximum yield may keep both alive (A+B), or may require exterminating one (A+C, where C alone contributes; A+B+C, where A is exterminated).
- Combined yield curves are not smooth; they show reentrant points near the exploitation rate at which a stock can no longer persist.
- A non-selective fishery can eliminate some runs in a region as an unavoidable side effect of reaching maximum sustained yield. If maximum yield is the objective, gear, season or boundary changes that allow separate harvest of each unit are desirable.

### 7. Management Implications

- Large fluctuations in catch and complete fishing closures in some years are a necessary condition for maximum average yield in salmon fisheries, not evidence of poor management.
- Conclusions from the models largely match long-standing salmon management practice and provide objective justification for regulations.
- Two checks support the robustness of the 24-generation averages: the factor sequence multiplies to nearly 1, and tests with 12 generations gave very similar comparisons.
- The conclusions remain reasonably valid when stocks vary from year to year because of the environment.
