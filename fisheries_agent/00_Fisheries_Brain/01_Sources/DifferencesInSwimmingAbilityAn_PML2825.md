# Differences in Swimming Ability and Behavior in Response to High Water Velocities Among Native and Nonnative Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Ward, Schultz & Matson (Environmental Biology of Fishes, 2003) tests whether differences in [[Swimming Performance]] explain why [[Nonnative Species|nonnative fishes]] are disproportionately removed by floods in southwestern U.S. streams. Laboratory [[Swim Tunnel]] tests on 8 native and 4 nonnative species of similar size found that most nonnative species swam about as well as native species. The authors conclude that physiological swimming ability does not adequately explain [[Differential Displacement]] during floods. Instead, behavioral responses to high flow, such as clinging to the substrate, are more likely the mechanism that lets native fishes persist.

## Background & Description

Many native fishes of the southwestern United States have declined because of interactions with nonnative species. In canyon-bound streams, [[Flash Flooding]] may allow native species to persist alongside established nonnative populations. Earlier work by [[Minckley & Meffe 1987]] documented shifts from mixed native and nonnative assemblages before floods to predominantly native assemblages afterward. They hypothesized that native fishes evolved under wide fluctuations in discharge and velocity, which would explain their lower displacement.

Swimming tests in the literature are hard to compare because methods vary, and results can depend on fish size and water temperature. This study therefore tested fish of similar size at a single temperature. Its stated objectives were to evaluate physiological and behavioral differences in swimming ability that might explain differential displacement.

**Study species (12 total, from the Table):**
- **Native (8):** [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Spikedace]]
- **Nonnative (4):** [[Mosquitofish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Green Sunfish]]

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Collection and holding:**
- Fish were collected by backpack [[Electrofishing]] from the Colorado, Gila, Little Colorado, Verde and Santa Cruz Rivers in Arizona, September 2001 to January 2002.
- About 10 to 22 individuals per species were transported to the Environmental Research Laboratory in Tucson.
- Fish were held in 0.5 m³ recirculating tanks at 20 °C (±1 °C) for at least 24 h before testing.
- Fish were tested within 72 h of capture, because earlier work found that long-term captivity reduces swimming ability.

**Size and temperature control:**
- Most species averaged about 70 mm total length.
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (about 40.5 mm) and [[Mosquitofish]] (about 41.6 mm) were smaller, but similar to each other.
- Spikedace was smaller than the other species, though its mean value is not legible in the source.
- Testing at 20 °C fell within the normal thermal range for all species.

**Apparatus:** A centrifugal pump circulated water between two 150 L reservoirs through a clear acrylic swim chamber (50 × 75 cm). A gate valve controlled flow, and a flow meter measured velocity to within 1 cm/s.

## Key Findings

**Test protocol**
- Fish were tested in pairs of the same species, after a 10 min acclimation at 75% of the average pilot failure velocity.
- Velocity rose in 4 cm/s steps every 5 min, up to a maximum of 96 cm/s.
- A fish was scored as failed if it became pinned against the downstream screen and stayed motionless for 10 s.
- Statistics: two-sample t-tests compared Gila topminnow and mosquitofish. ANOVA with Tukey-Kramer comparisons were used for the other species.

**Length comparisons**
- Gila topminnow and mosquitofish lengths did not differ significantly (p = 0.136).
- All other species except spikedace had similar mean lengths (about 70 mm; p > 0.05).

**Key patterns**
- **Native vs. nonnative swimming:** Nonnative [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] swam about as well as native [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Spikedace]]. [[Mosquitofish]] matched [[Gila Topminnow]].
- **Green sunfish:** Ranked lowest among the species tested, yet it persists in many unregulated southwestern streams.
- **Catostomids:** Large differences existed. [[Desert Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] outperformed [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], but their values are unreliable as true failure velocities. Most desert suckers and many bluehead suckers did not fail at the 96 cm/s maximum.
- **Behavioral resistance:** All desert suckers and 81% of bluehead suckers repeatedly suctioned their mouths onto the tunnel bottom to avoid swimming. About 47% of the 19 [[Speckled Dace]] tested pressed their pectoral fins against the bottom to hold position without swimming.

**Interpretation**
- Swimming ability does not adequately explain differential displacement of nonnative fishes.
- Earlier field observations by [[Meffe 1984]] and [[Minckley & Meffe 1987]] showed that [[Gila Topminnow]] oriented to high flows and held in low-velocity areas, while [[Mosquitofish]] moved into midstream and were displaced. The authors therefore attribute the difference to behavior rather than physiology.
- [[Harvey 1987]] found displacement susceptibility declines rapidly with fish size. Size-related swimming differences may therefore still matter in the field.
- Morphology also influences swimming. Desert and bluehead suckers have disc-shaped lips and rounded-above, flattened-below bodies that help them hold position and feed in fast water. Speckled dace may gain energetic advantages on natural substrates through similar bottom-holding behavior.

**Management implications**
- Barriers to upstream fish movement may reduce recolonization by nonnative species after floods.
- Dam releases on regulated rivers could be timed to mimic natural floods and displace nonnative fishes, though effects depend on release magnitude, timing and channel characteristics.
- Flooding gives native fishes only short-term advantages, but it may still be useful as a conservation tool.
- Field observations during actual or simulated floods are needed to identify additional native behaviors.
