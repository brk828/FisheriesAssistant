# Life History Studies of the Alabama Shad (*Alosa alabamae*) in the Apalachicola River, Florida

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by Laurence and Yerger (Florida State University) reports the first detailed life-history data for the [[Alabama Shad]], a Gulf coast anadromous clupeid about which knowledge was "almost nonexistent." Adults were sampled on their 1966 spawning run in the [[Apalachicola River]] system from February to April. The study covered age structure, sex ratios, abundance and movements, spawning conditions, fecundity, length-weight relationship, adult and juvenile diet, and juvenile growth. Spawning occurred abruptly at about 20°C, largely below [[Jim Woodruff Dam]], and adults died or left the area after spawning.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** *Alosa alabamae*, first noted ascending Gulf drainages around 1850 and described as distinct from the [[American Shad]] (*Alosa sapidissima*) by Jordan and Evermann in 1896.
- **Range:** Reported from most major Gulf coast drainages, from the Suwannee River (Florida) to the Washita River (Arkansas). In Florida it is the most abundant anadromous fish on the Gulf coast, most numerous in the Apalachicola system.
- **Study area:** The Apalachicola River is the largest drainage east of Mobile Bay. Its origin was historically the Flint–Chattahoochee confluence, but the completion of Jim Woodruff Dam created [[Lake Seminole]], so the river now begins below the dam. The Chipola River, a spring-fed clear stream from near Marianna, is the main tributary.
- **Motivation:** Lack of information and the potential sport and commercial value of the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Adults ascended the river and the Chipola River on spawning runs. Spawning was concentrated in the sand-and-gravel, moderate-current reach below Woodruff Dam. Juveniles used lower river banks, with the lower reaches of the Apalachicola as nursery habitat.
- **Water temperature:** Entry began at about 15°C (February 28). Large numbers appeared at 17°C (March 16). Spawning took place at about 20°C. Adults became scarce at 22°C and disappeared after 23°C was recorded on April 30.
- **Turbidity and mortality:** Turbid water, steep banks, rapid sinking of dead shad, and predator abundance in the spawning area made post-spawning carcasses difficult to find.
- **Food web:** Juveniles fed on small fishes, notably juvenile [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense* and *D. cepedianum*), which spawned just after the Alabama shad. Adults fed little or not at all during the run.
- **Methods:** Gill nets, dipnets at the Woodruff tailrace, seines, and a modified shrimp trawl. Scales were read using [[Cating Scale Method]]. Eggs were counted using a method modified from [[Davis Fecundity Method]] and Lehman (1953).

## Key Findings

**Sampling and abundance**
- 141 adult shad were captured (65 males, 76 females) from February 28 to April 30, 1966.
- Shad entered in late February and peaked in mid-to-late April. Males predominated early (over 3:1 before March 25). Females outnumbered males from March 25 onward.

**Age and size**
- Four age classes were found, with no fish older than four years:
  - Age I: 4 males, avg 10.6 in TL
  - Age II: 24 males (avg 13.4 in), 8 females (avg 14.5 in)
  - Age III: 35 males (avg 14.4 in), 49 females (avg 15.3 in); the most abundant class
  - Age IV: 7 males (avg 15.1 in), 14 females (avg 16.1 in)
- Annuli groove counts per age: 6–9 (age 1), 9–12 (age 2), 12–15 (age 3), 15+ (age 4).
- The authors suggest Alabama shad mature about one year earlier and at smaller size than American shad in the St. Johns River.

**Spawning repetition**
- Spawning marks were found on 2-, 3-, and 4-year-old males and 3- and 4-year-old females.
- Previously spawned fish: 16 of 35 age-III males (46%) and 22 of 49 age-III females (45%); 8 of 14 age-IV females (57%).
- The authors conclude some mortality occurs, but not necessarily after the first spawning.

**Length-weight and fecundity**
- Length-weight relationship (n = 120; 60 males, 60 females): log W = −2.8312 + 3.5657 log L. Lengths ranged 9.9–16.8 in (mean 14.6 in); weights 4 oz to 2 lb 6 oz (mean 1 lb 6 oz).
- Females were longer and heavier than males in every age class.
- Fecundity (31 females): 46,400 to 149,450 eggs. Ovary counts ranged 40,000–150,000 in the abstract. Complete counts on two females were 6% and 8% above calculated values.
- Fecundity is lower than American shad in the St. Johns River (277,000–659,000 eggs) and similar to Hudson River shad (116,000–468,000) once age and size are considered.

**Spawning conditions**
- Ovary weight peaked at 19°C (average 117.3 g on April 1) and dropped to 71.5 g at 20°C (April 18). Eggs could be stripped freely at 20°C, and the authors infer abrupt spawning followed by death or departure.
- Spawning was located largely below Woodruff Dam. The authors note that the dam's effect on historical headwater spawning is unknown.

**Diet**
- Adults: of 100 stomachs examined, unidentifiable insect remains and detritus appeared in only 2; 21 were coated with green slime. Unbaited silver hooks were effective, but the sport fishery is limited.
- Juveniles (summer 1966): Woodruff Dam area (101 stomachs) had greater mean volume per stomach (0.12 cc vs 0.03 cc) and more fish prey (35% vs 12% by volume). Dipterans dominated downriver (38% volume, 217 occurrences).

**Juvenile growth and origin**
- Juveniles collected at Woodruff Dam: average TL 1.8 in (May 26), 2.6 in (June 21), 4.5 in (September 2, a distinct larger group), 5.2 in (September 13).
- Downriver juveniles (August–September) were smaller, averaging 2.2–2.7 in. The authors hypothesize they came from the Chipola River, but this remains unproven.

**Conclusions**
- Alabama shad mature earlier and smaller than American shad, have lower fecundity, and likely spawn in at least two areas of the drainage.
- Juveniles are opportunistic feeders.
