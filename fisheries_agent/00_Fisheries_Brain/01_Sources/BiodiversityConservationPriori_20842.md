# Biodiversity Conservation Priorities for the Chihuahuan Desert Ecoregion Complex: Freshwater Approach and Workshop Agenda

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This document is a workshop planning packet, not a research study. It comprises a cover letter dated September 24, 1997, from Robin Abell of the [[World Wildlife Fund]] (WWF), a goals-and-approach statement, a 13-task workshop agenda, a threat-scoring method, and a glossary. Its purpose was to prepare participants for the Chihuahuan Desert workshop held September 30 to October 2, 1997, in Monterrey, Mexico.

The packet sets out a modified, freshwater-specific version of the [[Ecoregion-based Conservation]] planning approach. The freshwater approach follows the terrestrial method but uses the twelve [[Freshwater Ecoregions]] that overlap the [[Chihuahuan Desert Ecoregion Complex]] rather than terrestrial habitat types, and it applies different intactness and threat categories. The goal is a conservation strategy for freshwater biodiversity that can feed into a larger Chihuahuan conservation strategy.

## Background & Description

**Origin and purpose.** The letter notes that some aspects of the terrestrial site-identification approach were "not entirely appropriate to freshwater habitats." The sections substantially modified for freshwater are Tasks 1, 2, 7, 8 and 10.

**Conservation goal.** The primary goal is a biodiversity conservation strategy that:
- conserves the full range of distinct natural communities and ecological and evolutionary phenomena;
- maintains viable species populations;
- sustains the ecological processes and services that maintain biodiversity;
- protects habitat blocks large enough to respond to short- and long-term change.

**Four fundamental goals** guide the assessment: representation of distinct communities in protected areas; maintenance of ecological and evolutionary processes; maintenance of viable populations; and conservation of large natural habitat blocks.

**Four conservation targets** are proposed:
1. Distinct communities, habitats, and assemblages (e.g., closed basin lakes, gypsum dunes, springs and caves with high endemism).
2. Larger examples of intact habitats and biotas.
3. Keystone ecosystems, habitats, or phenomena (e.g., riparian habitats and desert springs that sustain surrounding vertebrate populations).
4. Distinct large-scale ecological phenomena, such as migration.

**Biogeographic framework.** The Chihuahuan Desert provides the spatial framework for both terrestrial and freshwater analyses. The terrestrial work uses four ecoregions (Saladan, Trans-Pecos, Mapimian, Apachean). Freshwater ecoregions, delineated in earlier WWF freshwater assessments, have different boundaries. Because they were delineated mainly from freshwater fish distributions, the freshwater ecoregion step was effectively complete before the workshop.

**Workshop structure.** Experts sat at tables by six taxonomic groups (plants, invertebrates, herpetofauna, birds, mammals, freshwater taxa) for the first phase, and then by four habitat types (desert scrub and woodlands, grasslands, chaparral and montane woodlands, aquatic habitats) for the later phase. Each task used a colored pen for mapping:

| Task | Focus | Pen |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | Species richness foci | Red |
| 4 | Endemism foci | Green |
| 4 (sub) | Unique higher taxa | Dark green |
| 5 | Rare ecological and evolutionary phenomena | Orange |
| 6 | Critical sites for large-scale phenomena | Purple |
| 2 | Taxon-specific subdivisions | Blue |

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Scale and boundaries.** Freshwater analysis covers all portions of freshwater ecoregions within the Desert Complex or draining into it. Upstream activities are treated as capable of affecting downstream communities. Sites outside and downstream of the Complex are not prioritized. For example, the lower [[Rio Grande-Rio Bravo]] reach beyond the desert is excluded, while upper Rio Grande headwaters upstream of the desert may be considered.

**Regional features named in the text:**
- [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], cited for pronounced radiations, unusual adaptations, and highly local endemism.
- Closed basin lakes, springs, and caves, cited as sites with high endemism.
- [[Madrean Sky Islands]], treated as a northern extension of the Sierra Madre Occidental.
- Montane areas of the northeast, treated as a northern extension of the Sierra Madre Oriental.
- Meseta Central, Apachean region, and Madrean Sky Islands, included in the analysis.

**Habitat intactness categories** apply to aquatic habitats:
- **Intact:** upstream land uses limited or well managed; few exotic species; most native communities and ecological processes retained.
- **Altered:** species composition changed, with significant native species absent but likely to return given recovery time. Examples include streams receiving point-source pollution, reaches isolated by lowhead dams, and areas where riparian cover was removed.
- **Heavily Altered:** habitat nearly completely altered, with recovery unlikely without large, expensive restoration. Examples include dewatered or heavily channelized streams in agricultural areas and highly polluted urban or industrial lakes.

**Threat types** are grouped into three classes: conversion, degradation, and wildlife exploitation. Catchment-scale threats include intensive logging, intensive grazing (especially in riparian zones), agricultural expansion, and urbanization. Habitat threats include degraded water quality, altered hydrographic integrity from dams and withdrawals, fragmentation, and siltation. Biota threats include unsustainable fishing or hunting, commercial wildlife extraction, and established exotic species.

## Key Findings

The packet does not report new empirical data, so its key points are its plans, methods, and rules.

**1. Freshwater analysis is adapted to watersheds.** Freshwater ecoregions differ from the four terrestrial units. Representation is therefore assessed across twelve freshwater ecoregions, and habitat types are assessed within those ecoregions. The stated rationale is that species and assemblages within a given habitat type differ among ecoregions.

**2. Richness and endemism use tiered thresholds.** Experts were to identify:
- very high richness (top 10% of richest sites, approximate) and high richness (top 20%);
- very high endemism (top 10%) and high endemism (top 20%).

Comparisons were limited to assemblages within the Chihuahuan ecoregion complex. Endemism was to be judged at subdivision or local scales (ranges, basins, dune systems). Species endemic to the whole ecoregion were not recommended, except for specialized habitats such as gypsum dunes.

**3. Representation rules.** Each habitat type must appear in the priority portfolio for the entire ecoregion. Larger intact blocks should be selected first, with at least three sites per habitat type for replication. Additional sites may be added for distinct phenomena, critical processes, genetic resources, ecosystem services, educational value, or the need for biological inventories.

**4. Site numbering.** Freshwater sites are numbered starting with "5" (e.g., 5.1, 5.2, ... 5.10) to distinguish them from terrestrial sites.

**5. Threat scoring system.** Each site receives a 0–100 threat index:

| Component | Maximum points | Scoring |
|---|---|---|
| Catchment-scale threats | 40 | 25% or more of catchment altered within 20 years = 40; 10–24% = 25; 5–9% = 10; none = 0 |
| Habitat threats | 30 | High = 30; Medium = 15; none = 0 |
| Biota threats | 30 | High = 30; Moderate = 15; none = 0 |

Overall threat level: 70–100 High; 20–69 Medium; 0–20 Low. Catchment threats are weighted most heavily because they are the most far-reaching and difficult to reverse.

**6. Priority site characterization.** Experts were to complete information sheets for each site, covering:
- primary reason(s) for selection (ideally one or two categories);
- priority rank (1 = Highest, 2 = High, 3 = Priority);
- important biodiversity features;
- threats, land ownership, appropriate conservation activities, and partners.

Priority sites were defined as areas of a few tens of square kilometers (single valleys, springs, small ranges, riparian corridors, dunes). Priority areas were larger units, such as whole basins or mountain ranges.

**7. Conservation status focus.** Experts were first to map intact and partially degraded habitats, since time was limited. Heavily altered areas were deferred to the final day for discussion of ecoregion-wide threats such as grazing policy. Rare habitat types whose only examples are heavily degraded were flagged for immediate attention.

**8. Workshop outputs.** The planned products were:
- data layers (biogeographic subdivisions, habitat types, intactness of habitats and biotas, richness and endemism foci, and important areas for phenomena and genetic resources);
- a prioritized set of characterized priority sites with threat analyses;
- a definition of conservation success for the Chihuahuan complex;
- a discussion of general threats and how to use the products.

**9. Defining success.** Task 13 was designed to define measurable endpoints and milestones for ecological integrity, a step the packet calls "perhaps the most critical" and one that planning efforts often omit.
