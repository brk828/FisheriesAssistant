# Importance of Wetlands to Endangered and Threatened Species

## Executive Summary
This 1978 review by Williams and Dodd synthesizes evidence for the dependence of endangered and threatened U.S. flora and fauna—including fishes, reptiles, amphibians, birds, and mammals—on wetland habitats. While much of the paper centers on the [[American Alligator]] and other wetland vertebrates, it provides critical ichthyological data on wetland-dependent fishes, particularly desert spring endemics and small-stream spawning specialists. The authors report that of 41 U.S. fish species/subspecies listed as endangered or threatened at the time, 22 (53.6%) depend on wetlands during part of their life cycle—the highest proportional dependency of any taxonomic group examined. The paper highlights the fragility of desert wetland systems (e.g., [[Ash Meadows]], Nevada) as centers of fish endemism and extinction risk, and documents the first documented U.S. fish extinction attributable to human habitat alteration, the [[Harelip Sucker]].

## Background & Description
The paper is a review/case-study synthesis rather than a primary field study, drawing on secondary sources to illustrate how specific wetland types support endangered taxa. Fish-relevant case studies include:

- **[[Slackwater Darter]]** (*Etheostoma boschungi*, family [[Percidae]]): a small (50–60 mm) colorful darter endemic to northern Alabama and southern Tennessee. It inhabits small-to-moderate slackwater creeks (up to 12 m wide, 2 m deep) but is obligately dependent on ephemeral seepage wetlands in adjacent open fields for reproduction.
- **[[Harelip Sucker]]** (*Lagochila lacera*, family [[Catostomidae]]): cited as apparently the first U.S. fish species driven extinct by human activity (pre-1900), attributed to habitat alteration rather than direct exploitation.
- **[[Ash Meadows Killifish]]** (*Empetrichthys merriami*): a desert spring endemic of the Death Valley System, confirmed extinct at the time of writing due to spring and wetland habitat loss.
- **[[Owens Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon radiosus*, family [[Cyprinodontidae]]): a Death Valley System endemic from the Owens River Valley, California, once believed extinct; rediscovered and rescued via a multi-agency wetland restoration and captive/refuge program.
- Unnamed native fishes of gator holes in the Everglades/Big Cypress Swamp, documented at extraordinary densities during dry-season refugia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Wetland types discussed relevant to fish:**
- **Alligator-engineered wetlands ("gator holes")**: Dry-season refugia created and maintained by the [[American Alligator]] in the Everglades and Big Cypress Swamp. These concentrate aquatic organisms and are critical stepping-stone habitats.
- **Slackwater creek/seepage wetland complex**: Supports the reproductive migration and larval development of the Slackwater Darter.
- **Desert spring and seep systems**: Ash Meadows (Nevada/California) and the Owens River Valley (California), both part of the Death Valley System, represent isolated Pleistocene-relict aquatic habitats supporting high endemism and extreme vulnerability to groundwater pumping and diversion.
- **Salt marsh and estuarine systems**: Referenced in the context of the [[Whooping Crane]] and salt marsh snake, illustrating broader wetland food-web linkages (crustaceans, fish) though not fish-specific data.

**Threats identified:**
- Groundwater pumping and surface-water diversion for agriculture (Ash Meadows, Owens Valley).
- Drainage and channelization of small wetlands and seepage areas (Slackwater Darter spawning habitat).
- Introduction of exotic/predatory fishes (contributing to Owens Pupfish decline).
- Water-level manipulation via dams and dikes reducing prey concentration in alligator holes, with cascading effects on piscivorous birds (e.g., [[Wood Ibis]]).
- Historical habitat destruction predating modern conservation awareness, implicated in the extinction of the Harelip Sucker.

## Key Findings

- **Gator hole fish densities**: Kushlan (1972) recorded densities of **1,600 fish and prawns per square meter** in an alligator hole in Big Cypress Swamp, with as many as **23 fish species** concentrated in a single gator hole during dry-season drawdown.
- **Trophic linkage to bird breeding success**: Concentration of fish in gator holes during the dry season (November–April) is essential to successful reproduction of the [[Wood Ibis]] (*Mycteria americana*); in abnormally wet years when fish fail to concentrate, breeding may fail entirely.
- **Slackwater Darter reproductive ecology**: Spawning migrations of 1.6–5 km upstream occur December–January, with eggs deposited exclusively on *Juncus acuminatus* in shallow seepage wetlands (2–8 cm deep); larvae remain in wetland habitat 4–6 weeks post-hatch before moving to the main stream. Loss of these small, easily overlooked wetlands would directly threaten population persistence.
- **Ash Meadows fish endemism and extinction**: Of 18 fish species/subspecies endemic to the Death Valley System, 3 species and 2 subspecies occurred in Ash Meadows; the **Ash Meadows Killifish is confirmed extinct**, and remaining populations were "seriously depleted" due to groundwater pumping, especially in the late 1960s–early 1970s.
- **Owens Pupfish recovery case study**: A successful multi-stakeholder conservation model—California Department of Fish and Game, Los Angeles Water Department, and Federal agencies—established the Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary in Fish Slough (1968), rehabilitating wetland habitat and reintroducing the species, demonstrating that cooperative habitat restoration can reverse near-extinction.
- **First U.S. fish extinction from human habitat alteration**: The Harelip Sucker (*Lagochila lacera*) is cited as the first documented U.S. fish extinction caused by human activity, occurring prior to 1900—predating the overkill-driven extinctions typical of that era.
- **National-level statistics**: Of 41 listed U.S. endangered/threatened fish species and subspecies, **22 (53.6%)** were found in wetland habitats during part of their life cycle—proportionally greater wetland dependency than reported for mammals (15.2%), birds (31%), or reptiles (30.8%), though lower than amphibians (50%, comparable).
- **Amazonian crocodilian-fish linkage** (context for broader nutrient-fish dynamics): Removal of crocodilians (*Melanosuchus niger*, *Caiman crocodilus*) in nutrient-poor Amazonian mouth-lakes—intended to reduce fish predation—instead caused a **sharp decline in fish production**, illustrating an indirect trophic/nutrient-cycling dependency of fisheries on top predators in wetland systems, with anecdotal parallel reports from the Okefenokee Swamp linking alligator abundance to fishing success.
