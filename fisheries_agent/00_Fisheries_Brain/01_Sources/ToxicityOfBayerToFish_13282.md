# Toxicity of Bayer 73 to Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife report (Resource Publication 36, April 1967) by [[Leif L. Marking]] and James W. Hogan evaluates the toxicity of [[Bayer 73]] (sold as [[Bayluscide]]), a [[Molluscicide]] used against snails that transmit [[Schistosomiasis]], to 18 freshwater fish species. Standard 96-hour [[Static Bioassay]] tests gave 50-percent mortality (LC50) values of 0.043 to 0.230 p.p.m. Temperature and water hardness had little effect on toxicity, while extreme pH (5 and 10) markedly reduced it. The authors conclude that the compound is highly toxic to fish at molluscicidal concentrations and deserves consideration as a general fish toxicant because it biodegrades and is relatively safe in water.

## Background & Description

- **Compound:** 2',5-dichloro-4'-nitrosalicylanilide, supplied as a 2-aminoethanol salt (control No. C910, 99 percent purity, from Chemagro Corporation). It is a bright yellow crystalline powder.
- **Origin:** Its molluscicidal activity was first recognized by Gonnert and Schraufstatter (1958). It belongs to the [[Salicylanilides]] class, which has antimicrobial, germicidal and antitumoral activity.
- **Prior knowledge:** Earlier work showed the compound is ovicidal and cercaricidal to snails (Gillet and Braux, 1962), is relatively nontoxic to mammals (Hecht and Gloxhuber, 1962; Duhm, 1963), and is highly toxic to [[Sea Lamprey]] larvae and [[Rainbow Trout]] (Howell et al., 1964). Fish kills had been reported at 0.3 p.p.m. (Gonnert, 1962; Webbe, 1963).
- **Purpose:** Define the compound's toxicity to fish across laboratory environments and assess its potential for controlling fish populations, given its planned wide use in aquatic habitats.
- **Test facilities:** [[Fish Control Laboratories]] at La Crosse, Wisconsin, and Warm Springs, Georgia.

### Methods

- **Test fish:** Juveniles (typically about 1.5 to 2.3 inches and under 3 grams) from national, state and private hatcheries, acclimated per [[Lennon and Walker]] (1964). Eighteen species were tested (Table 1).
- **Preliminary screening:** 15 liters of medium at 12 °C (La Crosse) or 17 °C (Warm Springs), exposed to 0.1, 1.0 and 10.0 p.p.m.
- **Delineative tests:** 10 fish per concentration, at least 9 concentrations plus a control. Variables were temperature, [[Reconstituted Water]] hardness (soft, medium, hard; Table 2) and buffered pH (Table 3).
- **Statistics:** Probit-type analysis after [[Litchfield and Wilcoxon]] (1949) for LC50 values, slope functions and 95-percent confidence intervals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mode of action and response:** Exposed fish became irritated and excitable, breathed rapidly and irregularly, swam aimlessly, lost equilibrium at the surface, and died with spastic movements and extended opercula.
- **Degradation:** Toxicity changed little between 1 and 96 hours, which suggests rapid breakdown by water or fish. Rats excrete the compound mainly as a reduced, detoxified amino form (Duhm et al., 1963); fish may detoxify it similarly.
- **Light and water chemistry:** In hard water, sunlight reduced concentration from 1.0 to 0.57 p.p.m. in 16 hours, while darkness caused no change over 312 hours (Meyling et al., 1962). Solubility rises with pH from below 1.0 to 5.0 p.p.m. between pH 6 and 8 (Meyling and Pitchford, 1966).
- **Snail and plankton effects:** Gonnert (1961) reported that minimum lethal concentrations for snails were 0.2 to 0.5 p.p.m., and that 1.0 p.p.m. destroyed snails in standing and flowing water. Shiff and Garnett (1961) recorded short-lived reductions in planktonic life after 1.0 p.p.m., with populations normal again after 32 days.
- **Non-target safety:** Dogs tolerated 0.1 g/kg orally (Hecht and Gloxhuber, 1962). Plants survived molluscicidal concentrations (Foster, 1962; Holz and Hwa, 1963).

## Key Findings

**1. Broad toxicity across species (96-hour LC50, Table 4)**
- The compound is toxic to all 18 species tested, with 96-hour LC50 values from 0.043 p.p.m. (flathead catfish) to 0.230 p.p.m. (goldfish).
- Most sensitive: flathead catfish, rainbow trout, brown bullhead, smallmouth bass, brook trout and largemouth bass, in that order.
- Most resistant: goldfish, then carp (LC50 0.148 p.p.m. as stated in the conclusions).
- Intermediate: white sucker, yellow perch, channel catfish, black bullhead, redear sunfish, bluegill, green sunfish, tilapia and fathead minnow.
- The compound is not specific to undesirable species; it is generally toxic to game and rough fish.

**2. Exposure time**
- Exposure beyond 24 hours added little mortality; 24- and 48-hour LC50 values overlapped for all species.
- Rainbow trout, brook trout and yellow perch responded within 1 to 2 hours. Carp, white suckers and black bullheads responded more slowly.
- Slope functions (1.060 to 1.245) are low, so the dose-response curve is nearly vertical. Complete survival and complete mortality lie close together. For comparison, the slope for p,p'-DDT in goldfish is 6.02.

**3. Temperature (7 to 22 °C)**
- No significant effect on rainbow trout, and goldfish, channel catfish and bluegills were not significantly affected in general.
- Bluegills were clearly more toxic at 22 °C than at 12 or 17 °C, and the effect increased with longer exposure.

**4. Water quality (hardness)**
- Rainbow trout were more sensitive in soft water. The largest drop in toxicity occurred between medium and hard water, which the authors link to degradation at higher pH and alkalinity.
- Channel catfish were less affected by hardness until hard water, where toxicity dropped significantly.
- Bluegill LC50 values in hard water were two or more times those in medium water.
- Overall, water quality does not greatly change toxicity across the pH range 6.4 to 8.0.

**5. pH extremes**
- Buffered pH 5 and pH 10 significantly reduced toxicity to goldfish. Concentrations above 1.0 p.p.m. were needed to kill fish only at these extremes.
- Likely causes: decreased ionization or solubility, or metabolic reduction stimulated in fish.

**6. Comparison with other toxicants**
- Catfishes, though sensitive to Bayer 73, resist antimycin A and rotenone.
- The compound may therefore be a useful tool for fish species resistant to other chemicals.

**7. Conclusions and recommendations**
- Concentrations lethal to snails in the laboratory are also toxic to fish.
- Applications of 1.0 p.p.m. are probably toxic to fish in natural waters unless pH reaches the extremes of 5 or 10.
- Because the compound biodegrades and is safe in water, the authors recommend considering it as a general fish toxicant.

**Note on the source:** The report is a research publication (static laboratory bioassays with an extensive reference list). The report notes the "Bayer 73" name, but the common trade name "Bayluscide" is the same product.
