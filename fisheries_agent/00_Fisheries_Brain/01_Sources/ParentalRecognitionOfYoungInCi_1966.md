# Parental Recognition of Young in Cichlid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cichlidae #parental-care #chemoreception #ethology

## Executive Summary

This 1966 paper by Arthur A. Myrberg Jr. re-examines the mechanisms by which parental cichlid fishes discriminate between their own offspring and those of other broods or species, resolving conflicting conclusions from earlier studies (Noble & Curtis 1939; Baerends & Baerends-Van Roon 1950; Greenberg 1963a, 1963b). Myrberg demonstrates that cichlid parents generally cannot distinguish eggs of different species but **can** discriminate between free-swimming and wiggling young of different species, and that this discrimination relies on a **developmental switch in sensory modality**: chemoreception dominates during the wiggler stage, while vision becomes dominant once young reach the free-swimming stage. The paper also defends Myrberg's earlier (1961/1964) methodology against methodological criticisms raised by [[Greenberg 1963B]].

## Background & Description

- **Central research question**: Can parental [[Cichlidae|cichlid]] fishes discriminate between young of their own species versus other cichlid species, and between their own young versus foreign young of the same species, across different developmental stages (eggs, wigglers, free-swimming fry)?
- **Study species** (Major series): [[Hemichromis Bimaculatus]] (jewel fish), [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]], [[Cichlasoma Nigrofasciatum]]; secondary data from [[Hemichromis Fasciatus]].
- **Developmental stages defined**:
  - *Eggs* — pre-hatching.
  - *Wigglers* — immediate post-hatching phase, lasting ~3 days, non-free-swimming.
  - *Free-swimming young* — fry capable of independent swimming.
- **Historical context**: [[Noble & Curtis 1939]] concluded parents could not distinguish eggs or wigglers between species but could distinguish free-swimming young under specific conditions related to age differences and prior brooding experience. [[Tinbergen 1951]] and [[Thorpe 1956]] interpreted this recognition ability as an example of **adult imprinting**, a claim Myrberg revisits and partially refutes.
- **Methodological approach** (Myrberg's Major series, 1961/1964):
  - Isolated breeding pairs in individual aquaria (chemically and visually isolated from other pairs).
  - Egg slates exchanged between pairs immediately after spawning, using eggs of matching developmental stage in 260 of 280 exchanges (>90%).
  - Foreign spawns left undisturbed with the test pair (not removed once introduced) to observe natural acceptance/rejection dynamics over a ~30-day rearing period.
  - A shorter follow-up series (**MP series**, 1964) used similar exchange logic without full isolation between pairs.
- **Critique of Greenberg (1963a, 1963b)**: Myrberg rebuts claims that his methodology introduced bias via (1) mistimed parental cycles during spawn exchange, (2) use of unhatched eggs rather than wigglers, (3) lack of pre-conditioning of test pairs to reduce "over-sensitivity." Myrberg argues Greenberg's own protocols (e.g., pair 11 accepting 6–7-day-old free-swimming young when its own eggs were only 1 day old) fail to support Greenberg's proposed variables (developmental correspondence, behavioral similarity, cycle timing) as consistent predictors of acceptance/rejection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Territoriality as a natural chemical isolation mechanism**: Myrberg proposes that strong territorial behavior typical of many cichlids in the wild would functionally isolate broods chemically and visually during the vulnerable early wiggler stage (housed in a nesting pit deep within the territory), permitting the parent to learn the visual characteristics of its own young via chemosensory mediation before the free-swimming stage — when territorial boundaries loosen and reliance shifts to vision.
- **Species variability in preferential care**: Not all cichlids show discriminative brood care. [[Collins & Braddock 1962]] found *Tilapia sparrmani* and *Aequidens portalegrensis* accepted wigglers and free-swimming young of both species indiscriminately, indicating preferential care is **not a universal cichlid trait** and likely reflects ecological/behavioral diversity across the family's hundreds of species.
- **Cross-genus acceptance anomaly**: A pair of *C. biocellatum* (pair E) accepted and reared young of *[[Aequidens Pulcher]]* after tending its own young previously — attributed to close similarity in chromatic pattern and size between the two species' young, illustrating how visual mimicry between unrelated species' offspring can override normal discrimination.
- **Sensory ecology implication**: The proposed modality switch (chemoreception → vision) aligns with the ecological transition of larvae from a sheltered, low-visibility nest/pit environment (wiggler stage) to open-water, high-visibility conditions (free-swimming stage), suggesting sensory reliance tracks habitat use during ontogeny.

## Key Findings

- **Eggs**: Most parental pairs in the Major series did **not** discriminate between eggs of different species — indicating no strong innate/early recognition cue at the egg stage (partial confirmation of [[Noble & Curtis 1939]] and [[Baerends & Baerends-Van Roon 1950]], though the latter found some egg-stage discrimination indications).
- **Wigglers and free-swimming young**: Parents reliably discriminated between young of the species being tended and young of another species, **regardless of developmental stage**, once past the egg stage.
- **Foreign young of the same species**: Parents accepted foreign, differently-aged young of the same species up to a point; *H. bimaculatus* was notably more sensitive/restrictive in this regard than *Cichlasoma* species.
- **Evidence consistent with imprinting-like learning in *Cichlasoma***: Pairs tending a foreign species' spawn continuously would, after gaining experience tending their own species' young, begin rejecting (mouthing, scattering, then eating) the foreign brood within days — a pattern also observed in *Hemichromis*, suggesting acquired species-recognition through experience with own young.
- **H. bimaculatus preference plasticity**: Preferential care could be altered by tending a foreign species' young during the *initial* brood period — unlike *Cichlasoma*, where this shift required prior experience rearing their own young first.
- **MP series results**: Showed little to no persistence of preferential care across later brood periods, contrasting with the Major series — attributed to lack of chemical isolation between pairs (all pairs shared water/chemical exposure despite optical separation).
- **Chemoreception dominates in early (wiggler) stage discrimination**:
  - [[Kühme 1963]] found *H. bimaculatus* parents possess an acute chemical sense mediating preferential care of young irrespective of age.
  - Myrberg replicated this in *C. nigrofasciatum*: brooding females rapidly located, via three-outlet apparatus, the water source connected to their own wigglers, fanning before it while biting at outlets connected to foreign young — even when tubing was transposed.
  - Sealed/transparent flasks containing young (visual-only cues) did **not** elicit preferential care, showing vision alone is insufficient at the wiggler stage.
  - When exposed simultaneously to chemical cues from own young and visual cues from foreign young (chemical cue placed beneath a sealed flask of foreign young), a parent could **learn** new visual preferences within ~15–18 hours — but only in the presence of chemical stimuli from its own young, implicating a chemically-mediated learning mechanism underlying eventual visual recognition.
  - Similar chemosensory (likely gustatory) discrimination reported in *[[Nannacara Anomala]]* by Kunzer (1964), with no evidence for non-gustatory chemoreception involvement.
- **Vision dominates in free-swimming stage discrimination**: Rapid, precise ("pin-point") discrimination between individual free-swimming young suggests vision becomes the primary modality once fry reach this stage ([[Noble & Curtis 1939]]; personal observation), even though chemical-only discrimination remains possible ([[Kühme 1963]]).
- **Proposed developmental sensory switch**: A transition from chemoreception to vision as the dominant discriminative modality occurs somewhere between the late wiggler phase and early free-swimming phase — vision-based recognition is hypothesized to be learned via association with chemical cues present during the wiggler stage.
- **Egg-stage chemical learning**: Experienced *C. nigrofasciatum* females began showing preferential care toward their own eggs only when eggs were within a few hours of hatching (coinciding with early breakdown of egg membranes), suggesting chemical information about a pair's own spawn is acquired during a **previous** brood period rather than being innate/genetically transmitted.
- **Species differences in learning predisposition**: *C. biocellatum* and *C. nigrofasciatum* showed rapid, stable learning suggesting a predisposition to learn species-specific cues in a set direction; *H. bimaculatus* learning was less predisposed and depended more heavily on initial tending experience.
- **Rebuttal of Greenberg's non-imprinting conclusion**: Myrberg argues Greenberg's (1963a, 1963b) failure to demonstrate discrimination in many trials reflects methodological confounds (uncontrolled variables, mismatched developmental stages, lack of chemical isolation) rather than genuine absence of a learned, imprinting-like recognition process.
- **Non-universality of preferential care**: [[Collins & Braddock 1962]]'s finding that *Tilapia sparrmani* and *Aequidens portalegrensis* fail to discriminate at all underscores that preferential brood care and associated recognition mechanisms are not fixed traits across [[Cichlidae]].
