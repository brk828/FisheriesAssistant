# The Reproductive Behavior of the Green Sunfish (Lepomis cyanellus)

## Executive Summary

This study by John R. Hunter (1963) provides the first detailed account of the reproductive behavior of the [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), based on two breeding seasons (1960–1961) of field observation in artificial ponds at the University of Wisconsin Arboretum. The central discovery is that male green sunfish exhibit **cyclical nest-construction periods**, occurring at a remarkably consistent interval of **8–9 days**, when nesting occurs in colonies. This synchronization breaks down when nests are not colonially arranged. The paper documents nest-site selection, territoriality, courtship/spawning mechanics, sexual recognition cues, nest-site homing, and duration of nest occupancy, situating green sunfish behavior within the broader context of [[Centrarchidae]] reproductive biology.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Lepomis cyanellus* (Rafinesque), the [[Green Sunfish]], family [[Centrarchidae]].
- **Study system**: Four artificial "Gardner Ponds" — two large ponds (D and E, ~2,023 m², max depth ~2 m, cinder-dammed) and two small ponds (Theta and Delta, ~5 m², max depth 1–1.5 m, with artificial cinder-filled spawning boxes).
- **Co-occurring species**: Northern redfin shiner (*Notropis umbratilis cyanocephalus*), northern black bullhead (*Ictalurus melas melas*), and brook stickleback (*Eucalia inconstans*).
- **Methods**: Daily monitoring of spawning grounds; individual nest mapping, marking, and protocol recording (construction date, occupation duration, desertion date); thermograph records of water temperature; sunshine data from the U.S. Weather Bureau; tagging of 25 male and 2 female sunfish with plastic identification tags; field experiments (nest/landmark displacement, model decoys) to test nest recognition and sexual recognition cues.
- **Sexual dimorphism**: During breeding season, females display a lateral series of dark vertical bars (also seen in frightened fish of both sexes). Males are larger, more brilliantly colored, and develop a yellowish-white margin along the dorsal, caudal, and anal fins roughly one week before first nesting; smaller males mature later in the season.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Substrate preference**: Males overwhelmingly built nests on cinder/gravel substrate rather than muck margins; when muck was used, nests were dug deep enough to expose underlying marl.
- **Microhabitat**: Nests were concentrated in unshaded, sun-exposed shallow water (typically <35 cm deep, as shallow as 4 cm for small males), often near shelter (rocks, logs, grass clumps).
- **Colonial nesting vs. dispersed nesting**: In large, densely populated ponds (E, D), nests formed tight colonies (as close as <2 cm apart); in small ponds (Theta), nests were widely dispersed (up to 30 m apart).
- **Temperature relationship**: A thermograph in Pond E linked rising mean water temperature to the onset of nesting periods, and declining temperature to longer inter-nesting intervals — though temperature rises did not *always* trigger nesting, indicating other regulatory factors (likely male reproductive/gonadal cycling) were also at play.
- **Sunshine**: No relationship was found between daily percent possible sunshine and nesting activity; sunfish nested and spawned during overcast conditions and thunderstorms alike — contrasting with Breder's (1936) report that pumpkinseed (*Lepomis gibbosus*, cf. [[Lepomis Gibbosus]]) retreat to deep water when clouds pass over the sun.
- **Anthropogenic disturbance**: Poaching of tagged males in Pond E during 1961 partially disrupted continuous behavioral records after July 10.

## Key Findings

### Nest Construction & Sexual Dimorphism
- Males dig nests via vertical rises above the substrate followed by vigorous outward tail thrusts, displacing sand/gravel to form a shallow depression — behavior typical of the family [[Centrarchidae]].
- Abandoned nests, especially large/deep ones, were sometimes reused; artificially deepened depressions created by the researcher were also colonized and used throughout the season.
- Pre-nesting aggregations near spawning grounds began 1–2 days before nest establishment, initially composed of large males, later including females and males of all sizes.

### Territorial Behavior & Aggression
- Colonial males (Pond E) defended only the nest area itself (nests <2 cm apart); isolated males (Pond Theta) defended a much larger territory (1–1.5 m diameter), with nests up to 30 m apart.
- Male-male combat (mouth-to-operculum "rotating" contests) occurred only on crowded spawning grounds during active nesting/spawning, though it could be artificially provoked by manipulating territorial boundaries (e.g., overlapping stone-covered nests).
- Nesting males became markedly more active during spawning: one male observed executing 5 spawning acts, 10 nest excursions, 1 threat display, and 39 in-nest gyrations within 10 minutes.

### Spawning Behavior
- Classic centrarchid spawning: male and female circle side-by-side in the nest, pause, and release gametes; female reclines/vibrates while male remains upright.
- Males often spawned with multiple females sequentially or occasionally simultaneously (a second female could align beneath the male during an ongoing spawning act).
- Spawning was not observed after dark, but aggregations remained on spawning grounds overnight.
- Unlike pumpkinseed (Breder 1936), green sunfish spawning was unaffected by cloud cover, rain, or thunderstorms.

### Sexual/Nest Recognition
- Experiments with a rotating latex model sunfish suggested that **female circling behavior** may be a key stimulus enabling males to recognize females as mates (rather than visual sexual dimorphism alone), since males did not distinguish sexes outside the nest context.
- Females were most attracted to males already engaged in spawning, possibly due to heightened male activity (gyrating, frequent nest excursions) or chemical cues (odor) released during spawning — extending Breder's (1936) hypothesis that male gyration alone is the attractant.

### Breeding Cycles & Synchronization (Key Novel Finding)
- Breeding season: late May/early June through early August.
- Nest construction occurred in discrete pulses rather than continuously, with an average periodicity of **8 days** (Pond E, 1960) and **9 days** (Ponds E and Delta, 1961).
- Colonies in different ponds (E vs. Delta) started slightly out of phase but gradually synchronized by the seventh nesting period.
- **Synchronization required colonial nesting**: In Pond Theta, where only 2 large males held dispersed territories (with 2 small males relegated to muck nests), nesting frequency remained ~9 days per male but was NOT synchronized across individuals — supporting the hypothesis that social facilitation (attraction to already-spawning males) drives population-level synchrony.
- In Pond D (1960), synchronization occurred only during the period when nests were arranged in a single compact colony; after a water-level drop dispersed the colony, synchronization ceased.
- Water temperature correlated with nesting onset (nesting periods coincided with rising temperature; longer inter-nest intervals coincided with declining temperature), but nesting periods never occurred less than **6 days** after the previous period regardless of temperature spikes — implicating an intrinsic ~8–9 day male reproductive (spermatogenic/motivational) cycle rather than temperature alone as the primary driver.
- Smaller/younger males (including one-year-olds observed in Pond B) entered the breeding population later in the season (late July–early August) but nested in phase with larger males once recruited.
- Tagged females (n=2) were observed spawning across multiple nesting periods (one across 3 periods, another across 6), indicating female green sunfish mate repeatedly across the season rather than once.

### Nest-Site Homing & Landmark Use
- Of 25 tagged males in Pond E, most subsequent nests were built within 1 meter of the male's previous nest location, indicating strong site familiarity/fidelity rather than random relocation.
- Schwassmann's unpublished experiments (cited) and Hunter's own trials demonstrated:
  - Rotating a gravel-filled nest pan 180° caused males to abandon the old nest (even containing eggs/fry) in favor of the new pan position matching the original *spatial* location — implying landmark-based rather than nest-object recognition.
  - Disturbing small stones/sticks around the nest caused disorientation, though males remained near the general area.
  - Displacing a nest with its landmarks 1–2 m within a colony caused oscillation between old and new locations before settling at the new site.
  - Males could relocate and reoccupy nests covered by gravel pans, indicating nest recognition doesn't depend on direct visual/tactile access to the nest depression itself.
- Conclusion: small-scale landmarks strongly influence local nest recognition, but larger-scale spatial recognition (e.g., general colony region) likely relies on additional unidentified cues.

### Duration of Nest Occupancy
- Nest occupancy ranged from 1–15 days; distribution was **bimodal** rather than normal, with peaks at day 1 (short-term/"trial" nests, or nests abandoned due to aggression from larger males or temperature drops) and days 6–7 (nests sufficiently long-lived for offspring to reach free-swimming stage).
- Laboratory-reared larvae (27–28°C) never became free-swimming earlier than 7 days post-oviposition, yet median nest occupancy was only 4–5 days — suggesting many nests were abandoned before offspring were viable, supporting the two-class (bimodal) interpretation.
- Rapid water temperature drops were linked to premature nest desertion in at least one clear case (8 nests deserted en masse after a steep temperature drop in May 1961).
- Aggressive takeover of nests by larger males was also identified as a proximate cause of short occupancy by smaller males.

### Broader Comparative Context
- The 8–9 day cyclical nesting behavior appears to be a novel finding not previously documented in centrarchids.
- The author contrasts green sunfish with species like the [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), which often has only a single annual breeding period (James 1946; Kramer & Smith 1962), proposing that cyclic multi-peak breeding may be characteristic of species with prolonged breeding seasons that nest colonially.
- Findings are broadly consistent with Breder's (1936) general description of [[Centrarchidae]] reproductive behavior, while adding new detail on cyclicity, synchronization, and nest-site homing mechanisms.
