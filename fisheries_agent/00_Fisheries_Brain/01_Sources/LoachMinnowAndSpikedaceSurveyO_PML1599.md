# Fish Survey of Corduroy Creek, Gila and Navajo Counties, Arizona

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report documents a backpack electrofishing survey of [[Corduroy Creek]] in [[Gila County]] and [[Navajo County]], Arizona, conducted 29–31 August 1991. The survey was commissioned because the [[Arizona Department of Transportation]] proposed realigning [[U.S. Highway 60]] adjacent to the creek, and environmental requirements called for a biological inventory of the project area. The survey specifically targeted two federally listed threatened fishes, [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]], which could potentially inhabit the stream.

Six fish species were documented. Neither Loach Minnow nor Spikedace was collected. [[Roundtail Chub]], a species listed as threatened by the State of Arizona and a federal Category 1 candidate, was present in substantial numbers. Habitat suitability for the listed species was judged limited and marginal in quality.

## Background & Description

The Arizona Department of Transportation proposed a project to realign U.S. Highway 60 adjacent to Corduroy Creek. Under federal and state environmental requirements, a biological survey of the project area was required. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) asked for special attention to the presence of Loach Minnow and Spikedace, both federally listed as threatened (USFWS 1986a, 1986b). Loach Minnow currently occurs upstream in the East and West Forks of the White River and in the Salt River drainage. Spikedace historically occupied the Salt River and major tributaries near the project area.

Collections were made under federal and state permits, and under a special use permit from the [[Fort Apache Indian Tribe]]. The survey was conducted by the senior author ([[Marsh]]) with Robert A. Johnson of Johnson & Associates, Inc., Tempe, Arizona.

Corduroy Creek flows northwesterly from the [[Mogollon Rim]] in northeastern Gila County into [[Harris Creek]], which drains to the [[Salt River]]. Five survey sites were established: Site 1 was the lowest, Site 2 was adjacent to and downstream of the proposed project area, and Sites 3 through 5 were upstream. The entire reach between Sites 1 and 5 was also walked to visually assess habitat suitability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Fish were collected by daylight backpack electrofishing with a Smith-Root Model 15-A unit. Output voltage and frequency were varied among and within sites to optimize capture efficiency. Sampling proceeded upstream, and all habitats were sampled except pools deeper than about one meter, which backpack equipment cannot effectively sample. Up to six collection runs of about one-half hour each were required per site. Special attention was given to habitats typical of Loach Minnow and Spikedace, including turbulent rocky riffles, shear zones along travel-sand bars, quiet eddies on the downstream edges of riffles, and broad, shallow areas above gravel-sand bars. Specimens were identified and most were released alive. Subsamples of Roundtail Chub, Speckled Dace, Sonora Sucker, and Desert Sucker were preserved for later analysis at Arizona State University. Electrofishing effort was recorded for each site, and abundance was estimated along with life stage (juvenile or adult).

**Stream conditions.** During the survey, the stream was near base flow. Local residents reported no flushing floods since the late 1970s. Typical maximum discharge occurs during snowmelt and in summer runoff. Discharge varied among sites because of underflow and tributary seepage. Water clarity was generally high, with the streambed visible in all places except a few deep pools exceeding about 3 m.

**Habitat.** Stream morphology and substrate varied within and among sites. Primary habitats were long, slow, mostly shallow pools and runs; true riffles were uncommon. Gravel and cobble dominated runs, while most pools had softer, finer bottom materials and abundant boulders, especially in canyon reaches. Sandy areas were rare. Bedrock was covered by silt in most reaches. Benthic algae and aquatic plants were restricted, with *Elodea* occurring along channel margins at Site 5 and occasional beds of cattails (*Typha domingensis*) and bulrush.

## Key Findings

**Species composition.** Six fish species were collected:
- Native: Roundtail Chub (*Gila robusta*), Speckled Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), Sonora Sucker (*Catostomus insignis*), and Desert Sucker (*Pantosteus clarki*)
- Non-native: [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*)

Undetermined hybrids of green sunfish with another *Lepomis* species were also present.

**Relative abundance.**
- Speckled Dace was the most abundant species overall, closely followed by Roundtail Chub.
- Roundtail Chub had a relatively large local population with a range of age classes.
- Fathead Minnow was common to abundant only locally, at Site 4, in marginal habitat.
- Green sunfish and its hybrids were sporadic and uncommon.
- Native fishes clearly predominated the fauna.

**Reproduction.** All species were represented by both juvenile and adult life stages, indicating that in-stream reproduction occurs.

**Habitat associations.** Adult Roundtail Chub and Sonora Sucker, plus adult Fathead Minnow, were mostly restricted to pools and other slow-moving habitats, particularly deep water with abundant cover. Juveniles of the native species, along with juvenile and adult Speckled Dace, Desert Sucker, and Green Sunfish, were found mainly in swift runs and riffles. These associations are typical of each species throughout its range.

**Site-level patterns.**
- Site 1 contained runs with abundant Speckled Dace and Desert Sucker, plus some Roundtail Chub.
- Site 2 had few riffles and was dominated by pools.
- Site 4 was a long, shallow pool reach where fish were confined to pools; Fathead Minnow occurred here.
- Site 5 supported juvenile Roundtail Chub, Fathead Minnow, Speckled Dace, Sonora Sucker, Desert Sucker, and Green Sunfish.
- The most extensive habitat, at Site 3, consisted of large, deep pools (to about 1 m) with extensive cobble. This was judged the best potential minnow habitat observed, though not exceptional in quality.

**Listed species.** No Loach Minnow or Spikedace was collected. Habitat for both species was limited in availability and considered marginal in quality at the time of the survey.

**Parasites.** Roundtail Chub and Fathead Minnow at Site 5 were heavily infected with the protozoan parasite *[[Ichthyophthirius multifiliis]]* ("white spot" or "ich").

**Conservation status and conclusions.**
- Roundtail Chub was the only federally listed-candidate species documented in Corduroy Creek. It is a Category 1 candidate for federal listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]], meaning listing is possibly appropriate but current data on biological vulnerability and threat are insufficient to propose a rule.
- It is also listed as threatened by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (1988), which notes its populations are declining from habitat destruction and impacts of non-native fishes. Roundtail Chub is widely distributed across central Arizona, but the Corduroy Creek population's size and viability are considered significant.
- The report recommends that special attention be directed toward minimizing or eliminating potential impacts of the proposed U.S. Highway 60 project on this population.
