## Executive Summary

This document is a 1996 U.S. Geological Survey (Biological Resources Division, Midwest Science Center) funding proposal titled *"Assessment of Factors Related to Decline of the Roundtail Chub (Gila robusta)."* The proposal, authored by James F. Fairchild, seeks $40,000 (of a $90,000 total project cost) to investigate the causes of decline in the [[Roundtail Chub]], a fish native to the [[Colorado River Basin]] recognized as a species of concern by USFWS Region 2. The project has two core components: (1) a quantitative historical analysis of hydrologic and water-quality data (discharge, temperature, turbidity) from pre- and post-impoundment periods, and (2) empirical laboratory studies on larval ontogeny, predation vulnerability, and thermal shock sensitivity using wild-caught broodstock spawned at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The work aims to disentangle the relative contributions of altered thermal/turbidity regimes and non-native predator pressure (notably [[Channel Catfish]]) to the species' decline, informing future river management and predator-control strategies.

## Background & Description

The proposal does not provide detailed external morphological descriptions of adult *Gila robusta*, but includes relevant reproductive and developmental biological markers used as study endpoints:

- **Sexual/reproductive indicators**: Appearance of nuptial tubercles and changes in coloration are used as meristic markers of reproductive readiness.
- **Behavioral reproductive endpoints**: Aggregation, chasing, and fanning/sweeping behaviors signal spawning activity.
- **Size class in study design**: Adult broodstock organized in groups of 7 (2 males, 5 females) per replicate tank.
- **Predator size reference**: Channel catfish predators used were approximately 10 g body weight and 5 cm length (juvenile/subadult size class).
- **Larval developmental milestones tracked**: time to first hatch, percent hatch, time to first swim-up, time to first feeding, and survival — key ontogenetic stages relevant to the [[Larval Ontogeny]] of the species.
- **Spawning thermal cue**: Reported to spawn at water temperatures of 18–21°C (Hamman 1981), during August–October (Kaeding et al. 1990).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native range**: Endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], historically one of the most endemism-rich fish faunas in North America — Miller (1959) reported ~74% endemism among native Colorado Basin fishes due to high geologic isolation.
- **Habitat requirements**: Roundtail chub, along with other native Colorado Basin fishes, requires warm backwater habitats for successful spawning and recruitment — conditions largely eliminated by cold-water discharge below dams.
- **Co-occurring imperiled congeners**: [[Bonytail Chub]] (Gila elegans, nearly extinct, federally endangered) and [[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha, federally endangered) share overlapping habitat and similar life-history vulnerabilities.
- **Threats — hydrologic alteration**: Dam construction has fundamentally altered discharge, velocity, turbidity, and temperature profiles compared to historical, pre-impoundment conditions. Cold-water releases below dams rarely provide the warm backwater conditions needed for native fish spawning.
- **Threats — non-native species introduction**: Holden and Stalnaker (1975) found that 19 of 29 fish species sampled in the Middle/Upper Colorado system (1967–1973) were non-native. Key introduced predators implicated in decline include [[Channel Catfish]] (Ictalurus punctatus), [[Walleye]], and [[Green Sunfish]] — all capable of altering native fish community composition through predation.
- **Predator-prey dynamics under study**: Predation vulnerability of larval/juvenile roundtail chub to channel catfish was tested as a function of two mediating environmental factors: physical refugia (cobble substrate presence/absence) and turbidity (5 vs. 100 NTU), reflecting the ecological role of turbidity as a natural anti-predator mechanism referenced in [[Turbidity Refugia Hypothesis]].
- **Thermal ecology**: Larval/juvenile survival was tested against cold-shock scenarios (declining temperature regimes of 0, 1, 2, 4 °C/hour from an 18°C baseline), simulating cold-water dam discharge effects on early life stages.

## Key Findings

*(Note: This is a research proposal, not a completed study — "findings" below represent documented background evidence and proposed methodology/hypotheses rather than final results.)*

- **Documented decline**: Roundtail chub populations have declined to "perilously low levels" across the Colorado River Basin, attributed to two primary factors — dam-driven hydrologic alteration and non-native species introduction.
- **Historical baseline data**: Holden and Stalnaker (1975) documented that 19 of 29 species sampled in the Middle/Upper Colorado (1967–1973) were non-native, establishing a historical benchmark for invasion extent.
- **Reproductive biology reference points**: Spawning is thought to occur at 18–21°C (Hamman 1981) during August–October (Kaeding et al. 1990), forming the basis for artificial photoperiod/temperature conditioning protocols proposed at Dexter National Fish Hatchery.
- **Proposed experimental design for predation study**: A 2×2 factorial design (substrate presence/absence × turbidity level: 5 or 100 NTU) with channel catfish predators, replicated 4-fold, analyzed via two-way ANOVA (p≤0.05), to determine how physical refugia and turbidity mediate predation risk across chub ontogeny (tested at monthly intervals over 4 months).
- **Proposed cold-shock experimental design**: Declining temperature regimes (0, 1, 2, 4 °C/hour, max 16°C total change) applied to surviving larvae/juveniles from predation trials, with survival tracked at half-hour and 8-hour intervals to determine median time-to-death and minimal thermal tolerance thresholds.
- **Institutional capacity**: Dexter National Fish Hatchery (NM) is identified as having established expertise in spawning and culturing native chub species (Johnson and Jensen, 1991), making it a suitable partner for broodstock propagation.
- **Anticipated management application**: Results were expected to inform hydrologic management (restoring temperature/turbidity regimes during critical life-history windows) and non-native predator control efforts, including citizen education and targeted fishery exploitation programs.
- **Project scope/timeline**: Total budget $90,000 ($40,000 requested, $50,000 matching); project scheduled March 1997–September 1998, with reproductive studies (Jul–Dec 1997) followed by larval research (Dec 1997–May 1998).
