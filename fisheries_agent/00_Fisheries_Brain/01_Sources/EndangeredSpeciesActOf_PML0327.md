# Endangered Species Act of 1973 (As Amended Through 1988)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-law

## Executive Summary

This document is the full statutory text of the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 as amended through Public Law 100-707 (1988), reproduced from the Environment Reporter compilation. It is a legal instrument rather than a research study. It defines the federal framework for listing endangered and threatened fish, wildlife and plants, designating [[Critical Habitat]], regulating federal actions, prohibiting harmful acts (“take”), and funding conservation through state cooperative agreements and an exemption process administered by the [[Endangered Species Committee]] (the "God Squad"). The text covers 18 sections; the species-level content is limited to general definitions and rules that apply to all listed taxa, including fish.

## Background & Description

The Act was enacted December 28, 1973 (Pub. L. 93-205, 87 Stat. 884) and has been amended more than a dozen times, notably in 1976, 1978, 1982, 1984, 1986 and 1988. The table of contents lists 18 sections, running from findings and definitions (Sec. 2–3) through listing (Sec. 4), land acquisition (Sec. 5), state cooperation (Sec. 6), interagency consultation (Sec. 7), international cooperation (Sec. 8, 8A), prohibited acts (Sec. 9), exceptions (Sec. 10), penalties (Sec. 11), endangered plants (Sec. 12), conforming amendments (Sec. 13), repealer (Sec. 14), appropriations (Sec. 15), effective date (Sec. 16), relation to the [[Marine Mammal Protection Act]] (Sec. 17) and annual cost analysis (Sec. 18).

Key definitions include:
- **Species**: any subspecies of fish, wildlife or plants, and any distinct population segment of vertebrate fish or wildlife that interbreeds when mature.
- **Fish or wildlife**: any member of the animal kingdom, including finfish, mollusks, crustaceans, arthropods and other invertebrates.
- **Endangered species** and **threatened species**: threatened means likely to become endangered within the foreseeable future throughout all or a significant portion of its range.
- **Take**: to harass, harm, pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture or collect, or attempt to do so.
- **Critical habitat**: specific geographic areas containing physical or biological features essential to conservation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Act's stated purposes are to conserve the ecosystems on which listed species depend. Section 2 finds that many fish, wildlife and plant species have been rendered extinct or depleted by economic growth and development, and that these species hold aesthetic, ecological, educational, historical, recreational and scientific value. Section 4(a) lists five listing factors: (A) destruction or modification of habitat or range; (B) overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific or educational purposes; (C) disease or predation; (D) inadequacy of existing regulatory mechanisms; and (E) other natural or manmade factors.

Listing decisions for marine species fall to the Secretary of Commerce, who coordinates with the Secretary of the Interior. The Act references several international agreements, including the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES), the International Convention for the Northwest Atlantic Fisheries, and the International Convention for the High Seas Fisheries of the North Pacific Ocean. Section 9 applies prohibitions to activities on the high seas and in U.S. jurisdictions. Section 10 allows incidental take permits tied to habitat conservation plans, and Section 9 treats the import and export of species listed under CITES as regulated trade.

## Key Findings

- **Statutory scope**: 18 sections, amended 1976–1988, covering listing, habitat protection, interagency consultation, state partnerships, penalties and funding.
- **Listing standard**: species are listed on the basis of the best scientific and commercial data available, and the Secretary must consider economic impact before designating critical habitat, though areas may be excluded only if the benefits of exclusion outweigh the benefits of designation, unless extinction would result.
- **Petitions**: the Secretary must make a finding on a petition within 90 days, and must act on warranted petitions within 12 months, with reviews of listed species at least every five years (Sec. 4(c)(2)).
- **Recovery planning**: recovery plans must include site-specific management actions, objective and measurable delisting criteria, and time and cost estimates. The Secretary must report to Congress every two years.
- **Federal consultation**: all federal agencies must ensure their actions do not jeopardize listed species or adversely modify critical habitat (Sec. 7(a)(2)). Biological assessments are required for major actions.
- **Prohibitions**: Section 9 bars the take, import, export, interstate or foreign sale, and trade in violation of the Act for listed species. A rebuttable presumption applies to fish or wildlife taken outside commercial activity after the listing period.
- **Cooperative funding**: federal share of state conservation programs may not exceed 75% of program cost, rising to 90% when two or more states jointly agree to a program.
- **Exemptions**: the Endangered Species Committee, composed of seven senior officials (including the Secretaries of Agriculture, the Army and the Interior, the CEA Chairman, and the EPA and NOAA administrators) plus a representative from each affected state, may grant exemptions from Section 7(a)(2) only if no reasonable alternatives exist and benefits clearly outweigh alternatives.
- **Penalties**: civil penalties of up to $25,000 per violation for knowing violations (with lower caps for other provisions), and criminal fines of up to $50,000 and one year imprisonment for knowing violations.
- **Alaska Natives**: subsistence taking by Alaska Natives is permitted except where it materially and negatively affects a listed species, with regulation possible under Section 10(e).
- **Annual reporting**: the Secretary of the Interior must submit an annual species-by-species cost accounting for conservation of endangered and threatened species, first due January 15, 1990.
- **Related laws**: the Act amends the Marine Mammal Protection Act, the Land and Water Conservation Fund Act, the Migratory Bird Conservation Act, and the Federal Environmental Pesticide Control Act, and requires an Endangered Species pesticide labeling study by the EPA.
