# Ecological Effects of Winter Conditions on Trout and Trout Foods in Convict Creek, California, 1951

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 study, conducted at the [[Convict Creek Experiment Station]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) in the Sierra Nevada near Bishop, California, examined how winter conditions affect [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] and the stream organisms they eat. Snowfall was light, but freezing temperatures produced extensive [[Anchor Ice]], [[Frazil Ice]] and surface ice. Trout remained active and fed throughout the winter, while the daily cycle of ice formation and breakup drove large pulses of bottom fauna into the drift. Wild trout suffered an early-January mortality caused by subsurface ice blocking side-channel flow, and marked trout over-winter survival was about 50 percent.

## Background & Description

The study was undertaken because winter conditions in trout waters had been neglected; inland fishery work was largely limited to summer (as noted by [[Hubbs and Trautman]], 1935). The work was a cooperative effort between the Fish and Wildlife Service and the University of California, running from November 1950 through April 1951.

The principal fish species were brown trout (*Salmo trutta*, by far the most abundant), rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*), and rare eastern brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*). Suckers and cyprinids were common in lower Convict Creek and [[Lake Crowley]].

The study area lies on the eastern escarpment of the Sierra Nevada at 7,200 ft. Convict Creek drops about 2,300 ft into [[Convict Lake]], then about 400 ft over 2 miles to the experimental area. Below the lake, the stream runs through a boulder-strewn canyon before entering sagebrush meadows, where the channel averages about 10 ft wide over rubble, gravel and sand. The experimental system comprised four quarter-mile channels, with flow split between two screened sections each. Winters there typically bring 2 to 4 ft of snow; the 1950-51 winter was comparatively mild, with under 2 ft of snow and only two periods of extreme cold.

Methods were standard stream-survey techniques: [[Surber Sampler]] collections of bottom fauna, a stationary drift net, trout stomach analysis (seine, trap and angling), flow records from the City of Los Angeles, and continuous air and water temperature recording.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature regime.** Water reached freezing whenever subsurface ice was present, regardless of weather or time of day. Water at or below 32 °F totalled 222 hours in January, 166 hours in February and 117 hours in March. Mean monthly water minima were about 32.8 to 32.9 °F, while mean water maxima ranged from 37.9 to 44.5 °F.

**Ice types.** Three ice types were described: surface (sheet) ice, which was most common and reached about 8 in. thick; frazil, a soft, spicular subsurface ice driven by turbulence; and anchor ice, a granular subsurface ice that formed nightly under clear skies by terrestrial radiation. Colloidal discs preceded anchor ice formation and reached up to 3 mm in diameter.

**Hydrology.** Anchor ice impounded water and blocked side channels at night, leaving them empty, while morning breakup produced surges. Daily flows varied by about 4 cfs typically, and on February 23 flow more than doubled from the early-morning minimum to a noon measurement of 16.4 cfs. Mean monthly discharge ranged from 8.3 to 23.0 cfs.

**Cover and disturbance.** Sheet and shelf ice gave trout shelter along open reaches. Strong winds on January 19 produced frazil slush that flooded channels and destroyed two foot-bridges, though no trout harm was found.

**Food supply.** Winter was a period of abundant immature aquatic insects. Oligochaetes ([[Helodrilus tetraedrus]]) were unusually numerous and formed about 10 percent of available drift and bottom food. Emergence of blackflies, midges and stoneflies occurred on warm days.

## Key Findings

- **Feeding continued through winter.** Trout were active and fed regularly at all water temperatures recorded, including 32 °F. Fish caught on bait at 32 °F, and surface feeding was seen at 35–42 °F. Only 1 of over 100 healthy trout had an empty stomach, and 3 brown trout had only trace food.
- **Feeding linked to flow pulses.** Trout fed mainly during and after peak flows, when dislodged riffle insects (mayflies, stoneflies, caddisflies) entered the drift. Bottom, drift and ingested foods showed a positive correlation.
- **Species differences in diet.** 40 brown trout stomachs held 578 organisms (14.5 per fish); 53 rainbow trout held 1,337 (23.2 per fish). Mean stomach volumes were similar (0.29 vs. 0.28 cc). Rainbow trout ate many small midge and blackfly larvae; brown trout ate proportionately more caddisfly larvae. Dipterans gave the highest number share in both species (31.6% brown, 54.9% rainbow).
- **Digestion at low temperature.** In a force-feeding test at 32–35 °F, at least 50 percent of food was digested in 14 hours.
- **Winter abundance of bottom food.** Winter bottom samples averaged 134 lb/acre (five samples, February 1942), above a 109 lb/acre five-season summer average. The 1951 winter samples averaged 107 organisms per square foot versus 373 in 1942.
- **Initial mortality.** On January 10, 63 trout were found stranded in a flood-control bypass after subsurface ice blocked inflow, and suffocation followed. Later, more dead trout were found in side channels up to 1.5 miles upstream.
- **Hatchery vs. wild susceptibility.** Of dead and emaciated fish recovered from the experimental sections (November 7, 1950 to April 21, 1951), 7 were brown and 46 were rainbow trout, which the authors attributed to greater hatchery-rainbow susceptibility to winter emaciation. Mortality of this type did not occur during freezing weather.
- **Survival.** About 50 percent of marked trout stocked in November 1950 survived to late April 1951.
- **Spawning disturbance.** Only two eyed trout eggs were found in stomachs, suggesting slight anchor-ice disturbance of spawning gravels.
- **Operational hazards.** Ice clogged screens, created dams and forced overflow, allowing fish escape. Trout were reluctant to migrate below 42 °F.
- **Management implication.** The authors concluded that stream populations are unstable and that arbitrary stocking tables should be modified to account for seasonal, annual and fishing-pressure variation.
