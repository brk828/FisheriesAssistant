# Verde River Advanced Identification (ADID) Public Materials, 1993

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1993 public information packet from the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]] (EPA), Region IX, and the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] (Corps). It invites the public to workshops on the proposed [[Verde River]] [[Advanced Identification]] (ADID) under [[Section 404 Permit Program|Section 404]] of the [[Clean Water Act]]. The packet contains a question-and-answer sheet, a summary of the Section 404 program, a fact sheet on the 1993 Clean Water Act rulemaking, a resource-values summary, proposed site identifications, and maps.

The ADID would identify areas along the upper Verde River that are "potentially suitable" or "generally unsuitable" for discharges of dredged or fill material. It is a planning tool and does not approve or deny any permit. The proposal lists restoration sites, existing bridges and river crossings, and utility crossings as suitable for specified activities. Public comments were due by November 1, 1993.

## Background & Description

The Section 404 program regulates the discharge of dredged and fill material into [[Waters of the United States]]. The Corps and EPA administer it, with advisory roles for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]]. The packet defines key terms:

- **Ordinary high water mark:** the boundary of waters, identified through field indicators such as scour lines, shelving, soil changes, debris, and destroyed vegetation. The Corps and EPA use the [[1987 Wetlands Delineation Manual]].
- **Fill material:** any material used to replace an aquatic area with dry land or change a waterbody's bottom elevation, such as rock, sand, dirt, or concrete.
- **Discharge of dredged or fill material:** includes redistribution of dredged material within waters, even where no material is added.

Riparian boundaries for the ADID were taken from [[National Wetlands Inventory]] maps. The planning area covers 125 miles of river. The NWI areas are broader than the land actually subject to Clean Water Act jurisdiction, which the Corps determines case by case.

A [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] report, *Functions and Values of the Verde River Riparian Ecosystem*, assessed the riparian ecosystem from Sullivan Lake to Horseshoe Reservoir and evaluated adverse impacts from land use. It is intended to support ADID implementation and future Section 404 reviews.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The packet describes the Verde River as a high-value riverine system. Documented threats include:

- Sand and gravel mining, which has discharged fill material, removed riparian vegetation, and altered channel morphology.
- Floodplain development concentrated near Cottonwood, Clarkdale, and Camp Verde.
- Population growth, expected to nearly triple, which will increase residential and commercial development in the floodplain.

The 1993 floods reshaped channel morphology, eroded banks, deposited sediment, and removed vegetation. EPA and the Corps concluded that the floods did not significantly alter the river's overall functional values, and that the riparian corridor has high potential to revegetate naturally.

The packet's resource-values summary reports that:

- The river is a major perennial stream providing fish and wildlife habitat and recreation.
- It is a National Rivers Inventory "most significant free-flowing river" in the nation.
- 39.5 miles downstream of Beasley Flat were designated Wild and Scenic in 1984 (Reaches 5–7).
- Upper reaches from Sullivan Lake to Sycamore Canyon are unusual in Arizona for their predominance of native fish species (Reaches 1A and 1B).
- The cottonwood-willow riparian gallery forest between Sycamore Creek and West Clear Creek is one of five extensive stands of this type remaining in Arizona.
- Reaches between Perkinsville and Childs are important reintroduction sites for [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], both federally and state listed as endangered.
- All reaches provide habitat for one or more of 31 special-status species, and essential habitat for the [[Bald Eagle]] (federally endangered).
- Sixty percent of vertebrate species inhabiting the three National Forests within the ADID area occur along the river.

## Key Findings

**Program goals and objectives**
- Achieve a net gain in the quantity and quality of the Verde River riparian ecosystem, and maintain its physical, chemical, and biological integrity.
- Strengthen the 404 permit and enforcement program through public outreach.
- Seek avoidance of fill, encourage restoration, and augment state and local riparian management efforts.

**Proposed site identifications**
- EPA and the Corps propose identifying sites as "potentially suitable" for specific fill activities, and treating unlisted sites as "generally unsuitable." Sand and gravel mining and large-scale channelization and bank stabilization would be very difficult to permit outside listed sites.
- Proposed suitable sites and activities:
  1. Wetland and riparian restoration areas, for restoration and enhancement projects not already required by enforcement orders.
  2. Existing bridges and river crossings, for repair or replacement only.
  3. Utility crossings, for repair or replacement only.
- The last two categories exclude expanded facilities with additional indirect or secondary impacts.
- The site-based approach was chosen over listing entire stream reaches, because the upper Verde is functioning as a high-value system and this approach limits cumulative and downstream impacts.

**Benefits anticipated**
- Reducing loss and degradation of riparian habitat.
- Improving water quality and providing early indication of whether a permit is likely.
- Providing resource information for local governments, environmental groups, and regulated communities.

**Next steps and timeline**
- Mailing of background packets, followed by public workshops in Cottonwood and Camp Verde (late September 1993).
- Close of comment period on November 1, 1993.
- Technical summary and final site identifications (November to December 1993).
- Final site list distributed, followed by a Corps public notice in early 1994 (January and February).
- Potential follow-up includes regional conditions for nationwide permits or new general permits, and clearer technical bases for "generally unsuitable" designations.

**Clean Water Act rulemaking (August 1993 fact sheet)**
- The joint final rule clarifies "waters of the United States," "discharge of dredged material," and "discharge of fill material."
- Prior converted croplands, as defined by the Soil Conservation Service, are not regulated. The rule covers approximately 53 million acres.
- Mechanized landclearing, ditching, channelization, and excavation that destroy or degrade wetlands are now regulated, following the settlement of *[[Tulloch Settlement|Tulloch]]* (North Carolina Wildlife Federation v. Tulloch).
- Placement of pilings in waters of the United States is regulated when it has the effect of a discharge of fill material.
- The rule takes effect 30 days after Federal Register publication, with a grandfather provision for planned activities.

**Permit and enforcement framework**
- Section 404(b)(1) Guidelines require a demonstration that no practicable, less-damaging alternative exists, compliance with other laws, and mitigation of unavoidable impacts.
- EPA has veto authority under Section 404(c) over discharges causing unacceptable adverse effects.
- The Corps has primary responsibility for monitoring and enforcing permit conditions. Citizen notification of violations is one of the most effective enforcement tools.
- The ADID itself is exempt from formal National Environmental Policy Act review, since it is not a final agency action.
