# Murray Springs: A Mid-Postglacial Pollen Profile from Southern Arizona

#palynology #paleoecology #pollen-stratigraphy

## Executive Summary

This interim research report, by Peter J. Mehringer Jr., Paul S. Martin and C. Vance Haynes Jr. of the [[University of Arizona Geochronology Laboratories]], re-examines the Murray Springs pollen locality in the upper [[San Pedro River]] valley of southeastern Arizona. The authors revisit a key site in the debate over the [[Altithermal]] climate. They ask whether the mid-postglacial interval was wet or dry in the Southwest, and they note that the report is preliminary and not a formal publication.

The study re-collects and re-analyzes the profile to address four concerns: replication of pollen counts, the absence of comparable pollen zones at the [[Lehner Ranch Site]], the interpretation of pine pollen as a moisture indicator, and the stratigraphy and radiocarbon dating of the original section. The authors conclude that the deposit is a shallow pond sequence of roughly 4,000 to 5,000 radiocarbon years ago. They interpret it as evidence for increased effective moisture.

## Background & Description

The report opens with the idea of the [[Long Drought]], a dry interval from about 7,500 to 4,000 years ago proposed by [[Ernst Antevs]]. It notes that some regional pollen records show mid-postglacial minima in tree pollen, but that [[Paul S. Martin]] (1963) argued the Altithermal near the Mexican border was relatively wet, at least in summer.

The site is a tributary arroyo entering the Upper San Pedro River in Cochise County:

- **Location:** 46 km south of Benson and 2.7 km west of Lewis Springs.
- **Elevation:** 1260 m.
- **Vegetation:** Desert scrub above the terrace (e.g., *Acacia vernicosa*, *Larrea divaricata*, *Flourensia cernua*, *Ephedra trifurca*). Cottonwoods, willows and mesquite line the arroyo, indicating a shallow water table.
- **Hydrology:** Water flows only after heavy rains, but sand about 50 m upstream stays damp through the driest months.

The stratigraphy comprises eight units (A to H) described in Table I, from a coarse channel-fill sand and gravel (unit A) through pond silts and clays (units B to D), flood-plain alluvium associated with the [[Cochise Culture]] (units E to G), and Recent slope wash (unit H). Accumulated thickness at the base of the section is given as 3.80+ m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors argue that pine pollen frequency in southwestern desert sediments depends on the pollen-producing efficiency of local vegetation:

- Dense local producers (*Atriplex*, *Amaranthus*, certain Gramineae, *Ambrosia*) suppress the relative frequency of pine.
- Poor producers (zoophilous shrubs such as *Larrea*, *Acacia*, *Prosopis*, *Flourensia*) or bare ground, such as a dry lake bed, inflate it.

Because of this, a pine rise could reflect either wetter conditions or drought-driven shrub expansion. The authors use [[Ephedra]] as a diagnostic: it is under 1% of pollen in grassland surface samples, but at least 3 to 5% in desert scrub communities.

Unit B's pollen record includes abundant [[Cyperaceae]], [[Typha]] and pine, together with chenopods (cheno-ams). Pollen preservation was unusually good for southwestern alluvium. The geology of the deposit, with laminated fine sediments and a sharp contact with the underlying channel gravel, supports a shallow pond origin.

## Key Findings

**Stratigraphy and depositional history**

- Unit A (sand and gravel with rolled clayey peat lumps) is a former stream channel fill. Unit B, a silty clay, was deposited as a shallow pond, probably after a sharp shift from through-flowing stream to ponding. The authors cite [[Beaver Dams]] as a possible cause but describe this as unproven.
- Unit D is truncated by an erosional contact, indicating desiccation before unit E was deposited on a flood plain.
- Unit H (slope wash of the past 100 years or less) predates the entrenchment of the present arroyo, which probably occurred early in the 20th century.

**Radiocarbon dating (Table II)**

| Sample | Material | Age (B.P.) |
|---|---|---|
| A-617 | Charcoal (hearth, unit E) | 1550 ± 90 |
| A-186 | Earth (unit B) | 4120 ± 500 |
| A-697B | Humates (unit B) | 4230 ± 290 |
| A-696A | Organic residue (unit B) | 4340 ± 250 |
| A-696B | Humates (unit B) | 5890 ± 270 |
| A-187 | Earth (unit B) | 5280 ± 350 |
| A-580B | Humates (redeposited clayey peat, unit A) | 8270 ± 180 |

- Charcoal A-617 is regarded as the one reliable date.
- The residue and humate fractions of A-696 are not in statistical agreement. Older humic acids from the buried peat mat about 100 m upstream may contaminate the unit B samples when the water table stands above unit B.
- A weighted average of A-696 fractions gives 4670 B.P. The residue date is about 330 years younger, a reasonable estimate of error.
- The authors conclude that unit B was deposited between 4,000 and 5,000 years ago. Given its 40 cm thickness, they consider it more likely to have accumulated in less than 500 years.
- Note: the Figure 1 caption gives the charcoal date as 1550 ± 190 B.P., whereas Table II gives ± 90. The table value is used here.

**Pollen diagram (Figure 3)**

- Pollen was counted using double fixed sums: a first count of all types (200 grains) and a second count excluding cheno-ams and composites (mostly 100 grains; 200 grains for the surface sample and samples 23 to 27).
- Unit D shows a shift to short-spine composite dominance and a decline in Cyperaceae, Typha and pine. The authors read this as drying, though the site may still have been relatively mesic.
- Unit D's composite-rich spectrum suggests a higher-than-present water table.
- Ephedra pollen rises at about 1500 B.P., which the authors interpret as a shift toward desert scrub.
- Diagnostic criteria for the San Pedro Valley: about 2% pine with little or no Ephedra indicates effective moisture and grassland, whereas about 10% pine with 3% or more Ephedra indicates desert scrub.

**Interpretation and conclusions**

- The authors assume that the Typha and Cyperaceae pollen together with higher pine frequencies in unit B reflect a regional change in upland vegetation, either expansion of pine communities or greater pollen production by existing trees. They consider increased effective moisture probable in either case.
- The new stratigraphic and radiocarbon work verifies the age of the deposit within the limits of the dated material and substantiates Martin's (1963) original age for unit B.
- The authors summarize their findings in the Summary section. Ponding and high pine values during 4,000 to 5,000 radiocarbon years ago indicate a moister period. A downward displacement of present vegetation zones by about 300 m is sufficient to explain the unit B pollen record. Pollen records between 5,000 and 7,000 radiocarbon years ago remain unknown.

**Methodological caveats**

- The authors state that results are tentative, and that future investigations may alter them.
- Replicate counts can differ significantly, so the original profile was recounted to test reproducibility.
