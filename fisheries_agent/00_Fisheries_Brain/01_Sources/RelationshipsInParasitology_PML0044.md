# Parasitic Infections in Human Patients: Intestinal and Blood Parasites (Part I)

#parasitology #protozoology #helminthology

## Executive Summary

This article by Dr. Omar M. Amin, a parasitologist at [[Arizona State University]], is the first part of a series on parasites written for non-specialist readers. It reports a clinical survey of 188 patients examined at the [[Phoenix Diagnostic And Educational Laboratory]] between June and July 1994. The author argues that parasitic infection should be seen in relation to the whole person (physical, mental, emotional and intuitive states) and that host immune status largely determines whether a parasite causes disease. The article covers major intestinal protozoa and their transmission, including waterborne routes. Helminths and blood parasites are addressed in Part II.

## Background & Description

- **Author and context:** Dr. Amin directs the Institute of Parasitic Diseases and its diagnostic lab in Tempe, Arizona. He combines a clinical practice with wildlife parasitology fieldwork, and the article is written for a general and non-specialist audience.
- **Diagnostic methods:** Fresh and mailable fecal samples were tested mainly by sugar flotation and formalin-ether sedimentation. Blood samples were examined as stained thin blood films.
- **Patient population:** Most patients had gastroenteric symptoms. Others reported fatigue, skin conditions, weight loss, or central nervous system and related complaints. The author cautions that this clinic population is at risk and that its findings should not be extrapolated to the general public.
- **Conceptual framework:** The author links immune suppression, chronic stress, and psychological trauma to increased susceptibility to parasites. He treats the host, its environment, and its emotional state as one system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water as a vehicle:** [[Giardia Lamblia]] and [[Cryptosporidium Parvum]] cysts and oocysts are spread through contaminated drinking water. In a survey of filtered drinking water from 66 surface water-treatment plants in 14 states and one Canadian province, 17% of samples were contaminated with Giardia cysts. Cryptosporidium oocysts were found in 27% of the same samples. Ultraviolet radiation was reported to affect disinfection of drinking water containing known numbers of Cryptosporidium.
- **Animal reservoirs:** Beavers, muskrats, dogs, and sheep are implicated as natural reservoirs of Giardia. Cryptosporidium transmission involves cats, dogs, rodents, and puppies or kittens, with drinking water as a vehicle. Balantidium coli is transmitted through food and water contaminated with pig feces and, occasionally, monkey feces.
- **Soil and food contamination:** Ascaris and hookworm eggs or larvae persist in soil. Ascaris eggs can survive more than 10 years in typical household yard soil. Eggs are transmitted by unwashed fresh produce grown in fields fertilized with human waste. Blackfly-, cockroach-, and fly-mediated spread of amoebic cysts is also described.
- **Climate:** Ascaris egg development stops below 15.5 °C and eggs are killed above 38 °C. This explains the distribution of Ascaris within the warm belt of the world.
- **Zoonotic and wildlife links:** Sylvatic Chagas disease has been documented in opossums and raccoons and in triatomine bugs in several U.S. states, with recent reports from North Carolina.

## Key Findings

**1. Prevalence in the 188-patient sample**

- [[Blastocystis Hominis]] was the most prevalent parasite, found in about half of the patients tested. Infection was more frequent in adults and in immune-compromised patients with chronic fatigue syndrome, which suggests that repeated or long-term exposure may be needed for infection.
- [[Entamoeba Histolytica]] and other amoebas were the second most common group. [[Giardia Lamblia]] was the third most common intestinal flagellate in the tested population.
- Helminths (mostly tapeworms and roundworms) occurred at markedly lower prevalence than protozoa.
- Some patients had concurrent infections with as many as four or five species.
- Exact prevalence percentages for each parasite are in Table I of the original, which is partly illegible in the scan and is not reproduced here.

**2. Blastocystis hominis**

- The author proposes that *B. hominis* should be treated as an invasive intestinal parasite. Associated conditions include inflammatory bowel disease, gastroenteritis, arthritis, abdominal cramps, vomiting, nausea, weight loss, and dizziness.
- A few healthy controls tested positive without symptoms, which the author takes as evidence of an asymptomatic carrier state.
- Treatment with [[Iodoquinol]] or [[Metronidazole]] is reported as successful.

**3. Amoebiasis**

- *E. histolytica* is estimated to infect about 400 million people worldwide. It can cause diarrhea, bloody stool, abdominal pain, weight loss, and fatigue. Serious complications include liver abscess, colitis, and brain or lung abscess.
- Infection is fecal-oral and spreads through food, water, and person-to-person contact. Most positive patients had travelled to endemic areas, such as Mexico, or had eaten unsanitary food.
- Several stool specimens (up to seven in some cases) may be needed to confirm infection, because shedding is intermittent.

**4. Giardiasis**

- Giardia causes malabsorption, diarrhea, abdominal distension, and jaundice when the bile duct is involved. Relapses may last for years.
- Associations with reactive arthritis, urticaria, and ophthalmic changes have been reported.
- Milk and duodenal fluid were lethal to Giardia in laboratory tests. In gerbils, a high-fiber diet reduced infection.
- Treatment options include [[Metronidazole]], [[Albendazole]] (described as a promising broad-spectrum agent), quinacrine, and furazolidone.

**5. Cryptosporidiosis**

- *C. parvum* causes short-term, self-limited diarrhea in immune-competent people. In immune-compromised patients it can be life-threatening and cholera-like.
- Prevalence is 0.6–4.3% in North America and Europe (seroprevalence 25–35%) and 3–20% in South America. In Sao Paulo, it was found in 25.6% of stool specimens from AIDS patients.
- Four large waterborne outbreaks are reported: Carrollton, Georgia (1987, about 13,000 illnesses, 40% of the exposed population); Jackson County, Oregon (1992, 15,000); Milwaukee, Wisconsin (1993, 37,000); and Oxfordshire, Scotland (1989, 55,000).
- Paromomycin sulfate is a promising drug, and discontinuing immune-suppressive chemotherapy resolved infection in several patients.

**6. Balantidiosis**

- *B. coli* was found in two adult females and one male, one of whom had chronic fatigue syndrome. Most infections are asymptomatic, but overt cases cause colitis and mucosal ulceration.
- Treatment includes oxytetracycline, iodoquinol, or [[Metronidazole]].

**7. Helminths (Part I summary)**

- **Ascaris lumbricoides:** More than 1.2 billion people are estimated to be infected worldwide. About 85% of cases are symptomless. Mebendazole is the usual treatment, and about 85% of patients respond to a single dose. In concurrent infection with Giardia or Entamoeba, the author advises treating ascariasis first. Infections were more prevalent in males, children, and immune-compromised patients.
- **Hookworms (*Necator americanus*, *Ancylostoma duodenale*):** Up to one billion people are estimated to be infected, mainly in warm regions. Infection begins with larval penetration of the skin, which causes ground itch. Adults cause iron-deficiency anemia. Mebendazole or pyrantel pamoate is the drug of choice, with iron therapy as needed.
- **[[Strongyloides Stercoralis]]:** Found in three patients with recent travel to the Middle East or South America. Autoinfection can cause chronic infection lasting years. Massive disseminated disease, sometimes fatal, can follow high-dose steroid therapy. Thiabendazole, mebendazole, or cambendazole are recommended, along with at least three stool examinations before immune-suppressive treatment.
- **Tapeworms:** *Taenia solium* can cause [[Cysticercosis]], with neurological damage and a WHO estimate of 50,000 deaths per year attributed to it. *Taenia saginata* is common in the sampled population. *Hymenolepis nana* is usually symptomless and is treated with praziquantel or niclosamide.

**8. Blood parasites (preliminary observations)**

- Babesia-like organisms were seen in two middle-aged females with frequent tick-bite histories in New England. One had been treated for Lyme disease without recovery.
- [[Trypanosoma Cruzi]]-like organisms, the agents of [[Chagas Disease]], were seen in two young adults, both severely fatigued and immune-compromised. The organisms were about half normal size, so their identity is uncertain.

**9. Conclusions and public-health points**

- The author argues that the belief that Americans are free of parasites is an illusion. He estimates that 55 million US children are infected with worms.
- He identifies endemic zoonotic infection, border and import pathways for infected labor and goods, and lack of public awareness as key concerns.
- He argues that imbalance between physical, mental, and emotional states depresses immunity and increases susceptibility to parasites. Factors cited include population density, pollution, poverty, war, malnutrition, and emotional stress.
- The author states that natural remedies are not covered and are planned for future work.
