## Executive Summary

This paper by Bruce B. Collette (1990) is not a species-level biological study but a critical commentary on the epistemic infrastructure of [[Fishery Science]]. It examines the pervasive problem of **gray literature**—written information produced and distributed without adequate peer review—within fishery science organizations such as the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS), the [[Canadian Department of Fisheries and Oceans]], and international bodies like [[ICNAF]], [[NAFO]], [[ICES]], [[IATTC]], and [[ICCAT]]. Collette argues that gray literature undermines scientific credibility, is difficult to retrieve, can jeopardize subsequent formal publication, and disadvantages scientists professionally. He recommends that fishery agencies minimize gray literature production and channel research efforts toward peer-reviewed, permanently archived scientific literature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Not applicable in the biological sense. However, the paper describes the "ecosystem" of fishery science publishing — an information hierarchy analogous to a trophic structure, comprising:

1. **Peer-reviewed journals** (highest scientific rigor): e.g., [[Fishery Bulletin]], [[Marine Fisheries Review]], [[Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences]].
2. **Irregular peer-reviewed series**: e.g., [[NOAA Technical Report NMFS]].
3. **Irregularly issued, variously edited reports**: e.g., [[NOAA Technical Memorandum NMFS-F]], [[Canadian Technical Report of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences]].
4. **Gray literature** (lowest rigor, limited distribution, no peer review): e.g., Woods Hole Laboratory Reference Series, Middle Atlantic Coastal Fisheries Center Technical Publications, Sandy Hook Laboratory Technical Series Reports, Northwest Center Processed Reports, Southwest Center Administrative Reports, [[Canadian Data Report of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences]], and "Working Documents" produced for meetings of ICNAF, NAFO, and ICES.

International tuna commissions (IATTC, ICCAT) exhibit a similar tiered structure, publishing peer-reviewed Bulletins alongside non-citable internal working papers (e.g., ICCAT's Collective Volume of Scientific Papers).

## Key Findings

- **Definition of gray literature**: Per the Council of Biology Editors ([[Day 1983]]), valid scientific publication requires peer review, permanence, unrestricted availability, and indexing by recognized secondary/abstracting services. Gray literature fails to meet these criteria. Librarians alternatively define it simply as "material not available through normal bookselling channels" ([[Vickers and Wood 1982]]).

- **Institutional hierarchy**: NMFS, Canadian DFO, ICNAF/NAFO, ICES, and the tuna commissions (IATTC, ICCAT) all maintain multi-tiered publication systems ranging from rigorously peer-reviewed journals to informal, restricted-access gray literature and "Working Documents" that explicitly warn against citation without author permission.

- **Core problems with gray literature**:
  - **Credibility deficit** due to lack of peer review.
  - **Poor retrievability** — often absent from libraries and abstracting/indexing services; sometimes obtainable only directly from the author, creating data-access problems especially after an author retires or dies.
  - **Publication jeopardy** — prior release in gray literature can disqualify data from subsequent peer-reviewed publication, since the scientific norm is to publish data only once.
  - **Career disadvantage** — universities and agencies generally do not credit gray literature in promotion evaluations, unlike peer-reviewed papers.
  - **False sense of completion** — authors often believe producing a report fulfills their scientific and public responsibility, but without peer review and accessibility, this obligation remains unmet.

- **Drivers of gray literature production**: contractual/grant reporting requirements, perceived time savings, perceived "credit" for compiling information, claimed training value for young scientists, and sometimes a desire to avoid external peer scrutiny or criticism.

- **Rise of desktop publishing** was identified as an emerging threat, enabling scientists to self-publish without any review process.

- **Recommendations**:
  - Fishery scientists and agencies should minimize gray literature and prioritize peer-reviewed publication.
  - Managers should require justification before releasing gray literature and encourage journal submission instead.
  - Contract-driven gray literature should be archived in national repositories or issuing institutions for retrievability.
  - Recurring gray literature (e.g., annual stock assessments) should be periodically synthesized and published in the permanent peer-reviewed literature.
  - Funding agencies could mandate that final contract reports be formatted for journal submission.

- **Broader implication**: The overproduction of unreviewed, poorly archived gray literature threatens the integrity, accessibility, and long-term utility of fishery science data for both the research community and fishery managers who rely on publicly funded research.
