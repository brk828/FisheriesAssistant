# Habitat Use and Associated Habitat Characteristics of Hatchery-Reared Adult Razorback Suckers Implanted with Ultrasonic Transmitters and Released into the Lower Imperial Division, Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department contract report (Contract Report No. 4, Phase III of a multi-phase study, May 2000) examines how [[Hatchery-Reared Adult Razorback Sucker]]s use the [[Imperial Division]] of the lower [[Colorado River]]. Sixty-two adults (413–553 mm TL) were implanted with [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] transmitters and released between January 1998 and December 1998. Fish were located during 32 reconnaissance surveys from January 22, 1998 to November 11, 1999, producing 603 contacts.

Fish used habitat types disproportionately to availability (χ² = 431.63, df = 3, n = 603, P < 0.001). [[Backwater]]s were strongly preferred, the [[Impoundment]] was also selected, the [[Main Channel]] was avoided, and [[Side Channel]]s were used slightly less than available. Logistic regression models predicted backwater use poorly but predicted non-use fairly well. The authors concluded that backwater selection relates mainly to physical characteristics (size, shape, depth) rather than the water-quality variables measured.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a catostomid, is an endangered species of the Colorado River basin. Recovery efforts began around 1981 and relied largely on stocking hatchery-reared young fish. Because survivorship of young fish was low, current efforts use larger hatchery-reared adults.

The study was part of a multi-phase project funded through a Cooperative Agreement between the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]:

- **Phase I:** Developed [[Habitat Assessment Indices]] (HAI) from literature, mostly from upper basin and reservoir studies, whose applicability to the Imperial Division was questionable.
- **Phase II:** In-situ study of habitat use by surgically implanted adults.
- **Phase III (this report):** Measured habitat characteristics concurrently with fish surveys before restoration. Objective 1 is addressed here; objectives 2 and 3 await Phase IV results.
- **Phase IV:** Post-restoration monitoring, begun December 1998 alongside restoration work.

Study fish came from [[Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery]] (Lake Mohave broodstock). Transmitters (Sonotronics CT-82-2, 73.80 kHz, 8 g) were implanted into the peritoneal cavity, and passive integrated transponders were also used. Fish were released at sites chosen randomly, with habitat types stocked in proportion to availability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Imperial Division comprises about 64 km of river between Imperial Dam and Adobe Ruins. The study covered the lower 9.6 km, with Imperial Dam as the southern boundary. Habitats were:

| Habitat type | Sites | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Backwaters | 26 | Permanent water bodies connected to, but distinct from, the main channel; 0.06–28.10 ha |
| Side channels | 9 | Flowing segments adjacent to the main channel; 0.21–4.02 ha |
| Main channel | 6 segments (1.6 km each) | 48% of total surface area (279.65 ha) |
| Impoundment | 1 | 1.32 ha, abutting Imperial Dam |

Backwaters and the impoundment together made up about 47% of total study-area surface area.

**Measured variables.** Dynamic characteristics were specific conductance, turbidity, temperature, pH, water velocity, and mean backwater depth. Dissolved oxygen was dropped after probe malfunction. Static characteristics were surface area, perimeter, [[Shoreline Development Index]], distance to main channel, and backwater entrance sinuosity.

**Environmental conditions.** Dynamic readings from the survey tables show backwater specific conductance mostly around 1.0–1.7 mS/cm, temperatures from about 5 to 33 °C across seasons, and pH generally 7.4–9.0. Imperial Dam mean daily flow ranged from roughly 6,000 to 10,000 cfs in the sampled records. Full site-by-date listings appear in the appendices and are not reproduced here.

## Key Findings

**Habitat selection (combined data, Phase III):**

| Habitat type | Observed use (% of contacts, n = 603) | Expected use (%) | Jacobs' D | Selection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Backwaters | 79 | 42 | +0.67 | Positive (selected) |
| Impoundment | 8 | 8 | +0.36 | Positive |
| Main channel | 8 | 48 | −0.78 | Negative (avoided) |
| Side channels | 3 | 3 | −0.26 | Negative (slight) |

Note: The Table 4 values for observed use and expected use are read from the rows of the source table; the overall backwater observed-use figure is 79%, and backwaters plus impoundment together accounted for 89% of contacts.

**Key results:**

- Study fish contacted at least once: 47 of 62 released; maximum contacts per fish: 25.
- Of 50 fish located more than once, 23 (46%) were found in more than one habitat type, and 9 (18%) in three or more.
- Based on inferred habitat exposure, 36 (72%) fish were exposed to more than one habitat type and 5 (10%) to all available habitat types.
- Usage was significant in both years: Year 1 (χ² = 219.34, df = 3, P < 0.01) and Year 2 (χ² = 221.55, df = 3, P < 0.01). Use did not differ significantly between years, and seasonal and reproductive-period differences were not significant.
- 36 contacts involved unidentified fish (coded 999).

**Correlation analyses (backwaters, Spearman):** Significant associations with use were found for surface area (r = 0.591), perimeter (r = 0.570), shoreline development index (r = 0.370), mean depth (r = 0.379), sinuosity (r = 0.139), distance to main channel (r = 0.163), and specific conductance (r = −0.107, P < 0.05). Velocity, temperature, and pH were not significant.

**Logistic regression models:**

- **[[Model A]]** (static and dynamic variables): correct classification was 94.3% for non-use and 59.4% for use, with overall correct classification of 84.0%. Validation gave similar rates.
- **[[Model B]]** (dynamic variables only; specific conductance, temperature, pH, and mean depth retained): non-use classified correctly 91.9%, use 22.8%, overall 72.1%.
- Both models predict non-use better than use, so they are most useful for identifying backwaters unlikely to be used by razorback suckers, which may be lower-priority restoration sites.

**Interpretation.** Physical parameters, especially backwater size, irregular shape, and depth (preferred sites generally had mean depth above 1.5 m), appear more important than water-quality factors such as turbidity, pH, specific conductance, or temperature. The authors caution that unmeasured variables, such as cover, current direction, and micro-habitat structure, may influence habitat selection, and that the models should not be applied to river systems that differ substantially from the Imperial Division.

**Management implications:**

- Adult hatchery-reared fish actively select off-channel backwaters, so loss of those habitats could be detrimental to recovery.
- Historical flood events maintained off-channel access by removing sediment and vegetation. Under current flow regimes, sedimentation and vegetation growth have restricted access, making mechanical dredging and excavation necessary.
- Because dredging is costly, the logistic models can help prioritize which backwater restoration sites are most likely to be used, supporting the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]]'s obligations under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**Limitations noted by the authors:** Habitat was assessed at macro-scale only; low visibility in deep backwaters limited structure characterization; the presence of observers sometimes caused fish to move away; and results may not transfer to wild fish.
