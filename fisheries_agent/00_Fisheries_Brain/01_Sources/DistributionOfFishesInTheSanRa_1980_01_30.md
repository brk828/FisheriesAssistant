# Distribution of Fishes in the San Rafael River System of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae #upper-colorado-river-basin

## Executive Summary
This 1977 survey documented the fish fauna of the San Rafael River and its four major tributaries (Cottonwood, Huntington, Ferron, and Rock Canyon creeks) in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], Utah, ahead of anticipated coal development in the region. Nine stations were sampled across three seasons (March–April, June, October) using backpack electrofishing and seine netting, paired with water quality measurements. Four native species — [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Roundtail Chub]] — dominated upstream tributary and mid-river stations, while four introduced species — [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Black Bullhead]], and [[Channel Catfish]] — dominated the lower river near its mouth. A single young-of-the-year [[Colorado Squawfish]] was collected in October at the lowermost station, constituting the first documented record of this endangered species in the San Rafael River. The study links this native/introduced fish segregation to habitat differences driven largely by irrigation-related water diversion and drought conditions.

## Background & Description
The paper is primarily a distributional/ecological survey rather than a morphometric study, but it notes:
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: young-of-the-year specimen collected was 35 mm total length.
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]**: specimen collected alongside the squawfish measured 44 mm total length.
- General habitat-substrate associations (see Ecological Niche) are used as proxy morphological/behavioral adaptations (e.g., benthic sucker mouth morphology of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] suited to silt/cobble substrates respectively).
- No detailed meristic, morphometric, or coloration data were reported for other species in this text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
**System context**: The San Rafael River (~175 km long, draining ~4500 km² of the arid Colorado Plateau/San Rafael Swell) is a wide, shallow, sand/silt-bottomed stream with sparse riparian vegetation (tamarisk, willow). Upper reaches have more rubble/gravel and diverse pool-riffle habitat; the lower river is more uniform, shallow, and sand-dominated. 1977 was a drought year (65% of average precipitation), causing intermittent flow and irrigation-driven dewatering, especially in late summer/fall.

**Habitat associations of native species**:
- [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]]: deep riffles or shallow glides over cobble/rubble bottoms.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]: lower portions of glides/pools with mud or silt bottoms.
- [[Roundtail Chub]]: deepest parts of pools.

**Spatial segregation**: Native fish dominated at seven upstream/tributary stations (3–9); introduced fish dominated at the two farthest downstream stations (1–2) near the river's mouth. This pattern mirrors broader Western U.S. trends of introduced species displacing natives in lower river reaches (cf. [[Moapa River]] studies by Deacon and colleagues).

**Water quality**: Highly variable and often extreme — dissolved oxygen ranged from 11 mg/l (spring) down to 1 mg/l in isolated October pools; total alkalinity 150–510 mg/l; hardness 1,400–1,920 mg/l; conductivity 2,250–7,800 µmhos; pH 7.1–9.1; water temperature ranged from 1°C (March) to 31°C (June). Low dissolved oxygen in isolated, beaver-inhabited pools (linked to decomposing organic material) coincided with reduced fish presence.

**Drought/diversion effects**: Combined drought and irrigation diversion caused the lower river to become intermittent by October, stranding fish in isolated pools (stations 1–3). Ferron Creek (station 9) showed rapid repopulation after a June dewatering event once flow resumed by October, consistent with known patterns of swift fish recolonization after stream drying or pollution kills.

**Colorado Squawfish note**: Considered a large-river specialist typically restricted to major rivers of the basin (Yampa, Green, Gila, Colorado); its presence as a young-of-the-year in the San Rafael River is notable and unresolved — unclear whether it was spawned locally or migrated upstream from the [[Green River]].

## Key Findings
- First documented record of [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the San Rafael River (a single young-of-the-year, October, station 1).
- Native fish assemblage: [[Speckled Dace]] (most abundant), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]].
- Introduced fish assemblage: [[Red Shiner]] (most abundant introduced species), [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Channel Catfish]].
- Clear spatial segregation: native fish upstream/mid-river (stations 3–9), introduced fish confined to the two most downstream stations (1–2).
- Of >3,000 fish collected across the study, 61% were caught in June, likely reflecting improved seining efficiency at lower water levels rather than a true seasonal abundance peak.
- Electrofishing was ineffective due to high water conductivity; switched to 3 mm mesh seine after the March–April sampling round.
- Habitat heterogeneity (pool depth, cover, substrate diversity) in upper reaches favored native fish; homogeneous, shallow, sand-bottomed lower reaches favored introduced species.
- Irrigation water diversion, compounded by 1977 drought (65% of normal precipitation), was identified as the primary driver of habitat degradation and intermittency in the lower river.
- Low dissolved oxygen (as low as 1 mg/l) in isolated, organically enriched (beaver-influenced) pools was associated with fish stress/decimation during low-flow periods.
- Rapid fish repopulation observed in Ferron Creek following resumption of streamflow after a June dewatering event, consistent with literature on stream fish recovery after drought or dewatering.
- Findings support broader Western U.S. pattern in which introduced species tend to replace natives in lower, more degraded river reaches (cf. [[Moapa River]], [[Muddy River]] studies).
