# Inventory of Percid and Esocid Habitat in North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 American Fisheries Society symposium paper by [[Kenneth D Carlander]], [[J Scott Campbell]], and [[Robert J Muncy]] estimates the freshwater area occupied by five [[Coolwater Fishes]]: [[Northern Pike]], [[Muskellunge]], [[Walleye]], [[Sauger]], and [[Yellow Perch]]. Data came from a questionnaire sent in April 1977 to the chief fishery staff of each U.S. state and Canadian province, supplemented by correspondence, telephone follow-up, and in some cases the [[Great Lakes Fishery Laboratory]] estimates. Results are grouped by drainage pattern and by 4 east-west and 15 north-south regional groupings. The paper's main conclusion is that the five species occupy major concentrations of habitat around the [[Great Lakes]], and that the estimates are conservative for several species.

## Background & Description

The five species are cool-water percids and esocids with significant sport and commercial fisheries, but no prior continental estimate of their habitat existed. Geographic ranges were known; the actual water area inhabited or manageable was not.

- **Data categories:** lakes and impoundments were grouped into four size classes (>5,000 ha; 1,000–5,000 ha; 500–1,000 ha; <500 ha). Streams were grouped into three width classes (>100 m; 25–100 m; <25 m).
- **Area conversion:** where stream widths were not supplied, arbitrary widths of 150 m, 50 m, and 20 m were multiplied by stream length in km.
- **Management categories:** unmanaged, stocked only, managed, or managed primarily for other species.
- **Reliability:** the authors note that yellow perch and northern pike estimates are probably low, whereas muskellunge estimates are more accurate. Estimates for northern Canada may be conservative because small lakes were not fully enumerated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** northern pike use shallow weedy lake areas, marshes, and stream backwater sloughs. Spawning and nursery areas may be subject to winterkill.
- **Impoundments:** large reservoirs created new habitat, particularly in the U.S. Plains and Rocky Mountain states. Dams hinder upstream movement but warm water and favour yellow perch expansion in New England.
- **Habitat loss:** loss of aquatic vegetation at spawning sites after reservoir construction, and draining of lake marshes and backwaters, may have reduced northern pike habitat.
- **Salinity:** native yellow perch range extends into estuarine waters with salinities up to 5‰. Estuarine area in Delaware and Maryland raised the Central Atlantic figure to 53% of freshwater area.
- **Degradation:** Great Lakes habitat estimates were adjusted downward for habitat degradation.
- **Glacial history:** northern pike and walleye spread northward from the upper Mississippi system during deglaciation, reaching the Hudson Bay and Mackenzie River drainages.
- **Biogeographic hub:** Minnesota contained the largest freshwater area for all five species in the United States, and Ontario contained the largest in Canada.

## Key Findings

**Estimated proportion of North American freshwater occupied (Table 6):**

| Species | Percentage of total freshwater |
|---|---|
| Northern Pike | 54% |
| Muskellunge | 1% |
| Walleye | 32% |
| Sauger | 10% |
| Yellow Perch | 26% |

- **Total freshwater area** in Table 6 is 128,380 thousand hectares.
- **Grand totals (thousands of hectares, Tables 1–5):** northern pike 68,969; muskellunge 1,262; walleye 41,121; sauger 13,386; yellow perch 33,577.
- **Regional extremes:** Ontario's percentages were 100% for northern pike, 90.6% for walleye, and 63.4% for yellow perch. Canadian Plains figures were 46.6% for walleye and 34.1% for yellow perch. Alaska held 20% for northern pike.
- **Stocking:** no state or province proposed stocking yellow perch. No state or province listed northern pike for expanded distribution, though Alaska suggested it could become economically important. Several southern and western states reported unsuccessful stocking of northern pike, muskellunge, or walleye.
- **Hybrids:** muskellunge × northern pike hybrids are stocked in Virginia and Missouri, and results there were judged to justify continued restocking.
- **Commercial catches:** Canadian northern pike averaged about 3,500 t (1955–1976). Canadian walleye averaged 5,319 t over the same period. Canadian sauger exceeded 1,747 t on average since 1956. Canadian yellow perch fluctuated between 3,318 and 14,136 t (1955–1976). Yellow perch peaked in the U.S. at 7,401 t in 1934.
- **Management trends:** creel limits were the most common tool for walleye. Muskellunge minimum sizes were typically 61–76.2 cm. Canadian commercial management uses quotas, minimum sizes, mesh regulations, and limited entry.
- **Distribution:** muskellunge are concentrated in the Great Lakes states, Lake St. Clair, and Ontario. Walleye range is expanding in the Columbia River system. Yellow perch range continues to expand through natural spread and unplanned introductions.
- **Native-range limits:** the St. Lawrence River system did not effectively establish muskellunge, walleye, and sauger in Canadian Atlantic provinces.
- **Conclusion:** the authors suggest that large impoundments will increasingly be considered coolwater habitat, and that stream habitat data are limited in most states.
