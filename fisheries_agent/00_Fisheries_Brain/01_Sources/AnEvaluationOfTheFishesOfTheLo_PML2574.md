# An Evaluation of the Fishes of the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document is a technical evaluation by R.A. Valdez (October 1994, revised December 1994) of the [[Fishes of the Lower Colorado River]], compiled as an appendix to a [[Bureau of Reclamation]] biological assessment. It reviews the historical and current ichthyofauna, the status of federally listed native species under the [[Endangered Species Act]], the stocking and recovery efforts for [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and the division-by-division fish communities of the river from Davis Dam to the International Boundary. The document also includes Reclamation's 1995 notice of informational workshops and an information-needs questionnaire for the biological assessment, both of which are appended.

## Background & Description

The lower basin's fish fauna became known in the mid-1800s through military and exploratory expeditions. The Colorado River Basin has one of the highest levels of fish species endemism of any North American basin (figure given as 84% in the OCR text, cited to Miller 1959). Minckley (1979) recorded 46 species (9 native, 37 nonnative) between Davis Dam (RM 422.0) and the International Boundary (RM 704.2). Of these, 15 were uncommon or rare, and 22 were considered the existing ichthyofauna, with usually no more than 5 or 6 species at any one location.

Nine native species are discussed:

- Extirpated from the mainstem: Colorado squawfish, Pacific tenpounder (machete), desert pupfish
- Restricted mainly to reservoirs: bonytail, razorback sucker
- Restricted to tributaries: roundtail chub, woundfin, flannelmouth sucker
- Catadromous, found only in small numbers in the Limitrophe Division: striped mullet

Five of the nine are listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (Colorado squawfish, bonytail, razorback sucker, woundfin, desert pupfish). Flannelmouth sucker and roundtail chub are Category 2 candidate species. Pacific tenpounder and striped mullet are unlisted.

Additional species profiled include [[Humpback Chub]] (endangered; six populations), the [[Virgin River Chub]] and [[Pahranagut Roundtail Chub]] (endangered subspecies), and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (state-sensitive, no federal protection).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The river is divided into ten operational divisions defined by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], ranging from the channelized, dam-controlled Mohave Valley reach to the braided, marsh-rich Limitrophe Division near Mexico. Key habitat features include:

- **Mohave Valley and Topock Gorge:** dam-regulated flows; Topock Marsh and Beal Lake, the largest remaining backwater system on the lower river.
- **Havasu Division:** largely inundated by [[Lake Havasu]], formed by Parker Dam (built late 1930s).
- **Parker Division:** the longest free-flowing segment of the lower basin below Grand Canyon.
- **Imperial and Laguna Divisions:** sedimentation behind Imperial Dam has created extensive marshes; Mittry Lake is a major off-river backwater.
- **Yuma and Limitrophe Divisions:** silt-laden, tamarisk-lined channels whose fish communities are dominated by introduced and tolerant species.

Across divisions, nonnative species dominate. Introduced fishes such as [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Threadfin Shad]] form most of the community. Predation by flathead catfish and smallmouth bass is identified as a significant cause of low survival for stocked squawfish and razorback suckers.

Water-quality and habitat monitoring is listed as a needed baseline item, with specific reference to dissolved oxygen, temperature, plankton and nutrients (Biological Assessment information needs).

## Key Findings

**Species status and distribution**

- Colorado squawfish was extirpated from the lower basin around 1960; it persists in the Upper Basin (Green, Colorado, San Juan, Gunnison, Yampa, White and Dolores rivers).
- Between 1985 and 1989, 770,210 Colorado squawfish were released into the Salt and Verde rivers. Only 44 were captured in intensive surveys. Present distribution of the released fish is unknown.
- Razorback suckers: about 11,888,900 were released from 1981 to 1989 across the Salt, Verde and Gila rivers. Only 519 were captured or recaptured in intensive surveys.
- Lake Mohave held an estimated 60,000 adult razorback suckers in 1991, declining to about 25,000 in 1993. Little or no natural recruitment appears to occur anywhere in the basin, except possibly Lake Mohave.
- Bonytail: the largest number collected from Lake Mohave since 1976 was 34 adults (through 1988); young have not been observed in the lower basin since about 1965.
- Humpback chub: the largest population is in Grand Canyon, including the lower 8 miles of the Little Colorado River and nine mainstem aggregations (RM 30 to 213).
- Woundfin is now restricted to the Virgin River system; desert pupfish persists in small populations in Sonora and Baja California, Mexico.

**Lake Mohave Native Fish Rearing Program (started 1991)**

- Goal: produce and stock 50,000 razorback suckers. Releases were 0 (1991), 229 (1992) and 487 (1993).
- Reported problems included larval and egg mortality, invertebrate predation, and predation at Davis Cove after a retention net was breached during storms.
- In 1994, over 10,000 wild larvae were collected; about 2,300 adults over 200 mm TL were PIT-tagged and released.

**Division-level fish communities**

- Mohave Valley: 22 species reported (3 presumed extirpated, 15 introduced, 4 native).
- Topock Gorge: 20 species (17 introduced, 3 native).
- Havasu: 19 species (15 introduced, 3 native, 1 hypothetical).
- Parker: 23 species (18 introduced, 3 native, 2 hypothetical).
- Palo Verde: 19 species (15 introduced, 4 native).
- Cibola: 17 species (13 introduced, 3 native, 1 hypothetical).
- Imperial: 17 species (17 introduced, 5 native, 1 hypothetical per the source); red shiners made up 98.2% of mainstem numbers.
- Laguna: 21 species (15 introduced, 6 native).
- Yuma: 34 species (20 introduced, 9 native, 5 hypothetical); sailfin mollys were over 62% of mainstem numbers.
- Limitrophe: 26 species (19 introduced, 7 native); red shiners were 68% of mainstem fish.

**Regulatory and planning items**

- Reclamation initiated a biological assessment under section 7 of the Endangered Species Act covering operations from Lake Mead to the international boundary.
- Public informational workshops were scheduled for April 4 (Las Vegas), April 5 (Phoenix) and April 6 (Yuma), 1995.
- An information-needs questionnaire asks stakeholders to supply operational data, environmental baseline data, past and present stocking records, and reasonably certain future non-federal activities for cumulative-effects analysis.
