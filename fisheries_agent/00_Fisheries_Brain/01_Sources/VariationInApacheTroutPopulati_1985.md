# Variation in Apache Trout Populations in the White Mountains, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #freshwater-ecology #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This 1985 study by John N. Rinne examined **644 trout** from **46 streams** in the White Mountains of east-central Arizona (1977–1982) to assess the taxonomic purity and population status of the native **[[Apache Trout]]** (*Salmo apache*, now classified as *Oncorhynchus apache*), relative to introduced **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Salmo gairdneri*). Using three meristic characters (vertebrae counts, pyloric caecae counts, scales above the lateral line) and six morphometric characters, Rinne demonstrated that the original type description (Miller 1972) understated the natural range of morphometric variability in Apache trout. Populations on the **Fort Apache Indian Reservation** showed substantially less evidence of hybridization/contamination than those on **Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest** lands, a pattern strongly correlated with historical rainbow trout stocking practices. The paper concludes with a management classification scheme ("pure," "mostly pure," and unsuitable) intended to guide the **Apache Trout Recovery Plan**, which aimed to establish 30 self-sustaining native populations.

## Background & Description

### Meristic Characters (primary diagnostic tool)
Three characters were identified from the literature (Needham & Gard 1959; Miller 1972; Behnke & Zarn 1976) as most effective for separating Apache trout from rainbow trout:

| Character | Apache Trout Range (Miller 1972) | Management Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Scales above lateral line | 32–40 | ≥31 (mean) |
| Pyloric caecae | 22–40 | ≤33 (mean) |
| Vertebrae | 58–61 | <60 (mean) |

- Meristic characters were judged **more taxonomically stable** than morphometric characters, being less influenced by non-hereditary factors (size, sex, allometric growth) and more reflective of innate genetic identity.
- Vertebral counts were obtained via X-ray methodology (modified from R.R. Miller 1957).

### Morphometric Characters (secondary/supportive tool)
Six characters, expressed as per mille (‰) of standard length, were selected due to minimal overlap with rainbow trout ranges:
- **SNL** — Snout length
- **DFB** — Dorsal fin base length
- **DFDL** — Depressed dorsal fin length
- **AFB** — Anal fin base length
- **AFDL** — Depressed anal fin length
- **ADFDL** — Depressed adipose fin length

**Key finding:** The type-series description (Miller 1972, based on East Fork White River specimens) *underrepresented* the true morphometric range of pure Apache trout populations. When ranges of data from the type locality (East Fork of the White River) and **[[Ord Creek]]** (paratype locality) were used instead of Miller's original published ranges:
- Only **2 of 35 (6%)** nontypical morphometric ranges were classifiable as Apache trout under Miller's original ranges.
- **18 of 35 (51%)** were classifiable as typical when using the broader East Fork/Ord Creek ranges — reclassifying 5 of 14 previously "atypical" streams as pure *Salmo apache*.
- On an individual fish-character basis, only 10% (East Fork) and 6.5% (Ord Creek) of per mille values fell outside the revised ranges, versus 34% outside Miller's original (1972) ranges.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Native to headwater streams and cienegas of the White Mountains, Arizona, part of the Salt/Black/White River system (Gila River drainage).
- **Co-occurring/introduced species:** [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), and [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) — all non-native salmonids introduced via stocking, posing hybridization and competitive displacement risks.
- **Land tenure and habitat protection:** Streams on the **Fort Apache Indian Reservation** were historically protected from stocking and public angling access since 1955 tribal conservation measures, preserving genetic integrity. Streams on **Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest** and **Coronado National Forest** lands were more road-accessible and heavily stocked with hatchery rainbow trout, resulting in greater introgression risk.
- **Stocking history:** Rainbow trout stocking on Apache National Forest streams shifted from fingerling releases (1930s–1950s, greater reproductive/hybridization risk) to catchable-size releases (20–25 cm) from the 1960s onward, which reduced (but did not eliminate) introgression risk due to poor upstream movement, high angler harvest, and low survival/reproductive success of hatchery-reared catchable fish.
- **Movement ecology:** Consistent with broader salmonid literature (Newell 1957; Bjornn & Mallet 1964; Cresswell 1981), stocked rainbow trout in this system exhibited limited upstream movement, remaining near stocking points or dispersing downstream — limiting contact with upstream native Apache trout populations.

## Key Findings

1. **Widespread but patchy variation:** 24 of 46 stream samples had meristic means within Miller's (1972) published range for Apache trout, but morphometric means for many of these fell outside the range — indicating the type description was too narrow, not necessarily that hybridization was occurring.
2. **Reservation vs. National Forest disparity:** Nearly all Fort Apache Indian Reservation samples tributary to the Black River matched published Apache trout meristic ranges; fewer than half of the equivalent Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest samples did — attributed to differential stocking histories and access.
3. **Low-frequency "contamination":** In 46 of 69 (67%) cases of meristic disagreement, only one or two fish per sample deviated from expected ranges, and overall disagreement was low (6.5%) — suggesting localized/natural variation or minor hybridization rather than wholesale genetic replacement.
4. **No clear intermediacy/hybrid gradient:** Contrary to expectations under active introgressive hybridization (Hubbs 1955), meristic characters in "atypical" populations did not consistently trend toward rainbow trout values, and no gradient/zone of impurity was observed in several National Forest streams (Fish, Corduroy, Double Cienega creeks) — arguing against pervasive ongoing hybridization in these systems.
5. **Revised morphometric ranges recommended:** Ranges of variation from the type locality (East Fork of White River) and Ord Creek should supersede Miller's (1972) original type-series ranges for morphometric classification purposes.
6. **Management classification scheme established**, based on combinations of the three meristic characters (means):
   - **Pure Apache Trout populations (13 total):** North Canyon, Mamie, East Fork of White River, Firebox, Ord, Sun, Boggy (Fort Apache), Big Bonita, Big Bonita Cienega, Little Bonita, Flash, Boggy (National Forest), Soldier creeks.
   - **Mostly Pure (one character outside range, 18 total):** Grant (Coronado NF), Mineral, Deep, North/South Fork Diamond, Little Diamond, Paradise, Bear Cienega, Crooked, Hughey, Paddy (Fort Apache), Squaw, Centerfire, Conklin, Corduroy, Double Cienega, Hanagan, Snake, Fish creeks.
   - Remaining populations were **not recommended** for use in recovery efforts.
7. **Reservation dominance in pure stocks:** 10 of 13 "pure" populations occur on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation; only 10 of 18 "mostly pure" populations do — reinforcing the Reservation's role as a genetic reservoir.
8. **Biochemical genetic corroboration:** Electrophoretic analyses (Loudenslager et al., in press) independently confirmed *Salmo apache* status in five "pure" populations (Flash, East Fork, North Canyon, Soldier, Big Bonita creeks) and two "mostly pure" populations (Squaw, Crooked creeks), validating the meristic classification approach.
9. **Management recommendation:** Given the ambiguity of morphometric variation (natural vs. hybridization-driven), the author recommends relying primarily on the **three meristic characters** (a "grading" approach previously applied to cutthroat trout management by Binns 1977) for classifying population purity in recovery planning.
10. **Recovery context:** Findings directly support the goals of the [[Apache Trout Recovery Plan]] (US Department of Interior 1979), which called for chemical renovation of contaminated streams and reintroduction of verified pure Apache trout stocks to establish 30 self-sustaining populations for eventual delisting.
