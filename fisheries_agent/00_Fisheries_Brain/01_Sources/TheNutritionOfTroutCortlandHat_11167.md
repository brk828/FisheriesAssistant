# Nutrition of Trout: Vitamin Requirements, Blood Oxygen Response, and Hatchery Practice (Cortland Hatchery Report No. 15, 1946)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This bulletin reports the 1946 season of the [[Cortland Hatchery]], an experimental trout-nutrition unit run jointly by the [[New York State Conservation Department]], the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and [[Cornell University]]. The work estimated daily dietary requirements for three vitamins (riboflavin, pantothenic acid and thiamin) in [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], and tested the effect of dissolved oxygen on blood red cell counts, anemia-curing supplements, paprika coloration, and factors affecting the carrying capacity of troughs and ponds. The study is largely a set of tentative requirement estimates and preliminary experiments rather than a complete model.

## Background & Description

The hatchery's vitamin program had run for several seasons, beginning with the unit's organization as an experimental station in 1932. Earlier reports in the series (Tunison et al., 1939 to 1944; Phillips et al., 1945) provided the methods and prior estimates. Experimental fish were held in troughs from late April to early October. Two temperature regimes were used, 47°F and 52°F, housed in separate buildings. Diets were prepared from hog spleen or beef liver mixed with basal ingredients, with vitamin levels varied across a set of diet numbers (Table 1). Fish were fed a constant percentage of body weight, so vitamin intake was expressed as milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day.

Liver vitamin content was measured with microbiological assays: [[Lactobacillus Casei Assay]] for riboflavin and pantothenic acid, and a [[Phycomyces Blakesleanus Assay]] for thiamin. Results were analyzed by analysis of variance on a split-plot design. The "tentative requirement" was defined as the dietary level that produced maximum liver storage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Hatchery trout are sensitive to water quality. The report links dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide to blood chemistry, and it notes that metabolic wastes (ammonia was chosen as an indicator) may limit carrying capacity in series-connected ponds and troughs. Anemia in hatchery-reared trout was also associated with diet and with the liver content of vitamins. Comparative data on [[Blueback Herring]] (cited as blueback salmon) and [[Menhaden]] (from Hall et al., 1926) placed the oxygen-blood response in a wider fish context.

## Key Findings

**Riboflavin**
- No difference in requirement among brook, brown and rainbow trout, and none between 47°F and 52°F.
- Tentative requirement: 0.44 to 0.68 mg per kg body weight per day, in agreement with earlier work.
- Maximum liver storage: 15 to 16 micrograms per gram of liver.
- Requirement did not change between the two analysis series.

**Pantothenic Acid**
- No difference in requirement among the three species; temperature did not alter it.
- Tentative requirement: 0.97 to 1.25 mg per kg body weight per day.
- Maximum liver storage averaged 18 to 20 micrograms per gram of liver.
- Liver storage was higher in the second (later, larger fish) analysis series for brown and rainbow trout. Authors attributed this to unmeasured factors such as fish size or age, while the requirement for maximum storage stayed the same.

**Thiamin**
- Requirement identical across the three species.
- Requirement: 0.150 to 0.186 mg per kg body weight per day.
- Maximum liver storage: 3.5 to 4.5 micrograms per gram of liver.
- Second-series liver levels were highly significantly higher than the first in all species. The authors concluded fish size or age was the likely cause, and that the temperature effect reported in 1945 probably reflected uncontrolled size differences.

**Oxygen and Red Cell Content**
- Brook trout (3 to 4 inches; 18 fish per experiment, repeated six times) were held in 10-liter unaerated aquaria.
- Dissolved oxygen fell from 10.2 ppm to 1.7 ppm, and carbon dioxide rose from 0.9 ppm to 6.3 ppm.
- Blood red cell count rose rapidly during the first hour and then plateaued, though oxygen kept falling.
- In a Cortland pond series, the last pond (5.2 ppm oxygen) held trout with a blood count 11.5 percent higher than the first pond (10 ppm oxygen).
- Results were described as preliminary, with dissolved oxygen possibly useful for early anemia diagnosis.

**Anemia Experiments**
- Synthetic diets with vitamins force-fed in gelatin capsules did not reverse anemia from pyridoxine, pantothenic acid and riboflavin alone.
- Those three vitamins plus folic acid caused a significant rise in blood count, far below beef liver (958 thousand cells/mm³ versus 572 thousand on the synthetic diet).
- Folic acid alone had no effect. The authors concluded that the anemia-curing factor ("Factor H") is probably a group of elements rather than a single factor.

**Paprika Coloration**
- Domestic paprika colored brook trout as well as or better than imported brands, reaching intense color in 5 to 6 weeks.
- Domestic paprika costs about half as much as the imported type.
- Brown trout fingerlings showed little or no coloration after four months, so they do not respond to paprika the way brook trout do.

**Supplements**
- A commercial riboflavin-rich supplement added to the Cortland No. 6 diet for 20 weeks to brook trout gave no measurable benefit. Cumulative lab data advised against supplements because their benefits did not justify their cost.

**Carrying Capacity**
- New experiments began to test metabolic wastes (ammonia as an index) against temperature, fish weight, water volume and water exchange rate. No conclusions were drawn yet.
