# Mortality Rates in the California Striped Bass Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 study by [[Harold K. Chadwick]] of the California Department of Fish and Game estimates annual mortality rates for the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin River Striped Bass]] population using disk dangler tag returns. Tagging was conducted each spring from 1958 to 1961 in the western [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]], with 11,823 nonreward tags used in the estimates. Exploitation rates varied widely between years (0.372 in 1958 to 0.190 in 1961), and the author concludes that tag returns give reasonably valid estimates, although exploitation is probably underestimated. The work was prompted by a decline in sport fishing success in the late 1940s and early 1950s and was part of a wider program to evaluate regulations.

## Background & Description

The [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*, cited in the source as *Roccus saxatilis*) is the focus of the study. The main California population spawns in the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta and its tributaries in spring, migrates to brackish and saltwater bays and the Pacific Ocean, and returns to the Delta in fall or the following spring.

The earlier [[Calhoun Tagging Study]] (1952) failed to yield satisfactory mortality estimates because tagged bass were lost in commercial salmon and shad gill nets. These commercial fisheries closed in 1957, so the present study was largely unaffected by commercial fishing.

Tagging used [[Disk Dangler Tags]], a modified Atkins design consisting of a cellulose nitrate disk fastened below the first dorsal fin with tantalum or stainless steel wire. Reward tags bore a "$5 REWARD" notice, applied at ratios of 1:2 (1958) and 1:4 (later years) against nonreward tags. Fish were classed as good, poor, or bleeding at release, and fork length, tagger, gear and date were recorded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Migration patterns shape the sample and the estimates. Fish tagged in the western Delta were caught in drift gill nets near the junction of the [[Sacramento River]] and [[San Joaquin River]], while others were taken at [[Fremont Weir]] (upper Sacramento River), at [[Prisoners Point]] (eastern San Joaquin Delta) and by angling in [[San Pablo Bay]]. Immature females do not migrate to the Delta, so the western Delta sample under-represents them, while males and larger fish may be overrepresented.

Commercial and angling vulnerability varied with weather and water conditions, which the author suggests drove annual changes in [[Catchability]]. Angling was the principal source of mortality, and the fishery was spatially widespread, with the [[Pacific Ocean]] fishery the most diffuse.

Natural mortality was estimated alongside fishing mortality. Blood lactic acid measurements (630 mg/l in good-condition fish versus 1,170 mg/l in poor-condition fish) supported the physiological validity of the condition classes.

## Key Findings

- **Sample:** 11,823 disk dangler tags from western Delta spring tagging (1958–1961) formed the basis of the estimates; 8 of 4,386 tagged bass were found dead within a week in 1958.
- **Exploitation:** The annual rate ranged from 0.372 (1958) to 0.190 (1961). The 1958 rate was significantly higher than any later year, and 1958 survival was significantly lower than in 1959 or 1960 (estimated survival 0.319 in 1958 to 0.662 in 1961).
- **Nonresponse:** Calculated nonresponse varied from 0.311 to 0.470 among years, with a weighted mean of 0.385 applied to all years. Nonresponse was assumed equal across localities.
- **Poor-condition fish:** Fish in poor condition at release were excluded from all estimates because returns were significantly lower in 1958 and 1960.
- **Tag effects:** Vulnerability was reduced by about a third to a half in the first month after tagging; shedding was judged to have an insignificant effect on mortality estimates.
- **Sex differences:** In 1960, returns were higher for females (17.3%) than males (13.6%), suggesting mature males have lower exploitation and higher survival, though evidence is limited.
- **Fall tagging:** Fall 1958 Delta tagging yielded a first-year exploitation rate of 0.33, intermediate between the 1958 and 1959 spring rates. Angling and natural mortality appeared to act at roughly proportional rates within the year.
- **San Pablo Bay:** First-year exploitation was considerably higher (0.551 for 1958 and 0.572 for 1959), attributed to the migration of immature legal-sized bass that remain in the Bay.
- **Fremont Weir:** Returns from upper Sacramento River fish (28.3%) exceeded western Delta returns (23.1%), indicating the Delta sample slightly underestimates fishing mortality.
- **Overall conclusion:** Tag-based estimates are reasonably valid, but exploitation is probably underestimated. The large annual fluctuations are attributed to catchability changes, and the author calls for better catch and effort measurements.
