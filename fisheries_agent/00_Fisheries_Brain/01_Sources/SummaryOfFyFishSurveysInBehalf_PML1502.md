# Summary of FY 1999 Fish Surveys for the Gila River Basin Long-Term Monitoring Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-monitoring

## Executive Summary

This report summarizes fish sampling carried out in fiscal year 1999 (1 October 1998 to 30 September 1999) under a long-term monitoring plan for fish populations in selected waters of the [[Gila River Basin]], Arizona. Sampling was a cooperative effort of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Arizona State University]] and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], and was submitted in partial fulfillment of a cooperative agreement between the Bureau and the University. Seven monitoring areas were sampled, covering rivers, a river reach below dams, and several canals.

A total of 27 taxa (including *Lepomis* hybrids) was captured. Only four were native (about 15% of taxa), and native fishes made up 0% to 74.3% of the catch depending on station. Community structure differed strongly among waters, from native-dominated [[San Pedro River]] and [[Salt River]] reaches to canals dominated by introduced centrarchids or cyprinids. The authors caution that data are not strictly comparable among stations because of differing gear, effort and sampling quality, and they close with recommendations on training, mapping, canal access, operator communication and electrofishing technique.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The survey was the FY 1999 component of a [[Long-Term Monitoring Plan]] for fish populations. Protocols followed Clarkson (1996a), with standard operating procedures for canals and for rivers and streams. Results are not compared with FY 1995 and 1996 data (Clarkson 1998) in this report.

**Monitored waters (standard stations):**
1. [[San Pedro River]], downstream from the U.S.–Mexico international boundary
2. [[Gila River]], between Coolidge Dam and the Ashurst-Hayden Diversion
3. [[Salt River]], between Stewart Mountain Dam and Granite Reef Diversion
4. [[Central Arizona Project Canal]] (CAP), at selected pumping plants
5. [[SRP North Canal]] (SRPn), Salt River Project
6. [[SRP South Canal]] (SRPs)
7. [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]] (FCG)

Additional opportunistic, non-standard sampling was done on SRPn and at the SRP Consolidated and Tempe canals. All standard sampling occurred between 29 September 1998 and 28 May 1999.

**Gear.** Methods included [[Backpack Electrofishing]], [[Boat-Mounted Electrofishing]] (used on canals and in a supplementary role), and seines used mainly for opportunistic, non-quantitative samples. Canal fish barriers, where present, were used as sampling divisions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fishes.** The four native taxa were [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]]. No single stream or canal produced more than three natives. Natives were present at 18% to 25% of stations' species lists, except in the CAP canal, which had none.

**Non-native fishes.** The remaining taxa were introduced. They included [[Mosquitofish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Carp]], [[Grass Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Redbelly Tilapia]] (*Tilapia zillii*), blue tilapia, and undetermined tilapia (mostly young of year). Non-native species were numerous in canals and in the lower reaches of the rivers.

**Canal barriers.** On the SRPn, SRPs and FCG canals, electric fish barriers divided the canals into upstream and downstream sections. The proportion of natives differed sharply above and below these barriers, as discussed under Key Findings.

**Limitations.** Total fish numbers varied widely among stations because of differing sampling effort and gear. Data from SRPn, SRPs and FCG were opportunistic and qualitative, except for samples above the electric fish barriers. Estimates of areal coverage were not measured and were judged unreliable. Electrofishing efficiency varied among crews.

## Key Findings

### Overall monitoring results
- **27 taxa** were captured (including *Lepomis* hybrids). By waterbody: FCG canal 8; San Pedro River and CAP canal 12 each; Salt River and SRPs 13 each; Gila River and SRPn 17 each (the SRPn figure is 17 in the summary and 15 for the Gila, per the table).
- **Native share of catch** ranged from 0% (CAP canal) to 74.3% (San Pedro River). Gila and Salt rivers and SRPn, SRPs and FCG canals had native shares within this range, shown in Table 3 of the report.
- Among stations, native taxa comprised **18% to 25%** of species counts, except the CAP canal, where none were found.

### San Pedro River (29 Oct to 26 Nov, 8 of 9 stations sampled)
- **12 species**, of which **3 were native** (longfin dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker), about a quarter of species.
- Longfin dace occurred at all stations; desert sucker at 6 of 8; Sonora sucker only at the two lower stations.
- Native fishes made up 59% of the catch in the upper reach, 96% in the middle reach, and 39.9% in the lower reach.
- **Longfin dace** was the most abundant species; **mosquitofish** (13% of catch) was the most abundant non-native and second overall. Red shiner (4.3%) and black bullhead (4.1%) followed.
- Desert sucker made up slightly under 10% of the catch; Sonora sucker was under 1%.

### Gila River (26 Oct to 10 Nov 1998, 10 of 12 stations sampled)
- **15 species** (including *Lepomis* hybrids), with **3 natives** (longfin dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker) comprising about a fifth of species.
- Native fishes were only **22.4%** of the total catch.
- Upper reach native proportions were 13%, 29%, 27% and 11% of catch across the four reaches (as reported by reach order).
- **Red shiner** was the most abundant species overall (**36%** of catch). It dominated reach 1 (followed by threadfin shad) and reach 2 (followed by Sonora sucker), and was second in reach 3.
- **Channel catfish** was the most abundant fish in the lower reach, followed closely by mosquitofish and yellow bullhead.
- Non-native species contributing under 5% each were considered uncommon or rare.

### Salt River (15 Oct to 4 Nov 1998, all 3 stations sampled)
- **13 species**: 3 native (23%) and 10 non-native.
- Native fishes were **44.7%** of the total catch.
- **Desert sucker** was the most abundant species overall (**35%**), and the most abundant at the two upper stations. Sonora sucker contributed 10%; roundtail chub was represented by one individual.
- **Largemouth bass** (17% of catch) was the second most abundant species overall and the most abundant fish at the lower station.
- **Mosquitofish** (15%) was the second most abundant at the middle station; **blue tilapia** (14%) was second at the upper station.
- Channel catfish and flathead catfish were each under 1% of the catch.

### CAP Canal (29 Oct 1998 above Phoenix; 25–29 Jan 1999 below Phoenix, all stations sampled)
- **12 taxa, all non-native**. No new species were detected relative to previous years.
- **Centrarchids dominated**: **bluegill** was the most abundant species (**53%**), followed by **carp** (22%), hybrid *Lepomis* (8%), redear sunfish (5%) and largemouth bass (4%). Other species each contributed under 3%.
- Hybrid sunfish were predominant at the upper station; carp at the middle station; bluegill at the lower stations (except one).
- Station 4-4-1 (Bouse) had no electrofishing because of equipment failure.

### SRP North Canal (SRPn; 3 Dec to 22 Dec 1998, sampled routinely plus two boat-electrofishing samples)
- **17 species** (including undetermined tilapia, mostly young of year) and **3 natives**.
- **16 species above** and **13 below** the electric fish barrier. Goldfish occurred only below; bigmouth buffalo, rainbow trout, undetermined tilapia and blue tilapia only above.
- Natives made up **17.1%** of the total catch. Desert sucker, Sonora sucker and roundtail chub accounted for about 7%, 6% and 2% respectively; these suckers were probably grossly underestimated because collectors took subsamples from large aggregations.
- Non-native tilapia and **red shiner** were the most abundant species (**57%** and **15%** of total numbers). Channel catfish (7%) and flathead catfish (1%) followed.
- Tilapias were predominant above the barrier but absent below, where red shiner dominated the upper reach, Sonora sucker the middle, and desert sucker the lower (though the lower reach was sparsely populated).

### SRP South Canal (SRPs; 24 Oct to 5 Nov 1998, all stations sampled)
- **16 species** including undetermined tilapia and **3 natives**. **12 species** were found above and **12** below the barrier.
- Desert sucker and Sonora sucker occurred at all three lower stations; roundtail chub at the upper two.
- Natives made up about **25%** of the total catch. Sonora sucker was the most abundant native, contributing 11%.
- Non-native tilapias (*T. zillii* plus undetermined) were the most abundant fishes overall, accounting for roughly 60% of the catch (reported in the source text as 6 to 9%-range OCR text; treat with caution).
- Above the barrier, tilapias dominated; below it, native desert sucker was predominant at the upper station and red shiner was the only non-native found at all stations.

### Florence-Casa Grande Canal (FCG; 1 October 1998)
- **8 species**, including **2 natives**.
- Native fishes comprised only **1.4%** of total numbers. Native Sonora sucker was found at five stations, mostly in the upper feeder canal; roundtail chub was found at one station.
- **Red shiner** was the predominant species overall, followed by mosquitofish (3%) and threadfin shad.

### SRP Consolidated Canal (24 Oct 1998, opportunistic)
- **10 fish species**, including native desert sucker (17 specimens, fourth in abundance).
- Mosquitofish was most abundant, followed by red shiner, redbelly tilapia and channel catfish. Blue tilapia, yellow bullhead, grass carp, carp and largemouth bass were rare.

### Tempe Canal (24 Oct 1998, qualitative visual observation)
- **5 fish species** observed, including native desert sucker and Sonora sucker, both considered abundant.
- Channel catfish were common; koi-variety carp and stocked grass carp were also present.

### Recommendations
1. **Train all personnel** in program goals, protocols, equipment use and data forms, to improve data quality and reduce variability in areal coverage and sampling efficiency.
2. **Draw a sketch map** for each station on each visit, showing wetted margin, macrohabitats, substrate, flow direction, north arrow and site notes.
3. **Obtain keys** to locked gates along SRPn and SRPs canal roads and provide them to authorized users.
4. **Improve communication** between canal operators (SRP and SCID) and monitoring crews so sampling can coincide with scheduled outages.
5. **Evaluate boat-mounted electrofishing** as a standard technique for SRPn and SRPs canals under normal flow, because future operations may lack scheduled drawdowns.
