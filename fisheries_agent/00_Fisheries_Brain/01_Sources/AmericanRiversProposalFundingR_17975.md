# American Rivers Grant Proposals to National Fish and Wildlife Foundation (1993)

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #conservation-policy #habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

This document comprises three grant proposals submitted by [[American Rivers]] to the [[National Fish and Wildlife Foundation]] on December 30, 1993. The proposals address: (1) native fish habitat assessment in the [[Gila River]] watershed using historical distribution data; (2) floodplain habitat restoration in the [[Mississippi River]] and [[Missouri River]] basins following the 1993 floods; and (3) urban river restoration through the Urban Lifelines program. Total funding requested across all three projects is $195,000 in NFWF grants with $205,000 in challenge grants, for a combined total of $400,000. The proposals emphasize non-structural conservation approaches, community engagement, and policy reform to protect aquatic biodiversity and restore degraded riverine habitats.

## Background & Description

**Native Fish Habitat Project (Gila River):** Arizona harbors more threatened or endangered fishes than any other U.S. state. Of 33 known native fish species in Arizona, one is globally extinct, five have disappeared from the state, and 18 are federally listed as threatened or endangered. The [[Gila River]] basin has the dubious distinction that all native freshwater fish species are either extinct, federally listed, or candidates for listing—no other river basin worldwide shares this status. The project, led by Dr. [[Wendell Minckley]] (Arizona State University Professor of Zoology), will compile and analyze historical fish collection data from museum specimens (Level I reliability), peer-reviewed literature (Level II), and agency reports (Level III) dating from 1800s military expeditions through the 1980s. The methodology assumes native species define habitat quality by their presence and population trends—areas where natives flourish retain higher ecosystem integrity than areas where they have disappeared.

**Floodplain Habitat Restoration:** The 1993 [[Great Flood]] in the Midwest exposed failures of century-old flood control policies relying on levees and channelization. More than 20 million acres of wetlands have been eliminated from the Mississippi-Missouri drainage since the late 1700s. Despite $25 billion spent nationally on flood control, per capita flood damages more than doubled between 1951 and 1986 (inflation-adjusted), with 1993 damages exceeding $12 billion. The proposal advocates non-structural alternatives utilizing natural floodplain functions, modeled on the [[Louisa County Iowa]] levee district acquisition project. Farmers owning more than 400,000 acres have expressed interest in buyouts or easements.

**Urban Lifelines Program:** Approximately 200 million Americans rely on urban rivers for drinking water; 130 of the 150 largest U.S. cities are located on sizeable rivers. Despite Clean Water Act improvements, urban rivers suffer from stormwater runoff, combined sewer overflows (affecting 1,000+ cities), trash accumulation, and channelization. A 1993 Natural Resources Defense Council study found 43% of water systems serving ~120 million Americans violated the Safe Drinking Water Act. The program will recognize exemplary restoration efforts, provide technical resources, coordinate river clean-ups in five target cities annually, and strengthen federal policies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Gila River Ecosystem:** The [[Gila River]] basin native fish fauna has declined dramatically over 150+ years of documented sampling. Arid lands of western North America have 105 freshwater fish taxa at risk, with nine extinct. The project will map distributions of both native and non-native fishes; non-natives typically indicate habitat degradation that allows invasion and establishment of introduced species, either through direct competition/predation or as indicators of altered conditions incompatible with native species survival.

**Mississippi-Missouri Floodplain Ecology:** Nearly all of the Mississippi has been straightened, leveed, or altered. Oxbows and meanders providing critical fish spawning habitat have been destroyed. Ten federally listed endangered species inhabit or visit the [[Upper Mississippi River]], and three listed species inhabit the Missouri. Of approximately 390 fish species in the Mississippi basin, 61 native riverine species are listed by states as threatened, endangered, or nearly extinct on the mainstem alone. Wetlands under normal conditions provide decaying plant material washed into rivers during spring floods, forming the base of food webs for juvenile fish and aquatic insects. The 1993 flood flushed high concentrations of atrazine and other herbicides, plus phosphates and nitrates from agricultural runoff, disrupting oxygen balance and photosynthesis.

**Urban River Habitat:** While cities are not natural environments for most wildlife, urban rivers and riverside greenways can provide rich habitat corridors linking species with larger river systems upstream and downstream. The [[Kenilworth Aquatic Gardens]] on the [[Anacostia River]] (Washington, D.C.) demonstrates restoration potential—a restored marsh now supports native grasses, wildflowers, insects, fish, and birds. However, most urban rivers are concrete-lined, culverted, and lack self-cleaning environmental resiliency that streamside vegetation and wetland communities provide. A U.S. Public Interest Research Group study found 21% of major industrial and waste treatment facilities (mostly urban) are in serious or chronic violation of discharge permits.

## Key Findings

**Native Fish Habitat Project Deliverables:**
- Two-year project duration (March 1994–February 1996)
- Year one budget: $104,000 ($44,000 anticipated federal support from five agencies; $60,000 from NFWF grant [$20,000] plus challenge grant [$40,000])
- Three final products: (1) narrative report interpreting faunal changes and recommending preserves for aquatic biodiversity conservation; (2) identification of knowledge gaps requiring additional species/site surveys; (3) GIS-compatible documented database for all freshwater fishes of entire Gila River basin
- Database will allow transfer to other base maps (geologic, topographic, climatologic, vegetation, land ownership) for rapid assessment of biodiversity preservation opportunities
- Methodology applicable throughout American Southwest for regional aquatic biodiversity preserve system

**Floodplain Restoration Project Outcomes:**
- One-year project (January 1994–January 1995)
- Budget: $75,000 ($15,000 NFWF grant; $60,000 challenge grant; $50,000 received from McKnight Foundation)
- Short-term goal: six pilot acquisition projects throughout Mississippi-Missouri basins based on Louisa County model
- FEMA estimates 200 towns (~10,000 structures) interested in relocation; American Rivers secured $81 million increase in relocation funding
- Nearly 13 million acres of farmland inundated in 1993 flood
- Long-term goal: reversal of century-old flood control policy by institutionalizing non-structural alternatives utilizing natural floodplain functions
- President Clinton created White House Floodplain Management task force in response to American Rivers advocacy

**Urban Lifelines Program Components:**
- One-year program (January–December 1994)
- Budget: $140,000 ($35,000 NFWF grant; $105,000 challenge grant); total program cost $200,000 including pass-through funds
- Annual awards ceremony recognizing ten exemplary urban river restoration efforts with $1,000 prizes
- Source Book compilation of "how-to" information for urban river protection
- River clean-up initiatives in five target cities during National River Clean-Up Week (30,600 volunteers cleaned 11,237 river miles in 1993)
- Seed grants ($500–$2,000) for organizational contacts in target cities
- Policy advocacy for Clean Water Act reauthorization (1994) emphasizing non-point source pollution from motor oil, trash, lawn chemicals
- Coordination with National Park Service Rivers, Trails & Conservation Assistance Program

**Cross-Project Management Implications:**
- All three projects emphasize collaboration among federal agencies (U.S. Forest Service, Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Land Management, National Park Service, Bureau of Reclamation, Army Corps of Engineers, EPA, FEMA), state agencies, NGOs, and local communities
- Prototype methodologies transferable to other regions and river systems
- Focus on engaging disadvantaged urban residents and minority communities often overlooked by conservation movement
- Integration of habitat restoration with economic development and employment opportunities, including revitalized Civilian Conservation Corps concept
