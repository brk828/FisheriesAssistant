# Trout Fishing at Cliff Pond, Brewster, Massachusetts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #fisheries-management #two-story-pond

## Executive Summary

This six-year creel census study (1949-1954) of [[Cliff Pond]], [[Brewster]], [[Massachusetts]], evaluated trout stocking success in a "two-story pond" containing both warmwater fish populations and suitable cold, oxygenated deep water for summer trout survival. The 193-acre pond maintains a 29-foot-thick layer of trout water (≤70°F, ≥5 ppm oxygen) between 30-59 feet depth during summer stratification, comprising 26% of total water volume. The study documented dramatic differences in first-year harvest rates (0.5-73%) based on stocking timing, with April plantings vastly outperforming February stockings. Annual angling pressure averaged 105 hours per surface acre, with warmwater species (primarily [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Chain Pickerel]], [[Brown Bullhead]], [[Yellow Perch]]) comprising the bulk of harvest. The report recommends partial poisoning of the epilimnion to reduce warmwater fish competition while preserving the valuable [[Smelt]] forage base for trophy trout production.

## Background & Description

[[Cliff Pond]] is located in [[Barnstable County]] on [[Cape Cod]], Massachusetts, at 26 feet altitude. Physical characteristics include 193 surface acres, maximum depth of 88 feet, average depth of 32 feet, and water transparency of 23 feet. The pond rarely freezes completely in winter and exhibits strong summer thermal stratification. Bottom substrates consist predominantly of sand, gravel, and rubble. The pond is readily accessible with habitation scarce; boats and motors are permitted.

The established fish community includes at least 8 species: [[Micropterus dolomieu]] (smallmouth bass), [[Esox niger]] (chain pickerel), [[Ameiurus nebulosus]] (brown bullhead), [[Perca flavescens]] (yellow perch), [[Lepomis gibbosus]] (pumpkinseed), [[Fundulus diaphanus]] (killifish), [[Notemigonus crysoleucas]] (golden shiner), and [[Osmerus mordax]] (smelt). Landlocked [[Anguilla bostoniensis]] (American eel) are occasionally taken. Bass and smelt represent introduced species, with smelt reproducing abundantly on wind-swept shores. Trout populations are maintained exclusively through hatchery stocking.

Historical stocking records show: 2,425 [[Brook Trout]] (6-11 inches, 1937-46); 17,158 [[Rainbow Trout]] (6-12 inches, 1932-48); and 4,800 [[Brown Trout]] (6-11 inches, 1941-47). During the six-year census period, stocking averaged 15 two-year-old trout (9-12 inches) per surface acre annually. Experimental salmon plantings included 3,375 [[Chinook Salmon]] fingerlings (1931-39) and 60,000 [[Sockeye Salmon]] fry (1939, 1946), none of which established populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The pond's thermal regime creates distinct habitat zones. Summer stratification produces a 29-foot-thick hypolimnetic layer (30-59 feet depth) with temperatures ≤70°F and dissolved oxygen ≥5 ppm, providing 42% of pond bottom area as suitable trout habitat. This represents approximately 26% of total water volume, creating the "two-story" structure that theoretically allows coexistence of warmwater and coldwater fisheries.

The warmwater fish community is estimated to support approximately 50 pounds per surface acre standing crop, with only 10 pounds (20%) of harvestable size. Catchable-size pickerel or smallmouth bass comprised less than 11% of warmwater populations by weight in comparable reclaimed ponds, generally under 5%. [[Yellow Perch]] at 8 inches are described as a nuisance species catchable in large numbers, representing significant competition for trout.

The [[Smelt]] population plays a critical dual role: as abundant forage for large carryover trout enabling rapid growth to trophy size, but potentially competing with fingerling trout for food resources. This creates a management dilemma regarding reclamation strategies.

Water quality during the critical summer period maintains the trout layer through thermal and chemical stratification. The sharp thermocline at approximately 30 feet depth creates a barrier effect important for both natural fish distribution and potential selective poisoning operations.

## Key Findings

**Angling Pressure and Harvest Patterns:**
- Annual angling pressure averaged 3,496 trips totaling 20,380 hours (105 hours or 18 trips per surface acre)
- Average trip duration: 5.8 hours
- Catch rate: 0.12 fish per hour (8+ hours per fish)
- Success rate: only 14-24% of angler trips were successful
- Despite poor catch rates, average fish size was satisfactory, attracting anglers from across Massachusetts and beyond (only 12.6% from local Barnstable County)

**Warmwater Fish Harvest:**
- Average annual harvest: 7.5 fish weighing 3.2 pounds per surface acre
- Composition by weight: Smallmouth bass 7.9%, Chain pickerel 5.2%, Brown bullheads 54.6%, Yellow perch 32.3%
- Estimated harvest represents ~30% of harvestable-size warmwater fish or ~6% of total fish population
- Marked year-to-year variation in warmwater catch

**Trout Stocking Success - Critical Timing Effects:**
The study revealed dramatic differences in first-year harvest based on stocking timing:

*1954 Experimental Comparison (equal numbers stocked):*
- **April 6 stocking:** Rainbow trout 73% harvest (636 of 1,500 checked), Brown trout 25% harvest (123 of 500 checked)
- **February 17 stocking:** Rainbow trout 2% harvest (20 of 1,500 checked), Brown trout 2% harvest (5 of 500 checked)
- Return ratios: 32:1 for rainbow, 25:1 for brown (April vs. February)

*Historical Range (1950-1954):*
- First-year rainbow trout harvest: 13.5-73% (healthy stocks 60-70% expected)
- First-year brown trout harvest: 0.5-25% (healthy stocks 40-45% expected)
- Extreme example: 1950 brown trout stocking yielded only 12 fish (0.5%) from 2,600 planted vs. 1951 rainbow stocking yielding 1,535 fish (59.1%) from 2,600 planted

**Explanation for Timing Effects:**
Early-stocked trout from [[East Sandwich Hatchery]] originated from fish in semi-hibernation due to low winter water temperatures and ice. February-stocked fish were in weakened condition, suffering high mortality from handling stress and environmental adjustment. Later stockings (March-April) represented hardier individuals with improved physical condition from increased feeding as water temperatures rose.

Comparative data from seven reclaimed ponds (September 1954): East Sandwich hatchery trout showed 1.7% recovery (19 of 1,100 stocked) vs. 7.7% recovery (544 of 7,030 stocked) from other state hatcheries, confirming inferior survival of early-season East Sandwich fish.

**Carryover Trout Performance:**
- Substantial carryover potential due to deep cold water and abundant smelt forage
- Spring-stocked rainbow trout growth (1951-52): 0.46 lbs in late April → 0.75 lbs by July
- Most carryovers surpassed 15-16 inches in first year; many reached 3-4 pounds by second spring
- Estimated carryover harvest ranged from 819.2 pounds (4.2 lbs/acre) in 1950 to 65.0 pounds (0.3 lbs/acre) in 1951
- Brown trout carryover poundage consistently exceeded rainbow trout
- Largest trophy fish were browns; one specimen: 32.5 inches, 19.5 pounds, age 7 years
- Rapid growth correlated with shorter lifespan; dead trophy trout found in prime condition were relatively young for size

**Seasonal Patterns:**
- May was consistently the best month for catching both current-year and carryover trout
- Three experimental fall fishing seasons (1950-52) yielded only a handful of trout
- Trout season regulations: April 15-July 31 (1949-53); third Saturday April through third Saturday October (1954)
- Daily bag limit: 6 trout, 9 inches minimum; night fishing prohibited

**Management Recommendations:**

*Option 1 - Maximum Hatchery Stocking (Non-reclaimed):*
- Annual stocking capacity: 11,000 trout (9-12 inches), ~5,000 pounds
- Annual cost: $5,000 ($1.00 per pound)
- Maintains current warmwater fishery and smelt population

*Option 2 - Complete Reclamation:*
- Potential standing crop: 10-25 pounds trout per acre
- Could produce 19,000 trout (9-12 inches), ~8,500 pounds annually
- Material costs: $5,000 (1,100 gallons 5% rotenone); total with labor: $6,000
- Prorated over 5 years: $1,500/year (70% cost reduction vs. hatchery stocking)
- Major drawback: eliminates valuable smelt forage base for trophy trout

*Option 3 - Partial Poisoning (Recommended):*
- Selective treatment of epilimnion (upper 15 feet) during August stratification
- Target: complete kill of yellow perch while preserving trout, smelt, and bullheads in deeper water
- Rotenone concentration: 0.5 ppm for top 15 feet (0.25 ppm effective concentration in full epilimnion)
- Material costs: $1,380 (300 gallons emulsifiable rotenone)
- Labor costs: ~$1,000
- Stock 20,000 fingerlings (5 inches): $1,100
- Prorated cost (3-year treatment cycle): ~$1,900/year
- Equivalent to current 4,000-5,000 hatchery trout stocking cost
- Preserves smelt and bullhead populations while eliminating competitive yellow perch

*Stocking Strategy:*
- Recommended ratio: 9 rainbow trout to 1 brown trout
- Objectives: diversify fishing, allow for trophy fish production
- Legalize night fishing for trout (especially brown trout)
- No justification for warmwater species regulations under trout management
- Salmon stocking not recommended (past failures, costly maintenance, inferior to existing trout fishing)

**Supporting Evidence from Similar Waters:**
Eight smaller reclaimed ponds (21 acres total) within 30-mile radius showed gross similarities: 40-60 pounds fish per acre, similar species complexes, and physical-chemical relationships, validating population estimates for [[Cliff Pond]].
