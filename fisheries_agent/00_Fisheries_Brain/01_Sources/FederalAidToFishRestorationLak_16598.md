# Anderson Ranch Reservoir Fishery Investigations, 1966 Annual Job Completion Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Idaho Fish and Game Department annual report, prepared by John T. Heimer, Fishery Biologist, covers four jobs under the [[Federal Aid To Fish Restoration]] Lake and Reservoir Investigations program (Project F 53-R-2) for March 1, 1966 to February 28, 1967 at [[Anderson Ranch Reservoir]]. The jobs were: (A1) experimental control of nongame fish, centred on [[Squawfish]]; (A2) production of [[Kokanee]]; (A4) limnological investigations; and (A5) survival of [[Rainbow Trout]].

The central finding is that Squawfish dominated the reservoir and competed with game fish and angling success. Chemical shoreline treatment with [[Rotenone Treatment]] was used to target fry and fingerlings, and the author recommended two to three more years of treatment before judging the control program's success. Kokanee plantings were largely unsuccessful. Rainbow trout returns to the creel were low.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** Anderson Ranch Reservoir lies at 4,196 ft elevation on the [[South Fork Boise River]] in Elmore County. The dam was completed in 1946 and backs water about 12 miles. Capacity is 493,000 acre-feet, maximum depth exceeds 300 ft, and maximum annual water level fluctuation is about 30 ft. The shoreline is steep and granitic. Primary purposes are irrigation and flood control.

**Fishery history.** Squawfish had become very prolific. They were a major food competitor with game fish and a nuisance to anglers. The normal minimum pool holds too much water for standard chemical treatment, but reducing squawfish was expected to improve fishing.

**Objectives (Job A1).** To explore the feasibility of reducing squawfish populations by chemically treating shoreline concentrations of fry and fingerlings, and to conduct a life history study of squawfish in the reservoir.

**Species recorded.** Ten species were taken by gill nets, traps, rotenone and anglers: Rainbow Trout, [[Dolly Varden]], Kokanee, [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Coarsescale Sucker]], [[Finescale Sucker]], Squawfish, [[Chiselmouth]] and [[Redside Shiner]].

**Methods (summary).**
- **Experimental gill nets:** 10 stations, 125 ft long by 6 ft deep, five mesh panels (3/4 to 2 inch), each sampled in mid-May and late August; 190 net-hours each spring and fall.
- **Vertical gill nets:** 6 mesh sizes (1/2 to 2 inch), 100 ft by 6 ft, fished at four stations in mid-August with simultaneous temperature profiles.
- **[[Lake Merwin Trap]]s:** two traps at Evans Creek Bay and above Lime Creek Bay, used for marking, movement and gonad condition.
- **Age and growth:** scales from the left side, read between glass slides, with back-calculated lengths.
- **Fry emergence:** SCUBA counts every fourth day, June 23 to July 9, 1966.
- **[[Drift Net Sampling]]:** on the South Fork Boise River mouth, July.
- **[[Kray-Meekin Trap]]s:** for kokanee downstream migration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Squawfish dominance.** Squawfish were the most abundant fish in the gill nets, and the report links their activity to poor fishing. During spring, squawfish became more active, and by June they were a nuisance to anglers.

**Spawning and fry.** Spawning concentrated in shoreline areas, especially near [[Fall Creek Bay]], Evans Creek Bay and Lime Creek Bay. Warmer upper-reservoir shoreline temperatures (66 to 73°F at 5 ft in June–July) appear to have advanced spawning and fry emergence there. The report notes that squawfish fry were not recruited from tributary streams. By contrast, large numbers of coarsescale sucker fry entered the reservoir from the South Fork Boise River.

**Depth and temperature.** Ninety percent of vertically netted squawfish were in the upper 50 ft, where temperatures ranged from the mid-fifties to the low seventies °F. Kokanee were concentrated deeper, at 50–100 ft in cooler water (48–59°F).

**Limnology.** [[Daphnia]] dominated the zooplankton. [[Cyclops]] was also common. Dissolved oxygen was ample throughout the reservoir in spring. In August 1966, 100,000 [[Mysis Oculata Relicta]] (opossum shrimp) from Waterton Lake, Alberta, were introduced.

## Key Findings

### Job A1: Experimental Control of Nongame Fish

- **Dominance.** Experimental gill nets caught 1,754 fish, of which 1,179 (67%) were squawfish. Catch per hour was 2.8 (spring), 3.4 (fall) and 3.1 combined. Coarsescale suckers made up 19% of the catch.
- **Vertical nets.** Forty-eight vertical sets caught 348 fish, 163 (47%) of them squawfish. Mesh sizes 3/4, 1 and 1 1/4 inch took 79% of the catch.
- **Trap catches.** The report states that 8% of Lake Merwin trap catches in July were squawfish (Table 6). However, the table lists 1,453 of 1,825 fish (80%). The text figure likely contains a transcription error. The table values are reported here, and the discrepancy should be checked against the original.
- **Length.** The modal fork length of squawfish rose from 11 inches (mid-May) to 12.5 inches (late August). Seventy-one percent of gill-netted squawfish measured 10.5–13.0 inches. Modal length for coarsescale suckers rose from 13.5 to 15.0 inches. Rainbow trout had a modal length of 12.5 inches (n = 97).
- **Age and growth.** Of 115 squawfish scale-aged (fork length 7.3–19.2 in), 81% were four or five years old. Average growth was about 2 inches per year.
- **Maturity.** More ripe males than females were found on the spawning grounds. Males arrived earlier and stayed later.
- **Spawning timing.** Peak spawning occurred in the last two weeks of June. Emergent fry were first seen June 23 near Badger Creek Bay and were present at all sampling sites by July 5.
- **Movements.** Marked squawfish returned to their release sites in high numbers. Evans Creek Bay fish: 232 of 3,620 recaptured (June–August 1966). Above Lime Creek: 42 of 1,134 recaptured. Badger Creek fish marked in 1965 (9,275 fish, 939 recaptured at the trap): 15 were taken in gill nets in 1966, most within four miles of the release site. Fish showed a shoreline spawning-area fidelity, with dispersal not random across the reservoir.
- **Drift netting.** Squawfish fry were not recruited from tributaries. Coarsescale sucker fry peaked in mid-July, with counts of 74 (July 8), 369 (July 14), 234 (July 22) and 10 (July 30).
- **Rotenone shoreline treatment.** Treatment ran July 25 to August 3–4, 1966, at about 1.7 ppm. The largest numbers killed were squawfish fry, with some shiner and sucker fry and large numbers of yearlings in flat shoal areas. Only 20 dead rainbow trout were found. Fry and fingerling mortality could not be quantified because the live-box holding results were inconclusive.
- **Recommendations.** Continue shoreline treatment. The author estimates two to three more years are needed to judge success and recommends measuring the percentage of fry and fingerlings killed.

### Job A2: Production of Kokanee

- **Stocking.** 390,000 fingerling kokanee were planted April 13 and 18, 1966, in the South Fork Boise River at Featherville bridge, 12 miles upstream. Eyed eggs came from a commercial hatchery in Soap Lake, Washington.
- **Migration.** A total of 357 kokanee were captured in traps at the river mouth. About 73% were taken within 12 hours of release, and 69% between 9 p.m. and midnight. Migration was largely nocturnal.
- **Distribution.** Vertical gill nets captured 26 adult kokanee in mid-August. Of these, 23 came from the Fall Creek station and 3 from the Lester–Wood Creek station. Ninety-two percent were at 50–100 ft. Lengths ranged from 10 to 13 inches. Four mature males (11.1–12.7 in) were caught in October.
- **Fishery contribution.** Only two kokanee were seen in the creel, both in November 1966.
- **Historical context.** A culvert placed in Fall Creek (1958–1962) blocked spawning runs. Embryo survival in shoreline spawning areas was poor due to receding water and silt. A temporary fish ladder installed in fall 1966 was not used by kokanee.
- **Recommendations.** Continue spring fingerling releases to establish a natural population, and conduct scale analyses for age and growth.

### Job A4: Limnological Investigations

- **Zooplankton.** A spring [[Daphnia]] peak of 125 organisms per liter occurred on May 5, 1966, at 15 ft. [[Cyclops]] peaks of 30 per liter occurred on April 21 (10 ft) and May 5 (40 ft).
- **Oxygen and temperature.** On May 25, temperatures were nearly uniform and dissolved oxygen was near saturation. By June 16, vertical stratification had formed, but oxygen stayed high.
- **Recommendations.** Discontinue temperature, oxygen and zooplankton monitoring, since data had been gathered from April through December.

### Job A5: Survival of Trout

- **Releases.** 94,500 catchable rainbow trout were released in fall 1965 (28,000 pelvic-clipped). 10,200 adipose-clipped catchables were released in spring 1966. 100,000 subcatchables were released in fall 1966 (10,000 marked).
- **Creel, spring 1966.** 236 anglers fished 851 hours and caught 417 trout (0.49 fish/hour, 1.77 fish/angler). Boat anglers averaged 0.55 fish/hour and bank anglers 0.34.
- **Creel, fall 1966.** 40 anglers fished 144 hours and caught 161 trout (1.12 fish/hour, 4.02 fish/angler).
- **Returns.** 72 of 28,000 fall-1965 marked fish (0.26%) and 79 of 10,200 spring-1966 marked fish (0.77%) were recovered. Spring-planted catchables returned about three times better, probably because of overwintering losses or emigration.
- **Other species.** Only two kokanee and five Dolly Varden were seen in the creel. Only four resident rainbow trout were observed.
- **Recommendations.** Stock catchables in spring. Design a stratified creel census to estimate total harvest and angler participation across the season.
