# Responses of Yearling Smallmouth Bass to Artificial Shelter in a Stream Aquarium

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined how juvenile [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*) use artificial shelter in flowing water. Wild yearlings were tested individually in a [[Stream Aquarium]] at two [[Water Velocity]] levels (12 and 28 cm/sec). Fish were offered opaque and clear plexiglass [[Covert Utilization|coverts]] that differed in four features: a [[Visual Reference Point]], a [[Thigmotaxis|tactual (thigmotactic) reference]], an [[Area of Darkness]], and an [[Quiet Water|area of quiet water]]. Use was driven mainly by opacity and by the number of features a covert combined. Opaque coverts were used heavily, while clear coverts were almost ignored. Darkness mattered more than quiet water, and the effect of quiet water depended on darkness and a visual reference being present.

## Background & Description

Earlier work had only briefly examined which features make coverts attractive to fish (Breder and Nigrelli, 1938; Carlisle et al., 1964; Hartman, 1963). This study isolated four candidate features and tested them in a controlled flow environment. The authors were affiliated with the [[Pennsylvania Cooperative Fishery Unit]] and the Department of Biology at [[Pennsylvania State University]]. The work was based on a Master of Science thesis by T.A. Haines and was published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada* (vol. 26, no. 1, 1969).

**Study fish:** Wild smallmouth bass were collected by electrofishing from [[Bald Eagle Creek]] near Julian, Pennsylvania. Aged as yearlings, they averaged 11.6 cm in total length (range 10.2–13.2 cm). Fish were anesthetized in MS-222, weighed, measured, and fin-clipped for individual identification. Between trials they were held in a live-holding box in the adjacent stream under natural light.

**Stream aquarium:** Water came by gravity from a small waterfall on [[Cedar Run]] through a 20.3 cm aluminum pipe. It was filtered through a 0.5 mm bag net to exclude most drift organisms. The aquarium measured 200 × 60 × 40 cm, with a 120 × 60 cm observation area containing a flush plexiglass window. The observation area was divided into 18 numbered 20 cm sections. Lighting came from four 40-W fluorescent lamps on a timer (08:00 to 20:00), giving 160–220 ft-c at the water surface. Black polyethylene sheeting screened the aquarium from outside light.

**Artificial coverts:** Coverts were 20 × 20 × 5 cm plexiglass units on wooden dowel legs. Five types were built in clear and black versions. A sixth type, a black bottom plate, was built only in black. Figure 2 in the source shows the designs, which included bottom plates, overhead plates with legs, two or three sides, and slanted plates.

**Recording:** A 20-pen event recorder logged the section each fish occupied, using an 18-button keyboard. Each trial lasted 2 hr (7200 sec).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water quality:** Temperature ranged from 7 to 18 °C. Water was alkaline, with a pH of 8.2 and total alkalinity of 105 ppm as CaCO₃.
- **Hydraulics:** Velocity was controlled by a butterfly valve and a sliding outlet door, and turbulence was reduced by a deflector plate and five 6-mm hardware cloth screens. Mid-depth velocity averaged 12 cm/sec (low) and 28 cm/sec (high). In the covert sections it ranged from 8.5 to 11 cm/sec (low) and 20 to 24 cm/sec (high). Fish were never forced against the lower screens.
- **Light and shelter in the barren tank:** Corners had the lowest light intensity. Section 18, next to the observation window and wall junction, was the preferred position when no coverts were present.
- **Food:** No food was supplied during trials, so feeding excursions from shelter did not occur.
- **Behavioral context:** The authors noted that subordinate fish in the holding box were suppressed from feeding by dominant fish. This likely caused the emaciation seen in some test subjects.

The study links to broader ideas about shelter in flowing water. Juvenile [[Brown Trout]] (Hartman, 1963) and juvenile [[Atlantic Salmon]] and trout (Kalleberg, 1958) show comparable responses to velocity and shelter.

## Key Findings

**1. Opacity was the central factor.** Clear coverts, whether they offered only tactual cues or tactual cues plus quiet water, were almost unused. Fish explored them briefly early on and then ignored them. Black coverts were strongly preferred over clear coverts of the same type at both velocities.

**2. Use rose with the number of features offered.** Among black coverts, which all had a visual reference, use increased as darkness and quiet water were added:

| Covert (black unless noted) | Use, low velocity (sec) | Use, high velocity (sec) |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom plate, two sides | 733 | 1607 |
| Bottom plate | 899 | 2359 |
| Overhead plate, legs | 2194 | 4073 |
| Overhead plate, two sides | 4314 | 5412 |
| Overhead plate, slanted | 5066 | 6854 |
| Overhead plate, three sides | 6969 | 7190 |

- The most complex covert at high velocity was occupied for a mean of 7190 ± 6 of 7200 sec.
- Some fish spent more than 99% of observed time under a covert.

**3. A visual reference alone was enough to attract fish.** The black bottom plate, with no sides and no darkness of its own, drew 899 sec (12%) at low velocity, well above the barren-tank baseline. Adding tactual sides to that plate did not change use.

**4. Darkness was more important than quiet water.** Fish strongly preferred a black two-sided overhead plate over a clear three-sided plate:

- Low velocity: 4781 ± 459 sec vs 444 ± 13 sec (P < 0.001)
- High velocity (eight fish, including three emaciated fish): 4585 ± 718 sec vs 1543 ± 518 sec (P < 0.030)
- High velocity (six healthy newly captured fish): 6301 ± 240 sec vs 485 ± 216 sec (P < 0.001)

**5. Quiet water mattered only when darkness and a visual reference were present.** Clear coverts that provided quiet water alone were not attractive. The slanted plate (quiet water) and two-sided plate (more darkness) were not significantly different at low velocity, but at high velocity the slanted plate was preferred. This suggests protection from current becomes more important as velocity rises.

**6. Thigmotaxis alone did not drive shelter use.** The authors could not demonstrate a role for tactual reference by itself. They suggest it may contribute through "stimulus summation" in complex coverts, but its effect could not be separated from the overall covert.

**7. Higher velocity increased use of all black coverts.** Use of every black covert rose at high velocity, most sharply for the simpler coverts (black bottom plate: 899 to 2359 sec). Clear coverts showed no velocity effect. The authors suggest that stress from higher velocity may have heightened shelter-seeking in general.

**8. Barren-tank preference.** Without coverts, fish spent 5534 sec (77%) at low velocity and 3857 sec (53%) at high velocity in section 18, next to the window. Reasons given include low velocity, low light, walls on two sides, and the visible window-wall junction.

**9. Learning was judged unlikely to have confounded results.** Presentation order did not match the order of increasing use, and no learning trend appeared within low-velocity trials. The authors note that collecting new fish for the high-velocity trials would have been preferable.

**10. Data quality issues.** Two fish were lost in the holding box and replaced without interrupting experiments. Three fish were emaciated, with weight losses of 5.5, 10.0, and 33.3% compared with gains of up to 11% in other fish. Their aberrant behavior was excluded from the main high-velocity comparison, and six healthy fish were tested to confirm the darkness preference.

**Conclusion:** Juvenile smallmouth bass are strongly shelter-oriented in flowing water. Shelter use depends on opacity, the number of environmental features offered (visual reference, darkness, quiet water, and to a lesser degree tactual contact), and water velocity.
