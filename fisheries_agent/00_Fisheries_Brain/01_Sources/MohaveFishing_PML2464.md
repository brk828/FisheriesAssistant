# Mohave Fishing on the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #mugilidae

## Executive Summary

This ethnographic article by [[Kenneth M Stewart]] describes the fish that the [[Mohave People]] caught in aboriginal times along the lower [[Colorado River]], the fishing techniques they used, and the cultural beliefs that governed fishing. The article reports that the construction of [[Laguna Dam]], twelve miles above [[Yuma]], in 1909 blocked upstream spawning migrations. As a result, the native fish the Mohave once depended on largely disappeared, and the river now holds mainly introduced [[Carp]] and [[Catfish]]. The text identifies the main food fishes, describes the seine, dip net and scoop techniques, and notes that fish became especially important in years of famine.

## Background & Description

The article is titled "Mohave Fishing" and appears in *The Masterkey*, with the page header of Paul C. Marsh. The author states that his fieldwork among the Mohave was financed by a grant from the Department of Anthropology, University of California.

The text draws on informants' names for fish and cites [[Castetter and Bell]] (page 218), who describe fish as "the leading animal food on the lower Colorado." Editorial footnotes give scientific identifications for several named fish:

- **Humpback (acijanap):** identified as *Xyrauchen texanus*, a sucker, known as the [[Humpback Sucker]].
- **Bony tail (acimifulk):** identified as *Gila elegans*, a minnow, known as the [[Bonytail]]. The Mohave preferred this smaller fish to the larger "salmon."
- **Acthan:** described by the Mohave as "like a salmon," reaching more than three feet in length but "hard to get." It is not identified scientifically in the text, although a footnote gives *Mugil cephalus*, a mullet ([[Striped Mullet]]), which may refer to this or another named fish.
- **Mawilk:** a smaller "salmon" caught in large quantities. A footnote identifies it as *Ptychocheilus lucius*, a minnow rather than a salmon, known as the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]].
- **Acijathily:** described as "like a humpback, but bigger and fatter." Not identified scientifically.
- **Acikuociokumir:** described as "the same as the mawilk, but a little smaller and thinner." Not identified scientifically.

One informant said that none of the fish mentioned in the paragraph on these species are seen "nowadays."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flood cycle and fish access.** The river overflowed its banks in May and June, carrying water from a very large drainage area. Fish spread over the bottomlands, and as the water receded they were stranded in lagoons and sloughs. The Mohave could not fish during the flood itself, saying "We couldn't do anything until the water went down." July and August were the best months for fishing, which the Mohave associated with hot weather. They seldom fished in the cold of winter.

**Dam impact.** Before [[Laguna Dam]] was built in 1909, fish could swim upriver to spawn. Afterward, the native species the Mohave had long caught could no longer reach their spawning grounds. The article states that fish had once made up as much as ten to fifteen percent of Mohave diet. Introduced carp and catfish, brought from the east, now dominate the river.

**Food security.** In years of famine or poor harvests, when the river did not rise enough to flood the farmlands, fish and wild plant foods became essential for survival. The informants said they preferred fish to corn or wheat.

**Cultural framing.** Fishing success depended on dreams. Expert fishers had dreamed properly and received power from the culture hero [[Mastamho]]. One informant said that when a man with the right dreams catches a fish, "the fish won't move." Any Mohave could still try fishing, and dream-endowed experts sometimes taught their techniques to others.

## Key Findings

**Fish species and diet**
- Several native fish were central to Mohave subsistence. The most important food fishes were the humpback, the bony tail, and the acthan, which is described as "like a salmon."
- The article lists six named fish in total. Four are linked to scientific names through footnotes, and two are not identified.
- Fish were a major part of diet until 1909, when the dam cut off upstream migration.

**Fishing techniques**

The article describes four principal methods:

1. **Seine or drag net (ihuly):** about 25 feet long and eight feet high, with arrow-weed sticks and stones to keep it vertical. The netting was made of cowpea fiber, with a square mesh of about two to three inches. Two men handled the poles and dragged the net through the water. Three or four seines could be linked across a lagoon or slough (thata'aruk), handled by 12 to 15 men.
2. **Dip net (suak):** six feet long and four feet deep, mounted on two six-foot willow poles. One man held the net in the water while others beat the water with sticks to drive fish toward it. The man then closed the poles and lifted the net to trap the fish.
3. **Small dip net (acisayul):** four feet long and three feet deep, used by one man in shallow water to scoop fish he had spotted.
4. **Basketry scoop (kwithata):** shaped like a canoe, five feet long and eighteen inches wide.

**Seasonal and social patterns**
- July and August were the peak fishing months.
- Fishing was largely a hot-season activity, not a winter one.
- Any Mohave could fish, but men who had the right dreams were regarded as the most skilled.

**Conclusions**
- The loss of spawning access after 1909 ended the Mohave's dependence on native river fish, and introduced species now dominate the lower Colorado.
- Fish were culturally valued above staple crops, and they were a critical buffer against famine.
