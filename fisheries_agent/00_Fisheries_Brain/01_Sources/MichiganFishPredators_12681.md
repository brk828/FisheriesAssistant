# Michigan Fish Predators

#ichthyology #marine-biology #wildlife-management #predator-prey-dynamics #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This 1952 Michigan Department of Conservation pamphlet provides a comprehensive review of vertebrate fish predators in Michigan waters, including fishes ([[Gar]], [[Bowfin]], [[Carp]]), amphibians ([[Mudpuppy]], [[Bullfrog]]), reptiles ([[Snapping Turtle]], [[Common Watersnake]], [[Garter Snake]]), birds (herons, bitterns, [[American Merganser]], [[Bald Eagle]], [[Osprey]], [[Belted Kingfisher]]), and mammals ([[Otter]], [[Mink]], [[Raccoon]]). The document concludes that except in special situations such as fish hatcheries or extreme concentrations, control of these predators is generally unwarranted and may even be counterproductive. Many warmwater lakes suffer from overpopulation and stunting of fish, where predation may actually improve angling quality by reducing numbers and allowing faster growth of survivors.

## Background & Description

### Fishes

**[[Longnose Gar]] (Lepisosteus spp.)**: Distinguished by bill-like jaws with sharp teeth, long slender cylindrical body, and heavy diamond-shaped enamel scales. [[Longnose Gar]] attains 5 feet length, found throughout [[Lower Peninsula]] and lower [[Great Lakes]]. [[Shortnose Gar]] limited to southernmost third of [[Lower Peninsula]], grows to 3 feet. Prefer warm lakes and slow streams; spawn in spring in shallow weedy areas with adhesive eggs on vegetation. Commonly seen gulping air at surface during summer.

**[[Bowfin]] (Amia calva)**: Known locally as "dogfish" or "grindle." Living fossil, sole surviving species of ancient family. Identified by extremely long dorsal fin extending over most of back length. Males have black spot rimmed with yellow/orange at tail base. Females larger, sometimes reaching 3 feet and over 5 pounds. Most abundant in slow rivers and weedy warmwater lakes of two southern county tiers. Spawn in spring; male builds saucer-like nest in soft sand/gravel, guards 2,000-5,000 eggs for 8-10 days, then guards fry school for additional time until young exceed 1 inch.

**[[Carp]] (Cyprinus carpio)**: Native of Asia, member of minnow family. Introduced to continental Europe in 12th century, brought to America from Germany around 1870, repeatedly stocked in Michigan 1880-1900. Now widespread except [[Lake Superior]] and tributaries. Distinguished by very long dorsal fin preceded by hard saw-toothed spine, similar spine on anal fin, two whisker-like barbels on each side of mouth. Varieties include fully scaled, "leather carp" (no scales), and "mirror carp" (few large scales size of fifty-cent piece). Specimens over 30 pounds common, lengths over 4 feet recorded. Extremely prolific; single female may deposit half million or more eggs in groups of 400-500. Spawn late spring in shallow weedy waters 1-2 feet deep or flooded swamps. Eggs adhere to vegetation, hatch in 5-12 days, young mature in 2-3 years.

### Amphibians

**[[Mudpuppy]] (Necturus maculosus)**: Large aquatic salamander resembling lizard but with slimy scaleless skin. Compressed tail for locomotion, three tufts of fluffy dark red gills behind flattened head on each side. Brownish body with spots or mottlings. Average size 10-12 inches. Prefers quiet waters of lakes and larger streams with flat rocks for daytime hiding. Strictly nocturnal. Found throughout Michigan in suitable habitat. Mates in fall, eggs not laid until late spring, suspended beneath rocks in groups of 65-140. Growth relatively slow.

**[[Bullfrog]] (Rana catesbeiana)**: Largest Michigan frog. Greenish brown back, distinguished from [[Green Frog]] by absence of ridges along back sides. Highly aquatic, seldom ventures far from sluggish backwaters and ponds. Common in preferred habitat throughout [[Lower Peninsula]], less common in [[Upper Peninsula]]. Emerges from hibernation late spring, breeds early summer. Eggs laid in large jelly-like surface mats. Tadpoles develop into frogs during third summer, sexually mature at least one year later.

### Reptiles

**[[Snapping Turtle]] (Chelydra serpentina)**: Recognized by large size, pronounced saw-tooth ridge on tail top, three low broken ridges running lengthwise on upper shell. Aquatic, non-basking, nocturnal. Found statewide in sluggish streams and quiet lake parts. Female lays approximately 24 eggs in June in shore nest with moderate moisture, well exposed to sun. Young hatch in about 90 days. Reported up to 86 pounds weight and over 25 years old.

**[[Common Watersnake]] (Natrix sipedon)**: Non-poisonous, found throughout Michigan near any lake, pond, or stream. Color usually mottled brown with dark brown saddle-shaped bands on gray-brown background. Bands become blotches toward tail. Each belly scute has pair of dark "half-moons." Bears 20-25 young in late summer. Hibernates on land during cold months. Commonly basks in sun in trees or bushes 6-8 feet above stream banks.

**[[Garter Snake]] (Thamnophis sirtalis)**: Common throughout Michigan, dark with three pale yellow stripes running total length (one down middle of back, two laterally). Found in almost any situation but shows marked preference for moist habitat. Large numbers of young (12-70) born alive in late summer.

### Birds

**[[Great Blue Heron]] (Ardea herodias)**: Blue-gray bird standing about 4 feet high, one of largest wading birds in Michigan. Gregarious, nesting and roosting in flocks. Feed in grassy marshes and open water of lakes and streams. Perch and nest in trees. Young born with scanty covering of long filamentous down, reared in nest.

**[[Green Heron]] (Butorides virescens)**: Small size, fairly short orange or yellow legs. Looks quite black and crow-like at distance but flies more slowly with more arched wing beats. Prefers tree-bordered waterways to open marshes or lake margins.

**[[Black-crowned Night Heron]] (Nycticorax nycticorax)**: Feeds largely at night or during dusk/dawn. Chunky bird with black back and white below. Call is unmistakable flat "quawk."

**[[American Bittern]] (Botaurus lentiginosus)**: Usually feeds in marshy areas, seldom seen by casual observer. Black wing-tips contrasting with yellowish brown body provide positive identification when flushed.

**[[American Merganser]] (Mergus merganser)**: Male has long white body with black back, greenish-black head, orange feet and long saw-toothed orange bill. Female gray with crested brownish-red head, orange-red bill and feet. Both sexes have large white wing patch. Breeds in northern North America, migrates south to winter mostly within United States. Remains throughout winter on open water in Michigan. Nests in hollow tree, stump or thicket, on or off ground, usually near water. Female incubates 6-17 eggs for 28 days.

**[[Bald Eagle]] (Haliaeetus leucocephalus)**: Recognized in adulthood by large size, white head and tail. Usually found near waters. Nest is large pile of sticks added to annually in tall tree or on ledge. Two pale brown eggs with cinnamon spots incubated by both parents for about 30 days.

**[[Osprey]] (Pandion haliaetus)**: Fish hawk with white underparts and peculiar kinked wings. Soars on bent wings, hovers before plunging into water to capture fish. Nest of sticks returned to and added to annually, usually in tree. Female incubates 2-4 eggs for 28 days, both parents defend nest.

**[[Belted Kingfisher]] (Megaceryle alcyon)**: Bird with ragged crest and big-headed, big-billed appearance. Found almost wherever there is water in Michigan. Larger than robin, blue-gray above with one blue breast stripe in male, blue plus rusty band in female. Rattling cry unmistakable. Nests at end of burrow in streamside bank or river bluff. Five to 14 white glossy eggs hatched in 16-17 days. Family remains together 10-15 days after young leave nest.

### Mammals

**[[Otter]] (Lutra canadensis)**: Large semi-aquatic member of weasel family. Long lithe body, proportionally short legs, webbed feet, long muscular tail. Rich glossy brown durable pelt. Not abundant in Michigan but usually found along wilder waterways of northern part of state. Ranges over large area but never far from water. Creates familiar "otter slide" path down banks into water. Mates in February, 1-5 young born late April. Young stay with parents most of year. Den usually in stream bank with underwater entrance.

**[[Mink]] (Mustela vison)**: One of most valuable fur bearers, common throughout state. Long lithe animal with sharp features and rich dark brown fur, typically found near waterways. Mates February or March with fierce fighting among males. Three to 8 blind helpless young born 42-51 days later, do not leave den for 6-8 weeks.

**[[Raccoon]] (Procyon lotor)**: Nocturnal furbearer with black mask and bushy ringed tail. Occurs throughout Michigan in wooded, well-watered areas. Breeds in February after leaving hibernation, 2-6 young born about 63 days later. Male helps rear young which stay with family for extended time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Michigan waters have always had numerous natural enemies of fish. The document addresses vertebrate predators most prominent to anglers. A critical ecological principle emphasized throughout is that many warmwater lakes suffer from **overpopulation and stunting** rather than too few fish. Every body of water has a definite limit on pounds of fish it will support—thus possible to have either smaller number of large fish or larger number of small fish. Overpopulation results in stunted growth.

Because anglers fish for large predators like [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Northern Pike]], small fish on which they feed often increase until overpopulation develops. Predators like [[Gar]] may prove useful by helping keep small fish population in check, tending toward faster growth for survivors and more large fish for anglers. Theoretically, certain overpopulation situations can be alleviated and angling success improved by introduction of predators.

The document notes relatively low rate of harvest of fish by fishermen in many warmwater lakes. In wilderness waters with least controlled predation, the best fishing both in numbers and size typically occurs.

**Habitat preferences** vary by species: [[Gar]] prefer warm lakes and slow meandering streams; [[Bowfin]] most abundant in slow-moving rivers and weedy warmwater lakes; [[Carp]] can live under wide variety of conditions with low oxygen demand and extreme temperature/pollution tolerance; [[Mudpuppy]] prefers quiet waters with numerous flat rocks; [[Bullfrog]] highly aquatic in sluggish backwaters and ponds; [[Snapping Turtle]] in sluggish streams and quiet lake parts; [[Watersnake]] aquatic but also terrestrial; herons in grassy marshes and open waters; [[Merganser]] on Great Lakes bays and river-mouth lakes normally, forced onto trout streams only during extreme freeze-ups.

## Key Findings

### Food Habits - Fishes

**[[Longnose Gar]]** (southern Michigan study): Diet consists of 59.9% sport fishes, 38.1% forage fishes and other fishes, 2.0% crayfish and insects. Some fishes eaten are of greater value to angler than gar itself. Under certain circumstances gars may compete with game fishes like [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Northern Pike]]. Sport fishes eaten generally young of species like sunfishes and perch which are too abundant in many waters anyway.

**[[Bowfin]]** (southern Michigan): 56.0% sport fishes (bullheads, perch, black bass, bluegills, sunfish, rock bass), 33.0% other fishes (gizzard shad, carp, suckers, forage minnows), 11.0% frogs, crayfish, earthworms, insects, snails, clams. Fish of small size and crayfish predominate. Appears to compete with more desirable fish, principally [[Black Bass]] and [[Walleye]].

**[[Carp]]**: Food consists mainly of vegetable matter (algae, roots, stems, leaves of aquatic plants) and some animal matter (worms, snails, clams, larval and adult insects). Fish eggs occasionally eaten, probably incidental to other feeding. Not believed to prey generally upon young of other fish. Does roil water and destroy or break loose some aquatic plants in feeding about roots. Extremely prolific and fast-growing young may temporarily provide food for predatory fish.

### Food Habits - Amphibians

**[[Mudpuppy]]** (105 stomachs, Evans Lake, southern Michigan, April): 13% fishes (bluegills, other sunfishes, darters, minnows), 9% insects, 20% earthworms, 55% crustaceans, 3% snails, 1% turtles and frogs. Generally reported to devour fish eggs but extent of damage never determined. Perhaps more important as competitor of fish for food than as predator. On basis of one Michigan study, not enough evidence to condemn as fish predator.

**[[Bullfrog]]**: Very little quantitative data. General statements indicate feeds on insects, fishes, crayfishes, birds, other frogs. Pennsylvania and New York studies: insects, spiders, snails chief food of smaller specimens; frogs, crayfish, mice principal diet of larger specimens. One large bullfrog found dead with 7-inch [[Brown Bullhead]] in mouth. Does take fish if opportunity arises but available evidence does not indicate serious predator.

### Food Habits - Reptiles

**[[Snapping Turtle]]** (extensive Michigan study): One-third water plants, one-third game fishes, one-third dead fishes and other carrion, insects, crayfishes, snails, clams. Easily capable of taking young waterfowl but little known about extent. Only in areas of heavy waterfowl concentration would likely cause sizeable losses. Janitorial services and commercial aspect far offset any harm to fish population.

**[[Common Watersnake]]**: Most workers agree eats fish primarily. Michigan studies show: (1) around trout-rearing stations, trout were dominant food; (2) on lakes, game/pan fishes and forage fishes made up over 50%; (3) on trout streams, forage fishes 55.8%, trout only 19.0%, lampreys 3.3%, burbot 7.3%, fish remains 0.2%, frogs 12.8%, miscellaneous invertebrates 1.6%. Of 106 watersnakes from trout streams, only 7 had captured trout. Minimizes importance as predator on most trout streams. Compete with larger trout for forage fishes but extent of harm difficult to determine. Only heavy concentrations could cause serious depredations.

**[[Garter Snake]]**: In regions of large fish concentrations (hatcheries, holding ponds), about 40% of diet is fish. In usual habitat near natural waters, fish make up only 6%. Thirty-five specimens from near streams and lakes in Michigan: fish 6.2%, frogs and toads 55.4%, earthworms 35.3%, remainder snails, leeches, salamanders, insects. No control needed on natural waters.

### Food Habits - Birds

**[[Great Blue Heron]]**: Michigan study on non-trout streams, heronries, miscellaneous lakes/ponds: 30-50% sport fishes, remainder predominantly forage fish (minnows), few frogs, mice, insects and other invertebrates. On trout streams (9 stomachs): 46.3% trout, 26.9% other fishes, 24.3% amphibians, 2.5% fish remains/insects/mammals. Eight herons from excellent Montana trout stream contained only 7 trout, bulk was suckers and frogs. U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service examined 189 stomachs from across country: valuable fish species composed only about one-quarter of total food.

**[[Green Heron]]** (255 stomachs, nationwide): Only 5.9% valuable fishes (mostly sunfishes and pickerel), 38.5% forage fish (minnows, killifish, silversides), 20.6% crayfish, 23.7% insects, remainder miscellaneous invertebrates.

**[[Black-crowned Night Heron]]** (117 stomachs, nationwide): 51.5% fish but sport species only 12.7% of this (rest minnows, killifishes, suckers, carp), 22% crustaceans, 16% aquatic insects, 6% frogs, 3% mice and rats, remainder spiders and worms.

**[[American Bittern]]** (133 stomachs, southern Canada to southern U.S.): 23.1% insects, 20.6% frogs and salamanders, 19.0% crayfish, 9.6% mice and shrews, 9.7% sport fish, 9.6% forage fish, 1.1% unidentified fish remains, 5.2% snakes, remainder miscellaneous invertebrates.

**[[American Merganser]]**: Almost entirely fish eater. On Great Lakes bays and river-mouth lakes (normal feeding areas): only 20.1% game fishes (predominantly [[Yellow Perch]]), 48.9% forage fishes, 20.3% unidentified fishes, 8.5% crayfishes, 1.6% miscellaneous.
