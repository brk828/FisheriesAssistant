# Fish sampling at ASU Research Park, autumn 1987

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #Cichlidae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary
This 1987 memo from Paul Marsh (Arizona State University, Center for Environmental Studies) reports results of trammel net and seine sampling conducted in three interconnected constructed ponds (Lake A, B, C) at the ASU Research Park, Tempe, Arizona. The ponds, supplied by the Salt River Project (SRP) canal system, support a mixed assemblage of native Gila River basin fishes — including endangered [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]] — alongside numerous introduced/exotic species such as [[Redbelly Tilapia]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Red Shiner]]. The study documents encouraging growth and apparent survival of stocked native species, raising the possibility of reproductive maturity and spawning by spring 1988, while also flagging conservation concerns about unauthorized exotic fish introductions and predation risk to native embryos/larvae.

## Background & Description
Growth and size data were tracked for two focal native species stocked as juveniles:

**[[Razorback Sucker]]**
- Stocked November 1986 at juvenile size (natural + hatchery-reared origin)
- By April 1987: averaged 26 mm growth trajectory noted as "remarkable"
- Growth rate: ~16 mm/month (comparable across Park ponds and other facilities)
- Comparison: Fish held at USFWS Dexter National Fish Hatchery (Dexter, NM) reached average length of 235 mm by 15 months of age (~16 mm/month) (McCarthy & Minckley 1987)
- Wild Lake Mohave (AZ-NV) fish, back-calculated via otolith analysis, averaged ~211 mm at 5 years of age — markedly slower growth than pond-reared fish

**[[Bonytail Chub]]**
- Stocked November 1986 at ~75 mm total length
- April 1987 (4 specimens): averaged 154 mm
- September 1987 (9 specimens): mean length 281 mm (~16 mm/month growth rate since stocking)
- Largest individual: 570 mm
- Comparison cohort at Buenos Aires NWR (same stocking size/date): averaged 145 mm by September 1987 (~60 mm/month cited, likely a growth-rate transcription artifact) with largest at 218 mm
- Dexter NFH bonytail: averaged 135 mm at age 2 years (Hamman 1985)
- Research Park bonytail: mean weight of 158 g at 19 months of age

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The ponds function as an experimental/refuge habitat within the urban Salt River Project canal system, supporting a mosaic of native and non-native fishes:

**Native species (Gila River basin endemics)**, marked with asterisk in original source:
- *[[Gila Sucker]] (Catostomus insignis)* — introduced via SRP canal connection, April 1987
- *[[Gila Mountain Sucker]] (Pantosteus clarki)* — via SRP canal, April 1987
- *[[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus)* — stocked by Arizona Game and Fish Department (AZGFD) in cooperation with USFWS and ASU, November 1986 & April 1987
- *[[Bonytail Chub]] (Gila elegans)* — stocked by AZGFD, same cooperative program
- *[[Colorado Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius)* — stocked by AZGFD, November 1986 & April 1987; status in ponds unknown post-stocking

**Non-native/exotic species**, largely arriving via the SRP canal or unauthorized stocking:
- [[Threadfin Shad]] (Dorosoma petenense) — via canal, February 1987
- [[Goldfish]] (Carassius auratus) — aquarium release
- [[Carp]] (Cyprinus carpio) — via canal, April 1987
- [[Red Shiner]] (Notropis lutrensis) — via canal
- [[Yellow Bullhead]] (Ameiurus natalis) — via canal, April 1987
- [[Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia affinis) — via canal, October 1986
- [[Yellow Bass]] (Morone mississippiensis) — via canal, September 1987
- [[Green Sunfish]] (Lepomis cyanellus) — via canal, October 1986
- [[Bluegill]] (Lepomis macrochirus) — via canal, October 1984
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (Micropterus salmoides) — unauthorized local resident introduction, plus official Park stocking of ~100 mm fingerlings, May 1987
- [[Black Crappie]] (Pomoxis nigromaculatus) — via canal, September 1987
- [[Blue Tilapia]] (Tilapia aurea) — unknown/unauthorized origin
- [[Mouthbrooder Tilapia]] (Tilapia mossambica) — stocked by Park (uncertain)
- [[Redbelly Tilapia]] (Tilapia zillii) — deliberately stocked by Park (3/84, 6/86) for nuisance algae control
- Malacca hybrid tilapia — stocked by Park (5/86, 10/86), likely contaminant of T. zillii stocking

This mixed native/exotic assemblage creates a functional predator-prey and competitive dynamic relevant to conservation of endangered Gila basin fishes; the memo explicitly raises concern about **predation on native embryos and larvae by abundant exotic fishes** as a research priority once native species reach reproductive maturity.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness by pond:** Lake A (northernmost) had highest richness among trammel-net catches (9 species), followed by Lake B (6 species), Lake C (5 species).
- **Trammel net catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE):** Similar across ponds — 14.8–16.8 (Lake A), ~15.0–19.0 (Lake B), 27.5 (Lake C); total catch 124 fish.
- **Trammel net dominant species:** [[Razorback Sucker]] dominated trammel net catches (62 individuals, ~50% of total catch), followed by [[Redbelly Tilapia]] (~28%).
- **Seine catch:** Total of 1,253 fish; CPUE varied more widely among ponds — 60.1–60.6 (Lake A), 1.5 (Lake B), 7.6 (Lake C).
- **Seine dominant species:** [[Redbelly Tilapia]] (27.5%), [[Red Shiner]] (26.7%), [[Green Sunfish]] (19.6%), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (12.5%) were most abundant; native [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Mosquitofish]] also present in moderate numbers.
- **Growth performance:** Both [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] exhibited strong apparent survival and growth in Park ponds, generally outperforming wild populations (e.g., Lake Mohave razorback sucker) and matching or exceeding hatchery benchmarks (Dexter NFH).
- **Reproductive potential:** Given rapid growth, both native species were projected to potentially reach reproductive maturity and spawn by spring 1988, opening opportunity to study exotic predation impacts on native early life stages.
- **Colorado squawfish status uncertain:** Not recovered in either trammel net or seine sampling; suspected due to **gear selectivity bias** — individuals likely too large/agile for seines but too small to be retained by trammel net mesh sizes used. Alternative methods (e.g., electroshocking) recommended for future sampling.
- **Conservation/management concern:** Documented unauthorized introductions (e.g., largemouth bass, blue tilapia, Malacca hybrid tilapia) highlight risks of uncontrolled stocking activity within research ponds intended for native species conservation; the memo explicitly discourages such undocumented activity.
- **Voucher specimens** of all collected taxa were deposited in the ASU Collection of Lower Vertebrates for future reference and verification.
