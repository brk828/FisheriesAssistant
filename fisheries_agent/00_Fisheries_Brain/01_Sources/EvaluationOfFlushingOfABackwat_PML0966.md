# Evaluation of Flushing a Backwater Channel: Selenium and Inorganic Elements in Water, Sediment, Invertebrates, Forage Fish, and Colorado Pikeminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Hamilton et al., 2003), prepared for the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]], evaluates whether operating a water control structure that admitted [[Colorado River]] flow into the backwater channel at [[Walter Walker State Wildlife Area]] (WWSWA) reduced selenium in the aquatic ecosystem. Sampling ran from May 1995 to September 1998. The structure was opened on December 5, 1996.

Selenium fell sharply in water, surficial sediment, aquatic invertebrates, and forage fish after flushing began. Concentrations in [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] muscle plugs did not show a corresponding decline. The authors conclude that flushing is a helpful remediation option for selenium-sensitive species, though continued monitoring is recommended.

## Background & Description

The upper Colorado River supports four endangered fishes: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]]. The [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] (established 1987, 15-year goal) includes a Floodplain Habitat Restoration Program. That program seeks restored floodplain sites that do not pose selenium risks.

Adult Colorado pikeminnow are piscivorous and occupy deep, fast waters and tributary pools. Young fish (<50 mm) feed on cladocerans, copepods, and chironomid larvae. Osmundson and Burnham (1998) estimated 598 adults in the upper 93 km and 344 adults and subadults in the lower 181 km. They reported that recruitment was highly variable and is probably the most important demographic factor for persistence.

Razorback sucker are now rare in the upper basin, with only 10 wild fish found between 1989 and 1996. WWSWA was historically a razorback sucker site and is a known Colorado pikeminnow aggregation area. Pikeminnow there had higher muscle-plug selenium than fish elsewhere in the upper basin (Butler and Osmundson 2000; Osmundson et al. 2000).

The study site is a gravel-pit backwater bordered by a dike. Groundwater inflow, seeps, and an adjacent marsh supplied selenium. [[North Pond]] is an isolated pond of about 1 ha that was used in earlier razorback sucker reproduction studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** Seven channel stations (WW1 to WW10 in the figures) were monitored. Water quality was measured weekly on site. Monthly sampling (sometimes at 60-day intervals) covered filtered and unfiltered water, [[Bottom Sediment]] (mixed grabs, then cores and traps from late 1996), aquatic invertebrates collected by [[Light Trap]] and benthic grabs, and forage fish. Pikeminnow were captured with a PIT tag check and a 4- or 5-mm muscle biopsy.

**Analytical methods.** Selenium was measured by atomic absorption with hydride generation (AA-HG), with confirmation at the University of Missouri-Rolla. Other elements were measured by [[Inductively Coupled Plasma Spectroscopy]]. Neutron activation was used for pikeminnow muscle plugs. Statistics included ANOVA, Spearman and Pearson correlations, multiple regression, and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The mean water limit of detection was 1.0 µg/L.

**Hydrology.** River runoff peaked on June 18, 1995, and June 23, 1996. High-flow years were 1995 and 1997. The years 1996 and 1998 were average. The water control structure was opened on December 5, 1996. Following operation, the channel became uniformly mixed, so water-quality sampling was dropped at WW5, WW7, and WW9 in 1997 and 1998.

**Sources.** The marsh outfall (WW4 and WW4b) was the main selenium input. Groundwater and irrigation-influenced discharge were the origin of elevated conductivity and hardness. Station WW7 was an additional entry point, probably from seeps.

## Key Findings

**Water**
- Mean filtered-water selenium (all channel stations, excluding the river and marsh-outflow stations) was 21.0 µg/L in 1995 (range <1–126), 23.5 µg/L in 1996 (range <1–135), 2.1 µg/L in 1997 (range <1–6), and 2.1 µg/L in 1998 (range <1–6.3).
- Marsh-area elements elevated with selenium included boron, chromium, iron, magnesium, manganese, molybdenum, strontium, and vanadium.
- Selenium in water correlated with nitrate (r = 0.90), calcium, hardness, magnesium, sulfate, and conductivity (all P = 0.0001).

**Sediment**
- Mean selenium in the upper sediment layer was 8.5 µg/g in 1995 (range 2.4–20.8), 8.2 µg/g in 1996 (0.4–19.6), 4.8 µg/g in 1997 (3.4–5.9), and 1.1 µg/g in 1998 (0.4–1.9). The 1998 value was significantly lower (ANOVA, F = 12.04, P < 0.0001).
- Before operation, cores were elevated at the top and middle. After operation, clean sediment was deposited over high-selenium layers.

**Aquatic invertebrates**
- Mean selenium was 27.4 µg/g (n = 13) in 1996, 15.5 µg/g (n = 9) in 1997, and 4.9 µg/g (n = 6) in 1998.
- Correlations with filtered water (r = 0.83), unfiltered water (r = 0.77), and sediment (Spearman rs = 0.81) were significant.
- Station WW7 was the only site without a decline.
- The 1998 mean exceeded the proposed dietary toxic threshold of 3 µg/g.

**Forage fish**
- Mean selenium was 27.2 µg/g (n = 24) in 1996, 20.2 µg/g (n = 23) in 1997, and 8.6 µg/g (n = 21) in 1998 (ANOVA, P < 0.0014). The reported decline was about 68% (the authors also cite a four-fold reduction in one analysis).
- Selenium correlated with filtered water (r = 0.58, P = 0.02) and sediment (r = 0.75, P = 0.003).
- Between 1996 and 1998, aluminum fell 5.8-fold, iron 3.7-fold, manganese 2.0-fold, and vanadium 2.3-fold. Lead rose two-fold. Other elements changed less than two-fold.
- Dominant species were fathead minnow and green sunfish. Eight other species were also collected.

**Colorado Pikeminnow**
- Mean muscle-plug selenium was 9.8 µg/g (n = 49) in 1995, 9.5 µg/g (n = 40) in 1996, 9.0 µg/g (n = 54) in 1997, and 10.3 µg/g (n = 3) in 1998. The 1998 sample was too small to be informative.
- Fish sampled in 1994 (before the study) averaged 16.1 µg/g (n = 17), significantly above 1995–1997.
- Residues fell with increasing length and weight (weight r = −0.34; length r = −0.37; both P < 0.0001).
- Muscle selenium correlated most strongly with mean March–July river flow (r = −0.85, P = 0.07).
- Recaptured fish kept similar residues across years, suggesting site fidelity.

**Context**
- Current WWSWA pikeminnow residues (9.0–16.6 µg/g) exceeded the 85th percentile of the [[National Contaminant Biomonitoring Program]] (about 2.5–3.0 µg/g).
- Estimated whole-body equivalents were 15–28 µg/g at WWSWA.
- Authors' conclusions: (1) water, sediment, invertebrates, and forage fish were substantially reduced after the structure opened; (2) pikeminnow residues did not decline; (3) flushing backwaters is a helpful approach to reducing stress in selenium-sensitive species.
- Recommendation: remediation by flushing flows is one option, with monitoring of invertebrates and forage fish to confirm reductions.
