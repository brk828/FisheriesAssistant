# Rainbow Smelt, Parasite Seasonality, and a New Topminnow Population: Notes from the Southwestern Naturalist (May 1982)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This *Southwestern Naturalist* notes section (May 1982, vol. 27, no. 2) contains three short reports. The first documents [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) juveniles in the [[Mississippi River]] of Arkansas and Tennessee, a range extension that raises questions about how a cold-water marine-origin species persisted in a warm river. The second summarizes seasonal infection patterns of the nematode [[Camallanus Oxycephalus]] and related parasites in freshwater sport and panfish hosts. The third describes a newly discovered population of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) in a spring system in Santa Cruz County, Arizona, and its co-occurrence with [[Mosquitofish]].

## Background & Description

**Rainbow smelt in the Mississippi River.** The smelt is primarily an anadromous species of the Western North Atlantic. Landlocked populations spread through the Great Lakes during the first half of the twentieth century through introductions. Earlier work by [[Burr and Mayden]] (1980) documented juveniles in the Mississippi River of Illinois and Kentucky in December 1978 and December 1979. [[Suttkus and Conner]] (1980) collected juveniles near St. Francisville, Louisiana, in April and December 1979, while the river was in flood.

**Gila topminnow in Arizona.** The topminnow has declined sharply in range over recent decades because of habitat destruction and introduced exotic fishes. The U.S. Department of the Interior lists it as [[Endangered Status]]. The authors describe a new locality on private land, the location of which was withheld at the landowner's request.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mississippi River conditions.** The December 1980 collections were made during a record low river stage for that time of year, and high water had not prevailed for the previous eight months. Burr and Mayden suggested that juveniles dispersed from Lake Michigan through the [[Chicago Sanitary Canal]] and the [[Illinois River]] during high water, or came down the [[Missouri River]]. They also argued that smelt could not survive Mississippi River summers, because the species had never been collected there in summer. The authors of this note propose that the abundance of juveniles from Illinois to Louisiana may instead reflect reproduction within the Mississippi River or its tributaries.

**Arizona spring system.** The site is a spring system at about 1,400 m elevation in an oak-grassland association. It is a grassland cut by an arroyo that apparently intersects the water table. Eighteen elongate pools (5 to 52 m long, 0.5 to 4 m wide, 0.2 to 2 m deep) are connected by a discharge channel and drain into a [[Cienega Habitat]] (marshland). Key water measurements were:

- Dissolved oxygen: well oxygenated; late-summer saturation varied among pools from about 65% to 110% (scan partly illegible).
- Conductivity: 290 to 580 µmhos/cm.
- Total alkalinity: 3.1 to 5.9 mg/L.
- pH: approximately 8.4 to 8.9 (partly illegible).
- Surface temperature: varied seasonally; fairly constant among pools on any given date.
- No discernible gradients along the system for these variables. Conductivity and alkalinity were higher in winter than in summer.

The system flows over organic cienega deposits underlain by gravel. Dominant aquatic plants include naiad (*Najas*), which forms dense beds in some pools; watercress (*Nasturtium officinale*); a rush (*Juncus saximontanus*); duckweed (*Lemna minor*); and *Azolla*, which covered one pool completely in winter and early summer. Riparian vegetation is dominated by willow (*Salix goodingii*), with cottonwood (*Populus fremontii*).

**Parasite seasonality.** Infection patterns in largemouth bass, sunfish, and white bass varied by season. Reported patterns included high winter and low summer infection in one study, peak infections in July to August, and steady intensity increases from February through August followed by a sharp decline in another. The authors suggest that parasite periodicity may not be tied to temperature alone, and that the timing of peaks may reflect host and copepod population dynamics.

## Key Findings

**Rainbow smelt (Mississippi River).**
- Sixty juveniles from the Tennessee site, measuring 34 to about 61 mm standard length (mean 51.4 mm), were examined for meristic characters. Dorsal rays were 9 to 11 (mean 9.5), anal rays 14 to 16 (mean 14.7), pectoral rays 9 to 13 (mean 11.8), and lateral scales 56 to 64 (mean 61.2). Pored lateral line scales ranged from 14 to about 18 (mean 16.2). Some values are garbled in the scan.
- On 30 January 1981, ichthyologists from the University of Alabama and the University of Tennessee collected additional juveniles (46 to 55 mm SL) at Tipton County, Tennessee (Duvall Landing). These specimens are deposited at both universities.
- Juveniles were also collected at Crittenden County, Arkansas (West Memphis, near the I-40 and US Highway 70 bridges) in December 1980. Collections were made with 3.2 mm mesh seines, mostly over sandy bottom in slow current at depths of 0.5 to 5 m.
- These records represent the first reports of *O. mordax* from the Mississippi River of Arkansas and Tennessee.

**Parasites.**
- The study reports that *Camallanus oxycephalus* infections in fish hosts varied seasonally, with intensity rising through summer and declining later in the year.
- Related reports noted increases in *Spinitectus carolini* infections from July to December and a July peak in sunfish.

**Gila topminnow (Arizona).**
- The new population is the first documented locality described in this note. It is on private land and the exact location is withheld.
- In the August survey, only topminnows occupied pools 6 through 12. In later surveys (September 1980), topminnows were found in pools 13 through 18. Thousands of topminnows were recorded, and juvenile abundance indicated active reproduction. In Pool 13, 133 fish (62.7%) of the catch were topminnows.
- Topminnows and mosquitofish were judged roughly equal in abundance in pools 13 through 17. Mosquitofish predominated in Pool 18.
- Juvenile topminnows were abundant in the upper pools, which lacked mosquitofish, and rare in the lower pools, where the two species were sympatric.
- Topminnow numbers decreased in absolute terms over the winter.

**Overall significance.** The authors conclude that the new population expands known distribution for an endangered fish, while the smelt records challenge the assumption that Mississippi River juvenile smelt cannot persist through summer. Both reports highlight the need for further field documentation of range and reproduction.
