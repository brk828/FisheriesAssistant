# Toxicity of Synthetic Detergents to Rainbow Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmo-gairdneri

## Executive Summary

This 1957 reprint from *The Water & Waste Treatment Journal* reports laboratory experiments by the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] (Stevenage) on how household [[Synthetic Detergents]] affect [[Rainbow Trout]]. The authors found that the toxicity of the detergent mixture came mainly from its anionic alkyl aryl sulphonate surfactant. Continuous exposure at about 3 p.p.m. active material killed half the fish in roughly 12 weeks. Sewage treatment markedly reduced toxicity, and the authors concluded that synthetic surfactants were unlikely to be a serious danger to fish in British rivers, although low [[Dissolved Oxygen]] increased the danger.

## Background & Description

Most household detergents contain an anionic surface-active constituent, usually an [[Alkyl Aryl Sulphonate]]. Normal [[Sewage Treatment]] does not remove it completely, so sewage effluents discharged to streams almost always carry residual surfactants or their partial breakdown products. The paper asks whether these substances harm fish.

Background concentrations were already documented:
- The Committee on Synthetic Detergents reported 0.2 to 4.9 p.p.m. in British rivers, with the highest value in a stream containing undiluted effluent.
- The laboratory measured 3 to 6 p.p.m. in the effluent of a Hertfordshire sewage works over several months. This was considered typical of most effluents.
- Because effluents are usually diluted by river water, concentrations in fish-bearing streams were expected to seldom exceed 2 to 3 p.p.m.

Short-term exposure at these levels was known to be non-toxic, but the authors wanted to test whether continuous exposure over weeks or months could be lethal.

**Test material:** Exact formulations were unknown, so the team used an equal-weight mixture of seven proprietary washing powders (Daz, Dreft, Fab, Spel, Surf, Tide, Wisk). It was intended to approximate the ingredients and proportions found in domestic sewage.

**Test animals and methods:**
- Rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*), up to about 18 months old, were taken from one trout-farm pond and held in one laboratory stock tank. Fish were allocated at random.
- Most aquaria held 10 fish. Experiment 5 used 11 fish, and the second test of Experiment 10 used 20.
- Fixed volumes of 30 or 40 litres were used in Experiments 1 to 7. Solutions were renewed daily, or every 2 hours in Experiment 3. Experiments 8 to 10 used continuous flow.
- Surfactant was measured by the [[Methylene Blue Method]] and expressed as Manoxol O.T. equivalent.
- Dilution water was dechlorinated tap water (about 350 p.p.m. CaCO₃) in Experiment 1, and unchlorinated borehole water (about 320 p.p.m. CaCO₃) in the others.
- Experimental temperatures ranged from 17.0 to 20.1 °C, with fish lengths from 5.5 to 15 cm (Table 1).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study links three environmental factors that act on fish in the same river reach:

1. **Surfactant concentration:** Detergent residues are carried into streams mainly through sewage effluent.
2. **Oxygen availability:** Sewage also contains oxidisable matter, so effluent-receiving rivers may have dissolved oxygen below air saturation.
3. **Treatment history:** Biological treatment changes the toxicity of the residues. Two systems were tested: [[Percolating Filters]] and the [[Activated-Sludge Process]].

The authors describe the dose–response relationship as similar to that for other poisons. They cite [[Potassium Cyanide]] toxicity studies by Herbert and Merkens for comparison.

## Key Findings

**1. Toxicity of the mixed detergents (Experiment 1)**
- Analysed surfactant concentration was 20 to 26% of the added detergent. Much of this variation was attributed to analytical error, about ±0.5 p.p.m. in the 1 to 10 p.p.m. range.
- Over 81 measurements, concentration fell by an average of 0.23 p.p.m. during roughly 24 hours in the test solutions.
- Controls with dechlorinated tap water only showed 100% survival for the full 85-day test.
- Median survival time fell from about 0.5 day at about 12 p.p.m. to about 12 weeks at about 3 p.p.m.
- The regression was log T = K − n log C, with K = 4.93 and n = 3.71, where T is median survival in hours and C is concentration in p.p.m. Manoxol O.T.
- Standard deviations of log survival times varied widely but were not correlated with the mean.

**2. Toxicity of washing-powder builders (Experiment 2)**
- Individual builders were tested at concentrations equivalent to 10 p.p.m. surfactant in a typical formulation, and at up to 64 times that level.
- No fish died within 24 hours at up to 8 times the lowest concentration for most substances. Sodium sulphate, sodium silicate, sodium tripolyphosphate and carboxymethyl cellulose caused no deaths at any multiple tested.
- Sodium pyrophosphate and sodium perborate caused 100% and 80% mortality, respectively, at 64 times the lowest concentration.
- A fatty acid alkylolamide caused 100% mortality at 16 times the lowest concentration.
- The authors concluded that builders contribute little to toxicity.

**3. Source of toxicity (Experiment 3)**
- One part of Manoxol O.T. surfactant in detergent mixture was as toxic as 1.21 parts of [[Sodium Tetrapropylene Benzene Sulphonate]]. Two other experiments gave ratios of 1:1.21 and 1:1.35.
- The authors concluded that most toxicity came from materials of this type, though they noted the identity was not certain.

**4. Effect of sewage treatment (Experiments 4–7)**
- Effluent from detergent-containing sewage was more toxic than detergent-free control effluent. It was far less toxic than either the detergent mixture or sodium tetrapropylene benzene sulphonate added directly to water.
- Percolating filter effluents (Experiments 4 and 5) had average surfactant concentrations of 9.2 and 9.6 p.p.m. Manoxol O.T.
- Activated-sludge effluents (Experiments 6 and 7) had average concentrations of 8.1 and 10.2 p.p.m. Manoxol O.T. Their toxicity was similarly low.
- Effluent concentrations fluctuated, so the authors could compare toxicity only qualitatively.

**5. Suspended solids do not explain the reduced toxicity**
- Removing suspended solids of 37 and 29 p.p.m. changed surfactant concentrations only from 9.8 to 9.7 p.p.m. and from 11.6 to 11.1 p.p.m. Manoxol O.T.
- These changes were too small to account for the large toxicity difference. The mechanism of the reduction remains unknown.

**6. Dissolved oxygen interacts with detergent toxicity (Experiments 8–10)**
- Experiment 8 used 12 p.p.m. Manoxol O.T. At 4 p.p.m. dissolved oxygen, median survival was about half that at 8 p.p.m.
- Experiment 9 held oxygen at about 2.2 p.p.m. In detergent-free water, no fish died in 12 hours. At 1.8 p.p.m. Manoxol O.T., 50% mortality occurred in about 6 hours (median 342 min).
- Experiment 10 compared control water, control effluent, detergent-spiked water and residual-detergent effluent, all equivalent to 3.1 p.p.m. Manoxol O.T., at lethal oxygen levels of 1.67 and 1.74 p.p.m.
- Detergent-spiked water significantly reduced survival (combined p < 0.05, Fisher's method). Survival in residual-detergent effluent was not significantly different from control effluent.

**7. Conclusions**
- In clean, well-oxygenated water, 3 p.p.m. Manoxol O.T. of the mixed detergents killed about 50% of rainbow trout in roughly 12 weeks.
- Such concentrations also increased susceptibility to low oxygen. If present in a river at about 3 p.p.m., they would probably endanger fish, particularly where oxygen is reduced.
- Residues remaining after biological treatment were much less toxic. The authors concluded that synthetic surfactants were unlikely to be a serious danger to fish in British rivers, since most would be residual material at concentrations well below 3 p.p.m.
