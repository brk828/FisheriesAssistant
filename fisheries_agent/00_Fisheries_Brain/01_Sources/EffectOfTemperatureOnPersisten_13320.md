## Executive Summary

This 1986 Research Information Bulletin details a study on the environmental persistence of **[[Rotenone]]**, the most widely used **[[Piscicide]]** in the United States since its registration in 1962. Researchers used high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to track rotenone residues in water, sediment, invertebrates, and fish tissue across two pond environments—one warmwater and one coldwater—following treatment with Noxfish® (0.25 mg/L active rotenone). The central finding is that **water temperature is the primary driver of rotenone degradation rate**, following first-order decay kinetics. This has direct practical implications for **[[Fishery Management]]**: warm-water treatments allow for rapid restocking, while cold-water treatments ensure more thorough, longer-lasting eradication of target fish populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study examines rotenone's behavior across a whole-pond ecosystem, tracking its movement and persistence through four distinct compartments:

- **Water column**: Primary medium of initial toxicant distribution and the fastest-degrading compartment.
- **Bottom sediments**: Acted as a temporary reservoir, accumulating rotenone residues before gradual decline.
- **Invertebrates**: Freshwater clams and crayfish bioaccumulated rotenone, showing residue levels that initially rose before declining — relevant to **[[Bioaccumulation]]** dynamics in non-target species.
- **Fish tissue**: Fish (the target organisms) showed the same uptake-then-decline pattern as invertebrates, with residue levels **consistently exceeding surrounding water concentrations** at all sampling points — indicating significant tissue-level bioconcentration relative to the aqueous phase.

Two contrasting thermal regimes were compared:
- **Warmwater pond**: 23–27°C
- **Coldwater pond**: 0–5°C

Temperature emerged as the master ecological variable controlling the compound's residence time in the system, governing microbial/chemical decomposition rates and thus the duration of toxic exposure to non-target and recovering fish communities.

## Key Findings

- **Decay kinetics**: Rotenone degradation in water followed **first-order decay** kinetics in both ponds.
- **Half-life comparison**:
  - Coldwater pond (0–5°C): **10.3 days**
  - Warmwater pond (23–27°C): **0.94 days** (~11x faster degradation)
- **Water residue detection limits** (<0.002 mg/L):
  - Coldwater pond: below detection after **57 days**
  - Warmwater pond: below detection after only **4 days**
- **Sediment residues**:
  - Coldwater pond: peaked at **0.1 µg/g** after 14 days, declined to <0.025 µg/g by day 64
  - Warmwater pond: below detection within **24 hours**
- **Biological tissue residues** (fish, freshwater clams, crayfish): increased over the first few days of exposure, then declined — consistent with an initial uptake/bioconcentration phase followed by depuration and/or environmental degradation.
- **Tissue vs. water comparison**: Rotenone concentrations in fish and invertebrate tissues were **higher than corresponding water concentrations** on all sampled days, indicating bioconcentration.
- **Management implication**: Because temperature governs degradation speed, fishery managers can strategically time rotenone treatments:
  - **Warm conditions** → faster degradation → shorter restocking wait times, minimized disruption to recreational water use.
  - **Cool/fall/winter conditions** → slower degradation → more complete and thorough eradication of undesired fish populations, useful for eradication-focused management goals in **[[Pond Rehabilitation]]** projects.
- **Regulatory context**: Findings were generated to support ongoing **[[EPA Registration]]** requirements for continued approved use of rotenone as a piscicide in the U.S.
