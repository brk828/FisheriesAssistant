# Native Fishes of Arid Lands: A Dwindling Resource of the Desert Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service General Technical Report (RM-206, September 1991) documents the native freshwater fishes of the arid American Southwest. It pairs photographs by John N. Rinne, including 44 species on the front matter (the preface gives 46), with a text by W. L. Minckley that covers regional geology, hydrology, habitat history, fish ecology and conservation. The central message is that native fishes are disappearing as water is diverted, impounded, degraded and taken over by introduced species. The report also argues that biological interactions with non-native fishes, rather than physical or chemical change alone, are the ultimate cause of native fish loss in the United States.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** To make these "unique, threatened and endangered fishes" known to managers and the public, since they are poorly recognized and rarely valued. Appendix I lists selected technical references; Appendix II gives common and scientific names.
- **Region:** The American Southwest spans 12 political units, including the Mexican states of Baja California Norte and Sur, Chihuahua, Coahuila and Sonora, and the US states of Arizona, California, Colorado, Texas, New Mexico, Nevada and Utah (Fig. 1).
- **Setting:** Elevations run from sea level to more than 3600 m. Annual precipitation ranges from less than 5.0 cm in the most severe desert to more than 128 cm at high elevations. Major drainages include the [[Colorado River]] and [[Rio Yaqui]], which flow to the Gulf of California.
- **Geologic history:** Deserts are geologically recent. Woodlands and prairies covered parts of the region only a few thousand years ago. Miocene extension and Plio-Pleistocene uplift produced the modern mountain-and-basin landscape and a persistently arid climate.
- **Conservation categories:** Follow the American Fisheries Society (AFS) system: "endangered," "threatened," and "special concern."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water and habitat.** Nearly all water comes from montane snowpacks. Channels with substantial mountain watersheds support perennial flow. Historically, desert rivers had high spring and summer runoff, seasonal flash floods, and extensive riparian forests and marshes. Water tables lay within a few feet of the surface.

**Human impacts.** Diversions, dams, groundwater pumping, channelization, overgrazing, logging and mining have changed these systems dramatically. The report notes:
- Groundwater pumpage has lowered water tables more than 50 m in most valleys and more than 200 m in some.
- Arroyo cutting, triggered by overgrazing and the 1870s livestock die-off, drained marshes and riparian zones. Mesquite and introduced saltcedar replaced cottonwood-willow woodlands.
- Dams convert turbulent streams into reservoirs and concrete canals, and sediment supply to the delta has been cut off.

**Elevation zones:**
- **High-elevation creeks (above about 1800 m in the US):** Cool summers and cold winters favor [[Trout]] and exclude many warmwater species. Riparian forest and woody debris make these streams heterotrophic, with detritus as the base of the food web.
- **Intermediate elevations (about 1800 to 900 m):** These creeks and rivers, including [[Aravaipa Creek]] and [[Cienega Creek]], support most remaining native fish populations. Summer temperatures near 30 °C in the afternoon can fall to 21.1 °C at dawn. Flows are dominated by flash floods, and streams are highly productive.
- **Low desert streams:** Pumping and diversion have dried most of them. Historically, water-table-fed marshes and oxbows supported many species.
- **Lower Colorado and Yaqui deltas:** Historically these were broad, sand-bottomed, nutrient-rich systems. Tidal bores of more than 9.0 m at the river mouth reached more than 100 km upstream.
- **Desert springs:** Constant temperature and chemistry allow year-round reproduction. Endemic springfishes and pupfishes live here, and some populations are isolated in thermal waters.

## Key Findings

**Fish diversity and distribution**
- The report discusses 41 strictly freshwater fishes: 10 occur only in Mexico, 9 only in the United States, and 22 are shared by both countries.
- The lower Colorado basin naturally held about 18 fish species. The Rio Yaqui supports 20, of which only three are shared with the Colorado.
- The original fish fauna of the Colorado River rose from 32 species to more than 80 after introductions.

**Conservation status**
- In 1989, 28 (68%) of the 41 taxa were listed by the AFS as threatened, endangered or of special concern.
- Three regional species are reported extinct: Las Vegas speckled dace, Pahranagat spinedace and Monkey Spring pupfish. Monkey Spring pupfish disappeared within months of [[Largemouth Bass]] introduction.

**Key species accounts**
- **[[Apache Trout]] (threatened):** Restricted to the White Mountains of Arizona. Hybridization with rainbow and cutthroat trout had reduced it to less than 5.0% of its former range by the 1970s.
- **[[Gila Trout]] (endangered):** Persists naturally in only five streams in New Mexico.
- **[[Spikedace]] (threatened):** Endemic to the Gila River basin.
- **[[Loach Minnow]] (threatened):** A bottom-dwelling species of riffles with coarse algae. It lives at most two years.
- **[[Longfin Dace]]:** Widely distributed and drought-resistant. It recolonizes streams within hours or days of flow returning.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]:** The most morphologically variable native species. The Paria River population has a unique, elongated, depigmented form.
- **[[Humpback Chub]] (endangered):** Lives in whitewater canyon eddies. The largest known population is in the lowermost Little Colorado River.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]] (endangered):** Largest North American minnow (about 1.8 m and 45 kg). Now extirpated from the lower Colorado basin.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]:** Lake Mohave holds more than 50,000 individuals, but larvae disappear before reaching the juvenile stage. No recruitment into the adult population has been documented for at least 30 years, and predation by introduced fishes is suspected.
- **[[Bonytail]]:** The report states fewer than 50 individuals have been caught since 1975, and all appear to be old adults (35-40 years). It is considered by many workers the most endangered freshwater fish in western North America.
- **[[Woundfin]] (endangered):** Now restricted to the Virgin River mainstream.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]] (endangered):** Restricted to three small natural localities in the US and a similar number on the Colorado delta in Mexico.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]] (endangered):** Fewer than 10 natural localities remain in the United States. Topminnows also include all-female hybrid lineages in the lower Rio Yaqui.
- **[[Moapa Dace]] (endangered):** Restricted to thermal pools of the upper Moapa River, preferring 31-34 °C.
- **[[White River Springfish]]:** Lives in thermal springs at temperatures above 36.7 °C and very low oxygen. Introduced fishes cannot invade these habitats.
- **[[Totoaba]]:** A giant endemic of the upper Gulf of California. Commercially important in the past, it has nearly disappeared from overfishing and dam-related changes.

**Causes of decline**
- Habitat destruction through dewatering, channelization, flow alteration and sedimentation.
- Non-native species. The report argues that native fishes evolved without piscine predators and were replaced by introduced game fishes, mosquitofish and forage species adapted to reservoirs.
- Introduction of [[Non-Native Fishes]] such as [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] is identified as the most logical explanation for disappearances in the US, even where physical and chemical conditions remain near natural.

**Central hypothesis and conclusions**
- The author hypothesizes that biological interactions with non-native fishes are the ultimate factor causing native fish disappearance in the US. The "how" of species replacement is left for future experimental research.
- Native fish populations remain in Mexico, where dams and introductions are less advanced. The author predicts that native species there will decline as non-native fishes become established.
- Public support, habitat protection in preserves and wilderness, hatchery broodstock and restocking, and exclusion of non-native trout from native ranges are recommended as management steps.
