# San Pedro River Native Fish Management and Water Rights Assessment

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1990 memorandum from W. L. Minckley to the [[San Pedro River]] ISF Quantification Team reassesses water-rights needs for native fish management on San Pedro River properties managed by [[U.S. Bureau of Land Management]] and [[The Nature Conservancy]]. The document emphasizes that the [[San Pedro River]] maintains its historical annual water yield but suffers from altered seasonal distribution, with most flow occurring during winter and summer floods while spring and autumn periods experience severe drought. Of 13 native fish species historically present, only two ([[Desert Sucker]] and [[Longfin Dace]]) currently occupy the mainstream, though six additional species persist in the basin and five are available through propagation programs. The memorandum argues that maintaining current mean annual discharge with improved seasonal distribution is essential for native fish recovery, and that six species are federally listed or proposed for listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Background & Description

The [[San Pedro River]] originally supported 13 of the 18 fish species known from the entire [[Gila River Basin]] on the same annual water volume it currently yields. The native fish fauna can be categorized into four ecological groups: (1) species of large, eroding rivers and associated floodplain habitats; (2) species of perennial streams of variable erosiveness; (3) species occupying spring-fed or river-associated habitats; and (4) ubiquitous species including those using intermittent streams.

Current species status includes:
- **Mainstream residents**: [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*) and [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- **Basin residents available for natural reinvasion**: [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), and [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)
- **Species under propagation or available for reintroduction**: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]

Six species are federally listed or proposed for listing as endangered, including [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Sonoran Topminnow]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Gila Chub]] are state-listed and/or federal candidates for listing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fundamental management challenge is seasonal water distribution rather than total annual yield. The document notes that "essentially the same amount of water passes down the channel each year as it did before the direct and indirect impacts of technological humans." However, most yield now occurs during winter and summer floods, with extensive channel drying during spring and autumn drought periods.

Native fishes demonstrate resilience to flooding events. Research by Minckley and Meffe (1987) demonstrated that native fishes are "scarcely influenced by flooding" and may actually benefit because non-native competitors and predators are removed. The critical limiting factor is spring and autumn drought, as "no fish can live without water" and "average flows that remain the same as in the past are of absolutely no use to a fish that faces death in a few moments of desiccation."

The ideal hydrological management involves establishing a recharge gradient to the water table during high discharge periods to create sufficient storage, allowing a reversed gradient during drought to maintain surface flow. The document emphasizes that "streams rarely cut below a water table, and channels tend to quickly aggrade after flood in reaches where the water table is high."

The enabling legislation for the [[San Pedro River]] properties refers to "enhancement" and potentially "re-establishment" of original faunal conditions. Unlike most aquatic systems in the American Southwest, the [[San Pedro River]] has high potential for achieving this goal due to its maintained annual water yield.

## Key Findings

**Water Rights Requirements**: The amount of water required to enhance and re-establish the native fauna equals the current annual yield (same as historical conditions) or somewhat more, but critically must be "distributed more equitably from season to season." Maintaining present mean annual discharge is mandatory for species currently present; reduction of spring and autumn drought impacts will allow other species to reinvade or be re-established through stocking.

**Recovery Plan Integration**: Draft recovery plans for [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]], and [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (USFWS 1987b-c, 1989, 1990b) all depend heavily on the [[San Pedro River]] properties as reintroduction sites for species recovery. Recommendations include immediate reintroduction efforts for [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]], with remnant populations already protected on [[Aravaipa Creek]] by [[U.S. Bureau of Land Management]] and [[The Nature Conservancy]].

**Genetic Considerations**: The [[Desert Pupfish]] was originally described from the [[San Pedro River]]. Genetic studies are underway to determine relationships of existing pupfish stocks and their genetic heterozygosities to ensure re-introduced populations are as similar as possible to the original and possess genetic diversities maximizing probability for re-establishment. Similar genetic models are being developed for [[Sonoran Topminnow]].

**Additional Protected Waters**: [[The Nature Conservancy]] owns additional water areas in the [[San Pedro River]] basin ([[Muleshoe Ranch]], [[Canelo Cienega]], [[Binghamton Swamp]]) that substantially add to the importance of the properties for endangered fish conservation, providing additional protection for [[Gila Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Sonora Sucker]].

**Management Strategy**: The document emphasizes that expensive and complex solutions involving construction of dams, delivery canals, and direct purchase of water rights (as used in other endangered species recovery efforts) will be unnecessary for the [[San Pedro River]] "so long as a natural, on-going process of natural repair can be promoted and achieved on the watershed and floodplain." The management approach should encourage natural repair of the watershed and floodplain to retain and store annual flood discharge and release it during periods of minimal runoff.

**Non-native Species Challenge**: Following hydrological restoration, "the formidible task of managing against non-native exotic species" remains necessary to allow indigenous fishes to persist.

**Habitat Reconstruction**: Only fishes of category 1 (large, eroding rivers and floodplain habitats) may be difficult to re-establish without direct habitat reconstruction, but even these may be recovered if the water table can be raised and natural processes of channel evolution create predictable meanders and alternating riffles and pools.
