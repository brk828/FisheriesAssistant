# Basic Survey of Lake Mohave (1955 Wildlife Restoration Completion Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 completion report, prepared by Richard A. Wagner for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] under the Wildlife Restoration Division, presents a basic survey of [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the [[Colorado River]] formed by [[Davis Dam]] below [[Lake Mead]]. The work aimed to determine fishing pressure, catch per angler hour, growth rate and average age of harvested fish, fish species and relative abundance, and fish food indexes, in order to build a comprehensive management plan as fishing pressure grew with new roads and nearby metropolitan demand. The fishery is dominated by [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], and the report recommends stocking, introduction of [[Kokanee]] and [[Threadfin Shad]], and continued [[Limnology]] studies.

## Background & Description

**Setting and morphology.** Lake Mohave extends about 67 miles along the Colorado River between Davis Dam (south) and Boulder Dam (north). Twenty-two miles lie in [[Black Canyon]], seven miles in [[Pyramid Canyon]], and the remainder in a middle section containing four narrows and five basins, the largest being [[Cottonwood Basin]] (about eleven miles long and five miles wide). The lake averages 0.5 miles wide, with a maximum theoretical volume of 1,892,000 acre-feet, a surface area of 28,000 acres, and a calculated storage of 8,530 acre-feet at 533.39 ft elevation (as given in the source; the figure is reported as stated). The shoreline exceeds 200 miles. The surrounding watershed is nearly barren, with steep-walled canyons.

**Access.** Four main ports of entry exist: [[Willow Beach]] and a south-end landing on the Arizona side, and [[El Dorado Landing]] and [[Cottonwood Cove]] on the Nevada side. Future National Park Service plans included hard-topping roads.

**Hydrology and operations.** Water is primarily used for electrical generation and irrigation. Inflow comes mainly from releases from [[Lake Mead]] through [[Boulder Dam]], with minor input from desert cloudbursts. Fluctuation is greatest in late spring (irrigation releases) and in fall (power generation). [[Davis Dam]] was built in 1950, but the basin did not fill until early summer 1951, when the level was about 70 feet lower.

**Fish species.** Table II lists the species present. Game and sport species include Rainbow Trout, Brown Trout, Brook Trout, Cutthroat-Rainbow hybrid trout, [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Bullhead Catfish]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Green Sunfish]]. Non-game and native-type species include [[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Carp]], and suckers. Several cyprinids and catostomids were reported but not observed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality and thermal structure.** Water quality was described as equal to or better than that of Lake Mead, because the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] releases the best-quality water downstream. Lake Mohave receives plankton from Lake Mead, whose supply is nearly devoid of it. Intake-exhaust turnover is high, making Lake Mohave the coldest warm-water fish lake in the state. Compressed air injected into penstocks supplies dissolved oxygen: 9 ppm was recorded directly below Boulder Dam, and surface wind mixing maintained dissolved oxygen above 7 ppm in most of the lake.

**Thermal zones.** Willow Beach lies in the middle of the cold-water area, El Dorado at its southern limit, Cottonwood in the middle of the warm-water area, and Katherine at the lower end of it. A zone of habitat integration (cold-warm) extends from Black Canyon south about six miles. Three thermal layers were identified: a warm surface layer (summer) or cold surface layer (winter), a middle mixing layer, and a coldest layer flowing through the lake as a bottom current. Surface temperatures in the protected coves in summer often exceeded 80°F, while a bottom stream of 55–60°F water flowed toward Davis Dam.

**Primary productivity.** Blue-green algae dominated the phytoplankton. Plankton concentration in Black Canyon averaged about 0.1 cc per 100,000 cc of water. Benthic and littoral vegetation (sago pondweed, naiad, widgeon grass, horned pondweed, muskgrass) was present in some areas, and scud and snail stocks were noted as potential food for trout. Filamentous algae were abundant in some areas.

**Trout habitat and migration.** Thermal stress, reported by the report at surface temperatures above 80°F, caused many trout to move to deeper, cooler water or downstream through Davis Dam, where releases of more than 16,000 cfs provided a strong current. Rainbow trout tagged at Boulder Dam were later recovered below Davis Dam, some with injuries from turbine blades.

**Fishing and angler behavior.** Trout fishing was concentrated in Black Canyon and declined over time as the catch per unit effort fell. By 1953 most boats from El Dorado sought largemouth bass. The "Basin" (near Pyramid Canyon) was the center of most bass production by numbers and pounds, and wind-driven waves there could mix water layers to depths exceeding 30 feet.

## Key Findings

- **Fish community:** Table II lists about 20 species, including 5 game species considered most important. Rainbow Trout and Largemouth Bass comprise the main fishery.
- **Fishing pressure and use:** Monthly visits in 1952–53 were recorded at Willow Beach, Lake Mohave Resort (Katherine Wash), El Dorado Cove and Cottonwood Cove. Total boat use was 3,706 in 1952 and 2,050 in 1953 for the main landings in the sampled tables; use was roughly split equally between Arizona and Nevada sides.
- **Trout fishery:** Rainbow trout fishing was mainly in Black Canyon. Catch-per-angler-hour values ranged roughly from 0.36 to 2.6 across landings and periods (Table VI). Reported trout growth was excellent to about 8 inches and slower thereafter.
- **Bass fishery:** The bass fishery was well established in the rest of the lake. The Basin was the most productive area. Average bass size was about 14 inches (standard length), and scale readings indicated third-year fish. Growth studies suggested slower growth than Lake Mead bass.
- **Rainbow trout stocking:** Between January 1945 and November 1953, rainbow trout were planted in numbers ranging from about 1,000 to 794,000 per plant, at sizes from 0.75 to 6 inches. The report noted natural reproduction was negligible due to fluctuating water levels and predation, making the trout fishery almost entirely dependent on hatchery fish.
- **Tagging:** About 250 tagged rainbow trout were released in the area before October 1953, with a return rate of about 10 percent.
- **Recommendations:**
  1. Establish and maintain a trout fishery with stocked fingerlings at a set density, with annual plantings in Black Canyon.
  2. Establish a trout fishery in a more suitable aquatic area.
  3. Establish a large rainbow trout population with annual Kokanee plantings.
  4. Introduce [[Threadfin Shad]] as forage for largemouth bass, crappie and similar game fish.
  5. Continue limnological studies to verify whether the fishery is stable.
- **Summary conclusions:** The lake covers about 28,000 surface acres and holds 1,900,000 acre-feet at capacity. Lake fluctuation is the main constraint on fish production, and the basin's large bass population was considered the lake's most important sport fishery. The report notes a growth rate that is comparable to other lakes, and that an age-6 to age-8 bass fishery exists in the state's reservoir system.
