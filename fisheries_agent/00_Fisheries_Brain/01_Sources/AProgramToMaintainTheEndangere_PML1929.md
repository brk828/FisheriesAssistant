# A program to maintain the endangered razorback sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt comes from the proceedings of the 1993 *Environmental Enhancement of Water Projects* seminar, held in Sacramento, California, and sponsored by the [[U.S. Committee on Irrigation and Drainage]] with co-sponsorship from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. It contains two items. The first is a short section on the [[Glenn-Colusa Fish Screen]] project, which covers screen alternatives and the schedule for environmental review. The second, and main, item is a paper by Mueller, Burke and Horn on the [[Razorback Sucker Program]], an on-site rearing effort to save the [[Razorback Sucker]], a federally endangered catostomid, in [[Lake Mohave]]. The program aims to bypass recruitment failure caused by [[Non-Native Fish Predation]] by rearing young suckers in predator-free backwaters before release.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system of western North America. It was once common in the mainstem and major tributaries from Wyoming to Mexico. It is now reduced to remnant populations in the Green River (Utah), the Upper Colorado River Basin (Colorado-Utah), and Lake Mohave (Arizona-Nevada), where most remaining fish occur (Minckley et al., 1991). The species was federally listed as endangered in 1991 under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, and critical habitat designation for much of the Colorado River, including Lake Mohave, was under consideration.

Key population facts reported:
- About 60,000 suckers remain, most of them in Lake Mohave, a regulatory reservoir.
- The fish are old, believed to exceed 30 years of age.
- Spawning, egg incubation and hatching have been documented, but larvae disappear before reaching 1 inch (25 mm).

Earlier restocking did not work. A 1980–1990 stocking program by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] released about 11.5 million larval and juvenile suckers, and follow-up surveys found they also disappeared. Predation studies by Marsh and Brooks (1989) identified [[Catfish Predation]] by non-native species as the cause, and recommended stocking larger fish. Hatchery space is limited, and the cost of rearing and hauling fish rises steeply with size.

A 1985 backwater experiment in Lake Mohave provided the key idea. Fish were removed from an isolated backwater, adult suckers were added just before spawning, and the backwater stayed isolated for a full year because of low reservoir elevations. Spawning succeeded, and the juveniles reached 8 inches (20.4 cm) by December. This led to the proposal of [[On-Site Spawning and Rearing]] as a low-cost alternative to hatchery production.

### Glenn-Colusa Fish Screen (Preceding Section)

The earlier section, from a [[Bureau of Reclamation]] project summary, describes screen alternatives for the Glenn-Colusa diversion. A meeting of project parties eliminated the drum screen and modular inclined screens. Vertical flat plate screens, angled or linear, became the preferred type, and sites close to existing screens were preferred. The gradient restoration facility was kept under consideration, but the author doubted it could be built and thought pumps might become necessary, because [[Agency Criteria]] prohibit pumping fish within bypass systems. ## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predation as the bottleneck:** Most scientists agree that predation by non-native fishes is the single most important cause of recruitment failure in Lake Mohave. Without recruitment, the population is expected to die off within the decade (Minckley et al., 1991).
- **Limits of habitat restoration:** In the lower basin, habitat restoration is not feasible because habitat alteration is extensive. Even with dams removed and reservoirs drained, non-native game fish could not be removed. The authors therefore argue for active management of the existing population.
- **Contrast with upper basin efforts:** The [[Recovery Program for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado]] aims at habitat restoration in more pristine areas, which the lower basin cannot support.
- **Odonate predation:** Removing fish from backwaters caused large increases in odonate nymphs (damselflies and dragonflies). Light traps set overnight caught up to 5,000 large odonates per set. Laboratory tests showed odonates can capture and eat larval suckers (Horn et al., in press).
- **Aquatic vegetation:** [[Sago Pondweed]] infested the shallow Yuma Cove backwater in summer 1992 and hampered netting. It was removed manually and a benthic liner was installed in the deepest half acre. Pondweed still grows in shallower areas, where it gives young fish cover.
- **Residual predators:** Predators that remained after poisoning and netting still caused heavy losses. Some avoided capture by hiding in pondweed beds or other cover.

## Key Findings

**Program design and status**
- The [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) was formed in 1990 by the Bureau of Reclamation. Its members are the Fish and Wildlife Service, Arizona Game and Fish Department, National Biological Survey, National Park Service, Arizona State University, and Nevada Department of Wildlife.
- The goal is to produce subadult suckers under "semi-natural" conditions: spawning and growth in predator-free waters, without supplemental feeding.
- Plan: isolate coves of 2–5 acres (0.8–2 ha) with temporary or permanent barriers, remove exotic fish, and stock spawners or their young.
- Target: survival assessment of at least 10,000 subadult suckers released from on-site ponds. If the concept fails, supplemental feeding, fertilization, pen culture or hatchery production would be used.
- Results will be analyzed annually and evaluated at the end of a 5-year program.

**Results to date**
- Two backwaters were isolated. A 2-acre (0.8 ha) earthen levee at one site has held, though storm wave action has eroded parts of it.
- Yuma Cove was poisoned in fall 1990. 100 adults (67 male, 33 female) were stocked just before spawning in 1991 and 1992. Spawning succeeded both years, and larvae were collected.
- No juveniles were produced in 1991, and the cause was not identified. In November 1992, 296 juveniles averaging 14 inches (35 cm) were collected. About half were tagged and released into the main reservoir, and the rest were moved to Davis Cove.
- In 1993, about 250,000 fertilized eggs were manually stripped and distributed in the shallows. Eggs hatched and larvae were collected, but final success was not known at the time of writing.
- Davis Cove, a larger, deeper grow-out backwater of about 3 acres (1.2 ha), was isolated with a net barrier (3/8 inch mesh; mouth 150 ft wide). It received 10,000 juveniles from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and 150 larger suckers from Yuma Cove in June 1992. Over 1,500 lb (680 kg) of non-native fish were removed by electrofishing, gill netting, fyke netting, seining and angling, and the remaining community was tested for continued predation pressure.
- Hatchery-produced fish averaged 24 inches (68 mm) at stocking, while Yuma Cove fish averaged 6 inches (150 mm). Later collections suggested survival of the smaller hatchery fish was much lower. The recapture ratio of Yuma Cove to hatchery fish was about 10:1.

**Returns from reservoir release**
- In fall 1992, 153 cove-reared suckers over 10 inches (25 cm) were released into Lake Mohave from Yuma and Davis Coves.
- Five of the 153 were recaptured during the 1993 spawning effort. They were found at four locations 3 to 21 miles (5–33 km) from their release points, had survived about 6 months with the reservoir population, and showed normal growth. The reservoir covers 28,500 surface acres (11,400 ha).
- The authors noted that the return rate was very low, but they expected higher returns as more subadults were released. Razorback suckers normally take 2–3 years to mature, so they had anticipated a delay in detection.

**Future plans**
- Replace the temporary Davis Cove barrier with a permanent dike in 1994.
- Add four more coves isolated by sand berms (added in 1993; results pending).
- From 1994, collect naturally spawned larvae with light traps across the 2-month spawning period and rear them to 12 inches. This aims to capture a wider genetic base than adult-collected broodstock.
- Expand the approach to other long-lived native fishes, such as [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*).
- Build low-cost rearing ponds along the Colorado River with gated controls. Fertilized ponds would be stocked and opened to "seed" the river every 2 or 3 years.
- A parallel on-site rearing effort is underway in [[Lake Havasu]], integrated with a sport fishery habitat program.
