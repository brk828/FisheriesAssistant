# Gross primary productivity-consumer ratios

## Executive Summary
This Arizona Game and Fish Department report (Project F-8-R-1&2, Job E) by William J. McConnell investigates the relationship between **gross primary productivity (photosynthesis)** and **fish/amphibian biomass production** in controlled 95-gallon aquatic microcosms. Eight tanks were stocked with [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Medaka]], [[Tilapia Mossambica]], [[Striped Mullet]], and [[African Clawed Frog]] tadpoles, with all food ultimately derived from in-tank algal photosynthesis (closed-system bioenergetics). The study found that carnivorous/omnivorous consumers converted roughly **0.69–1.26% of gross photosynthesis** into harvestable biomass, closely matching the **0.98%** ratio observed for game fish (largemouth bass, black crappie) in the natural system of [[Peña Blanca Lake]]. The herbivorous Tilapia achieved a much higher conversion efficiency of **~10%**, illustrating trophic level effects on production efficiency. The work supports using **gross photosynthesis measurements as a practical proxy for fisheries production potential**.

## Background & Description
While this is primarily a productivity/bioenergetics study rather than a morphological one, some growth and size data were recorded:

- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*):
  - Introduced females: ~0.02 g each (late May)
  - After 70 days: >1.5 g each
  - Offspring (F1 generation) after 70 days: averaged <0.1 g live weight (stunted due to crowding)
  - Harvested individuals (Tank #2, 50% weekly harvest): reached 26–27 mm total length
  - Tank #4 (20% weekly harvest): 21–22 mm total length
  - Tank #1 (unharvested): 23–24 mm total length

- **[[Medaka]]** (*Oryzias latipes*): largest individuals in Tank #3 reached **26–27 mm** total length without harvest, under population density comparable to Gila topminnow tanks.

- **[[Tilapia Mossambica]]**: no mortality after introduction (July 16) in Tanks 5, 7, 8; suffered unexplained mortality in Tank 6 (experiment terminated there).

- **[[Striped Mullet]]** (*Mugil cephalus*): stocked as 2-inch fingerlings; most individuals leaped out of tanks shortly after introduction and could not be safely returned due to injury; survivors showed slight weight loss early in the experiment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Trophic classification of study organisms**:
  - **[[Mullet]]** and **[[Xenopus laevis]]** tadpoles: detritivores/sediment feeders, functionally "mostly herbivorous" since sediment was composed largely of algae and feces, with only minor protozoan/animal content.
  - **[[Gila Topminnow]]** and **[[Medaka]]**: naturally prefer animal matter (omnivorous/carnivorous tendency) but demonstrated capacity to subsist on algae-based food webs; positioned closer to the base of the food web than centrarchids or trout.
  - **[[Tilapia Mossambica]]**: primarily herbivorous, consuming unicellular and Aufwuchs (periphytic) algal forms.

- **Producer community response**:
  - Tanks without effective herbivores (Tanks 1–4): developed large filamentous algae mats and intermittent phytoplankton blooms; organic residue was **3–4x greater** than in Tilapia tanks.
  - Tanks with Tilapia as dominant herbivore (Tanks 5, 7, 8): developed dense phytoplankton communities with minimal filamentous/attached algae — indicating strong top-down grazing control.

- **System design**: Closed microcosms simulating natural pond productivity; fertilized with nitrogen (50 lbs/acre/month) and phosphorus (10 lbs/acre/month); algae seeded from natural habitats including [[Peña Blanca Lake]]. CO₂ enrichment via controlled dry ice sublimation regulated pH. Temperature maintained between 75–85°F using a water-jacketed cooling tank design (95-gallon tank nested in 105-gallon tank with flowing well water).

## Key Findings

- **Production ratios (Vertebrate harvest as % of gross photosynthesis)**:
  | Tank | Species | Harvest Regime | % of Gross Photosynthesis |
  |------|---------|-----------------|---------------------------|
  | 1 | Gila topminnow | Unharvested | 1.26% |
  | 2 | Gila topminnow | 50%/week | 0.90% |
  | 3 | Medaka + Mullet + Xenopus | Unharvested | 1.92% (total); 0.86% (medaka only) |
  | 4 | Gila topminnow | 20%/week | 0.69% |
  | 5 | Tilapia | Unharvested | — |
  | 7 | Tilapia | 20%/week | 9.90% |
  | 8 | Tilapia | 20%/week | 12.70% |

- **Comparative benchmark**: Angler harvest of largemouth bass and black crappie in [[Peña Blanca Lake]] equaled **0.98%** of gross photosynthesis (McConnell 1963) — closely matching the topminnow/medaka microcosm ratios (0.69–1.26%), validating microcosm results as representative of natural systems despite differences in allochthonous input.

- **Trophic efficiency and herbivory**: Tilapia (herbivore) achieved ~10x higher conversion efficiency than the more omnivorous/carnivorous topminnows and medakas — underscoring that **herbivorous fish convert primary production far more efficiently** than higher trophic-level fish.

- **Harvest regime effects**: Contrary to theoretical expectation, increased harvest rate did not consistently improve individual growth rates or total biomass yield. Tank #2 (50% harvest) produced *larger* individuals (26–27 mm) than Tank #4 (20% harvest, 21–22 mm), suggesting harvest-density interactions are non-linear.

- **Management implications / practical guideline**: 
  - Native game fish production equal to **≥2% of gross photosynthesis** is considered highly satisfactory.
  - Production **below 0.5%** of gross photosynthesis indicates a **weak point in the trophic system**.
  - No native North American sport fish is expected to match Tilapia's herbivore-level efficiency (~10-20% of net production).

- **Experimental validity note**: Minor unintended organic inputs occurred (decomposing snails, clams, and one dead mullet), but these were argued to have negligible impact on final biomass conversion figures, preserving the integrity of the closed-system photosynthesis-to-biomass ratio methodology.

- **Related methodology references**: Oxygen-based measurement of gross photosynthesis methodology detailed in [[McConnell 1962 Carboy Microcosms]] and [[McConnell 1963 Peña Blanca Lake Productivity]].
