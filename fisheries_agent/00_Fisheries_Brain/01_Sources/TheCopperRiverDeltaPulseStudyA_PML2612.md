# The Copper River Delta Pulse Study: An Interdisciplinary Survey of Aquatic Habitats

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Forest Service General Technical Report (PNW-GTR-282, 1991), edited by [[Mason D. Bryant]], summarizes a two-week synoptic survey of the wetlands of the [[Copper River Delta]], Alaska, conducted 20–31 July 1987. An interdisciplinary team examined geomorphology, water chemistry and nutrients, algae and plankton, macroinvertebrates, juvenile salmonid distribution, and wetland plant ecology. The authors stress that results are limited by a single midsummer sampling point, but they report that all observations were made concurrently at similar sites, so the datasets are linked in time and place. The work was designed to build a baseline data set, identify research needs, and provide a starting point for managing aquatic habitat for juvenile salmonids.

## Background & Description

The delta covers about 283,000 hectares and supports significant runs of [[Sockeye Salmon]] and [[Coho Salmon]], as well as [[Dolly Varden]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]]. The Alaska Department of Fish and Game had collected catch and escapement data for commercially important species, but no research had examined what controls biological productivity in the wetlands or the abundance and distribution of juvenile salmonids there.

The study area lay between the Heney Range and the Ragged Mountains, extending from glacier bases ([[Scott Glacier]] and [[Sheridan Glacier]]) to intertidal mudflats and offshore islands. Habitat units were grouped into:

- **Fluvial systems:** glacial rivers, clearwater forest streams, braided channels, [[Interbasin Slough Channels]], and [[Distributary Slough Channels]].
- **Lacustrine systems:** lakes, [[Beaver Ponds]], and [[Perched Ponds]] (slough or "perch" ponds).

Sampling sites were in the [[Eyak River]] and [[Alaganik Slough]] basins west of the Copper River, and the [[Martin River Slough]] basin to the east. The main Copper River channel was not sampled extensively.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geomorphology.** The delta has prograded since the late Pleistocene (about 10,000–13,000 years before present). Sediment accretion averaged 4 mm/yr (range 2.8–4.6 mm/yr) on older surfaces and 16 mm/yr on the newly uplifted "new marsh" following the 1964 earthquake, which raised parts of the delta 1–2 m. Levees along slough channels were 0.3–0.5 m high and 21–56 m wide. The authors estimate that 0.1–0.2 m of levee height is needed to sustain a perennial pond, and that pond development takes roughly 1.5–3.5 centuries. Bed material fines from gravel and cobble near glacier termini to sand in the tidal zone. Slope correlated well with median grain size, suggesting that grain size could be predicted from map gradients, though this would need local calibration.

**Water chemistry.** Carbonate chemistry dominated buffering. Ca + Mg ranged from 30 to 582 µM and total alkalinity from 123 to 1,216 µeq/L. Carbonate alkalinity averaged 84% of total alkalinity (r = 0.98 for Ca + Mg vs. alkalinity, n = 96). Pond habitats had the highest dissolved organic carbon (DOC), at 5–19 mg/L. DOC correlated with dissolved inorganic nitrogen (r = 0.75, n = 36) and with methane flux (r = 0.64, n = 16), which the authors interpret as internal recycling of organic matter. Pond methane was supersaturated relative to the atmosphere. Low N and P concentrations suggested that [[Nutrient Limitation]] was widespread during summer, with P limitation in most habitats.

**Algae and plankton.** Phytoplankton was scarce in glacially turbid rivers. Interbasin slough channels had the highest phytoplankton abundance and diversity, with an estimated 30–50 diatom taxa. Perched ponds were dominated by vascular plants and had the highest biomass relative to phytoplankton. *Nostoc* sp., a nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium, was widespread in perched ponds and open fields.

**Macroinvertebrates.** Sixteen qualitative benthic samples (washed through a 500-micron sieve, with over 99% recovery) were dominated by Crustacea and [[Chironomidae]]. Cladocera occurred only in ponds. The mysid *Neomysis mercedis* dominated new marsh sloughs, and the amphipod *Corophium spinicorne* was most abundant in old marsh sloughs. Ephemeroptera occurred only in [[Ibek Creek]] and in ponds, and Odonata and Hemiptera only in perched ponds.

**Plant ecology.** Vegetation increased in cover with substrate age and elevation. Eyak Slough's youngest terrain was colonized by four species, mainly *Carex lyngbyei*, while the foreshore levee had continuous cover and an actively developing organic horizon. Species diversity was generally higher at Martin River than at Eyak River. Sphagnum dominated transitional sites.

**Herbivory.** Two moose exclosures (about 8 and 20 years old) were compared with adjacent browsed areas. Total soil N was significantly higher inside the older exclosure than inside the new one, while N-availability was lower outside the older exclosure, suggesting that moose browsing affects soil nitrogen dynamics and increases site-to-site variability. Total soil C averaged 3.5% across samples.

## Key Findings

- **Juvenile salmonid distribution:** Coho salmon were captured in all habitats sampled and comprised more than 90% of fish in all ponds. Forest streams had the highest proportion of fry (22% under 51 mm fork length), which the authors attribute to nearby spawning areas. Elsewhere, fry made up less than 11% of the catch.
- **Juvenile sockeye:** Sockeye were not caught in minnow traps but were captured in fyke nets in intertidal sloughs. Two 12-hour fyke sets on a [[Martin River Slough]] tributary caught 107 juvenile sockeye (mean fork length 65.5 mm) and 114 juvenile coho (mean fork length 70.2 mm), with Dolly Varden also present.
- **Habitat use:** The authors found juvenile salmonids throughout most aquatic habitats. Perched ponds and the glacially turbid main-stem rivers held few fish. Sculpins, which are potential predators of juvenile salmonids, were present in several slough sets.
- **Dissolved organic carbon and nutrients:** Pond habitats retained organic matter and recycled nutrients internally. Streams and sloughs more often exported material downstream.
- **Productivity gradient:** Phytoplankton and macrophyte abundance and diversity were highest in the middle wetlands. Glacially fed and cold, shaded systems had low productivity.
- **Geomorphic hypotheses:** Ponds fragment over time as interbasin channels migrate headward. Large ponds are young and found along active margins, while smaller ponds are older and farther inland. These hypotheses remain untested statistically.
- **Management recommendations:** Future work should address juvenile salmonid habitat use by season, geomorphic and hydrologic controls on channel and pond evolution, and the trophic linkages that limit primary and secondary production. A habitat classification model based on nutrient-recycling capacity was also recommended.
