## Executive Summary

This paper by Charles F. Cole reports a year-round creel census conducted from August 1957 through December 1961 on **Lake Fort Smith**, a 525-acre oligotrophic water supply reservoir in the Boston Mountains of Arkansas. The study was initiated because the lake's fishing reputation had declined, prompting experimental management centered on a **threadfin shad ([[Dorosoma petenense]])** introduction. The census documented a substantial decline in total angler use over the study period (fishing pressure fell from 28.70 to 12.72 man-hours/acre/year), driven primarily by a precipitous collapse of the **white crappie ([[Pomoxis annularis]])** and **bluegill ([[Lepomis macrochirus]])** fisheries. In contrast, the **largemouth bass ([[Micropterus salmoides]])** harvest remained comparatively stable, becoming the dominant species in the catch by the later years. Fishing pressure was strongly seasonal, peaking in spring (March–May) before turbidity from heavy rainfall degraded fishing conditions, with a secondary peak in mid-to-late summer. The study found that fishing effort tracked fishing success (catch per unit effort), consistent with a fishery sustained largely by knowledgeable local anglers rather than transient or tourist fishermen.

## Background & Description

This is a fisheries/creel-census management study rather than a morphological or anatomical investigation; no direct measurements of body form, meristics, or diagnostic anatomical features are reported. However, standard field biometrics were routinely collected:

- Each harvested fish was **weighed, measured, and scale-sampled** as part of the census protocol.
- Harvest data are reported both in **numbers of fish** and **total pounds harvested**, allowing indirect inference of average fish size/condition across species and years (e.g., largemouth bass consistently contributed a disproportionately higher percentage of harvest by weight than by number — e.g., 1960-61: 47.2% by number vs. 65.9% by weight — indicating relatively large average individual size compared to panfish species).
- Thirteen species total were recorded in the creel, spanning three families: [[Centrarchidae]] (basses and sunfishes), [[Ictaluridae]] (bullheads and catfishes), and [[Dorosomatidae]] (shad, via the threadfin shad introduction, though shad themselves were not part of the sport catch).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat/System:**
- [[Lake Fort Smith]] is a 525.5-acre impoundment on Clear Creek (Frog Bayou), a tributary of the [[Arkansas River]], created in 1936 for municipal water supply.
- Classified as **oligotrophic**, with heavily wooded, rocky, steep shorelines and an elongate triangular basin shape bounded by mountainous slopes and an earthen dam.
- The watershed (65 sq. mi. of oak-hickory forest) is prone to producing **dense turbidity following cloudburst rainfall events**, especially in May, which can persist into mid-summer and substantially disrupts fishing conditions and species availability (particularly [[White Crappie]]).
- A second, larger upstream impoundment, **Lake Sheppard Springs** (750 acres, created 1956), is hydrologically linked to Lake Fort Smith; the paper notes that a managed winter drawdown of the upper lake could theoretically buffer turbidity impacts on the lower lake, though this conflicts with the primary water-supply mandate.

**Species interactions and community structure:**
- The catch was numerically and functionally dominated by three species: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[White Crappie]], with secondary contributions from spotted bass ([[Micropterus punctulatus]]), black crappie ([[Pomoxis nigromaculatus]]), warmouth ([[Lepomis gulosus]]), green sunfish ([[Lepomis cyanellus]]), longear sunfish ([[Lepomis megalotis]]), redear sunfish ([[Lepomis microlophus]]), and minor catfish/bullhead species ([[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Black Bullhead]]).
- White crappie exhibited strong seasonal (spring) availability tied to water clarity, becoming largely unavailable to anglers during turbid periods — a direct linkage between abiotic water-quality conditions and catchability/vulnerability to angling.
- The threadfin shad introduction, referenced as the impetus for the study's experimental management framework, presumably functioned as a forage-base manipulation for the piscivorous largemouth bass population, though the paper itself focuses on angler catch data rather than shad population or trophic outcomes directly.

## Key Findings

- **Declining angler use over time:** Total fishing pressure fell from 28.70 man-hours/acre/yr (1957-58) to 12.72 man-hours/acre/yr (1960-61), roughly a 55% decline over four years, alongside declines in fishermen numbers (3,381 → 1,752), trips (1,647 → 773), and total harvest (5,489 → 1,580 fish).
- **Shift in species dominance:** The fishery transitioned from a [[White Crappie]]-dominated catch (43.2% by number in 1957-58) to a [[Largemouth Bass]]-dominated catch (47.2% by number, 65.9% by weight in 1960-61), even as total effort declined.
- **Stable bass harvest despite declining effort:** Largemouth bass harvest numbers remained relatively uniform across years (943, 1,354, 775, 746) compared to sharp declines in white crappie (2,369 → 379) and bluegill (1,564 → 122), suggesting sustained targeted effort by experienced local anglers for bass specifically.
- **Strong seasonality of effort:** Fishing pressure peaked in March–April, dropped sharply in May due to turbidity from heavy runoff, showed a secondary rise in July, and declined again by October; pressure was minimal in December–February.
- **Effort tracks success:** Catch-per-unit-effort (numbers and pounds per angling hour) generally paralleled fishing pressure, supporting the conclusion that this fishery was sustained by a localized, experienced angler base with accurate knowledge of when/where to fish — consistent with the pattern described by [[Churchill and Snow 1964]] in Wisconsin lakes, where ~10% of anglers can account for ≥50% of harvest.
- **Turbidity as primary driver of crappie decline:** The sharp decline in the white crappie fishery was attributed not to population decline but to increased frequency/severity of spring rainfall events causing turbidity that reduced crappie catchability during their normally productive season.
- **Management constraint:** A theoretical winter drawdown of the upstream Lake Sheppard Springs to buffer turbidity in Lake Fort Smith was considered but rejected as incompatible with the primary water-supply function of the reservoir system.
- **Angler behavior hypothesis:** The decline in overall (non-bass) angler interest was attributed to competition from newer, more heavily publicized reservoirs in Arkansas and eastern Oklahoma drawing away transient/occasional anglers, while dedicated local bass anglers continued to use the lake.
