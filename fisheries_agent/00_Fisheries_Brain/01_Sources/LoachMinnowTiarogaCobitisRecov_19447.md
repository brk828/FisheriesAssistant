# Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis) Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) is a small, bottom-dwelling cyprinid listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1986). It is endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] and has been extirpated from most of its historic range. Remaining populations occur in limited reaches of the upper Gila, San Francisco, Tularosa, and Blue river systems and in [[Aravaipa Creek]]. All existing populations are under threat from [[Habitat Degradation]], [[Dams and Impoundments]], water diversion, and [[Non-Native Fishes]].

The plan, prepared by Paul C. Marsh for [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region 2 and approved in 1991, sets the recovery objective as protecting existing populations, restoring depleted and extirpated populations, and achieving a self-sustaining species that can justify delisting. It lists nine action categories. The estimated minimum cost is $115,000 per year (1989 dollars) over at least 20 years, excluding land or water acquisition. Recovery cannot occur in fewer than 20 years, because reintroduced populations require a 10-year evaluation period.

## Background & Description

The loach minnow is the sole member of a monotypic genus, first described by Girard (1857). It was not considered imperiled by [[Miller 1961]] or [[Minckley 1973]], but it is now recognized as biologically imperiled. New Mexico lists it as a group 2 endangered species. Arizona lists it as threatened. Neither state specifically protects its habitat, and collection requires a special permit in both.

**Morphology.** The fish is elongated, slightly compressed, and flattened ventrally. It has eight dorsal fin rays, seven anal fin rays, about 65 lateral line scales, and a small, terminal, oblique mouth without barbels. Its pharyngeal teeth are arranged in two rows with formula 1,4-4,1.

**Coloration.** The body is olivaceous and heavily blotched with darker pigment, with whitish depigmented spots. Breeding males show red-orange coloration on the fins, head, and abdomen. Breeding females become yellowish on the fins and lower body.

**Historical distribution.** The species occurred throughout the Gila system in Arizona, including the Salt, Verde, San Pedro, and Blue rivers and Eagle Creek. In New Mexico it occurred in the Gila (East, Middle, and West forks), San Francisco, Tularosa, and Dry Blue Creek. In Mexico it was recorded only in the Rio San Pedro in northern Sonora. Historical abundance was probably local because of its specialized habitat needs.

**Current distribution.** The species is believed extirpated from Mexico, although surveys there are incomplete. In Arizona it persists in limited reaches of the White River, Aravaipa Creek, San Francisco and Blue rivers, and Campbell Blue Creek. It is rare to uncommon except in Aravaipa Creek and the Blue River drainage. In New Mexico it persists in the upper Gila, San Francisco, Tularosa, lowermost Whitewater Creek, and lowermost Dry Blue Creek. Populations in Whitewater and Dry Blue creeks were small in 1982–1985.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The loach minnow inhabits turbulent, rocky riffles of mainstream rivers and tributaries. A reduced gas bladder restricts it to a bottom-dwelling life. It occupies shallow habitat with moderate to swift current and gravel-to-cobble substrate. In [[Aravaipa Creek]] it is sometimes associated with dense filamentous green algae. Its habitat use varies by life stage, season, and location. Larvae and juveniles use shallower, slower margins than adults.

**Reproduction.** Spawning begins at age I in late winter to early spring in Aravaipa Creek and from late March into early June in New Mexico. Adhesive eggs are laid on the undersides of flattened rocks. Eggs per rock range from fewer than 5 to more than 250, with population means of 52 to 63. Individual fecundity is about 150 to 250 mature ova and increases with body size. Mature ova average about 1.5 mm in diameter. Embryos incubated at 18–20 °C hatch in 5 to 6 days, and larvae are about 5 mm long at hatching.

**Growth and longevity.** Age 0 fish in New Mexico reach 30 to more than 40 mm standard length (SL) by mid-summer and slightly more than 50 mm SL by year's end. Age I fish average near 55 mm SL. Age II fish reach a maximum of about 68 mm SL, although this size is infrequent. Most fish live 15 to 24 months, and exceptional individuals reach 36 months. Males appear to have higher survivorship than females.

**Diet.** The loach minnow is an opportunistic benthic insectivore. Its main prey are larval mayflies (Ephemeroptera), black flies (Simuliidae), and midges (Chironomidae). Stoneflies and caddisflies are seasonally important. Because it rarely swims in turbulent water, it forages on bottom substrates rather than on drifting prey.

**Co-occurring fishes.** Native species include [[Speckled Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Sonoran Sucker]], and [[Longfin Dace]]. Non-native species include [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Flathead Catfish]]. Channel catfish and juvenile flathead catfish feed in riffles on the same prey as loach minnow, and flathead catfish are piscivorous even when small. Red shiner is repeatedly implicated in declines of loach minnow and other natives, although no mechanism has been identified. Mosquitofish is known to harm the native Sonoran topminnow.

**Reasons for decline.** The plan identifies two broad causes, habitat alteration and non-native fishes. Their effects are difficult to separate because they often occurred in the same period. Main habitat threats are:

- **Dewatering** from groundwater pumping, channelization, diversion, and damming.
- **Impoundment**, which creates lentic habitat and alters flow, temperature, chemistry, and flood regimes.
- **Livestock grazing**, which increases turbidity, sedimentation, and channel erosion.
- **Mining, agriculture, timber harvest, and development**, which add contaminants and sediment. Wastewater from the [[Cananea Mine]] in Sonora killed all fishes in a 100-km reach of the San Pedro River during 1977–1979.

Moderate habitat alteration alone does not appear sufficient to eliminate the species. Severe alteration combined with the establishment of non-native fishes does.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objective and criteria.** The plan's primary goal is to protect existing populations, restore depleted and extirpated populations, and ensure a non-endangered, self-sustaining species in perpetuity. Quantitative delisting criteria cannot be set until further research is done. The plan therefore focuses on identifying the mechanisms needed to define them.

**Action outline.** The stepdown plan includes nine major actions:

1. Protect existing populations. This includes identifying populations, designating critical habitat, enforcing laws, ensuring perennial flows, curtailing non-native introductions, and acquiring lands and water rights.
2. Monitor existing populations, using standard sites and methods, a computerized database, and bi-annual spring and autumn population estimates.
3. Determine the nature and significance of interactions with non-native fishes, both direct (predation, displacement) and indirect.
4. Quantify habitat needs. Research should cover substrate, velocity and depth, temperature, water chemistry, watershed size, and their interactions.
5. Enhance or restore habitats of depleted populations.
6. Reintroduce populations into historic range, with monitoring and evaluation five years after each reintroduction.
7. Define quantitative criteria for a self-sustaining population, including minimum stock size, age-class structure, recruitment, and environmental variables.
8. Establish contingency captive propagation, since hatchery programs are not considered necessary at present.
9. Provide information and education to the public, professionals, and Mexican agencies.

**Reintroduction candidates.** [[Eagle Creek]] and the [[San Pedro River]] are considered most amenable to reestablishment. Loach minnow occurred in Eagle Creek as recently as 1950, when 13 individuals were collected, and reasons for its disappearance are unknown. The San Pedro River is the species' type locality. Bonita Creek and perennial reaches of the Redfield Canyon and Babocomari River are also candidates.

**Critical habitat.** Proposed critical habitat (FWS 1985) totals several reaches in Arizona and New Mexico. Major reaches include the Blue River (about 78 km), Gila River (about 37 km in Grant County), Aravaipa Creek (about 24 km), and Tularosa River (about 24 km). Designation was deferred and remained unfinalized at the time of the plan, so the proposal provides only limited protection.

**Implementation schedule.** Tasks are ranked by priority. Priority 1 actions are essential to prevent extinction, Priority 2 actions maintain current status, and Priority 3 actions provide full recovery. Costs include about $7,000 per year for monitoring after reintroduction, $10,000 per year for hatchery holding once stocks are taken, and $1,500 per year for supplying hatchery fish. The plan lists the Fish and Wildlife Service, Arizona and New Mexico game and fish agencies, the USDA Forest Service, and the Bureau of Land Management among responsible agencies.

**Key uncertainties.** Long-term population stability is unknown. Genetic structure is poorly known. Temperature and chemical tolerances are poorly characterized. The mechanisms of interaction with non-native fishes are unresolved. Unsurveyed populations may exist in Mexico and on tribal and national forest lands.
