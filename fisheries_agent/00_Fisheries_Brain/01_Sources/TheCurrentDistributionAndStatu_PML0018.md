# Current Distribution and Status of Virgin Spinedace in the Virgin River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 report, prepared for the [[Washington County Water Conservancy District]] by R. Craig Addley and Thomas B. Hardy of Natural Systems Engineering, documents the present distribution, abundance and status of the [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*), a cyprinid minnow endemic to the [[Virgin River Basin]]. Surveys were conducted from June 1992 to September 1992 at over 500 locations in the main stem and tributaries, using seining and [[Electrofishing]], with [[Depletion Sampling]] used to estimate populations. The species is a federal candidate (Category 2) with no federal protection at the time, although the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service was proposing listing as Threatened, and the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources and the American Fisheries Society classed it as threatened.

Spinedace have disappeared from about 32 percent of their postulated historic habitat in the Virgin River basin and suffered severe depletion in a further ~20 percent. The main drivers are water diversion, reservoirs and non-native fish.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and morphology.** Adults are typically 50 to 120 mm total length, with a broad, flat, silvery body and brassy sheen; mature males show sooty blotches, and breeding adults have reddish-orange fin bases. The name refers to the weakly fused spines of the first two dorsal rays. Diagnostic counts include 8 dorsal rays, 8 to 10 anal rays, 77 to 91 lateral line scales, and pharyngeal teeth in two rows, typically 2,5-4,2. The species is closely related to the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] and [[White River Spinedace]] (both federally listed) and to the [[Big Spring Spinedace]].

**Life history.** Age-0 fish are typically under 55 mm standard length; age-1 fish are 56 to 76 mm; age-2 fish are 76 to 95 mm; and age-3 fish reach about 125 mm. Spawning, cued by temperature (13 to 17 °C) and photoperiod (>13 hours/day), occurs from April to June. Reported fecundity is 459 mature eggs for one-year-old females, 780 for two-year-olds and 693 for three-year-olds (as given in the source). First-year females provide most reproduction, often more than 50 percent of spawners.

**Food and habitat.** Spinedace are primarily insectivorous, feeding continuously during the day on drifting insects at the surface and in mid-water. They prefer clear, cool, relatively swift streams, mostly in pools with cover (boulders, undercut banks), at depths of 10 to 90 cm and velocities of 10 to 100 cm/s over sand and gravel. Upper thermal preferendum is 23 °C, and the species is a thermal specialist.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainage.** The Virgin River basin covers about 5,100 square miles (59 percent Utah, 33 percent Arizona, 8 percent Nevada). The river is about 110 miles long. Nine major tributaries were surveyed: East Fork and North Fork Virgin River, [[North Creek]], [[La Verkin Creek]], [[Ash Creek]], [[Leeds Creek]]/[[Quail Creek]], [[Fort Pearce Wash]], [[Santa Clara River]] and [[Beaver Dam Wash]]. Elevations range from about 10,000 feet in the headwaters to below 1,900 feet at Littlefield, Arizona.

**Climate and hydrology.** Precipitation ranges from 35 inches in the mountains to 6 inches in the lower desert. Base flows of the largest tributaries (North and East Forks) average roughly 40 cfs; most others are about 10 cfs or less and some become intermittent. Floods can exceed several thousand cfs and carry heavy sediment. Diversions for irrigation dominate water use, and many dry-dam streams.

**Water quality.** Total dissolved solids rise downstream and increase sharply near La Verkin, where mineralized hot water enters from [[Pah Tempe Hot Springs]]. Suspended solids are high because of erosion on steep, sparsely vegetated slopes. Several tributary reaches exceed state standards for metals and temperature.

**Fish community.** The basin has six native species, including Virgin spinedace, and about 13 non-native species. Associated natives include speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), desert sucker (*Catostomus clarki*) and flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*). Non-natives of concern include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Red Shiner]].

## Key Findings

**Distribution and abundance**
- Largest populations occur in the upper main stem Virgin River above the Quail Creek diversion and in the Santa Clara River and Beaver Dam Wash drainages.
- Intermediate populations occur in North Creek, the North Fork and East Fork Virgin River.
- Small populations occur in Ash Creek, La Verkin Creek, the lower main stem below Pah Tempe Springs and Coal Pits Wash.
- Spinedace are absent from Leeds/Quail Creek (since 1985 inundation and earlier dewatering), Fort Pearce Wash, Mill Creek, Horse Valley Wash and South Creek.

**Population estimates (1992, as reported)**
- Beaver Dam Wash: 75,613 total (64,163 YOY, 11,450 adults); density at Lytle Ranch about 736/100 m² (upper reach) versus 77/100 m² (lower reach).
- Santa Clara River drainage: 213,350 total (173,845 YOY, 39,505 adults); the upper Gunlock reach (about 753/100 m²) is the densest in the basin.
- Upper Moody Wash: 29,166 total; upper Santa Clara about 1,727 total.
- La Verkin Creek: 1,248 total (about 890 YOY, 359 adults), the second smallest population surveyed.
- Ash Creek lower reach: 8,594 total; the lower Ash Creek reach is the main basin stronghold of adults in that drainage.
- North Creek upper reach: 24,753 total (about 8,664 YOY); the third largest adult population.
- North Fork Virgin River: 21,792 total; East Fork similar in size with a higher adult proportion.
- Upper Virgin River above Quail Creek: about 187,784 estimated fish (population density 46.5/100 m²), the largest adult population in the basin; this figure is an approximation from seine data.
- Shunes Creek: 3,898 total, 98 percent YOY.

**Historic loss**
- About 32 percent of postulated historic habitat has been lost; ~20 percent further has suffered severe depletion.
- Beaver Dam Wash: spinedace now occupy about 11.4 of 24.3 historic miles (~47 percent remaining); extirpated from reaches below Schroeder Reservoir.
- Santa Clara River drainage: spinedace currently occupy 49 percent of historic range; about 19 miles of the lower Santa Clara were lost to dewatering and reservoir inundation.
- Quail/Leeds Creek: extirpated from Quail Creek after reservoir inundation in 1985.

**Limiting factors**
- Natural barriers (waterfalls, steep narrow sections such as The Narrows on Beaver Dam Wash and North Fork) limit upstream distribution in most tributaries.
- Water diversions and reservoirs have caused the largest reduction in numbers and distribution.
- Non-native fish (trout, bass, red shiner) compete with and prey upon spinedace; trout are a presumed cause of upstream exclusion in upper Beaver Dam Wash.
- Thermal stress: La Verkin Creek reached 30 °C or higher in July 1992 (maximum 33 °C), near the species' thermal maxima.

**Management recommendations**
- Maintain or enhance instream flows (for example below [[Gunlock Reservoir]]) to protect populations.
- Remove or limit non-native fish, especially trout in upper Beaver Dam Wash.
- Reintroduce spinedace into historic habitat or into suitable habitat above natural barriers (e.g., above waterfalls in the East Fork Virgin River and in La Verkin Creek).
- Provide fish passage at diversions and maintain monitoring of red shiner expansion in the lower Virgin River and Beaver Dam Wash.
- Eradication of red shiner and other introduced species from the main stem Virgin River is considered the most important action for protecting upstream populations.
