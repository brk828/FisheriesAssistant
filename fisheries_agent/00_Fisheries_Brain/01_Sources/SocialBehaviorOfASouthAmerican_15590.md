# Social Behavior of a South American Leaf Fish, *Polycentrus schomburgkii*, with an Account of Recurring Pseudofemale Behavior

#ichthyology #marine-biology #nandidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory ethology study by [[George W. Barlow]] (University of Illinois, Urbana) describes the social behavior of the South American leaf fish [[Polycentrus Schomburgkii]], focusing on hostile, sexual, and parental behavior. Observations were made on aquarium-held fish over roughly six months, supplemented by strobe-light photography. The paper provides a glossary of hostile motor patterns, an account of spawning in which the female spawns inverted while the male washes sperm up to the eggs, and a detailed description of recurring pseudofemale behavior in males. Barlow also argues that the species' hostile displays are ritualized to an unusual degree, with almost no damaging bites, and that parental males never eat their own young. The work is framed as a contribution to the behavioral study of [[Nandidae]] and to questions of family placement within [[Percoidei]].

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic context:** Nandid fishes (sensu lato) are a small family of primary freshwater fishes with a disjunct distribution across tropical Asia, Africa, and South America. The two South American leaf fishes, *Monocirrhus polyacanthus* and *Polycentrus schomburgkii*, have been placed by some authors (Jordan, 1923) in a separate family, Polycentridae, together with the African *Polycentropsis abbreviata*.
- **Prior knowledge:** Nandid behavior was poorly documented. Aquarist literature described these fish as lurking, aggressive predators with parental care, and most species had rarely been bred. Comparative notes were given for [[Badis badis]], whose courtship and parental behavior differ from *Polycentrus* and which the author links to labyrinth fishes.
- **Study animals:** Most observations used 12 animals and their offspring obtained from a Munich aquarium shop, bred in that city. Males reached about 100 mm standard length; females of the same age were about 50% of male length. Further observations used eight fish obtained in Chicago.
- **Husbandry:** Fish were kept in 60-liter aquaria with fine gravel and dense planting, circulated water (hardness about 70-100 ppm CaCO3, pH about 6.5-7.0, 26-28 °C), and a single 25-watt fluorescent tube with 12-14 hours of daylight. Diet was live prey only (amphipods, insect larvae, *Daphnia*, tubificid worms, earthworms, small fishes), as the fish feed only on moving animals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Colour phases:** Five general patterns were recognised: banded juvenile (four to six body bands), nonbreeding brown adult, territorial black male, pale spawning female, and dark parental male. Colour phase numbering follows [[Beebe]] (1945).
- **Territoriality:** Reproductively mature males hold territories centred on a lair, often no more than 20-30 cm in diameter in aquaria. Lairs included flowerpots, opaque plastic tents, and piled rocks; [[Myers]] (1953) reported use of the underside of a broad leaf. Spawning occurred whether lairs were near the surface or on the bottom.
- **Spawning substrate and timing:** Spawning under aquarium conditions was tied to time of day in one group. Munich fish spawned invariably one to three hours before night, whereas Chicago fish spawned at almost any time of day.
- **Predation and prey:** Adults fed on small moving animals and removed snails (*Planorbis*) from the lair, but did not eat their own free-swimming young.
- **Comparative behavior:** The author notes that *Polycentropsis abbreviata* builds a surface bubble nest, in contrast to the hole and underside-of-leaf spawning of the South American species.

## Key Findings

- **Hostile behavior is highly ritualized.** Barlow describes a glossary of motor patterns (Frontal Approach, Head-jerk, Lateral Spread, Tail-beat, Charge, Butt, Champ, Bite). Despite frequent displays, real biting was rare: the author saw only a few bites in all observations, and only one male was damaged by another. He describes this as nearly complete elimination of damaging attack.
- **Mouth morphology.** The large mouth looks ominous but functions as a net for small prey. One male was observed with a dislocated mouth that could not be retracted for hours after a missed strike.
- **Cichlid comparison.** Two *Polycentrus* housed with two slightly smaller *Cichlasoma beani* were near death within a day, with fins torn and bodies wounded, while the cichlids were unscathed.
- **Sexual behavior of the male.** Males approach, Butt, Mouth-touch, and Twitch, and in one case a male held a female's anal fin in his mouth.
- **Inverted spawning.** The female capsizes and skims the ceiling of the lair, depositing eggs, while the male remains below in an upright position, washing sperm up to the clutch with fin Twitching. Barlow notes that Myers (1953) reported essentially the same pattern, and that the cichlid [[Apistogramma trifasciatum]] shows convergent behavior when a larger male fertilises eggs in a small lair.
- **Pseudofemale behavior.** Subordinate males take on female coloration and ovipositor-like posture, approach spawning pairs, and occasionally skim against lair walls. In one case a pseudofemale entered a flowerpot, remained between the resident male and the spawning female, and became more masculine (Twitching) when the resident male left. Pseudofemales never achieved full female behavior (complete Capsizing and Quivering). Barlow contrasts this with [[Morris]]'s (1952, 1954) view that pseudofemales are truly behaving as females, and suggests they are sexually aroused but frustrated and may be attempting to fertilise.
- **Dominance and role switching.** When several males share a tank, dominant males eventually relinquish territories, and former pseudofemales become dominant while former dominants adopt pseudofemale behavior, so all males can express either role.
- **Parental care.** Eggs hatch around Day 3 and larvae leave the lair around Day 6. Fanning is infrequent and brief, with bouts usually under three minutes per hour (one bout lasted 65 seconds). Mean fanning tempo was 4.2 beats/sec, measured on Days 2 and 3 in one male, and the author calls this a first approximation. Males remove snails but seldom retrieve progeny; one male ate eggs that fell to the bottom.
- **No cannibalism of young.** No adult, male or female, was seen to eat free-swimming conspecific young, even though adults readily ate young cichlids placed in the same tank. Barlow attributes this to natural selection against conspecific cannibalism in a highly specialised predator.
- **Systematic implications.** Barlow concludes that the study offers little direct evidence for the systematic placement of *Polycentrus*, because the behavior of other nandids is largely unknown. Its behavior resembles that of *Monocirrhus*, consistent with Jordan's (1923) Polycentridae, but it differs from *Polycentropsis* and *Badis*. The author calls for further comparative work on nandid behavior.
