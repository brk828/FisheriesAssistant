# Proposal for Continued Support of NSF Research Grant G13261: A Cytotaxonomic Study of the Characeae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Characeae #aquatic-plants #cytotaxonomy

## Executive Summary

This is a research proposal submitted by Dr. Arland T. Hotchkiss, Associate Professor of Biology at the [[University of Louisville]], requesting continued NSF support for a three-year cytotaxonomic study of the [[Characeae]] (charophytes). The proposal seeks funding from September 1, 1961 to September 1, 1964. Charophytes are simple aquatic plants, worldwide in distribution, usually in freshwater, agreeing with green algae in pigmentation but placed in a separate class [[Charophyceae]] due to their distinctive complexity. The project aims to establish chromosome numbers (karyotypes) across genera and species, investigate polyploidy patterns, and use cytological data to resolve taxonomic questions. Progress to date includes chromosome counts from 1957-1961 covering 21 species across genera [[Chara]], [[Nitella]], [[Tolypella]], [[Protochara]], and [[Lamprothamnium]], revealing apparent polyploid series (14:28 in Chara, 6:12:18 in Nitella). The proposal requests $32,220 total over three years for salaries, travel (including expeditions to northern Mexico), equipment, and publication costs.

## Background & Description

The [[Characeae]] are simple aquatic plants with worldwide distribution, primarily in freshwater. They share pigmentation, food reserves, and cell morphology with green algae but are distinguished by distinctive stem node and internode organization, whorled branchlets, and elaborate gametangial structures. Taxonomically, they are placed either in a separate class [[Charophyceae]] of the [[Chlorophyta]] (following G.M. Smith) or in a separate Division [[Charophyta]] (following Groves and Bullock-Webster, Zaneveld, and Sachs).

Within the charophytes, there is a single Order [[Charales]] with one extant family, [[Characeae]], subdivided into two tribes: [[Chareae]] and [[Nitelleae]]. The Tribe [[Nitelleae]] contains two genera: [[Nitella]] (153 species, the largest genus) and [[Tolypella]] (13 species). The [[Nitelleae]] are entirely ecorticate with two tiers of 5 crown cells surmounting the oogonium. In [[Nitella]], branchlets are forked, stem branches two per node, oospore laterally compressed, and antheridium terminal in branchlet furcation. In [[Tolypella]], branchlets are monopodial, stem branches more than two per node, oospore terete in section, and antheridium lateral.

The Tribe [[Chareae]] contains four or five genera, of which [[Chara]] (116 species) is the largest. The [[Chareae]] are mostly corticated but a few are entirely ecorticate. The oogonium is capped by a single tier of 5 crown cells, and branchlets are monopodial and never forked. Important specific characters in [[Chara]] include degree of cortication, number of whorls of stipulodes, arrangement of gametangia on branchlets, spore characters, and monoecious versus dioecious species.

Fossil forms of charophytes are well known in paleobotany. Several genera are known from the Devonian, [[Paleochara]] with six spiral cells from the Carboniferous, and entire plants of the Jurassic genus [[Clavator]] described by Harris from England. Gyrogonites from the late Triassic to recent times are well documented.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The proposal emphasizes field collections from diverse geographic regions including eastern North America (New York, Indiana, Kentucky, Rhode Island, Connecticut, Michigan, Quebec, Ontario), northern Mexico, Australia (Western Australia, Tasmania, New South Wales), New Zealand, and California. Collections span various freshwater habitats including lakes ([[Lake Wawasee]], [[Lake James]], [[Keuka Lake]], [[Cayuta Lake]]), ponds (Jenkins Pond near Cayutaville, New York; sinkhole ponds near [[Doe Run]], Kentucky), rivers ([[Ottawa River]], Quebec), and streams.

A particularly notable ecological study site is Jenkins Pond near Cayutaville, New York, constructed in 1947-1948 to a depth of nine feet. This pond contains spatially separated populations of [[Chara contraria]] and [[Chara vulgaris]], apparently segregated by microhabitat conditions formed by a cold spring. [[Chara vulgaris]] (N=14 chromosomes) occupies shallow water (up to 1 ft depth) near the spring where temperature is 10°F cooler than other surface waters. [[Chara contraria]] (N=28 chromosomes) grows in deeper water (2-3 feet) at distance from the spring. This natural ecological control provides an ideal setting for comparative study of the two species.

A large and constant population of [[Nitella flexilis]] is available in [[Doe Run]], [[Meade County]], Kentucky, with stable aquatic conditions including constant water temperature (56°F), making it suitable for studying seasonal variations and developmental stages under natural conditions.

The proposal notes that [[Chara contraria]] from the [[Fort Knox]] area shows cytological radiomimetic effects with aberrant chromosome numbers ranging from rare 14s up to 42s, alongside normal filaments with expected 28 chromosomes. This population inhabits a small lake treated annually with sodium arsenate for aquatic vegetation control, suggesting environmental chemical influences on cytology.

**Polyploidy Patterns:**

The data reveal apparent polyploid series:
1. A 14:28 series in genus [[Chara]] correlated with sex organ distribution (dioecious vs. monoecious)
2. A 6:12:18 series in the [[Nitelleae]]
3. Possible 9:18 series or indication that entire group is based on a 3 multiple
4. Hypothetical base numbers of 7 for [[Chara]] and 6 for [[Nitella]]

**Correlation Between Ploidy and Sex:**

In corticate [[Chara]] species (with one exception), haploid species (N=14) are dioecious or disjunct, whereas diploid species (N=28) are invariably monoecious. This positive correlation indicates a common mechanism controlling sex expression that produces different effects concomitant with ploidy level changes.

In ecorticate [[Chara]] species (including [[Chara braunii]], [[Chara australis]], [[Chara brittonii]]), all species are invariably haploid (N=14) whether dioecious or monoecious, indicating no ploidy beyond this level. The monoecious condition evolved without benefit of ploidy, suggesting a different mechanism controlling sex expression than in corticate species.

**X-Radiation Studies:**

Preliminary radiation experiments on [[Nitella flexilis]] using 250 kilovolt X-rays at Fort Knox Radiation Laboratory established radiation tolerance limits. A total of 17 experiments were conducted on [[Nitella flexilis]] and 2 on [[Nitella opaca]]. Dosages of 5000 roentgens produced complete cell destruction, with material surviving up to nine days showing steady cell death. Lower dosages (1/2 and 1/4 strength) showed beginning recovery after two days with lagging chromosomes, metaphase clusters, anaphase bridges, and occasional successful telophases. At 1/8 strength dosage, cells remained resting for three days followed by slow recovery over six days, with divisions showing aberrations and anaphase bridges. By the sixth day, several cells showed interesting pairing of like chromosomes and beginning separation into two like masses.

**Endopolyploidy:**

A striking example of endopolyploidy was found in [[Chara leptopitys]] (Wood collection 60-10-11-1). Fertile branchlets arranged in whorls in loose heads contained antheridia mostly of the same size, but some strikingly larger ones. The number 14 was found constantly in most antheridia, but in larger ones (differentiating toward maturity), mixed with filaments containing mature sperm were filaments of dividing cells with 35, 42, etc., in paired and connected groups of 3-4 chromosomes.

**Taxonomic Implications:**

The [[Chara contraria]]-[[Chara vulgaris]] problem: [[Chara contraria]] consistently shows N=28 chromosomes while [[Chara vulgaris]] shows N=14. Jenkins Pond, New York, provides a natural ecological control where both species grow in separated populations with distinct morphological and cytological differences, supporting their recognition as separate species rather than a single variable species as proposed by Wood and Muenscher (1956).

The [[Chara braunii]] question: Gillet (1959) reported N=12 for [[Charopsis braunii]] (=[[Chara braunii]]) from European material and proposed removing it from tribe [[Chareae]] to place it near [[Nitella]] in the [[Nitelleae]]. However, the proposal's consistent counts of N=14 from American materials (agreeing with Karling's 1928 European count) contradict this. The cytological evidence (all ecorticate species being haploid N=14 regardless of monoecious/dioecious condition) supports separating ecorticate [[Chara]] species (including [[Chara braunii]], [[Chara australis]], [[Chara brittonii]]) as a group generically distinct from corticate [[Chara]], but not placing them with [[Nitelleae]].

**Proposed Research Objectives (1961-1964):**

1. Continue cytotaxonomic studies of [[Characeae]] with extended geographic coverage (Mexico, Australasia, other new areas)
2. Analyze presumed polyploids through radiation treatments, chemical treatments, and elucidation of meiotic divisions
3. Attempt synthesis of polyploid chromosome complements through chemical treatments and breeding experiments
4. Conduct life cycle studies correlating changes in form with seasonal and environmental factors
5. Special study of [[Nitella flexilis]] biology, ecology, and entire life cycle as a primitive member of the genus
6. Intensive study of Characeous flora of northern Mexico and adjacent southwestern United States
