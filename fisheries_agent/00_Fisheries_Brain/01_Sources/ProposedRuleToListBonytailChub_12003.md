# Proposed Rule to List Bonytail Chub as Endangered and Razorback Sucker as Threatened

## Executive Summary

This 1978 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Federal Register notice proposes listing the **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) as **Endangered** and the **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) as **Threatened** under the Endangered Species Act of 1973. Both species are endemic to the **[[Colorado River Basin]]**, spanning California, Nevada, Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado, and Utah. The proposed listings stem primarily from severe habitat alteration and destruction caused by damming, impoundment, and irrigation diversion, compounded by predation and competition from introduced fish species. The rule outlines Section 9(a)(1) take prohibitions, with a special exception allowing regulated sport fishing of the razorback sucker under state law.

## Background & Description

### Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*)
- Member of the genus *[[Gila]]* within minnow family Cyprinidae.
- Adapted to large, turbid, fast-flowing riverine systems.
- Spawning requires warm water temperatures (~65°F).
- Historically thrived in swift-current eddies within mainstream rivers.

### Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- Distinguished by a **pronounced, bony keel** on its back immediately behind the head — the source of its common name.
- Bottom-feeding morphology suited for detritus and possibly plankton consumption.
- Prefers slow backwater habitats within large river systems.
- Estimated **maximum lifespan of ~30 years**.
- Frequently confused with the more abundant **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** (*Catostomus latipinnis*) at juvenile/subadult stages, complicating field identification and recruitment surveys.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Bonytail Chub
- Historic range: large turbid mainstream rivers alternating between torrential rapid canyons and slow, meandering sand-bottomed stretches.
- Microhabitat preference: eddies adjacent to swift current.
- **Population status**: Nearly extinct in the lower Colorado basin; dramatic decline in the upper basin over the preceding 10–20 years.
- Notable decline documented in the **[[Green River]]** within **[[Dinosaur National Monument]]** following the 1962 closure of **[[Flaming Gorge Dam]]**; reservoir stabilization near capacity by 1966 correlated with subsequent lack of reproduction (no young found in 10+ years).
- Most recent (1974) adult collection recorded from **[[Desolation Canyon]]**, Green River, Utah.
- Adults persist in **[[Lake Mead]]** and **[[Lake Mohave]]**, with observed spawning but **no recruitment of young fish**, suggesting eventual senescence-driven population collapse in these reservoirs.
- Cold tailwaters below high dams do not provide the warm temperatures needed for spawning, rendering this artificial habitat largely unused.

### Razorback Sucker
- Historic range: entire Colorado River Basin large-river habitat.
- Feeds on bottom detritus and possibly plankton in slow backwater areas.
- Historically abundant enough to be harvested and sold as food by early settlers.
- Large adult populations persist in reservoirs (**[[Lake Mead]]**, **[[Lake Mohave]]**, **[[Lake Havasu]]**) for approximately 30 years post-dam-closure before disappearing; some recruitment evidence exists, but insufficiency for long-term population maintenance is suspected.
- Extirpated from the **[[Grand Canyon]]** segment of the Colorado River, likely due to cold tailwater conditions below high dams.
- In the upper basin, congregates in backwater areas producing a misleading appearance of abundance; actual numbers are considered much lower than historical baselines.
- Evidence of spawning (ripe/spawned fish) documented at the mouth of the **[[Yampa River]]** (1975–1976) and in a gravel pit near **[[Grand Junction, Colorado]]** (1975).

## Key Findings

1. **Primary threat**: Habitat destruction/modification via mainstream and tributary dam construction, impoundment, and irrigation diversion — converting free-flowing river habitat into reservoirs and cold tailwaters unsuitable for spawning.
2. **Reservoir population dynamics**: Both species show a pattern of large adult populations persisting for a period after dam closure (with observed spawning) but with **no or insufficient juvenile recruitment**, implying eventual population collapse as adults senesce.
3. **Predation pressure**: Introduced species — particularly **[[Red Shiner]]** (*Notropis lutrensis*) and **[[Redside Shiner]]** (*Richardsonius balteatus*) — are implicated as significant predators of larval bonytail chub and razorback sucker, though the extent is not precisely quantified.
4. **Competitive displacement**: Introduced bass, sunfish, and catfish contribute to decline; exotic species now **outnumber native species** in the Colorado River basin. A documented case in the lower **[[Black River]]** (Arizona) showed introduced bass virtually eliminating native fishes, including two sucker species, within two years of introduction.
5. **Regulatory mechanism**: The proposed rule invokes Section 9(a)(1) of the Endangered Species Act (via 50 CFR 17.21) for the bonytail chub, prohibiting take, import/export, and interstate commercial sale. A special rule (50 CFR 17.31(c)) for the razorback sucker permits continued sport fishing under state law, despite otherwise equivalent prohibitions to Endangered status.
6. **No Critical Habitat proposed** at time of this rule, though Section 7 interagency consultation requirements apply regardless.
7. **Data gaps identified**: The rule explicitly solicits additional biological data on threats, range/distribution, and habitat designation, reflecting acknowledged uncertainty in upper-basin decline causes and current reproductive/recruitment status of both species.
