# Rainbow Trout in Mexico and California: Meristic and Linear Variation Across 17 Population Samples

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This University of California Publications in Zoology monograph (Needham and Gard) analyzes variation in 306 rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdnerii*) from 17 population samples, ranging from Eagle Lake in northeastern California to headwater streams near Ciudad Durango, Mexico. Fourteen of the 17 samples come from Mexican waters, many of them previously uncollected or undescribed. The authors analyze 40 characters (12 meristic, 28 linear) statistically and graphically, and describe a previously unnamed [[Mexican Golden Trout]] from the Verde, Sinaloa and Culiacán basins. They conclude that the observed differences are largely environmentally induced and recommend provisionally grouping all rainbow forms, including [[Cutthroat Trout]] forms, under a single species, *Salmo gairdnerii*, without formal subspecies.

## Background & Description

The rainbow–steelhead–cutthroat complex has long confused taxonomists because of extreme variability in color, spotting, body form and meristic traits. Earlier descriptions often rested on one or a few specimens, and taxonomic treatments were subjective. Nominal species counts range from "over 15" (Jordan, Evermann and Clark, 1930) to 33 when cutthroat forms are included (Miller, 1950).

Key background points:

- **Nomenclature:** [[Richardson]] described *S. gairdnerii* in 1836 from the Columbia River; Gibbons described *S. iridia* (*irideus*) in 1855 from San Leandro Creek. The authors retain *gairdnerii* by priority and restrict "steelhead" to sea-run rainbows.
- **Mexican records:** *Salmo nelsoni* (the Santo Domingo or Baja California rainbow) was described by Evermann (1908) from specimens collected by E. W. Nelson in 1905. Earlier reports of trout in Chihuahua and Durango (Cope, Meek, Evermann) were largely unverified or unpublished.
- **Introductions:** Hatchery stocking, including Shasta/McCloud River fish from 1874, spread rainbows worldwide. Introduced rainbows were especially successful in Australia, New Zealand, Chile and Peru.
- **Collections:** Trips in 1936, 1937, 1938, 1952, 1953, 1955 and 1956 produced the samples analyzed. Table 2 summarizes all known Mexican collections.
- **Sample composition:** 17 populations (306 specimens) were analyzed; the largest were San Pablo Creek (30 specimens) and Hondo (30 specimens), and the smallest the two Gavilán specimens. Most fish were small, immature, and still bearing parr marks; Eagle Lake fish (424–513 mm) were the exception.

Table 1 lists the samples and museum catalog numbers, with the California Academy of Sciences and University of Michigan Museum of Zoology as the main repositories.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hatchery contamination:** The authors judged that genetic dilution of native stocks was minimal in most samples, since one- to three-inch hatchery fish survive poorly. However, extensive hybridization and replacement of native trout have occurred in the Sierra Nevada and Great Basin, e.g. the Lahontan cutthroat at Sagehen Creek.
- **Temperature:** Water temperatures recorded at collection ranged 57–76 °F. Single morning or afternoon readings are of limited value, though the authors note that Mexican streams in the Verde–Sinaloa–Culiacán system were generally shaded, forested and at moderately high elevations (about 5,000–7,000 ft).
- **Habitat threats:** Mexican streams were often turbid from crop cultivation, grazing, road construction and timber removal. The Black Canyon watershed was badly eroded, and a Pacheco Creek site was polluted by sawdust.
- **Protected refuge:** The San Pablo Creek west fork is closed to angling and lies within EBMUD watershed land, which helps keep the population largely unaltered.
- **Migration barriers:** Impassable falls and high-elevation headwaters isolate many resident populations. The authors argue this isolation reduces gene flow and shapes local character expression.
- **Ecological examples:** The Eagle Lake form, a lacustrine population spawning in Pine Creek, shows adaptations for lake life (small orbits, high gill raker counts, short fins). The authors interpret the high gill raker count as an aid to plankton feeding.

## Key Findings

**Meristic characters**

- **Vertebrae:** Means ranged from 56.9 (Verde '52) to 64.4 (Tabacatiado), a difference of 7.5 vertebrae. The overall range was 55 to 66. The authors judge these differences larger than any produced experimentally by temperature shock.
- **Scales:** The lowest lateral line and above-lateral-line means occur in the Sinaloa fish. Eagle Lake fish have the highest lateral line mean (122.0), and Gila trout the highest above-lateral-line mean (31.8).
- **Branchiostegal rays:** Verde-Sinaloa-Culiacán fish are set apart (Verde '52 mean 8.9); Eagle Lake is highest at 11.6.
- **Gill rakers:** Casas Grandes lowest (mean 17.2); Eagle Lake highest (mean 20.1), possibly a plankton-feeding adaptation.
- **Fin rays:** Pectoral, dorsal, pelvic, anal and caudal counts show little variability; the Sinaloa fish have the lowest pectoral and anal means.

**Linear characters**

- Twenty of 28 linear characters show no definitive pattern.
- The best linear diagnostic characters were least interorbital width, head length, occiput to snout tip, and adipose fin length.
- Eagle Lake fish have the shortest heads and smallest orbits (orbit 49–53 thousandths of standard length) but the widest interorbital distance.
- Diamond Creek (Gila trout) fish have the longest adipose fins (mean 108, range 98–117) and the largest orbits (mean 94).

**Taxonomic conclusions**

- The authors place all forms in *Salmo gairdnerii* and do not recognize subspecies, arguing that many characters are environmentally modified.
- The [[Mexican Golden Trout]] (Verde, Sinaloa and Culiacán basins) is described for the first time but left unnamed. Its vertebral means (56.9–58.4) are the lowest of the 17 samples, which the authors attribute to warm embryonic temperatures.
- [[Eagle Lake Rainbow Trout]] (*S. aquilarum*) is not recognizable as a subspecies. Its distinguishing traits include a small orbit, short fins and high gill raker counts.
- [[Gila Trout]] (*S. gilae*) was retained within the rainbow series, though it stands apart in several characters.
- Coefficients of difference (C.D.) above 1.28 are common, but the authors choose not to use them as a basis for trinomials.

**Cutthroat and hybridization**

- Natural rainbow–cutthroat hybrids are scarce due to spawning-site and timing differences. Hatchery crosses produced fertile F1 and F2 hybrids with high mortality.
- The [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. clarkii henshawi*) has been nearly extirpated from Pyramid Lake and Sagehen Creek, and is being restored in Walker Lake by the Nevada Fish and Game Commission.
- Basibranchial teeth, a key cutthroat character, appeared in one Santo Domingo specimen, which the authors could not explain.

**Hatchery effects**

- Santo Domingo fish reared 2.5 years in hatchery conditions differed from wild streamside fish. Pectoral fin lengths (159 vs 186 thousandths) and orbit lengths (68 vs 79 thousandths) differed, with C.D. of 1.09 and 0.91.
- Miller's hatchery Gila trout had orbit lengths of 55–66 thousandths, versus 86–101 in wild Diamond Creek fish.

**Migration and origin**

- Several marking experiments showed "resident" rainbows migrating to sea and returning as steelhead (e.g. a fish that traveled about 100 miles down the Umpqua and about 300 miles southward).
- Santo Domingo rainbows are considered probably derived from steelhead in comparatively recent times and are "landlocked" except in high-flow years.
- The authors favor a Pleistocene southward dispersal for Mexican rainbows.

**Conservation and management**

- The Rio Truchas stock (about 710 fish) was introduced into the United States in 1956. The authors later noted in a footnote that all of these fish were lost after introduction.
- The authors recommend that future taxonomic work emphasize vertebrae, lateral line and above-lateral-line scale counts, branchiostegal rays, and pectoral and pelvic ray counts, plus least interorbital width, head length, occiput–snout distance and adipose fin length.
