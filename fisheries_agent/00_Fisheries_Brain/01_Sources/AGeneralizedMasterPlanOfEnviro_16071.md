# Generalized Master Plan of Environmental Studies on the Lower Colorado River, Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #lower-colorado-river-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This 1972 document is a transmittal letter, a generalized master plan, and an attached prospectus from the [[Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory]] at [[Arizona State University]] (ASU). It proposes a multi-institution coalition to build a [[Strategic Environmental Assessment System]] for the lower Colorado River mainstream and its tributaries. The plan rests on three components: basic surveys, [[Impact Studies]], and long-term surveillance. It also sets out a research prospectus for the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP), with a personnel plan, a multi-year budget, and descriptions of university facilities and computing. The main text is ecological and planning in scope. Fish appear mainly as reservoir and fisheries concerns, not as a species-level study.

## Background & Description

The letter of 5 December 1972 was sent to the Environmental Protection Agency (R and D Procurement Section) in response to a Commerce Business Daily request (PSA-5698, 15 November 1972). It was signed by W. L. Minckley and Duncan T. Patten. The prospectus was first submitted to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] in Boulder City, Nevada, and was included to show how the group approaches large-scale studies. The study area is the lower Colorado mainstream, from the compact point near Lee's Ferry to the Sea of Cortez. The Salton Sea and northwestern Mexico are acknowledged as outside the defined area.

The authors describe the lower basin as the most controlled and heavily used river system in North America. Control measures were added in steps: [[Hoover Dam]], then downstream dams and diversions, then [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and Flaming Gorge upstream. Salt River control moved from Roosevelt Dam to later dams and pumped-storage operation. The authors argue that this stepwise control gives ecologists a natural set of pre- and post-modification comparisons.

The coalition has about 50 professionals at eight universities and agencies, plus at least twice that many graduate students and technicians. Participating institutions are the University of Nevada, Las Vegas (with the Desert Research Institute), Prescott College, the Museum of Northern Arizona, Northern Arizona University, New Mexico State University, the University of Arizona, and ASU. The coalition works through individual scientists with administrative backing rather than through a formal administrative coalition. The letter says the authors judged that administrative coalitions produce university competition that lowers productivity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality and salinity.** Roughly three-fourths of the total dissolved solids reaching Yuma enter through Glen Canyon Dam. Hot, dry conditions and evaporation concentrate them further, and saline springs and municipal, irrigation and industrial backflows add more. Water arrives at [[Imperial Dam]] in marginal condition for users.

**Demand.** Power use was estimated to double every 10 years. Domestic and industrial (including irrigation) demand is rising and beginning to rival domestic demand. Water-based and land-based recreation is growing faster than the population.

**Reservoir dynamics.** Several linked processes are described:
- Density currents in reservoirs depend on flow volume and generator releases. High discharge produces a different biological and hydrological response than low release.
- Flow-through reduces heat budgets, which may shift warm-adapted fish fauna toward cold-adapted species below thermoclines. The authors expect this to be unlikely in [[Lake Mead]] but possible in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]].
- Desert reservoir productivity depends on the intensity and frequency of rainfall and flooding. Two consecutive high-runoff years yield little organic input, while drought followed by a flood can drive productivity high enough to approach eutrophication.

**Desert variability.** The authors stress that variability is the rule. Models built elsewhere, or data from non-desert reservoirs, are considered invalid. Because of ongoing modifications, the authors say only a "quasi-baseline" can be established.

**Biotic change.** Stabilized water tables and reservoirs are expected to develop new riparian and aquatic vegetation. That would support new vertebrate and invertebrate assemblages. Aqueducts could act as corridors connecting [[Mojave Desert]] and [[Chihuahuan Desert]] assemblages with the [[Sonoran Desert]]. Fluctuating reservoirs, by contrast, support little riparian or aquatic plant life.

**Specific concerns.** The text names the introduced Asiatic clam [[Corbicula]] in Salt River canals, aquatic vegetation growth, and sedimentation. It also identifies entrainment of aquatic organisms at intake facilities and dissolved oxygen problems at [[San Carlos Reservoir]], where low oxygen and high hydrogen sulfide occur at certain times of year.

## Key Findings

**Recommended research structure.** Three areas are proposed:
1. **Basic surveys**, including centralized compilation of all published and unpublished literature and field biological surveys, especially where data are scarce or changes are expected.
2. **Impact studies** in areas of imminent change, such as [[Lake Havasu]] intake stabilization for the CAP.
3. **Surveillance** at control and to-be-modified sites, at least monthly, to establish quasi-baseline information. This is described under [[Quasi-Baseline Monitoring]].

**Methods.** The plan relies on historical [[USGS Water Quality Records]], which begin before major control structures were built. The authors also propose the following:
- Historical simulation of pristine conditions using correlations such as silt load and nitrate-nitrogen.
- Trapping records and historical photographs as sources of animal and vegetation change data.
- Remote sensing, including USGS "Arizona test area" overflights and [[EROS-1 Satellite]] imagery roughly every 18 days.
- Time-lapse photography, transects across lakes and streams, and cluster and non-parametric statistics to reduce unnecessary sampling.

**Staffing and training.** Undergraduate and advanced-class students provide much field labor. The authors say 10 to 20 students can be placed on a project, and they are paid mainly in travel and per diem during the school year.

**CAP research prospectus.** The prospectus requires at least 18 months of site data before construction, with permanent, protected control areas established early. The authors say 18 months is minimal for credibility. Lake Havasu lead-time information covers six years. Roosevelt Lake has been studied intensively for six years. The construction sequence considered most probable is:
1. Intake and pumping plant on the Bill Williams Arm of Lake Havasu.
2. Granite Reef Aqueduct.
3. Salt-Gila Aqueduct.
4. Buttes Reservoir.
5. Orme Reservoir.
6. Charleston Reservoir and San Pedro Aqueduct.
7. Hooker Reservoir and its aqueduct structures.

Full-scale operational impact (Section 4) is described as essentially beyond the funding and personnel given, although the authors call it imperative.

**Personnel plan (minimum ranges).** Research associates (post-doctoral) 6 to 8; graduate research assistants 15 to 20; computer programmers 1 to 2; secretaries and key-punch operators 2 to 3; undergraduate technicians 5 to 10. Work-study funds may cover part of the hourly wages.

**Budget (average per year, by 3-to-4-year period).** Figures are as reported in the source table:
- Operations: $300,000 to $500,000, with University overhead of $30,000 to $45,000 included in parentheses.
- Travel and per diem: $50,000 to $100,000.
- Salaries and wages (including fringe benefits): $200,000 to $300,000.
- Capital equipment: about $20,000 to $60,000. The OCR table alignment makes the first-period value uncertain.

Overhead rates are 57.3% of on-campus and 30.98% of off-campus salaries and wages. Mileage is paid at 10¢ per mile for University sedans and 15¢ for larger vehicles. Per diem is capped at $20 per day in-state. Budgets may vary 10 to 15% per year.

**Facilities.** The laboratory occupied about 1,000 square feet in centrally located buildings. It was to be consolidated in Krause Hall, which has over 5,000 square feet of usable floor space. Campus computing is planned around a single large system. The Univac 1110 was the reference configuration for the proposed computer. Remote teletype ties between computers are proposed, including the Univac, the University of Arizona's CDC-6400, and Bureau of Reclamation data.

**Reference data.** Hayden Library held 1,244,160 volumes as of 30 June 1971. ASU graduate enrollment was 9,818, with 30,319 total undergraduates (26,500 full-time).

**Summary of plan.** The authors conclude that implementation depends on free access to operational data from controlled habitats and on rapid inter-agency cooperation. They propose centralized data storage, computer retrieval, and interagency connection by remote terminals.
