## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary scientific paper but a **legal declaration** submitted by Dr. Wendell L. Minckley, a prominent desert fish biologist, in litigation challenging the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's handling of conservation planning for the [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*). Dr. Minckley, testifying from decades of expertise in [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes, criticizes the exclusionary process of the "Virgin Spinedace Conservation Coordinating Team" and the inadequacy of its draft Conservation Agreement. While the source contains minimal direct morphological or quantitative ecological data on the species itself, it provides valuable expert-derived context on the species' conservation status, habitat threats, and the political/scientific dynamics surrounding its (non-)listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: The [[Virgin Spinedace]] inhabits the **Virgin River Basin**, spanning Utah, Nevada, and Arizona, within the larger [[Colorado River Basin]] drainage system.
- **Primary Threats Identified**:
  - **Habitat destruction from water development** — cited as one of the two most serious threats to the species.
  - **Competition and predation by non-native sport fish** — cited as the second major threat, reflecting a common pattern in [[Desert Fish Conservation]] where introduced game species (e.g., trout, bass) displace or prey upon native minnows.
- **Geographic Scope Gaps**: The draft Conservation Agreement reviewed by Dr. Minckley reportedly failed to address populations of the spinedace in **Nevada and Arizona**, focusing narrowly on a subset of its range (implicitly Utah).
- **Habitat Flow Requirements**: The declaration notes the Conservation Agreement lacked any **minimum streamflow provisions**, a critical omission for a desert stream-dwelling cyprinid whose survival is tightly linked to hydrological regime (flow volume, timing, and stability).
- **Co-occurring Species**: The Agreement also failed to address other listed species sharing the same habitat, implying the Virgin River ecosystem supports a broader assemblage of at-risk aquatic taxa beyond the spinedace alone.

## Key Findings

1. **Expert Exclusion from Process**: Dr. Minckley, despite extensive relevant expertise (fishes of the Colorado River Basin since 1963, specific experience with the Virgin River Basin and [[Virgin Spinedace]] habitat), was not meaningfully included in the Virgin Spinedace Conservation Coordinating Team's deliberations — receiving meeting notifications only days in advance, insufficient for travel/schedule arrangements, with no expense reimbursement offered.

2. **Inadequacy of Draft Conservation Agreement**: The declaration identifies four specific deficiencies in the draft Agreement:
   - No minimum streamflow provisions.
   - No provisions for other listed species sharing the spinedace's habitat.
   - No coverage of Nevada and Arizona populations.
   - No enforceable standards; existing standards are minimal and permit new habitat disturbance.

3. **Composition Bias of the Conservation Team**: According to Dr. Minckley, no members of the Team supported listing the [[Virgin Spinedace]] as threatened. Team biologists were characterized as either:
   - Consultants to water development interests, or
   - Professionals with backgrounds in sport-fish biology,
   
   rather than specialists in **[[Conservation Biology of Desert Fishes]]**, despite the existence of qualified independent experts (implicitly including Minckley himself).

4. **Identification of Core Threats**: The two most serious threats to the Virgin Spinedace's persistence are explicitly named as (a) habitat destruction via water development, and (b) competition/predation from non-native sport fish — a threat pattern broadly consistent across many imperiled [[Desert Fishes]] of the American Southwest, such as other [[Cyprinidae]] and [[Catostomidae]] species in the Colorado River system.

5. **Credibility and Standing of Declarant**: Dr. Minckley's declaration establishes his authority via a long academic career at Arizona State University (Professor of Zoology since 1977), extensive graduate mentorship (50 graduate students), and direct historical involvement in Colorado River Basin fish management and recovery actions predating and following the 1973 Endangered Species Act — lending significant weight to his critique of the Team's scientific composition and process.
