# Mexican Blindcat Research Expeditions in Northern Mexico (1997–1999)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This NSS News feature, authored by Jean Krejca, Steve Taylor, and Dean Hendrickson as the Mexican Blindcat Research Team, reports a series of cave expeditions from March 1997 to July 1999 aimed at documenting the range and biology of the eyeless, depigmented cave catfish genus [[Prietella]]. The team was international and multidisciplinary, combining [[Cave Diving]], hydrology, geology, invertebrate biology, mapping, and photography. Across the trips, five new sites for [[Prietella Phreatophila]] and one new site for [[Prietella Lundbergi]] were reported, along with many new invertebrate localities, several of them new species. The authors also state that the known blindcat population at [[Sotano de Amezcua]] is small, under 100 fish, which makes it highly vulnerable to aquifer pumping and contamination.

## Background & Description

- **Genus:** [[Prietella]], eyeless and depigmented cave catfish (family Ictaluridae).
- **Species named in the text:**
  - [[Prietella Lundbergi]]: the southern species, previously known only from its type locality, the spring at San Rafael de los Castro, Tamaulipas.
  - [[Prietella Phreatophila]]: the northern species, first found in 1954 by Jorge Carranza in a water well at Muzquiz, Coahuila.
- **Research team:** Includes cavers, divers, and ichthyologists from Ciudad Victoria and Mexico City, with support from institutions such as the University of Texas and the Illinois Natural History Survey.
- **Expedition chronology:** August and March 1997; March, June, and November 1998; March and July 1999.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Fish occupy deep water-filled passages. Sightings at many localities were in cave springs, sumps, and flooded mine shafts, often reached only after heavy floods. The authors infer the fish spend most of their time in distant, inaccessible passages.
- **Catfish observations:** Five catfish were seen at Cueva de la Juana (30-meter drop to water, surveyed to a cobble choke in 60 meters of water). Fish were recorded at depths of 50–60 meters at the first new site, Cueva del Nacimiento del Rio Frio.
- **Associated fauna:** The expeditions documented many stygobitic invertebrates, including [[Cirolanid Isopods]] and mysid shrimp, a new cirolanid isopod species (under description), cave shrimp, troglobitic harvestmen, and a ricinuleid arachnid.
- **Threats and sensitivity:** The authors stress the small population at Sotano de Amezcua and its vulnerability to aquifer pumping and contamination. At Muzquiz, a flooding event about 50 years earlier drowned 40 miners and redirected the city's spring flow through the mine. The city was experiencing water shortages at the time of the visit.
- **Hydrology:** Preliminary water quality parameters suggested that water in one cave differed from that of a nearby spring. Comparative water chemistry data were collected with assistance from Hydrolab, Inc.

## Key Findings

- **New localities:** Five new sites for [[Prietella Phreatophila]] and one new site for [[Prietella Lundbergi]] were discovered during the August 1997 to March 1997 campaign described in the first portion of the article (three weeks in Mexico, March 1997).
- **Successful dives:** Divers recovered blindcats at [[El Socavon]] (Muzquiz) and at Sotano de Amezcua. In June 1998 divers marked 16 catfish at two sumps. In November 1998 the team recaptured previously marked fish, which validated the marking technique and allowed population estimates.
- **Population status:** Fewer than 100 fish are believed to live in the known portions of Sotano de Amezcua (unpublished data), so the population is considered especially vulnerable.
- **Unsuccessful sites:** No blindcats were found at Sotano de San Rafael de los Castro, Cueva Zumbadora, Cuatro Cienegas, the Ojo Encantado area, San Felipe Springs, Emerald Sink, or Dandridge Spring Cave. Several wells had restrictions that prevented access.
- **Exploration results:** The Cueva del Nacimiento del Rio Frio was resurveyed to 410 meters (first surveyed in 1965). Ojo Encantado yielded virgin stream passage (7.5 m high by 4.5 m wide) and stygobitic isopods.
- **Research directions:** Ongoing work covers population size, phylogenetics, breeding, behavior, and distribution. Coordination is led by Dr. Dean Hendrickson (University of Texas), Dr. Francisco Garcia de Leon (Instituto Tecnologico de Cd. Victoria), and Dr. David Hillis (University of Texas).
- **Funding:** Major support came from the U.S.-Mexico Foundation for Science (project 54-E-94), with additional grants from the Cave Research Foundation and others.
