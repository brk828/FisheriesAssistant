# Approaches to Recovery and Section 7 Consultation for Colorado River Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1985 discussion paper, prepared by the [[Colorado Department of Natural Resources]], outlines state-level options for recovering the four endangered Colorado River fishes and for conducting [[Section 7 Consultation]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It was written after a memorandum of understanding (MOU) among the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (USFWS), and the states of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming. The paper is explicitly a discussion document, not a final state position. It argues that recovery and Section 7 have different purposes and should be kept distinct, and it proposes a set of recovery components, Section 7 conditions and next steps for the [[Coordinating Committee]].

## Background & Description

The paper addresses four fish species of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: the focus of most Upper Basin research. It is wide-ranging and needs specific habitats at each life stage.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]**: research has identified important habitats and addressed taxonomic questions. Its habitat is more site-faithful, with documented movements not exceeding six river miles.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: the USFWS recovery plan calls for immediate capture of brood stock to prevent extinction or extirpation. The plan did not consider recovery realistic at that time. Hatchery rearing is the reasonable first approach.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: state-listed as endangered and a federal Category 2 candidate. Many recovery components may apply, but species and habitat research is needed before a recovery plan can be written.

The paper is organized in four parts: Introduction, Recovery Components, Section 7 Components, and Recommendations for Proceeding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper ties each management option to specific river reaches and life stages:

- **Growth, migration, spawning and larval transport** occur roughly May to October. These months are the focus for temperature and flow management.
- **[[Flaming Gorge]]** operations directly affect Colorado squawfish in the Upper [[Green River]]. The nearest suspected humpback chub habitats, Whirlpool and Desolation Canyons, lie downstream of its direct influence.
- **Exotic fishes** (northern pike, channel catfish, redside shiner, largemouth bass, green sunfish) appear to compete with or prey on squawfish and increase predation and displacement risk for humpback chub. Trout were judged to have no major conflict with the endangered species.
- **Existing structures** have limited the range of endangered fishes by blocking passage, except for humpback chub.
- **Native Yampa fishes** reproduced successfully in the higher flows of 1983-84, while exotic counterparts did not.

## Key Findings

### Recovery Components (Part II)

1. **[[Fish Hatchery]]**: all three federally listed fishes and the razorback sucker can be reared. Two existing hatcheries (New Mexico and Arizona) raise "several hundred thousand" fish a year. Hatcheries are proposed in both the Green River and Colorado River subbasins, ideally tied to spawning areas. A [[Rifle Hatchery]] feasibility study could finish within one year. Brood stock assembly is expected to take four to five years. Permanent facilities must be operating when squawfish reach breeding age, seven to nine years. The USFWS should lead required research on imprinting, passage use and interspecific interactions.

2. **[[Flaming Gorge]] regulation**: the cause of recruitment losses is unclear. Temperature differences of 20° C reportedly eliminated age classes two years in a row, but lab results at Utah State University suggested temperature effects were much smaller. The USFWS later suggested excess water in nursery areas also contributed. Temperature and flow changes could be tested from May to October, with a planned one-to-two-year research period. Costs include turbine wear and lost power revenue. Bureau priorities are streamflow regulation, water conservation, power generation, endangered species and game fish, in that order. A [[Reregulation Dam]] downstream could reduce impacts, but siting may be constrained by proximity to Dinosaur National Monument.

3. **[[Ruedi Reservoir]] releases**: releases could augment flows between the Government Highline Canal roller dam and the Gunnison confluence, but only if they are administrable under Colorado water law. Key obstacles include the Fryingpan-Arkansas Project authorization and operating principles, reimbursable cost allocation, Colorado River Water Conservation District water rights, recreation trade-offs, and conflict with the Fryingpan River gold medal trout fishery.

4. **Curtailing exotic stocking**: all Basin states and the USFWS should curtail stocking, except where need is identified off the mainstem and no conflict is shown in consultation with the USFWS. Live-fish bait use in mainstem habitat, allowed by a September 1984 regulation change, should be reviewed. Eliminating established exotics would be a massive, probably unrealistic effort, though spot treatments are possible. Flow manipulation to favor native reproduction is a candidate for mid-range research.

5. **[[Fish Passage Facilities]]**: a prototype is under construction at the Redlands diversion, with monitoring planned after completion within two years. Few resident squawfish may be available for testing. Taylor Draw on the White River fragments a squawfish population and should be retrofitted with USFWS funds. Passage flows could reduce power generation water.

6. **[[Instream Flows]]**: the flow-habitat relationship is not yet quantified with precision. The model is imprecise, especially in non-average years. If the Biological Subcommittee supports protecting status quo flows, instream rights should be purchased or obtained through existing filings. Key institutional options include Colorado Water Conservation Board appropriations, Nature Conservancy purchases of Yampa senior rights, Division of Wildlife transfers (currently unfunded), and federal water designations or purchases. The Yampa Maybell Gage delivery obligation (5,000,000 acre-feet over any ten consecutive years to Utah) may ease Yampa flow protection.

7. **[[Groundwater Development]]**: extensive groundwater under the Piceance Basin could augment flows in the White or Colorado Rivers. Colorado law gives surface owners the exclusive right to develop groundwater. A one-year USFWS feasibility study is proposed.

8. **[[Agricultural Conservation]]**: improved irrigation could free water for purchase for instream use, with possible salinity-control benefits. Junior users must be protected or compensated. A preliminary USFWS study is recommended now.

9. **[[Stream Habitat Improvements]]**: mechanical habitat work, such as backwaters and gravel pits, may help squawfish. Impacts are minor, and work should await better habitat parameters.

### Section 7 Components (Part III)

- Section 7 should protect against further endangerment while recovery improves conditions. Its requirements should be limited to conditions on future water projects tied to project-specific impacts. Decisions must use the "best scientific and commercial data available."
- Completed consultations on unbuilt projects represent substantial potential depletions, creating uncertainty about future flows.
- Diligence could be enforced through expiring or periodically renewed consultations, or through required fixed development periods.
- Alternative approach: a recovery plan could assume predetermined depletion levels, charging developers for a share of recovery costs. This may blur the line between recovery and Section 7 duties.
- Project-level components include [[Fish Passage Facilities]], [[Release Temperatures]], [[Release Timing]], [[Maximum Depletions]], and restrictions on channel alteration, sediment changes and peaking-power fluctuations.
- Proponents may contribute to recovery tasks, such as eliminating exotics downstream, funding hatchery work, supporting research, or contributing to water-right acquisition. Contributions must be clearly earmarked.

### Recommendations for Proceeding (Part IV)

- A recovery plan must be developed. Recovery requires an aggressive, clearly defined program.
- The Coordinating Committee should draft the plan, including components, implementation schedule, and named staff and funding commitments, and should oversee it for an agreed period.
- An interagency subcommittee should schedule a 10-year research program and coordinate data management.
- Recovery is a federal obligation, but aggressive recovery will far exceed current USFWS funding. The Committee must make realistic staffing and funding decisions.
- Recovery elements for consideration include hatcheries, passage retrofits, Flaming Gorge regulation, Ruedi releases, instream flow maintenance, groundwater development, and agricultural conservation.
- The key open issues are the distinction between Section 7 and recovery requirements, and the relationship between [[Reasonable And Prudent Alternatives]] and recovery components.
