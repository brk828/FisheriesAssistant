# Environmental Conditions Within the Orme, Buttes, Charleston, and Hooker Reservoir Sites, 1800 to 1870-90

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by W. L. Minckley of [[Arizona State University]] in January 1972 for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], reconstructs the environmental and biotic conditions of four proposed [[Central Arizona Project]] reservoir sites (Orme, Buttes, Charleston, and Hooker) as they existed between roughly 1800 and 1870-90. It is intended as a historical baseline, since present-day biotas have already been substantially altered. The author argues that water tables were higher, extensive [[Cienega]] marshes were widespread, and native aquatic and mesic-adapted biota was far more abundant. Changes since then are attributed to stream incision (largely linked to [[Arroyo Cutting]] around 1890), the spread of introduced species, and direct human modification. The document is an introductory, explicitly negative-leaning overview; its author notes that aquatic-life reports would supply the detail.

## Background & Description

The report frames itself as a prologue to four assessments requested from Arizona State University. Its evidence base is mostly historical: earlier reviews by Hastings (1959), Miller (1961), Hastings and Turner (1965), and Minckley (1972); expedition diaries (notably the [[Army of the West]], 1846-47, and the [[Mormon Battalion]]); early photographs and newspaper accounts; and oral testimony from "old timers" and their descendants.

The author notes that fishes offer the most complete record of native decline and of increasing non-native populations, while other groups are inferred from vegetation patterns, dispersal, migration, emergence times, and food plants. Only a few non-native biotic elements were present before 1870-90, apart from domestic livestock, people, and their associated canids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Orme Reservoir Site.** Located near the base level of the [[Verde River]] at its confluence with the [[Salt River]], about 15-20 miles upstream of the Salt-[[Gila River]] junction. The lower Verde was reportedly dammed by Salt River sediments for periods in the 1800s and bordered by cienegas extending at least 10, and perhaps 15, miles upstream. Beaver activity, oxbows, and backwaters supported waterfowl and furbearers. [[Peck's Lake]] near Cottonwood is a surviving oxbow. The Salt River was swift, turbulent, and flood-prone, with mesquite bosques on its terraces. The Salt-Gila confluence held extensive "impassible marshlands" bordered by cottonwood, willow, ash, and mesquite.

**Buttes Reservoir Site.** Historical sources are sparse. Accounts describe steep canyon terrain, repeated river crossings, dust, and abundant waterfowl, quail, and wild pigs. Descriptions imply a relative lack of large phreatophytic vegetation.

**Charleston Reservoir Site.** Documented in detail (Hastings and Turner, 1965). Before late-1800s mining and smelting, grassland reached nearly to the river, the stream was barely incised, and beaver dams created fish-filled pools. Cienega habitat occurred along nearly the full length of the [[San Pedro River]], with a low sedge-grass-forb vegetation rather than dense cottonwood gallery forest. Malaria reports in military posts are cited as indirect evidence of extensive marshes.

**Hooker Reservoir Site.** Montane canyon at higher elevation. Early accounts describe a diverse gallery forest including walnut (Juglans), alder (Alnus), sycamore (Platanus), juniper (Juniperus), cottonwood, and mesquite. Its vegetation probably changed less than at other sites. Grassland and marshy floodplain areas are also noted.

**Post-incision changes.** Once channels cut down, floodplains supported gallery forests of cottonwood with Baccharis understory, while terraces became mesquite-dominated with annual grasses and forbs. Invasive species include [[Salt Cedar]] (Tamarix), which has spread across nearly all Southwestern watercourses; [[Arundo Donax]]; Bermuda grass (Cynodon); the [[Asiatic Clam]] (Corbicula); the snail Melanoides; the starling; and European sparrow. Introduced fishes reportedly outnumber native species in Arizona by at least 2 to 1.

## Key Findings

- **Historical baseline:** The four sites differed radically in the 1800s, with higher water tables, widespread cienegas, and a larger share of aquatic and mesic biota than today.
- **Causes of change:** The author identifies (1) stream incision that drained cienegas, (2) replacement of marsh vegetation by phreatophytic forests and shrubs, (3) introduction and spread of non-native species, and (4) direct human alteration, including dams, recreation, mining, lumbering, and grazing.
- **Arroyo cutting:** Attributed to a combination of a drier, warmer climate and overgrazing, it incised nearly all Southwestern streams in a brief period around 1890, rapidly lowering water tables.
- **Fish declines:** The [[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus) was abundant in the upper San Pedro and was commercially fished and marketed in Tombstone before incision. The [[Giant Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius), reaching 5-6 feet and nearly 100 pounds, was present in the upper Verde. Both are now extinct in central Arizona; the razorback persisted in Peck's Lake into the 1950s.
- **Invertebrate and herpetofaunal losses:** Likely declines included rotifers, crustaceans, aquatic snails, numerous aquatic insect families, frogs (Rana), musk turtles (Kinosternon), and garter snakes (Thamnophis). These are inferred rather than directly measured.
- **Avifauna and mammals:** A shift from waterfowl, shorebirds, and kingfishers toward xeric-adapted birds; beaver and muskrat were probably reduced by trapping.
- **Site-specific status:** Orme is heavily used and regulated; Buttes lies below a stratifying reservoir and is largely fishless except seasonally; Charleston has shifted to a Chihuahuan-like vegetation; Hooker has experienced the least direct impact.
- **Conclusion:** The author states that the report is "basically negative," that more specific aquatic-life reports will include positive impacts, and that the sites collectively have changed substantially since the 1800s.
