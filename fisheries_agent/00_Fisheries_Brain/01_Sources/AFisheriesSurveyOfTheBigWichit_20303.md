# A Fisheries Survey of the Big Wichita River System and Its Impoundments

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #fisheries-survey

## Executive Summary

This Texas Game and Fish Commission report (Inland Fisheries IF Report Series No. 2, February 1957) by [[Leo D. Lewis]] and [[Walter W. Dalquest]] documents a two-year survey (June 1953 to May 1955) of the Big Wichita River, its tributaries, springs and three impoundments: [[Lake Kemp]], [[Lake Diversion]] and [[Lake Wichita]]. The study combined water chemistry, seasonal fish sampling at permanent stations, gill-net population analyses and assessments of pollution and erosion. The central finding is that the river is naturally saline, and that salinity, mostly derived from [[Permian Red Beds]] and oil-field brine, controls the distribution of game fishes. The report lists 50 species, dominated by salt-tolerant minnows and rough fishes, and recommends salt reduction, oil-field brine control and watershed management.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The Wichita is the largest Texas tributary of the [[Red River]]. It drains more than 4,000 square miles and runs about 165 miles in a straight line from its permanent headwaters to its confluence with the Red. Its catchment lies within the [[Mesquite Plains Biotic District]].
- **Geology:** The drainage lies within [[Permian Red Beds]], largely marine and deltaic sandstone, shale, gypsum and salt. The [[Blain Formation]] is the main salt-contributing stratum.
- **Divisions:** The authors recognized six river segments: the upper river, Lake Kemp, the middle river, Lake Diversion, Lake Wichita (on Holliday Creek) and the lower river.
- **Physical features:**
  - *Upper river:* steep canyons, clear swift water, and sand or gravel beds.
  - *Middle river:* flow controlled by releases from the Lake Kemp dam, often nearly dry.
  - *Lower river:* a mature, turbid, meandering stream with oxbow lakes.
- **Survey design:** Nine permanent monthly stations, about 100 water-sample localities, and seines, gill nets, hoop nets and rotenone checks in enclosed lake areas.
- **Program context:** The work was part of [[Dingell-Johnson Federal Aid Program]] projects Texas F-7-R-1 and F-7-R-2, funded 75 percent federally and 25 percent by the state.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salinity.** The dissolved-salt burden is the defining feature of the system. Nearly one-third of the river length is unsuitable for freshwater game fishes. Sodium chloride and calcium sulfate dominate. Individual springs and tributaries either raise or dilute salinity, so the river changes chemically every few hundred yards in places. Examples from the sampling:
- Johnson Oil Field tributary: total salts reported "in excess of 27 percent" at times.
- South Fork at Benjamin Station (April 23, 1955): chloride fell from 14,777 ppm to 11,893 ppm and 8,387 ppm downstream, and total salts from 27,782 ppm to 21,986 ppm and 17,938 ppm.
- Lake Diversion dissolved solids: 1,420 to 2,800 ppm during the study.

**Hydrology.** The river is intermittent. The South Fork is often dry except near Guthrie, Benjamin and its mouth. The middle river depends on releases from Lake Kemp, and the North Fork is dry for most of the year except at its springs. Flow data for the lower river at Wichita Falls (October 1952 to September 1953) were supplied by the U.S. Geological Survey, with total annual runoff of 63,910 acre-feet.

**Lakes.**
- [[Lake Kemp]]: dam built 1923, 560,000 acre-feet at spillway level, about 22,800 acres, and lacks rooted vegetation. Water clarity is high because dissolved salts flocculate suspended particles; Secchi readings reached 735 mm in calm conditions.
- [[Lake Diversion]]: about 3,420 acres and 40,000 acre-feet, rich in Chara and pondweed (Potamogeton), with mayflies of the genus Hexagenia. It is the storage reservoir for irrigation.
- [[Lake Wichita]]: about 2,500 acres, built in 1901 as a water supply for Wichita Falls. It was reduced by drought to about 1,425 acre-feet in 1953, after which a selective rough-fish kill removed about 73,000 pounds of drum and gizzard shad. Zooplankton was far richer than in the other lakes.

**Pollution.** Oil-field brine is the main industrial source of salt, and the report notes it has been released for nearly fifty years. Domestic pollution is considered slight. A crude oil pipeline break near Wichita Falls on March 17, 1955 released several hundred barrels of oil; the oil sank after oxidation and fouled bottom sediments for months.

**Erosion.** Overgrazing and poor land use have removed prairie soils, exposing Permian clays and creating cedar brakes and badlands. Silt from heavy rains reddens the river and settles into reservoirs, favoring rough fishes that tolerate soft mud bottoms.

## Key Findings

**Fish fauna**
- A total of 50 fish species is recorded for the river and impoundments, including introduced or suspected introduced forms. Most were examined and deposited at Midwestern University, and a few records come from the literature.
- Dominant forage and salt-tolerant species include the [[Red Shiner]] (described as the most abundant forage fish in the watershed) and the [[Gizzard Shad]] (extremely abundant, outbreeds and crowds out desirable species).
- The [[Plains Minnow]] is the most valuable commercial minnow. The [[Pupfish]] and [[Plains Killifish]] tolerate salt concentrations several times that of sea water.
- The [[Longnose Gar]] is the most abundant gar outside Lake Kemp and is a predator of sunfishes and gizzard shad.
- Commercially important rough fishes include the [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] and the [[River Carpsucker]], the latter regarded as the most serious present menace to sport fisheries.
- Popular game fishes include the [[Largemouth Black Bass]] (the most prized game species), [[White Bass]], [[White Crappie]], [[Channel Catfish]] (up to 14 pounds recorded) and [[Freshwater Drum]].

**Salinity effects on game fish**
- No popular game fishes were found in waters averaging more than about 2,000 ppm chloride.
- Local salinity spikes after drought-ending rains caused fish kills, with game species making up a larger share of the dead fish.
- Salt may favor salt-tolerant rough fishes at the expense of sport species.

**Quantitative data**
- Table 24 (river, 1954–55): 836 larger fishes sampled, with gizzard shad (28.7 percent) and longnose gar (23.1 percent) the most numerous.
- Table 25 (Lake Kemp gill nets, June 1953 to May 1954): 1,003 fishes, with river carpsucker the largest group (42.7 percent).
- Table 26 (Lake Diversion gill nets): 1,482 fishes, with gizzard shad (29.9 percent) and river carpsucker (19.4 percent) the most numerous.
- Table 27 (Lake Wichita gill nets, June 1954 to May 1955): 1,076 fishes; smallmouth buffalo (21.0 percent) was the largest group by number.
- Table 28 (Lake Wichita rough fishes): 774 individuals and 1,897.8 pounds recorded.
- Table 29 (commercial catch, Lake Wichita, February to April 1955): 10,927 individuals and 34,899 pounds.
- Forage-fish seine samples (Table 34): 2,412 fishes from Lake Kemp, 2,430 from Lake Diversion and 2,008 from Lake Wichita.

**Pollution and erosion**
- Oil-field brine augments the natural salt load without changing the proportions of the salts present.
- Chloride and total salts between Diversion Dam and Deadman Bridge stations were "between two and three times greater," mainly due to the Kaymay Oil Field.
- Pollution from accidental releases of petroleum or detergents can be disastrous to the lower river fishery.

**Recommendations**
1. Require reinjection of brine from all oil wells and ban surface brine pits, with strict enforcement. The authors estimate this would eliminate more than half of the salt in the lower river.
2. Consider diverting or canaling the upper river around the impoundments to protect lake fisheries, and possibly pumping upper-river water to another basin or evaporation basin.
3. Use the saline system as a trial site for introducing salt-water game fishes. The report notes stocking of [[Redfish]] in Lake Kemp in 1954 and of [[Speckled Trout]] in 1955, with results still to be determined.
4. Encourage good land management on the watershed to prevent siltation.
