# Designation of Critical Habitat for the Woundfin and Virgin River Chub in the Virgin River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Final [[Environmental Assessment]] (November 16, 1999), prepared by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Utah Field Office, evaluates the formal designation of [[Critical Habitat]] for two endangered fishes, the [[Woundfin]] and the [[Virgin River Chub]], on the mainstem [[Virgin River]] and its [[100-Year Floodplain]]. The action responds to the [[Endangered Species Act]] requirement to designate critical habitat and to a U.S. District Court order (August 27, 1999). Three alternatives were assessed: No Action (A), the preferred designation of 87.5 miles (B), and designation of 94.8 miles (C). The assessment concludes that designation will not cause a significant impact on the human environment, so no [[Environmental Impact Statement]] is required.

## Background & Description

**Species status.** The woundfin was listed as endangered on October 13, 1970 (35 FR 16047). The Virgin River chub was listed as endangered on August 24, 1989 (54 FR 35305). The chub was originally treated as a subspecies (*Gila robusta seminuda*). [[DeMarais et al. 1992]] recommended reclassification as *Gila seminuda*, which the Service and the American Fisheries Society accepted.

**Regulatory history.** Critical habitat was first proposed for the woundfin in 1977 and later withdrawn under 1978 Act amendments. Proposals were re-issued in 1995 (60 FR 17296) for the woundfin, Virgin River chub, and [[Virgin Spinedace]]. Congressional spending moratoria and a [[Listing Priority Guidance]] backlog delayed the final rule. A [[Tenth Circuit Court]] ruling in 1998 on the silvery minnow later shaped the enforcement order in this case.

**Taxonomic and population scope.** The [[Muddy River]] population of the Virgin River chub, geographically isolated by [[Lake Mead]], was excluded from this designation and is to be addressed in a separate listing determination.

**Native fauna.** The Virgin River Basin contains six native fish species: woundfin, Virgin River chub, Virgin spinedace, speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), desert sucker (*Catostomus clarki*), and flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*). Fourteen introduced fish species are also documented.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The Virgin River is a desert tributary of the [[Colorado River]] flowing about 320 km (200 mi) through southwestern Utah, northwestern Arizona, and southeastern Nevada. The basin covers about 15,281 km² (5,900 mi²): 59% in Utah, 33% in Arizona, and 8% in Nevada. Elevations range from 3,048 m (10,000 ft) in the headwaters to 579 m (1,900 ft) at Littlefield, Arizona. Flows are highly variable, with extreme summer lows and spring or storm peaks. Mean annual flow at Littlefield (water years 1930–1992) is 170,600 acre-feet. Mineral springs such as [[La Verkin Springs]] (about 12–19 cfs, 35–39 °C, 9,650 mg/l salinity) strongly affect water quality.

**Essential habitat features.** The assessment defines three [[Primary Constituent Elements]]:
- **Water:** adequate quantity and quality (temperature, dissolved oxygen, contaminants, nutrients, turbidity) delivered on a life-stage-specific hydrologic regime.
- **Physical habitat:** channels, side and secondary channels, backwaters, and springs supporting spawning, nursery, feeding, and rearing, plus connecting corridors.
- **Biological environment:** food supply, predation, and competition, which are out of balance because of nonnative fishes.

**Floodplain dependence.** The Virgin River has little aquatic vegetation and low autochthonous production, so the fauna depends on allochthonous energy from the floodplain. Seasonal inundation supports spawning and reproductive success. Large flow events let the river meander and renew habitat mosaics. Preserving the channel alone is considered insufficient.

**Woundfin ecology.** Woundfin are omnivores (filamentous algae, detritus, tamarisk seeds, aquatic insects). Adults occupy runs and quiet waters beside riffles at 0.5–1.4 ft depth and 0.8–1.6 ft/s velocity, with sand or sand-gravel substrates. Larvae use backwaters with filamentous algae. Spawning occurs during declining spring runoff and is earlier downstream. Woundfin now occupy less than 15% of their historic habitat.

**Virgin River chub ecology.** Adults and juveniles favor slow to moderate velocities in deep runs and pools with boulders or root snags over sand. Chub tolerate velocities up to 2.5 ft/s. Young feed on debris and invertebrates, while adults feed almost exclusively on filamentous algae. Reproductive biology is poorly known; sexually mature fish have been captured in April through June.

**Threats.** Dewatering, dam construction, water diversions, altered hydrographs, nonnative species, and habitat loss have caused declines in all native Virgin River fishes. The [[Red Shiner]] is the most numerous and most problematic nonnative. It competes with and preys on eggs and early life stages, and the [[Virgin River Fishes Recovery Plan]] identifies it as a limiting factor. Black bullhead, channel catfish, and introduced crayfish also prey on young fish.

**Invertebrates and other fauna.** The invertebrate fauna is sparse. Reaches below the Washington Fields Diversion have elevated invertebrate populations from irrigation return flows and wastewater. Taxa include chironomids, oligochaetes, simuliid larvae, caddisflies, mayflies, cladocerans, ostracods, and copepods. No snails occur in the mainstem or large tributaries. Amphibians of concern include the Arizona toad, lowland leopard frog, and relict leopard frog (extirpated). Terrestrial species of concern include the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Bald Eagle]], [[Desert Tortoise]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], and [[Mexican Spotted Owl]]. Appendix A lists well over 200 native terrestrial and aquatic species.

**Land ownership and use.** Designated critical habitat totals 87.5 miles under Alternative B (37.3 mi in Utah, 31.6 mi in Arizona, 18.6 mi in Nevada). Shoreline ownership is 57.7% federal (50.5 mi), 2.4% state (2.1 mi), and 39.9% private (34.9 mi). Local uses include hydroelectric facilities, irrigation diversions, grazing, municipal supply, golf courses, river float trips, and fishing.

**Socioeconomic setting.** Washington County, Utah, has high growth (48,560 residents in 1990, projected 380,592 by 2040), with services as the largest sector. Agriculture is the largest per-capita water user, with on-field efficiency of 40%. Clark County, Nevada, grew 62.5% from 1980 to 1990 and is projected to reach 2,103,500 by 2040. Its on-field efficiency is 65%.

## Key Findings

**Alternatives evaluated**
- **Alternative A (No Action):** maintains the status quo. Section 7 consultation and Section 9 take prohibitions still apply, but the legal obligation to designate critical habitat is unmet, and the Court order is not addressed.
- **Alternative B (Preferred):** designates 87.5 miles from the La Verkin Creek confluence, Utah, downstream to Halfway Wash, Nevada. The lower boundary was moved from Lake Mead after public comment showed few fish below Halfway Wash, where sand deposition created a braided, shallow reach.
- **Alternative C:** designates 94.8 miles, extending to Lake Mead. It differs from B only by 7.3 miles in Nevada.

Under Alternative B, critical habitat protects about 12.5% of the woundfin's historic range and 65.8% of the Virgin River chub's historic range.

**Regulatory effect**
- Critical habitat adds an adverse-modification analysis to Section 7 consultation for federal actions only. It does not create a wilderness or refuge and does not restrict private land use lacking a federal nexus.
- The assessment finds that critical habitat has rarely given greater protection than the listing itself, because jeopardy and adverse-modification outcomes usually coincide.
- Excluded from critical habitat: existing paved roads, bridges, parking lots, dikes, levees, railroad tracks, residential, commercial, and industrial developments, water diversion canals outside stream channels, active gravel pits, and cultivated agricultural lands.

**Economic analysis (Brookshire et al. 1995)**
The analysis covers 1995–2040 (45 years) with a 3% discount rate, using input-output models for Washington and Iron counties (UT), Clark County (NV), and part of Mohave County (AZ). Three scenarios were compared with the "without fish" baseline:
- **WFST (structural):** Washington County present-value output change of -$47.5 million (0.0016% of baseline; annualized -$1.95 million). Clark County -$10.63 million (0.00001%). Regional -$59.8 million (0.0001%; annualized -$2.45 million).
- **WFCO (conservation):** Washington County -$13.7 million (0.00046%; annualized -$0.56 million). Regional -$20.9 million (annualized -$0.86 million). No conservation-scenario impacts occur in Clark County.

The Service states that reallocating water and building delivery projects would occur regardless of designation; designation affects only timing and cost. Agricultural conversion remains voluntary.

**Instream flow context**
- An existing 86 cfs minimum flow below the Washington Fields Diversion stems from a 1982 non-jeopardy biological opinion for the Quail Creek Reservoir.
- A 3 cfs year-round flow below the Quail Creek Diversion was established under the Virgin Spinedace Conservation Agreement.
- Flow requirements for the woundfin and chub are not otherwise specifically designated.

**Population trends cited in comment responses**
- Woundfin declined between 1976 and 1993, with regressions negative (p < 0.05) at all long-term stations except one in fall (Holden and Zucker 1996).
- Utah Division of Wildlife Resources monitoring showed woundfin falling from 456 (spring) and 604 (fall) in 1994 to 77 (spring) and 162 (fall) in 1999.
- Virgin River chub density is the lowest of any native fish. Recruitment is linked to high-flow years, and the 1983 cohort made up about 90% of the sampled population in 1985.

**Public process**
- The proposed rule drew 14 written letters and testimony from 6 people at a May 9, 1995 public hearing in St. George.
- The draft EA (August 9, 1999) drew 12 written comments. Eight parties generally supported designation, and one party requested an EIS.

**Principal conclusions**
- Designation will not significantly affect the human environment, so no EIS is required.
- Environmental justice effects were not identified.
- Cumulative impacts are not significant, because the designation mostly shifts the timing of activities already expected to occur.
- Designation is consistent with the [[Virgin River Management Plan]] and the proposed [[Virgin River Resource Management and Recovery Program]], which together are intended to serve as reasonable and prudent alternatives under Section 7 for Utah.
