# Population Status and Distribution of the Gila Trout in New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Endangered Species Report (No. 6, 1980) by Kim Mello and Paul R. Turner documents stream surveys of [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*) conducted from 1974 to 1976 on seven streams in the [[Gila National Forest]], New Mexico. The surveys estimated population sizes, measured habitat and water chemistry, sampled aquatic invertebrates, and assessed the effects of [[Stream Improvement Structures]]. The total Gila trout population in the seven New Mexico streams was estimated at fewer than 7,600 fish. The report concludes with management recommendations for the Gila Trout Recovery Team.

## Background & Description

The Gila trout is native to the Gila River drainage in Arizona and New Mexico. Its original range has been drastically reduced, mainly through [[Hybridization]] with [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). It has been listed as Endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service since 1966. According to local testimony, native trout were not present in the San Francisco River drainage of New Mexico; trout were reportedly introduced into five of its tributaries in 1905 by men hired by local miners.

Diagnostic field characters for Main Diamond Creek fish include dark, oval to round spots that are small and dense, typical lack of spots on the anal fin, few spots below the lateral line except on the caudal peduncle, and golden to bronze coloration with visible parr marks (both variable between specimens). Fish under 300 mm were the focus of the description.

Seven streams in the Gila National Forest support populations:

- **Natural populations (5):** [[Main Diamond Creek]], [[South Diamond Creek]], [[McKenna Creek]], [[Spruce Creek]], and [[Iron Creek]].
- **Transplanted populations (2):** [[Sheep Corral Canyon Creek]] (established 1970 or 1972 from Main Diamond Creek stock) and [[McKnight Creek]] (established 1970 and 1972 from Main Diamond Creek stock).

A transplanted population was also established in Gap Creek, a Verde River tributary in Arizona, in 1974. Reproduction was documented in 1977, with fewer than 100 fish estimated.

Most prior data came from Main Diamond Creek. Before the 1976 surveys, data for Iron, South Diamond, McKnight and Sheep Corral Creeks were unavailable or limited.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Habitat surveys followed the [[Stream Habitat Evaluation Technique]] (McKirdy 1970). Measured parameters included temperature, percent shade, pool-riffle ratio, flow (cfs, by the Embody method), pool and riffle dimensions, substrate (boulder, rubble, gravel, fines, bedrock), siltation, riparian cover, dissolved oxygen, pH and carbon dioxide (Hach reagents). Populations were estimated by [[Electrofishing]] multiple 61 m sections and extrapolating to total stream length, with single upstream passes due to endangered-species permit limits. Aquatic insects were sampled with [[Surber Sampling]] and classified after Usinger (1963/1974). Condition factors (K) and length-weight relationships followed Lagler (1956).

**Main Diamond Creek** (Black Range Primitive Area, Sierra County; watershed 1,788 ha). Over 100 log [[Stream Improvement Structures]] built in the 1930s by the Civilian Conservation Corps create pools that support high trout densities and provide drought refuge. Hanson (1971) found 28 of 128 inspected structures washed out. Riparian cover was mainly mature Arizona alder with Douglas fir in the floodplain.

**South Diamond Creek** (Black Range Primitive Area, Sierra County; watershed 2,307 ha). About 5.6 km of intermittent channel likely prevents upstream fish migration. Burnt Canyon, a 0.4 km tributary, had 95% shade, small artificial pools (mean 1.8 m long, 1.8 m wide, 27 cm deep) and few invertebrates.

**McKenna Creek** (Gila Wilderness, Catron County; watershed 1,270 ha). A 2 m waterfall isolates the stream from the West Fork of the Gila. A log-and-rock barrier formed after 1972 raised concerns about population purity, but morphometric and meristic analysis found no significant differences between subpopulations.

**Spruce Creek** (Gila Wilderness, Catron County; watershed 726 ha). Brown trout were found 305 m upstream from the Big Dry Creek confluence and are blocked by a 2 m waterfall. Old mining debris is present. Caddisflies made up 60% of Surber samples. A 1975 flood may have reduced trout and invertebrate densities.

**Iron Creek** (Gila Wilderness, Catron County; upper watershed 311 ha). A 210 m intermittent section and a gravel barrier separate Gila trout from downstream brown trout. Mayflies and caddisflies together made up nearly 80% of invertebrates. Stonefly larvae (11.1%) were more abundant here than in any other Gila trout stream.

**Sheep Corral Canyon Creek** (Silver City Ranger District, Grant County; watershed 804 ha). Cattle grazing was heavy in 1975, but was not evident in 1976. Dipterans were more abundant and diverse than in other Gila trout streams, indicating degraded water quality conditions (McKirdy 1970).

**McKnight Creek** (Mimbres Ranger District, Grant County; watershed above barrier 1,374 ha). An artificial barrier built in 1970 followed rotenone treatment to remove Rio Grande sucker. Grazing reduced riparian regeneration, and a fence was later built to exclude cattle from the Middle Section.

## Key Findings

**Population estimates (from the Abstract and text)**

| Stream | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Main Diamond Creek | 4,750 trout (November 1974), including young-of-the-year |
| Sheep Corral Canyon Creek | < 50 trout (Age I and older, July 1976) |
| Five other streams | 400 to 1,000 trout each (Age I and older) |
| Total, seven streams | < 7,600 fish |

- Main Diamond Creek holds more than 50% of all known Gila trout. Earlier estimates were about 4,300 (Regan 1964) and 2,983 to 4,170 (Hanson 1969-70).
- McKnight Creek increased substantially from 1974 to 1976; its estimate was 800 to 1,000 fish (Age I and older).
- South Diamond Creek was estimated at 500 to 700 fish (July 1976), including Burnt Canyon.
- Spruce Creek was estimated at 500 to 700 fish; Bickle (1972) had estimated 1,200.

**Growth and condition**

- Main Diamond Creek fish (1974) were stunted: mean TL 144.7 mm, mean K 0.91. Crowded populations and drought mortality were suspected.
- South Diamond Creek fish were larger in 1975 (mean 191.0 mm, K 1.16) than in 1976 (mean 158.1 mm, K 0.99), reflecting strong year-class variation.
- Iron Creek Gila trout had mean K 1.04, higher than brown trout (mean K 0.91). The brown-to-Gila ratio was 3.9:1.0 in a 183 m reach below the barrier.
- Sheep Corral Canyon (July 1976) had mean K 0.97 in a sample of six fish; the stream stocked 89 fish in 1972 and now holds fewer than 50 trout.

**Taxonomic findings**

- R. E. David (1976) found Iron Creek fish pure *S. gilae*, with live colors, parr marks and spotting indistinguishable from Main Diamond Creek. Only predorsal length differed significantly.
- David suggested Spruce Creek fish may deserve subspecific recognition, possibly due to basibranchial teeth and pyloric caeca differences. R. R. Miller noted possible cutthroat trout (*S. clarki*) hybrid influence.

**Key threats and drivers**

- Hybridization with rainbow trout (Miller 1950; Behnke and Zarn 1976).
- Drought-driven dewatering of riffles and loss of pools.
- Flood damage (Spruce Creek, 1972 and possibly 1975).
- Livestock grazing in canyon bottoms (Sheep Corral Canyon, McKnight Creek).
- Past mining activity (Spruce Creek).
- Brown trout competition and interaction (Iron Creek).

**Management recommendations (summarized)**

- **Main Diamond Creek:** repair stream improvement structures; reduce the stunted population by removing about 500 fish for transplant; transplant only to streams that originally held similar stock; evaluate riparian thinning to reduce fire risk.
- **South Diamond Creek:** repair structures in Burnt Canyon; reduce dense riparian stands and plant natives where streambanks are bare; study factors affecting year-class strength.
- **McKenna Creek:** monitor and control forest fires in the controlled-burn area; maintain current conditions.
- **Spruce Creek:** conduct further taxonomic research (including relation to *S. apache*); restrict mining until its impacts are evaluated.
- **Iron Creek:** prohibit rainbow trout stocking; survey the headwaters and the lower creek; evaluate channel stabilization with riparian plantings.
- **Sheep Corral Canyon:** no further transplants unless conditions change; repair or redesign non-functional log structures; control dry-season grazing or fence off occupied reaches.
- **McKnight Creek:** continue restricting cattle in the Middle Section; add structures in the Headwater Section; transplant only with personnel able to distinguish *S. gilae* from rainbow trout hybrids; survey below the barrier for rainbow trout after high runoff.
