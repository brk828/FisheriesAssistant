# Hybridization and the Origin of *Ambystoma tigrinum stebbinsi*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ambystomatidae

## Executive Summary

This study uses restriction-site analysis of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) in three tiger salamander subspecies of the American Southwest to test the origin of the geographically restricted [[Huachuca Tiger Salamander]] (*A. t. stebbinsi*). The mtDNA of *stebbinsi* is derived from a [[Arizona Tiger Salamander]] (*A. t. nebulosum*) haplotype, whereas its color pattern and allozymes point toward a shared history with the [[Barred Tiger Salamander]] (*A. t. mavortium*). The authors conclude that *stebbinsi* most likely originated through past hybridization between these two subspecies, with mixed nuclear and mitochondrial ancestry. Because the lineage is now isolated, the case is presented as a model for how hybrid origins can confound cladistic analysis, and it carries direct implications for endangered-species management. The paper is a peer-reviewed research article in *Molecular Phylogenetics and Evolution* (1995).

## Background & Description

- **Study taxa:** Three subspecies of *Ambystoma tigrinum*, a North American species recognized as eight subspecies by metamorphosed color pattern (Dunn, 1940; Stebbins, 1985).
  - *A. t. stebbinsi*: a geographically restricted race from the San Rafael Valley, Arizona, whose taxonomic status had been debated.
  - *A. t. mavortium*: grasslands and deserts east of the Rocky Mountains.
  - *A. t. nebulosum*: widely distributed in the southern Rocky Mountains and intermontane Southwest.
- **Conceptual framing:** Phylogenetic systematics assumes dichotomous branching, but hybridization can create incompatible character distributions. Hybridization is broadly accepted in botany but remains marginal in zoological phylogenetics.
- **Methods (summary):** Liver or skeletal muscle tissue was sampled, and mtDNA was digested with 11 restriction endonucleases (6-bp recognition sequences), then probed and visualized via Southern blotting. Haplotypes were defined by unique restriction-site combinations. Parsimony analysis was run in PAUP 3.1.1 (Swofford, 1993), with the outgroup *A. t. tigrinum* haplotype TC.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** *Mavortium* occupies grasslands and desert east of the Rocky Mountains. *Nebulosum* is found in the southern Rockies and intermontane southwestern United States. The *stebbinsi* sampling sites are stock tanks in the San Rafael Valley near Lochiel, Harshaw, and Parker Canyon.
- **Contact zone:** A hybrid contact zone between *mavortium* and *nebulosum* lies in central New Mexico (see [[Jones and Collins 1992 Hybrid Zone Study]]).
- **Biogeographic history:** The authors cite Pleistocene north-south and east-west faunal exchange, Plio-Pleistocene elevational shifts in vegetation zones, and the narrow endemism of other San Rafael Valley taxa (a hydrobiid snail and the orchid [[Spiranthes Delitescens]]). Fossil *Ambystoma* from Papago Springs Cave (Wisconsin-age deposits) and the late Pliocene San Pedro Valley confirm the genus's regional presence.
- **Conservation status:** The USFWS listed *stebbinsi* as a Category 2 candidate species, so more data were required before Endangered Species Act listing.

## Key Findings

- **mtDNA:** 12 haplotypes were mapped, including the outgroup. *Mavortium* (n = 36) had 3 haplotypes, and *nebulosum* (n = 21) had 7. No haplotypes were shared between the two. The *stebbinsi* population (7 sites, n = 17) carried a single haplotype, A31-s, which falls within the *nebulosum* clade and differs from the common *nebulosum* haplotype by the absence of one Xmnl site.
- **Phylogenetic result:** Three equally parsimonious trees, each 16 steps long (CI = 0.750, RI = 0.882). Constraining *stebbinsi* and *mavortium* to be monophyletic produced trees 4 steps longer, a difference judged significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 0.02).
- **Color pattern:** About 60% of mature field-collected *stebbinsi* from the San Rafael Valley have dorsal patterns indistinguishable from *mavortium*. The rest show a distinctive reticulate pattern not reported for either parent.
- **Allozymes:** *Stebbinsi* was nearly monomorphic (virtually fixed at 21 loci), with H = 0.0015 across 7 populations, percent polymorphic loci = 9.5, and mean alleles per locus = 1.1. The authors interpret this as a severe or prolonged bottleneck. Diagnostic loci show *stebbinsi* fixed for a *mavortium*-type MPI allele, a *mavortium*-favored ACON-1 allele (0.178 in *mavortium* versus 0.009 in *nebulosum*), and a common *nebulosum* MDH-1 allele absent from *mavortium*.
- **Diversity comparison:** Mean heterozygosity was 0.118 ± 0.033 for *nebulosum* (23 populations, 22 loci) and 0.143 ± 0.046 for *mavortium* (9 populations, 21 loci).
- **Competing hypotheses:** Lineage sorting from an ancestral population was considered but judged improbable, because the absence of a bottleneck in the other subspecies and the lack of *nebulosum* haplotypes across 40 Colorado and Nebraska *mavortium* populations (n = 388) argue against retained ancestral polymorphisms. Past hybridization, followed by geographic range shifts and isolation of relict hybrid populations, is favored.
- **Broader significance:** The authors argue hybrid zones can be "sites of species origination," that isolated populations may reveal past hybridization, and that cladistic methods should separate homoplasy from hybridization.
- **Conservation implications:** Citing Dowling et al. (1992), the authors state that denying protection "due to a lack of [perceived] 'purity' defeats the purpose of our efforts." Taxa with unique character sets retain evolutionary and legal importance even when hybrid in origin.
