# Annual Production, Production/Biomass Ratio, and the Ecotrophic Coefficient for Management of Trout in Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review synthesizes published estimates of annual production, the annual production/biomass (P/B) ratio, and the [[Ecotrophic Coefficient]] for [[Stream Trout]] populations. The ecotrophic coefficient, defined as the ratio of angler harvest to annual production, is proposed as a practical management tool because it quantifies the proportion of fish energy removed by angling. The author argues that P/B ratios vary with the age structure of populations, and that ecotrophic coefficients respond to regulations, habitat alteration, and fishing pressure. Using Wisconsin and Minnesota data, the paper proposes management guidelines for stream trout fisheries.

## Background & Description

Annual production refers to the total new tissue generated by a population over a year, and it is widely used to assess the productive potential of fish stocks. Early work by [[Ricker Method]] and others established the P/B ratio as a convenient index of production, since it can be estimated from standing stock alone. The paper notes that roughly half a century of research on stream fish production has produced a body of data, but that estimates are often poorly comparable because of differing methods and age structure.

The principal species discussed are:

- [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*): longer-lived, typically five or more age-groups.
- [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*): intermediate, typically about four age-groups. A key study site is [[Lawrence Creek]] in Wisconsin.
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*): juvenile populations with two or three age-groups, including anadromous forms.
- [[Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Coho Salmon]]: reported less often, with few production data.

Two central concepts are defined:

- **P/B ratio:** annual production divided by mean annual standing stock. The paper favors the annual P/B ratio for fisheries management.
- **Ecotrophic coefficient:** annual harvest expressed as a percentage of annual production. It represents the fraction of production removed by anglers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Production in streams depends strongly on habitat fertility. The paper reports that annual production by stream salmonids in fertile streams ranged from 100 to 300 kg/hectare, and that most estimates for small streams in the literature are about 15 to 50 kg/hectare. Many published values exceed earlier suggested upper limits for small streams, so the author proposes that 100 to 300 kg/hectare indicates a highly productive stream ranking in the upper category of trout fisheries for management purposes.

Population age structure strongly influences P/B. Strong young age-groups raise the ratio, while weak young age-groups lower it. Unusually slow-growing populations, such as those in infertile streams, show low annual P/B ratios. Management practices also affect the ecotrophic coefficient, including:

- Catch-and-release, slot limit, trophy, and lure restrictions, which typically reduce harvest and therefore lower the coefficient.
- Reduced angling pressure, which also lowers harvest.
- Habitat alteration, which can increase production and raise the coefficient.
- Stocking of hatchery fish, which can affect production positively or negatively.

The study sites and data sources include the Lawrence Creek long-term brook trout program, several central and northern Wisconsin streams, the Valley Creek (Minnesota) brook trout population, and southeastern Minnesota trout streams such as the [[Whitewater River]].

## Key Findings

**1. Annual P/B ratios depend on species and age structure.**
- Juvenile rainbow trout populations with two or three age-groups: annual P/B of 2.2 (Waters et al. 1990).
- Brook trout with four age-groups: annual P/B of 1.7 (Waters et al. 1990).
- Brown trout with six age-groups: annual P/B of 0.9 (Waters et al. 1990).
- Juvenile anadromous populations with two or three age-groups: about 2.0 (Table 1 summary).
- For brook trout in Valley Creek over a 14-year series: average annual P/B of 1.4 (Waters 1983).
- Brown trout with five or more age-groups: typically about 1.0.
- Normally growing, stream-resident populations generally show an annual P/B of about 1.0 to 1.5.

**2. Year-class strength alters P/B.** For brown trout in a Wisconsin stream, Brynildson and Brynildson (1984) reported 18 annual estimates averaging 1.2. Ratios of 2.2 to 3.3 occurred in four years when age-groups 0 and 1 were strong, and ratios of 0.4 and 0.8 occurred in two years when those age-groups were weak.

**3. Ecotrophic coefficients in Lawrence Creek were low under restrictive regulations.**
- Four experimental sections: 0.10 to 0.16.
- Seven years under consistent regulations: 0.10 to 0.26, average 0.16 (Hunt 1974). The author attributes low values partly to high minimum-size limits, low daily bag limits, and fly-only rules.
- Habitat alteration increased the coefficient in an altered section from 0.09 to 0.22, compared with a change from 0.11 to 0.15 in an unaltered section (Hunt 1971).

**4. Normal-regulation streams in Wisconsin showed higher coefficients.**
- Four central Wisconsin brown trout streams: production of 56 to 152 kg/hectare over two years, ecotrophic coefficients 0.34 to 0.70, average 0.52 (Avery and Hunt 1981). Fishing pressure reached 929 to 983 hours/hectare, which the authors considered excessive.
- Two northern Wisconsin streams (Avery 1983): brown trout coefficients of about 0.09 to 0.12 and brook trout coefficients of 0.10 to 1.08. A value of 1.08 indicates that more brook trout biomass was harvested than produced that year. Fishing pressure was 74 to 185 hours/hectare.

**5. Brook trout are more vulnerable to anglers.** Brook trout were harvested at a higher rate relative to production than brown trout, consistent with Cooper (1952). The author notes this difference persisted even at low angling pressure.

**6. Southeastern Minnesota data show wide variation.** Under normal regulations, annual coefficients in the Whitewater River's Middle Fork were 0.23 and 0.72 in two years. A different reach of the same stream yielded 0.39. Under special regulations, a restricted-regulation reach showed a lower value.

**7. Proposed management guidelines.**
- A stream with high production (about 100 kg/hectare) and moderately high angling pressure might have an ecotrophic coefficient of 0.25 to 0.50, implying harvest of 25 to 50 kg/hectare.
- If angling pressure increases, the coefficient could rise to values such as 0.75 to 0.90. In that case, restrictive regulations may be needed.
- Where the management objective is a dense population with large fish, a low coefficient of about 0.10 may be appropriate.
- High coefficients may indicate excessive harvest and may justify special protection measures.

**8. Methodological recommendations.**
- Production should be estimated with attention to population age structure, not only standing stock.
- Approximate production estimates from routine monitoring can be combined with empirical harvest data to calculate ecotrophic coefficients.
- Future experiments on regulation, habitat alteration, and species changes could strengthen these inferences.

**9. Advantages of the ecotrophic coefficient.** The coefficient is simple, numerical, and can be compared across fisheries. It is based on annual rates of harvest and production rather than standing stock, and it directly shows the proportion of energy removed by anglers. The author suggests it complements traditional management tools rather than replacing experienced fishery biologists.

**Limitations noted by the author.** Many production estimates are derived from wild trout populations, and results from hatchery-influenced systems may differ. Several ecotrophic coefficients were calculated from approximate production values, so uncertainty remains. Recommended ranges are therefore preliminary.
