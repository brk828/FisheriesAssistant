# Habitat Use by Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in the Little Colorado River and Other Colorado River Tributaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) annual report for the 1993 field season of [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Phase II documents habitat use by the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and other native fishes in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), and assesses the potential of smaller Grand Canyon tributaries to support second spawning aggregations. Exceptional 1993 reproduction, following a winter flood season and stable base flow in summer, produced a very strong year class of humpback chub. The report also records methodological problems, unmet objectives, and a proposal for additional FY94 field studies, sperm banking, and fish-health work, with coordination recommended with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The humpback chub was described in 1946 from a single specimen of unknown origin in Grand Canyon. Historically abundant in the Colorado, Green, Yampa, White and Little Colorado rivers, it is now restricted to scattered reaches and is federally listed as endangered.
- **Native range context:** Remaining populations occur in the Green, Yampa and mainstream Colorado reaches, and in the lower 13 km of the LCR, which is the principal successful spawning area in the lower basin.
- **Study area:** The LCR (entering the Colorado at river mile 61) is used as a habitat model for candidate tributaries: [[Paria River]], [[Bright Angel Creek]], [[Shinumo Creek]], [[Tapeats Creek]], [[Deer Creek]], [[Kanab Creek]] and [[Havasu Creek]].
- **Fish assemblage:** Table 1 lists 15 species from 9 families (Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Ictaluridae, Cyprinodontidae, Percichthyidae, Centrarchidae, Salmonidae). Native species are humpback chub, speckled dace, bluehead sucker and flannelmouth sucker. The remainder are introduced, including channel catfish, black and yellow bullheads, fathead minnow, common carp, plains killifish, striped bass, green sunfish, rainbow trout and brown trout.
- **Legal status:** Humpback chub is state endangered and federally listed endangered; bluehead sucker is state special concern.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mainstream recruitment limitation:** Cold hypolimnetic releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] are thought to suppress growth and recruitment of juvenile humpback chub in the mainstream. Most remaining mainstream adults are associated with the LCR, so its population appears dependent on LCR reproduction.
- **Historical tributary role:** The authors argue that pre-dam tributaries and confluences likely provided spawning habitat and nurseries, and that their current absence of humpback chub reflects mainstream population decline rather than unsuitability.
- **Flow and water quality:** Winter 1992–93 floods (peaks above 17,000 cfs in January and about 15,000 cfs in late February) scoured fine sediment, deepened pools and slowed currents. Base flow from late April to late August allowed rapid travertine formation. Summer water clarity was unusually high (turbidity as low as 1.7 NTU; secchi above 3 m), versus about 50–100 NTU in 1991.
- **Water chemistry limits:** Mattes (1993) found dissolved CO₂ fell below the published physiological limit of 200 mg/L for trout and carp at Atomizer Falls, matching the upper distributional limit of humpback chub and bluehead sucker.
- **Parasites and disease:** Lernaea (anchor worm) infestations were common on Kanab Creek fish and on YOY humpback chub in the LCR. Adult humpback chub passing tapeworms were also observed. Infection prevalence and health effects are unknown.
- **Exotic species:** Exotic fish catches (channel catfish, fathead minnow, plains killifish) rose after summer floods. Brown and rainbow trout dominated or were established in several tributaries.

## Key Findings

**LCR sampling and catches (1993)**
- Ten LCR field trips totalled 69 field days. Sampling included 42 ASU hoopnet habitat measures, 1,388 FWS minihoopnet sets, 803 minnow trap sets, 243 seine samples, 266 habitat transects and 71 fish observation transects.
- More than 17,000 fish were sampled (17,411 handled). Humpback chub made up 58% of catch; 54% of all fish were young-of-year (YOY) humpback chub.
- YOY made up 91% of humpback chub sampled versus 56% of speckled dace. Native fishes were 99.8% of all captures.
- Speckled dace were second in abundance at 34% of catch.

**Reproduction and growth**
- Peak adult humpback chub and bluehead sucker capture rates occurred in April–May, matching predicted spawning from otolith studies.
- YOY humpback chub grew from a modal 35 mm to 55 mm TL between June and July, then to 65 mm by August and about 75 mm by September. Some reached 95 mm.
- YOY humpback chub moved from shallow edges into deep main-channel habitat at 40–50 mm TL. Condition factors declined noticeably during this shift.

**Mortality**
- 53 incidental mortalities occurred, 33 of them humpback chub, all YOY under 75 mm TL. Most were "dead in net" or regurgitated by adults. The authors conclude sampling mortality was very low.

**Habitat use**
- Speckled dace and humpback chub show near-opposite daytime habitat use. Speckled dace use shallow edges with small substrates and low cover; humpback chub use deeper water with larger substrates, more vertical structure and greater cover.
- Humpback chub shift from stream-edge areas by day to the whole channel by night. Adult and subadult humpback chub partly segregate by depth, lateral position and cover.
- Day/night sampling was found essential, as most species show very different patterns between periods.
- Seine data from September 1992 gave similar patterns to passive sampling data.
- ASU hoopnet data (1991) were judged unrepresentative because net placement favoured high-capture sites, so measuring at ASU hoopnets was discontinued.
- A test of pole-based current measurement found pole velocities comparable to current meters and about 10 times faster.

**Spawning and distribution**
- Humpback chub spawning was documented in the LCR over the lower 14 km, and in springs at river mile 29 (South Canyon reach).
- Upstream distribution of YOY humpback chub reached about km 12.6; bluehead sucker YOY reached km 13.6. Atomizer Falls (about km 13.5) appears to be the upper boundary, and the Triple Drop–Atomizer Falls reach is suspected as a spawning area.

**Tributaries**
- Native fishes were common in most tributaries. Speckled dace and bluehead sucker dominated the Paria, Kanab and Shinumo assemblages, and confluence reaches were used seasonally by flannelmouth sucker and humpback chub.
- Exotic trout were present in Bright Angel, Deer, Shinumo and Tapeats creeks. Tapeats was dominated by rainbow trout.
- Havasu Creek data analysis was suppressed due to a jurisdictional dispute between the National Park Service and the Havasupai Tribe.
- Four graduate theses were completed (Allan 1993, Mattes 1993, Weiss 1993, Otis 1994 projected), covering Shinumo, Atomizer Falls, Paria and Bright Angel/Kanab creeks.

**Objectives and shortfalls**
- Objectives 1 and 2 were met in the LCR. Objective 3 (humpback chub spawning habitat) was not achieved because peak spawning is short (2–3 weeks) and occurs during flood-muddy spring conditions; only one opportunity arose. Objective 4 (river modelling) was officially abandoned, though data may support instream flow models later.
- Tributary data were judged inadequate for a full assessment of seasonal habitat and confluences.

**Proposed FY94 work**
- Five additional 10-day LCR trips (two in spring, three in summer–fall) to follow the 1993 year class, examine spawning habitat and survey Blue Springs to Atomizer Falls.
- Three 12-day Grand Canyon tributary trips (spring, early summer, late summer), plus four 4-day Paria River trips.
- Sperm cryopreservation for humpback chub and other natives, starting with LCR collections in February–April, with [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] maintaining banks.
- A fish-health epidemiology study linked to Glen Canyon Dam operations and future multi-level intake structures.
- Coordination with AGFD: AGFD focuses on early life history and mainstem spawning; USFWS focuses on habitat use and tributaries, including confluences.

**Data management**
- LCR data to November 1993 expected in dBase files by March 1, 1994; 1991–1993 files to be archived by mid-1994.
- Tributary data remained with the University of Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit, with transfer expected in early 1994.
