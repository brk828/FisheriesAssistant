# Observations on the Food Habits of Darters in Kansas

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Percidae #darters #food-habits

## Executive Summary

This study documents stomach contents of four darter species collected from Kansas streams in 1957. The author examined specimens from the [[Kansas University Museum]] and personal collections to identify food items consumed by [[Etheostoma spectabile]], [[Etheostoma caeruleum]], [[Etheostoma flabellare]], and [[Percina caprodes]]. Stomachs were dissected and contents observed under binocular microscope at 6x magnification. No organisms were identified to species level. The study provides occurrence frequencies, average numbers, and ranges of prey items found in stomachs for each darter species.

## Background & Description

The darters examined belong to Family [[Percidae]] and represent common species in Kansas freshwater systems. Collections were made from multiple stream systems including [[Drywood Creek]], [[Marmaton River]], [[Spring River]], [[Deep Creek]], [[Big Creek]], [[Little Caney River]], and [[Arkansas River]], all within or near [[Bourbon County]], Kansas.

**Etheostoma spectabile spectabile** ([[Orangethroat Darter]]): Collected from Drywood Creek and Marmaton River in Bourbon County. Total of 31 specimens examined from April 6, 1957 collections.

**Etheostoma caeruleum** ([[Rainbow Darter]]): Collected from Marmaton River in Bourbon County. Total of 42 specimens examined from April 6, 1957.

**Etheostoma flabellare** ([[Fantail Darter]]): Collected from multiple locations including Spring River, Deep Creek, Big Creek, and Little Caney River. Specimens collected at different dates on different streams.

**Percina caprodes** ([[Logperch]]): Total of 17 specimens examined.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study represents snapshot sampling of darter feeding ecology in Kansas stream systems during 1957. Collections were made from small streams where these species were encountered. The document notes that only-fish and [[Plecoptera]] were excluded from one table, suggesting these prey items may have been considered incidental or requiring separate analysis.

Stream systems sampled represent typical Kansas freshwater habitats supporting diverse darter assemblages. The presence of aquatic insects (Chironomidae, Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Odonata) and other invertebrates in stomachs reflects the benthic invertebrate communities characteristic of these systems.

## Key Findings

**Etheostoma spectabile spectabile** (31 specimens examined):
- [[Chironomidae]] larvae: 10.3% occurrence, average 1.2 per stomach, range 1-2
- [[Ephemeroptera]]: 51% occurrence
- [[Trichoptera]]: 10% occurrence
- [[Simuliidae]]: 3% occurrence
- [[Cladocera]]: 3% occurrence
- Unidentified eggs: 3% occurrence

**Etheostoma caeruleum** (42 specimens examined, April 6, 1957):
- [[Chironomidae]]: 25% occurrence
- [[Ephemeroptera]]: 50% occurrence
- [[Trichoptera]]: 25% occurrence
- [[Chironomidae]] pupae: 2% occurrence
- [[Cladocera]]: 2% occurrence
- Mites and Nematodes: 15% occurrence
- [[Simuliidae]]: 10% occurrence
- [[Isopoda]]: 5% occurrence

**Etheostoma flabellare** (specimens from multiple locations and dates):
- Spring River (1957): [[Chironomidae]] 50%, [[Ephemeroptera]] 63%, [[Trichoptera]] 25%
- Deep Creek (1957): [[Chironomidae]] 39%, [[Ephemeroptera]] 69%, [[Trichoptera]] 9%
- Big Creek (1957): [[Chironomidae]] 50%, [[Ephemeroptera]] 25%
- Little Caney River: [[Chironomidae]] 40%, [[Ephemeroptera]] 47%, [[Trichoptera]] 13%

**Percina caprodes** (17 specimens examined):
- [[Chironomidae]]: 47% occurrence, average 10 per stomach
- [[Ephemeroptera]]: 35% occurrence
- [[Trichoptera]]: 18% occurrence
- Branch stumps: 6% occurrence
- Amphipods: 6% occurrence
- Fish eggs: 12% occurrence
- Stones: 12% occurrence

The data reveal that aquatic insect larvae, particularly [[Chironomidae]] and [[Ephemeroptera]], constitute the primary food items across all darter species examined. [[Trichoptera]] appear as secondary prey items. The presence of non-food items (stones, branch stumps) in [[Percina caprodes]] stomachs may indicate incidental ingestion during benthic feeding. Sample sizes and occurrence percentages varied by species and collection location, reflecting both species-specific feeding preferences and local prey availability.
