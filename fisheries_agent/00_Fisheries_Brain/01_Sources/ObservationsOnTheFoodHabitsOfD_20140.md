# Observations on the food habits of darters in Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This short paper lists the food items recovered from the stomachs of several darter species (family [[Percidae]]) collected from Kansas streams. Each species is treated separately with its own table. Stomachs were removed, slit open and examined under a binocular microscope, usually at about 6x magnification, and organisms were not identified to species. The study covers [[Etheostoma Spectabile]] (two subspecies), [[Etheostoma Nigrum]], [[Etheostoma Zonale]] and [[Percina Copelandi]]. The dietary data are dominated by [[Chironomidae]] (midge larvae), with occasional [[Simuliidae]] (black fly larvae), crustaceans, fish eggs and empty stomachs. Note that the source is an OCR scan and several table entries are garbled, so the figures below are reported only where they are legible.

## Background & Description

The stated purpose is to catalogue the food items found in darter stomachs from Kansas streams. Specimens came from two sources: the Kansas University Museum collection and personal collections. The paper is organized so that each species has a short introduction, a data table and a brief discussion.

Taxa covered:
- **[[Etheostoma Spectabile]] spectabile**, the orangethroat darter, collected from [[Drywood Creek]] and the [[Marmaton River]] in [[Bourbon County]]. A total of 31 specimens were examined (Table 1).
- **[[Etheostoma Spectabile]] pulchellum**, collected in Carmen (possibly Cedar) Creek, [[Pottawatomie County]], on August 13, 1957. Eight stomachs were examined (Table 2). A second Pottawatomie County site, Carnahan Creek, is also mentioned in the table heading.
- **[[Etheostoma Nigrum]]** (johnny darter) and **[[Etheostoma Zonale]]** (banded darter), listed in Table 3 with KU museum numbers, sex, total length and stomach contents. Collection localities include Carnahan Creek, Shoal Creek and the [[Spring River]].
- **[[Percina Copelandi]]** (channel darter), with 17 specimens examined from the Elk River, Spring River, Shoal Creek and the [[Big Caney River]] (Table 5).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Collections were made at several Kansas streams on different dates, and the paper notes that the collections were widespread. Gut content was used as a direct indicator of what each darter had eaten at the time of capture. Fish length ranged from roughly 35 to 67 mm in the sampled specimens listed in the tables. The Spring River and Shoal Creek collections include both sexes. The recorded diet items indicate that these darters fed on benthic, small-bodied invertebrates typical of stream riffles and pools, which is consistent with the bottom-dwelling habits of the group. The paper does not analyze habitat or water chemistry.

## Key Findings

- **[[Etheostoma Spectabile]] spectabile (Bourbon County):** 31 specimens examined. Table 1 lists food items with percent occurrence, average number and range per stomach. The table text is largely unreadable after OCR, so exact values cannot be reliably reproduced.
- **[[Etheostoma Spectabile]] pulchellum (Pottawatomie County):** 8 stomachs examined, collected 13 August 1957. Table 2 is partly legible and records prey items for this subspecies.
- **[[Etheostoma Nigrum]] and [[Etheostoma Zonale]]:** Recorded items include:
  - *E. nigrum*: 3 individuals containing Chironomidae.
  - *E. zonale*: 10 individuals with Chironomus-type larvae (*Chironomus* sp. as OCR-legible), one 50 mm Spring River fish with Chironomus sp., one fish with an empty stomach, and one 67 mm fish with *Simulium* sp. (5 items).
- **[[Percina Copelandi]]:** 17 specimens examined. Stomach contents include empty stomachs, fish eggs, Chironomidae, stone fragments and microcrustaceans. One entry reports Microcrustacea at 90 %, the only percentage legible in the scanned Table 5.
- **Dietary pattern:** Across the species, [[Chironomidae]] larvae are the most frequently reported prey. [[Simuliidae]] and [[Microcrustacea]] occur less often. Several stomachs were empty, and fish eggs were found in P. copelandi.
- **Methodological limits:** Organisms were not identified to species, and no quantitative diet index is given beyond the table entries. The source is an incomplete scan; page 8 is a header fragment for a second paper, "Observations on the Food Habits of Bates," which is not included.
