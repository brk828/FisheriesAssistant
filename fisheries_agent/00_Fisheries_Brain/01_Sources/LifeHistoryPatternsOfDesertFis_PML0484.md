# Life History Patterns of Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[George D. Constantz]] (Academy of Natural Sciences, Philadelphia) compares the [[Life History]] traits of native fishes in North American desert springs and streams. It covers 16 focal species from the [[Great Basin Desert]], [[Mojave Desert]] and [[Sonoran Desert]], chosen because their natural history is well documented in the literature. The central thesis is that the stable, predictable habitats of desert springs and the variable, catastrophe-prone habitats of desert streams select for contrasting life history strategies, consistent with [[R- and K-Selection]] theory. The chapter is part of the volume *Fishes in North American Deserts* (1981, edited by R. J. Naiman and D. L. Soltz, John Wiley & Sons).

## Background & Description

**Scope.** The review focuses on ultimate, evolutionary causes of genetically based traits. It excludes major rivers, which rise in montane forests, and desert lakes, which are mostly man-made with transplanted fishes. The sample is 16 focal species from a literature-based subset of the ichthyofauna. Of these, 8 occur in streams, 4 in springs, and 4 can complete their life cycles in both habitats. The focal taxa include five cyprinodontids, three poeciliids, six cyprinids and two catostomids.

**Habitat definitions.**
- *Desert springs* are small (usually < 20 m across and < 3 m deep), warm, often oxygen-poor, and physicochemically constant. Least variable are outflows with no surface drainage. Springs are geographically isolated and highly endemic.
- *Desert streams* (arroyos or washes) are dry for most of the year but hold permanent pools that stratify and fluctuate dramatically and unpredictably in volume. Flows range from modest discharge to flash floods, which can increase discharge by five orders of magnitude.

**Ecological framing.** Noy-Meir (1973) characterized deserts as water-controlled systems with infrequent, discrete and unpredictable water inputs. Springs and streams are presented as the two ends of a continuum in their response to local weather.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spring ecosystems** are closed and autochthonous, with a depauperate fauna and few resident species. Seasonal change is driven mainly by the solar cycle and is gradual and predictable. Biotic interactions are reduced, and lack of catastrophe allows individuals to track food availability.
- **Stream ecosystems** are more complex and heterogeneous in space and time. Low flows import plant material and nutrients, and floods scour algae and invertebrates and displace fishes downstream. Dry-season refuge competition in permanent pools ("roulette competition," sensu Sale 1977) drives large interannual variation in reproductive success and density.
- **Interpretive caveat.** Constantz notes that a constant spring can be less predictable from a young fish's point of view than a fluctuating arroyo, because the environmental cycle has a longer wavelength than the fish's lifespan. The scale of observation matters.
- **Parallel patterns** in desert lizards and birds show large interannual fluctuations tied to rainfall, which the author links to the variability of stream fish populations.

## Key Findings

**Morphology**
- Stream fishes are mostly fusiform, with pointed fins, forked caudal fins and fully developed pelvic fins and lateral lines. Spring fishes are chubby and truncated, with rounded fins, rounded caudal fins, posteriorly placed dorsal and anal fins, and some lack pelvic fins or have degenerate lateral lines. [[Relict Dace]] (*Relictus solitarius*) is the type example of spring morphology.
- Body form predicts habitat type in the [[Speckled Dace]] and related forms: spring dwellers are blunt-nosed, riverine forms are more attenuated.

**Diet**
- Of the 16 focal species, 25 records are omnivorous (the review reports "25 omnivores, 1 carnivore, 2 herbivores" among the species it categorized). All spring species are omnivorous. Streams host a more diverse set of specialized carnivores and herbivores, which the author attributes to greater food-web complexity.

**Breeding season**
- Spring species breed longer. The mean breeding season is about 9.8 months (range 4–12) in springs versus about 4.3 months in streams.
- Year-round breeding is found in the [[White River Springfish]] and [[Devils Hole Pupfish]]. The [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Amargosa Pupfish]] breed year-round in warm springs but only from spring to fall in streams.
- The [[Spikedace]] breeds in a restricted period in the Red River of Texas, though a prolonged season of optimal water may explain the exception.

**Body size and age at maturity**
- Stream species mature at larger sizes on average. Half the stream species are small-bodied (26–76 mm TL). Large-bodied species occur only in streams.
- Female Amargosa pupfish from a warm spring and a desert stream matured at 24.0 and 25.9 mm SL, respectively. This difference is attributed to genetic differences.
- Gila topminnow females matured at about 20 mm SL in springs and 24 mm SL in flowing waters.
- Spring populations of the [[Desert Pupfish]], Amargosa pupfish and Gila topminnow mature in a few (1–5) months or less than one year. Stream forms often mature in about one year.
- Spring fishes show less plasticity in generation time than stream fishes, a finding the author calls counterintuitive.

**Fecundity and offspring investment**
- Stream species produce more and smaller eggs than spring fishes. Fecundity ranges from about 11 to 10,000 eggs per female in streams, while spring species average about 9 eggs (range 4–19).
- Egg diameter averages 1.5 mm (range 0.9–2.1) in six stream fishes and 1.7 mm (range 1.5–1.9) in two spring populations. The mean investment per offspring may not differ by habitat.
- Gila topminnow eggs weigh about 2.2 mg in a warm spring and 1.6 mg in a wash.
- Egg size varies seasonally: in the Gila topminnow it varies by about 22 percent over an annual cycle.

**Reproductive effort**
- Gila topminnows allocated 4.8 percent of their energy budget to reproduction in a desert wash, compared with 2.1 and 3.5 percent in a warm spring (depending on life history type). The direction fits r- and K-selection predictions.
- Warm springs may limit reproduction by high metabolic costs, while oxygen-rich streams with density-independent mortality may allow higher per capita output.

**Social behavior and mating**
- Spring males are more territorial and brightly colored. Alternative mating strategies (large territorial males versus small satellite males) appear in spring populations of the Gila topminnow and Amargosa pupfish but are not obvious in streams.
- The author attributes this to the short stream breeding season, which produces a unified cohort of males within a small size range.

**Dispersal**
- Stream fishes such as the longfin dace and speckled dace disperse over vast reaches. Spring species, with chubby bodies and rounded fins, are assumed to have poor dispersal ability.
- Desert pupfish, Amargosa pupfish and Gila topminnow swim from permanent pools into local temporary waters (about 0.5 km) after rains.
- A Cocio Wash study tested whether risk of desiccation is offset by increased fecundity. Females that dispersed to temporary pools produced more embryos and more reproductive mass than sedentary females, despite a 0.55 probability of death by desiccation. The author proposes that a genetic polymorphism for dispersing and sedentary genotypes may exist, with proximate condition triggering dispersal.

**Population characteristics**
- Stream populations show large interannual changes in density and reproductive success, sometimes local extinction (for example, the [[Gila Topminnow]] in Cocio Wash).
- In warm springs, population numbers are more stable. The Devils Hole pupfish cycles between about 200 individuals in October and about 700 in August.
- Spring populations have fewer males than stream populations, possibly because warm water accelerates male aging.

**Causes of trends**
- The author proposes three explanations: (1) water temperature, which limits energy for growth and reproduction in warm springs; (2) homology, since the important families differ by habitat (cyprinids and catostomids in streams; cyprinodontids and poeciliids in springs); and (3) analogy, through convergent selection under r- and K-selection.
- The author considers these three factors together to account for the observed differences but declines to rank them.

**Conclusions**
- Desert stream and spring fishes differ in morphology, diet, breeding season, body size, fecundity, offspring investment, reproductive effort, social behavior and dispersal, and these differences are consistent with r- and K-selection theory.
- The author finds no striking difference between desert fishes and fishes of surrounding mesic biomes, and notes that unique adaptations to desiccation occur only in other arid zones, such as diapausing annual-fish eggs and aestivating adults.
