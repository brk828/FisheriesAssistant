# Statewide Survey of 1986, 1989 and 1992 Arizona Anglers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-angling

## Executive Summary

This report, by Todd Pringle of the [[Fisheries Branch]] of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], presents the third in a series of statewide mail surveys of licensed anglers (1986, 1989, 1992). It is a socioeconomic and management document rather than a biological study. Its purpose is to help fisheries planners understand angler behavior, preferences, spending and attitudes, particularly toward sport fish and [[Native Fish Management]]. The 1992 survey, part of the Department's [[Responsive Management]] and strategic planning process, received 2,169 completed questionnaires.

Estimated angler effort in 1992 was about 7.2 million user-days, of which 2.5 million involved trout and 4.7 million warmwater species. Anglers fished mostly close to home, and user-days stayed near 7 million across the three surveys even as license sales declined. Anglers fish for multiple species, prefer [[Trout]] and [[Largemouth Bass]], and most mix bait and artificial lures. Opinions on native fish management were divided, with many respondents undecided.

## Background & Description

**Survey design.** The target population was all people licensed to fish in Arizona in 1986, 1989 and 1992, spanning eight license classes (general resident and nonresident, combination hunting-and-fishing resident and nonresident, [[Colorado River]] nonresident, 9-day nonresident, 5-day nonresident, and Urban). Samples were drawn from license sales:

| Survey | Sample | Completed | Response rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1986 | about 15,000 (4%) | 7,643 | 50% |
| 1989 | 8,000 | 2,024 | 25% |
| 1992 | 7,982 | 2,169 | 27% |

The 1986 survey used three mailing waves. Response was 33% from the first wave, 17% from the second and 8% from the third. The 1989 and 1992 surveys used a single mailing. Roughly 800 1992 questionnaires were returned as undeliverable for lack of apartment numbers. Questionnaires were double-keypunched for verification.

**Nonresponse bias.** Following Scott (1961) and Filion (1974), the 1986 study used a linear regression of observed characteristics against cumulative response rate to estimate values for nonrespondents. The same adjustment was applied in 1992. It lowered the percentage of anglers who fished by 4%, because anglers who fished were more likely to return the survey.

**Questionnaire.** The 1992 instrument repeated many 1986 and 1989 questions and added new items on attitudes and desires. Its 26 questions cover fishing participation, license purchase, effort by water type and area, lure use, species preference, retention of catch, expenditures, native fish opinions, [[Special Use Stamp]] willingness, license checks, club membership, information sources, years fished, origin, age and sex.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Where anglers fished.** Resident trout anglers mainly used lakes such as [[Woods Canyon Lake]], Big Lake and Willow Lake. Resident warmwater anglers focused on large inland reservoirs such as [[Roosevelt Lake]], Canyon Lake and [[Bartlett Lake]]. Nonresident anglers fished the Colorado River and its reservoirs. The highest-use fish management area was Area 22, with about 1.3 million user-days a year. It contains major Salt River Project reservoirs and borders the Phoenix metropolitan area. Area 25 (Phoenix metro) rose 198% since 1986, and Tucson-area use rose 142%.

**Effort by water type.** Between 1986 and 1992, Colorado River reservoir trout user-days fell from 211,000 to 77,000 (−37%). Streams and rivers declined about 26%. City Waters rose 255% and Irrigation Canals 223%, the latter two linked to the Department's Urban Program. Anglers are increasingly fishing close to home.

**Population and license context.** Arizona's population grew steadily since 1960, but license sales declined. License sales were down about 18% from 1986 to 1992. The Department found that the historical link between population and license sales was no longer reliable because of changing demographics and economic conditions.

**Statewide context.** The survey places fishing within a broader economic picture. The Department cited the 1991 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service National Survey, which reported about $79 per day spent on fishing in Arizona, and a state multiplier of 1.8 from the U.S. Department of Commerce.

## Key Findings

**Effort and participation**
- Anglers fished about 7.2 million user-days in 1992 (2.5 million trout, 4.7 million warmwater). Across the three surveys, effort averaged about 7.1 million user-days.
- 93% of 1992 respondents fished in Arizona, up from 89% in 1986.
- Mean days fished rose from 17.7 in 1986 to 22.7 in 1992. The median rose from 9 to 12 days. The decline in occasional anglers (under 3 days) suggests a shift toward more committed participants. The difference between 1986 and 1992 was significant (p < 0.05).
- Roughly 60% of anglers fished 6 to 50 days per year. Anglers who fished 11 or more days in 1992 made up a larger share than in 1986.
- Over 60% of anglers fished in one or two management areas. About 38% fished only one.

**Target species and preferences**
- Trout and largemouth bass were the top preferred species in every survey year (trout 34.3% and largemouth bass 29.0% in 1992). [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Striped Bass]] followed.
- Time spent on trout dropped from 39.2% (1986) to 29.3% (1992). Largemouth bass rose from 15.2% to 24.0%. Mean effort across years was 33.8% trout, 23.5% "anything that bites," 18.2% largemouth bass and 9.2% striped bass.
- About 75% of anglers fished for multiple species. The share fishing only for trout fell from 20.5% (1986) to 16.3% (1992).
- Anglers preferred fewer, larger trout. Asked to choose, 46% of respondents (about 4 of 12-inch trout rather than six 8.5-inch trout) selected the 12-inch option. Some respondents wrote in other choices, and the "Other" category rose from 4% to 14%.

**Fishing methods and retention**
- In 1992, 67% of anglers mixed bait and artificial lures, 18% used only lures or flies and 15% used only bait. Bait-only anglers fell from 31% (1989) to 15% (1992).
- The belief that keeping some fish is needed for a successful trip rose from about one third in 1989 to 54% in 1992. Overall, about 43% held this view across the surveys.

**Spending**
- The median spending was $50 per day, the mean $57.27 (n = 1,870). The most common band was $26 to $50 (35%).
- Multiplying about $50 per day by 7.2 million user-days gives roughly $360 million a year. Using the federal figure of $79 per day gives roughly $569 million. Applying the 1.8 multiplier to the $360 million estimate gives an economic benefit of about $648 million.
- Nonresident anglers spent more per day than residents ($75 vs. $50). Bait-only anglers spent the least (about $39 per day). Largemouth and smallmouth bass anglers spent the most (about $68.75).

**Native fish management**
- 37% of anglers said sport fish management should not be disrupted to protect native fish. 26% wanted more protection even if it restricted sport fishing. 37% were undecided.
- 31% supported managing native fish such as [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Apache Trout]] as sport fish. 22% opposed this and 47% had no opinion.
- In the 1992 general-public Trend Survey, 65% favored protecting native fish, 22% favored sport fish and 13% were unsure. [[Desert Pupfish]] was cited in the question as an example of a rare native species.

**Management options**
- Special use stamp (Question 14): 41% in favor, 43% against, 16% no opinion. Support was strongest among anglers aged 20 to 39.
- [[Lee's Ferry]] (Question 16): 46% preferred the present management, 29% a special use stamp, 5% no harvest, and 20% gave other answers.
- [[Becker Lake]] quality-fishery regulations (Question 17): 68% had no opinion, 23% supported, and 9% opposed. Many respondents were likely unfamiliar with the lake.

**Enforcement and community**
- 38% of 1992 anglers (about 126,000) had their license checked by a Department officer. Officers were seen by 67% of anglers (about 221,000), and those fishing more than 6 days were most likely to encounter them.
- 11% of anglers belonged to a fishing club or organization, mostly men aged 20 to 59 who fish with artificial lures and flies.

**Information sources and demographics**
- Weekly newspaper fishing reports were used "some" by most anglers. Local tackle shops were used "a little." Residents relied more on newspaper reports, and nonresidents on tackle shops.
- The average angler had fished for 32 years, but only 17 in Arizona. 41% had fished in Arizona for 10 years or fewer, suggesting a new-to-state angling public. [[California]] was the most common prior residence (20.8%).
- The 1992 resident profile: 76% male, median age 36, 94% of user-days, mainly in the Phoenix and Tucson metro areas, fishing large reservoirs and mountain trout lakes.
- The 1992 nonresident profile: 84% male, median age 44, 6% of user-days (about 430,000), mainly fishing the Colorado River and its reservoirs.

**Planning implications**
- The Department uses these data in its Coldwater and Warmwater Fisheries Strategic Plans and will incorporate the 1992 results into the [[Wildlife 2000 Strategic Plan]], its guiding document through the year 2000. The goal is to provide varied fishing opportunities while protecting Arizona's fisheries.
