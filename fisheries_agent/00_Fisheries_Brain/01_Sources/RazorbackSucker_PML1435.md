# Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from [[Life On The Edge]], Volume 1: Wildlife, profiles the [[Razorback Sucker]], a long-lived endangered fish of the [[Colorado River]] basin. It is listed as endangered by both federal and state agencies. The text covers the species' biology, habitat needs, spawning behavior, range, and population decline, and describes the causes of decline: dam construction, cold-water releases, and predation by nonnative fishes. It also summarizes conservation actions, including hatchery propagation, reintroduction programs, and a critical habitat proposal. The document is a book excerpt, not a research study, so it reports no original sample data beyond a few historical counts.

## Background & Description

- **Common names:** Called "humpback," "bullhead," or "buffalo fish" in the past. The name "razorback" refers to the bony hump behind the head.
- **Appearance:** Dark olive or brown above and creamy yellow below, with a variably colored yellowish-to-reddish band along the side. Colors are usually brightest during spawning season.
- **Breeding traits:** Small bumps (tubercles) appear on the back half of the body of both sexes and are most pronounced on breeding males. Females are larger than males.
- **Longevity:** Individuals are long-lived. All razorbacks sampled in two early-1980s surveys of [[Lake Mohave]] were 22 to 44 years old, which raises the possibility that the species may no longer reproduce successfully in the wild.
- **Diet:** Algae, detritus, and aquatic insect larvae are believed to be the principal foods of adults. Juveniles feed mainly on plankton and detritus.
- **Historical importance:** Razorbacks were an important food fish for prehistoric Native Americans, who caught them in specially designed weirs, and they supported a commercial fishery in southern Arizona into the early 1900s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and seasonal behavior**
- Native to large rivers of the Colorado River basin. Adults prefer reaches with sandy, muddy, or rocky bottoms and sparse aquatic vegetation.
- For much of the year, small schools hold in shallow, slow-moving eddies and backwaters beside stronger currents. Some individuals occur over sandbars in faster main channels.
- In winter, fish gather in schools along lake shorelines. Specific habitat requirements are poorly understood and likely vary through the year.

**Spawning**
- Spawning runs occur from January through April, when water temperatures reach about 10–12 °C (50–54 °F). Spawning sites include lake bottom areas and river riffles where coarse sand or gravel has been cleaned of silt by currents.
- A ready female leaves the school with two or more males. The males fertilize the eggs she releases into a depression at least 18 cm (7 in) deep.
- Each female may spawn repeatedly and releases only a fraction of her roughly 12,000–140,000 eggs per spawning act. Eggs hatch in about a week, depending on water temperature.

**Range and distribution**
- Large populations once occupied the major rivers and tributaries of the Colorado River basin from Wyoming to northern Baja California, Mexico. The fish was also present in the [[Salton Sea]] from about 1905 until 1929, when the sea became too saline.
- Wild river populations now persist only in the Green, Colorado, lower Yampa, and lower San Juan rivers of the upper basin, which spans Wyoming, Colorado, and Utah.
- Apart from a large population in [[Lake Mohave]], recent records in the lower basin are few. A small, possibly relictual, nonbreeding population may persist in [[Lake Havasu]].

**Threats**
- **Dams:** The 15 dams built in the lower basin since the mid-20th century have severely altered habitat. They block spawning migrations, and cold water released from reservoir depths chills rivers for many miles downstream. Below [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], summer water temperatures average below 15 °C (59 °F) for 105 km (65 mi), and below [[Lake Powell]] for 383 km (238 mi). Razorbacks prefer 22–25 °C (72–77 °F) and are rarely seen in these chilled stretches.
- **Temperature effects:** Cooler water can slow egg development, reduce hatching success, and lower larval survival.
- **Nonnative species:** Introduced sport fish, such as [[Trout]] and bass, compete with razorbacks. Predation by [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Green Sunfish]] is also significant. Biologists believe nonnative fish that eat eggs and larvae are the main reason little recruitment is occurring.
- **Rotenone poisoning:** In a still-controversial 1962 project at Flaming Gorge Reservoir, the piscicide rotenone was released along roughly 216 km (135 mi) of river to remove "trash fish" and nonnative species.

## Key Findings

- **Decline:** Surveys from 1980 to 1989 estimated fewer than 1,000 razorbacks remained in the upper basin. Only sporadic records existed in the lower basin in recent years, except at Lake Mohave, which supports about 60,000 fish.
- **Reproductive concern:** Razorbacks in the wild are believed to be not reproducing successfully, largely because nonnative fish eat their eggs and larvae.
- **Legal status:** The species was added to California's list of rare species in 1971, reclassified as endangered by the state in 1974, and listed as federally endangered in 1991.
- **Critical habitat:** A proposal would protect 2,935 km (1,824 mi) of the Colorado River for the razorback sucker and three other Colorado River fishes under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- **Conservation history:**
  - In the 1970s, biologists began documenting the decline and propagating the fish in hatcheries.
  - State and federal agencies initially opposed reintroductions and habitat protection, citing concerns about sport fisheries and land use.
  - In 1981, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the Arizona Game and Fish Department signed a ten-year agreement allowing reintroduction of hatchery-raised razorbacks into Arizona rivers. Reintroductions continue today.
  - California began a lower Colorado River reintroduction program in 1996 but abandoned it in 1999, citing budget constraints among other reasons.
