# Life-History Strategies Predict Fish Invasions and Extirpations in the Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether [[Ecological Niche Theory]] combined with the [[Winemiller and Rose Model]] of fish [[Life-History Theory]] can explain long-term patterns of nonnative fish spread and native fish range contraction in the [[Colorado River Basin]] over roughly 150 years. The authors compiled 22 morphological, behavioral, physiological, trophic and life-history traits for 90 freshwater fish species (28 native, 62 nonnative). They then mapped these species onto a triangular life-history adaptive surface and compared the positions of species with historical range change, using about 28,755 georeferenced locality records from the [[SONFISHES Database]].

The central conclusions are that nonnative species occupy a much broader functional space than natives, that the fastest-spreading nonnatives are equilibrium-leaning species occupying niche space vacant of natives, and that native species with the greatest declines fall into two groups. One group overlaps strongly with rapidly spreading nonnatives, which is consistent with biotic interactions. The other is near the periodic endpoint, which is consistent with environmental change. The authors conclude that life-history and niche perspectives are useful for distinguishing biotic from abiotic drivers of fish invasions and extirpations in the American Southwest.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** The Colorado River drains approximately 632,000 km² across seven US states and northwestern Mexico. [[Glen Canyon Dam]] marks the boundary between the upper and lower basins. The upper basin is fed mainly by snowmelt, while the lower basin receives flow from winter storms and late-summer monsoons. Long isolation and fluctuating conditions have produced one of the most distinctive freshwater fish faunas in North America. Since European settlement, dam building, irrigation and nonnative introductions have sharply reduced native fish populations.

**Theoretical framework.** The authors treat a species' ecological niche as a multidimensional space defined by traits. Niche opportunities arise when a nonnative species possesses traits that differ from natives and are favored by the environment (sensu [[Niche Opportunities]] and Shea and Chesson 2002). The [[Winemiller and Rose Model]] (1992) describes three life-history strategies that lie at the endpoints of a triangular continuum:

- **Periodic:** large-bodied, late maturation, high fecundity, low juvenile survival, seasonal environments. See [[Periodic Strategy]].
- **Opportunistic:** small-bodied, early maturation, low fecundity, low juvenile survival, disturbed and unpredictable environments. See [[Opportunistic Strategy]].
- **Equilibrium:** small- to medium-bodied, moderate maturation age, low fecundity, high juvenile survival through parental care, constant environments. See [[Equilibrium Strategy]].

This model extends the classic r-K framework by splitting the r strategy into periodic and opportunistic types. Before this study, it had been applied to fish population responses only once, and never to fish invasions or extinctions.

**Hypotheses.** The authors made three predictions:

1. Native and nonnative species pools differ in total niche space, with nonnatives occupying niches created by hydrologically stable, dam-regulated flows.
2. Native and nonnative pools occupy different regions of the life-history adaptive surface.
3. Rates of nonnative spread and native decline over 150 years reflect differential selection of life-history strategies under contrasting environmental and biological regimes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Trait and species data.** Traits were compiled from state fish-fauna texts, primary literature, agency reports, theses, online databases ([[FishBase]], Arizona Heritage Data Management System, Biota Information System of New Mexico) and expert knowledge. Trait values were drawn from Colorado River Basin research where possible. Traits covered habitat (fluvial dependence, current velocity, substrate, temperature preference), trophic guild and diet breadth, and reproductive traits (longevity, age and length at maturation, fecundity, egg size, parental care, time to hatch, larval length). Ordinal and nominal traits were assigned by majority evidence, and continuous traits by median values.

**Distribution data.** The [[SONFISHES Database]] holds more than 38,000 occurrence records spanning over 150 years across the Lower Colorado River Basin, georeferenced to within 1 km. Analyses used 28,755 locality records from 1840 to 2000, excluding translocations and reintroductions, for 23 native and 47 nonnative species present in the lower basin. Historical records were those from 1840–1979, and extant records were those from 1980–1999. Range size was measured as total river kilometers occupied, and percent decline was calculated from the change in range size.

**Statistical approach.** Because the 90 species span 15 families and 49 genera, the authors judged ecological constraint more likely than phylogenetic constraint to shape patterns. A [[Mantel Test]] showed only a marginal link between trait and phylogenetic distance (p = 0.08). Gower's similarity coefficient was used for mixed data types, and [[Principal Coordinate Analysis]] summarized trait variation. Life-history overlap was calculated as the inverse Euclidean distance between a species and the centroid of the opposing pool in three-axis life-history space (log maturation length, log mean fecundity, and investment per progeny). Analyses were run separately for species preferring slow currents and those preferring moderate to fast currents.

**Representative species.**

- **Native:** [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Moapa Dace]], and cutthroat trout. The Gila topminnow and cutthroat trout are the native exceptions with more endpoint-like positions.
- **Nonnative:** [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Red Shiner]], common carp, channel catfish, bullheads, buffalos, [[Largemouth Bass]], western mosquitofish, guppy, and brown trout, among others.

## Key Findings

**1. Niche space differs between pools.** The first two PCoA axes explained 35.5% of total trait variation. Nonnative species occupied most of the ordination space and showed much greater niche diversity than natives. The first axis separated most natives from most nonnatives (t = 2.54, P = 0.01). The lower-left quadrant held almost exclusively nonnatives, except for the razorback sucker and bonytail. Nonnatives in this "new" niche space generally lack a fluvial requirement, prefer slow, warm water, are generalist feeders, mature early and small, and produce small, quick-hatching eggs.

**2. Pools occupy different life-history positions.** Most native species lie at intermediate positions, mainly along a line from the opportunistic endpoint toward the midpoint of the periodic–equilibrium edge. Nonnatives span all three endpoints. Their positions define the opportunistic–periodic edge strongly, and several sit in the equilibrium region that is vacant of natives.

**3. Distributional trends follow life-history strategy.**

- Natives nearest the periodic, opportunistic and equilibrium strategies showed relatively large declines. The greatest declines were in Colorado pikeminnow, desert pupfish, Moapa dace and bonytail.
- Nonnative equilibrium strategists and species along the opportunistic–equilibrium axis spread fastest. The three fastest-spreading species were fathead minnow, green sunfish and red shiner.
- Nonnatives nearer the periodic region spread more slowly.

**4. Three patterns link native decline to nonnative spread.**

- Along the opportunistic–equilibrium axis, the natives with the greatest declines overlap with the fastest-spreading nonnatives.
- Natives near the periodic endpoint declined most but have no overlap with fast-spreading nonnatives, which are absent from that region.
- Regression results depended on current preference:

| Group | Relationship | Statistics |
|---|---|---|
| Native, slow-water | No relationship | P = 0.84 |
| Native, moderate–fast | Significant negative | P = 0.01 |
| Nonnative, slow-water | No relationship | P = 0.46 |
| Nonnative, moderate–fast | Significant negative | P = 0.04, R² = 0.21 |

Nonnative species that prefer slow water (typical of reservoirs) spread independently of life-history overlap with natives. Those that prefer riverine habitats spread less where they overlap with natives, which suggests [[Biotic Resistance]] matters when niches are matched.

**5. Mechanistic interpretation.**

- Nonnatives exploit niche opportunities created by [[River Regulation]] and reservoirs. Natives are less adapted to these novel, stabilized environments.
- Native declines along the opportunistic–equilibrium axis are consistent with biotic interactions, namely predation and competition with nonnatives.
- Native declines near the periodic endpoint are consistent with [[Environmental Resistance]]. Periodic life histories suit seasonal conditions that have become temporally stable, and these fish face high predation and reduced floodplain inundation.
- The equilibrium strategy is favored under low habitat variation and strong biotic interactions. This matches present-day lower-basin conditions with heavy predation by piscivorous nonnatives and reduced resource pulses from regulated flows.

**6. Caveats.** The study uses adult traits and therefore misses overlap at early life stages, where predation between natives and nonnatives is important. The authors note that the negative relationship between native decline and overlap may partly reflect this limitation. Museum-based data also carry sampling biases, although the authors state that records underwent strict quality control.

**7. Conservation implications.** The authors argue that life-history and niche theory can support conservation planning and risk assessment for imperiled fishes, particularly by identifying which native species are vulnerable to nonnative spread and which are vulnerable to flow alteration. They note that present conservation plans for the lower Colorado River need to balance these competing requirements.
