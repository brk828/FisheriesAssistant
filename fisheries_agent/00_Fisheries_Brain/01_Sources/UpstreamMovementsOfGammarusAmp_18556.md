# Upstream Movements of Gammarus (Amphipoda) in Doe Run, Meade County, Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #amphipoda

## Executive Summary

This 1964 paper by W. L. Minckl7ey (Department of Zoology, Arizona State University) documents large-scale upstream movements of amphipod crustaceans in [[Doe Run]], a large spring stream in [[Meade County]], Kentucky. Among six amphipod species in the system, only [[Gammarus Bousfieldi]] migrated upstream in large numbers. The paper frames these movements against the classic [[Drift Hypothesis]] and the [[Colonization Cycle]] proposed for stream insects, arguing that amphipods which spend their whole lives in water may recolonize denuded upstream reaches by moving against the current. The study is observational, based on about 350 field days (October 1959 to August 1962), with detailed observations on only three days.

## Background & Description

**Stream drift.** Benthic invertebrate drift had received growing attention, beginning with [[Needham 1928]] studies of fish food organisms and [[Denham 1938]], who coined the term "benthoplankton" and showed drift to be a constant feature of streams rather than a flood-only event. Amphipods are major drift components in northern Europe and the northern United States.

**Directed movements.** Upstream movement in invertebrates may reflect rheotaxis, ontogeny, flood avoidance, or responses to temperature and food. A notable example is the mayfly *Blasturus cupidus* (= *Leptophlebia cupida*), whose nymphs were recorded moving up to 600 ft in a day before emergence. Few upstream amphipod movements had previously been documented; prior work included [[Hynes 1955]] and [[Hynes 1960]] on *Crangonyx pseudogracilis* and *Gammarus pulex* in England, and [[Schmitz 1960]] on *G. tigrinus* in Germany, where upstream spread was only a few meters.

**Study site.** Doe Run is a spring stream with a modal discharge of about 30 cubic feet per second (cfs). Discharge is usually 15 to 20 cfs from June through early November and 60 to 100 cfs in the remaining months. Maximum discharges near 600 cfs were recorded at the source on two occasions between February 1959 and July 1962. The creek is 9.7 miles long and enters the [[Ohio River]]. Five stations were established in 1959, from near the source (station I) to 7.7 miles downstream (station V). Three mill dams lie between stations I and III, and the downstream reach was impounded in 1961, inundating stations IV and V.

**Amphipod fauna.** Six species occur in the system. *Gammarus bousfieldi* and an aberrant form of *G. minus* are abundant. Three *Crangonyx* species are known, two undescribed and *C. forbesi* (all rare), and *Synurella dentata* occurs in some tributaries but not the main stream. *G. bousfieldi* is identifiable in the field by its pronounced banding, lacking in *G. minus*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Conditions during the main observation (May 15, 1960):** discharge about 25 cfs at the source, water temperature 13.3 °C at the source to 18.2 °C at station IV, turbidity 15 mg/l, and no unusual chemistry.
- **Habitat use:** *G. bousfieldi* is a pool-inhabiting form that appears endemic to Doe Run ([[Cole and Minckley 1961]]). *G. minus* lives in channel mosses. Upstream of station III, thick aquatic vegetation occurs; downstream, marl deposition limits vegetation to algae and a few emergent plants.
- **Flow context:** Discharge had been slowly declining from a December to March peak. Movements in January 1961 followed the first spate since mid-July 1960. Movements in March 1961 followed high February discharges (near 600 cfs) that were decreasing.
- **Flood response:** Both *Gammarus* species likely drift during floods, but *G. bousfieldi* appears better able to hold position in pools and eddies and then recolonize upstream once modal flow resumes.

## Key Findings

- **Mass upstream migration:** On May 15, 1960 (10:00 am to 4:00 pm) large numbers of *G. bousfieldi* moved upstream from station IV through station I. Movement used silty banks, eddies, riffle margins and riparian roots, and was partly directed by shorelines and vegetation edges rather than current alone.
- **Foraging interruptions:** Near dense vegetation at station II, one tagged male (17.3 mm) traveled about 15 linear ft in roughly half an hour. It stopped 19 times, foraged, and was swept downstream three times before collection.
- **Winter migration:** On January 8, 1961, a Surber sampler set reversed, opening downstream, captured about 50 individuals per ml (205 ml displacement) in about 4 minutes. Captured animals were mostly large adults of *G. bousfieldi* (mean length 11.8 mm, range 6.8 to 18.2 mm), with about 20 *G. minus* and 2 *C. forbesi*. No upstream movement was seen at stations II and III that day.
- **Spring movement:** On March 18, 1961, fewer than 100 *G. bousfieldi* were seen moving upstream along banks below station I, consistent with earlier patterns.
- **Species contrast:** Of six amphipod species, only *G. bousfieldi* migrated in large numbers. Two drift samples from August 1961 under modal flow caught only *G. minus*.
- **Proposed mechanism:** Upstream movement appears to be tied to recolonization after floods, and the author suggests it may reflect a highly specialized behavior of *G. bousfieldi*.
- **Compensation for drift:** Short upstream movements of only a foot or two per day could maintain headwater populations, and the presence of nonflying invertebrates at spring sources supports this idea.
- **Summary conclusion:** Some amphipod species may recolonize denuded upstream reaches by moving against the current. Other species may use more subtle, shorter-distance movements to offset washout of upstream populations.
