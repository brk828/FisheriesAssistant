# A Predator-Prey Interaction in Freshwater Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 paper, based on a PhD thesis, examines predation by [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) on [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus*) in [[Paul Lake]], near Kamloops, [[British Columbia]]. Paul Lake was chosen because it holds only these two fish species, which allowed the predator-prey relationship to be studied with few confounding factors. The study finds that predation is governed by the distribution and movement of both species, lake morphometry, shoal water temperature, and fish characteristics such as size. Adult trout hunt shiners in a stereotyped, visible surface pattern. Trout under 10 inches rarely eat shiners in the wild, though they will do so in captivity. The estimated seasonal kill is about 150,000 shiners, against a shiner population of at least several million. Predation therefore does not limit shiner recruitment or population size. Predation by large trout has instead partly offset the competition that shiners impose on small trout, and growth of trout over 14 inches now appears higher than before the shiners arrived.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** Paul Lake is a small lake in the Kamloops region of British Columbia. Its physical and chemical features were described by Rawson (1934) and Larkin et al. (1950). The redside shiner, a cyprinid, was introduced to the lake around 1945 and expanded rapidly. Trout are Kamloops-type rainbow trout, and the lake has been stocked with liberated trout since 1953.

**Trout population.** A sample of 1,649 trout from the anglers' catch in 1955 and 1956 ranged from 5.25 to 23.5 inches fork length, with a mean of 12.4 inches (315 mm). The largest recorded trout weighed 9.5 lb (4.3 kg), taken in 1931. Maximum age appears to be 5 to 6 years.

**Shiner population and life history.** A sample of 5,321 shiners taken in minnow traps from June 8 to September 15, 1955, had a seasonal size mode of 2.5 to 3.5 inches, with the largest fish at 4.5 to 5 inches. Growth estimates for September 1 were roughly 1 to 1.5 inches in year 0, 1.5 to 3 inches in year I, and 3 to 4 inches in year II. In five samples totalling 178 shiners, there were 87 females and 91 males, which did not differ significantly from a 1:1 ratio (P = 0.8). Spawning ran from about late May to early August. Eggs are adhesive, broadcast between dusk and dawn, and hatch in about 8 to 10 days.

**Methods.** Stomach analyses of anglers' catches, visual observation of predation, trough experiments on captive trout, and growth analysis using [[Size-Specific Growth Rate]] calculations were all used. Observations were unrepeated and the author describes them as non-conclusive, though suggestive.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and movement.** Trout are generally spread through the lake except during spawning migration and midsummer, when they concentrate in areas with heavy shiner density. Shiners show a daily cycle: they move randomly over the shoal by day, move into deep water at night, and return to the shoal at dawn. Seasonally, they shift with shoal water temperature. They occupy the shoal in mid-May and June, the shoal edge in mid-July and August, the shoal again in September, and deep water (about 30 feet) in winter.

**Overlap in time and space.** These movements bring predator and prey together for about 82 days in summer, on the shoal and at the shoal edge where shoal development is extensive. Where shoal development is low, shiners do not congregate and predation is low. Predator and prey are thought to be largely separated in winter.

**Habitat features.** Visible predation occurred only in restricted areas with the greatest shoal development and highest concentration of [[Chara]]. No predation was seen along the rocky, steep shoreline. Visibility in calm, clear July and early August conditions was exceptional, and sunlight reflecting off shiner scales may have made them easier for trout to detect.

**Seasonal diet.** Bottom organisms dominate the trout diet in spring, late fall, and winter. Shiners dominate in July, August, and early September. Shiners were never the dominant food for trout under 10 inches, nor for any trout size in winter.

## Key Findings

**1. Predation intensity depends on trout size.** In August 1956, shiners made up 6% of food volume for 106 trout under 10 inches, 79% for 439 trout of 10 to 14 inches, and 94% for 253 trout over 14 inches. The proportion of trout that had eaten shiners in the anglers' sample was 5% for 5 to 10 inches, 16.3% for 10 to 13 inches, and 46.7% for trout over 13 inches.

**2. Visible surface predation follows a fixed pattern.** Adult trout (over 10 inches, often 16 to 20 inches) swim beneath schools at about 6 feet depth, then rise rapidly, producing two audible splashes as they engulf one or two shiners at the surface. They pursue individual shiners, typically targeting the less active 3- to 4-inch fish on the school's fringe. Intensive predation was recorded on 21 days in July 1956 (heavy), with medium to light intensity in August and light to medium in September.

**3. Small trout rarely feed on shiners in the wild.** Small trout (4 to 6 inches) were seen only with shiner schools and did not usually enter water shallower than 15 inches. In captivity, four 6-inch trout fed on shiners after a one-week fast at a mean rate of 0.14 shiner per trout per day. The presence of larger shiners did not stop them from attacking smaller shiners.

**4. Diet and stomach data.** In a 1955 sample of 783 trout, 627 were immature and 156 were maturing. Of these, 128 had eaten shiners, 76 immature and 52 maturing. In a separate sample of 815 trout in 1955, 134 had eaten shiners. The mean corrected stomach volume was 4.3 cc for trout that ate shiners and 2.0 cc for those that did not. Trout over 14 inches averaged 2.5 shiners per stomach when feeding on them, compared with 1.2 for 6 to 10 inch trout.

**5. Estimated kill is modest relative to the population.** Mid-season estimates were 12,500 trout of 5 to 10 inches, 2,490 of 10 to 14 inches, and 855 over 14 inches. About 1,430 trout, under 9% of the estimated population of roughly 15,845 trout over 6 inches, had eaten shiners on any one day. Shiner consumption over the 82-day season was estimated at about 148,000, in a table totalling 148,576. The abstract gives the annual kill as 150,000. Predation peaked July 1 to late September. The text gives the total trout population as 15,845, while the table total is 15,875; both are reported as printed.

**6. Shiner numbers are not controlled by predation.** Lindsey (1953) estimated Paul Lake held 5,000,000 to 100,000,000 shiners before the 1950 spawning. Larkin and Smith (1954) estimated several million in 1952. Against these figures, the kill is small. Only about 5% of eaten shiners were young of the year. Most were adults that had probably already spawned in August, so predation neither limits recruitment nor removes large numbers of breeding adults before reproduction.

**7. Predation has partly offset competition effects on trout growth.** Shiners competed strongly with small trout for food, which depressed their growth by 1952. Growth of trout over 8 inches in 1955 to 1956 exceeds that in 1946 to 1949 (the period before shiners), and the curves cross at 8 to 12 inches. Growth of small trout remained depressed. The author notes that weight-based gains may be underestimated by length-based growth rates. Trout eating shiners were also deeper and thicker in body form.

**8. Marked liberated trout eat less than unmarked trout.** In 1955 and 1956, marked trout of nearly all sizes ate smaller volumes of shiners than unmarked trout. Among trout over 14 inches, the mean volume for unmarked trout in 1956 was 5.9 cc, compared with 2.6 cc for marked trout. The author attributes this to shorter lake residence, which supports the idea that trout need time to shift from omnivory to piscivory.

**9. Summary conclusions.** Trout predation on shiners does not control shiner numbers. Predation increases with trout size and lake residence time, and it follows a fixed, observable pattern. Large trout have partly compensated for shiner competition, and small trout have not. The paper concludes that trout abundance is set by factors other than shiner abundance.
