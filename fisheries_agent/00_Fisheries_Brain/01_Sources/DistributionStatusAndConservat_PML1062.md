# Pecos Pupfish: Distribution, Status, and Conservation of *Cyprinodon pecosensis*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This New Mexico Department of Game and Fish technical report (Hoagstrom and Brooks, 1998) assesses the distribution, status, and conservation of the [[Pecos Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon pecosensis*), a small endemic cyprinodontid of the [[Pecos River]] Basin. Sampling of all known populations from 1987 to 1998 found the species persisting in only two populations: a relatively large population in Chaves County, New Mexico, and a small population in [[Salt Creek]], Texas. Habitat loss from groundwater depletion, flow regulation, saltcedar invasion, and channel degradation has reduced the species from roughly 750 km of historic mainstem range to about 70 km of valley habitat. Genetic contamination by [[Sheepshead Minnow]] hybrids is a further major threat. The report closes with short-term and long-term conservation measures.

## Background & Description

The Pecos pupfish likely originated in the late Pleistocene, possibly from an ancestor similar to the sheepshead minnow (*C. variegatus*), after isolation of the Pecos River drainage (Echelle and Echelle, 1978, 1992). Its close relatives include the Leon Springs pupfish and the Comanche Springs pupfish, which do not typically co-exist with it.

Morphology and behavior described in the document:
- Deep-bodied, with a silver-white body bearing brown-gray vertical bars; upturned mouth; a single dorsal fin; no lateral line.
- Males defend polygonal (often hexagonal) breeding territories over uniform substrates in dense populations at peak breeding season (Echelle et al., 1990).
- Females were reported to produce fewer, larger eggs per clutch at higher density (Kodric-Brown and Mazzolini, 1992).
- Diet was described by Davis (1981) as omnivorous, mainly diatoms, animal material, and detritus.
- Tolerates salinities well above typical freshwater species but is excluded above roughly 35 ppt for egg and larval development (Kinne and Kinne, 1962, as cited).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Pecos River runs from the Sangre de Cristo headwaters through the Roswell, Carlsbad, Delaware, and Toyah basins to Texas. Historically, the river was perennial in New Mexico and wetlands, playas, and sinks (e.g., Bitter Lake, Laguna Grande de la Sal) were widespread, fed by artesian [[San Andres Limestone]] and [[Rustler Formation]] groundwater.

**Anthropogenic change.** Key drivers of habitat loss include:
- Artesian flow decline in the Roswell Basin after 1900; water table drops of up to 8.5 m.
- Groundwater pumping in the Toyah Basin after 1940, causing a depression of more than 61 m.
- Reservoir construction (e.g., Sumner Dam, 1932; Brantley and Avalon dams) and flow capture.
- Saltcedar (*Tamarix* spp.) introduced around 1912, linked to channel incision and reduced water tables.

**Habitat types used by the species.** Springs and spring-fed wetlands (under 20 ppt) support robust, sympatric populations. Isolated sinkholes and evaporative pools (over 20 ppt) often hold only pupfish, which are stunted and at risk of desiccation.

**Sinkhole dynamics.** Extinctions occurred in sinkholes from low dissolved oxygen, high salinity, and desiccation. Dispersal between sinkholes, through overland flood flow or canals, shows the value of [[Habitat Connectivity]].

**Hybridization.** [[Sheepshead Minnow]] established in the Pecos River between 1980 and 1984. Hybrids with *C. variegatus* spread through the mainstem, and genomic introgression at 15 localities in 1984 ranged from 18 to 84% sheepshead minnow ancestry.

## Key Findings

1. **Current status.** Pecos pupfish are present along roughly 70 km of the Pecos River valley, with populations limited to small, isolated localities. Formerly they were common along roughly 750 km.
2. **Surviving populations.** Only two populations remain: the Chaves County, NM population (Bitter Lake NWR, Bottomless Lakes SP, BLM Overflow Wetland, Salt Creek NM) and the Salt Creek, TX population.
3. **Salt Creek, TX.** This is the only known genetically pure population outside Chaves County, NM. It is threatened by erosion, livestock trampling, saltcedar, road construction, drought, and hybrid invasion, and is rated a high conservation priority.
4. **Extirpations.** The Laguna Grande de la Sal population (Pupfish Spring) was presumed extirpated after salinity rose to 92.8 ppt in 1997 and 95.7 ppt in 1998. Malaga Bend losses were also documented.
5. **Hybridization threat.** Hybrid pupfish were collected from riffles and runs in the Pecos mainstem, and hybrids reached Loving Crossing and Pierce Canyon. Genetic contamination was documented at the Phipps gravel pits in Texas by 1998.
6. **Micro-population dynamics.** Pecos pupfish colonized sinkholes by natural dispersal and stocking. At Salt Creek, NM, a population fell from 15-20 ppt to 200 ppt after a rainstorm following a range fire in 1994, then recovered to 32 ppt by 29 September 1994.
7. **Survey effort.** Sites were sampled 1987-1998 by seining, minnow trapping, and dip netting. Minnow traps were the most common method, and dip netting was least effective. Specimen records came from the Museum of Southwestern Biology, the Texas Natural History Collection, and the Oklahoma State University Collection of Vertebrates.
8. **Appendices.** Appendix I lists extant localities with recent survey data (Chaves County, Eddy County, and Texas sites). Appendix II lists sites where the species was absent. Appendix III documents hybrid localities. Appendix IV compiles historic collections. The full tables were summarized rather than reproduced here.
9. **Short-term conservation measures.** These include protecting floodplain and tributary habitat, controlling saltcedar, monitoring Salt Creek discharge and water quality, regulating oil and gas drilling and pipelines near BLNWR/BLSP, preventing non-native introductions, and monitoring for hybrids. If Salt Creek populations decline, hatchery stocks should be established.
10. **Long-term conservation measures.** These include regulating groundwater pumping (begun in Chaves County in 1966, which reversed habitat loss), protecting groundwater from contamination, restoring river channel and floodplain, eliminating [[Sheepshead Minnow]] from the Pecos River, and reducing the range of hybrids.
11. **Overall conclusion.** Habitat loss caused the decline, and genetic contamination now threatens the remaining pure populations. Recovery depends on maintaining suitable habitat through groundwater regulation, saltcedar control, range management, and protection of occupied habitat.
