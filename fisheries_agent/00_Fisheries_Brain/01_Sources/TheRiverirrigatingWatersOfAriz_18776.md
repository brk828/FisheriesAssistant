# The River-Irrigating Waters of Arizona: Their Character and Effects

## Executive Summary

Bulletin 44 of the [[University of Arizona Agricultural Experiment Station]] (R. H. Forbes, Tucson, September 30, 1902) reports a year-long sampling programme (August 1899 to August 1900, with some records to 1901) of the silt and soluble salt content of Arizona's three main irrigation rivers: the [[Salt River]], the [[Gila River]] and the [[Colorado River]]. The study finds that water quality, not only quantity, determines agricultural value. Flood waters carry heavy silt and fertilizing nitrogen; low waters carry concentrated salts that threaten soils through alkali accumulation. The bulletin also quantifies reservoir siltation risk and the monetary value of river-borne fertilizer.

## Background & Description

The Station began regular sampling in summer 1899 using a stoppered tin cylinder (13 in. long, 2 in. diameter, about 1.25 lb of water) lent by [[Elwood Mead]] of the USDA. Daily samples were combined into sets of 6 to 10 and analyzed by [[W. W. Skinner]], assistant chemist. The author notes that the method under-represents coarse bed-load, which is important during floods. The study places the work in the context of salinity-driven farm abandonment in the [[Pecos Valley]] and orange-tree losses at [[Lake Elsinore]].

Key methodological notes:
- Analytical chemistry used Hehner's method for alkalinity and permanent hardness.
- Specific gravity of silts was measured for each sample set.
- Settling tubes and glass cylinders were used to measure mud volume relative to solid sediment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salt River.** Watershed of 6,260 sq. mi. above McDowell (Verde watershed about 6,000 sq. mi.). The Salt catchment is largely within the [[White Mountain Indian Reservation]] and remains in near-primeval condition. The Verde has been degraded by grazing, mining and lumbering, and produces sharper, muddier floods. Study-year flow was about one-third of the normal average.

**Gila River.** Watershed of 17,834 sq. mi., drier and less forested than the Salt. Heavy overstocking has depleted grasses and produced violent, sediment-laden floods; the author describes it as perhaps "the muddiest river in the world."

**Colorado River.** Drainage of about 225,000 sq. mi., with a normal flow near 15,000,000 acre-feet per year. Snowmelt gives a June peak near 50,000 second-feet, and the river is far fresher in character than the desert rivers.

**Soils.** Salt River Valley soils are calcareous, containing calcium and magnesium carbonates, with little organic matter. Caliche (limy hardpan) underlies many fields. The river's chloride salts dissolve this hardpan over years of irrigation.

## Key Findings

- **Salt River silt:** about 676,000 tons removed by canals in the year; about 85% (575,000 tons) carried in 38 flood days. With seepage loss allowance, about 1,000,000 tons of flood-water mud were estimated for the year.
- **Reservoir filling:** the proposed [[Tonto Basin Reservoir]] (840,000 acre-feet capacity at 190 ft) would take a severe minimum of 180 years to fill with sediment, and far less if the White Mountain reservation were logged or leased.
- **Specific gravity of silts:** Salt River 2.604 average (range 2.249 to 2.703); Gila 2.672 (2.580 to 2.766); Colorado 2.652 (2.581 to 2.694).
- **Mud volume:** after 10 to 30 days of settling, mud was 6.2 times the solid volume on average for the Salt, Gila and Colorado (ranges 5.6 to 7.5, 5.0 to 8.3 and 4.5 to 9.5 by river and period).
- **Fertilizing value (Salt River):** about $1,733,000 at Eastern market values for nitrogen, phosphoric acid and potash taken out by canals; net value after deductions about $500,290, or about $4.17 per acre over 120,000 acres.
- **Salt concentrations:** Salt River ranged from 52 to 157 parts per 100,000; 139 parts averaged June to August 1900. Gila ranged from 39.4 to 120 parts. Colorado averaged roughly 93 parts in winter and as low as 21 to 32 in spring and early summer.
- **Threshold:** the author judged about 100 parts per 100,000 (about 2,700 lb per acre-foot) an objectionable but manageable salinity. Salt River exceeded it for 302 days; Gila for 138 days; Colorado for 50 days.
- **Maximum silt:** Gila 9.41% by weight (Sept. 8 to 14, 1900); Colorado 2.072% (Oct. 8 to 13, 1900); Salt River 0.951% (Sept. 1 to 9, 1899). Minimum values were 0.0036% (Salt, June-July 1900) and 0.0326% (Colorado).
- **Remedies:** flooding leaches salts; deep irrigation and deep cultivation prevent surface salt accumulation; drainage is needed for subirrigated districts such as the Tempe, Phoenix and Buckeye areas.
- **Colorado as "American Nile":** the author compares the Colorado delta to the Nile delta in agricultural potential and proposes the [[Salton Basin]] as a site for reclaiming alkaline land by leaching.

**Source-level limitations:** the bulletin reports single-year data, uses tin-cylinder sampling that misses coarse bed-load, and contains no biological (fish or invertebrate) observations.
