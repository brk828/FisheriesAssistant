# Annotated List of the Freshwater Fishes of Ecuador

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ostariophysi

## Executive Summary

This volume extract (pages 236–269 of a journal issue) contains three items. The main item is M. M. Ovchynnyk's annotated checklist of the [[Freshwater Fishes of Ecuador]]. It records 306 species in 159 genera and 44 families, compared with 118 species reported by [[Fowler 1954]]. The list is organised by [[Ecological Groups]] (primary, secondary and peripheral) and by the three [[Ecuadorian Geographic Regions]]. The first item is a reference list from a study of catfish cranial morphology (Gauba), and the last is an unrelated paper on [[Monogyny in Ant Societies]] by C. Baroni-Urbani. The fish checklist is the only substantive scientific content in the extract.

## Background & Description

Ecuador is the smallest South American republic except Uruguay, with an area of about 287,925 km². It has three regions: the eastern [[Oriente]] (Amazon slope, about half the territory), the Andean [[Sierra]] (about one quarter), and the [[Coastal Zone]] on the Pacific slope. The author notes that the country's freshwater fish fauna is poorly known. Little is known of the biology of any Ecuadorian species, and the total number of freshwater species and subspecies is estimated at about 400.

The checklist uses the [[Myers Classification]] of ecological groups:
- **Primary**: confined to fresh water.
- **Secondary**: live in fresh water but can tolerate sea water for a limited time.
- **Peripheral**: closely connected with the sea, often entering rivers for reproduction or feeding.

Species are arranged alphabetically within their families, and families are arranged systematically. Each entry gives locality data, often with provinces, rivers and museum catalogue numbers. Collections examined include the [[Michigan State University Museum]], the [[United States National Museum]], the [[American Museum of Natural History]], the [[Field Museum of Natural History]] and the Escuela Politécnica Nacional in Quito.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography and climate**
- Oriente: dense tropical forest and flat jungle, with rivers in the [[Amazon Basin]] (Napo, Pastaza, Marañón systems). Lakes include Zancudo Cocha, Cuyabeno, Garza Cocha and Limón Cocha. Temperatures never exceed 38 °C.
- Sierra: two Andean cordilleras with inter-Andean valleys. Plateaus lie at 2,378–2,896 m, and the average temperature is about 15 °C. Lakes include Yaguarcocha, Cuicocha, San Pablo and Quilotoa.
- Coastal Zone: 19–160 km wide, mostly flat and drained by rivers flowing to the Pacific, including the [[Rio Guayas]] (to the Gulf of Guayaquil) and the [[Rio Esmeraldas]]. The climate is moderated by the cold [[Humboldt Current]], and average temperature at Guayaquil is 27 °C.

**Biogeographic hypotheses**
Citing Eigenmann, the author summarises four ideas on the origin of the fauna:
1. The tropical American fauna of the Pacific slope resembles that of the Atlantic slope and predates the uplift of the Andes.
2. Peculiar genera and species in western rivers arose after the Andes became a barrier.
3. The Coastal (Guayas) fauna differs considerably from the Magdalena (Colombia) and the Peruvian Pacific slope, except for a small northern Peruvian region.
4. Some western Ecuadorian fauna derives from Central America, and some from the ocean.

**Distribution patterns**
- Coastal Zone (195 entries in Table 2): primary 57.44%, secondary 5.13%, peripheral 37.43%. The Rio Chone has the highest peripheral share among rivers (44.44%). Guayaquil has 88.23% peripheral fishes.
- Oriente: primary 90.7%, secondary 6.2%, peripheral 3.1%. Rio Curaray is 100% primary and Rio Bobonaza 95.8%.
- Sierra: 100% primary (excluding introduced species).

The peripheral fishes of the Oriente, including *Potamotrygon*, *Plagioscion*, *Potamorrhaphis* and *Colomesus*, are described as marine relicts. They lie more than 3,000 miles from salt water at the mouth of the Amazon.

## Key Findings

**Checklist totals**
- 306 species, 159 genera, 44 families (Table 1). Fowler (1954) had reported 118, so the new figure is more than twice as large.
- The author estimates about 400 species and subspecies in Ecuadorian freshwaters.
- Ostariophysi dominate: 16 families (37%), 109 genera (69%) and 224 species (74%).
- Characidae, Anostomidae and Hemiodontidae, which some ichthyologists treat as one family, contain 51 genera and 105 species. The body text gives 33% and 34%, and the summary gives 32%, for the genus share. The discrepancy is in the source.
- Largest families by species (Table 1): Characidae (73 species, 35 genera), Pimelodidae (20 species, 8 genera), Loricariidae (37 species, 16 genera), Anostomidae (24 species, 12 genera), and Trichomycteridae (12 species). Characidae and Loricariidae are the most species-rich families.

**Notable records**
- Introduced highland species: *Salmo gairdneri* and *Salmo trutta* (both now important in fish culture), and *Carassius carassius* and *Cyprinus carpio*. Dr. Gustavo Orcés reported the introductions.
- *Electrophorus electricus* (Electric Eel) is recorded from Garza Cocha and the Río Aquarico.
- Five *Astroblepus* species and two *Trichomycterus* species are endemic to the highlands, with *Rhoadsia minor* found at 300–1,260 m.
- The Oriente lakes and rivers hold large characins and piranhas (*Serrasalmus*, *Myleus*, *Metynnis*).
- Several species were described from cave systems, including *Astroblepus pholeter* from a cave emptying into the Misahuallí River.

**Other items in the extract**
- Gauba (Catfish Cranial Morphology): this section is only a bibliography of catfish skull studies (e.g., Ramaswami 1948, Rastogi 1963, Stadtler 1951, Sharma 1958, Nelson 1954). It contains no data of its own.
- Baroni-Urbani (Monogyny in Ant Societies): this paper reviews initial polygyny versus monogyny in ant colonies. It notes that colonies founded by several queens (pleometrosis) can become monogynous, and that *Myrmecocystus melliger mimicus* in Arizona has cooperating queens, as many as five in exceptional cases (Wheeler 1917). It is an entomological paper with no fish content.
