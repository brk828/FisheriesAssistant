# Production and Harvest of Bait Fishes in Ponds (Michigan, 1949–1950)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This Michigan Department of Conservation report (Miscellaneous Publication No. 6, June 1953, by [[Sheldon B. Hedges]] and [[Robert C. Ball]]) tests whether native bait fishes can be reared and harvested in farm-type ponds at the [[Hastings State Fish Hatchery]]. Pressure on natural streams from bait demand and new seining restrictions motivated the work. Four species were raised in 1950 ponds: [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Northern Creek Chub]], and [[White Sucker]]. The authors conclude that small ponds can produce a marketable crop within one season, and that [[Glass Trap]]s are the most flexible harvest method, though they require adequate light and a clear-water pond.

## Background & Description

Bait dealers were unable to meet fishermen's demand for specific sizes and species year-round, and relied on distant natural streams. Michigan had altered minnow regulations so that only designated streams could be seined commercially, and all streams were opened to glass-trap harvest only. The Institute for Fisheries Research therefore examined pond rearing, reasoning that it would cut transport costs, supply bait when demand peaks, and reduce pressure on natural waters.

The experiments used ponds 2, 4, 5 and 6 at the [[Hastings State Fish Hatchery]], formerly used for bass and bluegill. The ponds were artificial, drainable, earth-bottomed, and fed by a spring-fed creek through a screened supply reservoir. Dominant vegetation was pondweed (*Potamogeton*) and stonewort (*Chara*).

Four species were studied:
- **Fathead minnow** (*Pimephales promelas*): a small fish (never over 3.5 in), tolerant of handling and well suited to pond culture.
- **Golden shiner** (*Notemigonus crysoleucas auratus*): up to 10 in, a preferred bait but prone to scale loss when handled in warm weather.
- **Northern creek chub** (*Semotilus atromaculatus*): one of the most desirable Michigan bait species, reaching about 10 in.
- **White sucker** (*Catostomus commersonii*): a bottom feeder, related to but not in the minnow family (Cyprinidae).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fertilization and water clarity.** Ponds were fertilized with a 10-6-4 commercial fertilizer to boost fish food organisms and reduce higher plants. Repeated applications (100 to 300 lb per pond) never produced the intended algal bloom. Visibility fell in some ponds but never below about 1 ft, and the bottom was visible by July 18. Pond 6 kept its bottom visible all season and carried heavy plant growth, which may have caused fish losses on draining.

**Water quality and temperature.** Dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide and alkalinity were checked every two weeks and showed no detrimental conditions. Water temperature started above 75 °F and did not drop below 69 °F during summer.

**Spawning biology.** Fathead minnows spawned on boards placed in the pond. Spawning peaked in June and July and ceased around August 14, with a pause during cool weather. Seven nests averaged 7,387 eggs per nest (506 eggs per square inch). Creek chubs spawn in gravel streams at about 60 °F, and white suckers spawn in swift streams. Both fail to reproduce successfully in small ponds, so their fry were obtained from the Department.

**Interference.** Tadpoles became a nuisance by late August, requiring finer sorting screens. Bullheads were considered as a predator on tadpoles but complicated harvest.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and production (1950)**

| 4 | 0.7 | White sucker | 70,000 fry | 40,311 fish (drained Sept 4) | 64.5% return on stocking |
| 5 | 1.4 | Golden shiner | 450 breeders (321/acre) | Final harvest data lost | 18,007 salable, partial harvest |
| 6 | 0.4 | Creek chub | 30,000 fry (75,000/acre) | 17,462 fish (drained Sept 5) | 58.2% return on stocking |

- **Fathead minnow:** Harvest began late July. Only 13% of fish at final draining were salable, and the authors suggest glass traps selectively took larger fish.
- **Golden shiner:** The final fall harvest was lost when a drain-box structure failed.
- **Creek chub:** Harvest began August 10. Fish averaged over 3 in after 96 days.
- **White sucker:** Pond was probably overstocked. Fish were too small for the late-summer market, so holding them over until spring was suggested.

**Values.** Retail prices were roughly twice wholesale. Typical per-fish values ranged from $0.01 to $0.10 wholesale for various sizes. Creek chubs and suckers commanded higher prices as bass- or pike-size bait.

**Harvest methods compared**
- **[[Seine]]s:** Effective in a competent crew but injure fish, are hampered by filamentous algae and *Chara*, and are unsuitable for deep or soft-bottomed ponds. A 200 ft seine was suggested for a 1–2 acre pond.
- **Drainable ponds with catch basin:** Essential for complete harvest. The catch basin should be outside the pond to avoid sediment. Lowering water in hot weather kills many fish.
- **[[Lift Net]]s:** Ineffective without artificial feeding, because unfed golden shiners scattered before the net was lifted.
- **Glass traps:** Recommended as the most flexible method. Operated by one person, causing less injury than wire traps, and offering about $3 per hour for 12 traps at wholesale prices.

**Glass trap experiments**
- Several short lifts (every 15 minutes) produced more minnows than one long lift: 253 vs. 82 fish per trap hour.
- Suckers required 60 to 90 minute sets with traps pushed into the sand so the funnel sat level with the bottom.
- Catch per trap hour: fathead 0.316 fish/hr (salable), sucker 0.065, golden shiner 0.229, creek chub 0.388.
- Daylight produced the best catch, with none after dark. Soda crackers were the most effective bait. Decoys were unnecessary. Shallow sets outperformed deep sets. Turbid or cloudy water reduced catches.
- Preliminary barometric data suggested steady pressure favored catches, but the authors called this inconclusive.

**Conclusions**
- Small ponds can raise marketable bait within the first summer, especially golden shiners and fathead minnows, which spawn readily in ponds.
- Creek chubs and white suckers need fry from external sources, typically the Department's hatchery program.
- Early and continuous harvest is recommended, since food availability appears to be the limiting factor.
- Pond bait-rearing carries high capital costs and business risk. The authors advise beginners to start it as a side line.
