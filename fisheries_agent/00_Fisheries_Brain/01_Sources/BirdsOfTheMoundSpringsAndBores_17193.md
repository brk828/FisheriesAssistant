# Birds of the mound springs and bores, south and west of Lake Eyre with special reference to the Coward Springs area

#ornithology #mammalogy #arid-zone-ecology #mound-springs #wetland-ecology #lake-eyre-basin

> **Note on scope:** This source paper does not contain fish or ichthyological data. It documents the avifauna and, secondarily, the mammal fauna associated with South Australia's artesian mound springs and bores. The note below has been structured to faithfully reflect the terrestrial/wetland vertebrate ecology actually covered, per the aquatic-ecosystem-context framing (mound springs as an inland freshwater habitat).

## Executive Summary

A 1978 survey (supplemented by field notes from 1976–1982) examined bird and mammal communities associated with the **mound springs and artesian bores** south and west of [[Lake Eyre]], South Australia, with special focus on the **Coward Springs** area. Approximately **150 bird species** were recorded across four main habitat types: mound springs, artesian bores, creek beds, and desert plains (salt lakes were not surveyed). The study found that **artesian bores**, not the mound springs themselves, are the primary refuge and staging habitat for birds — supporting almost **120 species**, including regionally significant populations of Palaearctic migratory waders. Mammal trapping and observation revealed a **general paucity of small mammals** directly associated with the springs, with native fauna being largely desert-adapted and only incidentally dependent on spring/bore water.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Mound springs and artesian bores south and west of Lake Eyre, South Australia, centered on the [[Coward Springs]] district.
- **Sites surveyed:** Mound springs — Welcome, Davenport, Emerald, Horse, Blanche Cup, The Bubbler, Coward, Jersey, Warburton, Strangways, and springs near the Peake Telegraph Station ruins.
- **Bores surveyed:** Welcome, Nunn's, Morriss, Angas, Coward Springs Bore, Peake Ruins Bore, Jacob's Bore, Beautiful Valley Bore, Kaladare, Pandiburra, Big Bore, Crow's Nest Bore, Purni Bore, Raspberry Creek Bore.
- **Other habitats:** Creek beds (Gregory, Stuart, Margaret, Dillinna, Paisley, Anna Creeks), desert plains (gibber and sand-plain), and salt lakes (not visited).
- **Vegetation:** Dominant emergent/riparian vegetation at productive bores included Cumbungi (*Typha* sp.) and sedges (*Cyperus* spp., especially *C. laevigatus*), the latter critically important as foraging/concealment habitat for inland waders.
- **Historical baseline:** Prior to bore drilling and cattle introduction, mound springs reportedly had much stronger flows, larger sheets of surface water, and denser vegetation, suggesting a historically greater ecological role than observed in 1978.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Mound Springs
- Few birds recorded overall (~50 species); most were **opportunistic species**, not habitat specialists.
- Large bird numbers occurred only at springs with **extensive open shallow water** (e.g., [[Emerald Spring]], [[The Bubbler]]).
- **Fencing from stock** correlated strongly with vegetation density and resident breeding success — Emerald Spring (fenced) supported breeding [[Australian Crake]] (*Porzana fluminea*) and [[Clamorous Reed Warbler]] (*Acrocephalus stentoreus*).
- Unfenced springs supported breeding only by common, widespread opportunists: [[Masked Lapwing]] (*Vanellus miles*), [[Red-capped Plover]] (*Charadrius ruficapillus*), [[Black-fronted Plover]] (*Charadrius melanops*).

### Artesian Bores
- Support the **largest and most diverse avifauna** in the study area (~120 species vs. ~50 at springs).
- Function as critical **habitat, drought refuge, and staging posts** for migratory shorebirds (Palaearctic waders).
- Key hydrological/vegetative features driving bird use: **open water area** and **surrounding vegetation** — both absent at low-flow bores (e.g., Jacob's, Beautiful Valley), which then resemble depauperate mound-spring habitat.
- **Bore capping/flow control** case study: Welcome Bore, controlled in June 1980, saw species counts per visit drop from 35–45 down to 10–15, with no new species recorded post-control — direct evidence that **artesian flow volume determines avian carrying capacity**.
- Isolation from roads (reduced human disturbance) also correlated with higher bird use (Welcome Bore vs. the more road-accessible, similarly vegetated Coward Springs Bore).

### Creek Beds
- Generally poorly vegetated watercourses, but riparian woodland/shrubland patches support **120+ species**.
- Permanent/semi-permanent waterholes (e.g., Blower, Wood Duck on Stuart Creek; Yard Waterhole on Paisley Creek) provide breeding sites for [[Australasian Grebe]] (*Tachybaptus novaehollandiae*) and at least 5 duck species; ≥11 raptor species, 3 parrot species, and many passerines breed in adjacent trees.
- Dry, well-vegetated creek beds still support raptors and drought-tolerant insectivores; some species survive on dew alone in winter.

### Desert Plains
- Small resident/opportunist avifauna with wide central-arid Australian distributions, e.g., [[Cinnamon Quail-thrush]] (*Cinclosoma cinnamomeum*), [[Gibberbird]] (*Ashbyia lovensis*), [[White-winged Fairy-wren]] (*Malurus leucopterus*), [[Thick-billed Grasswren]] (*Amytornis textilis*).

### Salt Lakes
- Not surveyed directly, but noted to provide massive episodic waterbird habitat (pelicans, cormorants, ducks, gulls, terns) and island breeding sites during high-rainfall years.

### Mammal Ecology (Supplementary)
- Mound springs' hard ground and sparse vegetation = **unsuitable habitat for small mammals**; none trapped at spring sites.
- Denser vegetation around uncontrolled bore outflows was more structurally suitable but still yielded **no captures**.
- Springs/bores primarily serve as **watering points for large mammals**: [[Dingo]] (*Canis familiaris dingo*), [[Red Kangaroo]] (*Megaleia rufa*), [[Euro]] (*Macropus robustus*).
- Native small-mammal record (via skeletal remains, notably from a raptor eyrie on Hamilton Hill) included [[Long-Haired Plague Rat]] (*Rattus villosissimus*), *Leggadina forresti*, *Sminthopsis macroura*, *Pseudomys australis* — all desert-adapted species with independent water-conservation mechanisms, indicating **springs/bores have no direct necessity for their survival**.
- Introduced mammals: [[Rabbit]] (*Oryctolagus cuniculus*), cattle, Dingo.
- One [[House Mouse]] (*Mus musculus*) trapped near water on the Margaret River — vegetation regeneration around bores could favor this species.

## Key Findings

1. **Bores >> Springs for avian biodiversity:** Artesian bores support ~120 bird species vs. ~50 at mound springs, due to superior open-water area and vegetation cover.
2. **Palaearctic wader importance:** Coward Springs–area bores are confirmed regular (not just passage) habitat for [[Sharp-tailed Sandpiper]] (*Calidris acuminata*) and [[Greenshank]] (*Tringa nebularia*), plus regular small numbers of [[Wood Sandpiper]] (*Tringa glareola*), [[Marsh Sandpiper]] (*Tringa stagnatilis*), [[Red-necked Stint]] (*Calidris ruficollis*), and [[Curlew Sandpiper]] (*Calidris ferruginea*) — overturning earlier assumptions that these species were rare inland.
3. **Vagrant records:** [[Grey Plover]] (*Pluvialis squatarola*), [[Little Curlew]] (*Numenius minutus*), [[Terek Sandpiper]] (*Tringa terek*), [[Latham's Snipe]] (*Gallinago hardwickii*), [[Red Knot]] (*Calidris canutus*, at Lake Eyre South), and potential vagrants [[Mongolian Plover]] (*Charadrius mongolus*) and [[Long-toed Stint]] (*Calidris subminuta*).
4. **Notable single-event record:** ~1,000 migratory waders observed at Welcome, Nunn's, and Coward Springs Bores in September 1978 (>750 at Welcome Bore alone), most departing within a week — illustrating the **ephemeral, condition-dependent nature** of inland wader staging.
5. **Cox's Sandpiper distinction:** A [[Pectoral Sandpiper]] (*Calidris melanotos*) was positively identified at Welcome Bore, distinguished from the then-newly-described, morphologically similar [[Cox's Sandpiper]] (*Calidris paramelanotos*).
6. **Waterfowl:** [[Grey Teal]] (*Anas gibberifrons*) is the dominant duck species (flocks to 500), one of few ducks confirmed breeding at bores; [[Pacific Black Duck]] (*Anas superciliosa*) also common. Deep-water specialists [[Musk Duck]] (*Biziura lobata*) and [[Blue-billed Duck]] (*Oxyura australis*) were absent from bores due to insufficient water depth.
7. **Rare/vagrant waterbirds:** [[Freckled Duck]] (*Stictonetta naevosa*) occasional; [[Chestnut Teal]] (*Anas castanea*) recorded as a vagrant at Crow's Nest Bore.
8. **Other notable breeding/resident species at bores:** [[Australian Crake]], [[Purple Swamphen]] (*Porphyrio porphyrio*, >40 individuals at Welcome Bore), [[Spotless Crake]], [[Black-tailed Native-hen]], [[Dusky Moorhen]]; vagrants [[Buff-banded Rail]] and [[Baillon's Crake]].
9. **Raptors and iconic species:** [[Marsh Harrier]] (*Circus aeruginosus*) frequent despite being considered scarce inland; [[Brolga]] (*Grus rubicundus*) regular at Welcome Bore with confirmed breeding at Morriss Bore; [[Blue-winged Parrot]] (*Neophema chrysostoma*) uses bores as apparent overwintering habitat after breeding in Tasmania/southern Victoria/SE South Australia.
10. **Historic first SA records:** [[Eastern Grass Owl]] (*Tyto longimembris*) and [[Grey Grasswren]] (*Amytornis barbatus*) first recorded for South Australia at Pandiburra Bore during a 1975 survey.
11. **Management implication — flow control impact:** Direct causal evidence that reducing bore flow (Welcome Bore, 1980) sharply reduces avian species richness and abundance, with no compensatory recolonization — supporting conservation recommendations to leave select isolated, off-road bores at undiminished flow (e.g., Peake Ruins, Kaladare, Pandiburra, Big Bore, Crow's Nest Bore, Purni Bore).
12. **Mammal paucity:** Low mammal trap/observation success at springs and bores attributed possibly to preceding dry years; native mammal fauna is fundamentally desert-adapted and not water-dependent on spring/bore systems, unlike the avifauna.
13. **Excessive/wasteful flow noted:** Raspberry Creek Bore, flowing at ~13.5 megalitres/day (mostly wasted), produces a *Typha*-choked swamp with no open water — suitable only for crakes, rails, grassbirds, and reed-warblers, but **unsuitable for waders**, flagged as a candidate for flow control.
