# Suckers of Utah Lake: Taxonomy, Identification and Nomenclatural History

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This review by A. Gaylon Cook (Bioconservation International) organizes and critically reanalyzes the published descriptions of the catostomid suckers of [[Utah Lake]], central Utah. The author argues that long-standing confusion over the number and identity of Utah Lake suckers arose from incomplete use of the taxonomic literature, from unresolved synonymies, and from disputed hybrid hypotheses. The paper compares morphological, osteological and larval characters of the three year-round catostomids: [[June Sucker]] (*Chasmistes liorus*, federally endangered), [[Webug Sucker]] (*Chasmistes fecundus*) and [[Utah Sucker]] (*Catostomus ardens*). It also addresses the [[Exceptional Sucker]] / [[Mountain Sucker]] complex (*Catostomus generosus* / *C. platyrhynchus*). The stated aims are to provide a basis for future DNA-DNA hybridization and other chemical taxonomic work, and to give field biologists characters for identifying suckers during [[Recovery Plan]] surveys.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** Utah Lake is a 38,800-ha freshwater body measuring 23.5 by 38.0 km, with a mean depth of 2.9 m and a maximal depth of 4.2 m. It drains more than 6,700 km² and formerly received a flow of about 740 million m³/yr (figures as cited in the source from Carter 1969, Radant and Sakaguchi 1981b and Arnold 1960).

**Core problem.** The author states that a dispute over species number reflects ignorance of the ichthyological literature. Some Utah Lake taxa may be endemic species or subspecies deserving endangered status, but this remains unknown.

**Nomenclatural history.** The paper traces the names through the 1800s and early 1900s, including:
- Specimens of the Exceptional and Mountain Suckers collected near Provo by [[Cope and Yarrow 1876]] and placed in several genera (*Minomus*, *Pantosteus*, *Acomus*, *Notolepidomyzon*).
- [[Jordan and Gilbert 1881]] and [[Jordan and Evermann 1896]], who moved *fecundus* between *Catostomus* and *Chasmistes* and eventually settled it in *Chasmistes*.
- Doubts raised by [[Snyder 1922]] about the provenance of *Catostomus generosus* from Cottonwood Creek, Utah, which the author judges unsupported. The author argues that the Gunnison/Beckwith collections are authentic and that the Utah stream was probably Little or Big Cottonwood Creek.

**Mountain Sucker synonymy.** The author concludes that the status of *platyrhynchus*, *jarrovii* and *generosus* is unresolved. Measurements of the type material suggest that *generosus* is probably not *platyrhynchus* and may equal *jarrovii*. The author notes that Utah examples "must be reexamined."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Ecological relevance.** Utah Lake supports year-round catostomids and spawning runs. The June Sucker spawns in the lower [[Provo River]], and larval identification there is central to recovery surveys. The author notes that the June Sucker is named for the month in which spawning most often peaks.

**Spawning timing.** Reported spawning months differ:
- June Sucker: April, and June for the peak cited by Lowder (1951).
- Webug Sucker: likely June.
- Utah Sucker: March, late March to early April, April, or April–May, depending on the source.

**Population and size changes.** Little recruitment of young into the June Sucker population in recent years has been associated with a rising average size. Reported spawner means were 2.4 kg among 35 fish in 1991 and 2.3 kg among 57 fish in 1992. The 1978–1979 netting sample of 126 suckers (81% June Suckers) had a mean mass of 1.6 kg.

**Hydrologic history.** A severe drought from 1932 to 1935 left Utah Lake almost dry in the winter of 1934–1935. [[Miller and Smith 1981]] tied this event to their hybrid-origin hypothesis.

## Key Findings

**1. Taxonomic confusion is largely historical and unresolved.** The Exceptional/Mountain Sucker synonymy is still disputed. The author shows that several published synonymies rest on lost or unexamined type material. The author judges the claim that *Catostomus generosus* was collected at Cottonwood Creek, Utah, to be well founded, and rejects Snyder's (1922) argument for a Colorado origin.

**2. Diagnostic characters for the Mountain Sucker complex** (from the source's compiled literature):
- HL/TL 17.4–21.4%; anal rays 7 (+2 rudimentary); lateral-line scales 79–87; scale rows above the lateral line 14–17; below 11–14.
- Body depth/TL 13.5% in *platyrhynchus* versus 17.4% in *jarrovii*; dorsal rays 11 versus 9–10.
- Skull: thick and lacking a frontoparietal fontanelle in *generosus*; thin with a slit-like fontanelle in *platyrhynchus*.

**3. Larval identification problem.** Larval suckers collected from the lower Provo River in 1982–1984 and reared in 1985 were identified by the Colorado State University Larval Fish Laboratory as Mountain Suckers, not June Suckers. Shirley's (1983) ontogeny of the putative June Sucker was also based largely on Mountain Sucker material, according to the author. Diagnostic larval values include:
- Myomeres from snout to vent in the first two larval stages: 36 ± 1 (n = 22) in Mountain Sucker versus 38 ± 1 (n = 13) in June Sucker.
- Predorsal length as % of body length in stages 3 and 4: 50 ± 1 and 51 ± 1 (Mountain) versus 48 ± 1 and 49 ± 1 (June).
- Dorsal-fin length as % of body length: Mountain 13 and 17; Utah 15 and 19; June 17 and 22 (stages 3 and 4 as cited from Snyder and Muth 1988).

**4. Adult morphology of the three year-round suckers.**
- *Webug Sucker*: smallest year-round catostomid, TL 30–33 cm, mass normally under 0.5 kg. Has a decurved, protuberant snout, a long thin caudal peduncle and large mandibles (~36% of HL). Stubbs (1966) concluded it is much more closely related to the June Sucker than to the Utah Sucker.
- *June Sucker*: head dorsally depressed, with a nearly flat, broad interorbital; smooth, nonpapillose lips; large, oblique mouth; very small fins. Gill rakers 37–53 (mean ~44) on the first arch.
- *Utah Sucker*: most elongate and fusiform; TL ~46 cm, modal mass ~0.7 kg, maximum ~2.3 kg; thick, wide, papillose upper lip (4–8 rows); smallest mouth (horizontal or slightly oblique mandibles, under the stated % of HL); long, broad anal fin reaching the caudal base.

**5. Meristic counts are not discriminating.** Lowder (1951) recorded completely overlapping scale counts. The author shows that lateral-line scale counts (S₁) overlap widely across all three taxa, and that dorsal-fin ray counts have increased with time in all three species. Anal ray counts were 7 in most reports, with 8 in some Lowder samples.

**6. Weberian mechanism as a useful character set.** [[Stubbs 1966]] showed that the [[Weberian Mechanism]] carries many interspecific differences. Intraspecific variability is greatest in the Utah Sucker and least in the Webug Sucker. Key differences include:
- Shape of the scaphium's dorsal process (acute in June Sucker, caniniform in Utah Sucker, gently peaked in Webug Sucker).
- Shape of the intercalarium (cochlear in June, falcate in Utah, with no dorsal flange in Utah).
- Neural arch of vertebra 2 (scutiform in Webug, ephippioid in June, lachrymiform in Utah).

**7. Hybrid hypothesis is challenged.** [[Miller and Smith 1981]] proposed that the Webug Sucker is a hybrid of *Catostomus ardens* and *Chasmistes liorus* that arose before the 1932–1935 drought, and that the post-drought *Chasmistes liorus mictus* is a self-perpetuating intergeneric hybrid. The author argues that:
- The hypothesis rests on very small samples (3 pre-drought and 9 post-drought June Suckers) and untested statistics.
- Photographs in the report show mouth forms inconsistent with the authors' categories.
- Stubbs found many non-intermediate characters in the Webug Sucker, which argues against hybrid origin.
- Gel electrophoresis on *Chasmistes cujus* shows that a presumed hybrid was a variant of that species, suggesting pleomorphism within *Chasmistes*.

**8. Prior identification studies are weak.** Lowder (1951) examined about 6,700–6,783 suckers but did not statistically compare measurements or set morphometric boundaries. Stubbs (1966) examined more than 1,000 catostomids and osteologically examined 25 specimens. The author notes that Stubbs found "not one" intermediate specimen among these, though individual characters were intermediate.

**9. Recommendations for future work.** The author calls for:
- [[DNA-DNA Hybridization]] and other biochemical, immunological and protein-sequencing studies on Utah Lake suckers.
- Reexamination of existing Utah specimens, especially Mountain Sucker material.
- Careful larval identification to avoid repeating the misidentification errors in June Sucker recovery surveys.
- Comparative study of the temporal incidence (run timing) of the Utah Lake catostomids, to be treated in a future paper.
