# Native Fish Populations in the Virgin River, 1976–1993

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft report by [[BIO/WEST]] re-analyzes the [[Virgin River Database]], a centralized record of fish monitoring at multiple stations since 1976, to explain declines in native fishes. The primary focus is [[Woundfin]], a federally endangered minnow, with secondary attention to [[Virgin River Chub]], [[Virgin Spinedace]], and the invasive [[Red Shiner]]. The authors first test whether the data are reliable enough to support population comparisons, then evaluate season, [[Water Temperature]], [[Habitat Type]], [[Discharge]], [[Drought]], and reservoir effects. Woundfin abundance declined significantly at the four primary stations, with the sharpest losses at [[Beaver Dam Wash]] and [[Ash Creek]]. [[Hurricane Bridge]] remained the most stable population. The report concludes that the causes of the declines are not fully resolved, with red shiner invasion, drought, and [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] operations all implicated at different sites.

## Background & Description

The study originated from concerns among state and federal biologists about the loss of native Virgin River fishes over roughly 20 years. Woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) was listed as Endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. It had been abundant along more than 100 miles of river but was common in only about 30 miles by the mid-1990s. The Virgin River chub (*Gila seminuda*) was listed as Endangered, and the Virgin spinedace (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*) was proposed for Threatened status because of habitat loss and fragmentation.

Monitoring was typically conducted in spring (April–May) and fall (September) by the Virgin River Fishes Recovery Team using [[Seine Sampling]]. Earlier analyses by Deacon, Hardy, Gregory, Lentsch, and others used the same database, but with differing metrics such as fall-only data and mean fish per [[Seine Unit]]. Early researchers disagreed on causes, and the authors note that a July 1995 researchers' workshop at UNLV revealed fundamental doubts about data consistency and reporting.

Key questions posed by the Nevada Division of Wildlife included:
1. Why woundfin declined at most monitoring stations.
2. The effect of prolonged drought.
3. The effect of Quail Creek Reservoir (completed early 1985).
4. The effect of the [[Quail Creek Dam Breach]] (January 1989).
5. Factors behind the red shiner increase.
6. The effect of the 1988 chemical treatment in the Utah section.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Virgin River is a regulated desert river in which irrigation withdrawals, reservoir operations, and sinkhole-driven spring flows strongly influence habitat. [[Pah Tempe Springs]] flow changes, linked to sinkholes near the Quail Creek diversion, were documented as affecting water quality at Ash Creek in 1985. Minimum flows included 86 cfs year-round below Quail Creek Reservoir, with a proposed 50 cfs winter scenario and a 5 cfs summer minimum below Washington Fields Diversion.

Red shiners spread upstream and became abundant at Beaver Dam Wash and Twin Bridges in the mid-1980s, and [[Ecological Competition]] with woundfin is repeatedly proposed. Flow extremes were also considered: Deacon and Hardy (1984) reported reduced reproductive success at spring flows below 130 cfs and above 1000 cfs. Hardy et al. (1989) estimated overwinter mortality of about 30 percent regardless of winter flow.

## Key Findings

**Data reliability and methods**

- The database held 3,068 samples; 69 were excluded (single pass or missing or erroneous pass data), leaving 2,983 for analysis.
- Four primary stations were analyzed: Ash Creek (RM 133–135), Hurricane Bridge (RM 123–129), Twin Bridges (RM 111.6), and Beaver Dam Wash (RM 75.25).
- Seasons were defined as fall (8/15–11/14), winter (11/15–2/14), spring (2/15–5/14), and summer (5/15–8/14).
- Natural log transformation of (woundfin + 1) was used because the transformed data were more normally distributed and less affected by outliers.
- Density per 100 m² was rejected because of unreliable area values and subjective sampling.
- First-pass counts and seine-unit totals were highly correlated: across 16 station-season combinations, mean R² was 0.915 (range 0.855–0.971), and slopes averaged 1.103 (range 1.021–1.174).
- Overall CV was 235.3 for first-pass counts and 217.1 for seine-unit totals. The difference was small (about 7.5 percent), so the authors concluded the two measures are essentially interchangeable.
- The seine-unit measure was retained for most analyses for consistency with earlier work.

**Season, temperature, habitat, and discharge**

- Season affected woundfin catch at three of four stations. Beaver Dam Wash showed no seasonal difference (p = 0.35). Hurricane Bridge had more woundfin in fall than spring (p = 0.006).
- Discharge on sample days was significantly higher in spring than fall at all four stations (p < 0.001).
- Water temperature was estimated for 2,219 of 2,983 samples. Its predictive model had R² = 0.782, but temperature explained essentially none of the variation in woundfin catch (maximum R² = 0.052).
- Runs dominated sampling (2,183 samples, versus 315 pools and 248 riffles). Excluding non-run habitats reduced CV by only about 2.5–5 percent, so all habitats were retained.
- Discharge explained only a modest share of woundfin variation in spring and summer (R² = 0.038–0.267).

**Trends over time**

- Woundfin abundance differed significantly among years at all four stations in both spring and fall (overall ANOVAs, p ≤ 0.0001 in most cases).
- Woundfin declined significantly at Beaver Dam Wash and Ash Creek in both seasons. At Hurricane Bridge and Twin Bridges, fall showed no linear trend, while spring showed a significant decline at Hurricane Bridge.
- At Beaver Dam Wash, woundfin were locally extirpated by fall 1988. Shiners rose sharply in fall 1985, and the authors suggest that about one year of high shiner abundance preceded the collapse.
- Ash Creek's decline is not explained by red shiners or drought and is tentatively linked to [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] depletions and Pah Tempe flow changes.
- Hurricane Bridge, considered the best remaining habitat and a stronghold for woundfin, remained the most stable station. Red shiners had not invaded it.

**Drought and flow**

- Flow duration curves (Littlefield gage, 1970–1993) and K-means cluster analysis grouped years into five clusters. Drought years were defined as 1970–1972, 1974–1977, and 1989–1992.
- Drought reduced woundfin at Hurricane Bridge, where abundance in early and late droughts did not differ significantly (p = 0.763).
- At Ash Creek, Twin Bridges, and Beaver Dam Wash, drought did not explain the declines. High peak-flow years (1978, 1980, 1993) were associated with higher woundfin abundance at these three sites.
- The authors conclude that drought influences woundfin but is not the primary determinant of population dynamics.

**Shiners, dace, and red shiner effects**

- Shiner abundance at Beaver Dam Wash and Twin Bridges was significantly higher after 1985 (Beaver Dam Wash: spring p = 0.0002, fall p = 0.0001; Twin Bridges: spring p = 0.0057).
- Woundfin and shiners were negatively related at Beaver Dam Wash (fall R² = 0.458; spring R² = 0.234). At Twin Bridges the relationship was weaker (spring R² = 0.066; fall not significant).
- Dace populations showed no directional trend and were not clearly affected by red shiners.
- Woundfin declined after 1985 at Ash Creek, Twin Bridges, and Beaver Dam Wash in fall, with no significant change at Hurricane Bridge.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- Woundfin declines are real, not artifacts of sampling variation, and differ by station.
- No single cause explains all declines. Red shiner competition or replacement explains much of the Beaver Dam Wash loss, while Ash Creek's decline remains unexplained.
- The authors recommend a statistically valid baseline of abundance per station, use of cluster groupings to compare years, and standardized sampling protocols to address data reliability concerns raised at the 1995 workshop.
