# Commercial and Sport Fishes of the Mississippi River Between Caruthersville, Missouri, and Dubuque, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1951 bulletin from the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] reports a 1944 and 1946 test-net survey of the commercial and sport fishes of the [[Mississippi River]] over 689 river miles, sampled at 31 stations. The authors, [[Paul G. Barnickol]] and [[William C. Starrett]], combine survey data with historical catch statistics, river hydrology, and pollution records. They conclude that the river fishery has declined mainly because of habitat change (canalization, siltation, levee and drainage development, and pollution) rather than intensified fishing, and that the fishery is now dominated by [[Carp]], [[Buffalofishes]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], and [[Freshwater Drum]].

## Background & Description

The survey was sponsored by the [[Upper Mississippi River Conservation Committee]], formed at Dubuque in December 1943 by representatives of Illinois, Iowa, Missouri, Minnesota, and Wisconsin, together with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. Because state boundaries limited research, the work was split into upper and lower co-operative units. Field operations began in March 1944 in the Missouri-Illinois section and in March 1946 in the Iowa-Illinois section. The river from Caruthersville to Dubuque comprises 306 miles of the lower Mississippi (MR-C, mouth of the Missouri to Caruthersville) and 383 miles of the upper Mississippi (D-MR, Dubuque to the Missouri mouth).

Historical accounts, including those of the [[De Soto Expedition]] and [[Thomas Jefferson]], describe a muddy, sand-barred river with abundant fish, frequent floods, and extensive levee construction dating from 1727. Navigation improvements from 1824 onward progressed to a canalized 9-foot channel, with roughly 14 locks and dams between Alton and Dubuque (Nos. 12–26, excluding 23).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sediment and turbidity:** Erosion silt is a constant pollutant. Turbidity averaged 300 ppm above the Missouri mouth and 1,880 ppm below it (Platner 1946). Silt has reduced spawning and invertebrate habitat, and impoundment encouraged siltation in the pools.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Seasonal pulses were recorded, from 12.40–14.95 ppm in midwinter to 3.00–7.10 ppm at low water in August–September.
- **Pollution:** Sewage and garbage from [[St. Louis]], brewery and packing-plant wastes at [[Keokuk]], and wastes at [[Clinton, Iowa]] caused fish-quality complaints, clogged nets, and poor catches. Commercial fishing was reported almost nonexistent from St. Louis to the Kaskaskia River by 1950.
- **Habitat differences:** The MR-C section has sand and mud bottoms, swift flow, and extensive levees, and is less productive. The D-MR section has more backwaters and sloughs suitable for spawning, and is more productive. Within canalized pools, upper reaches (swift, sand-gravel bottoms) held more game fish than the silt-covered middle and lower reaches.
- **Flow:** Velocities ranged from about 2 to 4 mph at usual to high pool stages, and up to 8 mph through the Chain of Rocks.

## Key Findings

**Methods and effort**
- Collections at 31 stations in 1944 and 1946 used minnow seines, trammel nets, seines, hoop and wing nets, trap nets, basket traps, and trotlines. Minnow seine data were excluded.
- A total of 26,037 fishes weighing 28,294.25 pounds were taken.
- Commercial fishes made up 76.4% of the total catch by weight. The 1946 commercial catch of Illinois, Iowa, and Missouri was valued at $396,824 at local prices.

**Commercial fishes**
- **Carp** (*Cyprinus carpio*), introduced in the 1870s, is now the most important commercial species. It was 5.9% of the Illinois catch by weight in 1894 and 46.2% in 1946. Survey catches averaged 2.65 lb.
- **Buffalofishes** (smallmouth, bigmouth, black) ranked second to carp in the commercial catch. They were 48.7% of the Illinois catch in 1894 and 19.8% by 1931. Smallmouth buffalo was the most numerous, averaging 1.17 lb.
- **Catfishes:** the channel catfish was the most numerous catfish in the upper sections; the flathead was the most important by weight in all sections.
- **Freshwater drum** was among the most abundant fishes and was second in survey weight among species. It matures at about 14 inches, at ages of about 5–6 years.
- **Shovelnose sturgeon** (*Scaphirhynchus platorynchus*) was the only commercially important sturgeon. The 1946 catch was 30,181 lb. Its catch is far below 1899 levels in Illinois and Missouri. Males predominated in April collections, and a 25-inch minimum length was proposed to protect immature fish.
- **Lake sturgeon** was essentially absent: only one specimen (32 lb) was observed.
- **Paddlefish** (*Polyodon spathula*) declined, and Iowa placed it on its protected list. A school of paddlefish was observed at Cincinnati Landing in 1944.
- **American eel** (*Anguilla rostrata*) was scarce. It was 0.1–0.2% of fishes taken and was of little or no commercial importance.
- **Blue sucker** was rarely taken, and no adult was caught in the MR-C section. The northern redhorse was more abundant above Burlington.
- **Carpsuckers** (*Carpiodes* spp.) were abundant. They were 7.2% of the 1944 catch by weight and 14.2% of the 1946 catch, and form a valuable commercial resource if processing methods improve.

**Sport and game fishes**
- **Crappies** (black and white) were the most abundant game fishes. The white crappie dominated most upper-river collections; the black crappie dominated at Grafton.
- **Bluegill** was the second most abundant game fish. Six-inch fish averaged about 3 years of age.
- **Sauger** outnumbered [[Yellow Pikeperch]] (walleye) in the survey (67 vs 2 in MR-C). Yellow perch was not collected.
- **Pike** (*Esox lucius*) occurred only above New Boston; more than 91% of pike were taken at the four uppermost 1946 stations.
- **Black bass:** the largemouth was the only black bass numerous enough to matter; the spotted bass was rare, and no smallmouth was taken in test nets.
- **White bass** was more widely distributed than yellow bass, although one 1946 New Boston wing net yielded 198 yellow bass.

**Predatory and forage fishes**
- Predatory fishes included the shortnose, longnose, and alligator gars, and the bowfin. Alligator gar (85 specimens) was almost entirely in MR-C.
- Forage fishes included the gizzard shad, mooneye, goldeye, and skipjack. The skipjack was not taken above Warsaw and had declined since the locks and dams were built.

**Catch differences by section and reach**
- Sport fishes made up a smaller share of the catch in MR-C than in D-MR.
- Game fish abundance was highest in upper pool reaches; the commercial share rose in middle and lower reaches.

**Management conclusions**
- The authors attribute the decline mainly to habitat change (canalization, siltation, levee and drainage development, pollution) rather than to fishing intensity.
- Suggested measures included a 25-inch minimum length for shovelnose sturgeon, adding white crappie to the commercial list, improved handling of carpsuckers in warm months, and further study of gars as predators.
- Aggregated commercial statistics (buffalofishes, catfishes, carpsuckers) hinder species-level management.
