## Executive Summary
This 1995 *Economist* article reports on an annual list of "most endangered rivers" in the United States, published by the conservation group American Rivers. The piece highlights the **Lower Snake River** in Washington State as the top-ranked endangered river, citing four dams built in the 1960s–70s that have pushed migrating **[[Salmon]]** and **[[Steelhead Trout]]** populations toward extinction. The article frames river degradation as a combined product of urban sprawl and nearly two centuries of engineering intervention by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, while noting some cautious optimism from successful river remediation cases elsewhere.

## Background & Description
- **American Rivers**, a U.S. conservation organization, annually publishes a list of the ten "most endangered rivers" in the country.
- **1995 ranking extremes**:
  - **#1 — Lower Snake River (Washington State)**: Four dams constructed during the 1960s and 1970s have obstructed and altered river flow, severely threatening migratory fish populations.
  - **#10 — Bear River (Utah)**: Diversion of freshwater supply to the Great Salt Lake by Salt Lake City is harming birds and other wildlife (non-fish impact, included for ecosystem context).
- The **U.S. Army Corps of Engineers** is identified as a major historical driver of river alteration, having spent nearly 200 years removing snags, straightening and deepening channels, damming rivers, and building levees and floodwalls.
  - Cumulative impact: more than **30,000 miles of rivers and streams** dramatically altered.
  - This has placed "hundreds of species at risk of extinction," implicitly including anadromous fish like salmon and steelhead.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Primary threat mechanism**: Dam construction on the Lower Snake River disrupts the migratory life cycle of anadromous salmonids, which require unobstructed passage between freshwater spawning habitat and ocean feeding grounds.
- **Species at risk**: [[Salmon]] and [[Steelhead Trout]] (both members of family [[Salmonidae]]) are explicitly named as being pushed "to the point of extinction" due to the Snake River dams.
- **Broader habitat degradation drivers**:
  - Urban sprawl (linked to the Bear River freshwater diversion case).
  - Engineering practices by the Army Corps of Engineers (snag removal, channelization, damming, levee and floodwall construction) — a chronic, systemic pressure on river ecosystem integrity across the U.S.
- **Cross-taxon impact**: The Bear River case demonstrates that river/watershed degradation affects not just fish but also avian and other wildlife populations dependent on connected water bodies like the Great Salt Lake.

## Key Findings
- **Lower Snake River dams (1960s–70s)** are identified as the principal cause of population collapse risk for migrating [[Salmon]] and [[Steelhead Trout]].
- Nearly **200 years** of Army Corps of Engineers river modification has altered **30,000+ miles** of U.S. rivers and streams, contributing to species endangerment nationwide.
- **Positive remediation precedents** cited, suggesting recovery is possible with policy intervention:
  - **Clarks Fork (Yellowstone River, Montana/Wyoming)**: Topped the endangered list from 1994–1996; water quality improved significantly after President Clinton imposed a mining moratorium.
  - **Potomac River**: Ranked #7 in 1997; improved after Maryland's 1998 decision to limit agricultural runoff (specifically chicken waste) into the watershed.
- The article's closing note expresses cautious optimism that, following these precedents, the Snake River's salmon populations may not be irreversibly doomed, contingent on similar policy and engineering interventions (e.g., potential dam removal or modification, recruitment of "environmentally sensitive" engineering experts within the Corps).
- Implicit link established between **dam infrastructure**, **river engineering practices**, and **anadromous fish population viability**, relevant to ongoing [[Dam Removal]] and river restoration policy debates.
