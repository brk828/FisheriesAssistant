# Management Strategies for Native Fishes in Blue River, Arizona and New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This March 2001 technical report by [[Paul C. Marsh]] (Arizona State University) and [[Robert W. Clarkson]] (U.S. Bureau of Reclamation) sets out a management plan for native fishes of the Blue River, a tributary of the San Francisco River within the [[Gila River Basin]]. The Blue River still supports its full historic native fish fauna of five species, including the federally threatened [[Loach Minnow]]. The plan prioritizes protecting that population, reducing non-native fishes, building a barrier against upstream invasion, repatriating [[Spikedace]] and [[Gila Trout]], controlling watershed impacts from grazing, timber and roads, monitoring outcomes, and securing instream flows and private inholdings.

## Background & Description

The Blue River drains approximately 506 square miles of the White Mountains in eastern Arizona and western New Mexico. Land is mostly U.S. Forest Service, within the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]] and [[Gila National Forest]], with small private inholdings. Elevations range from about 3,865 ft at the mouth to more than 9,000 ft in headwater tributaries.

- **Discharge:** At Forest Service Road 475, about 6 miles above the confluence, monthly mean discharge ranged from 16.2 cfs (June) to 168 cfs (March). Annual means ranged from 14.8 cfs (1977) to 244 cfs (1983) over 26 years of record.
- **Major tributaries:** Pigeon, Squaw, Strayhorse, Raspberry, KP, Grant, Foote, Campbell Blue, Dry Blue and Little Blue creeks, plus Lamphier Canyon. Many smaller tributaries are perennial but are often cut off from the main stem by dry reaches or falls.
- **Native fauna:** Five species: longfin dace, loach minnow, speckled dace, desert sucker and Sonora sucker.
- **Listed species:** Loach minnow is federally threatened, with an approved recovery plan. Spikedace is also federally threatened. Razorback sucker is federally endangered and has been introduced to the river by AZGFD. Critical habitat was redesignated for loach minnow and spikedace in 2000, covering portions of the Blue River and several tributaries.
- **Gila trout:** Likely occupied suitable headwater habitat historically, but there are no verifiable records. Spruce Creek (NM) stock has been transplanted into Raspberry Creek, with Strayhorse Creek identified as a candidate site.
- **Non-native fishes:** Red shiner, fathead minnow, channel catfish and mosquitofish are reported. Rainbow, brown and Apache trout, plus brook trout, occupy cold headwaters. Trout are mostly maintained by stocking, though reproducing brown trout populations occur in some tributaries. Virile crayfish is also present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Blue River's native fish community remains intact, and the stronghold of loach minnow is considered significant. Loach minnow is common to locally abundant across suitable habitat. In some reaches, including Pace Creek, it occurs in very small, low-flow tributaries and in shallow pools. This departs from the typical habitat described elsewhere (clean cobble riffles of moderate depth with swift current), and the authors cite this as added justification for protection.

Non-native warm-water fishes are largely confined to the lower reaches and are relatively few in both species and individuals. Red shiner occupies main-channel runs and pools, often in shear zones at riffle bases, overlapping with spikedace habitat. Fathead minnow and mosquitofish use quiet, warm peripheral and off-channel habitats, which may also be used by early life stages of native fishes. Catfish are mostly restricted to deeper water and pools in the main channel.

Landscape pressures identified include improper livestock grazing, road construction and use, timber harvest, and stock tanks. Stock tanks can act as reservoirs for non-native species and can threaten transplanted or repatriated populations. The report cites [[Sediment in Streams]] effects (Waters 1995) as part of the broader watershed concern.

## Key Findings

1. **Protect the loach minnow population.** The Blue River is a priority 1 site under the loach minnow recovery plan. The population is genetically distinct (Tibbets 1993; Tibbets and Dowling 1996).
2. **Repatriate spikedace (priority 2).** There are no spikedace records for the Blue River, but the species was historically present in the San Francisco River and Eagle Creek. The authors propose transplanting it from Eagle Creek to re-establish a likely historic component of the community.
3. **Repatriate Gila trout after removing non-native salmonids.** Spruce Creek stock is the identified source. Strayhorse Creek is a candidate site. Apache trout stocked in the system must be removed before Gila trout stocking.
4. **Build a barrier on the lower Blue River** near the San Francisco confluence. This would block upstream invasion by non-native species and allow removal of invaders. A physical drop structure was judged feasible by USBR (1998), and USBR and USFS are cooperatively funding construction.
5. **Reduce or eliminate non-native fishes** where they co-occur with natives, using angling, spearing, electrofishing, mechanical removal and piscicides.
6. **Manage watersheds** by maintaining excellent range condition where possible, discontinuing grazing where that is not achievable, restricting timber harvest that threatens aquatic systems, closing unneeded roads, and inventorying and treating stock tanks.
7. **Impose a complete moratorium** on non-native fish stocking by agencies and private parties. Angler removal of non-native sport fish is encouraged. The plan supports relaxed regulations, but prohibits live bait.
8. **Restrict angling for Gila trout.** Federal law prohibits angling for endangered species, so sport fishing cannot begin until Gila trout are downlisted to threatened.
9. **Monitor the system** with a baseline established before barrier construction and other actions. A draft barrier monitoring plan (Marsh 1999) has been prepared.
10. **Secure instream flows and land.** Water rights and easements should be acquired where feasible, with partners such as [[The Nature Conservancy]]. The USFS should buy private inholdings with flowing water or high-quality range as they become available.
11. **Consider additional native transplants.** [[Gila Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] are proposed for consideration with expert input.
