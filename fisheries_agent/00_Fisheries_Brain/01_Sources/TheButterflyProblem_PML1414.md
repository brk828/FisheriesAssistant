# The Butterfly Problem: Endangered Species Act Trade-offs and Conservation Policy

#ichthyology #marine-biology #lepidoptera

*Note: the source is a policy feature on the Endangered Species Act rather than a fish study. The species name above is inferred from the Oregon silverspot's listing; the OCR text gives it as "S. hippolyta" (Speyeria zerene hippolyta), and it is recorded here as such in the Background section.*

## Executive Summary

This long-form magazine feature examines how the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of 1973 operates in practice, using the Oregon silverspot butterfly (*Speyeria zerene hippolyta*) as its central case. A proposed $100 million golf resort at Gearhart, Oregon, was halted by the butterfly's presence. The article argues that the ESA rests on a "[[Noah Principle]]" of equal worth for all species, which produces haphazard, unbalanced spending and failure to prioritize. It also documents how the Act's species-by-species focus has led to costly last-ditch efforts, and it proposes ecosystem-level approaches such as [[Gap Analysis]] as alternatives.

## Background & Description

- **Focal species:** The Oregon silverspot, a finger-sized reddish-brown butterfly listed as threatened under the ESA. Its larvae feed only on the common blue violet (*Viola adunca*), so it occurs only on open coastal grassland.
- **Central case:** [[Richard Schroeder]] planned an 18-hole golf course and resort on about the 1970s-1980s site in Gearhart; the project was reportedly a $100 million scheme that stalled after the butterfly was found.
- **Legal framework:** The ESA directs the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to maintain lists of endangered and threatened species and prohibits harassment, harm, or killing of listed species. Its 1978 Supreme Court case, [[Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill]], halted the [[Tellico Dam]] to protect the [[Snail Darter]].
- **Historical context:** The first federal endangered-species list, the 1964 "redbook," contained 63 species. The 1973 ESA was passed by a large majority and signed by President [[Richard Nixon]].
- **Dusky seaside sparrow:** Once native to brackish marshes of east-central Florida, the subspecies (*Ammodramus maritimus nigrescens*) declined from habitat drainage, impoundment, and fire. The last male, Orange Band, died in 1987, twenty years after its initial listing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Silverspot habitat depends on fire. Native Americans burned grasslands, which kept Scotch broom and lodgepole pine from overtaking the violets. Fire suppression after the 1930s allowed succession, and the article states that more than nine-tenths of the habitat loss was natural.
- Developers and ranchers both affect habitat: pasture burning, ditching, and development.
- The article cites figures on biodiversity loss: "Worldwide, Wilson guesses the rate may be 50,000 species a year." Peter Raven estimated about 4,000 domestic U.S. species "at risk of extinction within five to ten years."
- Ecosystem losses in the Midwest are cited: 99 percent of Iowa's wetlands, 90 percent of Nebraska's and Missouri's, and 80 percent of Minnesota's drained or converted.
- The Pacific yew (*Taxus brevifolia*) is a case where a previously unprotected tree became valuable because it yields [[Taxol]], a drug for ovarian cancer. Yew populations recovered because of market demand from [[Weyerhaeuser]] and [[Bristol-Myers Squibb]], not because of federal action.

## Key Findings

1. **Priority decisions are inconsistent.** The Fish and Wildlife Service's recovery priority system has changed multiple times since 1973. The dusky seaside sparrow ranked sixth in the 1983 system, shared with 38 other species, and the service spent nothing on the northeastern beach tiger beetle in 1990 while spending $3.8 million on the Florida panther.
2. **Spending is highly unequal.** More than half of the roughly $100 million spent in 1990 went to eleven species. A hundred and fourteen species received nothing. The article cites a fourteenfold higher spend on "charismatic megafauna."
3. **Endangered species receive less than threatened ones on average.** Two of the three most expensive species, the northern spotted owl ($9.7 million) and the grizzly bear ($5.9 million), are threatened, not endangered.
4. **Habitat conservation plans are costly and rare.** The Hildreth/Wudtke plan cost over $250,000 before completion, and only ten habitat conservation plans had been accepted in nine years. An Austin, Texas, plan would cost more than $93 million.
5. **Ecosystem approaches are proposed.** The article describes [[Gap Analysis]] by J. Michael Scott, which overlays species distributions on land ownership maps. In Hawaii, the maps showed high-diversity areas outside protected reserves. Scott's Sacramento National Wildlife Refuge comparison found that annual management of 257 vertebrate species cost about $1 million, less than the annual condor recovery spend.
6. **Economic incentives are proposed.** The authors argue for compensating private landowners and for market-based solutions, and they discuss the [[Dusky Seaside Sparrow]] failure as an example of a species lost under budget constraints.
7. **Policy conclusion.** The article argues the ESA's species-by-species approach is "treating the symptom and not the disease," and it calls for protecting ecosystems that maximize biodiversity. The author concludes the Act has been partially successful, citing species such as the whooping crane and the Attwater's prairie chicken, but has failed to restore them to viability.
