# Channel Catfish Farming in Louisiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Louisiana Wild Life and Fisheries Commission bulletin (B-875, 1967) by fisheries biologists James T. Davis and Janice S. Hughes is a practical manual for starting a commercial [[Channel Catfish]] farming operation in the state. It draws on nine years of experimentation, with all recommendations tested under commercial conditions. The bulletin covers site selection, [[Catfish Pond Construction]], [[Fingerling Production]], stocking, [[Catfish Feeding and Fertilization]], [[Harvesting Methods]], [[Holding and Hauling]], [[Causes of Mortality]], and [[Return on Investment]]. Its central message is that catfish farming is profitable but capital-intensive, and that beginners should start small, with no more than 10 acres in the first year.

## Background & Description

The document is a technical extension bulletin, not a research paper. It was written because demand for information on catfish farming had grown faster than the time available from qualified biologists. The authors describe the work as a practical guide for beginners, with later use as a reference for experienced growers.

The bulletin notes that catfish farming resembles other farming in requiring constant attention to detail and long hours. Gross income is high, but so are expenses. Initial expenditures are at least as high as for other farming operations, while net income is higher than most conventional farm enterprises.

Put-and-take or catch-out ponds are excluded because none had been attempted in Louisiana at the time. The bulletin also refers readers to related publications, including *Managing Louisiana Farm Ponds* by Max W. Summers and *Culture and Diseases of Game Fish* by H. S. Davis.

The bulletin acknowledges [[Monroe Tackle Company]] and Collinston Minnow Farms for providing facilities without charge for research, along with Harry E. Schafer, Joe L. Herring, and others.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Soil and water requirements**
- Soil must have enough clay to hold water. The authors recommend checking at least the surface eight feet with a soil auger.
- Groundwater from wells is preferred. An 8-inch well delivering 1,000 gallons per minute is considered essential for every 80 acres.
- Surface water can be used, but it brings in wild fish and eggs. If used, the intake must be screened with at least 18 mesh per inch (for example, saran filter). For a six-inch pump, the recommended screen is a sock-like filter at least six feet long and one foot in diameter.
- Water salinity above 1,500 parts per million is reported to adversely affect production, based on a very limited number of studies.
- Low soil fertility can largely be offset by adding fertilizer to the water.

**Pond structure**
- Ponds of 5 to 20 acres are considered the most efficient. They can be rotated with row crops, and Louisiana farmers have used rotation with soybeans or rice.
- Average depth is 3 to 6 feet, and no water should be less than 30 inches deep to avoid weed problems.
- A catch basin at least one foot deeper than the rest of the pond, covering at least 10 percent of pond area, is needed for harvest drainage.

**Water quality and oxygen**
- Low dissolved oxygen is the most common cause of death. It can result from shallow water, overfeeding, overcrowding, successive cloudy days, plankton die-off, or a combination of these.

## Key Findings

**Site and infrastructure**
- Levees should have a slope of at least 3:1 and a crown of about 10 feet to allow vehicle access.
- Ponds of 50 acres or more require at least five one-acre ponds for holding fish during harvest. The bulletin also recommends at least two one-acre ponds for any large harvest.
- Pumping drainage costs exceed $10.00 per acre for an average pond.

**Fingerlings**
- Fingerlings cost $0.03 to $0.10 each, depending on quantity, size, and season.
- Buy from reputable growers. Recommended pre-delivery treatment includes a 15 parts per million [[Formaldehyde Treatment]] bath for 24 hours and a 0.001 percent [[Acriflavine]] bath during hauling.
- Home production costs about 1.5 cents per three-inch fingerling for natural spawning and 1 to 2 cents for penned brooders with artificial hatching. Overwintered fingerlings cost up to four cents.
- Brooders are stocked at 10 to 30 pairs per acre in spawning ponds with grease-can spawning containers.

**Stocking density and timing**
- Fry stocked in a nursery pond reach the following sizes in about 90 days:

| Fish per acre | Size |
|---|---|
| 150,000 | 2 in |
| 100,000 | 3 in |
| 75,000 | 4 in |
| 60,000 | 5 in |
| 40,000 | 6 in |
| 30,000 | 7 in |
| 20,000 | 8 in |

- If fed, stock 2,000 fingerlings per acre. Eight-inch fingerlings at 2,000 per acre in spring reach marketable size in 100 days.
- Stock only channel catfish. Where [[Blue Catfish]] are available, up to 10 percent of stock may be blue catfish.
- Fall stocking (September or early October) is acceptable only with rapidly growing fingerlings of 4 inches or more and when fish will be fed. Otherwise spring stocking is mandatory, because fish in large ponds often fail to find feed in winter.

**Fertilization**
- Begin fertilizing by February with about 80 pounds per acre of 20-20-0 or an equivalent water-soluble fertilizer.
- Repeat with 50 pounds per acre when a six-inch white disc is clearly visible in 18 inches of water. If fish are fed, the initial application is usually sufficient.

**Feed specifications and rations**
- Commercial feed should contain at least 32 percent crude protein (half from animal protein), at least 4.5 percent fat, and no more than 12 percent fiber. Pellets should hold together for at least ten minutes.
- A homemade formula (approximate cost $110 per ton) is based on fish meal, bloodmeal, feather meal, rice bran, rice mill dust, soybean meal, distillers dried solubles, dehydrated alfalfa, mineralized salt, and vitamin premix. Its ingredient amounts are in the source text and are not repeated here.
- Feed about 3 percent of body weight daily until fish reach one pound, then about 2 percent. Under optimum conditions, fish double their weight each month. Sample fish by seining at least monthly.
- Use underwater feeding trays and check them after one hour. Reduce feeding if feed remains; increase it if all feed is eaten.

**Feeding rules**
1. Do not feed in the rain.
2. Cut the feeding rate by half when water temperature exceeds 90°F.
3. Stop feeding after more than four consecutive days of overcast weather. Resume after the first sunny day.
4. Do not exceed 30 pounds of feed per acre per day in July, August, and September.
5. Below 60°F, feed only two days per week, on warm, sunny days.

**Markets and harvest**
- Demand is generally poor from Thanksgiving to early February and in spring, when commercial fishermen oversupply local markets. June through September is generally good for fish of 1/2 to 2 pounds.
- Best growth per pound of feed is in fish under 2 pounds. Stocker fish weighing 1/10 to 1/2 pound have a market. Restaurants and supermarkets prefer 3/4 to 1-1/4 pound live fish, while local fish markets usually prefer 1-1/2 to 2 pounds.
- Partial harvest uses tar-treated seines, which are the most efficient method, and baited basket traps for small quantities. Trammel and gill nets are discouraged because catches are usually dead.
- Total harvest requires drawing the pond to the catch basin. For a 20-acre pond, 25,000 to 40,000 pounds of live fish may need holding space within 24 hours, which is a serious problem in hot weather.
- Channel catfish dress out at 60 to 65 percent of live weight. Dressed fish should command about twice the live-weight price per pound. No catfish processing plants were operating in Louisiana at the time, though two firms had expressed interest.

**Holding and hauling**
- Holding ponds are about one acre, at least four feet deep, with a bottom slope of at least 4 inches per 100 feet and a drain pipe of at least 6 inches.
- Holding capacity is a maximum of 5,000 pounds per acre for short summer periods, and up to 10,000 pounds per acre in winter.
- Fish should be held in troughs for at least 24 hours before long-distance hauling to empty their guts and reduce ammonia build-up.
- Example troughs: two 6-foot-wide, 75-foot-long, 2-foot-deep troughs with agitators can hold 10,000 pounds for three days to a week.
- Most long-haul truckers carry 6,000 to 10,000 pounds per load. Maximum loading is three pounds of fish per gallon of water.
- Copper must not be used for holding equipment. Metal troughs must be treated against zinc poisoning, and concrete troughs should be lined with polyethylene film to prevent abrasions.

**Mortality and disease**
- Oxygen depletion is the main cause of death. Circulating the top foot of water with a pump is effective in small ponds. The optimum solution is a pump that moves the total pond volume in 48 hours.
- Disease-prevention steps are to start with clean fingerlings and maintain a good ration. Fish that stop feeding or swim at the surface ("piping") need diagnosis by a fisheries biologist. The Monroe laboratory runs a diagnostic service.
- Serious diseases include [[Ich]], *Pseudomonas* infection, fungal infection, and gas bubble disease. Parasites identifiable with a microscope include *Costia*, *Chilodon*, *Trichodina*, *Chilodonella*, and *Scyphidia*.

- The bulletin stresses that first-year operations should not exceed 10 acres.

**Licensing and regulation**
- A $10.00 annual fish farmer's permit from the [[Louisiana Wild Life and Fisheries Commission]] is required before starting any fish farm, including catfish, crawfish, frogs, and turtles. The law was passed to protect native fish populations.
- Farmers selling out of state also need a wholesale fish dealer's license ($50.00).
- Eight district fisheries offices provide guidance across Louisiana, with Monroe, Minden, Tioga, Ferriday, DeRidder, Opelousas, Baton Rouge, and New Orleans among the listed locations.
