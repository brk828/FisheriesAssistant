# San Carlos Reservation Fishery Management Report (1963)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 fishery management report, issued by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] (Albuquerque, New Mexico) and signed by Fishery Management Biologist Richard J. Navarre, assesses the fish resources of the [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]]. Seventy stock-water tanks and the [[Black River]] and [[Salt River]] were surveyed. Most tanks were found suitable only for warm-water species, so the report recommends an extensive stocking and manipulation program, centred on [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Channel Catfish]], together with the construction of new trout lakes and access roads. The stated goal is a recreational fishery that produces income and jobs for tribal members.

## Background & Description

The report reviews fish stocking history on the Reservation:

- **1944:** Applications requested fish for roughly 20 named waters, but no records show these waters were actually stocked. The requested mixes were [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Channel Catfish]].
- **1958:** The last planting before 1963 covered about 25 tanks. Species were largemouth bass, channel catfish, bluegill, [[Green Sunfish]], and crappie. Stocking counts ranged from roughly 150 to over 1,000 fish per tank. Follow-up studies were not conducted, so success was not measured.

The 1963 survey covered tanks holding a combined 300 surface acres. Many tanks stocked in 1958 had dried and refilled without fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stock-water tanks.** Of 70 tanks surveyed, only a few held satisfactory fish populations. Nine of the 15 tanks judged suitable for trout were devoid of fish. Three of the best trout waters ([[Bobcat Tank]], [[Elwood Tank]], and [[East Deer Tank]]) held warm-water populations that would need eradication first. The report notes that trout suitability depends on year-round water, and that water shortages would cause die-offs.

**Black and Salt Rivers.** The Salt River forms at the confluence of the Black and White Rivers, so the system was treated as one unit. About 32 miles of the Salt and 76 miles of the Black form Reservation boundary, shared with the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]].

- The lower 40 to 50 miles hold an excellent catfish population.
- The upper 16 miles hold a poor-to-fair trout population that could be developed into a rainbow trout fishery with access.
- The 42 to 52 miles between these zones hold no important game fish. [[Smallmouth Bass]] were suggested for this reach.
- Anglers reportedly caught 4 to 5 pound catfish a few miles above the diversion dam.

**Fishing pressure and economics.** Permit sales suggested fairly stable pressure despite growing demand. The report estimates about 11,000 man-days of fishing per year on the Reservation, with some 20 percent by enrolled Apaches. A survey cited in the report gives average Arizona trout-angler spending of about $13.68 per day.

## Key Findings

**1. Stocking and fish management strategy**
- [[Rainbow Trout]] were stocked in 1963 for public demand. The report says trout provide more fishing days than any other sport fish here. About 10,000 catchable-size trout were stocked in fall 1963, with 1964 plantings dependent on angler use.
- [[Channel Catfish]] were stocked in 44 tanks in 1963. The report notes they generally do not reproduce in most ponds.
- Largemouth bass plantings were recommended for 1964 in tanks suited to them.
- Forage fish were recommended to match conditions. [[Green Sunfish]] suit persistently turbid waters. [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Threadfin Shad]] suit relatively clear ponds with dense phytoplankton blooms. Threadfin shad must be re-stocked each spring because they die when water temperatures fall below 45°F.
- Trout should be stocked with no other species except channel catfish, which is described as an exception rather than a rule.
- Several tanks should be chemically treated with [[Rotenone]] to remove existing fish before restocking. The report lists 300 pounds at $120.00 and 110 pounds at $44.00 (both at $0.40 per pound) for the treatment. The figure for Bobcat Tank is partly illegible in the scan.
- Fish manipulation (transferring bluegill and largemouth bass between over-populated tanks) and seining of low tanks during drought are recommended to maintain balanced populations.

**2. Proposed trout lakes and projected values**

| Site | Proposed size | Projected annual use | Projected annual value |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Willow Creek]], upper dam | ~300 surface acres, 55 ft dam | 50,000 man-days | $212,500 |
| Willow Creek, lower dam | 80 acres, 45 ft dam | 15,000 man-days | about $63,750 |
| [[Tanks Canyon Creek]] | 25 acres, 40 ft dam | 10,000 man-days | about $42,500 |
| Cienega Creek | 10 to 15 acres, 30 ft dam | Not fully legible | Not fully legible |

- Willow Creek sites were rated as having an extremely favourable cost-to-benefit ratio. Outlet structures must draw water from both top and bottom to manage warm surface water.
- At [[Point of Pines]], [[Drylake Tank]] is too shallow for trout and should be abandoned for a site with at least 75 surface acres downstream. Home-site leases were estimated at about $100 per lot per year.
- The Seneca Dam was completed; it is projected to draw 10,000 man-days yearly. Its income figure is not legible in the scan.

**3. Benchmarks and economic conclusions**
- The [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] recreation enterprise had about 150,000 man-days of fishing in 1962 and about $850,000 in revenue, of which at least 75 percent came from trout anglers. The report derives an average expenditure of about $4.25 per fishing man-day.
- Restricting use through high fees is considered counterproductive because more anglers create more jobs for tribal members.
- An all-weather road across the Black River near [[Point of Pines]] is described as badly needed. It would link Globe, Arizona, to the eastern Fort Apache area and to [[Big Boni Tank]]-area lakes, and is expected to serve about 400,000 annual visitors to the nearby Big Lake area.

**4. Summary conclusion**
Surveys found that most Reservation waters are fit only for warm-water species. Development of new trout lakes, access roads, and supporting services is essential to build a self-sustaining recreational fishery that produces substantial tribal income and employment.
