# Winter Food Habits of Pikeperch in Oneida Lake

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #percidae #walleye #seasonal-diet

## Executive Summary

This study examined winter food habits of [[Pikeperch]] (Stizostedion vitreum) in [[Oneida Lake]], New York during the winters of 1951-52 and 1952-53. Of 143 pikeperch stomachs collected via ice-fishing, 78.3% were empty—nearly double the empty-stomach rate reported for summer and fall periods in the same lake. Fish (primarily [[Emerald Shiner]]) comprised 64.5% of food occurrences when present, while invertebrates (mainly [[Hexagenia]] mayfly larvae) comprised 35.5%. The high proportion of empty stomachs suggests reduced prey availability in winter habitats frequented by pikeperch, contrasting with well-fed winter populations in other systems like [[Norris Reservoir]].

## Background & Description

The [[Pikeperch]] (Stizostedion vitreum) is well-documented as primarily piscivorous, with food habits extensively studied across its range. Previous investigations by [[Raney and Lachner]] (1942) provided detailed documentation of food taken by young, juvenile, and adult pikeperch in [[Oneida Lake]], including a comprehensive literature review. Most food habits studies have focused on summer and fall collections, with winter feeding patterns less thoroughly documented.

Pikeperch are known to consume many fish species when available, with [[Yellow Perch]] (Perca flavescens) frequently reported as a dominant prey item—likely due to availability rather than preference. The species' feeding ecology varies seasonally and is strongly influenced by prey distribution and habitat use patterns.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Oneida Lake]] provides a well-studied system for pikeperch ecology. During winter ice-fishing surveys, specimens were collected from various lake areas, including deep water over mud bottom near Cleveland harbor. The winter habitat preferences of pikeperch may limit their access to forage species, particularly in contrast to summer conditions when prey are more broadly distributed.

In [[Norris Reservoir]], Tennessee, pikeperch showed different winter feeding patterns, with stomachs generally well-filled with [[Gizzard Shad]]. [[Dendy]] (1946) noted that in Norris Reservoir, empty stomachs coincided with pikeperch movement into deeper, cooler water in early summer where forage was less available, resulting in declined growth rates. This pattern suggests that prey availability in preferred thermal strata is a critical factor controlling feeding success.

The nine pikeperch found with mayfly larvae were all collected on one day (February 2, 1952) from deep water over mud bottom, suggesting localized feeding opportunities where benthic invertebrates were accessible.

## Key Findings

**Stomach Content Analysis (n=143):**
- 112 stomachs (78.3%) were empty
- 20 stomachs (14.0%) contained fish
- 11 stomachs (7.7%) contained insect larvae

**Food Items Identified (n=31 stomachs with food):**

*Fish (64.5% frequency):*
- [[Emerald Shiner]] (Notropis atherinoides): 12 occurrences (38.7%)
- [[Yellow Perch]] (Perca flavescens): 1 occurrence (3.2%)
- [[Golden Shiner]] (Notemigonus crysoleucas): 1 occurrence (3.2%)
- Undetermined fish: 6 occurrences (19.4%)

*Invertebrates (35.5% frequency):*
- Mayfly larvae ([[Hexagenia]] sp.): 9 occurrences (29.0%)
- Undetermined invertebrates: 2 occurrences (6.5%)

**Comparative Empty Stomach Rates:**
- Winter (this study): 78.3% empty
- Summer/fall ([[Raney and Lachner]] 1942): 41.5% empty (n=65)
- Summer/fall ([[Stone and Pasko]] 1946): 46% empty (n=64)

**Bait Consumption:** Nine of 20 stomachs containing fish held [[Emerald Shiner]]s with definite hook scars near the dorsal fin, indicating consumption of angler's bait. Additional fish may have been bait but could not be positively identified as such.

**Size Distribution of Mayfly Feeders:** The nine fish containing mayfly larvae ranged from 12.2 to 15.0 inches total length, with one individual at 17.9 inches. Eleven were sub-adult or juvenile fish. All were collected from one location over mud bottom.

**Prey Availability Hypothesis:** The high percentage of empty stomachs during winter likely reflects reduced prey availability in habitats frequented by pikeperch during this period, rather than reduced feeding motivation. This contrasts sharply with [[Norris Reservoir]] where winter pikeperch were well-fed on abundant [[Gizzard Shad]].
