# An Electrical Surface Device for Carp Control and Fish Collection in Lakes

## Executive Summary
This 1955 study by Howard A. Loeb (New York State Conservation Department) describes the design, deployment, and electrical characterization of a four-electrode, alternating current (AC) surface "electrofishing" device intended for [[Carp]] collection and population control in lakes. Tested extensively on Lake Lamoka (~600 acres) over nine days in summer 1954, the device successfully collected approximately 100 carp along with incidental catches of largemouth bass and chub suckers. The study is foundational to the development of [[Electrofishing]] methodology, establishing empirical relationships between voltage gradients, fish body length, and paralysis thresholds. The authors conclude that while effective as a research/collection tool in structurally complex habitats (weedy, stumpy areas), the technology is fundamentally **inefficient for large-scale population control** due to the small effective radius of incapacitating electrical fields and low fish retrieval rates (~25% of visibly shocked fish were captured).

## Background & Description
While this is primarily a methods/engineering paper rather than a morphological study, several body-size-dependent electrophysiological findings were documented:

- **Size-selectivity of shock response**: Larger fish are more easily paralyzed (lower voltage-per-foot threshold) but are simultaneously more capable of physically evading the electric field due to greater burst swimming speed and range.
- **Carp sampled**: 100 individuals via electroshocking (few ounces to 12 lbs, mean ~7 lbs) vs. 170 individuals via seining (mean ~0.5 lb lighter), suggesting electrofishing selectively captures larger-bodied fish.
- **Paralysis voltage thresholds** (using Haskell 1954 methodology):
  - Sixteen carp (12.5–18 inches) tested; ~50 observations recorded.
  - **~3.0 volts per fish** effective against active carp at 53°F.
  - Comparable to Haskell's finding of **3.1 ± 0.3 volts per fish** required to paralyze hatchery [[Brown Trout]] (3–11 inches).
  - Corroborated by McMillan (1928): 3.1-inch chinook salmon fry required minimum 1.23 volts/inch gradient in high-resistivity water.
  - A 17-inch carp was immobilized (without visible movement) at 3.3 volts/fish; a 12.5-inch carp failed to recover after a 5.2 volts/fish jolt.
- **Tissue resistivity**: Carp flesh measured ~**1,470 ohms/cm³** at 53°F (15-inch specimen), nearly identical to Haskell's brown trout flesh value of ~1,500 ohms/cm³, suggesting freshwater fish flesh may share broadly similar electrical resistivity properties regardless of taxon.
- **Swimming speed context** (Wales 1950, cited for comparison): 12-inch western suckers (*[[Catostomus occidentalis]]*) reach ~10 ft/sec for ~5 sec; 13-inch trout reach ~11 ft/sec — explaining why most 12-inch+ fish can evade slow-moving electrofishing gear (device speeds: 3 ft/sec underwater trawl, 6 ft/sec surface device).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat tested**: Lake Lamoka (NY), a shallow-to-moderate lake with weedy and stumpy structural complexity; earlier underwater trawl trials conducted in a small, deep (45 ft), soft-bottomed lake and in [[Oneida Lake]].
- **Vegetation effects**: Few carp were captured in areas with dense subsurface mats of pondweed (*Potamogeton robbinsii*), indicating vegetation structure impedes device effectiveness (likely via field distortion/obstruction) despite carp otherwise being ubiquitously distributed ("everywhere and anywhere").
- **Species-specific responses**:
  - **Carp**: Abundant, exhibited erratic dashing behavior when shocked, sometimes displaying anticipatory flight responses to boat approach or current onset — suggesting the ability to detect the field before direct exposure (mirroring findings from Michigan sea lamprey control studies, Applegate et al. 1952).
  - **Pickerel**: Abundant in the lake but never observed within the electric field — inferred to be an alert, fast-swimming species capable of complete field avoidance.
  - **Largemouth Bass, Chub Suckers**: Occasionally incapacitated and retrieved, but in low numbers.
  - **Species NOT captured** in earlier underwater trawl trials despite co-occurrence: [[Pickerel]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Brown Trout]], sunfish, suckers.
  - **Small fish/fry (<4 inches)**: Hundreds of thousands incapacitated by front electrodes, demonstrating field effectiveness on small-bodied fish despite overall system inefficiency for large-scale removal.
- **Water chemistry**: Lake Lamoka water resistivity measured 5,540 ohms/cm³ at 70°F (Dec. 1954 sample); Rome Hatchery spring water used in flesh-resistivity trials measured 5,400 ohms/cm³. Authors note that softer/lower-resistivity water (e.g., seawater) would require substantially lower voltages to achieve equivalent fish paralysis — an important consideration for extrapolating results to marine or brackish [[Electrofishing]] applications.

## Key Findings
1. **Device design**: Four electrodes (front: solid brass rods, 3/4 inch diameter, ~7 ft long; rear: braided copper cable) suspended from a 16.5 ft wooden scow, powered by a 230V AC Homelite generator; electrode spacing 9.2 ft (front pair) and 11.5 ft (rear pair), separated by 31 ft front-to-rear.
2. **Voltage field geometry**: Effective incapacitation radius extended no more than ~8 feet from any single electrode for fish up to 36 inches; contour lines defined as:
   - "A" line (1 volt/ft) → incapacitates fish up to 3 ft long
   - "B" line (1.5 volts/ft) → incapacitates fish up to 2 ft long
   - "C" line (3 volts/ft) → incapacitates fish up to 1 ft long
3. **Doubling voltage output** (to 460V AC) would only extend the effective field radius by ~2–3 feet (to 10–11 ft) — a diminishing-returns relationship, and considered impractical/unsafe.
4. **Field distortion**: When electrodes trailed at an angle during boat motion, voltage readings dropped sharply (e.g., from 2.8 to 0.8 volts/ft at 5 ft ahead of the boat), meaningfully shrinking the functional capture zone during active towing versus static (anchored) conditions.
5. **Asymmetric electrode failure**: Disabling one electrode (right rear) strengthened the field around its same-polarity partner (right front) while weakening the opposite pair — demonstrating circuit interdependency among electrodes.
6. **Retrieval inefficiency**: Only ~25% of visibly shocked fish were successfully captured, with failure attributed to: missed net/spear/gaff strikes, boat overtaking too fast, turbidity obscuring fish, operator preoccupation, fish recovery before retrieval, and fish escaping out of sight.
7. **Non-lethality**: Even prolonged (2-minute) shocking of spawning carp aggregations did not cause immediate mortality; affected fish remained alive (lying on sides, feebly moving) up to 2 hours post-shock. No dead fish were observed during the study.
8. **Comparative yield**: The device achieved far lower catch rates than a comparable Illinois device (200–300 lbs/hour at Crab Orchard Lake per Laramore et al. 1950) — Loeb's team could not match that yield even after 6–8 hours of continuous shocking.
9. **Current type experiments**: Pulsating AC showed no clear advantage over standard AC; non-pulsating 110V direct current (DC) was tested briefly but yielded no successful captures.
10. **Practical/theoretical implications**: Because freshwater fish flesh resistivity appears roughly consistent across taxa (~1,470–1,500 ohms/cm³), the authors propose that a generalized voltage-gradient table could theoretically be developed to predict paralysis thresholds for any 12-inch fish species, with required field strength inversely related to ambient water resistivity — a principle directly relevant to adapting [[Electrofishing]] gear for varying water chemistries, including future marine or estuarine applications.
11. **Overall conclusion**: The surface device is a useful supplementary collection tool for fishery biologists (especially in structurally complex, weedy/stumpy habitats where netting is impractical) but is **not economically or operationally viable as a carp population control method** due to limited field range, high labor/equipment cost, and low overall capture efficiency.
