# LC Identification of Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-species-identification

## Executive Summary

This methods paper by Ashoor, Marsh and Knopp (Arizona State University) describes a [[Liquid Chromatography]] (LC) method for identifying fish species from their water-soluble [[Sarcoplasmic Proteins]]. Chromatographic profiles from 31 fish species were separated, quantified and used as species fingerprints. The method is described as rapid, reliable and requiring no special skill. It works on fresh and frozen whole, sliced, filleted and minced fish. Freezing for six months did not alter the profiles, but cooking did, so the method cannot identify heat-treated fish in its current form.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Existing species identification relies on morphology, which requires a whole specimen and cannot be applied to fillets or mince, or on electrophoretic methods. Electrophoretic methods are slow, labour-intensive and poorly reproducible.
- **Electrophoretic methods reviewed:** [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]], [[Polyacrylamide Disc Gel Electrophoresis]], thin-layer polyacrylamide gel, [[Cellulose Acetate Strip Electrophoresis]], [[Isoelectric Focusing]] in thin-layer polyacrylamide gel, and agarose gel. Four are listed as [[AOAC Official Methods]] final action methods. The authors note that polyacrylamide contains the neurotoxic monomer acrylamide and that agarose gel focusing is highly variable.
- **Rationale for LC:** Previously reported LC separation of fish sarcoplasmic proteins offers simplicity, rapidity, reproducibility and high resolution typical of high-performance liquid chromatography.
- **Scope:** The method was applied to 31 fish species whose identities were first confirmed morphologically, using standard keys and voucher specimens retained intact.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a food-analysis study rather than a field ecology study. Specimens were bought fresh from local retail outlets and identified by morphological keys. Habitat, ocean basin and collection conditions for the species are not described, so these fields are left as N/A. The authors note that most fish sold in grocery stores is frozen, which makes the freezing results practically relevant to seafood identification and market monitoring.

## Key Findings

**Instrumentation and reagents**
- Waters Associates HPLC system with a Model 5000A pump, U6K injector and Model 720 gradient controller.
- Data module set to a chart speed of 0.2 cm/min, with run stop at 60 min.
- Column: 250 × 4.6 mm Ultrapore RP304 with a guard cartridge.
- Detector: UV at 230 nm, sensitivity 0.02 AUFS.
- Mobile phase: solvent A is 0.1% TFA in water; solvent B is acetonitrile–water–TFA (90:5:0.1). The gradient runs from 3% to 70% B over 90 min at 1.5 mL/min.
- Internal standard: bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution.

**Sample preparation**
- Fresh fish is skinned, deboned if necessary, and cut into pieces. A 20–40 g sample is blended for 5 min with an equal weight of distilled water. The blend is filtered, treated with sodium azide to 0.01% for preservation, and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min.
- Triplicate specimens were prepared for each species. The source text is partly garbled on specimen numbers, so exact counts should be checked against the original.

**Results**
- Water-soluble sarcoplasmic protein profiles were obtained for all 31 species. Most peaks were well resolved, and each species had one or more species-specific peaks. All chromatograms were unique.
- Column efficiency was maintained by frequent solvent cleaning and guard column changes. The column was replaced after about 250 injections.
- Reproducibility improved when blends were analysed the same day. Blends became turbid or precipitated on refrigeration. If analysis cannot be done the same day, samples should be refrigerated or frozen intact and blended only when analysis is ready.
- BSA was used as the internal standard for relative retention times. It did not interfere with most samples, but in some species it did, so those samples had to be injected separately. No alternative standard was tested.

**Effects of freezing and cooking**
- Freezing for six months had no major effect on chromatograms, so frozen fish can be identified.
- Cooking by boiling, boiling in 2% salt solution, or autoclaving for 5–15 min altered the chromatograms, so heat-treated fish cannot be identified by the current method.
- The authors suggest that modification with the dissociating agents sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and 2-mercaptoethanol may allow identification of cooked fish, but this was left for future work.

**Identification protocol (seven-step workflow)**
1. Inject 15 μL of the unknown blend alone. Decide whether BSA should be co-injected or injected separately.
2. Inject the same or larger volume, depending on peak size, with or without BSA.
3. Inject 5 μL of BSA separately if required.
4. Calculate average retention times and area percentages for all peaks in both chromatograms.
5. Compare the relative retention time and area percentage of the major peak with those of authentic samples, which eliminates many candidates.
6. Repeat with the second and third major peaks and eliminate non-matches.
7. Compare the unknown visually with the reference chromatograms.
8. Confirm identity by matching relative retention times and area percentages of the remaining peaks.

Identification is possible only for species that have authentic reference standards, which must be established after taxonomic verification.

**Conclusion:** The LC method is rapid, reproducible and applicable to fresh and frozen fish in whole, sliced, filleted and minced form. It cannot yet identify cooked fish.
