# Liquid Chromatographic Identification of Common Fish Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-identification

## Executive Summary

This study presents a simple [[Liquid Chromatography]] (LC) method for identifying fish species from their [[Sarcoplasmic Proteins]], the water-soluble proteins extracted from fish blends. The method was applied to 31 fresh fish species (23 marine, 8 freshwater), each first authenticated by morphological methods. Each species produced a unique chromatogram, allowing reliable identification. Freezing for six months did not alter species-specific patterns, but cooking, including boiling and autoclaving, produced significant changes that prevent identification of heated fish by this method alone. The authors describe the technique as rapid and reliable, requiring no special skill, and applicable to whole, sliced, filleted, and minced fish.

## Background & Description

Fish species identification has traditionally relied on morphological keys, which require a whole specimen and therefore cannot be applied to fillets or minced product. Electrophoretic methods address this limitation but are described as time-consuming, lacking reproducibility, and requiring many preparation steps (gel formation, fixing, staining, and destaining). Among these, [[Isoelectric Focusing]] in thin-layer polyacrylamide gel offers high resolution, but the gel contains the toxic monomer acrylamide, and agarose-based isoelectric focusing shows high variability. Four electrophoretic methods (starch gel, polyacrylamide disc gel, cellulose acetate strip, and thin-layer isoelectric focusing) are official AOAC final action methods.

The LC method builds on earlier work by Ashoor and Knox (1985) and is intended to combine the advantages of [[High Performance Liquid Chromatography]]: simplicity, rapidity, reproducibility, and high resolution. The study had two aims: to apply the method to identification of 31 species, and to evaluate the effects of freezing and cooking.

**Methods overview**

- **Instrumentation:** Waters Associates liquid chromatograph (Model 6000A pump, U6K injector, gradient controller); Hi-Pore RP-304 column (250 x 4.6 mm) with guard cartridge; UV detector set at 280 nm.
- **Mobile phase:** Solvent A was 0.1% TFA in water; Solvent B was acetonitrile-water-TFA (95:5:0.1). A linear gradient of 38 to 70% B over 90 min at 1.5 mL/min was used.
- **Internal standard:** [[Bovine Serum Albumin]] (BSA) at 4 mg/mL, used to calculate relative retention times.
- **Sample preparation:** 20 to 40 g of skinned, boned tissue was blended for 5 min with twice its weight of distilled water, filtered, preserved with sodium azide (0.01% final), and centrifuged at 5000 x g for 5 min before refrigerated storage.
- **Specimens:** Triplicate specimens of each species were obtained fresh from local dealers or collected from area waters. Common names follow the standard reference list cited by the authors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study is laboratory-based and does not address field ecology, population dynamics, or habitat conditions. Its ecological relevance lies in the taxonomic discrimination of marine and freshwater species that are commonly traded or consumed. The authors note that most fish sold in grocery stores is frozen, which makes the demonstrated frozen-sample compatibility practically important. Species represented include commercially important marine fishes (e.g., [[Pollock]], [[Haddock]], [[Pink Salmon]], [[Rainbow Trout]]) and freshwater species (e.g., [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Walleye]], [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]]).

Sample handling affects results. Some blends became turbid, and some formed a precipitate on refrigeration; neither condition reversed at room temperature. The authors recommend that fish not analyzed on the day of receipt be refrigerated or frozen intact and blended only when analysis is ready.

## Key Findings

**Species discrimination**
- All 31 species produced unique chromatograms, and most peaks were well resolved.
- Each species showed one or more specific peaks, enabling identification.
- Chromatograms were compared using relative retention time (relative to BSA) and percent of total peak area.
- Major peaks are reported in Table 1, which lists relative retention times and area percentages for every species. Examples include the major peak at 1.22 relative retention time in Largemouth Bass (20.4%) and Rainbow Trout (18.2%), and the prominent peak at 1.37 in Channel Catfish (63.2%).

**Effect of freezing**
- Skinned, deboned fish stored at -40 °C for six months showed no major chromatographic differences from fresh samples.
- Freezing did not change species-specific LC patterns, so the method applies to frozen fish.

**Effect of cooking**
- Boiling in water or in 2% salt solution (5 or 15 min) and autoclaving at 121 °C (5 or 15 min) produced chromatograms significantly different from fresh or frozen samples, even after short heating times.
- The authors conclude that the LC method as currently developed cannot identify heated or canned fish.
- They suggest that heat denaturation is the cause and propose a modified method using dissociating agents (sodium dodecyl sulfate, urea, and mercaptoethanol) as future work.

**BSA internal standard**
- BSA could be co-injected with most fish blends without interference.
- In some species, BSA overlapped with sample peaks and had to be injected separately.
- No alternative internal standard was tested.

**Column performance**
- High resolution was maintained through frequent solvent cleaning and guard column changes.
- The column was replaced after about 250 injections.
- Reduced column efficiency can cause adjacent peaks to merge, altering chromatograms.

**Recommended identification protocol**
The authors validated an eight-step procedure on frozen commercial and unmarked samples:
1. Inject 15 µL of the unknown blend and decide whether BSA should be co-injected.
2. Inject the same or a larger volume, with or without BSA.
3. Inject 5 µL of BSA separately if needed.
4. Calculate average retention times and area percentages for all peaks.
5. Compare the major peak with authentic standards (Table 1).
6. Repeat with the second and third major peaks, eliminating non-matches.
7. Compare the unknown visually with the matching reference chromatograms (Figure 1).
8. Confirm identity by comparing the remaining peaks.

Identification is possible only for species that have authenticated reference standards.
