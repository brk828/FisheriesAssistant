# Bank-Full Discharge of Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fluvial-geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Garnett P. Williams]] (U.S. Geological Survey, Denver) examines how bank-full discharge is defined, measured, estimated from channel geometry, and related to flow recurrence frequency in natural river channels. Of eleven published definitions of "bank-full," the study evaluates five: the [[Active Floodplain]], the [[Valley Flat]], the minimum width/depth (W/D) ratio, the [[Riley Bench Index]], and the cross-sectional area versus width relation. Sixteen methods of determining bank-full discharge are compared using 28 gaged stations in the western United States. A general power-law regression equation is derived from 233 stations, and bank-full discharge is shown not to have a common recurrence interval among rivers.

The document is a research article published in *Water Resources Research* (December 1978) and does not concern fish or a specific aquatic species. It addresses the physical hydrology of river channels, which is relevant to aquatic habitat, though no biological taxa are studied.

## Background & Description

**Significance.** Bank-full discharge is the flow at which a river begins to overflow its banks, marking incipient flooding. It also influences channel cross-sectional shape and size. It is of interest to engineers, planners, landowners, and [[Fluvial Geomorphology]] researchers.

**Floodplain terminology.** Most authors define a floodplain as the part of the valley floor covered by floodwater, composed of stream-deposited sediment. The study distinguishes:
- An **active floodplain**: an overflow surface periodically built and eroded by the river, undergoing net growth in the past ~10 years. It is often narrow (1–3 m wide) and lower than the valley flat.
- An **inactive floodplain** (terrace): inundated so rarely that it no longer grows by alluvial processes.
- The **valley flat**: the main flat surface in the valley bottom, which may be either active or inactive.

The author recommends that "floodplain" be used for the active floodplain and "terrace" for inactive surfaces, abandoning the vague use of "floodplain" for the valley flat.

**Eleven definitions of bank-full.** Definitions requiring recognition of sedimentary surfaces include the valley flat, active floodplain, low bench, middle bench, most prominent bench, and average height of channel-bar tops. Definitions requiring boundary observations include the lower limit of perennial vegetation and the upper limit of sand-sized bed sediment. Definitions requiring measured cross sections include the minimum W/D ratio, the Riley bench index, and a change in the area–width relation (the study's addition). The eleven definitions could yield up to eleven different bank-full levels at one cross section.

**Methods of measurement.** Four general approaches are described:
1. Rating-curve methods (stage–discharge relation, requiring gaged sites).
2. Hydraulic geometry (plots of discharge versus area, width, or depth).
3. Flow-frequency methods (recurrence intervals from annual maximum series).
4. Flow equations, principally the [[Gauckler-Manning Equation]] (the only method applicable to ungaged sites).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses physical channel processes rather than biological communities. Relevant environmental context includes:

- **Vegetation and sediment as indicators.** Lower limits of perennial vegetation and grain-size changes were considered as bank-full indicators but rejected as unreliable. Vegetation has irregular placement and varies with species and soil/water tolerances.
- **Study sites.** The 28 primary stations span diverse climatic and geographic conditions in the western US (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming, New Mexico, Arizona, Oregon). Stations were selected for natural channels, at least 10 years of record, an active floodplain, perennial flow, and an active gaging program.
- **Range of conditions in the final equation.** Bank-full discharges span 0.5 to 28,320 m³/s; cross-sectional areas 0.7 to 8510 m²; slopes 0.000041 to 0.0810 m/m; bank-full widths 2.1 to 877 m; mean annual precipitation about 200–2000 mm; drainage areas about 7 to 614,000 km²; and bed-material median diameters 0.19 to 190 mm.
- **Bed material.** Median bed-particle diameter (d₅₀) was measured using pebble counts for coarse material and sieving for fines.
- **Channel-width variability.** Bank height and configuration vary along a reach, and the study found that bank-full discharge at a single cross section may differ by up to a factor of 14 within a reach.

## Key Findings

**Definitions and methods**
- The active floodplain is the most meaningful bank-full level to fluvial geomorphologists; the valley flat is most important to engineers because of potential damage to crops, bridges, roads, and buildings.
- Of the eleven definitions, six were not evaluated (low bench, middle bench, most prominent bench, average bar-top height, vegetation limit, grain-size change). The five studied are the active floodplain, valley flat, minimum W/D, Riley bench index, and area–width relation.
- Of 16 methods compared (see Appendix, Table 1), methods 1 and 2 (gage-only) were of doubtful practical use. Method 6 (visual field identification with planimetered area) was indispensable.
- The area–discharge (A vs. Q) plot was the most diagnostic hydraulic-geometry graph; W/D plots scattered more, and depth–discharge plots gave larger errors.
- The minimum W/D and Riley bench index methods often did not match field-identified bank-full levels, with the Riley index sometimes placing bank-full far back on the floodplain.

**Recommended procedures**
- At gaged sites, bank-full discharge should be read from the station's rating curve, using a bank-full gage height derived from a longitudinal floodplain profile along the entire reach (averaging AH across the reach, or the best-fit profile method).
- At ungaged sites, bank-full discharge can be estimated with the empirical equation (4) or the Gauckler-Manning equation.
- Manning's *n* for the Gauckler-Manning approach should be estimated at bank-full conditions, not taken from a measured low-flow value, because low-flow *n* was often unrealistically high (over 0.10 at 11 of 28 stations, maximum 0.53). Method 16 (photograph-based *n*) gave more reasonable results.
- Investigators should specify which bank-full definition, indicator, and discharge method they used.

**Empirical regression equations**
- Equation (1) (active floodplain, 68 stations): Q_b = 3.7 A_b^{0.98} S^{0.25}-type power-law form, standard error 0.181 log units (about 43%).
- Equation (2) (valley flat, 55 stations): standard error 0.150 log units (about 35%). The equations for the two surfaces were statistically indistinguishable at the 5% level, so one general equation was adopted.
- Final general equation (4), from 233 stations: **Q_b = 4.0 A_b^{0.92} S^{0.28}** (Q_b in m³/s, A_b in m², S dimensionless), with standard error 0.174 log units (about 41%) and R² equivalent to 96% of the log sums of squares. Precision is better for large rivers than small streams.
- Width, depth, roughness-related variables, and D₅₀ were insignificant in the regression.
- Equation (4) performed comparably to [[Riggs Equation]] (1976), with a standard error of 0.176 versus 0.180 on the 116 independent stations, and outperformed specialized regional equations (e.g., Emmett 1972, 1975; Kellerhals 1967; Dury 1976; Henderson 1961). The [[Leopold Recurrence Method]] (1.5-year flood) had a standard error of 0.277 log units (about 69%).
- On the 28 basic stations, equation (4) and Gauckler-Manning with photographic *n* had nearly identical standard errors (0.292 vs. 0.297 log units), so their relative precision could not be determined.

**Recurrence frequency**
- Bank-full discharge does **not** have a common recurrence frequency among rivers. For 36 active-floodplain stations, the mode was about 1.5 years on the annual maximum series, but only about one third of cases fell near that peak. The range was 1.01 to 32 years.
- Because of the wide spread and skewness, an average recurrence interval is of little value. Eight Oregon and Colorado stations could not be analyzed because all annual peaks exceeded bank-full discharge.
- Among channel variables, only slope was related to recurrence interval: steeper slopes corresponded to longer intervals between bank-full flows (T = 5–28 years in the range studied), which the author considered too crude for practical use.
- The 1.5-year (or 1.58-year) recurrence interval will not represent bank-full discharge in many cases.

**Overall conclusion**
- Bank-full discharge at a single cross section is of questionable significance because it can vary widely along a reach. A reach-averaged estimate is more meaningful.
