# The Conservation Dilemma of Non-native Versus Native Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This essay by [[W. L. Minckley]], Department of Biology, Arizona State University, argues that [[Non-Native Fishes]] are among the greatest threats to the survival of native fishes in the [[Sonoran Desert]]. It traces how dams, canals, and deliberate stocking transformed Arizona's aquatic fauna, then reviews the mechanisms of decline, notably [[Predation]] and [[Hybridization]], and the policy forces that favored exotics. Recovery efforts are described as partly successful, including [[Native Fish Reintroduction]] and [[Stream Renovation]] projects. The author concludes that removing exotics from isolated habitats is the only workable solution, and that legislation support and public education are essential.

## Background & Description

Before Spanish arrival, the Sonoran Desert region had little surface water and at most 36 fish species within Arizona's present borders. By the time of writing, about 80 species occupied Arizona waters, more than 50 of them from elsewhere. Of the original 36:

- 6 species persist throughout much of their natural ranges.
- 1 is extinct.
- 12 are endangered, 7 threatened, and 10 are of special concern.
- 8 of the threatened or endangered species survive in adjacent states but have vanished from Arizona.

Large dams and canals built early in the twentieth century disrupted natural habitats. Carp, buffalofish, and catfish were stocked as foodfish before the 1920s. By 1960, at least 20 exotic fishes were established. Exotics arrived through several routes: deliberate stocking of gamefish, accidental transfer with gamefish shipments, discarded aquarium pets (such as [[Guppy]]), bait release, and illegal movement by anglers. Some species, including [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Walleye]], and [[Striped Bass]], were imported as larger predators. Non-game species were removed in the 1950s and 1960s, and [[Fish Toxin Renovation]] was applied to hundreds of miles of western rivers before gamefish were planted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The essay identifies several pressures on native fishes:

- **Habitat loss and alteration:** Drying, pollution, diversion-driven temperature changes, grazing, logging, road-building, and erosion reduce ranges. Dams block migrations, and cooler water halts reproduction in warm-water species.
- **Predation:** Carp, basses, sunfishes, and catfishes eat eggs and juveniles of suckers and chubs. Long-lived natives may persist for decades, but populations vanish once old fish die without recruitment. [[Mosquitofish]] eat young topminnows and shred adult fins, leaving them open to infection.
- **Competition:** [[Brown Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] eat native trouts while competing for space and food.
- **Hybridization:** [[Rainbow Trout]], planted in nearly all cooler waters, hybridize with native trouts and can genetically swamp them.
- **Parasites and other aliens:** Non-native fishes carry alien parasites, and an Asiatic tapeworm is spreading among natives with unknown effects. Plants, [[Crayfish]], clams, snails, and [[Bullfrog]]s are also described as dangerous.

Minckley notes that all native fishes tested so far do well in artificial waters without exotics, and that exotics invariably appear in reservoirs and stabilized habitats where natives then decline. Management is complicated because agencies such as the [[U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service]] both enforce the [[Endangered Species Act]] and historically promoted non-native fisheries. Public health agencies also continued to release [[Mosquitofish]] for insect control, a practice the author says may violate ESA "take" rules.

Protected sites include [[Aravaipa Canyon]], [[San Bernardino-Leslie Creek National Wildlife Refuge]], [[Canelo Cienega]], and [[West Turkey Creek]]. Even within these refuges, invasive exotics remain major problems. Other concerns include the [[San Pedro River]] and the exclusion of the [[Colorado Delta]] from imperiled-species plans.

## Key Findings

1. **Exotics are the central threat.** The author argues that non-native fishes are today's greatest deterrent to conserving native species, with predation as the most important mechanism.

2. **Policy explains the imbalance.** Federal policy favored spreading "most desirable" eastern species, immigrants preferred familiar fishes, and agencies kept introducing new kinds to sustain interest and license sales.

3. **Recovery is possible but partial.** Wild razorback sucker larvae were reared in predator-free ponds and repatriated once too large to be eaten, re-establishing a life-history link between young and adults. Topminnow reintroductions have almost doubled the number of occupied sites. Some rare species are held in hatcheries for future reintroduction.

4. **Renovation is the practical solution.** Because exotics are too widespread for total eradication and exclusion is difficult, the author supports isolating habitats. This involves removing exotics by chemical or electroshock methods (for example, at [[Sabino Canyon]], where native chubs were removed before sunfish eradication), restocking native species, and installing barriers such as artificial waterfalls. [[Bylas Springs]], Sabino Creek, and West Turkey Creek are cited as recent examples.

5. **Regulation must be enforced and strengthened.** "No-fishing" rules and bans on live fish and baitfish transport aim to prevent reintroduction of exotics. The author argues that the ESA remains the most significant environmental law yet enacted, but that new or revised conservation legislation is needed for progress to continue.

6. **Education is essential.** Most desert residents are new and assume familiar sportfish are native. The author calls for clearer communication about native versus non-native species and about natural versus artificial aquatic systems, so that "life underwater" receives the same conservation attention as desert wildlands.
