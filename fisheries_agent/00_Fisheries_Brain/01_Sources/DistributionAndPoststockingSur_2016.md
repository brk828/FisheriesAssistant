# Distribution and Post-Stocking Survival of Bonytail in Lake Havasu, 2013-2016

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #telemetry #conservation

## Executive Summary

This multi-year study (2013-2016) documented post-stocking survival, distribution, and habitat use of endangered [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) in [[Lake Havasu]], [[Colorado River]]. Six telemetry iterations and five remote [[PIT Scanning]] deployments tracked 85 acoustic/radio-tagged fish across four study sites: [[Bill Williams River]], [[Blankenship Bend]], [[Regional Park Moabi]], and [[Laughlin Lagoon]]. Survival was extremely low: 44 of 85 telemetry fish (52%) were confirmed mortalities, and 35 (41%) were permanently lost. [[Avian Predation]], particularly by [[Double-Crested Cormorants]] and [[Great Blue Herons]], was identified as a major mortality source. Tags were recovered under roosting sites and on land, with direct observations of predation events. Remote PIT scanning contacted 5-68% of stocked fish depending on site, with most contacts occurring within 2 weeks post-release. Fish showed minimal dispersal from release sites and utilized [[Bulrush]] (Scirpus sp.) habitat when available. Larger fish (>400 mm TL) survived longer than smaller individuals. The study recommends stocking larger fish, utilizing sites with cover and limited egression points, and timing releases to avoid peak avian predator abundance.

## Background & Description

[[Bonytail]] is federally listed as endangered and considered functionally extirpated from its historical range in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species now relies entirely on hatchery stocking programs. [[Lake Havasu]], a main stem reservoir extending approximately 45 km from [[Parker Dam]] upstream to [[Lake Havasu City]], serves as one of few release locations. Since 1981, approximately 218,662 bonytail have been stocked into Lake Havasu, with only 315 recaptured during routine monitoring. The [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (LCR MSCP) established a goal to stock 4,000 bonytail >300 mm TL annually for 40 years beginning in 2006.

Study fish were propagated at [[SNARRC]] (Dexter, New Mexico) or [[Achii Hanyo]] (Parker, Arizona) and surgically implanted with acoustic transmitters (PT-4, 2.3 g; IBT-96-9-I, 3.8 g) or radio tags (F-1810, 6 g). Mean TL ranged 306-401 mm across study iterations. Four study sites represented different habitat types: riverine (Bill Williams River), reservoir (Blankenship Bend), and confined backwaters (Regional Park Moabi, Laughlin Lagoon). Active tracking used directional and omnidirectional hydrophones; passive tracking employed arrays of submersible ultrasonic receivers (SURs) with 200 m nominal detection radius.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Havasu is a highly modified system with extensive non-native fish populations supporting recreational angling. Physical modifications for agriculture and urbanization have drastically altered native fish communities. Non-native predators include [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), and [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*). Piscivorous birds are abundant, including [[Double-Crested Cormorants]] (*Phalacrocorax auritus*), [[Great Blue Herons]] (*Ardea herodias*), [[Great Egrets]] (*Ardea alba*), and [[Osprey]] (*Pandion haliaetus*).

Habitat characteristics varied among sites. [[Bill Williams River]] exhibited higher turbidity (mean 2.08 NTU) from tributary discharge and extensive cattail (*Typha* sp.) coverage. [[Regional Park Moabi]] and [[Laughlin Lagoon]] featured dense bulrush stands providing potential cover. Water temperatures ranged 12.6-20.9°C across studies. Mean depths at contact sites ranged 1.57-3.62 m. Distance to shore averaged 9.88-102.23 m depending on site and mesohabitat type.

Bonytail are documented to be more active nocturnally, with 49-84% of passive contacts occurring between sunset and sunrise across study iterations. Previous studies documented bonytail use of riprap and near-shore habitats in other Colorado River locations.

## Key Findings

**Survival and Mortality:**
- Of 85 telemetry-tagged fish, 44 (52%) were confirmed mortalities and 35 (41%) were permanently lost (never contacted again)
- 83% of lost fish were last located within study areas, suggesting removal by predators rather than emigration
- Mortality was highest at [[Bill Williams River]]: 100% in spring 2013 (N=6, all dead <2 weeks) and 89% in autumn 2014 (N=19)
- At [[Blankenship Bend]], 55% were lost or confirmed dead (N=22)
- At [[Regional Park Moabi]], 29% confirmed dead, 71% lost (N=14)
- At [[Laughlin Lagoon]], 54% confirmed dead, 46% lost (N=24)
- Most mortalities occurred within 1 month post-release except at Laughlin Lagoon where decline was more gradual

**Avian Predation Evidence:**
- 7 acoustic tags and 14 PIT tags recovered under [[Double-Crested Cormorant]] roosting sites
- 5 acoustic tags triangulated under power lines documented as cormorant roosts (2 downstream from [[Parker Dam]], 2 downstream from [[Davis Dam]], 1 upstream from Parker Dam)
- 3 radio tags recovered on land at [[Laughlin Lagoon]]
- Direct observation of [[Great Blue Heron]] capturing and consuming bonytail at [[Regional Park Moabi]]
- Trail cameras documented increased bird presence immediately after stocking events
- One deceased bonytail found with talon markings
- Estimated daily consumption rates suggest birds could consume 30% of stocked fish within 1 month

**Size Effects:**
- Mean TL of study fish: 324 mm (Bill Williams autumn 2014), 349 mm (Regional Park Moabi), 401.5 mm (Laughlin Lagoon acoustic), 404 mm (Laughlin Lagoon radio)
- Larger fish at [[Laughlin Lagoon]] (mean 400+ mm) survived longer: 83% actively tracked through week 4 vs. 0-58% at other sites
- Literature indicates avian predators can consume fish up to 390-500 mm TL depending on species

**Movement and Dispersal:**
- Mean dispersal from release sites: 2.66 km (Bill Williams River), 2.98 km (Regional Park Moabi), 1.14 km (Laughlin Lagoon)
- Mean displacement: 180 km/day (Bill Williams), 163 km/day (Regional Park Moabi), 26.5 km/day (Laughlin Lagoon)
- Fish showed minimal emigration from release sites
- At [[Bill Williams River]], 83% of inlet-released fish remained in inlet; 17% moved to arm
- No evidence of egression from [[Regional Park Moabi]] or [[Laughlin Lagoon]] based on PIT scanning patterns
- Some individual fish displayed site fidelity, remaining in specific locations for multiple days

**Habitat Use:**
- 15-22% of active tracking contacts occurred in or near [[Bulrush]] habitat at [[Regional Park Moabi]] and [[Laughlin Lagoon]]
- 8 bonytail visually observed in shallow water near or within bulrush patches
- Multiple fish actively tracked several meters into bulrush (up to 10 m)
- Acoustic signal completely attenuated within 2 m of thick bulrush
- At [[Bill Williams River]], 6 of 17 recovered tags found under heavy cover (cattails, overhanging debris, woody debris)
- Fish utilized culverts at [[Laughlin Lagoon]]; 3 fish tracked within 200 m of culvert for minimum 3 days
- Mean habitat at contact sites: 48.5% riverine, 45.6% lacustrine, 4.9% peripheral channels, 1% cove (Bill Williams River)

**Remote PIT Scanning Results:**
- [[Blankenship Bend]]: 27% of stocked fish contacted (N=509 released), 96% of contacts in first week
- [[Bill Williams River]] autumn 2014: 5% contacted (N=2,080 released), 70% of contacts in first week
- [[Bill Williams River]] winter 2014-15: 5% contacted (N=4,019 released), 72% of contacts in first week
- [[Regional Park Moabi]]: 68% contacted (N=424 released), 73% of contacts in first 2 weeks
- [[Laughlin Lagoon]]: 23% contacted (N=947 released), 55% of contacts in first 2 weeks
- Unique contacts declined >50% after 2 weeks at all sites
- Baited PIT scanners did not increase contact rates compared to unbaited scanners

**Piscine Predation:**
- One bonytail found in digestive tract of [[Largemouth Bass]] during February 2014 monitoring
- One acoustic-tagged fish exhibited behavior consistent with consumption by [[Striped Bass]]: increased displacement (17 km in 3 days), movement to atypical locations, eventual mortality
- Striped bass can retain consumed tags up to 20 days, complicating mortality determination
- 6 tags recovered under cover in [[Bill Williams River]] inlet may indicate flathead catfish predation
- Trammel netting captured high numbers of largemouth bass (11-20% of total catch) vs. bonytail (0-1%)

**Seasonal and Site Differences:**
- Spring releases at [[Blankenship Bend]] showed greater dispersal (mean 5.7 km upstream) than autumn releases
- Warmer spring temperatures may increase fish metabolism and activity
- [[Laughlin Lagoon]] winter study showed slowest mortality decline, possibly due to larger fish size and dense bulrush availability
- Turbidity at [[Bill Williams River]] (mean 2.08 NTU) lower than previous studies when survival was higher
- [[Double-Crested Cormorant]] populations peak in Arizona during March-April and October-November, coinciding with typical stocking periods

**Monitoring Methods:**
- Off-channel sites with constricted connections ([[Regional Park Moabi]], [[Laughlin Lagoon]]) ideal for tracking survival
- Radio telemetry successfully located 25% of tags removed from water at [[Laughlin Lagoon]]
- Combined radio/acoustic tags recommended but require larger fish (>520 mm TL for available tags)
- Trammel netting during annual "Roundup" captured 8 bonytail in 2014, 0 in 2015, 0 in 2016
- PIT scanning under roosting sites contacted 14 bonytail PIT tags (released 2010-2014)

**Recommendations:**
1. Stock larger fish (>400 mm TL) to reduce predation vulnerability
2. Release in predator-free backwaters with available cover (bulrush, structure, turbidity)
3. Prioritize sites with limited egression points for effective monitoring
4. Time releases to avoid peak [[Double-Crested Cormorant]] migration periods
5. Consider artificial cover structures or exclusion netting to reduce avian predation
6. Implement predator conditioning for hatchery fish prior to release
7. Continue PIT tagging all stocked cohorts for long-term monitoring
8. Deploy PIT scanners under known roosting sites to quantify avian predation rates
9. Use predation-detection acoustic tags when available
10. Conduct controlled studies in enclosed sites to isolate habitat preferences from predation effects
