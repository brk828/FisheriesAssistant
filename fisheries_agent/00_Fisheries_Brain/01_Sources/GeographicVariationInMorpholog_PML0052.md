# Geographic Variation in Morphology of Spikedace (Meda fulgida) in Arizona and New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Anderson]] and [[Hendrickson]] (*Southwestern Naturalist*, 1994) tests whether the four remaining subpopulations of [[Meda Fulgida]] (spikedace), a threatened minnow endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]], differ in body shape and whether males and females differ. Using 147 preserved specimens and 35 morphometric variables, the authors found small but statistically significant morphological differences among subpopulations. The [[Verde River]] subpopulation is most distinct, with a smaller mouth and a longer body relative to body depth, while the [[Gila River]] (New Mexico) and [[Eagle Creek]] groups are intermediate. Sexual dimorphism was present but largely unrelated to geographic variation.

## Background & Description

*Meda fulgida* is a small cyprinid that historically occupied most major tributaries of the Gila River in Arizona, New Mexico and Sonora, Mexico, as well as the mainstem above Phoenix. The species has been extirpated from the [[San Francisco River]], the [[Agua Fria River]] and most of the [[San Pedro River]]. Remnant subpopulations persist in the upper Verde River, Eagle Creek, [[Aravaipa Creek]] and the Gila River in New Mexico, together occupying about six percent of the species' former range. The decline, combined with new threats, led to a federal threatened listing in 1986.

Earlier work described the biology and abundance of the species, mainly at Aravaipa Creek and in the New Mexico Gila system, but did not examine geographic variation in morphology. Marsh et al. (1991) noted that spikedace was not found in Eagle Creek until 1985, despite earlier collecting.

The authors' stated goal was to explore morphological variation among isolated subpopulations and between sexes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat fragmentation:** Recent habitat fragmentation, water extraction and stream impoundment have isolated subpopulations, and the authors conclude that gene flow among them has likely been eliminated. Before European settlement, the Gila and San Pedro rivers were largely permanent, with abundant vegetation, marshes and [[Cienegas]].
- **Environmental influence:** The authors note that environmental factors such as temperature can affect meristic characters, and that some phenotypic variation may reflect environment rather than genetics. They could not control for these factors.
- **Seasonality and condition:** Specimens were collected across all seasons. Variation in the leading size-free components did not show clear geographic, sexual or seasonal patterns, which the authors attribute to general ecological condition, seasonality and reproductive condition.
- **Competition:** Earlier literature cited in the study raises possible habitat competition with the [[Red Shiner]], although this is not tested here.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**
- 147 specimens were measured, with 7 to 10 per locality, from four subpopulations: Verde River, Aravaipa Creek, Eagle Creek and Gila River (New Mexico).
- 35 morphometric variables were recorded with digital calipers, including a box truss of landmarks. Fin ray counts came from 10 individuals per subpopulation.
- Data were log-transformed. Analyses used [[Sheared Principal Components Analysis]] (sheared PCA), followed by regression of each variable against PC1 (a general size component) to remove size effects. The size-free residuals were then tested with [[Analysis of Variance]] (ANOVA) and [[Tukey HSD Test]] post hoc comparisons.

**Geographic variation**
- The second sheared component (H2) explained 92.6% of the size-free variance and was dominated by caudal peduncle width (CPWID), with body width (BODWID) and dorsal-to-pelvic origin distance (DORS-PELV) also contributing. H3 explained a further 4.8%. The authors interpret these components as reflecting ecological condition, season and reproductive state, not geography.
- The fourth component (H4) explained 2.6% of the variance and showed a geographic trend. Mean H4 scores were: Aravaipa −0.595, Verde −0.741, Eagle −0.659 and Gila −0.652.
- Verde and Aravaipa fish differed significantly on most variables tested with Tukey HSD. The exception was interorbital distance (INTRORB).
- Verde fish had smaller mouths relative to body length and longer pre-pelvic and post-pelvic lengths than Aravaipa fish. Eagle and Gila populations were intermediate and partly overlapped the others.
- The authors conclude that no single measurement is diagnostic, so Verde fish can be distinguished from Aravaipa fish only by the overall combination of traits.

**Meristic counts**
- Dorsal fin ray counts were invariant. Pectoral ray counts varied in only three specimens. Caudal and anal counts varied but showed no geographic pattern.

**Sexual dimorphism**
- Dissections of 75 specimens (25 from each of three subpopulations) gave 40 males and 35 females. Subpopulation breakdowns were Verde (11 females, 14 males), Gila (12 females, 13 males) and Aravaipa (12 females, 13 males). Eagle Creek specimens were too scarce to dissect.
- Males had longer pectoral fins. Females had wider bodies, greater pectoral-to-pelvic fin distances and shorter pre-pectoral measurements, and deeper mid-body measurements.
- Caudal peduncle depth (CPDEP) was smaller in females, which the authors link to spawning.
- The authors conclude that geographic variation is essentially unlinked to sexual dimorphism.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Remnant subpopulations differ, but only slightly. Verde is the most distinct, and Gila and Eagle are intermediate.
- The authors suggest that historic specimens from extirpated subpopulations could show whether the remnant groups are discrete parts of a former continuous range of variation.
- Future work should test genetic variation directly to determine whether spikedace should be managed as a single taxon or as a complex of differentiated forms. The authors warn that managing populations without understanding their genetic structure risks losing them, and that morphological variation can lead to taxonomic over-splitting.
