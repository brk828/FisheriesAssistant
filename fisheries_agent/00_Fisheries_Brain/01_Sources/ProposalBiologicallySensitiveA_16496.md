# Biologically Sensitive Aquatic Areas of the Mexico-United States Borderlands

#aquatic-ecology #conservation-biology #borderlands #desert-aquatic-systems

## Executive Summary

This document is a research pre-proposal submitted in March 1993 by W.L. Minckley ([[Arizona State University]]), Salvador Contreras-Balderas ([[Universidad Autonoma de Nuevo Leon]]), and James P. Collins to the Mexico-United States Foundation for Science. The proposal seeks $94,000 over two years to compile, evaluate, and disseminate information on biologically sensitive aquatic areas in the [[Mexico-United States Borderlands]]. The project aims to create a comprehensive, bilingual (Spanish/English) atlas and bibliography identifying sites of conservation importance and development sensitivity, particularly in light of [[NAFTA]] implementation. The proposal emphasizes the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] and [[Sonoran Desert]] regions, which support Earth's largest indigenous aquatic biota persisting under arid-land conditions. Correspondence between investigators discusses budget details, institutional matching funds, and the urgent need for baseline conservation data as regional development accelerates.

## Background & Description

The [[Mexico-United States Borderlands]] span a vast region where the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] and [[Sonoran Desert]] dominate. These arid landscapes harbor exceptional aquatic biodiversity despite extreme water scarcity. The proposal defines "biologically sensitive" sites through two interacting concepts: **rarity** and **uniqueness**. Rare organisms or habitats are inherently more vulnerable to perturbation, while unique taxa carry greater regional conservation value than widespread species. Sites supporting endangered or threatened species, or representing the last remnants of once-widespread habitats, qualify as highly sensitive.

The project would focus on a 100 km band on either side of the international boundary, plus watersheds contributing significantly to borderlands water resources. Examples include the [[Rio Conchos]] (supporting large percentages of original fauna), [[Cuatro Cienegas]], Independence Creek, and springs at Muzquiz and Apodaca. The proposal notes that nearly 200 fish species inhabit arid northern Mexico, with 120 facing extinction threats and 13 extinctions already recorded by 1990. At least 72 springs have gone dry, and some local fish faunas show eradication of 68% of species by 1962-85.

The three principal investigators bring over 20-30 years of regional experience. Minckley and Collins have studied aquatic habitats in southwestern United States and Mexico (primarily [[Coahuila]] and [[Sonora]]). Contreras has worked extensively in Mexican borderlands and in [[New Mexico]] and [[Texas]]. All have collectively trained more than 100 graduate students and advised governmental and non-governmental conservation groups in both countries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The proposal emphasizes that implementation of [[NAFTA]] will accelerate development pressures on already-stressed aquatic ecosystems. Water scarcity is the defining constraint: surface waters have diminished, phreatic (groundwater) levels are dropping, and salinization threatens multiple regions. In [[Sonora]], saline water intrusion (possibly marine) has contaminated agricultural wells. In the Comarca Lagunera, reversed phreatic circulation has introduced arsenic to agricultural waters, threatening the Torreón urban area. In the lower [[Rio Bravo]] (Rio Grande), salinization of the lower 400 km caused replacement of 32 native freshwater or slightly brackish species by 75 species, mainly marine or salt-tolerant forms.

The proposal argues that conservation planning must precede rather than follow development. In the United States, environmental concerns typically emerge only after National Environmental Protection Act, Clean Water Act, or Endangered Species Act Section 7 consultation requirements trigger costly delays and unsatisfying compromises. The borderlands offer a brief window to integrate conservation prerogatives into large-scale [[NAFTA]]-driven development across an entire biotic region.

The project would create permanent literature repositories at [[Universidad Autonoma de Nuevo Leon]], [[Arizona State University]], and potentially other regional institutions. This infrastructure would support both conservation efforts and sustainable development planning by making historical and current data readily accessible to all stakeholders.

## Key Findings

**Project Objectives:**
1. Compile information on geographic areas (sites) of biological sensitivity in the borderlands
2. Publish bilingual (Spanish/English) reports locating biologically sensitive species and habitats with full documentation
3. Establish computerized resource bibliographies and library repositories for all source documents

**Methodology:**
- Begin with recent regional compilations (Hurlbert & Villalobos-F. 1982, Hocutt & Wiley 1986, Ramamoorthy et al. 1993)
- Work backward through literature to earliest exploration records (often <100 years, rarely >200 years)
- Employ labor-intensive classical literature search plus computer databases
- Target unpublished agency reports of limited circulation
- Train graduate students as project bibliographers
- Index by author, subject (taxa, habitat, biological subjects), and geographic locality (compatible with GIS)
- Produce geographic atlas with maps, sensitivity rationales, and complete documentation

**Budget (2 years, total $94,000):**
- Mexico: $51,000 (salaries $25,000; equipment/materials $10,000; domestic travel $1,500; international travel $4,500; other costs $10,000)
- United States: $43,000 (salaries $25,000; equipment/materials $2,000; domestic travel $1,500; international travel $4,500; other costs $10,000)
- Student assistants budgeted at $7.50/hour minimum; computer input personnel at $10.00/hour
- No indirect costs to institutions; matching funds required (estimated >30% in-kind support)

**Conservation Context (from Contreras abstract):**
- Nearly 200 fish species in arid northern Mexico
- 120 species face extinction threats
- 13 extinctions recorded by 1990
- 72 springs documented as dry
- Local fish fauna eradication up to 68% (1962-85)
- Lower [[Rio Bravo]]: 32 native freshwater species replaced by 75 marine/salt-tolerant species over 400 km

**Innovation:**
- Dual intent to enhance both conservation and development
- Binational emphasis and execution
- Integration of research objectives with student education
- Shortening lag-time between data compilation and application
- Bilingual accessibility for bicultural audience
