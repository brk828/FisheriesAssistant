# Endangered and Threatened Fishes of the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 *FRONTIERS* article by [[James D. Williams]] and Dona K. Finnley reviews the decline of native American fishes and the legal and scientific efforts to protect them. It traces habitat loss, pollution, overfishing and nonnative species as the main threats, and it documents the extinction of the [[Harelip Sucker]]. Its central case study is the [[Snail Darter]], whose critical habitat in the Little Tennessee River was not enough to stop the [[Tellico Dam]]. The article also covers Colorado River fishes, desert pupfishes, the [[Cui-ui]], Great Lakes fishes and the international [[Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species]]. It concludes that protection programs are working for some species, and that endangered fishes can still be saved.

## Background & Description

- **Historical diversity:** When European settlers arrived, over 725 fish species swam in the continental United States. Within a few hundred years, many streams were degraded.
- **Current status:** 34 species and subspecies were listed as endangered or threatened, and more than 90 additional fishes may need protection. Worldwide, about 10 percent of terrestrial and freshwater life was considered in danger of extinction.
- **Extinction:** The harelip sucker (*Lagochila lacera*), described by David Starr Jordan and A. W. Brayton, was last seen in Ohio's Auglaize River in 1893. It was the first U.S. fish known to die out entirely because of human activity. At least 11 other species and 4 subspecies are also lost.
- **Legal timeline:**
  - Wildlife protection legislation began around 1900.
  - The [[Endangered Species Preservation Act of 1966]] created an official list and allowed land acquisition for habitat.
  - The [[Endangered Species Conservation Act of 1969]] banned illegal trade and restricted importation, but listing required threat of "worldwide extinction."
  - The [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]] covered all animals and plants, recognized "threatened" species, and required federal agencies to protect [[Critical Habitat]].
- **Representative species:**
  - [[Shortnose Sturgeon]]: once ranged from the St. Johns River in Florida to the St. John River in New Brunswick. Overfishing and estuarine pollution and dredging reduced it.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]]: a protected species in California and Nevada, identified by a sharp-edged hump behind the head.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Main threats:** The article identifies habitat alteration as the most serious threat, in three forms:
  - **Physical:** dams, water diversion, channelization and dredging.
  - **Chemical:** pollution that lowers dissolved oxygen and upsets acid-base balance. Metals, pesticides and [[PCB's]] are described as subtle but lethal.
  - **Biological:** introduced exotic species causing predation, competition and hybridization.
- **Overfishing:** It can decimate a species, but endangerment generally requires habitat alteration as well.
- **Why save species:** Arguments include ecological stability, genetic value, medical and economic potential, research value, and moral or ethical obligation.
- **Colorado River:** This system may have the most depauperate fish fauna in North America. Fewer than ten native species thrived in the main channel before nonnative introductions. The river flows about 1,700 miles, and its spring flood reached 380,000 cubic feet per second.
- **Desert pupfishes:** These are survivors of [[Pleistocene Lakes]] of the southwest. They live in extreme conditions, with temperatures from near freezing to over 110 degrees.
- **Great Lakes:** Jurisdiction is split among eight states and one Canadian province, which complicates management.

## Key Findings

- **Snail darter (*Percina tanasi*):**
  - Discovered in 1973 by David Etnier and listed as endangered in November 1975.
  - Critical habitat (the lower 17 miles of the Little Tennessee River) was designated in April 1976.
  - The [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] (TVA) continued [[Tellico Dam]], which would bury the habitat under about 50 feet of water.
  - The District Court of Knoxville denied an injunction in May 1976. On January 31, 1977, the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals halted the project despite $100 million already spent.
  - More than 700 darters were transplanted to the Hiwassee River, and recent evidence suggests reproduction has occurred.
- **Colorado River fishes:**
  - The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is North America's largest minnow, reaching 4 to 5 feet and up to 80 pounds before 1900. It is now endangered.
  - Four big-river species (squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail and humpback sucker) face trouble. Squawfish and humpback chub are endangered. The other two were under review.
  - Over 20 dams have been built since [[Hoover Dam]] (completed 1935). Exotic fishes now dominate the main channel.
- **Devil's Hole pupfish (*Cyprinodon diabolis*):**
  - Its entire habitat is a pool 10 by 50 feet, 60 feet below the desert floor in Death Valley National Monument.
  - The U.S. Supreme Court ruled in its favor in 1976.
  - After the water level was stabilized, the population grew to nearly half its original size.
- **Owens pupfish (*Cyprinodon radiosus*):** Believed extinct in 1940 after bass predation, it was rediscovered in 1964 in Fish Slough, with fewer than 200 individuals. It has since been reintroduced.
- **Amistad gambusia (*Gambusia nobilis*):** Discovered in 1968 in Goodenough Spring, its habitat was submerged by Amistad Reservoir. Forty individuals were collected before this, and the species is now extinct in the wild. It propagates well in captivity, so prospects are considered fair.
- **Cui-ui (*Chasmistes cujus*):**
  - This Pyramid Lake, Nevada, sucker was blocked from its [[Truckee River]] spawning grounds by the Newlands project (1906) and Derby Dam.
  - The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] began a recovery program in 1972. In 1976 the hatchery produced 1.4 million eggs and 800,000 fry.
  - A fish ladder was built in 1975, but its success was not yet known.
- **Great Lakes fishes:**
  - The [[Longjaw Cisco]] and [[Blue Pike]] are listed as endangered.
  - Blue pike landings were nearly 15 million pounds in 1922 from Lake Erie alone, and near zero by 1960. Genetic swamping through hybridization with walleye is a likely final blow.
  - The deepwater cisco (last seen 1951) and blackfin cisco (last taken 1969) may be extinct.
- **Trout recovery:**
  - In July 1975, Lahontan cutthroat, Paiute cutthroat and Arizona trout were moved from endangered to threatened.
  - Greenback cutthroat trout were believed extinct by 1937. Two unhybridized populations were later found. In 1976, 65 fish were transplanted to Bear Lake in Rocky Mountain National Park, bringing known populations to nine.
- **International protection:**
  - The [[Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species]] was created in 1973 with 80 nations.
  - The U.S. implemented it through a presidential Executive Order in April 1976, with regulations completed in February 1977.
  - The U.S. Act protects some foreign fishes not on the Convention appendices, and the reverse is also true.
- **Conclusion:** The authors argue that endangered species protection is working, and that with proper legislation and attention, vanishing fishes can be saved.
