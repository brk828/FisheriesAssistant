# Microchemical Analysis of Aravaipa Creek and Adjacent Aquatic Habitats

## Executive Summary
This document is a quarterly progress report (and follow-up correspondence) for a contracted study by [[Milton R. Sommerfeld]] of Arizona State University, conducted for the [[Bureau of Land Management]], examining microchemical (dissolved and total elemental) water chemistry of [[Aravaipa Creek]] and its tributaries in relation to land and water use management. The study was conducted in parallel with a broader aquatic ecosystem investigation by [[W.L. Minckley]]. While the report is primarily a water-chemistry and watershed-management document rather than a fish morphology study, it contains ecologically relevant data regarding potential mercury contamination and its implications for the aquatic food chain, including fish as apex consumers.

## Background & Description
No morphological data on fish species are presented in this source. The document is administrative/analytical in nature, focused on stream chemistry methodology (Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry) rather than organismal biology. Fish are referenced only functionally, as the presumed terminal consumers in the aquatic food web relevant to mercury bioaccumulation studies (see [[Key Findings]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **System type**: [[Aravaipa Creek]] is characterized as a desert stream with an "intermittent and variable flow regimen," posing sampling challenges typical of arid-land lotic systems.
- **Spatial scope**: Sampling encompassed 15 locations along the main stem of Aravaipa Creek plus several tributaries — [[Paisano Canyon]], [[Turkey Creek]], [[Virgus Canyon]], and an unnamed tributary — reflecting a hierarchical stream network with distinct main-stem vs. tributary chemical signatures.
- **Temporal scope**: Four sampling periods were designed to capture major differences in stream discharge and to represent summer, fall, and winter seasonal conditions.
- **Trophic context**: Fish are identified as occupying the top of the aquatic food chain in this system, making them the most relevant biotic indicator for bioaccumulative contaminants such as mercury.
- **Anthropogenic pressures noted**: Old mine and milling sites (historical residue inputs to the streambed), potential reduction in water flow, and agricultural chemical use in the upper Aravaipa Valley were flagged as vulnerability factors for the watershed's aquatic habitat.
- **Hydrological data caveat**: All discharge (flow) data in the report derive from a single U.S.G.S. gauging station in lower Aravaipa Creek, meaning upstream hydrological conditions were not directly captured by this dataset (supplementary upstream discharge data were collected separately by [[W.L. Minckley]]).

## Key Findings
- Over 800 individual elemental analyses were performed on Aravaipa Creek samples (34 samples across 4 sampling periods, 15 locations, 12 chemical elements, each measured as both dissolved and total concentration), plus over 300 additional analyses on tributary samples.
- Approximately 40 replicates per element were obtained for both dissolved and total concentration categories across the sampling area and period.
- Instrumentation problems with the Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer caused a 6–8 week delay in sample analysis during the reporting quarter but were ultimately resolved.
- **Mercury** was identified as the one element for which data were judged inadequate, due to (a) high variability among measurements, (b) fewer analyses performed owing to instrument issues, and (c) the short viable storage window (a couple of weeks) for mercury samples before degradation/contamination risk.
- Several mercury measurements exceeded 1–2 ppb; an extremely high mercury value recorded in November in Aravaipa Creek was independently rechecked via dilution series and verified as either a real environmental signal or a sample contamination artifact — the cause was not conclusively resolved.
- A follow-up mercury sampling plan was proposed: 10 of the original 15 Aravaipa Creek sites (sites 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 11, 13, 14, 15) plus the four tributaries with elevated readings (>1 ppb), with 5 samples per site (~70 samples per visit) across 2–3 visits, estimated at $1,500–$1,700.
- Contingent recommendation: if dissolved/total mercury levels proved abnormally high, **fish tissue analysis** would be pursued specifically because fish represent the top of the aquatic food chain and were expected to show the highest bioaccumulated mercury concentrations; if levels were not abnormally high, tissue analysis of selected biota would be foregone.
- Known/potential contamination sources flagged by BLM included legacy mine and milling site residues entering the streambed, plus concerns over reduced water flow and agricultural chemical inputs in the upper Aravaipa Valley — both relevant to long-term aquatic habitat and fish community health.
- Figures originally combining main-stem Aravaipa Creek and tributary chemical data were revised to exclude tributary values, in response to concerns that overlapping unconnected/connected data points obscured clarity given the tributaries' markedly different chemical concentrations.
