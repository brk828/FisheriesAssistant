# Experimental aquatic vegetation control

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-vegetation-management

## Executive Summary

This 1960 completion report from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] documents an early experimental effort to identify effective mechanical and/or chemical (herbicide) methods for controlling nuisance aquatic vegetation in District I waters. The study is not fish-focused but addresses aquatic habitat management, specifically targeting invasive/nuisance macrophyte growth ([[Myriophyllum]] and [[Ranunculus]]) that can degrade fish habitat, alter water chemistry, and impede recreational and fisheries management activities. Two herbicides—**Simazine** and **Endothall**—were field-tested at limited scale due to project time constraints. Simazine, applied at 100, 150, and 200 lbs/acre, showed **no observable control** of dense Myriophyllum growth. Endothall was applied late in the season (September) to a small pond with heavy Ranunculus growth, and no effect was observed by the end of the 1960 reporting period, with follow-up evaluation recommended for 1961.

## Background & Description

Not applicable — this report concerns aquatic macrophyte (vegetation) control rather than fish or faunal morphology. No fish species, invertebrates, or plankton taxa are described. The target organisms are vascular aquatic plants:

- **[[Myriophyllum]]** (watermilfoil) — described as exhibiting "heavy growth," implying a dense, submerged, feathery-leaved macrophyte capable of forming nuisance stands in still water.
- **[[Ranunculus]]** (water buttercup) — also described as forming "heavy growth" in a small pond environment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** Small standing freshwater bodies (ponds) within [[District I]] waters of Arizona, management under the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] fisheries program.
- **Vegetation role:** Both [[Myriophyllum]] and [[Ranunculus]] are submerged/emergent aquatic macrophytes capable of forming dense monotypic stands that can be considered "weed pests" — problematic for water access, recreational use, and potentially fish habitat quality (e.g., oxygen depletion, overcrowding of open water, altered predator-prey dynamics).
- **Management context:** The study fits within a broader fisheries management objective — aquatic vegetation control was pursued to maintain healthy, usable, and ecologically balanced pond/lake systems for game fish populations, though no direct fish data were collected or reported in this segment.
- **Chemical control agents:**
  - **[[Simazine]]** — a triazine herbicide, applied as 10% granular formulation, hand-broadcast from a boat.
  - **[[Endothall]]** — a contact herbicide, applied via surface spray.

## Key Findings

- **Test design:** Three 50 ft × 50 ft plots treated with granular Simazine at increasing rates: 100, 150, and 200 lbs/acre (applied May 16, 1960).
- **Simazine result:** **No control effect** observed on heavy [[Myriophyllum]] growth at any tested dosage, up to 200 lbs/acre — indicating ineffectiveness of Simazine for this species under the tested application method and conditions.
- **Endothall trial:** One small pond with heavy [[Ranunculus]] growth was treated with Endothall (applied September 6, 1960).
- **Endothall result:** **No effect observed during 1960**, likely attributable to the **late-season application timing**; further monitoring recommended into 1961 growing season.
- **Recommendation:** Granular Simazine up to 200 lbs/acre is **ineffective** for Myriophyllum control and should not be pursued further at these dosages. Endothall requires an additional year of observation before conclusions can be drawn. Continued herbicide trials recommended as scheduling permits.
- **Methodological notes:** Visual assessment was the sole method used to determine herbicide effectiveness — no quantitative biomass, density, or water chemistry measurements were reported.
- **Broader implication:** This report highlights the trial-and-error nature of early aquatic herbicide research and underscores the importance of **application timing** (phenological stage of target vegetation) in determining herbicide efficacy, a recurring theme in aquatic vegetation management literature.
