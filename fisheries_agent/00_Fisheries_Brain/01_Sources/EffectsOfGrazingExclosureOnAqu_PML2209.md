# Effects of Grazing Exclosure on Aquatic Macroinvertebrates in a Montane Stream, New Mexico

#freshwater-ecology #aquatic-macroinvertebrates #entomology

## Executive Summary

This 1986 report by John N. Rinne examines whether [[Livestock Grazing]] exclusion for roughly a decade changed the [[Aquatic Macroinvertebrates]] community of the [[Rio de las Vacas]], a third-order montane stream in northern New Mexico. Macroinvertebrates were sampled in 1982–1984 in fenced (ungrazed) and grazed reaches. Densities and biomass were often higher in the grazed reaches, but the community composition differed clearly between treatments. Ungrazed reaches supported more pollution-sensitive taxa, which the author interpreted through [[Tolerance Quotient]] and [[Biotic Condition Index]] measures and a [[Chi-Square Index]] of community structure. The author concludes that the study demonstrates the need for pre-treatment data and a [[Watershed Approach]] to grazing research.

## Background & Description

**Context.** Livestock grazing is widely linked to the degradation of riparian and stream habitats in the western United States, and exclosure fencing is a common management response. The [[U.S. Forest Service]] fenced sections of this stream to exclude domestic livestock and improve instream and streamside habitat and biota. The effects were studied independently by the [[Rocky Mountain Station]], roughly a decade after fencing.

**Study area.** The Rio de las Vacas drains the San Pedro Parks Wilderness Area within the [[Santa Fe National Forest]], about 17 km southeast of Cuba, New Mexico, at roughly 2,600 m elevation. The exclosures were established in 1972 and 1975 and are separated by private land. Each exclosure is about 1 km long and 50 m wide, for a total of about 10 ha of fenced stream. The exclosures lie within the Cuba Community Allotment, which is about 11,000 ha (figure garbled in the scan). Nearly half the allotment is classed as "non-allowable capacity" for grazing, and roughly 17% of the land is private and/or riparian. Grazing from 1949 to 1980 was continuous and season-long, June 1 to October 31.

**Methods (summary).**
- **Sampling:** Summer low-flow surveys using a standard [[Surber Sampler]] in grazed and ungrazed reaches. Samples were preserved in formalin and later in Kahle's fluid.
- **Habitat stratification:** From 1983 onward, samples were stratified by substrate, targeting pebble-cobble habitat (16–24 mm) with uniform depth and velocity, to reduce variability.
- **Identification:** Specimens were identified to the lowest practical taxonomic level using [[Ward and Cummins Keys]] (Usinger 1956; Merritt and Cummins 1984).
- **Biomass:** Estimated from length–dry weight equations (Smock 1980), with body length sorted into seven size classes.
- **Indices:** Tolerance quotients (TQ), the Biotic Condition Index (BCI), and the Chi-Square Index (Pearsall and Wagner 1983).
- **Statistics:** Non-parametric Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests and standard t-tests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream conditions.** The lower grazed reach had higher total alkalinity and dissolved solids than the ungrazed reach. Mean conductivity was significantly higher in grazed reaches (216 vs. 164 µmhos, as read from the scan). The lower grazed reach also had more silt in its substrate and a lack of riparian vegetation, which increased solar radiation. The author suggests these conditions, together with slightly elevated water temperatures, may have favored higher invertebrate densities.

**Pattern of change.** The fenced reaches differed from the grazed reaches in both stream-bank stability and riparian vegetation. The two areas were therefore not structurally identical before fencing, which limits direct before-and-after comparison.

**Related fauna.** The report mentions salmonid fish and a riparian garter snake (*Thamnophis* study, Szaro et al. 1985) as related monitoring subjects, but no fish data were collected in this study. The author notes that macroinvertebrates may be more sensitive to grazing effects than fish, and that [[Stream Continuum Concept]] principles suggest downstream effects may be buffered.

## Key Findings

**1. Community composition differed between treatments.**
- Mean Chi-Square index values were 1.23 for 1983 and 2.3 for 1984, which the author describes as indicating a "marked difference" in community structure between grazed and ungrazed reaches.
- Ungrazed reaches had higher mean BCI values in 1983 and 1984.

**2. Tolerance patterns favored sensitive taxa in ungrazed reaches.**
- Mean TQ was significantly higher in ungrazed reaches in 1982 and in the lower grazed reaches in 1984.
- Two sensitive taxa, *Helicopsyche* sp. (TQ = 18) and *Epeorus longimanus* (TQ = 48), averaged 95 and 37 individuals per m² in grazed reaches, compared with 137 and 77 in ungrazed reaches.
- Two tolerant taxa, *Calopsectra* sp. and *Tricorythodes minutus* (TQ = 10 to 10.8, as read), averaged 396 and 95 individuals per m² in grazed reaches. Ungrazed reaches held far fewer (only a few individuals each).

**3. Density and biomass were often higher in grazed reaches.**
- Densities were significantly greater at grazed sites in all years except 1984 (when upper and lower grazed reaches were separated).
- Biomass differed significantly between treatments only in 1982.
- Macroinvertebrate biomass was consistently greater and more variable in grazed reaches.

**4. Taxonomic diversity was high.**
- The study recorded 11 orders, 39 families, and 65 genera.
- Ephemeroptera, Diptera, and Trichoptera dominated biomass. Dipterans included Chironomidae and Ceratopogonidae.
- Five orders and several taxa were common enough to support the treatment comparisons.

**5. Terrestrial insects were recorded in samples.**
- Average counts per sample were 8.22 vs. 2.15 (1983) and 1.4 vs. 0.9 (1984), with the grazed and ungrazed values listed in that order in the text.

**6. Interpretation.**
- Grazing appears to increase invertebrate abundance while shifting the community toward more tolerant taxa. The author attributes this to higher alkalinity, dissolved solids, and substrate silt in the grazed reaches, and possibly to increased solar radiation.
- The author notes that dissolved solids may benefit high-elevation headwater communities, and that macroinvertebrate communities are sensitive to these water-quality components (Kreuger and Waters 1983).
- Because pre-fencing data are lacking, the author cannot definitively attribute changes to exclosure alone. Fencing did not clearly "greatly enhance" salmonid populations in the stream, and no fish data were collected here.

**7. Recommendations.**
- Collect pre-treatment data before implementing management actions, so that baseline variability is known.
- Use a [[Watershed Approach]] (Gregory et al. 1986; [[Likens]] 1984) that considers upstream and downstream linkages rather than single reaches.
- Use standardized, stratified sampling to reduce variability.
- Macroinvertebrates, measured with BCI and Chi-Square indices, are a valuable early indicator of habitat change from grazing and other management actions.
- Coordinated research between scientists and managers can increase result reliability and help resolve multiple-use conflicts.
