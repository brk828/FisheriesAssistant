# Potential Economic Consequences of Designating Critical Habitat for the Rio Grande Silvery Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This April 1996 cover letter from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (New Mexico Ecological Services Field Office) and the attached February 1996 draft report by ECONorthwest assess the economic effects of designating critical habitat for the [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (RGSM), an endangered species listed in 1994. The Service proposed designating the mainstream of the Rio Grande from Cochiti Dam to the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe Railroad crossing near San Marcial, New Mexico. The report concludes that the designation's economic effects are probably net-neutral to slightly positive for the nation, with localized costs concentrated on irrigators and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] river-maintenance program. The draft is released for public comment; the letter notes that no final decision on critical habitat had been made, and that public hearings would follow.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The RGSM (*Hybognathus amarus*) was listed as endangered by the Service on July 20, 1994. It once inhabited much of the Rio Grande but now survives in roughly a 120-mile stretch of the Middle Rio Grande, the sole survivor of a guild of five related species.
- **Legal context:** Under the [[Endangered Species Act]], critical habitat designation prohibits federal agencies from authorizing, funding or undertaking actions that destroy or adversely modify habitat. The listing itself prohibits "taking" of the fish. The letter states that a 1995 congressional appropriations moratorium (Public Law 104-6) prohibited work on the final listing and final critical-habitat determination for a period; the economic report was prepared to inform that process.
- **Report scope:** The report, prepared by [[ECONorthwest]] and framed around a six-step economic analysis process (Figure 1.2), examines the proposed designation through a with-versus-without framework.
- **Study area:** The analysis covers counties adjacent to and downstream of the habitat, anchored by four metropolitan areas: Santa Fe, [[Albuquerque]], Las Cruces and El Paso (about 98 percent of study-area population resides in these MSAs).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Average inflow to the Middle Rio Grande is about 1,060,000 acre-feet per year (af/yr) at Otowi Bridge, of which roughly 200,000 af/yr reaches [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]]. Agriculture and municipal users consume most of the remainder.
- **Flow regulation:** Cochiti and Elephant Butte dams, El Vado and Abiquiu dams on the Chama system, and diversion dams at Angostura, Isleta and San Acacia shape flows. The [[Low-Flow Conveyance Channel]] has been inoperable since 1985 due to sedimentation.
- **Riparian habitat:** The [[Bosque]] riparian forest provides recreation, wildlife habitat and aesthetic value. Riparian areas are described as among the most diverse habitats in the terrestrial environment, and loss of their natural character is a concern.
- **Water rights:** Water is allocated by prior appropriation under New Mexico law, with Indian water rights, the [[U.S.-Mexico Water Treaty of 1906]] (60,000 af/yr to Mexico) and the [[Rio Grande Compact of 1938]] forming senior claims. New Mexico does not recognize instream water rights.
- **Institutions:** The [[Middle Rio Grande Conservancy District]] (MRGCD) manages irrigation across about 123,000 acres with rights to roughly 258,861 af/yr, though it actually irrigates about 54,000 acres per year.

## Key Findings

**1. Mechanism of impact.** The designation affects the economy through a single mechanism: restrictions on federal agency activities. Private activities on non-federal land are not directly restricted unless federal permits, licenses or funding are involved.

**2. Agency responses.** Of the federal agencies queried, twelve either did not respond or indicated no impact on their activities (Table 5.2). The Bureau of Reclamation was the only agency reporting it would alter operations. The [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] stated that changes required by the listing would also satisfy critical-habitat requirements.

**3. Bureau of Reclamation river maintenance.** The BuRec estimated river-maintenance costs of $3–5 million per year (Cochiti Dam to San Marcial). The designation could raise costs by up to 40 percent. The program previously salvaged about 14,400 af/yr; under the designation, up to 4,000 af/yr less water might be available. A 31 percent budget reduction would lower program spending to $2.1–3.4 million.

**4. Dollar values of lost water.** Under a loss of 4,000 af, the economic loss is about $90,000–$96,000 if the water curtails irrigated pasture (net earnings near $19/af per acre, with marginal value of BuRec water estimated at zero), or about $1 million if it reduces urban supply (marginal value above $300/af). Lost instream recreational fishing value could reach about $400,000.

**5. Irrigation effects.** Reduced water would likely fall first on pasture and alfalfa, the lowest-earning crops. The report estimates a loss of roughly $26,000 to irrigators in the Socorro area, with about $10,000 among agricultural workers.

**6. Non-use value.** A 1995 University of New Mexico survey (Berrens et al.) found New Mexico households willing to pay about $30 per year for five years to protect the RGSM, implying about $1.7 million per year statewide (about $8.5 million over five years). The report notes these figures are aggregates, not per-acre-foot values, and do not apply solely to the RGSM.

**7. Quality of life.** The designation is expected to maintain or modestly improve the bosque's visual and recreational amenities, and to support Native American cultural uses of the river. The report cannot quantify the net amenity effect.

**8. Dynamic adjustment.** Static estimates overstate impacts. Because firms, workers and water users can adapt, the report expects minor and short-lived effects on capital, property and labor markets. Potential responses include crop shifts, pricing reforms and conservation, which may simply move forward existing trends.

**9. Subsidy findings.** Federal subsidies to irrigators are substantial (the report cites a 1994 Interior estimate of about $30 per acre-foot in 1982 for Bureau water, with some estimates above $50/af today), so the marginal net benefit of subsidized irrigation water may be zero or negative. The designation may thus improve national efficiency.

**10. Overall conclusion.** The report concludes the proposed designation has both positive and negative effects, with the overall net national effect "probably close to zero" and likely positive. Costs are localized and visible, while benefits are dispersed. The authors note the designation does not affect private property values except through federal activity restrictions, and that fairness judgments are left to the Director.

**11. Public comment.** The report requests comments on physical characteristics of the Rio Grande, economic linkages, valuation of nonuse benefits, agency impacts and effects on groups of special concern (poor, minority and Native American communities). The Service invites comments to the New Mexico Ecological Services Field Office in Albuquerque, with Jennifer Fowler-Propst as contact.
