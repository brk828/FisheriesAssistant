# Food Habits of the American Merganser in Relation to Fish Populations in Certain New Mexico Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anatidae

## Executive Summary

This New Mexico Department of Game and Fish bulletin (No. 9, 1959) by [[Earl H. Huntington]] and [[Austin A. Roberts]] examines whether [[American Merganser]] (*Mergus merganser americanus*) predation seriously reduces game fish in warm-water sport fisheries. Based on 949 birds collected in 1954–1957 and fish population surveys from several reservoirs and river reaches, the authors conclude that the proportion of game fish eaten is small, that selectivity is slight or absent, and that mergansers cause little or no damage to warm-water sport fishing. Diet largely reflected fish availability, dominated by [[Gizzard Shad]].

## Background & Description

The American merganser is a large, colorful diving duck with a serrated beak suited to catching fish. Local names include sawbill, big fish duck, and dun-diver. The question of how many game fish mergansers consume had long interested sportsmen, and no current fish population data existed for the study waters, so the authors compared merganser stomach contents with independent population surveys.

Fish populations were sampled with rotenone, seines, gill nets (300 ft, 1–4 inch mesh), hoop nets, and traps. Merganser stomachs were collected from shot birds, preserved, and analyzed at the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] laboratory under Howard L. Leach. Austin Roberts conducted field observations and population surveys over six and a half months each winter from 1953 to 1957, using [[Aerial Survey Methods]], blinds, and boats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Elephant Butte Reservoir** (Rio Grande, completed 1916, up to 38,000 acres): fluctuating water levels prevented rooted aquatic vegetation. Rotenoning showed 95.5% rough fish, with [[Gizzard Shad]] at 91.5% of catch. Game fish were about 4.5%.
- **Caballo Reservoir** (about 20 miles south of Elephant Butte, 11,500 acres): fluctuating levels and high turbidity produced barren shorelines. Seining and gill netting gave 98.2% rough fish.
- **Conchas Reservoir** (Canadian River, 16,640 acres, Upper/Lower Sonoran zone merge): the exception, with 97.4% game fish in rotenone samples, mainly [[Bluegill]] (56.0%) and [[Crappie]] (35.0%).
- **Lower Rio Grande** (20-mile reach below Elephant Butte Dam): flow controlled by dam outlets, with flash floods adding silt. Winter pools concentrate fish, increasing predation exposure.
- Water chemistry and temperature were not measured. Weather influenced feeding mainly through freezing of waters, and [[Wind Velocity]] reduced fishing success.

## Key Findings

**Fish populations**
- Most waters were dominated by rough fish. Conchas was the exception, dominated by game fish.
- Rotenone tests on the lower Rio Grande (winter 1957–58) killed 87,261 lb of rough fish (99.0% by weight) and 893 lb of game fish (1.0%), mainly carp (40,976 lb), carpsucker (37,606 lb), and gizzard shad (8,084 lb).
- Species checklists: Elephant Butte held about 24 species, Caballo about 21, Conchas 13, and the lower Rio Grande 18 (summarized in Tables 3, 4, 6, and 8).

**Merganser diet**
- A total of 949 mergansers was collected (512 Elephant Butte, 209 Caballo, 117 Conchas, 22 lower Rio Grande, 14 upper Rio Grande, 62 Alamogordo, 13 other areas).
- Elephant Butte: gizzard shad 88.9% of fish eaten (rotenone 91.5%); carp 5.2% (rotenone 3.2%); game fish 6.3% (rotenone 4.6%).
- Caballo: 100% of fish eaten were rough fish, 95.2% gizzard shad and 4.8% carp.
- Conchas: 89.4% of fish eaten were game fish, mainly sunfish, crappie, and largemouth bass.
- Lower Rio Grande: 100% rough fish, mainly carp and shad.
- Across all waters, 54.0% of birds contained gizzard shad, 9.5% carp, 5.2% crappie, and 3.1% sunfish. 17.8% of birds had no identifiable food.
- Of 295 gizzard shad measured from gullets, 228 were 0–4 inches. Largemouth bass to 11.0 inches, crappie to 11.8 inches, and carp to 10.0 inches were also recorded.
- Of 344 fish measured from 128 digestive tracts, the 0–4 inch class dominated, with 228 gizzard shad in that size class.

**Selectivity and behavior**
- Diet broadly matched fish availability, indicating minor or no selectivity. The authors identify availability as the most important factor.
- A single 12–14 inch carp was lodged by its dorsal spine in a merganser's jaw. This was the largest fish recorded in the study.
- Birds in flocks of more than 1,200 averaged 6.28 fish per bird (103.3 cc) versus 2.04 fish (24.4 cc) in flocks under 20. Birds in winds of 20 knots or less averaged 6.52 fish (111.7 cc) versus 2.48 fish (77.0 cc) in stronger winds.
- Mergansers may drive fish into shallows through group feeding, but this was not demonstrated.

**Population and migration**
- Estimated New Mexico merganser populations (mid-January surveys) ranged from 7,139 (1956) to 27,816 (1954), with 8,883 (1950), 21,215 (1951), 8,840 (1952), 26,598 (1953), 7,464 (1955), and 8,478 (1957).
- Mergansers move south when northern waters freeze, concentrating on Conchas, Alamogordo, Elephant Butte, and Caballo reservoirs.

**Conclusions**
- Little, if any, damage to warm-water sport fishing results from merganser predation. Rough fish reduction may be beneficial.
- Damage could occur on very small waters holding valuable fish, given the generally non-selective feeding.
- Cold-water species were not studied, so these conclusions do not extend to them.
