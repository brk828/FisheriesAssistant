# Native Arizona Fishes, Part III: "Chubs" (with Insect and Agency Notes)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This issue of *Wildlife Views* (Arizona Game and Fish Department magazine) contains three main items. The first is a short popular account of stinging and biting insects of Arizona, covering [[Fire Ant]], [[Harvester Ant]], [[Velvet Ant]], [[Paper Nest Wasp]], and [[Honeybee]] stings and first-aid advice. The second, and the main scientific article, is Part III of the "Native Arizona Fishes" series by John N. Rinne and W. L. Minckley (Department of Zoology, Arizona State University, Tempe). It covers the native "chubs" of the genus *Gila* (family [[Cyprinidae]]), which make up almost half of Arizona's native minnows. The third item is a light quiz of federal, state, and conservation agency abbreviations, with answers promised on page 27.

## Background & Description

**Insects (page 1).** The article describes:
- **Harvester ants:** red with brown abdomens. They do not normally bite but can inflict a painful sting. The reaction usually stays local but can become general, spreading through the body along the lymph channels.
- **Velvet ants** (also called cow ant, ground wasp, cow killer, wolly ant): actually wingless female wasps, not true ants. They are brightly colored (black with red, yellow, orange, or chocolate-brown abdomens), hairy, and considerably larger than ordinary ants. They are good runners with potent venom, emit a faint squeaking sound when handled, and are parasites of bees and other wasps. The most common Arizona forms measure from "14 to 1 inch" in length (OCR text; likely 1/4 to 1 inch).
- **Wasps:** the main Arizona concern is the paper nest wasps (*Polistes* spp., *Vespula* spp., and *Mischocyttarus* spp.). Spider wasps such as the [[Tarantula Hawk]] are also present but are not discussed further.
- **Honeybees:** widespread and established in desert conditions, but usually found only in apiaries or wild bee trees. Stings are normally local, but some people are allergic, and there are records of people and domestic animals dying of shock after many stings. First aid: scrape the sting out with a knife blade or fingernail, and never pull it out, because squeezing the poison sac can inject more venom.

**Fish article (pages 2–9).** The authors state that the name "chub" is applied to many thick-bodied, dark, relatively large minnows of North America, but in Arizona it is generally reserved for six species of *Gila*:
- **Two Mexican species, mostly southern in distribution:**
  - [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*): enters the United States only in the extreme headwaters of the Rio Yaqui on the San Bernardino Ranch, east of Douglas, Cochise County. It is deep-bodied, darkly pigmented, has rounded fins, relatively large scales, and a distinct caudal spot. About four inches in total length (Fig. 1). It now survives in a single spring alongside [[Sonora Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*). A pre-1900 record from Morse Canyon (Wilcox Playa drainage) is unverified.
  - [[Sonora Chub]] (*Gila ditaenia*): known to science only since 1945. It lives in Sycamore (Bear) Canyon, west of Nogales, Santa Cruz County. It is fine-scaled, darkly olive, with a whitish belly and two lateral bands (the name *di* means "two" and *taenia* means "bands"). About five inches in total length (Fig. 2). Adults rarely exceed six or seven inches in Arizona waters.
- **Three widespread species and subspecies:**
  - [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*): restricted to the Gila River system of southern Arizona, most abundant in pools of smaller creeks, swampy areas, and sometimes artificial ponds. About six inches in total length (Fig. 3). Males become reddened on fin bases, jowls, and lower body during spring spawning.
  - [[Colorado River Chub]] (*Gila robusta*): the most widespread and abundant chub in Arizona streams. It has three subspecies: *G. r. robusta*, *G. r. grahami*, and *G. r. seminuda* (Map 2; Fig. 4). Adults can reach about 20 inches and more than 1.5 pounds. Local names include "Verde trout," "River trout," "Gila trout," and "bonytail," the last of which the authors say should technically be reserved for *G. elegans*.
- **Swift-water specialists, restricted to the Colorado River mainstream:**
  - [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*): formerly lived throughout the larger rivers of the lower Colorado basin but is now restricted to the Colorado mainstream (Map 3). It has a depressed skull grading gently to a smooth dorsal hump; Fig. 5 shows a specimen from the Green River, Utah, about 15 inches in total length.
  - [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): one of the most unique fishes in North America. It differs from the bonytail in having an abrupt dorsal hump, a shorter caudal peduncle, and an inferior, horizontal mouth. It often has greatly reduced scalation, possibly to reduce friction in the swift, silt-laden Colorado. Described by R. R. Miller (1946) from Grand Canyon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat change:** Changes in southern Arizona aquatic habitats over the past century have had adverse effects on the Gila chub, and it is now absent from many former localities.
- **Restricted ranges:** The Yaqui chub survives in one spring; the bonytail is confined to the Colorado mainstream; the humpback chub lives in swift, turbulent Colorado habitats.
- **Hybridization:** The authors report "massive hybridization" among *G. elegans*, *G. cypha*, and *G. robusta* near Glen Canyon Dam, Page, Arizona, during the past few years. Specimens from that area were to be carefully preserved or frozen and sent to the university for analysis.
- **Diet and interactions:** Adults are principally carnivorous, feeding on aquatic invertebrates and sometimes other fishes. Smaller individuals feed mainly on aquatic plants, usually algae. Chubs may compete with game species as adults, but the authors note this has not been specifically studied in Arizona.
- **Human use:** Larger chubs are taken by anglers. They provide sport in the Phoenix canal system for children and on light fly rods for adults (e.g., a large "Verde trout"). Flesh is described as sweet and firm but bony. Indigenous peoples of Arizona ate them in the past.
- **Biogeography:** The genus *Gila* is distributed across much of western North America and northern Mexico. Its relationships and probable ancient water connections may shed light on prehistoric drainages of the arid Southwest.
- **Cited work:** The bibliography includes Miller (1945, 1946, 1961), Miller and Lowe (1967), Hubbs (1941), Minckley (1968, 1969), Minckley and Alger (1968), and Rinne (1969, unpublished M.S. thesis).

## Key Findings

- Arizona has six native *Gila* chub species: two Mexican (Yaqui and Sonora chubs), three widespread (Gila chub, Colorado River chub, and bonytail/humpback in the swift-water group), with the Colorado River chub being the most widespread and abundant.
- The Yaqui chub is restricted to a single spring, and the Sonora chub is known from Sycamore (Bear) Canyon; both are small, rarely exceeding six or seven inches in Arizona.
- The Gila chub has declined from many former localities due to habitat change in southern Arizona.
- The bonytail and humpback chub are restricted to the swift, turbulent Colorado mainstream, and the humpback chub has reduced scalation as an adaptation.
- Hybridization among *G. elegans*, *G. cypha*, and *G. robusta* near Glen Canyon Dam is a concern, and the authors requested preserved or frozen specimens for genetic analysis.
- Chubs are principally carnivorous as adults, eating invertebrates and fishes, while juveniles eat algae.
- The authors identify the greatest value of chubs as scientific, for biogeographic insight into prehistoric drainages of the Southwest.
- The issue's insect section advises on stings from harvester ants, velvet ants, paper nest wasps, and honeybees, including the rule to scrape rather than pull out honeybee stings.
- The closing page is a quiz on agency and organization abbreviations relevant to Arizona outdoor management (e.g., BLM, FWS, NPS, USFS, USDA, BIA, DU, AG&F, KOFA), with answers on page 27.
