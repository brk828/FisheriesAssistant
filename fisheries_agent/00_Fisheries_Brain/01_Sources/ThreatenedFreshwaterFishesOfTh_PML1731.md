# Threatened Freshwater Fishes of the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1972 paper by [[Robert Rush Miller]], published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (vol. 101, no. 2, pp. 239–252), is the first national compilation of threatened native freshwater fishes of the United States. It lists threatened taxa for 49 of the 50 states; [[Rhode Island]] had none reported. The list contains approximately 305 kinds, and the paper is a report of the [[Endangered Species Committee]] of the [[American Fisheries Society]] and the [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]], with additional input from the [[IUCN Red Data Book]] and the U.S. Department of the Interior.

The author states that the lists are incomplete. State coverage is uneven: some states have complete lists, while for many, current information is almost nonexistent and urgent surveys are needed. The paper's stated purpose is to stimulate protective legislation and to build broader conservation concern for all native wildlife.

## Background & Description

**Context and motivation.** Serious recognition of threats to U.S. native fishes emerged mainly after 1963. Conservation committees were formed within scientific societies, and the [[Endangered Species Preservation Act 1966]] marked the first concerted federal effort to protect native fauna. Since the late 1960s, states, particularly in the West, had been passing protective legislation, creating refugia, and managing habitats. In June 1971, a bill was introduced in Congress to establish a Desert Pupfish National Monument to protect the [[Desert Pupfish]] and related species.

**Threat categories.** The paper uses IUCN-derived definitions:

- **Endangered:** actively threatened with extinction; continued survival unlikely without special protective measures.
- **Rare:** not under immediate threat of extinction, but occurring in small numbers or in a restricted or specialized habitat that could quickly disappear. Requires careful watching.
- **Depleted:** still occurring in numbers sufficient for survival, but declining substantially faster than it can be sustained.
- **Indeterminate:** apparently threatened, but insufficient data are available to assess status reliably.

An asterisk marks taxa recognized nationally as endangered. The author notes that a "rare" species may be relatively stable, and that a "depleted" species may remain listed for a period after its populations begin to increase through better management. Borderline cases were decided individually.

**Taxonomy.** Family arrangements and common and scientific names follow the [[Names of Fishes Committee]] of the American Fisheries Society (Bailey et al., 1970), with a few exceptions. Species described after that publication are referenced individually. State submissions were reviewed by state agencies before publication.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper identifies several main drivers of decline:

- **Pollution:** industrial, agricultural, and toxic chemical inputs.
- **Habitat alteration:** excessive damming of rivers, which converts lotic (flowing) habitat to lentic (still-water) habitat; channelization; and removal of water.
- **Introduced species:** exotic predators or competitors with broader ecological tolerances than native fishes. Exotics may also transmit parasites and diseases. The role of such factors was treated in earlier work, including Deacon and Bunn (1970) and Lachner et al. (1970).

The author emphasizes that status often reflects vulnerability to future threats rather than present decline. Many species are rare but relatively stable. Numerous factors, including genetic and environmental ones, affect the probability of survival for rare or pressured species. Maintaining diversity of life is presented as a goal that depends on combining the efforts of dedicated researchers, agencies, and legal frameworks.

## Key Findings

**Scope and structure of the list**

- Approximately 305 threatened freshwater fish kinds in the United States (per the Introduction).
- Lists are organized by family, then by state. Each state section gives common names with status: endangered, depleted, rare, indeterminate, or possibly extinct.
- Lampreys (Petromyzontidae) account for 7 listed taxa and sturgeons (Acipenseridae) for 6. Gars (Lepisosteidae) and herrings (Clupeidae) each have 3.
- The largest groups by number of entries are the minnows and shiners (Cyprinidae), darters and perches (Percidae), and trouts, whitefishes, and ciscoes (Salmonidae).
- Hawaii's listed fishes include native gobies (*O'opu* species) and the Hawaiian stream snail-eating goby *Lentipes* taxa.

**Representative nationally significant entries**

- **[[Shortnose Sturgeon]]** (*Acipenser brevirostrum*): endangered in several Atlantic states.
- **[[Gila Trout]]** (*Salmo gilae*): listed in New Mexico as rare and endangered.
- **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon diabolis*): Nevada, rare and endangered.
- **[[Pallid Sturgeon]]** (*Scaphirhynchus albus*): listed across several Midwestern and Plains states.
- **[[Cape Fear Shiner]]** (*Notropis mekistocholas*): described by Snelson (1971) as a new herbivorous cyprinid endemic to the Cape Fear River Basin, North Carolina.
- **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): Arizona and Utah, rare and endangered.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): rare and endangered in Arizona, Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming.
- **[[Cui-ui]]** (*Chasmistes cujus*): endangered in Nevada.
- **[[Ozark Cavefish]]** (*Amblyopsis rosae*): rare in Arkansas and Missouri.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*): rare and endangered in Arizona, with propagation underway.
- **[[Atlantic Salmon]]** (*Salmo salar*): endangered in Connecticut and Maine (sea-run form).
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): endangered in several Western states.

**State-level examples from the paper**

- **Alabama:** Lake sturgeon, Atlantic sturgeon, and shovelnose sturgeon are depleted; the Cahaba shiner is listed.
- **California:** Has the largest number of listed entries in the West, including the Lahontan cutthroat trout (depleted), Unarmored threespine stickleback (endangered), and several pupfishes, including the Owens pupfish (endangered).
- **Florida:** The Key silverside is rare and endangered; the Suwannee bass is rare.
- **Texas:** Multiple endemic taxa are listed, including the San Marcos gambusia, Comanche Springs pupfish, and Rio Grande darter.
- **Nevada:** Devils Hole pupfish, Cui-ui, and Railroad Valley springfish are all listed as rare or endangered.

**Conservation implications**

- Recognition of threatened status is a necessary first step; without lists, appropriate action cannot be taken.
- Lists are provisional; state data quality varies greatly and many states need ongoing surveys.
- The author argues that the lists will help stimulate protective legislation and broader conservation attention to all native wildlife.
