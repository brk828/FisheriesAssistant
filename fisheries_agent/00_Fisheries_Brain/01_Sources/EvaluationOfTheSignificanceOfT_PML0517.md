# Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River: Recovery Needs, Peer Review and Workplan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document combines a 1998 peer-review cover letter from the [[Colorado River Water Conservation District]] with three technical items: a critique by [[Mussetter Engineering]] of the [[15-Mile Reach]] flow recommendations, a draft review by [[Tyus and Saunders]] of recovery needs for four endangered fishes, and a prioritized workplan derived from both. The four listed species are [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]]. The central arguments are that flow recommendations for the 15-Mile Reach rest on untested assumptions and a macro-scale analysis, that nonnative fishes are the most significant current threat to recruitment, and that recovery requires a multispecies or ecosystem framework with explicit numerical targets.

## Background & Description

**Peer review request.** In a letter dated 10 August 1998, Ray Tenney of the River District asked Dr. Paul Marsh (Arizona State University) to peer review three draft reports (January and October 1997–1998). The sponsors intended to use the reviews to inform the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] (RIP) and did not seek RIP approval of the work.

**Mussetter Engineering report (October 1997).** Commissioned to evaluate the significance of the 15-Mile Reach to basinwide recovery. The reach represents about 2 percent of the currently designated critical habitat. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) flow recommendations for the reach would require delivery of as much as 400,000 acre-feet of additional water annually.

**Tyus and Saunders report (1998).** A review and synthesis of life history, limiting factors and recovery actions for the four big river fishes, prepared at the [[Center for Limnology]], University of Colorado at Boulder. It is the source of most biological data in the document.

**Workplan overview.** A prioritized set of recovery tasks for the UCR, plus program-level issues, designed as a logical extension of the two reports.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Basin alteration.** More than 50 dams and major diversions were built on the mainstem by the 1960s. Flow regulation has reduced average instantaneous flows in the Grand Valley reach to about 8 percent of historic conditions, and mean peak flow at the head of the 15-Mile Reach is about 56 percent of historic levels. Some reaches have lost about 90 percent of silt load. Nonnative fish introductions and habitat conversion have displaced native fishes; some habitat now supports at least 17 nonnative species.

**Stream zones and history.** The basin's native fish fauna shows high endemism (38 of the recent native species and subspecies are endemic). Native fishes evolved under extreme seasonal flow variation and turbidity.

**Geomorphic context.** The 15-Mile Reach has been heavily modified by levees, bank revetments, gravel mining and reduced flows. Side channel and slackwater habitat declined by roughly 36 percent between 1937 and 1995 in the broader study reach, while main-channel area changed little. Bed material is mobile for only a few weeks per year, and the Mancos Shale outcrop limits lateral migration.

**Nursery and spawning habitat.** Colorado squawfish spawning in the upper basin is confirmed mainly in the Green and Yampa systems; in the UCR, spawning sites remain only suspected. Larvae drift downstream to backwater nurseries, and nonnative predators dominate these backwaters.

## Key Findings

**Critique of the 15-Mile Reach flow recommendations (Mussetter Engineering)**
- The USFWS flow recommendations for the 15-Mile Reach (Osmundson et al. 1995) represent an improvement over the earlier 1991 report but remain methodologically inadequate. They rely on untested assumptions that a lack of appropriate flows is the primary limitation to habitat formation and recovery.
- Extrapolating data from the [[Lower Yampa Canyon]] (a wider, steeper reach with a well-developed floodplain) to the 15-Mile Reach is not valid. The reach has about 20 percent flatter gradient and different bed material conditions.
- The habitat-flow analysis works at macro scale, whereas habitat formation is a meso- and micro-scale process. A one-dimensional hydraulic model (such as HEC-2), coupled with sediment data and incipient-motion analysis, is the minimum needed.
- Claims that flushing fines is needed for any life stage are unsupported by data. Repeat cross-section surveys did not confirm annual flushing.
- Off-channel gravel pits are not a valid surrogate for inundated floodplain habitat because depths and substrates differ.
- Squawfish spawning in the 15-Mile Reach is uncertain. Available physical process models suggest that natural conditions probably did not meet spawning-habitat requirements there, and that any current spawning reflects man-made changes.
- Recommended actions: broaden the review team to include a geomorphologist and hydraulic engineer; develop testable habitat-formation models for each life stage; build a one-dimensional hydraulic model of the full reach; and quantify sediment transport at macro, meso and micro scales.

**Recovery needs and conclusions (Tyus and Saunders)**
- After 20 years of effort in the UCRB, none of the four species has been recovered. The Colorado squawfish and humpback chub are at best holding even, the razorback sucker has declined, and the bonytail is considered gone from the basin.
- Nonnative fishes are likely the single greatest threat, especially for early life stages. Removing nonnatives from backwater nurseries is recommended, with a target reduction of at least 50 percent for several months and a three-year pilot program.
- Adult Colorado squawfish abundance may be more limited by prey abundance than by physical habitat, which has implications for flow management.
- Stocking has not increased any extant population. For example, about 1,500 six-year-old squawfish were released near Moab in 1980, and only 5 of 411 adults checked (1.2 percent) carried coded-wire tags. Razorback stocking survival was roughly 10 percent or less.
- Recovery targets should specify the number of populations and the target size of each. Current IMO targets are considered not defensible.
- Priorities: nursery habitat evaluation; spawning site identification; adult habitat improvement in the 15-Mile Reach; characterization of habitat upstream of barriers; passage of migration barriers; and evaluation of restoration potential in the 15-Mile Reach.

**Workplan tasks (selected)**
- Immediate: remove nonnative fishes from squawfish nursery habitat (Task 1-1); initiate population dynamics monitoring (Task 1-2); determine squawfish spawning locations (Task 1-3); translocate squawfish and humpback chub above Debeque Canyon (Task 1-4); locate potential spawning sites basin-wide (Task 1-5).
- Within 3–5 years: determine relative importance of habitat and prey (Task 2-1); determine flow–nursery habitat relationships (Task 2-2).
- When resources allow: physical habitat modifications as an alternative to flow manipulation (Task 3-1); local rearing facilities for razorback suckers (Task 3-2); test whether stocked fish behave differently from wild fish (Task 3-3).
- Program issues: population size and number of populations (Task A); broader review and synthesis (Task B); tracking of all introduced individuals (Task C).

**Management implications**
- Physical habitat is necessary but may not be sufficient for recovery; integrated control of nonnatives and flow management is needed together.
- Recovery planning should move toward a multispecies or ecosystem recovery plan and geographically prioritized targets.

**Program-level and monitoring points**
- The Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program (ISMP) cannot produce population estimates for target species and is not system-wide. A population-estimation program repeated at roughly five-year intervals is recommended.
- The Green River flows-to-nursery-habitat relationship was crucial for instream-flow recommendations, and an equivalent study for the UCR is needed.
- Selenium and bottomland acquisition were deliberately left out of the workplan because information was insufficient at the time.
