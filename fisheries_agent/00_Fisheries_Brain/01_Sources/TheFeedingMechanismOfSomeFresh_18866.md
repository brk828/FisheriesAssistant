# Feeding Mechanism of Some Freshwater Cyclopoid Copepods

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyclopidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper by G. Fryer describes how freshwater cyclopoid copepods capture, hold, and ingest their food. Using direct observation of captive animals, the author describes the mouthparts of [[Macrocyclops Albidus]] in detail and documents the feeding behaviour of carnivorous and herbivorous cyclopoids. The study concludes that cyclopoids are raptorial, not filter feeders, as some ecologists had assumed. The paper then uses feeding functions to speculate on the evolution of parasitism and on the phylogeny of the Cyclopoida and [[Calanoida]].

## Background & Description

The paper is a companion to Fryer's earlier work on the food of freshwater cyclopoids and its ecological significance. Earlier authors, including Hartog (1888) and Sars (1913–18), described the mouthparts as grasping organs, yet ecologists working on freshwater plankton often treated cyclopoids as filter feeders. Fryer states that this assumption is erroneous.

The mouthparts examined in detail were those of *Macrocyclops albidus*, which the author regards as representative of the freshwater Cyclopoida. Reduction occurs mainly in the mandibles and maxillipeds of aberrant genera.

Key appendages described:

- **Labrum:** a flat, chitinised plate that closes the mouth when raised.
- **Mandibles:** stout shafts with toothed masticatory processes, twisted about 90° and dorsally curved so the teeth lie inside the oesophagus. Each bears three posterior setae, two very long.
- **Paragnathae:** serrated knobs on a post-oral bar that close like pincers.
- **Maxillules:** the main clasping organs, with three heavily chitinised distal teeth. Their offset arrangement lets them act like secateurs.
- **Maxillae and maxillipeds:** jointed appendages with internal muscles that move segments independently. Their spines and basal setae push food toward the mouth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Carnivorous cyclopoids, apart from planktonic species, are closely confined to the substratum and swim over the bottom or plant surfaces. Antennular and antennal setae contact the substratum and act as tactile sensors for locating prey. The ocellus and frontal discs are positioned so that they are close to objects encountered during normal movement.

Captive carnivores did not appear to detect prey by sight or smell. They relied on chance encounters, and *Macrocyclops albidus* was observed making fifteen attacks on a large chironomid larva over about ten minutes. Fryer notes that cyclopoids are nonetheless widespread and successful, possibly because suitable food is abundant enough to be encountered frequently even with a clumsy search method.

Herbivores, which fed on diatoms and filamentous algae, appeared to discriminate between organic and inorganic particles, possibly through gustatory or odour perception. Herbivores required four to five days of starvation before they would feed reliably.

Food types and foods used in the study include chironomid larvae, oligochaetes (*Nais* sp.), other copepods (such as *Diaptomus gracilis*), coracidia of *Triaenophorus* tapeworms, trematode cercariae, nematode larvae, and algae such as *Scenedesmus*, *Micractinium*, *Hydrodictyon*, and *Spirogyra*. Vertebrate prey, including small fishes and amphibian larvae, is also recorded for carnivorous species.

## Key Findings

- **Raptorial, not filter feeding:** Cyclopoids use a "raptatory" method. Even herbivores that create water currents do not filter; the mouthparts are essentially grasping organs.
- **Prey seizure and handling:** Prey is seized by the maxillules, which act as the principal clasping organs. The maxillae and maxillipeds embrace prey and push food toward the mouth.
- **Mandibular action:** Mandibles tear rather than masticate. Movements are intermittent, lasting about two to four seconds, with pauses averaging about one minute during chironomid feeding. Large objects are often swallowed nearly whole.
- **Feeding duration:** A chironomid larva of 3 mm or more took more than thirty minutes to eat. The shortest recorded meal was nine minutes, for a 2 mm larva, with the head rejected. A *Nais* of 4 mm was engulfed in 3–5 minutes.
- **Head severance:** Chironomid heads were removed by the mandibles, assisted by vigorous kicking from the three posterior appendage pairs. In one *Acanthocyclops viridis* case, four buffetings of four to five seconds each preceded severance.
- **Stomach capacity:** The volume of a 3–4 mm *Nais* was estimated at about 0.4 mm³, against an estimated *M. albidus* stomach capacity not greatly exceeding 0.3 mm³. The author attributes the difference to compression of broken food.
- **Herbivore feeding on algae:** *Hucyclops macruroides* fed on Scenedesmus and Micractinium without damaging the cells. Filamentous algae were eaten by severing filaments (maxillules alone) or by puncturing them and apparently sucking out contents. Suction is suspected, with the oesophagus as the likely site.
- **Limited digestion of algae:** *Spirogyra* (average diameter about 92 µ) was largely not ingested. Chloroplasts passed through the gut undigested.
- **Herbivores on Scenedesmus and Micractinium:** These algae passed through the gut apparently undamaged.
- **Swimming in herbivores:** *H. macruroides* swam slowly using only the first pair of swimming legs.
- **Evolutionary inference:** A carnivorous diet is proposed as the primitive condition of the Cyclopoida, with herbivory a later acquisition. Herbivory may have enabled the rapid speciation of *Hucyclops* and *Microcyclops*.
- **Parasitism:** Prolonged feeding on large prey followed by quiescence resembles the behaviour of semi-parasitic marine cyclopoids such as *Ascomyzon*, suggesting a route from free-living to parasitic habits.
- **Conservatism of the mouthparts:** The same structures function similarly in extreme carnivores and herbivores, and differences between species are of the kind used in systematics.
