# Habitat Use by Fishes of the San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Master's thesis from the [[University of Arizona]] (1995) quantifies microhabitat use by three endangered or threatened Rio Yaqui fishes, the [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Yaqui Topminnow]], and [[Beautiful Shiner]], on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (SBNWR). Field work used snorkel transects and bank observations in three artesian ponds (Tule, North, Oasis) and in [[Leslie Creek]], with 385 fish observations recorded over 18 months. The study tests how depth, substrate, cover, and velocity relate to life stage, season, and co-occurring species, and it closes with management recommendations for refuge ponds and for stocking decisions.

## Background & Description

The refuge lies in the San Bernardino Valley, part of the Rio Yaqui Basin of Sonora and Chihuahua, Mexico. Groundwater pumping, diversion, grazing, and non-native fishes such as [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and [[Largemouth Bass]] led to the extirpation of most Rio Yaqui fishes from the United States by the mid-1960s. The refuge was established by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] as a refugium, with land and water rights acquired after 1979 following purchase by [[The Nature Conservancy]]. The refuge is closed to the public to limit fish transfers between water bodies.

Six Rio Yaqui species are the refuge's focus (Table 1). The Yaqui chub and Yaqui topminnow are federally endangered, and the beautiful shiner is threatened. Yaqui catfish, Yaqui sucker, and Mexican stoneroller do not currently occur on the refuge but are planned for reintroduction. Longfin dace (Yaqui form) occurs in Leslie Creek, and roundtail chub is managed incidentally.

Species profiles from the text:
- **Yaqui chub**: occupies deep pools near undercut banks, debris, and dense aquatic plants. Diet ranges from algae and terrestrial insects to aquatic insects and small fishes. Its taxonomy is disputed: populations in Sonora were described as *Gila eremica* (Demarais 1991), which would make *G. purpurea* rarer.
- **Yaqui topminnow**: a small livebearer found in shallow, warm, quiet water. Diet includes detritus, algae, and invertebrates.
- **Beautiful shiner**: found in riffles, pool midwaters, and ponds; feeds on drifting insects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Refuge aquatic habitats are mostly artificial ponds fed by artesian wells and springs. The ponds are isolated from one another and closed to immigration and emigration, so they may become the last refugia for these species. Vegetation thinning is used to maintain them. Historically, the region had monsoon-drought cycles, and fishes persisted in isolated pools, which the authors use to justify the pond habitats.

Study sites:
- **Tule Pond** (~0.03 ha, maximum depth 15 m): contains only Yaqui topminnows. Dissolved oxygen fell as low as 0.5 mg/l near the bottom, linked to decomposition of organic matter.
- **North Pond** (~0.10 ha, maximum depth 2.0 m): Yaqui topminnows co-occur with Yaqui chubs. Summer growth of Typha and submergent plants was thinned periodically.
- **Oasis Pond** (~0.12 ha, maximum depth 2.1 m): topminnows, chubs, and beautiful shiners co-occur. Lacks woody riparian vegetation.
- **Leslie Creek**: a permanent reach of shallow riffles and pools in the Swisshelm Mountains, averaging about 843 m historically and measuring about 1032 m in an April 23, 1994 survey. Mean annual flow was 0.0074 m³/sec in the 1989 water year.

Water chemistry included pH 7.82 ± 0.39 and CO₂ of 83 ± 60 mg/l from refuge well water (Longsworth 1991), and temperatures of 10–31 °C across observations. Bullfrogs (*Rana catesbeiana*) were a possible predation factor, with removal efforts most intensive at North Pond and least at Tule Pond.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Snorkel transects with a weighted buoy, supplemented by bank observations in shallow water. Habitat availability was sampled at 1-m intervals in ponds and 0.25-m intervals in Leslie Creek.
- Use and availability were measured about every 8 weeks. Statistics included Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis, chi-square, and Bonferroni simultaneous confidence intervals.
- Life-stage definitions: Yaqui chub YOY < 15 mm TL, juveniles 15–40 mm, adults > 40 mm (Demarais and Minckley 1993). Yaqui topminnow juveniles < 25 mm, adults > 25 mm.

**Sample sizes (Table 6)**
- 385 total observations: 214 Yaqui chub (56%), 149 Yaqui topminnow (39%), 22 beautiful shiner (6%).
- Observed fish: 2,322 chubs, 4,827 topminnows, and 105 beautiful shiners.
- Oasis Pond community: 85% topminnows, 11% beautiful shiners, 4% chubs. North Pond: 83% topminnows, 13% chubs.

**Yaqui topminnow**
- Tule Pond juveniles preferred the surface (relative depth mean 0.92 ± 0.21). Adults showed no vertical preference (mean 0.58 ± 0.30).
- In North/Oasis Ponds, juveniles preferred the surface (mean 0.72 ± 0.32), with adults at 0.52 ± 0.30.
- Adults and juveniles selected open substrates over vegetated ones. Topminnows in Oasis Pond were close to cover (14 ± 17 cm); North Pond averaged 46 ± 77 cm.
- Topminnows were observed in water < 80 cm about 63% of the time, and in water up to 2 m deep over submerged vegetation.
- Large schools congregated at a groundwater inlet in North Pond during daylight.

**Beautiful shiner (Oasis Pond only)**
- Mean school size 4.8 ± 6.5 fish; sizes 8–55 mm.
- Most often found at relative depth 0.6–0.8 in water 40–100 cm deep. Avoided water < 40 cm.
- Did not select dense vegetation; cover was weakly associated (23 ± 16 cm from cover).

**Yaqui chub**
- Ponds: juveniles favored silt substrates, while adults were almost exclusively over plants (97.3%).
- Leslie Creek: both life stages selected silt substrates, interpreted as a preference for pools > 40 cm deep.
- Adults selected deeper water (typically > 1 m in ponds) and avoided velocities > 0.04 m/s. Nose velocities were commonly at or near 0 m/s.
- Chub distance to cover in Leslie Creek averaged 41 ± 52 cm, versus 24 ± 27 cm in ponds.

**Biotic interactions**
- Yaqui chub habitat profiles did not change with the presence of other species.
- Topminnows selected cover in Oasis Pond (with chubs and shiners) but not in North Pond (with chubs) or Tule Pond (alone). The authors consider differing bullfrog predation a plausible confound.

**Management recommendations**
- Maintain ponds in an early successional stage. Chub need water > 1 m deep; shiners need areas free of dense vegetation > 40 cm deep.
- Tule Pond became choked with sedges, cattails, and Phragmites, causing low oxygen. Authors recommend techniques to augment oxygen or limit organic matter.
- Leslie Creek topminnows declined severely after a mid-1980s drought and have not recovered; restocking should be experimental and monitored.
- Postpone stocking beautiful shiners with Yaqui topminnows until interactions are better understood.
- Consider outflow from ponds to Black Draw to improve interconnectedness, and genetic monitoring or exchange between populations.
- Yaqui chub show high fecundity and fast population growth, which may buffer against genetic loss, but drought bottlenecks in Leslie Creek may reduce numbers to 10s–100s of individuals.
