# Meeting Notes: Lower Salt River Winter-Time Releases (June 13, 1997)

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #salmonidae #riverine-management

## Executive Summary
This document is a set of meeting notes and follow-up correspondence from the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), addressed to Dr. W.L. Minckley (Arizona State University), regarding a proposed collaborative water-management project on the lower [[Salt River]]. The core proposal involves integrating winter-time water releases (50–150 cfs) from [[Stewart Mountain Dam]] with operations of the [[Granite Reef Underground Storage Project]] (GRUSP), using released water for aquifer recharge instead of [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water. The stated goals are to extend a coldwater recreational fishery into the winter months while establishing baseline biological monitoring to evaluate effects on native fish assemblages and [[Bald Eagle]] foraging habitat. While the document is primarily a policy/logistics record, it contains embedded ichthyological observations about the differential success of native fish species in this regulated reach compared to upstream reaches, and notes the potential ecological trade-offs of altering historic winter flow patterns.

## Background & Description
The source text is administrative/policy in nature and does not provide morphological, meristic, or anatomical descriptions of the fish species mentioned. Species referenced by common name only:

- **[[Desert Sucker]]** (*Catostomus clarkii*) — native catostomid
- **[[Sonora Sucker]]** (*Catostomus insignis*) — native catostomid
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*) — native cyprinid, a species of conservation concern in the Gila River Basin
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) — non-native, stocked salmonid supporting the recreational fishery

No morphometric, coloration, or life-history-stage data are given in the source.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context**: The reach between [[Stewart Mountain Dam]] and [[Granite Reef Dam]] is a regulated tailwater section of the lower [[Salt River]], subject to hydrologically-controlled flow regimes tied to hydropower and water-supply operations.
- **Historic flow pattern**: Winter releases from Stewart Mountain are typically low (roughly mid-October/mid-November through March), driven by hydrologic conditions rather than ecological considerations. Releases below 300 cfs do not generate hydropower.
- **Effect of low winter flows on fishery management**: Low winter flows currently cause AGFD to cease [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking in the river reach, shifting stocking efforts to the impoundment above [[Granite Reef Dam]].
- **Native fish productivity**: The document notes that this specific reach "has been very productive for some native fishes" — [[Desert Sucker]] and [[Sonora Sucker]] "appear to have done very well," and [[Roundtail Chub]] are also present. This is explicitly contrasted with the reach of river **above** [[Roosevelt Diversion Dam]], where conditions apparently differ (implying lower native fish success upstream).
- **Uncertain causal link to flow regime**: The authors explicitly flag uncertainty about whether this native fish productivity is a *result* of the existing low winter-flow conditions — a key open question for the proposed baseline monitoring.
- **Avian predator interaction**: [[Bald Eagle]] use this same reach for foraging, suggesting a linked prey base (likely including these fish populations) and creating a direct conservation concern if flow alterations or increased recreational angling pressure affect eagle foraging success.
- **Anthropogenic pressure**: The reach currently supports approximately 20,000 angler-days annually as a coldwater sport fishery; the proposal would extend this pressure into previously low-use winter months, raising concerns about compounding effects on land management (USFS) and special-status species.

## Key Findings
1. **Proposed intervention**: Integrate 50–150 cfs winter releases from Stewart Mountain Dam with GRUSP recharge operations, substituting river water for CAP water without net loss of water yield to the Salt River Project (SRP).
2. **Native species differential**: [[Desert Sucker]], [[Sonora Sucker]], and [[Roundtail Chub]] appear notably more successful in the Stewart Mountain–Granite Reef reach compared to the reach above [[Roosevelt Diversion Dam]] — a finding attributed tentatively to existing low-flow winter conditions, but unconfirmed.
3. **Management trade-off identified**: Current low winter flows suppress the recreational [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery (halting in-river stocking) but may simultaneously benefit native fish populations and support [[Bald Eagle]] foraging — creating a direct tension between the proposed recreational enhancement and native species/avian conservation.
4. **Experimental, phased approach recommended**: Rather than immediately altering flows, the group agreed to first collect one to two years of baseline fisheries and bald eagle data before evaluating the effects of any winter flow modification — an explicit acknowledgment that ecological effects (positive or negative) on native fishes are currently unknown and require empirical assessment.
5. **Data gap flagged**: Item (d) in the "Benefits and Liabilities" section explicitly states: "Effects to the native fishes (positive or negative) that persist in this reach of the river need to be evaluated" — confirming no existing quantitative fish population data was available to the group at the time of the meeting.
6. **Regulatory/recreational lever**: Dr. Yamaguchi is noted as raising the potential ecological value of imposing more restrictive fishing regulations if the winter fishery were established, implying angling pressure itself is recognized as a variable affecting the native/introduced fish community balance.
