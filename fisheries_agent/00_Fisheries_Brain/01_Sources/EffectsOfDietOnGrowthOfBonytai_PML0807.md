# Effects of Diet on Growth of Bonytail Chub (Gila elegans)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This draft study by Chester R. Figel, Matt D. Campbell, Marc Jackson, and Manuel E. Ulibarri compares two diets for hatchery-reared [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), an imperiled freshwater fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. A live worm/frozen krill diet produced significantly greater growth than an [[Artificial Catfish Pellet Diet]] over a 127-day period at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. The authors conclude that the live diet better meets the nutritional needs of larval bonytail and recommend developing an artificial diet that can match natural-diet growth rates.

## Background & Description

Bonytail are described as one of the most imperiled freshwater fish species, existing mainly as a small population in the lower Colorado River basin. Few individuals have been collected in the past decade, and recruitment is rare to nonexistent.

- **Regulatory driver:** Two 1993–1994 biological opinions issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] require production of large numbers of 25–30 cm (total length) bonytail for reintroduction into [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]].
- **Growth challenge:** Bonytail take up to three years to reach 30 cm, whereas [[Channel Catfish]] can reach that size in one growing season.
- **Prior work:** Facilities previously used for [[Rainbow Trout]] at Willow Beach were adapted for endangered warm-water fish, including [[Razorback Sucker]] and bonytail. Warming the water and reducing fish density increased growth and survival.
- **Study goal:** Refine [[Aquaculture Rearing Techniques]] to maximize bonytail growth and shorten rearing time, testing an artificial diet (catfish pellet #2, Silver Cup, mixed with plankton/*Spirulina*) against a live worm/frozen krill diet, using unique family lots of young fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental setup**

- **Facility:** 22 aquaria (34 L each) at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery, supplied with ultraviolet-treated, solar-heated river water. Aeration came from a regenerative blower (⅓ h.p.) with air stones.
- **Water quality monitoring:** Temperature (°C), dissolved oxygen (mg/L), pH, NO₂ (mg/L), and NH₃ (mg/L) were recorded daily ([[Water Quality Monitoring]]).
- **Source fish:** Eleven bonytail family lots spawned at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. All fish were initially fed [[Live Brine Shrimp]] 8–10 times daily for 21 days.
- **Stocking:** On 31 May 2000, family groups (reported as 300 fish) were split into paired aquaria, with the source text stating 150 fish per aquarium. The manuscript does not reconcile these figures, so the stocking density should be checked against the original records.
- **Treatments:** Starting 7 June 2000 (day 1), tanks 1–11 received the [[Live Worm-Frozen Krill Diet]], and tanks 12–22 received the artificial feed. Fish were fed 6–8 times daily to satiation.
- **Density reductions:** Fish were reduced to 100 per aquarium on 11 July 2000 (day 35) and to 75 per aquarium on 23 August 2000 (day 78), by random removal.

**Measurements and statistics**

- Mean weight per aquarium was recorded on day 1, day 35 (11 July), day 78 (23 August), and day 127 (11 October 2000).
- Survival was approximately 99.5%, so survival was not used as a covariate.
- Feed effects were analyzed with [[Repeated Measures ANOVA]] (Wilks test) for growth over time and one-way ANOVA for final weight.

## Key Findings

1. **Family lots were homogeneous.** No significant differences in mean weight among the 11 family lots (ANOVA, p > 0.05), so the lots were pooled as replicates.
2. **Live diet produced faster growth.** Fish on the live worm/frozen krill diet grew faster than fish on the artificial diet (repeated measures ANOVA, Wilks test, p < 0.05).
3. **Final weight differed by treatment.** At the end of the experiment, live-diet fish averaged 2.44 g (± 0.22 g), more than twice the artificial-diet mean of about 1.07 g (± 0.17 g). The difference was significant (ANOVA, p < 0.05).
4. **Density effects were observed but not tested directly.** Reducing density by one-third on day 35 was followed by a weight increase. Reducing it by one-fourth on day 78 produced no comparable effect.
5. **Palatability was not limiting.** Bonytail consumed both diets readily, so the growth difference is unlikely to reflect feed acceptance.
6. **Nutritional inference.** The authors conclude that the live diet better meets the nutritional requirements of larval bonytail. How the diets affected digestion and absorption remains unknown.

**Recommendations**

- Use the live worm/frozen krill diet to shorten rearing time and potentially reduce cost.
- Study feed effects on all bonytail life stages.
- Develop an artificial diet that matches the growth rates achieved on natural feeds, to improve captive culture of bonytail.
