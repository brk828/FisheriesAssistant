# The Fresh Water Clam *Pisidium tremperi* (Hannibal): Synonymy and Zoogeography of Isolated San Bernardino Mollusks

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sphaeriidae

*Note: this document concerns freshwater molluscs, not fish. The tags above follow the system format; the taxon-specific tag for the principal species is #sphaeriidae.*

## Executive Summary

Taylor and Herrington re-examine *Pisidium tremperi* (Hannibal), a freshwater clam previously known only from Bluff Lake Cienaga in the San Bernardino Mountains of southern California. After reviewing the type lot and associated specimens, they refer *P. tremperi* to the synonymy of the widespread [[Pisidium Obtusale]]. The authors conclude that the species is not endemic to southern California, but that its isolated occurrence there remains zoogeographically significant, alongside several other locally restricted mollusks in the same mountain range.

## Background & Description

The authors note that *Pisidium tremperi* was described by [[Hannibal]] (1912b) as *Corneocyclas tremperi*, with a brief original diagnosis: a minute, globular-trigonal shell with anterior, broad, somewhat elevated beaks and a much-reduced hinge, from marshy lakes. Recorded dimensions were length 1.4 mm, altitude 1.3 mm and valve depth 1 mm. The type locality was Bluff Lake Cienaga, San Bernardino Mountains. The species was named after Dr. R. H. Tremper, the first conchologist to visit that part of the range.

The authors describe the original description as inadequate, and its illustration as poor. Later workers misinterpreted the species: [[Sterki]] (1916) considered it close to *P. ashmuni*, a synonym of *P. casertanum*, while [[Gregg]] (1947) suggested it was probably the young of *P. ashmuni*.

Material examined by the authors comprised three lots:
- Berry collection, Lower end of Bear Lake (alt. 6700 ft), August 1908, identified as *P. rotundatum* by Sterki (January 26, 1920).
- Berry collection, swamp at Bluff Lake (alt. 7550 ft), August 1908, identified as *P. rotundatum* by Sterki.
- Stanford University Paleontological Type Collection, Bluff Lake Cienaga, including the type and three others.

All specimens were judged closely similar and referable to *P. obtusale* form *rotundatum*, differing only in trivial features (smaller size, weak pseudocallus). The type measured 1.8 mm long, 1.3 mm broad and 1.5 mm high, which the authors noted does not agree well with Hannibal's published measurements. They acknowledge that the type identification may be erroneous.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper focuses on the zoogeography of the San Bernardino Mountains. The authors list seven other mollusk species isolated in these mountains, and they summarise the following species records for southern California:

- **Freshwater clams (Sphaeriidae):** *Pisidium obtusale* (known in California only from Bear Lake and Bluff Lake); *Sphaerium lacustre* (Bluff Lake only); *Sphaerium hanhami* (Bear Lake only).
- **Freshwater snails (Valvatidae):** *Valvata humeralis californica*, living only at Bear Lake and Bluff Lake, and recorded as an upper Pleistocene fossil from the Palos Verdes Sand.
- **Lymnaeidae:** an unidentified *Stagnicola* sp., known in southern California only from Bluff Lake and Bear Lake.
- **Planorbidae:** [[Menetus Centervillensis]], known only from Bear Lake.
- **Land snails (Pupillidae):** *Vertigo modesta* form *microphasma* and *Vertigo occidentalis*, both known only from the San Bernardino Mountains.

Biogeographic context is drawn from [[Hannibal]] (1912a), who explained the aquatic fauna of the Pacific Coast drainage through stream capture of former Mojave headwaters, Pleistocene uplift of the San Bernardino and San Gabriel Mountains, and the recent origin of present Mojave stream courses. The authors consider this plausible, citing geological work by Noble (1954) and Bowen (1954), but they argue that the isolation of these populations may predate recent drainage changes.

## Key Findings

- ***Pisidium tremperi* is a synonym of *Pisidium obtusale*** (form *rotundatum*). The authors judged the type lot and associated specimens to be a single species; they conclude that the name is a synonym of either *P. casertanum* or *P. obtusale*.
- **Identity remains uncertain.** The original description is inadequate, the type measurements differ from those published, and the authors state that the type identification may be wrong. If the specimen is not the type, the identity of *P. tremperi* is unresolved.
- **No southern California endemic.** Although the authors removed a supposed endemic species from the region, the isolated occurrence of *P. obtusale* in the San Bernardino Mountains retains zoogeographic interest.
- **Historical isolation of multiple taxa.** Seven other mollusk species are isolated in the same mountains, suggesting that the disjunct distribution has historical significance.
- **Valvata history is more complex.** Living *Valvata humeralis californica* occurs only in the Bear Lake area, headwaters of the Santa Ana, yet it is known as an upper Pleistocene fossil from the Palos Verdes Sand in the Los Angeles River drainage. The authors infer that its restriction is a relatively recent feature, probably caused by the loss of other suitable habitats.
- **Open question:** whether *P. obtusale* shares this history with *Valvata* is unresolved.
