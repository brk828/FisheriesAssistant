# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies: Executive Review Committee Final Report (May 1988)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This May 1988 policy report from the [[Executive Review Committee]] (ERC) of the Department of the Interior translates the technical results of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) into recommendations on [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations. The ERC agreed that dam operations affect downstream natural and recreational resources in [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]] and [[Grand Canyon National Park]], and that some operating patterns are more harmful than others. It did not agree that the impacts justify immediate operational change. The committee recommended further studies, interim test flows, and a coordination mechanism. It could not agree on initiating [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) compliance. The report is accompanied by sharply divided agency and constituent positions, principally from [[Western Area Power Administration]] (Western), which argued that interim minimum flows violate the [[Colorado River Storage Project Act]] (CRSP Act) and would cost firm power revenues.

## Background & Description

- **Origin:** GCES began on December 8, 1982, after public controversy over the uprating and rewinding of Glen Canyon generators and proposed peaking operations. Comments suggested the concern was broader: long- and short-term environmental and recreational effects of dam operation.
- **Mandate:** The studies asked whether dam operations affect downstream resources and whether alternative operations, within existing legal mandates, could reduce those impacts.
- **Committee:** The ERC comprised the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR), [[National Park Service]] (NPS), [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS), the Department of the Interior's Office of Environmental Project Review, and Western. It was charged with defining each agency's management objectives, a matrix of concurrence and nonconcurrence, the need for further study, and recommendations to the Secretary.
- **Key species:** The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), listed as endangered, and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), which support a "blue-ribbon" sport fishery, are the focal fish. Other native fishes are discussed as a group.
- **Review:** The GCES Final Report was reviewed by the [[National Academy of Sciences]] review committee. The report is a technical document, not a NEPA document, and does not address economic costs and benefits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dam effects on downstream resources (as described by the agencies):**

- **Flood releases** (above powerplant capacity, 31,500 cfs in most discussion; FWS cites 33,000 cfs in one place): erode alluvial sand beaches, destroy vegetation and riparian habitat, and increase rapid and boating hazards. NPS describes beach loss as potentially irreversible.
- **Fluctuating flows:** strand adult trout, dry spawning beds (eggs and young killed below 8,000 cfs), can dry humpback chub spawning areas in the [[Little Colorado River]], and reduce larval native fish backwater habitat.
- **Low releases** (below 8,000 cfs): expose trout redds, restrict tributary access for spawning, and increase boating and angler danger. They may benefit some other native species by creating backwaters.
- **Sediment:** The [[United States Geological Survey]] (USGS) estimated tributary sand supply at about 3.6 million tonnes per year (2.9 from three major tributaries and 0.7 from 310 ungaged tributaries), an estimate it called "extremely tenuous."
- **Historical context:** Dam construction moderated seasonal flows, reduced sediment, and lowered water temperatures, which allowed the trout fishery and white-water rafting to develop.

**Resource conflicts:** Operations that benefit humpback chub and native fishes can harm trout fisheries, and vice versa. Hydropower objectives also compete with recreation and fish objectives.

## Key Findings

### 1. Agreed findings and recommendations
- All members agreed that dam operations affect downstream resources, and that some operations are less damaging. Disagreement centers on the significance of impacts and whether the studies were complete enough to determine that significance.
- **Future studies:** immediate research on fluctuating flows and aquatic habitat (including endangered species); research on fluctuating flows and beach building and degradation; conservation measures and studies for humpback chub.
- **Funding:** monitoring studies should be a normal operating cost of Glen Canyon Dam, with funding levels approved annually.
- **Interim operations (all ERC members):** keep the 31,500 cfs powerplant limit until NEPA compliance for the Uprating Program is reevaluated; restrict powerplant use above 31,500 cfs to bypass reduction, power emergencies of under six hours, and grid emergencies; ensure flood frequency is less than one in twenty years.

### 2. Disputed interim minimum flows (Interior members)
- **April 15 to October 30:** minimum 5,000 cfs on all weekends; weekdays minimum 3,000 cfs with a daily average of 8,000 cfs or greater and historic load-following.
- **October 30 to April 15:** actual minimum 5,000 cfs.
- Exceptions apply when Lake Powell is filling and runoff is less than forecast. Releases would then be curtailed, and the 5,000 cfs minimum violated, roughly once in ten years.
- Current criteria require 1,000 cfs in the non-recreation season (September 1 to June 1) and 3,000 cfs in the recreation season.

### 3. Agency positions
- **FWS** fully supports the ERC draft. Studies found adverse effects from flooding and flows below 5,000 cfs, including impaired tributary access, reduced backwaters, dewatered *Cladophora* beds, and trout spawning beds dewatered. An estimated 70 percent of trout spawning beds would be functional under the recommended flows. FWS recommends using them as base study flows for two years.
- **NPS** supports interim flows but wants tests to span at least two life cycles of species of concern, and a ban on the "emergency" exception in 2a. NPS states that the one-in-four current flood risk is unacceptable and recommends a risk of no more than one in twenty years. Its management goals include minimum releases of 5,000 to 8,000 cfs, high flows of 20,000 to 28,000 cfs from May to September for chub, and a maximum powerplant discharge of 28,000 cfs pending study. NPS states that NEPA compliance is required.
- **BOR** supports further study but argues that the interim minimums should not be established. Its position is that tests should run within existing criteria, in consultation with the seven Colorado River Basin States, so no additional NEPA would be needed.
- **Western** supports data collection and monitoring but strongly objects to interim minimum flows, arguing they violate the CRSP Act and would prejudice future NEPA analysis (see below).

### 4. Western minority opinion and economic costs
- Western says the CRSP Act (Section 7, 43 U.S.C. 620f) requires powerplants to produce the "greatest practicable amount of firm power and energy," subordinate only to Compact obligations and irrigation and domestic water. Recreation and conservation (Section 8) are subservient to these purposes.
- Western estimates the proposed test minimums would reduce winter firm capacity by 89 MW and energy by 218.9 GWh, and summer firm capacity by 11 MW and 34.5 GWh.
- Two-year test revenue loss: roughly $10 million per year. If indefinite beyond 1999: $21.8 million per year.
- Appendix C (Western) gives: a 5,000 cfs minimum would cost about $5 million per year through 1999 and about $30 million thereafter; an 8,000 cfs minimum would cost $14.7 million per year through 1999 and $60 million thereafter.
- Western concludes that "impacts" were measured against an idealized baseline and that existing operations should form the baseline. It argues a properly scoped NEPA process must precede any interim or long-term change.

### 5. NEPA compliance (unresolved)
- Decision options: (1) no change, (2) further interim studies, or (3) NEPA compliance. ERC members agreed the impacts exist but could not agree on option 3.
- Open questions include whether NEPA should be piecemeal, which priorities drive decisions (endangered fish, downstream water, water rights, or power contracts), and whether existing flexibility makes NEPA unnecessary.
- The Solicitors and Western's counsel issued initial legal rulings, included in [[Appendix A]].

### 6. Coordination
- A steering/coordinating committee should set research goals and conduct annual assessments. FWS and NPS should review the Operating Plan for effects on endangered species, riparian zones, and recreation. A cooperative effort should explain operating criteria and power benefits to constituents.

### 7. Constituent and technical critiques (summarized)
- **Upper Colorado River Commission** and **Colorado River Board of California** argued the draft contained legal inaccuracies, the flood frequency was overstated, and no further studies were needed. The Commission reported a probability of under one in twenty-five years (about 4 percent annual chance) of exceeding powerplant capacity under the 1988 operating plan, versus 25 percent in the draft.
- **USGS** concluded that data were insufficient to define how large or frequent floods can be before a net sand loss occurs. It judged the claim that one flood in twenty years would cause long-term loss of camping beaches "seems highly unlikely."
- **Arizona Game and Fish Department** criticized the draft for underemphasizing limits on fluctuating-flow data, overextending ecological knowledge, and ignoring the trout fishery below [[Lee's Ferry]]. It reported angler use there of 18,986 to 52,725 anglers per year for 1980 to 1985.
- **Sierra Club** and **Trout Unlimited** complained about short review periods (30 days and about two weeks), limited distribution (about 200 to 300 copies), and inadequate data access, and requested a full EIS.
- Agency reviewers, including the NPS Western Regional Director and Grand Canyon Superintendent, asked for more research on beach erosion, humpback chub life history, and fluctuating-flow effects before operational changes.

- Individual management objectives are summarized in [[Appendix D]], including ERC-level Bureau priorities under the Endangered Species Act and Western's Section 7 CRSP mandate.
- Constituent comments in [[Appendix E]] cover about 15 letters from BOR, FWS, and NPS constituencies.
