# Status of Mohave Chub Introductions in California and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The Mohave chub (*Gila bicolor mohavensis*), federally and state listed as endangered, is the only known native fish of the [[Mojave River]] in California. Hybridization with the introduced [[Arroyo Chub]] (*Gila orcutti*) before 1957 led to the belief that the species was extinct in the river system. The last genetically pure population was then known only from [[Fort Soda]], near Baker, California. Beginning in 1967, biologists moved Mohave chubs from Fort Soda to new waters to improve survival odds. This bulletin reports on twelve introductions made between 1967 and 1981. Only two are described as successful, most have failed, and the fate of one is undetermined. The species' status is considered secure mainly because of the [[Lark Seep Lagoon]] population and the [[Desert Research Station]] refugium. Delisting is conditional on successful reintroduction into the Mojave River.

## Background & Description

*Gila bicolor mohavensis* is a small cyprinid fish that is federally and state listed as endangered. Its native range is the Mojave River system of California.

- **Hybridization:** Mass hybridization between the Mohave chub and the arroyo chub followed the arroyo chub's introduction to the river before 1934 (Hubbs and Miller 1943). Sampling to date in the Mojave River has found only arroyo chubs.
- **Fort Soda refuge:** Fort Soda, about 10 miles southwest of Baker, California, held the only genetically pure population. Fish lived in two ponds (one being [[Lake Tuendae]]) and a small drainage ditch. These waters needed regular maintenance, including vegetation removal and pumped water inflow. Land ownership litigation in the late 1960s added to the risk.
- **Introduction program:** [[St. Amant]] and [[Sasaki]] (1971) reported on the first three introductions and a planned fourth. The document summarizes all twelve introductions through 1981.
- **Earlier relocations:** [[Robert Miller]] introduced Fort Soda fish to San Felipe (San Diego County) in 1939 and 1940, and to Rio Santo Tomas, Baja California, in 1955. Both relocations failed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The introduction sites were mostly small, managed waters such as springs, ponds, and ornamental pools. Several failures were linked to habitat conditions:

- **Oxygen and gas stress:** At [[Two Hole Springs]], the first failure was attributed to [[Oxygen Depletion]] from dense aquatic vegetation. The second was possibly caused by [[Hydrogen Sulfide]]. At [[Busch Gardens]], algal blooms and low oxygen were the most likely cause of death.
- **Predators and competitors:** At [[South Coast Botanic Garden]], a large [[Goldfish]] population developed, and an asphalt pond sealant may have contributed. At [[Norconian]], a large [[Crayfish]] population was assumed to be the cause of failure.
- **Flooding and degradation:** At [[Piute Creek]], chubs survived several major storms and flash floods, but severe habitat degradation was found in 1978.
- **Human disturbance:** At [[Barstow Way Station]], illegal fishing reduced the population, and a flood washed out the remaining fish in late 1979.
- **Litigation and maintenance needs:** Fort Soda's ownership dispute and its need for constant management increased the risk of local extinction.

The document notes that the Mohave chub appears adaptable to a variety of conditions, but more habitat research is needed before evaluating new introduction sites.

## Key Findings

**Introduction outcomes (as reported):**

- Twelve introductions are summarized. The document reports **2 successful**, **19 failed**, and **1 undetermined**. These totals sum to 22, which does not match the twelve introductions listed. The discrepancy is in the source.
- **Successful:** [[Lark Seep Lagoon]], China Lake Naval Weapons Center (introduced 12 July 1971 with 400 chubs; 75 more added 5 November 1976). Surveys in 1979 and 1980 found an abundant population. The [[Desert Research Station]] refugium (Barstow Unified School District; 16 chubs introduced December 1978) has shown good recruitment, with population estimates of at least 1,650 fish.

**Notable failed introductions:**

- [[Paradise Spa]] (Clark County, Nevada): 50 chubs introduced June 1967. Fish were seen on several occasions, but a thorough April 1981 survey found none. Reasons are unknown.
- [[Piute Creek]] (San Bernardino County): 150 chubs stocked 18–19 December 1969. Last observed 9 November 1976.
- [[South Coast Botanic Garden]] (Los Angeles County): 147 stocked 27 January 1970; 55 restocked 20 July 1972; 105 added March 1975. No chubs found in 1976 surveys.
- [[Two Hole Springs]] (San Bernardino County): 41 stocked 20 August 1970; 150 more 9 July 1971. No live chubs two weeks later.
- [[Dos Palmas Spring Area]] (Riverside County, near the [[Salton Sea]]): 100 chubs into Dos Palmas Spring and 100 into the nearby Shrimp Pond in 1972. None found on 8 January 1980.
- [[Eaton Canyon Nature Center]] (Altadena, Los Angeles County): 20 chubs introduced 5 June 1972. None survived; cause unknown.
- [[Busch Gardens]] (Van Nuys, Los Angeles County): 49 chubs introduced into two flight-cage ponds on 27 July 1972. Failed, probably from low oxygen caused by algal blooms.
- [[Barstow Way Station]] (San Bernardino County, a BLM facility): 60 chubs introduced 21 July 1975. Illegal fishing and a flood led to loss. A new pond received 30 chubs on 1 July 1981.
- [[Lion Country Safari]] (Orange County): 272 chubs introduced 1 June 1972 into ponds; the 1977 survey found no chubs.
- [[Norconian]] (Riverside County): chubs introduced in 1978; none found. Crayfish were suspected to be the cause.

**Management and conservation conclusions:**

- The [[Bureau Of Land Management]] adopted a [[Habitat Management Plan]] for Fort Soda (Bicket 1978). It calls for pond renovation and enlargement, control of emergent aquatic growth in Lake Tuendae, creation of a third pond, installation of pump-up systems, and periodic vegetation removal in the "spring" habitat.
- The lower urgency now allows introductions to be made more selectively, restricted to the Mojave River drainage.
- **Delisting** can only be considered if the species is successfully reintroduced into the Mojave River.
- **Downlisting** to rare and threatened could occur if additional successful introductions are made outside the Mojave River.
- Additional surveys are needed to determine whether Mohave chubs remain in the Mojave River or its tributaries. Stocking the river is possible only under conditions that prevent hybridization with arroyo chubs, which the authors consider unlikely.
