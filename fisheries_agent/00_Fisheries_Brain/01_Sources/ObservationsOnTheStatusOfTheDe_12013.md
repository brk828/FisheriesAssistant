# Devil's Hole Pupfish Status in the Hoover Dam Refugium

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 report by J. E. Williams, prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], assesses the status of a [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) population established in a thermal cement pool below [[Hoover Dam]]. The refugium was built in August 1972 as a backup for the wild population in [[Devil's Hole]], a spring-fed cavern in Ash Meadows, Nevada, whose survival was in doubt because of groundwater pumping. The study compared the refugium population with the source population in population size, sex ratio, body measurements, meristic counts, and community composition, using data collected from October 1975 through March 1977.

The refugium population has persisted and reproduces year-round, and the author calls it the first established population of the species outside Devil's Hole. Its numbers and sex-ratio fluctuations track the source population. Fish are larger on average, and the refugium has gained algal and invertebrate diversity. The author concludes that the refugium is a useful insurance measure but cannot replace Devil's Hole.

## Background & Description

**Regional context.** Miller (1961) described the fish fauna of the western United States as depauperate, with many relicts, monotypic genera, and endemic species. Human activity has caused the extinction of four species and six subspecies in California, Nevada, and Arizona over the past 35 years, and 50 species in the Great Basin states and northern Mexico are listed as threatened or endangered.

**The species.** The Devil's Hole pupfish was confined to a single spring source until the refugium transplant. Devil's Hole lies about 15 m below the surface in a collapsed cave within a cavern system more than 100 m deep. Virtually all reproduction and feeding occur on a 2 × 3 m shelf near the surface (James 1969). The National Park Service describes it as the most restricted habitat of any vertebrate in the world. Devil's Hole was designated a disjunct part of [[Death Valley National Monument]] in 1952.

**Threat and legal action.** In the 1960s, agricultural pumping lowered the water level over the critical shelf, and reproduction fell. The Department of the Interior asked for pumping from four nearby wells to stop. The request was ignored, and litigation followed. In 1976 the U.S. Supreme Court upheld a lower-court order requiring a water level that would let the pupfish survive and reproduce.

**The refugium.** A spring below Hoover Dam with a temperature near 33 °C was located in 1971. A tripartite agreement between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the Bureau of Reclamation, and the [[Nevada Department of Fish and Game]] led to the refugium's completion by August 1972. The pool is about 2 × 6 m, 1 to 3 m deep, and fed by a hot spring at 40 °C. Flow is adjusted to hold water near 33 °C, with more hot water added in summer. Water enters at the deep end and leaves through an outflow weir near the surface.

**Transplant history.** In September 1972, substrate, planarians (*Dugesia*), hydrobiid snails (*Tryonia*), elmid beetles (*Stenelmis*), and green algae (*Spirogyra*) from Devil's Hole were placed in the refugium. Seven pupfish were added on October 2, 1972. Two died soon after transfer, and the remaining five appeared to thrive. The report states that 20 more fish were added on "October 13, 1976," which conflicts with the reproduction and sampling dates in the same text. The date may be a transcription error. Reproduction was observed on October 21 of the stated year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population dynamics.** Fluctuations in the refugium follow the pattern of Devil's Hole, with the fewest fish in late winter and the most in late summer and early fall. Population estimates in the refugium were:
- March 14, 1974: 194 to 204 (after an initial population explosion)
- November 1974: 110 to 117
- October 1975: 62
- October 1975 to March 1977: 48 to 69 (low of 48 in February 1977; high of 69 in October 1976)

For comparison, Devil's Hole counts in 1976 ranged from 180 (February) to 410 (September). The author says the population has been relatively stable since October 1975.

**Sex ratio and reproduction.** Male:female ratios were highly variable, from 2.92:1 to 0.75:1, and were similar to the variation in Devil's Hole (James 1969). Males slightly outnumbered females for most of the year. Reproduction occurred year-round, with peak activity from late May through August. Juveniles (15 mm or less) made up about one-third of the fish counted in June through August 1976 (20 of 63, 22 of 63, and 21 of 66). Only 1 of 48 fish was juvenile in February 1977.

**Morphometrics.** Eleven live fish were measured in October 1976 to the nearest 0.1 mm with dial calipers. Live measurement was necessary because of collecting-permit restrictions. Standard length averaged about 25 mm in the refugium compared with 19 mm in Devil's Hole. A female reached 31.5 mm. Body depth, body width, and caudal peduncle depth were proportionally greater in the refugium, and head length was proportionally smaller. Predorsal and anal-to-caudal lengths were similar in both populations.

**Meristics.** Fin-ray counts showed little difference between populations. Dorsal rays averaged 11.0 in the refugium (range 11) and 11.13 in Devil's Hole (range 10 to 13). Pelvic rays were absent in the refugium but averaged 0.02 in Devil's Hole.

**Community structure.** Community diversity has increased since the 1972 introduction:
- Algae: from one species at introduction to at least six genera by fall 1976. Dominants were *Spirogyra* and the blue-green *Mastigocladus laminosa*. *Oscillatoria* and *Aphanothece* were also present, and the diatoms *Achnanthes* and *Cymbella* grew on the blue-greens.
- Snails: *Tryonia* persists as a fluctuating population, abundant from March through August and reduced to a remnant in winter.
- Absent: elmid beetles and planarians did not establish. The author suggests the vertical refugium walls and lack of shallow, sloping substrate (minimum depth 1 m) may be responsible.
- Insects: dragonfly larvae, which prey on fish and fish eggs, are common and are often removed during counts. They do not occur in Devil's Hole. Their predation could matter when fish numbers are low in winter and spring.

**Study design.** Monthly counts were made in seven sections, starting in October 1975 with a regular sampling schedule. A scuba diver was used from January 1976 onward to chase fish to the surface for more accurate counts.

**Related refugia.** The [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]] (2.3 ha, Fish Slough, completed October 1969) protects the [[Owens Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon radiosus*), [[Owens River Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor snyderi*), [[Owens Sucker]] (*Catostomus fumeiventris*), and a speckled dace. The author prefers natural refugia within the species' home basin. Cement-tank refugia, such as the Hoover Dam site, are a last resort that need constant management. The same design was being considered for the [[Pahrump Killifish]] (*Empetrichthys latos*).

## Key Findings

1. The refugium population is the first established population of *Cyprinodon diabolis* outside Devil's Hole.
2. Population fluctuations match those of Devil's Hole, with lows in late winter and highs in August through October.
3. Counts ranged from 48 (February 1977) to 69 (October 1976) during October 1975 to March 1977.
4. Male:female ratios ranged from 2.92:1 to 0.75:1.
5. Mean standard length rose to about 25 mm (25.1 mm in the Conclusions; 25.2 mm in the Results), compared with 19 mm in Devil's Hole.
6. Predorsal and anal-to-caudal lengths were very similar between populations.
7. Body depth, body width, head length, and least caudal peduncle depth differed significantly. Morphometrics differed in five of seven measurements.
8. Meristic counts showed no meaningful variation between populations.
9. Community diversity has increased, with algal diversity rising from one species to at least seven.
10. Dragonfly larvae may threaten the refugium population, especially when numbers are low.
11. The refugium is a successful temporary environment, but Devil's Hole's unique conditions probably cannot be permanently duplicated.

**Management implications.** The refugium works as "insurance" against losing the species, not as a replacement for Devil's Hole. Because the environments select for different traits, the refugium population may undergo genetic drift away from the Devil's Hole population. The report calls for continued monitoring, possible habitat changes to support snails and other invertebrates, and control of dragonfly predation.
