# Growth of the Fingernail Clam *Sphaerium transversum* in Field and Laboratory Experiments

#malacology #freshwater-ecology #sphaeriidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 study by William F. Gale (Iowa State University) measured how fast the freshwater fingernail clam [[Sphaerium Transversum]] grows and reproduces in [[Pool 19]] of the [[Mississippi River]]. Growth experiments ran in the summer and fall of 1967 in a field laboratory and in river-bottom retainers. The clam completed its life cycle in about a month or less. Newborn clams often entered a resting state for 33 days before growing, and medium-sized clams produced offspring that frequently outgrew their parents. Clams moved from a high-density open-river site to a low-density cove grew larger than both clams left at the collection site and clams in the laboratory. The author argues that this rapid life cycle may be a major reason the species thrives under heavy predation.

## Background & Description

*Sphaerium transversum* is a small freshwater bivalve (fingernail clam) that was abundant in Pool 19 during a 1966–68 benthos survey. Densities reached over 100,000/m² in some areas despite heavy predation by [[Leech Predation|leeches]], fish, and waterfowl. In autumn 1967, [[Diving Ducks]] harvested about 24% (2,085,125 kg) of the September standing crop, according to earlier work by Gale (1973) and Thompson (1973). The study set out to determine the cause of the clam's success by measuring its growth rate.

Newborn clams in Pool 19 average 2.2 mm long. The species usually does not exceed 12.0 mm there, although individuals up to 15.9 mm long and 81 mm thick were found. The author notes that the species appears "very clean," so the dark deposits that later accumulated on its shells were unexpected.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Laboratory setup.** Water was pumped from near the river channel into a field laboratory above Ft. Madison, Iowa. This kept temperature and chemistry close to river conditions and supplied food. Clams were held in individual plexiglass rearing chambers (3.8 cm square, 5.0 cm deep) containing about 10 cc of strained clayey silt from the river bottom. Nine chambers were placed in each of nine compartments. Flushing was variable, with a maximum rate of about 6 minutes per flush, and clogged tubing reduced it further.

**Field setup.** Two retainers, each holding 40 rearing tubes with two clams per tube (80 clams per retainer), were submerged on July 6, 1967:
- **Station 2:** A sheltered cove with emergent and submergent vegetation, where there were fewer than 1,000 clams/m².
- **Station 14:** Open water, where there were about 42,000 clams/m².

**Predation and parasitism.** The study identifies leeches, fish, waterfowl, and larval trematodes as pressures on the population. Trematode infestations may have weakened clams at station 14.

## Key Findings

**1. Laboratory growth and reproduction**
- Experiments began July 21, 1967, and ran for 33 days (to August 23) and then until November 15.
- Chambers held one large (over 10.0 mm), two medium (4.0–6.0 mm), or four newborn clams. The large clams died shortly after the start, but 53 young (reported as 1.6–9.0 mm, which is likely an OCR rendering) were produced.
- One offspring grew to about 7.0 mm within 33 days or less, close to the species' maximum size.
- Reproduction in the newborn-stocked chambers (7 and 8) showed the species can grow large and complete its life cycle within a month.
- Many newborn clams did not grow by August 23. Some were dormant, and their periostraca were discolored and chalky. By November 15, many of these clams had grown to 9.0 mm or more.
- Most medium-sized survivors (12 in chambers 10–18) grew slowly. The reported mean length increase is 13 mm, which is probably 1.3 mm given the starting sizes. Many of these parents died without reaching 9.0 mm and were smaller than their offspring.
- Reproduction occurred in all but two of the 27 chambers by November 15. It was greatest in chambers where one large clam had been stocked (19–27), where one clam left 79 descendants.

**2. Field growth**
- At station 2, clams grew well. After 62 days (July 6 to September 6), 45 clams were 4.0 mm or more thick. The largest was 13.8 mm long and 6.6 mm thick.
- Over 5,000 clams examined at station 14 in mid-September included none 4.0 mm thick.
- One large clam produced 35 young, and another contained 86 embryos.
- Clams in 21 tubes died, possibly from senility, leech predation, or other causes.
- The station 14 retainer was lost after its buoys disappeared in September.

**3. Interpretation**
- Field growth at station 2 exceeded laboratory growth, even though both groups came from the same parental stock. The author suggests that laboratory conditions stunted the clams in some way.
- High density at station 14 (about 42,000 clams/m²) may have limited growth there. Medium-sized clams at that site grew only about 2 mm in July and 3 mm in August, far less than in the experiments.
- Growth that was arrested early was not fully compensated later, so some medium clams stayed smaller than their offspring.
- The lag in initial growth does not appear to be genetically controlled. The author proposes that a metabolic slowdown may let clams bury deeper to escape predation, parasitism, or poor water conditions.

**4. Comparison with other sphaeriids**
- *S. partumeium* grew large in 7–10 weeks in the lab (Thomas, 1965) and matured in under 14 weeks. Krull (1936) reported growth to 4.5–5.0 mm in about two months.
- *S. corneum*, *S. rivicola*, and *S. solidum* grew rapidly in the Elbe River (Thiel, 1928, 1930), and some *S. corneum* matured and died within the same autumn.
- Thiel also found that *S. corneum* did not reach maximum size in overpopulated areas, which supports the density explanation.

**5. Life-history conclusion**
- If field growth were typical, several generations per year could occur. The author notes, however, that *S. transversum* rarely occupies temporary ponds, which suggests rapid growth evolved for other reasons, possibly as a form of preadaptation.
- The main conclusion is that completing the life cycle in about a month may be a major factor in the species' success in Pool 19. The shorter the time before reproduction, the greater the chance an individual reproduces before being eaten.
