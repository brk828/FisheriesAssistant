# Gila ditaenia (Sonora Chub) Proposed Threatened Listing and Critical Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document comprises a 1984 cover letter from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) to [[W.L. Minckley]] and a reprint of the *Federal Register* proposed rule (Vol. 49, No. 110, June 6, 1984, pp. 23402–23409). The proposal would list the [[Sonora Chub]] (*[[Gila Ditaenia]]*) as a threatened species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of 1973, as amended, and designate its [[Critical Habitat]] in the United States. The proposal also includes a [[Special Rule]] allowing certain take under Arizona law. The fish's U.S. range is restricted to [[Sycamore Creek]] and [[Yank's Spring]] on the [[Coronado National Forest]]; it is also known from the [[Rio Magdalena]] in Sonora, Mexico. The FWS identifies threats from habitat disturbance (mining, siltation, flow depletion, pollution), exotic fish and their parasites, and hybridization with the [[Yaqui Chub]] (*[[Gila Purpurea]]*). The Service concluded that threatened, rather than endangered, status was appropriate.

## Background & Description

*Gila ditaenia* is a cyprinid minnow, generally under 125 mm (5 in) total length. It is moderately chubby and dark colored, with two prominent black lateral bands and a dark oval spot at the tail base. Breeding males show red at the lower-fin bases and some orange on the belly. It is primarily a pool dweller, but it is highly secretive, and its behavior and habitat preferences are poorly known.

- **Discovery:** First collected by E. A. Mearns in 1893 from Sycamore Creek, Arizona. Described by [[R.R. Miller]] (1945) from fish collected by R.G. Miller from the Rio Magdalena near La Casita, Sonora.
- **Life history:** Information is limited to feeding observations of a few individuals and spawning inferred from the presence of young in collections.
- **U.S. distribution:** Sycamore Creek and its headwater tributary, Yank's Spring, northwest of Nogales, Santa Cruz County. Yank's Spring is perennially flowing but has been impounded in a concrete tank for many years. Sycamore Creek begins about ½ mile below the spring and flows 3.7 miles (USDA 1982) through pools and riffles over bedrock and rubble. It is intermittent, persisting as pools that are maintained by underground flow in shaded areas. In heavy-rainfall years, water reaches the International Border about 5 miles downstream.
- **Mexican distribution:** Known from very few localities. The 1940 type locality, the Rio Magdalena near La Casita, was then a clear stream 4–5 ft wide and about 1 ft deep, with sand and gravel substrate and watercress in backwaters. Whether habitat still exists there is unknown. Specimens were collected as recently as 1981 at [[Campo Carretero]] and [[Cienega La Atascosa]] (Hendrickson, pers. comm., 1983).
- **Status history:** Listed in Category 2 of the December 30, 1982 Vertebrate Notice of Review. A 1983 status report by Minckley supplied the information needed for the proposal. The [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]] lists it as Threatened Group 3 in Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and setting.** Sycamore Canyon is largely unaltered. About 1.5 miles of stream lie within the [[Goodding Research Natural Area]], a Forest Service special-use area withdrawn from mineral entry except under lease and closed to grazing. Recreation is limited to dispersed uses, though the canyon receives fairly heavy visitor use. Yank's Spring serves as the trailhead parking site.

**Associated species.** The [[Tarahumara Frog]] (*Rana tarahumarae*) occupied the same waters but experienced a catastrophic die-off in 1974 and has not been found since. Environmental toxicants are suspected. [[Green Sunfish]] occur in small numbers in the lower Sycamore Creek; removal is tentatively planned.

**Mining.** No mining currently occurs in the Sycamore Creek watershed, and none is expected soon (USFS, pers. comm., 1983). Active mining is ongoing in [[California Gulch]] one watershed to the west. Uranium exploration in 1981 covered about 4–5 square miles on the upper eastern slopes; uranium was found, and the claims are maintained, but no active mining is planned. The drainage is mineralized, and development would pose severe risks through water withdrawal, habitat disturbance, siltation, and pollution.

**Grazing.** The canyon is in a livestock allotment, but direct effects are minimal because of steep, rocky streambanks. Indirect effects (erosion, siltation) are minor now but could become significant if grazing increases.

**Mexican habitat.** Little is known. Habitat near Cienega La Atascosa was in good condition in 1981. No protection exists for the species or its habitat in Mexico. Irrigated agriculture occurs along the river, with little groundwater pumping; cultivation, diversion, pollution, and riparian damage appear fairly constant over time (G. Mabry, pers. comm., 1983).

**Hybridization.** The [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*), native to the Yaqui, Matape, and Sonora river drainages, is now present in the Rio Magdalena alongside *G. ditaenia*. Hybridization may be occurring at at least one location, and its extent is unknown.

## Key Findings

### Threat assessment (ESA Section 4(a)(1) factors)

- **A. Habitat destruction or curtailment:** Very limited range makes the species highly vulnerable. Threats include siltation and runoff from mining, flow depletion, and pollutants. The species could be extirpated from its U.S. range in a relatively short time (Minckley, 1983).
- **B. Overutilization:** No indication of overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific, or educational purposes.
- **C. Disease or predation:** Introduced game fish would likely cause disastrous predation and extirpation. Parasites introduced with exotic fish are documented as harmful to other *Gila* species.
- **D. Regulatory inadequacy:** Arizona Group 3 status carries no habitat protection and no management plan exists. Mexico provides no legal protection.
- **E. Other factors:** Drought could eliminate the Sycamore Creek water supply, and human activities (poor grazing, mining, roads, off-road vehicles, stock tanks, groundwater pumping) can lower water tables and reduce flow.

### Rationale for threatened (not endangered) status

The Service found the U.S. population currently stable, with some protection from State laws and Forest Service management. Mexican populations also exist. Without listing, however, the species would probably become endangered in the foreseeable future because of its small U.S. population, its vulnerability to a single or multiple sources of damage, potential mineral development, the uncertain Mexican status, and increasing water demand.

### Proposed critical habitat

- **Area:** Sycamore Creek from Yank's Spring downstream to the International Border in Santa Cruz County, including a 25-foot riparian zone around the spring and along each creek side. All of the area lies within the Coronado National Forest.
- **Primary constituent elements:** Clean permanent water with pools and intermediate riffles, and/or intermittent pools maintained by subsurface flow in canyon-wall shade.
- **Rationale:** The riparian zone is essential to maintaining the spring, creek ecosystem, and channel.
- **Adverse activities identified:** Intensive collecting or mining during intermittent flow; flow depletion or alteration (excessive pumping, impoundment, diversion); changes in water chemistry from point or dispersed pollutant release; and introduction of exotic fish or parasites.
- **Economic analysis:** Under ESA Section 4(b)(2), the Service was preparing an economic analysis and requested data from interested parties.

### Federal protections under the proposal

- **Section 7(a):** Federal agencies must ensure that their actions are not likely to jeopardize the species or adversely modify its critical habitat, and must consult formally with the Service where a listed species may be affected. No significant effects are expected, since no Federal activities are currently planned and management of the Sycamore Creek drainage is compatible with the species.
- **Section 9 prohibitions (50 CFR 17.31):** Generally prohibit take, import, export, interstate sale, and possession of illegally taken specimens.

### Proposed Special Rule (50 CFR 17.44)

- **Allowed take:** Educational, scientific, propagation or survival enhancement, zoological exhibition, and other conservation purposes, provided a State collecting permit is obtained and State laws are met. No separate Federal permit is required.
- **Incidental take:** Incidental capture by State-licensed recreational anglers is allowed if the fish is immediately returned to its habitat. Angling is an unlikely capture method.
- **State linkage:** Any violation of State fish and wildlife laws concerning this species also violates the ESA. Possession, sale, transport, and attempts or solicitation to commit these offenses are prohibited.

### Other notes

- The FWS will consider whether the species should be placed on the annex of the Western Hemisphere Convention under ESA Section 8A(e) and other international agreements.
- No NEPA documentation was prepared, following a Council on Environmental Quality recommendation and *PLF v. Andrus* (6th Cir., 1981).
- Public comments were due August 6, 1984. Hearing requests were due by July 23, 1984 (within 45 days of publication), to the Regional Director in Albuquerque, New Mexico. Contacts were Gerald Burton (Endangered Species Biologist) and Dr. James E. Johnson.

### Data gaps flagged by the document

- Life history and feeding/spawning behavior are poorly documented.
- The status of the Rio Magdalena habitat is unknown.
- The extent of hybridization with *G. purpurea* is unknown.
- The impact of green sunfish is unknown.
- The cause of the Tarahumara frog die-off is not fully known.
- Stream length is given inconsistently (3.7 miles in the background section; "5 miles or less" and "approximately 5 stream miles" elsewhere).
