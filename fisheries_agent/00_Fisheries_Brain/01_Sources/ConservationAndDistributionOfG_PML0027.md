# Conservation Genetics of Cutthroat Trout (*Salmo clarki*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Allendorf]] and [[Leary]] (*Conservation Biology*, Vol. 2, No. 2, June 1988) examines the conservation genetics of the polytypic [[Cutthroat Trout]]. At least 16 subspecies are recognized, and their genetic divergence ranges from levels typical of conspecific populations to levels comparable to congeneric species. The greatest threat to the species is [[Hybridization and Introgression]] with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and other cutthroat subspecies. The authors argue that [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] at multiple [[Diagnostic Loci]] is a more reliable detection tool than morphology, and they make management recommendations on population conservation, [[Hatchery Populations]], and taxonomic revision.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon and range:** *Salmo clarki* is a polytypic western North American trout. Its range spans coastal and interior waters from Alaska to the Pecos River, New Mexico.
- **Subspecies:** At least 16 subspecies have been recognized (Behnke 1979; Johnson 1987). Eight are "major" subspecies native to large regions, and eight are "minor" subspecies that are undescribed, native to very small areas (e.g., a single lake), or both. Eleven subspecies have legal protected status in one or more states, and two minor subspecies are apparently extinct.
- **Subspecies of note:** [[Westslope Cutthroat Trout]], [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]], [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], [[Coastal Cutthroat Trout]], [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Snake River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]], and [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]].
- **Taxonomic problem:** Protein data indicate some subspecies have been separate evolutionary units for long periods. The westslope and Yellowstone lineages are estimated to have been isolated for 1 to 2 million years (Behnke, personal communication, from biogeographic evidence). The authors propose that the westslope cutthroat trout should be recognized as a distinct species, but they note that a full taxonomic revision is beyond the paper's scope.
- **Methods used:** Horizontal starch-gel electrophoresis assayed 46 protein loci in muscle, liver, or eye tissue. Genetic distance was calculated with [[Nei's Genetic Distance]], and principal components analysis was applied to arcsine-transformed square-root allele frequencies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Threats:** Habitat alteration and introductions of [[Brook Trout]], rainbow trout, and other cutthroat subspecies outside native ranges are the main threats. The authors stress that introductions mainly harm native trout through interbreeding rather than through competition or pathogens.
- **Biology favoring hybridization:** Natural hybridization is more common among freshwater fishes than among other vertebrates. Factors include external fertilization, weak behavioral isolation, and competition for limited spawning habitat. Salmonid hybrids are often developmentally compatible and sometimes fertile, possibly due to the polyploid ancestry of salmonids (Allendorf & Thorgaard 1984).
- **Population structure:** Anadromous salmonids generally show little divergence among populations, while sedentary freshwater fishes often show substantial divergence. The authors attribute this to differences in [[Gene Flow]], since mobility limits [[Genetic Drift]] and selection.
- **Headwater dynamics:** Many western headwater lakes were historically fishless because of glaciation and barriers. Non-native trout introduced there now spread downstream, often unidirectionally past barrier waterfalls.
- **Pyramid Lake:** The [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] of Pyramid Lake evolved in a continuous lake environment for 50,000 to 100,000 years with an abundant prey species (tui chub, *Gila bicolor*). It reached up to 20 kg. The population disappeared in the late 1930s, primarily due to loss of spawning habitat.

## Key Findings

**1. Genetic divergence among subspecies is highly variable.**
- Colorado, Snake River, greenback, and Yellowstone cutthroat trout showed very little divergence, with Nei's distances typical of conspecific populations.
- Coastal, Lahontan, and westslope cutthroat trout showed exceptional divergence, similar to or greater than values between many fish species. These three are as similar to, or more similar to, rainbow trout than to the other four subspecies.
- Principal components analysis supported this: the first component explained 50% of the variance and the second explained an additional 25%.
- The data suggest cutthroat trout may be polyphyletic, but morphological, karyotypic, and mtDNA data point to a single lineage. The authors propose that secondary contact with rainbow trout may have caused past introgression.

**2. Electrophoresis detects hybridization in most taxa but not all.**
- Diagnostic loci were absent among Colorado, Snake River, greenback, and Yellowstone trout. Only one locus distinguished Rio Grande trout from the Yellowstone group.
- Two or more diagnostic loci separated coastal, Lahontan, westslope, and rainbow trout from each other and from the other five subspecies. Electrophoresis is therefore useful for detecting hybridization in these pairs.

**3. Hybridization is widespread and underestimated.**
- Of 80 samples from populations classed as "genetically pure" westslope cutthroat trout, 32 (40%) showed hybridization with rainbow or Yellowstone cutthroat trout. All samples from streams classed as hybridized showed hybridization.
- Genetic analysis of over 125 Montana populations found hybridization far more common than previously thought. About half of Montana westslope populations had been classed as hybridized by morphology alone.
- In the South Fork Flathead River drainage, only 2 of 19 headwater lakes contained pure westslope populations.
- Among 16 Yellowstone River tributary samples, half showed rainbow trout introgression.
- In Utah, introgression from rainbow trout was found in 7 of 39 cutthroat populations.
- Morphology is unreliable: less than 10% rainbow trout genes in introgressed westslope populations did not measurably alter meristic counts.

**4. Mitochondrial DNA is a limited tool.**
- mtDNA is maternally inherited and cannot distinguish hybrids from parental types. It is also more affected by drift and may be lost from hybrid populations when only males of one taxon participate in hybridization.
- The authors conclude that analysis of multiple diagnostic nuclear loci is the most sensitive and reliable method for identifying genetically pure populations.

**5. Hybrids show reduced fitness.**
- Hybrids between rainbow and westslope trout grew more slowly than parental types under hatchery conditions.
- Hybrids between rainbow and three cutthroat subspecies showed reduced developmental stability. Westslope and Yellowstone hybrids also showed reduced developmental stability, though one study reported some evidence of increased growth in westslope-Yellowstone hybrids.
- Outbreeding depression may be a concern, but the authors judge it less serious than homogenization, given the widespread success of hybrid trout populations.

**6. Westslope cutthroat genetic structure is highly divergent among populations.**
- Protein analysis of 29 loci in 103 populations gave a total gene diversity (H) of 0.0287. This was partitioned as follows:
  - Among 10 drainages: H = 0.0048 (16.7%)
  - Among populations within drainages: H = 0.0045 (15.7%)
  - Within local populations: H = 0.0194 (67.6%)
- A large number of alleles (29, or 43.9%) were common at frequencies above 0.95 across all populations. A high proportion (37.8%) of the remaining alleles were found in only one or two populations, often at high local frequencies.
- Conserving westslope diversity therefore requires many populations across the range.

**7. Yellowstone cutthroat trout shows little population divergence.**
- Only 5 of 46 loci were polymorphic. Allele frequency differences among samples accounted for just 3.7% of total variation, far lower than among westslope populations.
- Conserving Yellowstone diversity may need only a few populations, though the authors caution that low isozyme divergence does not rule out locally adapted populations, such as those adapted to warmer or saline waters.

**8. Hatchery management has produced clear genetic losses.**
- A Montana westslope broodstock founded from about 15 males and 15 females (collected in 1955 and 1967) lost substantial genetic variation by 1976 (analysis of 35 loci). By 1983 it showed high rates of morphological deformity and fluctuating asymmetry. Poor fertility and survival led the Montana Department of Fish, Wildlife, and Parks to abandon the broodstock in 1986.
- A Yellowstone broodstock founded in 1969 from McBride Lake periodically receives gametes from wild fish. Analysis of 71 loci coding for 31 enzymes (1985 year-class, N = 50) showed significant allele frequency differences from wild progeny at three of seven polymorphic loci. The hatchery fish had low developmental instability and high fertility and survival.
- The authors conclude that founding hatchery stocks from many individuals and periodically introducing wild genes maintains variation.

**9. Management and restoration recommendations.**
- Conservation should focus on alleles rather than allele frequencies, because lost alleles cannot be recovered except by rare mutation.
- Conservation of westslope trout requires many populations throughout the range.
- Killing introgressed populations with poison can harm other aquatic life. It is also impractical in remote or large waters.
- Montana's restoration policy for westslope trout involves poisoning highly introgressed South Fork Flathead populations, followed by periodic introduction of hatchery fish. Where poisoning is impractical, stocking is the only option. The authors suggest that a foreign gene level of 1% or less may suffice to count as effective restoration.

**10. Introgression is the greatest danger.**
- The authors identify introgression as the most important factor in the loss of native cutthroat populations. Fragmentation into isolated refuges increases extinction risk, and the lack of gene flow among refuges accelerates genetic drift.
- The eventual outcome of widespread introgression is the homogenization of western North American trout into a single "mongrel" species. This would eliminate millions of years of divergent lineages and locally adapted populations, with major losses for biodiversity and recreational fisheries.
