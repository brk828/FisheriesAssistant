# Southwestern Fishes and the Enigma of "Endangered Species"

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1968 *Science* article by [[W. L. Minckley]] and [[James E. Deacon]] on the decline of native freshwater fishes in the American Southwest. The authors argue that declines are driven mainly by habitat change from water diversion, impoundment, groundwater pumping and [[Exotic Species]] introductions. They propose a four-category framework for classifying organisms by habitat need, illustrate it with fish case studies, and call for documentation, basic ecological research and laboratory maintenance of depleted species. The document also includes a short opening of a second article by [[David Pimentel]], "Population Regulation and Genetic Feedback," which discusses population stability in animals.

## Background & Description

The authors note growing public concern for biota, reflected in conservation groups such as [[The Nature Conservancy]] and the [[International Union for the Conservation of Nature]]. State action included the Nevada Game and Fish Commission accepting responsibility for endemic fishes in January 1967, and California starting similar action in December 1967. The [[U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] defined rare and endangered species and began cataloguing them, and a laboratory for studying such organisms was set up at the Patuxent Wildlife Center in Maryland.

The authors state that their aim is to provoke constructive inquiry rather than to resolve conflicts. They argue that the only objective definition of "endangered" is an organism's inability to maintain its populations in nature, which requires continuously available suitable habitat.

### Four Habitat Categories

1. **Category 1:** Species favored by human-made or modified habitats. Relevant only where introduced forms harm native species.
2. **Category 2:** Wide-ranging, environmentally tolerant species. Declines in gross abundance have occurred, but these species are not currently under major pressure.
3. **Category 3:** Species dependent on a major environmental feature, such as large, strongly flowing rivers. Many big-river fishes fall here.
4. **Category 4:** Species confined to small, unique habitats such as isolated springs and closed basins. These are the most vulnerable to catastrophe.

The authors use the American bison as an example of a category 3 species that was nearly eliminated and later became common under domestication.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Big-River Fishes (Category 3)

Changes in water quality from impoundment (siltation, increased light penetration, altered temperature) can produce new habitats that favor different fauna. Channelization and drying of streams and riparian habitats can destroy whole faunas.

**Salt River, Arizona (near Tempe).** Extirpation of much of the native fauna occurred between 1890 and 1926. This coincided with early modification of the stream by settlers and the impoundment of Roosevelt Lake in 1910. Bartlett Reservoir on the Verde River (closed 1939), together with another dam, left the channel almost totally dry by the late 1950s. Only subsurface percolation, largely from municipal waste water, sustained isolated fish habitats. All species that occupied the river at Tempe in 1890 still survive somewhere in the Colorado River basin, but their success varies widely.

**Lower Virgin River, Utah/Arizona/Nevada.** The [[Virgin River Woundfin]] population here is the last reproducing population of its genus. A proposed U.S. Bureau of Reclamation dam would affect about 80 of the approximately 90 miles of suitable habitat. Predicted downstream effects include decreased turbidity, increased salinity and equalized flow. The authors argue that any change is likely to be detrimental and should be avoided until habitat requirements are defined.

### Desert Springs as Aquatic Archipelagos (Category 4)

Desert springs are isolated "islands" that often contain relict organisms from past ages. Their restricted, simplified ecology makes them susceptible to faunal destruction, especially where water demand exceeds supply. Minor changes in a small spring can affect an entire species.

## Key Findings

### Colorado River Squawfish and Humpback Sucker

- In 1962, fish-control operations eliminated both species from about 250 miles of the Green River mainstream and 250 miles of tributaries above the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] site.
- Neither species was found above the dam or in the 55-mile cold-water stretch to the Yampa River mouth, but both were common in the Green River between Echo Park and Ouray, Utah.
- Both species appear to persist in and above [[Grand Canyon]]. Below the canyon, the squawfish declined from being abundant at Yuma in the early 1900s to increasingly uncommon after 1950. Only two specimens were reported from the lower Colorado in 1962–67.
- In the Gila River basin, both species are considered virtually, and perhaps actually, extinct. [[Coolidge Dam]] (1929) blocked the Gila headwaters, and the river is now a dry wash over most of its lower course.
- Only the Salt River canyon remained a possible habitat. Despite intensive sampling since 1963, no squawfish or humpback sucker was obtained there.
- Humpback suckers were once common in the Salt River impoundments. Commercial fishermen took 6 tons from Saguaro Lake in 1949, but none was found when the lake was drained in 1966.
- Large specimens (some over 14 pounds and 30 inches) in 1949 may have signaled a population that was already failing to reproduce. No humpback shorter than about 15 inches has been caught in recent years.

### Virgin River Woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*)

- The woundfin once occurred in the Salt River at Tempe and in the Gila River at Yuma, Dome and Gila City (1890–95).
- It disappeared from the Gila River early in the twentieth century. It has not established itself in the Moapa River despite invading it.

### Gila Spinedace and Gila Topminnow

- The Gila spinedace (*Meda fulgida*) was once widespread in the upper Gila basin. It has not been recorded in the Verde drainage in recent years, where it was once abundant. The introduced [[Red Shiner]] appears to have replaced it throughout the system.
- The Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) once ranged throughout the Gila drainage and was described as one of the commonest fishes in the southern Colorado drainage. It now survives in only one spring area in Santa Cruz County, Arizona.
- The [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), introduced over roughly 40 years, is considered the most important factor in the topminnow's decline. In Arivaca Creek, topminnows were introduced in 1936 and were replaced by mosquitofish by 1959.
- The authors suggest that the topminnow's restriction to a single isolated drainage now places it in category 4, though it was originally a category 3 species.

### Ash Meadows Killifish and Pahrump Valley Groundwater

- Of the three known *Empetrichthys latos* stocks in the Pahrump Valley, Nevada, two are extinct. Pahrump Spring failed in 1958, and Raycraft Spring was filled for mosquito control. Manse Spring supports the remaining population, which was estimated at about 1,300 individuals in July 1967. Goldfish were recently introduced there.
- The estimated annual recharge of Pahrump Valley is 22,100 acre-feet. Annual withdrawals of nearly 41,000 acre-feet virtually guarantee continued decline of the water table.
- Manse Spring flow declined by 0.14 cubic feet per second annually since 1959. At this rate, the spring was predicted to fail in 10 to 11 years.
- Pumping increased sharply in the 1940s and 1950s. Mean spring flow was reduced to about half its original volume by 1916.

### Moapa Dace and White River Springfish

- Moapa dace (*Moapa coriacea*) density dropped after the introduction of the [[Shortfin Molly]]. It recovered about 2 years later, despite stable stream flow. The authors attribute the decline mainly to altered biotic habitat, possibly via parasitism and competition.
- White River springfish (*Crenichthys baileyi*) populations show marked physiological and morphological differences. Some tolerate very high temperatures and low dissolved oxygen. Several stocks have been depleted by introduced aquarium fishes, and one population became extinct after introduction of [[Largemouth Bass]] and mollies.
- In three Nevada springs selected as uncontaminated "controls," exotic fishes appeared within 2 months of study.

### Other Cases

- The Devils Hole pupfish (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) is represented by no more than 700 individuals and is now protected in Death Valley National Monument.
- The desert dace (*Eremichthys acros*) was listed as endangered on a premature judgment about irrigation. Expansion into irrigation ditches in Soldier Meadows, Nevada, shows it is not endangered.
- Exotic fishes contributed to the extinction of six listed taxa. Habitat destruction caused the extinction of two others.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- Historical declines were mainly due to large-scale physical change, such as river diversion, impoundment and stream downcutting. Later threats include lowering of water tables, eutrophication, pollution and exotic introductions.
- Category 3 fishes have retreated to inaccessible parts of their ranges and may recover rapidly after wet years. The Little Colorado spinedace (*Lepidomeda vittata*) showed such a resurgence between 1963 and 1966.
- The authors criticize listing "peripheral" species such as the Mexican stoneroller and Atlantic salmon, and argue that the "supply" of species in nature must be weighed against public "demand."
- Sampling is unreliable. Winter data may underestimate a fish that retires to deep pools, and electrofishing attempts failed to capture the Yaqui chub in an area where it remained abundant.
- Poor information, misinformation, public apathy and poor communication among scientists all hamper conservation. Eradicating exotic fishes can harm other organisms.
- Three objectives are proposed: (i) documentation of past and present population status; (ii) basic ecological research and life-history studies; and (iii) laboratory maintenance of depleted species.

### Population Stability (Pimentel Section)

The fragment of the second article presents evidence that natural populations are relatively constant:
- [[Tack]] states that birds in undisturbed conditions fluctuate within restricted limits, with the highest recorded count usually two to six times the lowest, and rarely as much as ten times.
- [[MacFadyen]] argues that the same species usually occupy the same habitats for many years, with numbers of the same order of magnitude.
- In Ontario, [[Snyder]] found that over about 70 years, 2 bird species became extinct, 23 increased and 6 decreased. This represents a total change of 9 percent of 351 species.
- A Finnish study reported an 11 percent change for 149 bird species.
