# Biological Investigations on the Caves of Chiapas (Mexico) and Adjacent Countries: Introduction

#subterranean-fauna #troglobite #biospeleology

## Executive Summary

This introductory paper, by [[Sbordoni]], [[Argano]] and [[Zullini]], reports the biospeleological results of the second Italian zoological mission to Mexico, sponsored by the [[Accademia Nazionale dei Lincei]]. The expedition ran from 24 January to 11 April 1971 and explored or visited 29 caves in [[Chiapas]] and two adjacent areas: the Tabasco lowlands and the eastern side of the [[Rio Usumacinta]] in Guatemala. The caves were grouped into four vegetation and climate zones, from about 50 m in [[Tropical Rain Forest]] to 2400 m in [[Pine-Oak Forest]]. For each cave the paper gives location, morphology, microclimate, ecological notes and a preliminary list of collected taxa. Fish are a minor component: the only cave-dwelling fish reported is a cavernicolous population of [[Poecilia sphenops]]. The paper is mainly a survey of cave invertebrates and bats.

## Background & Description

The authors describe Mexican karst as a natural laboratory. Caves at different elevations, climates and energy inputs can be compared within one region. This approach was intended to address basic evolutionary questions in cave populations. Earlier work had made Mexican cave fauna notably rich, but many karst areas remained unexplored. The authors chose Chiapas because caves lie only tens of kilometres apart, spanning less than 100 m above sea level in the rain forest to about 2600 m in pine-oak forest. A detailed fauna of the [[Yucatan Caves]] was already available for comparison.

**Expedition scope**
- Dates: 24 January to 11 April 1971.
- First trip (to 22 February): information gathering through interviews and air reconnaissance. Data were gathered on more than 100 caves and five were preliminarily explored.
- Second trip: detailed study of selected caves and collection from other habitats, including edaphic and interstitial stations (mainly artificial pits). A separate list of these stations was promised for later publication.
- Topographic surveys were made with compass and metric tape. Altitudes were taken with a compensated altimeter, zero-corrected at San Cristóbal de las Casas (2115 m). The authors note large discrepancies between map and sign-post altitudes.

**Regional physical setting (Chiapas)**
- Area: 74,000 km², the southernmost Mexican state.
- Relief and vegetation are arranged in six bands running SW to NE, from the Pacific coastal plain to the northern alluvial plains.
- Geology: a pre-Cambrian basalt and granite base. A large Cretaceous outcrop covers the Central Valley (Rio Grijalva) and parts of the plateau around San Cristóbal and Comitán, where the most striking karst is found and most biospeleological work was done.
- Climate: rainfall ranges from about 800–1000 mm in the Grijalva Valley and around Tuxtla Gutiérrez to about 4000–5000 mm at the wettest sites. The annual mean temperature runs from about 15 °C on the Central Plateau to 25–28 °C on the Pacific coastal plains.
- Vegetation (after [[Leopold Vegetation Zones]]): pine-oak forest (about 2000 m), tropical deciduous forest (500–700 m), [[Cloud Forest]], [[Tropical Rain Forest]] (Selva Lacandona) and savannah.

**Regional fauna**
- Terrestrial molluscs: about one third of species (26) were known only from Chiapas. Only eight were judged true endemics. The authors estimate 180–200 terrestrial and aquatic mollusc species for the state, against 88 recorded over the previous century.
- Ants show affinities with Guatemala and Veracruz.
- The Laguna Ocotal fish fauna of the Selva Lacandona (Characidae, Poeciliidae, Cichlidae) includes some endemics.
- Opilionids: 17 of 42 species collected (44%) were new to science.
- Prior cave records were mostly bats and their parasites, plus some crustaceans, orthopterans and frogs.
- The blind crab [[Potamocarcinus Mocinoi]] is a notable cave feature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Cave zones and sampled caves**
The 29 caves fall into four altitude groups (no. of caves in brackets, altitudes as given in the text):

1. **Tropical rain forest, Usumacinta and Tabasco (80–240 m):** [[Cueva de Yaxchilán]], [[Cueva del Diablo]], [[Cueva del Tepescuintle]], [[Grutas de Cocona]], [[Cueva del Azufre]].
2. **Tropical deciduous forest, Tuxtla Gutiérrez area (650–950 m):** [[Cueva del Chorreadero]], [[Cueva de Cerro Hueco]], [[Cueva de la Ramillete]], [[Cueva de Cepona]], [[Sima del Tempisque]], [[Sima del Ojito]].
3. **Cloud forest and temperate-tropical transition (1250–1820 m):** [[Cueva del Arco]], [[Cueva de Chinkultic]], [[Cueva de Tío Tícho]], [[Cueva del Panteón]], [[Cueva de Cerro Brujo]], [[Cueva de las Canicas]], [[Cueva del Nacimiento del Río San Domingo]], [[Cueva de la Golondrina]].
4. **Temperate conifer and pine-oak forest, San Cristóbal (2190–2430 m):** [[Grutas de Rancho Nuevo]], [[Cueva de Tulanca]], [[Cueva Grande de San Agustín]], [[Cueva Chica de San Agustín]], [[Pozo San Agustín]], [[Cueva del Rayo de San Felipe]], [[Cueva del Rayo de San Francisco]], [[Cueva de las Floresillas]], [[Cueva de la Cruz Belén]].

**Microclimate and hydrology examples**
- Yaxchilán: 24.3 °C, with large bat colonies and abundant guano fauna.
- Cueva del Diablo: 20.5–24.5 °C across chambers, with bats present.
- Cocona (Tabasco): a semi-fossil spring cave with a permanent watercourse, abundant dripping and very high humidity.
- Azufre (Tapijulapa): a sulphurous, milky-blue stream running for over 500 m. Humidity was about 99%, with water temperatures of about 20.6–20.9 °C in the terminal parts. Bats and swifts (*Streptoprocne zonaris mexicana*) roost in its chambers.
- Chorreadero: a 22 m waterfall and a deep subterranean lake.
- Cerro Hueco: 26.2 °C at the back, falling to 20 °C at the entrance, with a gradient in relative humidity.
- Cueva del Arco: 16.6 °C air, 98–100% humidity, water 20 °C.
- Rancho Nuevo: 8.8 °C at the entrance rising to 15.3 °C in the end chamber. Trophic resources are poor and the fauna is scanty in numbers.
- San Agustín (Cueva Grande): 9.5 °C in the end pitch.

**Trophic resources and fauna patterns**
- Most caves depend on guano from bats or swifts, rotten wood, or vegetable detritus carried in by water. Rancho Nuevo is an exception, with almost no food input.
- Fauna is typically abundant on guano and in wall communities (Amblypygi, Pholcid and other spiders, Orthoptera). Many taxa are troglophilic or trogloxene. Clearly troglobitic taxa are concentrated in a few caves.
- Several caves show signs of former human use, including funerary remains at Cerro Brujo and Cueva de las Floresillas and ritual use at Cruz Belén.

**Aquatic fauna**
- Crabs (*Potamocarcinus mocinoi*) and crayfish (*Procambarus mirandai*, Cerro Hueco) occur in several cave waters.
- Amphipods, notably *Bogidiella tabascensis*, are troglobitic and abundant in Cocona and at Rancho Nuevo.
- Palaemonid shrimp, identified as a new troglobitic species (*Bythinops luscus*), were common at Cueva del Arco.
- Cocona's stream held a troglobitic planarian (*Opisthobursa mexicana*).
- Insect larvae (Chironomidae, Trichoptera, Ephemeroptera) occur in some streams.

## Key Findings

- **Fish:** The only cave-dwelling fish recorded is [[Poecilia sphenops]] in the Cueva del Tepescuintle on the Guatemalan bank of the Usumacinta. Gordon and Rosen (1962) had studied this population. Its cave forms range from almost colourless, with reduced eyes and thick-lipped terminal mouths, to the normal surface form. These traits vary with distance from the cave mouth. The authors interpret this gradient as the product of hybridisation between surface populations and an undiscovered truly cave-adapted population.
- **Other aquatic vertebrates:** Some normally pigmented bagrid catfish were seen in the stream at Sima del Tempisque. Small epigean fish were noted in the Río San Domingo cave.
- **Species richness:** The collections contained many new taxa, with a high proportion of new species. The paper stresses that the main significance will emerge only once all groups are studied and cave populations can be compared.
- **Troglobite pattern:** Early data indicate a fairly high proportion of terrestrial troglobites even in tropical-type caves.
- **Endemism:** Chiapas hosts notable endemics, including the blind crab *Potamocarcinus mocinoi* and several cave-adapted invertebrates described in the accompanying "Subterranean Fauna of Mexico" volume.
- **Scope:** The paper is an introduction to a series. Detailed taxonomic descriptions appear in companion papers. The authors also planned a later publication listing edaphic and interstitial stations.
- **Limitations:** Altitude data are uncertain because of discrepancies between maps and sign-posts. Exploration was incomplete in several caves due to time limits, and some collections are preliminary.
