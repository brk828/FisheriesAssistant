# Radioactive Strontium in the River Thames

#radioecology #freshwater-ecology #water-chemistry

## Executive Summary

This 1962 paper by [[N. T. Mitchell]] and [[G. E. Eden]] reports a 19-week survey (from 17 July 1959) of the two strontium radioisotopes, [[Strontium 89]] and [[Strontium 90]], in the [[River Thames]]. The study was commissioned to test whether radioactivity discharged from the [[Atomic Energy Research Establishment]] at [[Harwell]] is retained in bottom deposits near the outfall. The authors found a progressive change in the Sr 89 to Sr 90 ratio downstream, which they explain as evidence that radiostrontium is not removed by river mud and moves downstream in solution on hydraulic timescales. Concentrations at water-abstraction points stayed well below accepted limits for lifetime consumption, although flows were exceptionally low.

## Background & Description

The Thames supplies a large share of London's water and receives discharges from several [[United Kingdom Atomic Energy Authority]] establishments, as well as [[Nuclear Weapons Fall-Out]] from world-wide dispersion. The investigation was confined to Sr 89 (half-life 51 days) and Sr 90 (half-life 28 years) because of limited facilities. The principal radioactive constituent of the Harwell effluent, [[Caesium 137]], was studied separately by [[Morgan and Arkell]] (1961).

Both isotopes form in fission of heavy nuclei such as U 235, in weapons fallout and in nuclear power wastes. Fission yields are roughly equal by weight, but disintegration rates differ widely:

- At fission, Sr 89 and Sr 90 disintegrate at 650 and 4.0 per million fissions, a ratio of 162:1.
- At one year after fission the ratio falls to 1.2:1.
- The ratio therefore indicates the time elapsed since fission and can distinguish sources of fission-product activity.

The two isotopes are chemically indistinguishable and cannot be separated by normal chemical or biological processes. Because they are among the most hazardous beta emitters, drinking-water limits are very low.

Possible contamination sources considered were:

- **Fall-out:** rain entering tributaries, falling directly on the water surface, and washing off built-up areas via sewers.
- **Controlled discharges:** Harwell (AERE) and the [[Radiochemical Centre]] at Amersham, whose effluent reaches the River Colne via the Maple Lodge sewage works. The Aldermaston (AWRE) discharge was judged very small and disregarded.
- **Other discharges:** laboratory tracer wastes from Oxford, which reach the river through the Sandford-on-Thames works.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The survey was designed around steady conditions. Rainfall was low and river flow comparatively constant during much of the study, which the authors considered essential for quantitative accounting.

Key features of the riverine system:

- **Sampling sites:** five locks (Eynsham, Day's Weir, Caversham, Bray, Teddington) plus snap samples from major tributaries, including the Evenlode, Cherwell, Ock, Thame, Kennet, Loddon, Colne and Wey.
- **Transit time:** water takes about 4 weeks to travel from Eynsham to Teddington at survey flows.
- **Sorption and sediment:** resuspended bottom deposit at 100 ppm divided radiostrontium between solution and suspension in the ratio 99.4:0.6, so uptake was readily reversible and small.
- **Soil and catchment:** radiostrontium is usually retained in upper soil layers (Russell and Milbourne 1957), so little Sr 90 was expected to leach from arable land.
- **Rain:** the mean Sr 89/Sr 90 ratio in rainwater was 3.8, higher than in most tributary and river samples.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Composite weekly samples (about 30 L per lock) were prepared from seven daily sub-samples, proportional to flow.
- Filtration was through a Metafilter (retaining particles above 0.5 µm), with a 30 mg stable strontium carrier added.
- Chemical separation used cation exchange, nitric acid precipitation and [[Thenoyltrifluoroacetone]] extraction of yttrium-90 for Sr 90 determination.
- Analyses were carried out in a laboratory remote from radioactive tracer work, to limit contamination at very low activity levels.
- Sr 89 values were decay-corrected to 20 July 1959, so later samples were multiplied by correction factors of up to about 5.

**Downstream ratio change**
- The Harwell effluent had an Sr 89/Sr 90 ratio of 0.68.
- At Eynsham, above the Harwell discharge, the ratio was 2.3.
- The ratio rose progressively downstream and was 1.8 at Teddington (Table I). The authors judged the difference between Eynsham and Teddington statistically doubtful.
- For the comparable period (about 14 weeks), Sr 90 was 64.0 mc at Day's Weir and 163.5 mc at Teddington.

**Harwell contribution**
- After subtracting Harwell inputs, the residual Sr 89/Sr 90 ratios were reasonably constant and agreed with the value at Eynsham.
- Harwell accounted for about 72% of Sr 90 and 45% of Sr 89 at Day's Weir, and about 28% of Sr 90 and 11% of Sr 89 at Teddington.
- Results support the conclusion that uptake by bottom deposits was negligible.

**Fall-out and run-off**
- Fall-out on the catchment (Eynsham to Teddington) was 8,230 mc Sr 89 and 2,200 mc Sr 90 by Teddington.
- Run-off transported about 3.2% (Sr 89) and 5.4% (Sr 90) of catchment fall-out to Teddington.
- Direct fall-out on the river surface was 1.6 mc Sr 89 + 0.5 mc Sr 90 (Eynsham to Day's) and 7.6 mc Sr 89 + 2.0 mc Sr 90 (Day's to Teddington).

**Sewage effluents**
- Sandford and Maple Lodge contributed negligible activity relative to tributary water and Harwell effluent.

**Interpretation of the rainwater ratio**
- Corrected rainwater ratios were constant across weekly samples, implying a uniform fission-product reservoir not replenished during the survey.
- The authors attributed the low river ratios to slow leaching of "old" Sr 90 from bottom deposits in ditches and minor tributaries, rather than to catchment retention.
- The same type of discrepancy was reported for 1958 by [[Morgan and Stanbury]] (1959), who attributed it to time delay between fall-out and sampling.

**Conclusions**
- Radiostrontium appears to pass downstream largely unaffected by mud contact, reaching Teddington (or the water-works intakes above the weir) after a hydraulically determined delay.
- Concentrations in the abstraction regions were consistently well below accepted lifetime-consumption limits, even at exceptionally low flows.
- The Harwell authorization limits radioisotopes of calcium and strontium at the discharge point to 20 "pyc." per litre (as printed), provided no other isotopes are present. Table I values were clearly below this.
- Cs 137 studies by Morgan and Arkell (1961) found about 3% of Harwell Cs 137 in river mud within 25 miles of the outfall. Because river mud binds Cs 137 much more strongly than Sr 90, the authors expected very little Sr 90 to be retained on the bed.
- Radiostrontium in the Thames and Harwell effluent was almost entirely in solution.

**Limitations noted by the authors**
- No allowance was made for longitudinal mixing, which was assumed to balance out at period boundaries.
- Weekly composites and the time delay prevented tracking of individual activity peaks.
- Dry-period flows may make the Harwell proportions higher than yearly averages.
