# Freshwater Fishes of Central America, Mexico and the West Indies: A Regional Survey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter by Robert Rush Miller (reprinted in Hurlbert and Villalobos-Figueroa, eds., 1982) reviews the freshwater fish fauna of [[Central America]], [[Mexico]] and the [[West Indies]]. It classifies fishes as primary (obligate freshwater), secondary (salt-tolerant freshwater) and marine derivatives, and organizes Central America into four [[Fish Provinces]]. It also summarizes the regional taxonomic literature, notes the lack of an up-to-date taxonomic treatment for Central America and Mexico, and describes the biogeographic reasons for the scarcity of primary fishes in the region and the Antilles.

## Background & Description

**Geographic scope.** Central America is defined biogeographically as extending north across the [[Isthmus of Tehuantepec]] to the Papaloapan basin (east) and Tehuantepec basin (west). Mexico is treated separately because its fauna is more closely allied with that of the United States. The West Indies comprise the Greater Antilles, Lesser Antilles and Bahamas.

**Fish categories.** The chapter distinguishes:
- *Primary freshwater fishes*: obligate freshwater species.
- *Secondary freshwater fishes*: salt-tolerant species that live in fresh water.
- *Marine derivatives*: marine-origin species that have established permanent residence in fresh water.

**Faunal totals.**
- Central America: nearly 500 freshwater or nearly freshwater species recorded. This chapter treats 354 species: 112 primary, 185 secondary and about 57 marine derivatives.
- Mexico (as delimited): about 300 species in 36 families occur in fresh water.
- West Indies: about 58 secondary freshwater species in 12 genera, plus about 50 marine-family species that occasionally enter fresh water.

**Taxonomic resources.** [[Regan 1906-08]] (*Biologia Centrali-Americana*) is described as outdated but still fundamental, with keys and excellent illustrations. [[Miller 1966]] (updated 1976a) and [[Bussing 1976]] provide species lists and distribution data. Several areas, including Honduras and Panama, have unpublished recent work in doctoral theses.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography and climate.** About 90 percent of Central America lies north of the lowland depression across Nicaragua, and only 25 percent rises above 600 m. The continental divide lies close to the Pacific coast, so most lowlands and major rivers lie on the Atlantic slope. The Atlantic slope receives two to three times as much rainfall as the Pacific lowlands and highlands. Annual rainfall ranges from less than 500 mm to more than 7,000 mm.

**Biogeographic history.**
- South America was isolated from Central America for most of the Cenozoic; the land connection was established in the late Pliocene.
- The narrow, mountainous isthmus impeded freshwater fish dispersal.
- North American primary fishes (minnows, suckers, sunfishes, darters) could not adapt readily to tropical conditions or compete with Neotropical groups.
- Multiple natural crossings of the continental divide, mainly from Atlantic to Pacific slopes, have occurred. Bussing (1976) documents 14 primarily Atlantic species that reached the Pacific slope in lower Central America.
- Transfers across the Sierra Madre Occidental in Mexico, related to Pleistocene uplift and stream capture, are documented for the Yaqui, Mayo, Fuerte and Mezquital rivers.

**Mexican Plateau.** The [[Mexican Plateau]] (Mesa Central and Mesa del Norte) is highly endemic. Its fauna is shaped by the Sierra Madre Oriental, the Sierra Madre Occidental and the [[Neovolcanic Axis]]. The Atlantic coastal plain forms a transition zone, where the fauna shifts from about 75 percent northern to 95 percent southern types over roughly 500 km.

**Antilles.** No primary freshwater fishes occur anywhere in the West Indies. Explanations offered include no continuous land connections during the late Cretaceous to early Cenozoic, and extinction after earlier connections. Lakes are scarce, and many are brackish or saline.

## Key Findings

**Fish provinces of Central America (Miller 1966; Bussing 1976):**
- **Usumacinta Province** (Atlantic slope; the largest number of species): 145 species, 34 genera, 10 families. Cyprinodontoids and cichlids account for 106 species. Divided into four subprovinces.
- **Chiapas-Nicaraguan Province** (Pacific coast; the most depauperate): 42 species, 18 genera, 9 families. About one-third are primarily Atlantic forms. It contains the range of [[Anableps dowi]] (cuatro-ojos).
- **San Juan Province** (Atlantic slope, Nicaragua to Panama): 59 species, 25 genera, 8 families. Its greatest diversity lies in the [[Rio San Juan]] drainage and the [[Lake Nicaragua]] system. Marine invaders include the [[Bull Shark]] (*Carcharhinus leucas*) and two sawfish species (*Pristis*).
- **Isthmian Province** (western Panama to Punta Mala, Costa Rica): 104 species, 52 genera, 12 families. It is the most diversified province and holds about two-thirds of the families and nearly three-quarters of the species of primary fishes in Central America. South American influence is strong.

**Family-level patterns in Central America:**
- Secondary cyprinodontoids (notably Poeciliidae, about 65 species in 15 genera) and cichlids (about 75 species in five genera) dominate, together with marine invaders. These three groups total 237 species.
- Primary fishes number 113, all cypriniforms (Ostariophysi). Most are restricted to Panama and parts of Costa Rica.
- Only three northern primary elements (two catostomids and one ictalurid) penetrated Central America from North America.
- [[Lepisosteus tropicus]] (tropical gar) occurs on both slopes.
- Freshwater species of Dorosoma (three) and Profundulus (Cyprinodontidae, restricted to fresh water) are notable.

**Mexico:**
- Four primary cypriniform families (Characidae, Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Ictaluridae) total 93 species in 25 genera. Cyprinidae is the largest family with 66 species in 16 genera.
- Secondary cyprinodontoids (Cyprinodontidae, Goodeidae, Poeciliidae) total 91 species in about 26 genera.
- The Lerma basin is dominated by the viviparous [[Goodeidae]] (about 35 species in 15 genera) and the atherinid genus Chirostoma. Together they make up about 70 percent of the Lerma fish fauna.
- The Balsas Province is depauperate, with 12 species in seven families.
- The Rio Grande faunal area includes the [[Cuatro Cienegas Basin]], an important centre of endemism.
- The Pacific northwest (Sinaloa and Nayarit) is marked by a near-absence of primary fishes and by secondary poeciliids (*Poeciliopsis*) and marine-origin groups.

**West Indies:**
- Secondary freshwater fishes total about 58 species in 12 genera. The livebearers (Poeciliidae) dominate with 43 species in five genera. *Limia*, an Antillean endemic genus, includes 19 species.
- Cuba has the only West Indian gar (*Lepisosteus tristoechus*), a flowering of Girardinus and Quintana, and two blind cave-dwelling ophidiids (*Lucifuga*).
- Hispaniola has about 16 Limia species, and Lake Miragoane hosts a species flock of Limia. Lake Saumatre (about 10 ppt salinity) holds *Cyprinodon bondi*. Lake Enriquillo (about 70 ppt) has a very limited fauna.
- Puerto Rico has only one secondary freshwater species, *Poecilia vivipara*, and its natural occurrence has been questioned.
- Jamaica has one cyprinodontid and four poeciliids (three *Gambusia*, one *Limia*), largely endemic.
- The Bahamas have four secondary species. Lake Forsyth (Andros Island) is a hard-water lake of about 1.5 ppt salinity.
- The Lesser Antilles have only four secondary species: *Rivulus harti*, *R. marmoratus*, *Poecilia vivipara* and *P. reticulata*.

**Notable marine-family penetration:** Families with freshwater representatives include Elopidae, Ariidae, Centropomidae, Gerreidae, Haemulidae, Mugilidae, Eleotridae, Gobiidae and Syngnathidae. In the West Indies, only the Ophidiidae include species restricted to fresh water.

**Conclusion:** The chapter concludes that the regional fish fauna remains poorly documented taxonomically. Regan (1906-08) is the only comprehensive taxonomic work for Central America, and the bibliographies by Ziesler (1979) have gaps in taxonomic coverage. Mexico lacks a comprehensive, up-to-date freshwater treatment, and no modern checklist exists for West Indian fishes.
