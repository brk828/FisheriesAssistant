# Speciation Rates in Western North American Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This symposium contribution by [[Robert Rush Miller]] (University of Michigan, published in the *Age and Origin of Species* volume) asks how fast freshwater fishes evolve in nature. The author argues that the fauna of western North America is a useful test case because it is made up of relict, monotypic, and highly endemic forms isolated in drainages and springs since the late Tertiary and Pleistocene. The paper proposes that the postpluvial period (about 10,000 to 12,000 years) was long enough for subspecies differentiation and, in some cases, full species formation. It focuses on [[Death Valley System]] fishes, especially [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], to show how population size, isolation, environmental stability, and generation time may accelerate speciation in desert waters.

## Background & Description

The paper's central question concerns the rate of evolutionary change, defined as morphological divergence relative to elapsed time. Freshwater fishes are treated as ideal organisms for this question because they are confined to drainages and cannot easily cross dry land.

The western North American freshwater fauna is compared with its eastern counterpart. The western fauna contains relicts, numerous monotypic genera, and a few widespread species. Of roughly thirty families recorded from the area west of the Continental Divide, the primary and secondary freshwater fishes are placed at 114 species in nine families, many with restricted ranges. Some occupy habitats of only a few thousand gallons of water or less than an acre.

Fossil evidence is sparse. The [[Green River Formation]] fauna is too early in fish evolution to help with the origin of Recent fishes. The author concludes that the present fauna did not originate before the Miocene.

Relict taxa mentioned include:

- [[Sacramento Perch]] (*Archoplites interruptus*), the endemic Sacramento genus and the sole western survivor of the sunfish family, which the author notes was once more widespread.
- A minnow (*Novumbra*), restricted to boglike waters of the Puget Sound region.
- A trout-perch (*Columbia*), known only from the Columbia River system.
- The relict genus *Empetrichthys*, which was far more widespread in the late Pliocene, based on fossils from Los Angeles County.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geological history.** The Miocene landscape was roughly like modern southeastern Brazil or eastern China, with lowland fishes (sunfishes, pirate perches, and the sucker genus *Anyzon*) common. These groups are now extinct in the west except for one relict sunfish in California. The close of the Miocene brought renewed crustal unrest that disrupted drainages and set the stage for isolated faunas. Late Pliocene and Pleistocene uplift, including elevation of the Sierra Nevada and block faulting in western Nevada, created many interior basins.

**Climate and pluvial periods.** Continental glaciation barely reached the northern edge of the study area, but lowered temperatures and pluvial periods were strong. Some extinction of genera and local extermination of families occurred, for example in southern Idaho and southeastern Oregon. Pliocene rainfall in the Great Basin was estimated at about 20 to 25 inches in the north and 15 to 20 inches in the south, which suggests that the Sierra Nevada was then a relatively minor topographic feature.

**Death Valley system.** During maximum late Pleistocene extent, the system consisted of isolated valleys whose waters reached Death Valley. The fauna comprises three families, five genera, and ten species, most of which are endemic. Nine of the ten species are thought to have been isolated for a long period, possibly since the last pluvial. The fauna is notable for cyprinodont fishes adapted to alkaline, often hypersaline water.

**Colorado River Basin.** This basin contains about 40% of the genera and 80% of the species of the major western fish faunas. The author attributes its high endemism (about 87%, the highest of any major western drainage) to two factors:

1. The river formed from isolated segments that joined the main basin at different times.
2. The Colorado has fewer competing fish groups of marine origin than the Sacramento, Klamath, or Columbia systems.

**Other systems.** The Gila-related Verde and Bill Williams River groups, and the White River of eastern Nevada, show distinctive faunas and high local endemism. The Santa Ana River fauna of the Los Angeles Basin may date to the Pliocene.

**Springs and desert water.** Desert springs vary in temperature, salinity, and boron content. The author notes that high temperature may speed differentiation, which could explain similarities among isolated populations of the same species in warm springs of similar temperature. Elevated salinity seems to produce similar morphological effects, such as decreased body size and increased scale counts.

## Key Findings

**1. Postpluvial timing.** Desert fish differentiation is linked to the postpluvial disruption of streams, lakes, and springs. The author estimates a span of about 10,000 to 12,000 years since desiccation. This is enough for some full species to differentiate, but more commonly it corresponds to subspecies or lesser morphological distinction. Most local endemism among desert fishes probably dates from the onset of the postpluvial period.

**2. Duration of isolation.** The author proposes a general rule of a positive correlation between duration of isolation and degree of differentiation, while cautioning that endemic forms in permanent rivers also contribute to this pattern. Six subspecies of [[Cyprinodon Nevadensis]] in the Amargosa River Basin are restricted to springs and flows within about 100 miles of channel. Geologists believe the Amargosa was continuous only a few centuries to a few thousand years ago, but the author suggests that forms may have differentiated earlier and then survived in separate localities.

**3. Devils Hole Pupfish.** [[Cyprinodon Diabolis]] lives in a single cave-spring pool and is one of the most extraordinary fishes of the Death Valley system. It shows little meristic variation, probably due to long isolation, inbreeding, and a stable environment. Its differences from relatives are considered non-adaptive, except for dwarfing, and the author attributes them mainly to genetic drift in a small population. Food is probably the main factor limiting population size: the diet includes green algae, diatoms, a planarian, a small bivalve, an amphipod, and copepods. The population was estimated from sampling; the exact figure is not clearly legible in the scanned text. The species cannot be maintained in water cooler than about 80°F for long, and reproduction fails above about 104°F.

**4. Breeding rates in warm springs.** Warm-spring pupfish spawn throughout the year, producing perhaps eight to ten generations annually, compared with two or three in stream stocks. In culture, young matured when only two months old. The author suggests that small, warm, isolated populations may experience an accelerated mutation rate and more generations per year, which together favor rapid speciation.

**5. Dead-end species.** Species such as *C. diabolis* may be evolutionary dead-ends because low genetic variability limits their capacity to respond to environmental change, so extinction is more probable than survival. Nonetheless, the species has persisted for many thousands of years in a very limited habitat.

**6. Other pupfish.** *Cyprinodon salinus* lives in Salt Creek, a short briny stream in Death Valley. The author suggests it may have diverged from the ancestral stock within about the last 25,000 years, but this is speculative. The Pahrump Valley *[[Empetrichthys Latos]]* and the Ash Meadows species are closely related to the *Fundulus* line, and fossils suggest a common ancestor for *Empetrichthys*, *Crenichthys*, and *Cyprinodon*.

**7. Evolutionary factors.** Four factors are identified as shaping evolutionary rates in desert fishes:

- Population size, especially the effective breeding population
- Duration of isolation
- Geological conditions
- Number of generations produced per year

The author argues that genetic drift may matter more than selection in small isolates when interspecific competition is absent.

**8. Colorado River distinctiveness.** Two conditions explain the distinctiveness of Colorado River fishes: the basin's long history as separate segments, and the absence of competing marine-derived fish groups. Colorado River types such as *[[Ptychocheilus Lucius]]* (squawfish) appear in northern Arizona by mid-Pliocene.

**9. Research priorities.** The discussion highlights the need for:

- Studies of intrinsic and extrinsic isolating mechanisms
- Hybridization and reinforcement experiments, with a suggestion to mix populations in a natural experiment
- Osteological studies of living and fossil forms to clarify relationships
- Better knowledge of food habits, which the author calls fundamental to systematics

The author notes that about 20% of the western fresh-water fauna is still undescribed (a rough estimate given in discussion). Rearing experiments in backyard pools showed that diagnostic characters of *[[Cyprinodon Nevadensis]]* subspecies largely held, with some environmental influence.

**10. Cautions on intervention.** In discussion, [[Robert Rush Miller]] rejects any idea of mixing the existing species of the Great Basin system, stating that this is not an appropriate situation for such experiments.
