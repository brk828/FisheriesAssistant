# Critical Habitat Designation for Rio Grande Silvery Minnow - Environmental Assessment

#ichthyology #endangered-species #Cyprinidae #conservation-policy #ESA

## Executive Summary

This draft Environmental Assessment evaluates the designation of critical habitat for the endangered [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (Hybognathus amarus) pursuant to section 4 of the Endangered Species Act. The document responds to a February 22, 1999 court order (Forest Guardians and Defenders of Wildlife v. Bruce Babbitt) requiring the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to publish a final critical habitat determination by June 23, 1999. The proposed critical habitat encompasses approximately 163 miles (262 km) of the [[Middle Rio Grande]] from the downstream side of State Highway 22 bridge (immediately below [[Cochiti Dam]]) to the Atchison Topeka and Santa Fe Railroad crossing near San Marcial, upstream from [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]]. This represents the only remaining occupied habitat for this historically widespread species, which once ranged from Española, New Mexico to the Gulf of Mexico. The assessment compares a No Action Alternative with the Preferred Alternative of finalizing the critical habitat designation as originally proposed in 1993.

## Background & Description

The [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] is one of seven species in the genus Hybognathus. Adults reach 90 mm total length and are brilliantly silver with emerald reflections. The species exhibits a brief spawning period (one month) in late spring-early summer coinciding with spring runoff at water temperatures of 20-24°C. The majority of spawning individuals are Age 1 fish; Age 2 fish constitute less than 5% of the spawning population. Mature females produce 3-18 clutches of eggs in a 12-hour period, with mean clutch size of approximately 270 eggs. Age 2 females produce about twice as many eggs as Age 1 females but contribute substantially less to population maintenance due to their rarity.

Following fertilization, semi-buoyant, non-adhesive eggs drift with the current for up to 50 hours. Hatching time is temperature-dependent; larvae are approximately 3.7 mm standard length upon hatching. Larvae continue to drift for at least one day after hatching before moving to low-velocity habitats where food (mainly phyto- and zooplankton) is abundant. In backwaters and embayments, growth is rapid with lengths of 39-41 mm attained by late autumn.

The species exhibits high spawning mortality. By December, the large majority of individuals are Age 0. This ratio remains relatively constant between January and June, with Age 1 fish constituting over 95% of the population just prior to spawning. The population generally consists of only two age-classes. Maximum longevity is approximately 25 months; few survive more than 13 months.

The elongated and coiled gastrointestinal tract indicates herbivorous diet. Presence of sand and silt in gut contents suggests epipsammatic algae is an important food source.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Selection and Requirements:**
Investigations by [[Dudley and Platania]] (1997) found that [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] selects habitats that are not the most commonly available in the river. The species utilizes low-velocity areas including eddies formed by debris piles, pools, and backwaters. Main channel runs are generally avoided—only 1% of individuals were found in higher-velocity habitat despite this being the most abundant habitat type. Most individuals occupy habitats with depths less than 20 cm or between 31-40 cm; few utilize areas with depths greater than 50 cm. The species is most abundant in areas with little or no water velocity (less than 10 cm/second), occasionally in moderate velocity (11-30 cm/sec), and rarely in velocities greater than 40 cm/sec. Silt substrate supports more than 90% of individuals; sand is the second most common substrate; gravel and cobble collectively account for less than 1%.

**Historical Distribution and Decline:**
The silvery minnow was historically one of the most abundant and widespread fishes in the basin, occurring in the [[Rio Grande]] from Española, New Mexico to the Gulf of Mexico, and in the [[Pecos River]] from Santa Rosa, New Mexico downstream to its confluence with the Rio Grande. The species has been completely extirpated from the Pecos River through habitat degradation and hybridization with the introduced congener, [[Plains Minnow]] (Hybognathus placitus). In the Rio Grande, the species was preceded to extinction by four other members of its reproductive guild (pelagic broadcast spawners): [[Rio Grande Bluntnose Shiner]], [[Phantom Shiner]], [[Rio Grande Shiner]], and [[Speckled Chub]].

**Threats and Habitat Degradation:**
The only extant population is threatened by annual dewatering of large portions of habitat. Dewatering results primarily from diversion of the river for agriculture within the [[Middle Rio Grande Valley]]. During below-average water years, the river channel may be dry from [[Isleta Diversion Dam]] downstream about 179 km (111 mi) for two months or more. Consecutive below-average flow years severely impact this short-lived species.

Diversion dams entrain silvery minnows into irrigation canals and prevent upstream movement (recolonization). As low-velocity habitats are eliminated in upper reaches, eggs, larvae, and young are transported downstream. Diversion dams prevent upstream movement, depleting upstream habitats and increasing vulnerability of downstream populations to dewatering and other impacts.

Mainstem dams permit flow regulation, trap nutrients, alter sediment transport, and create reservoirs favoring nonnative fish species. These changes affect silvery minnows by reducing food supply, altering and diminishing preferred habitat, increasing predation, and preventing dispersal.

Physical modifications from channelization, levee construction, and jetty fields have resulted in: straightened and shortened river reaches, increased current velocity, reduced overall habitat availability, removal of riparian vegetation, loss of instream cover, altered substrate composition, severance from floodplain, reduced habitat complexity, channel narrowing, curtailment of meander and oxbow formation, and channel incision. Meanders, oxbows, and historic floodplain components have been eliminated to pass water quickly for agricultural and downstream deliveries. Sandy and silty substrates have been replaced by gravel and cobble in upstream reaches; backwaters where young develop have been eliminated in straightened, deepened, and managed "floodway" sections.

Water quality degradation from municipal and agricultural discharge during low-flow periods may affect survival and recovery.

**Current Distribution:**
Extensive collecting since 1990 documents the species' rarity. Forty-six collections between [[Cochiti Pueblo]] and [[Bosque del Apache National Wildlife Refuge]] from 1990 through spring 1992 produced 23 species and 38,501 specimens, but only 10 specimens of Rio Grande silvery minnow. Sites where the species had previously (1987-1988) numerically dominated yielded no specimens in subsequent sampling.

**River Reaches within Critical Habitat:**

*Cochiti Reach* (Cochiti Dam to Angostura Diversion Dam): Since Cochiti Dam completion in 1973, average annual discharge increased by 18%. Flow is generally clear, cool, and sediment-free. Substrate immediately downstream is often armored cobble. Relatively little channel braiding; depositional areas with reduced velocity and sand/silt substrates are uncommon. Rio Grande silvery minnow is rare in this reach.

*Angostura Reach* (Angostura Diversion Dam to Isleta Diversion Dam): Includes metropolitan Albuquerque. River gains sediment, becoming predominantly sand bed with low sandy banks and numerous sand bars. Distribution of silvery minnow increases downstream but nowhere achieves high population levels observed in lower reaches. Flow is perennial through this reach due partly to City of Albuquerque water rights and agreement with [[Middle Rio Grande Conservancy District]] to maintain minimum 250 cfs flow.

*Isleta Reach* (Isleta Diversion Dam to San Acacia Diversion Dam): Typically supports few silvery minnows, believed to result from extensive water diversion at Isleta Diversion Dam and limited downstream drift from small upstream populations. Up to 1,070 cfs can be diverted; water generally remains in 445 miles of drains and canals with few return points. Extensive portions frequently isolated during summer and autumn, eventually drying. Most adversely impacted reach due to water diversions.

*San Acacia Reach* (San Acacia Diversion Dam to Elephant Butte Reservoir): The longest uninterrupted segment (75 miles) supporting the largest population. Individuals may have originated in any upper reach. Leakage at San Acacia Diversion Dam often provides only flow for upper portions. Until 1996, no points for return of diverted water to river channel, leaving extensive portions dry. In 1996, emergency connection opened between [[Low-Flow Conveyance Channel]] and Rio Grande about 9 miles downstream of San Acacia Diversion Dam.

**Primary Constituent Elements:**
The proposed critical habitat contains physical and biological features essential to conservation:
- Stream morphology supplying sufficient flowing water to provide food and cover for all life stages
- Water quality preventing stagnation (elevated temperatures, decreased oxygen, carbon dioxide build-up)
- Water quantity preventing formation of isolated pools that restrict fish movement, foster increased predation, and congregate disease-causing pathogens

**Water Management Infrastructure:**
The [[Rio Grande Compact]], treaty obligations with Mexico, reservoir legislation, and flood protection legislation govern water management. Major facilities include: [[Heron Reservoir]] (400,000 acre-feet, storing [[San Juan-Chama Project]] water), [[El Vado Dam]] (186,000 acre-feet), [[Abiquiu Dam]] (1,200,000+ acre-feet potential, 200,000 acre-feet authorized for San Juan-Chama storage), [[Jemez Canyon Dam]] (113,100 acre-feet), [[Galisteo Dam]] (79,600 acre-feet flood control, 9,400 acre-feet sediment storage), and [[Cochiti Dam]] (primary flood control structure, 47,000 acre-feet permanent pool, 539,000 acre-feet flood control capacity).

Three diversion dams for irrigation are located downstream of Cochiti Dam: Angostura, Isleta, and San Acacia. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] maintains river maintenance program including bank stabilization, river training works, sediment removal, vegetation control, snag removal, and levee maintenance.

**Socioeconomic Context:**
The [[Middle Rio Grande Valley]] includes four metropolitan areas: Santa Fe, Albuquerque, Las Cruces, and El Paso. In 1992, 57,529 acres were actively irrigated with an additional 12,241 acres temporarily fallow. About 90% of river water use is agricultural. In 1993, 51,202 acres were cropped: alfalfa (60%), pasture (31%), corn silage (7%), green chile (2%). Total private-sector agricultural employment in 1993 was 14,078 (about 2% of total employment). Agricultural income was approximately $269 million (1% of total income).

Six sovereign Pueblos are contained in the valley: Cochiti, Santo Domingo, San Felipe, Santa Ana, Sandia, and Isleta.

**Associated Biota:**
The riparian zone is dominated by cottonwood trees with understory including coyote willow, seepwillow, false indigo bush, and New Mexico olive. Introduced species (Russian olive, salt cedar, Siberian elm) are increasingly abundant. Six structural vegetation types were recognized by [[Hink and Ohmart]] (1984).

Avian studies documented 277 bird species from Velarde to San Acacia Diversion Dam. Bird densities reach 2,159 birds per 100 acres with up to 55 species in one community. The riparian corridor is critical for migratory landbirds using the Great Plains-Rocky Mountain flight route. Of 325 avian species in New Mexico, 241 (74%) have been detected within Middle Rio Grande riparian habitat. About one-third occupy the area only during migration. Average density is 300-600 birds/100 acres; densities over 1,000 birds/100 acres recorded in some native riparian habitats.

The historic native fish fauna consisted of 17-27 species. Big river fishes (longnose gar, shovelnose sturgeon, gray redhorse, blue sucker, freshwater drum) were historically present but eliminated by early 20th century. More recently, [[Speckled Chub]], [[Rio Grande Shiner]], [[Phantom Shiner]], and [[Rio Grande Bluntnose Shiner]] were extirpated; the latter two are thought extinct. From 13-19 nonnative fish species have been introduced, now comprising 38-76% of total species. [[Platania]] (1993) found fish communities contained abundant native specimens but species diversity was almost twice as great for nonnatives. [[Red Shiner]] was dominant (53% of total catch).

Listed species potentially occurring in the action area include: endangered [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (Empidonax traillii extimus), threatened [[Bald Eagle]] (Haliaeetus leucocephalus), endangered [[Interior Least Tern]] (Sterna antillarum athalassos), experimental nonessential population of [[Whooping Crane]] (Grus americana), and endangered [[American Peregrine Falcon]] (Falco peregrinus anatum). The proposed [[Mountain Plover]] (Charadrius montanus) may also be present.

## Key Findings

**Purpose and Legal Requirement:**
The purpose is to designate critical habitat for [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] pursuant to section 4 of the Endangered Species Act by legally designating areas essential to survival and recovery and describing physical and biological features requiring special management considerations. The action responds to court order (Forest Guardians and Defenders of Wildlife v. Bruce Babbitt, CIV 97-0453 JC/DIS) requiring final determination by June 23, 1999.

**Proposed Critical Habitat Boundaries:**
The preferred alternative designates critical habitat as the mainstem Rio Grande from the downstream side of State Highway 22 bridge crossing (immediately downstream of Cochiti Dam) extending south approximately 262 km (163 mi) to where the Atchison Topeka and Santa Fe Railroad crosses near San Marcial, Socorro County. This encompasses the Rio Grande in New Mexico counties of Socorro, Valencia, Bernalillo, and Sandoval. This is the only area where the species has been collected in the recent past (50 years) and where it currently exists.

**Alternatives Considered:**
- *No Action Alternative*: Forgo designation of critical habitat
- *Action Alternative (Preferred)*: Finalize designation as proposed in Federal Register (58 FR 11822, March 1, 1993)
- *Additional areas*: Removed from consideration because no additional areas meeting critical habitat definition were identified
- *Deletion of portions*: Removed from consideration because river habitat is continuous and all subject to interrelated impacts; no basis exists for excluding specific areas

**Environmental Consequences Analysis:**
The assessment concludes that designation of critical habitat will provide no significant additional protection beyond that already afforded by the 1994 listing of the species as endangered. Section 7(a)(2) of the Act prohibits Federal agencies from actions that would jeopardize continued existence of listed species OR destroy or adversely modify designated critical habitat. The definitions of "jeopardize the continued existence" and "destruction or adverse modification" are virtually identical in regulations (50 CFR Part 402)—both require appreciable detrimental effect on both survival and recovery. Therefore, actions severe enough to result in adverse modification finding would also result in jeopardy finding.

**Economic Analysis:**
A draft economic analysis contracted in 1994 and provided in 1996 estimated annual costs of $800,000 to $1.4 million attributed to critical habitat designation, based primarily on anticipated changes to [[Bureau of Reclamation]] river maintenance program. However, the Service determined these conclusions were based on erroneous assumption that changes from critical habitat designation would be significantly greater than changes already resulting from species listing. The Service expects no incremental economic effects associated with critical habitat designation above and beyond effects of listing the species as endangered.

**Tribal Consultation:**
Pursuant to Secretarial Order 3206, the Service assessed the designation with respect to six Middle Rio Grande Pueblos. The Service determined: (1) designation is reasonable and necessary and least restrictive available; (2) conservation purpose cannot be achieved by reasonable regulation of non-Indian activities due to contiguity of habitats and interdependence of Indian and non-Indian activities; (3) designation does not discriminate against Indian activities; (4) voluntary tribal measures alone are not adequate. Information was provided to tribal representatives at Six Middle Rio Grande Basin Pueblos Coalition meeting on March 4, 1999.

**Public Process:**
The comment period was reopened on April 7, 1999 (64 FR 16890) to close May 7, 1999. A public hearing was scheduled for April 29, 1999 in Albuquerque. The draft Economic Analysis and draft Environmental Assessment were made available for public comment. Written comments received on the 1996 draft Economic Analysis were retained for consideration.

**Critical Habitat Definition and Elements:**
Critical habitat under section 3(5)(A) of the Act includes: (i) specific areas within geographical area occupied by species at time of listing containing physical or biological features essential to conservation that may require special management considerations or protection; and (ii) specific areas outside geographical area occupied at time of listing determined essential for conservation. Physical and biological features (50 CFR 424.12) include: space for individual and population growth and normal behavior; food, water, or nutritional/physiological requirements; cover or shelter; breeding, reproduction, or rearing sites; and habitats protected from disturbances or representative of historic geographical and ecological distributions.

**Current Status and Threats:**
The species was determined endangered because of significant habitat loss. The only extant population is threatened by annual dewatering of large habitat portions. During below-average water years, the river channel may be dry from Isleta Diversion Dam downstream about 179 km for two months or more. Consecutive below-average flow years can severely impact or eliminate the species from desiccated reaches. Additional threats include: diversion dam entrainment and prevention of upstream movement, mainstem dam effects on flow regulation and sediment transport, physical channel modifications from channelization and levee construction, water quality degradation, and loss
