# Methods of Sampling the Bottom Fauna in Stony Streams

#benthic-macroinvertebrates #ephemeroptera #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

T. T. Macan's 1958 methods paper (Mitteilungen No. 8 of the [[International Association of Theoretical and Applied Limnology]]) addresses how to sample the bottom fauna quantitatively in fast-flowing streams whose beds consist of loose stones of varying size. The paper reviews five categories of samplers used in running water, judges each against stony substrata, and describes the author's own [[Shovel Sampler]], a push-through device that samples a fixed area of 1/20 m². Field comparisons at Ford Wood Beck examine how the shovel, a fine hand-net and a coarse hand-net catch mayfly nymphs and beetle larvae differently, and the paper concludes that each method carries species-specific biases. Macan argues that any user of a single method must understand the habits of the dominant species.

## Background & Description

The paper begins by noting that most standard samplers were designed for soft or shallow beds, and that stony streams pose a distinct problem: current speed and large stones prevent sealing a sampling area. Macan groups sampling methods into five categories:

1. Lifting individual stones by hand.
2. Providing a known area of removable substratum for colonization.
3. Boxes and cylinders enclosing a given area.
4. Fixed nets downstream of a disturbed area.
5. Nets pushed forward through the substratum.

**Hand-lifting and hand-nets.** Stones are lifted and animals swept into a net. The method, used earlier by Schrader (1932) and Müller (1953), is limited to beds of large stones. Macan's own [[Hand Net]] was a pond-net with a 28.5 cm internal diameter, fitted with coarse bolting silk (about 20 threads per inch, holes roughly 1 mm square) and fine phytoplankton netting (180 threads per inch). Collecting lasted exactly 5 or 10 minutes, with the collector working upstream. Results were expressed per unit time rather than per area.

**Colonization trays and blocks.** Moon (1935) used a tray on a frame of known area in [[Windermere]], but in swift streams the tray could not be bedded down, and [[Gammarus Pulex]] congregated under it rather than on it. Britt (1955) used scored concrete blocks, and Aubrecht (1953) used tiles, but the artificial surfaces were not colonized by the same fauna as natural stones.

**Boxes and cylinders.** Jonasson (1948) used a tin box of 450 cm² on the River Susaa; Macan notes that a closed box cannot seal against a rough bed and that any solid object deflects current downward and scours the target area. Welch (1948) described a brass cylinder of one square foot (about 929 cm²), and Neill (1938) a cylinder of 1000 cm² cross-section with flanges and sliding doors. Hess's sampler is a wire-and-canvas hybrid that does not isolate its area.

**Fixed nets.** The [[Surber Sampler]] (Morgan 1936; Surber 1937) uses two square frames of one square foot each; a net on the downstream frame catches material disturbed within the upstream frame. The [[Surber Sampler]] principle was also used by Bagnall (1949) and Illies (1952). Leonard (1939) found it very difficult to seat such a net in rough rubble. Dittmar's modification adds a blade to be driven into the bed. Fixed nets fail where flow is fast, because a single displaced stone can destabilize a wide area.

**Pushed nets.** The [[Pfahlkratzer]] (Steiner 1919; Wundsch 1936) is a stout pond-net with a sharpened frame edge. Percival and Whitehead (1926), Allen (1940), Macan and Dittmar (1955) developed related "shovel" designs. Macan chose the term "shovel" over "scoop" and rejected the literal translation "pile-scratcher" for the German name.

**Dredges.** For deep rivers, only a [[Dredge]] is practicable. Macan describes the Foerst and Steinmann designs and the modern drag-type sampler of Ussinger and Needham (1956), a frame 45 cm long with 17 projecting bars and a zip-fastened net.

**Ancillary operations.** Samples were floated in a calcium chloride solution of specific gravity 1.1 to separate animals from stones, though Ancylus and Trichoptera in stone cases stayed behind. Britt dipped stones in a 0.03–0.06% HCl solution in 2–5% alcohol, which caused clinging animals to release. Moon used an elutriation trough 1.3 m long for sorting, and a plankton-counting trough was used for microscopic counting.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study stream, Ford Wood Beck, drains a plateau, flows sluggishly over parts of it, then descends steeply over flat Silurian rock sheets separated by pools of boulders. It unites in a valley that is a rock basin filled with post-Ice Age material of clay, stones, and boulders. Near the head of the valley the bed is floored with relatively large stones, many about the size of a man's outstretched hand. Current has not been strong enough to carry the largest stones far, so the largest stones tend to decrease downstream, though wall-builders' rejects complicate the gradient. Large stones protect smaller stones, gravel, sand and clay beneath.

Macan identifies several biological factors that affect results. Free stone surface and the length of crevices where animals can press against stone probably matter more than areal extent. Animals are not randomly distributed. Baetis nymphs swim quickly and escape both the shovel and the net. Small Rhithrogena and Baetis pumilus seek shelter among small stones and gravel, while Baetis rhodani nymphs do not. Ecdyonurus torrentis lives on larger stones, and Gammarus pulex was washed into the net.

## Key Findings

**Sampler performance and design**
- The [[Shovel Sampler]] is 22.5 cm wide and is pushed 22.2 cm, sampling about 500 cm² or 1/20 m². It penetrates 5–8 cm into the bed.
- Macan's first design, which had a broad blade, lost animals that washed around the sides. The final design has a narrow cutting edge, a net closed at the end, and a 22.5 cm opening.
- Allen's sampler (30.5 cm wide) required two people to operate, which Macan considered undesirable because the second person disturbs the bed.
- Dittmar's *Bachgrundschaufel* (35 × 23 cm) combines shovel and sieve, with a detachable gauze cylinder.
- A fine net can sample only about 1/19 m² in 5 minutes, which is "an unexpectedly small area."

**Sampling error**
- Macan identifies three sources of variation between two shovel catches: edge error, unknown factors determining abundance, and non-random distribution.
- Morley, Rayner and Rainwater (1939) estimated up to 30% error from edge effects with a Surber sampler.
- In four replicate shovel catches (table 1; totals 362, 309, 245, 403), *Rhithrogena semicolorata* averaged 104 per sample with a standard deviation of 21. Mean ± 2 SD gives 62–146 per 1/20 m², or about 1240–2920 per m². Macan concludes that a greater number of samples is needed for productivity estimates.

**Method-specific catch biases**
- Percentages of each species caught in the net rather than the shovel: *Rhithrogena semicolorata* 61%, *Baetis rhodani* 89%, *Baetis pumilus* 95%, *Gammarus pulex* 87%.
- The shovel caught twice as many *Rhithrogena semicolorata* as the net (568 vs 284 in table 2), mainly 1–2 mm nymphs, whereas the net caught more of the larger specimens.
- *Baetis rhodani* was caught far more often in the net than the shovel at all sizes, probably because nymphs escape the shovel and congregate, which the net is more likely to hit.
- Coleoptera larvae (Dryopidae, Helodidae, Helmis, Hydraena) occurred predominantly in small stones and gravel, so the shovel is better suited to them.

**Mesh-size comparison (65 parallel collections)**
- The coarse net caught about 20% of the fine-net count of 1–2 mm *Rhithrogena*, and about 40% of the next size class.
- Specimens of about 8 mm appear to be the maximum length that can pass through the coarse mesh.
- Nearly every *Baetis* under 1 mm and most under 2 mm passed through the coarse net. Above 5 mm the coarse net caught significantly more *Baetis* than the fine net.
- *Baetis rhodani* produced more than 40,000 individuals under 2 mm in the fine net, compared with three-figure totals for other species. Macan admits the origin of these numbers "remains a mystery."

**Additional methods**
- Calcium chloride (specific gravity 1.1) floats most fauna but not Ancylus or stone-cased Trichoptera.
- Britt's dilute HCl-in-alcohol solution causes clinging animals to let go with little apparent harm.
- Macan's concluding recommendation: any study using a single method must know the habits of the important species, and an investigation sampling only stones of a specified size during 5-minute periods would likely be profitable.
