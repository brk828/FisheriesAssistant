# Parental Behaviour and Recognition of Young in Cichlasoma biocellatum

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study tests whether experienced [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]] parents imprint on the species identity of their young, a claim that appeared in general ethology texts. The author conducted foster-exchange experiments in which six pairs that had reared their own first spawn were given wrigglers of [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]]. Every exchanged brood was accepted, and the results contradict a strict imprinting mechanism. The author attributes earlier conflicting reports largely to the timing of foster young relative to the parents' brooding cycle. Because the two species have nearly identical developmental timetables, the success of the exchanges is explained by stage matching rather than by species recognition.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]] is a cichlid whose parents guard eggs, wrigglers and free-swimming schools. Parental care proceeds through three phases: fanning eggs, guarding wrigglers in pits, and herding the free-swimming school. Females fanned more persistently than males.

**Imprinting debate.** Earlier work gave conflicting accounts:
- [[Noble And Curtis 1939]] reported that cichlid parents recognise their free-swimming young by size and colour, and that experienced pairs reject other species' young. Baerends & Baerends-van Roon (1950) concluded that parents imprint on the species characteristics of their young.
- Greenberg (1961, 1963) exchanged broods between [[Hemichromis Bimaculatus]] and [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]]. Experienced pairs of both species accepted each other's young when the foster young's age matched the foster parents' brooding cycle.
- Collins & Braddock (1962) reported that experienced [[Tilapia Sparrmani]] and A. portalegrensis adopted young of the other species.
- [[Myrberg]] (1961) argued for adult imprinting: [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]] and [[Cichlasoma Nigrofasciatum]] reared alternate broods, then never again accepted a foreign species once they had raised their own. Myrberg also reported that imprinted pairs ate foreign young shortly before the free-swimming stage.

**Aims.** The study was designed to test the suggested imprinting mechanism in [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]] using [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]] as donors of foster young.

**Methods.** Thirty-five immature [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]] were obtained in August 1961 from Chicago sources and kept in groups of 5 or 6 until sexual maturity. Pairs were housed in 200 litre aquaria (45 x 45 x 90 cm) with sand, artificial plants, clay tiles and flower pots as nesting sites. A fibreglass partition with a closable doorway divided each tank, allowing the pair to be separated from its young. Room temperature was held at 80-85 °F and lighting was 10 hours daily. Six [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]] pairs raised their own first spawn for 18 to 29 days. Twenty [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]] pairs supplied donor wrigglers. All exchanges were made at the wriggler stage during the second and third spawnings, with foster wrigglers of similar age replacing the resident brood while the pair was held on the far side of the partition.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, so the ecological relevance is indirect. The two species showed closely matched developmental timetables under the same conditions (about 82 °F), which the author identified as the key variable in the transfer experiments:

| Developmental stage | [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]] | [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]] |
|---|---|---|
| Hatching time | 47-54 h (5 cases, avg 51 h) | 48-53 h (18 cases, avg 52 h) |
| Wrigglers become free-swimming | 84-108 h (8 cases, avg 96 h) | 91-101 h (10 cases, avg 98 h) |
| Spawning to free-swimming | avg 147 h (4 cases, range 146-150) | avg 148 h (8 cases, range 146-150) |

Within the schools, size and age asymmetries mattered. In one mixed school, the [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]] young swam a day earlier and grew faster, and after several weeks they were seen preying on the smaller [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]] by tearing their fins. The author notes this predation was not previously recorded at such an early age or in heterospecific groups.

## Key Findings

**1. Experienced parents accepted foster young.** All exchanges with [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]] wrigglers were accepted by the six [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]] pairs. Several pairs raised pure foster schools, and two raised mixed schools. These results do not fit a strict imprinting hypothesis.

**2. Mixed schools.**
- Pair 2: about 100 of its own wrigglers were left hidden under a clay tile, and the pair raised them with the foster wrigglers.
- Pair 4: the female was overly aggressive at its second spawning, so the pair was separated and the clutch was split. The male received [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]] wrigglers, and the female received [[Hemichromis Bimaculatus]] wrigglers, which soon disappeared. Incomplete removal of the male's own wrigglers left him with a mixed brood.
- Parents did not eliminate either species from mixed schools. Predation between the species was detected within three weeks.

**3. Foster school size.** Schools were usually larger than 500 young at transfer. Pair 1 received the combined spawn of two [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]] pairs and later had over 1,000. Pair 3 kept only 78 of several hundred young after two weeks. The author attributes this to the wrigglers becoming free-swimming a day ahead of the parental schedule.

**4. Stage matching drives success.** The author concludes that successful exchanges depend on matching the foster young's developmental stage to the host pair's brooding cycle. Pair 3's second exchange, using 3-day-old instead of 4-day-old wrigglers, went more smoothly. The author suggests the brooding schedule "stretches more readily than it contracts."

**5. Critique of Myrberg's evidence.** The author argues that several of Myrberg's cases were poorly timed. In one, the host pair was brooding eggs nearly ready to hatch, and in another the pair's own first spawn had not hatched, so no imprinting could have occurred. The author concludes the importance of matching foster young to parental timing was not fully understood.

**6. Stable reference points.** The author identifies three points from this and an earlier study:
- [[Hemichromis Bimaculatus]] and [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]] pairs can be induced to brood each other's young after prior experience with their own.
- [[Cichlasoma Biocellatum]] pairs with prior experience accept [[Aequidens Portalegrensis]] young.
- These relationships hold when foster young are developmentally matched to the parental cycle.

**7. Conclusion.** The reputed ability of cichlid parents to distinguish their own young may depend on the mismatch between the host pair's parental cycle and the developmental stage of the foreign young. The author suggests that a true imprinting mechanism is not supported by these data, and that method differences explain the disagreement between studies.
