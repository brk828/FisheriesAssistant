# Shorter Contributions: Ichthyology (Copeia 1991, No. 4)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #gadidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from the *Copeia* 1991 "Shorter Contributions: Ichthyology" section contains three components: (1) a bibliography of [[Red Hake]] and sibling [[White Hake]] life-history studies; (2) a full research note on the [[Maximum PH Tolerance]] of three endemic [[Klamath Basin]] fishes; and (3) the opening of a note on [[Gambusia Hybridization]] contacts in the Savannah and Chattahoochee drainages, which is truncated in the source. The central study shows that [[Shortnose Sucker]], an endangered species, loses equilibrium at a significantly lower pH than two co-occurring species, a possible factor in its decline in [[Upper Klamath Lake]].

## Background & Description

**Red hake and sibling hakes (reference list).** The bibliography cites roughly 15 works on [[Urophycis Chuss]] and [[Urophycis Tenuis]], a pair of sibling Atlantic hakes in the family Gadidae. Cited topics include:
- Spatial and temporal co-occurrence of the two species' life-history stages (Maurer et al., 1982).
- Seasonal distribution of the sibling hakes in New England (Musick, 1974), drawn from a 1969 Harvard dissertation on their comparative biology.
- Behavior, prey consumption, and growth of juvenile red hake (Luczkovich et al.).
- Responses of prejuvenile red hake to experimental thermoclines (Olla and co-authors, 1985).
- Association between post-juvenile red hake and sea scallops (Wicklund and Tsuneaux, 1971).
- Larval size and recruitment mechanisms in fishes, a conceptual framework (Mahon et al., 1988).

The author affiliations list Luczkovich (East Carolina University), Wilson (University of Washington), and Olla (NMFS/NOAA, Oregon State University). The hake material was accepted 15 October 1990.

**Klamath Basin fishes.** The [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*Chasmistes brevirostris*, Catostomidae) is endemic to the Klamath Basin of south-central Oregon and north-central California. It spawns in spring in the Sprague and Williamson rivers. Its abundance declined sharply over the previous decade, and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] listed it as endangered in 1988. The [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*) is a related endangered catostomid. The comparison species were the [[Klamath Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor bicolor*, Cyprinidae) and the [[Klamath Largescale Sucker]] (*Catostomus snyderi*, Catostomidae).

**Gambusia.** The note on [[Gambusia Affinis]] and [[Gambusia Holbrooki]] reports previously undescribed geographic contacts between the two species in the Savannah and Chattahoochee drainages. Its opening states that an electrophoretic survey of *G. holbrooki* from these rivers (Lydeard, unpublished) revealed several populations of interest. The source is cut off before the results.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Upper Klamath Lake water quality.** Human-caused stress has degraded water quality in Upper Klamath Lake over the past 20 years. Altered nutrient inputs and cycling have produced massive summer and fall blooms of blue-green algae (*Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*). During peak algal production, lake pH commonly reaches 9.5–10.5 and stays high for weeks. Diurnal fluctuations occur, but pH rarely falls below 9.0 in early morning. Mass mortalities of shortnose and Lost River suckers were observed during these peak periods. Other studies link high pH (above 9) to physiological stress and death in fishes.

**Experimental conditions.** Fish were held at 14–16 °C, a typical autumn temperature in Upper Klamath Lake, in [[Sprague River]] water for at least three weeks before testing. The Sprague River water is pH 8.05. The study notes that average mole percentages of the major dissolved solutes in the Sprague River and Upper Klamath Lake are similar.

**Comparative pH tolerance in other fishes.** The authors note that published pH limits are hard to compare because of differing methods:
- Atlantic salmon and brown trout alevins: upper incipient limiting pH of 9.0 (Bishai, 1960).
- [[Brook Trout]]: lethal maximum pH of 9.8 (Daye and Garside, 1976).
- [[Threespine Stickleback]]: avoids water more alkaline than pH 11.4 (Jones, 1948).
- [[Creek Chub]]: prefers pH 10 over less alkaline water (Peterson et al., 1989).

**Physiological effects of high pH.** Studies on [[Rainbow Trout]] and other species report the following effects of high pH:
- Reduced olfactory responsiveness to amino acids above pH 10 (Hara, 1976).
- Increased gill ventilation and "coughing" above pH 9.0 (Hargis, 1976).
- Elevated blood ammonia at pH 10 (Randall and Wright, 1989), which may cause death.
- Hypertrophy and excessive mucus secretion in gill, nare, and skin mucous cells at pH 9.0; epithelial necrosis in the esophagus at pH 9.8 (Daye and Garside, 1976).
- Gill lamellar edema in [[Black Bullhead]] in alkaline water (McKenna and Duerr, 1976).

## Key Findings

**Maximum pH tolerance study (Klamath Basin)**

| Species | n | Mean body mass | Mean maximum pH tolerance (± SD) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Klamath tui chub | 21 | 2 g | 10.75 ± 0.48 |
| Klamath largescale sucker | 12 | 1 g | 10.73 ± 0.41 |
| Shortnose sucker | 20 | 2 g | 9.55 ± 0.43 |

*Note: total n = 53 fish in the sample; the source reports 20 juvenile shortnose suckers from hatchery stock.*

- **Method:** Critical pH maxima were measured with an apparatus modeled on critical thermal maxima. Fish (1–3 g) were exposed to decreasing [H+] (pH rising at 0.025–0.030 units per minute) using NaOH-adjusted Sprague River water. The endpoint was the sustained loss of equilibrium. No fish were reused.
- **Species differences:** ANOVA showed significant differences in pH maxima among species (F = 45.31, df = 50, P < 0.0001). Shortnose sucker had a significantly lower mean pH maximum than both other species (Scheffé's test, P < 0.0001).
- **Body mass:** Mass differed among species (F = 10.91, df = 50, P = 0.0001). Klamath largescale sucker was significantly smaller than the other two species (Scheffé's test, P = 0.001). However, mass had no significant effect on pH maxima. Correlations within species were not significant, and an ANCOVA with mass as a covariate still showed significant species differences (P < 0.0001).

**Conclusions.** The authors conclude that shortnose sucker is more susceptible to elevated pH during high phytoplankton production in Upper Klamath Lake. Its lower pH tolerance may contribute to its declining abundance. The cause of the equilibrium loss was not determined. The authors call for further investigation of links between physiological stress and high-pH environments.

**Hake bibliography and Gambusia note.** The hake references document a long research program on juvenile life history, behavior, and habitat associations. The Gambusia note's conclusions are not included in the source.
