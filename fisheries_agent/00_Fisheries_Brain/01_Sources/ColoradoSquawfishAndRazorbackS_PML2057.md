# Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker Grow-Out Pond Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final report (November 1989, Colorado River Fishery Project, Grand Junction) evaluates [[Grow-Out Ponds]] as a rearing tool for two endangered fishes of the upper [[Colorado River Basin]], the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]]. The study ran from 1985 to 1989 in four gravel-pit ponds, which were stocked with hatchery-reared fish, monitored for growth, condition, diet, survival, disease and water quality, and then rotenoned to recover survivors. Squawfish grew rapidly in some ponds but survival was low or zero, and the report identifies forage availability, timing of stocking, parasites and possibly hydrogen sulfide as major constraints. Razorback suckers in [[Humphrey Pond]] grew quickly and survived at about 99%. The authors conclude that pond culture is useful but that squawfish survival remains unexplained.

## Background & Description

**Study rationale.** Declines of Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker in the upper basin were attributed largely to high mortality of early life stages. If that mortality cannot be reduced, stocking may be needed, and stocking experiments can also reveal wild growth and survival. Earlier work by [[Osmundson]] (1987) showed that squawfish grown in ponds could grow more than twice as fast as fish in rivers or hatcheries, but survival varied greatly and its causes were unclear.

**Species notes.**
- *Colorado Squawfish* (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is a large cyprinid that becomes piscivorous as it grows. Published size at maturity in the report ranges from about 400 mm TL (Seethaler 1979) to 465-507 mm TL (Tyus et al. 1987).
- *Razorback Sucker* (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a catostomid that feeds on plankton, detritus and filamentous algae in the study pond.

**Methods.** Fish were sampled by seining and [[Electrofishing]], and mark-recapture was used for one population estimate. Condition was calculated with Fulton's coefficient K = W × 10^5 / L^3. Stomachs were preserved in 10% buffered formalin and analyzed. Dissolved oxygen, temperature, conductivity and clarity were measured biweekly. Survival was estimated after final rotenone treatments in 1989. Fertilization used triple superphosphate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Forage and predation.** Fathead minnows (*Pimephales promelas*) were the intended squawfish forage base. Fish Chalet Pond diets were initially dominated by cladocerans, copepods and chironomid insects. Gape-limitation analysis (Table 2) showed that forage fish were largely unavailable to most squawfish in 1985 and 1987. Predatory fish were absent from three of four ponds after treatment. Carp were a problem at Fish Chalet because they were either too large to eat or present in unsuitable size classes.

**Asian tapeworm.** *[[Asian Tapeworm]]* (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) was introduced to West Pond via fathead minnows from T & F Pond. It infected squawfish at West and Humphrey ponds but was absent from Fish Chalet and 13th-Hole ponds. Infection rates reached 100% at Humphrey Pond by late July 1988 and mean adult worm density was 25 per fish in late October 1988.

**Water quality.** Dissolved oxygen fell in the lower depths of Fish Chalet Pond in July-August 1987. Hydrogen sulfide was detected in winter at Fish Chalet (up to 0.101 mg/L) and West (0.002-0.014 mg/L) ponds. These levels were compared with a 95-hr LC50 of 0.0477 mg/L for juvenile bluegill, so H2S toxicity was a possible contributor. Water clarity was low in Fish Chalet Pond, and fertilization at West Pond in 1987 produced an algal bloom with Secchi visibility of 0.9 m.

**Temperature and growth.** Squawfish growth begins above about 13°C. Black and Bulkley (1985) found maximum growth near 25°C. Fish Chalet temperatures exceeded 20°C from mid-May to mid-September.

## Key Findings

**Colorado Squawfish**

- **Fish Chalet Pond:** About 7,000 fish (28-61 mm) stocked on 28-31 August 1986. Condition fell from 0.74 to 0.57 by November 1986. Only 1.5% survived to 29 September 1987 (107 estimated, 95% CI 80-135). The last squawfish were captured on 15 June 1988. Rotenone treatment in September 1989 recovered none, so mortality was 100% over three years.
- **West Pond:** Stocking was on 28 August 1986, and at least 34.9% survived to June 1987 (school estimate 2,440 fish). Survival was 4.7% (330 fish) at the 1989 rotenone recovery. Asian tapeworm was common.
- **13th-Hole Pond:** Stocked on 20 May 1986 with 234 survivors of the Jackson shipment (about half had died in transit). Growth was fast (mean length 318 mm by late October 1987). Only five fish survived after December 1985 (2.1% of stocked fish after seven months), and a last capture occurred on 29 June 1988. Roundtail chub predation was a likely cause.
- **Humphrey Pond:** About 50,000 swim-up fry (6-8 mm) stocked on 11 June 1987. Mean length reached 317.2 mm by mid-October 1989. A total of 131 squawfish were recovered on 11 October 1989, for an overall survival of about 0.3%. Asian tapeworm infected 100% of fish by July 1988.
- **Reproduction:** Captive adult squawfish from the wild failed to spawn in 1985-1986. Stress and handling were the likely causes, and in 1987 four fish died at Hotchkiss NFH.
- **Sexual maturity:** Squawfish at West Pond matured early, at about 263-264 mm TL at age III, with 13.3% of fish showing signs of maturity. This is smaller and younger than previously reported and suggests plasticity in maturity.

**Razorback Sucker**

- **Humphrey Pond:** 430 fingerlings (44-76 mm, about 15 weeks old) stocked on 17 June 1987. Mean length was 54.8 mm and weight 1.4 g. By 12 October 1989, mean length was 462 mm and mean weight 1,088 g. Largest fish: 505 mm, 1,400 g.
- **Survival:** Overall survival was about 99.3% (355 recovered plus 62 removed earlier, out of 430 stocked). Only squawfish and green sunfish were present, and both were too small to prey on the suckers.
- **Maturity:** Of 34 razorbacks dissected in fall 1989, all had developing gonads (65% male, 35% female), so all were capable of spawning at age III.
- **Diet:** Razorbacks fed on plankton, detritus and filamentous algae in the fertilized pond, and growth was strongly linked to this productivity.

**Recommendations (summary)**

1. Conduct artificial spawning at Dexter NFH or a similar facility. Leave wild adult squawfish undisturbed for about a year before spawning attempts.
2. Eliminate all predatory fish before stocking squawfish.
3. Screen forage fish for parasites, especially Asian tapeworm, before introduction to ponds.
4. Allow an adequate forage base (fathead minnows) to establish before stocking, and time stocking to match prey of appropriate size. Carp were a poor forage species in these ponds.
5. Remove squawfish from ponds at 200-250 mm TL, before survival declines significantly.
6. Plan recovery methods before stocking. Draining ponds with pumps and seining was suggested as preferable to trammel nets, which killed 62 razorbacks through predation and stress.
7. Test survival vs. stocking time, and consider stocking razorbacks at about 300 mm TL after one growing season.

**Conclusions.** Pond culture has been useful for fast growth of both species. Causes of low squawfish survival, and how to set stocking densities without reducing growth, remain unresolved.
