# Protocol for the Handling and Transport of Wild Bonytail in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila

## Executive Summary

This protocol, recommended by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], directs field personnel to retain every wild [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) they encounter, alive, for placement in refugia. It sets out the required equipment, the field criteria for confirming a bonytail, handling and holding procedures during transport, notification steps, and the temporary and permanent holding facilities. The document is a procedural guidance note rather than a research study, so it reports no population or experimental data.

## Background & Description

The bonytail is a native fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The protocol is written for the upper basin, where wild individuals are rare and recovery depends on removing them from the river for captive holding. Field researchers may need to keep a captured fish under appropriate conditions for two or more days until [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) staff take possession. Researchers have full discretion to judge whether safe transport is feasible. If the risks are too great, the fish is returned to the river.

**Required equipment** (provided by the FWS and carried by field teams in likely reaches):
- One 100 to 150-quart cooler
- One or two 23 cubic foot oxygen cylinders (about 3 lb each) with regulator, rubber tubing, and carbon diffuser
- One live net
- One vernier caliper
- One identification key for morphometric characters (copy attached to the source)
- One container of anesthetic, either [[MS-222]] or [[Quinaldine]]

Cooperators obtain this equipment from the Vernal or Grand Junction field stations of the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] (CRFP).

**Field identification criteria** must be strictly applied. A fish qualifies as a bonytail only if it meets all of the following:
1. Total length of at least 300 mm.
2. Dorsal/anal fin-ray count of 10/10 or greater.
3. Caudal peduncle length divided by head length greater than 1.0 (after Minckley 1973).
4. Head length divided by caudal peduncle depth greater than 5.0 (after Minckley 1973).

Morphometric measurements follow [[Lagler, Bardach and Miller]] (1977, *Ichthyology*, p. 421). Fish are anesthetized before meristic counts or other measurements to reduce stress.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sensitive reaches.** Teams that intensively sample reaches with [[Humpback Chub]] populations, or where chubs were recently captured, should expect to encounter bonytail and must carry the equipment. The applicable reaches come from the UCRBCC Sensitive Reach document, which covers humpback chub captures within the last five years. The source lists these reaches by river mile, though some values are illegible in the scan:

- **Upper Colorado River:** Westwater Canyon (RM 111 to 125), Cataract Canyon, Moab Canyon (RM 72), and Black Rocks (RM 135 to 136)
- **Green River:** Gray Canyon (RM 146 to 171) and Whirlpool Canyon
- **Yampa River:** Yampa Canyon to Cross Mountain (RM 18 to 56)

The list also contains one entry at RM 342 whose location is not clear in the source.

**Reaches without recent records.** In reaches where humpback chub or bonytail have not been captured recently, or are not expected, teams are not required to carry the equipment at all times. If a bonytail is caught without the equipment, the person in charge decides whether recovery is feasible. If it is not, the fish's welfare is the priority. The fish is measured (length, weight, photograph), checked for injury, and returned alive to the capture site as soon as possible.

**Remote sites.** Where capture occurs in a remote area, the fish must not be left for later retrieval. Transport takes priority regardless of inconvenience.

**Holding and transport conditions.** The fish is placed as soon as possible into a cooler or container filled with fresh water, preferably from the capture area. Individual measurements are not taken at this stage, and handling is kept to an absolute minimum even after anesthesia. Key holding practices include:
- Aeration from the oxygen cylinder is used only when water temperature exceeds air temperature.
- Cooling, if needed to keep temperature stable or slightly depressed, can use crystal ice, ice or frozen solids in sealed leak-proof containers, cooled beverages in sealed containers, or river water from the area.
- If holding exceeds 12 hours, or is otherwise convenient, the container can be partly submerged in surrounding water.
- Tarred or knotted holding nets are prohibited because they are too abrasive.
- The fish is moved downstream to the nearest designated takeout as quickly as reasonably possible.
- The container stays closed except when water is added and the fish is inspected. Water is partially exchanged by pouring at intervals of one to several hours. Water replenishment is not recommended unless the fish shows respiratory stress, such as rapid ventilation or gaping at the surface.

**Records.** Capture-site data (date, time, location, and anecdotal notes) must be recorded and kept with the fish or sent to the final recipient as soon as practicable.

## Key Findings

- **Retention policy:** Every wild bonytail encountered should be kept alive for refugia, per the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] recommendation.
- **Holding period:** Fish may need two or more days of holding before FWS takes possession.
- **Discretion:** Researchers decide whether transport is feasible and must return the fish to the river if risks are too high.
- **Identification:** Positive identification requires all four criteria: at least 300 mm total length, a fin-ray count of 10/10 or greater, and two morphometric ratios (peduncle/head length above 1.0; head length/peduncle depth above 5.0).
- **Targeted equipment:** Teams sampling sensitive reaches (intensive electrofishing, entanglement nets, seines, or angling where humpback chub were recently captured) must carry the full equipment kit.
- **Notification:** Teams notify the appropriate [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] facility one week before each trip. Notice must include departure and takeout dates and points, trip length, and the person in charge. The Green River subbasin contact is Harold Tyus (Vernal, Utah). The Colorado River subbasin contact is Lynn Kaeding (Grand Junction, Colorado).
- **Transport logistics:** A 250-gallon fish distribution tank mounted in a pickup carries fish from takeout to the temporary facility. Vernal CRFP handles Green and Yampa river captures, and Grand Junction CRFP handles Colorado River captures.
- **Temporary holding:**
  - [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] (Vernal, Utah; Green River subbasin) uses earthen ponds isolated from the mainstem Green River, or circular tanks.
  - [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] (Hotchkiss, Colorado; Colorado River subbasin) uses an eight-foot circular fiberglass tank supplied with continuous groundwater.
- **Health management:** Staff must be ready to treat abrasions, fungal infections, and bacterial invasion of abraded areas. If [[Lernaea]] parasites are present, fish are anesthetized and parasites removed manually. Removal is recorded so that immature parasites not visible to the naked eye can be treated at the permanent facility.
- **Permanent holding:** Fish move to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (Dexter, New Mexico) by airplane or by fish distribution tank and truck. The transfer should occur within two weeks of capture, or sooner if possible. Fish are permanently marked on arrival.
