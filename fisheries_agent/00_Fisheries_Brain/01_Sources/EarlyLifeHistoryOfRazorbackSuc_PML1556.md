# Early Life History of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 final report, prepared for the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Lower Colorado Region) by Daniel R. Langhorst and Paul C. Marsh of the [[Center For Environmental Studies]] at Arizona State University, examines the early life history of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) larvae in [[Lake Mohave]] during 1985. The population is the only substantial remaining one in the [[Colorado River]] system and shows no evidence of adult recruitment since the 1950s. Larvae were abundant from February to mid-April but disappeared by late spring. The study tested four objectives: predation impacts, larval food habits relative to food availability, dispersal from spawning areas, and survival in predator-free habitat. Growth in lake water was not precluded, but predation by introduced fishes, especially [[Green Sunfish]], and possible down-reservoir transport are proposed as the main fates of larvae.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large catostomid fish endemic to the Colorado River system. It has declined in distribution and abundance across its native range, from Wyoming to Sonora, Mexico. Scattered individuals are still taken, but Lake Mohave holds the only substantial population. That population consists of large, old individuals. Adults spawn successfully and free-swimming larvae appear each winter, yet recruitment has not been detected. Causes of presumed larval mortality were unknown and were hypothesized to include predation, resource limitation, or physical stresses.

Study areas included [[Arizona Bay]], [[Hammerhead Cove]], and the Six Mile Area, with Arizona Bay and Hammerhead Cove as primary sites. An adjacent natural backwater, separated from the lake by a gravel berm, was used for isolated rearing. The backwater had maximum depths of 1.7 to 3.7 m and surface areas of 0.85 to 2.1 ha at lake elevations of 193.5 to 195.5 m. Its water temperatures paralleled the lake's but were slightly cooler in winter and warmer in spring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning:** Spawning began on 8 January 1985 in Arizona Bay and on 15 January in Hammerhead Cove. More than 1000 individuals were observed on days of optimum viewing. Wave heights above about 25 cm usually stopped spawning, which was a stronger determinant of activity than time of day.
- **Adult distribution:** Adults concentrated in Arizona Bay, where spawning occurred within about 1000 m² of shoreline. Most suckers were at depths under 2 m, over coarse gravel or rubble.
- **Habitat use:** Larvae were captured in shallow (<3 m) littoral zones of Hammerhead Cove, often near partially submerged vegetation. Larvae in the backwater shifted toward deeper water as they grew beyond about 12 mm TL.
- **Fish community:** [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Rainbow Trout]] were abundant in seine and trawl samples from spawning areas. The backwater originally held threadfin shad, carp, channel catfish, mosquitofish, largemouth bass, green sunfish, and bluegill; these were removed by ichthyocide in autumn 1984 before stocking.
- **Zooplankton:** Cladocerans (*Bosmina*, *Daphnia*), copepods and rotifers dominated the lake. Rotifers dominated the backwater, and total backwater densities were generally below 1000/m³, while lake densities ranged from under 100 to over 7,500/m³.
- **Hydrology:** On 4 April 1985 a rising lake breached the berm. Fishes including green sunfish, bluegill and threadfin shad then invaded the backwater. The backwater stayed connected to the lake until 5 May.

## Key Findings

- **Larval abundance and size:** Larvae in Lake Mohave averaged 10.6 mm TL from February through April, with no increase in mean or maximum length. The largest captured measured 12.2 mm. Most were collected from shoreline areas; only one was taken from open water.
- **Growth in protected habitat:** Larvae reared in the predator-free backwater reached over 16 mm by 28 April, and the largest measured 20.3 mm TL. Caged larvae in Lake Mohave survived and reached up to 34 mm TL. The authors conclude growth is not precluded by physical or chemical constraints in the lake.
- **Food habits:** Larvae under 12 mm fed mainly on zooplankton, and most stomachs were empty. Larger larvae (>12 mm) had a more diverse diet, including chironomid larvae. All larvae selected *Daphnia* averaging 0.66 to 0.96 mm, about half the average size available in the plankton (1.42 to 1.60 mm).
- **Predation by green sunfish:** Predation on larvae was confirmed only in [[Green Sunfish]]. In Hammerhead Cove, 2.7% of 37 examined sunfish contained larvae, averaging 2.0 larvae per predator. In the 1 April breach, five green sunfish were observed eating larvae, and sunfish that had consumed larvae averaged 3.9 per fish. Experimentally, sunfish consumed 10 larvae within 0.25 hours, and larvae were undetectable after 8 hours of digestion.
- **Disappearance:** Larvae reared in the backwater disappeared within about 4 weeks after the lake invaded, and adults could no longer be collected by 5 May. The authors attribute the decline at least partly to predation. Surveys in October 1986 found only introduced species in the backwater.
- **Dispersal:** The 1.0 mm mesh [[Larval Trawl]] caught only one larva in 22.0 net-hours, despite an experimental catch rate of about 5 larvae per minute in the backwater. The authors conclude larvae were either unavailable offshore or lay deeper than 15 m or near bottom. A hypothesis of benthic offshore movement and down-reservoir transport is proposed. Calculated current velocities of 1.0 to 25 cm/s are considered plausible.
- **Recruitment history:** More than 2000 suckers were taken in Lake Mohave over the past decade, but none smaller than about 400 mm TL. The last juveniles recorded were 6,100 specimens (12 to 42 mm) seined in 1950 before the lake filled. Small suckers have also been collected in canals below Davis Dam.
- **Recommendations:** Future work should sample the Colorado River below Davis Dam for dispersing larvae, measure down-reservoir currents, repeat digestion experiments with threadfin shad and rainbow trout, monitor the backwater in 1986, and examine mouth and anal development during the switch to active feeding.
