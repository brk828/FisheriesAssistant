# Biology and Population Dynamics of Corbicula manilensis in the Delta-Mendota Canal

#malacology #freshwater-ecology #corbiculidae

## Executive Summary

This doctoral dissertation in zoology (University of California, Davis) examines the population biology of the [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula manilensis*) in the concrete-lined [[Delta-Mendota Canal]] (DMC) of California's San Joaquin Valley. The clams occur there at densities well above those reported elsewhere. Fieldwork ran from January 1973 to November 1974 and focused on distribution, reproduction, recruitment, and growing seasons. The study identifies two primary habitats, the side-lining incrustations and the sediment bars, and links clam population success mainly to water temperature and current. It also reports biannual reproductive peaks that produce two size classes per year, and growth that is somewhat to markedly slower than in other populations. The author attributes the very high densities (over 100,000/m²) to favorable hydraulic conditions that promote accumulation rather than exceptional productivity.

## Background & Description

*Corbicula manilensis* is a small freshwater bivalve in the family Corbiculidae, originally described by Philippi and placed in the family by Prime (1864). Its taxonomy is reviewed in a long series of works on the genus, including [[Prashad Revisions of Corbicula]] (1924 to 1929) and [[Dall Classification of Cyrenacea]] (1903). The reference list shows that the species was already a recognized nuisance organism by the 1950s and 1960s, fouling water-supply and industrial systems. Major works include [[Ingram Asiatic Clams as Pests]] (1959), [[Sinclair and Isom Tennessee Studies]] (1963), and [[Mackie Corbicula Story Series]] (1969 to 1973), a series of progress reports on the species' spread.

The dissertation is organized as a standard research thesis: a summary, a literature cited list of roughly 150 entries, and attached publishing and library materials (see Key Findings for what is substantive).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study site:** The [[Delta-Mendota Canal]] is a concrete-lined water conveyance channel in California's Central Valley. Its clam populations are unusually dense, and the study treats the canal as a model system.
- **Habitat 1, side-lining incrustations:** Thin biological crusts formed by the tube-building amphipod *Corophium spinicorne* and the hydrozoan *Cordylophora lacustris* on the canal walls. These crusts act as a facultative nursery area for young clams.
- **Habitat 2, sediment bars:** Discrete sediment deposits on the canal floor, where the adult clam populations are concentrated.
- **Key environmental drivers:** [[Water Temperature]] and [[Current Velocity]] are identified as the major factors affecting the clams.
- **Related literature:** The sediment and incrustation work draws on [[Bio-geochemical Incrustations]] and [[Sedimentation in the Delta-Mendota Canal]] studies by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, which also cover water quality (see [[Delta-Mendota Canal Water Quality Investigations]]) and organic life in the canal.
- **Related species:** Comparative studies include other freshwater and brackish bivalves such as [[Corbicula japonica]], [[Dreissena polymorpha]], and [[Corbicula fluminea]]. Predators and parasites noted in the literature include [[Chaetogaster limnaei]] (an oligochaete living in the mantle cavity) and the blue catfish (see [[Blue Catfish Introduction]]).

## Key Findings

1. **Study scope and timing:** Populations were monitored from January 1973 to November 1974, with the aim of determining distribution, reproduction, recruitment, and growing seasons.
2. **Two primary habitats:** Clams occupy (1) thin biological incrustations on the side-lining, formed by *Corophium spinicorne* and *Cordylophora lacustris*, and (2) discrete sediment bars on the canal floor.
3. **Nursery function:** The side-lining incrustations serve as a facultative nursery area that supplies young clams to the sediment bars.
4. **Controlling factors:** Water temperature and current are the major environmental factors affecting clam populations.
5. **Reproduction and recruitment:** Biannual reproductive peaks result in the recruitment of two distinct size classes each year.
6. **Growth:** Growth in the DMC ranges from slightly to markedly less than that reported for other populations.
7. **Population density:** Densities exceeded 100,000 per m² in places. The author concludes these reflect favorable hydraulic conditions and accumulation of clams, not exceptional population productivity.
8. **Literature base:** The literature cited (about 150 entries, 1864 to 1976) covers taxonomy, invasion history, control methods, growth-estimation techniques, and related bivalve biology. Key cited sources include [[Aldridge and McMahon Population Growth]] (1976) on the life cycle of *C. manilensis* and [[Gardner et al. Altamaha River Invasion]] (1976) on its spread in Georgia.
