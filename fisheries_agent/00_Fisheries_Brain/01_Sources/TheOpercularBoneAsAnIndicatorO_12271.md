# Age and Growth of the Carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) from the Opercular Bone

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 study, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, tests whether the [[Opercular Bone Method]] can estimate the age and growth of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) in northern Utah. It was part of a non-game fish investigation begun in 1948 by the Department of Wildlife Management at Utah State Agricultural College. Ages and growth were derived from the opercular bones of 330 carp collected at [[Ogden Bay Bird Refuge]] in 1950–51, out of 401 carp used to establish the bone–length relationship. The author concludes that the opercular method is superior to [[Scale Ageing]] for this species, while stating that its validity has not been established conclusively.

## Background & Description

- **Study context:** Part of a statewide survey of non-game fish. Sigler (1949) estimated at least one million acres of potential carp range in Utah, largely created by artificial impoundments.
- **Why not scales:** Scales from Utah carp were unsuitable for age determination. Only 20 percent could be aged with confidence, due to cloudy centres, false annuli, and crowded annuli after the fourth year.
- **Chosen structure:** The opercular bone, used by [[Le Cren (1947)]] on perch (*Perca fluviatilis*), was selected because it is a bony structure known to work for age determination in fish (Menon, 1950).
- **Annuli:** Distinct, narrow bands parallel to the posterior margin. Each annulus has a dark posterior line and a translucent anterior band. The annuli resemble those on perch opercula.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Ogden Bay Bird Refuge is a shallow, marshy site. Construction in 1938 converted a barren salt flat into a heavily vegetated marsh.
- **Eutrophication:** The habitat has been eutrophied since 1938 through silt and municipal sewage deposited by the Weber and Ogden rivers, which are impounded there.
- **Growth implications:** The author attributes a progressive decline in growth rate at each year of life across successive age groups to gradual environmental change, as the habitat shifted over several years.
- **Water temperature:** Early spring growth in known-age carp was thought to be linked to warm springs in the pond where they were recovered.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**
- 401 carp measured to establish the opercular radius–length relationship; 330 opercular bones used for age and growth determinations.
- Carp were collected with seines: a 10-foot minnow seine for small fish and a 100-foot seine (¾-inch bar mesh) for large fish.
- Length conversions: standard length to total length ×1.2512; to fork length ×1.0862; to total length in inches ×0.04934.
- Opercular assemblies were removed, boiled for about one minute to clean them, and dried. Measurements were taken from the posterior margin of the fulcrum to the posterior-most point of each annulus, using a compass-pen and a custom ruler with a 7 mm upturn. Readings were recorded to the nearest half millimetre.

**Annulus interpretation**
- Transmitted skylight gave the best annulus visibility. Polarised light and infrared film gave no improvement.
- The first annulus was hard to locate on about half of the samples. The team set an arbitrary 200 mm threshold to decide whether the first visible annulus formed at the end of year one or year two.
- Only 20 percent of calculated lengths for the first and second years fell between 176 and 225 mm, so misclassification was judged to be small.

**Back-calculation**
- Relationship between opercular radius (R) and standard length (L) is curvilinear: L = 6.854 × R^1.139 (Figure 3, 401 carp, 1950–51).
- Growth was calculated with a logarithmic nomograph after Hile (1950).

**Validation**
- Scale vs. opercular agreement: 66% of readable scales matched the opercular age, 25% were within one year (91% within one year total), and 9% differed by two or more years. Of the mismatches, 73% gave lower ages from scales.
- Right and left opercula from each fish gave the same age and closely matching back-calculated growth.
- Length-frequency modes for carp under 500 mm showed three groups. These matched the opercular ages and the modes of pooled calculated lengths for ages I–III.
- Known-age carp (recovered at the end of year two, 27 March 1951) showed one annulus. All were above 275 mm standard length at capture.
- The author notes that the data came from a single year, so dominant year-class tracing was not possible.

**Growth anomaly**
- Calculated first-year length rose with age class, while later-year lengths fell with each succeeding age class. Two causes were proposed: a sampling bias (inner marks became invisible with age, so first-year marks were measured further from the fulcrum and more fast-growing fish were included), and a real decline in growth linked to habitat change at the refuge.

**Conclusions**
- Annuli are clearer on opercula, and single interpretations are usually possible.
- Ages are readily determined past the fourth year from opercula.
- Opercula are quicker to prepare: they only need cleaning and drying, while scales need temporary wet mounts.
- Opercular bone–length variation is lower than that of the scale–length ratio.
- No optical equipment is needed to back-calculate growth from opercula.
- The method's validity is not established conclusively, though the evidence is considered significant.
