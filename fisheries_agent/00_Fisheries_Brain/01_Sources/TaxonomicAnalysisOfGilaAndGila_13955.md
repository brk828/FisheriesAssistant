# Taxonomic Analysis of Gila and Gila x Rainbow Trout in Southwestern New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 M.S. thesis by Robert Earl David (New Mexico State University, Fishery Science) assesses the taxonomic purity of [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*) populations in the [[Gila National Forest]] and identifies streams containing [[Gila x Rainbow Trout Hybrids]]. Trout from 19 streams were sampled by electrofishing and compared on 23 taximetric characters, of which 10 were selected as diagnostic. Pure Gila trout were identified in Main Diamond, South Diamond, McKenna, Iron, and Spruce Creeks. Spruce Creek, previously thought to be an introduced population, is proposed as a possibly native San Francisco drainage population, perhaps an intergrade with [[Arizona Trout]], and may merit subspecific recognition. Hybrid populations were found in numerous Gila and San Francisco tributaries, and pure rainbow trout were identified in lower Sheep Corral and Turkey Creeks. The author recommends restoration stocking sources be matched to watershed of origin to preserve population individuality.

## Background & Description

The Gila trout is one of two native salmonids of New Mexico. At the time of study its range was restricted to headwaters of the Gila and San Francisco Rivers in New Mexico, plus an Arizona population (Gap Creek, a Verde River tributary) established by a 1974 transplant from Main Diamond Creek. The species was listed as endangered by the U.S. Department of the Interior (1973) and the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish (1975), and an Interim Gila Trout Recovery Team formed in May 1974.

The original description (Miller 1950) contained information the author considered no longer valid, and Arizona populations had previously been misidentified as Gila trout before *Salmo apache* was described (Miller 1972). The most popular origin hypothesis links the species to late Pliocene or early Pleistocene dispersal of Pacific salmonids up the Gila River, followed by isolation and inbreeding; no adequate fossil record exists.

Study objectives were to determine the purity of known and newly discovered Gila trout populations and to identify streams with hybrids, to support restoration and management.

**Methods (summary):** Fish were electrofished, fixed in 10% formalin with Ionol, and stored in 40% isopropyl alcohol. Measurements were standardized to thousandths of standard length. Basibranchial teeth and gill rakers were counted after alizarin red staining; vertebrae were counted from X-rays. Spot density was measured in a 5 x 10 mm acetate window (spots per cm²). Comparisons used Student's t-test with pooled variances against the Main Diamond Creek topotype series, plus means with 95% confidence intervals. Regression confirmed proportional growth of characters (required r = 0.735 for Gila trout, 0.708 for rainbow trout at the 99.5% level).

**Reference populations:** Main Diamond Creek (collected November 1, 1974; 11 to 12 fish) was accepted as pure Gila trout. Turkey Creek at Corral Canyon (12 fish) was accepted as pure rainbow trout, though two specimens showed fin-abrasion scars suggesting hatchery origin.

**Diagnostic characters (10 of 23):** head length, upper jaw length, predorsal length, adipose fin length, caudal peduncle length, vertebrae count, scales above the lateral line, lateral series scale count, and pyloric caeca count. Spot density was analyzed separately.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Isolation:** South Diamond Creek is isolated by dry stream beds, likely for centuries. Its spot density (31.0 per cm² versus 22.2 in Main Diamond Creek) is attributed to this isolation.
- **Barriers:** McKenna Creek has a 2.4 m waterfall and a tree and boulder jam about 1.2 km upstream. Above the jam, spot density was 22.1 per cm² versus 11.4 below. The difference is attributed to light-coloured rhyolite tuff periodically deposited below the second barrier, which creates a pale background.
- **Iron Creek:** Gila trout are protected from rainbow hybridization by intermittent gravel streambed flow, though brown trout (*Salmo trutta*) are present and their extent is unknown.
- **Spruce Creek:** Brown trout occur below a 2 m waterfall. Series of barriers 10 to 15 m high block movement from Dry Creek, even in floods.
- **Black Canyon Creek:** No effective barrier exists against further rainbow introductions.
- **Sheep Corral Creek:** A 3 m rock barrier separates the upper fishless section (stocked with about 75 Main Diamond fish in 1972; 15 found in 1975) from lower rainbow-dominated water.
- **Study area:** The Gila National Forest covers 3.3 million acres and includes the Gila Wilderness (the world's first, designated 1924). Elevations range from 4,500 ft to nearly 11,000 ft. Six of Merriam's seven Life Zones are represented. Three rivers drain the area: the San Francisco and Gila (west of the Continental Divide) and the Mimbres (into a closed eastern basin).

## Key Findings

**Pure Gila trout populations:** Main Diamond, South Diamond, McKenna, Iron (upper), and Spruce Creeks. McKnight and upper Sheep Corral Creeks hold introduced Gila trout but were not analyzed.

**Spruce Creek:** Four of 12 specimens had basibranchial teeth, a trait absent from Main Diamond Creek. Its mean predorsal length (523.3) and vertebrae count (59.3) fall close to [[Arizona Trout]] paratype values (511 and 59.5). Spot density averaged 11.9 per cm², and spots carried light halos similar to those described for Arizona trout. Spruce Creek is presented as a possible native San Francisco drainage population, an intergrade of Gila and Arizona trout, and a candidate for subspecific status. Its pyloric caeca mean was 48.2, though caeca morphology differed from rainbow trout.

**Hybrid populations:** Black Canyon, Sycamore, White, Langstroth, Trail Canyon, upper Mogollon, upper Turkey, and West Fork of Mogollon Creeks (Gila x rainbow). Whitewater, Lipsey, and Dry Creeks show rainbow hybridization with cutthroat or Gila influence. Lipsey Creek had large, cutthroat-like spots and significant differences in all characters. Dry Creek fish were identified as cutthroat x rainbow hybrids, and their lateral series count (143.5) fell between rainbow (134.1) and cutthroat (150–200) values.

**Pure rainbow trout:** Turkey Creek at Corral Canyon and lower Sheep Corral Creek.

**Character reliability:** Caudal peduncle length was unreliable because it did not differ significantly between Gila and several hybrid populations. Adipose fin length, vertebrae counts, scalation, and pyloric caeca counts were more reliable indicators. Overlap in 95% confidence intervals between the Main Diamond and Turkey Creek reference populations reduces the value of some means.

**Black Canyon Creek:** Phenotypes are similar to Main Diamond Creek, so the author recommends avoiding future rainbow stocking to retain them.

**Restoration recommendations:**
- Restore tributaries of the upper San Francisco River with Spruce Creek trout only.
- Use Main Diamond or South Diamond Creek trout for East Fork Gila River streams.
- Use Iron Creek trout for the Middle Fork and McKenna Creek trout for the West Fork.
- Restore from within the same watershed to avoid mixing distinct populations.

**Documented change over time:** Langstroth and White Creek hybrid populations showed no significant change in pyloric caeca or vertebrae means between 1970 (Behnke) and 1975 collections.
