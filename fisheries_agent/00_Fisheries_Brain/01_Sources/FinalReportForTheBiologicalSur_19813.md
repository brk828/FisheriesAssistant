# Biological Survey of the George Whittell Wildlife Preserve, 1979–1981: Amphibians, Reptiles, Birds and Vegetation

#herpetology #ornithology #botany

## Executive Summary

This final report (1981), edited by Terry B. Johnson for the Trust for the George Whittell Wildlife Preserve, closes a three-year biological survey of the [[George Whittell Wildlife Preserve]] in southeastern Arizona. The Preserve covers 26,104 acres (6,029 deeded, 20,075 leased) on the north slopes of the Galiuro Mountains and lies within the [[Aravaipa Creek]] watershed. The report covers amphibians and reptiles, birds, and vegetation. The mammal study was still ongoing (to 1983) and its results are not included here. The document contains no fish survey data. It mentions the Preserve's "aquatic features and native fish" only in passing, as the basis for its overall significance.

## Background & Description

The survey was a companion to hydrological studies of the Aravaipa watershed and was supported by the Trust, the Safford District of the [[Bureau of Land Management]], and the [[Arizona Natural Heritage Program]]. Fieldwork totaled 148 field man-days over three years, with most observations made in summer. Methods included:

- Seasonally repeated strip-transect censuses (≥700 m) in each biotic community, with strip widths of 2 m (inconspicuous species) to 5 m (conspicuous species).
- Spot sampling of microhabitats such as rock piles, brush piles and edificarian (human-built) structures.
- Road censuses on access roads at both ends of Aravaipa Canyon.

Nomenclature follows Collins et al. (1978, 1979). The report uses a five-category relative abundance scale (Abundant, Very Common, Common, Uncommon, Rare), with different interval values for amphibians, lizards and snakes. Species were classed as either "Confirmed Resident" or "Hypothetically Occurring," based on in-hand specimens, photographs or knowledgeable sight records.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Setting and habitat.** Elevation ranges from about 2200 ft in the West End lowlands to 6254 ft on Little Table Mountain. Annual rainfall is 14–20 inches. Permanent or semi-permanent water is comparatively abundant for southern Arizona. It comes from perennial flow in [[Aravaipa Creek]], side canyons, [[Stock Tanks]] and springs.

**Biotic communities.** Six major communities hold 24 vegetation associations:

1. [[Sonoran Desertscrub]] (~2200–3500 ft, West End)
2. [[Desert Grassland]]/Semi-desert Scrubland (~3000–4700 ft, East End and Uplands)
3. [[Chaparral]] (~3500–6000 ft)
4. Evergreen Woodland (~4400–6150 ft, Uplands)
5. [[Riparian Forest]] (~2500–3900 ft, along drainages)
6. Second Growth (~3050–3500 ft, East End of Aravaipa Creek)

**Herpetofauna and water.** Amphibians and several reptiles depend on surface water. The [[Leopard Frog]] was the most abundant and widespread amphibian and was locally Common to Abundant at stock tanks such as Willow, Dagger and Oak Spring. The [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] was the most common turtle, restricted to streams and tanks. The [[Blackneck Garter Snake]] was the most frequently observed snake, and was tied to permanent water and Leopard Frogs.

**Human influence.** Channelization, lowered water tables and loss of breeding sites are the main concerns. Lizards and snakes have benefited from roads, fences, corrals and wooden structures. Collecting, road mortality and shooting affect snakes and turtles. The report also notes invasion by [[Red Brome]], which has likely reduced whiptail and horned lizard numbers.

## Key Findings

### Amphibians and reptiles
- **Totals:** 8 amphibians and 46 reptiles are confirmed residents (54 in total). Another 14 species (4 amphibians, 10 reptiles) are hypothetical, for 68 species overall. The checklist includes 8 amphibians (all anurans), 3 turtles, 20 lizards and 23 snakes.
- **Amphibians:** [[Leopard Frog]] populations declined after severe floods and freezes in the late 1970s and were extirpated from Buzan Canyon in 1981. Recolonization from Aravaipa Creek is expected. The report flags [[Southwestern Woodhouse's Toad]] as the species most likely to be extirpated. It is now confined to the West End, where [[Great Plains Toad]] has replaced it downstream, and hybrid forms have been detected.
- **Turtles:** The Desert Box Turtle and [[Desert Tortoise]] are rarely seen and their populations are believed small. Both are often collected by visitors.
- **Lizards:** Of 20 species, the Tree Lizard is likely the most abundant. The [[Arizona Night Lizard]] was a range extension record for the Aravaipa watershed. [[Gila Monster]] is the only poisonous lizard in the United States. It is the only Aravaipa herpetofauna species fully protected and listed by the Arizona Game and Fish Commission.
- **Snakes:** Of 23 species, six are venomous and dangerous to humans. These are the Arizona Coral Snake, Western Diamondback, Blacktail, Mojave, Tiger and Arizona Black rattlesnakes. Three of the top five most frequently observed snakes were rattlesnakes. The Blackneck Garter Snake was observed 63 times, the most of any snake. The Tiger Rattlesnake and Pima Leafnose Snake were range extensions.
- **Threats:** Rattlesnakes are being killed by people, with four large individuals found decapitated in one 1981 evening. Population changes in Blacktail Rattlesnakes at the East End were attributed to one individual's killing campaign, though the report notes this may be coincidental.
- **Conservation:** No federally listed amphibian or reptile species occurs in the watershed. The report argues the Preserve's significance lies more in its aquatic features and native fish than in any single herpetofauna element.

### Birds
- **Richness:** 216 species recorded on the Preserve and 19 more in the adjacent watershed. The annotated checklist covers 216 species plus 19 watershed species, 2 probable non-occurrences and 3 unsuccessful introductions. The summary states a total of 238 birds.
- **Status:** 55 permanent residents, 41 summer residents, 33 winter residents and 87 migrants or visitors. Nest records exist for 72 species, with 22 more probable breeders.
- **Riparian Forest:** The most diverse habitat in summer. Common summer breeders include Bell's Vireo, Yellow Warbler, Yellow-breasted Chat and Violet-green Swallow. Winter populations varied dramatically (White-crowned Sparrow counts rose from 1 in 1979–80 to 586 in 1980–81).
- **Raptors:** [[Black Hawk]] is an uncommon but conspicuous Riparian Forest breeder. Between 9 and 12 pairs nested along Aravaipa Creek from the Preserve's east boundary to below Brandenburg Wash, according to Schnell's studies from 1976 to 1980.
- **Aquatic birds:** Aravaipa Creek supports few aquatic birds. Stock tanks support many more. [[Woodrows Tank]] is the most productive, often with 50 or more ducks in spring, fall and winter. The Ring-necked Duck is the most numerous and frequently sighted duck on the Preserve.
- **Minor habitats:** Big Table Mountain's juniper savannah supports Montezuma Quail, which is essentially restricted to that habitat on the Preserve. Oak Spring Cabin area is notable for breeding Bell's Vireo and Black-throated Gray Warbler together.

### Vegetation and flora
- The vegetation study established permanent sampling plots and produced a vegetation map of the Preserve's major communities.
- The annotated flora checklist covers plant families from Acanthaceae to Umbelliferae, with elevation ranges and local abundance for each species. Notable records include a new Graham County record for Bird-beak (*Cordylanthus laxiflorus*) and Saguaro (*Carnegiea gigantea*) abundance on south-facing slopes below 3500 ft.
- Riparian species such as Fremont Cottonwood, Goodding Willow and seepwillow are abundant along streams.

### Recommendations and implications
- Management decisions should follow the Preserve's species-level patterns. The report recommends no changes to Second Growth succession for the amphibians' sake.
- Future work should target the Southwestern Woodhouse's Toad, the hypothetical species list and the hybrid toad question.
- The report emphasizes that the survey list is incomplete and asks for distribution and population data from any observer.
