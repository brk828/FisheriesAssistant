# Results of Fisheries Investigations at Twin Lakes, Colorado, 1973–76

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This joint report from the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] documents the preoperational fishery of Twin Lakes, Colorado, before the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]] began operation. The study covers 1973–76, with most field work in 1974–75. It addresses three components: the sport fishery and angler use ([[Creel Census]]), the population distribution and catch of [[Lake Trout]] (by [[Gill Netting]]), and the abundance of the forage crustacean [[Opossum Shrimp]] (by [[Trawl Sampling]] and [[Underwater Photography]]).

Angler effort exceeded 100,000 hours per year, with harvests of nearly 2,000 lake trout and 50,000 rainbow trout annually. The authors judged natural lake trout reproduction adequate to sustain the present fishery. The main risks identified from the powerplant were fish mortality in turbines, increased outlet losses, gas supersaturation, and turbidity.

## Background & Description

Twin Lakes lie about 24 km (15 mi) south of Leadville, Colorado, on Lake Creek in the Arkansas River drainage. The lakes are of glacial origin, formed by two terminal moraines. The lower lake has been used for irrigation storage since an earthfill dam was built in 1901. Combined maximum area is about 1,000 ha (2,470 acres), with a maximum volume of 1.60 × 10⁸ m³ (129,637 acre-ft). Physical and chemical limnology is described in detail by Sartoris (see Table 1 in the source).

The lakes are part of the [[Fryingpan-Arkansas Project]], a Bureau of Reclamation water development system. The pumped-storage powerplant will lift water from Twin Lakes to the Mt. Elbert forebay during off-peak hours and generate power at peak demand. Planned changes include a new dam that enlarges the lakes, an increase in annual water-level fluctuation from about 6 m (20 ft) to about 10 m (30 ft), and daily fluctuations that may exceed 0.3 m (1 ft).

**Fish community history:** Early records (late 1800s to about 1900) list landlocked salmon, greenback and yellowfin cutthroat trout, McCloud River rainbow trout, brook trout, and lake trout. Only brook trout and lake trout persist. Rainbow trout, [[Kokanee Salmon]], cutthroat trout, Bonneville cisco, and American smelt have been planted in recent years. Lake trout are self-reproducing, and opossum shrimp were introduced in 1957 and now form a major part of the lake trout diet. Management over the last 15–20 years has focused on rainbow trout, lake trout, and kokanee salmon.

**Regulations:** The lake trout minimum size limit was 380 mm (15 in) from 1961, raised to 508 mm (20 in) in 1972, and returned to 380 mm in 1974.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking:** Catchable rainbow trout plants averaged nearly 165,000 per year from 1965 to 1973. Plants were reduced by about 50 percent after 1974. The report found that plants made before mid-May had too low a survival rate to be practical. Plants of 75,000–100,000 fish at 2-week intervals from mid-May through August appeared to return to the creel at an acceptable rate. Marked lake trout fingerlings (15,000–35,000 per year since 1971) had not reached legal size, and the authors doubted they would contribute to the fishery. Possible causes were predation or intolerance to water quality.

**Limnology:** The upper lake (263 ha, maximum depth 28 m) and lower lake (736 ha, maximum depth 27 m) differ in productivity. [[LaBounty and Sartoris]] reported much greater standing crops in the lower lake in 1974–75. In winter 1974–75, anaerobic conditions in the upper lake recycled heavy metals from bottom mud. The spring overturn carried toxic substances through the upper lake and sharply reduced aquatic invertebrates. Seasonal minimum dissolved oxygen was 3.6 mg/L (upper) and 1.7 mg/L (lower).

**Predicted powerplant impacts (from the report's literature review):**
- **Turbine mortality:** Fishery workers at other pumped-storage plants reported 55–65 percent mortality for fish passing through pump-turbines during pumping, and about 40 percent during generation. Only trashracks with 150 mm (6 in) openings were planned at the Mt. Elbert intake. Gill-net data showed lake trout movement confined to early evening and just before dawn, while the planned pumping phase runs 1000–0500 hours.
- **Outlet losses:** Trout escaping the Twin Lakes Dam outlet are often harvested in Lake Creek below the dam. Over 100 legal lake trout were taken there in May–June 1974. Mitigation includes a screened diversion to a stilling basin and stream improvement of Lake Creek.
- **Opossum shrimp:** Gregg found shrimp move from the bottom into upper water layers in late evening, coinciding with the pumping cycle. Water velocities above 0.15 m/s (0.5 ft/s) entering the tailrace could transport shrimp. Severe shrimp losses could affect lake trout growth and survival.
- **Gas supersaturation:** Air introduced into turbines could supersaturate afterbay water with nitrogen. Ebel and Raymond reported 40–95 percent juvenile salmon mortality below powerplants on the Snake and Columbia rivers.
- **Turbidity and temperature:** Bureau models predicted little temperature change. Resuspension of glacial flour sediment could occur under low lake levels and isothermal conditions. Reduced light could lower primary productivity, and sedimentation could affect lake trout natural reproduction.

## Key Findings

**Angler use and harvest (Creel Census):**
- Summer 1973: 2,073 anglers fished 4,042 hours, catching 1,193 rainbow trout and 11 legal lake trout.
- Summer 1974: 2,590 anglers fished 8,876 hours, catching 2,251 rainbow trout and 105 lake trout.
- Summer 1975: 2,578 anglers fished 7,762 hours, catching 3,189 rainbow trout and 97 lake trout.
- Fishing pressure at lower Twin Lake was stable across all three years. Upper lake pressure fell significantly in 1974.
- Rainbow trout harvest differed significantly between years. Lake trout catch remained relatively constant, once the 1973 size-limit throwbacks are considered.
- Winter (December–January) ice fishing on the lower lake yielded 72, 138, and 150 lake trout in the 1973–74, 1974–75, and 1975–76 seasons.
- Over 100,000 man-hours were expended yearly since 1973, producing nearly 2,000 lake trout and 50,000 rainbow trout. The 1959 survey recorded about 850 lake trout and 6,800 rainbow trout from 30,000 hours.

**Rainbow trout stocking:** Survival of early-season plants was poor. Only about 19 percent of the 1973 plant (208,360 fish) returned, and in 1975 only 5 percent of an early plant was harvested by June 1.

**Lake trout populations (Gill Netting):**
- 206 lake trout were captured in the lower lake in 1974 and 218 in 1975.
- Mean catch per net was 4.90 in 1974 and 5.19 in 1975, with no significant year effect.
- Catch differed significantly among months (May 3.64, August 4.54, November 6.96 per net) and between deep (>15 m) and shallow (<15 m) stations (6.40 vs. 3.25).
- Mean lengths differed significantly between May and November 1974 and between May and August 1975. Length ranges were 177–865 mm.
- The authors concluded that no clear population trend was shown from 1974 to 1975.
- Upper lake catch per net (3.20) was 37 percent lower than the lower lake (5.1).

**Opossum shrimp (Trawl Sampling and Photography):**
- Densities rose through the summer, peaked in August or September, and then declined. The distribution was clumped (negative binomial model, per Gregg).
- Densities were significantly different among months and sections along the traverse line. Densities at random stations showed no significant year effect.
- Average density in the lower lake was about 50 shrimp per m² in 1974 and 32 in 1975. The upper lake averaged about 33 and 15 per m², respectively.
- Juvenile shrimp stayed near the surface until September, so trawl estimates in June–August mainly reflect adults.
- Trawl counts correlated with photo counts (r = 0.9183), but the regression suggested trawl counts could be up to 60 percent below actual densities.
- Trawling was impossible in shallow, vegetated littoral zones where large concentrations were seen at depths as shallow as 2.4 m.

**Planned follow-up:** Postoperational studies were scheduled to begin in early 1981 to measure the effects of pumped-storage operation on fish, shrimp, and limnology.
