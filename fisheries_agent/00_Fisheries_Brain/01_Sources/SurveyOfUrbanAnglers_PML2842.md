# Survey of 1985 Urban Anglers in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 Arizona Game and Fish Department report by urban fisheries biologist Bill Bratt evaluates the state's Urban Fishing Program through a mail questionnaire sent to 1,021 adult [[License Holders]] drawn from a population of 10,242 licensed urban anglers. Of these, 240 usable responses were returned (23.5 percent). The survey finds heavy fishing pressure on five designated urban lakes, a mostly male and older angler base that often fishes other state waters, and a harvest of stocked fish roughly four times the number stocked. Anglers largely supported the program (nearly 72 percent graded it C or better) and 52 percent supported reducing the channel catfish creel limit from six to four.

## Background & Description

The [[Urban Fishing Program]] began in January 1983 and grew rapidly, with license sales rising from 3,300 in 1983 to 11,300 in 1985. Juvenile license requirements were dropped in April 1985, so juveniles were excluded from the sample. The program is partly supported by tax dollars from participating cities, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department needed data to set stocking rates and maintain acceptable catch rates.

Five designated urban lakes totaling 46 acres were surveyed:

- [[Kennedy Park Lake]] (Tucson, 10 acres)
- [[Silverbell Park Lake]] (Tucson, 13 acres), the most heavily fished lake and located in a more rural setting
- [[Chaparral Park Lake]] (Scottsdale, 10 acres), in a high-density residential area
- [[Kiwanis Park Lake]] (Tempe, 13 acres), partly drawn down in October 1985 for an irrigation intake and not stocked from September 20, 1985 until February 6, 1986
- [[Canal Park Lake]] (Tempe, 3 acres), added to provide continuous fishing and surveyed over only a two-month period

Stocking is conducted 26 times per year. [[Rainbow Trout]] are stocked 10 times between November 1 and March 31 at 34 pounds per acre (about 2.5 fish per pound, 8 to 12 inches). [[Channel Catfish]] are stocked 16 times from April through October at 100 pounds per surface acre (about 1.5 pounds per fish), with up to 5 percent larger fish. Fish are purchased from private growers under bid-contract agreements. The Department does not publicly disclose stocking dates, to discourage a "fish truck" mentality associated with put-and-take fisheries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The urban lakes are man-made, put-and-take fisheries with no natural reproduction implied; success depends entirely on stocking. Urban lakes received fishing pressure of 23 to 96 hours per acre per day, which the author notes exceeds that of most rural southeastern Arizona lakes (compared using creel survey data from 1985-86). The survey also notes that the mail survey's accuracy may be lower than creel interview data, although the relative frequency of use is striking.

Urban angling activity was linked to local context. Tucson residents participated at 15.8 licensed anglers per thousand residents, versus 6.2 in Scottsdale and 6.0 in Tempe, which the author attributes to greater availability of non-urban waters (such as the Salt and Verde rivers and reservoirs) near Scottsdale and Tempe. Phoenix (1.6 per thousand) and Mesa (2.3 per thousand) rates suggest that having a designated urban lake stimulates license purchases.

## Key Findings

**Angler demographics**
- 46 percent of urban license buyers were age 40 or above, versus about 31 percent of the general population.
- Men outnumbered women by 4.5 to 1.
- Age distribution of the sample: 32% aged 14–30, 22% in thirties, 16% in forties, 12% in fifties, 12% in sixties, 6% aged 70+.
- 79 percent of respondents also fished other Arizona waters in 1985; about 21.3 percent fished only urban lakes.

**Angler use and travel**
- Total angler visits to all five lakes: 273,461.
- Estimated total 1985 angler hours: about 1.19 million (roughly 116 hours per licensed adult angler).
- Silverbell Lake had the highest use and the longest average trip length.
- Average one-way travel distance varied by lake; about 71 percent of anglers traveled 10 miles or less, and only 5 percent traveled more than 20 miles.

**Catch rates**
- Rainbow trout catch rates ranged from 0.22 to 0.39 fish per hour (Kiwanis highest at 0.39; Silverbell and Kennedy at 0.22).
- Channel catfish catch rates ranged from 0.27 (Kennedy) to 0.55 (Kiwanis) fish per hour.
- The management objective for trout is 0.2 fish per angler hour (acceptable range 0.1–0.3); Kiwanis exceeded it and other lakes were on target. No catfish objective had been established.

**Harvest and stocking**
- Estimated harvest totaled about 330,541 fish (123,824 rainbow trout; 206,717 channel catfish), roughly four times the approximately 84,550 fish stocked in 1985 (48,800 catfish; 35,750 trout).
- Example: Canal Lake received about 900 catfish but anglers reported catching 1,269 fish; Silverbell Lake was stocked with about 10,500 trout but estimated harvest was 41,941.
- Catch-and-release: 11.1 percent of anglers released over 90 percent of trout caught; 29 percent released some trout and 32 percent released some catfish.

**Attitudes and regulation**
- 52 percent of all respondents would accept a reduction of the channel catfish creel limit from 6 to 4 fish per day.
- Program grades: average GPA of 2.1; 87 percent graded the program C or better; 71.5 percent graded it passing.
- About 80 percent planned to buy a 1986 urban fishing license; 15.8 percent said no.
- The author attributes lower ratings partly to a three-month transition in the Urban Fisheries Biologist position and a stocking contractor default that interrupted regular stocking for about two months.

**Recommendations**
1. Continue creel surveys in 1987 to monitor catch rates and angler use, and collect information on the under-14 user group and Pioneer Complimentary License holders.
2. Reduce the daily bag and possession limit for channel catfish to four fish.
3. Maintain current rainbow trout stocking levels; reduce channel catfish stocking at Kiwanis Lake (0.55 fish/hour exceeds other waters) and redistribute those fish to Kennedy or Silverbell lakes.
4. Evaluate individual stocking rates for Tucson lakes, which receive the majority of urban fishing pressure.
5. Increase Maricopa County participation through fishing clinics and education programs, and add more urban lakes since most anglers travel less than 10 miles.
6. Maintain the current balance between channel catfish and rainbow trout stocking periods.
