# Bonytail Chub Hatchery Holding and Treatment Log, Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery (May 1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This field and hatchery log documents the handling, holding, and treatment of [[Bonytail Chub]] (BTC) at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] between May 11 and May 26, 1993. Fourteen wild-caught adults were delivered by [[Charles Minckley]] of the Parker Fishery Resources Office and [[Dr. Paul Marsh]] of Arizona State University. The fish arrived with severe net injuries, skin loss, and fin damage, and many had been collected while spawning. Within a week, [[Saprolegnia]] fungal infections affected eight of the fourteen fish. Treatments included [[Formalin Treatment]], [[Potassium Permanganate Treatment]], and repeated [[Salt Bath Treatment]] under guidance from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] and [[Pinetop Fish Health Center]] staff. At least one fish died on May 26, and the log closes with a hatchery manager's recommendation to limit further handling and trauma to the species.

## Background & Description

The [[Bonytail Chub]] is a cyprinid of large western North American rivers and is treated here as a rare species requiring careful husbandry. The log describes the following:

- **Source and capture:** Nine fish were caught in trammel nets in Carp Cove on the morning of May 11, 1993. Five more were delivered later, and a further fish was added on May 12. The log states that fourteen were ultimately held.
- **Injury profile:** All fish had net marks encircling the head at the opercle, just in front of the dorsal hump. Lateral skin was scraped raw, most visibly on the caudal peduncle. Several fins were split and bloodied, most often the caudal fin. One fish had a discolored eye, probably a cataract.
- **Spawning stress:** The fish were collected while spawning, which the log notes lowered their stress threshold.
- **Holding facility:** Fish were kept in a covered hatchery tank under the observation of hatchery biologist [[Glenn Green]]. Tank dimensions in the scanned record are partly illegible.
- **Water conditions:** Arrival water was 76°F (May 11) and 72°F (May 13). Fish were tempered to 57–58°F before being placed in the holding tank.
- **Feeding:** On May 12, the fish were fed 454 grams of trout (recorded as approximately one fish per gram).

Personnel named in the log include [[Roger Hamman]] (Dexter NFH & TC), [[Jim Brooks]] and [[Caryl Williams]] (Dexter Fishery Resources Office), [[Gary Carmichael]] (treatment advisor), [[Carol Shanks]] (Pinetop Fish Health Center), and [[Lyle R. Miller]] (Hatchery Manager).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The log focuses on disease, injury, and husbandry rather than on habitat or community ecology. Its environmental relevance lies in the following:

- **Pathogen pressure:** Saprolegnia spp. was the most persistent problem. Open wounds and fungal-infected areas, especially on the caudal peduncle, became secondary infection sites.
- **Water quality in holding:** Stagnant salt baths and standing treatments caused toxin buildup. Carmichael advised flushing the tank with fresh water once or twice daily, or every 8 to 12 hours, to remove metabolites. A water level that was "very low" was noted on May 22, and a standpipe seal problem was fixed on May 25.
- **Treatment chemistry (as transcribed from the record):** Formalin flow-through at 1/6,000 for one hour at 20 GPM. Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) at 2 g/L painted on wounds. Salt baths at NaCl 5.9 g/L and MgSO₄ at 118 mg/L in a stagnant bath, with industrial-grade oxygen supplied through a diffuser.
- **Capture and transport context:** Plans for transfer to Dexter NFH & TC were discussed from late May, with a planned transport date of about May 26, 1993. The log also notes a decision on whether to move fish or release them.

## Key Findings

1. **Injury at capture:** All fourteen fish arrived with net-related and abrasion injuries. The three most severely injured fish (swimming stiffly, with large open wounds on the sides and along the caudal peduncle) were of highest concern.
2. **Saprolegnia outbreak:** By May 18, eight of the fourteen fish had severe Saprolegnia spp. infections. Hamman recommended painting wounds with KMnO₄. On May 18, after treating seven fish, the most severely affected fish rolled over and lost equilibrium. Treatment was halted and the tank covered to allow recovery.
3. **Repeated treatment regimen:** Between May 13 and May 25, the log records near-daily cycles of formalin flow-through baths, standing salt treatments (often changed every 4–6 hours), flushes, and full-tank salt dips (for example, all BTC dipped in a salt bath on May 22).
4. **Partial response:** Some improvement was noted on May 19, though one fish still lay on its side. By May 24, "another BTC looking pretty weak" had rolled over, and on May 25 several fish were still lying on their sides.
5. **Mortality:** One BTC was found dead on the morning of May 26 and was immediately frozen for later analysis.
6. **Expert consensus on handling:** Hamman advised against spawning attempts, judging them likely to cause more stress and damage. The hatchery manager's observation states that the species should not be subjected to such trauma and recommends leaving fish undisturbed where possible.
7. **Conclusion:** The log's closing conclusion is that capture and holding of wild Bonytail Chub under the conditions described caused severe, cumulative stress and disease. Holding and transport decisions were made under time pressure and with limited expertise, and the species' pre-stressed condition made recovery difficult.
