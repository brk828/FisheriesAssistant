# Taxonomic and Ecological Patterns Among Fish Communities in Rivers and Streams of Western Jalisco, Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #goodeidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study applies an indirect multivariate gradient analysis to fish assemblages in rivers and streams of western Jalisco, Mexico. It compares the influence of large-scale zoogeographic factors with local habitat features on species composition and on ecological guild composition. Data come from 35 sites in 23 rivers and streams across five basins: [[Ameca Basin]], [[Armeria Basin]], [[Coahuayana Basin]], [[Marabasco Basin]] and [[Purificacion Basin]].

Basin was the dominant explanatory variable. Once basin was accounted for, riparian vegetation, depth and cover explained significant additional variation, while substrate contributed little. Riparian vegetation was the most consistently important habitat feature. The authors conclude that taxonomic differences among assemblages also reflect ecological differences, and that human modification of riparian zones strongly shapes the ecological attributes of these fish assemblages.

## Background & Description

Fish assemblages in running waters are shaped by a hierarchy of factors. Large-scale, long-duration factors such as the uplift of mountain ranges, which isolate basins and drive speciation or extinction, determine which species can potentially occupy a region. Smaller-scale, shorter-duration factors, such as pH, temperature and local substrate and depth, determine which species actually occupy a given reach.

Earlier multivariate studies in temperate regions have addressed these relationships, but tropical and Mexican studies are scarce. The authors cite only three prior Mexican examples, all from the Lerma and Balsas basins in central Mexico. Those studies also found longitudinal patterns, with sub-basins supporting distinct fish communities linked to geography, elevation and physicochemical variables.

The western Jalisco basins share many faunal affinities but also contain endemic species, as documented by [[Uyeno Et Al 1983]] and [[Miller And Smith 1986]]. The study therefore analyzed the data in two ways: first by species composition, emphasizing zoogeographic drivers, and then by ecological guilds, emphasizing habitat drivers. The region's fauna includes the family [[Goodeidae]] (live-bearing endemics such as [[Allodontichthys]]) and the family [[Poeciliidae]], which together give the region its characteristic endemic character.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Each site was sampled once between 1986 and 1997. Data were compiled from earlier work by the authors and collaborators. The authors argue that although collection years differ, the species composition changed only slightly in abundance, and the dominant species remained dominant. Between-basin differences were therefore judged more likely to reflect geography than time. Sites affected by severe water-quality problems, such as an industrially and municipally polluted reach of the [[Ayuquila River]], were excluded.

**Zoogeographic variables.** Four were considered: basin, upstream watershed area, distance along the river to the Pacific Ocean, and site elevation. Distances and elevations were derived from topographic maps at 1:50,000 and 1:250,000 scales.

**Habitat variables.** Four were coded on a 1–3 scale from field observations (see the criteria table in the source):
- Depth: under 0.5 m, 0.5–1.0 m, over 1.0 m.
- Cover: under 5%, 5–10%, over 10% of the site area with shelter such as rocks, macrophytes, logs or undercut banks.
- Substrate: dominance by fine material, coarse material, or a mixture of three or more types.
- Riparian vegetation: from bare or agricultural banks with no shade, to healthy natural vegetation providing partial or full shade.

**Sampling method.** Fish were collected with hand nets, seines, cast nets or electrofishing, depending on site conditions. Sampling covered all habitat types at each site and continued until repeated efforts yielded no new species or major compositional change. Catches were expressed per 100 m of river and log-transformed. Voucher specimens were deposited in the University of Wisconsin Zoological Museum, the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology and the Universidad de Guadalajara.

**Ecological guilds.** Species were grouped into guilds using five traits: native or exotic status, reproductive mode, maximum adult size, diet and typical position in the water column. Each unique combination of these traits defines one guild. The authors justify guilds as capturing the functional role of organisms within a habitat, whereas species reflect taxonomic and speciation history.

**Analytical approach.** A two-phase indirect gradient analysis (after Ter Braak & Prentice 1988) was used. In phase one, [[Detrended Correspondence Analysis]] (DCA) ordinated species and sites, separately for the species and guild data. Rare species and guilds were downweighted. Each DCA produced three axes. In phase two, ordination scores were correlated with the zoogeographic and habitat variables, and compared across basins and across habitat categories using ANOVA with Student-Newman-Keuls tests (SAS 1990).

## Key Findings

**Basin structures fish assemblages.** Of the zoogeographic variables, basin explained the most variation in species and guild composition. Four of the five basins had unique species or guilds. Purificacion and Marabasco assemblages were broadly similar, whereas Ameca, Armeria and Coahuayana could be distinguished from each other and from the Purificacion-Marabasco group. Elevation, distance to the Pacific and watershed area explained relatively little variation once basin was considered.

**Riparian vegetation is the key habitat feature.** After basin effects were removed, riparian vegetation, depth and cover were significant. Substrate had little explanatory power. Sites with extensive, well-developed natural riparian vegetation supported fish assemblages significantly different from those lacking it. Riparian vegetation also correlated with watershed area: sites with well-developed vegetation had significantly smaller upstream watersheds (mean 127 km²) than those with moderate (950 km²) or poor (806 km²) vegetation (F = 6.72; p < 0.0037).

**Richness and distribution.**
- 25 species were recorded across 35 sites, including 9 endemics and 4 exotics.
- 17 ecological guilds were identified, 4 of them exotic.
- No species or guild occurred in all five basins.
- [[Ilyodon Furcidens]] was the most widespread species, found at 24 sites in four basins.
- Six species occurred at only one site.
- Four guilds occurred at only one site.
- Most guilds contained only one or two species.

**Endemic species.** The nine endemics were [[Scartomyzon]] mascotae (Ameca), [[Allodontichthys]] polylepis (Ameca), A. tamazulae (Coahuayana), A. zonistius (Armeria), Ilyodon furcidens (four basins), [[Xenotaenia Resolanae]] (Marabasco, Purificacion), Poecilia butleri (Marabasco, Purificacion), Poeciliopsis baenschi (Armeria, Marabasco, Purificacion) and P. turneri (Purificacion).

**Species-level patterns.**
- On DCA axis 1, Yuriria alta, S. mascotae and Allodontichthys polylepis scored highest; they are restricted to the Ameca basin.
- On axis 2, Xenotoca melanosoma, Goodea atripinnis and Poeciliopsis infans scored highest; they are restricted to the Ameca basin and the upper Armeria.
- On axis 3, Lepomis macrochirus and Ictalurus dugesi scored highest (both restricted to Armeria), and Oreochromis aureus was found in all basins except Coahuayana.

**Basin differences in site scores.** Ameca sites scored significantly higher on species axis 1 (mean 415) than Marabasco (149) and Purificacion (115) (F = 5.49; p = 0.0019). Elevation correlated positively with axis 1 (r = 0.50; p = 0.002). On axis 3, Coahuayana scored significantly lower (14) than the other four basins (117–167) (F = 4.00; p = 0.0102).

**Habitat effects on species.** Riparian vegetation was significant on axis 2. Sites with well-developed riparian vegetation scored lowest (73), versus moderate (155) or poor (207) vegetation (F = 12.13; p < 0.0001). Depth was also significant on axis 2: moderately deep sites scored highest (172), versus deep (98) or shallow (87) sites (F = 4.71; p = 0.0161). The authors attribute these axis-2 patterns to three species, A. zonistius, A. monticola and S. multipunctatum, which occur in shallow, fast riffles and are sensitive to degradation. Two other species, G. atripinnis and P. infans, tolerate a wide range of conditions and were little affected by depth or riparian conditions.

**Guild-level patterns.** Guilds showed a stronger link to habitat than to basin.
- Basin correlated with guild axis 1 only. Purificacion (403) and Marabasco (379) scored higher than Armeria (200), Coahuayana (191) and Ameca (182) (F = 11.62; p < 0.0001). Axis 1 correlated negatively with elevation (r = 0.52; p = 0.0015) and distance to the ocean (r = 0.44; p = 0.0083).
- Riparian vegetation was significant on guild axes 2 and 3. Extensive vegetation scored highest on axis 2 (180) and lowest on axis 3 (83), with F = 4.16 and p = 0.0247 for axis 3.
- Depth was significant on guild axis 2 (deep 184, shallow 142, moderate 116; F = 4.86; p = 0.0143).
- Cover was significant on guild axis 3 (limited cover 164, moderate 116, extensive 84; F = 4.75; p = 0.0156).

**Guilds versus species.** Guilds were less restricted to single basins than species. Six species but only four guilds were found in just one basin, and two guilds (though only one species) occurred in four of five basins. Guild patterns therefore showed greater inter-basin similarity.

**Evolutionary interpretation.** Xenotaenia resolanae was the only member of its guild (native, viviparous, small, carnivorous, pelagic), suggesting that selection differs between the Purificacion and Marabasco basins. In contrast, the three [[Allodontichthys]] species share one guild despite occurring in different basins. The authors infer that isolation and speciation occurred without major niche shifts.

**Conservation implications.** The authors suggest that the strong effect of riparian vegetation reflects the impact of anthropogenic modification of riparian zones on the ecological attributes of fish assemblages.

**Methodological notes.** The authors acknowledge that the five-trait guild classification is only one of many possible approaches. An alternative would be to classify guilds by a single trait, such as reproductive mode (oviparous versus viviparous), which would yield two broad, widely distributed guilds.
