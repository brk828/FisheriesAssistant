# Blue River Roads, Emergencies and BMPs: Interim Biological Opinion (June 16, 1997)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #riparian-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biological opinion (Reference 2-21-94-F-243) evaluates [[Forest Road 281]] and connected County-maintained roads along the upper [[Blue River]] in Greenlee County, Arizona. The Forest Service is lead agency, with the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] in support. The opinion finalizes six emergency flood-repair consultations (1994–1995) and adopts [[Best Management Practices]] (BMPs) for normal maintenance and repair over a 12-month interim period, while initiating steps toward a long-term road solution.

The Service found that the road, combined with the degraded baseline, **jeopardizes** the continued existence of the [[Loach Minnow]], but is **not likely to jeopardize** the [[Bald Eagle]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] and [[American Peregrine Falcon]] for the interim period. Jeopardy for the loach minnow was avoided by shortening the proposed project from 5 years to 12 months, adding spawning-season work restrictions, and committing to long-term planning.

## Background & Description

**Consultation history.** Consultation began in August 1993 when Greenlee County asked about Endangered Species Act (ESA) compliance for FEMA flood-repair funding. Six emergency consultations were documented between December 1994 and March 1995. Repairs were undertaken before consultation was requested, which the Service identified as a compliance problem. The formal request was received February 14, 1997, and the consultation was shortened in response to a jeopardy concern raised May 7, 1997.

**Proposed action.** The project covers roughly 30 miles of the upper Blue River and tributaries (Campbell Blue and Dry Blue Creeks), including:
- FR 281 (approx. 27 miles)
- FR 30 (approx. 3 miles)
- FR 16 (approx. 0.75 miles, partly in New Mexico)
- FR 567 (approx. 12 miles) and FR 232 (approx. 4 miles) within the drainage

Work includes bridges, low-water crossings, culverts, roadbanks, slides, and protective structures. The roads are all-weather gravel roads with light to moderate use (100 vehicles per day or less, rising to 300 during hunting season).

**BMPs.** Nine activity categories are covered, including wet and dry crossing maintenance, structure maintenance, resurfacing, slide removal, gravel borrowing, bank restoration, river channelization, and routine maintenance. The BMPs are described as a "living document."

**Long-term planning.** A multi-party team (Forest Service, COE, Service, Greenlee County, Arizona Game and Fish Department) is to prepare geomorphic studies and historic-condition habitat studies. A five-year follow-up opinion is anticipated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Degraded baseline.** Aldo Leopold (1921) described the Blue River as "ruined." Overgrazing, timber harvest, beaver removal, and road construction on the floodplain have produced a wide, shallow, braided channel with eroding banks and sparse riparian vegetation. Base flows have declined, and portions of the river lose all surface flow in early summer, notably near Joy Crossing.

**Hydrology.** The only USGS gauge (Juan Miller/Stacy crossing) recorded a maximum of 30,000 cfs (1972), a minimum of 1.4 cfs (1978), and a median yearly mean of 43 cfs. The river is "flashy," with summer storm discharges often an order of magnitude above mean daily flow.

**Fish community.** Nine native species (about 65% of the native fauna) are considered extirpated. Four native species remain alongside the loach minnow: speckled dace, longfin dace, desert sucker, and Sonora sucker. Nonnative species include rainbow trout, brown trout, fathead minnow, western mosquitofish, and red shiner. The razorback sucker was reintroduced from 1986 to 1989, but no individuals were found in recent surveys.

**Riparian and wildlife context.** Riparian vegetation is sparse, with seep willow, young cottonwoods, and some sycamore. Sedges are uncommon. Three active peregrine eyries lie 1.5 to 6 miles from the road, and about 16 linear miles of potential nesting cliffs occur within one-half mile of the road. The nearest known bald eagle nest is at [[Luna Lake]], about 20 air miles away.

**Cumulative effects.** Private valley-bottom uses, including a private fish hatchery that diverts base flow, grazing, cropping, and residential development, add to degradation. The Service views the road as a substantial and ongoing contributor.

## Key Findings

**Species status and effects**

- **[[Loach Minnow]] (threatened):** The rarest native fish in the Blue River, typically under 10% of the fish community. The Blue River population is genetically distinct and considered critical to recovery. The road affects about 20% of the known range. Main threats include crushing by machinery, sedimentation of under-cobble habitat, flow loss, and temperature fluctuations. Jeopardy was found for the baseline with the proposed action; the Service recommended the species be considered for uplisting to endangered.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]] (endangered):** Not found in recent surveys. Effects center on the loss of pool habitat, which the road helps maintain by keeping the river in riffle-run form.
- **[[Bald Eagle]] (threatened):** Winter use of the Blue River is documented (three adults in 12 minutes of helicopter survey, 1996). Effects are mainly loss of large riparian perch and roost trees and disturbance during work.
- **[[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (endangered):** Not known to breed on the Blue River, though the species occurs nearby. Current habitat lacks the density needed for nesting. Migrating birds may be affected by suppressed riparian recovery.
- **[[American Peregrine Falcon]] (endangered, under review for delisting):** Disturbance during the March 1 to July 15 breeding season is the main concern.

**Incidental take thresholds**

- Loach minnow and razorback sucker: more than 20 dead fish of any species within the work area or 500 yards downstream; work outside the 33-foot road easement; any toxic spill.
- Bald eagle: none anticipated.
- Southwestern willow flycatcher: unquantified harm to migrants.
- Peregrine falcon: up to 10 adults and 20 young over 12 months.

**Terms and conditions**

- No work in the wetted channel during loach minnow spawning (March 1 to June 1 and September 1 to October 31), except in emergencies.
- Minimize removal of large woody debris, channel rerouting, bypass roads, and riparian vegetation loss.
- Limit disturbance sites to six per year.
- Biologist present during river diversion; immediate notification if more than 20 dead fish are found.
- Non-emergency work near known peregrine sites restricted to outside the breeding season.
- Annual reporting to the Forest Service, Service, and COE.

**Conservation recommendations (discretionary)**

- Terminate gravel mining in the Blue River and tributaries while long-term solutions are developed.
- Conduct annual flycatcher surveys using [[Sogge Survey Protocol]].
- Complete standardized riparian mapping as a monitoring baseline.
- Conduct peregrine occupancy monitoring across the entire project area in 1998.
- Study bald eagle winter habitat, prey base, and the source of prey for [[Luna Lake]] breeders.

**Key conclusions**

- The interim action "is not likely to jeopardize" four species, but the Service found that the road with the degraded baseline jeopardizes the loach minnow.
- Further action will be needed beyond the 12-month interim period to remove jeopardy for the loach minnow.
- Gravel bar skimming is described as the least damaging extraction method, but the BMPs lack criteria on reentry intervals, so disturbance may not be temporary.
