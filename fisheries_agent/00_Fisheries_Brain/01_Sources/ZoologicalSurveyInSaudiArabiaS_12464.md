# Zoological Survey in Saudi Arabia, Spring 1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

In March 1990 a joint zoological survey of central and western [[Saudi Arabia]] sampled 28 freshwater localities between 8 and 28 March. The survey was a collaboration between the [[National Commission For Wildlife Conservation And Development]] (NCWCD, Riyadh) and the [[Senckenberg Research Institute]] (Frankfurt), and continued earlier expeditions led by [[Wilhelm Buttiker]]. Emphasis fell on freshwater fishes and invertebrates, with attention also paid to small mammals near water bodies. The authors documented site conditions, collected specimens and measured basic water chemistry at nine localities. The data are intended as a baseline for long-term monitoring of arid freshwater ecosystems, which the authors describe as sensitive and vulnerable to human impact. The paper contains no species-level identifications in the text provided.

## Background & Description

Before this work, [[Saudi Arabia]] was described as largely unexplored for most zoological taxa. Systematic sampling began only in 1975. Earlier expeditions, reported in a series of papers in the journal *Fauna of Saudi Arabia*, focused mainly on medical, veterinary and agricultural zoology and on invertebrates.

The survey had two stated aims:

- **Comparative analysis:** provide specialists with material for comparing fish and invertebrate populations in isolated desert water bodies, a question relevant to [[Systematics]] and [[Zoogeography]].
- **Baseline monitoring:** document the current state of freshwater ecosystems and compile species inventories, so that future ecological change and human impact can be assessed and conservation measures planned.

Collection methods included nets of several mesh sizes, traps and [[Electric Fishing]]. At several sites, dry mud was collected so that [[Ephemeral Crustaceans]] could be hatched in the laboratory. Specimens were to be deposited in the Saudi Museum of Natural History (Riyadh) and the Senckenberg Museum (Frankfurt). Coordinates were taken from maps for sites 1–15 and with a Loran-C receiver for the rest. Altitudes are approximate because the altimeter could not always be calibrated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drought and water loss.** In March 1990 most of the study area had received little rain. The eastern and central regions, the eastern Asir drainages and the northwest were especially dry, and the Asir Tihama and Hijaz were only slightly better off. Pumping for irrigation had also lowered water levels considerably.

**Site conditions noted in the field:**

- **Ain Hit cave** (south of Riyadh), previously reported to hold a population of blind cave fish, was completely dry and no fish were seen.
- **[[Laila Lakes]]** (al-Aflaj), the only natural lakes on the Arabian Peninsula, had dropped by about 10–15 m. Several smaller pools were dry and others were inaccessible because of steep banks. The bank vegetation, which had previously supported a unique insect fauna, was dry. Plate captions note that [[Tilapiine Fishes]] had been introduced to the lakes.
- **Eastern [[Asir Mountains]] wadis** held water only in a few short streamlets and pools. These had high biomass but relatively low species diversity.
- **[[Red Sea Coastal Plain]] wadis** had very short stretches of slow or stagnant water, often polluted by cattle. At [[Wadi Ilyab]], dry-season biomass in a shallow water body was described as extremely high.
- **[[Jabal Lauz]]** in the northwest had very little perennial surface water. Temperatures were low and there was little insect flight at altitude.

**Water chemistry.** Basic analyses were made at nine localities (field nos. 03, 07, 10, 14, 15, 17, 18, 20 and 22). Oxygen, conductivity and pH were measured electronically. Other parameters used Aquamerck® test sets. Across the nine sites, water temperature ranged from about 22 to 34.5 °C, pH from 7.8 to 8.8, and conductivity from about 470 to 4010 µS/cm (the highest value was at site 03, 4010 µS/cm). Some values in the scanned table are illegible, so only the clearly readable figures are reported here. Total and carbonate hardness were given in degrees German hardness and mmol/l.

**Habitat diversity.** Sampled habitats ranged from spring-fed canals and oasis irrigation channels to rock pools in juniper forest, mountain wadis with Potamogeton and Chara, ephemeral desert pools, and brackish mangrove estuaries on the Red Sea coast. Altitudes ranged from about sea level (site 12, near Ash-Shuqaiq) to about 2550 m (site 6, near Abha).

## Key Findings

- **Scope:** The survey covered 28 freshwater localities in central and western Saudi Arabia during March 1990. It documented fish and invertebrate habitats and, where possible, monitored water chemistry. An annotated gazetteer of all sites is provided (Table 1).
- **Severe drought and abstraction:** Almost all sites were affected by low rainfall and irrigation pumping. Several formerly perennial systems were dry or reduced to isolated pools. The cave of Ain Hit was entirely dry and no fish were found there.
- **Declining natural lakes:** At the [[Laila Lakes]], water levels had fallen 10–15 m, leaving many pools dry and affecting bank vegetation and its associated insect fauna.
- **Refugial pools:** Perennial pools (such as at [[Wadi Al-Matraq]]) served as refuges where fishes concentrated during low water. The authors suggest this makes them important for populations.
- **Pollution and degradation:** Stagnant Red Sea coastal wadis were polluted by livestock, and the authors describe the high biomass in some shallow water bodies as a notable feature.
- **Water chemistry:** Water was generally alkaline (pH 7.8–8.8). Conductivity varied widely, from roughly 470 to 4010 µS/cm, reflecting differences in water source and salinity.
- **Methodological and monitoring plan:** The survey provides baseline data for long-term monitoring. Its main stated purpose is to allow human impacts on [[Freshwater Ecosystems]] in arid regions to be measured and managed, and to support conservation planning.
- **Collections:** Material was to be deposited in the Saudi Museum of Natural History (Riyadh) and the Senckenberg Museum (Frankfurt), making it available for comparative systematic and population studies.
