# Desert Pupfish Monitoring at Quitobaquito Pond, Channel and Springs, 1999 Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This National Park Service report summarizes monitoring of the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*) at Quitobaquito Springs and Pond, within [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]] on the [[U.S.-Mexico Border]]. It covers the post-construction period from the 1989 [[Quitobaquito Habitat Project]] through the 1990 monitoring season. The period included a non-native fish discovery at nearby Aguajita Springs, its removal, and the annual population census. The 1990 census recorded no non-native fish in the pond or channel and concluded that the pupfish population was in good condition, with fish shifting toward the new stream channel.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*), an endangered endemic pupfish of the spring outflows and pond.
- **Companion species:** [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon sonoriense longifemorale*) young, which the habitat was also designed to shelter.
- **Site:** Quitobaquito Springs and Pond sit in the southwestern part of the monument, next to the international border. Two springs north of the pond feed it.
- **Habitat project:** Initiated in November 1989 in consultation with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and completed in December 1989. It built an open, concrete-lined stream channel of about 700 feet from the springs to the pond, with an underground pipeline backup. The channel's width and depth match historic farm-era dimensions and include slow and fast water, pools, overhangs and islands.
- **Goals:** Enhance habitat for the pupfish and associated fauna, and reduce catastrophic pond-level drops that had twice threatened pupfish habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Pupfish reached the southwest spring within one week of the channel opening, showing they had moved the full channel length. By the report's writing they occupied the channel in large numbers, mainly in shallow pools.
- **Threat of non-native fish:** Non-native [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) was the main concern, since it could be devastating to native pupfish. Other introduced species of concern included [[Sonora Chub]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*).
- **Aguajita Springs incident:** On May 6, 1990, fish were found in a pool at Aguajita Springs, about one mile east of Quitobaquito. That site had no known fish history and no water link to the Rio Sonoyta. Human introduction from nearby Mexican communities was judged the most likely cause.
- **Management response:** The fish were removed and the pool was filled with gravel. The pool continued to be checked weekly. The removed pupfish were frozen and sent to Arizona State University for [[National Science Foundation]]-sponsored genetics research.
- **Historical precedent:** Expert [[Wendel Minckley]] cited a 1969 golden shiner case that required removing pupfish, draining the pond and poisoning it.

## Key Findings

**1990 Monitoring Activities**

- Weekly staff inspections of the pond, springs, channel and orchard were carried out all year. No pupfish were ever handled or trapped during these inspections.
- Emergency survey, May 12, 1990: 1,373 pupfish were caught in the pond and about 623 in the channel. Two were caught in the overflow. All captured fish were pupfish, with no mortalities.
- Suspected non-native fish at Aguajita Springs were confirmed on May 18, 1990, as Western Mosquitofish and Sonora Chub. Removal was recommended and a formal Section 7 consultation under the Endangered Species Act was started.

**Annual Census, September 17-18, 1990**

- Pond: 47 perimeter traps, emptied six times over two days. Total catch was 1,158 fish. Nine mortalities occurred over the two days.
- Channel and springs: 1,134 fish on day one and 309 on day two. Day-two fish were measured and then released.
- Combined total: 2,272 fish (+/- 309). Visual observation indicated these were only a small fraction of the total population.
- No non-native fish were captured or seen in the pond or channel.

**Historical Comparison**

- Kynard counts (1975-1981) ranged from 7,294 (1975) down to 1,800 (1981), with other years between 3,000 and 6,700. The report notes these figures were questioned in reliability.
- The method changed from mark-recapture (left pectoral fin clipping) to a depletion-based estimate from successive trap runs, adopted in 1985. The authors judged both earlier and depletion estimates to be too low.
- The 1990 pond catch was less than half the 1989 pond catch. Channel captures were high despite fewer traps.

**Conclusions**

- Pupfish are shifting to the stream channel's shallow pools and overhangs, as the habitat project predicted. Total captures and size classes were essentially the same across years; the main change is location.
- The population appears healthy, with a range of age and size classes, and the report judges it to be in good condition.

**Future Plans**

- Continue ongoing monitoring of the pupfish population.
- Establish consistent census techniques for the pond and channel.
- Combine weekly inspections with an annual census to track the population and check for non-native fish.
