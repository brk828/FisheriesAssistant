# Surface-Hyporheic Interactions in a Sonoran Desert Stream: Hydrologic Exchange and Diel Periodicity

#freshwater-ecology #hydrology #hyporheic-zone #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study by H. Maurice Valett (Arizona State University, published in *Hydrobiologia* 259, 1993) examines how water exchange between the [[Hyporheic Zone]] (the saturated sediments beneath and beside a stream channel) and the surface stream changes over a 24-hour cycle in [[Sycamore Creek]], Arizona. The work combines well-based sampling of interstitial water with [[Mini-Piezometers]] that measure [[Vertical Hydraulic Gradient]] (VHG). Interstitial water was low in [[Dissolved Oxygen]] but strongly enriched in [[Nitrate Nitrogen]] (NO3-N) and [[Soluble Reactive Phosphorus]] (SRP). The author concludes that the direction and magnitude of hydrologic exchange control physical, chemical and biological conditions in both the subsurface and the surface stream. No fish or other animal taxa are studied; the focus is on hydrology and biogeochemistry.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** Sycamore Creek lies about 32 km northeast of Phoenix, Arizona, and drains a catchment of 505 km² with elevations from 427 to 2164 m. The stream is about 55 km long, running from ephemeral headwaters to its confluence with the Verde River. In the mid-elevation reaches, water is alternately pushed to the surface by bedrock outcrops or absorbed into channel alluvium. Coarse sand or fine gravel (0.5–5.0 mm) averages 80 cm deep in low-gradient runs and often exceeds 2 m. Cobble and boulder substrata characterize steeper riffles.

**Study sites.**
- **Site I** (650 m elevation, sandy-bottom run) included a single permanent transect perpendicular to the stream. Dye injections there indicated positive hydraulic heads beneath the wetted perimeter and an average interstitial velocity of 0.62 mm s⁻¹. Site I was used to monitor diel interstitial conditions in an area of groundwater discharge.
- **Site II** (riffle-run transition, about 100 m long) was used for mini-piezometer measurements. Its head is mainly an upwelling zone, while downstream portions are downwelling zones.

**Sampling design.** At Site I, 10 wells of PVC pipe were installed in summer 1988 and allowed to equilibrate for three weeks. Three wells lay beneath the wetted perimeter and seven lay laterally in exposed alluvium. Well distances from the stream ranged from 10 cm to nearly 10 m. Surface water was sampled at 10 cm depth in midstream. Samples were collected over 24 hours on September 24, 1988, with nutrient samples taken every four hours and temperature every two hours. Dissolved oxygen was measured by the [[Winkler Method]], and nutrients were analyzed spectrophotometrically (SRP by molybdate-antimony method; NO3-N by cadmium reduction followed by nitrite colorimetry).

**Statistics.** Nutrient data were analyzed with two-way ANOVA (time and well location as factors) in SAS, with separate analyses for lateral wells and wells beneath the wetted perimeter. Multiple comparisons used Tukey's HSD at α = 0.05.

**Hydrologic monitoring.** At Site II, mini-piezometers (9 mm internal diameter polyethylene, installed 20–30 cm deep) measured hydraulic head. VHG was calculated as head divided by piezometer depth. Positive VHG indicates upwelling (interstitial water entering the stream); negative VHG indicates downwelling (stream water infiltrating the hyporheic zone). Eight piezometers were monitored on June 28, 1990, every four hours for 24 hours, and surface NO3-N was sampled at five of them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime.** Maximum air temperature was 37.4 °C, with a 30.9 °C range over the 24-hour period. Exposed alluvial surfaces exceeded 50 °C. Minimum air temperature was 6.5 °C. Temperatures 5–15 cm deep in dry bed material were more than 20 °C lower at maximum and varied less. In the saturated zone, mean water temperature was higher than surface water in 11 of 16 hyporheic locations. Strong thermal gradients occurred within two metres of the wetted perimeter, and interstitial temperature range fell from 9 °C at 10 cm depth to under two degrees at distances of two to ten metres.

**Oxygen and metabolism.** Diel DO cycles reflect whole-system metabolism, and the author notes that intense daily metabolic cycles occur in Sycamore Creek during summer. The timing and amplitude of interstitial DO change were used as indicators of connection with the surface stream.

**Nutrient context.** Surface inorganic nitrogen in the desert Southwest is strongly shaped by biotic uptake in nitrogen-limited streams. SRP is described as present in excess in both surface and interstitial water, so its variation is attributed mainly to physical-chemical equilibria, whereas nitrogen dynamics are attributed mainly to biotic transformations.

**Hyporheic habitat.** The author cites earlier work showing that deep sediments harbor a rich biota (see [[Boulton Et Al 1992]]) and that the hyporheic zone affects stream metabolism and nitrogen transformations.

## Key Findings

### 1. Dissolved oxygen is reduced in the hyporheic zone
- Mean DO in shallow wells (1, 6, 9) was 18%, 20% and 24% saturation. Deeper wells (2, 5, 7) averaged 28%, 31% and 34%.
- Maximum hyporheic DO was 49.9% saturation. Surface water reached 156.3% and averaged 82.9%.
- No anoxic water was collected, although minimum oxygen was below 1.0 ppm in two wells and below 2.0 ppm in all well water.

### 2. Hyporheic water is enriched in nutrients
- Surface NO3-N ranged from 1 to 10 µg L⁻¹ (mean 6 µg L⁻¹). Average hyporheic NO3-N was up to 25 times higher. In 8 of 10 wells, mean NO3-N was 90–150 µg L⁻¹.
- Mean hyporheic SRP was typically 1.7 times the surface mean. Surface SRP averaged 22 µg L⁻¹ (range 13–31 µg L⁻¹). Hyporheic SRP enrichment was less pronounced than for NO3-N.

### 3. Diel patterns differ by well location
- **Beneath the wetted perimeter:** Time and location interacted significantly for both NO3-N (time × location F = 11.81, P = 0.001) and SRP (time × location F = 3.25, P = 0.002). Diel responses depended on position within the hyporheic zone.
- **Lateral wells:** NO3-N differed significantly among locations (F = 337.3, P = 0.0001) and with time (F = 3.27, P = 0.008), but multiple comparisons found no differences among sampling times. Lateral NO3-N curves were essentially flat.
- **Surface:** Minimum NO3-N occurred at midday during periods of maximum insolation.

### 4. Nutrients are coupled to oxygen in some locations
- NO3-N and DO were strongly negatively correlated in surface water (r = −0.82, P = 0.02), which the author attributes to assimilatory demand from high algal standing crops.
- In hyporheic well #1, NO3-N and DO were strongly positively correlated (r = 0.82, P = 0.02). This is interpreted as possible nitrification by hyporheic sediments.
- A single significant SRP–DO correlation occurred in well #6 (r = −0.78, P = 0.03).

### 5. Vertical hydraulic gradient varies strongly along the reach
- Across the reach, VHG ranged from +0.29 to −0.84 over a 90 m study reach.
- **Upwelling (piezometers 1–3):** mean VHG between +0.12 and +0.25.
- **Mid-reach (piezometers 4–5):** little exchange, with mean VHG of +0.03 and −0.02.
- **Downwelling (piezometers 6–8):** VHG between −0.70 and −0.95, and in piezometers 7 and 8 often below −1.0. This indicates rapid surface infiltration that reduces surface flow.

### 6. Hydraulic heads change over the day
- Hydraulic heads varied by as much as 9 cm on a diel basis, and by 2.5 cm in upwelling piezometers 1–3.
- Maximum VHG in upwelling piezometers occurred at 0200 h.
- Piezometer 6 showed a steady head decrease from early morning until 6 PM, followed by a night-time increase.
- The author suggests that riparian phreatophytes common in the Sonoran Desert may drive diel head changes, following earlier observations elsewhere.

### 7. Hydrologic exchange controls surface nitrate
- Surface NO3-N maxima occurred at 0200 h in the upwelling regions (piezometers 1 and 4). Maxima were 50% lower in regions lacking exchange or dominated by infiltration.
- Mass flux of NO3-N over 24 hours (using surface discharge of 0.017 m³ s⁻¹ at piezometer 4):
  - Piezometer 4: 30.3 g d⁻¹
  - Piezometer 1: 19.5 g d⁻¹
  - Piezometers 5, 7 and 8: 14.2, 10.8 and 13.5 g d⁻¹
- Flux at piezometer 4 was more than twice the downstream values and was highest at the downstream extent of the upwelling zone. The author reports a longitudinal lag and dilution by upstream water low in NO3-N at the head of the upwelling zone.

### 8. Interstitial velocities vary widely
- Interstitial velocity at Site I averaged 0.62 mm s⁻¹. Near well #10 it was 0.0035 mm s⁻¹, about an order of magnitude lower than beneath the wetted perimeter (1.25 mm s⁻¹, unpublished data cited). The author suggests longer residence times there may favor denitrification.

### 9. Conceptual conclusions
- Interactions between surface and groundwater are largely determined by water flow. Downwelling zones supply oxygen to the hyporheic zone, supporting aerobic respiration and affecting nitrogen dynamics. Upwelling of chemically rich hyporheic water can supply limiting nutrients to the surface.
- The author refers to two models of the hyporheic zone: a "surface zone" and an "interactive zone" (Triska et al. 1989b), and an ecotone whose boundaries shift with time and hydrology (Vervier et al. 1992; Gibert et al. 1990).
- The main message is that upwelling and downwelling zones are dynamic entities made up of interacting hyporheic and surface components. Simple characterization of system hydrology would support cross-system comparisons.

### Limitations noted in the text
- Lateral NO3-N patterns may be weakly responsive to diel processes, and the author states that NO3-N in the hyporheic zone is probably too complex to serve as an indicator of surface-hyporheic linkage.
- The reach length is given as 90 m in the abstract and about 100 m in the Methods.
- Several correlations were not significant in other wells, and some piezometer heads were unobtainable because water was lost from interstitial spaces.
