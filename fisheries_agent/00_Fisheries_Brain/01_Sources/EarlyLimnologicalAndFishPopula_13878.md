# Early Limnological and Fish Population Conditions of Canton Reservoir, Oklahoma, and Fishery Management Recommendations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #Cyprinidae #Ictaluridae #limnology

## Executive Summary

This report by Homer Buck and Frank Cross (Oklahoma Agricultural and Mechanical College) documents the first 24 years of limnological and fish population development in [[Canton Reservoir]], a mainstream flood-control impoundment on the [[North Canadian River]] in western Oklahoma. Sampling over roughly two years, starting six months after initial storage, used gill nets, hoop nets, wire traps, seines, poison, and hook and line to collect about 30,000 fishes representing 31 species. Age and growth were determined for ten principal species. The strongest year class and greatest growth for most species occurred in 1948, the first year of impoundment, which the authors attribute to rapid flooding of more than 4,000 acres of vegetated bottomland. Reproduction and growth declined sharply in 1949 and 1950, and angling peaked during rising water levels and in an unusual winter [[Crappie Concentration]] in 1950. The authors recommend waterlevel regulation for periodic refertilization and population control, attempted duplication of the winter concentration conditions, curtailed stocking, revegetation of exposed shoreline, rough fish control, and continued research.

## Background & Description

The study was a project of the [[Oklahoma Fish and Game Council]], with cooperation from the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Oklahoma Game and Fish Department]], and the University of Oklahoma. It was motivated by a widely observed pattern: productivity of old reservoirs is high initially and then declines in fish growth and angling success. Earlier work had concentrated on declining "old lakes"; the Canton study was designed to collect "new lake" data and test preventive controls.

The watershed covers 11,640 square miles, reaching the Rocky Mountain foothills. Preimpoundment, the river flowed over alluvium with outcrops of late Tertiary and Permian formations. The lake basin largely overlies wind-deposited sand dunes, partly stabilized by vegetation. The watershed mean annual temperature is about 56°F, with 21 inches of average annual rainfall (17 to 26 inches across the basin), and about 73 percent falling from early April to late September. A preimpoundment creel census (May 1 to June 30, 1947) recorded 921 fish (488 pounds) taken by 485 anglers, averaging 0.6 fish per man-hour; the catch was 35 percent bream, 31 percent bullhead catfish, 25 percent channel catfish, 6 percent carp, and 3 percent largemouth bass.

Sampling methods included [[Hoop Nets]], [[Gill Nets]], [[Wire Traps]], [[Hardware-Cloth Traps]], seines, and [[Poisoning Sampling]] (derris or cube root, 5 percent rotenone). Hoop nets were judged the most representative gear and served as the benchmark for evaluating selectivity of the others.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake level and cover.** The conservation pool of 1596.5 feet (3,350 acres) was reached July 4, 1948. Peak elevations were 1614.5 feet (May 30, 1949) and 1623.8 feet (August 5, 1950). The May 1949 rise added about 3,000 acres, and the July to August 1950 rise added 4,600 acres, a 75 percent increase. Recession left a denuded area of about 2,200 acres in 1949. Flooded vegetation, including sorghum, corn, and skunk brush, was rapidly removed by dissolution, wave action, and carp activity.

**Water chemistry.** Carbon dioxide was nearly always absent, with 1 to 5 ppm found only on three summer 1949 occasions below temporary thermoclines. The pH ranged from 7.5 to 8.6, usually 8.3 to 8.4. Carbonates averaged 6.8 ppm (0.0 to 13.0), and bicarbonates averaged 138.4 ppm (115 to 179). Both were reduced after high-water periods that flooded vegetated areas.

**Temperature and oxygen.** Summer mid-day surface temperatures ranged from 73.4°F to 80.6°F. Wind circulation generally prevented stratification, but calm periods produced rapid stratification with severe hypolimnetic oxygen depletion; on August 18, 1949, dissolved oxygen fell to 0.2 ppm at 15 to 19 feet. Water temperatures reached freezing briefly in winter.

**Cutoff pools.** Recession isolated pools that were rich in organic debris and supported larval chironomids, mayflies, and dragonfly larvae. Pool fish assemblages varied widely and were not reliable indicators of the lake population.

**Clarity.** Secchi visibility ranged from a few inches to about three feet from June 1949 to August 1950, reaching three to five feet in August 1950 after the flooding-driven clearing.

## Key Findings

**Population and year-class strength**
- About 30,000 fishes were collected, representing 31 species.
- Most species had their largest year classes and greatest annual growth in 1948. The carpsucker was the exception, growing most in 1949.
- The 1948 year class was the product of spawning during a stable 46-day lake level (May 10 to June 27, 1948) over about 1,350 newly flooded acres. The lake area grew from about 1,000 acres on May 1 to 4,000+ acres by July.
- Reproduction in 1949 was much reduced, attributed to fluctuating water levels exposing eggs and nests, increased predation by the abundant 1948 class, and loss of cover. The 1950 spawn was also small.

**Species highlights**
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: Growth was determined from 509 fish across 1947 (77), 1948 (385), and 1949 (47) classes. Maturity appeared mainly at the beginning of the third year. The 1949 class was poorly represented in 1950 (14 fish, versus about 400 of the 1948 class in 1949). Bass were caught mostly as sub-legal yearlings in 1949, with about 95 percent of hook-and-line catches released. Diet was dominated by young gizzard shad, with dragonflies important in August 1950 after flooding.
- [[White Crappie]]: The 1948 class dominated the catch. Of 7,117 white crappie from gill, hoop, and trap collections (32.8 percent of the catch), an estimated 26.4 percent were 1948-class fish. Age and growth were determined for 838 fish. The 1948 class averaged 8.2 inches after two seasons of growth before the winter harvest. Gizzard shad were the principal food.
- [[Black Crappie]]: Only 216 black crappie were taken in nets, about 1 percent of total catch and about one-thirty-third of the white crappie catch, despite heavier stocking. Growth was slower and more variable than white crappie.
- [[Bluegill]]: Hoop nets averaged 21.7 bluegills per summer set. Growth calculations were considered biased by net selectivity, as bluegills larger than about seven inches were rarely captured. Sport fishing yielded about one bluegill per three man-hours.
- [[Green Sunfish]]: Growth (81 fish) was faster than in most other reported areas. The authors judged the species a likely competitor to bass and a future problem.
- [[Common Carp]]: The 1948 year class was about 70 percent of aged fish. Carp growth declined with successive year classes, suggesting population saturation after the first impoundment year. Carp spawning in shallow newly flooded areas in May 1950 produced nearly 90 percent milt-releasing males in poisoned samples.
- [[Northern Carpsucker]]: Catches totaled 1,040 fish (13.1 percent of total catch by weight). The species grew most in 1949, which the authors link to richer bottom conditions for mud-feeders. Carpsuckers were scarce as young, suggesting spawning mainly in the tributary stream.
- [[Black Buffalo]]: Collections (244 fish) were dominated by 1947 and 1948 classes. Buffalo were most effectively sampled by gill nets, and the 1948 class grew more in its second year than the 1947 class.
- [[Channel Catfish]]: Growth was determined by pectoral spine sections for 100 fish. The species was rarely netted, but angling in the flooded river channel in July 1950 produced catches of limits of 15 per line. The authors thought Canton may become a good channel catfish lake.
- [[White Bass]]: Only 22 specimens were recorded (five in 1949, 17 in 1950), all of the 1949 class, suggesting a small population. Their origin was probably natural reproduction. Gizzard shad are a key requirement, and the authors expected the population to grow.
- Other species: [[Spotted Gar]] (common in 1949 gill nets), [[Gizzard Shad]] (abundant as young and primary forage), [[Plains Red Shiner]] (most abundant minnow), and [[Walleye]] (75 stocked in fall 1950, the first introduction in Oklahoma).

**Sampling method evaluation**
- Hoop nets produced the largest and most representative catches, taking 17 species.
- Gill nets were less effective overall (13 species) and strongly size-selective. The 1 1/2-inch mesh caught 960 white crappie in 13 sets in 1949, but only 72 in 12 sets in 1950.
- Wire traps took 15 species. Baiting with dead fish caused bullhead dominance.
- Hardware-cloth traps caught small fish only, and were effective against small channel catfish and young crappie.
- Poisoning in cutoff pools gave inconsistent pictures of lake populations but was the main source of spawning success estimates.

**Winter crappie harvest (1950)**
- Between January 10 and March 19, 1950, an estimated 570,000 legal white crappie were taken from a three-acre boatdock cove. Including the irrigation channel (more than 200,000), the total was about 626,897 fish (approximately 194,338 pounds).
- Roughly 27 pounds per surface acre came from the cove averaged over the lake, and about 39 pounds per acre overall.
- The harvest was almost entirely 1948-class fish. The authors believe it markedly reduced the population and biased later growth estimates downward.
- Likely causes: the cove received a continuous warm seepage inflow from a pond dam, creating a temperature gradient during a cold ice-covered period. This attracted fish after a sudden temperature drop in early January 1950.

**Recommendations**
- Implement a waterlevel manipulation plan to permit periodic lake refertilization and fish population control.
- Attempt to duplicate the conditions that caused the winter concentration.
- Curtail stocking, given that green sunfish, bluegill, and shad populations already appear critically high.
- Revegetate areas exposed by drawdown, such as with horseweed or sandbur grass.
- Develop rough fish control, such as supervised poisoning of carp in spawning shallows.
- Conduct winter fishing investigations and continued research.

**Limnological principle**
- The authors argue that drawdown-and-reflood cycles improve productivity by releasing organic matter from decomposing vegetation, though fertility was partly lost through a 73-day drawdown that exchanged about 380,000 acre-feet of water.
