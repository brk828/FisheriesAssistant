# Estuarine Programs: Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife Interim Report (1967)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #estuarine-ecology

## Executive Summary

This interim report, prepared by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] in January 1967, reviews the Bureau's activities in estuaries during fiscal years 1966 and 1967 and recommends future action. Estuaries are defined broadly to include the mixed sea-freshwater body together with salt meadows, marshes, bays, tidal river reaches, lagoons and sounds. The report frames estuaries as critical habitat for migratory waterfowl and for marine and anadromous game fishes, and argues that a national effort to save estuaries is emerging. In FY 1966 the estuarine programs used 355 man-years and $7.1 million, with FY 1967 showing an increase of about $0.5 million and 7 man-years. The report concludes that estuarine research and management are fragmented, that no comprehensive national estuarine research program exists, and that a national estuarine survey should be initiated.

## Background & Description

**Scope and definition.** The report treats estuaries as an integrated ecosystem of aquatic and terrestrial environments. Estuaries support waterfowl such as ducks, geese, swans, rails and snipe, and serve as nursery grounds for shrimp, menhaden, bluefish, weakfish, croaker and red drum. Oysters, clams and [[Blue Crab]] are estuarine residents, and anadromous species such as [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[Striped Bass]] and shad pass through on migration.

**Bureau missions.** The Bureau's principal missions are conservation of migratory birds, including waterfowl, and of marine and anadromous game fishes. Its programs span:

- **Fishery research:** [[Marine Gamefish Research]] centred on coastal and estuarine zones, with laboratories at Sandy Hook (NJ), Tiburon (CA) and Narragansett (RI), and planned Gulf laboratories at Panama City (FL) and Port Aransas (TX).
- **Wildlife research:** studies of waterfowl, pesticides, control methods and species such as the [[Brown Pelican]] and [[Bald Eagle]].
- **[[National Wildlife Refuge System]]:** 312 units, 78 of them coastal.
- **Water development review, law enforcement, Federal Aid, university cooperative units and pesticide monitoring.**

**Legal framework.** The appendix lists the authorities underpinning the programs, including the [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]] (1918), the [[Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act]] (1934), the [[Migratory Bird Conservation Act]] (1929), the [[Pittman-Robertson Act]], the [[Dingell-Johnson Act]], the [[Anadromous Fish Act]] (1965), the [[Federal Power Act]] (1920) and the Study of Migratory Game Fish Act (1959).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report identifies several threats to estuarine habitat: channelization, industrial and real estate development, hurricanes, river flow regulation, marsh drainage for mosquito control, and pollution from many sources. Dredging and filling for industrial, commercial and real estate projects, particularly under Corps of Engineers permits, are described as damaging estuarine resources extensively. Declining and polluted habitat and estuarine complexes "drastically altered by man" are cited as main factors in refuge needs.

Pesticide concerns are central. Declining or at-risk species include the bald eagle, osprey and brown pelican, with black duck, herring gull, white pelican, Fulvous tree duck, black-bellied tree duck and cormorant also under study.

The report notes that the reasons for rising public concern include visible estuarine degradation, high economic, recreational and aesthetic values, and conflicts among competing public and private interests.

## Key Findings

- **Growing recreational pressure:** Salt-water fishing participation grows about 35 percent every five years, faster than population growth or inland fishing. There were 8.3 million salt-water anglers in 1965.
- **Refuge estate:** Of 312 refuge units, 78 are coastal and 42 contain significant estuarine areas. Estuarine acreage by region: Gulf coast 306,925 acres on 11 refuges (about 45 percent of the total); Alaska 226,584 acres on 9 refuges; Atlantic coast 145,227 acres on 20 refuges; Pacific coast 3,393 acres on 2 Washington refuges. The coastal refuges total more than 18 million acres and 682,000 estuarine acres, with over 500 miles of shoreline.
- **Acquisition needs:** About 300,000 acres of coastal and estuarine land and water are needed for migratory birds, and 30,000 acres for rare and endangered species. Estuarine refuge management operates at only 66 percent of normal.
- **Waterfowl enforcement:** Of 132 migratory game bird species, 54 are waterfowl. Waterfowl management costs $288,000 and 20.5 man-years annually. Over 50 percent of the enforcement staff conducts estuarine patrols. The Division maintains 33 aircraft for surveys.
- **Water development:** Of 173 estuarine-related studies in FY 1966 and 1967 (about $400,000 and 27 man-years per year), 60 are on the Pacific coast and 113 on the Gulf and Atlantic coasts. The Corps of Engineers is the construction agency for 159 projects (92 percent).
- **Federal Aid:** Only about 5 percent of Dingell-Johnson funds are allocated by States to estuarine and marine inshore efforts, mainly due to a shortage of matching funds and because license revenues are rarely generated from coastal fishing. About $350,000 (Federal share) goes to estuarine and marine projects through Dingell-Johnson, and about $130,000 to anadromous fish in estuaries in FY 1967.
- **Cooperative units:** Of 18 Cooperative Wildlife Research Units, 7 are in coastal States, and 2 do estuarine work. Of 23 Cooperative Fishery Units, 12 are coastal and 4 are engaged in estuarine studies (FY 1966: 13 man-years and $34,000; FY 1967: 12 man-years and $30,000). Studies include American eider breeding in Penobscot Bay, winter flounder and tautog in the Weweantic River Estuary, and fish ecology on Sapelo Island, Georgia.
- **Pesticide monitoring:** Estuarine work is minimal, confined to refuges in New Jersey and an Air Force Base in Florida. About 10 percent of available funds go to estuaries. The Bureau's program samples 14 estuaries twice monthly.
- **Budget summary:** Research was $1.342 million (FY 1966) and $1.567 million (FY 1967). Coastal refuges were $4.503 million and $4.737 million. Water development review, law enforcement and Federal Aid are also tabulated. Total FY 1966 expenditure was $7.182 million.
- **Recommendations:** The report recommends eight actions: a comprehensive national survey of estuarine fish and wildlife; delineation of areas needing protection (possibly through an "estuarine authority"); Bureau-wide ecological and experimental management studies on coastal refuges; expanded refuge recreation programs; accelerated estuarine marine gamefish research; a greater Federal Aid share for estuarine-dependent species; interagency inventories; and joint programs with the [[National Park Service]] in coastal parks and seashores.
- **Gaps identified:** Estuaries have not been treated separately in Federal or State research, management, acquisition or development goals. The report states that "a comprehensive estuarine research program for fish and wildlife is nonexistent," although interagency coordination is generally adequate.
