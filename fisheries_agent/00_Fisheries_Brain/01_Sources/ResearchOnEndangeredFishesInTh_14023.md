# Research on Endangered Fishes in the National Parks, with Special Emphasis on the Devils Hole Pupfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae #Cyprinidae #ConservationBiology #DesertFishes

## Executive Summary

This 1976 conference paper (Deacon & Deacon) synthesizes research on native and endangered fishes in two contrasting U.S. National Park Service units: the **Colorado River system in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]**, characterized by torrential, silt-laden, temperature-fluctuating mainstream habitat, and the isolated desert spring/marsh systems of **[[Death Valley National Monument]]** and **Ash Meadows, Nevada**, home to highly endemic, thermally- and osmotically-adapted pupfishes (genus *Cyprinodon*). The paper's central conservation case study is the **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon diabolis*), which occupies what may be the smallest habitat in the world containing an entire vertebrate species' genome — a single limestone cavern pool. Groundwater pumping for agriculture in the 1960s–70s lowered water levels in Devils Hole, causing measurable population crashes, which prompted a landmark permanent injunction (upheld by the U.S. Supreme Court) mandating minimum water levels to protect the species. The authors present quantitative relationships between water level, light/primary productivity, and pupfish population size, and discuss thermal physiology and dam-related habitat alteration for Colorado River native fishes.

## Background & Description

### Colorado River Mainstream Fishes (adapted to torrential, silt-laden currents)
- **Humps** (most extreme in [[Humpback Chub]], *Gila cypha*) — hypothesized to aid stability on the substrate in strong currents.
- **Fusiform bodies with slender caudal peduncles and expansive fins** (most extreme in [[Bonytail Chub]], *Gila elegans*) — maximize swimming power in swift current.
- **Large body size** — [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is the largest minnow in the world, exceeding 80 lbs.
- **Reduced/embedded scales and small eyes** — typical adaptation to silt-laden stream conditions.
- **Bizarre/humped body form** in the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), associated with less torrential river reaches and reservoir persistence.

### Death Valley Pupfishes (genus *Cyprinodon*)
- Small-bodied cyprinodontids adapted to extreme, fluctuating salinity and temperature.
- Distinct populations/subspecies (*C. diabolis*, *C. milleri*, *C. nevadensis* subspecies) show strong morphological and physiological differentiation correlated with isolation in individual spring systems.
- Breeding coloration develops in males during summer but is suppressed above ~35–38°C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Grand Canyon / Colorado River System
- Native fishes ([[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]]) evolved in a warm, turbid, high-velocity mainstream environment.
- **[[Glen Canyon Dam]]** operation cooled summer tailwater temperatures from ~20°C (1968) to ~10°C after Lake Powell reached full pool, restricting introduced species (carp, red shiner, green sunfish) more than natives, which appear more cold-tolerant or reliant on tributary spawning refugia (e.g., the lower [[Little Colorado River]] for *Gila cypha*).
- Native fishes compete primarily with warm-water exotics moving upstream from [[Lake Mead]] rather than with managed cold-water sport fisheries.

### Death Valley Pupfish Habitats
- Extreme habitat variability: winter ice films to summer temperatures of ~43°C (109°F); salinities ranging from low to several times seawater.
- **[[Devils Hole]]**: a disjunct limestone cavern unit of Death Valley National Monument (designated 1952), water temperature nearly constant at ~33°C, *C. diabolis* restricted to the photic zone (occurs to 26 m depth but concentrated near surface); minimal sunlight penetration due to ~23 m rock overhang creates strong seasonal variation in primary productivity.
- **[[Saratoga Springs]]** marsh and main spring pool habitats for *C. n. nevadensis*: marsh temperatures fluctuate widely (8–44°C), main pool remains stable (26.5–31.0°C).
- **[[Crenichthys baileyi]]** and **[[Crenichthys nevadae]]** (Goodeidae) show comparable activity restriction under high temperature/low dissolved oxygen conditions in warm springs.
- Extinction case study: **[[Empetrichthys latos pahrump]]** went extinct in 1958 when irrigation withdrawals dried its only spring habitat (Pahrump Ranch, Nevada); the surviving subspecies *E. l. latos* nearly suffered the same fate in 1975 at Manse (Bowman) Ranch before transplant populations were established.

## Key Findings

- **Thermal tolerance**: Critical thermal maxima (CTM) for *C. diabolis*, *C. milleri*, and *C. nevadensis* populations range ~42–44°C (Brown & Feldmeth 1971; Otto & Gerking 1973; James 1968); long-term survival limit ~38°C; low-temperature tolerance ~2°C.
- **Diel activity**: *C. n. nevadensis* shows a bimodal (morning/evening) activity pattern under moderate temperatures (15–38°C); activity ceases below ~7°C and is reduced/erratic above 38°C, with brief voluntary excursions up to 44°C.
- **Reproduction and thermal limits**: Eggs of *C. n. nevadensis* develop at constant temperatures between 20–36°C (not at 18° or 38°C) but tolerate fluctuating regimes (e.g., 30–38°C, 20–28°C, 28–36°C) (Shrode, 1974). Territorial defense and breeding coloration cease above 38°C. Critical thermal maxima for ovulation are narrower than for general survival (Gerking, pers. comm.); two-month-old juveniles acclimated to 36°C show CTM ~44°C, higher than adults — implying a mortality sequence under thermal stress: eggs first, then adults, with juveniles most resistant.
- **Devils Hole population dynamics**:
  - Historic minimum water level recorded 20 September 1972 (1.19 m below USGS reference point), coinciding with a population crash from a near-record high.
  - Habitat manipulation (gravel/rubble removal, supplemental lighting) temporarily boosted population to ~250 by January 1973.
  - A June 5, 1973 permanent injunction (Special Water Master appointed) mandated water levels not fall below 1.01 m below reference point; from July 1976 levels were managed to remain above 0.91 m (actual levels reached ~0.82 m).
  - Population rebounded to ~400 individuals by 24 September 1976.
  - Authors recommend a **minimum viable population of 200** and a **desirable maximum of ~400** individuals.
- **Statistical relationships**:
  - Water level vs. maximum population size: r = 0.86, R² = 0.73 (1967–76 data); refined dataset (1972–76 only): r = 0.81, R² = 0.65. A water level of ~0.73 m below reference point predicts a population maximum of ~400.
  - Light intensity/duration (2-month lag) vs. population size: r = 0.76, R² = 0.57 (Fig. 9).
  - Primary productivity index (1-month lag) vs. population size: r = 0.86, R² = 0.75 (Fig. 10); a productivity index of 5.47 g O₂/day predicts a summer population maximum of ~400.
  - Surface area/volume vs. water level (Fig. 11): at 0.73 m below reference, ~90% surface coverage/51% volume; at 0.82 m, ~85% coverage/38% volume; at 0.91 m, ~70% coverage/25% volume; at 1.01 m, ~40% coverage/16% volume — supporting a management target near 0.82 m (≈200–400 fish).
- **Legal/conservation significance**: The Devils Hole pupfish case (Federal Judge Roger Foley's 1974 injunction, upheld by the U.S. Supreme Court) is cited as a landmark precedent establishing federal water-use priority for endangered species protection under the [[Endangered Species Conservation Act]] (16 U.S.C. 668aa), reinforcing the National Park Service's role in balancing water allocation between agricultural/human use and ecological/qualitative values.
- **Broader implication**: Dam-regulated thermal regimes in the Colorado River (e.g., below Glen Canyon Dam) may differentially disadvantage introduced warm-water species relative to natives, suggesting management potential to enhance native fish populations via thermal regime manipulation — an area flagged for further research alongside the metabolic costs of osmoregulation and digestion under thermal/osmotic stress in pupfish.
