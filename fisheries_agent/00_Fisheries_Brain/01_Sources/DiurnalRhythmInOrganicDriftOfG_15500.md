# Diurnal rhythm in 'Organic Drift' of Gammarus pulex

## Executive Summary
This note synthesizes a 1963 *Nature* communication by K. Müller describing a **diurnal periodicity in the downstream "organic drift" of the freshwater amphipod [[Gammarus pulex]]**, sampled continuously over nine months (May 1962–January 1963) in the Breitenbach brook, a tributary of the River Fulda, West Germany. The study demonstrates that drift is not a passive hydraulic phenomenon but an **active behavioral rhythm** tied to the light–dark cycle, corroborating earlier findings on [[Baetis rhodani]] and [[Baetis vernus]] (Ephemeroptera). Two unrelated but co-published short communications are appended: an observation of **shell deformities in [[Mytilus edulis]]** populations inhabiting power-station intake culverts, and a report on **warfarin resistance in wild rat populations** (the latter outside the aquatic scope but included in the original source).

## Background & Description
- **Gammarus pulex**: No detailed morphometric data given beyond its identity as a freshwater amphipod (Crustacea: Amphipoda); behavior rather than morphology is the focus of this paper.
- **Mytilus edulis (deformed specimens)**: 
  - Deformity Type 1: Posterior valve edges diverge permanently, leaving siphons exposed even when valves are closed (example specimen: 1.9 cm shell length).
  - Deformity Type 2: Valves diverge, then re-fuse, producing a raised "hump" on the shell (example specimen: 2.4 cm shell length).
  - Deformity prevalence: **2% of 47,450 mussels** examined over 10 months in the Brighton 'B' Generating Station intake culvert; similar deformities also documented at Marchwood Generating Station.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
### *Gammarus pulex* – Freshwater Lotic Drift Behavior
- Habitat: Breitenbach brook, sampling point ~500 m downstream from the source.
- Drift collected at both sunrise and sunset from filtered water volumes (35 m³ May–Oct; increased to 170 m³ Nov–Jan to prevent catching-device freezing).
- **Drift activity was consistently higher at night than during the day** across all nine months sampled (see Table 1 data below).
- Fine-scale (2-hour interval) sampling in August, October, and December 1962 confirmed that **drift maxima occur exclusively during the nocturnal phase**, regardless of seasonal changes in day/night length.
- The number of nocturnal maxima (2 or 3 peaks) appears **determined by night length**; the timing of these peaks relative to midnight or dusk/dawn shifts seasonally.
- **Light intensity acts as the behavioral "time signal"**: half-hourly measurements (17:00–20:00) show a direct correlation between **decreasing light intensity at nightfall and increasing drift** (Fig. 4, actinograph correlation).
- Follow-up work (cited but not detailed here) indicates that **temperature primarily governs the height of the nocturnal drift maxima**, while it has less influence on the daily minimum drift level.
- This behavioral pattern parallels earlier work by the same author on [[Baetis rhodani]] and [[Baetis vernus]], reinforcing that **stream invertebrate drift is an active, rhythmic behavior** rather than passive downstream displacement — a concept building on foundational work by [[Dendy 1944]], [[Wolff 1947]], and [[Waters 1961]].

### *Mytilus edulis* – Fouling Community in Anthropogenic Culverts
- Habitat: Concrete intake culvert (10 ft square cross-section), seawater pumped directly from the sea; salinity 27–31‰; water velocity 0–8 ft/sec; depth 4–8 ft.
- Absence of **tidal fluctuation** and **low predator density** (due to high water velocity) distinguishes this habitat from natural intertidal/subtidal mussel beds.
- Hypothesis: Continuous, uninterrupted feeding (valves rarely closing due to lack of predation/tidal stimuli) permits **abnormal, asymmetric shell growth**, producing the observed deformities.
- Ecological implication: Such deformed mussels are likely **unable to survive intertidal exposure** and would be **vulnerable to predation** if relocated to natural subtidal populations, suggesting these deformities are essentially confined to artificial, predator-free culvert environments.

## Key Findings
1. **Drift of *Gammarus pulex* is behaviorally driven, not passive**, with nocturnal drift consistently exceeding diurnal drift across nearly all months sampled (Table 1, May 1962–Jan 1963).
2. **Nocturnal drift maxima number (2–3 peaks) tracks night length**, and their timing shifts seasonally relative to midnight/twilight — indicating a light-entrained circadian mechanism.
3. **Decreasing light intensity at dusk is the immediate behavioral trigger ("Zeitgeber")** for the onset of increased drift activity, demonstrated via correlation with actinograph light readings.
4. **Temperature modulates the magnitude of nocturnal drift peaks**, but not the daytime minimum, suggesting distinct proximate controls for peak versus baseline drift levels.
5. This work extends findings previously established for [[Baetis rhodani]] and [[Baetis vernus]], supporting a broader generalization that **diel drift periodicity is a widespread behavioral trait among lotic freshwater invertebrates**.
6. In an unrelated marine observation, **anthropogenic, predator-free, non-tidal culvert habitats induce shell deformities in *Mytilus edulis*** at a measurable rate (2%), illustrating how engineered aquatic environments can alter mollusk growth morphology independent of natural selective pressures (predation, tidal stress).
