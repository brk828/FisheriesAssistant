# Chemical Characteristics of New York Farm Ponds

#limnology #water-chemistry #farm-ponds #aquatic-ecology #nutrient-dynamics

## Executive Summary

This study examined the chemical characteristics of 48 farm ponds in central [[New York]] during 1953-1954, documenting substantial chemical diversity across these artificial impoundments. Total alkalinity ranged from 5 to 226 p.p.m., with concentrations varying considerably within individual ponds in response to agricultural runoff and biological activity. The research measured temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, alkalinity, total dissolved solids, phosphorus, and nitrogen content, providing baseline data for understanding fish production potential in these agricultural water bodies. Ponds ranged from 0.2 to 4.0 acres with maximum depths of 5 to 12 feet, supplied primarily by runoff from agricultural lands.

## Background & Description

The study focused on 48 artificial impoundments located within a 60-mile radius of [[Ithaca]], New York. These farm ponds represented typical agricultural water bodies in the region, with water supplied primarily by runoff from agricultural lands and to some extent by ground water. The ponds varied in surface area from 0.2 to 4.0 acres with maximum depths ranging from 5 to 12 feet.

Chemical analyses included field measurements of temperature, dissolved oxygen (using sodium azide modification of the [[Winkler Method]]), and pH (using Hellige color disc comparator). Laboratory analyses within 12 hours of collection measured total alkalinity, free ammonia (by nesslerization following distillation), organic nitrogen (by [[Kjeldahl Method]]), phosphate (by ceruleomolybdic method), total phosphorus (after oxidation with nitric acid), and total dissolved solids (by evaporation). All phosphorus and nitrogen tests were run in duplicate with allowable difference of 0.01 p.p.m. between duplicates.

Eight ponds were sampled periodically at approximately 6-week intervals from September 1, 1953, to August 18, 1954, for detailed seasonal monitoring of temperature, oxygen, and pH at both surface and bottom waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The chemical diversity of central New York farm ponds reflected the underlying geology and soil types of the region. Total alkalinity concentrations were highest in the [[Lake Plains]] region bordering [[Lake Ontario]] and generally low in the Southern Tier counties adjacent to Pennsylvania, reflecting the lime content of underlying soils.

Summer thermal stratification was often pronounced but seldom persisted continuously more than 2 weeks. Temperature differences between surface and bottom waters averaged 2.4°F in four unfertilized ponds and 4.0°F in four fertilized ponds. The greater stratification in fertilized ponds was attributed to increased plankton turbidity resulting in absorption of more radiant energy in surface waters. [[Secchi Disc]] transparencies averaged 27 inches in fertilized ponds compared with 49 inches in unfertilized ponds.

Surface temperatures reached individual pond maxima of 84°F at the surface and 79°F at the bottom. Oxygen concentrations in surface waters were usually near saturation during the ice-free period. Temporary oxygen deficiencies occurred in bottom waters of two thermally stratified fertilized ponds in September 1953 and summer 1954, coinciding with plankton pulses. Supersaturation of oxygen occasionally occurred in subsurface water when transparent water permitted light penetration to weedbeds on the bottom.

The pH range was 7.0 to 9.5 for both surface and bottom waters. Values of 8.5 to 9.0 were common during summer while values of 7.2 to 7.8 generally prevailed in winter.

## Key Findings

**Total Alkalinity Dynamics:**
- Total alkalinity in 48 central New York ponds ranged from 5 to 226 p.p.m., demonstrating considerable chemical diversity
- In 13 ponds with at least seven analyses over 6- to 12-month periods, the range in alkalinity was 42 to 92 percent of the mean alkalinity
- Total alkalinity concentrations were often low in summer and high in winter, possibly resulting from biogenic precipitation of carbonates during summer and return to solution in winter when assimilation is limited by low water temperature
- Minimum alkalinity concentrations were recorded in March and April 1954, coinciding with inflow of considerable surface runoff
- Surface runoff from Dunkirk and Ontario soils at Geneva, New York, contained calcium equivalent to alkalinity of 6.2 to 29.0 p.p.m., substantially lower than typical pond concentrations of 80+ p.p.m.

**Total Dissolved Solids:**
- Total dissolved solid concentration in 42 central New York ponds ranged from 50 to 420 p.p.m.
- Strong correlation between total dissolved solids and alkalinity (r=0.84, P>0.01)
- Correlation of r=0.42 (P>0.01) existed between total alkalinity and noncarbonate substances, indicating waters high in carbonates are generally high in other solutes
- Changes in total dissolved solids within a pond were not directly correlated with fluctuations in total alkalinity
- Both alkalinity and dissolved solid concentrations were consistently higher in December than in February, but the ratio of dissolved solids to alkalinity did not remain constant

**Phosphorus Concentrations:**
- [[Pond 33]] showed high total phosphorus concentrations in January (0.76 p.p.m.) and February (0.70 p.p.m.) 1953, resulting from influx of surface water from a watershed fertilized with manure
- Composite samples of runoff water contained phosphate concentrations of 0.36-0.80 p.p.m. and total phosphorus of 0.86-1.76 p.p.m. during January-March
- Between late June and August 8, 1953, total phosphorus in [[Pond 33]] increased from 0.13 p.p.m. to 0.96 p.p.m. despite negligible runoff, indicating large exchange of phosphorus between bottom sediments and seston
- Most phosphorus in August was bound up in dense phytoplankton bloom
- Phosphorus concentrations in February were uniformly high compared with December values across multiple ponds
- During May-September, fertilized ponds (n=13) averaged 0.16 p.p.m. phosphate and 0.33 p.p.m. total phosphorus (range 0.00-0.38 and 0.07-0.60 p.p.m., respectively)
- Unfertilized ponds (n=13) averaged 0.03 p.p.m. phosphate and 0.14 p.p.m. total phosphorus (range 0.00-0.12 and 0.02-0.72 p.p.m., respectively)

**Nitrogen Concentrations:**
- Normal ammonia concentration in unfertilized ponds ranged from 0.00 to 0.16 p.p.m.
- Fertilized ponds showed ammonia concentrations up to 0.45 p.p.m., well below the 2.5 p.p.m. considered toxic to fish
- During May-September, fertilized ponds (n=11) averaged 0.26 p.p.m. ammonia and 1.14 p.p.m. organic nitrogen (range 0.10-0.45 and 0.51-1.80 p.p.m., respectively)
- Unfertilized ponds (n=8) averaged 0.07 p.p.m. ammonia and 0.36 p.p.m. organic nitrogen (range 0.00-0.27 and 0.28-0.45 p.p.m., respectively)
- Organic nitrogen concentrations were lower in winter than summer, with less pronounced differences between fertilized and unfertilized ponds
- [[Pond 33]] showed peak organic nitrogen concentration of 3.63 p.p.m. during a plankton pulse
- Dissolved organic nitrogen in surface runoff from Ontario and Dunkirk soils varied between 1.0 and 9.5 p.p.m.

**Management Implications:**
- Chemical diversity of central New York ponds (alkalinity 5-226 p.p.m., total dissolved solids 50-420 p.p.m.) suggests proper fish management may necessitate classification of ponds based on solute content
- Runoff has pronounced transitory influence on solute concentration: generally reduced total alkalinity and total dissolved solids, and increased phosphorus concentrations
- Fertilization of pond watersheds can cause marked increase in nutrient concentration of pond water
- Bottom sediments exert profound influence on phosphorus concentration, with exchangeable fraction potentially of primary importance in determining fertility of shallow impoundments
