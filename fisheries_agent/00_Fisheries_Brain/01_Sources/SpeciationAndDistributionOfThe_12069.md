# Speciation and Distribution of the Crayfishes of the Ozark Plateaus and Ouachita Provinces

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This monograph-style study by Austin B. Williams revises the freshwater crayfish fauna of the mountainous Ozark Plateaus and Ouachita Provinces of [[Missouri]], [[Arkansas]], [[Oklahoma]] and southeastern [[Kansas]]. The region holds 31 known species and subspecies in three genera of the subfamily [[Cambarinae]]: *Procambarus*, *Orconectes* and *Cambarus*. The fauna is dominated by endemics, with incomplete invasion by widespread species from surrounding areas. The [[Hylas Group]] of *Orconectes* has diversified strikingly and forms four centers of endemism. The study combines taxonomic keys, distribution maps, ecological notes and faunal interpretation. Although the paper is a crayfish study rather than a fish study, it covers the aquatic ecosystem of clear, rocky streams, caves and burrow habitats.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** The work grew from a 1946 study of Kansas crayfishes, which suggested that the distribution of the Kansas fauna might be explained by relationships to the east in the Mississippi Valley. The Ozark and Ouachita regions were then surveyed, with extensive fieldwork in summer 1948.
- **Scope:** Five components: systematic position, diagnostic characters, geographic ranges, explanation of present distribution, and notes on ecology and life history.
- **Limitations:** Earlier surveys were incomplete, field work was concentrated in late summer (possible seasonal bias), and cave and burrowing species were under-sampled.
- **Taxonomic framework:** Hobbs' 1942 revision split the former genus *Cambarus* into six genera. Four occur in the study area: [[Procambarus]], [[Cambarellus]], [[Orconectes]] and [[Cambarus]]. Older names such as *Faxonius* are superseded.
- **Key characters:** The first pleopod (gonopod) of form I males and the annulus ventralis of females are the most reliable taxonomic structures. Hooks on the ischiopodites are considered unreliable for relationships because they arose independently in divergent stocks. Rostrum shape, areola width, chela shape and antennal scale vary with environment and burrowing habit.
- **Life history:** Only *Orconectes immunis* (studied by [[Tack]] 1941) and a few others had sustained life-history work. Data on *O. immunis* were summarized by [[Williams and Leonard]] (1952).
- **Physiography:** The Ozark Plateaus include the [[St. Francois Mountains]], [[Springfield Plateau]], [[Salem Plateau]] and [[Boston Mountains]] (summit about 2,250 ft). The Ouachita Province includes the Ouachita Mountains and the Arkansas Valley.
- **Methods:** Field collecting used dip nets, seines, spades and rock turning. Specimens were preserved in 5% formalin, then transferred to 75% alcohol. Specimens were catalogued in the [[University of Kansas Museum of Natural History]], with additional material from the USNM, MCZ, University of Michigan and University of Oklahoma.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Nearly all species in the region inhabit swift, clear, rocky streams. Some occupy deep, muddy pools, ditches or ponds. Many live under rocks or in shallow burrows.
- **Ecological types:**
  1. *Primarily burrowing* forms, such as *Procambarus gracilis* and *Cambarus diogenes*.
  2. *Secondary burrowers* living under rocks in lentic or lotic water, which make up most of the fauna.
  3. *"Rock crawlers"*, small depressed species that live under rocks or in gravel near shoals. *Cambarus hubbsi* is the most extreme example.
- **Cave fauna:** Cave species such as *Cambarus setosus* (eyes reduced, unpigmented) were found in clear, cold caves in southwestern Missouri, including [[Smallins Cave]].
- **Drainage relationships:** The Spring and Eleven Point rivers, the Black and St. Francis rivers, and the [[Arkansas River]] appear to act as biogeographic barriers or corridors.
- **Parallels:** All three genera show ecological parallels, mainly in secondary burrowing under rocks.

## Key Findings

1. **Species count:** 31 known species and subspecies in three genera (*Procambarus*, *Orconectes*, *Cambarus*). Procambarus has 4 species (plus subspecies), Orconectes has the majority (about 23 taxa including subspecies), and Cambarus has 3 species.
2. **Endemism:** The fauna is dominated by endemic crayfishes. Widespread species have invaded only incompletely. Examples of invaders include *P. simulans* and *P. gracilis* from the west and centre, *P. blandingii acutus* from the southeast, and *O. nais* and *O. immunis* from the north.
3. **Hylas group:** Contains eleven species and subspecies and presents "a remarkably complete phylogenetic assemblage." Its most primitive member is *O. eupunctus*, which shows affinities to the propinquus assemblage of the Ohio Valley.
4. **Four centers of endemism:**
   - St. Francois Mountains, eastern Missouri (*O. peruncus*, *O. quadruncus*, *O. hylas*).
   - Eleven Point and Spring rivers, southeastern Missouri and northeastern Arkansas (*O. eupunctus*, *O. marchandi*).
   - Four-state area of Arkansas, Kansas, Missouri and Oklahoma (*O. nana nana*, *O. nana macrus*).
   - Central Ouachita Mountains (*O. leptogonopodus*, *O. menae*, with the endemic *P. tenuis*).
5. **Relationships to the east:** The regional fauna shows definite relationships with the fauna east of the Mississippi Embayment. The two areas were probably faunistically continuous at one time.
6. **Former connectivity:** The Arkansas River was probably less of a barrier in Pleistocene times, allowing the hylas group to enter the Ouachita Mountains from the north.
7. **Distribution of *O. neglectus neglectus*:** The subspecies now has a discontinuous range, with its main range in the four-state area and an isolated population in Wabaunsee County, Kansas. Habitat loss from silting after prairie settlement may have eliminated it from many areas.
8. **Ecological equivalents:** The three genera have developed comparable ecological types, with secondary burrowing under rocks as the main parallel.
9. **Conclusions:** Isolation and discontinuous distribution are signs of primitiveness, following Ortmann. Some species have crossed headwater divides, possibly through stream piracy or wandering by crayfish.
