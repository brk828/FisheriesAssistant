# Effect of Water Hyacinths and Fertilization on Fish-Food Organisms and Production of Bluegill and Redear Sunfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #freshwater-ecology #aquatic-plants #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1967 experimental pond study at Auburn University investigated how [[Water Hyacinth]] (*Eichhornia crassipes*) rafts, combined with two fertilization regimes (0-8-0 and 8-8-0 N-P-K), affected fish-food macroinvertebrate communities and the resulting production of [[Bluegill Sunfish]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) and [[Redear Sunfish]] (*Lepomis microlophus*). Eighteen 0.1-acre ponds were assigned to six treatment combinations (fertilized/unfertilized × hyacinth/no-hyacinth), each replicated three times. Contrary to the initial hypothesis that hyacinth roots would enhance invertebrate habitat and boost fish yield, fertilized ponds with hyacinths produced markedly less fish biomass than fertilized ponds without hyacinths — despite (or because of) lower macroinvertebrate abundance in the latter. The study concludes that water hyacinths compete with phytoplankton for nutrients, reducing the plankton-based food chain, and that hyacinth roots do not adequately compensate as an alternative food source under fertilized conditions.

## Background & Description

While this paper is primarily an ecological/production study rather than a morphological one, relevant biological notes include:

- **Bluegill Sunfish** (*Lepomis macrochirus* Rafinesque): stocked as 1–3 inch fingerlings; selected as the primary experimental species due to well-documented feeding on macroinvertebrates.
- **Redear Sunfish** (*Lepomis microlophus* [Günther]): co-occurred as a contaminant in the bluegill fingerling supply from the National Fish Hatchery (Marion, Alabama); difficult to distinguish from bluegill at small size, so both species were stocked and analyzed together.
- Fish ≥3 inches in length at stocking were assumed non-reproductive recruits (used to calculate percentage survival, distinguishing stocked individuals from reproduced offspring).
- Pre-stocking treatment: formalin bath at 20 ppm for 3 hours to remove external parasites.
- Stocking density: 4,000 fingerlings/acre (mixed bluegill + redear).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Structure
- Ponds: 60 × 72.5 ft, sandy clay substrate, sloping banks, depth ~1.5–6 ft.
- [[Water Hyacinth]] rafts: 22 × 22 ft enclosures anchored with pine board stakes, covering ~10% of pond surface area (484 ft² / 45 m² of a 4,356 ft² / 405 m² pond); initially stocked at one-third capacity (~1,400 plants) to allow fertilizer-driven growth.
- "Edge" defined as open water between plant roots allowing light penetration — a key variable linked to invertebrate habitat quality.

### Treatment Design (18 ponds total, 3 replicates each)
| Treatment | Hyacinths | No Hyacinths |
|---|---|---|
| 0-0-0 (control) | 3 ponds | 3 ponds |
| 0-8-0 fertilization | 3 ponds | 3 ponds |
| 8-8-0 fertilization | 3 ponds | 3 ponds |

- Fertilizer applied at 100 lbs/acre per application, 10 applications total (biweekly, later extended to 4-week intervals after a May plankton bloom).
- N source: ammonium nitrate (33.5%); P source: triple superphosphate (54% P).

### Trophic/Food-Web Dynamics
- 24 distinct macroinvertebrate taxa collected from hyacinth roots (Table 1), spanning Platyhelminthes, Annelida, Mollusca (Gastropoda, Pelecypoda), Acarina, Crustacea (Ostracoda, Copepoda, Cladocera, Amphipoda), and Insecta (Ephemeroptera, Hemiptera, [[Odonata]], Coleoptera, Trichoptera, Lepidoptera, Diptera).
- In **control (0-0-0) ponds**: snails (Gastropoda) dominated biomass, followed by odonate nymphs, then amphipods and midge (chironomid) larvae.
- In **fertilized ponds (0-8-0, 8-8-0)**: snails and odonate nymphs were not important; overall invertebrate numbers and biomass were suppressed, likely due to heavy predation by stocked sunfish and reduced "edge" habitat from denser hyacinth growth.
- Two competing food chains identified:
  1. **Fertilizer → phytoplankton → zooplankton → fish** (dominant in non-hyacinth ponds; associated with dark green "good" plankton blooms).
  2. **Fertilizer → hyacinth roots → macroinvertebrates → fish** (dominant pathway in hyacinth ponds; associated with poor, light-green-to-clear plankton blooms, suggesting nutrient competition between hyacinths and phytoplankton).
- Hyacinths likely reduced fish production by (a) competing with plankton for nutrients, and (b) reducing "edge" habitat available to fish-food organisms as root mass increased under fertilization.

## Key Findings

1. **Fertilization suppressed hyacinth-associated invertebrate biomass over time**: Early season (May) samples showed *higher* invertebrate numbers in fertilized (0-8-0, 8-8-0) ponds vs. control, but this reversed as the season progressed — control ponds showed increasing invertebrate numbers/biomass while fertilized ponds declined, attributed to heavy fish cropping and loss of root "edge."

2. **Statistical significance**: Fertilization, sampling date, and their interaction significantly affected both macroinvertebrate numbers and dry weight (highly significant, p<0.05 implied). Due to significant interaction effects, monthly (not seasonal) comparisons were required.

3. **Amphipods and midge larvae** showed no significant difference in dry weight between control and fertilized treatments — remained scarce across all treatments.

4. **Fish production patterns** (Table 2, at draining):
   - **Control (0-0-0) ponds**: fish production was approximately equal between hyacinth and non-hyacinth ponds.
   - **0-8-0 ponds**: non-hyacinth ponds had standing crop 2.5× and net production 3.0× greater than hyacinth ponds.
   - **8-8-0 ponds**: non-hyacinth ponds had standing crop 2.1× and net production 2.5× greater than hyacinth ponds.
   - Raising fertilization from 0-8-0 to 8-8-0 increased standing crop by ~20 lbs/acre and net production by ~28 lbs/acre in non-hyacinth ponds; increases were smaller in hyacinth ponds (~19 and ~22 lbs/acre respectively).

5. **Survival rates**: Percentage survival averaged higher in non-hyacinth ponds (76%) vs. hyacinth ponds (61%) across all fertilizer treatments.

6. **Inverse relationship between invertebrate abundance and fish yield**: Ponds with the greatest macroinvertebrate numbers/biomass (0-0-0 hyacinth ponds) paradoxically had the *lowest* fish production — attributed to possible undesirable food quality, sampling error, lack of complementary plankton, or sparse root habitat in unfertilized hyacinths. Conversely, high fish production with low invertebrate counts in fertilized hyacinth ponds suggests heavy, undetected predation cropping the population before sampling (Figure 3).

7. **No correlation** was found between wild fish biomass and bluegill/redear biomass, suggesting minimal interspecific competition effects from incidental wild fish.

8. **Overall conclusion**: Water hyacinths did **not** complement standard fertilization practices for increasing [[Centrarchid]] production in Gulf Coast pond aquaculture under these experimental conditions. The transplantation of hyacinths from natural habitats (Lake Seminole, GA) may have caused loss of naturally associated fish-food organisms, further limiting their functional value in new pond systems.

9. **Confounding factor**: Because ponds were newly constructed, low organic matter accumulation in pond bottoms may have limited benthic food-organism habitat independent of hyacinth/fertilizer treatments, potentially depressing overall production baselines.
