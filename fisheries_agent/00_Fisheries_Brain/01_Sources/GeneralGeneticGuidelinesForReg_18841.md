# General Genetic Guidelines for Region 2 Fish Hatcheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hatchery-genetics #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This 1993 technical document from the [[San Marcos National Fish Hatchery]] provides comprehensive genetic management guidelines for federal fish hatcheries in Region 2. The document addresses growing concerns that hatchery operations may threaten wild fish populations through genetic degradation, inbreeding, and loss of genetic diversity. It establishes protocols for broodstock management, distinguishes between limited-geographical and wide-geographical genetics management strategies, and recommends minimum breeding population sizes to maintain genetic integrity. The guidelines mandate that all Region 2 hatcheries develop production plans maintaining appropriate genetic integrity for each cultured stock by September 30, 1994.

## Background & Description

Federal fish hatcheries serve multiple purposes: restoration of extirpated populations, augmentation of declining wild stocks, artificial genetic refuges for [[Endangered Species]], and development of recreational fisheries. However, critics have raised concerns that hatcheries produce "mongrel fishes" ill-adapted to specific water systems and may represent "the greatest single threat to wild fishes."

The document identifies three primary genetic problems in hatchery operations:

1. **Bottlenecking**: Genetic variation reduced due to small hatchery population sizes that cannot completely represent wild populations
2. **Inbreeding**: Matings between closely related fish (e.g., siblings) leading to reduced vigor, lower fertility, and physical deformities
3. **Selection pressure**: Both intentional (for large size) and unintentional (for domestic behavior) selection reduces genetic variation and may select for detrimental traits like reduced disease resistance

The document distinguishes two management categories:

**Limited-geographical genetics management** applies to populations with distinct genetic characteristics, typically native populations including endangered and threatened species, or strains adapted to limited geographic ranges (sometimes as restrictive as individual drainages). Examples include [[Fountain Darter]], [[Yaqui Catfish]], and [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]]. These fishes should have genetic makeups almost identical to corresponding wild stocks.

**Wide-geographical genetics management** applies to populations without distinct genetic characteristics, resulting from natural selection pressure, migration and straying, or history of indiscriminate stock manipulation. Examples include many populations of [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Striped Bass]], and [[Paddlefish]]. Fish cultured for wide distribution should have genetic makeup representing variation across all receiving waters.

Some species do not fit neatly into either category. [[Florida Largemouth Bass]], originally having limited geographical distribution but now scattered throughout the country, should be cultured to maintain within-strain genetic diversity where management goals include maintaining introduced populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document emphasizes that where self-sustaining native fish populations still exist, hatchery production should supplement rather than dominate wild populations. Knowledge of genetics in both wild populations and hatchery stocks is required to meet this objective.

Critical information for developing genetics plans includes:
- Source(s) and numbers of initial stock
- Number of generations maintained and breeding methods used
- Number of individuals in each generation
- Separability of fish (marks)
- Sex ratios of all fish
- Ideally, a genetics profile of on-hand fishes

The document stresses that producing and maintaining fit and genetically appropriate fishes requires adequate lead times and clear understanding of expected products. The identity of cultured fishes and their destination waters should be "tightly woven together for an ecologically suitable hatchery program."

## Key Findings

**New Broodstock Development Requirements:**
- Minimum of **50 successfully-spawning pairs** of non-closely related fish required for each genetically distinct group (species, strain, etc.)
- Collect broodstock from entire area where wild population(s) occur, assuming geographic distance relates to genetic distance
- Collect fish of various sizes and shapes over entire spawning period
- Do not cull healthy fish

**Alternative Approaches:**
When collection and maintenance of 50 pairs is impractical, wild stocks may be used directly (e.g., [[Paddlefish]], [[Striped Bass]]). Attempts should still be made to collect gametes from as many pairs as possible during the entire spawning period.

**Broodstock Maintenance:**
Unless broodstock are replaced with wild fish periodically, hatchery broodstock must be maintained from original stock. The document recommends **rotational line crossing** in which three or more breeding lines are kept for each distinct group, with offspring produced between lines for a given year class (Figure 1). Each line should ideally be composed of at least 50 pairs of adults. This method reduces the rate of inevitable inbreeding.

Occasional crossings of hatchery stock to unrelated fish (preferably wild) is an effective and perhaps less tedious method to reduce inbreeding. Breeding different year-classes from the same broodstock is NOT an effective means for reducing inbreeding.

**Production of Fish for Stocking:**
- Ideally, hatchery broodfish should be paired and isolated for breeding
- Unequal sex ratio promotes unequal genetic influence of one gender
- Non-isolation of pairs can lead to some individuals dominating matings
- The recommended minimum of 50 pairs assumes all paired breeders contribute equally to offspring genetics
- More fish (at least several hundred) needed if sex ratio is unequal or if paired fish are not isolated
- If no effort is made to pair and segregate brooders, minimum of **1000 fish** may be sufficient

**Wild Fish as Direct Brooders:**
Ideally, wild fish used directly as brooders should be paired and their gametes isolated at least until after fertilization to maximize effective population size and prevent either gender from having larger genetic influence on offspring.

**Implementation Mandate:**
Production plans maintaining appropriate genetic integrity for each stock of fish cultured at each federal hatchery in the Region must be developed by **September 30, 1994**. Plans should be practical, usable blueprints developed at individual hatcheries and reviewed by the regional geneticist at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]].
