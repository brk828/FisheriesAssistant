# Additional introductions of tropical fishes to southern Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a multi-article excerpt from *The Southwestern Naturalist* (Vol. 9, No. 4, December 1964). It is mostly a fish paper. A short opening fragment and a literature list come from an earlier paper on the thermal and oxygen tolerance of [[Crenichthys Baileyi]]. The main article, by Hubbs and Deacon, reports the spread of exotic fishes into desert springs of southern Nevada. Two further articles cover unusual sex ratios in [[Perognathus]] pocket mice and vocalizations of [[Spizella]] sparrows, and the excerpt of the latter is only partial. The central message is that introduced [[Mosquitofish]] and mollies are spreading quickly through spring systems and threaten endemic fishes, including [[Moapa Dace]], whose survival depends on tolerance of high temperature and low oxygen.

## Background & Description

The excerpt contains three distinct parts:

- **Opening fragment (earlier paper, pp. 245–248):** Experiments on [[Crenichthys Baileyi]] and [[Preston Fishes]] tolerance of high temperature and low dissolved oxygen. The text is cut off mid-sentence, and the methods section is absent. The reference list includes [[Hubbs and Hettler]] (1964), who studied Crenichthys tolerance.
- **Main article (pp. 249–251):** "Additional Introductions of Tropical Fishes into Southern Nevada" by Clark Hubbs (University of Texas, Austin) and James E. Deacon (University of Nevada, Las Vegas). The article reports new records of exotic cichlids and mollies in springs of southern Nevada.
- **Other articles (pp. 252–296, partial):** "Unusual Sex Ratios in Winter and Spring-Taken Pocket Mice" by Donald F. Hoffmeister, and the opening of "Voice Structure in *Spizella*" by L. Irby Davis. Neither is a fish study.

The literature cited section lists classic works on fish ecology and physiology, including [[Sumner and Lanham]] (1942) on respiratory metabolism of warm- and cool-spring fishes, [[Kopec]] (1949) on the ecology and breeding of Crenichthys baileyi, [[Deacon et al.]] (1964) on effects of introduced fishes, and [[Miller and Hubbs]] (1960) on the spiny-rayed cyprinids of the Colorado River system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spring environments and water chemistry**
The desert springs studied are warm, oxygen-variable systems. Dissolved oxygen at one site ranged from 4 to 5 ppm at night and 6 to 8 ppm during the day. In a pool at Warm Springs Ranch, [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) avoided water below 3.5 ppm. At night, when oxygen was lower, these fish stayed in the surface film. Endemic [[Crenichthys Baileyi]] and [[Moapa Dace]] (*Moapa coriacea*) swam freely near the bottom.

**Oxygen and temperature thresholds of exotics**
At Ash Spring, the exotics *Mollienesia latipinna*, *M. mexicana*, and *G. affinis* were not collected in water below 3 ppm dissolved oxygen. [[Convict Cichlid]] (*Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*) was not collected below 5 ppm. Native [[Preston Fishes]] could not be acclimated to Mormon Spring water, which supports the earlier conclusion that they cannot withstand high temperatures.

**Habitat threats and refugia**
Most introduced fishes were found in lotic (flowing) water. The lower Moapa River was free of mollies for about 6 miles of its 95-mile length. The mollies were otherwise widespread through the stream system, except in a few spring source ponds. These ponds and their outflows form a restricted refugium for [[Moapa Dace]], which is known only from the headwaters of the Moapa River.

**Regional fish community**
In the Pahranagat Valley, native fishes at risk include [[Pantosteus Intermedius]], [[Gila Robusta]], [[Rhinichthys Osculus]], and Crenichthys baileyi. The authors consider *Lepidomeda altivelis* already exterminated there.

**Spread and regulation**
The Nevada Game and Fish Commission restricts the transport of live bait, and local officials are active in enforcing this rule. The authors note that the movements occurred where transfer laws exist.

## Key Findings

**Exotic fish introductions (Hubbs and Deacon, 1964)**

- *Cichlasoma severum* was recorded as a second cichlid from Rogers Spring. The authors state that it had not previously been reported as successfully introduced into the United States.
- Three exotics (*Mollienesia latipinna*, *M. mexicana*, and *Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*) were moved more than 80 miles within 15 months.
- Removal attempts at Rogers Spring on 8 December 1963 failed because some exotics escaped.
- On 12 October 1963, many *M. mexicana* were collected from the Home Ranch on the Moapa River system, 30 airline miles northwest of Rogers Spring. Local residents said the mollies were introduced in winter or spring 1963 at the ranch yard. Migration through Lake Mead was judged unlikely.
- At Ash Spring near Alamo, Nevada, *G. affinis* was the only exotic seen in March 1963. On 3 June 1964, both mollies and *C. nigrofasciatum* were collected there with established breeding populations of adults and young. The site lies 60 airline miles north-northwest of Home Ranch, with no permanent connection, so the authors conclude these fish were deliberately introduced, probably from Rogers Spring.
- The absence of *C. severum* at Ash Spring is explained by its preference for deeper water, which makes it harder to catch.
- Earlier work by Deacon et al. (1964) showed decline of [[Crenichthys Baileyi]] with *G. affinis*. [[Empetrichthys Latos]] also declined but later increased after dense vegetation returned to the spring pond.

**Thermal and oxygen tolerance**

- Native [[Moapa Dace]] is considered likely to tolerate higher temperatures and lower oxygen than the introduced mollies (Hubbs and Hettler, 1964). This is the basis for concern about competition in lotic habitats.
- Males of an endemic population were held in water at temperatures up to 36°C and could be chased to 38°C. Dissolved oxygen was 4 to 5 ppm at night and 6 to 8 ppm by day. Distribution was unaffected by a 10°C difference in spring temperatures.

**Pocket mice sex ratios (Hoffmeister, pp. 252–254; not fish)**

- Of 225 January–May records of *Perognathus amplus* from the Southwest, 97.3% were males. From January through the first week of April, only 2 of 209 specimens were females. Overall, females made up 2.7%.
- All 11 *P. apache* collected in April were males, though the sample is small.
- Female percentages were approximately 41% for *P. longimembris*, 32.5% for *P. flavus*, 26.3% for *P. penicillatus*, and 28.5% for *P. intermedius*. For *P. intermedius*, only 14.3% of January–February specimens were female.
- The author suggests the male bias may reflect female hibernation, rearing young below ground, or trap-shyness. The cause is not established.

**Sparrow vocalizations (Davis, pp. 255–296; not fish)**

- The excerpt gives only the introduction and early species accounts of *Spizella arborea* (Tree Sparrow).
- Saunders (1933) reported frequencies of about 3900 to 4300 cps in four examples.
- Borror (1961) reported fundamental frequencies from about 3500 to over 7000 cps and song lengths of 2 to 2.5 seconds.
- The abstract states that all species have distinctive vocalizations and that individuals use phrases different from one another.
