# Sediment Transport from Big Sagebrush Watersheds

#ecology #hydrology #artemisia-tridentata

## Executive Summary

This study measures suspended sediment and bedload transport from two perennial-flow [[Big Sagebrush]] watersheds, [[Loco Creek]] and [[Sane Creek]], in south-central Wyoming. The work was conducted at the [[Stratton Sagebrush Hydrology Study Area]] over seven years (1968 to 1974 record, with sediment data mainly 1970 to 1975). Sediment movement is concentrated in the [[Snowmelt Runoff]] period. The highest sediment concentration, 862 ppm, occurred during an [[Over-Snow Runoff]] event in 1973. The author concludes that the watersheds' sediment yields are low and comparable to forested subalpine lands, but that unusual high-runoff years raise sediment movement regardless of watershed stability.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** Land management decisions aim to keep erosion near a geologic or "natural" rate. Representative natural sedimentation rates for small watersheds in major rangeland types were poorly known. These measurements provide sedimentation data for one location in the big sagebrush type.

**Study area.**
- Location: 32 km (20 miles) west of Saratoga, Wyoming, at 2,377 m (7,800 ft) elevation.
- Annual precipitation: about 530 mm (20.9 in.), with 55 percent falling as snow (the source reads "§5"; this is interpreted as 55 percent).
- Summer (June to September) rainfall: about 114 mm (4.5 in.).
- Terrain: rolling, maximum sideslope relief 100 m (328 ft), most slopes under 20 percent.
- Soils: developed in place from sandstone of the Brown's Park Formation, loam or sandy loam in the A and B horizons. Infiltration and soil stability are good.
- Vegetation: [[Mountain Big Sagebrush]] (*Artemisia tridentata* subsp. *vaseyana*) on moister, deeper-soiled sites. [[Wyoming Big Sagebrush]] (*A. t.* subsp. *wyomingensis*) on drier, shallower upland sites.
- Herbaceous production, including current sagebrush growth: over 1,120 kg/ha (2,060 lb/acre) for mountain big sagebrush stands; about 672 kg/ha (600 lb/acre) for Wyoming big sagebrush.

**Experimental watersheds.**

| Watershed | Area |
|---|---|
| [[Loco Creek]] | 664 ha (1,639 acres) |
| [[Sane Creek]] | 238 ha (587 acres) |

Both have perennial flow. Each is gauged with a [[Stream Gage]] consisting of a [[Stilling Pond]] upstream of a concrete cutoff wall with a 120° V-notch weir. Each station has a PS-67 [[Pumping Sampler]] that collects a 400 mL water sample at preselected times. Samples are filtered through 45 µm membrane filters, and sediment residue is determined by weighing. Bedload is measured each fall by an elevational survey of the stilling ponds.

**Flow regime.** Both creeks have similar annual flow patterns. Channels are snow-covered in winter. Baseflow of about 0.05 × 10⁻² m³/s per km² (0.05 cfs per square mile) comes solely from groundwater. Snowmelt runoff begins in late March or April and lasts about 105 days on Loco Creek and 55 days on Sane Creek. No severe convectional thunderstorms occurred at the Stratton site in seven years of gauge operation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stream setting.** The Loco Creek gage lies 1.6 km (1 mile) downstream of a spring that sustains perennial flow. Water travels three to four times that distance because the creek meanders. The Sane Creek spring is only 45 m (148 ft) above its gage, and during snowmelt surface flow extends upslope above the spring. Both channels are well vegetated and stable, with 1 to 2 percent slopes on lower reaches.
- **Sediment comparability.** Sediment in Sane Creek is generally lower than in Loco Creek, except during unusually high snowmelt runoff. Suspended sediment discussion is therefore focused on Loco Creek.
- **Sampler calibration.** V-notch dip samples averaged slightly lower sediment content than pumped samples. The 1974 regression correlation was 0.99 for Loco Creek and 0.81 for Sane Creek. The regression corrected pumped-sample values to sediment discharge over the weir. Sane Creek 1973 values were not corrected because too few samples existed for regression.
- **Over-snow flow.** This occurs when inflow exceeds the rate at which water can percolate through dense snow filling the channel. Water then flows over the snow surface and erodes a channel into it. Conveyance losses are low, so water moves quickly off the watershed. Sediment is retained partly within the snowpack and deposited in the channel as the snow melts.

## Key Findings

**1. Winter regime.** Suspended sediment was low on Loco Creek from October 1 to March 31 (1974–75 winter, one sample daily).
- Average: 6 ppm; range: 1 to 28 ppm.
- Below 11 ppm 89 percent of the time.
- 72 percent of samples fell between 3 and 6 ppm.

**2. Snowmelt regime.** Two samples per day were taken on Loco Creek (morning at minimum flow, evening at maximum flow).
- Maximum: 296 ppm on May 7, 1974, one day before the yearly hydrograph peak.
- 13 evening samples exceeded 100 ppm; no morning sample exceeded 100 ppm.
- A strong diurnal pattern persisted until mid-June (for example, 65 ppm morning vs. 296 ppm evening on May 7; 87 vs. 289 ppm the next day).
- Most yearly sediment transport occurs during snowmelt, when high flow and high concentration coincide.
- Sediment content fell faster than streamflow receded. Concentrations were below 30 ppm by June 20 and about 20 ppm by the end of June, even though discharge stayed high.

**3. Summer regime.** Suspended sediment declined steadily to below 10 ppm by the end of September.
- Highest summer value: 72 ppm, on a day with 23 mm (0.91 in.) of rain; otherwise values stayed below 40 ppm.
- Distribution (incomplete record, because the sampler was inoperative for about half the period, especially late August and September): above 30 ppm 3 percent of the time; 21 to 30 ppm 21 percent; 11 to 20 ppm 57 percent; below 11 ppm 16 percent.
- No convectional storms occurred in the 6 preceding study years.

**4. Over-snow flow events.** The highest runoff rates and sediment levels in seven years occurred at Sane Creek during over-snow events, in 1970 and 1973.
- Causes: a late, cold, cloudy spring that limited melt; shallow windward snowpacks; a warm, cloud-free May; and a southwest to southeast slope exposure that increased solar input.
- Snow water equivalent on windward slopes on April 15, 1973 was about 89 mm (3.5 in.). An additional 129 mm (4.69 in.) of precipitation fell during the rest of the month.
- Maximum daily flows: about 5.1 × 10⁻² m³/s per km² (4.7 cfs per square mile) in 1973. The 1970 value is garbled in the source. Usual snowmelt rates are below 2.2 × 10⁻² m³/s per km² (2 cfs per square mile).
- Suspended sediment reached 862 ppm at the weir in 1973 and exceeded 50 ppm for 5 days. In 1974, a normal year, it stayed below 20 ppm throughout runoff.
- Much of the sediment came from soil eroded from the channel sides by concentrated flow along the snow–channel interface, which caused localized bank cutting. Bank erosion volume was not estimated.

**5. Bedload deposition** (fall survey of stilling basins, 1970–74; yields in 10⁻² m³/km² per year):
- Loco Creek: 132 to 388 (121 to 355 cu ft per sq mile).
- Sane Creek: 9 to 196 (8 to 179 cu ft per sq mile).
- Both weirs recorded higher deposition in over-snow years. At Sane Creek, deposition in 1970 and 1973 was 12 and 6 times the average for years without over-snow flow.

**6. Comparison with other watersheds.**
- [[Leaf 1966]] reported average bedload deposition of 269 and 183 (10⁻² m³/km²) at two undisturbed subalpine watersheds on the [[Fraser Experimental Forest]], Colorado.
- [[Rich and Thompson 1974]] reported 0 to 280 (10⁻² m³/km²) for pine-fir and mixed conifer watersheds in Arizona.
- The sagebrush values fall within this range, so the author describes them as comparable to forested subalpine lands.

**7. Extreme-event context.** The author cites the [[Black Hills Flood]] of June 9 and 10, 1972 (Orr, 1973), which devastated Rapid City, South Dakota, as an example of how high-runoff events increase sediment movement regardless of watershed stability. Good vegetative cover and soil stability can still reduce sediment yield in extreme years.

**8. Conclusions.**
- Loco and Sane Creeks may not represent much of the big sagebrush type. They lie in the higher-precipitation zone, have productive vegetation reflecting past sound management, and have stable soils.
- Erosion from intense summer rain is important elsewhere in the type but was not a factor during the seven study years at Stratton.
- Sediment transport is concentrated in snowmelt, and over-snow flow is described as "a unique hydrologic phenomenon" that elevates sediment movement.
- Future work will test big sagebrush and snow management practices at Stratton for their effects on water yield and sedimentation.
