# Provenance of Quartzite Clasts in the Eocene–Oligocene Sespe Formation: Paleogeographic Implications for Southern California and the Ancestral Colorado River

#geology #sedimentology #paleogeography #provenance

*Note: This document is a geological research article rather than an ichthyological or aquatic-ecology study. No fish, invertebrate, or aquatic taxa are addressed, so the fish-specific fields are marked N/A.*

## Executive Summary

This [[Geological Society of America]] Bulletin paper (Howard, 2000) uses petrography to determine the provenance of quartzite clasts in fluvial [[Sespe Formation]] conglomerates of the Santa Monica Mountains, Los Angeles trough. Clasts were compared with candidate source rocks in the Mojave Desert, Sonora Desert and [[Transition Zone]] provinces. The study concludes that the Sespe paleoriver was a bifurcating system drawing on both Mojave Desert and Sonora Desert sources. After correcting for Neogene displacement on the [[San Andreas Fault]], the Sespe paleodelta lies roughly where the mouth of the [[Colorado River]] would have been in the Eocene, which suggests the Colorado River may be much older than previously thought (possibly as old as late Paleocene).

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Corral Canyon section, in the central Santa Monica Mountains about 47 km west of Los Angeles and 8 km northwest of Malibu Beach, is about 1300 m thick and comprises three informal members. It is part of the only fault-bounded outcrop belt in the range where both lower and upper contacts are exposed.
- **Lithology:** The lower and middle members are conglomeratic, with granitoid, gneiss, quartzite and silicified metavolcanic clasts. Quartzite makes up as much as 35% of clasts. Granitoid clasts reach 100 cm in diameter.
- **Depositional setting:** Braided and meandering streams in an alluvial coastal plain, late middle Eocene to early Miocene.
- **Age:** No internal age control exists. The section is tentatively assigned to the Eocene and Oligocene, based on lithological similarity to strata near Simi Valley beneath a late Uintan fauna, and on a regional intraformational unconformity.
- **Terminology:** Following Krynine (1948) and Jackson (1997), "quartzite" is treated as a quartzose rock with little or no porosity, either orthoquartzite (diagenetic, depositional texture preserved) or metaquartzite (metamorphic recrystallization). Microfabric (mortar, foam, porphyroblastic texture) distinguishes metamorphic grade.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The source material is geological rather than biological, so no fish, invertebrate or aquatic habitat data are reported. The aquatic relevance is indirect and paleogeographic:

- Sespe conglomerates are part of a series of westward-draining [[Paleogene Paleorivers]] that developed as Laramide deformation migrated inland, forming a broad alluvial plain across southern California and the Basin and Range.
- The text links the Pliocene–Pleistocene inland fish faunas of Death Valley and modern Colorado River species to a possible fluvial or estuarine connection (Hubbs and Miller, 1948; Blackwelder, 1954). The author notes later workers attributed these faunas to avian transport, while [[Hershler et al. 1999]] DNA evidence supports a firm link between inland faunas and Colorado River species.
- The modern Colorado River is thought to have developed in the late Miocene or Pliocene, after headward erosion from the [[Gulf of California]] rift captured an older upstream course.

## Key Findings

1. **Orthoquartzite provenance:** Sespe orthoquartzite clasts are mainly quartzofeldspathic and derived from craton-interior sources, probably the [[Stirling Quartzite]] and [[Wood Canyon Formation]] in the northern Mojave Desert. Black clasts best match the Stirling Quartzite; red clasts resemble the Wood Canyon Formation.
2. **Quartzolithic clasts:** Clasts with abundant detrital jasper are probably from the [[Mazatzal Peak Quartzite]] of central Arizona, the only known jasper source being the Yavapai iron formation.
3. **Metaquartzite provenance:** Metaquartzite clasts are mainly from southern Mojave Desert sources. Some may come from the [[Yavapai Terrane]] of central Arizona, but no colloform Yavapai-type grains were found in the Sespe conglomerates.
4. **Dual provenance:** The mixed sources indicate the Sespe paleoriver was a bifurcating system with a Sonora Desert branch (informally "Gila paleoriver") and a Mojave Desert branch (informally "Amargosa paleoriver").
5. **Clast counts and petrography:** One hundred quartzite clasts were counted at each of three stratigraphic positions. Stratigraphic color changes (black and white metaquartzite, then red orthoquartzite, then black orthoquartzite) suggest several changes in provenance during deposition, though their regional significance is unresolved.
6. **Heavy minerals:** Orthoquartzite clasts contain a stable assemblage of rounded apatite, tourmaline, rutile, zircon and opaque oxides, consistent with long transport from craton-interior sources. Medium- to high-grade metaquartzite assemblages show well-developed crystal faces from recrystallization.
7. **Paleogeographic reconstruction:** After about 315 km of cumulative dextral slip on the southern San Andreas Fault since 23.5 Ma, Yuma, Arizona would have lain directly east of the Los Angeles area in the Eocene–Oligocene. The Sespe delta therefore coincides spatially with the inferred ancestral Colorado River mouth.
8. **Implication for Colorado River age:** If the river originally drained the Mojave Desert before stream capture, the Colorado River may be much older (possibly late Paleocene) than the late Miocene–Pliocene age usually accepted.
