# Indarri Falls Travertine Dam, Lawn Hill Creek, Northwest Queensland

#ichthyology #marine-biology #travertine-geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This short communication describes [[Indarri Falls]], a travertine dam impounding the middle reaches of [[Lawn Hill Creek]], a perennial karst stream draining the [[Barkly Tableland]] in northwest Queensland, Australia. The dam is at least 13.5 m high, which the authors describe as the largest feature of its kind known in Australia. Carbonate precipitation is favoured by downstream changes in the bulk chemistry of karst spring waters, and biological activity (notably cyanobacteria, algae and caddis fly larval nets) appears to enhance deposition at the microenvironmental scale. The dam has transformed the middle reaches from fluvial to lacustrine conditions. The note also aims to add to the sparse literature on semi-arid and tropical travertine, which has been studied mainly in temperate regions.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Indarri Falls (18°41'S, 138°29'E) lies where Lawn Hill Creek crosses from the Barkly Tableland, developed on Cambrian [[Thorntonia Limestone]], onto the [[Carpentaria Lowlands]]. The creek has cut a gorge through Proterozoic [[Constance Sandstone]]. Extensive ancient and modern travertine occurs for at least 10 km in and below the gorge.
- **Dimensions:** The exposed wall is only c. 2.5 m high, but the base lies at least 1 m below the water surface, giving a total height of at least 13.5 m.
- **Morphology:** The arcuate planform is typical of travertine dams. In section the dam has three elements: an upstream-dipping ramp, a horizontal overhang of at least 2 m, and a pseudo-plunge pool below the Falls. Flow over the crest is confined to five narrow overflow zones for most of the year, beneath which fan-shaped carbonate lobes form.
- **Submerged microreefs:** Earlier, drowned dam crests at the rear of the Falls form submerged [[Microreefs]] on the shallow ramp, abandoned as the active crest advanced downstream.
- **Terminology:** The authors note a confusing range of classification schemes for travertine forms (waterfalls, cascades, barrages, stream-bed dams).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spring chemistry and carbonate precipitation**
- Two surface springs (about 4 km upstream) were just saturated with respect to calcite, with dissolved CO2 several hundred times atmospheric levels.
- CO2 outgassing on emergence raises the water to supersaturation, so conditions for precipitation are reached by the time the water reaches the Falls. Deposition is greatest at turbulent sites where CO2 release is highest.
- Four sampling dates (June 1992 to April 1994) are tabulated for pH, CO2 partial pressure and calcite saturation index. Creek water above the Falls consistently had higher pH (roughly 7.6 to 7.7) and positive saturation indices (about 0.68 to 0.82), compared with spring values (pH about 6.6 to 6.9).

**Biological controls**
- Travertines are typically rich in microflora, especially [[Cyanobacteria]].
- Larvae of [[Caddisfly Larvae]] build linear nets on travertine surfaces to filter food particles. These nets act as nucleation substrates and increase surface roughness, enhancing turbulence, outgassing and accumulation.
- The submerged dam face hosts algae (including diatoms), bacteria and cyanobacteria, which provide a framework for calcite encrustation at about 3 m depth.
- Microflora and microfauna persist year-round under perennially warm conditions. Local deposition therefore tracks small-scale hydrological changes and organism life cycles, not seasons as in temperate travertines.

**Weathering and erosion**
- Soil on dormant crest areas supplies CO2, driving dissolution, karren formation and case hardening.
- Root growth mechanically weathers the travertine, and floods can remove loosened material, scour the crest and break off carbonate lobes. The authors judge mechanical erosion minor for what is essentially a constructional feature.

**Hydrology**
- Maximum recorded depth upstream of the dam is 14 m. Upstream of the Cascades, about 1.5 km downstream, water depth exceeds 27 m at one point.
- Fossil travertine drapes gorge walls up to almost 3 m above present water levels, indicating higher stream levels during earlier accretion episodes.
- Overflow points shift periodically, through differential accretion, debris damming or crest scour.

**Palaeoenvironmental significance**
- Travertine formation in a neighbouring catchment began at least 30 ka BP and continued through the Last Glacial Maximum ([[Drysdale and Head 1994]]).
- At [[Riversleigh]], about 40 km south-southeast, carbonate deposition began as early as the Oligo-Miocene.

## Key Findings

- [[Indarri Falls]] is at least 13.5 m high and is the largest travertine dam of its kind known in Australia.
- Downstream CO2 outgassing from karst springs drives supersaturation and precipitation, with greatest deposition at turbulent overflow points.
- Biological factors, including cyanobacteria, algae and [[Caddisfly Larvae]] nets, enhance microenvironmental carbonate deposition and accumulation rates.
- Growth is fastest on active, turbulent dam faces. Impounded microreef sections accrete at less than 0.4 mm per year, up to two orders of magnitude slower.
- The dam has converted the middle reaches of Lawn Hill Creek from fluvial to lacustrine conditions, with maximum depths of 14 m and above 27 m locally.
- Mechanical erosion is minor compared with constructional growth.
- Tropical travertine shows year-round biological deposition, unlike seasonally controlled temperate deposits.
