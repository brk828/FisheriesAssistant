# Bonytail Chub Recovery Plan: Reference Compilation and Narrative Stepdown (1980-1987)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service document, dated April 12, 1987, combines two components for the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), a Cyprinidae fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: a bibliography of roughly 40 references (1980-1986) and the working narrative and stepdown plan of the bonytail recovery effort. The species is described as the most endangered fish in the basin. No self-sustaining wild population is known. The plan's first priority is preventing extinction through [[Refugia Management]], habitat protection, [[Hatchery Propagation]] and [[Stocking Program]] activities, and enforcement of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Life-history research, especially on spawning, is a stated gap to be filled.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), one of the [[Colorado River Endangered Fishes]] alongside the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- **Status:** Described as "the most endangered species in the Colorado River Basin." Individuals are still occasionally captured, and captive stocks are maintained, but no self-sustaining population is documented.
- **Wild population structure:** Few fish remain in nature. Studies indicate they are old and apparently not reproducing, so the wild stock will decline through natural mortality.
- **Known life-history gaps:** The document states that "little is known about this species, particularly in riverine habitat." Spawning requirements are poorly understood, and differences between riverine and reservoir requirements must be documented.
- **Identification:** Taxonomic verification of specimens, including larval and juvenile identification (Muth et al. 1985; Snyder 1981), is needed because of similarity among *Gila* species.
- **Document scope:** The bibliography spans reports from the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]], Utah Division of Wildlife Resources performance reports (Radant, Heggen), Wyoming Game and Fish Department reports, and hatchery culture papers by R.L. Hamman and others.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality and habitat tolerance.** Cited work by Bulkley et al. (1982) and Bulkley & Pimentel (1983) examined habitat parameters and total dissolved solids concentrations that endangered Colorado River fishes prefer or avoid. The narrative links water quality and flow criteria to federal consultation on projects that may affect the species.

**Early life stages.** Marsh (1985) tested incubation temperature effects on embryo survival in native Colorado River fishes. Snyder (1981) and Muth et al. (1985) provided larval identification guides, which the plan treats as necessary for distinguishing bonytail from related species.

**Reservoir and riverine habitat.** The plan notes that bonytail requirements may differ between riverine and reservoir environments. [[Lake Mohave]] is one of the key sites. Bozek et al. (1984) studied factors affecting reproductive success of bonytail and [[Razorback Sucker]] in Lake Mohave.

**Named habitat for protection.** The narrative identifies these areas as possibly essential to bonytail and deserving full protection under the Endangered Species Act:
- [[Lake Mohave]]
- [[Dinosaur National Monument]]
- [[Desolation Canyon]]
- [[Gray Canyon]]

Removing bonytail from these areas for recovery purposes is stated not to affect designated critical habitat.

**Threats.** The plan names competition and impacts from nonnative fish stocking as threats. Task 2.61 calls for discontinuing introductions of nonnative species that may harm bonytail.

**Disease.** Flagg (1982) surveyed diseases in Colorado River fishes, and Berry (1984) reported hematology data for four rare species. The document provides no numerical results for either.

## Key Findings

### 1. Prevent Extinction in the Wild (Priority 1)

- Immediate action under the Endangered Species Act is needed because no self-sustaining population is known.
- **Refugia (1.1):** All available fish should be placed in refugia to serve as broodstock.
  - The existing refugium is at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in the Lower Basin (New Mexico).
  - A second refugium is needed in the Upper Basin to guard against losing broodstock to a catastrophic event. A potential site is [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]].
- **Holding facilities (1.13):** Temporary tanks should be placed in each river reach where fish are likely to be found.
- **Obtaining wild fish (1.14):** A mechanism should capture fish reported by anglers and researchers.
- **Transport and quarantine (1.15):** Workers must be prepared to hold and transport captured fish, and quarantine procedures must be enforced.
- **Verification (1.151):** Fish received at refugia must be inspected by qualified personnel and photographed against a cm grid. Each fish is tagged and its origin recorded.

### 2. Protect Habitat and Enforce Law (Task 1.2 and 2.6)

- Full protection is recommended for the four named habitat areas.
- **Nonnative species (2.61):** Stocking of competing nonnative species should be discontinued if studies show a negative impact.
- **Enforcement (2.62):**
  - Agencies should be informed of their management and enforcement responsibilities (2.621).
  - Federal agencies should comply with Section 7 of the ESA and consult with the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] on projects involving federal permits or funds (2.622). Water quality and flow criteria can then be applied.
  - Current regulations and management should be assessed for effectiveness, with additional regulations drafted or protection increased where needed (2.623).

### 3. Augment Populations with Hatchery-Reared Fish (Task 1.3)

- Wild-caught fish held in refugia will serve as broodstock.
- Propagation techniques must be refined (1.311), and broodstock spawned using the best available stock (1.312).
- Fish should be reared to Age 1 or larger before stocking (1.32).
- Stocking sites and rates must be designated (1.33), then stocked (1.34).
- Survivorship must be confirmed after stocking (1.4).

### 4. Fill Life-History Gaps (Task 3)

- **Spawning requirements (3.1):** Reproduction is poorly known. Spawning and survival of young must be documented in both riverine and reservoir habitats.
- Scientific use should be maximized for any specimens that are accidentally killed or die during studies.

### 5. Initiate Recovery Later (Task 2.0)

- Recovery activities can only be developed after the threat of extinction is removed.

### Document Status

The narrative appears to be a working or stepdown draft within the broader recovery effort. It is organized by numbered tasks, and some task numbers are garbled in the source (for example, "1.21 - insert original 2.62 - 2.623" and duplicated task labels). The references include a draft 1984 bonytail recovery plan and a 1986 multi-species recovery implementation program, which the narrative builds on.
