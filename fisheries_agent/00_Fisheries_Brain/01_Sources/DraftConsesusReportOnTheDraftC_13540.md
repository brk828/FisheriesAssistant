# Draft Consensus Report on the Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program Conservation Strategy

#conservation-biology #riparian-ecology #endangered-species #habitat-restoration #peer-review

## Executive Summary

This consensus report presents findings from a scientific peer review panel convened by the American Institute of Biological Sciences (AIBS) to evaluate the draft Conservation Strategy for the [[Lower Colorado River]] Multi-Species Conservation Program (LCR MSCP). The panel met July 12, 1999 in Las Vegas, Nevada to assess a conservation strategy prepared by Ogden Environmental and Energy Services Company. The strategy addresses 11 species (3 fish, 6 bird, 2 plant) across 22 geographic opportunity areas along the LCR. The panel found fundamental flaws in the approach, noting the plan is "very much a selective species conservation plan" incompatible with current scientific thinking, rather than a true multi-species ecosystem approach. The document proposes adding 12,000-16,000 acres of riparian vegetation but lacks detailed implementation plans, funding mechanisms, or timelines. Critical concerns include the apparent prioritization of water and power development over species recovery, inadequate attention to habitat restoration processes, and failure to address root causes of species decline including non-native species impacts and altered hydrology.

## Background & Description

The LCR MSCP represents the most recent in a series of planning efforts concerning preservation, conservation and management of riparian habitats along the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The Conservation Strategy document under review was prepared for the steering committee to guide development of the full conservation plan. The volume contains five main sections with three appendices covering: philosophical framework, species conservation goals for 11 target species, 22 geographic opportunity areas for implementation, and recommendations for native fish, marsh species, riparian scrub woodland and narrow endemic plants.

Target species include three fish ([[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]), six birds ([[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[California Black Rail]], [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Western Yellow-billed Cuckoo]], [[Bald Eagle]], [[American Peregrine Falcon]]), and two plants. The strategy focuses on the 100-year floodplain of the LCR from the international boundary with Mexico northward.

The stated objectives are to: (1) conserve habitat and work toward recovery of included species pursuant to the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) and reduce likelihood of additional listings; (2) accommodate current water diversions and power production and optimize opportunities for future development; and (3) provide basis for take authorizations under federal and California ESA.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Lower Colorado River]] riparian and wetland complex historically represented the largest such system in the Southwest, with 50,000-100,000 acres of [[Cottonwood]]-[[Willow]] habitat. This system served as a major population center for many riparian bird species and supported a diverse native fish fauna. Over the past century, hundreds of thousands of acres have been removed by development activities, water diversions, and dam construction.

The floodplain and associated riparian habitats are no longer connected to the river—a transformation that has occurred over decades. These habitats have evolved and are now largely unsuitable ecologically for plant species that formerly dominated these areas. [[Saltcedar]] (Tamarix) has been introduced and is now the dominant species, being well-adapted to the transformed conditions including altered hydrology, increased soil salinity, and deep fluctuating groundwater tables.

The native fish community has been devastated by introduction of non-native predators, which represent "the single most important factor preventing completion of the life cycle of native fishes." The riverine ecosystem no longer functions with natural flood pulses, sediment transport, or connectivity between main channel and floodplain habitats. Hydroelectric dams have fundamentally altered flow regimes, water temperatures, and sediment dynamics.

Current conditions include: disconnected floodplains, altered water tables, increased soil salinity from agricultural return flows, dominance of non-native species (both plants and fish), loss of natural flood disturbance regimes, and ongoing development pressure from rapidly growing human populations dependent on LCR water.

## Key Findings

**Fundamental Conceptual Problems:**

The panel found the strategy is not a true multi-species conservation plan but rather a selective species conservation plan focused on rare and endangered species. This approach is incompatible with current scientific thinking in conservation biology. Two of six bird species are raptors with negligible correlation to other LCR mainstem species. Two are marsh birds (rails) that have greatly increased their distribution because of hydroelectric dam construction, raising questions about their prominence in a restoration plan aimed at historical conditions. Other endangered species like pupfish and squawfish are excluded without explanation.

The panel emphasized: "It is the restoration of the various habitats that must be a goal of the plan. If this goal is attained, the wildlife species will respond favorably." The current approach treats habitats as means to an end rather than ends themselves, likely resulting in "slipshod" restoration efforts.

**Incompatible Goals:**

Goals 1 and 2 are fundamentally contradictory as written. Endangered species cannot be recovered while accommodating current water diversions and power production—the very factors responsible for species decline. The panel stated: "The species conservation goals cannot be taken seriously unless a revised document is created that would indicate that wildlife conservation takes priority over the interest of water and power agencies."

The panel recommended incorporating a "net increase" principle: every modification of existing water or power facilities and all future developments must demonstrate a net increase in both conserved habitats and populations of targeted species within the 100-year floodplain. This would make the goals compatible rather than antagonistic.

**Taxonomic Gaps:**

The document mentions no mammals, reptiles, or amphibians. The panel noted this represents an inadequate scientific approach for large-scale conservation planning. The avifauna associated with [[Honey Mesquite]] habitats is significant and substantially different from those occupying cottonwood/willow habitats, yet no strategies for mesquite habitats are included despite claims the plan addresses the LCR "ecosystem."

**Native Fish Recovery:**

The proposed focus on continued stocking or augmentation into sites where species do not reproduce successfully is not true "recovery" of self-sustaining populations. The panel stated: "Naturally reproducing, viable populations of bonytail and razorback sucker will not be established in the Lower Colorado River as a consequence of actions defined in this conservation strategy."

The panel recommended developing an adaptive management strategy to maintain adult populations at approximately carrying capacity using a fluctuating combination of juveniles from refugia, hatcheries, and protected backwaters throughout the LCR. This would simulate probable pre-dam metapopulation dynamics. Special care must be taken in designing connected backwaters and sloughs to permit temporary isolation for removing non-native predators long enough to allow larval fish to become juveniles.

Numerical goals do not appear based on biological considerations. The only biologically defensible goal would be maintaining adult populations at approximate carrying capacity. The strategy should also include reestablishment of [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (squawfish), originally the top predator and pivotal in structuring the fish community.

**Habitat Restoration Challenges:**

Simply reconnecting the floodplain and river will not lead to old habitat types—"it will never return to the way it once was." Reclaiming land for native plants will be monumental, requiring careful planning. The strategy lacks detailed discussion of autecological requirements for native riparian plant species and strategies for converting large tracts (400+ hectares) to something resembling former riparian habitats.

All opportunity areas need preliminary analysis including: soil texture, electrical conductivity of soil profile, depth to water table, soil moisture levels, exchangeable sodium percentage, and quantitative description of existing plants and their condition. This data will lead to rational decisions about land treatment (clearing, chemical treatments for soil reclamation, ripping, leaching methods, etc.).

The proposed addition of 12,000-16,000 acres of riparian vegetation, while commendable, represents a relatively small quantity compared to the hundreds of thousands of acres removed over the past 100 years. Recovery of targeted riparian species is therefore likely to be modest.

**Root Causes Not Addressed:**

The strategy does not fully address root causes of species decline. Greater long-term success would be achieved by: removing some dams and restoring in-channel flood pulses rather than piping water to riparian terraces; purchasing water rights and recharging aquifers rather than surface-watering sites underlain by deep groundwater; restoring riparian habitats to long stretches of mainstem by reducing peaking power use or relying more on non-hydroelectric power sources.

The strategy needs higher priority on managing current non-natives and preventing further introductions/invasions from agricultural activities, suburban development, recreational fishing, and landscaping. This applies to fish, plants (saltcedar), and nest parasites (cowbirds).

**Implementation Concerns:**

The document lacks target goals, timetables, funding commitments, and methods for achieving stated objectives. There is no indication of when projects will begin or where monetary support will originate. The stakeholders, especially water and power agencies, need to be explicit about their contributions.

The strategy is nonfunctional unless existing core areas receive protection. Given opposing forces (landowner rights, widespread development, livestock use, water rights), expanding habitat without securing current core areas is impractical. Under current ESA enforcement, habitat modification cannot be initiated if an endangered species occupies that habitat, creating a paradox where management actions needed for recovery cannot be implemented.

**Research and Monitoring:**

Research and monitoring must be top priority and significantly expanded. The document admits trends and stability of populations are often unknown, yet "take authorizations" necessitate knowing population status and impacts of proposed activities. Sound decisions must be made about monitoring frequency, data collection and analysis schedules, and report preparation. Water and power agencies must be separated from success determination to ensure objective evaluation.

**Geographic Scope:**

The document mentions the LCR ecosystem but fails to include the area from the international boundary south to the Gulf of California. This entire suite of species and habitats is excluded if not encompassed in the plan.

**Specific Species Concerns:**

For [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] and [[Western Yellow-billed Cuckoo]], populations have declined so rapidly (close to extirpation from LCR) that recovery may not be feasible. The cuckoo is declining 30% over three decades throughout its range; assuming significant increases with habitat restoration is tenuous. Non-breeding factors (wintering grounds, migration) are heavily impacting this species.

[[Bald Eagle]] is about to be declassified with no evidence of significant historical wintering population on LCR warranting special conservation attention. [[American Peregrine Falcon]] is ill-suited for inclusion as its status is due to lack of critical habitat (cliffs); it is also being considered for declassification.

For annual plants, basic seed biology is unknown—it is not even known if inundation kills seeds. Simple studies (soil cores, germination trials) would contribute hugely to understanding potential habitat and conservation needs. This represents testing basic assumptions on which conservation elements are based and should be highest priority.

**Recommended Approach:**

The fundamental support must be altered if success is serious objective. The objective should be to protect, enhance and improve riparian (including marsh) habitats and re-establish native fish fauna including many more species than currently considered. Major goals should be two-pronged: (1) habitat types themselves as ends, not means; (2) enhance the fauna rather than only handful of endangered species. This represents the reverse order of the current approach. Without these changes, "this strategy will only represent another failure, but on a grander scale."

An ecologically defensible LCR MSCP would focus on a process shifting floodplain and riverine biotic communities toward those occurring prior to human modification. This could be accomplished over time with requirement that each modification of existing facility and each new project must result in net increase in habitat and populations of targeted native species.
