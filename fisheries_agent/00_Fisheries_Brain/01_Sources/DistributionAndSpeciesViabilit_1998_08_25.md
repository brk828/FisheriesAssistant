# Distribution and Species Viability of Native Fishes

#ichthyology #freshwater-fishes #conservation-biology #GIS-database

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1998 Human Resources Agreement proposal submitted by [[Arizona State University]] to the [[U.S. Forest Service]] (Tonto National Forest) to fund continued development of a large-scale **distributional GIS database** for native inland fishes of the intermountain western United States and adjacent Mexico. Rather than presenting new biological or morphological data on a specific species, the paper describes an existing three-tiered database architecture (museum vouchers, peer-reviewed literature, and gray literature), its coverage status (~90% for [[Arizona]], ~25,000 entries), and a plan to use funded student assistants to expand data entry, quality control, and eventual application toward **species' viability assessment**. The proposal explicitly distinguishes its presence/absence distributional approach from formal Population Viability Analysis (PVA), positioning the database as a tool for prioritizing conservation and habitat-restoration efforts across watersheds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Geographic scope**: The database targets [[Native Inland Fishes]] of the intermountain region south of the [[Columbia River Basin]], extending east to the Continental Divide and south into Mexico to the boundary where tropical fish faunas begin to dominate over temperate species.
- **Current coverage areas**: Arizona (~90% of Levels I & II data, ~25,000 records), with additional partial coverage (~15,000 records) in:
  - Southern California (east of the Sierra Nevada Crest)
  - Southern Oregon and Idaho
  - Nevada, Utah, southwestern Colorado, western New Mexico
  - Baja California, Sonora, and western Chihuahua, Mexico
- **Habitat framing**: Fish distribution is treated as an indirect but reliable proxy for **habitat type and quality**, since western fish ecology is described as "well known," allowing present distributional data to be extrapolated confidently into historic habitat conditions.
- **Scale of analysis**: The system is designed to query data at multiple ecological/political scales — from a single sampling site, to stream or watershed, to county/state/regional levels — supporting [[Watershed-Level Conservation Planning]].
- **Threat indicator concept**: Range contraction and fragmentation in the distributional record are used as an ecological index of anthropogenic threat, since heavily impacted species show extirpation across large portions of former range.

## Key Findings

- **Three-tiered data structure**:
  - **Level I** — Historic museum voucher specimen records (native and non-native fishes).
  - **Level II** — Peer-reviewed literature records, cross-referenced with Level I vouchers.
  - **Level III** — Field notes, agency reports, and gray literature; used only after specialist review due to lack of voucher backing.
- **Data quality improvement**: Input error rate dropped from ~5% at project inception (1994–95) to ~1% at time of writing (1998), reflecting improved [[Data Quality Control]] protocols.
- **Database platform**: Built on **ArcInfo** and **ArcView** GIS software, cross-referenced with fields including genus, species, subspecies, country, state, county, major river basin, drainage, watershed, locality, specimen counts, dates, collectors, depositories, and catalog/reference numbers.
- **Funding partners**: American Rivers, ASU, [[The Nature Conservancy]], U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and U.S. Forest Service have all contributed direct or in-kind support; Arizona and New Mexico Game and Fish departments are committed collaborators.
- **Analytical applications proposed**:
  a) Identify species/assemblages most at risk of extinction or elimination.
  b) Classify watersheds as intact, degraded, or candidates for restoration.
  c) Generate site- and watershed-specific habitat information to guide species maintenance and restoration strategies.
- **Important scientific caveat**: Because the database contains only presence-absence distributional data, it **cannot generate true Population Viability Analyses (PVAs)**; it instead informs prioritization that could later support more rigorous demographic PVA work.
- **Human capital model**: Project relies on hiring and training undergraduate and graduate student assistants (10–20 hrs/week minimum) in GIS, biogeography, and conservation biology methods, supervised by three co-PIs: [[W. L. Minckley]] (ichthyological/historical expertise), [[William F. Fagan]] (viability/survivorship analyses), and [[Jana Fry]] (GIS technical/database management).
- **Budget**: Total requested funding of $64,284 for FY 1998–1999, allocated primarily to student hourly wages (~$48,500 combined undergraduate/graduate wages and workman's compensation), with smaller allocations to supplies, maintenance contracts, and data acquisition (interlibrary loans, museum/library travel).
- **Broader conservation framing**: The proposal frames this multi-tiered distribution-to-viability-assessment approach as a transferable model applicable beyond fishes to other aquatic and even non-aquatic taxonomic groups.
