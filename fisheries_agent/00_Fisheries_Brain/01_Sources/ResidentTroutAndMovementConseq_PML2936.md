# Resident Trout Movement: Consequences of a New Paradigm

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service Fish Habitat Relationships (FHR) Currents technical bulletin (Number 18, April 1995), authored by [[Michael K. Young]], argues that stream-resident trout are far more mobile than the long-held paradigm of restricted movement assumes. Telemetry and trap studies on [[Brown Trout]], [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Brook Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] show movements from tens of metres to over 90 km. The author contends that this mobility undermines assumptions behind restrictive angling regulations, habitat-based stream management, one-time fish abundance sampling, population delineation, and genetic assessment. The paper concludes that managers should recognise when and where movement is necessary for maintaining wild trout populations.

## Background & Description

- **Prevailing paradigm:** Stream-resident trout were considered largely immobile, with movements usually only a few tens of metres (Northcote 1992). The idea of restricted movement had persisted for over 50 years (Gerking 1959) and was applied to cutthroat trout (Miller 1957) and brown trout (Stefanich 1952; Bachman 1984).
- **Limits of earlier studies:** Most mark-recapture work marked fish in short reaches, resampled the same reaches, and reported recaptures only. The fate of 15 to 90% of marked fish that were never recaptured was rarely addressed, and studies often missed fish during all seasons or at night. Potential movement was inadequately evaluated until the last five years.
- **Bulletin context:** The FHR Currents series disseminates research findings and techniques to USDA Forest Service field biologists. The bulletin includes a directory of regional FHR program leaders and associates, which is summarised here rather than reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Movement drivers:** Trout movement is described as a possible response to food, growth, competition, predation, environmental disturbance, and daily and seasonal cycles. Movement may be minimal where macroinvertebrates are abundant, habitat is complex, and the environment is stable, but most streams are spatially and temporally heterogeneous.
- **Fragmentation:** In-stream structures such as water diversions and dams can block seasonally critical spawning or overwintering habitat and isolate populations, increasing extinction risk without natural recolonisation.
- **Non-native stocking:** Stocking of non-native trout has led to the loss of many indigenous populations except where barriers prevented invasion. A single stocking of [[Brook Trout]] in a headwater lake apparently led to replacement of [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] across most of the Battle Creek, Wyoming watershed, except where pollution blocked invasion (Eiserman 1958).
- **Metapopulations:** Trout populations may be better understood as [[Metapopulation]] networks of linked subpopulations. Persistence requires periodically mobile individuals and habitats without continuous barriers (Gilpin 1987). Most salmonid populations were probably founded by mobile individuals from large populations (Milner and Bailey 1989).

## Key Findings

**1. Brown trout are highly mobile (Young, in press; Clapp et al. 1990; Meyers et al. 1992)**
- Clapp et al. (1990) and Meyers et al. (1992) used [[Radiotelemetry]] to track large brown trout in Michigan and Wisconsin, with seasonal movements of over 30 km.
- Young tagged over 50 adult brown trout in North Platte River tributaries, Wyoming. Fish moved as far as 96 km.
- The author hypothesised a [[Spawning Migration]] pattern: movement from the river into tributaries in late July, overwintering in tributary pools, and return to the river during spring high flows.

**2. Colorado River cutthroat trout and other species move substantially (Young, in review)**
- Radio-tracked [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] detected movements averaging over 300 m, up to 2.4 km, in mid-summer.
- 24-hour observations revealed numerous movements over 100 m and up to 1.1 km (unpublished data).
- [[Brook Trout]] in small Colorado streams showed extensive, continuous movement in mid to late summer (Riley et al. 1992) using two-way traps.
- Movement was also documented in rainbow trout in Idaho (Bjornn and Mallet 1964; Young, unpublished data).

**3. Special regulations may be undermined**
- Clapp et al. (1990) found large brown trout tagged in a no-kill section of the South Branch of the Au Sable River spent most of their time in a standard-regulation reach.
- A slot limit protecting 254–406 mm trout in the North Platte River since 1982 may have increased spawning adults in tributaries, which are under standard regulations. Once fish enter tributaries (often by July), they are unprotected, so harvest there may limit population gains.

**4. Local habitat effects are not isolated**
- Riley and Fausch (in press) attributed higher trout abundance in structurally enhanced reaches of six Colorado streams to retention of mobile fish arriving from outside those reaches, rather than improved survival.
- Edge habitat for [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] fry was usually unoccupied unless spawning habitat was nearby (Bozek 1990).

**5. One-time sampling and population estimates are unreliable**
- Decker and Erman (1992) found species abundances varied asynchronously across repeated electrofishing of adjoining reaches, questioning one-time sampling.
- Sherrer and Hazzard (1938) concluded that stream fish populations are relatively unstable over summer, and that estimates from one or two short sections sampled once a year are unreliable.
- Herger et al. (in review) found that redistribution of [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] between two basin-wide surveys, one month apart, changed estimates of habitat-specific densities and overall abundance.

**6. Genetic and habitat assessments are affected**
- Binns (1977) classified [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] genetic purity at three locations on the North Fork Little Snake River using meristic and morphometric analyses.
- Radio tracking (Young, in review) showed one adult occupied all three locations within 23 days, and nearly all fish from Harrison Creek and Green Timber Creek migrated to the main stem. Classifications from single sampling may therefore misrepresent population structure.
- Habitat models built from single-time data may miss mobility-driven variation in abundance, which could add substantially to unexplained variation.

**7. Populations are often defined geographically rather than biologically**
- Trout in a small stream are often treated as an isolated population, but immigration and emigration are common, so a population's range may include far more waters than the type location.

**8. Conclusions**
- A new paradigm treats mobility as one possible response to environmental conditions, not a fixed trait.
- Managers should recognise when and where movement is advantageous or necessary for maintaining wild trout populations.
