# Conservation-Reliant Species: Toward a Biology-Based Definition

#conservation-biology #endangered-species #vertebrate-conservation

## Executive Summary

This BioScience perspective (Rohlf, Carroll and Hartl, 2014) argues that the term [[Conservation-Reliant Species]] has become so broadly applied that it has lost practical value and has become entangled with legal and policy questions under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). The authors propose a biology-based definition: conservation reliance is a spectrum describing the degree to which a species needs direct, ongoing human intervention in its life cycle or environment to persist at a secure level in the wild. They contend that the ESA's core policy goal is self-sufficiency in the wild, and that the concept should inform, rather than justify, decisions about [[Species Recovery]] and [[Species Delisting]]. The paper is a conceptual and legal analysis; it reports no new empirical data.

## Background & Description

The term was coined by [[Scott et al. 2005]], who defined conservation-reliant species as those that can maintain self-sustaining wild populations only if ongoing management of proven effectiveness is implemented. Later authors described conservation reliance as a continuum of management intensity, from species that exist only in captivity, through those needing captive-reared releases, to those needing only periodic habitat management ([[Scott et al. 2010]]). [[Goble et al. 2012]] initially tied the concept to listing status, then revised it to hinge on whether delisted species require "assurances" that management will continue.

The [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) has used the label to support delisting for species needing ongoing intervention, including the Wyoming gray wolf, the Yellowstone grizzly bear and the Kirtland's warbler. The authors note that nearly all or an overwhelming majority of ESA-listed species are now labeled conservation reliant in recent literature.

The paper also relies on the ESA's statutory history. The Wilderness Act of 1964 and the ESA of 1973 reflect a preservation-oriented ethic. The ESA defines "conserve" to include direct human interventions, such as habitat maintenance, live trapping, propagation and transplantation, but only until measures "are no longer necessary" (16 U.S.C. 1532(3)).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame the concept against threats that may be managed but not eliminated, including climate change, invasive species, and disruption of natural disturbance regimes. They argue that such threats often cannot be overcome by reserves or regulation alone, and may require continued intervention, such as controlling competitors or replacing lost individuals.

Illustrative cases include:
- **Gray wolves (Northern Rockies):** weakly conservation reliant. Migratory corridors and reduced take could sustain populations without translocation.
- **Black-footed ferrets and Hawaiian songbirds:** strongly conservation reliant. These species face sylvatic plague and introduced competitors and may need intervention for centuries.
- **Kirtland's warbler:** at least moderately conservation reliant because it depends on prescribed burning or fire-mimicking management in lieu of a natural fire regime.
- **Salmon:** moderately conservation reliant. Restoring self-sustaining runs may require dam removal, with substantial legal and financial barriers.
- **Desert tortoise:** labeled conservation reliant by others, but the authors argue its needs are mainly regulation of human activities, so it would be weakly reliant.

## Key Findings

- **Overbroad usage undermines utility.** Current definitions encompass most listed species, reducing their value for setting recovery priorities.
- **Law and biology are conflated.** Earlier definitions depended on listing status, regulatory adequacy and whether protections were species-specific or pre-existing. The authors call these distinctions murky and inappropriate for a scientific term.
- **The ESA emphasizes self-sufficiency.** The statute, its regulations (50 C.F.R. 402.02), and court opinions (e.g., [[Trout Unlimited v. Lohn]], 2009) treat recovery as self-sustaining populations without human interference. The ESA also requires consideration of the "inadequacy of existing regulatory mechanisms" (15 U.S.C. 1533(a)(1)(D)), which the authors see as proof that Congress anticipated species-specific protections.
- **Proposed definition.** Conservation reliance is a spectrum based on the degree to which a species needs direct human intervention to manipulate individuals at some life-cycle stage, or ongoing physical or biological changes to its environment, to persist in the wild. The "touchstone" is biological self-sufficiency, not listing status or regulatory novelty.
- **Thought experiment.** Removing humans would help species like the desert tortoise but endanger the northern spotted owl, black-footed ferret and Hawaiian songbirds.
- **Policy consequences.** Species classed as conservation reliant could not be delisted under the current ESA. Resource expenditure and political will can shorten the time a species remains reliant (e.g., wolf corridors or dam removal).
- **Prioritization.** Species needing intervention to avoid extinction should be protected, but conservation-reliant species should not be delisted while still requiring translocation or intervention for genetic diversity. The authors specifically advise against the USFWS's Northern Rockies wolf delisting rationale.
- **Value judgments.** Whether the ESA should be amended to accept permanent human management is a societal choice. The authors take no position but urge deliberation. They suggest the [[Threatened Status]] category could offer flexibility for stabilized, intervention-dependent species.
- **Notable precedent.** [[Doremus 2000]] predicted some species might remain listed "forever"; the authors argue that a clear biological definition helps frame that debate.
