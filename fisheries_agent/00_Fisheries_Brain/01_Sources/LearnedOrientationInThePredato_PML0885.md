# Learned Orientation in Predator Avoidance Behavior of the Mosquitofish, *Gambusia affinis*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This Goodyear (c. 1972-1973) study in *Behaviour* XLV examines how the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) orients itself to shore to escape [[Predator Avoidance]] by [[Predatory Fish]] such as [[Largemouth Bass]]. Using a [[Sun Compass]] and landmark and inspection cues, fish learn a shoreward direction in laboratory training arenas and in a field pond. The work is largely experimental, combining field marking and tracking with laboratory arena tests under varied light, temperature and conditioning conditions. The author concludes that orientation is learned, relies mainly on the sun compass, and is supported by a short-term memory that can become long-term memory in the field.

## Background & Description

- **Organism:** *Gambusia affinis*, a small live-bearing freshwater fish that keeps to shallow pond margins where predators are less able to attack.
- **Orientation problem:** Unlike amphibian shoreline orientation, which is linked to breeding migrations, the mosquitofish's shoreward orientation appears to be principally an anti-predator mechanism. It was absent in non-predator-exposed populations.
- **Cue systems examined:** [[Sun Compass Orientation]], [[Landmark Recognition]], [[Optomotor Response]], [[Social Facilitation]] and [[Shadow Direction]] cues.
- **Prior work cited:** Sun-compass use has been shown in several fish species, including sunfishes and cichlids, and in amphibians and reptiles. Training methods include reward and, more often, [[Avoidance Conditioning]].
- **Training technique:** Predator-avoidance chambers with a shallow refuge compartment, adapted from earlier work by Goodyear & Ferguson (1969).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study pond:** Church Pond, about 40 m on a side and 1.5 m deep at its deepest point. Mosquitofish occupied a narrow belt around the margin where water was shallow enough to protect them from predators.
- **Predators present:** Largemouth Bass, Green Sunfish and Bluegill Sunfish.
- **Movement pattern:** Fish usually stayed within about 2 m of shore. Between April and May 1968, they formed distinct groups, and in September they moved toward the shore as water levels changed. During intense predation they moved into water less than 5 cm deep.
- **Environmental influences:** Heat stress (above about 33-35 °C) caused naive fish to move toward deep water, while the sun compass was less reliable under overcast skies and at dawn or dusk.

## Key Findings

**Field movement and homing (Church Pond)**

- Movement between marked groups was limited and sporadic. Of 56 marked fish released at site A on 24 April 1968, none were found outside the AB group during the following week.
- Of 37 fish moved from the AB group to group J, none returned to their home group. After 24 hours, 22 of the 37 were found between I and J, where they remained. Homing did not occur.
- Within the AB area, most of 30 marked fish roamed freely across the whole area, though a few (for example No. 9 and No. 11) stayed within a limited area.
- Of 40 naive fish from a predator-free pond released at site I on 5 June 1968, many were eaten by bass within minutes and none were recovered the next day.

**Laboratory and arena training**

- Mosquitofish learned to orient toward shore by sun compass and inspection, with the sun compass the more important of the two.
- Neonatal fish oriented to shore without parental guidance, and the shoreward direction was learned.
- Laboratory-trained adults formed only short-term memory. Fish from populations previously exposed to predation learned the shoreward direction within two days.
- Training with a new shore direction required about 1 day for conditioned fish, while naive fish needed longer; fish disoriented after a new arena placement recovered direction within 1 to 6 days, depending on group.
- Adults trained to the west shore moved toward shore after 6 days when moved to an east-shore arena.

**Test conditions and environmental variation**

- Trained fish tested between 09:00 and 15:00 under sun reliably oriented to shore, with the best results at midday and early afternoon.
- Fish tested under full moon at night (released in groups of five) were found along the shoreward margin within 15 minutes. Fish tested under uniformly lit clear skies or overcast midday skies appeared disoriented.
- Fish held in darkness for 24 hours oriented to shore when tested, while fish held in constant light for 24 hours were disoriented.
- Fish displaced in view of the sun moved in a predicted direction opposite to the displacement, indicating sun-compass use. Undisplaced controls moved randomly.
- Naive fish exposed to 38 °C water were lethal, and at temperatures above 30 °C they tended to orient toward deep water instead of shore.

**Social behavior and retention**

- Fish released in groups showed improved performance, probably through [[Social Facilitation]].
- Fish were attracted to schools and shelters, and about 2 of 7 tested fish oriented to a school when given a choice.
- Some fish were trained in a school-based design, and results showed that sun-compass orientation was more important than inspection of the shelter.

**Conclusions**

- Mosquitofish learn a shoreward direction and use it as a predator-avoidance strategy. Sun-compass orientation is the primary mechanism, supplemented by landmark inspection and optomotor responses.
- Short-term memory supports laboratory retention; long-term memory is expected in the field, as the author could not rule it out.
- Adults learn the new direction more slowly than young after a change of shore, but both groups eventually learn it.
