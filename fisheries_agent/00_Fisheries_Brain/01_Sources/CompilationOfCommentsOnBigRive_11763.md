## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of agency and stakeholder comments (1999) responding to a **Lower Colorado River Basin "Big-River Fishes" Management Prospectus** issued by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS). Rather than presenting primary morphological or ecological data, the source captures institutional and scientific critique from agencies including the [[Arizona Department Of Water Resources]], [[Arizona Ecological Services Office]], [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]], [[Bureau Of Land Management]], [[Bureau Of Reclamation]], [[Central Arizona Water Conservation District]], [[Colorado River Commission Of Nevada]], [[Los Angeles Department Of Water And Power]], [[Salt River Project]], and [[Western Area Power Administration]]. The comments collectively address four target big-river fishes — [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Humpback Chub]] — and focus heavily on recovery strategy ambiguity, nonnative fish interactions, hydrological management, and the feasibility of restoring "free-flowing river" conditions in a heavily dammed system.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide direct morphological descriptions of the species discussed. The four focal taxa are well-documented elsewhere in the ichthyological literature as large-bodied, pelagophilic (broadcast-spawning) endemics of the [[Colorado River Basin]]:

- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) — largest North American cyprinid; historically a top piscivorous predator in mainstem reaches.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) — streamlined cyprinid adapted to swift, turbid mainstem currents.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — distinguished by a pronounced dorsal keel ("razorback"), a catostomid adapted to riverine and reservoir habitats.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) — characterized by a pronounced nuchal hump, associated with canyon-bound turbulent reaches (e.g., Grand Canyon).

No new morphometric, meristic, or physiological data are presented in this document; discussion is restricted to management and policy status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Historically dependent on lotic (free-flowing, turbid, warm-water) mainstem river habitats; the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] has been converted predominantly to lacustrine (reservoir) habitat due to large dam infrastructure, a key distinction raised relative to the Upper Basin (Western Area Power Administration comment).
- **Hydrology dependence**: Multiple commenters emphasize that natural hydrographs and self-sustaining wild populations are central to Upper Basin recovery paradigms (San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program), creating tension with Lower Basin realities of dam-regulated, largely non-self-sustaining populations.
- **Nonnative species interactions**: Repeatedly flagged as an underemphasized ecological threat — competition and predation from introduced fishes are cited by [[Arizona Department Of Water Resources]], [[Colorado River Commission Of Nevada]], [[Los Angeles Department Of Water And Power]], and [[Western Area Power Administration]] as critical limiting factors for native fish recovery.
- **Reservoir refugia**: The [[Colorado River Commission Of Nevada]] suggests reservoir populations may represent the most manageable habitat option for [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] for the foreseeable future, given the improbability of restoring natural riverine conditions.
- **Genetic management**: Multiple agencies support formal genetic management plans, particularly for reintroducing [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] into the Lower Basin, where wild populations are locally extinct and any reintroduction would rely on captive broodstock genetics.
- **Backwater and grow-out habitats**: LADWP and others endorse backwater grow-out facilities, hatcheries, and riparian restoration as interim conservation tools supporting genetic variability and extinction prevention, short of full habitat restoration.
- **Contaminants and physiological conditioning**: Western Area Power Administration recommends contaminant screening and physiological/chemoreception conditioning of hatchery-reared fish prior to release, indicating concern for post-release survivorship in altered habitats.

## Key Findings

1. **Ambiguous plan classification** — Nearly all agencies question whether the prospectus constitutes a recovery plan, management plan, or strategic framework; lack of clarity in goals, timeframes, and success criteria (e.g., delisting/downlisting criteria) was a near-universal critique.
2. **"Free-flowing rivers" language contested** — Multiple stakeholders (Central Arizona Water Conservation District, Colorado River Commission of Nevada, Salt River Project, Robert S. Lynch) questioned the feasibility and intent behind restoring free-flowing river conditions, with explicit concern this implies dam removal or altered water release regimes.
3. **Colorado Pikeminnow redesignation controversy** — The proposed redesignation of [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] populations in the Salt and Verde Rivers was the single most frequently contested item, raised independently by at least six agencies due to implications for water and power management operations.
4. **Basin division is political, not biological** — Several commenters (Arizona Department of Water Resources, Colorado River Commission of Nevada) note that the Upper/Lower Basin division stems from the 1922 Colorado River Compact rather than any fisheries or ecological rationale, complicating basin-wide recovery coordination.
5. **Upper Basin coordination gap** — Repeated calls to integrate the plan with the [[San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program]] and Upper Basin Recovery Implementation Program (RIP), particularly given contradictory recovery philosophies (natural hydrograph/self-sustaining populations vs. maintained/managed populations).
6. **Self-sustaining vs. maintained populations tension** — A structural contradiction was identified between the goal of "self-perpetuating" native fish populations and the practical acknowledgment that ongoing human maintenance (stocking, hatchery support) will likely be required indefinitely in the Lower Basin.
7. **Nonnative fish management underemphasized** — Widely cited as a missing or insufficiently developed objective; several agencies recommended adding explicit nonnative fish control/management objectives to the plan's framework.
8. **Institutional and funding process concerns** — Comments emphasized prioritized, sequential funding of objectives, broad multi-stakeholder cost-sharing, unclear governance (selection of management team/coordinator), and the omission of state water/power agencies from relevant appendices.
9. **Scope limited to four species** — Several agencies (Bureau of Reclamation, others) requested the plan's scope be broadened beyond the four designated "big-river fishes" to include other native fish species and river systems outside the mainstem Colorado River.
10. **Habitat restoration feasibility doubted** — Reestablishing pre-dam conditions was deemed largely unacceptable or unrealistic by multiple agencies given existing large dam infrastructure and entrenched nonnative fish populations.
