# Flow-Sediment-Biota Relations: Implications for River Regulation Effects on Native Fish Abundance

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether [[River Regulation]] reduces the food base of the endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the upper [[Colorado River]] through fine-sediment accumulation in the riverbed. Sampling across a 373-km study area divided into 11 strata, the authors found that periphyton, macroinvertebrate and detrital biomass were correlated with fine-sediment metrics, and that fish biomass tracked these food resources. Regulation has lengthened the recurrence interval of bed-flushing flows, which the authors argue has extended fine-sediment accumulation and depressed biotic production. The paper concludes that restoring functional elements of the natural flow regime is needed to support the native fish community.

## Background & Description

The Colorado pikeminnow is a large, long-lived piscivorous cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It is listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]], along with three other large-river species: two chubs (*Gila cypha* and *G. elegans*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Populations were extirpated from the lower sub-basin by the 1970s and now persist primarily upstream of [[Glen Canyon Dam]], mainly in Utah and Colorado.

Larvae drift 100–200 km from high-gradient gravel-cobble reaches to low-gradient silt-sand backwaters, which serve as nursery habitat. Diet shifts from insects to fish in the first year, and young fish rely on abundant non-native minnows. Maturing adults (6–9 yr) disperse upstream in search of larger native prey such as suckers and chubs, producing clumped adult densities near the upstream margin of the range.

The study area (rk 0–373, western Colorado and eastern Utah) includes the mainstem upstream of the [[Green River]] confluence. Its hydrograph is dominated by spring snowmelt, and most sediment comes from erosion of shale in low-elevation basins. Three low-head diversion dams near Palisade divert about 43 m³/s during irrigation season, and 15 upstream reservoirs were built mostly between 1943 and 1968.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow change:** Median peak discharge at rk 298 fell from 838 m³/s (1902–1942) to 411 m³/s (1969–1999), a 51% decline. Gunnison River peak flow fell 56%.
- **Sediment:** Suspended sediment inputs have probably not declined, so fines that were once carried downstream now accumulate on the bed and channel margins.
- **Habitat loss:** Backwater and side-channel area declined by 25% in the 51-km Grand Valley reach and 31% in the 58-km DeBeque-to-Rifle segment.
- **Recurrence intervals:** Bed-mobilizing flows historically recurred every 1.3–2.7 yr (depending on stratum). After regulation, intervals lengthened to 2.7–13.5 yr.
- **Substrate:** Riffles stayed relatively free of fines, while runs contained progressively more fine sediment downstream.

## Key Findings

- **Sediment–biota link:** Chlorophyll *a* and macroinvertebrate biomass were significantly correlated with physical measures of fine-sediment accumulation in gravel-cobble substrates. Detritus biomass was also linked to invertebrate biomass.
- **Longitudinal gradient:** Biomass of fish, invertebrates and periphyton was highest upstream. Adult pikeminnow were concentrated in upstream strata where prey fish were most abundant.
- **Habitat preference:** Riffles held more invertebrate and detrital biomass and were less sediment-laden than runs. Native suckers were generally more abundant in riffles.
- **Body condition:** Downstream decline in body condition of the dominant native fish was consistent with food limitation. Adult pikeminnow body condition declined in both upstream and downstream strata during 1993–2000.
- **Flushing frequency:** Mean duration between bed-flushing events roughly doubled in strata 9–11, tripled in stratum 8 and increased fivefold in strata 6–7. Strata 6–7 went 26 yr (1957–1983) without a widespread mobilizing event; the longest historical interval for strata 6–11 was 3 yr.
- **Sediment dynamics:** Monitoring of [[Depth of Free Space]] in strata 8 and 9 (1996–2001) showed fines accumulating within the first year after a flushing event. Flows at or above the initial-motion threshold did not reverse these declines.
- **Adult population:** Osmundson and Burnham (1998) estimated only 300–400 adult pikeminnow (>500 mm) in the mainstem Colorado River during 1991–1994, and the population was considered vulnerable to extirpation.
- **Management implications:** Traditional instream flow methods focused on base flows and preferred physical habitat may have limited success without measures to enhance food availability. The authors recommend restoring the frequency of flushing flows, possibly through short experimental spikes from dam releases, and extending the upstream range through fish passage. They also recommend enhancing lower trophic levels in both off-channel and within-channel habitats.
- **Conclusion:** Fish numbers and biomass correspond with detritus, periphyton and invertebrate biomass, which suggests food limits the population. The authors argue that reduced flushing frequency represents an additional pathway by which regulation depresses native fish assemblages, and that the [[Natural Flow Regime Paradigm]] should guide restoration.
