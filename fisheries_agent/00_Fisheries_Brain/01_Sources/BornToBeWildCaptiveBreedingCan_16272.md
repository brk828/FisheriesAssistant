# Born to Be Wild: Captive Breeding and the Limits of Species Rescue

#conservation-biology #mammalogy #ornithology #mustelidae #felidae

## Executive Summary

This feature article by Jon R. Luoma examines whether [[Captive Breeding]] can pull endangered animals back from extinction, and what is lost when wild populations are replaced by animals in cages. The central paradox is that [[Black-footed Ferret]] numbers rebounded in captivity while no ferret was known to be living free in the wild between early 1987 and autumn 1991. The article surveys several case studies, including the [[Florida Panther]], [[Golden Lion Tamarin]], [[Whooping Crane]], [[California Condor]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], [[Red Wolf]] and [[Arabian Oryx]]. It also argues that captive breeding is a weak substitute for habitat protection, and it links the issue to federal forest policy and the [[Northern Spotted Owl]] controversy.

## Background & Description

The [[Black-footed Ferret]] is a small, nocturnal predator that lives and hunts in prairie dog burrows on western grasslands. By the mid-1980s it was close to extinction, with only 18 individuals known to survive. In 1985 biologists with the [[Wyoming Game and Fish Department]] found that the last wild population had been infected with [[Canine Distemper]], which led officials to accept captive breeding. The captive population grew from about 50 in 1988 to 325 in 1991. Today more than 300 are alive.

The [[Florida Panther]] is a subspecies of the cougar. Hundreds once ranged across Florida, Georgia and Alabama. Only 30 to 50 are believed to survive, mostly around [[Big Cypress National Preserve]] and [[Everglades National Park]].

The article also profiles other programs:
- **[[Golden Lion Tamarin]]:** a small Brazilian monkey whose rainforest habitat fell to about 2 percent of its former extent. Captive reproduction was poor until diet and social problems were addressed.
- **[[Whooping Crane]]:** egg incubation began in the late 1960s when fewer than two dozen birds remained. Later reintroduction using sandhill crane nests had produced no breeding pairs.
- **[[California Condor]]:** the last five wild birds were captured in 1986 and placed in captive breeding at the San Diego Wild Animal Park and the Los Angeles Zoo.
- **[[Peregrine Falcon]]:** after [[DDT]] damage, the species was reintroduced through [[Hacking]], a technique adapted from sport falconry.
- **[[Red Wolf]]** and **[[Arabian Oryx]]:** cited as further examples of captive-bred animals returned to the wild.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Captive breeding was long resisted by many environmentalists and by wildlife managers. Wyoming Game and Fish director Bill Morris said he had "no compunction about killing an animal, but I have a hell of a time putting one in a cage." By the late 1980s, captive breeding had become much more prominent in recovery plans.

Conservation biology has highlighted the dangers facing small populations confined to "islands" of habitat. The article identifies [[Inbreeding]] and [[Edge Effects]] as major threats. For example, raccoons can invade small forest fragments and destroy the nests of deep-forest songbirds. [[Population Viability Analysis]] (PVA) has become the main statistical tool for estimating extinction risk, though its practitioners admit it is an imperfect "crystal ball."

Zoos have also adopted cooperative management. The [[Species Survival Plan]] of the American Association of Zoological Parks and Aquariums coordinates breeding for many species, and the International Species Information System shares animal records between institutions.

Habitat remains the core problem. Roads such as Interstate 75, cattle grazing, urban development and the citrus industry all threaten panther habitat. In the Pacific Northwest, the article describes how the [[Forest Service]] and the administration resisted protections for old-growth forest. Federal Judge William L. Dwyer found that the agency had deliberately refused to comply with wildlife laws, and a report led by Jack Ward Thomas concluded that preserving old-growth habitat was the only plausible way to protect the northern spotted owl.

## Key Findings

- **Black-footed ferret:** no wild ferrets were known from early 1987 to autumn 1991. Forty-nine captive-bred ferrets were released into Shirley Basin prairie dog towns in September and October 1991. By the end of October, some had died from badgers, coyotes, disorientation or starvation. Six had been confirmed dead about six weeks after release, while the others were learning to hunt and avoid predators. Wyoming's recovery effort reportedly cost millions of dollars.
- **Predator training:** Brian Miller's experiments with Siberian ferrets found that animals trained against a pet-dog "predator" escaped danger more skillfully than untrained controls. The 1992 release was planned to use similarly trained ferrets.
- **Florida panther:** researchers estimated that the panthers had lost about 70 percent of their original genetic diversity. According to Melody E. Roelke, about 70 percent of males had only one testicle, compared with about 15 percent in the early 1980s. Sperm counts were low, with up to 90 percent of sperm cells abnormal. Heart-valve defects had also appeared. In 1991 the Fish and Wildlife Service allowed capture of six kittens after a February settlement with the [[Fund for Animals]]. Biologists argued that kitten removal would not reduce the wild population, although the basis for that was largely speculative.
- **Golden lion tamarin:** the 1984 release of 14 captive-bred tamarins into the Pogo das Antas reserve had mixed results. Three of the 14 survived the first year, and one female mated with a wild male and produced twins. By summer 1991, 91 tamarins had been introduced and 33 survived. Captive-bred tamarins had produced 57 offspring by the time of the article.
- **Whooping crane:** the 1975 attempt to introduce birds by placing eggs in sandhill crane nests was "largely a bust." Some hatchlings reached maturity, but none of the introduced cranes had bred.
- **California condor:** by the article's writing there were 52 captive condors, and two were planned for release in January. The first captive-hatched chick was reported in 1988.
- **Peregrine falcon:** the population fell after DDT contaminated eggs and embryos. The 1970s program released more than 3,500 birds. The article reports over 500 breeding pairs and another 1,000 juveniles and nonbreeders, with territories ranging from California's Big Sur to Delaware Bay. The reference table gives 60 pairs in the wild (U.S.) in 1975 and 700 pairs in 1991.
- **Red wolf:** the table lists 14 in the wild in 1972 and 35 in 1991.
- **Arabian oryx:** the last wild animals were captured in 1964. Captive animals were returned to the Middle East starting in 1982. The article reports a wild population of 109 in the Oman desert.
- **Northern spotted owl:** the Forest Service was preparing to endorse the Thomas report, which concluded that preserving old-growth habitat was the only plausible protection. Instead, the administration called for further study and invoked the "God Squad," a group of seven officials who could decide whether to exempt logging from endangered species protections.
- **Overall conclusion:** the author concludes that captive breeding can help rescue critically endangered species, but it is a poor replacement for protecting habitat. He warns that bureaucracies may focus on a few expensive, charismatic species while avoiding the broader problems of wild ecosystems.
