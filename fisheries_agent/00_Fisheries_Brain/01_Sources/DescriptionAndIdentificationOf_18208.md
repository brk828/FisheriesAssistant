# Larval Identification Key for Three Gila River Catostomids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft by Kevin R. Bestgen (Colorado State University Larval Fish Laboratory), prepared for the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AZGF) in October 1989, describes and illustrates the eggs and larvae of three catostomids from the [[Gila River]] drainage: [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The work supports restoration of the razorback sucker, whose early life stages were poorly described and hard to identify. The core product is a dichotomous key for eggs, protolarvae, mesolarvae, metalarvae, and juveniles, supported by pigmentation descriptions, developmental size tables, and original figures.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** The razorback sucker is an endemic species of the [[Colorado River Basin]], formerly widespread. Habitat alteration, development projects, land use, and introduced non-native fishes caused its decline. In the Gila River drainage in Arizona, it was reduced in distribution and abundance by 1950 and was largely gone shortly after (the scan is partly garbled here).
- **Listing:** The species is listed as endangered by the State of Arizona and was proposed for federal listing as threatened or endangered.
- **Restoration:** A [[Hatchery Stocking Program]] for hatchery-reared razorback suckers began in 1981. Successful restoration requires accurate identification of razorback sucker larvae and of other Gila River catostomids, because early life survival and spawning data are needed for management.
- **Gap addressed:** Larval taxonomy for these species was incomplete, and diagnostic criteria were known only for certain life stages (Winn and Miller 1954). Accurate illustrations of the larvae were lacking.
- **Purpose:** To outline diagnostic criteria and provide original illustrations for identifying early life history stages of *X. texanus*, with comparisons to the two co-occurring species.

Specimens were drawn from the upper Gila River, New Mexico (surveys funded by the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish), and from hatchery-reared stock at Dexter National Fish Hatchery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Developmental variability:** Many pigment and developmental patterns are transitional. Their timing may be shaped by hatchery or aquarium conditions, light, and environmental factors such as water temperature, flow, and turbidity. The author urges caution until an investigator is familiar with the key.
- **Rarity in the field:** Just-hatched protolarvae are extremely rare in most field collections and are usually found only in drift net samples.
- **Hatchery effects:** Some features of body shape in hatchery-reared *X. texanus* may reflect the well-fed condition of the specimens.
- **Threats cited from literature:** Predation by ictalurid catfishes was cited as a possible deterrent to reestablishing hatchery-reared razorback suckers (Marsh and Brooks 1989). This is context from the references, not a finding of the study.
- **Rearing conditions:** Eggs were held at 15–16 °C or 20–22 °C. Hatching began about 7 days after fertilization and was complete within 8 days. One *P. clarki* lot held at 15 °C hatched in 12–15 days (P. R. Turner, personal communication).

## Key Findings

### Diagnostic key highlights

- **Eggs:** Eggs of *X. texanus* are small (2.5–2.8 mm). Eggs of *P. clarki* and *C. insignis* are larger (3.0–3.5 mm).
- **Protolarvae:** Unpigmented protolarvae under 9.5 mm SL indicate *X. texanus*. Unpigmented protolarvae and early mesolarvae at 9.5–14 mm are *C. insignis*. Dense, uniform melanophores over the head (80–100) and nuchal surface (200–400), with more than 30 in the predorsal midline, indicate *P. clarki*.
- **Mesolarvae:** *X. texanus* has sparse, uneven dorsal melanophores with a few large stellate ones (50–80 total in flexion mesolarvae). *C. insignis* has three parallel dorsal rows forming a "tractor tread" pattern (100–200 total dorsal melanophores). Lateral myomere pigment separates *P. clarki* from the other two.
- **Metalarvae and juveniles:** *X. texanus* has 13–16 dorsal fin rays. Fewer than 13 separates *P. clarki* (lips notched, peritoneum dark) from *C. insignis* (lower lip cleft to the midline, peritoneum light). A note records that Snyder (1981, 1989) reported some hatchery-reared *X. texanus* with 12 rays, but the author found none in more than 200 specimens examined.

### Developmental size (SL, mm; from Table 2)

- **Hatching:** *X. texanus* 6.4–6.9 mm; *P. clarki* 8.8–9.6 mm; *C. insignis* 9.6–10.3 mm. *X. texanus* is clearly smallest at hatching.
- **Protolarvae:** *P. clarki* 10.2–11.5 mm; *C. insignis* 10.5–12.5 mm; *X. texanus* about 10 mm (range 8–11, bracketed values from Snyder 1989).
- **Juveniles:** *P. clarki* 23.0–24.2 mm; *C. insignis* 23.5–26.0 mm; *X. texanus* 22–23 mm.
- **Gut loop (90°):** *P. clarki* 13.5–14.6 mm; *C. insignis* 16.4–17.8 mm; *X. texanus* about 15 mm.
- **Developmental timing:** Developmental events occur earliest in *X. texanus*, next in *P. clarki*, and last in *C. insignis*. Size at events such as gut coiling, yolk absorption, fin bud and fin ray formation, and transition to metalarvae discriminates the species well.

### Species-specific features

- ***Pantosteus clarki*:** Protolarvae show dorsal pigment at 50x magnification, at 9.5–14 mm SL. The gut pigment darkens the coelomic cavity in later mesolarvae. Metalarvae show a black peritoneum, and juveniles develop a bicolored body with a mid-lateral band. Mouth width expands and lips are notched in later stages.
- ***Catostomus insignis*:** Gut loop forms at a relatively large size, a key diagnostic. The lower lip is cleft to the midline and the lips are rounded and not notched. Its body is more rectangular in cross-section than *P. clarki* (rounded) or *X. texanus* (ovate).
- ***Xyrauchen texanus*:** Hatchlings are opaque and show no pigment at 30–70x magnification. Late larvae have a sharp, relatively long snout, an oblique lower jaw, and a more terminal mouth. The lower lip is cleft to the jaw margin with a visible gap. Juveniles show a uniformly distributed melanophore pattern above the lateral midline. A nuchal ridge should develop at adult size.

### Conclusions

- Pigmentation pattern is the most diagnostic character for protolarvae. Hatching size is a useful criterion when pigment is absent.
- Pigment differences effectively separate nearly all mesolarvae of the three species.
- Size at developmental events and body and mouth morphology separate metalarvae and juveniles. Scale counts are useful, but scales are not fully formed until late in the juvenile stage.
- Quantification of body shape parameters was still pending at the time of writing.
