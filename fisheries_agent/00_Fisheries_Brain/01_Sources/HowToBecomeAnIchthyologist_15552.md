# How to Become an Ichthyologist

#ichthyology #marine-biology #actinopterygii

## Executive Summary

This 1970 T.F.H. Publications booklet, written by [[George S. Myers]], is a career guide rather than a research report. It advises prospective ichthyologists (scientists who study fishes) on the education, subject preparation, university choice, and graduate training needed to enter the field. Its central message is that a deep, lasting interest in fishes must be backed by demanding preparation: strong English, foreign languages (German and French), Latin, mathematics through calculus, and physics in high school, followed by an undergraduate biology degree and a doctorate. The booklet also describes the branches of ichthyology, the employment prospects in each, and lists of universities and museums with significant fish collections.

## Background & Description

The booklet was written by Dr. George S. Myers, then Henry Bryant Bigelow Visiting Professor of Ichthyology at the Museum of Comparative Zoology, Harvard University. He was also a Research Associate at the California Academy of Sciences and a former Professor of Biology at Stanford University. The text was suggested by [[Herbert R. Axelrod]], President of T.F.H. Publications, and published in Jersey City, New Jersey. Its four chapters are: (1) In General; (2) High School Preparation; (3) The Undergraduate University Years; and (4) Graduate Studies. Photographs show fieldwork in Brazil, aquarium fishes named after the author, and hatchery operations. Several pages are advertisements for [[Exotic Tropical Fishes]] (a book described as 892 pages with almost 600 color photographs) and [[Tropical Fish Hobbyist]] magazine.

Myers notes that several fishes are named in his honor, including Corydoras myersi, Aplocheilichthys myersi, and Rasbora myersi. He also describes aquarium species he named, such as Hyphessobrycon flammeus, Apistogramma ramirezi, and Hyphessobrycon innesi.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The booklet touches on aquatic ecology only briefly, mainly to argue for the value of field-based biology. Myers writes that some of the most serious biological problems, including "the rapidly increasing destruction and poisoning" of the environment by detergents, pesticides, sewage, and careless use of land and water, will require biologists who work outdoors and know local fishes and their habitats.

Several field topics are mentioned:

- **Field collecting:** Ichthyologists visit remote areas for new specimens and data. Examples given include a collecting station in Brazil and sampling in the [[Rio Negro]] and [[Rio Purus]].
- **Local knowledge:** A Brazilian Indian informed visiting scientists about a shark species that regularly travels far inland in South American rivers.
- **Fish disease:** The study of fish diseases is described as increasingly important as fish become a food source.
- **Aquaculture:** Myers notes that [[Tilapia]] have proven adaptable and useful as food fish, though enthusiasts have often used them unwisely.
- **Hatcheries:** Large commercial hatcheries that supply the aquarium trade, such as Gulf Fish Farms in Florida, offer limited employment.

## Key Findings

**Career economics and employment**
- Starting salaries for well-educated ichthyologists just out of university were "something between $8,000 and $10,000 yearly" (figures as given in 1970).
- Older professionals earned "something between $12,000 and $16,000," with a very few earning considerably more.
- Ichthyology is a small but growing profession. There is little call for ichthyologists in industry, but there is demand in government work such as the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].

**Branches of ichthyology**
- **Systematic or taxonomic ichthyology:** the study of classification, evolution, and distribution. About 20 North American institutions maintain research collections of this kind. Fewer than a dozen systematists work for government.
- **Marine fishery biology:** the study of ecology and life history of food fishes in the ocean. Largely confined to government bureaus, international organizations, and state fish and game departments. Much of this work involves mathematical analysis and [[Oceanography]].
- **Freshwater fishery biology:** similar work on freshwater fishes, often game fishes such as salmon, trout, and black bass.
- **Fish physiology:** a small and poorly recognized field, often pursued under the label of general physiology.
- **Fish behavior:** an experimental field that is small but growing, often pursued under the label "animal behaviorist."
- **Other specialties:** [[Fish Paleontology]], fish anatomy and embryology, [[Fish Genetics]], and larval fish studies. These areas often merge with systematics.

**Education requirements**
- A Ph.D. or D.Sc. is necessary for advancement or for most first professional jobs. This requires 3 to 4 years of university study after the A.B., and in practice often 4 to 5 years.
- A master's degree (A.M.) alone is not sufficient for most scientific careers.
- Graduate students often support themselves through half-time teaching or laboratory assistantships. Many marry, and the spouse often works. Students usually finish at about age 27 or 28.
- A "B" average is cited as the practical minimum for entering graduate study. Students with a few D's can still be admitted if their B's and A's raise the average to a B.

**High school preparation (in ranked order)**
1. **English:** the most important subject. Clear writing and precise expression are central to scientific work.
2. **Foreign languages:** German and French are preferred, with 2 years of at least one of them recommended. Russian is of lesser importance. Spanish is not advised for most scientific students.
3. **Latin:** one year recommended, mainly to improve precision in English, since much scientific vocabulary derives from Latin.
4. **Mathematics:** essential, with calculus completed in high school if possible.
5. **Science:** physics preferred over chemistry, and high school biology is not recommended because it is repeated in college.
6. **Other subjects:** geography, world or European history, weather and climate, sociology, and shop, in that order of preference.

**Undergraduate choices**
- The undergraduate major should be zoology or biology. Specialization is limited at this stage.
- Myers strongly advises choosing an undergraduate institution with at least one professor who specializes in the student's preferred branch of ichthyology, and making contact in the freshman year.
- He warns that professors can unintentionally steer students toward their own fields, and that many students change their aims during college.
- He also criticizes the "pride, prejudice, and intolerance" among biology specialties, observing that medically oriented biology usually receives the most research funding.

**Recommended institutions (partial list)**
- **West:** University of Hawaii, University of Washington (fisheries), UCLA, University of Southern California
- **Midwest:** University of Kansas, University of Michigan (systematics and fisheries), Indiana University (fish ecology), University of Wisconsin (fish ecology)
- **South:** University of Texas, Tulane, University of Miami
- **East:** Cornell, Harvard, Yale, New York University

**Museums and research collections**
- The booklet lists about nine major non-university museums with fish collections, several of which offer joint graduate programs with local universities. Examples include the [[Field Museum of Natural History]], [[American Museum of Natural History]], and [[Smithsonian Institution]].
- The "Big Two" university collections are Harvard's Museum of Comparative Zoology and the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology. Myers considers them comparable in importance to the leading museum collections.

**Final advice**
- Students who are interested in fishes in high school should keep one small aquarium or otherwise maintain their interest, but should not devote more time to fishes than that. Myers states that without curbing their hobby interests, they "better give up the idea of ichthyology entirely."
- Dabbling in aquarium fish, skin-diving, or simply working outdoors is rarely a route to professional ichthyology.
