# Feeding Ecology of Pit Sculpin, Hermaphroditic Sturgeon, and Lamprey Literature (Bulletin of the Southern California Academy of Sciences, Vol. 75)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae #acipenseridae #petromyzontidae

## Executive Summary

This scanned bulletin section (accepted May 1, 1976) contains three parts: a bibliography of [[Lampreys]] (roughly 30 entries), a study of the feeding ecology of the [[Pit Sculpin]] in [[Ash Creek]], California, and a short note on hermaphroditism and teratoma-like gonadal growths in [[Sturgeon]] from the [[Hudson River]]. The main research result is that Pit sculpins feed mainly on benthic aquatic insect larvae, select prey highly selectively, and feed at all hours with a peak in early morning. The sturgeon note reports ovotestes and teratoma-like structures in two hermaphroditic adults.

## Background & Description

**Lamprey reference list.** The first part is the tail end of a literature list on lampreys (Petromyzontidae). It covers larval development and metamorphosis of [[Sea Lamprey]] and [[American Brook Lamprey]], life cycles of Mordacia and Ichthyomyzon species, distribution records in the eastern United States and Quebec, and early embryology. Several references are on the Delmarva Peninsula, West Virginia, Maryland, and Ohio. The list is citation-only and contains no new data.

**Pit sculpin study.** The Pit sculpin (*Cottus pitensis*) was recently separated from the riffle sculpin, *Cottus gulosus*. It is abundant in the Pit River and its tributaries in northeastern California and is considered rare and potentially endangered in Oregon. The authors compared its diet with that of three co-occurring species: [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Sacramento Sucker]] (*Catostomus occidentalis*), and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). Fish were collected by electrofishing in Ash Creek, a tributary of the upper Pit River with typical summer flows of 400–700 L/sec. Samples were taken in May, June, July, and October 1973, and a 24-hour series was taken on 7–8 June 1974 at four-hour intervals. Stomach contents were analyzed by point-volume estimates. Selectivity was measured with [[Ivlev Electivity Index]] values, which range from +1.00 (complete positive selection) to −1.00 (complete avoidance). Bottom samples (N = 88) were collected with a Surber-type sampler and the invertebrates were identified and weighed. Caloric values were measured with a Parr semimacro bomb calorimeter.

**Sturgeon note.** Atz and Smith examined two hermaphroditic sturgeon caught in the Hudson River: an [[Atlantic Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser oxyrhynchus*, about seven pounds, caught off Stony Point on 15 November 1965) and a [[Shortnose Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser brevirostrum*, caught in upper Haverstraw Bay on 19 March 1969). Only the gonads of the Atlantic sturgeon were available for study. The specimens came from the small commercial fishery at Verplanck, about 40 miles upstream from the Battery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Ash Creek invertebrate community.** The Pit sculpin diet was dominated by benthic insect larvae. The most abundant prey in both bottom samples and stomachs were baetid mayfly nymphs, [[Hydropsyche]] caddisfly larvae, and the stonefly *Acroneuria*. Snails were rarely eaten, and the authors attributed this to their low caloric value and large indigestible shell mass.

**Habitat segregation.** Pit sculpins were collected mainly in the fast water of riffles. Speckled dace are more widespread, occurring in shallow stream edges and pools as well as riffles. The Sacramento sucker feeds mostly on algae and detritus, with the small invertebrates living among them. The authors describe the four species as ecologically segregated, though the sampling bias toward riffles may have inflated the apparent overlap between sculpins and dace. In October, dace switched to algae and detritus while sculpin diets stayed the same. The authors suggest this reflects declining invertebrate production as winter approaches.

**Sturgeon habitat.** The specimens came from tidal Hudson River waters where commercial fishing took place. The paper gives no further habitat data.

## Key Findings

**Pit sculpin feeding ecology**
- Diet is mainly benthic invertebrates, similar to that of other stream-dwelling *Cottus* species.
- Feeding occurs at all hours, with peak intensity in the early morning (0400 and 0800 samples in June 1974).
- Sculpins showed consistently positive electivity for *Acroneuria* stonefly nymphs on nearly all sampling days except 24 June 1973. Baetid mayflies and *Paragyractis* (Lepidoptera) were also positively selected.
- Electivity values for other prey, including some of the most abundant organisms, varied from one sampling period to another.
- No Spearman rank correlations between electivity and prey size, abundance, or caloric value were significant (P > 0.05). Comparisons of stomach and bottom percentages were also not significant.
- No fish were found in sculpin stomachs during the study. However, sculpins with distended stomachs collected in other Pit River studies had eaten smaller sculpins.
- Nighttime feeding was confirmed by the 27–28 October 1973 comparison and the June 1974 24-hour series. The authors say future ecological studies of *Cottus* should include both day and night samples.

**Diet of co-occurring fishes**
- Speckled dace fed on smaller prey than sculpins and showed strong negative selection for *Acroneuria* nymphs. Dace feed mostly during the day, while sculpins feed day and night.
- Juvenile Sacramento suckers mostly ate detritus, with the early instars of baetid mayflies and chironomid larvae as the main invertebrates. Suckers averaged 2.3 species per stomach in the reported comparison, the same as speckled dace (sculpin 2.6 species per stomach).
- Rainbow trout (13 stomachs, average SL 86 mm, from three sampling days) ate 49 percent terrestrial and 51 percent aquatic insects by volume. Baetid mayfly nymphs were the most abundant single prey item at 13 percent. Trout had the most varied diet, with an average of 5.0 prey types per stomach.

**Methods and limitations**
- Fish were collected only in the middle of riffles, which may have biased the apparent diet overlap.
- The authors used point-volume estimates for stomach contents and wet weight for benthic samples, and they note that electivity values may depend on the bottom sampling method.

**Hermaphroditic sturgeon**
- Both sturgeon had ovotestes approaching ripeness, with large pigmented eggs and white testicular tissue. In the Atlantic sturgeon the male and female tissues were largely separate. In the shortnose sturgeon the tissues were intermixed, and nearly all the ovotestes consisted of eggs in a testicular matrix.
- One shortnose ovotestis contained small cystic structures made of cartilage, bone, blood vessels, gut epithelium, and connective tissue. These are classed as teratoma-like.
- The authors argue these structures may have arisen from abnormal development of a parthenogenetic or self-fertilized egg rather than from a neoplastic process. They also state that no abnormal growths described as teratomas in fishes can be regarded as a true tumor or neoplasm without question.
