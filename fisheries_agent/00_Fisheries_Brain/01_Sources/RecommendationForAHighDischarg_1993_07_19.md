## Executive Summary

This document is a 1993 scientific recommendation for an **experimental high-discharge flow** from [[Glen Canyon Dam]], intended to mimic pre-dam flooding regimes on the [[Colorado River]] through [[Glen Canyon]] and [[Grand Canyon]]. The proposal calls for a discharge of approximately **45,000 cfs sustained for one week** in early April 1994, bracketed by stable 8,000 cfs flows for baseline data collection. The core purpose is to test the hypothesis that periodic high flows benefit the riverine ecosystem — particularly through **sediment redistribution** into eddies and beaches — while assessing risk to native and non-native fish populations, aquatic invertebrates, riparian vegetation, and endangered species. The panel concluded that short-term ecological benefits were likely, with minimal to no long-term detrimental effects on fish or other biota, supporting a "finding of no significant impact" for regulatory purposes.

## Background & Description

The source is a management/policy document rather than a morphological study, so detailed anatomical data are absent. However, it references ecologically relevant behavioral/physiological traits tied to morphology and life history:

- **[[Humpback Chub]]** ("chub"): A long-lived cyprinid species with multi-year recruitment cycles; young-of-year exhibit strong association with the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) as nursery habitat, timing their presence there through at least April.
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** and **[[Bluehead Sucker]]** ("suckers"): Benthic-oriented body plan and behavior — they "tend to stay on the bottom out of the impact of faster moving water," a trait likely tied to depressed/flattened ventral morphology suited for benthic flow refuge.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]**: Population at [[Lees Ferry]] exhibits **late fall/early winter spawning phenology**, meaning an April flow event falls outside the critical incubation/emergence window for young fish.
- **Non-native fish species** (unspecified taxa): Noted as more physiologically/behaviorally adapted to **lentic (lake) or slow-moving stream conditions**, making them more vulnerable to flushing during high-discharge lotic pulses compared to native fish adapted to historical flood regimes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **[[Humpback Chub]]**: Occupies mainstem shoreline and eddy habitats as adults; juveniles rely on **backwaters (eddy return channels)** and the LCR for nursery habitat. The species has **coevolved with natural flooding disturbance regimes**, suggesting adaptive resilience to periodic high flows.
- **[[Suckers]]** (native): Benthic niche specialists, occupying channel-bottom habitat that buffers them from high-velocity flow disturbance during flood events.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]**: Occupies the Lees Ferry reach; reproduction is temporally decoupled from the proposed spring experimental flow window, reducing niche-overlap risk during the disturbance event.
- **Non-native fish (unspecified)**: Occupy niches favoring slack water/lake-like conditions; ecologically function as **predators on native fish young**, with their populations expected to be temporarily suppressed by flushing flows, indirectly benefiting native fish recruitment.
- **Aquatic food base**: [[Cladophora]] (filamentous algae) and [[Gammarus]] (amphipod) form key components of the trophic base; Cladophora dislodgement may translocate food resources downstream, while Gammarus is expected to recover quickly post-disturbance.
- **Riparian and backwater habitat**: Eddy return channels (young fish backwater habitat) are expected to be temporarily altered but to "quickly reform" post-flood, and new backwaters may be created — an important niche-generating process.
- **Broader food web/endangered species interactions**: [[Bald Eagle]] foraging is temporally linked to trout spawning; [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] nesting and [[Kanab Ambersnail]] habitat show minor, largely inconsequential spatial overlap with the flood disturbance zone.

## Key Findings

- **Recommended experimental discharge**: ~45,000 cfs for ~1 week, with up-ramp ≤4,000 cfs/hr and down-ramp ≤1,500 cfs/hr; bracketed by 3–5 days pre- and 3 days post-experiment at stable 8,000 cfs for data control.
- **Timing rationale**: Scheduled for **early April 1994** specifically because [[Humpback Chub]] young-of-year for that cohort would still be resident in the LCR (not the mainstem), minimizing recruitment-loss risk; similarly, [[Rainbow Trout]] spawning at Lees Ferry occurs earlier (late fall/winter), avoiding impact to trout young.
- **Fish recruitment risk assessment**: Possible downstream displacement of 1992–1993 cohort young chub from the mainstem was acknowledged as a risk, but deemed **non-significant** for a long-lived species with a history of successive strong recruitment years.
- **No expected direct mortality risk to adult native fish**, including chub, due to evolutionary history with natural flood disturbance; native fish shoreline habitat use is not expected to differ meaningfully between 31,500 cfs and 45,000 cfs discharge levels.
- **Suckers identified as least vulnerable native taxon** due to benthic habitat use avoiding high-velocity flow zones.
- **Non-native fish suppression hypothesis**: Flooding may flush non-native fish (adapted to lentic conditions) out of the system, temporarily reducing predation pressure on native fish young until non-native populations recover.
- **Invertebrate/algal food base impacts**: Minimal expected harm — [[Cladophora]] dislodgement could subsidize downstream food webs; [[Gammarus]] populations expected to rebound quickly even if initially impacted.
- **Comparative discharge justification**: 45,000 cfs (vs. powerplant capacity of 31,500 cfs) was selected because it represents a genuinely experimental (historically atypical for the interim-flow period) discharge level capable of producing measurable sediment transport and eddy deposition responses, whereas lower discharges risk an ambiguous or "wasted" sediment response.
- **Endangered species risk**: Judged minimal — partial [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] nest inundation possible but occurring early in nesting season; [[Kanab Ambersnail]] habitat overlap minor; [[Bald Eagle]] foraging patterns temporally offset from the flow event.
- **Overall conclusion**: The panel found **short-term ecological benefits likely** (sediment redistribution, backwater regeneration, potential non-native fish suppression) with **no significant long-term detrimental impacts** to fish populations or other Canyon biota, recommending regulatory classification as a "categorical exclusion" or "finding of no significant impact."
- **Contingency**: If planning coordination failed to meet readiness by late October 1993, a one-year delay was deemed low-risk, since sediment supply was expected to persist under interim flow conditions.
