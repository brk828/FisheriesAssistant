# Gillard Hot Springs, Arizona: Geothermal Setting and Water Chemistry

#hydrogeology #geothermal-springs #basin-and-range (no fish or other aquatic taxa are addressed in this document)

## Executive Summary

This 1974 article in [[Geothermal Energy]] describes [[Gillard Hot Springs]], a set of warm seeps at the confluence of the [[Gila River]] and [[San Francisco River]] in southwest Greenlee County, Arizona. The author, [[Anthony Tellier]], presents the local geology, structure, and water chemistry as a geothermal prospect. The springs issue at about 175 °F where [[Volcanic Breccia]] meets Gila River terraces. The breccia acts as the primary aquifer. Faulting from the [[Basin and Range Faulting]] controls spring locations. Water analysis shows the spring water is far more mineralized than the river. The paper concludes that the site is geologically uncomplicated but has difficult access and significant construction and industrial-use constraints. The document contains no biological survey and reports no fish or invertebrate data.

## Background & Description

The site was chosen for four reasons: the author's thesis identified it as a prime geothermal site, its physiographic and structural features were interesting, structural control was evident, and the author had detailed photographs and information available. The thesis was based on research in [[Arizona State University]] (Tellier, 1973).

Gillard Hot Springs lies about 10 miles southwest of [[Clifton Arizona]], in the Guthrie quadrangle (1:62,500). The location is given as T5S R29E Section 27 NE¼ NE¼ NE¼. The site is at 3,000–4,000 feet elevation. Access is extremely difficult. Locating the springs required repeated visits, and the author notes that four-wheel-drive vehicles are insufficient for the final miles, which require foot or motorcycle access. Published references (Stearns and others, 1937; Waring, 1965) were too vague to establish a route.

The author describes the springs as seeps with a distinct hydrogen sulfide odor. They lack the mineral deposits typical of hot springs such as those at [[Yellowstone National Park]]. Some seeps show "jetting" discharge and "black sands." Several seeps display weak artesian-like flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regional setting.** The site lies in the [[Basin and Range Province]], which has sharply rising mountain ranges and broad basins. Vegetation is sparse, consisting of mesquite, grasses, and prickly pear cactus. This cover does little to slow infrequent rainfall, so flash floods and arroyo incision are common. Local slopes are steep, with cliffs about 100 feet high.

**Hydrology.** The Gila River floods periodically and overruns its terraces. Spring discharge dilutes the muddy river water slightly where the two meet. Perennial Eagle Creek Spring, about 5 miles northwest, supports a more extensive floral community and is drawn from the same aquifer (Waring, 1965). The confluence with the San Francisco River lies about 150 feet downstream from the springs, and the San Francisco River enters at a 60° angle.

**Geology.** Tertiary–Quaternary volcanic activity produced breccias, tuffs, and agglomerates. No lava flows were observed, and the author found no unconformities. Quaternary sand, silt, and gravel alluvium is confined to the Gila and San Francisco floodplains and terraces. Faulting is northwest–southeast and is associated with the Clifton–Safford area. The author proposes an unmapped fault under the San Francisco River channel, but this is acknowledged as conjecture.

**Access and infrastructure.** Jeep trails are poorly defined and often end abruptly. The Safford–Clifton highway ([[Highway 666]]) crosses deep, vertical-sided canyons. The bridge and road near the Gila have been washed out, and planks now serve as a substitute.

- The author's industrial-use assessment says the Gila River is low in sodium and potassium, so these constituents would not be a problem there.
- The high [[Silica Deposition]] levels in both waters could foul machinery and equipment such as cooling towers and turbomachinery. The author says this must be investigated before any use of the river or springs.

**Geology and structure:**

- [[Volcanic Breccia]] is the primary aquifer. It is weakly consolidated and not well suited to compaction-based foundations.
- Faulting controls spring positions by intersecting aquifers with impermeable layers.
- The river terraces show cross-bedding in only a few locations. Breccia makes up most of the banks.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**

- The geology is not particularly complex, but the terrain is rugged and difficult to traverse.
- Construction could range from moderate to severe difficulty. Risks include slumping, slope breakdown, and road-surface failure in the breccia.
- Flood hazards are substantial because of periodic inundation and severe storm runoff.
- Access is a major constraint for any proposed activity.
- The author states that the area needs substantial geologic and civil-engineering evaluation tied to the specific planned use.
