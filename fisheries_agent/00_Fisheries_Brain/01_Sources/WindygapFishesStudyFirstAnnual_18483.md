# Windy-Gap Fishes Study: First Annual Report (1983)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #colorado-river-fishes

## Executive Summary

This first annual report from the [[Windy-Gap Fishes Study]] covers 1982 field work on endangered fishes of the upper [[Colorado River]] system. The study was run by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under a cooperative agreement with the Northern Colorado Water Conservancy District. Its aim is to identify the factors that limit survival of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] during their first year of life, so that populations might eventually be recovered. The work combined radiotelemetry of adults, larval and young-of-the-year (YOY) surveys, and physicochemical and biological sampling of [[River Backwaters]] and [[Gravel Pits]] near Grand Junction, Colorado. The report also outlines 1983 work plans, including a fish weir at [[Walter Walker Wildlife Area]] and a proposed spawning pond.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River fish community was historically dominated by endemic species. Three of these, Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), humpback chub (*Gila cypha*), and bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*), declined enough to be listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, federal agencies must ensure their actions do not further reduce listed populations. This has made the species a controversial factor in Colorado River water projects.

The work builds on the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] (CRFP), a 1979 cooperative effort between the Fish and Wildlife Service and the Bureau of Reclamation. CRFP found that Colorado squawfish and humpback chub appear to suffer unusually high mortality in their first few years of life.

The study set six objectives:

1. Locate and describe reproductive habitats in the Grand Junction area.
2. Locate and quantify rearing areas for young fish.
3. Identify major factors affecting first-year survival.
4. Modify backwaters and gravel pits between Debeque Canyon and the Green River mouth to enhance survival.
5. Evaluate natural and modified backwaters as nursery habitat.
6. Determine the extent of movement within the Colorado River and its tributaries.

Objectives 1, 2, 3, and 6 form the movement, spawning, and rearing element. Objectives 4 and 5 form the backwater and gravel pit element.

Study areas in 1982 included the Colorado River from Palisade, Colorado to Hite, Utah; the Gunnison River; the lower 20 miles of the [[Yampa River]]; and the [[Green River]] from the Yampa confluence to [[Gray Canyon]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and habitats.** The Colorado River near Grand Junction has many [[River Backwaters]] (naturally indented areas with little or no current) and [[Gravel Pits]] (man-made pits in the floodplain). Gravel pits connect to the river at high flows but may or may not connect at low flows. The authors judged them hydraulically similar to some natural backwaters. Gravel pits are common in the Grand Valley and continue to be developed.

**Backwater conditions.** Backwaters with young squawfish usually hold too few squawfish for detailed study of fish-environment interactions. Researchers therefore supplemented natural populations with hatchery fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico).

**Predators in pits.** [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Green Sunfish]] were the dominant predators in [[Fish Pond]] and [[Labor Camp Pond]]. Fish Pond also held large numbers of [[Bluegill]] and [[Black Crappie]]. Predators are a major concern because they consume young squawfish.

**Hydrology and temperature.** The 1982 runoff was very high. Shallow backwater habitat was almost absent on the Green River during YOY collections, which was attributed to these high water levels. Squawfish aggregated to spawn as river flows receded and water temperatures approached 20 °C.

**Habitat context.** The report notes that backwaters and gravel pits may supply important nursery habitat. It also raises the possibility that warm pit environments could affect gonadal maturation, movement, and pathogen exposure.

## Key Findings

### Radiotelemetry

- **Methods:** Adult Colorado squawfish longer than 500 mm TL were surgically implanted with radio transmitters weighing about 10–11 g in air, with an expected life of about 6 months.
- **Upper Lake Powell:** Six fish were implanted between April 6 and May 28, plus one ultrasonic transmitter on April 6. Contact was lost with three fish soon after release. The remaining four were located from 5 days to 6 months after release.
- **Long upstream movement:** One Lake Powell fish moved about 160 miles upstream between early July and mid-August, reaching RM 140. In September it was near Clifton, about 40 miles farther upstream.
- **Grand Junction area:** Sixteen fish were tagged in 1982 (four from the Gunnison, Colorado, and confluence areas; twelve from gravel pits). Releases occurred between May 14 and June 18. Nine fish were released inside pits and three outside, and all pit-released fish later moved into the Colorado River.
- **Movement distances:** No movements exceeded 50 miles. Some fish moved about 40 miles. Fish from Walter Walker Wildlife Area generally moved farther than upstream fish. One Gunnison fish and four Walter Walker fish reached the Black Rocks region (RM 136).

### Spawning Evidence

- **Clifton pool (July 13):** Three radio-tagged fish were found in a small side-channel pool at RM 178.3. Trammel netting collected nine squawfish, including one radio-tagged fish and five ripe males.
- **Eddy at RM 176:** Five radio-tagged fish visited this eddy over two weeks. A ripe male was collected there, and a recaptured tagged fish appeared to be a maturing female that had gained about 500 g in six weeks.
- **Movement of ripe males:** Two radio-equipped ripe males from Walter Walker Wildlife Area moved 10 and 15 miles while ripe. The authors note that male minnows often ripen earlier than females and may travel before spawning.
- **Confirmation:** Larval squawfish collected in August confirmed spawning in the Grand Junction area.

### Green River Radiotelemetry

- Eleven adult squawfish (522–810 mm TL) were radio-tagged by the Fish and Wildlife Service in May 1982.
- Larger fish tended to move into or remain in whitewater canyon areas after spawning.
- Radio-tagged fish led researchers to a previously suspected spawning site in **Gray Canyon**. Ripe fish collected there, and the YOY studies that followed, provided the first documentation of this site.
- One 810 mm fish (No. 3001) moved about 322 miles during the study period.
- Seven ripe squawfish were collected in Gray Canyon between July 13 and 22. Only one ripe fish was collected in the lower Yampa River.
- Spawning in Gray Canyon occurred about 3 weeks earlier than in Yampa Canyon, and Yampa spawning was reported as 3–4 weeks later than sampling in 1982.

### Larval and YOY Surveys

- **Larvae:** 148 larval Colorado squawfish were collected throughout the study area. The reach below Westwater Canyon yielded 107 of them, with the highest relative abundance. Larvae appeared later in the upper reach than the lower reach, possibly indicating different spawning times.
- **YOY (Colorado River):** YOY (30–40 mm TL) were collected only below Westwater Canyon. Areas with high YOY abundance matched earlier high larval abundance.
- **YOY (Green River):** Catch per effort was about half the average of previous years, which the authors attribute to high water levels rather than poor spawning. Collections yielded 637 YOY fish, and the authors interpret this as reproduction comparable to earlier years. YOY survival could still have been reduced by unfavorable water conditions.

### Gravel Pits and Backwaters

- **Adults in pits:** Adult Colorado squawfish and [[Razorback Sucker]] were collected from nearly all gravel pits investigated. When pits became isolated from the river, squawfish were no longer found there, but razorback suckers persisted in four pits.
- **Departure:** Fyke nets at Walter Walker Wildlife Area caught 12 squawfish leaving in late June as the river receded, plus one more in early July. Two adults left and one entered Labor Camp pit in the same period.
- **Larvae in pits:** Only two larval squawfish were found in gravel pits, both in Island Backwater pit on August 11.
- **Predator removal:** Before stocking, Fish Pond yielded 155 largemouth bass, 247 green sunfish, 881 bluegill, and 1,483 black crappie.
- **Predation on stocked fish:** Largemouth bass under 350 mm most frequently contained stocked squawfish. This predation was highest early in the study and declined as stocked fish decreased and water temperatures fell.
- **Population estimates:** Labor Camp had appreciably larger largemouth bass populations than Fish Pond. Green sunfish remained common in Fish Pond despite removal efforts.
- **Stocking:** About 30,000 hatchery squawfish (about 3 months old, 35–85 mm) were microtagged and stocked on October 6, 1982. Groups were two of 10,000, four of 2,500, and each group had unique microtags. Two tagged fish were later recovered about 3 miles downstream of a study backwater.
- **Catch trends:** Catch per effort in backwaters declined to near zero by early November, and no squawfish were found in backwaters in late November.

### 1983 Work Plans

- **Gravel pit monitoring:** The broad 1982 monitoring program will not be repeated. A more detailed study will focus on fewer pits.
- **Weir:** A semi-permanent weir will be installed in the outlet of Walter Walker Wildlife Area in winter 1982–1983 to trap fish moving in and out, with pathogen analysis and environmental monitoring.
- **Spawning pond:** A spawning pond is planned in an existing or planned gravel pit in cooperation with the Colorado Division of Wildlife and Corn Construction Company. It would have controlled inlet and outlet structures to admit squawfish and exclude predators, allowing study of spawning site selection, success under different conditions, and predator effects.
- **Radiotelemetry:** Work will continue on the Colorado and Green rivers, with each Colorado River reach surveyed biweekly. Up to 20 squawfish will be tagged in the lower Green River, and eleven fall-1982 fish carry 18-month transmitters. A radio receiving station is planned on the Gunnison River above Redlands Diversion. Humpback chub radio-tracking in the Black Rocks area will begin in March 1983, with up to ten fish tagged, possibly with pressure-sensitive transmitters.
- **YOY monitoring:** The YOY program will continue, with all known spawning sites checked and success correlated with 1983 river flows.
