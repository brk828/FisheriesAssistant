# Canyon Creek Aquatic Habitat Improvement Project: Operational Plan (1986)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 operational plan, compiled by [[Sue Morgensen]] for an interagency and citizen team, sets a five-year habitat improvement blueprint for [[Canyon Creek]] from its spring source to the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] boundary. The team was drawn from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), the [[Tonto National Forest]] (TNF), and public angling groups including [[Trout Unlimited]] and [[Anglers United]].

The stated goal is to emphasize [[Brown Trout]] and a native fish forage base, and to determine whether a catch-and-release "[[Blue Ribbon Fishery]]" for brown and [[Rainbow Trout]] can be attained by 1991, then maintained through riparian management, modified stocking, and restrictive regulations.

The main problems are the absence of natural trout reproduction, high fine-sediment levels, thermal loading from lack of shade, a shortage of spawning gravels and deep pools, and legacy impacts from grazing, logging, roads, and recreation. The plan prioritizes reaches 2-6 and lists 16 actions with approximate costs.

## Background & Description

**Setting and history.** Canyon Creek is a coldwater tributary of the Salt River, draining roughly 32.5 square miles (see the data discrepancy note below). Perennial flow comes from springs and limestone sinks on the Mogollon Plateau. [[Mule Creek]] is a first-order tributary. The creek was isolated until the Forest Service improved access in the late 1970s. A 1963 description called it one of the better brown and rainbow trout fisheries in Arizona.

In 1963 the AGF Commission passed a fly-fishing-only regulation, which secured public access through private land along the lower creek. The Forest Service later acquired that land. In 1986 the regulation was changed to allow artificial lures as well as flies. Canyon Creek is the only cold-water stream in Arizona restricted to artificial flies and lures, apart from Fee's Ferry on the Colorado River.

**Public use.** Recreation visitor days rose from 14,000 in 1970 to 66,500 in 1985, and the Forest Service expects them to double by 2025. In 1981, 6,600 angler days were recorded, with an average catch of 1.5 trout per angler per day. Stocking since 1948 has included an average of 5,780 catchable rainbow trout per year, plus 5,000 fingerling brown trout. A total of 26,000 fingerling brown trout were stocked from 1978 onward, and the last fingerling stocking was in 1983.

**Native fauna.** Native fish are [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*). Canyon Creek supports a moderate aquatic invertebrate assemblage despite high silt, and benthic samples have been used to calculate Biotic Potential Indices.

**Management framework.** The plan follows the AGFD Cold Water Strategic Plan and the TNF Forest Plan, and it is being tiered to the forest-wide Environmental Impact Statement through an [[Environmental Assessment]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Watershed and climate.** The watershed is 32.5 square miles, about half above the Mogollon Rim on level terrain and half below on steeper slopes. Elevation is 6,200-6,500 ft, with 24-32 inches of mean annual precipitation from a bimodal pattern. Peak flow is spring runoff, typically February through April. The plant communities are [[Ponderosa Pine]] forest with [[Gambel Oak]], meadows, and [[Riparian Vegetation]] (narrow-leaf cottonwood, willow, and alder).

**Soils and sediment.** Soils exceed 1 meter in depth, formed mainly from Pennsylvanian-Permian sandstone and shale. The top two inches are highly erodible with unusually high silt content, creating a [[Siltation]] hazard. Water from the springhead carries colloidal particles from an unknown source above the Rim.

**Hydrology.** Springs average about 10 cfs. The USGS gauge two miles above the Salt River confluence operated only from 1976 to 1981. Estimated 25-year peak flows (Q25) are 1,600 cfs for Canyon Creek above the Mule Creek confluence (14.9 sq mi), 700 cfs for Mule Creek (8.4 sq mi), and 2,800 cfs at the Forest Service boundary (32.5 sq mi). Stream slopes are 4.3% (springhead to Mule Creek), 4.7% (Mule Creek), 1.8% (to Valentine Canyon), and 1.1% (to the boundary).

**Water quality.** Forest Service data since 1968 generally meet Arizona wildlife and fish standards. Water temperature, however, exceeds the 68°F cold-water maximum. No sediment data existed for the study area, though the abundance of silt and clay in the channel indicates a problem.

**Land-use pressures.**
- *[[Timber Harvest]]:* Sales in 1986-1988 (Misquito, Sheep Creek, Valentine, Canyon Creek) affect the basin, with roughly 10 million board feet on 842 acres. Buffer zones protect riparian areas, but haul roads and skid trails are a sedimentation source.
- *[[Livestock Grazing]]:* Sheep driveways cross the creek, and the [[OW Ranch]] cattle allotment was historically yearlong. Overuse of streamside cottonwood and willow removed shade, and trampling aggravated soil loss. Since 1979 the allotment has used a summer seasonal yearling program with deferred rotation.
- *Recreation and roads:* [[Forest Road 188]] runs along the creek and is a major sediment source. Off-road vehicle use is widespread on old logging roads, and a pole fence provides only partial deterrence.

**Beaver activity.** [[Beaver]] damage threatens riparian regeneration, and the plan calls for trapping.

## Key Findings

**1. Habitat assessment (February 1986 mid-winter survey).** Seven reaches with seven transects each were sampled along Canyon Creek, at 500-foot intervals, and compared against optimum values for brown and rainbow trout (Raleigh et al. 1984a,b). Limiting factors, ranked by priority, are:

| Limiting factor | Reach 1 | Reach 2 | Reaches 3-6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fines too high | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| Lack of spawning substrate | 2 | 2 | 6 |
| Lack of pools | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| Lack of instream cover (adults) | - | 3 | 3 |
| Lack of shade / max temperature too high | - | 5 | 1 |
| Bank instability | - | 6 | 4 |

Summer maximum temperatures of 22.6-25.6 °C in reaches 3-7 exceed the brown trout optimum of 12-19 °C. Reach 1 at 15 °C is within range, but its habitat is considered limited by watershed conditions and cannot easily be improved.

**2. Team conclusion on Reach 1.** Habitat for large fish is currently unavailable. Natural reproduction would likely produce stunted trout. Effort is therefore concentrated on reaches 2-6, from the sheep driveway/OW boundary fence to the box canyon.

**3. Reach 7.** Temperatures here should become acceptable once upstream thermal loading is reduced, because the steep canyon walls provide shade.

**4. Solutions for reaches 2-6 (Table 5).**
- Plant narrow-leaf cottonwood poles at 10-20 ft intervals, and plant clumps of 20-30 cottonwoods at 50-100 ft intervals.
- Experiment with alder, other riparian species, and summer planting.
- Build a riparian pasture exclosure, slope and vegetate cut banks, and maintain the pole fence.
- Install a headgate for OW irrigation, close or control FR 188, and add logs and boulders for immediate instream cover.
- Enhance pools over 3-4 years.
- Control beavers by trapping.

**5. Five-year action plan and costs.** Items are listed in priority order.

| Priority | Action | Approximate cost |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Finish Implementation Plan and EA | 10 person-days |
| 2 | Fence riparian pasture | $20,000 |
| 3 | Beaver trapping | $3,000/yr |
| 4 | Monitoring: thermographs; crest stage gauges; automatic sediment samples | $4,200; $1,300; 30 person-days |
| 5 | Riparian planting: cottonwood poles; modified poles; experimental summer, alder, and sycamore | $6,000/mi; $10,000/mi; $5,000 each |
| 6 | Cutbank modification and seeding (12 locations) | $5,000/location |
| 7 | Structural enhancement of pools | $7,000/mi |
| 8 | Ongoing monitoring: macroinvertebrates, fisheries surveys, cover/photo points | $600 plus 2 person-days/yr; 16 person-days/yr; 40 person-days |
| 9-10 | Finish Mule Creek inventory; manage Mule Creek under Canyon Creek rules | 6-9 person-days; 2 person-days |
| 11 | Close FR 188 and build new road to OW | $3,000; $15,000 |
| 12-13 | Maintain/expand pole fence; install OW headgate | 200 person-days; $3,500 |
| 14 | Study sinkholes on the Rim | $50,000 over 3 years |
| 15-16 | Modify fisheries regulations; evaluate and revise plan | 3 person-days; 20 person-days |

**6. Monitoring priorities.** Maximum stream temperature is the primary success criterion. Crest stage gauges are recommended above and below Mule Creek and Valentine Canyon. A USGS gaging station was judged prohibitively expensive ($20,000-30,000 to build, plus $10,000-15,000 per year). Fisheries surveys should move to a biannual schedule, using length-weight, size-class, and population data to judge whether a catch-and-release Blue Ribbon fishery is feasible.

**7. Information gaps.** The plan calls for coordination on upper-watershed and sinkhole effects, macroinvertebrate abundance and diversity studies, Mule Creek bank and pool data, and creel and size-class surveys. It also recommends re-evaluating stocking rates, size and bag limits, and take methods.

**8. Data note.** Section 1.1 gives the Canyon Creek drainage as "approximately 325 square miles," while Section 2.11 and the watershed table give 32.5 square miles. The 32.5 figure is used in this note, because it matches the watershed table.
