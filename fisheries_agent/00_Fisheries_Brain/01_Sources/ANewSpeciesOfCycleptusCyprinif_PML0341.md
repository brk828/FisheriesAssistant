# A New Species of *Cycleptus* (Cypriniformes: Catostomidae) from Gulf Slope Drainages of Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

Burr and Mayden (1999) describe a new North American catostomid sucker, [[Southeastern Blue Sucker]] (*Cycleptus meridionalis*), from Gulf Slope drainages between the Mobile Basin, Alabama, and the Pearl River, Louisiana. The paper also reviews the distribution, biology, and conservation status of the genus *Cycleptus*, which was previously regarded as monotypic. Analysis of more than 390 museum specimens revealed geographically concordant variation in dorsal fin-ray count, scale counts, body shape, and lip morphology. The authors recognize two species: the restricted *[[Blue Sucker]]* (*C. elongatus*) and the new *C. meridionalis*. Samples from the Rio Grande are allocated to *C. elongatus* with an explicit caveat that further study may support a separate taxon. Both species are long-lived, reach large sizes, aggregate in large numbers for spring spawning, and are of commercial value. The authors recommend monitoring population numbers, spawning sites, and recruitment.

## Background & Description

*Cycleptus* was described by [[Charles Alexander Lesueur]] in 1817 as *Catostomus elongatus* from a dried specimen collected by Thomas Say in the Ohio River. Rafinesque's genus name was later resurrected by Agassiz (1854). The genus is endemic to North America and was historically regarded as basal within the Catostomidae or as sister to the Chinese *Myxocyprinus*. Smith (1992) placed it as sister taxon to *Myxocyprinus*, and a reported Asian species, *C. robustus*, was rejected because the fossil teeth were misidentified.

Morphological and meristic traits used for diagnosis include:

- **Body:** Elongate, oval in cross-section; body depth roughly 4–5 times into SL in the original generic description.
- **Head and lips:** Small mouth; lips covered in papillae that range from short and blunt to elongate and barbel-like, most extreme in southwestern rivers.
- **Fins:** Falcate dorsal fin with long anterior rays; large, widely forked caudal fin; small anal fin.
- **Scales:** Lateral-line scales usually 49–58 in *C. elongatus*; caudal-peduncle scale rows usually 19–20 in *C. elongatus*.
- **Breeding males:** Covered in white epidermal tubercles during spring.
- **Chromosomes:** 2n = 96–100, reflecting tetraploidy ([[Tetraploidy in Catostomids]]).

Methods included the [[Truss Network Morphometrics]] protocol (28 measurements, plus 5 additional head and lip measures) analysed with [[Sheared Principal Components Analysis]] to remove size effects, run separately for each sex. Species concepts followed the [[Evolutionary Species Concept]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range.** The historical range spans the Mississippi River basin from western Pennsylvania to central Montana and south to Louisiana, plus Gulf Slope drainages from the Sabine River to the Rio Grande. *C. meridionalis* is restricted to the Mobile Basin (Alabama and Mississippi), and the Pascagoula and Pearl river drainages. Notably, the species is extirpated from Pennsylvania, and the authors document an unexplained gap in central Texas between the Neches and Rio Grande Basins.

**Habitat.** Both species occupy strong currents in main channels of medium to large rivers. Juveniles use shallower riffles (<1 m) than adults (1.0–2.5 m). Both species use human-made structures such as wing dykes, dam bases, reservoir depths, and bridge abutments. *C. elongatus* tolerates turbidity where currents are strong enough to prevent silt deposition.

**Biology.**
- *C. elongatus* is reported to live at least 12 years, with some estimates of 30+ years; Mississippi River scale-aging recorded ages up to X, while [[Opercular Ageing]] in *C. meridionalis* produced an age of 22 (Cahaba River) and up to 31–33 in Mississippi.
- Spawning occurs in late April–May at 10–20 °C in *C. elongatus*; *C. meridionalis* spawns from February–April, and in the Alabama River, spawning occurred around 4–5 April.
- Diet is dominated by aquatic insect larvae, with *C. meridionalis* consuming trichopteran larvae in 83.0% of 206 adult stomachs examined.
- Both species follow a "flood-pulse" reproductive strategy.

**Threats and Decline.** Dams block upstream spawning migrations, and tailwater aggregations are vulnerable. Other drivers include degraded water quality, excessive spawning-run catches, channel sand-choking, and surface water depletion in New Mexico. Pearson and Froedge (1989) recorded 556 adults stranded below McAlpine Dam on the Ohio River.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomy:** A new species, *Cycleptus meridionalis* (Southeastern Blue Sucker), is described. Its holotype is UAIC 11431.03, a 500 mm SL male from the Alabama River, Wilcox County, Alabama. Paratypes total 9 specimens.
- **Diagnostic counts:** *C. meridionalis* usually has 25–29 dorsal rays (vs. 28–34 in *C. elongatus*), usually 16 caudal-peduncle scale rows (vs. 19–20), 37–40 body-circumferential scale rows (vs. 41–49), and 49–53 lateral-line scales (vs. 53–58). It also has a shorter snout and shorter dorsal fin base.
- **Specimen basis:** More than 390 museum specimens were examined, with range records summarised across large numbers of catalogue entries.
- **Rio Grande populations:** These differ in elongate lip papillae, faint side striping, and brassy-golden body colour. The striping was inconsistent across hundreds of live individuals. These populations are allocated to *C. elongatus* pending further study of tubercle patterns, spawning colours, and genetic data.
- **Conservation status:** Federal Category 2 status for *C. elongatus* confers no legal protection. The species is listed as threatened or of special concern in multiple states. Recent records show ongoing reproduction and recruitment in many river reaches, including the Big Bend region of the Rio Grande, where *C. elongatus* was the most abundant large fish.
- **Recommendations:** The authors urge monitoring of population numbers, spawning sites, and recruitment, maintenance of water quality, and minimal dam construction and habitat modification.
- **Genetic work:** Isozyme and allozyme studies by Buth and Mayden reveal fixed differences in Rio Grande samples, suggesting a possible unique taxon.
