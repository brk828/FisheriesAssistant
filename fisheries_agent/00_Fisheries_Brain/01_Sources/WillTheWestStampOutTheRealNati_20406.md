# Endangered Fishes of the Colorado River Basin and Columbia River Salmon Decline

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 *High Country News* feature examines the decline of four endemic Colorado River fishes, led by the [[Colorado Squawfish]], and places it alongside the collapse of salmon runs in the [[Columbia River Basin]]. Dams, water diversions, cold tailwater releases and non-native predators are identified as the main drivers. The article presents recovery efforts, chiefly the [[Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Implementation Plan]] of 1988, and argues that the central obstacle is political: recovery depends on acquiring water rights and instream flows. Scientists disagree on whether the programme is working.

## Background & Description

The article follows U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) biologists on the Colorado River near Grand Junction, Colorado, where technicians collect larval fish by seine to estimate spawning. Project leaders quoted include [[Lynn Kaeding]], who heads the Grand Junction office, and [[Harold Tyus]], who oversees Green and Yampa river work.

Four endangered fishes are discussed:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): the largest North American minnow. Modern adults reach about three feet and 15 pounds; bone remains from Anasazi middens suggest historic fish reached five or six feet and 80 pounds or more. Lifespan can exceed 40 years. Known locally as "Colorado salmon" or "white salmon."
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*), **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) and **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): the three companion endangered species.

Of the 13 fish species native to the basin when settlers arrived, seven occur in cold mountain headwaters and are also found in Columbia, Missouri and Great Basin streams. The other six are endemic and ancient; some fossils are estimated to exceed three million years old.

The article also covers the [[Idaho Sockeye Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*), reported as functionally extinct, in the Columbia system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Colorado River threats**

- Large dams: [[Hoover Dam]] (1930) and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (1963) blocked upstream spawning migrations. Squawfish need water above 68 °F to spawn, and cold tailwater releases prevented this below the dams.
- Non-native predators: eggs in reservoirs were eaten by introduced sportfish such as bass, crappies, sunfish and walleye. The introduced [[Green Sunfish]] is considered a probable cause of low larval survival, feeding in nursery areas.
- Habitat change: irrigation diversions, channelization, timbering and overgrazing removed quiet backwater nurseries.
- [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (1963) cold releases eliminated squawfish for 70 miles downstream until outlet works were modified in 1978. Rotenone poisoning by federal biologists, used to create a rainbow trout fishery, also caused losses.
- Over-harvest: squawfish were pitchforked from the Gila and Salt rivers around 1850 and sold in Grand Junction into the early 1900s.

**Columbia River system**

- The [[Dalles Dam]] (1956) replaced Celilo Falls. The basin contains 79 hydropower projects, including 11 dams on the Columbia and 10 on the Snake.
- Up to 90 percent of migrating young salmon may die in some years, from turbines, warm water, disease and habitat loss. Hatchery fish make up 70 percent of the population.

**Key habitats**

- [[Yampa River]]: the last undammed tributary in the Colorado basin, with near-historic flows.
- [[Green River]]: with the Yampa, about 350 miles of spawning and nursery habitat.
- 15-mile reach near Grand Junction: a priority spawning area.

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** an average sampling year finds only 100 to 125 adult squawfish in the whole river. The Colorado mainstream population is estimated at 1,000 to 2,000 fish over 205 miles, and is not stable. A remnant population in the San Juan River may have only two or three adults.
- **Strongholds:** Yampa and Green river populations are estimated at about 10 times the mainstream population.
- **Recovery plan (January 1988):** signed by Interior Secretary Donald Hodel and the governors of Utah, Wyoming and Colorado. Goal: delist the fish in 15 years. Five approaches: water acquisition, habitat manipulation (such as fish ladders), hatcheries and restocking, control of non-native fish, and research and outreach.
- **Governance:** an implementation committee (meets twice yearly) sets budget and priorities. A management committee meets every other month. A technical committee was meant to guide research, but field biologists say decisions are driven by administrators and special interests.
- **Funding:** $1 million from Congress has been raised to buy water, with a promise of $6 million more.
- **Water actions:** 10,000 acre-feet from Ruedi Reservoir (Frying Pan River) was released in July, raising 15-mile reach flows by 65 cfs (to 130 cfs). Recommended late-summer flows are 700 to 1,200 cfs, against current averages of 200 to 300 cfs.
- **Yampa:** the Nature Conservancy is negotiating purchase of a ranch supplying rights to 3,400 acre-feet. The proposed Juniper-Cross Dam near Craig, Colorado, is contested; the state has offered $6 million for its water rights.
- **1982 precedent:** the USFWS conservation plan proposed instream flows above historic levels. Peer review and water developers rejected it, and it was withdrawn.
- **Legal context:** the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973) could block river development until the fish are protected.
- **Columbia losses:** the Snake River coho was declared extinct in 1987. Oregon Trout reports 101 salmon and steelhead stocks extinct since 1850, and at least 16 current stocks endangered.
- **Central dispute:** Kaeding and Tyus stress flows and habitat. Environmental representative Carse Pustmueller describes the process as cumbersome. Water developers resist allocating water, and Tom Pitts argues the burden falls on the recovery programme. Oregon Trout's Bill Bakke argues that hatchery fish weaken wild stocks' disease resistance, and that buying natural spawning habitat is the best protection.
