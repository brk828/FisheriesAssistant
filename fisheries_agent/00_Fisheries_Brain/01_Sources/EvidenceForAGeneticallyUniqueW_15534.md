## Executive Summary

This study by Brian R. Murphy (1990) presents electrophoretic evidence for a genetically unique, homozygous population of Walleye (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*) inhabiting the upper [[Tombigbee River]] system in northeastern Mississippi. Unlike every other studied Walleye population in North America, this group displayed **zero heterozygosity** across four examined presumptive loci. The author argues this genetic fixation supports the existence of a distinct "[[Gulf Coast Strain]]" of Walleye, separate from the more genetically diverse Mississippi River strain. This unique population is now considered threatened by potential genetic introgression from Tennessee River Walleye migrating through the man-made [[Tennessee-Tombigbee Waterway]] (TTW).

## Background & Description

While this paper is primarily genetic/electrophoretic rather than morphological, it references distinguishing biological traits associated with the proposed Gulf Coast Strain of Walleye compared to the Mississippi River Strain:

*   **Body Size:** Individuals of the Gulf Coast strain reportedly do not reach the large sizes typical of northern (Mississippi River strain) Walleye populations.
*   **Population Density:** This strain does not achieve high abundance even within preferred habitats.
*   **Habitat Establishment:** Unlike northern strains, the Gulf Coast strain reportedly fails to establish viable populations in reservoir environments.
*   **Thermal Tolerance:** Prior research (Colby et al. 1979) suggests unique thermal tolerance traits may be linked to this strain's distinct genetic character.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

*   **Habitat:** Walleye were collected from riverine and tributary systems, specifically the [[Luxapallila River]], Yellow Creek, the [[Buttahatchie River]], and Sipsey Creek, all tributaries within the upper Tombigbee River drainage in Lowndes and Monroe County, Mississippi.
*   **Spawning Behavior:** The majority of specimens (n=27) were collected during the spring spawning season (1985) within 5 km of the Luxapallila River's confluence with the Tombigbee River, representing fish on a spawning migration run up the tributary.
*   **Geographic Range:** The species' natural range spans from northwestern Canada to the southeastern United States. The specific population studied belongs to a hypothesized **[[Gulf Coast Strain]]** distributed from the western Florida panhandle to the Pearl River, Mississippi.
*   **Threats/Invasive Interaction:** The primary ecological threat identified is the [[Tennessee-Tombigbee Waterway]], an artificial channel connecting the Tennessee River to the Tombigbee River. This waterway creates a passage allowing genetically distinct Mississippi River strain Walleye to invade and potentially hybridize with the native, genetically isolated Gulf Coast population.

## Key Findings

*   **Genetic Fixation:** Electrophoretic analysis of skeletal muscle malate dehydrogenase (MDH-B), muscle myogen (MYO-A), liver isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH-A), and serum albumin (ALB-A) revealed **no heterozygosity** at any locus in the Tombigbee River Walleye population (Table 1).
*   **Contrast with Other Populations:** This complete genetic uniformity starkly contrasts with the significant genetic variability documented in Walleye populations across Canada, Minnesota (MN), Tennessee (TN), Texas (TX), Virginia (VA), Pennsylvania (PA), Kansas (KS), and Nebraska (NE).
*   **Evidence Against Transplantation:** The extreme genetic uniformity contradicts the assumption that this population resulted from modern transplantation efforts, as introduced fish typically carry significantly more genetic diversity. This supports the theory of a long-isolated, native population.
*   **Genetic Bottleneck Hypothesis:** The depauperate genetic diversity is theorized to be the result of a historical **genetic bottleneck**, possibly linked to a limited stream capture event in the region's geological past.
*   **Conservation Implications:** Because the [[Tennessee River Walleye]] (likely genetically similar to Cumberland River Walleye studied by Dryden et al. 1988) possess distinct, non-overlapping allele frequencies, any future genetic introgression via the TTW could be readily detected through monitoring.
*   **Management Recommendation:** The paper concludes that if invasion by non-native strains occurs, a captive breeding and transplantation program may be the only viable method to preserve the genetic integrity of the upper Tombigbee River Walleye population.
