# Reclamation Response to Provision 13(b) of the Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (Lower Colorado River)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #water-management

## Executive Summary

This draft letter and accompanying account from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Lower Colorado Regional Office (dated September 1, 1998) responds to Provision 13(b) of the Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) in the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) Biological Opinion of April 30, 1997 on Lower Colorado River operations, maintenance, and sensitive species activities. Provision 13(b) requires Reclamation to identify opportunities to increase its discretionary action flexibility with other parties, within 18 months of the final Biological Opinion. The account is intended to define the legal, regulatory, and contractual limits on Reclamation's management of the river and to identify parties with action flexibility who could help supply water for fish and wildlife.

Fourteen stakeholder meetings were held in June and July 1998 across Arizona, California, and Nevada, followed by a public forum on August 4, 1998 in Las Vegas. Stakeholders were generally willing to help meet fish and wildlife needs but largely questioned whether the 13(b) process was appropriate, arguing it was premature and should be folded into the [[Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (MSCP). Comments were due October 1, 1998, and the final document was to be submitted to FWS by October 30, 1998.

## Background & Description

The document is a regulatory and administrative record rather than a field study. It contains:

- A cover letter from the Regional Director, inviting comments to Bill Rinne of Reclamation staff.
- An executive summary explaining the purpose of Provision 13(b), quoting FWS's stated intent that the component will clarify legal limits, identify other parties, determine the need for section 7 or section 10 discussions, and assess their involvement in the MSCP Steering Committee.
- An introduction listing the fourteen stakeholder groups consulted: five tribes ([[Fort Mojave Indian Tribe]], [[Chemehuevi Indian Tribe]], [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]], [[Quechan Indian Tribe]], [[Cocopah Indian Tribe]]), Upper Basin States, Environmental Organizations, Lower Division States, California, Arizona, Power, Nevada, and Federal Agencies interests.
- A list of stakeholder concerns, followed by nine proposed strategies and several additional ideas, each with stakeholder comments.

Water apportionment is framed as follows: under normal conditions, the Lower Basin's 7.5 million acre-feet (maf) per year is apportioned among Arizona (2.8 maf), California (4.4 maf), and Nevada (0.3 maf). Use requires contracts with the Secretary, except for certain federal establishments with perfected rights identified in Article II(D) of the 1964 Decree in [[Arizona v. California]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The water-use framework is directly tied to habitat management:

- **System losses:** Lower Basin system losses average 1.75 maf/year, comprising about 1.235 maf/year of evaporation from Lakes [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]], and [[Lake Havasu]], and about 0.515 maf/year of phreatophyte (riparian vegetation) use.
- **Vegetation quality:** According to Reclamation's Vegetation Management Study, only quail bush is classed as high-quality habitat. Salt cedar is considered low quality, though it has value to the Colorado River Indian Tribes as dove nesting habitat.
- **Yuma area hydrology:** Stakeholders flagged that higher in-stream flows could raise groundwater tables in the Yuma area and affect sedimentation near Mexico's diversion points.
- **Endangered species:** Several comments stressed that habitat replacement and fallowing must avoid disturbing listed species and that fallowing could jeopardize species through water loss.
- **Power operations:** Releases at [[Hoover Dam]], [[Davis Dam]], and [[Parker Dam]] are linked to hydropower contracts; fluctuating lake levels benefit power but are unfavorable to fish and wildlife.

## Key Findings

**Stakeholder reception and process concerns**

- Most stakeholders expressed general willingness to help meet fish and wildlife needs, but questioned whether 13(b) was premature because specific needs (quantity, location, timing) had not yet been defined.
- Many participants wanted the activity turned over to the MSCP, which is developing those specific needs and conservation elements.
- Concerns were raised that FWS and Reclamation had not yet responded to comments on the April 30, 1997 Biological Opinion.
- Unresolved questions included funding sources, cost-effectiveness of every approach, legal obstacles (contracts, law, guarantees), and who benefits from any water provided.
- Mexico was not contacted during information gathering; it would be involved if future actions there proved necessary.

**Strategies evaluated (summary)**

1. **System losses:** Replace lower-quality with higher-quality habitat while charging use as system loss. Stakeholders doubted water savings between vegetation types were significant.
2. **Flood control water:** Use releases above downstream entitlement needs (spillway and anticipatory releases) for MSCP purposes, not charged against state apportionments. Comments stressed careful planning of anticipatory releases.
3. **In-stream flow:** Keep more water in the channel for delivery to Mexico at the Northerly International Boundary. Strongly opposed by the Power/operations interests due to possible loss of generation at Pilot Knob.
4. **Pay for conservation:** Fund extraordinary conservation and land fallowing under California law, which counts such water as beneficially used. Comments warned of bidding wars and that short-term leasing offers little benefit.
5. **Buy or lease water:** Acquire land with water rights (examples: Planet Ranch, Cibola, Imperial Irrigation District lands). Requires legal review and raises consistency questions with the law of the river.
6. **Intentionally created unused apportionment:** Entitlement holders reduce consumptive use under cooperative agreements, and the Secretary releases that apportionment for MSCP purposes. Described as a method, not a strategy, and noted as a long process with rising monitoring costs.
7. **Banking water:** Store water in offstream banks via cooperative agreement for later fish and wildlife use. Described as a method; comments noted difficulty recovering banked water and that timing was not right.
8. **Hoover Dam release timing:** Constrained by power contracts (ramping, regulation, reserves) and plant capability; some groups argued the Endangered Species Act takes precedence.
9. **Davis and Parker Dam release timing:** Further restrictions would be offset by spot-market energy purchases by Western. Stakeholders called it a water scheduling rather than power issue and considered it not a great opportunity.

**Additional ideas (about 20 listed)** included lease negotiations with landowners, paying for conservation under BLM concessionaire contracts, purchasing unused Upper Basin or Indian water rights, delisting species, relaxing conservation requirements to create incidental habitat, allowing wheeling, purchasing Virgin River water, using groundwater or drainage water, negotiating with Mexico, revisiting the Colorado River Compact, draining Lake Powell, clarifying Las Vegas Wash objectives, and dredging for capacity.

**Overall conclusion:** The document finds that some limited opportunity exists to increase Colorado River management flexibility for fish and wildlife through collaboration with entitlement holders, power users, and the Lower Division States, and that the relevant parties are the active MSCP stakeholders.
