# Managing Vegetation to Increase Flow in the Colorado River Basin

#hydrology #watershed-management #vegetation-management

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service General Technical Report (RM-66, November 1979) by [[Alden R. Hibbert]] estimates how much surface water could be added to the [[Colorado River Basin]] by managing vegetation and snow to reduce [[Evapotranspiration]]. The report is a summary of a longer companion paper prepared with the Pacific Southwest Interagency Committee. It treats the basin's 11 cover types individually, sets arbitrary augmentation goals, and works out the treatment areas that would be needed to meet them.

The central conclusion is that the basin's water supply could in theory rise by about one-third (6 million acre-feet per year) if vegetation and snow on 16% of the basin were managed solely for water yield. The author states that multiple-use constraints, economics, and social and environmental concerns would substantially reduce both the treatable area and the effectiveness of treatments. The illustrative plan is 500,000 acre-feet per year (3.5%) from the [[Upper Basin]] and 250,000 acre-feet per year (8%) from the [[Lower Basin]].

## Background & Description

**Basin setting.** The Colorado River drains nearly 250,000 square miles (160 million acres) across seven western states before reaching the Gulf of California. The Upper Basin covers about 70 million acres and the Lower Basin about 90 million acres. The division point is [[Lee Ferry]], which is the official Compact point for apportioning Upper Basin flow.

**Water supply and demand.**
- Precipitation averages 15.7 inches per year in the Upper Basin and 13 inches in the Lower Basin.
- Streamflow is 16% (2.5 inches) of precipitation in the Upper Basin and 3% (0.4 inch) in the Lower Basin.
- Lee Ferry annual yields have ranged from 37% to 163% of the 83-year mean of 14.7 million acre-feet. A 450-year tree-ring reconstruction by Stockton and Jacoby (1976) gives a long-term mean of 13.5 million acre-feet.
- Under the [[Colorado River Compact]], the Upper Basin apportionment is 7.5 million acre-feet per year. Together with the Mexican delivery obligation of 1.5 million acre-feet per year, total commitments are 16.5 million acre-feet.
- Lower Basin [[Ground Water Overdraft]] averages nearly 2.5 million acre-feet per year.
- Major reservoirs provide about 32 million acre-feet of storage in each basin.

**Evapotranspiration.** Of the average 190 million acre-feet (14.2 inches) of annual rain and snow, more than 90% evaporates. A 1% reduction in basin-wide evapotranspiration would add about 1.75 million acre-feet per year.

**Management methods.** The report lists four ways to reduce evapotranspiration:
1. Reduce stand density (thinning), which lowers transpiration and interception.
2. Convert cover types to lower-water-use vegetation ([[Type Conversion]]).
3. Create forest openings that reduce transpiration and redistribute snow ([[Clearcutting]] and patch cutting).
4. Establish trees or shrubs in windswept areas to trap snow in drifts ([[Snow Management]]).

Clearcutting and type conversion usually produce the largest yield gains. [[Thinning]] is judged the least effective, because about 50% of crown cover must be removed before yield rises appreciably.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate control.** Vegetation follows climate. Lower Basin deserts receive less than 5 inches of precipitation, while the wettest mountain areas exceed 50 inches. Water yield rises rapidly with precipitation, from near zero in deserts to as much as 40 inches in alpine areas.

**Thresholds.** Little opportunity exists where precipitation is below about 18 inches and is exceeded by potential evapotranspiration. Opportunity is also limited in very cold, wet climates, where plant water use is already low. The greatest opportunity lies where precipitation exceeds 18 inches and potential evapotranspiration (Thornthwaite method) exceeds 15 inches. [[Upstream Riparian Areas]] are a special case, because they have access to extra water and high evapotranspiration.

**Non-water values.** Treatments affect other resources. Type conversion can increase forage and wildlife edge habitat and reduce fire hazard. Treatments can also reduce scenic quality, timber value, and soil stability, and they can be beneficial for some wildlife species and harmful to others. Aspen regeneration may require clearcutting. Pilot studies such as the [[Beaver Creek Woods Canyon Watershed]] are examining multiresource management in ponderosa pine.

**Cover types.** Eleven cover types cover about 76 million acres, nearly half the basin. About 50 million acres are big sagebrush and pinyon-juniper, which have little potential. The focus is on about 26 million acres (16% of the basin).

**Main findings**

- **Overall potential.** The theoretical maximum is about 6 million acre-feet per year if 16% of the basin were managed only for water yield. Realistic augmentation is much smaller.
- **Upper Basin plan.** About 500,000 acre-feet per year (3.5%) could be generated by treating up to 22% of each cover type, or 40% of aspen. About half of the gain would come from [[Subalpine Forests]] treated on 22% of their area. Mountain brush and aspen follow in importance. The alpine and big sagebrush contributions are the least certain.
- **Lower Basin plan.** Another 250,000 acre-feet per year (8%) would require more extensive treatment. About 92% of the gain would come from treating roughly 20% of the [[Chaparral]] and 33% of the [[Ponderosa Pine]].
- **Snow fences.** Tests suggest about 60–120 feet of fence per acre-foot of extra snow storage. At 2,000-foot spacing the potential is 2.2–4.4 inches, which the author considers tentative. Fences also have high costs and visual impacts.
- **Patch cutting versus clearcutting.** In the [[Mixed Conifer Forests]], clearcuts give larger gains than in subalpine forests. In subalpine forests, patch cutting is the most water-productive method. Shelterwood and individual-tree selection methods would give less than half the patch-cut increase.
- **Aspen.** Gains from clearcutting decline within 10–15 years if regrowth is allowed. Averaged over an 80-year rotation, the gain is about 0.3 inch per year. On a 25-year rotation the gain is about 1 inch per year on the cut area.
- **Chaparral conversion.** Onsite gains from experimental conversions averaged 3.8 inches at 22 inches of precipitation. The report adjusts this to 2.4 inches downstream, allowing for losses to riparian vegetation. Only about 1 acre in 5 is considered treatable, and steep slopes (above 50–60%) and wilderness designations restrict the area.
- **Wet-year concentration.** More than 80% of chaparral yield gains occur in wetter-than-average years, so [[Storage Facilities]] are essential.
- **Upstream riparian areas.** Eradication could save 6–24 inches, but the report concludes these areas are unlikely to be a significant source because of their high scenic, wildlife, and recreational value.
- **Costs.**
  - Commercial forest management: about $1–5 per acre-foot (mid-to-late 1960s estimates), the least expensive source.
  - Chaparral type conversion: $10–50 per acre-foot, averaging near $20.
  - Preliminary snow fence estimates: $10–20 per acre-foot on favorable sites.
  - Salt River Valley value of added water: $12.50 per acre-foot (1972 prices). Chaparral conversion produced a net return of $2.51 per acre.
  - [[Central Arizona Project]] water: $46 per acre-foot charge for municipal-industrial users, with full economic costs above $100 per acre-foot.
- **Timeframe.** Full implementation in commercial forests may take several decades, since subalpine harvest rotations run about 120 years. Aspen product demand may take 25–40 years to grow. Chaparral conversion is in pilot testing and could act sooner.
- **Weather modification synergy.** Snowfall can be raised by 5–30% (about 10% on average). Combined with vegetation treatment, the efficiency of vegetation management may rise by about 5–10%.
- **Verification.** Gains are difficult to measure at downstream reservoirs because they blend with other flows. Pilot demonstrations on watersheds of several thousand acres are recommended.
- **Desalting plant example.** The proposed [[Yuma Desalting Plant]] would produce about 42,000 acre-feet per year of reject water. Replacing it would require about 250,000 acres of subalpine forest management or about 210,000 acres of brush-to-grass conversion.
