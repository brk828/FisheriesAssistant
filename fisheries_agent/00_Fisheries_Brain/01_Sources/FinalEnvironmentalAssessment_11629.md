# Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service programmatic environmental assessment (November 1987) analyzes the environmental consequences of [[Department of the Interior]] participation in a Federal/State/private [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for three endangered fishes ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]]) and one rare candidate fish ([[Razorback Sucker]]) in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The program was developed by the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Coordinating Committee]] to avoid a major confrontation between water development and endangered species instream flow needs.

Two alternatives were evaluated in depth: the [[No Action Alternative]] (continued current management) and the [[Proposed Action]] (program participation). The Finding of No Significant Impact concludes that approving Department participation does not constitute a major federal action significantly affecting the environment. The greatest adverse impacts (electrical generation, recreational boating, coldwater sportfishing) stem from mandatory [[Section 7 Consultation]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]], which are common to both alternatives. The program is largely an acceleration of existing efforts, with the principal difference being the acquisition of water rights for instream flows.

## Background & Description

The four target species are large-river, warmwater fishes endemic to the Colorado River system. Historically more abundant, they declined because dams and reservoirs altered flows, obstructed migration, and created lentic and coldwater habitat, while introduced nonnative species were believed to compete with and prey on them. The Service federally lists the first three as endangered and classifies the razorback sucker as a candidate. The four are collectively termed "rare" in the document.

The program's stated goal is to recover and delist the three endangered species and manage the razorback sucker so it no longer requires [[Endangered Species Act]] protection, within an initial 15-year timeframe. The goal must proceed without disrupting [[State Water Rights]], [[Interstate Compacts]], or court decrees allocating [[Colorado River]] water.

The program has five recovery elements:
1. Habitat management (instream flow acquisition and Federal reservoir operations)
2. Habitat development and maintenance (backwaters, spawning habitat, fish passage)
3. Stocking of rare fish species (hatchery and grow-out pond production)
4. Nonnative fish control and sportfishing management
5. Research, monitoring, and data management

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species distributions and habitat**
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: Adults most abundant in the upper and lower Green River; spawning in July-August in the lower Yampa River and Gray Canyon. Young-of-year concentrate in shallow backwaters with silt and sand substrates.
- [[Humpback Chub]]: Restricted to canyon habitats (Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon on the Colorado, Gray Canyon on the Green, Yampa Canyon). Spawning April to July.
- [[Bonytail Chub]]: Extremely rare; only incidental collections in recent years, with the last sightings in 1984 and 1985.
- [[Razorback Sucker]]: Largest population in the upper Green River; recruitment apparently unsuccessful since 1979, with no young or juveniles collected despite intensive sampling.

**Native and nonnative fishes**
- Seven nonrare native species remain in the Upper Basin (e.g., [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Mountain Whitefish]]).
- 41 nonnative species from 11 families are established (Table III-D-1). Concerns focus on problem species such as [[Channel Catfish]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Green Sunfish]].

**Other biological resources**
- Four State and Federal waterfowl areas (e.g., [[Browns Park National Wildlife Refuge]], [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]]) depend on Green River flows.
- Six endemic fishes are endangered or rare, including the [[Kendall Warm Springs Dace]] and [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], which the program does not directly target.
- Listed terrestrial species near rivers include [[Bald Eagle]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], [[Whooping Crane]], and [[Uinta Basin Hookless Cactus]].

**Water and infrastructure**
- Average annual precipitation ranges from over 50 inches in headwaters to under 6 inches in deserts. Natural flow of the Colorado at Lee's Ferry averaged 15,328,000 acre-feet (1906-1986).
- Key Federal projects: [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], [[Aspinall Unit]], [[Ruedi Reservoir]], and [[Green Mountain Reservoir]].
- Principal water quality concern is salinity; water temperature below reservoirs may affect squawfish reproduction.

**Socioeconomics**
- Sportfishing yields about 5 million angler-days and roughly $72.5 million to the economy.
- [[Western Area Power Administration]] faces a short-term loss of $858,451/year from the assumed flow regimes.

## Key Findings

- **Program is an acceleration, not a new direction:** The Proposed Action differs from the No Action alternative mainly in pace and in the water rights acquisition program.
- **Impacts are largely shared:** Refinement of Federal dam operations drives most impacts in electrical generation, coldwater sportfishing, and recreational boating, and these are nearly identical under both alternatives.
- **Water rights:** The Secretary will not condemn water rights or acquire water from unwilling sellers. Acquisition relies on willing sellers. The Program will fund purchases, which the No Action alternative does not.
- **Section 7 offset:** Under the Proposed Action, depletion impacts may be offset by a monetary contribution at $10/acre-foot (adjusted for inflation), rather than project redesign.
- **Pace of recovery:** If funded only by the public sector, research and recovery would proceed 53% faster under the Proposed Action. With private contributions, the estimate is 37% to 103% faster.
- **Funding:** The Proposed Action assumes at least $15 million from Congress (including a $10 million water rights fund and a $5 million construction fund), $2.1 million/year from Federal agencies, and $0.2 million/year from States. The No Action alternative assumes $1.0 million/year Federal funding.
- **Electrical impacts:** Short-term financial loss to Western of $858,451/year, with a 1.1% SLCA-IP rate increase after 1999. A thermal replacement plant would cost $5.8 million/year and could raise retail rates up to 4%. Operational flexibility at Flaming Gorge could reduce the retail rate impact below 0.2%.
- **Recreation:** Ruedi boat ramps face increased unusable probability in July (2%) and August (8%). Flaming Gorge flows fall 0-100 cfs below the 1,500 cfs minimum 11% of the time in August-September in an average year.
- **Waterfowl:** Reduced summer Flaming Gorge flows likely increase pumping costs at managed waterfowl areas in average to wet years.
- **Nonnative control:** Impacts to problem warmwater nonnative fish are not expected to be major, and control is limited and selective.
- **Incidental take:** Up to 10% of marked Colorado squawfish in the Green River were reported caught by anglers in some years. A 2 to 4 year creel survey is recommended.
- **Single-strategy and Federal-only alternatives** were rejected because they cannot achieve recovery, and strong Federal action would strain State-Federal relationships.
- **Cumulative effects:** Without the program, the Service may be unable to identify reasonable and prudent alternatives for future water development, potentially forcing a choice between halting development and seeking Endangered Species Committee exemptions.
