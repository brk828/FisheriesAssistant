# Changes in Macroinvertebrate and Fish Populations of the Green River Following Inlet Modification of Flaming Gorge Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1981 final report by BIO/WEST, Inc. (Logan, Utah), prepared for the Water and Power Resources Service and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, documents three years (1978–1980) of benthic macroinvertebrate and fish monitoring below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] after warmer penstock inlet modifications became operational on June 30, 1978. The modification aimed to warm cold tailwaters to improve a [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery and to enhance reproduction of rare native fishes, especially the [[Colorado Squawfish]], in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. The study found that warming increased warm-water fish presence and reproduction in the Green River above the Yampa confluence, reduced cold-water trout abundance there, raised invertebrate diversity near the dam, and was associated with the first documented Colorado squawfish reproduction in the Yampa-Echo Park reach in over a decade, though the authors describe that link as unproven.

## Background & Description

- **Dam and modification:** Flaming Gorge Dam became operational in November 1962. A trout fishery in the first 26 miles of cold tailwater was rated among the best in the U.S. from 1963 to 1967, then declined. Penstock inlets were modified in early 1978 to warm releases.
- **Study design:** The Utah Division of Wildlife Resources studied the trout fishery to the Utah border. BIO/WEST studied macroinvertebrates over 85 miles and fishes over 75 miles, plus the Yampa River under additional funding.
- **Objectives:** (1) document existing species composition and relative abundance of benthic macroinvertebrates and fishes; (2) compare macroinvertebrate densities before and after penstock modification; (3) assess changes in distribution and reproduction of native and introduced fishes linked to altered temperatures.
- **Study area:** The Green River from the dam to near Jensen (110 miles) and the Yampa River from Cross Mountain Gorge (Lily Park) to its mouth (55 miles). Stations included Little Hole, Taylor Flats, Wade and Curtis, Jensen (invertebrates), and Lodore, Alcove Brook, Echo Park, Island Park, Jensen, Lower Yampa and Lily Park (fishes), with Box Elder Park on the Yampa.
- **Pre-modification baseline:** Because sampling began too late for a full pre-modification year, "before" conditions relied on a literature review and the 1978 data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime:** Before 1963 the Green was natural. From 1963 to 1967, reservoir filling produced constant, cold, low winter-spring flows. From 1967 to 1978, post-filling flows roughly doubled, which the authors argue lowered tailwater temperatures only modestly (1–2 °C) but limited warming by solar and air heating. At Jensen, June 1978 and 1979 mean temperatures reached about 12–17 °C and July–August reached 20–23 °C.
- **Yampa influence:** The [[Yampa River]] ameliorated cold, low Green River flows downstream of its mouth, so native reproduction was more natural below the confluence than above it.
- **Lodore and Alcove Brook:** Lodore was nearly 100% sand with low habitat diversity and strong daily fluctuations from dam releases. Alcove Brook had cobble-rubble and sand with more habitat. Both lie upstream of the Yampa confluence.
- **Invertebrate drivers:** Authors propose that periphyton stimulated by cool, clear releases supports collector-gatherer and scraper groups, while thermal stability favors tolerant taxa such as [[Baetis]] spp. ([[Ward and Stanford]] 1979, cited).
- **Concerns:** Proposed power peaking at Flaming Gorge could affect downstream invertebrates and fishes.

## Key Findings

**Macroinvertebrates**
- Standing crop and diversity were highest near the dam at Little Hole (7 MBD) and Taylor Flats (16.5 MBD). Total densities at Little Hole and Taylor Flats rose from 1978 to 1979 and fell in 1980 to near 1978 levels; the change was statistically significant there.
- Peak [[Gammarus lacustris]] density at Little Hole rose from 452/m² (1978) to 3,225/m² (1979) and 3,992/m² (1980). [[Hydroptila]] sp. rose from 0 to 724/m² (Little Hole) and 886/m² (Taylor Flats) in 1980.
- Species diversity at Little Hole and Taylor Flats increased, and in 1980 no significant differences in diversity appeared among stations. Little Hole's 1980 mean diversity was significantly higher than 1965–1979 values (P<.05).
- The authors conclude the inlet modification was positive for invertebrates near the dam, while noting the community remained in flux.

**Fishes**
- A total of 28 species and 3 hybrids were collected (11 native, 17 introduced), with more than 63,000 fish caught over the study.
- Warm-water and rare species extended upstream. [[Red Shiner]] and [[Sand Shiner]] moved into Alcove Brook; carp, speckled dace, flannelmouth and bluehead suckers reproduced at Lodore; and fathead minnows were the only introduced species reproducing at all stations.
- Cold-water trout declined at Lodore and Alcove Brook in 1979–1980, except for March peaks.
- Young-of-the-year [[Colorado Squawfish]] were found at Jensen in all three years and at Lower Yampa, Echo Park and Island Park in 1980. Yearling squawfish at Island Park and Jensen indicated reproduction in 1977–1980.
- Razorback sucker catches at Jensen fell from 38 (June 1978) to 12 (1979) to 2 (1980).
- Tag returns: of 55 recaptured fish (9 species and 1 hybrid), 17 came from Alcove Brook. Recapture rates indicated large flannelmouth sucker populations (1.6%, 17 of 1,033) and smaller razorback sucker populations (10%, 4 of 40).

**Gear efficiency (March 1979, 1,200 vs 4,000 cfs)**
- Electrofishing catch rates changed little with flow, but seining catches fell sharply at high flow because backwaters and sand bars were lost. Habitat quality appeared to matter more than flow alone.

**Reproduction and Colorado squawfish**
- Native reproduction above the Yampa confluence was largely absent after 1967 (cold temperatures). The 1980 Yampa-derived squawfish young were fewer than Vanicek's 1964 (275) and 1966 (650) counts; the authors note 1980 had a cold spring-early summer (4 days above 60 °F versus 17 in 1965), yet still saw reproduction, which they could not fully explain.

**Recommendations**
1. Continue studies for several years in the Lower Yampa–Echo Park–Island Park reach to confirm squawfish response to warming.
2. Study invertebrate responses below Flaming Gorge, including the effects of proposed power peaking.
3. Install temperature recorders throughout the river to verify actual temperature change.
4. Note that further warming would be unlikely to benefit rare fishes in Lodore Canyon, which is dominated by daily fluctuations, low flows, and habitat alteration.
5. Manage the selective withdrawal system for both trout and rare fish, pending temperature–flow studies.
