# Comparison of Fish Collection Methods after Rotenone Application in the New River, Virginia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 field study, published in the *Virginia Journal of Science*, compares two methods for collecting fish killed by [[Rotenone]] in a large river: a passive [[Block Net]] placed downstream of the treated reach versus six experienced biologists using standard [[Dip Net]]s. The work was carried out at nine stations on the upper [[New River]] in North Carolina and Virginia. The authors found that the block net captured significantly more species and specimens per locality than dip netting alone. Dip netting contributed about 14 percent of specimens, and the largest gap appeared among darters of the [[Etheostomatini]] (Percidae), 92 percent of which were taken in the block net. The authors conclude that a block net should always be used during rotenone sampling, while dip netting should still be performed to recover taxa the net misses and to obtain better-preserved voucher specimens.

## Background & Description

Rotenone is a piscicide long used in fisheries management. According to the review by [[Schnick 1974 Rotenone Review]], it was first used as a fisheries tool in the United States in 1934, and by 1949 thirty-four states and several Canadian provinces were using it for management and survey work. By 1974, rotenone was registered in the United States under 30 registration numbers from 17 companies, in three formulations: 5 percent emulsifiable concentrate, 2.5 percent synergized emulsifiable concentrate, and 5 percent wettable powder.

Earlier work by [[Hocutt Et Al 1973]] had estimated qualitatively that less than 10 percent of rotenone-affected fish were collected by dip nets compared with a block net placed immediately downstream. The present study was designed to quantify that estimate and to measure the relative capture efficiency of each method for several fish families.

The authors were fishery biologists and ichthyologists working on Appalachian fishes, including [[Michael T. Masnik]], [[Jay R. Stauffer Jr.]], and [[Charles H. Hocutt]]. Masnik was affiliated with the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission, while Stauffer and Hocutt were at the Appalachian Environmental Laboratory. Costs were supported by the Department of Biology at Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University under contract with the Appalachian Power Company.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Field design.** A 91 m × 3.6 m nylon block net with 6.3 mm mesh was set downstream of each treatment area. Depending on current velocity and substrate, a minimum width of 36 m was blocked. Approximately 68 to 91 m of river immediately upstream was treated with five percent powdered rotenone, applied just above riffles where possible to aid mixing. [[Potassium Permanganate]] neutralized the toxicant below the net. Collections were made at nine stations on the upper New River.

**Dip-net sampling.** Six experienced biologists worked the treated area with D-frame kick nets for about 30 minutes, until the effective rotenone period ended. Their instructions did not specify taxa, but a bias toward larger fish was likely. Dip-netted fish were placed in formalin immediately, while block-net fish were preserved separately. Specimens were deposited in the Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University fish collection.

**Behavioral differences among families.** The authors chose Cyprinidae, Centrarchidae, and Percidae for separate analysis because they respond differently to rotenone. Darters (Etheostomatini) move erratically at or just below the surface for a few seconds and then sink quickly. Their small size, short observation window, and tendency to be carried along the bottom and into the flow made them hard to catch by dip net. Cyprinids and centrarchids rise to the surface and lose equilibrium, often remaining there long enough to be caught by either method, though some large centrarchids may tolerate higher concentrations and recover enough to escape the block net.

**Known bias.** Because the block net is passive, dip-netted samples would not change if the net were absent. The authors noted that dip netting itself contributed to the total. Some dip-netted specimens would likely have drifted into the block net anyway, so the 14 percent dip-net share is probably an overestimate. Specimens that appeared only in the block net would have been lost without it, so the authors considered that gap more significant.

## Key Findings

**Overall catch**
- Total of **6,381 specimens** representing **36 species** collected across nine stations.
- Species totals: 28 species by dip net combined and 34 by block net combined.
- At four stations the block net collected every species taken. At the other five stations, dip netting added only one species each, usually represented by one or two large specimens that would likely have drifted into the net.
- Dip-net share of specimens ranged from **7 to 38 percent** per station, with an overall average of **14 percent** (block net 86 percent). The 38 percent figure came from one station, and the figures support the earlier qualitative estimate of about 10 percent.

**Family-level results**
- **Percidae (Etheostomatini):** **92 percent** of darters were taken in the block net. This was the largest difference between methods and is the main reason the authors call dip netting alone inadequate for darters.
- **Cyprinidae:** about **87 percent** of cyprinids were taken in the block net. Cyprinids were abundant in the treated area and often drifted past dip-net crews.
- **Centrarchidae:** about **63 percent** were taken in the block net, reflecting the bias of dip-net crews toward large fish and new taxa, and the greater tolerance of some sunfishes.
- Catostomidae, Cottidae, and Ictaluridae each made up less than four percent of the specimens. Their sporadic occurrence did not allow firm conclusions.

**Specimen condition**
- Block-net specimens were in poor condition because they spent extended time impinged and asphyxiated in the net.
- Dip-netted specimens were preserved almost immediately and are better suited for taxonomic work.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The block net captured more fish and taxa in a given period than six trained biologists using dip nets.
- Some families, especially Percidae, may be lost if only dip netting is used after ichthyocide application.
- The authors recommend that a block net always be used in rotenone stream surveys and that personnel numbers depend on the difficulty of setting, cleaning, and removing the net, not on dip-net collecting effort.
- Dip netting should still be performed, because some taxa may be missed by the block net, and dip-netted specimens are preferable as taxonomic vouchers.

**Other notes**
- The study was framed as a quantitative refinement of the Hocutt et al. (1973) qualitative estimate, and the authors describe the block net as a methodological requirement for realistic community sampling in large rivers.
