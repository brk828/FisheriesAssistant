# Current Status of Endangered, Threatened, and/or Rare Mollusks of New Mexico and Arizona

#malacology #conservation #hydrobiidae #endemic-species #desert-springs

## Executive Summary

This 1981 contract report to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] presents a comprehensive status assessment and prioritization of 27 mollusk species in [[New Mexico]] and [[Arizona]]. The investigation categorizes species into three priority classes: (1) endangered and threatened requiring immediate management, (2) threatened/rare associated with listed fish or other species, and (3) rare and of special concern with extremely limited distributions. The majority of listed species are hydrobioid gastropods endemic to desert springs, habitats increasingly threatened by urbanization, water development, and groundwater pumping. Two species are considered critically endangered: [[Tavasci Spring Fontelicella]] (possibly extinct by 1980) and [[Burns Spring Tryonia]] (marginal population of ~30 individuals). The report emphasizes that undescribed species rarely receive federal protection, risking extinction before formal description. Desert spring habitats face severe pressure from increased water resource demands, exotic species introductions (particularly *Melanoides tuberculatus*), and livestock impacts.

## Background & Description

The document addresses aquatic and terrestrial mollusks across [[Arizona]] and [[New Mexico]], with emphasis on hydrobioid gastropods (family [[Hydrobiidae]]). Species descriptions follow standardized format including:

**Taxonomic characteristics**: Shell morphology (size typically <3-4 mm for hydrobioids), verge (male copulatory organ) structure (bifid or trifid configurations), operculum color and shape, and anatomical features distinguishing species.

**Key taxa include**:
- **Fontelicella** species (multiple undescribed): Globose shells with bifid verges consisting of narrow penis and ductless accessory process
- **Tryonia** species (multiple undescribed): Elongate-conic shells with simple vas deferens extending to verge tip, multiple papillae
- **Amnicola neomexicana** ([[Socorro Hydrobioid]]): Minute (<1.6 mm) globose shell, nuclear whorls eroded
- **Anodonta californiensis** ([[California Anodonta]]): Large (~80 mm) thin-shelled freshwater mussel, requires fish hosts for glochidial larvae
- **Sonorella eremita**: Large terrestrial snail with light brown band, wide toothless aperture

Most species are endemic to single spring systems or small spring complexes. Hydrobioid snails occupy specific microhabitats: hard substrates (rocks, dead leaves) in moderate current for *Fontelicella*; soft sediment in slow current for *Tryonia*. Population densities in suitable habitat range from 1-5 individuals/cm².

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat characteristics**: Desert springs in the [[American Southwest]] represent isolated aquatic refugia in arid landscapes. Spring systems typically consist of source pools, outflow runs (5-30 meters), and terminal marshes or cienegas. Water temperatures range from ambient to thermal (32-44°C at [[Gila Thermal Snail]] habitat). These springs support endemic assemblages including mollusks, fish ([[Gila Topminnow]], [[Gila Chub]], pupfish species), and invertebrates ([[Thermospheroma thermophilum]] isopod).

**Threats documented**:
1. **Groundwater depletion**: [[Roswell]] area springs dried due to pumping; [[Palomas, Chihuahua]] spring near extinction by 1971
2. **Physical modification**: Spring channelization, impoundment, cattle trampling, road construction
3. **Exotic species**: *Melanoides tuberculatus* (Oriental snail) contributed to *Tryonia* declines in Nevada/California; *Corbicula fluminea* (Asian clam) present at [[Montezuma Well]]; *Gambusia affinis* (mosquitofish) displacing native fish that serve as mussel hosts
4. **Chemical treatments**: Fish hatchery disinfection protocols at [[Page Springs]] reducing mollusk populations
5. **Urban development**: [[Sonorella eremita]] threatened by Tucson expansion
6. **Livestock impacts**: Pig farming modified [[Burns Spring]]; cattle trampling at multiple sites

**Ecological relationships**: [[California Anodonta]] requires native fish (likely *Gila* species) as glochidial hosts; elimination of native fish caused mussel extirpation from most Arizona drainages. Mollusks serve as indicators of spring system health and contribute to understanding evolutionary rates, zoogeography, and paleohydrology of the region.

## Key Findings

**Priority Category 1 - Endangered/Threatened (Immediate Action Required)**:

1. **[[Tavasci Spring Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Tavasci Spring]], [[Yavapai County]], Arizona): Population declined from viable in 1976 to no living specimens found in 1980. Habitat reduced to 2 meters. Spring owned by [[Phelps Dodge Corporation]], involved in land trade to become part of [[Tuzigoot National Monument]]. Artificial pooling and canal construction contributed to decline. **Status: Possibly extinct**.

2. **[[Burns Spring Tryonia]]** (*Tryonia* n. sp., [[Burns Spring]], [[Mohave County]], Arizona): Marginal population of ~30 individuals collected since 1972 across four visits. In 1980, specimens found only in pool inside old tunnel, not in previous upper 3-meter habitat. Spring modified during historical pig farming. Adjacent to [[Bureau of Land Management]] proposed wilderness area. **Recommendation**: Immediate endangered listing and habitat modification to increase suitable microhabitats.

3. **[[Socorro Hydrobioid]]** (*Amnicola neomexicana*, [[Sedillo Springs]], [[Socorro County]], New Mexico): Presumed extirpated from type locality. Similar snail discovered at [[Torreon Springs]] in 1980 raises hope species survives. Springs used for Socorro city domestic water. Co-occurs with endangered isopod *Thermospheroma thermophilum*. **Recommendation**: Compare Torreon specimens with museum material; coordinate management with isopod recovery.

4. **[[Roswell Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Roswell Country Club Spring]], [[Chaves County]], New Mexico): Formerly in [[South Spring]] and other Roswell-area springs, now dried from groundwater pumping. Single remaining population significantly reduced after 1973-74 springhead modification. Population stable but at reduced levels by 1980. **Status: Threatened**.

5. **[[Roswell Tryonia]]** (*Tryonia* n. sp., same location): Rare in 1966-1971, abundant by 1973 (1/cm²), declined after springhead modification. **Recommendation**: Transfer both Roswell species to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] within historic range as permanent refuge.

6. **[[Sonorella eremita]]** (terrestrial snail, [[San Xavier Hill]], [[Pima County]], Arizona): Restricted to northwest end of hill near Tucson. Threatened by urban development. **Recommendation**: List as threatened; consider transplanting to nearby suitable hills.

**Priority Category 2 - Associated with Listed Species**:

7. **[[Fort Harmony Tryonia]]** (*Tryonia* n. sp., [[Ojo Caliente Springs]] and [[Warm Springs]], [[Socorro County]], New Mexico): Abundant populations in excellent habitat. Suggested as transplant site for endangered [[Socorro Isopod]]. Springs on private land adjacent to state land.

8. **[[Blue Spring Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Blue Spring]] and [[Castle Spring]], [[Eddy County]], New Mexico): Populations monitored 1968-1980. Castle Spring fluctuated from rare (1968) to common (1971-1980). Blue Spring stable with densities to 2/cm². Oil exploration ongoing threat. Co-occurs with native darters and livebearers. **Recommendation**: Acquire water rights, lease back to owner; establish 0.8 km natural sanctuary from source to cement canal.

9. **[[Father Kino's Tryonia]]** (*Tryonia* n. sp., [[Quitobaquito Springs]], [[Organ Pipe National Monument]], [[Pima County]], Arizona): Endemic to spring complex. Two-three nearby springs uninventoried. Co-occurs with [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius* n. subsp.). Within National Monument but requires inclusion in pupfish recovery planning.

10. **[[Bylas Fontelicella]]** and **[[Bylas Tryonia]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp. and *Tryonia* n. sp., eight springs near [[Bylas]], [[Graham County]], Arizona): Monitored since 1969. Habitat deterioration observed in two springs from cattle trampling, salt cedar, human activity. Land divided among private, [[San Carlos Apache]] Reservation, and BLM. Co-occurs with [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*); recent *Gambusia affinis* introductions threaten both. Different niche partitioning: *Fontelicella* on hard substrate in moderate current, *Tryonia* in soft sediment/slack water. **Recommendation**: Fence springheads, eliminate *Gambusia*, coordinate fish-mollusk recovery.

11. **[[Canelo Cienega Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Canelo Hills Cienega]], [[Santa Cruz County]], Arizona): Discovered 1973, monitored through 1980. Spring complex <5 acres but high densities. [[The Nature Conservancy]] purchased land and applied for water rights. Co-occurs with *Gila intermedia*.

12. **[[Monkey Spring Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Monkey Spring]] and [[Cottonwood Springs]], [[Santa Cruz County]], Arizona): Stable populations since 1969 in fenced springs. Historically supported endemic pupfish (*Cyprinodon* n. sp., extinct by 1970), *Gila intermedia*, and *Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis* (only topminnow remains). Private land with protective owners. **Recommendation**: Acquire water rights for long-term protection; coordinate with topminnow recovery.

13. **[[Sheehy Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Sheehy Spring]], [[Santa Cruz County]], Arizona): Restricted to 5-meter diameter headspring area with extremely high density (5/cm²). Discovered 1976, monitored 1978-1980 showing slight population increase and reduced cattle activity. Co-occurs with *Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis* and *Gila intermedia*. Several nearby springs toward Mexican border uninventoried.

14. **[[San Bernardino Ranch Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[San Bernardino Ranch]], [[Cochise County]], Arizona): Recently acquired by [[The Nature Conservancy]] and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. Found in springs near headquarters and feeding main pond. Population declines noted in two domestic water springs over 12 years. **Recommendation**: Eliminate or lower main ranch pond to increase 10-15 meter habitat to larger cienega toward Mexican border.

15. **[[Littlefield Springs Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., five springs near [[Littlefield]], [[Mohave County]], Arizona): Three springs modified during Interstate Highway construction 1972-73; one population nearly extirpated but recovered post-construction. All populations "normal" by 1980. Three springs on highway right-of-way, two on public land. Springs suggested for desalinization (would cause extinction). Co-occurs with unique [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) population and *Rana pipiens*. **Recommendation**: Inform state highway maintenance and Arizona DOT; establish natural area at private land spring via BLM land trade.

**Priority Category 3 - Rare and Special Concern**:

16. **[[Torreon Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Torreon Springs]], [[Socorro County]], New Mexico): Recently discovered. Habitat <4 meters of spring run. Relationship to *Amnicola neomexicana* undetermined. Portions of spring impounded. Private land. **Recommendation**: Systematic comparison with *A. neomexicana*; acquire water rights/land.

17. **[[Gila Thermal Snail]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., hot spring adjacent to [[Gila River]], [[Gila National Forest]], [[Grant County]], New Mexico): Single hot spring in wilderness area. Source 44°C, snails inhabit 32-33°C zones. **Recommendation**: Inform Gila National Forest for inventory/management plans.

18. **[[California Anodonta]]** (*Anodonta californiensis*, [[Black River]], [[Apache County]], Arizona): Historically throughout Arizona drainages (Black, Salt, Santa Cruz, Verde, Gila, Colorado rivers); now only in upper Black River from Crosby Crossing to [[White Mountain Apache Reservation]]. Glochidial larvae require native fish hosts (likely *Gila* spp.). Decline parallels native fish elimination. Probably distinct species requiring systematic revision. **Recommendation**: Declare state-threatened immediately; identify fish hosts; federal threatened listing upon species confirmation.

19. **[[Three Forks Hydrobioid]]** (n. gen., n. sp., [[Three Forks]], [[Apache National Forest]], [[Apache County]], Arizona): Undescribed genus. Four spring sources in one acre, habitat restricted to upper 5 meters of runs (densities to 2/cm²). Recent road rerouting and dirt road closure improved conditions. Extreme importance to paleohydrology and zoogeography. **Recommendation**: Monitor carefully; inform U.S. Forest Service.

20. **[[Huachuca Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Peterson Ranch Springs]], [[Cochise County]], Arizona): Single spring on private land, <30 m² total habitat. Water piped to Sunnyside. Stable populations 1973-1980 with possible seasonal fluctuations. **Recommendation**: Purchase water rights; notify Nature Conservancy for Peterson Ranch acquisition; inform Coronado National Forest.

21. **[[Goldroad Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Dripping Spring]], [[Goldroad Well Spring]], [[Black Mountains]], [[Mohave County]], Arizona): Three springs north/south of Oatman Road. Discovery led BLM to modify wildlife water development plans. Near proposed wilderness study areas. **Recommendation**: Inventory additional Black Mountains springs slated for wildlife/cattle developments.

22. **[[Grand Wash Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., minimum four springs including [[Grapevine Spring]] and [[Whiskey Spring]], [[Grand Wash]], [[Mohave County]], Arizona): Discovered 1980. Excellent populations at Grapevine Spring (2/cm²). Heavy cattle and burro use. Wild grapes prevent livestock entry at some springs. Borders proposed BLM wilderness. **Recommendation**: Notify BLM; study livestock impacts; determine ownership; explore water rights/land acquisition.

23. **[[Fossil Springs Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Fossil Creek]], [[Tonto National Forest]]/[[Coconino National Forest]], Arizona): Springs and seepages feeding Fossil Creek. Densities 1-2/cm². Old diversion dam present; [[Arizona Public Service]] closed road to headsprings. Stable since 1969. **Recommendation**: Designate natural area protecting mollusks, native fish, riparian vegetation.

24. **[[Verde Rim Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Nelson Place Spring]], [[Yavapai County]], Arizona): Two spring areas within 100 meters. No development in seven years. Feeds [[Sycamore Creek]] with native trout hybrids. **Recommendation**: Determine ownership; inform Prescott National Forest; consider fencing against cattle.

25. **[[Page Springs Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Lo-Lo-Mai Spring]], [[Bubbling Pond]], [[Page Springs]], [[Yavapai County]], Arizona): Three springs, two at [[Arizona Game and Fish]] hatchery. Highly modified spring sources. Chemical/physical disease treatments (last November 1980) reduced but did not extirpate populations. Multiple spring sources provide redundancy. **Recommendation**: Designate natural areas within hatchery; carefully plan future development or status will become threatened/endangered.

26. **[[Montezuma Well Fontelicella]]** (*Fontelicella* n. sp., [[Montezuma Well]], [[Montezuma Castle National Monument]], [[Yavapai County]], Arizona): Abundant in outflow (2/cm²) inside and outside Well. Outside populations eliminated twice by flooding since 1969, rapidly repopulated within one year. Within National Monument. Exotic *Corbicula fluminea* in outflow picnic area but not yet in Well proper. **Recommendation**: Include in Monument management plan; eliminate exotic clam.

**Additional Findings**:

- **Exotic species threat**: *Melanoides tuberculatus* not yet established in New Mexico/Arizona natural waters but present in aquarium trade. Contributed to *Tryonia* declines in southern Nevada/California. Serves as intermediate host for mammalian parasites. **Recommendation**: Screen aquarium imports; educate dealers.
