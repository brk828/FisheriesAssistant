# Diet of Gerres Fry in the Kosi System and Marine Diet of Rhabdosargus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gerreidae #sparidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt compiles three South African research notes from the 1983 *South African Journal of Zoology* and *Suid-Afrikaanse Tydskrif vir Dierkunde*. The first is a fragment (title and opening missing) on the diet of a mongoose, based on scat analysis. The second, by D.P. Cyrus and S.J.M. Blaber, examines the diet of [[Gerres Fry]] (<40 mm standard length) in the [[Kosi System]], Natal. The third, by C.D. Buxton and H.M. Kok, reports the marine diet of adult [[Rhabdosargus Holubi]] and [[Rhabdosargus Globiceps]] from trawl samples. The Gerres study shows that fry diet shifts from copepods to benthic polychaetes as they grow, and that food use differs among species and seasons. The Rhabdosargus note describes prey composition for both species, which are broadly similar.

## Background & Description

**Mongoose fragment.** Only the closing portion survives. Green grass occurred in 46 scats and dry grass in 19, which the authors read as deliberate intake. Seeds of *A. cyclops* occurred in 4,5% of scats. The authors could not rule out deliberate seed taking given the relatively high occurrence. The species is not named in the surviving text.

**Gerres fry study.** [[Gerres Fry]] were collected quarterly from July 1978 to July 1980 at five sites in the [[Kosi System]]: Estuary, Water Level Recorder (W.L.R.), Makhawulani, Mpungwini and Nhlange. Fry were taken with a 10 m × 1.5 m × 4 mm bar-mesh seine and preserved in 10% formalin. Stomach contents were analysed by three methods: numerical occurrence, the [[Ricker Points Method]] (an approximate volumetric analysis), and percentage energy contribution based on [[Calorific Values]]. Four species were collected: [[Gerres Acinaces]], [[Gerres Filamentosus]], [[Gerres Rappi]] and [[Gerres Oyena]].

**Rhabdosargus study.** Both species are endemic to the shallow marine waters of South Africa. Adults occur less often in estuaries, and juveniles are estuary-associated. Earlier dietary work focused mainly on juveniles in estuaries, so adult marine diet was poorly documented. Samples were taken in 1980 by the R.V. *Thomas B. Davie* during small-mesh trawling between [[Mossel Bay]] and [[Algoa Bay]]. The sample comprised 98 *R. globiceps* and 114 *R. holubi*. Prey were quantified by frequency of occurrence and percentage volume. A rank was computed as frequency of occurrence multiplied by mean percent volume (after Hobson 1974).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Estuarine feeding and ontogeny:** Gerres fry feed on plankton-like prey when small and move to benthic prey as they grow, a shift linked to the development of the adult gut. Comparable shifts were reported for *Eucinostomus gula* in Florida by [[Carr And Adams]] (1973).
- **Habitat-dependent diets:** The authors note that food choice depends on food availability and whether the habitat is marine, estuarine or mangrove. A published comparison (Table 4) covers Gerreidae from Puerto Rico, India, Australia, Florida and South Africa, using mixed analytical methods, so direct comparison is limited.
- **Soft-bottom prey:** The Rhabdosargus prey assemblage matches the soft substrate where fish were sampled. Empty tubes of Chaetopteridae polychaetes were common.

## Key Findings

### Mongoose scat analysis (fragment)
- Green grass occurred in 46 scats and dry grass in 19, indicating deliberate intake.
- *A. cyclops* seed occurred in 4,5% of scats, possibly incidental or deliberate.

### Gerres fry diet (Kosi System)
- **Dominant prey by energy:** Polychaetes made up 86% of the energy value of the diet of *G. filamentosus* at the Estuary. Chironomid larvae and polychaetes dominated the energy diet of *G. acinaces* at W.L.R. In Makhawulani the siphon tips of [[Hiatula Lunulata]] (a bivalve) made up 48% of the energy in *G. rappi*, and polychaetes 36%.
- **Energy totals from Table 3:** The dominant items accounted for 96% (*G. acinaces*), 99% (*G. filamentosus*) and 82% (*G. rappi*) of the energy diet. Table 3 lists *H. lunulata* at 33% and 29% for *G. acinaces* and *G. rappi*, and polychaetes at 44%, 86% and 22% respectively. Chironomid larvae contributed 19% to *G. acinaces*.
- **Size-related shift:** Copepods dominated the diet of small *G. acinaces* (<21 mm), and polychaetes dominated up to 30 mm. Above 31 mm no single item exceeded 20%. *G. filamentosus* switched to polychaetes above 25 mm. *G. rappi* took copepods across all size classes.
- **Seasonal dominance:** Dominant items made up 94% (summer), 92% (autumn), 83% (winter) and 69% (spring) of the *G. acinaces* diet. For *G. filamentosus* the figures were 99% (summer) and 94% (winter). For *G. rappi* they were 75% (autumn), 96% (winter) and 95% (spring). Cirolana fluviatilis and *H. lunulata* were rare by number but contributed substantially through their high energy values.
- **Ontogenetic change:** The authors place the change-over from planktonic to benthic feeding at about 21 mm S.L. Day & Morgans (1956) found bottom feeding in *G. acinaces* at 45–60 mm in Durban Bay. Carr & Adams (1973) found a similar transition for *Eucinostomus gula*.
- **Limited sample:** Only four *G. oyena* were collected (two each from W.L.R. and Makhawulani, in winter). Results for this species are tentative.

### Rhabdosargus marine diet
- **Sample:** 98 *R. globiceps* and 114 *R. holubi* were collected by trawl (Mossel Bay to Algoa Bay, 1980).
- **Prey richness:** 14 prey types were recognized for *R. holubi* and 15 for *R. globiceps*.
- **Dominant prey:** For *R. holubi* the most important items were [[Echinocardium Cordatum]], polychaetes and isopods. For *R. globiceps* they were polychaetes, *E. cordatum*, ophiuroids and isopods.
- **Similarity:** The two species have essentially similar diets, although the relative importance of individual prey differs slightly.

### Overall conclusions
- Fry of the four Gerres species partition food by size, season and species. No more than two species relied extensively on the same food source.
- The Kosi data suggest that food resources are partitioned among co-occurring Gerres fry. Comparisons with other systems are limited by differing analytical methods.
