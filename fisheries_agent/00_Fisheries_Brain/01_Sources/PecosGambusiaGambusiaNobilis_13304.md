# Pecos Gambusia Recovery Plan (Gambusia nobilis)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This [[Recovery Plan]] was prepared by the [[Rio Grande Fishes Recovery Team]] (dated November 16, 1981) for the [[Pecos Gambusia]], a small livebearing fish listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1970. The stated ultimate goal is to secure survival of the species so that it can be downlisted, with the immediate objective of securing the populations in the four major areas of occurrence. Reclassification to Threatened is tied to stable monitored populations, and delisting is tied to the completion of reintroduction efforts. The plan describes the species' taxonomy, distribution, abundance, causes of decline, ecological relationships, and conservation history, then sets out an action plan (Part II), an implementation schedule (Part III) and agency review responses (Part IV).

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *G. nobilis* (order Atheriniformes, family [[Poeciliidae]]) was first described as *Heterandria nobilis* by Baird and Girard in 1853 from specimens collected at Leon and Comanche Springs, Pecos County, Texas. Leon Springs is the type locality, based on a lectotype designated by Hubbs and Springer (1957). Regan (1913) synonymized it with *G. senilis*, but later workers recognized both as valid species.
- **Diagnostic features:** Males have a modified anal fin, the [[Gonopodium]], used in copulation. Gonopodial tip characters separate *G. nobilis* from [[Gambusia Affinis]] and [[Gambusia Geiseri]], the other poeciliids in its native range. Color and morphometric traits (e.g., arched back, robust body, caudal peduncle depth about 2/3 of head length, and black-outlined scale pockets) help with field identification, though morphometrics are environmentally plastic.
- **Differentiation:** Echelle and Echelle (1980) found the [[Balmorhea]] population the most genetically divergent extant population, possibly meriting subspecific recognition. Populations across the four major areas show differentiation that the plan treats as important for conservation.
- **Historical range:** Endemic to the Pecos River basin, occurring from near Fort Stockton, Texas, north to near Fort Sumner, New Mexico. Recent records are restricted to springs and their outflows on the west side of the [[Pecos River]] drainage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Distribution and Abundance

- **New Mexico:** Natural populations occur in gypsum sinkholes and springs at the [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]], including the perennial Lost River formed by Sago and Dragonfly Springs. Bednarz (1975, 1979) estimated 26,550–28,650 adults on the refuge. Introduced populations exist in [[Salt Creek Wilderness Area]] (Ink Pot) and at the Living Desert State Park. [[Blue Spring]] supports about 900,000 individuals (Bednarz estimate, 1975).
- **Texas:** More than 100,000 adults occur in the Balmorhea area, about 88% of them in the head pool of East Sandia Spring. More than one million occur in [[Leon Creek]], with about 100,000 in the [[Diamond-Y Spring]] outflow and marsh.
- **Extirpated sites:** Comanche Springs, Leon Springs, North Spring River and the Pecos River near Fort Sumner, largely after groundwater pumping and flow loss.

### Reasons for Decline

1. **Habitat loss:** Irrigation withdrawals, five major mainstream dams (plus at least three lesser ones, with Brantley Dam planned), and groundwater pumping reduced flows and increased salinity. The fish now depend entirely on spring flow.
2. **Nonnative fishes:** [[Gambusia Geiseri]], introduced into Balmorhea springs in the early 1930s, is linked to declines there. Other exogenous fishes may cause predation, hybridization and disease.

### Habitat Requirements and Tolerances

- Abundant in springheads and spring runs; moderately abundant in marshes and sinkholes with overhead cover. Recorded from the surface to depths of 3 m.
- Elevation range of all populations: 822–1187 m (365 m range). Ink Pot (about 1080 m) is the highest and northernmost site.
- Critical thermal maxima of 38.1–39.3 °C (Gehlbach et al. 1978). Preferred temperatures were 21–25 °C in the morning and 26–30 °C in the afternoon. [[Gambusia Affinis]] preferred about 31 °C (Winkler 1979).
- Conductivity ranges from about 1,200 µmhos/cm at Blue Spring to 32,500 µmhos/cm in Sinkhole 27 (about 1 to 30 ppt TDS). Salinity is not a major limit within this range, but 30 ppt is probably near the upper tolerance.
- Silt deposition after pipeline construction above Blue Spring in 1978–1979 demonstrated sensitivity to watershed disturbance.

### Predation, Diet and Competition

- Predation by [[Lepomis Cyanellus]] and [[Micropterus Salmoides]] may have eliminated an introduced population at Lake St. Francis and contributed to a failed Geyser Spring introduction. Fish persist in shallow marshy areas where centrarchids are absent or scarce.
- The species is a carnivorous surface feeder with a broad, opportunistic diet (filamentous algae, insects, amphipods). Food availability is not thought to be a major limit.
- *G. affinis* outcompetes *G. nobilis* in unstable habitats, while *G. nobilis* dominates spring-fed areas. At Blue Spring, *G. affinis* dominates the lower run and *G. nobilis* the spring source.
- In Balmorhea's freshwater, *G. geiseri* is competitively superior. In the saline waters of Leon Creek, *G. nobilis* appears to outcompete it.

### Hybridization and Reproduction

- *G. nobilis* hybridizes with *G. affinis* (most common) and *G. geiseri*. Hybridization depends on mate discrimination and relative abundance. The plan finds no immediate threat to most populations from *G. affinis* hybridization, but long-term management must account for it. *G. geiseri* discriminates against heterospecific mates.
- Reproduction data come only from Blue Spring: gravid *G. affinis* averaged 56 embryos versus 38 in *G. nobilis* (Bednarz 1979). The lower rate of *G. nobilis* may suit stable spring habitats.

### Conservation History

- **Transplants:** Of 20 transplants made in 1972–1973 at Bitter Lake and Salt Creek, 4 established or supplemented populations (Sinkholes 2 and 10, Ink Pot and Sinkhole 20), while 16 failed. Further transplants were made in July–August 1981, with viability not yet known.
- **Hatchery propagation:** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and a joint New Mexico program at Carlsbad both raised the species successfully, but both were terminated.
- **Protection:** Federal land at Phantom Lake Spring and restricted access at Bitter Lake provide protection. Diamond-Y Spring is protected by a dike against oil spills. [[Leon Creek]] was designated critical habitat for [[Cyprinodon Bovinus]] in 1980, which also protects *G. nobilis* habitat. [[Executive Order 11987]] directs federal agencies to restrict the introduction of exotic species.

## Key Findings

**Action plan goals (Part II)**
- Ultimate goal: secure survival and downlist. Reclassify to Threatened once four major populations are stable (monitoring, Section 1.0). Remove from the federal list after reintroduction efforts (Section 2.0).
- **1.0 Maintain and enhance existing populations:** monitor populations and habitat; protect water sources (including groundwater regulation); protect riparian vegetation; regulate introductions; preclude immigration with physical barriers; study ecology; determine systematic relationships among the four areas; remove exotic fishes.
- **2.0 Reestablish the species within its historic range:** survey and select sites (criteria in Appendix A); remediate habitats; transplant from the nearest pure population to preserve genetic diversity; monitor establishment; reintroduce sympatric natives one species at a time; establish stocks for mosquito control, using *G. nobilis* rather than *G. affinis*.
- **3.0 Public and professional information** and **4.0 Hatchery propagation**, to continue until the species is delisted or the populations are secure.

**Appendix A criteria for restoration sites:** complete elimination of other gambusiines and hybrids; isolation assured; physical, chemical and biological evaluation (high dissolved solids, hardness and salinity may have caused past transplant failures); stability under drought and flood; and assessment of impacts on other endangered species.

**Review and implementation status**
- The agency draft lacked an implementation schedule. The later Part III schedule assigns tasks to the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish and Texas Parks and Wildlife Department, with cost estimates for fiscal years 1983–1985 that refer to U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service spending only.
- The New Mexico Department of Game and Fish (October 19, 1982) withheld endorsement until the schedule was complete. Its technical comments were later incorporated.
- Texas Parks and Wildlife (October 25, 1982) found the plan a realistic approach and incorporated its minor comments.
- The [[Bureau Of Land Management]] asked that restoration on BLM lands near oil and gas operations be closely coordinated with BLM managers.
- The [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] noted that the plan lacked specific, feasible protection methods, that water supply at each area needed assessment, and that it owns 17.56 acres around Phantom Lake Spring. It suggested a management plan developed with the Bureau and the local water district.
- The Bitter Lake refuge manager flagged omitted waterways (Lost River), a possible unsurveyed source spring, and unsurveyed springs at Impoundment Units 3 and 6.
- The Fish and Wildlife Service Office of Endangered Species asked for clarification on siltation effects, the propagation trigger criteria, the protection measures for the Blue Spring area, and specific barrier and study information. Responses noted that Lost River was added and that the "last resort" tasks needed clearer sequencing.
