# Natural Disturbance and Recovery of Desert Streams: A Challenge to Wetland Management

#freshwater-ecology #aquatic-invertebrates #arid-stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This paper, a conference contribution to a Mediterranean wetland restoration volume, argues that aridland stream-riparian ecosystems in the southwestern United States cannot be managed with models derived from mesic (wet-climate) streams. Using a long-term study of [[Sycamore Creek]] in the [[Sonoran Desert]] of central Arizona, the author shows that natural disturbance, chiefly [[Flash Flooding]] and [[Drought]], is the main organizing force, and that the ecosystem moves through a wide range of states in space and time. The central management principle is to maintain variability through normal, multiple disturbance regimes rather than optimize any single state. Where pristine reference systems no longer exist, the paper proposes [[Adaptive Management]] and comparative ecosystem science as the practical route to restoration targets.

## Background & Description

The paper's stated objectives are to (1) describe ecological patterns and their sources of variance in space and time, and (2) discuss the implications of those patterns for management and restoration. The author notes that wetlands in the arid southwest have experienced more than 150 years of intensive use, modification, and degradation. Predominant wetland types in Arizona are artificial reservoirs, playa lakes, rivers and streams (many intermittent), marshes ([[Cienegas]]), and groundwater. Permanent natural lakes do not occur in Arizona. Stream-riparian ecosystems are the most valued and probably the most degraded wetland type in the region.

The study site, Sycamore Creek, is described as near the pristine end of a pristine-to-degraded spectrum. Key site attributes:

- **Catchment:** 505 km², elevation 427 to 2164 m; studies occur at 500 to 700 m in desert scrub dominated by [[Saguaro Cactus]] and Foothills Palo Verde.
- **Riparian vegetation:** willow, cottonwood, ash, mesquite, sycamore, walnut, and the shrub seepwillow (*Baccharis salicifolia*).
- **Geology:** upper 85% of watershed is igneous and metamorphic rock with minor unconsolidated alluvium.
- **Climate:** annual precipitation about 30 cm/yr at 510 m to 51 cm/yr mid-catchment; mean pan evaporation 313 cm/yr, about six times precipitation.
- **Flow regime:** flash floods occur 2 to 15 times per year, most severe in July and August.

The author's general thesis is that aridland riparian systems are highly variable because of many natural and anthropogenic disturbances, and that the effects of these forcing functions vary with spatial and temporal scale.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mesic stream model.** Most stream theory comes from well-watered regions. In these streams, flow is continuous, primary production is low under dense shade, and food webs rely on leaf litter. Shredders, collector-gatherers, and filterers depend on this allochthonous input. Annual P/B ratios are 2 to 5, and most organisms are univoltine. The [[River Continuum Concept]] describes downstream change in these systems, and disturbance has received little attention in them.

**Aridland deviations.** Desert streams differ in several ways:

- Channels are wide (40 to 60 m total width at Sycamore Creek) while wetted width is only 4 to 6 m at summer flow, and the stream is well lit.
- Leaf litter residence time is short (recognizable leaf material appears only briefly in autumn and winter), compared with nearly one year in New England streams.
- Shredders are absent; invertebrates feed mainly on fine particulate organic matter derived from in situ algae.
- Invertebrates are dominated by small mayflies and chironomids with 1 to 2 week life cycles.
- Production is nitrogen-limited, with [[Nitrogen Fixation]] by cyanobacteria during low flow acting as a major nitrogen subsidy.
- The stream is a "green island" in a "barren sea," with energy and nutrients flowing from stream to land rather than the reverse.

**Riparian value.** The author identifies four amenities of desert riparian zones: water supply (reservoirs and groundwater filled by stream-channel runoff), an oasis for wildlife (nearly 60% of terrestrial wildlife species depend on the riparian zone for water, food, or both), support for [[Cattle Grazing]], and recreation ranging from bird watching to destructive firewood harvest and off-road vehicle use.

**Regional threats.** Virtually all large rivers in Arizona are impounded or diverted. Reservoirs such as [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Mead]] block migration routes of native river fishes, alter downstream flows, and provide habitat for introduced sport fishes. Smaller streams, including Sycamore Creek, remain relatively free-flowing but have been altered by recreation, grazing, highway construction, fire, firewood harvest, and exotic species.

## Key Findings

**1. Disturbance organizes the system.** Flash floods and extensive drying are the most influential disturbances, though siltation, cattle grazing, algal pathogens, water diversion, exotic species, and recreation also have strong effects. The author argues that disturbance has a naturally central role and that human use usually decreases variability, so restoring variability should be a primary management goal.

**2. Multiple states with trade-offs.** Stream discharge ranges from zero to 600 m³/s; temperature from 5 to 33 °C annually, with diel excursions up to 15 °C in summer; and turbidity from undetectable to unreadable (flash floods carry a slurry that is 5% solid). Macroinvertebrates reach 500,000 individuals per m² in some states. Specific conductance ranges from 290 µS/cm in floods to nearly 700 µS/cm at low flow, and nitrate from 3 mg/l to undetectable. Each state provides different services:

- Nutrient retention is highest during the aggradation (recovery) stage after disturbance.
- Water delivery peaks at flood stage.
- Insect emergence per linear meter peaks before drying shrinks the system area.
- Forage is best after flood-free periods but declines under extended drought.

Services cannot be optimized simultaneously, so management for multiple use involves compromise.

**3. Flash-flood recovery is rapid but state-dependent.** Flash floods devastate biota, but succession recovers within 2 to 3 months. Effects vary with disturbance magnitude, season, discharge, frequency, and nitrogen availability. Silting floods are different: in a 3-year study, 4 of 34 floods were of the silting type, and after one silting flood algal recovery took 60 days and macrophyte recovery 48 days, compared with immediate recovery after larger flash floods.

**4. Community structure responds to drought more than to flooding.** Analysis of eight macroinvertebrate successional sequences suggested high resistance and that seasonality controlled structure more than disturbance. Abrupt shifts in invertebrate community structure occurred mid-way through a 3-year study, coinciding with unusual drought, suggesting interannual drought influences long-term patterns more than frequent flooding does.

**5. Riparian trees respond to flood and drought on longer cycles.** Shrubs and seedlings are killed by floods, while larger trees are relatively resistant. Cottonwood (*Populus angustifolia*) seedling establishment conditions occur every 4.3 years on average, but large-tree recruitment occurs only every 11 years on the Animas River, Colorado. Riparian trees show little drought tolerance, and saplings die in drought events with 5 to 10 year recurrence intervals. The riparian zone is expected to lag far behind in-stream recovery.

**6. Pathogens cause localized disturbance.** Spring diatom stands are subject to a presumed bacterial pathogen that produces expanding circular mortality patches that coalesce. The effect was observed only in 3 of 15 years of study, only in spring, and only when diatoms dominated.

**7. Drought reduces ecosystem-scale production.** Permanently wetted sites serve as refuges. About 6 sites in 65 km of mainstem channel remained wet throughout 15 years of study, though some shrink to as little as 100 m of surface water in extreme years. Stanley (1993) reported a 77% change in stream surface area over 21 months in a 12 km reach. Failing to account for ecosystem contraction can produce 7- to 300-fold errors in production and standing-stock estimates.

**8. The hyporheic zone is a major component.** A distinct hyporheos (at least 56 species, none seen in over 10 years of surface collections, several possibly new to science) occurs within 30 cm of the stream surface and in shallow wells to 1.5 m. [[Sycamore Creek]] has a P/R ratio above 1 (autotrophic) from surface metabolism alone, and P/R approaches unity when the [[Hyporheic Zone]] is included. Management decisions and mass-balance estimates that ignore hyporheic processes are therefore likely to be wrong.

**9. Longitudinal patterns are driven by nitrogen, not litter.** Permanent flow emerges at sources every 5 to 10 km where bedrock forces groundwater to the surface. Nitrate-rich sources support algal growth, and uptake depletes nitrate downstream over tens to hundreds of meters. Upwelling zones, tens of meters apart, and small sand bars (1 to 2 m long) add further nitrogen-driven heterogeneity.

**10. Lateral connections matter.** Only 10 to 20% of the channel supports surface flow in a typical cross section. The [[Parafluvial Zone]] (sand bars) is saturated and moves water downstream at 1 to 2 m/hr. About 1% of flow passes through lateral sediments, but residence time is long, nitrate increases, oxygen declines, and anaerobic processes are favored.

**11. Cattle grazing is a chronic but moderate disturbance here.** Riparian areas make up 1 to 2% of land in typical western grazing allotments but produce 20% of available forage and nearly 80% of the forage consumed by cattle (Gillis 1991). At Sycamore Creek, the author judges cattle impact moderate: channel effects recover within days, and grazing of sand-bar grasses may stabilize sediments. Seedling recruitment is depressed. Cattle effects are synergistic with hydrology and are overwhelmed by large floods. Elsewhere, overgrazing combined with climate change triggered [[Arroyo Cutting]] in the late 1800s, draining many [[Cienegas]].

**12. Siltation and drought are key modifiers.** Silting floods prolong disruption through substrate change and light extinction. Sequence matters: silting floods followed closely by typical floods are erased. Drought and flash floods are largely independent, so organisms face both. The biota shows low resistance and high resilience, selected for rapid recolonization and growth.

**13. Control of ecosystem state shifts with succession.** Immediately after floods, abiotic factors and colonization dominate. Later, biotic interactions and resource availability (nitrogen depletion shifts algae from greens and diatoms to blue-green algae) gain importance. Late in succession, morphometric factors and fragmentation dominate, and founder effects shape isolated pools. No single control (biotic, abiotic, or disturbance) explains the system.

**14. Management framework.** The author proposes:

- Define management objectives in terms of ecosystem services.
- Map each service to ecosystem state, using univariate graphs for single services and multivariate state-space for complex systems.
- Manage forcing variables that move the system out of the desired state-space.
- Recognize that some desired states require occasional extreme excursions (e.g., floods for fish production in floodplain pools, fire for grassland productivity, scouring for nutrient retention).
- Use [[Adaptive Management]] when knowledge is incomplete: set objectives, run monitoring and research in parallel, and revise plans from interim results. This approach has been applied on the Colorado River in the Grand Canyon.

**15. Comparative science is needed where reference systems are gone.** Natural lakes do not occur in Arizona, and large free-flowing rivers are rare in many regions. Desert streams similar to Sycamore Creek exist in Spain and Australia, yet there is virtually no species overlap among them. The author concludes that comparative ecosystem science may be the only reasonable route to restoration targets where pristine models do not exist, although a definitive answer cannot yet be given.
