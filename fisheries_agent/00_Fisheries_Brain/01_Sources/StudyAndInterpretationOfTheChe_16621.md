# Study and Interpretation of the Chemical Characteristics of Natural Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #water-chemistry

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Water-Supply Paper (No. 1473, by John D. Hem, 1959) is a reference treatise on the chemistry of natural waters. It explains how dissolved constituents arise from rock weathering and decomposition, how water samples should be collected and analyzed, how results are expressed and interpreted, and how water chemistry relates to geology, hydrology, and water use. The document is aquatic-chemistry and hydrology focused; it does not address fish or other aquatic organisms as study subjects, so species fields are recorded as N/A.

## Background & Description

The report was prepared under the supervision of W. F. White (Chemical Quality Section) and S. K. Love (Quality of Water Branch). It was originally conceived as a joint effort with R. M. Jeffords, formerly of the Ground Water Branch. The text covers:

- **Water properties:** Water is H₂O; hydrogen bonding gives it unusually high boiling and freezing points, heat of vaporization, dielectric constant, and surface tension (75.6 dynes/cm at 0 °C; 71.8 dynes/cm at 25 °C).
- **Crustal composition:** Based on [[Rankama and Sahama Composition Data]] (Table 1), which compares average element concentrations in igneous rocks and three sedimentary classes: resistates, hydrolyzates, and precipitates. Sea-water composition is summarized in Table 2.
- **Sampling and analysis:** Guidance on representative sampling of surface streams and [[Ground-Water Sampling]], field testing, and [[Electric Logs]] as indicators of water quality.
- **Reporting units:** Parts per million (ppm), equivalents per million (epm), grains per gallon, and related conversions (Tables 3–5), with [[Ionic Dissociation Theory]] underpinning ionic expression.
- **Constituent-by-constituent chemistry:** Specific conductance, pH, residue on evaporation, silica, iron, manganese, aluminum, calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium, alkalinity, acidity, sulfate, chloride, fluoride, nitrate, phosphate, boron, trace metals, dissolved gases, and the sodium-adsorption ratio.
- **Interpretation techniques:** Ratios, averages, [[Palmer Geochemical Classification]], scatter and bar diagrams, [[Piper Trilinear Diagram]], hydrographs, and quality-of-water maps.
- **Geologic and use relationships:** Water quality by rock type, modifications of water chemistry, and quality criteria for domestic, agricultural, and industrial use.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report frames water chemistry as an environmental record of the hydrologic cycle:

- **Solvent denudation:** Computations from 1950 Survey records show soluble loads of 70 to 86 tons per square mile per year for the James River (Virginia), Iowa River, Colorado River (above Grand Canyon), and Similkameen River (Washington). The Wind River basin (Wyoming) is reported at 98 tons per square mile per year. A rate of 80 tons per square mile per year is equivalent to lowering the land surface by about 1 foot every 20,000 years.
- **Carbon dioxide control:** Dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid, greatly increasing the solvent power of water; the calcium carbonate–bicarbonate system buffers most natural waters to a pH of roughly 6 to 8.
- **Salinity sources:** Salt beds and saline lakes in closed basins result from accumulated solutes; connate and juvenile waters, sea-salt aerosols, and evaporite dissolution all contribute chloride and sodium.
- **Life processes:** Plants and microorganisms influence iron, manganese, and potassium cycling, and aquatic plants or algae can remove CO₂ and promote carbonate precipitation.
- **Human influence:** Industrial wastes, acid mine drainage, sewage, and irrigation return flows alter water chemistry (for example, acidic iron-rich drainage from coal mines in the Pennsylvania analysis).

## Key Findings

**Methodological conclusions**

- More than 50 constituents or properties can be determined, but routine analyses include only those judged essential by the requesting agency.
- Samples must be representative: flowing streams require cross-sectional or depth-integrated sampling and sampling interval design; water bodies are not assumed homogeneous.
- For streams, the Survey's standard practice was daily sampling, with daily samples typically composited in groups of about 10. Extreme-conductance samples are not composited together.
- Single samples or infrequent sampling can miss significant changes; the Rio Grande at San Acacia, New Mexico, showed a nearly 38-fold concentration increase within a few hours during summer flash floods.
- Ground-water composition varies vertically and laterally; a well yields only a weighted average of its screened interval. Changes are slow, so monthly, seasonal, or annual sampling is usually adequate.
- Field conductance tests are useful for planning sampling programs but are less accurate than laboratory work; field results should be treated as supplementary.

**Analytical and reporting conclusions**

- Results are reported in ppm (mg/L, assuming unit density below about 7,000 ppm dissolved solids) and in epm (cation and anion totals should balance).
- Hardness is conventionally expressed as ppm CaCO₃; alkalinity is converted to bicarbonate by dividing by 50 for epm.
- Specific conductance (micromhos at 25 °C) approximates dissolved solids; the factor relating them usually falls between 0.55 and 0.75 (range 0.5 to 1.0).
- Conductance rises about 2 percent per degree Celsius, so all values are referred to 25 °C.
- Residue-on-evaporation results for waters of 100 to 1,000 ppm agree on duplicates to within 5 to 10 ppm.
- Sodium computed by difference can be wrong by about 25 percent in waters of a few hundred ppm; determined sodium is accurate to about 3 to 5 percent.

**Chemical range findings**

- Surface-water conductance ranges from about 50 micromhos in areas of abundant precipitation to about 50,000 micromhos for ocean water; ground water may approach or exceed sea-water conductance locally.
- Natural-water pH is usually between about 5.5 and 8 for ground water and between 7 and 8 for most surface waters.
- Dissolved silica is typically 1 to 80 ppm, with concentrations up to 100 ppm fairly common; sea water contains less than 1 ppm.
- Iron is usually below 0.50 ppm in aerated water near neutral pH, often below 0.10 ppm; acid waters may exceed 100 ppm.
- Manganese in most natural waters is below 0.20 ppm; amounts above 0.3 ppm are objectionable in public supplies.
- Aluminum is generally below about 1 ppm except in acid waters, where it can exceed 100 ppm.
- Sea water contains more than 1,000 ppm of magnesium and about 10,000 ppm of sodium; saturated salt brine can contain about 100,000 ppm of sodium.
- Potassium seldom exceeds 15 ppm in ordinary waters, though brines may reach several thousand ppm.
- Fluoride is normally very low but may reach 30 ppm or more; phosphate is rarely above 1 ppm.
- Dissolved solids range from as little as 25 to 30 ppm in high-precipitation areas to about 300,000 ppm in brines.

**Interpretive conclusions**

- Calcium-to-magnesium ratios of about 5:1 to 1:1 are typical; high values suggest limestone or gypsum sources, while low values suggest magnesium silicate weathering, dolomite attack, or sea-water contamination.
- Water from igneous terranes tends to be high in dissolved silica; water from evaporite sediments is high in dissolved salts; water from precipitate sediments tends to be high in calcium and magnesium bicarbonate.
- Sodium is generally the dominant cation in saline waters, while calcium dominates in fresh limestone and gypsum waters.
- Potassium is less abundant in water than sodium because it is reincorporated into clay minerals during weathering.
- Specific-conductance and dissolved-solids relationships are sample-specific and cannot be exactly predicted for all waters.

**Practical implications**

- Quality standards for domestic, agricultural, and industrial water use have been published by agencies including the U.S. Public Health Service and the Department of Agriculture; waters that fail these standards require users to anticipate and manage difficulties.
- Sampling and analysis design should be planned jointly so that key determinations guide which samples merit full analysis, lowering costs without sacrificing data.
