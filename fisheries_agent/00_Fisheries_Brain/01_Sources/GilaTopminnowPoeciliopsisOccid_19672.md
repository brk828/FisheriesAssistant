# Gila Topminnow Revised Recovery Plan (1998)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service revised recovery plan (September 1998, prepared by David A. Weedman for Region 2) covers the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), the U.S. subspecies of the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] complex. Both subspecies were listed as endangered in 1967 with no critical habitat designated. The plan replaces the 1984 recovery plan for the U.S. portion of the range; the [[Yaqui Topminnow]] is covered by a separate 1995 Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan.

The species persists at 13 natural localities in Arizona, mainly in the Santa Cruz River system and the middle Gila River. [[Habitat Destruction]] and introduction of the nonnative [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) are the main causes of decline. Delisting is not considered feasible in the foreseeable future. Downlisting to threatened is judged possible, with recovery estimated at 20 years; the recovery category is 9C.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described by Baird and Girard (1853) as *Heterandria occidentalis* from a specimen collected in 1851 near Tucson; redescribed by Hubbs and Miller (1941).
- **Morphology:** Tan to olive body with a white belly. Breeding males turn black with bright yellow fins and a gonopodium that reaches beyond the snout. Males seldom exceed 25 mm standard length (SL); females average 30 to 45 mm SL and can reach 50 mm.
- **Identification:** Gila topminnows are told apart from mosquitofish by the lack of a dark suborbital bar and dorsal/caudal spots, weaker spatulate teeth, and a longer gonopodium.
- **Reproduction:** Viviparous, with internal fertilization, sperm storage for several months, and superfetation (occasionally three embryonic stages). Brood size ranges from 1 to 31. Breeding occurs mainly January through August.
- **Diet:** Opportunistic omnivore with detritus, vegetation, amphipods, ostracods, and insect larvae as primary items.
- **Hybrid:** An all-female hybrid, *P. monacha-occidentalis*, reproduces by [[Hybridogenesis]] and depends on sperm from the parental species; it is absent from the Gila River drainage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use:** The species tolerates a broad range of conditions. Recorded temperatures run from near freezing to 37°C, with a brief maximum tolerance of 43°C. pH ranged from 6.6 to 8.9, dissolved oxygen from 2.2 to 11 mg/l, and salinity from fresh to seawater. Topminnows can survive near-total drying by burrowing into mud for 1 to 2 days. They favor shallow, warm, quiet water with dense algae and debris, and they occupy pools, glides, and backwaters more than marshes or fast flow.

**Historic distribution:** Gila topminnows were widespread in the Gila River drainage below about 1500 m, from the San Francisco River (Frisco Hot Springs, New Mexico) to the Gila near Yuma, Arizona. The species was once one of the most common fishes of the southern Colorado River basin (Hubbs and Miller 1941).

**Threats:** The late 1800s and early 1900s brought overgrazing, woodcutting, and drought (1891 to 1893). Arroyo cutting followed, and groundwater pumping and water diversions further dewatered streams. Ongoing risks include livestock grazing, mining, urban growth, channelization, and pollution. Other nonnative species include the [[Largemouth Bass]], green sunfish, the parasitic copepod *Lernaea cyprinacea*, the Asian clam *Corbicula fluminea*, and the predatory [[American Bullfrog]].

**Mosquitofish interaction:** Mosquitofish were first collected in Arizona in 1926. They are more carnivorous, with strong conical teeth, and they prey on young topminnows and fin-nip adults. Flooding removes a larger share of mosquitofish than topminnows. Coexistence rarely exceeded three years in habitats without flooding, while topminnows persisted more than 30 years with mosquitofish in flood-prone Santa Cruz River habitats.

**Genetics:** Mitochondrial DNA studies found no detectable diversity within Gila basin populations (Quattro et al. 1996). Later work by [[Parker et al.]] supported [[Monkey Spring]] as a separate [[Evolutionarily Significant Unit]]. Until more data are available, each natural population is to be kept separate.

## Key Findings

**Status and distribution**
- 13 natural localities currently support Gila topminnows in the U.S., 11 in the Santa Cruz River system and 2 along the Gila River on the San Carlos Apache Reservation (Bylas Springs and Middle Spring).
- Natural populations are grouped into nine metapopulations at 13 locations. Two recently lost populations, Sheehy Spring and North Fork of Ash Creek, should be searched for.
- 18 wild reestablished populations remained in 1997, 17 within historic range. Seven were considered secure.
- Twelve captive populations persisted in 1997 (e.g., [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], [[Boyce-Thompson Arboretum]], [[Roper Lake State Park]]).

**Reintroduction history**
- Stocking began in 1936 for mosquito control. Since the 1981 MOU among the Service, U.S. Forest Service, and AGFD, more than 350 documented stockings have been made, the most of any short-lived North American desert fish.
- Reintroductions total about 206 at 178 wild locations, plus about 141 captive sites (figures vary between the plan's sections).
- Reestablishment success is low (about 8%). Reasons for failure include mosquitofish, desiccation, flooding, low dissolved oxygen, and cattle damage.

**Recovery criteria and objectives**
- Downlisting requires: (1) protection of natural populations and captive refugia; (2) replication of each natural metapopulation in Level 2 and Level 3 sites; (3) funded monitoring and genetic protocols.
- Level 2 sites must persist at least 10 years. The plan calls for 19 Level 2 populations (two replicates per metapopulation, with extra replicates for Lower Sonoita Creek) and 27 Level 3 populations (three per metapopulation).
- A viable population is defined as at least 500 overwintering adults with all age classes represented and reliable annual recruitment.

**Priority actions**
- Protect natural and long-lived reestablished populations and designate critical habitat.
- Prohibit introduction of nonnative aquatic species, especially mosquitofish. The Arizona Game and Fish Commission already bans mosquitofish as baitfish in the Verde River above Horseshoe Dam and the Salt River above Roosevelt Diversion Dam.
- Build fish barriers and conduct periodic surveys for nonnative invaders.
- Monitor populations at least annually (preferably twice yearly) with standardized protocols; AGFD is the data repository.
- Research minimum temperature and oxygen needs, flood resistance, niche partitioning with [[Desert Pupfish]], and disease.
- Educate the public and resource managers, using school ponds as possible refugia.

**Implementation**
- Priorities are ranked 1 (prevent extinction) to 3 (other necessary actions). Responsible agencies include AGFD, U.S. Forest Service, BLM, San Carlos Apache Tribe, The Nature Conservancy, Arizona State Parks, and New Mexico Department of Game and Fish.
- Cost estimates are given in the implementation tables but are not budgeted expenditures.
