# Long-Term Effective Population Size of Three Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study applies a maximum likelihood estimator of long-term effective population size (N_e), based on [[Coalescent Theory]], to mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) sequences from three endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]]: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Unlike common recent-timescale methods, the approach captures the genetic legacy of historical demography. The authors find three distinct patterns: humpback chub retains historical equilibrium variation despite recent declines; razorback sucker shows signs of a large, expanding historical population with rare alleles surviving; and bonytail chub shows a population bottleneck that may have erased much historical variation. The authors conclude the results are consistent with known natural history and useful for endangered species management.

## Background & Description

- **Effective population size (N_e):** the size of an idealized population experiencing the same genetic drift as the observed population. N_e is typically smaller than census size (N) because of variance in family size and temporal fluctuations. Reduced N_e increases inbreeding and reduces adaptive potential.
- **Estimation approaches:** Temporal allele-frequency methods (e.g., [[Waples Temporal Method]]) measure current N_e. Coalescent-based methods, implemented in the program [[FLUCTUATE]] (Kuhner et al., 1998), estimate long-term female N_e (N_ef) and an exponential growth rate from DNA sequences.
- **Study species:** All three are endemic to the Colorado River basin and federally listed as endangered. Historically, bonytail chub and razorback sucker were widespread and abundant in large streams; humpback chub was restricted to canyon-bound reaches in and upstream of the Grand Canyon. Declines were driven by dam and reservoir construction, water diversion, desiccation, and non-native predatory and competitive fishes.
- **Captive propagation:** Propagation of bonytail and razorback sucker began in the early 1980s; hundreds of thousands to millions of young have been distributed, yet recruitment failure persists across known wild populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Humpback chub:** Only one of the three species with apparently self-sustaining populations. The largest known population is near the [[Little Colorado River]] in Grand Canyon, with perhaps as many as 10,000 adults. Stable populations of unknown size occur in Westwater Canyon (Utah) and Black Rocks (Colorado).
- **Bonytail chub:** Extirpated from most of its range and extremely rare; regularly found only in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona–Nevada), where captures are too few for census estimates.
- **Razorback sucker:** Historically the most abundant of the three. The Lake Mohave population declined from perhaps hundreds of thousands to fewer than 10,000 fish in about 50 years. A smaller middle Green River population (Utah) shows a similar trend.
- **Life history:** Average lifespan is 20–50 years. Predation on early life-history stages is the primary obstacle to recovery. Because of longevity and recent declines, losses of historical genetic variation may have been small to date.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and genetic methods**
- Tissue samples: humpback chub (n = 18, Little Colorado–Colorado confluence, Grand Canyon); bonytail chub (n = 16, Lake Mohave); razorback sucker (n = 49, Lake Mohave).
- Humpback and bonytail chub were assayed for two ND2 fragments using single-stranded conformation polymorphism (SSCP) and direct sequencing. Razorback sucker was assayed using a cytochrome b fragment.
- Statistics: Tajima's D, nucleotide diversity, and coalescent simulations (10^4 replicates, as the text reports "10ffi"; treated as 10,000 in the original design).

**Humpback chub (ND2)**
- Five composite haplotypes; six mutations, one singleton; all transitions, none amino acid altering.
- Average of 1.54 pairwise nucleotide substitutions; Tajima's D = −0.3935 (P = 0.358), non-significant, consistent with constant-size equilibrium.
- Long-term N_ef ≈ 150,000 (assuming a mammalian-like mutation rate); constant-size estimate ≈ 97,000. The difference is not significant. Growth rate does not differ significantly from zero.
- Retained historical variation is attributed to past connected subpopulations and the relatively isolated Grand Canyon population.

**Bonytail chub (ND2)**
- Three haplotypes, nearly equal in frequency (31.3%, 25.0%, 43.7%); six transitional mutations, none singletons, one amino acid substitution.
- Average 2.82 pairwise substitutions, almost double that of humpback chub.
- Tajima's D = 1.909 (P = 0.028), significantly positive, suggesting a population bottleneck or balancing selection.
- Growth-model N_ef ≈ 62,000 versus constant-size estimate ≈ 89,500; the growth rate is significantly negative, and the two estimates differ significantly.

**Razorback sucker (cytochrome b)**
- Ten haplotypes; 13 mutations, four singletons, two amino acid substitutions. The common haplotype 'E' occurs in 59.2% of individuals.
- Average 1.50 pairwise substitutions, similar to humpback chub.
- Tajima's D = −1.46 (P = 0.058), marginally non-significant but trending toward an excess of rare mutations, suggesting population expansion or directional selection.
- Growth-model N_ef ≈ 940,000 versus constant-size estimate ≈ 669,000; the growth rate is significantly positive, indicating a significant excess of rare mutations.

**Interpretation and management implications**
- The three species illustrate three outcomes: retained equilibrium variation (humpback chub), signs of historical expansion with surviving rare alleles (razorback sucker), and bottleneck signatures (bonytail chub).
- Razorback sucker results reconcile genetic and census data: the species was once a large, basin-wide population, and the authors suggest a northward post-Pleistocene expansion (Dowling et al., 1996a). The authors note their N_e estimate may be conservative if variation has already been lost in Lake Mohave.
- Bonytail chub results may reflect the hatchery history: a few wild adults founded a broodstock, and Hedrick et al. (2000) estimated the effective founding size at 3.5–8.5 individuals. The authors cannot unambiguously separate a founder effect from recent declines or long-term contraction.
- Coalescent estimates capture historical N_e over a long timescale and do not estimate current N_e. Combining them with temporal methods gives a more complete view of population dynamics.
- Coalescent estimators are more efficient and have lower variance than summary-statistic estimators, which matters for small endangered-species samples. The probability that adding one more sample would change the final coalescence time was estimated at 0.006 (humpback chub), 0.007 (bonytail chub) and 0.001 (razorback sucker), per the text's reported values.
- Adding more polymorphic sites improves reliability of the genealogical estimate.
