# Self-Purification of Small Freshwater Streams: Phosphate, Nitrate, and Ammonia Removal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #freshwater-ecology #stream-ecology #algae #Zygnemataceae #eutrophication

## Executive Summary

This study by R.H.S. McColl (1974) investigates the capacity of small freshwater streams to remove dissolved nutrients (phosphate, nitrate, ammonia) via biological and physical processes on the stream bed — a phenomenon termed **self-purification**. Two contrasting stream systems were compared: a nutrient-poor stream (exotic forest catchment, Taita) and a nutrient-rich stream receiving sewage effluent (Wharemauku Stream, Paraparaumu). Results show that nutrient removal efficiency is strongly dependent on the **baseline nutrient loading** a stream is accustomed to. In the oligotrophic stream, phosphate and ammonia were rapidly and efficiently absorbed by a mat of [[Filamentous Algae]] and trapped sediment, while nitrate removal was inconsistent. In the eutrophic, sewage-impacted stream, no significant nutrient removal was detected over 100 m, despite the presence of abundant algae and macrophytes. These findings have direct implications for understanding nutrient loading to lakes and the role of stream-bed processes in mitigating [[Eutrophication]].

## Background & Description

- **Study system**: Two experimental freshwater stream sites in New Zealand:
  1. A flat-bottomed concrete channel (~1.3 m wide, 7 m long) below a V-notch weir in the **exotic forest catchment**, Taita Experimental Basin (3.97 ha, conifer plantation with gorse *Ulex europaeus* and native scrub). Base flow: ~0.1–0.3 L/s.
  2. The **Wharemauku Stream**, Paraparaumu, below the Coastlands extended-aeration sewage treatment plant outfall — a gravelly-bottomed stream with abundant [[Spirogyra]] and a vigorous macrophyte community dominated by watercress (*Rorippa nasturtium-aquaticum*).

- **Primary biological agents of nutrient uptake**:
  - Filamentous algae mats: *Zygnema*, *Spirogyra*, *Oedogonium* — forming loose mats that trap sediment and channel stream flow.
  - Associated invertebrate fauna: the snail *Potamopyrgus* and a variety of other invertebrates inhabiting the algal mat (Taita site).
  - Macrophyte beds of watercress at the Wharemauku site providing additional surface area for nutrient uptake.

- **Methodology**: Controlled nutrient spike additions (phosphate, nitrate, ammonia, plus sodium as an inert dilution tracer) were introduced upstream and sampled at timed intervals downstream. Sodium was validated as a conservative tracer (per Crisp 1970) because electrical conductivity was found to be confounded by photosynthetic activity. Analytical methods followed Strickland & Parsons (1968) for phosphate/nitrate and Harwood & Kühn (1970) for ammonia; sodium measured via flame emission spectrophotometry.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Nutrient loading history determines uptake capacity**: The central ecological finding is that a stream's nutrient removal capacity is conditioned by its accustomed nutrient regime — nutrient-poor streams exhibit strong "buffering" uptake, while nutrient-saturated streams show little additional removal capacity.
- **Role of the algal mat as a biological filter**: In the Taita stream, the algal mat (total dry weight 80.2 g over a 3 m² area) plus trapped sediment (15–50% of mat dry weight) acted as an effective nutrient sink, disproportionate to the small fraction of total biomass P and N represented by the experimental nutrient additions (~2% of biomass P, ~0.8% of biomass N).
- **Temperature dependence**: Nutrient uptake efficiency varied with water temperature (range 4.5–15.0°C), with phosphorus uptake highest at warmest temperatures tested (13–15°C: 97% removal) but still substantial at the coldest (4.5–5.0°C: 62% removal).
- **Sewage-impacted systems and cattle disturbance**: At the Wharemauku Stream, physical disturbance by cattle trampling and defecation in the stream bed introduced confounding nutrient inputs, complicating detection of biological uptake signals.
- **Eutrophication management implications**: The study underscores that nutrient budget studies of streams feeding lakes must account for in-stream removal processes — nutrient concentrations measured far upstream may not reflect what actually reaches a lake, and this effect is contingent on where a stream sits along its "nutrient loading trajectory" (nutrient-starved vs. nutrient-saturated).
- **Photosynthesis-conductivity confound**: Electrical conductivity, commonly used as a hydrological dilution tracer, was found unreliable in vegetated streams because photosynthetic activity of algae/macrophytes alters ionic concentrations, whereas sodium (largely biologically inert) was a more robust tracer.

## Key Findings

- **Phosphate (PO₄–P) removal** (oligotrophic stream): Up to **97% removal** at 13–15°C; **62% removal** at 4.5–5.0°C. Uptake was often extremely rapid — near-complete within 2 minutes of the nutrient pulse passing over the algal mat.
- **Ammonium (NH₄–N) removal**: Nearly total removal at intermediate temperatures (7.0–7.5°C); **82% removal** at the lowest temperatures (4.5–5.0°C). In one trial, ammonium was apparently fully absorbed within the first minute.
- **Nitrate (NO₃–N) removal**: Markedly less efficient — only **20% removal** at 7.0–7.5°C, and apparent **net release** (rather than uptake) at the lowest temperatures. Kinetic analysis suggested a ~6-minute lag phase before nitrate uptake began, possibly reflecting time needed to activate algal nitrate-uptake systems.
- **First-order kinetics**: Uptake of PO₄, NH₄, and (after lag) NO₃ followed first-order reaction kinetics; log-transformed ratios of observed:expected concentration decreased linearly with time, allowing derivation of reaction rate constants.
- **Algae removal control experiment**: When the algal mat and sediment were physically scraped from the stream bed, nutrient uptake was drastically reduced — nutrient pulses moved faster downstream and returned to baseline concentrations more quickly, confirming the algal mat/sediment complex (not the water column or bare substrate) as the primary site of nutrient removal.
- **Sewage outfall stream (eutrophic conditions)**: Across two sampling series (31 January and 19 February 1973) at 20–25°C, **no statistically significant removal of PO₄–P or NO₃–N** was detected over 100 m downstream of the outfall when sodium was used as the dilution reference. Conductivity-based dilution estimates suggested apparent uptake, but this was attributed to confounding by photosynthetic effects on conductivity rather than true nutrient removal.
- **Algal tissue phosphorus content**: Algal material collected below the sewage outfall had modestly higher phosphorus content (0.27–0.35 g P/100g) compared with algae collected above the outfall (0.23–0.24 g P/100g), suggesting some P uptake into biomass despite no detectable change in water-column concentrations. No significant difference was found in Kjeldahl nitrogen content of algae above vs. below the outfall.
- **Cattle disturbance effect**: Trampling and defecation by cattle in the lower 30 m of the Wharemauku Stream appeared to increase phosphorus and nitrogen inputs beyond ~42–45 m downstream of the outfall, confounding nutrient uptake measurements in the first sampling series.
- **Methodological conclusion**: Sodium was validated as a superior conservative tracer over electrical conductivity for measuring stream dilution in nutrient studies, because conductivity is sensitive to photosynthetic ion uptake/release by [[Filamentous Algae]] and macrophyte beds.
- **Broader significance**: The stream-bed's capacity to buffer nutrient inputs depends on prior nutrient loading history, plant community vigor/extent, substrate type, and disturbance level. Once this buffering capacity is saturated, nutrient "breakthrough" and downstream enrichment occur — directly relevant to modeling nutrient delivery to lakes and managing [[Eutrophication]] risk.
