# Morphological Variation in Gila (Pisces: Cyprinidae) and Geologic History: Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #systematics

## Executive Summary

This Master's thesis by Bruce Douglas DeMarais (Arizona State University, May 1986) investigates morphological and meristic variation among populations of the cyprinid genus [[Gila]] in the lower [[Colorado River Basin]], with emphasis on [[Gila intermedia]], [[Gila robusta robusta]], and problematic intermediate forms historically referred to as [[Gila robusta grahami]]. The author employs multivariate morphometric and meristic analyses (Principal Components Analysis, Canonical Discriminant Analysis) on 140 specimen series, plus osteological comparisons, to assess taxonomic relationships. DeMarais argues for specific status of [[Gila intermedia]] based on phenotypic distinctiveness, mosaic sympatric distribution with [[Gila robusta]], and temporal stability of populations. Intermediate "grahami" populations are interpreted as products of past hybridization and introgression between [[Gila intermedia]] and [[Gila robusta robusta]], facilitated by Miocene-Pliocene drainage reorganization along the [[Colorado Plateau]] margin. The study integrates ichthyology with regional Tertiary geology, proposing that basin-and-range tectonism, formation of endorheic basins (e.g., [[Verde Formation]], [[Bidahochi Formation]], [[Safford-San Carlos Basin]]), and subsequent drainage integration created opportunities for secondary contact and gene flow. Pure [[Gila intermedia]] stocks persist in the [[Santa Cruz River]] and upper [[San Pedro River]] drainages, areas of long geologic isolation. The work refutes ecophenotypic and ecotypic explanations for intermediacy, emphasizing instead historical biogeography and hybridization.

## Background & Description

### Taxonomic Context and Controversy

The genus [[Gila]] (subgenus *Gila*) exhibits pronounced phenotypic diversity across the [[Colorado River Basin]], ranging from streamlined [[Gila elegans]] of large rivers to thick-bodied [[Gila intermedia]] of creeks and marshes. [[Gila robusta]] occupies intermediate-sized streams. Historically, distinctive forms were described as separate taxa; more recently they have been lumped as polytypic species or "ecological subspecies," leading to nomenclatural confusion. [[Gila intermedia]] was long treated as a subspecies of [[Gila robusta]] (Hubbs 1940, 1941; Miller 1945, 1946) until elevated to species by Rinne (1969, 1976). This elevation remains controversial (Robins et al. 1980) due to the existence of phenotypically intermediate populations (often called [[Gila robusta grahami]]) that grade between [[Gila intermedia]] and [[Gila robusta robusta]].

DeMarais reviews the status of [[Gila intermedia]] and examines its relationships to [[Gila robusta robusta]] and intermediate populations. He notes that [[Gila intermedia]] shares low meristic counts, basal radii on scales, and robust morphology with species of subgenus *Temeculina* ([[Gila purpurea]], [[Gila ditaenia]], [[Gila orcutti]]), yet lacks the plate-like dermosphenotic diagnostic of that group (Barbour and Miller 1978). Osteologically, [[Gila intermedia]] closely resembles [[Gila robusta]], [[Gila nigrescens]], and [[Gila pandora]] of the [[Rio Grande]] and [[Laguna de Guzman]] basins, differing mainly in relative bone sizes and skull proportions. The skull of [[Gila purpurea]] (subgenus *Temeculina*) is shorter and broader, with frontals overlying autosphenotics and reduced lateral temporal fossae, contrasting with the more elongate skulls and well-developed fossae in [[Gila intermedia]] and [[Gila robusta]].

### Morphological and Meristic Differentiation

[[Gila intermedia]] differs from [[Gila robusta robusta]] in being proportionally much thicker-bodied, possessing 8 dorsal and 8 anal fin rays (vs. 9 and 9), fewer vertebrae, and fewer lateral-line scales (averaging <70 vs. ≥80). Caudal skeleton morphology also differs: vertebral spines of [[Gila robusta robusta]] are strongly depressed nearly parallel to the body axis, with spines of the last 5–6 vertebrae often appressed and contacting procurrent rays, creating a flared caudal fin base. In [[Gila intermedia]], spines remain at a consistent angle and do not touch one another; only 3–4 spines contact procurrent rays. Intermediate populations exhibit intermediate spine depression and ray support (4–5 spines asymmetrically involved).

Populations from the [[Santa Cruz River]] and upper [[San Pedro River]] drainages are remarkably homogeneous and represent "genetically pure" stocks of [[Gila intermedia]]. These areas have long geologic drainage continuity and isolation from [[Colorado Plateau]] margin drainages. In contrast, populations along the southern [[Colorado Plateau]] margin (e.g., [[Verde River]], [[Salt River]], [[Gila River]] tributaries) span a morphological continuum from [[Gila intermedia]]-like to [[Gila robusta robusta]]-like, with many intermediate. Canonical Discriminant Analysis (Figure 4) demonstrates this continuum, with no single population exhibiting the full range of variation.

### Study Region Physiography

The lower [[Colorado River Basin]] south of the Arizona-Utah boundary encompasses two major physiographic provinces: the [[Colorado Plateau]] (elevated ~1700 m above sea level, drained by the [[Little Colorado River]]) and the [[Basin and Range Province]] (characterized by northwest-southeast-trending mountains and valleys, elevations from near sea level to >3000 m peaks, drained largely by the [[Gila River]] watershed). Between them lies a Transition Zone of variable relief, approximating the former [[Colorado Plateau]] margin (the [[Mogollon Rim]]) that has migrated northward through erosion and faulting.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Associations and Ecophenotypy

Ecological correlates of morphology are well documented: [[Gila robusta robusta]] inhabits mainstream rivers and larger tributaries with higher current velocities; [[Gila intermedia]] occupies small, low-gradient creeks, springs, and marshes. Intermediate forms occur in streams of intermediate character. This pattern led to the "ecological subspecies" concept, wherein morphology reflects adaptation to current velocity and turbulence. However, DeMarais systematically refutes ecophenotypic and ecotypic (genetic adaptation) explanations:

1. **Temporal Stability**: Rinne (1976) documented intrademic stability over time, inconsistent with sensitive ecophenotypic responses.
2. **Poor Correlation with Stream Size**: Absolute stream size does not predict morphology reliably. [[Gila robusta robusta]] occurs in small streams (tributaries of [[Little Colorado River]], [[Salt River]]); [[Gila intermedia]]-like forms occur in larger systems ([[San Carlos River]]).
3. **Habitat Heterogeneity**: Heterogeneous streams do not produce multiple phenotypes in different microhabitats; populations remain uniform.
4. **Temperature**: Meristic development is temperature-sensitive (Hubbs 1922, 1926), yet expected patterns are not realized. Cold headwaters of [[Eagle Creek]] and [[West Clear Creek]] support populations with *lower* meristics than downstream. [[Fossil Springs]] (constant warm temperature) exhibits high variability in dorsal fin rays.
5. **Geographic Restriction**: [[Gila intermedia]] and intermediates are restricted to the [[Gila River Basin]]; similar forms are absent from the upper [[Colorado River]] and Mexican drainages inhabited by [[Gila robusta]], despite similar habitat spectra. [[Gila robusta]] is morphologically uniform throughout the upper Colorado.
6. **Character Correlation**: Intermediate populations show strong intercorrelation among meristic and morphometric characters (low meristics correlate with chubby morphology), unlikely under independent ecotypic selection in different habitats.

### Allopatry and Sympatry

[[Gila robusta robusta]] and [[Gila intermedia]] exhibit a mosaic distribution, with populations interspersed both within and adjacent to one another. This technically places them in geographic sympatry (sensu Mayr 1969), inconsistent with subspecific status (which requires geographically defined subgroups). Allopatry is the rule in the [[Verde River]] system: [[Gila robusta robusta]] occurs in the mainstream and creek mouths, while intermediate or [[Gila intermedia]]-like forms occupy headwaters, often isolated by waterfalls and dry reaches. No evidence exists for phenotypic alteration of mainstream [[Gila robusta robusta]] by gene flow from tributaries. Headwater populations (e.g., [[Fossil Springs]]) are isolated by >500 m of precipitous channel and numerous vertical falls, precluding upstream gene flow.

## Key Findings

### Specific Status of Gila intermedia

DeMarais supports specific status of [[Gila intermedia]] for three major reasons:

1. **Phenotypic Distinctiveness**: Extremes represented by [[Gila intermedia]] (especially [[Santa Cruz River]] and upper [[San Pedro River]] populations) and [[Gila robusta robusta]] are so divergent that specific status would be unquestionable without intermediates. [[Gila intermedia]] populations are remarkably homogeneous, inhabiting an area of long geologic drainage continuity and isolation.
2. **Mosaic Sympatric Distribution**: [[Gila intermedia]] and [[Gila robusta robusta]] are interspersed within and adjacent to one another, inconsistent with subspecific status. This distribution existed before anthropogenic dewatering isolated many populations.
3. **Maintenance of Independence**: Contiguous populations have maintained and continue to perpetuate their independence over geologic and recent time. Geographic variation in [[Gila intermedia]] is not clinal and cannot be attributed to environmental determinism or recent genetic exchange.

### Hybrid Origin of Intermediate Populations

DeMarais hypothesizes that intermediate populations (historically referred to as [[Gila robusta grahami]]) originated through past hybridization and introgression between [[Gila intermedia]] and [[Gila robusta robusta]]. Three morphological states are evident:

1. **Near-Identity with [[Gila intermedia]]**: Populations in upper [[Walker Creek]], [[Oak Creek]], [[Big Chino Wash]] ([[Verde River]] system), upper [[Agua Fria]] watershed, [[Spring Creek]], [[Fish Creek]], upper [[Gila River]] tributaries ([[San Carlos River]], [[Blue River]], [[Bonita Creek]], upper [[Eagle Creek]]), and lower [[San Pedro River]] tributaries ([[Redfield Canyon]], [[Bass Canyon]]). These trend only slightly toward [[Gila robusta robusta]] and may reflect minor introgression.
2. **Clear Intermediacy**: Populations in [[Fossil Springs]], upper [[West Clear Creek]], [[East Verde River]], [[Tonto Creek]] (all associated with the ancestral Verde-Payson-Tonto Mio-Pliocene basin system), and [[San Carlos River]], [[Bonita Creek]], [[Eagle Creek]] (associated with the [[Safford-San Carlos Basin]]). These exhibit intermediacy in essentially all characters and are interpreted as F₁ hybrids or stabilized hybrid populations.
3. **Near-Identity with [[Gila robusta robusta]]**: Populations in [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Burro Creek]] ([[Bill Williams River]] drainage), [[Carrizo Creek]] ([[Salt River]]), lower [[Fossil Creek]] ([[Verde River]]), and upper [[Gila River]] mainstream. These represent [[Gila robusta robusta]] with minor introgression of "intermedia-like" characters, possibly reflecting genetic swamping of ecologically peripheral populations.

### Tertiary Hydrography and Vicariance

DeMarais integrates detailed Tertiary geology to explain fish distributions and hybrid origins. Key events include:

- **Oligocene-Miocene Drainage Reversal**: Oligocene drainage in southern Arizona was northeast to southwest. Development of the [[Colorado Plateau]] margin (prior to 20 MYA) interrupted north-flowing drainages, isolating south-flowing tributaries of the forming [[Gila River Basin]] from the upper [[Colorado River]]. The ancestral [[Little Colorado River]] flowed southeast to pond in the [[Bidahochi Formation]] area (northeastern Arizona), occupied by species of [[Gila]] including one similar to [[Gila robusta]] (Uyeno and Miller 1965).
- **Miocene Basin-and-Range Tectonism**: Large-scale block faulting and volcanism (peak 13–10 MYA, ending 6–3 MYA) created northwest-trending deep basins and high ranges. Partially integrated basins aligned with the [[Santa Cruz River]] valley (Tucson, Red Rock, Picacho, Higley, Paradise, Luke basins) accumulated deposits into the Pleistocene, interconnected by northwest-flowing streams or spillways. Closed basins also existed in the [[San Pedro Valley]], [[Sulphur Springs Valley]], and [[Safford Valley]]. West- or southwest-flowing drainages were interrupted and diverted northward, allowing southward access for fishes into the developing [[Gila River]] system.
- **Plateau Margin Basins**: Structural troughs parallel to the [[Mogollon Rim]] (Perkinsville, [[Verde Formation|Verde]], Payson, Tonto, [[Safford-San Carlos Basin|San Carlos]] basins) developed and accumulated Miocene-Pliocene deposits. The largest ([[Verde Formation]]) formed when faulting and volcanism impounded Plateau drainage in the [[Verde Valley]]. This trough was subject to periodic desiccation but had more constant water supplies in the Pliocene, with lacustrine and perennial lotic habitats. Initial transfer of headwater streams with Plateau margin development and later drainage adjustment due to collapse and erosion introduced [[Gila robusta]] southward to the forming [[Gila River Basin]].
- **Hybridization Opportunities**: Mutually exclusive distributions of [[Gila]] species would have been disrupted as drainages were altered and rising water levels incorporated formerly discrete mainstream, tributary, and headspring habitats into lacustrine systems. Sporadic overflow re-established Plio-Pleistocene external drainage, captured by the ancestral [[East Verde River]] flowing southeast to the Payson-Tonto and possibly [[Safford-San Carlos Basin|Safford-San Carlos]] basins. Intermediate stocks could have originated independently at multiple places along the Plateau margin or originated in one place and dispersed to closed systems. Renewed Pleistocene faulting allowed breaching of volcanic-tectonic dams, basin dissection, and southward integration to the [[Gila River]] via [[Salt River]]. Subsequent dissection left hybrid stocks in headwaters, trapped above barrier falls.
- **Late Pliocene Integration**: Integration of upper and lower [[Colorado River Basin]] occurred as headward erosion captured the upper segment in late Miocene-early Pliocene. The [[Colorado River]] entered the [[Gulf of California]] after retreat of the marine Bouse Embayment (after 5.5 MYA). The lowermost [[Gila River]] also entered the Gulf <5.5 MYA, ending nearly 10 MYA of internal drainage. Rapid southward advance of the Colorado River Delta captured the [[Gila River Basin]] in the Pliocene, opening a route for invasion of cyprinoids ([[Ptychocheilus lucius]], [[Gila elegans]], [[Gila robusta robusta]], [[Catostomus latipinnis]], [[Xyrauchen texanus]]) from the north. These fishes spread throughout larger streams except the [[Santa Cruz Basin]], suggesting lack of suitable habitat, isolation due to aggradation, or other factors. Long-term isolation is supported by absence of [[Gila River]] endemics ([[Meda fulgida]], [[Tiaroga cobitis]]). Following Pliocene integration, vagile [[Gila robusta robusta]] invaded suitable mainstream habitats and replaced hybrid stocks.

### Relationships to Other Gila Species

DeMarais notes that [[Gila intermedia]] and intermediate populations morphologically and meristically resemble [[Gila nigrescens]] and [[Gila pandora]] of the [[Rio Grande]] and [[Laguna de Guzman]] basins more closely than [[Gila robusta]]. [[Gila pandora]] is similar to [[Gila intermedia]]; [[Gila nigrescens]] resembles "grahami." Miller and Chernoff (1980) demonstrated character gradation from [[Gila pandora]] through [[Gila nigrescens]] to [[Gila robusta]], similar to the [[Gila intermedia]]–"grahami"–[[Gila robusta robusta]] gradient. A speculative alternative is that [[Gila intermedia]] and "grahami" represent species derived independently of the [[Gila robusta]] lineage, as ancient inhabitants of an early [[Gila River]]–upper [[Rio Grande]] drainage complex. Geologic evidence exists for common drainage of the upper [[Gila River]], uppermost [[Rio Grande]], and intervening watersheds, and for subsequent vicariant events. For example, Eocene to early Miocene east-flowing drainage off the ancestral [[Mogollon Highlands]] may have represented an ancestral upper [[Rio Grande]] (Belcher 1975). Fishes such as [[Pantosteus plebeius]], [[Gila pandora]], [[Gila nigrescens]], [[Gila intermedia]], and [[Pantosteus clarkii]] may represent derivatives of an ancestral fauna distributed in this system. The upper [[Gila River]] east of the Pinaleno Mountains was impounded or flowed southeastward in the Pliocene into [[San Simon Valley]], possibly joining an ancestral [[Rio Grande]] in northern Mexico.
