# Spikedace Recovery Plan - Technical Review Draft

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #conservation-biology #threatened-species #desert-ecology

## Executive Summary

This technical review draft outlines a comprehensive recovery plan for the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), a small stream-dwelling minnow endemic to the [[Gila River]] system of Arizona and New Mexico. Listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1986, the species has experienced dramatic reductions in distribution and abundance due to habitat destruction, water diversion, and interactions with non-native fishes. The plan establishes objectives to protect existing populations, restore depleted stocks, reintroduce populations to historic habitats, and ensure long-term self-sustainability. Critical threats include damming, channel alteration, groundwater pumping, riparian destruction, and predation/competition from introduced species, particularly [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*). The recovery strategy encompasses habitat protection and restoration, population monitoring, research on exotic fish interactions, augmentation of depleted populations, and potential reintroduction to the [[San Pedro River]] system.

## Background & Description

The spikedace is a small, sleek, stream-dwelling member of the family [[Cyprinidae]]. The body is slender, almost spindle-shaped, and slightly compressed laterally. Scales are present only as small plates deeply embedded in the skin. Two spinose rays occur at the leading edge of the dorsal fin, the first being obviously the strongest, sharp-pointed, and nearly as long as the second. Eyes and mouth are both large; barbels are absent. The dorsal fin contains seven rays, and the anal fin usually has nine. Pharyngeal teeth are arranged in two rows with formula 1,4-4,1.

Coloration is bright silvery on the sides with vertically-elongated black specks. The back is olive-gray to brownish, usually mottled with darker pigment. The underside is white. Males in breeding condition become brightly golden or brassy, especially on the head and at fin bases.

**Historical Distribution**: The species was endemic to the upper [[Gila River]] basin of Arizona and New Mexico, with widespread distribution in large and moderate-sized rivers including the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers and their major tributaries upstream of Phoenix, plus the Agua Fria, San Pedro, and San Francisco river systems. The species undoubtedly occurred in the San Pedro River in Sonora, Mexico, though never collected there.

**Present Distribution**: In Arizona, spikedace now occurs only in [[Aravaipa Creek]] (tributary to San Pedro River in Graham and Pinal counties), [[Eagle Creek]] (tributary to Gila River in Graham and Greenlee counties), and upper [[Verde River]] with lower [[Sycamore Creek]] (Yavapai County). In New Mexico, the species is restricted to the mainstem Gila River and the East, Middle, and West Forks. Only the Cliff-Gila Valley, New Mexico population is considered comparable in abundance to earlier years; others have been substantially diminished.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Requirements**: Spikedace occupy flowing waters in streams, usually less than one meter deep. Adults are often aggregated in eddying currents or near downstream ends of riffles, often including pools. Larvae and juveniles generally occupy marginal areas. In larger rivers, adults most often occur near tributary mouths, while smaller fish are found in quiet water along pool margins over softer, finer-grained substrates. The fish use shallower, strongly flowing areas in springtime, often over sandy-gravelly substrates. Specific habitat associations vary seasonally, geographically, and ontogenetically.

**Life History**: 
- **Reproduction**: Breeding occurs in spring (April-June) in response to stream discharge and water temperature. Males patrol shallow, sandy-gravelly riffles with moderate current. No territoriality is evident. Receptive females are approached by up to six males, with two remaining alongside during spawning. Sex ratio varies from near unity among younger fish to greater abundance of females among older individuals. Females may be fractional spawners with 90-250 ova; ovum diameter at spawning is near 1.5 mm or greater.
- **Growth**: Young grow rapidly during first summer/autumn, attaining 35-40 mm standard length (SL) by November. Fish average near 40 mm SL at end of one year and 50-63 mm SL at end of second year. Maximum size is near 65 mm SL in [[Aravaipa Creek]] and 68 mm SL in upper Gila River. Longevity typically is one to two years; exceptional individuals may survive four years.
- **Foods**: Carnivores feeding primarily on terrestrial and aquatic insects entrained in stream drift. Important prey includes larval ephemeropterans, hydropsychid trichopterans, and chironomid dipterans. Prey body size typically ranges 2-3 mm long. Diet diversity is greatest among smaller spikedace; adults specialize on larger, drifting nymphal and adult ephemeropterans.

**Co-occurring Native Fishes**: [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*).

**Non-native Fish Threats**: [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) occupies similar habitat and now occurs in all places formerly occupied by spikedace. Continuing contraction in spikedace range has been attributed in part to upstream expansion of red shiner populations. Other introduced species include [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*M. dolomieui*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanelius*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*S. gairdneri*). Direct predation and displacement have been suggested as potential impacts.

**Reasons for Decline**: Habitat destruction or alteration and interactions with non-native fishes have acted independently or in concert to extirpate or deplete populations. Major impacts include dewatering, impoundments, altered flow regimes, stream channelization, bank stabilization, cattle grazing, mining, agriculture, timber harvest, sedimentation, and toxic contaminants. Natural flooding may play a significant role in native fish life histories by rejuvenating habitats and because desert fishes effectively withstand such disturbances while non-native forms apparently do not.

## Key Findings

**Recovery Objective**: Protect existing populations and restore depleted and extirpated populations of spikedace and their habitats, ensuring opportunity for the species to become non-endangered and self-sustaining in perpetuity. Realization of this objective will constitute justification for delisting.

**Critical Management Actions**:

1. **Protect Existing Populations**:
   - Designate critical habitat (initially proposed but deferred; no current legal designation)
   - Identify and acquire important private lands
   - Discourage detrimental land and water uses
   - Insure perennial flows
   - Enforce existing laws and regulations
   - Discourage introduction of non-native bait fishes

2. **Monitor Status**: Establish standard monitoring locations and techniques for extant populations; determine range of natural variation in absolute abundance and age-class structure; conduct bi-annual (spring, autumn) population estimates; monitor community composition.

3. **Research Priorities**:
   - Identify nature and significance of interaction with exotic fishes (direct predation/displacement and indirect interactions)
   - Quantify effects of physical habitat modification on life cycle completion (substrate, velocity, depth, water temperature, and their interactions)

4. **Population Augmentation**: Identify target areas amenable to management; determine necessary habitat and landscape improvements; implement habitat improvement; identify stocks capable of supplying individuals for transplant; augment with wild adult individuals; monitor augmented populations.

5. **Reintroduction Program**: Focus initially on [[San Pedro River]] system (type locality). Steps include: determine habitat suitability; enhance habitat as necessary; assess status of non-native fishes; assure closure of potential immigration routes; reclaim as necessary to remove non-native fishes; identify suitable stocks; reintroduce spikedace; monitor success/failure.

6. **Quantitative Criteria for Self-Sustaining Populations**: Determine acceptable levels of natural variation in absolute numbers, age-class structure, reproduction, and recruitment; establish minimum stock size; characterize suitable environmental variables (physical, chemical, biological community).

7. **Contingency Planning**: Consider captive holding, propagation and rearing programs. While not currently necessary due to substantial remaining populations, conditions could change rapidly requiring hatchery intervention.

8. **Information and Education**: Maintain open communication among States, Federal agencies, local residents and water users; provide information to public sector through local media, statewide campaigns, and national exposure; ensure professional information exchange through meetings, presentations, and peer-reviewed publications.

**Protected Lands**: Significant stream reaches occupied by spikedace flow through lands under jurisdiction of U.S. Bureau of Land Management, Defenders of Wildlife, The Nature Conservancy, New Mexico Department of Game and Fish, National Park Service, U.S. Forest Service (Gila, Prescott, and Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests, including Gila Wilderness), State of Arizona, and San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation. However, protection can only be realized when critical habitat is designated and Endangered Species Act compliance is implemented.

**State Protections**: Arizona classifies spikedace as Group 3 threatened species; New Mexico as Group 2 endangered species. The species is prohibited from take except by angling in Arizona or by special permit in both states. Neither state listing otherwise protects spikedace or the habitats it occupies.

**Proposed Critical Habitat** (initially proposed 1985, not yet designated):
- Arizona: Aravaipa Creek (~24 km), Verde River (~37 km), Sycamore Creek (~1.5 km), Eagle Creek (~33 km)
- New Mexico: Gila River sections totaling ~73 km including mainstem, West Fork, and Middle Fork

**Data Gaps**: Substantial gaps exist in basic ecology despite relatively intensive study. Specific needs include: fertilized ova recovery, incubation times, size at hatching, mechanisms of interaction with exotic species, quantitative habitat requirements, and long-term population dynamics under natural variation.
