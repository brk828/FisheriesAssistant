# Colorado River Lower Basin Fisheries Management Prospectus — Review Memorandum

## Executive Summary
This document is not a primary morphological or ecological research paper but an **interagency review memorandum** (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Arizona Ecological Services State Office, 1995) critiquing a draft "Lower Basin Fisheries Management Prospectus" for the [[Colorado River]] system. The memo addresses conservation strategy for four federally significant "big-river fish" species: [[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (Colorado Pikeminnow). It emphasizes that the **lower Colorado River basin populations of bonytail and razorback sucker are more robust than upper basin populations**, making their protection critical to preventing species-wide extirpation. The memo raises concerns about organizational clarity, geographic scope (lower Colorado River mainstem vs. entire lower basin including Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers), and the balance between short-term genetic rescue and long-term riverine recovery.

## Background & Description
The source document does not provide morphological descriptions, measurements, or taxonomic keys for the species discussed. It is a management/policy text rather than a biological characterization study. Relevant species requiring morphological documentation elsewhere include:

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat alteration**: Historic riverine conditions in the [[Colorado River]] were fundamentally altered by dam and reservoir construction, causing:
  - Complete drying of some river segments
  - Altered seasonal flow regimes
  - Blocked historic migration/movement corridors
  - Changes in turbidity and temperature, likely precluding successful spawning by indigenous fish (citing Minckley et al. 1991; USFWS 1990a-b, 1991b)
- **Native turbidity-adapted fish**: The Colorado River's naturally turbid character (in both canyon and slow-moving reaches) is highlighted as ecologically relevant to native "big-river" fish life history.
- **Non-native predation pressure**: Non-native predatory fish exert **significant predation on young native fish (larvae/fry)**, with comparatively less impact on adults. Fine-mesh net refugia intended to exclude predators can inadvertently allow ingress of non-native fry, establishing predator populations within refuges — potentially causing near-total losses of young [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- **Refugia habitats**: Riverine cove refugia and reservoir embayments (e.g., [[Lake Havasu]], [[Lake Mohave]]) function as semi-natural nursery/holding habitats distinct from hatchery-based captive propagation facilities.
- **Genetic sampling bias**: Larval collection methods for genetic management are biased — larvae with lower phototaxic response have a **reduced probability of capture**, introducing potential selection bias in broodstock genetic representation.
- **Current distribution notes**:
  - No [[Colorado Squawfish]] populations exist in the Gila River; all lower basin recovery actions to date have occurred in the **Salt and Verde River drainages**.
  - All [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] critical habitat in the lower basin is characterized as **occupied habitat**, meaning further stocking there constitutes population *augmentation* rather than *reintroduction*.
  - Reintroduction of [[Colorado Squawfish]] or bonytail outside Lakes Havasu/Mohave would involve **unoccupied habitat**, requiring additional regulatory coordination under the Endangered Species Act.

## Key Findings
- **Species status and priority**: [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] are identified as the most imperiled and are the primary focus of the prospectus; [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] occupy secondary roles, with the Glen Canyon Dam Biological Opinion potentially addressing humpback chub recovery needs.
- **Experimental reintroduction outcomes**: The experimental non-essential population establishment effort for [[Colorado Squawfish]] has had **"less than hoped"** success, indicating need for revised direction.
- **Short-term vs. long-term goals**:
  - *Short-term*: Prevent extirpation and loss of genetic material via secure rearing facilities, genetic management plans, and holding/refugia areas.
  - *Long-term*: Restore self-sustaining riverine populations across historic range, requiring habitat surveys, mapping, and negotiated improvements to river management — extending beyond the lower Colorado River mainstem to tributaries such as the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]].
- **Refugia management issue**: Fine-mesh net enclosures used to create predator-free refugia can fail if predatory fish larvae/fry breach the mesh, necessitating periodic **renovation** (predator removal) to maintain refuge productivity — a method already in practical use at [[Lake Mohave]] for several years.
- **Water management uncertainty**: Controlled water releases are not automatically beneficial; water temperature, turbidity, and broader water quality parameters must be evaluated to ensure changes benefit native fish without simultaneously favoring non-native competitors/predators.
- **Ecosystem-scale context**: Numerous non-fish listed and candidate species also occur along the lower Colorado River, requiring integration into "big-river fish" management actions for holistic ecosystem management.
- **Institutional/programmatic ambiguity**: The prospectus's unclear geographic scope (lower Colorado River mainstem vs. full lower basin) and inconsistent terminology (e.g., "plan" vs. "prospectus," "refuge" vs. "refugia") were identified as significant barriers to effective implementation and partner coordination.
- **Stakeholder context**: Water users in the lower basin were reportedly evaluating regulatory pathways (Section 10 HCP vs. Section 7 consultation with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]) in response to anticipated recovery actions for these species.
