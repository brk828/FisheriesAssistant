# Cultural and Environmental History of Cienega Valley, Southeastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #paleoecology

## Executive Summary

This monograph (University of Arizona Press, 1983; Anthropological Papers no. 43, authors Frank W. Eddy and Maurice E. Cooley, with sections by Paul S. Martin and Bruce B. Huckell) reconstructs roughly 3,500 years of interrelated human and environmental history in the [[Cienega Valley]], the floodplain of [[Cienega Creek]] and its tributary [[Matty Wash]] in the Empire Valley of southeastern Arizona. Using alluvial stratigraphy, [[Pollen Analysis]], charcoal and bone identifications, nonmarine molluscs, and [[Radiocarbon Dating]], the authors link [[Cochise Culture]] hunter-gatherers and later [[Hohokam Culture]] farmers to a sequence of stable, cienega-dominated and unstable, arroyo-cutting environments.

The document contains no fish data. Its aquatic content concerns perennial and ephemeral streams, marshes (cienegas), ponds and freshwater molluscs, which are treated as indicators of wetland habitat and past climate.

## Background & Description

The study area lies about 96.6 km southeast of Tucson, between the Empire and Whetstone mountains, at the V-shaped junction of Matty Wash and Cienega Creek (Sections 25 and 26, T18S, R17E). The authors use "Cienega Valley" for the floodplain plus adjacent ridges and terraces. Work was done in the late 1950s and early 1960s: three weeks of excavation (Eddy 1958), about 4,424 m of arroyo-wall mapping at 1 inch to 50 feet, and seven measured stratigraphic sections (MC-1 to MC-7). Earlier investigators included [[Byron Cummings]], [[Emil W. Haury]], and [[Earl H. Swanson]].

The Foreword by Haury places the work within the history of Southwestern archaeology, noting the [[Folsom Site]] discoveries and the San Pedro stage of the Cochise culture (radiocarbon dated to the first millennium B.C. and the time of Christ). Haury also reports severe flooding in summer 1980 that exposed new cultural features.

Appendix C by Huckell recalibrates the radiocarbon dates using [[Radiocarbon Calibration]] tables (Klein and others 1982) at 95 percent confidence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and setting.** No weather records exist for the valley; values are estimated from stations at Benson, Canelo, Elgin, Fairbank, Fort Huachuca, and Cienega. The valley probably receives 30.5 to 35.6 cm of annual precipitation, with a mean annual temperature of 15.6°C (60°F) and a growing season of 170 or more days. Rain falls in winter (December to February) and summer (July and August).

**Vegetation.** The valley is grassland between Sonoran desert below about 909 m and oak woodland above. Mesquite (*Prosopis juliflora*) has invaded the floodplain over the past 50 years. Before 1900, Cienega Creek was sluggish, flowing through dense cienegas (bogs) with tall grass. Dominant range grasses (Table 1.2) include [[Blue Grama]] and sideoats grama.

**Wildlife.** Modern fauna includes jackrabbit, cottontail, kangaroo rat, javelina, mule deer, pronghorn, skunk, porcupine, badger, coyote, and mountain lion. Beaver and migratory waterfowl used the historic cienegas. Bison are recorded prehistorically only at Babocomari, not in this valley.

**Aquatic and wetland biota.** Historic accounts describe swamps, pools, beaver, waterfowl, and stream trout seen by John Bartlett in 1851. Nonmarine molluscs (Table 4.1, identified by Robert J. Drake) occur in Units 1 through 7. Freshwater taxa include *Pisidium*, *Helisoma*, *Lymnaea*, and *Physa*; land snails include *Vertigo*, *Gastrocopta*, and *Pupoides*. Freshwater molluscs came from 13 of 14 samples and indicated shallow-water habitats. The authors note the collection was too small for climatic interpretation.

**Hydrology and geomorphology.** The floodplain was built by alluviation over about 3,500 years (average thickness 9 m of alluvium). Arroyo trenching began about 1900 and lowered the water table, draining cienegas. Late Recent alluvial Units 1 to 7 are described in Chapter 1. Mean deposition rates were about 0.24 cm per year, ranging from 0.21 to 0.31 cm per year in the valley.

## Key Findings

- **Study scope:** No fish species were recorded. The aquatic evidence concerns cienega marshes, ponds, perennial and ephemeral streams, and freshwater molluscs.
- **Preceramic occupation:** Three San Pedro stage sites (Arizona EE:2:12, EE:2:30, EE:2:35) were buried by an average of 5 m of alluvium. The main midden at EE:2:30 was 61 cm thick and extended more than 36 m. More than 140 stone tools were recovered, and eight San Pedro burials were found.
- **Subsistence:** Ground stone and chipped stone tools were roughly 52% and 48% at EE:2:30, suggesting a near balance between plant gathering and hunting. Maize pollen from Units 4 and 6 suggests incipient agriculture.
- **Fauna:** Mammal bones at EE:2:30 include antelope (31 bones), mule deer (55 bones), and jackrabbit (25 bones), indicating mixed grassland-shrub habitat.
- **Chronology:** Radiocarbon dates from EE:2:30 span about 1300 B.C. to A.D. 1. The Unit 7 date of 3570 ± 210 B.P. is the oldest from the study area.
- **Pottery arrival:** Pottery first appeared around A.D. 1 in this area, later than in the Hohokam and Mogollon regions by the second or third century B.C.
- **Hohokam phases:** Vahki-Estrella, Snaketown, Canada del Oro, Undifferentiated Canada del Oro-Rillito, Rincon, Tanque Verde, and Tucson phases are documented, with settlement shifting from floodplain to ridges after the Rillito phase.
- **Environmental history:** Stable cienega conditions prevailed until about A.D. 300. Channel cutting occurred between A.D. 200 to 300 and 600. Drying and arroyo cutting occurred after A.D. 900 to 1200, with the Sanford formation (Unit 2) deposited afterward.
- **Abandonment:** Occupation appears continuous until about A.D. 1300, with a sharp decline in the Tucson phase. The cause of abandonment is not known with certainty but is linked to sharp environmental change.
- **Historic period:** The Sanford Ranch was established in the early 1870s, bought by Walter Vail in 1876 (Empire Ranch). Arroyo cutting began between 1890 and 1905, with 1900 as the approximate start on Cienega Creek.
- **Conclusion:** The authors judge the Hohokam culture in the valley to have gradually developed from a preceramic Desert Culture base, with a mixed hunting, gathering, and farming economy.
