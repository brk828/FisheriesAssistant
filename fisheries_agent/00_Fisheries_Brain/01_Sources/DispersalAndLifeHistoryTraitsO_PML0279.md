# Dispersal and Life History Traits of Notropis girardi, Introduced into the Pecos River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study documents the introduction, dispersal, and life history of the [[Arkansas River Shiner]] (*Notropis girardi*) in the [[Pecos River]], New Mexico. The species was first collected in 1978 just below [[Sumner Dam]], most likely after release of bait fish. By 1981 it had colonized about 260 km of mainstream river. Its growth, age structure, and mortality resembled those of a native population in [[Revuelto Creek]], New Mexico, and the timing of spawning was similar in both. The authors link reproduction and downstream dispersal to high discharge pulses, and suggest that Pecos River flow regimes resemble those of the species' former native streams, which may explain its establishment.

## Background & Description

The Arkansas River Shiner is endemic to the Arkansas River drainage of New Mexico, Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas, and Arkansas. It was once common in warm, sandy-bottomed, fluctuating streams and rivers, but its range and abundance have declined sharply across much of its native range. The decline is mainly attributed to disruption of natural flow patterns, and possibly to interactions with the introduced [[Red River Shiner]] (*Notropis bairdi*). The westernmost native populations occur in the [[Canadian River]] drainage of New Mexico, where the species remains relatively common.

The study used seine collections (3.2–6.4 mm mesh) from July 1986 to August 1987 in the Pecos River and selected tributaries, sampling all habitat types during low flow. Specimens were measured to 0.1 mm standard length (SL), and age was assigned from length-frequency histograms and scales. Scale-based ageing was unreliable beyond Age I, so only fish with two distinct annuli and relatively large scales were classed as Age II or older. Revuelto Creek specimens (UNM 4672, n = 438) served as the native comparison.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pecos River.** The river drains 50,022 km² in New Mexico and is impounded by several reservoirs. Since Sumner Dam closed in 1937, mean annual discharge has declined slightly (from 6.7 m³/sec, 1927–1936, to 5.7 m³/sec, 1938–1986). Peak flows have diminished and zero-flow days are common. Flows are highest in May and June, and during the irrigation season (May–September) discharge varies up to 28.4 m³/sec. Winter flows depend on spring input and aquifer recharge. Water temperature ranges from 0 °C to 32 °C, reaching 38 °C at times. Between Sumner Dam and McMillan Reservoir the river is shallow and braided, with sandy runs and short riffles. Downstream habitat is mainly pools separated by riffles.

**Revuelto Creek.** This Canadian River tributary near Logan, New Mexico, is 5–15 m wide and generally less than 0.15 m deep, with shifting loose sand and some gravel. Mean annual discharge is 1.25 m³/sec (range 0.03–38.3 m³/sec, 1959–1985). Flows below 0.05 m³/sec are common in winter, and irrigation return flows supplement the stream.

## Key Findings

**Introduction and dispersal**
- The first Pecos River specimens (n = 16, ENMU NM-DB 78-171.12) were collected on 7 September 1978 just below Sumner Dam. They were Age I+ adults, 37–46 mm SL.
- Collections from 1974–1977, including twenty collections from Santa Rosa to below McMillan Reservoir, did not contain the species. The authors conclude that the initial release occurred in 1978.
- The species dispersed downstream at least 260 km by autumn 1981. By 1987 it had been verified another 98 km, to the New Mexico–Texas border.
- Possible vectors include passage through McMillan, Avalon, and two smaller reservoirs (about 15 km of lentic habitat), or multiple introductions via the poorly monitored bait trade.

**Abundance**
- In September 1981–February 1982, the species was found at 14 of 17 sites, making up 6.4% of specimens. Mean catch was 48 per collection, and the largest was 472 at Lake Arthur Falls.
- In 1986–1987 it was found in 21 of 24 collections at 14 of 16 localities. A total of 2213 specimens (8.8% of catch) was taken, with a mean of 105 per collection and a maximum of 704 at Lake Arthur Falls (B9).
- Abundance was highest in the middle 1 and middle 2 reaches. It was low just below Sumner Reservoir and between McMillan and Red Bluff reservoirs, where shallow, braided, sandy habitat was scarce and water was clear.
- At station B8 the species made up 39% of prespawning collections (March and May 1986) but was uncommon in July 1986 and August 1987.
- Fifteen individuals were collected in the Rio Felix, an intermittent tributary, 4.0 km above its confluence with the Pecos River. The same collections included two juvenile [[Pecos Bluntnose Shiner]] (*Notropis simus pecosensis*, 36.8 and 32.7 mm SL), a federally listed threatened species and the first tributary record known to the authors for that taxon.

**Life history**
- The Pecos River population contained at least three and possibly four age classes (Age 0–III) and was dominated by Age 0 and Age I fish.
- Prespawning collections were mostly Age I, which the authors attribute to high spawning and/or overwinter mortality of older fish. Age I and older fish were very uncommon in July and August, indicating high postspawning mortality.
- Growth was fastest in the first summer and then slowed sharply. First-year growth matched values for laboratory-reared fish reported by Moore (1944).
- Spawning began in mid-May 1986 and continued to late July, peaking between mid-June and mid-July. Mean June and July water temperatures near Artesia (B11) were 25 °C and 26.5 °C.
- Pecos and Revuelto Creek populations both appear to show multiple spawning peaks. Age I bimodality supports this, although Cross et al. (1983) reported uniform egg size and a single annual spawning in Kansas fish.
- Spawning coincided with a large flow increase. Discharge rose on 3 May 1986 from 0.42 m³/sec to 22.2 m³/sec for the rest of May.

**Flow and success**
- The authors propose that high discharge triggers spawning, keeps eggs and larvae buoyant, and carries them downstream. Buoyancy may reduce abrasion and suffocation on shifting sand.
- Irrigation releases from Sumner Reservoir maintain habitat and create discharge pulses during the spawning season. Spring input and groundwater recharge keep water in the channel when releases decline.
- Flow regimes in the Pecos River, especially in 1986, resemble the variable regimes of the species' former native streams, such as the [[Cimarron River]] in Kansas and Oklahoma.

**Competition and co-introduction**
- [[Plains Minnow]] (*Hybognathus placitus*) has also been introduced to the Pecos River. It now makes up more than 80% of the catch at some sites and has displaced the native *Hybognathus amarus*, not recorded there since 1968. The authors suggest that *N. girardi* may have arrived in a bait shipment of plains minnows.
- The native fish community is more diverse than those where *N. girardi* occurs in its native range. This would normally be expected to hinder establishment, which the authors describe as puzzling.

**Conclusions**
- The Pecos River population's growth, age structure, mortality, and spawning timing closely match native populations in New Mexico, Oklahoma, and Kansas.
- Establishment is attributed to flow regimes that reproduce former native conditions and to high-discharge pulses that drive both spawning and dispersal.
