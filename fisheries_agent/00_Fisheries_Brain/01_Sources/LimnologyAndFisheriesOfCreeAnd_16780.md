# Limnology and Fisheries of Cree and Wollaston Lakes in Northern Saskatchewan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 Fisheries Branch report (Fisheries Report No. 4, by D. S. Rawson) compares the limnology, plankton, bottom fauna, fish populations and commercial fisheries of two remote Precambrian Shield lakes in northern Saskatchewan: [[Cree Lake]] (surveyed 1955) and [[Wollaston Lake]] (surveyed 1956). Both lakes are cold, very low in dissolved minerals and oligotrophic. Cree Lake has a sandy basin, a sparse bottom fauna lacking the deep-water amphipod [[Pontoporeia]], and a fish community dominated by coarse fish. Wollaston has a richer bottom fauna dominated by Pontoporeia and a higher share of useable fish. The report concludes that both lakes have limited productive capacity, roughly two pounds of useable fish per acre per year, and recommends revised commercial harvest limits and mesh sizes.

## Background & Description

The study was part of the Fisheries Branch's effort to understand the productive capacity of large northern lakes that were becoming accessible by air. Cree Lake (about 446 sq. miles of water, 546 islands) lies in the Athabaska-Mackenzie drainage and is surrounded by glacial sand, drumlins and eskers. Wollaston Lake (about 796 sq. miles, 370 islands) is less sandy, has more rock outcrop, and uniquely drains into two systems: the Cochrane River to the Churchill and the Fond-du-Lac River to the Athabaska-Mackenzie.

Field work covered June to September 1955 at Cree Lake and June to September 1956 at Wollaston Lake, with an earlier preliminary Wollaston survey in August 1949. Methods included six to ten sampling stations, weekly temperature and oxygen profiles, Secchi disc transparency, plankton hauls with No. 20 silk bolting cloth, Ekman and Peterson dredging, standard gangs of gill nets, seine hauls, scale-based age determination and stomach analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions.** Cree Lake's surface temperatures stayed around 15 to 16°C in summer, while bottom temperatures ranged from 4.4 to 5.2°C. Dissolved oxygen never became limiting; the lowest bottom value was 4.8 c.c./l. (54% saturation). The pH was 6.8 to 7.1 at the surface. Wollaston's surface pH was about 7.2, with bottom values as low as 6.2 in Fidler Bay and the deepest waters, and the lowest oxygen recorded was 3.8 c.c./l. (about 50% saturation). Both lakes are extremely soft. Total solids were 22 p.p.m. in Cree Lake (December 1956) and 35 to 40 p.p.m. in Wollaston. Secchi transparency in Cree ranged from 5.6 to 11.4 m, and in Wollaston Secchi readings averaged about 6 m.

**Plankton.** Plankton was dominated by diatoms (about 60% of Cree's phytoplankton, mainly [[Asterionella]] and [[Melosira]]) and copepods, with cladocerans and rotifers prominent. Standing crop was 25.2 kg dry weight/ha in Cree Lake and 14.8 kg/ha in Wollaston. These are among the lowest values recorded in northern Saskatchewan lakes.

**Bottom fauna.** Cree Lake's bottom is about 43% sand and 45% ooze, with 274 animals per m² and 1.6 kg/ha, the lowest of any lake in the report. Chironomid larvae made up about 60% of the population, and the deep-water amphipod Pontoporeia was absent. Wollaston's bottom averaged 814 organisms per m² and 4.7 kg/ha, with Pontoporeia (614 per m² on average) as the dominant food item. The report's comparison table lists Waskesiu at 24.6 kg/ha, Big Peter Pond at 73.4 kg/ha and Reindeer Lake at 1.6 kg/ha.

**Fish fauna and populations.** Cree Lake yielded 16 species (11 large, 5 minnow-sized), and Wollaston 19 species (11 large, 8 minnow-sized). Both share the northern assemblage, including [[Round Whitefish]] and [[Grayling]]. In Cree Lake, longnose sucker (42.5%) and common whitefish (30.5%) dominated the standard gill-net catch, with lake trout at 4.5% by number but a larger share by weight. In Wollaston, common whitefish (42%) and longnose sucker (25%) were the leading species, and lake trout made up 11.2%.

**Diet.** In Cree Lake, whitefish fed mainly on chironomid larvae and sphaeriid clams, with unusual frequency of fish remains. In Wollaston, whitefish fed mainly on Pontoporeia (about 45% of volume). Lake trout were piscivorous (about 85 to 95% fish in both lakes).

**Parasites.** The tapeworm [[Triaenophorus]] infested whitefish in both lakes. Cree Lake's commercial whitefish averaged 7.3 cysts per fish in December 1945, so fillet processing was recommended there. Wollaston's moderate infestation led to its classification as an "A lake," where filleting is not required, though filleting is still used there because of transport costs.

**Human context.** Cree Lake's population was about 50 persons in 1956, and Wollaston's was probably under 150 (about 100 Chipewyan, 25 Métis and about a dozen whites). Fishing and trapping were the main livelihoods.

## Key Findings

**Cree Lake**
- The sandy basin and absence of deep-water amphipods (Pontoporeia) and of most Mysis result in very low bottom and plankton production, which limits whitefish food.
- Coarse fish (longnose and common suckers) made up 54% of the test-net catch, far more than the 30% limit found in other lakes compared.
- Average catch was 96.5 fish and 103 lb per standard gang, similar to Athabaska, Wollaston and Reindeer, but far below La Ronge, Waskesiu and Big Peter Pond.
- Commercial catch averaged 263,000 lb per year (1946 to 1956), with about 94% lake trout and 4% whitefish.
- Trout production averaged 0.87 lb/ac, compared with 0.36 lb/ac in Lac la Ronge and 0.55 lb/ac in Wollaston.
- Recommended management: commercial limit of 200,000 lb trout and retention of the 400,000 lb whitefish limit, for a combined harvest of about 2.1 lb/ac. Growth rates of trout and whitefish were similar to other northern lakes, and pike growth was slow (a 25-lb pike takes nearly 25 years).

**Wollaston Lake**
- The main bays and bottom fauna are richer, particularly Pontoporeia, which makes up the main whitefish food, and there are fewer coarse fish than in Cree.
- Commercial catch averaged 534,000 lb per year over the 12 years to 1957, with 56% trout and 41% whitefish. The catch exceeded 800,000 lb in 1948 and 1949.
- Lake trout grew faster than in La Ronge or Cree Lakes, and the report suggests the trout population may have thinned as a result of heavy fishing.
- Recommended management: maintain the 450,000 lb trout limit (about 0.87 lb/ac, a rate the report regards as high) and raise the whitefish limit, possibly to 650,000 lb, for a total harvest of about 2.14 lb/ac. The 5-inch mesh is reaffirmed.
- Estimated sustainable useable production is somewhat more than two pounds per acre, comparable to Reindeer Lake.

**Shared conclusions**
- Both lakes are low in productivity because of minimal minerals, cold water and sparse plankton.
- Useable fish production is estimated at about two pounds per acre per year.
- The report recommends a 5-inch gill-net mesh (replacing 5½-inch, which was changed in 1948) and suggests that angling should be prioritized in the future while commercial fishing is still encouraged to minimise conflict.
- A proposed northern road could change the value of both fisheries.
