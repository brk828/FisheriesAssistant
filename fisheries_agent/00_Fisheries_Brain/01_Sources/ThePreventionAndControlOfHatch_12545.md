# Prevention and Control of Hatchery Diseases by Treating the Water Supply

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1932 New York State Conservation Department bulletin, by [[O. R. Kingsbury]] and [[G. C. Embody]] of the [[Cornell University]] Laboratory of Fish Culture, describes a device that treats hatchery fish diseases by adding a constant, metered flow of chemical reagent to the water supply of a trough or pond. The apparatus, called the [[Fish Disease Eradicator]], lets treatment proceed without draining the rearing unit, handling the fish, or shutting off the water. The authors base their tables on one-hour treatments of [[Goldfish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] and report that the device works especially well with [[Potassium Permanganate]], with [[Copper Sulphate]] and [[Formaldehyde]] as alternatives. It is effective against external flukes and several protozoan and bacterial conditions, but not against internal diseases.

## Background & Description

Two traditional methods were in use at New York State hatcheries. The [[Salt Bath]] dissolved common salt in a trough until the fish became distressed. The [[Dip Method]] immersed affected fish in a reagent for a set time using a dip net or deep tray. Both held fish in relatively strong solutions for one to fifteen minutes.

[[Walter N. Hess]] (1930) had shown that immersing fish in a very dilute potassium permanganate solution for a long period controlled external fluke worms on goldfish. The authors found this more effective than the acetic acid dip against [[Gyrodactylus]] and [[Dactylogyrus]] and less harmful to fish. It could not be applied to trout, however, because prolonged immersion in small volumes with large numbers of fish would cause suffocation. The paper adapts Hess's approach by using a steady-flow device that does not shut off the water supply.

Tests were run partly at the Cornell laboratory, financed by the Conservation Department, and partly at State hatcheries to check performance under production conditions.

### The Fish Disease Eradicator

- **Storage tank (A):** a metal tank of several gallons holding the reagent. A four-gallon tank with one filling treats a 10 gallon-per-minute flow for one hour, and refilling about every fifteen minutes allows a 40 gallon-per-minute flow to be treated.
- **Distributing vessel (B):** a metal vessel about 10 × 6 × 6 inches that feeds the reagent at a constant rate. One vessel can treat one to six troughs at a time.
- **Float valve (C):** a toilet-type valve (about 1¼-inch pipes) that keeps a constant liquid level, and so a constant outflow.
- **Distributing cocks (D):** gas cocks with 3/16-inch or ¼-inch openings, connected to troughs by rubber tubing.

Apparatus of this size handles flows up to about 35 gallons per minute at 1:100,000 potassium permanganate. For 70 gallons per minute, the authors suggest ¾-inch float valves and ½-inch distributing cocks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Treatment effectiveness depended on water temperature, fish size and condition, and reagent concentration.

- **Temperature:** Trough temperatures from January to July ranged from about 34°F to 72°F. The authors found that fish and parasites were more susceptible to permanganate at higher temperatures, so stronger solutions are needed in cold water.
- **Size:** Young trout about one inch long, fed for one month, were the least resistant to treatment. Beginning at about one inch, larger fish tolerate stronger reagent, other factors being equal.
- **Nutrition:** A diet deficient in [[Factor H]] (McCay et al. 1927) made trout far more sensitive. In one trial, two troughs of ten-month-old [[Rainbow Trout]] (200 fish each) were treated at 1:100,000 and 40°F for one hour. The liver-fed lot had no mortality. The lot fed only canned fish, which earlier trials had found deficient in Factor H, lost 6 fish during treatment and 19 the next morning.
- **Water flow in trough:** A trough of 20,000 one-inch trout could often be reduced to 5 to 8 gallons per minute for one hour without endangering the fish.

## Key Findings

### Measuring water flow
Three methods were described:
- **Procedure A:** timed change in water depth in a plugged trough (cubic inches per minute, converted to gallons using the 231 cubic-inch gallon).
- **Procedure B:** a 90-degree [[Triangular Weir]] with depth read by spirit level and square. Table I gives gallons per minute for weir depths of 1 to 5 inches. The authors state the error should not exceed 2 per cent.
- **Procedure C:** timed collection of outflow (30 seconds to one minute) in a galvanized pan measured with a graduate.

### Operating the device
Solutions are made from the tables and, because hatchery balances are too coarse, weighed on a laboratory balance sensitive to about 0.1 gram. The distributing cocks are set by collecting outflow in a 100 cc graduate for half a minute, with checks at 3 and 15 minutes. The trough level is lowered to bring fish into contact with the reagent, then raised once the water is well colored. Treatment lasts one hour.

### Recommended concentrations
- **[[Potassium Permanganate]] (Table II):**
  - Eggs (green or eyed): 1:100,000 below 45°F; 1:125,000 at 46–52°F.
  - Trout 1–2 inches: 1:150,000 below 45°F; 1:175,000 at 46–55°F.
  - Trout 3 inches and longer: 1:75,000 below 38°F, up to 1:200,000 above 65°F.
  - To eliminate the fin worm *Gyrodactylus*, 1:150,000 to 1:100,000 is advised. Weaker solutions (1:175,000 to 1:250,000) suffice for other diseases.
  - Preventive weekly treatment at 1:200,000 below 55°F is suggested for small fish in infected water.
- **[[Copper Sulphate]] (Table IV):** 1:50,000 below 45°F, 1:75,000 at 46–55°F, 1:100,000 above 55°F. Copper sulphate is recommended for gill bacteria and fin rot, but concentrations that kill *Gyrodactylus* and *Dactylogyrus* are harmful to fish.
- **[[Formaldehyde]] (Table V, 40 per cent solution):** 1:2,000 at 55°F, 1:2,500 at 55–70°F, 1:3,000 above 70°F. It is especially effective against *Gyrodactylus*, *Dactylogyrus* and protozoa, and trout tolerate it better than permanganate or copper sulphate.

### Worked example
For a trough of four-inch trout at 53°F with flow reduced to 8.2 gallons per minute, the authors recommend 1:150,000 permanganate. This requires 12.45 grams of potassium permanganate dissolved in 5,904 cc of water, delivered at 98.4 cc per minute.

### Diseases controlled
- **Effective:** external flukes ([[Gyrodactylus]], [[Dactylogyrus]] on goldfish); the protozoa [[Chilodon]] and *Cyclochaeta*; bacterial fin rot and gill disease; fungus on eggs and fish.
- **Partly effective:** [[Ichthyophthirius]] is not controlled as thoroughly as with a [[Salt Bath]]. [[Costia]] was not tested directly, but repeated treatments at about three-day intervals are suggested.
- **Not effective:** internal diseases such as furunculosis and *Octomitiasis*.

### Cost of treatment
Table VI gives the chemical cost of treating 20,000 one-inch trout:

| Treatment | Cost (as printed) |
|---|---|
| Salt bath, 2½% solution in trough | $.08 |
| Potassium permanganate 1:100,000, 10 gal/min | $.0251 |
| Copper sulphate 1:75,000, 10 gal/min | $.0134 |
| Formaldehyde 1:2,500, 10 gal/min | $.43 (OCR reading) |

Prices quoted were ¾ cent per pound of salt, 50 cents per pound of powdered permanganate, 20 cents per pound of powdered copper sulphate and 44 cents per quart of 40 per cent formaldehyde. The authors argue that the eradicator cuts labor compared with the dip method, because it avoids repeated dipping, handling fish and sterilizing ponds.

### Stated advantages (two-hatchery trial, 1932)
1. More thorough control, since fish face a constant reagent concentration throughout treatment.
2. No handling of fish, and the trough or pond is sterilized during treatment.
3. Several troughs can be treated with one apparatus by adjusting the cocks.
4. Large savings in time and labor.
5. Eggs at any stage and sac fry can be treated without being disturbed.
6. Permanganate treatment controls fin and gill worms.
