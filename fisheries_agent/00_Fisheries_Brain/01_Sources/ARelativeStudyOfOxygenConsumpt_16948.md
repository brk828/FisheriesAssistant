## Executive Summary
This study, "A Relative Study of Oxygen Consumption in Five Species of Fish," employed a sealed-vessel methodology to comparatively measure oxygen consumption rates across five Kentucky fish species: [[Lepomis Cyanellus]] (Green Sunfish), [[Gambusia Affinis]] (Mosquitofish), [[Carpiodes Carpio]] (River Carpsucker), [[Fundulus Notatus]] (Blackstripe Topminnow), and [[Chaenobryttus Gulosus]] (Warmouth). Fish were sealed in 400cc beakers of aquarium water for 8-hour periods at two temperatures (22°C and 6.3°C), with dissolved oxygen measured via a modified [[Micro Winkler Method]] before and after using a Bausch and Lomb Spectronic 20 Colorimeter. Results demonstrated that oxygen consumption was strongly temperature-dependent (all species consumed markedly less O₂ in cold water) and highly species-specific, correlating with relative activity levels and body size. The Green Sunfish exhibited the highest metabolic rate, while the Blackstripe Topminnow showed the lowest.

## Background & Description
This study did not focus on detailed morphological description but recorded body mass as a critical variable for normalizing metabolic data (see Weight Determination Chart below). Fish were juvenile/small-bodied specimens relative to typical adult sizes for their species, a factor specifically noted as a possible driver of elevated mass-specific metabolic rates in [[Lepomis Cyanellus]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The five species represent a range of ecological strategies and habitat tolerances within Kentucky freshwater systems:
- **[[Lepomis Cyanellus]]**: An active [[Centrarchidae|centrarchid]] noted for high metabolic demand, possibly linked to its small size relative to adult body plan.
- **[[Gambusia Affinis]]**: Described as a "highly active fish," consistent with its high oxygen consumption and known ecological role as a surface-dwelling, high-energy forager.
- **[[Carpiodes Carpio]]**: Noted as a species "often found in polluted and low oxygenated waters," consistent with its comparatively low metabolic rate and tolerance for hypoxic conditions.
- **[[Fundulus Notatus]]**: Exhibited the lowest oxygen consumption, attributed to its characteristically low activity levels.
- **[[Chaenobryttus Gulosus]]**: A moderately active centrarchid with metabolic demand between the sunfish and topminnow extremes.

Activity level was directly assessed via spot-checks (see Activity Chart), correlating behavioral activity with measured [[Oxygen Consumption]] rates across both thermal regimes.

### Temperature Effect
- All five species showed **reduced oxygen consumption** in cold water (6.3°C) compared to warm water (22°C), consistent with expected poikilothermic metabolic depression.
- A natural rise in dissolved oxygen was observed in control beakers in both trials due to temperature drops (22°C→20°C in trial 1; deliberate cooling to 6.3°C in trial 2), attributed to increased O₂ solubility at lower temperatures, not biological activity.

### Methodological Limitations (per Discussion)
- The **aluminum foil seal** was identified as inadequate — difficulty maintaining a total fill without breaking the meniscus allowed air entry, introducing potential error.
- The **closed (sealed vessel) method** does not account for the buildup of CO₂ and nitrogenous waste during the trial, a limitation previously noted by [[Keys 1930]].
- Confounding variables include **light effects** and **natural activity fluctuations**, as discussed in [[Keys 1930]] and [[Clausen 1936]].
- Oxygen consumption rate was **assumed constant** over the 8-hour period for graphing purposes, following the approach of [[Wells 1932]], though this may not reflect true metabolic dynamics.

### Conclusion
Mass-specific oxygen consumption was inversely related to body size trends and directly related to observed activity level. Small, highly active fish ([[Lepomis Cyanellus]], [[Gambusia Affinis]]) exhibited the highest metabolic rates, while sedentary or hypoxia-tolerant species ([[Fundulus Notatus]], [[Carpiodes Carpio]]) showed the lowest. These findings support using relative oxygen consumption as a proxy for correlating species-specific metabolic and ecological oxygen requirements in future studies.
