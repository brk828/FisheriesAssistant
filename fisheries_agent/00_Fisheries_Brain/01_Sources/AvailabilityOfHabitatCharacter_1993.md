## Executive Summary

This FY93 progress report from the Colorado River Recovery Program (Project 18-7) investigates the importance of seasonally flooded bottomland habitats to the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the Green River, Utah. The study combines controlled predation/competition experiments, radio telemetry of subadult and adult razorback suckers, and habitat-use monitoring in [[Old Charley Wash]] and the broader Ouray area. Key findings include catastrophic predation of larval and juvenile razorback sucker by nonindigenous [[Red Shiner]], evidence of avian predation on radio-tagged subadults released into wetlands, and telemetry patterns suggesting a possible discrete, locally spawning population of razorback sucker in the middle Green River near the Duchesne River confluence. The research is intended to inform instream flow recommendations and floodplain habitat restoration for these endangered [[Cyprinidae]] and [[Catostomidae]] species.

## Background & Description

- **Larval razorback sucker**: mean mass of 0.0062 g/fish at the start of the predation experiment (10 June 1993).
- **Juvenile razorback sucker**: mean mass of 0.092 g/fish at the start of the second experiment (28 July 1993).
- **Subadult razorback sucker**: total length range of 178–254 mm; large enough to surgically implant radio transmitters.
- **Adult razorback sucker**: size not explicitly given, but large enough to carry radio transmitters and undertake long-distance movements (implanted individuals sourced from [[Razorback Bar]], [[Echo Park]], and [[Island Park]]).

No detailed external morphological descriptions (fin counts, coloration, etc.) are provided in this project report; the focus is on life-stage size classes relevant to survival and telemetry experiments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Seasonally flooded bottomlands/backwaters (e.g., [[Old Charley Wash]]), used as suspected nursery habitat for larval and juvenile razorback sucker, and as potential postspawning adult habitat.
- **Depth preference**: Subadults tended to use the deepest areas of flooded bottomlands; adults selected run and eddy habitats typically deeper than 2 m in the main river channel.
- **Movement patterns**:
  - Adult razorback suckers implanted with transmitters moved downstream for 2–3 weeks immediately post-implantation (possibly a stress response).
  - No adults used flooded bottomlands during the spring high-water period post-implantation.
  - Fish implanted at Razorback Bar tended to move upstream; fish implanted above [[Split Mountain Canyon]] tended to move downstream.
  - No adults traveled downstream of River Mile 270 (RM 270), suggesting spatial fidelity to a localized reach.
- **Interspecific interactions**: Strong evidence of predation/competition pressure from nonindigenous [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] on larval/juvenile razorback sucker within shared cage enclosures — red shiners rapidly and near-completely consumed razorback sucker larvae and juveniles.
- **Predation risk**: Avian predation (implicated cormorant predation) appears to be a significant mortality factor for subadult razorback sucker stocked into natural wetland habitats.
- **Population structure hypothesis**: Fish captured in spawning condition at the mouth of the [[Duchesne River]] and later recaptured at the [[Escalante Razorback Bar]] area suggest the existence of a distinct, locally spawning population within the middle Green River, separate from previously documented populations.

## Key Findings

1. **Red shiner predation is severe and rapid**: In the larval experiment (three replicate cages of 250 razorback sucker + 250 red shiners), all razorback sucker larvae were consumed by red shiners within 45 minutes of exposure.
2. **Juvenile razorback sucker also highly vulnerable**: In a follow-up juvenile experiment (300 and 150 fish treatments, 28 July–26 August 1993), only one razorback sucker survived across all three sympatric cages, while over 50% of red shiners survived — indicating juveniles remain highly susceptible to nonindigenous competitor/predator pressure even at larger size.
3. **Avian predation threatens stocked subadults**: Of five radio-tagged subadult razorback sucker released into Old Charley Wash, only two could be relocated by late August; telemetry indicated at least one transmitter near a cormorant rookery, implicating avian predation as a major mortality source.
4. **Subadults favor deep bottomland habitat**: Located subadults tended to use the deepest available areas within the flooded bottomland.
5. **Adults avoided flooded bottomlands post-spawn (or post-implant)**: None of the seven radio-tagged adults used flooded bottomland habitat during the spring high-water period following transmitter implantation, though this may reflect surgical/handling stress rather than natural behavior.
6. **Adults preferentially use deep run/eddy habitat**: Tagged adults generally occupied river runs and eddies deeper than 2 m and did not travel great distances from release/capture sites.
7. **Possible distinct spawning population identified**: Directional movement patterns (upstream near Razorback Bar, downstream near Split Mountain Canyon) combined with a strict downstream range limit (no fish below RM 270) and recapture linkages between the Duchesne River mouth and Escalante Razorback Bar suggest a previously unrecognized, locally spawning population of razorback sucker in the middle Green River.
8. **Programmatic recommendation**: The report recommends expanding radio telemetry efforts on adult razorback sucker to clarify spawning site fidelity, movement triggers, and postspawning habitat selection, integrating this work with ongoing [[Old Charley Wash]] habitat studies.
9. **Budget**: FY93 project funded and fully expended at $37,000, within a multi-year study (FY1991–FY1994) under the [[Colorado River Recovery Program]].
