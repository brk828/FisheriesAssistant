# Ecological and Zoogeographic Relationships of Amphibians and Reptiles of the Cuatro Ciénegas Basin

#herpetology #zoogeography #biogeography #amphibians-reptiles #chihuahuan-desert

## Executive Summary

This 1984 journal article by C. J. McCoy analyzes the ecological and geographic relationships of the amphibian and reptile fauna of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], an arid, closed-drainage valley in Coahuila, México. The native herpetofauna comprises 66 species, plus two introduced species. Although the fauna superficially resembles a typical [[Chihuahuan Desert]] assemblage, the basin's persistent aquatic habitats and position on a major faunal boundary add a rich aquatic and semiaquatic element and a substantial number of relict and endemic forms. The author describes the analysis as provisional, because the fauna is based on an incomplete sample of a complex and elusive fauna.

## Background & Description

The basin is roughly 30 by 40 km, with a floor at about 740 m elevation and surrounding mountains near or above 2,500 m. Annual precipitation averages less than 200 mm, and summer daytime temperatures may exceed 44 °C. Major springs arise on the basin floor and feed a hydrologic system ranging from flowing rivers to ponds, lakes and marshes. These aquatic habitats are considered of great antiquity and permanence.

Herpetological exploration began with E. G. Marsh, Jr., in 1939. Schmidt and Owens (1944) reported on his collections, which included the type series of the aquatic box turtle [[Terrapene Coahuila]]. Intensive study resumed in 1958 with a University of Kansas field party, led in later work by [[W. L. Minckley]], whose interests centered on fishes. The author's own studies began with two expeditions in 1964. The basin's herpetological literature includes nearly 100 scientific papers and articles (Appendix 1). Despite thousands of specimens, some species are known from only a handful of records.

**Scope and definitions:** The analysis covers species occurring naturally below 1,000 m in the Cuatro Ciénegas Basin and the adjacent [[Sacramento Basin]]. Montane species above 1,000 m, such as *Sceloporus grammicus*, *S. ornatus* and *Gerrhonotus liocephalus*, were excluded as montane relicts. Two species are introduced: [[Hemidactylus Turcicus]] (well established in the town of Cuatro Ciénegas de Carranza) and [[Bufo Marinus]] (reported only from the Marsh collection of 1939, not taken since). *Drymarchon corais* is tentatively included on the basis of a single specimen and may also be an introduction. *Gopherus berlandieri* near town is regarded as escaped pets.

**Classification:** Species were assigned to four ecological categories based on modal habitat in the basin: Desert, Riparian, Semiaquatic and Aquatic. Geographic affinities were assigned to three categories: [[Chihuahuan Fauna]], [[Tamaulipan Fauna]] and ubiquitous. The Chihuahuan category has four subcategories (basin endemics with Chihuahuan affinities, Chihuahuan Desert endemics, Chihuahuan Desert/Great Plains species, and widespread desert/plains species). The Tamaulipan category has three (basin endemics, [[Gulf Edge Species]], and species that extend across the Great Plains to the desert edge).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Ecological categories (Table 2 totals):**

| Category | Species | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Desert | 41 | 62% |
| Riparian | 13 | 20% |
| Semiaquatic | 6 | 9% |
| Aquatic | 6 | 9% |

- **Desert species** include 6 of 8 amphibians (including [[Scaphiopus Couchi]] and *Hylactophryne augusti*), 58% of snakes and about 74% of lizards.
- **Riparian species** are limited to watercourses or areas of high subsurface water, though they are not dependent on aquatic habitat. Examples include the skinks *Eumeces obsoletus* and *E. tetragrammus*, the snakes *Diadophis punctatus* and *Micrurus fulvius*, and [[Bufo Valliceps]], the only riparian amphibian.
- **Semiaquatic species** live in marshes, sedge mats and similar habitats. Examples are *Scincella lateralis*, *Thamnophis proximus* and Terrapene coahuila. Placement of *Sistrurus catenatus* here rests on scanty evidence.
- **Aquatic species** require permanent standing or flowing water. They include the Rana sp. (pipiens group), the turtles [[Trionyx Ater]], *T. spiniferus* and [[Pseudemys Scripta Taylori]] (*P. scripta*), and the water snakes [[Nerodia Erythrogaster]] and *N. rhombifera*. No lizards are aquatic.

The author concludes that the aquatic and semiaquatic habitats enhance herpetofaunal diversity by roughly one third. Overall, the fauna is about 60% desert species and 40% aquatic, semiaquatic and riparian species.

**Geographic affinities (Table 3):**

- **[[Chihuahuan Fauna]]:** 42 species (64%). Most are wide-ranging and not habitat-limited, so endemism is rare. Only two forms are endemic to the basin: *Gerrhonotus lugoi* and *Cnemidophorus scalaris pallidus*. Ten species are Chihuahuan Desert endemics, and five range across the Chihuahuan Desert and western Great Plains.
- **[[Tamaulipan Fauna]]:** 18 species (27%), including 4 basin endemics (*Trionyx ater*, *Terrapene coahuila*, *Pseudemys scripta taylori*, *Scincella lateralis* ssp.). Five species (*Bufo valliceps*, *Eumeces tetragrammus*, *Cnemidophorus gularis*, *Drymarchon corais*, *Micrurus fulvius*) reach the basin via the Rio Salado drainage. Nine are more widespread east of the Great Plains and occur as mesic-adapted relict isolates at the desert edge.
- **Ubiquitous species:** 6 species (9%), with coast-to-coast distributions in the United States extending onto the Mexican Plateau.

**Faunal boundary:** The basin lies at the extreme eastern edge of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] Province. An abrupt topographic boundary produces a sharp transition with the Tamaulipan zone, so that 16 Chihuahuan species (38%) reach their eastern limit at the basin edge, and 14 Tamaulipan species (77%) reach their western limit there. Only two aquatic or semiaquatic forms (*Nerodia erythrogaster* and *Rana* sp.) extend farther west into the Chihuahuan Desert. The boundary is illustrated by the paired species *Sceloporus olivaceus*/*S. magister* and [[Cnemidophorus Gularis]]/[[Cnemidophorus Scalaris]], where secondary hybridization occurs between the latter two (Walker 1981).

## Key Findings

1. **Fauna composition:** 66 native species (8 anurans, 4 turtles, 23 lizards, 31 snakes) and 2 introduced species, with a further tentative record (*Drymarchon corais*). The author notes that 68 species have been collected within the study area.
2. **Dominant ecological element:** Desert species make up 62% of the native fauna, and aquatic, semiaquatic and riparian species together make up about 40%.
3. **Dominant geographic element:** Chihuahuan affinities account for 64% of species, while Tamaulipan species make up 27%, including most basin endemics.
4. **Relict populations:** The fauna includes a high proportion of relict populations (39%, per the abstract and summary). About 10% of species (5 species, almost 10% of the total) are differentiated at some taxonomically recognizable level.
5. **Patterns of differentiation:** These form a "mosaic of differentiation" that reflects successive faunal invasions over a long history:
   - *Terrapene coahuila* is highly divergent, and its phylogenetic relationships remain controversial.
   - *Trionyx ater* hybridizes freely with invading *T. spiniferus* in parts of the basin.
   - *Pseudemys scripta taylori* intergrades with *P. s. elegans* in a narrow zone beginning at Puerto Salado.
   - Conant (1969) noted "some suggestions of local endemism" in basin populations of *Nerodia erythrogaster*.
   - Other relict populations, such as *Eumeces tetragrammus*, *Coluber constrictor*, *Micrurus fulvius* and *Sistrurus catenatus*, appear undifferentiated.
6. **Diversity drivers:** Two factors account for the basin's remarkable diversity: the presence of an extensive aquatic and semiaquatic fauna, and the overlap of Chihuahuan and Tamaulipan species at a sharp faunal boundary.
7. **Research needs:** The author states that reconstructing the history of these faunal invasions requires comparative studies of genetic divergence among relict populations, which had not yet been carried out.
8. **Provisional status:** Because some species are known from few specimens and others likely remain undiscovered, the analysis is explicitly provisional.
