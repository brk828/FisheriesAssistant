# Native Fish Monitoring of the Little Colorado River, Spring 2005

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service trip report (Document No. USFWS-AZFRO-FL-05-007, May 2005, prepared for the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring And Research Center]]) documents two spring 2005 sampling trips on the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), 29 March to 8 April and 26 April to 6 May. The primary goal was a [[Mark-Recapture]] estimate of the abundance of [[Humpback Chub]] over 150 mm total length (TL) in the lower 13.6 km of the river. Secondary goals were to monitor other native and nonnative fish, using [[Hoopnet Sampling]] at three camps.

Humpback chub catches were low, particularly for fish under 200 mm. The authors attribute this mainly to strong flooding in fall 2004 and winter 2005, which likely flushed young fish into the mainstem [[Colorado River]]. Final abundance estimates were deferred to an annual report.

## Background & Description

The trips were run by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, SWCA Inc., and volunteers. Three camps were established by distance upstream of the LCR confluence:

- **Salt Camp:** 10.4 rkm
- **Coyote Camp:** 5.0 rkm (relocated for the May trip to provide shade)
- **Boulders Camp:** 1.9 rkm

Each camp fished 60 hoopnets (0.5–0.6 m diameter, 1.0 m length, 6 mm mesh, single throat) along shoreline habitat. Nets were checked every 24 hours and fished for three days and nights in each of three sub-reaches. Standard data collected included:

- Total length, fork length, sex, and sexual condition of [[Humpback Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and [[Bluehead Sucker]]
- Counts of the external parasite [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] on fish
- Total length of all other captured fish
- Stomach contents of large nonnative fish (ictalurids and trout)
- [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging]] of humpback chub and carp over 150 mm TL, and other native fish over 150 mm TL (except [[Speckled Dace]])

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical conditions.** Water temperature and [[Turbidity]] were measured daily at Salt Camp. Flow data came from the USGS Cameron gage (station 09402000), which excludes the spring-fed baseflow of the LCR.

- **April trip:** The river was in moderate flood, with flows roughly 100 to 600 cfs above base flow. This was attributed to spring 2005 snowmelt plus storms in fall 2004. Turbidity peaked at 3,452 NTU on the first day and fell to 967 NTU by the last day. Afternoon water temperatures ranged from 12.6 to 15.9 °C (mean 13.8 °C).
- **May trip:** Flooding continued. Turbidity peaked at roughly 19,470 NTU (partly illegible in the scan) and declined to 1,650 NTU by the last fishing day. Temperatures ranged from 15.7 to 18.5 °C (mean 17.2 °C).

**Effort and CPUE.** Each camp set 180 nets per trip. Net-hours were 12,638 for April and 12,714 for May, with 25,352 net-hours overall. Humpback chub [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] was higher in May (0.030 fish per net-hour) than April (0.016), possibly because of warmer water.

**Nonnative species.** [[Black Bullhead]] was the most abundant nonnative fish on both trips. Nonnatives made up 1.7% of catch in April and 2.5% in May.

## Key Findings

**Catch totals**
- 891 fish were captured across both trips.
- The dominant native species were [[Humpback Chub]] (576 fish) and [[Speckled Dace]] (130 fish).
- Humpback chub captures were 198 in April and 378 in May. The May increase came largely from age-0 fish and the 150–200 mm class.
- April yielded 24 unique humpback chub over 150 mm. May yielded 217 unique fish, and 10 of these were recaptures (five of them over 200 mm).

**Length structure and cohorts**
- The 2005 length distribution had a markedly smaller proportion of fish under 200 mm than the previous four years (2001–2004).
- Expected cohort progression was weak. The strong 2003 age-0 cohort barely registered in 2005, and a 2004 peak was not seen as expected.
- The authors state that cohort movement was difficult to detect because of low catches.
- Boulders and Coyote reaches had higher proportions of fish under roughly 175 mm.

**Other native catostomids**
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]: 38 captured in April and 30 in May, mostly from the Boulders reach. Two presumed age-0 fish were caught in May.
- [[Bluehead Sucker]]: 53 captured in April and 43 in May. Catches were mostly adults (150–300 mm), with ten age-0 fish and no strong cohort.

**Reproductive condition**
- April: 16 ripe humpback chub (14 male, 2 female), 7 ripe flannelmouth sucker (6 male, 1 female), and 31 ripe bluehead sucker (18 male, 13 female).
- May: 29 ripe humpback chub (28 male, 1 female), 1 ripe male flannelmouth sucker, and 14 ripe bluehead sucker (12 male, 2 female).

**Predation and parasites**
- Stomach contents from ten black bullheads were examined. Two contained speckled dace (48 and 50 mm prey), and two contained insect remains.
- No direct predation on humpback chub was detected, but one 282 mm humpback chub had a presumed catfish bite mark.
- No trout or catfish were captured.
- Lernaea cyprinacea infected 12 humpback chub in April (6% of captures, 1–4 parasites per fish) and 19 in May (5.0%, 1–2 per fish). No other species carried the parasite.

**Interpretation**
- The authors suggest the low catches were driven mainly by flood-related displacement of young fish. Turbidity and cool water (April mean 13.8 °C, compared with 19 °C in April 2004 and 19 °C in April 2003) may have contributed but do not fully explain the shortfall.
- Flooding appeared to reduce some nonnative species. Red shiner, plains killifish, and trout were absent, and only two fathead minnows were captured. Black bullhead and common carp persisted, suggesting greater flood tolerance.

**Data management**
- Data were delivered to the Grand Canyon Monitoring And Research Center as six Microsoft Access files, one for each camp and trip.
