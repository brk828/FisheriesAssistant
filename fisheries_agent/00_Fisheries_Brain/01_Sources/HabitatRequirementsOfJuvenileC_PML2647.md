# Habitat Requirements of Juvenile Colorado River Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 Fish and Wildlife Service report, prepared by Paul B. Holden of BIO/WEST, Inc. under an interagency agreement with the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, examines the habitat use, movements and spawning areas of rare fishes in the Green River, Utah. The study focused on young [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), an endangered species, and also addressed [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Sampling ran from Jensen to Green River between August and September 1977. The study found that young squawfish were concentrated in low-current backwaters, that spawning apparently occurred only in the lower study area in 1977, and that backwaters are a key habitat for both rare and introduced fishes.

## Background & Description

The upper Colorado River basin contains two fishes listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service: the Colorado Squawfish and the Humpback Chub. The Bonytail Chub and Razorback Sucker were also rare and under consideration for endangered and threatened status. The report identifies two factors as central to self-sustaining populations: adequate spawning and rearing habitat, and interactions with exotic fishes.

The [[Green River]] reaches studied were the least altered large-river habitat in the upper basin with reproducing squawfish populations. Sampling was divided into two segments: Jensen to Sand Wash (August 5–20, 1977) and Sand Wash to Green River, through Desolation and Gray canyons (September 7–26, 1977). Habitats were classified as backwaters, eddies, runs and riffles. Velocity was measured with a [[Marsh-McBirney Current Meter]], and fish were collected by seining, electrofishing and a fry shovel. Preferred habitat was expressed as [[Electivity Curves]] within an [[Instream Flow Methodology]] developed by the Cooperative Instream Flow Service Group.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow and temperature:** Flows were lower than normal in 1977. Daily Flaming Gorge Dam releases caused a 16-inch daily fluctuation at Jensen but only 3–4 inches in Desolation Canyon. Morning temperatures at Jensen in August were usually 15–17 °C; backwaters warmed 3–8 degrees above the main river in late afternoon. September temperatures in Desolation Canyon fell to a low of 10.5 °C.
- **Turbidity:** Turbidity was highest after rainstorms, reflecting the highly erodible watershed. The report notes native fishes are adapted to sudden turbidity changes.
- **Water chemistry:** Dissolved oxygen, pH, ammonia, nitrate, nitrite, sulphate, alkalinity, conductivity and salinity changed little during the study and remained within normal ranges.
- **Fish community:** Nineteen species were collected, totaling over 19,000 specimens. The introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) was by far the most abundant fish; the Flannelmouth Sucker was the most abundant native species. Introduced species such as [[Carp]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Bullhead]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] were present, and the Walleye was recorded as occasional.

## Key Findings

**Objectives**
1. Determine physical and chemical habitat parameters for all sizes of rare fishes, with emphasis on young fish.
2. Determine whether spawning occurs and where.
3. Determine the movements of size (age) classes.
4. Identify fish species associated with the study area.
5. Assess the relative importance of river habitats.

**Habitat preferences**
- Young-of-the-year squawfish (30–64 mm) were found only in backwaters with no current, a firm silt bottom and a depth of one to two feet.
- Juvenile squawfish (65–200 mm) also preferred backwaters with no current and silt bottoms, but were less selective of depth.
- Subadult and adult squawfish (200+ mm) used a range of velocities and depths but preferred sandy-bottomed eddies and runs over backwaters.
- No size class of squawfish showed a temperature preference.
- Six young-of-the-year [[Razorback Sucker]]s preferred eddies with about 0.2 fps velocity, 1.0 foot depth and soft silt.
- Young-of-the-year humpback chub preferred backwaters with no current and a firm silt bottom, but also used eddies and runs at 0.4–0.8 fps. Adults used eddies and runs with sand bottoms.

**Distribution and abundance**
- Over 330 samples were taken: 125 in runs, 121 in backwaters, 59 in eddies and 19 in riffles.
- Young-of-the-year squawfish were found only in the lower study area.
- Only one adult bonytail chub was collected in the upper study area; a juvenile chub from Desolation Canyon was tentatively identified as a bonytail.
- The chubs of Desolation and Gray canyons were tentatively referred to as humpback chub, though some characteristics suggested roundtail chub.
- The first young razorback suckers reported from the upper Colorado Basin were tentatively identified (six specimens, middle study area).

**Spawning**
- Squawfish spawning in 1977 apparently occurred only in Gray Canyon, below Range Creek.
- Young humpback chub indicated spawning in Desolation and Gray canyons.
- Young razorbacks indicated spawning near and just below Ouray, Utah.
- Low water likely limited spawning in the upper study area. Early August temperatures near Jensen (15–20 °C) were below the 21 °C threshold for squawfish spawning.

**Movement and marking**
- Twenty juvenile squawfish were tagged, 13 more were anal-fin clipped, and four subadults were tagged. One tagged juvenile was recaptured 4.5 hours later at the release site.
- In one backwater, 4, 31, 29, 11 and 12 young squawfish were captured across five seine samples; marked fish were rarely recaptured, suggesting movement within the backwater.
- Partial anal fin clipping was judged a better marking technique than Bismarck brown dye, which caused stress in test fish.

**Fish associations and competition**
- Young-of-the-year squawfish were most often found with red shiners, fathead minnows, juvenile carp and young channel catfish.
- Juvenile squawfish were associated mainly with native species (red shiners, roundtail chub, young flannelmouth and bluehead suckers).
- Exotic species dominated young-of-the-year squawfish and humpback chub collections, indicating potential competition and predation.

**Backwater ecology and management**
- Backwaters are nutrient-rich and warm, supporting crustaceans and small insects that young squawfish feed on. The report hypothesizes that backwater abundance may be a limiting factor for young squawfish.
- Short-lived backwaters (changing with a 6-inch level shift) may suffice for young squawfish. Long-lived backwaters favored exotic sunfish, bullheads and fatheads, so artificial construction of long-lived backwaters could increase exotic populations.
- Water pump intakes should avoid slow-water areas, especially backwaters, and be placed in swift runs.

**Reliability and limitations**
- Under Cooperative Instream Flow Service Group criteria, young-of-the-year squawfish and humpback chub curves rated "good," and the others "fair." No curve rated "excellent."
- The author recommended several years of data to improve reliability for juvenile and adult fish.
