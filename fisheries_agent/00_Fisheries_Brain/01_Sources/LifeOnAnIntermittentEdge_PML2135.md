# Life on an Intermittent Edge

#arachnology #population-ecology #orb-weaving-spiders

## Executive Summary

This Trends in Ecology & Evolution essay by [[Stuart Pimm]] examines why some species are rare, how year-to-year population variability relates to [[Extinction Rate|extinction risk]], and why the usual expectation (more variable populations go extinct faster) does not hold in simple form. Pimm reviews a study by [[Schoener And Spiller]] on orb-weaving spiders on small islands in the central Bahamas. The study found that, across most measures, higher variability was associated with lower extinction rates, and that very small populations are constrained to low variability by rapid extinction. Pimm endorses the study's careful analysis of imperfect natural data and closes with an open question about whether a population of fixed mean size is better off with normal variability or with many independent, highly variable subpopulations.

## Background & Description

The essay opens with the point that rarity comes before extinction. Pimm distinguishes three sources of rarity: species that are born rare, species whose rarity is imposed by human action or neglect, and species that become intermittently rare because of their own life histories. Population sizes vary widely between years. In some species the maximum over a 20-year period is only twice the minimum, while in others numbers can change 100-fold from one year to the next.

Pimm outlines the mechanisms that make rarity dangerous:

- **Small populations:** Few young per generation may mean that few individuals find a mate, let alone an unrelated one.
- **Demographic chance:** In the smallest populations, isolated accidental deaths can cause extinction quickly and unpredictably.
- **Environmental catastrophe:** A single event can kill many individuals at once, creating an extra risk for modestly common but geographically restricted populations.

Pimm then compares body size across animals. Smaller-bodied species are more abundant, have more variable populations, recover faster from low numbers, and are more prone to extinction at the lowest sizes. Because these traits pull in opposite directions, greater variability does not necessarily mean greater extinction risk when other factors are not held equal.

Pimm also argues that the first two moments of a population's distribution over time, abundance and variance, are not enough. Skewness (the third moment) and kurtosis (the fourth) may also matter. A right-skewed population that is usually rare but occasionally abundant may be less vulnerable than a common, left-skewed population whose rare low points are still fatal. Calculations of skewness are rare in the literature, and Pimm cites only two studies that attempt them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study system was a set of tiny islands in the [[Bahamas]], where orb-weaving spiders of the species [[Argiope Argentata]] were counted. Pimm notes that small islands and conspicuous webs made complete counts feasible and likely accurate. Key details from the study, as reported in the essay:

- **Islands:** 106 islands, all small (the largest under one hectare).
- **Taxa:** Seven spider species were common enough to analyze, and not all occurred in sufficient numbers on every island.
- **Sample units:** 174 island-species combinations.
- **Duration:** Nine years of observation.

Pimm points out several measurement problems. Variability can be estimated from all years, including zeros, or only from years when the species is present. For example, a nine-year census of 0,0,0,0,1,1,0,0,0 gives a [[Coefficient Of Variation]] of nearly 200%, while the presence-only years give 0%. Another common measure, the standard deviation of log population sizes (SDL), cannot take logarithms of zeros unless one is added. Combining these choices yields seven variability estimates, which produce 35 correlations with extinction rate across all species and the four most common species.

Comparisons with birds and other animals provide context. Spider populations have SDLs of about 0.5 to 1.0, a range typical of animals of similar size. Birds are much less variable from year to year than spiders. Pimm also notes that the spider and bird comparison is informative because it contrasts two very different levels of year-to-year fluctuation.

## Key Findings

- **Variability and extinction are not simply linked.** Of the 35 correlations between [[Extinction Rate]] and [[Population Variability]], only seven were positive. Most indicated that higher variability went with lower extinction rates, and 24 were significantly negative.
- **Methods change the answer.** Pimm stresses that no single variability measure has statistical primacy. Results depend on choices such as using zeros or not, using CV or SDL, and adding one to counts.
- **Small populations are constrained.** At or below about ten individuals, 23 of 30 populations went extinct within two years. High extinction rates restrict variability, and this restriction grows stronger at lower densities. Plots of SDL against mean log abundance must fall below the limit set by populations that persist only two years (see Figure 1).
- **A threshold of about eight individuals.** Schoener and Spiller estimated that variability stops increasing with population size at about eight individuals (roughly five on the log scale used in the figure). Below this threshold, immigration and rapid extinction dominate. Above it, birth and death processes set variability.
- **Above the threshold, the usual pattern holds.** For populations averaging eight or more, increasing variability accompanies increasing extinction risk, in line with conventional wisdom and earlier bird results.
- **Spiders went extinct more readily than birds.** Among spider populations averaging more than ten, six of 12 became extinct within the nine years of study, three of them averaging over 25 individuals. Among 41 bird populations averaging more than ten, only three became extinct over periods of more than ten years, and none with more than 20 individuals did.
- **Open question on strategy.** Pimm asks whether a population of mean size n persists longer when it varies normally (SDL about 0.5 to 1.0) than when it is split into m independent subpopulations, each of mean size n, varying at the greatest possible rate. He frames this as a choice between living dangerously but independently and living less dangerously but contingently.
- **Pimm's assessment of the study.** He describes the paper as dense and laborious but finds its arguments that the decline in extinction with variability is "real" convincing. He values it as a careful way to work through relationships in data that cannot be obtained by experiment, because the questions concern spatial, temporal and organizational scales too large for conventional experiments.
