# Additions to the Known Kansas Fish Fauna

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #fundulidae #percidae #leuciscidae #faunal-survey

## Executive Summary

This 1963 faunal note by Branley A. Branson reports three new fish species records for the state of Kansas, discovered during a biological survey of the [[Spring River System]] and its major tributary, [[Shoal Creek]]. The additions — **[[Fundulus sciadicus]]** (Plains Topminnow), **[[Etheostoma microperca]]** (Least Darter), and **[[Notropis galacturus]]** (Whitetail Shiner) — raised the total known Kansas fish fauna to 130 species. The paper also flags **[[Fundulus catenatus]]** as an imminent addition pending confirmed capture within Kansas state lines, and compares Arkansas-collected **[[Notropis camurus]]** specimens to regional populations. The findings underscore the disproportionate ichthyological influence of the Ozarkian [[Shoal Creek]] drainage on the overall Kansas fish fauna.

## Background & Description

The data derive from an ongoing biological survey (conducted by the author over roughly two years) of the **Spring River System** spanning Missouri and Kansas. **Shoal Creek**, one of the system's largest tributaries, originates near Exeter, Missouri, and flows approximately 90 miles to its confluence with the Spring River at Lowell, Cherokee County, Kansas. This drainage is characterized as the **only Ozarkian system in the state of Kansas**, and its biogeographic influence is substantial: roughly 62% of all fish species known from Kansas also occur in Shoal Creek, while 14 species (11% of the state total) are known from Kansas *only* via that stream.

**Collection Site (primary):** Clear backwater pools, 6–8 inches deep, heavily overgrown with watercress and choked with decaying leaf litter, located ~2.5 miles southeast of Lowell (Section 21, T34S, R25E), Cherokee County, Kansas, adjacent to swift riffles. Sampled 25 May 1963; a total of 17 species were obtained here, two representing new Kansas records.

### Species Accounts

**1. [[Fundulus sciadicus]] (Plains Topminnow)** — KSC-217
- 34 specimens: 14 females (30.0–54.6 mm SL), 20 males (33.0–51.0 mm SL)
- Voucher specimens (4 females, 5 males) deposited at University of Kansas Museum of Zoology (KU-7444)
- Both sexes possess 14 anal rays
  - Female: anal fin convexly rounded; anterior rays strongly clasped by genital sheath; remaining rays directed ~90° from body
  - Male: anal fin nearly straight-edged due to longer rays directed slightly caudad
- Coloration:
  - Body olivaceous in both sexes
  - Female: all fins dusky-white
  - Male: pectorals dusky-white; pelvics uniformly deep orange; anal fin outer 1/4 orange, basally dusky-white; dorsal outer edge orange; caudal dusky with a caudally-convex orange bar (lower edges directed anteriad)
- This is the first detailed color description recorded for the species
- Breeding observed in progress (young escaping from eggs on collection date); reproduction continues at least through late June
- Regional distribution: collected from multiple Missouri localities in Shoal Creek (most abundant in headwaters), plus northeastern Oklahoma and adjacent Arkansas
- Habitat: exceedingly spotted/patchy distribution range-wide; prefers spring-fed pools and vegetated backwaters
- Note: common name "Plains Topminnow" considered a misnomer, as species is rarely found in typical plains streams

**2. [[Etheostoma microperca]] (Least Darter)** — KSC-216
- 9 adults: 2 tuberculate males (29.6–30.0 mm SL), 7 females (26.0–28.5 mm SL)
- Collected syntopically with *F. sciadicus*
- Voucher specimens (1 male, 2 females) deposited at University of Kansas (KU-7445)
- Exhibits a "very spotted" (patchy) distribution in its western range, attributed to both inadequate collecting effort and the species' stringent habitat requirements

**3. [[Notropis galacturus]] (Whitetail Shiner)** — KU-7448
- Single male specimen, collected 17 November 1962 by Gerald Ulrikson and James Campbell
- Locality: Spring River, just below the low-water dam at Baxter Springs, Cherokee County, Kansas
- Morphometric/meristic data:
  - Pharyngeal teeth: 1,4–4,1
  - Anal rays: 9
  - Lateral-line scales: 42
  - Caudal peduncle depth: 6.5 mm
  - Body depth: 14.0 mm
  - Eye diameter: 3.5 mm
  - Standard length: 67.3 mm
  - Last two interradial membranes bear a black blotch
  - Basal one-third of caudal fin milky white
- Previously, this species' known range approached no closer to Kansas than the main bodies and larger tributaries of the White, King, and Current rivers in Arkansas and Missouri

### Additional Comparative Records

**[[Fundulus catenatus]]** — collected from Shoal Creek just across the Missouri state line (not yet within Kansas). Noted as exceedingly abundant in the White and King river systems (Arkansas) and Elk River (Missouri/Oklahoma, a Neosho River tributary). Predicted to be added to the Kansas fauna soon.

**[[Notropis camurus]]** — two specimens (KSC-509) collected from White River, ~10 miles east of Fayetteville, Arkansas. Noted as morphologically typical of specimens found in the upper reaches of Shoal Creek and in Elk River near Noel, Missouri.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat specificity:** The new Kansas records were concentrated in a microhabitat of shallow (6–8"), clear, watercress-dominated backwater pools with heavy leaf litter accumulation adjacent to swift riffles — illustrating the importance of within-stream habitat heterogeneity for supporting range-edge and disjunct fish populations.
- **Biogeographic significance:** Shoal Creek, as the only Ozarkian drainage in Kansas, acts as a conduit for Ozark-endemic and Ozark-associated fish fauna to reach the western edge of their ranges in Kansas, explaining the state's otherwise anomalous species records.
- **Disjunct/patchy distributions:** Both *F. sciadicus* and *E. microperca* are described as having spotty, discontinuous distributions across their known ranges, likely due to a combination of specialized habitat requirements (vegetated spring-fed pools, stringent water quality) and undersampling.
- **Potential human-mediated dispersal:** The unexpected occurrence of *N. galacturus*, far outside its previously known range (nearest known populations in the White, King, and Current river systems), is attributed either to a bait-bucket introduction or to a natural headwater stream capture/exchange event between the White River and Spring River systems — a hypothesis the author considers supported by other (unspecified) lines of evidence discussed elsewhere in his broader survey work.
- **Breeding phenology:** *F. sciadicus* was documented breeding in late May, with reproduction extending at least to the end of June, providing rare life-history/phenological data for the species.

## Key Findings

- Three fish species were newly documented for the state of Kansas: **[[Fundulus sciadicus]]**, **[[Etheostoma microperca]]**, and **[[Notropis galacturus]]**, bringing the statewide total to **130 known species**.
- **~62%** of all Kansas fish species occur in [[Shoal Creek]]; **14 species (11%)** are known from Kansas exclusively via this Ozarkian tributary.
- First detailed sexually dimorphic color description published for *Fundulus sciadicus*, including breeding coloration.
- Active spawning of *F. sciadicus* documented in situ (25 May 1963), with reproductive activity extending into late June.
- Full meristic/morphometric dataset recorded for the single Kansas *Notropis galacturus* specimen, notable for representing a major range extension (a "highly unexpected find") relative to its previously known White/King/Current river distribution.
- Two additional species — **[[Fundulus catenatus]]** and **[[Notropis camurus]]** — are identified as imminent or comparative additions to the Kansas fauna based on nearby Missouri/Arkansas collections.
- The findings support a hypothesis of historical or ongoing hydrological connectivity (headwater exchange) between the White River and Spring River drainage systems, alternatively explained by anthropogenic (bait-bucket) introduction.
