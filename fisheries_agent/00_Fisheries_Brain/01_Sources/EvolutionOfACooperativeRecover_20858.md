# Evolution of a Cooperative Recovery Program for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter (Wydoski and Hamill, in Minckley and Deacon, 1991) traces the history of the cooperative recovery program for four endangered large-river fishes of the upper [[Colorado River]] basin: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. It describes how water development, dam construction, rotenone treatments, and non-native fish introductions reduced these endemic species, how the [[Endangered Species Act]] section 7 consultation process shaped water-project approvals, and how the [[Upper Colorado River Implementation Committee]] was formed in 1987 to deliver a fifteen-year, $60 million recovery program.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River rises in clear, cold streams of the Rocky, Uinta, and Wind River mountains and historically was warm, turbid, and highly variable in flow. Of its native fish fauna, 74% was endemic (R. R. Miller 1959). Three endemic large-river fishes are federally listed as endangered, and the razorback sucker had been proposed for listing. The river is among the most heavily used and controlled in the world, supplying more than fifteen million people despite receiving less precipitation per square kilometer of drainage than any other major U.S. watershed.

Legal control began with the [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922, followed by the 1948 Upper Colorado River Compact, which apportioned water among the upper basin states of Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming. The [[Colorado River Storage Project]] Act of 1956 authorized main-stem reservoirs including Blue Mesa, Crystal, Flaming Gorge, Morrow Point, Navajo, and Powell.

Federal legislation shaping recovery included the [[Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act]] (amended 1958), the Endangered Species Preservation Act of 1966, the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (1969), and the Endangered Species Act of 1973 with amendments in 1978, 1982, and 1988. Section 7 requires federal agencies to ensure their actions do not jeopardize listed species; Section 9 prohibits take.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Dams and reservoirs altered natural flow, water temperature, and sediment transport. Peak spring flows were reduced and low stable flows were elevated, and habitat was lost through inundation and blocked migration routes. In the early 1960s, the largest rotenone treatment in the United States to that date was applied to the Green River above Flaming Gorge Dam, which also affected [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Cold-water releases below the dam were later associated with the disappearance of the four endangered species from a 105-km reach, while the Green River below the Yampa confluence retained them.

Non-native fishes increased sharply. In 1976, twenty species (40%) were native and thirty (60%) introduced in the system; by 1982, non-natives comprised 76% of the fifty-five fishes known from the upper basin. Predation and competition by introduced fishes are widely believed to be major threats.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) identified five effects of water depletion: reduced spring backwater habitat used for migration and spawning; reduced nursery and rearing habitat; reduced sediment transport and river productivity; creation of habitat favoring non-native predators and competitors; and reduced future flexibility to manage flows for the fishes.

In the lower basin, Roosevelt (1913) and Hoover (1935) dams converted much of the free-flowing river to lacustrine habitat, and [[Colorado Squawfish]] were extirpated there. A viable [[Humpback Chub]] population persisted in the [[Little Colorado River]].

The [[San Juan River]] was historically of low priority, but 1987–1988 surveys by Platania and Young (1990) changed that view.

## Key Findings

- **Program scale:** The recovery program covers 159 km of critical river reaches (Colorado, Green, Yampa, and White rivers), with a planned fifteen-year, $60 million effort and a projected annual budget of $2.3 million. More than half the budget targets in-stream flows.
- **Funding mechanisms:** Under the Windy Gap process, sponsors of private projects paid roughly $1.3 million to the USFWS between 1981 and 1987. The recovery program sets indirect-impact contributions at $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion, adjusted to $10.91 for 1991.
- **Flow acquisition:** Congress appropriated $1 million in 1988 for in-stream flow acquisition, focused on the Yampa River and a 24-km reach near Grand Junction, Colorado. Two capital funds were proposed: at least $10 million for water rights and $5 million for fish passage and rearing facilities.
- **Program goal:** Recover, delist, and manage the three endangered fishes and free the razorback sucker from ESA protection by 2002.
- **Five recovery elements:** in-stream flows; habitat development (effectiveness not yet demonstrated); native fish stocking; non-native species and sport fishing management; and research, monitoring, and data management.
- **Colorado Squawfish research:** Early studies recorded strong year classes in several years, but weak ones in 1962 and 1965. Juvenile recruitment in Echo Park was abundant in 1968, scarce in 1969, and absent in 1970. Seethaler (1978) provided the first reproduction, fecundity, and maturity data.
- **San Juan River:** Platania and Young (1990) confirmed successful reproduction in 1987 and 1988, with eighteen young-of-year captured, and found suitable habitat throughout the river. The USFWS draft opinion (May 1990) found the [[Animas-La Plata Project]] likely to jeopardize the species. A revised opinion (May 1991) allowed a scaled-down project with conditions including natural-hydrograph releases from Navajo Reservoir and a seven-year research program.
- **Coordination structures:** The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] (formed 1975) has written recovery plans for three of the species; the humpback chub and bonytail plans were revised in 1990. Annual researcher meetings have been held since 1983.
- **Outcome:** The authors describe the effort as the largest and most comprehensive recovery project of its kind in the United States, while noting that water allocation, non-native fish control, and knowledge gaps remain challenges.
