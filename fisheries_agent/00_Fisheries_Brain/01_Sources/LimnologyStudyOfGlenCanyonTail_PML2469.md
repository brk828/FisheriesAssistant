# Limnology of the Glen Canyon Tailwater and Creel Census of the Colorado River Below Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document comprises two linked reports on the Colorado River tailwater at [[Leees Ferry]], Arizona, immediately below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The first, a limnological survey covering roughly the first six to seven months of study, examined physical and chemical water conditions, plankton and benthos, and fish collections. It found that water temperatures suited a trout fishery, but that large flow fluctuations were the dominant adverse factor, causing silt removal and turbidity swings. Biological production was very limited. The second, a creel census running from November 1963 to July 1964, estimated substantial angler success (1.05 fish per man-hour; 81.9 percent of anglers successful) and tracked the return of stocked [[Rainbow Trout]]. A 1963 Arizona Game and Fish Department creel report is appended. The authors deemed firm management recommendations premature and proposed continued monitoring.

## Background & Description

The study was part of the [[Tailwater and Reservoir Fisheries Investigations]] under the [[Glen Canyon Unit]] of the [[Colorado River Storage Project]]. It was the first segment of a planned six-year program, to be extended in the second segment to include [[Lake Powell]]. Only the last seven months of the first year could be completed because of an initial shortage of personnel.

Leees Ferry lies about 12 river miles below the dam and is the only public vehicular access point on the river between Glen Canyon Dam and [[Lake Mead]]. Roughly 13 miles of water are fishable from this point; about three miles are accessible from shore and six by boat at low water, with roughly 12 miles fishable from boats at high water.

Eleven fish species were recorded in the Leees Ferry area. Rainbow trout, channel catfish, carp, and flannelmouth sucker were abundant; largemouth bass, fathead minnow, and plains red shiner were common or rare; [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado River Squawfish]] were rare or very rare. The reports also mention [[Mosquito Fish]] as common. Sampling methods included gill netting, electrofishing, cove renovation, creel census, and stomach content analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature.** Water temperatures ranged from 45.5 to 56°F at Leees Ferry, close to the optimum for trout (about 55°F). Releases came through diversion tunnels roughly 30 feet above the lake bottom, which kept the tailwater cold. Travel time from dam to Leees Ferry was about nine hours, and temperature changed by only one to four degrees.

**Flow.** Flow fluctuation was the most damaging factor. Discharge ranged from 1,000 to 22,800 cfs, with daily swings of 5,000 cfs not uncommon. These swings resuspended silt and removed much of the upper-reach sediment, with the risk of losing silt down to the [[Paria River]] confluence.

**Turbidity and color.** Both tracked flow. High flows produced turbid, coffee-with-cream water, while low flows gave clear water.

**Substrate.** The bottom was mostly rubble and sand, with about 20 percent of the area in mixed material.

**Water chemistry.** Dissolved oxygen ranged from 8.5 to 10 ppm, and alkalinity from 121 to 202 ppm. Hardness, sulfate, and conductance data were collected at several stations by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[Arizona State Health Department]], and the [[U.S. Geological Survey]]. Specific conductance was roughly 1,340 to 1,390 micromhos, pH 7.5 to 8.3.

**Plankton and benthos.** Phytoplankton was present in small amounts and zooplankton was scarce until June. Benthic organisms were mainly bloodworms (chironomids) and snails, found in small quantities in the riffles and in 3 to 4 feet of water. Bait organisms and algae made up part of trout stomach contents.

## Key Findings

- **Physical conditions:** Temperature was suitable for trout, but flow fluctuation was the main limiting factor.
- **Biological production:** Plankton and benthos were very limited throughout the study period.
- **Fish collections:** Gill netting, electrofishing, and cove renovation caught species including rainbow trout, carp, channel catfish, flannelmouth sucker, and largemouth bass. Average condition factors for trout were roughly 1.2 to 1.5, with variation linked to location and stocking.
- **Recommendations (limnological):** Continue plankton and benthos studies; weigh, measure, and mark catchable fish; evaluate the advisability of introducing a new game species or food organism near Leees Ferry; and recognize that further recommendations are premature until the effects of flow changes are known.
- **Creel census (Nov 1963 – July 1964):** 128 census days; 1,461 angler trips checked, producing 6,297 rainbow trout and 61 channel catfish in 5,112 hours. Success was 1.05 fish per man-hour, and 81.9 percent of anglers were successful. Estimated total effort was 2,477 trips and 9,765.9 hours, yielding 10,654 trout.
- **Stocking:** About 60,000 catchable rainbow trout were planted, with roughly 7,500 tagged or fin-clipped. Estimated return to the creel was 11.7 percent of stocked fish. Most anglers were nonresident, and boat fishing was used for most of the catch.
- **1963 Arizona Game and Fish report (June–August 1963):** Creel census results showed a catch of about 5.2 fish per trip, with recommendations to match future plantings to fishery size and trout sizes suited to the water.
