# Virgin River Fishes Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #recovery-planning #Virgin-River

## Executive Summary

This recovery plan addresses two endangered cyprinid species endemic to the [[Virgin River]] system: the [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and the [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*). The woundfin was listed as endangered on October 13, 1970, and the Virgin River chub on August 24, 1990. Both species have experienced dramatic range contractions from their historical distributions. Woundfin historically occurred in the Salt River, Gila River near Yuma, Colorado River near Yuma, Virgin River, and Moapa River but now persist only in the mainstem Virgin River from [[Pah Tempe Springs]] downstream to [[Lake Mead]]. Virgin River chub occur in the Virgin River and have recently been described in the [[Moapa River]].

The primary threats to these species include: (1) alteration of flow regimes from dams and diversions, (2) degraded water quality, (3) habitat destruction, and (4) introduction of nonnative species, particularly the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*). Since red shiners were first collected near St. George in 1984, they have become the most abundant species in much of the Virgin River, coinciding with significant declines in native fish populations.

Recovery objectives focus on downlisting to threatened status by 2015 through: protecting essential flows, upgrading degraded habitat from Pah Tempe Springs to Lake Mead, establishing fish barriers, eliminating red shiners, and reducing other nonnative species. Interim delisting criteria for woundfin include establishing two additional self-sustaining populations within historical range. The plan outlines comprehensive monitoring, habitat restoration, flow management, propagation, and research programs.

## Background & Description

**Woundfin Taxonomy and Description:**
The woundfin is the most highly specialized species in the cyprinid tribe Plagopterini, subfamily Leuciscinae. This unique tribe comprises three genera: *Plagopterus* (monotypic), *Meda* (monotypic), and *Lepidomeda* (four species). The entire taxon is endemic to the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] and its ancestral tributary, the White River.

The woundfin is a streamlined, silvery minnow with a flat head and conspicuous sharp dorsal spine from which its common name derives. It is the most silvery of all American minnows, reflecting blue in bright sunlight. The species rarely exceeds 7.5 cm (3 inches) standard length. The head and belly are flattened, giving an anteriorly depressed, torpedo-like appearance characteristic of fish inhabiting swift, shallow, sand-bottomed streams. Adaptations include expansive falcate fins, barbels on lips, reduced eyes, and extensive sensory buds on the gular region and leading pectoral fin-rays. Woundfin are essentially scaleless except for small bone plates in the leathery skin near the nape. Unique modifications include enlarged, elongated spinose rays in the first two dorsal fin-rays, thickened spine-like pelvic rays, and spine-like development near the base of pectoral fin-rays.

**Virgin River Chub Taxonomy and Description:**
The Virgin River chub was originally considered a subspecies (*Gila robusta seminuda*) but current research confirms it is a full species, *Gila seminuda*. The [[Moapa River Chub]], originally classified as an undescribed form of *G. robusta*, is conspecific with *G. seminuda* (DeMarais et al. 1992). The Virgin River chub is a silvery, medium-sized minnow averaging about 20 cm (8 inches) total length but can grow to 45 cm (18 inches). It can be distinguished from subspecies of *G. robusta* by having 9-10 rays in the dorsal, anal, and pelvic fins, and 24-31 gill rakers. The back, breast, and part of the belly have small, deeply embedded scales that may be absent in some individuals, hence the name *seminuda*.

**Virgin River Basin Description:**
The Virgin River flows approximately 320 km (200 miles) southwest along the boundary between the [[Colorado Plateau]] and the [[Great Basin]], separated roughly by the [[Hurricane Fault]], before flowing into Lake Mead. The river is characterized by steep-walled narrow canyons including [[Zion Canyon]], [[Parunuweap Canyon]], [[Timpoweap Canyon]], and the [[Virgin River Gorge]]. The system exhibits widely variable discharges (floods often follow summer thunderstorms), high sediment load, and sparse riparian vegetation. The basin lies at the intersection of the Great Basin Desert to the north and the Mojave Desert to the south, with vegetation communities ranging from fir-pine at headwaters through juniper-pinon, blackbrush, to creosote bush desert communities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Requirements:**
Woundfin adults and juveniles are most often collected from runs and quiet waters adjacent to riffles over sand and sand/gravel substrates. Woundfin greater than 4.0 cm TL utilize depths between 0.15-0.43 m in velocities between 0.24-0.49 m/s. Juveniles use habitats generally slower and deeper than adults. Larvae are collected in backwaters or slow-velocity habitat along stream margins, often associated with dense filamentous algae. Deacon et al. (1987) reported preferred water temperature for adults was approximately 18°C, indicating the species is eurythermal. When temperatures approach 30°C, woundfin leave shallow areas and congregate in deeper, cooler portions.

Virgin River chubs are most often associated with deep runs or pool habitats of slow to moderate velocities with large boulders or instream cover such as root snags. Adults and juveniles are often associated together, though larger adults are collected most often in deeper pools. Chubs less than 80 mm TL utilize depths greater than 0.18 m in velocities between 0.08-0.15 m/s over sand substrates with large boulders or cover. Chubs 80-140 mm TL utilize depths greater than 0.30 m in velocities 0.00-0.76 m/s. Chubs greater than 140 mm TL utilize depths greater than 0.61-0.91 m in velocities 0.00-0.55 m/s. Schumann (1978) and Deacon et al. (1987) found final adult thermal preferendum was approximately 24°C, suggesting the species is more stenothermal than woundfin.

**Food Habits:**
Woundfin are omnivorous and shift food habits in response to changing availability. Stomach contents include filamentous algae, detrital material, tamarisk seeds, and insects (Ephemeroptera, dipteran adults, chironomid larvae, ceratopogonids, simuliids). Greger and Deacon (1988) documented seasonal shifts in food selectivity corresponding to shifts in habitat foraging areas, and dietary differences between populations in disturbed versus undisturbed river segments. Significant dietary overlaps exist between woundfin and red shiners.

Virgin River chubs are omnivorous, showing considerable dietary shifts with age. They feed mainly on debris and chironomids in February; *Cladophora* and debris in June; debris, *Spyrogyra*, and *Cladophora* in September; and unidentified drift animals, dragonfly larvae, debris, and *Cladophora* in December. Young fish (<70 mm TL) feed almost entirely on macroinvertebrates while adults (>110 mm TL) feed almost exclusively on algae and debris. Cross (1975) reported up to 90 percent of diet consisted of filamentous algae.

**Reproductive Biology:**
Woundfin reproductive cycle appears initiated by some combination of increasing water temperatures, lengthening daylight, and declining spring runoff. Gonadal maturation occurs March-May, and larvae appear May-August. Spawning occurs April-July depending on snowmelt runoff timing; late summer spawning in August has been observed. Hardy et al. (1989) and Deacon and Hardy (1984) found spawning success increased as flows during spawning increased from 2.83 to 22.66 m³/sec (100-800 cfs). Woundfin in downstream reaches begin spawning more than 1 month earlier than fish in upper reaches, probably related to warmer water temperatures.

Greger and Deacon (1982) observed spawning in artificial streams at 20-25°C. Females leave pools to join males in swifter water over cobble to gravel substrates. Spawning occurred at velocities 0.06-0.09 m/sec in depths 0.07-0.10 m. Substrate choice appeared fairly specific to cobble or gravel, though Dexter observations indicated little preference. Deacon and Hardy (1982) showed reduced survival of young woundfin at flows below 5.66 m³/sec (200 cfs), though monitoring data indicate high initial survival below [[Washington Fields Diversion]] at flows less than 0.57 m³/sec (20 cfs). Mean monthly flows of 22.66 m³/sec or higher during reproduction resulted in diminished recruitment. When populations were severely depleted (1977 drought), a 2-year period of favorable conditions was required to rebuild densities. Average winter mortality of approximately 30 percent was observed, independent of population density.

Very little information exists on Virgin River chub reproductive biology. Hickman (1987a) reported ripe females and males in April, May, and June, but spawning time has not been determined. Good spawning years for Virgin River chub coincided with good years for woundfin. Virgin River chubs successfully spawn in both artificial pond habitats and mainstem Virgin River.

**Associated Species:**
Native species occurring with woundfin and Virgin River chub include [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*), which shows marked proclivity for swifter waters and solid substrates; [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), usually collected in deeper, slower waters behind boulders or debris and commonly occurring with adult Virgin River chub; [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*), most abundant above Pah Tempe Springs and in tributaries; and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), associated with shallow, low gradient riffle habitats with gravel or small cobble substrates.

More than 10 introduced fish species are known from the Virgin River. The [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) and [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*) are most abundant. Red shiner appear to have greatest negative impacts on woundfin, apparently through competition for food and space and possibly predation on larvae. In 1984, four red shiner were collected near St. George for the first time. By 1986, red shiner was the most abundant species in this reach while woundfin populations decreased significantly. Predators include piscivorous birds (kingfishers, herons), soft-shelled turtles, [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), black bullhead, [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*).

**Limiting Factors and Historical Decline:**
Major limiting factors are modification and loss of habitat and introduction/establishment of nonnative fish, particularly red shiner. Building of dams, reservoirs, water diversion structures, canals, laterals, aqueducts, and stream dewatering causes loss or degradation of available habitat. This habitat loss has been exacerbated by exotic species establishment, further reducing habitat suitability.

White settlers first used the Virgin River system for irrigation in 1854. The [[Washington Fields Diversion]] and canal were completed in 1891; the [[La Verkin Diversion]] and canal in 1901. Between 1854 and 1910, numerous dams and hundreds of miles of canals were built. During summer months, demand for irrigation water exceeded available supply; entire river sections were commonly diverted into irrigation canals. About 99 percent of water rights were already allocated by 1910 and probably fully used. Livestock grazing depleted riparian vegetation, increasing erosion and siltation and decreasing fish cover.

Collections from the 1930s indicate woundfin and Virgin River chub abundance in the mainstem above Mesquite, Nevada, may have remained relatively stable, but populations below Mesquite declined significantly. Above Mesquite, populations have declined subsequent to red shiner invasion. Since 1984, woundfin and Virgin River chub populations have declined, and red shiner has nearly replaced most native fish from Washington Fields Diversion to Lake Mead.

Woundfin have been extirpated from almost all historical range except mainstem Virgin River. Six major dams on the Salt and Verde Rivers and two on the Gila River effectively cut off natural flows in lower reaches. Formation of Lake Mead in the 1930s flooded lower portions of the Virgin and Moapa Rivers. Reduction in flows below major irrigation diversions resulted in habitat reductions throughout the Virgin River. Altered flow regimes resulted in habitat loss due to complete dewatering during low flow periods. During late summer 1989 and 1990, over 4.8 km (3 miles) below Washington Fields Diversion was completely dewatered. Significant alterations in physical river structure also occurred due to [[Quail Creek]] dike failure on January 1, 1989.

## Key Findings

**Recovery Objectives and Criteria:**

*Downlisting Criteria:*
The woundfin may be downlisted to threatened when: (1) Virgin River flows essential to survival of all life stages are ensured through operational criteria for existing dams, reservoirs, and diversions; acquisition of priority water rights to ensure instream flows from Pah Tempe Springs to Lake Mead; and agreements ensuring passage, timing, and magnitude of flows for channel maintenance; (2) degraded Virgin River habitats from Pah Tempe Springs to Lake Mead are improved and maintained to allow continued existence of all life stages at viable population levels; and (3) barriers to upstream movements of introduced fishes are established, and red shiners and other nonnative species presenting major threats are eliminated upstream of those barriers.

If Virgin River chub is not listed in the Moapa River, downlisting criteria will be identical to woundfin. If listed in the Moapa River, recovery criteria addressing fish in both rivers will be developed.

*Interim Delisting Criteria:*
The woundfin may be delisted when: (1) two additional self-sustaining populations are established in the wild within historical range, with adequate habitat and instream flow protection, populations self-sustaining for minimum 10 consecutive years, and a plan for genetic exchange developed and implemented; and (2) essential habitats, important migration routes, required streamflows, and water quality of both Virgin River habitat and transplanted population habitats are legally protected, and threats of significant physical, chemical, or biological modification are removed.

Delisting criteria for woundfin are considered interim because opportunity and potential locations for reestablishment are uncertain. Delisting criteria for Virgin River chub cannot presently be determined. Estimated date for downlisting both species is 2015.

**Priority Recovery Actions:**

1. **Monitor existing populations:** Review and update population and genetic monitoring protocols; implement monitoring procedures; maintain and update [[Virgin River Fishes Data Base]]; prepare standardized annual reports on population trends.

2. **Eliminate red shiners:** Establish fish barriers at suitable sites; eradicate or reduce nonnative fish species from below [[Johnson Diversion]] to Lake Mead; reestablish native fishes in treated reaches. In 1988-1989, three rotenone treatment attempts created a buffer zone from Washington Fields Diversion to Johnson Diversion inhibiting upstream red shiner migration. Red shiner reinvaded below Johnson Diversion when fish barrier failed during 1990 flooding but remain eliminated above Johnson Diversion.

3. **Develop viable operating protocol:** Develop and implement operational criteria for regulated flows within Virgin River affected by [[Quail Creek Reservoir System]] and other proposed systems.

4. **Culture programs:** Perfect propagation techniques for Virgin River chub and woundfin; develop propagation protocols and establish production goals; conduct propagation and reintroduction programs. Brood stocks established at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] in 1988. Woundfin successfully propagated in 1993. Virgin River chub successfully spawned in 1989 but genetic problems with F1 offspring resulted in destruction rather than reintroduction.

5. **Habitat protection and enhancement:** Monitor habitat conditions; develop and implement habitat improvements; develop instream flow recommendations; implement flows and monitor response; establish gaging stations; identify existing water use and legal water rights; acquire high priority water rights for instream flows; develop legally binding agreements to maintain instream flows; acquire land and/or protective easements along Virgin River.

6. **Reintroduction sites:** Recommend specific sites suitable for reintroduction of additional woundfin populations; secure appropriate habitat and flow protection; introduce woundfin to selected sites; develop and implement monitoring programs for reintroduced populations. Potential sites include Verde River between Perkinsville and Sycamore Creek, Gila River mainstream above Safford, San Francisco River above Gila confluence, Tonto Creek, and Hassayampa River. Previous Arizona reintroduction attempts (1969-1972) to Hassayampa River, Salt River, Sycamore Creek, and Paria River were unsuccessful.

7. **Research needs:** Define genetic identity; determine historical variation in population abundance; determine relationship between environmental conditions and recruitment; determine effects of habitat conditions on various life stages; assess interaction between flow dynamics and food production; determine effects of timing, magnitude, and duration of flows on physical habitat; determine native fish community structure and interactions; determine migration movements; prevent loss of fish in irrigation canal diversions; further define downlisting and delisting criteria.

8. **Education:** Develop and implement educational and informational programs highlighting recovery needs and ongoing efforts for Virgin River fishes.
