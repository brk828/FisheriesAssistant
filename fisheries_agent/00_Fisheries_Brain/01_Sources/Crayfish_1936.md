## Executive Summary
This 1939 Institute for Fisheries Research report (No. 562) provides a natural history and aquaculture overview of Michigan [[Crayfish]] (family [[Astacidae]], genus *Cambarus*). It describes their taxonomy, anatomy, molting, reproduction, feeding habits, and ecological role, with particular emphasis on their economic importance as fish bait — especially for [[Smallmouth Bass]] — and their incidental production in state fish hatchery ponds. The report also outlines species-specific habitat preferences and practical guidance for pond-based crayfish culture and bait maintenance.

## Background & Description
- Classified under **Crustacea**, sharing the trait of an external hard shell (**exoskeleton**) with lobsters, crabs, shrimp, pillbugs, and barnacles.
- Family: **[[Astacidae]]**; genus: ***Cambarus***; 8 native species in Michigan, 4 commonly used as bait.
- Respire via **gills** located along the sides of the trunk, protected by the hard shell — functionally analogous to fish gills despite no taxonomic relation.
- Shell composed of **chitin** and **calcium carbonate**; growth requires periodic **molting** (ecdysis).
  - Old shell splits along the upper surface under internal pressure; new shell is soft for several days post-molt.
  - Juveniles molt more frequently (~6 molts in first two months) than adults; growth halts in winter.
- Possess **swimmerets** (small leg-like abdominal appendages) used to carry and aerate eggs.
- Capable of **regeneration** of lost antennae, eyes, claws, walking legs, and other body parts.
- Locomotion: swim only **backwards**; otherwise crawl and hide in burrows, under logs/rocks, or in vegetation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Found in **freshwater** habitats across North America (absent only from Antarctica among broader crustacean distribution); most diverse/abundant in tropics.
- Occupy both **burrowing** and **non-burrowing** ecological strategies:
  - **Non-burrowers**: shelter under stones, logs, in small holes or shallow depressions.
  - **Burrowers**: dig burrows 1–3 feet deep using pincers, depositing excavated material as "**mud chimneys**" at burrow openings; burrows may be single or branched with internal enlargements.
- **Nocturnal** and largely **omnivorous/scavenging** feeders — consume worms, insect larvae, other crayfish, injured or dead fish, garbage, plant seeds, and aquatic/terrestrial vegetation.
- Serve as a **key prey species** for freshwater fishes (notably [[Smallmouth Bass]]), as well as snakes, turtles, birds, and raccoons.
- Some parasitic crustaceans (e.g., the **gill louse** on [[Brook Trout]]) are noted as a related but distinct ecological interaction within Crustacea.
- Ecological impacts on habitat:
  - Burrowing species can damage **dams and levees**.
  - Some species cause **crop destruction**.
  - In fish ponds, crayfish can eliminate plant growth and increase turbidity, sometimes considered undesirable, though partially controlled by predation from bass.

## Key Findings
- **Reproduction**: Breeding timing varies by species (spring or fall); eggs fertilized during passage from oviducts, then attached to swimmerets via sticky secretion. Clutch size ranges **50–260 eggs**, dependent on female size/age. Eggs hatch in ~3 weeks; young remain attached to swimmerets for another ~3 weeks before independence.
- **Longevity**: Some species believed to live up to **4 years**.
- **Economic Importance**:
  - Valued as **fish bait** (whole live crayfish or tail meat), especially effective for [[Smallmouth Bass]] in early fall.
  - Anglers prefer **"soft shells"/"peelers"** (recently molted individuals), which can be preserved in a soft state for 1–2 weeks in rainwater-filled barrels or refrigeration.
  - Considered a table delicacy by some.
  - Harvested commercially by bait dealers from natural waters; no known attempts (as of 1939) at deliberate propagation for bait, though incidental production occurs in state hatchery ponds.
- **Aquaculture Findings**:
  - Comstock Park State Hatchery (near Grand Rapids) produced **~600,000 crayfish (over 1.5 tons) annually** from a 4-acre bass rearing pond.
  - Estimated yield potential: **~1 ton of crayfish per acre** of pond.
  - Recommended culture practices: shallow ponds/ditches; dikes to increase burrowing shoreline for burrowing species; ample shade (crayfish avoid direct sun); artificial feeding (fish, meat scraps, plants, potatoes, table scraps) under heavy stocking.
  - Breeder stock should be matched to pond substrate type (muddy-bottom breeders for muddy ponds, stream breeders for stream conditions).
  - Collection methods: seining (most effective at night, as crayfish are nocturnal) or baited traps (dead fish/meat scraps).
  - Live storage for bait: tanks, troughs, or live boxes with running water; requires regular feeding (small fresh meat pieces), avoidance of overcrowding, and daily removal of dead individuals.
- **Taxonomic Context**: Crayfish are gill-breathing like fish but are **not phylogenetically related** to fishes — a common point of public misconception addressed in the report.
