# Limnology of Flint Hills Farm Ponds (1956) and Growth Studies of Stocked Fishes

## Executive Summary
This study presents limnological data from 18 farm ponds within a 30-mile radius of Manhattan, Kansas, collected during summer 1956, alongside preliminary growth data for three commonly stocked pond fishes ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Channel Catfish]]) from 22 ponds stocked since 1953. The research emphasizes the strong influence of **cattle-induced turbidity**, **thermal and chemical stratification**, and **drought conditions** on pond productivity and fish growth. Despite largely unmanaged and cattle-accessible conditions, fish growth rates generally met or exceeded national averages compiled by Carlander (1953), suggesting the Flint Hills ponds have inherent productive potential that could be substantially improved through basic management practices such as fencing to exclude livestock.

## Site & Methodological Context
- **Location:** 18 farm ponds near Manhattan, Kansas (Flint Hills region); growth data drawn from 22 ponds since 1953.
- **Climate context:** Summer 1956 rainfall was 76–117% of average (wetter than the severe drought of 1955 at 41–66%), but cumulative 5-year drought left water levels critically low; some ponds dried completely, others dropped 6–8 feet.
- **Temperature:** Air temperatures near average (+0.6°F mean for the season).
- **Techniques:** Followed [[Swingle and Johnson 1953]] methodology; max-min thermometer for temperature, platinum wire turbidimeter for turbidity, total length to nearest 0.1 in., weight in grams.

- All samples exceeded Carlander (1953) medians for both age groups (Age I median: 3.7 in/0.5 oz; Age II median: 5.5 in/1.9 oz).

Growth classified as **Poor**, **Average**, or **Good** based on population density and food availability:

| Age Group | Poor (Length/Weight) | Average (Length/Weight) | Good (Length/Weight) |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 3.9–5.0 in / 8.2–16.2 g | 8.7 in / 86 g | 11.1–11.8 in / 174–208 g |
| II | 5.6 in / 17 g | 11.6 in / 186 g | 17.1–19.6 in / 742–1050 g |
| III | — | 17.2 in / 667 g | — |

- "Poor" growth attributed to overcrowding in a pond with unmanaged natural spawning (washtub spawning containers).
- "Good" growth linked to low stocking density (100 fingerlings/year in a 3/4-acre pond) with abundant natural forage (salamander larvae, crayfish, tadpoles) — stomach content analysis confirmed predation on salamanders.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Water Quality Parameters (Summer 1956)
- **Turbidity:** Classified per [[Buck 1956]] and modified by [[Tiemeier and Moorman 1957]]:
  - Clear: ≤40 ppm average (2 of 18 ponds)
  - Intermediate: 41–100 ppm (6 of 18 ponds)
  - Muddy: >100 ppm (10 of 18 ponds)
  - Primary driver of turbidity: **cattle wading and bank trampling**, especially at low water levels; deferred grazing plans demonstrably reduced turbidity (26–85 ppm) versus active grazing periods (up to 350 ppm) in the same pond.

- **Dissolved Oxygen:**
  - Surface (0 ft): 1.7–13.3 ppm (generally >7 ppm, adequate)
  - 3 ft: 0.0–14.0 ppm (adequate, averages >4 ppm)
  - 6 ft: 0.0–14.0 ppm (frequently zero at depth from mid-June to late August — **not conducive to fish growth** below the 4 ppm threshold)
  - Fish kill event: Barnyard manure inundation caused severe oxygen depletion (0.0–1.4 ppm), killing channel catfish and bluegills; recovery required ~5 weeks via algal photosynthetic activity.

- **pH (Hydrogen-ion Concentration):** Range 6.8–10.6; ponds alkaline to strongly alkaline; strong stratification (0.4–0.7 pH unit difference between surface and 6 ft depths).
  - Notable event: pH of 10.6 (Horne Pond No. 6) coincided with a white bass ([[Roccus chrysops]]) die-off and one bluegill death, linked to carbonate spike (27→75 ppm) from evaporative concentration; authors suggest an associated factor rather than pH alone was the lethal agent.

- **Carbon Dioxide:** Range 0.0–20.0 ppm across depths; inversely correlated with pH; never reached limiting/harmful levels but occasionally low enough to potentially limit photosynthesis.

- **Carbonates/Bicarbonates:** Carbonates 0.0–75.0 ppm; bicarbonates 76–457 ppm — both notably higher than comparable studies in Oklahoma ([[Wallen 1955]]), Alabama ([[Swingle 1947]]), and southeastern Kansas ([[Ratzlaff 1952]]).

- **Thermal Stratification:** Evident by late May, severe by mid-June; surface temps never exceeded 90°F in 1956; water temperature not considered a limiting factor for stocked species.

### Community/Stocking Composition
Ponds were stocked in various combinations:
- Bass only (1 pond)
- Channel catfish only (8 ponds)
- Bass + bluegill (4 ponds)
- Bass + bluegill + channel catfish (5 ponds)
- White bass + bluegill (2 ponds)
- Bass + channel catfish (1 pond)
- Channel catfish + bluegill + crappie (1 pond)

Incidental/accidental species present: [[Black Bullhead]], [[Crappie]], [[Green Sunfish]], and various [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows).

### Predator-Prey/Trophic Notes
- Bass reproduction failed in muddier ponds; bass disappeared entirely within 2–3 years post-stocking in three turbid ponds, while bluegill persisted (though with poor young-of-year growth).
- Channel catfish growth strongly tied to natural forage base (salamander larvae, crayfish, tadpoles) and population density rather than pond chemistry alone.

## Key Findings
1. **Cattle access is the primary driver of pond turbidity** in unfenced Flint Hills ponds, directly threatening successful bass-bluegill management by preventing balanced predator-prey population dynamics.
2. **Hypolimnetic oxygen depletion** (below 3 ft, especially 6 ft) from mid-June through late August restricts fish to upper water strata or shallow zones, though this was not unique to any single pond type.
3. **Organic fertilization (manure) is not recommended** for pond management due to unpredictable oxygen depletion risk and difficulty in controlling application amounts.
4. **High alkalinity (pH to 10.6) coincided with a white bass kill event**, though the paper cautions that pH alone (versus an associated factor) may not be the direct lethal mechanism, consistent with literature suggesting pH 12 is the more typical lethal threshold.
5. **Carbonate/bicarbonate levels in Flint Hills ponds substantially exceed** those reported in comparable studies from Oklahoma, Alabama, and eastern Kansas, likely driving elevated pH via evaporative concentration during drought.
6. **Water temperature and CO2 levels were not limiting factors** for stocked species growth or survival in these ponds.
7. Despite **unmanaged, cattle-accessible, and drought-stressed conditions**, initial growth rates of [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Channel Catfish]] compared favorably to or exceeded national averages (per [[Carlander 1953]]), indicating strong latent productivity potential in Flint Hills ponds.
8. **Channel catfish growth is highly density-dependent**: overcrowded self-recruited populations showed stunted growth (as low as 3.9 in/8.2 g at Age I), while low-density, food-rich ponds produced "good" growth (up to 19.6 in/1050 g at Age II) — a >6x weight difference at comparable ages.
9. Authors recommend **fencing ponds and piping water to livestock tanks** as a cost-effective intervention to extend pond storage life, reduce turbidity, and improve fish production and recreational value.
