# Colorado River Fishes Monitoring Project: First Annual Report (1982 Field Season)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This first annual report of the [[Colorado River Fishes Monitoring Project]] (CRFMP), issued January 1983 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) and the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (BR), documents the 1982 field season on three endangered Upper Colorado River fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The study had three objectives: (1) define adult [[Colorado Squawfish]] movement and spawning sites using [[Radiotelemetry]]; (2) verify flow requirements for spawning and larval survival; and (3) determine the effects of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] flow fluctuation on young-of-the-year (YOY) and juvenile survival. Results cover adult movements, spawning-season observations, larval and YOY surveys, and a preliminary [[Peaking Power]] impact assessment. The peaking-power work was only partly completed because of high flows and release-scheduling problems.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is a large cyprinid with a wide distribution, long movements and specific life requirements. The authors argue that meeting its needs should also satisfy the requirements of other endangered species, which is why it was the focus of management effort. The report builds on the 1979–82 [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] (CRFP), whose final report was submitted to BR in April 1982 (Miller et al. 1982).

Adult fish were collected by electrofishing, gill nets, trammel nets and traps. Colorado squawfish over 500 mm total length (TL) were surgically implanted with radio transmitters weighing about 10–11 g in air, with an expected life of about 6 months. Tracking was at least biweekly, mainly by boat, with aircraft and helicopters also used. Locations were given as river miles (RM), counted from the Green–Colorado confluence (RM 0).

Larval fish were collected in July and August using seines, dip nets and a pilot [[Larval Drift]] study with 1 m ichthyoplankton nets set at 0600, 1200, 1800 and 2400 h. YOY surveys used 3×5 mm mesh seines on backwater and shoreline sites from mid-September through October, sampling fish of 25–60 mm.

The [[Peaking Power]] study focused on the Green River between Jensen and Ouray, Utah, where young squawfish are common. It planned aerial photography, staff-gage and photo monitoring, and marked-fish behavior observations under controlled high and low releases from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study reaches (1982):** Colorado River from Palisade, Colorado to Hite, Utah; Gunnison River from the Redlands Diversion to its confluence; the lower 20 miles of the Yampa River; and the Green River from the Yampa confluence to Gray Canyon.
- **Spawning temperature:** Mature aggregations occurred as flows receded and water temperatures were near 20 °C, which the report cites as the temperature at which [[Colorado Squawfish]] spawn successfully (Miller et al. 1982).
- **High-water year:** 1982 had very high water levels. Shallow backwaters on the Green River were nearly absent during YOY collections.
- **Walter Walker Wildlife Area and gravel pits:** Fish captured in flooded gravel pits connected to the Colorado River (including [[Walter Walker Wildlife Area]]) were tagged; all fish released in pits later moved into the main river.
- **Flow regulation:** Maximum releases from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in late summer and fall 1982 prevented the low flows needed for the planned study.

## Key Findings

**Adult movement and radiotelemetry**
- Of seven fish implanted in upper Lake Powell (April 6 to May 28), contact was lost with three at release, including one ultrasonic unit. The remaining four were tracked from 5 days to 6 months.
- One upper Lake Powell fish moved about 160 miles upstream to RM 140 by mid-August and reached Clifton, Colorado (about 40 further miles) by September.
- In the Grand Junction area, 16 fish were tagged (May 14 to June 18). No movements exceeded 50 miles, though some moved about 40 miles. Fish from [[Walter Walker Wildlife Area]] generally moved farther than upstream-tagged fish.
- Eleven adult fish (522–810 mm TL) were radio-tagged in the Green River starting May 4 and by May 20. One 810 mm fish moved about 322 miles during the study period.
- Four White River fish that entered the Green River were tracked with Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (DWR).

**Spawning evidence**
- On July 13, three radio-tagged fish were found at RM 178.3 near Clifton. Trammel netting collected nine fish, including five ripe males; others appeared to be mature females.
- A large eddy at RM 176 was visited by five radio-tagged fish in late July. A ripe male was collected, and a recaptured tagged fish that had gained about 500 g was probably a maturing female.
- Ripe males were found in the Walter Walker area one month before the RM 178.3 aggregation; two tagged fish from there moved 10 and 15 miles while ripening.
- Seven ripe fish were collected in Gray Canyon (July 13–22), and one ripe fish from the lower Yampa. Gray Canyon is documented as a potential spawning site for the first time. Spawning in Gray Canyon was reported to occur about 3 weeks earlier than in Yampa Canyon.

**Larval and YOY surveys**
- 148 larval Colorado squawfish were found in the Colorado River, 107 of them below Westwater Canyon, which had the highest relative abundance.
- In the Green and Yampa rivers, 98 larvae were confirmed plus 19 potential (9.3–22.0 mm TL). The largest count was 52 confirmed plus 16 potential at RM 314.9 (below Split Mountain) on August 3–7. No larvae were found above the Yampa confluence (RM 345).
- Of 111 seine samples, 14,032 larval fishes of all species were collected. Of 115 ichthyoplankton-sampler samples, 131 larval fishes were collected, and no Colorado squawfish were positively identified.
- YOY fish (30–40 mm TL) in the Colorado River were collected only below Westwater Canyon. In the Green River, 637 YOY were collected. Catch per effort there was about half that of previous years, which the authors attribute to high water rather than poor spawning, though YOY survival may have been reduced.

**Peaking power impacts**
- Between October 31 and November 4, 1982, a change from 800 to 4,000 cfs at Flaming Gorge Dam produced a 1.82-foot stage change at Ouray, 155 miles downstream. This occurred while the Yampa was discharging three times its normal flow.
- The full peaking-power study was not completed in 1982. Plans for 1983 include controlled high and low releases to evaluate nursery habitat changes.
