# Minutes of the Desert Fishes Recovery Team Meeting, November 7-8, 1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

These minutes record the November 7-8, 1990 meeting of the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] in Phoenix, Arizona, transmitted by Team Coordinator Sally Stefferud on March 15, 1991. The meeting centered on two issues. The first was whether [[Red Shiner]] posed a threat to native fishes in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, and how the Team should respond. The Team could not agree on whether a problem existed, so it did not reach consensus on removal. It did agree on a set of monitoring, research, barrier, and oversight actions. The second issue was revision of the draft [[Desert Pupfish]] recovery plan, especially downlisting and delisting criteria. The Team also addressed [[Gila Topminnow]] reintroduction and removal of nonnative fish, [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] management, team membership, and funding.

## Background & Description

The Team is a group of agency, university, and conservation biologists charged with recovering endangered desert fishes of the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico. [[Minckley]] was Team Leader and withdrew his resignation at this meeting. Draft recovery plans for [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] were published in the Federal Register for public comment, which closed November 26, 1990.

Species discussed include:
- **[[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]:** listed as threatened. The Team agreed that their range-wide status has deteriorated.
- **[[Red Shiner]]:** a nonnative cyprinid. A female can produce about 50 eggs every 10 days in warm water, with lifetime production of about 30,000 eggs.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]:** reintroduced in several sites. Stock from Middle Spring is doing well at Roper Lake. Topminnow are nearly extinct at Salt Creek and Bos/Medicine, and extinct at Middle Spring, which dried in 1989.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]:** two subspecies are addressed, *C. m. macularius* and *C. m. eremus*. The Quitobaquito population at Bog Hole and Finley Tank is also discussed.
- **Nonnatives:** [[Green Sunfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], and bullheads (black and yellow). Mosquitofish are cited as damaging to Gila topminnow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aravaipa Creek.** Data from 1943 and from 1963 to the present (a 27-year record, with the post-1963 seine data collected by ASU students) show nonnative species rising from 3 in the 1960s to 7 in the 1980s. Nonnatives make up only a small share of the fish community, with no clear trend. Native species show no up or down trend, but [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] fluctuate widely. Minckley stressed that the data contain considerable "noise" and should only be used to identify broad patterns.

**Red shiner spread.** Small numbers of [[Red Shiner]] were found from about 2.5 miles above the lower wilderness boundary downstream. None were found in the large larval and juvenile collections from October 1990. Summer 1990 floods, reaching roughly a 20-year level, may have allowed red shiner to move upstream. Red shiner were abundant in the San Pedro River at the creek mouth. Later, [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] crews found red shiner above the mouth of [[Turkey Creek]], which changed the planning for a barrier.

**Coexistence elsewhere.** [[Spikedace]] and [[Red Shiner]] have coexisted for about 15 years in the upper [[Verde River]] and 9 years in the upper [[Gila River]]. Propst reported that red shiner in the upper Gila surged after 1980-81, then declined after floods in 1983 and 1984. Abundance was very low in 1990. The team members used these cases to argue that red shiner do not displace spikedace where habitat modification is minor and no major diversion or impoundment exists. Median flows are about 20-25 cfs in Aravaipa Creek and about 24 cfs in the upper Verde.

**Habitat change.** [[Channelization]] above the upper end of perennial flow has triggered channel incision, which is worsening and is now visible on [[The Nature Conservancy]] lands. Marsh noted that red shiner now occupy large areas of former spikedace range. Possible causes include direct displacement, habitat degradation that favored red shiner, and effects from other nonnatives that arrived at the same time.

**Desert pupfish sites.** Pupfish in Mexico occur at three geographic areas: Santa Clara Slough/El Doctor, Cerro Prieto, and Laguna Salada. The [[Sonoran Tiger Salamander]] at Bog Hole is under consideration for federal listing, and any poisoning or draining plans must protect it.

## Key Findings

1. **Red shiner status was disputed.** The Team could not agree whether [[Red Shiner]] in Aravaipa Creek was a current problem. Minckley treated the arrival itself as the threshold and recommended a worst-case assumption. Stefferud and Propst advised waiting until native declines fall outside natural fluctuation. The Team agreed only that the issue required close watching and readiness to act.

2. **Rotenone trigger debated.** Minckley proposed a [[Rotenone]] treatment if a survey found zero [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]], provided the zero result was a true absence and not a sampling artifact. Propst suggested a trend aberration combined with high red shiner numbers. The Team agreed that a 100 percent kill was the only acceptable goal. Members doubted whether it was achievable in the Aravaipa system, and noted that nonnatives may be more resistant to poison than natives. Alternatives discussed included [[Antimycin]], chlorine, and sodium cyanide.

3. **Action items for Aravaipa Creek.** The Team agreed to:
   - Survey red shiner distribution, with a field trip on December 8-9, 1990.
   - Start [[Monitoring Protocol|monitoring]] immediately and continue it indefinitely. Monitoring should run monthly from March to October and less often in winter, covering adults, juveniles, and larvae. It is estimated to take 2 field days per effort with 3 people. [[The Nature Conservancy]] offered half the effort, and [[Francisco Abarca]] agreed to draft the protocol.
   - Build three [[Barrier Construction|barriers]]: a permanent lower barrier (designed by [[Bureau of Reclamation]], with no construction funding yet), a temporary barrier near Turkey Creek, and a possible middle buffer barrier.
   - Assess the genetic significance of Aravaipa populations within the species, using about $33,000 already available for the barrier and biological studies.
   - Begin [[Captive Propagation]] of spikedace, loach minnow, and the other five native fishes.
   - Form an interagency oversight group for Aravaipa management.

4. **Barrier effects.** The lower barrier would block native adults and larvae from returning upstream, a cost Minckley judged to be insignificant. It would also stop nonnative adults and larvae from spreading upstream. Jensen questioned whether barriers were allowed in wilderness. Stefferud noted that wilderness law does not prohibit them and that the Forest Service has built nonnative fish barriers in wilderness.

5. **Nonnative removal beyond red shiner.** Burton noted that bullheads are very hard to poison. The team agreed to resurvey stock tanks in the drainage for exotics. More than 100 tanks lie on the south side, with only one green sunfish found and eliminated there. One tank on the north side has nonnatives, but the landowner refused permission to poison it.

6. **Pupfish recovery criteria.** The Team agreed there should be no [[Delisting Criteria|delisting criteria]] for *C. m. macularius* or *C. m. eremus*. Delisting was judged unlikely to be possible because of insoluble threats and limited habitat. Downlisting criteria for *C. m. macularius* were rewritten into a three-tier system of recovery populations across California, Arizona, and the Colorado Delta, plus the Rio Sonoyta. Tier 2 populations must survive 10 years without human intervention. Tier 3 populations may come and go, but the required total must be maintained for 10 years. Security must include water rights as well as habitat, demonstrated over 10 years.

7. **Minimum viable population.** The Team raised the viable population threshold from 500 to 1,000 overwintering adult pupfish, referencing [[Minimum Viable Population|Soule's 50-500 rule]] plus a buffer. For *C. m. eremus*, a backup population will be established at Organ Pipe National Monument.

8. **Plan revisions.** Objections were raised to a recommendation that all nonnative fish be removed from pupfish habitat, and the Team proposed case-by-case removal instead. The Team also asked for site-specific monitoring protocols and questioned the sampling of 50 pupfish during biannual monitoring, which appeared to belong to the five-year genetic monitoring.

9. **Research and funding.** A Master's project by Jason Dunham on pupfish [[Population Genetics]] will model genetic drift in reintroduced populations. The Team agreed to drop a larval drift study as low priority and redirect its budget. Options considered for funding included [[Bureau of Land Management]] challenge cost-share funds (already fully allocated for FY91), Forest Service money, and possibly the Kellogg Foundation.

10. **Team membership and next steps.** Propst, Juarez-Romero, Burke, J. Stefferud, and Minckley were confirmed as continuing members. Invitations were planned for Simms and Abarca, and the vacant seat left by Bill Kepner remained open. Jim Brooks, Phil Pister, Richard Strauss, and Dean Hendrickson were suggested. Minckley will redraft the Aravaipa letter to the Regional Director for member comment. No date was set for the next meeting, whose likely topic is the draft revised [[Gila Topminnow]] recovery plan.
