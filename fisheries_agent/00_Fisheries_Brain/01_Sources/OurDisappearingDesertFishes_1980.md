# Our disappearing desert fishes

## Executive Summary

This article by David L. Soltz (California State University, Los Angeles) surveys the evolutionary history, ecological diversity, and conservation crisis of North American desert fishes, with a focus on the [[Death Valley System]] and the [[Colorado River]] Basin. Following the end of the Pleistocene (~10,000–20,000 years ago), extensive pluvial lakes and rivers across the American Southwest dried into isolated springs, streams, and marshes, stranding fish populations that subsequently diverged into numerous endemic species and subspecies — most famously the [[Pupfish|pupfishes]] (genus *Cyprinodon*). The piece frames these isolated aquatic "islands" as a natural laboratory for evolution comparable to the Galápagos, while documenting the severe anthropogenic threats — water diversion, groundwater mining, and introduction of exotic species — that have made desert fishes disproportionately represented among U.S. endangered species (21 of 23 at time of writing).

## Background & Description

- **Pupfish (*Cyprinodon* spp.)**: Small-bodied cyprinodontids; species vary in shape, markings, and physiological tolerances shaped by isolation. The [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) is the smallest of all pupfish, lacks vertical side markings, has **completely lost paired pelvic fins**, and shows non-aggressive behavior without the territorial breeding displays typical of the genus.
- **Colorado River Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): A giant predatory cyprinid, historically reaching lengths of **up to 6 feet**; known locally as the "Colorado salmon."
- **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*) and **Razorback Sucker** (*[[Xyrauchen texanus]]*): Both exhibit a pronounced dorsal hump immediately behind the head — a hydrodynamic adaptation that forces the body against the substrate, allowing the fish to hold position in fast-flowing water while feeding.
- **Speckled Dace** (*Rhinichthys osculus*): Widespread small cyprinid tolerant of variable desert stream conditions.
- Physiological extremes tolerated by Death Valley pupfishes represent **records for freshwater fishes**:
  - Temperature tolerance: 33.7°F to 107.6°F (brief exposure)
  - Salinity tolerance: up to ~70 parts per thousand (twice seawater)
  - Dissolved oxygen tolerance: as low as 0.5 ppm

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat types** described include remnant Pleistocene lakes (e.g., [[Pyramid Lake]], former [[Lake Manley]] in Death Valley), large regulated rivers (Colorado, Gila), small desert streams (e.g., [[Aravaipa Creek]]), marshes (e.g., [[Cottonball Marsh]]), and thermal springs (e.g., [[Ash Meadows]], [[Devils Hole]], [[Big Spring]]).
- **Aravaipa Creek, Arizona** supports a uniquely diverse native fish community of seven species (five minnows, two suckers), including two Gila River System endemics: [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), which coexist via niche segregation in feeding and habitat use.
- **Environmental variability** is extreme: Death Valley System streams range from 32°F to 113°F seasonally, with daily swings up to 45°F; salinity ranges from freshwater to hypersaline; dissolved oxygen can crash to near zero on hot summer nights.
- **Flash flooding** is a major natural disturbance; fish populations respond with rapid post-flood spawning (e.g., Amargosa River teeming with young pupfish two weeks after a 1972 flood event).
- **Isolation-driven speciation**: More than 20 pupfish populations in the Death Valley System have diverged into 10 distinct species/subspecies. Duration of isolation correlates with degree of divergence — the highly isolated [[Owens Pupfish]] and [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (>10,000 years) are most divergent, while the intermittently-connected [[Amargosa Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis*, isolated 400–5,000 years) has diversified only into subspecies.
- **Colorado River regulation**: Damming has converted the river into channelized, cold-water-releasing reservoirs lacking natural seasonal flow variation, eliminating most natural spawning/feeding habitat for native big-river fishes; remaining strongholds are largely restricted to the Grand Canyon and portions of the upper basin.

## Key Findings

- **21 of 23** federally endangered fish species in the United States (at time of writing) are desert-dwelling species, reflecting the disproportionate vulnerability of these isolated ecosystems.
- **Habitat loss via water diversion** has eliminated native fish from portions of nearly every desert stream, directly implicated in the endangered status of the [[Owens Pupfish]], [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), [[Moapa Dace]] (*Moapa coriacea*), [[Colorado River Squawfish]], and [[Humpback Chub]].
- **Groundwater mining** caused local extinction of the [[Pahrump Killifish]] (*Empetrichthys latos*) from its native valley and directly threatened the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] by lowering water levels over the limestone shelf critical for feeding/spawning — resolved by the 1976 U.S. Supreme Court decision affirming a National Park Service federal water right.
- **Exotic species introductions** drove the extinction of the [[Ash Meadows Killifish]] (*Empetrichthys merriami*, last recorded 1948) following introduction of mollies and mosquitofish, and caused decline of [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) via competition with mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*).
- **Hybridization threat**: Introduced coastal chub (*Gila orcuttii*) has hybridized with native [[Owens Chub]] (*Gila bicolor snyderi*) and [[Mojave Chub]] (*Gila bicolor mohavensis*), leaving only one genetically pure population of each subspecies remaining.
- **Conservation infrastructure**: The Nature Conservancy has established four preserves (Big Spring/Ash Meadows, Aravaipa Canyon [transferred to Defenders of Wildlife], Patagonia-Sonoita Creek Sanctuary, Canelo Hills-Cienega) protecting relatively intact native desert fish communities alongside associated endemic flora.
- The author underscores that preservation of **entire aquatic habitats with reliable water supply** — rather than piecemeal species protection — is the essential strategy for desert fish conservation, given that neither the species nor their habitats can be regenerated once destroyed.
