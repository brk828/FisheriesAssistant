# Spring Cavefish (Chologaster agassizi): Life History, Distribution, and Population Estimates in Southern Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #amblyopsidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey Biological Notes report (No. 104, May 1978, by [[Philip W. Smith]] and [[Norbert M. Welch]]) summarizes the taxonomy, life history, distribution, and conservation status of the [[Spring Cavefish]], *[[Chologaster Agassizi]]*. The species is a small, nocturnal, eyeless-in-function fish that bridges epigean and hypogean life, occurring mainly in springs and spring runs across parts of Tennessee, Kentucky, southern Illinois, and Missouri. The authors combine census data from three recent reports with a literature review. Mark-recapture estimates for eight springs in Union County, Illinois, gave an overall population of 785 ± 314 fish (excluding one anomalous spring), which the authors describe as crude but the only figures available. The species is rare to uncommon across most of its range, with the exception of an abundant population at Rich Pond, Kentucky. The authors recommend continued protection on the [[Shawnee National Forest]] and caution about groundwater contamination and a proposed reservoir.

## Background & Description

The genus name *Chologaster* means "mutilated belly," referring to the absence of pelvic fins. [[Agassiz 1853]] described the genus from South Carolina, and [[Putnam 1872]] described *C. agassizi* from a well in Wilson County, Tennessee. [[Forbes 1882]] described the Illinois population as *C. papilliferus* on the basis of color differences. [[Woods and Inger 1957]] synonymized *C. papilliferus* under *C. agassizi*, a arrangement generally followed later, although [[Clay 1975]] maintained the two as distinct. [[Jordan and Evermann 1927]] proposed *Forbesella* for the hypogean form, later replaced by *Forbesichthys* (Jordan 1929) because the name was preoccupied.

Key anatomical and behavioral literature includes [[Rosen 1962]] on amblyopsid anatomy and relationships, [[Poulson 1963]] on cave adaptation and metabolic rates, and [[Eigenmann 1909]] on eye structure. The species' eyes appear functional but serve only to distinguish light from darkness ([[Hill 1969]]). Fish are subterranean: they emerge at dusk and retreat underground an hour or two before dawn.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and behavior.** Surface activity in southern Illinois correlates strongly with spring flow and season. During dry periods some springs stop flowing, and others hold few or no fish. In the [[LaRue-Pine Hills Ecological Area]], fish occur year-round but are less numerous from January to March, and no gravid females were seen from January through May. Fish are often found in spring runs, tile pools, and the swamp at the base of the bluffs, especially in winter, when increased spring output and cold water appear to favor them.

- **Negative phototaxis** is strong, but **[[Thigmotaxis]]** (seeking body contact with surfaces or cover) is stronger ([[Weise 1957]]).
- Fish rely on taste rather than sight or smell to judge food, using clusters of head neuromasts to locate items.
- Agitation of the water frightens fish, which dash for cover.
- Fish were marked with tattoo ink to test for underground exchange between springs. None was observed.

**Temperature tolerance.** Weise (1957) found the species tolerates a wide temperature range without acclimation, an adaptation useful for fish swept into warmer surface waters.

**Food and feeding.** In Union County, Illinois, fish feed almost exclusively on the amphipod *[[Gammarus Troglophilus]]*, which is not known from other spring cavefish localities. Captive fish refused everything but Gammarus, including *Asellus*. In Kentucky, [[Hill 1969]] examined nearly 4,000 specimens and found that surface fish eat mostly chironomids plus small numbers of copepods, oligochaetes, nematodes, and ostracods, while adult and half-grown cave fish feed almost solely on other spring cavefish. Many young cave fish had no food at all.

**Predators and parasites.** Two of 30 [[Central Mudminnow]] (*Umbra limi*) examined at Pine Hills contained cavefish. Other predators include some fishes, water snakes, and aquatic birds and mammals. Small leeches were found attached to some fish. An endemic tapeworm, *[[Proteocephalus Chologasteri]]*, was described by Whittaker and Hill (1968), and Dr. George Garoian found 71 percent of fish examined from springs parasitized by tapeworms and other internal parasites.

**Water chemistry.** Most springs tested in 1973 were relatively free of pollution, but two showed elevated ammonia and phosphate, likely from animal wastes and fertilizers.

## Key Findings

### Reproduction and Growth

- Reproduction remains largely unknown. Attempts to strip eggs and sperm failed (Weise 1957; Welch).
- Most spawning-ready fish appear to disappear in late winter, presumably spawning underground, probably between January and April.
- Weise (1957) counted 80, 95, 101, 202, and 285 eggs in five females over 43 mm total length. Eggs averaged 0.25 mm in diameter in September and 1.5–2.0 mm near maturity.
- A 36.8 mm standard-length female with eggs was found in June, possibly unable to go underground.
- Scale primordia appear at about 6 weeks of age, and squamation is well developed by 11–12 weeks ([[Hill 1971]]).
- Growth is erratic, but overall estimated at 10–20 mm per year. Most fish probably live no more than 3 years. Fish appear sexually mature in their second year, and some individuals exceed 70 mm standard length.

### Distribution

- Range extends from south-central Tennessee northward through central and western Kentucky and westward across the Shawnee Hills of southern Illinois to Scott County, Missouri. The range was long thought disjunct, but recent collections from intermediate areas suggest it is likely continuous.
- Illinois records include single or few springs in Jackson, Johnson, Hardin, and Pope counties, and "many springs" plus one cave in Union County.
- Kentucky records are from Edmonson (including Mammoth Cave), Livingston, Logan, Lyon, Todd, Trigg, and Warren counties, with the Warren County population at Rich Pond the most notable.
- Tennessee records span roughly eight counties, including the type locality in Wilson County.
- An attempted introduction of 36 individuals in an Adams County, Illinois, cave spring failed; the fish were presumably washed out.

### Population Estimates (Union County, Illinois, 1973)

- A total of 180 fish from 10 springs was dipnetted and marked with colored printer's ink; recaptures occurred in 7 springs.
- Estimates used the Petersen formula, with the assumptions listed in the source (e.g., random sampling, stable population, marks retained).
- Summary of Table 1 estimates (marked fish, population ± 1 SE):
  - Spring 1: 15 marked, 63 ± 28
  - Spring 2: 31 marked, 180 ± 123
  - Spring 3: 46 marked, 302 ± 201
  - Spring 4: 1 marked, estimate 1
  - Spring 5: 7 marked, 18 ± 16
  - Spring 6: 12 marked, 250 ± 539
  - Spring 7: 56 marked, 73 ± 6 (excluded; fish stranded in tile, 88% recaptured)
  - Spring 8: 12 marked, 96 ± 180
  - Overall, excluding Spring 7: 124 marked, **785 ± 314**, 27% recaptured
  - Cave Spring Cave (estimate by [[Jeffrey J. Webb]]): 35–45
- Estimates were similar for 1–2 week and 6-month intervals. The authors stress the estimates are crude, cover only easily sampled springs, and likely understate the population because the largest spring could not be censused.

### Conservation

- In 1969 the Illinois Department of Conservation nominated the species for protection, and the Illinois General Assembly passed a bill limiting overcollection. The U.S. Forest Service also barred taking it on the Shawnee National Forest.
- The species now appears not to face imminent extirpation in Illinois, given new localities, but the Union County population is vulnerable to nighttime dipnetting. The authors suggest watching a gene pool that may number fewer than a thousand individuals.
- Rich Pond, Kentucky, supports an unusually abundant population (one museum series exceeds 1,000 specimens), where natural predation is seasonally heavy.
- Threats include groundwater contamination (ammonia, phosphate) and a periodically proposed reservoir on [[Clear Creek]] that could inundate spring mouths or disrupt groundwater flows.
- Recommendations include retaining existing restrictions and capping tiles with hinged metal grating to deter human collectors and predators.
