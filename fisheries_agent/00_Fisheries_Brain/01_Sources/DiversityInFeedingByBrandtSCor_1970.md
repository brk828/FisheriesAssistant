# Diversity in Feeding by Brandt's Cormorant Near San Diego

## Executive Summary
This classic study by Hubbs, Kelly, and Limbaugh (1970) documents the remarkable habitat and dietary diversity of **[[Brandt's Cormorant]]** (*Phalacrocorax penicillatus*) foraging near La Jolla and San Diego, California. Based on ~25 years of field observation and stomach-content analysis of shot birds, the authors show that this single seabird species exploits at least four distinct marine habitats — **kelp beds**, **open-water/pelagic zones**, **sandy-bottom shelf**, and **rocky reef/miscellaneous zones** — each characterized by a different assemblage of prey fish. Combined with otolith data from **[[Western Gull]]** pellets (Martini 1966), the total prey list comprises **35 fish species in 31 genera and 18 families**, nearly all of minor commercial value. The paper also documents cormorant diving depths (to 15 m), foot-only underwater propulsion, mixed-species feeding associations (with sea lions and gulls), and mass mortality events linked to red tide-poisoned prey.

## Background & Description
- **Study location:** La Jolla Caves cliff rookery (Scripps Institution of Oceanography), Point Loma, and Islas Los Coronados, Baja California, Mexico — all within the San Diego region of the Southern California Bight.
- **Study period:** Field observations 1944–1969 (most intensive 1944–1948); stomach samples collected 1952, 1955, and 1969.
- **Population context:** ~2,000 cormorants (99% Brandt's, 1% [[Double-crested Cormorant]], *Phalacrocorax auritus albociliatus*) occupied the La Jolla rookery in the 1940s; declined sharply by 1961 (attributed to pollution, pesticides, and human disturbance), with a later partial recovery to ~400 birds by 1962 (Martini 1966).
- **Nesting ecology:** Brandt's Cormorants nested on nearly vertical cliff faces (atypical for the species, which usually nests on sloping surfaces), while Double-crested Cormorants used adjacent sloping, vegetated ground — indicating strong habitat segregation between the two congeners in foraging, roosting, and nesting.
- **Methodology:** Direct visual observation of foraging flights/rafts from shore, plus dissection of cormorant stomachs and gular pouches (Table 1), cross-referenced against otolith remains recovered from [[Western Gull]] regurgitated pellets near the same rookery (Martini 1966).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The study frames Brandt's Cormorant as a **generalist, vision-dependent predator** exploiting multiple nearshore continental-shelf habitats within a ~10–50 mile daily foraging range:

- **[[Kelp Forest]] habitat** (*Macrocystis pyrifera* beds off Point La Jolla): Birds fed on midwater fishes associated with kelp fronds; feeding rafts were broken up by dense kelp canopy, forcing birds to use canopy openings.
- **Open-water / pelagic habitat** (La Jolla Bay): Birds formed dense, fast-moving "rafts" of frantic, diving birds apparently pursuing schooling fish such as anchovies or sardines. Movement was achieved by continuous leapfrogging of trailing birds to the raft's front edge — an unusual collective foraging strategy.
- **Sandy-bottom shelf habitat** (Del Mar to Oceanside, depths to ~50 m): Entire colonies made abrupt, unexplained mass shifts to this distant habitat for days at a time, feeding on benthic flatfish and sculpins.
- **Rocky reef / mixed habitat** (Islas Los Coronados): Diet included open-water species (jack mackerel) alongside reef/kelp fishes, suggesting opportunistic feeding near reef-kelp interfaces.
- **Interspecific associations:** Cormorants were observed foraging alongside **[[California Sea Lion]]** (*Zalophus californianus*), gulls, and pelicans, sometimes preferentially joining actively feeding sea lion groups over merely migrating ones — suggesting facultative commensal foraging or prey-location cueing.
- **Diving behavior:** Scuba observations recorded cormorants diving with sea lions to depths of 9–15 m in La Jolla Submarine Canyon and elsewhere; propulsion was by **foot action only**, with no confirmed underwater wing use, contradicting earlier claims (Dawson 1923).
- **Mortality event:** A red tide episode (Sept 1955) caused mass cormorant die-offs near La Jolla–Del Mar, coincident with a mass mortality of **[[Queenfish]]** (*Seriphus politus*), implicating trophic transfer of algal toxins from poisoned prey to birds.
- **Otolith cross-validation:** Fish otoliths in [[Western Gull]] pellets near the rookery were used to infer additional cormorant prey (gulls cannot dive to the depths where several species live), extending the known dietary breadth to species from 20–50+ m depth.

## Key Findings

### Kelp-Bed Feeders (8 birds, 33 fish, 5 species / 4 families)
| Species | Family | Common Name | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Oxyjulis californica* | Labridae | [[Señorita]] | Dominant kelp-bed species; most numerous prey item (21 individuals) |
| *Chromis punctipinnis* | Pomacentridae | [[Blacksmith]] | Abundant in kelp |
| *Brachyistius frenatus* | Embiotocidae | [[Kelp Perch]] | Essentially endemic to kelp habitat |
| *Phanerodon furcatus* / *P. atripes* | Embiotocidae | [[White Seaperch]] | Common in kelp and inshore habitats |
| *Sebastes miniatus* | Scorpaenidae | [[Vermillion Rockfish]] | Juvenile; one of few rockfishes using kelp midwater |

- Notably **absent**: cryptic, sedentary kelp-canopy fishes (kelp pipefish, kelp clingfish, striped/giant kelpfish, kelp gunnel) despite their abundance in the habitat — suggesting cormorants target actively swimming/free-moving prey rather than cryptic or spiny species, supporting a vision-based hunting strategy.

### Miscellaneous / Reef Feeders (Islas Los Coronados)
- **Near-surface/pelagic:** [[Jacksmelt]] (*Atherinopsis californiensis*) and [[Topsmelt]] (*Atherinops affinis*), family Atherinidae.
- **Mixed reef/kelp/open-water diet:** [[Blacksmith]] + [[Kelp Perch]]; [[Jack Mackerel]] (*Trachurus symmetricus*, family Carangidae) + [[Giant Kelpfish]] (*Heterostichus rostratus*, family Clinidae, red-brown color phase); *Embiotoca* sp. seaperch + [[Señorita]].

### Otolith-Derived Prey List (via Western Gull pellets, Martini 1966)
Extends confirmed/probable Brandt's Cormorant diet with deeper-water and additional inshore taxa across families: Engraulididae ([[Northern Anchovy]], slough anchovy), Merlucciidae ([[Pacific Hake]]), Bothidae ([[Pacific Sanddab]]), Serranidae (spotted bass), Atherinidae ([[Grunion]]), Haemulidae ([[Sargo]]), Sciaenidae ([[White Croaker]]), Embiotocidae (walleye surfperch, black seaperch, pile perch, rubberlip seaperch, pink seaperch, shiner seaperch), Scorpaenidae ([[Shortbelly Rockfish]]), Cottidae (*Icelinus* sp.), Gobiidae ([[Bluespot Goby]]), Batrachoididae (plainfin and specklefin midshipman), Ophidiidae (basketweave and spotted cuskeel).
- Total otolith counts: **P (cormorant-attributed) = 402; possibly gull = 18; ambiguous P or L = 78** (excluding 39 unidentified).

### Aggregate Diversity
- **35 total fish species, 31 genera, 18 families** documented or inferred as Brandt's Cormorant prey in the San Diego region — spanning benthic flatfishes, sculpins, midwater schooling fishes, reef/kelp fishes, and deep-shelf species to ~50 m.
- Diet is predominantly composed of species of **little to no commercial importance**, contrasting with earlier literature (Schorger in Palmer 1962) that emphasized commercially significant species (Pacific herring, cabezon, bocaccio).
- No invertebrates were found in cormorant stomachs (contra earlier reports of crab/shrimp), though invertebrates (notably goose barnacles, *Mitella polymerus*) dominated associated Western Gull pellets, attributable to gulls alone.

### Behavioral/Physiological Notes
- Underwater propulsion is **foot-only**; no confirmed wing use while swimming (contra Dawson 1923), confirmed via field and captive (Sea World) observation.
- Dive depths recorded via scuba: routinely 3–15 m, consistent with continental-shelf foraging range.
- Mass, colony-wide shifts between distant feeding grounds (kelp vs. sandy shelf) occurred abruptly and synchronously, with no identified environmental trigger — flagged by the authors as an unresolved behavioral phenomenon.
