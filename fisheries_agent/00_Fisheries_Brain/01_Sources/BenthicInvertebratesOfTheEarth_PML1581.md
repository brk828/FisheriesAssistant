# Benthic Invertebrates of Coachella Canal

#invertebrate-zoology #freshwater-ecology #benthic-macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This study, published in *California Fish and Game*, surveys the benthic macroinvertebrate community of the earthen [[Coachella Canal]] in California. Samples were collected at a set of stations in late 2023-era field work described as October and November sampling, and identified 17 taxa in the first sampling period, with two odonate taxa added during qualitative sampling after de-watering. The fauna was dominated numerically and by biomass by the introduced [[Corbicula Fluminea]] (Asiatic clam), with [[Oligochaeta]] and [[Chironomidae]] prominent in some habitats. The authors judge the earthen canal's invertebrate fauna to be depauperate, attribute this mainly to uniform flow, unstable sandy substrates and few cover objects, and predict that concrete structures will support a different, periphyton-based community.

## Background & Description

The paper concerns the Coachella Canal, an irrigation conveyance in the Coachella Valley, California, that the authors describe as earthen for most of its length, with concrete lining at check structures, siphons and bridge crossings. The work was framed as part of an inventory of benthic invertebrates in the canal, and the authors cite earlier fishery inventories of the canal and adjacent ponded areas, indicating the study sits within a broader resource assessment for the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation.

Sampling used [[Macroinvertebrate Sampling]] of benthic material at stations along the canal, with densities expressed per square meter and biomass estimated from organism sizes. Qualitative sampling, including drift nets and collections after de-watering, supplemented the quantitative work.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydraulics and channel form**
- Water velocity was relatively uniform across most open-channel stations (given in the source as roughly 0.3-0.7 m/s), slower near banks and immediately upstream of structures.
- East and west (bank) depths were less than 1 m; mid-channel depths were 2-3 m.
- Concrete structures created local turbulence and altered flow at check structures, siphons and bridge crossings.

**Substrate and cover**
- Substrates ranged from clayey sand and sand to pebble and gravel, with coarse and fine particulate organic matter (CPOM, FPOM) and detritus present at several stations.
- Submerged and emergent aquatic vegetation occurred in water shallower than 1 m; macrophytes were common near shore and absent or sparse in mid-channel zones.
- Concrete surfaces, including the sides and aprons of structures, provided the main hard substrate.

**Community structure**
- Seventeen aquatic invertebrate taxa were collected on 23-24 October, and two odonate species (in the genera *Erpetogomphus* and a gomphid) were collected on 10-13 November.
- Taxa included Corbicula fluminea, [[Oligochaeta]], [[Chironomidae]], [[Trichoptera]] (caddisflies, including *Smicridea* and *Nectopsyche*), an Hydracarina mite group, and Odonata.
- Shredders and collectors were associated with drifting detritus; microalgae and diatoms (periphyton) grew on concrete surfaces.

## Key Findings

**1. Community composition and dominance**
- Seventeen taxa were collected in the October sampling, with two additional odonate taxa collected in November, giving a documented fauna of about 19 taxa in the study.
- Corbicula fluminea was the most abundant and most important taxon by biomass, with Oligochaeta and Chironomidae also prominent.
- Near-shore samples contained more taxa than mid-channel samples, and the overall taxa mean was reported in the source table (Table 3), which lists only the most important entries of a fuller species-by-station list.

**2. Spatial patterns**
- The highest density occurred at the west-bank station with juvenile clams predominating.
- Large clams (greater than about 1.2 cm) averaged 1,277/m² across three replicate samples at the station with greatest clam biomass.
- Oligochaetes were abundant near both banks at several stations, and chironomids were abundant near shore at most stations.
- The west-bank station with the lowest water velocity supported two distinct benthic assemblages.

**3. Concrete-surface communities**
- Concrete surfaces exposed to periphyton growth were densely populated by invertebrates, including grazers and collectors.
- Densities on concrete were reported as high as about 1,500/m², and the caddisfly *Smicridea* was found on drifting materials caught in nets.

**4. Biological and sampling caveats**
- Qualitative sampling after de-watering added only two taxa (odonates) to the quantitative list, suggesting that the quantitative inventory captured most of the fauna.
- The authors note that some organisms have short life cycles and small body weights early in their development, so the data may underrepresent production relative to mean annual standing crops. They cite [[Secondary Production]] estimation methods (Winberg 1971; Rosenberg 1979) as the framework for relating biomass to production.

**5. Comparison with other systems**
- The authors describe the earthen canal's invertebrate fauna as depauperate compared with most lotic systems, reflecting limited taxa and low numbers of individuals.
- They attribute the scarcity mainly to uniform high-velocity flow, unstable sandy substrates, reduced organic matter and the absence of habitat complexity such as stones, rooted plants and woody debris.

**6. Conclusions and predictions**
- The earthen [[Coachella Canal]] supported a depauperate invertebrate fauna.
- Based on observations of concrete structures, the authors expect that concrete surfaces in the newly lined reaches will support dense periphyton and associated grazers, leading to higher local secondary production.
- They also suggest that lining the canal may reduce bank erosion and sediment disturbance, but that a lack of cover and rocky habitat in concrete reaches could limit habitat diversity.

**7. Literature cited**
- The reference list (about nine items) includes Hynes (1970) on running-water ecology, Merritt and Cummins (1978) on aquatic insects of North America, Rosenberg (ed., 1979) on benthic life histories, Schutz (1959), Winberg (1971) on production methods, and Prokopovich (1955) on canal fauna, along with California Department of Fish and Game fishery inventories of the canal.
