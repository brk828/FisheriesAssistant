# Studies of Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service compilation (Recovery Implementation Program Project No. 14, June 1997) binds three reports on the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the upper [[Colorado River]]: (A) growth and survival, (B) non-spawning movements, and (C) status and trends. One integrated study design and field effort, centred on spring-runoff [[Trammel Netting]] of backwaters with PIT tagging, supplied most of the data, sampled 1991–1994 with supporting records from 1990 and 1995.

The main conclusions are that growth slows markedly after about age 10 (older-age scale-based estimates were too high), adult annual survival is about 0.86 and not unusually low, and the population of roughly 600–650 subadults and adults is small. Recruitment depends on infrequent strong year classes, and the population appears vulnerable to extinction without management that raises reproductive and juvenile survival.

## Background & Description

*Ptychocheilus lucius* is a large, piscivorous cyprinid endemic to warm-water reaches of the Colorado River system. It is known for annual potamodromous spawning migrations and the long-distance downstream drift of larvae. Its historic range has been reduced by about 80%, and it was federally listed as endangered in 1967.

Previous age and growth work relied on scale analysis, which is unreliable for long-lived, older fish because outer annuli are compressed or lost. The studies therefore used recaptures of individually marked fish, made possible by [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT tags), which are internally implanted and read with a scanner. Tag-based marking was reported to show 98–100% verification after 20–24 months, with no growth difference from untagged controls (Burdick and Hamman 1993, cited in the report).

The "scare and snare" netting method was developed to capture fish seasonally concentrated in backwaters during spring runoff. A jon boat blocks the backwater mouth with a trammel net, then drives fish into it. Nets are pulled after 5–15 minutes of scaring effort.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Life-stage distribution:** Larvae drift downstream and rear in low-gradient backwaters of the lowest 103 km, above the Green River confluence. Adults concentrate upstream, with Westwater Canyon (RK 181–200) as the demarcation zone, and this canyon was not sampled.
- **Forage and prey:** Adult prey included [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*C. latipinnis*). Catch rates of these natives were significantly higher in the upper reach. Non-native minnows dominate lower backwater nurseries.
- **Mortality sources:** Choking on spines of ingested [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), an introduced species, is considered possibly the most serious new mortality factor. Other sources include stranding in gravel-pit ponds, angling and netting, avian predation by great blue herons and bald eagles, and high-water accidents.
- **Hydrology:** Spawning and hatching success appear linked to spring flows. Flow attenuation from dams and diversions may have reduced the frequency of strong cohorts.
- **Competitors and predators:** [[Red Shiner]] is implicated as a predator of larvae, and [[Fathead Minnow]] larvae as competitors.

## Key Findings

**Growth and age (Report A)**
- Mean annual growth was 82.0 mm/year at age 3, falling to 32.2 mm/year at age 6.
- Recapture-based increments: 42.7 mm/yr (400–449 mm TL), 22.2 mm/yr (500–549 mm), 8.7 mm/yr for fish ≥550 mm (range 3.9–15.0 mm/yr among size groups, not significantly different).
- Scale-based estimates overestimated real growth in fish older than about 10 years. Recapture-based growth was similar to earlier Carlin-tag studies (10–15 mm/yr), so the dangler-tag growth bias was judged unfounded.
- Simulated ages: 10–22 years (mean 15) to reach 600 mm; 16–30 (mean 25) to reach 700 mm; 20–40 (mean 32) to reach 800 mm. A 900 mm fish is at least about 32 years old, possibly 47–55 on average.
- Survival of fish ≥550 mm TL was estimated at 0.83–0.88, best fit 0.86, with stable-age and Cormack-Jolly-Seber corroboration. Survival of about 0.86 is not considered low for a long-lived fish.
- Among about 1,080 subadults and adults captured 1979–1995, only three exceeded 900 mm TL. The largest was 960 mm.

**Non-spawning movements (Report B)**
- Of upper-reach-tagged fish, 16.3% (15 of 92 recaptures) moved more than 10 km; 80% of these moves were upstream. No upper-reach fish were later recaptured in the lower reach.
- Of lower-reach-tagged fish, 58% (25 of 43 recaptures) moved more than 10 km; 68% of these moves were upstream. Between-reach moves from lower to upper made up 44% of these movements over five years, and this proportion rose each year.
- Displacement fell with size: fish under 550 mm averaged 33.6 km, while fish over 550 mm averaged 7.5 km.
- The smallest fish recorded moving from lower to upper reach was no longer than 449 mm TL.
- Body condition declined with size in the lower reach and rose with size in the upper reach. Forage catch rates were higher upstream. The authors concluded that adults move upstream toward food-rich areas.

**Status and trends (Report C)**
- Adult subpopulation (upper reach): 263 individuals (95% CI 161–440, CAPTURE) or 253 (95% CI 186–333, RECAP), about 4.0–4.2 fish per mile.
- Lower reach (subadults and adults): 344 (95% CI 196–604, CAPTURE) or 385 (95% CI 202–733, RECAP), about 3.1–3.4 fish per mile.
- Whole river (Palisade to Green River confluence): roughly 600–650 subadults and adults.
- Upper-reach adult survival: 0.86 (95% CI 0.66–0.95, CJS), consistent with 0.86 from the size-based method. RECAP gave 0.822 (95% CI 0.611–0.922).
- Upper-reach catch of adults over 550 mm did not change significantly, but catch of fish under 550 mm rose five-fold (0.07 to 0.35 fish/net, 1991–1994).
- Strong 1985–1987 year classes, with 1986 the strongest, recruited to the adult population. No similarly strong year class has been produced since 1987. Larval and YOY catch was significantly highest in 1986.
- Historical accounts (e.g., Jordan 1891, Granat interview) and the absence of fish under 450 mm in the upper reach since 1979 suggest a long-term downward trend.

**Management conclusions and recommendations**
- Mark–recapture effort should be repeated periodically: three effort years followed by three non-effort years, with the next effort starting in 1998.
- Raising adult survival from 0.86 to 0.90 would double the population in about 18 years.
- Reduce mortality from floodplain gravel-pit stranding, angling in the Grand Valley, channel catfish, and sampling (e.g., limit trammel sets to 15 minutes).
- Doubling recruitment (from strong year classes every 10 years to every five) could double the population in 5–6 years and grow it tenfold in 18 years.
- Bioenergetics studies are recommended to set recovery goals.
- The population is capable of recovering without augmentation if spawning and hatching limits are identified and addressed, and if adult habitat is adequate.
