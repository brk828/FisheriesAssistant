# Humpback Chub Life History and Ecology Data Collection Plan (Grand Canyon, 1990–1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft Data Collection Plan (June 15, 1995), prepared by [[BIO/WEST Inc]] for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Upper Colorado River Region, documents the field, handling, telemetry, habitat, drift, hydrology, water-quality and data-management methods used in a study of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the mainstem [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. It replaces a January 1991 preliminary plan and supplements the final report. Field work ran from October 1990 through November 1993. The plan is intended as a methodological reference for fishery investigators and is not a results report.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The contract objective was to "evaluate the ecological and limiting factors of all life stages of humpback chub in the mainstem Colorado River, Grand Canyon, and the effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations." The study objectives were to determine:

1. Distribution, abundance and movement
2. Resource use and availability (habitat, food)
3. Reproductive capacity and success
4. Survivorship of early life stages
5. Important biotic interactions with other species
6. The life history schedule

These objectives addressed Conservation Measures 5 and 7 of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion on Glen Canyon Dam operations.

**Study area.** A 364-km (226-mi) reach from Lees Ferry (RM 0) to Diamond Creek (RM 226) was divided into four study regions and 11 geomorphic reaches. A fifth region (Diamond Creek to Pearce Ferry, RM 280) was studied separately for the Hualapai Tribe. Sample sites were recorded to the nearest 0.05 river mile, with river miles referenced to Lees Ferry, which lies 15.8 river miles below the dam.

**Cooperating groups.** [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Arizona State University]], the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES), [[Grand Canyon National Park]], and the Hopi, Navajo and Hualapai tribes.

**Scale of effort.** 36 monthly field trips (12-, 16- and 20-day trips) were conducted. The 11 geomorphic reaches were subdivided into 34 sample strata of 3.2–19.5 km, with 8–16 strata randomly selected per monthly trip.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling framework.** A [[Stratified Random Sampling]] design approximated uniform spatial and temporal coverage. Effort was partitioned by season (winter, spring, summer, fall) and time of day (night, dawn, day, dusk), with day length adjusted by a sun-and-moon software program. Five major tributary inflows in Region II were treated as individual strata and sampled at least once per season.

**Gear used.** Six gear types were used: [[Electrofishing]], [[Gill Netting]] and [[Trammel Netting]], [[Seine]] nets, [[Minnow Traps]], [[Hoop Nets]] and angling. Gill and trammel nets were set in deep shoreline habitat, and electrofishing was conducted along shorelines by shoreline geomorphic type.

**Habitat classification.** Habitat was described at four nested levels: geomorphic reach, shoreline type (bedrock, cobble bar, debris fan, sand bar, talus slope, vegetation), hydraulic unit or macrohabitat (eddy, pool, rapid, return channel, riffle, run), and microhabitat parameters (depth, velocity, substrate, cover). Habitat maps were incorporated into a [[Geographic Information System]] built for GCES.

**Hydrology and water quality.** Flow was derived from five [[U.S. Geological Survey]] mainstem gages, several tributary gages and a [[Flow Routing Model]]. Water quality (temperature, turbidity, specific conductance, dissolved oxygen, pH) was logged with Hydrolab instruments, USGS stations and Ryan Tempmentors. Mainstem flow was classified as high (≥10,000 cfs) or low (<10,000 cfs), and ramping rate as high (≥300 cfs/hr) or low (<300 cfs/hr).

**Invertebrate drift.** Drift nets (560 µm mesh) were paired to sample surface and subsurface drift, with volumes filtered calculated from net mouth dimensions and velocity. Results were expressed as organisms per 100 m³ of water filtered.

## Key Findings

**Methods and protocols (plan content, not results):**

- **Fish capture.** Gill nets were 30.5 m long with 3.8 or 5.1 cm mesh. Trammel nets were 22.9 m long, with 30.5 cm outer walls and an inner panel of 1.3, 2.5 or 3.8 cm mesh. Nets were checked at least every 2 hr. Electrofishing used a Mark XX Complex Pulse System, with output reduced from 15–20 A and 300–350 V (1990–1991) to 8–10 A and 200–250 V (1992) after bruising was seen on trout.
- **Priority in netting.** Fish were removed in this order: endangered species, native species, trout, then other non-natives.
- **Handling.** Live wells were used for holding. Stressed fish were treated with salt solution. Mortalities were limited to 10 humpback chub per year, the number permitted under the federal collecting permit.
- **Processing.** Humpback chub were measured (TL, SL, FL), weighed, PIT-tagged and sexed. Morphometrics and meristics were recorded with calipers accurate to 0.01 mm. Scale samples were taken from fish under 200 mm TL.
- **Stomach pumping.** A nonlethal technique, adapted from the Seaburg design and first developed in 1992 on roundtail chub surrogates, was used on humpback chub. Fish were anesthetized with MS-222 (~250 mg/l).
- **Marking.** PIT tags were injected into fish of 175 mm TL and larger (reduced to 150 mm TL from February 1991). Juveniles were fin-punched from January 1993 by river subreach, and Arizona State University used fin clips to identify Little Colorado River capture regions.
- **Radio transmitters.** Two ATS models were used: 9 g (fish ≥450 g) and 11 g (fish ≥550 g). Signal extinction depth for 11 g external-antenna tags was 4.5 m at 50 m. An internal-antenna prototype (13.2 g) gave 3.2 m, 29% less, and was rejected.
- **Surgery.** Surgery was timed to 6 minutes or less from first incision to last suture. Incisions were along the linea alba or lateral to the midline. A custom "sleeved needle antenna guide" (SNAG) was developed. Absorbable 3-0 Maxon suture replaced CV3 Gore-Tex after inflammation was observed around the Gore-Tex sutures of the first six recaptured fish.
- **Telemetry analysis.** Movement was quantified as proportion of movement, Pm = BM/BT, using 1,831 analysis blocks (90% of total) with a combined 962.8 hr. Movement was defined as ≥25 m, the approximate triangulation error.
- **Remote stations.** Stations near the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence (KLCR, KRSH, KILR) and in Middle Granite Gorge (KBNE, KMGG) logged radio contacts. Average proportion of radio contacts (APRC) was arcsine-transformed for analysis.
- **Habitat maps.** 25 habitat maps were made at seven sites near the LCR (RM 59.75–64.2), and five bathymetry sites (A–E) were surveyed with a Super-Hydro system at 0.5 m contour intervals.
- **Drift analysis.** Statistical comparisons used ANOVA with P<0.05 significance, using Systat 5.03.
- **Data management.** Ten data forms were used, covering netting/trapping, electrofishing, seining, telemetry (observation and surveillance), chub meristics, water quality, juvenile habitat, stage reference, and drift. Error-trapping programs checked each trip's data before it was appended to aggregate dBASE IV files. Trip reports were issued 15 days after each trip. Copies of data were kept in three locations.

**Sample-size and database specifications (Tables 7–8):** Records include 16,643 netting/trapping samples, 4,612 electrofishing samples, 958 seine samples, 6,294 humpback chub morphometric records, 26,542 fish capture records, 1,600 surveillance records, 43,586 Datasonde records, and 5,161 Surveyor records. Detailed file structures are in Appendix C.

**Intended use.** The document is a reference for fishery investigators and administrators. It is not a results report and provides no population estimates.
