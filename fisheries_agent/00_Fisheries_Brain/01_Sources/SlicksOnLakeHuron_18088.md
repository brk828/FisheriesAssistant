# Slicks on Lake Huron

#limnology #marine-biology #surface-slicks

## Executive Summary

This short communication reports field observations of [[Surface Slicks]] on [[Lake Huron]] near [[Douglas Point]]. Using groups of [[Drift Bottles]] released simultaneously from a boat, the authors tracked how a cloud of bottles dispersed by [[Turbulent Diffusion]] and then re-concentrated when it encountered a slick. Bottles stayed in slicks for hours, and surface temperature measurements showed consistent temperature differences across slick boundaries. The paper was reprinted from *Nature* (December 1962) and was published with permission of Atomic Energy of Canada Limited.

## Background & Description

Slicks are smooth streaks of water on the surface, recognizable by eye, where [[Capillary Waves]] are absent and organic matter and other floating debris accumulate. The authors note that several mechanisms can produce slicks. All of them lead to surface material collecting through the confluence of surface water layers, which sink at the slick and leave floating material behind.

The study used small cylindrical jars, 5 cm in diameter and 8 cm high, with fluorescent yellow lids. The lids were weighted so they floated level with the water surface. The jars were released from a boat to follow dispersion by turbulent diffusion.

The authors are affiliated with the Great Lakes Institute at the University of Toronto, the University of Waterloo and the University of Windsor. They are G. T. Csanady, H. K. Ellenton and R. E. Deane. The paper cites the diffusion theories of [[Richardson-Stommel Diffusion Theory]] and [[Batchelor Theory]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The observations were made in May, under wind speeds of about 5 m.p.h. The authors report that slicks were most pronounced and persistent under these conditions, before the development of the [[Thermocline]]. Slicks were typically 1-2 m wide. Slicks disappeared when the wind changed.

Surface temperatures ranged from 8.0 to 14.0 °C. Temperature differed by 0.5 to 2.0 °C from one side of a slick to the other, often over a very short distance. On one occasion, under strong solar heating, the slick centre was 1.0 °C warmer than the warmer side. All observations were made within 500 m of shore in water 8-10 m deep.

The paper does not address fish, plankton or other biota. Its ecological relevance comes from the way slicks concentrate surface material and transport it along a narrow line.

## Key Findings

- **Relative turbulent diffusion:** In the early phase, the bottle cloud grew at a rate that increased with cloud size. This matches the Richardson-Stommel and Batchelor predictions.
- **Re-concentration at slicks:** When the cloud reached a slick, bottles re-collected along a line at an angle to the mean drift. When that angle was near 90°, the group became concentrated again, because lateral diffusion was much weaker than diffusion along the drift.
- **Retention within slicks:** Bottles moved along the slick at the local drift velocity and sometimes stayed for 5-6 hours. They left only when the slick disappeared with a change in wind.
- **Slick conditions:** Slicks were most pronounced and permanent at about 5 m.p.h. wind in May, before thermocline formation.
- **Temperature signature:** Slicks showed surface temperature differences of 0.5-2.0 °C across their edges, with a 1.0 °C warmer centre under strong solar heating.
- **Scope:** The study is a qualitative and descriptive field report. It gives no sample sizes beyond the bottle release groups and no statistical analysis.
