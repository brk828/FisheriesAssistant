# Some Effects of Spring Snowmelt Runoff on Aquatic Invertebrate Populations in a High Mountain Stream

#benthic-invertebrates #entomology #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Water Resources Series report (No. 50, April 1974, by William R. Good) is a thesis-based study of how a [[Benthic Invertebrate]] community in a high mountain stream responds to the [[Snowmelt Runoff]]. The study covered a 1 km reach of [[Nash Fork Creek]] in the [[Snowy Range]], Wyoming, with all data collected in 1970. The work combined benthos sampling, [[Drift Sampling]], and analysis of physical hazards, predation, and life-cycle adjustments. It found that drift rates rose with discharge, that behavioral drift was linked to temperature and suppressed by high flow, and that many insects breed or hatch during the runoff, which the author proposes as a survival strategy.

## Background & Description

- **Author and funding:** William R. Good, based on a thesis submitted to the Department of Zoology and Physiology, University of Wyoming. Partly funded by the Office of Water Research and Technology (U.S. Department of the Interior) through the [[Wyoming Water Resources Research Institute]] under the Water Resources Research Act of 1964 (Public Law 88-379).
- **Study area:** About 1 km of Nash Fork Creek, from the confluence with Telephone Creek at 3,048 m (10,000 ft) to a meadow section at about 2,984 m. A permanent tributary, [[Sally Creek]], joins at about 3,000 m. The stream runs through mature [[Subalpine Forest]] of Engelmann Spruce (*Picea engelmannii*) and Subalpine Fir (*Abies lasiocarpa*).
- **Physical setting:** Mean stream width at the beginning and end of the study was 5.65 m. Gradient ranged from 31% in the upper cascade section to 0.11% at the lower end. Substrate ranged from boulders and bedrock to sand and silt, with a primary substratum of 12-24 cm rubble.
- **Study sections:** Five sections were defined by gradient: section 1 (0-1.5%), section 2 (1.5-4.5%), section 3 (4.5-8%), section 4 (8-12%), and section 5 (more than 12%).
- **Sampling tools:** An enclosed [[Benthos Sampler]] (adapted from Waters and Knapp, 1961) enclosing 0.1 m², and drift nets of 0.1 m² mouth area. A [[Live-Sorting Apparatus]] separated active organisms from debris.
- **Objectives:** (1) describe seasonal abiotic change; (2) define runoff hazards to invertebrates; (3) describe life-history adaptations; (4) test whether runoff-related biomass loss could be measured in standing stock or in downstream drift loss.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and temperature (1970):**
- Runoff began on 18 May, with mean flow about 6 c.f.s. (0.17 c.m.s.).
- The peak was 125 c.f.s. (3.54 c.m.s.) on 25 June. Flow then declined through the summer.
- Water temperature rose from 0°C in mid-May to a daily range of 4.5-11.5°C on 25 June, and to 7-16°C on 21 July.
- Snow cover over the stream, present in mid-May, was gone by 4 June.
- Daily peaks arrived progressively later through the runoff as the contributing snowmelt altitude increased.

**Hazards identified:**
- **Physical damage** from moving bed material, including sliding, rolling, saltation, and bouncing of rubble.
- **Displacement** from habitat, through dislodgement or erosion of sand and silt patches. This produces [[Catastrophic Drift]].
- **Predation on drifting organisms** by fish and by the net-spinning [[Arctopsyche]] larvae.

**Biota and predators:**
- The native [[Longnose Sucker]] (*Catostomus catostomus*) is abundant below the study area and present in larger pools.
- Introduced trouts are present: [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta fario*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), and occasional hybrids. Predation data were not collected, but the author assumed these fish feed on drifting invertebrates.

## Key Findings

**Standing crop and benthos**
- Median total standing crop biomass was **3,600 mg/m²** for 1-4 June (early) and **5,980 mg/m²** for 27-29 July (late). These were not different at the .05 level (Mann-Whitney U-statistic).
- Early and late estimates differed significantly in sections 1, 2, and 5. Section 1 was larger early; sections 2 and 5 were larger late.
- In the early period, 5 of 10 section comparisons were significant. Section 1 exceeded sections 2, 3, and 5. Sections 2 and 4 exceeded section 5.
- In the late period, only section 1 versus section 5 differed significantly.
- Biomass generally declined with gradient, most clearly in early samples, probably because snow cover increased with gradient and reduced algal "windows".

**Drift**
- Drift rates for all invertebrates increased with discharge. Between 12-13 June and 22-24 June, day and night rates were each more than 20 times higher, while discharge rose about 5 times.
- Oligochaete and Tipulidae drift followed the same discharge trend. Tipulidae drift was higher at night.
- Oligochaete fragments made up a rising share of drifting biomass with discharge, correlated at **r = .9717** (significant at .05).
- Live-sortable taxa (mostly Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, Trichoptera, Chironomidae larvae, and Acari) rose with discharge to the 22-24 June peak, then fell to lower levels on 6-7 and 13-14 July. Night drift alone increased on 21-22 July.
- A net downstream loss of drifting invertebrates from the study area **could not be confirmed**.

**Behavioral drift and the Nocturnal Drift Index**
- Index = (Night − Day) / (Night + Day) drift rate.
- Single regressions were not significant at .05: discharge R² = .177, r = −.421; water temperature R² = .486, r = .696.
- Multiple regression: **R² = .86**, with the model Z = 0.0610X − 0.2034Y + 0.2984 (X = water temperature, Y = discharge in m³/s). Both coefficients were significantly non-zero at .05.
- Interpretation: behavioral drift increases with temperature and is suppressed at high discharge, possibly because invertebrates spend more effort holding position than foraging.

**Mortality and drift composition**
- About **11%** of drifting invertebrates were dead in samples taken 27 and 29 June. Table 5 lists 157 alive and 17 dead, and the author describes this as a minimum estimate.

**Drift/benthos ratios**
- Species with strong clinging adaptations (e.g., [[Rithrogena]] spp. and [[Ephemerella Doddsi]]) had the lowest and nearly equal drift/benthos ratios. *E. coloradensis*, which lacks a friction pad, had the highest of its group on the first two dates.
- Burrowing Tipulidae larvae showed low ratios on the first date and nearly equal high ratios on the last two dates.
- *Ephemerella inermis* and *Paraleptophlebia* sp. had high ratios, consistent with displacement from shrinking protected areas.

**Predation and Arctopsyche growth**
- Length-frequency data show two year classes of *Arctopsyche* in early samples. Over eight weeks, the younger cohort's mean length grew from 10.5 mm to 20.5 mm, and mean weight from 17.2 mg to 113.3 mg.
- The author concludes that most growth occurs during the runoff, and that the larvae act as an effective predator on drift.

**Life-cycle adjustments**
- *Diptera:* no pupae were found in early samples. Chironomidae larvae occurred in 45 of 52 early samples without pupae, while pupae were found in 40 of 45 late samples. The author interprets this as avoidance of the rising-phase peak.
- *Breeding (30 species, Hynes' classification):* 6 non-seasonal, 12 slow seasonal, and 12 fast seasonal. Of the 30, 18 bred during the period, 4 (all fast seasonal) were presumed not to breed, and 8 were unknown. Of 12 slow seasonal species, 11 bred during the period.
- *Hatching (48 species, presence of 1-2 mm young):* 5 hatched only in the rising phase, 6 only in the falling phase, 21 in both, and 16 in neither.
- *Redundancy:* Peak timing varied across 1967-1972, from 27 May (1969) to 25 June (1970). Peak discharge ranged from 56 c.f.s. (1969) to 247 c.f.s. (1971, Table 7). The text gives the upper value as 245 c.f.s., a minor discrepancy in the source.

**Community structure**
- The author hypothesizes that closely related congeners (*Baetis*, *Alloperla*, *Nemoura*, *Paraleuctra*, *Rhyacophila*) coexist because they use different runoff-survival strategies whose effectiveness varies from year to year. The author states that one year of data cannot support this hypothesis.

**Limitations noted by the author**
- Benthos samples had large standard errors, so only non-parametric tests were applicable.
- Some July non-sorting fractions were discarded. Only species with at least 50% live-sorting were included in July drift results.
- Live weights were back-calculated from preserved specimens using species-specific "b" values, and some "b" values were arbitrarily assigned.
- The [[Drift/Benthos Ratio]] is speculative because benthos and drift were not sampled simultaneously.
