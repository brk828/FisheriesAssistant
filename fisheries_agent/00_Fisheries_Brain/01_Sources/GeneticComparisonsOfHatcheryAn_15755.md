# Genetic Variation in Hatchery and Natural Stocks of Three Endangered Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the genetic structure of captive and wild stocks of three federally endangered, spring-dwelling desert fishes held at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]: the [[Leon Springs Pupfish]], the [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]], and the [[Pecos Gambusia]]. Using [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] at 24-28 presumptive gene loci, the authors found that heterozygosity and polymorphism in hatchery stocks matched those of their source populations. However, the captive stocks appear to have lost some rare alleles, probably because the founding groups were small. The authors conclude that spontaneously spawning species should not be managed with complacency. They recommend founding stocks with hundreds of individuals, monitoring genetic status and population size, and periodically adding wild genetic material.

## Background & Description

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service estimated that about 35 southwestern fish species might need artificial propagation to avoid extinction. The Dexter hatchery in New Mexico was the most intensive propagation program for endangered fishes at the time. It maintained 14 species in its ponds, and 22 species had been held there at various times over 15 years.

Management of captive gene resources is central to these programs, yet little was known about how captive fish populations compare genetically with their wild counterparts. Earlier comparative studies covered salmonids, the [[Desert Pupfish]], and the [[Razorback Sucker]]. This study addresses three small-bodied species that differ in management history:

- **Leon Springs Pupfish** (*Cyprinodon bovinus*): restricted to a short section of the Leon Creek drainage near Fort Stockton, Pecos County, Texas.
- **Comanche Springs Pupfish** (*Cyprinodon elegans*): restricted to a complex of interconnected springs and irrigation canals near Balmorhea, Texas, in an area of less than 80 km².
- **Pecos Gambusia** (*Gambusia nobilis*): a live-bearing poeciliid occurring in four widely separated spring systems in the Pecos River drainage of Texas and New Mexico.

All three species are listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. Hatchery stocks had been held for 6-8 years. Because these fishes typically produce more than one generation per year, the captive populations probably passed through 12 or more generations.

Hatchery species fall into two groups. Some, including these three, spawn spontaneously in large numbers in ponds. Others are spawned artificially by hormone injection and stripping. The authors note that the spontaneously spawning stocks had not been genetically assayed before this study, and that their short generation times could allow relatively rapid genetic change.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Founding history.** Each stock was founded from wild fish and placed directly into a 0.1-0.2-hectare hatchery pond. The founders included approximately equal numbers of males and females.

| Species | Founding date | Founders | Source site | Captive status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Comanche Springs Pupfish | November 1974 | About 40 adults | Irrigation canal carrying Griffin Spring water | Extirpated 1986 |
| Leon Springs Pupfish | January 1976 | 80 adults | Upper Leon Creek watercourse | Still maintained |
| Pecos Gambusia | November 1974 | 30-40 adults | Lower Leon Creek, Texas State Highway 18 bridge | Extirpated 1981 |

All three species reproduced spontaneously and exceeded 1,000 adults within one year of arrival. Population sizes above 500 adults were then maintained consistently. Captive populations were left largely undisturbed apart from annual transfers to newly prepared ponds.

**Wild population context.** The Comanche Springs Pupfish occupies a small, fragmented canal and spring system, and the Pecos Gambusia has widely separated populations. Both features make microgeographic genetic structure important for choosing source populations.

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- Wild fish were collected by seine from 12-18 August 1982. The hatchery Pecos Gambusia sample was taken in November 1981, when that population was extirpated.
- Fish were frozen on dry ice and stored at -60°C.
- Liver, epaxial muscle, and eye-and-brain tissue were analyzed by horizontal starch gel electrophoresis, with 24-28 presumptive loci examined.
- Average heterozygosity per individual was calculated with Nei's (1978) method. Loci were considered polymorphic if the most common allele had a frequency below 0.95.
- Hardy-Weinberg deviations were tested with a chi-square goodness-of-fit test with Levene's (1949) correction.

**Heterozygosity and variability (Table 1)**

- Comanche Springs Pupfish: natural sites averaged 0.051 ± 0.024 heterozygosity per individual (range 0.028-0.061). The hatchery stock averaged 0.046 ± 0.022.
- Leon Springs Pupfish: natural sites averaged 0.045 ± 0.024 (range 0.036-0.053). The hatchery stock averaged 0.045 ± 0.022.
- Pecos Gambusia: natural sites averaged 0.022 ± 0.015 (range 0.015-0.040). The hatchery stock averaged 0.021 ± 0.015.
- Mean number of alleles per locus, the proportion of polymorphic loci, and mean heterozygosity were similar between hatchery stocks, source sites, and all natural sites for all three species. The authors concluded that there was no significant change in overall genetic variability.

**Rare allele loss (Table 2)**

- One rare allele (frequency below 0.050) was absent from the Comanche Springs Pupfish hatchery sample, one from the Leon Springs Pupfish sample, and three from the Pecos Gambusia sample. All were present at source sites.
- Summed across species, five alleles were missing from the hatchery. Their average source-site frequency was 0.033 (range 0.011-0.047).
- If the hatchery frequency matched the source average, the probability of missing all five by sampling error was 3 × 10⁻⁵. At a hatchery frequency of 0.01, the probability was 0.02.
- The authors therefore conclude that the average frequency of these alleles has fallen below 0.01 in the hatchery, and that some rare-allele loss has occurred.

**Adh-A anomalies**

- Comanche Springs Pupfish showed a persistent heterozygote deficiency at the Adh-A locus across the seven wild samples. The hatchery sample showed a similar deficiency (χ² = 15.72, P < 0.001).
- The authors attribute this to a possible [[Wahlund Effect]] rather than inbreeding, since other loci showed no heterozygote deficiency. No null allele was detected.
- Adh-A frequencies also differed significantly between the hatchery and source sites in both pupfishes (Comanche: χ² = 7.2, P < 0.008; Leon: χ² = 9.3, P < 0.003). The authors describe these results as difficult to interpret because they are anomalous relative to the other loci.
- The Leon Springs Pupfish and Pecos Gambusia hatchery samples showed no significant Hardy-Weinberg deviations at any locus.

**Source population choice**

- Heterozygosity in the hatchery stocks could have been 18-48% lower or 12-60% higher had other natural sites been used as sources.
- The Comanche Springs Pupfish sample with the lowest heterozygosity (0.028) was only 46% of the maximum observed value and 55% of the mean. This illustrates how much microgeographic variation matters for choosing founders.

**Discussion and recommendations**

- The founding bottlenecks probably reduced effective population size (Ne) severely. However, a rapid population increase limits losses of overall heterozygosity, which explains why heterozygosity was retained. Losses of rare alleles, by contrast, persist after population growth.
- The Dexter hatchery now uses 200 or more adults in initial stocks whenever possible. [[Allendorf And Ryman 1987]] suggested a minimum of 25 females and 25 males, but the authors consider that guidance designed for artificially spawned fishes.
- For spontaneously spawning cyprinodontids, poeciliids, and some cyprinids (*Notropis* and *Gila*), the authors advise releasing roughly equal numbers of males and females. For poeciliids, a female-biased ratio may be advisable because females store sperm from multiple inseminations, so one pregnant female may carry offspring of several males.
- Captive stocks should be supplemented periodically with wild material, such as fertilized eggs or breeding adults. Allendorf and Ryman (1987) suggested that a 10% wild contribution every second or third generation would usually suffice. The authors add that infusions of about one effectively breeding wild individual per generation can substantially reduce allele loss and drift effects.
- Each species should be evaluated individually. Managers can monitor effective population size indirectly, or assess gene resources directly through [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] surveys.
- The best source population is usually the one with the highest genetic variability, though exceptions exist, such as populations with hybridization or genetically divergent stocks that warrant protection.

**Overall conclusion**

Captive stocks of these spontaneously reproducing endangered fishes at the Dexter hatchery have maintained genetic variability comparable to their wild source populations. Rare-allele loss is evident, however, so periodic supplementation with wild material and monitoring of [[Effective Population Size]] and [[Heterozygosity]] are needed to preserve adaptive potential.
