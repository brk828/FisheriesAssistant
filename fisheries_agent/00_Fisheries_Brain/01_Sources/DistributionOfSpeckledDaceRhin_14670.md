# Distribution of Speckled Dace Along Middle Sonoita Creek and Assessment of Effects of Proposed Railroad Abutment Removal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (Marie Sullivan, March 1995) reviews historical and recent records of [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) along middle [[Sonoita Creek]] in Santa Cruz County, Arizona, and evaluates the potential effects of a proposed [[Partners for Wildlife]] project to remove the most downstream railroad abutment within the [[Patagonia-Sonoita Creek Preserve]]. The removal is intended to restore natural hydrology and channel meandering, benefiting the federally endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] and riparian bird habitat. The Service concludes that project implementation will not have adverse long-term effects on the Sonoita Creek speckled dace population, while acknowledging probable short-term losses of individuals.

## Background & Description

- **Target species:** *Rhinichthys osculus*, a small cyprinid of riffle habitat. Its taxonomy is unsettled, with ongoing debate over subspecies or species partitioning (Minckley 1985).
- **Listing status:** On April 15, 1994, the Gila and Bill Williams basin forms were added to Category 2 of the candidate list. Candidate species receive no protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]], but the Service chose to assess project impacts to protect the wider Sonoita Creek ecosystem.
- **Project purpose:** Remove the southern (downstream) abutment (T. 22 S., R. 5 E., sec. 13) to eliminate an artificial channel constriction, allowing natural meandering, floodplain geomorphic processes, and riparian recruitment. The upstream abutment pair and the northern downstream abutment will remain.
- **Companion species:** The Intra-Service Biological Opinion (USFWS 1993) expects long-term benefits to the Gila Topminnow, which prefers low to moderate velocity backwaters, glides and pools. The project will eliminate the pool upstream of the abutment, to be offset by new meanders and backwaters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical distribution.** The species ranges from the Columbia River basin to the Gila River basin. Records in Sonoita Creek date to 1928 (Burt). The species was considered extirpated in 1959, a few individuals were found in 1963, and numbers rose after favorable rainfall in 1967. Population status is described as variable, tracking high-runoff streamflow. In the southern Gila basin, it persists only in Sonoita, Aravaipa and Cave creeks. The Sonoita population is the only remaining one in the Santa Cruz River watershed.

**Threats.** Dewatering, diversion, dam construction, channel and watershed erosion, non-native species introduction, and additional water development.

**Channel change.** In the late 1800s, Sonoita Creek flowed through a broad, marshy floodplain with low velocity and dense streamside vegetation. Land use and possibly climate have since channelized and entrenched the creek, particularly through the Preserve.

**Flow regime.** The creek is intermittent from about half a mile below Cottonwood Spring to Patagonia, perennial from Patagonia to [[Patagonia Lake]], and perennial for about seven miles below Patagonia Dam.

**Habitat use.** Speckled dace is frequently associated with swift, turbulent riffles over gravel-rubble substrate. Larger adults use low-velocity waters with cover from cut banks or debris. Spawning occurs in spring and late summer (bimodal cycle; John 1963). Pools provide refugia during low flow.

**Habitat by reach (November 11, 1994 survey):**
- Northern boundary to first abutments: low diversity, mainly sandy runs, a few short cobble riffles.
- First to second abutments: about 30% cobble/gravel riffle, 50% sand/gravel runs and riffles, remainder pools and backwaters; watercress mats provide the only notable cover.
- Second abutments to southern boundary: wide shallow runs and riffles, very few backwaters.
- Below the Preserve: broadened, braided floodplain with grazing-reduced banks and heavy sediment input from Temporal Gulch; about 25–30% suitable riffle habitat.
- Alum Canyon to Squaw Gulch: about 25% riffle habitat, increasing fine sediment.
- Below Squaw Gulch: 30–50% riffle habitat of small cobbles or large rocks.
- Highway 82 Rest Area to old USGS gaging station: 25–30% cobble/gravel riffles and runs; surface flow and gradient decrease.
- Upper reach to Patagonia Lake: mostly shallow sandy runs, riffles under 25%, embedded substrate.

Overall, about 30% of the creek between the sewage outlet and Patagonia Lake is suitable habitat, mostly within the roughly five miles between the northern railroad abutments and the old gaging station.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution:** Historical and recent records cluster in the vicinity of the upper abutments, from near Patagonia to the canyon above Patagonia Lake. No records exist below Patagonia Lake. Many older records (Chamberlain 1904; Miller 1961) are too vague for precise mapping, but they place fish "in the vicinity of Patagonia."
- **Lower abutment:** Speckled dace have been recorded near the lower abutment, but in significantly lower numbers than at other sites. Suitable habitat immediately below the abutment is minimal; only about 500 feet of suitable habitat lies within the roughly 0.3 mile between the abutment and the Preserve boundary.
- **Project effects on habitat:** Geomorphic influence is estimated to extend 0.25–0.3 mile downstream and 0.1 mile upstream of the southern abutment (Tashjian, pers. comm.).
- **Short-term risks:** Fish may be killed when abutment debris falls into the creek during detonation, or lost to disturbance during removal. Recontouring the cut bank may increase turbidity and deposition in the active floodplain. Recontouring outside the wetted channel is not expected to cause direct harm.
- **Incidental take:** The first amendment to the Intra-Service Biological Opinion (March 11, 1994) sets incidental take at 20 fish of any species. The Service will monitor take of both *P. o. occidentalis* and *R. osculus* during removal, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department will continue annual surveys under Section 6 of the Endangered Species Act.
- **Conclusion:** The Service finds no adverse long-term effect on the Sonoita Creek population, because records concentrate near the upper abutments and about 30% of the creek provides suitable habitat. Short-term losses are outweighed by long-term benefits to the Gila Topminnow and the downstream riparian ecosystem.
- **Data limitations:** Early habitat data are lacking, sampling was limited to one or two sites per collection, and the creek was never consistently surveyed, so temporal distribution change cannot be determined.
- **References:** The bibliography cites roughly 50 sources, mostly unpublished field notes, museum and Arizona State University specimen records, and Arizona Game and Fish reports. Key sources include Miller (1961), Minckley (1969, 1985), Gori (1993, 1994) and Lee et al. (1980).
