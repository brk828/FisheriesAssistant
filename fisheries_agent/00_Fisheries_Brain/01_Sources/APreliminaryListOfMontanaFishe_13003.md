## Executive Summary

This 1962 paper by J. D. Brown presents the most comprehensive statewide checklist of Montana fish fauna compiled to that date, superseding earlier partial surveys by [[Henshall 1906]] (13 families, 45 species), [[Schultz 1941]] (8 families, 23 species, Glacier National Park only), and [[Weisel 1955]] (11 families, 29 species, intermountain region only). Brown's list documents **17 families and 75 species**, based on approximately **800 collections** comprising more than **75,000 specimens** gathered across Montana's five major drainage systems: the [[Yellowstone Drainage]], [[Missouri Drainage]], [[St. Mary Drainage]], [[Clark Fork Drainage]], and [[Kootenai Drainage]]. Of the 75 species, **50 are considered native** to Montana, while **25 species across six families are classified as introduced (exotic)**. Notably, the [[Esocidae]] (pike family, represented by [[Northern Pike]]) and [[Poecilidae]] (topminnow family) are reported as **wholly non-native** to the state. Only three species — [[White Sturgeon]], [[Bigmouth Buffalo]], and sturgeon dace — are included based on documented presence rather than direct specimen collection, though their occurrence in Montana is described as indisputable. The specimen collection was deposited at Montana State College for future study, and the paper explicitly excludes subspecies (due to taxonomic uncertainty) and interspecific/intergeneric hybrids despite their known occurrence in wild populations.

## Background & Description

This is a faunal checklist rather than a morphological study; no detailed anatomical descriptions are provided for individual species. However, taxonomic organization by family implies characteristic morphological groupings:

- **[[Acipenseridae]] (Sturgeons)** — 3 species, including [[White Sturgeon]], [[Pallid Sturgeon]], and [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]]; cartilaginous, bony-plated body forms typical of Acipenseriformes.
- **[[Salmonidae]] (Salmonids)** — largest family after Cyprinidae with **13 species**, encompassing whitefishes ([[Prosopium]] spp., [[Coregonus Clupeaformis|Lake Whitefish]]), Pacific salmon ([[Kokanee]], [[Coho Salmon]]), trout ([[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Golden Trout]]), char ([[Brook Trout]], [[Dolly Varden]], [[Lake Trout]]), and [[Arctic Grayling]].
- **[[Cyprinidae]] (Minnows)** — most speciose family with **20 species**, including chubs, dace, shiners, and the introduced [[Common Carp]] and [[Goldfish]].
- **[[Catostomidae]] (Suckers)** — 9 species, distinguished by subterminal, ventrally-oriented sucking mouths adapted for benthic feeding.
- **[[Cottidae]] (Sculpins)** — 4 species, small benthic fishes with large pectoral fins and reduced/absent scales, typical of cold, clear streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Species distributions are mapped across Montana's five primary watersheds, reflecting distinct hydrogeographic and ecological zones:

- **[[Yellowstone Drainage]] (Y)** and **[[Missouri Drainage]] (M)**: Host the broadest diversity, including all three sturgeon species, [[Paddlefish]], and numerous cyprinids and catostomids adapted to warmer, larger river systems east of the Continental Divide.
- **[[Clark Fork Drainage]] (C)** and **[[Kootenai Drainage]] (K)**: West of the Continental Divide (Columbia River system), supporting distinct cold-water assemblages such as [[Torrent Sculpin]] (Kootenai only) and [[Slimy Sculpin]] (Clark Fork only).
- **[[St. Mary Drainage]] (S)**: A smaller northern drainage supporting species such as [[Spoonhead Sculpin]].
- **Cottidae** distribution illustrates strong drainage-specific endemism among sculpins: [[Mottled Sculpin]] is widespread ("All" drainages), while [[Slimy Sculpin]], [[Torrent Sculpin]], and [[Spoonhead Sculpin]] each occupy a single, distinct drainage — indicating strong biogeographic partitioning likely tied to postglacial colonization routes and thermal/habitat specialization.
- Collecting effort was **more intensive east of the Continental Divide**, meaning some western drainage diversity may be undersampled relative to eastern drainages.
- Introduced species (marked with asterisks in original text) such as [[Common Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Brown Trout]] reflect historical stocking practices altering the native fish community structure, particularly within [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes, 8 of unclear total introduced) and game fish stocking in [[Salmonidae]].

## Key Findings

- **Total diversity documented**: 17 families, 75 species — the most complete Montana fish checklist compiled up to 1962, roughly tripling the species count reported by [[Henshall 1906]].
- **Native vs. introduced composition**: 50 native species (66.7%) vs. 25 introduced species (33.3%) across six families, indicating substantial anthropogenic alteration of the state's ichthyofauna by the mid-20th century.
- **Wholly exotic families**: [[Esocidae]] and [[Poecilidae]] contain no native Montana representatives.
- **Most diverse native family**: [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows), with 20 species, followed by [[Salmonidae]] (13 species) and [[Catostomidae]] (9 species).
- **Undocumented-by-specimen species**: [[White Sturgeon]], [[Bigmouth Buffalo]], and sturgeon dace were included based on strong circumstantial/historical evidence rather than direct collection records.
- **Taxonomic scope limitations**: Subspecies and hybrids were deliberately excluded due to unresolved taxonomic validity, despite documented occurrence of hybrid populations in the wild — an acknowledged gap for future research.
- **Methodological basis**: Findings drawn from ~800 collections and >75,000 specimens, with material archived at Montana State College, establishing a voucher-specimen foundation for subsequent Montana ichthyological studies.
- **Nomenclatural standard**: The list adheres to the [[American Fisheries Society 1960]] naming conventions, aligning Montana's fish taxonomy with the broader North American standard of the era.
