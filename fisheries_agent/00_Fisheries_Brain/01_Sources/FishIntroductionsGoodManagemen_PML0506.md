# Introduced Fishes: Good Management, Mismanagement, or No Management?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #introduced-fishes

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Courtenay]] and Robins examines the introduction of non-native fishes into North American waters, focusing on the procedures used to evaluate them and how those procedures have been modified, neglected, or ignored. The authors argue that many introductions were made without adequate research, impact assessment, or independent review, and that they often produced ecological damage or unintended consequences. Case histories include the [[Salton Sea]], the [[Colorado River]] reservoirs, Texas, Florida's cichlid releases, North Dakota's zander proposal, Utah's rainbow smelt proposal, and the Amur pike in Pennsylvania. The paper concludes that a rigorous, multi-step protocol with independent peer review is essential, and that the "shotgun" approach to introduction should be abandoned.

## Background & Description

The paper traces the development of introduction policy. Earlier literature on introduced animals was largely descriptive and was often compiled after the fact, without consulting biologists. [[Courtenay]] and Robins (1973) proposed an eight-step protocol, later summarized as:

1. Rationale
2. Research and selection
3. Preliminary assessment of impact
4. Publicity and review
5. Experimental research
6. Publicity and independent review
7. Implementation

The authors stress that "rationale" requires demonstrating why native species do not fill the niche, and that selection requires matching biological specifications against candidate species. Public review is described as the "cornerstone" of the procedure because agencies otherwise cannot consult neighboring jurisdictions.

The [[American Fisheries Society]] (AFS) adopted a position statement in 1973 that applied only to exotic fishes. A 1986 revision, prompted by Kohler and Courtenay, deleted the requirement that agencies not release stock unless the procedure was reviewed, leaving agencies to submit reports and recommendations for evaluation. The authors view this deletion as detrimental.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salton Sea.** The Salton Sea is a saline lake in southern California, formed by Colorado River flooding in 1905–1907. Salinity rose steadily; it was about 33 ppt in 1929 and approached 37 ppt by the early 1960s, with agricultural drainage contributing to later increases. The original fish fauna included carp, bonytail chub, razorback sucker, cutthroat trout, striped mullet, and desert pupfish. By 1970, bonytail chub and razorback sucker had disappeared, and mullet had declined because they could not migrate to the sea.

**Colorado River reservoirs.** The construction of Hoover Dam in 1935 altered flow, temperature, and sediment regimes, and native fishes adapted to flowing water declined. Introductions in Lake Mead included threadfin shad, striped bass, and red shiner, which affected endangered native fishes in the Moapa and Virgin rivers.

**Texas reservoirs.** Many reservoirs were built for cooling water, and their stocking history is characterized by repeated failures, such as tilapia and cichlid introductions that could not survive winter temperatures.

**Florida and Everglades.** Peacock bass introductions in Dade County canals have spread into [[Everglades National Park]] and threaten native fishes.

## Key Findings

- **Salton Sea (1905–present):** Of 33 species introduced between 1948 and 1954, a few established. Anchovies failed, and [[Gambusia affinis]] and sargo became established. Redbelly and Mozambique tilapia were later introduced; Mozambique tilapia became dominant by 1970s.
- **Colorado River:** Striped bass introduced in the lower Colorado River expanded into Lake Mead and Lake Powell. Its predation and changes in nutrient supply contributed to the decline of threadfin shad, the main forage fish, which undermined the sport fishery for trout.
- **Texas:** Texas introduced hybrid [[Spotted Seatrout]] and orangemouth corvina, and released several marine species into inland waters, including red drum and black drum. Hybridization threatens native sunfish and drum populations.
- **Florida peacock bass:** Introductions into Dade County canals were made with limited assessment. Peacock bass have harmed native fish communities in [[Everglades National Park]].
- **North Dakota zander:** The proposal to introduce [[Zander]] into Lake Sakakawea was criticized because the claim that water-level fluctuations would limit spawning was unsupported, and the state had not established a strong rationale.
- **Utah rainbow smelt:** Proposed for Lake Powell as forage for trout and striped bass. Authors question whether the lake is nutrient-limited enough to support the planktivore and whether smelt would reach the Grand Canyon and Lake Mead.
- **Amur pike (Pennsylvania):** Introduced for recreational angling; the authors note the risk of hybridization with northern pike and the genetic consequences of introducing subspecies.
- **Hawaii marine introductions:** Of 21 marine species introduced, 9 became established; four were classified as mistakes, and several had adverse effects. The Mozambique tilapia is established in Hawaii and at Fanning Atoll.
- **Marine aquaculture and aquarium trade:** The authors recommend that the release of marine aquarium fishes be strictly prohibited, while noting that such introductions are unlikely to establish given their life histories.
- **Overall conclusion:** Introductions made without a rationale, independent review, and monitoring have repeatedly failed or caused harm. The authors call for interjurisdictional consultation, independent peer review, and clear prohibition of unreviewed releases.
