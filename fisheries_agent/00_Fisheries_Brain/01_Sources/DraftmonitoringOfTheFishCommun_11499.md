# Monitoring of the Fish Community in Redrock Canyon, Santa Cruz County, Arizona (1979 to Present)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Forest Service memorandum and attached report (Jerome A. Stefferud, Tonto National Forest, April 27, 1993) compiles fish monitoring records for [[Gila Topminnow]] in [[Redrock Canyon]] from 1979 to 1992. Redrock Canyon is the only [[Gila Topminnow]] site in Arizona on National Forest System land, and the report summarizes its status relative to competing [[Western Mosquitofish]] and nonnative sunfish and bass. It also outlines monitoring requirements under the [[Redrock Action Plan]] and the [[Arizona Trail]] project, both shaped by [[Endangered Species Act]] consultations.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) was among the most common fishes of the Gila River drainage in the 1940s but was nearly extinct in the United States when listed as endangered in 1967. Declines were attributed to habitat loss from dams, water diversion, livestock grazing and mining, and competition with nonnative fishes, principally [[Western Mosquitofish]]. Only eight Arizona sites remained at the time of writing.

The species is a small livebearer with broad habitat tolerance: shallow, warm, quiet water, temperatures from constant 80°F springs to streams fluctuating 43–99°F, and depths to about three feet. Because it bears live young, it does not require spawning habitat. [[Western Mosquitofish]] have similar habitat requirements, which underlies their competitive overlap.

Monitoring has been uneven. The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] sampled biennially from 1985, and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and [[U.S. Forest Service]] staff sampled annually from 1988. Differing sampling techniques, reporting methods and site descriptions make results difficult to compare directly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Competition and coexistence.** Gila topminnow have rarely coexisted with western mosquitofish, often being eliminated within a few years of the nonnative's introduction. In Redrock Canyon, mosquitofish have been present since the mid-1980s without generally becoming predominant. Whether this reflects stable coexistence or slow replacement is unknown. Coexistence appears linked to periodic flooding, habitat complexity and springhead refuges.

**Floods and drought.** Gila topminnow appear to survive or rapidly recover from periodic floods, while western mosquitofish are reduced; an extended flood-free period could allow mosquitofish to dominate. Flood scouring reduced topminnow numbers at Falls and Gate Spring in 1990–1991. A 1984 peak discharge of 16,000 cfs at the Sonoita Creek gage (watershed about 15% of the Redrock Canyon contributing area; discharge recorded at the gage as the highest since 1930) and estimated two-year and 10-year floods of 3,130 cfs and 7,190 cfs illustrate the flood regime. Discharge from Redrock Canyon itself has not been recorded.

**Habitat.** A 1989 survey found live water over only 3,700 ft (less than 7% of channel length) during a dry period. Aquatic habitat was dominated by shallow, transitory pools formed by boulder and cobble berms, with water appearing where bedrock forced subsurface flow to the surface. Below Cott Tank, trench pools 1–4 ft deep had vertical sides and little cover, with heavy livestock grazing and a braided primitive road causing erosion. By 1993, flood-deposited sand had raised the Gate Spring channel by up to 2 ft, and some pools at Falls had not reestablished. Reduced grazing has increased herbaceous stubble height.

**Nonnative fishes.** Cott Tank serves as a constant source of nonnative fish. [[Largemouth Bass]] (34 removed from Cott Tank drainage pools in July 1989), [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]] and mosquitofish occur in stock tanks at the head of the drainage. Poeciliids and [[Longfin Dace]] were absent from pools containing largemouth bass.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution.** Gila topminnow was first reported in the early 1960s; Rinne et al. (1980) documented the population in May 1979, estimating fewer than 100 fish at Gate Spring.
- **Mosquitofish dominance pattern.** Western mosquitofish dominate upper Cott Tank and Downunder Tank drainages; Gila topminnow dominate lower Cott Tank drainage down to Falls; both are rare below Falls.
- **Abundance trends.** Gila topminnow at Falls rose from 4 (October 1990) to 161 (November 1992); Gate Spring counts fluctuated from 0 (October 1990) to 119 (October 1989) and 85 (November 1992).
- **Upper Cott Tank drainage.** Gila topminnow were absent or minor (0–2 individuals in 1989–1991), with mosquitofish at 100% of collections. Extremely low topminnow numbers may be below the minimum needed to sustain a population.
- **Newly reported sites (1987).** Simons (1987) found Gila topminnow at four previously unreported sites, though by July 1987 three supported only longfin dace.
- **Management recommendations (Rinne et al., 1980).** Fencing of springs, protection from mining and water extraction, prohibition of vehicles at Gate Spring, and removal of mosquitofish were recommended. A small exclosure at Falls was never maintained, and three concrete deflectors at Gate Spring functioned as designed.
- **Desert Fishes Recovery Team position.** Mosquitofish removal is not recommended while Gila topminnow predominate across much of the drainage; biannual monitoring must continue to track relative abundance.
- **Redrock Action Plan monitoring (USFWS Biological Opinion, 1990).** Low-level monitoring (annual allotment inspections) and high-level monitoring (periodic netting, reproduction and nonnative assessment, coordinated with the Forest Service Zone Fisheries Biologist).
- **Arizona Trail monitoring (USFWS Biological Opinion, 1992).** Baseline monitoring at least six months before opening of the Canelo Pass to Patagonia segment, continuation during active use, annual reports to USFWS and AGFD, and protocols requiring approval by both agencies. A detailed plan is to be developed by Tom Deecken, Sierra Vista Ranger District.
- **Other sites.** Oak Springs and Lampshire canyons appear suitable for Gila topminnow but may dry in drought; livestock intensity limits reestablishment. The Rio San Cruz population was eliminated by mine discharge in 1982, so mining activities require assessment.
- **Data quality caveat.** Table 1 documents 1979–1992 sampling at more than a dozen sites from several sources (agency reports, memos, field notes), but methods and site descriptions vary, limiting direct comparison.
