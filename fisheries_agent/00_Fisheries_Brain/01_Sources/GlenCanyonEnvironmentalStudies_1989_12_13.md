## Executive Summary
This document summarizes the November 1989 [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Technical Team meeting, in which environmental and economic subgroups reviewed the results of the October 6–9, 1989 **5,000 cfs Low Flow Test** below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], evaluated impacts on aquatic habitat and fisheries, and began drafting the Integrated Research Plan (IRP) for Phase II studies. While the meeting is primarily a program-management record, it contains substantive aquatic ecology data on [[Rainbow Trout]] spawning habitat, tailwater substrate/bathymetry, backwater habitat loss, angler access, and forthcoming fisheries management changes (slot limits, proposed [[Cutthroat Trout]] introduction) relevant to the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and the broader Colorado River ecosystem in the Grand Canyon.

## Background & Description
No direct morphological, meristic, or anatomical data on fish specimens are reported in this document. The paper is administrative/programmatic rather than a species-level morphological study. Relevant habitat-structural characteristics documented instead include:

- **Tailwater substrate composition** (Glen Canyon Dam reach, per Mike Yard's bathymetric mapping):
  - Silt: 0.2%
  - Sand: 40%
  - Gravel: 17%
  - Cobbles (1"–6"): 24%, 5%, 8% (size subclasses)
  - Boulders: 6%
- Substrate gradient: coarser gravel near the dam, finer material downstream — relevant to [[Rainbow Trout]] spawning bar suitability at 5,000 cfs.
- Backwater habitat inventory (1984 baseline vs. 1989 resurvey): of 21 original backwaters, 52% were absent, 29% altered, 19% unchanged; 3 new backwaters formed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The studies described position the Colorado River tailwater below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] as a flow-regulated salmonid fishery, while downstream reaches and tributary confluences (e.g., [[Little Colorado River]]) support native cyprinids such as the [[Humpback Chub]].

- **Flow regime and sediment transport**: The rhodamine dye study (Julie Graf, USGS) found that a dye "slug" moved at ~1 mile/hour through the [[Lee's Ferry]] reach, with velocity slightly increased by two fluctuation cycles between Lee's Ferry and Grand Canyon gaging stations. This directly affects nutrient transport, spawning bar access, and habitat connectivity.
- **Spawning habitat**: Dennis Kubly (AGF) evaluated spawning bar suitability and access for [[Rainbow Trout]] at the constant 5,000 cfs low flow, noting substrate coarseness varies longitudinally from the dam (coarse gravel) to downstream reaches (finer material).
- **Backwater habitat dynamics**: Significant degradation/aggradation occurred in tailwater backwaters over 5 years, indicating habitat instability under fluctuating dam operations — a key concern for young native fish (including [[Humpback Chub]]) that rely on backwaters as rearing habitat.
- **Beach/bank ecotone hydrology** (Larry Stevens, NPS): Erosion was greatest in the <28,000 cfs zone under fluctuating flows; bank-stored water maintained higher temperature and alkalinity than the river, with lag times tied to substrate and channel proximity — relevant to invertebrate and fish nursery microhabitats along shorelines.
- **Angler access/human use interaction**: Michael Welsh (HBRS) documented boater/angler access above [[Three Mile Bar]] at 5,000 cfs: 296 attempts observed, ~87% successful, 25 boats failed to pass a channel constriction; most "unclean" runs resulted from prop strikes.
- **Fisheries management context**: New Lee's Ferry regulations (two-fish limit, new slot limit) and a proposed introduction of [[Cutthroat Trout]] were noted as necessitating additional recreational and ecological analysis.
- **Nutrient/organic loading**: USGS (Bob Hart) and AGF (Dennis Kubly) both examined nutrient loads and organic/nutrient loading during the low-flow test as drivers of tailwater productivity supporting the trout fishery food web.

## Key Findings
- The **October 1989 Low Flow Test (5,000 cfs)** was a controlled, multi-agency field experiment (>40 personnel) designed to replicate 1984 photography, assess ecosystem response, and evaluate angler access — providing baseline data for Phase II GCES research design.
- **Dye tracer studies** revealed unexpectedly slow water mass movement (~1 mph) in the Lee's Ferry reach, complicating attempts to track sediment/nutrient slugs to downstream gages (National Canyon, Diamond Creek).
- **Tailwater habitat has degraded** significantly since 1984: over half of previously mapped backwaters were lost, undermining rearing habitat potentially important to native fish alongside the [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery.
- **Substrate mapping** confirmed a coarse-to-fine gradient from the dam downstream, with implications for [[Rainbow Trout]] spawning bar suitability and future substrate-based habitat modeling.
- **Angler access studies** quantified a physical constriction (Three Mile Bar) as a significant navigation hazard at low flow, with implications for recreational fishery management under proposed research/experimental flow regimes.
- The **Draft Integrated Research Plan** (Duncan Patten) identified [[Rainbow Trout]], beach/sediment stability, and [[Humpback Chub]] as the three central GCES study foci, with an explicit need for controlled, replicated "normal" flow conditions to generate interpretable (non-random) ecological data — and warned that costs could double without such control.
- A **Biological Opinion/Biological Assessment process** (Reed Harris, BOR) was underway, with seven conservation measures developed by NPS/FWS/AGF/BOR specifically to address impacts on protected aquatic species (implicitly including the [[Humpback Chub]]) ahead of the Glen Canyon Dam EIS.
- Proposed **new sport-fishing regulations and non-native trout introductions** (cutthroat) at Lee's Ferry were flagged as requiring additional ecological and economic (non-use valuation) analysis, linking fisheries management directly to the EIS process.
- Several **externally funded research proposals** (Attachment 2) addressed related geomorphic and ecological processes (rapids stability, flood geomorphology, sediment budgets, riparian vegetation change) that indirectly bear on aquatic habitat structure in the [[Grand Canyon]] reach.
