# Proposed Rule: Loach Minnow Threatened Species Determination

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #threatened-species #Gila-River-basin #habitat-conservation

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed on June 18, 1985, to list [[Tiaroga cobitis]] (loach minnow) as a threatened species under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, as amended. This small cyprinid fish, historically distributed throughout the [[Gila River]] system upstream from Phoenix, Arizona, has experienced an approximately 85% range reduction. Of the approximately 2,600 kilometers of stream habitat historically occupied, only 380 kilometers currently support populations. The proposal includes designation of critical habitat in [[Aravaipa Creek]], portions of the [[Gila River]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Blue River]], [[Tularosa River]], and tributaries across Arizona and New Mexico. A special rule is proposed allowing take under state permits for educational, scientific, propagation, and conservation purposes. Primary threats include habitat destruction from impoundment, channel downcutting, sedimentation, water diversion, groundwater pumping, and competition/predation from exotic species, particularly [[Notropis lutrensis]] (red shiner).

## Background & Description

[[Tiaroga cobitis]] was first collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro in Arizona and described by Girard in 1856. This small fish (less than 80 millimeters total length) is slender and elongated, olivaceous in color with dirty white spots at the base of the dorsal and caudal fins. Diagnostic features include a highly oblique terminal mouth and markedly upward-directed eyes. Breeding males develop vivid red-orange markings.

The species inhabits small to large perennial streams, utilizing shallow turbulent riffles with primarily cobble substrate, swift currents, and growths of filamentous algae. Water depth of at least 3 centimeters over cobble and gravel substrate is required. Recurrent flooding is critically important to [[Tiaroga cobitis]] biology, maintaining substrate free of embedding sediments and helping preserve competitive advantage over invading exotic fish species (Minckley, 1973).

Historically, [[Tiaroga cobitis]] was locally common throughout much of the Verde, Salt, San Pedro, San Francisco, and Gila River systems upstream from Phoenix, occupying both mainstream and perennial tributaries up to approximately 2,200 meters elevation (Minckley, 1973). Current distribution is restricted to:

- Approximately 24 kilometers of [[Aravaipa Creek]], Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona
- Approximately 93 kilometers of upper [[Gila River]] upstream from Middle Box canyon through Cliff-Gila Valley and confluence areas of East, West, and Middle Forks, Grant and Catron Counties, New Mexico
- Approximately 167 kilometers of [[San Francisco River]] and [[Tularosa River]], Catron County, New Mexico
- Lower 1.5 kilometers of [[Whitewater Creek]], tributary to San Francisco River, Catron County, New Mexico
- Approximately 95 kilometers of [[Blue River]], Greenlee County, Arizona

The species has been extirpated from the San Pedro River (except Aravaipa Creek, last found 1961), Verde River drainage (last found 1938), and has retreated at least 60 kilometers upstream in the Gila River over the last 50 years. Status in the White River of the Salt River drainage is uncertain; last found in 1967 but no extensive surveys conducted since.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The native fish fauna of the [[Gila River]] system has been drastically affected by anthropogenic alterations, with 35% of species federally listed as endangered and another 35% considered threatened or endangered by Arizona and New Mexico states and/or the American Fisheries Society. [[Tiaroga cobitis]] was included on the American Fisheries Society's 1979 list (Deacon et al., 1979) as a species of special concern, on the 1972 threatened freshwater fish list (Miller, 1972), and in the International Union for Conservation of Nature Red Data Book (Vol. 4, 1977). Both Arizona and New Mexico list the species on their state threatened and endangered species lists.

Land ownership in existing habitat is mixed:

**Aravaipa Creek**: Approximately 75% USDI Bureau of Land Management (mostly Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness), most remaining perennial stream owned/leased by Defenders of Wildlife as George Whittell Wildlife Preserve, scattered private parcels.

**Gila River**: Mostly private in Cliff-Gila Valley and near Gila Hot Springs/East Fork; small Nature Conservancy parcel upstream from town of Gila; approximately 6.5 kilometers owned by New Mexico Department of Game and Fish downstream from West/Middle Fork confluence; large portions in Gila National Forest including Gila Wilderness, Lower Gila River Bird Habitat Management Area, and Gila River Research Natural Area.

**San Francisco, Tularosa Rivers, Whitewater Creek**: Substantial private areas near towns (Cruzville, Glenwood, Reserve, Alma); major portions through Gila and Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests.

**Blue River**: Almost entirely within Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest with large portion through Blue Range Primitive Area; interspersed private inholdings.

Habitat alterations causing the 85% range reduction include: conversion of flowing to still waters by impoundment; altered flow regimes (conversion of perennial to intermittent/no flow, reduction/elimination/modification of natural flooding); altered water temperatures; altered silt and bed loads; loss of marshes and backwaters; channel transformation from well-defined, surface-level, heavily vegetated channels with diverse substrate into deeply cut unstable arroyos with little riparian vegetation and uniform substrate. Causes include damming, water diversion, channel downcutting, excessive groundwater pumping, lowering water tables, channelization, riparian destruction, erosion, mining, timber harvest, grazing, and other watershed disturbances.

Exotic species, particularly [[Notropis lutrensis]] (red shiner), pose significant threats through competitive and/or predatory interactions. Although [[Tiaroga cobitis]] and [[Notropis lutrensis]] generally utilize different habitats, they appear to compete for some habitat factors (Minckley and Carufel, 1967). In suitable unaltered habitat, [[Tiaroga cobitis]] may maintain populations against exotic invasion; however, this balance is destroyed in extensively altered habitat where populations are already stressed. Natural flooding patterns are critical, as native species thrive under frequent moderate to severe flooding while exotics do not. Controlled flow from impoundment interrupts natural patterns and encourages exotic spread. Currently, [[Notropis lutrensis]] is absent from Aravaipa Creek and Blue River but present in San Francisco River upstream to Frisco Hot Springs and upper Gila River upstream to Highway 180 bridge near Cliff, New Mexico.

Other exotic predators include: green sunfish, channel catfish, black and yellow bullhead, flathead catfish, smallmouth and largemouth bass, rainbow and brown trout. Native roundtail chub is also a potential predator. Depletion of native fishes in East Fork Gila River noted in 1983-84 (Propst, in prep.) is probably due to increased smallmouth bass and catfish numbers.

## Key Findings

**Regulatory Status**: The Service proposes threatened (not endangered) status because while the species faces significant threats and has experienced massive range reduction (85%), it still persists in 380 kilometers of stream and does not appear in danger of extinction within the foreseeable future. Endangered status would be inappropriate given current distribution.

**Critical Habitat Designation**: Proposed critical habitat totals approximately 380 kilometers across seven areas:

1. **Aravaipa Creek** (Graham and Pinal Counties, AZ): ~24 km perennial section
2. **Gila River** (Catron and Grant Counties, NM): Four sections totaling ~93 km (37 km from St. Peters Rock to Mogollon Creek confluence; 12 km West Fork; 18 km Middle Fork; 28 km East Fork)
3. **San Francisco River** (Catron County, NM and Greenlee County, AZ): Two sections totaling ~21 km (15 km from US Highway 180 bridge to Kelly Flat; 6 km from Hickey Canyon confluence to Blue River confluence)
4. **Tularosa River** (Catron County, NM): ~24 km from Negrito Creek confluence to Cruzville
5. **Blue River** (Greenlee County, AZ and Catron County, NM): ~78 km from San Francisco River confluence to Dry Blue/Campbell Blue Creek confluence
6. **Campbell Blue Creek** (Greenlee County, AZ and Catron County, NM): ~18 km from Blue River junction to Coleman Creek confluence
7. **Dry Blue Creek** (Catron County, NM): ~3 km from Blue River confluence to springs in Section 32, T6S, R21W

Known constituent elements of critical habitat: permanent stream flow, unpolluted water, swift turbulent riffles, minimum 3 cm depth over cobble and gravel substrate, filamentous algae growths, and periodic flooding to maintain habitat quality.

**Threats Analysis** (per Section 4(a)(1) factors):

- **Factor A (Habitat destruction/modification)**: Primary threat. Approximately 2,220 km of 2,600 km historical habitat no longer supports the species. Specific losses include: San Pedro River now severely downcut with intermittent flow; lower Salt and Verde Rivers have limited/no flow due to agricultural diversion and upstream impoundments; Gila River affected by diversion, impoundment, channelization, and chemical fish management; San Francisco and Tularosa Rivers suffer erosion and extensive diversion with undependable water supply; Blue River subjected to channel downcutting and erosion. Ongoing threats include groundwater pumping upstream of Aravaipa Creek, channelization and mesquite clearing creating excessive sediment, and proposed Bureau of Reclamation dam construction on upper Gila River as part of Central Arizona Project Upper Gila Water Supply Study.

- **Factor B (Overutilization)**: No known threat.

- **Factor C (Disease/predation)**: Historically not significant, but exotic predatory fishes now increase predation role. Predation appears more significant under altered habitat conditions. Dam/reservoir construction exacerbates predation by increasing habitat for exotic predators, decreasing suitable habitat for [[Tiaroga cobitis]], and supplying ready source of exotics from reservoirs.

- **Factor D (Inadequacy of existing regulations)**: New Mexico lists species as endangered (Group 2) under Wildlife Conservation Act, prohibiting take except under scientific collecting permit. Arizona lists as threatened (Group 3) but provides no special protection beyond general prohibition on take except by angling. Neither state protects habitat. New Mexico water law lacks provisions for instream water rights for fish/wildlife habitat protection; Arizona only recently recognized such rights. State regulations in both states allow use of red shiner and other live minnows as bait in Gila and San Francisco Rivers, encouraging spread of detrimental exotic species.

- **Factor E (Other factors)**: Continued introduction and dispersal of exotic species, particularly [[Notropis lutrensis]], throughout Gila River system. Spread of exotics correlates closely with declining numbers and distribution of [[Tiaroga cobitis]] and other natives. Disturbance of natural flooding patterns appears major factor in displacement, as native species thrive under frequent flooding while exotics do not. Reservoir presence increases likelihood and rapidity of exotic spread.

**Proposed Dam Impacts**: Bureau of Reclamation currently studying four alternatives for Upper Gila Water Supply Study: (1) high dam at Conner site on mainstream Gila River near lower end of Middle Box canyon; (2) small dam at Conner site with offstream storage reservoir; (3) floodplain storage basins in Cliff-Gila Valley; (4) direct pumping to offstream storage. High dam could inundate up to 29 km of river (31% of existing Gila River range), alter downstream habitat through changed temperature/bedload/flow regimes including elimination of natural flooding. Small dam would inundate estimated 14 km. Direct pumping effects include potential fish entrapment in pipelines, impingement on intake screens, and stream flow depletion. Floodplain storage would require removal of 484 acres riparian vegetation, eliminate 18 km aquatic habitat through basin construction and channelization/diversion, and cause downstream impacts including increased sediments and altered temperature/flow regimes.

**Federal Activities Affected**: Section 7 consultation required for: proposed Bureau of Reclamation dam construction/water projects; future Federal Emergency Management Agency flood control funding or U.S. Army Corps of Engineers work on Gila River (Cliff-Gila Valley) or San Francisco/Tularosa Rivers/Whitewater Creek; federally funded highway/bridge construction; future federally funded irrigation projects; federal funding for pipeline, water diversion, and land-leveling projects on private agricultural lands.

**Special Rule Provisions**: Proposed special rule under Section 4(d) allows take in accordance with state laws/regulations for: educational purposes, scientific purposes, enhancement of propagation/survival, zoological exhibition, and other conservation purposes consistent with the Act. Also allows incidental take by state-licensed recreational fishermen if individual fish immediately returned to habitat. Rationale: species threatened primarily by habitat disturbance/alteration, not intentional direct taking or commercialization; states currently regulate direct taking through collecting permits; separate federal permit system not required to address current threats; angling is unlikely capture mode.

**Conservation Measures**: Recognition through listing encourages conservation actions by federal, state, and private entities. Act provides for land acquisition, state cooperation, and recovery actions. Section 7(a)(4) requires federal agencies to confer informally on actions likely to jeopardize proposed species or adversely modify proposed critical habitat. Upon listing, Section 7(a)(2) requires federal agencies ensure activities they authorize, fund, or carry out do not jeopardize species or destroy/adversely modify critical habitat. General prohibitions (50 CFR 17.21, 17.31) make illegal: take, import, export, interstate commerce shipment for commercial activity, sale/offer for sale in interstate/foreign commerce, and possession/transport of illegally taken specimens.

**Public Comment Period**: Comments due by August 19, 1985. Public hearing requests due by August 2, 1985.
