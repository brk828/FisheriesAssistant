# Vegetation of the Gila River Resource Area, Eastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #plant-ecology #vegetation-survey

## Executive Summary

This Desert Plants report by W. L. Minckley and Thomas O. Clark (Arizona State University) describes baseline vegetation of the [[Gila River Resource Area]] in eastern Arizona, northeast of Safford. The area is a transition zone between the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] to the east and the [[Sonoran Desert]] to the west. Field work ran from October 1976 through September 1979 under a Bureau of Land Management contract, with the goal of establishing baseline data before potential development. The document is a botanical survey; it contains no fish or aquatic animal data. Its aquatic relevance lies in the riparian vegetation along the [[Gila River]] and its tributaries, which shapes stream habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Lands lie mostly below 1,500 m elevation, within the Basin and Range Physiographic Province. Parallel mountain ranges, including the [[Pinaleño Mountains]], [[Graham Mountains]], [[Gila Mountains]] and [[Peloncillo Mountains]], rise above alluvial basins (the Safford and Duncan valleys).
- **Geology and soils:** Streams are deeply incised in conglomerates. Upland soils are derived from heterogeneous strata and granites, gneisses, schists and limestones. Alluvial soils are finer and sometimes saline. Soils are thermic, and the moisture regime is classed as aridic.
- **Climate:** Precipitation is bimodal, with winter rain, spring drought, and summer-autumn monsoons. Mean annual precipitation at Clifton is 32.3 ± 9.9 cm. Evaporation from water surfaces is 7 to 10 times annual precipitation. Freezing temperatures are rare at Safford but occur at Duncan from December to February.
- **Methods:** Permanent transects (Table 1; 1,050 m long unless noted; 27 transects labeled GL, B, E, SF, GU series) were used. Plant sampling combined frequency of occurrence, spot sampling in 250 m² plots at about 1 km intervals, and tree counts in 15 m belts on each side of transect lines. Foliage volume was estimated with a point-quadrat measurement, and leaf volume was computed from the measured distances. Surveys were taken at maximum development, May to July.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Upland habitats:** Semi-desert grasslands occur between about 1,250 and 1,750 m, but most have a high cover of cacti and shrubs. Interior chaparral species such as scrub live-oak, buckthorn and mountain-mahogany appear at upper elevations. Madrean evergreen woodland fringes extend downslope, with oaks, junipers and piñon pine on steep northerly slopes.
- **Desertscrub:** Below about 1,250 m, desertscrub is dominated by creosote-bush, ocotillo and prickly pear, with mesquite and blue palo-verde as sub-dominants in washes. Devil's cholla and Christmas cholla dominate more level surfaces.
- **Terraces:** Flat valley-fill surfaces, often sparsely vegetated, are affected by water standing after heavy rain (caliche or clay layers impede drainage) and by heavy livestock use. Livestock use is thought to promote dense, multi-stemmed mesquite.
- **Desert washes and side canyons:** Washes range from simple Desertscrub enhancement to mesquite bosque and broad-leafed riparian communities where permanent water exists, such as Hot Springs Canyon on Eagle Creek and Spring Canyon. Hackberry, willow, walnut, box elder and a few cottonwoods occur in perennial-water canyons.
- **Mesquite bosques:** Mesquite makes up 95 to 99% of trees on four permanent transects along the mainstream Gila River. Bosques are 2 to about 12 m tall. Much of the riparian vegetation, including mesquite bosques, was apparently destroyed in the period 1888 to 1917, and stands rarely exceed 35 cm in diameter. The authors estimate a stand age of 60 to 90 years.
- **Riparian shrubs and seepwillow:** Dense seepwillow thickets stabilize saturated sandbars and resist flood scour, armoring the bosque soils. Bermuda grass often grows with them.
- **Broad-leafed riparian:** Cottonwood, sycamore, walnut, willow and box elder dominate Bonita and Eagle creeks. Eagle Creek has the most developed cottonwood galleries, and the San Francisco River is more sparse.
- **Non-native and absent species:** [[Salt-Cedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) is largely absent, occurring only as individual plants in one reach, and [[Arrowweed]] was not found. Flash floods in 1978-79 removed much bosque vegetation.
- **Community boundaries:** The Gila River Resource Area contains transitions between [[Sonoran Desert]] and [[Chihuahuan Desert]] elements, and between desertscrub and madrean woodland.

## Key Findings

- The study area is a biogeographic transition between the Chihuahuan and Sonoran deserts, with access routes from the Rocky Mountain, Plains and northwestern Mexican Plateau regions.
- Survey sampling covered 27 transects (GL, B, E, SF and GU series), with frequency counts, spot plots and tree counts.
- Mesquite dominates riparian bosques, comprising 95 to 99% of trees on the four mainstream Gila River transects. Mesquite comprised 55% of 637 ha of riparian vegetation within the study area, and 43% of 780 ha upstream.
- Along Bonita Creek, 28 kinds of plants were identified across 46 sampling plots. Mesquite dominated throughout, followed by burro-bush, hackberry and seepwillow. Sycamore occurred in 43.5% of plots.
- Riparian vegetation along the Gila River was largely removed in 1888 to 1917 and during later flooding (notably 1978-79), leaving younger bosques.
- Salt-cedar and arrowweed were absent or very rare, possibly because canyon streams are too erosive for establishment.
- Vegetation patterns are controlled by altitude, slope exposure, substrate, land use, and (in lowlands) depth to the water table.
- Foliage volume is highest in pinyon-juniper and riparian habitats and lowest in desertscrub.
- The authors noted that the document is intended as baseline data prior to potential development, and that livestock grazing and flooding strongly influence present vegetation.
