## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although not about fish, the text's discussion of niche partitioning and environmental heterogeneity is conceptually relevant to aquatic ecology and could be applied by analogy to fish populations occupying heterogeneous habitats (e.g., reef vs. open water, or freshwater vs. brackish microhabitats):

- **Two-habitat model**: A single population is modeled as distributed across two habitat types (illustratively, "oak trees" and "fir trees"), with genotype-specific fitness values (W₁, W₂) measured along orthogonal axes.
- **Average population fitness** at a locality is a weighted function of habitat proportions:
  - W = p·W₁ + q·W₂ (Equation 16), where *p* and *q* are the relative proportions of each habitat type at a given locality.
- **[[Convex Fitness Set]]**: Arises when genotypes are physiologically/behaviorally flexible and can adapt similarly well to both habitats. Produces **gradual geographic variation** (a [[Cline]]) as habitat proportions shift smoothly across space.
- **[[Concave Fitness Set]]**: Arises when genotypes are inflexible and highly specialized for one habitat or the other. Produces **abrupt geographic discontinuities** between populations (sharp subspecies boundaries), even under a smoothly changing environmental gradient.
- This framework is proposed as an alternative/complement to classical single-locus [[Population Genetics]] approaches for explaining patterns of geographic variation and [[Subspecies]] differentiation.

## Key Findings

1. **Genetic Load Paradox (Lewontin & Hubby problem)**: If each of ~2,000 polymorphic loci independently imposes even a small fitness cost (e.g., 5% reduction from homozygote disadvantage), the multiplicative effect across loci yields an impossibly low relative population fitness (~10⁻⁴⁶), implying repeated population extinction — clearly unrealistic given observed levels of polymorphism.

2. **Resolution — Selection Acts on the Whole Phenotype**: Independently proposed by multiple geneticists, the solution is that natural selection acts on the **integrated phenotype of the whole organism**, not on isolated loci. Because alleles at different loci interact epistatically and often contribute cumulatively to the same trait, the real "genetic load" is far lower than predicted by naively multiplying single-locus selection coefficients.

3. **Environmental Heterogeneity and Selection Coefficients**: The selection coefficient (*s*) is not a fixed constant; it varies across space and time due to both physical (climatic) and biotic (species interactions, prey/predator/competitor shifts) environmental heterogeneity.

4. **Levins' Fitness Set Theory**: Introduces a graphical/geometric method (plotting genotype fitness in two or more environments) to model how varying habitat proportions across localities (A, B, C) select for different genotypes, producing either smooth clines (convex sets) or sharp subspecies boundaries (concave sets).

5. **Determinants of Fitness Set Shape**: 
   - Convex sets emerge from **phenotypic/behavioral flexibility** (generalist genotypes).
   - Concave sets emerge from strong **genetic canalization/specialization**, where each genotype is heavily predisposed toward a particular habitat.

6. **Heritability and Polygenic Traits**: Most phenotypic traits are controlled by [[Polygenes]] across multiple loci rather than single-locus allele pairs. Higher [[Heritability]] correlates with faster evolutionary response to selection. The text acknowledges the growing mathematical sophistication (special techniques, computer simulation) required for modeling polygenic systems, citing Li (1955), Lerner (1958), Falconer (1960), Wallace (1968), and Crow & Kimura (1970) as advanced references.

7. **Caveat on Theoretical Status**: The authors explicitly note that fitness sets and fitness curves are theoretical constructs ("products of the imagination") that had **not yet been empirically validated** in field or laboratory studies at the time of writing, and that classical population genetics remains adequate to explain most cases of geographic variation without invoking fitness set theory.
