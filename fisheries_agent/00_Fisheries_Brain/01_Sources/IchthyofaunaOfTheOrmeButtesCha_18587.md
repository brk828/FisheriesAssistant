# Ichthyofauna of the Orme, Buttes, Charleston, and Hooker Reservoir Sites, Arizona-New Mexico

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #conservation-biology #reservoir-impacts #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1972 report by W. L. Minckley assesses the native and non-native fish fauna at four proposed reservoir sites in the [[Gila River]] basin of Arizona and New Mexico: [[Orme Reservoir]], [[Buttes Reservoir]], [[Charleston Reservoir]], and [[Hooker Reservoir]]. Of 15-16 native fish species historically present, five are already extinct in the basin, two are endangered, and two are depleted. The report documents a dramatic shift in fish community composition, with introduced species outnumbering natives approximately 2:1. Two endemic species of monotypic genera—[[Tiaroga cobitis]] (loach minnow) and [[Meda fulgida]] (Gila spikedace)—face particular threats. The analysis predicts that reservoir construction will eliminate all native species from impounded areas and adversely affect populations both upstream (through introduction of competitors via bait fish) and downstream (through habitat homogenization and flow regulation).

## Background & Description

The [[Gila River]] basin historically supported 17 native fish species (18 if subspecies are counted), representing a highly endemic fauna. Two species are monotypic genera found nowhere else: [[Tiaroga cobitis]] and [[Meda fulgida]]. Five species are endemic to the Gila system, and ten likely originated there before dispersing to adjacent drainages ([[Little Colorado River]], [[Bill Williams River]], [[Yaqui River]]). Only seven species ranged widely throughout the [[Colorado River]] drainage. With one exception ([[Rhinichthys osculus]], speckled dace), all species are restricted to the Colorado River basin, emphasizing the fauna's uniqueness.

The native fishes occupy three broadly overlapping ecological categories: (1) big-river species capable of penetrating canyons to high elevations; (2) species inhabiting smaller, lower-elevation desert creeks and cienégas; and (3) montane elements ranging from native trouts at highest elevations to certain minnows and suckers in turbulent, solid-bottomed habitats at lower elevations.

The pattern of faunal change began slowly around 1900 (fewer than four non-native species present) but accelerated dramatically. By 1950, the ratio was approximately one native to one introduced species statewide; by 1960, one native to 1.5 introduced; and by 1972, approximately one native to two introduced species. This shift reflects both influx of non-native species and elimination of native elements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The four reservoir sites differ in their anticipated fish communities. [[Orme Reservoir]] is expected to support approximately 25 non-native species and develop a strong "warm water" artificial fauna. [[Buttes Reservoir]] may contain about 15 species, also warm-water dominated. [[Charleston Reservoir]] could support 8 species (though the upper [[San Pedro River]] in Sonora, Mexico remains inadequately surveyed) with intermediate thermal characteristics. [[Hooker Reservoir]] might harbor 14 species and could potentially support a marginal "cold water" (trout) fishery with warm-water species in epilimnetic and littoral zones.

The introduced fish assemblage shows poor ecological balance. Excluding salmonids, doubtful records, western suckers (which perform poorly in reservoirs), and tropical fishes, only 5 species provide a forage base for predatory species. In contrast, 18 carnivorous forms are present, and 5 large "problem" fishes (carp, buffalofishes, goldfish) are rarely utilized by either predatory fish or humans except in youngest stages.

Native fishes demonstrate intolerance to regulated flow conditions. No native species in the [[Gila River]] basin (except possibly the already-extirpated [[Xyrauchen texanus]], razorback sucker) maintains populations in reservoirs, and few survive downstream under flow regulation.

## Key Findings

**Extinction and Endangerment Status:**
- Five species extinct in Gila River basin: [[Gila elegans]] (bonytail chub), [[Ptychocheilus lucius]] (Colorado River squawfish), [[Plagopterus argentissimus]] (woundfin), [[Xyrauchen texanus]] (razorback sucker), [[Cyprinodon macularius]] (desert pupfish)
- Two species endangered: native trouts (under semi-domestication programs)
- Three species depleted and approaching endangered status: [[Gila intermedia]] (Gila chub), [[Catostomus latipinnis]] (flannelmouth sucker), [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis]] (Gila topminnow)
- [[Tiaroga cobitis]] (loach minnow) and [[Meda fulgida]] (Gila spikedace) essentially extirpated from Arizona waters; substantial populations exist only in [[Aravaipa Canyon]], Graham and Pinal counties

**Reservoir Impact Predictions:**
- Complete elimination of native species from reservoir areas (excepting wanderers)
- Upstream impacts: introduction of competitors through bait fish use in reservoirs and upstream movement of non-native fishes enhanced by lake conditions
- Downstream impacts: decreased environmental heterogeneity producing faunal monotony, detrimental to both native and resident non-native fishes
- [[Hooker Reservoir]] position almost certainly ensures additional introductions and downstream habitat modifications incompatible with [[Tiaroga cobitis]] and [[Meda fulgida]] populations

**Species Vulnerability:**
- [[Tiaroga cobitis]] and [[Meda fulgida]] appear susceptible to competition from [[Notropis lutrensis]] (red shiner) and perhaps other non-native minnows
- [[Catostomus latipinnis]] (flannelmouth sucker) maintains substantial populations elsewhere in Colorado River basin but is depleted in Gila system
- Native fishes show intolerance to suppression of natural flow variability and increased habitat monotony downstream of reservoirs

**Ecological Imbalance in Introduced Fauna:**
- Non-native species outnumber natives approximately 2:1
- Forage base limited to ~5 species
- 18 carnivorous species present
- 5 "rough" fish species (carp, buffalofishes, goldfish) provide minimal food value

**Conservation Implications:**
- Two endemic monotypic genera ([[Tiaroga]] and [[Meda]]) face severe threats
- Reservoir operations will fundamentally alter downstream biota through flow regulation
- Established populations of both native and desirable non-native fishes vulnerable to post-impoundment changes
- Cold-water put-and-take salmonid fisheries difficult to maintain at economically feasible levels without advance planning
