# Effects of Electrofishing Fields on Juvenile Humpback Chub and on Developing Eggs and Early Larvae of Razorback Sucker—A Phase II Study

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #electrofishing-impacts #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This research proposal, submitted by [[Colorado State University]] Larval Fish Laboratory to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], outlines a Phase II controlled experimental study to evaluate the acute and latent effects of electrofishing on endangered [[Colorado River]] fishes. The study focuses on two critical species: juvenile [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and developing eggs and early larvae of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The proposal responds to concerns about potential adverse effects of electrofishing on endangered and native fishes during monitoring and collection activities. Principal Investigator Robert T. Muth and Graduate Research Assistant Jack B. Ruppert propose a three-part study (pilot experiments, definitive experiments, and final reporting) to establish more definitive policy and guidelines for electrofishing use in the [[Colorado River System]]. The study will examine spinal and soft-tissue injuries, survival, growth, and developmental anomalies under various electrofishing field conditions and waveforms.

## Background & Description

The [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (Upper Colorado Region) recommended a three-phase approach to address concerns over electrofishing impacts on endangered Colorado River fishes. Phase I consisted of a comprehensive literature review and synthesis by Snyder (1992) establishing interim guidelines. Phase II (this proposal) involves controlled laboratory and field experiments to answer remaining questions. Phase III would consist of field experiments to verify Phase II results.

An ad hoc working group convened in Salt Lake City, Utah (summer 1992) identified high-priority research needs including effects on juvenile [[Humpback Chub]] and on spawning adults, eggs, and larvae of [[Razorback Sucker]]. The four endangered "big-river" fishes of concern are [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*).

Within the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program, electrofishing of juvenile humpback chub in shoreline habitats of the lower [[Colorado River]], [[Grand Canyon National Park]], has proven successful and is being considered for long-term monitoring. Within the Five-Year [[Flaming Gorge Flow Investigations]] (FGFI) program, electrofishing over spawning grounds during spawning season is the preferred method for monitoring adult razorback sucker in the [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]], Colorado-Utah. Recent research has documented successful reproduction by razorback sucker in these rivers through larval collection.

Information on electrical field effects on fish gametes or early life stages is sparse and limited primarily to salmonids. However, researchers suggest that electric fields harmful to adults would probably also be dangerous to young fish. Newman and Stone (1992) found significant reductions in egg viability in walleye (*Stizostedion vitreum*) exposed to pulsed DC current. Marriott (1973) found that mortality of eggs from shocked female pink salmon (*Oncorhynchus gorbusha*) was nearly double that of unshocked females. Dwyer et al. (1993) indicated that electrofishing over recently deposited trout eggs can increase mortality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River System]] supports four endangered "big-river" fish species that evolved in the unique conditions of this large, turbid, warm-water system. [[Humpback Chub]] inhabit shoreline habitats in the lower Colorado River within [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. [[Razorback Sucker]] spawn in the [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]] systems in Colorado-Utah, including areas in and below [[Dinosaur National Monument]].

Electrofishing is widely used for monitoring and capturing these endangered species, but concerns exist about potential injuries and stress to fish, particularly during sensitive life stages. For juvenile humpback chub, electrofishing in shoreline habitats has proven effective and is being considered for long-term population monitoring. For razorback sucker, electrofishing over spawning grounds is believed less stressful than trammel netting for capturing adults, but repeated exposure may harm spawning adults, gametes, developing embryos, or larvae before swim-up and dispersal.

Water conductivity and temperature vary seasonally and spatially in the Colorado River system. The proposal notes that in situ conductivities at USGS gages (Bright Angel Creek, Little Colorado River, Lee's Ferry, National Canyon, Diamond Creek) and in the [[Green River]] are similar to laboratory water supply sources, requiring minimal adjustment for experimental conditions.

## Key Findings

**Study Objectives:**
1. **GCES Objective**: Evaluate acute and latent effects of presently used and potentially less harmful electrofishing fields on body (spinal and soft-tissue injuries), survival, and growth of juvenile (young-of-year and yearling) [[Humpback Chub]].
2. **FGFI Objective**: Evaluate acute and latent effects of presently used and potentially less harmful electrofishing fields on body (spinal and soft-tissue injuries; developmental anomalies), survival, and growth of embryonic and larval [[Razorback Sucker]].

**Proposed Methodology:**

**Part 1—Pilot Experiments (Fall 1993-Spring/Summer 1994):**
- Develop and refine techniques using approximately 115 juvenile humpback chub (40-60 mm TL as of 1 April) obtained from [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], reared from [[Little Colorado River]] stock
- Supplement with surrogate species: [[Bonytail]] or [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), as roundtail chub is the most similar non-endangered species and readily available
- Determine voltage gradient (volts/cm) at which tetany occurs using common waveforms: Coffelt's VVP-15 (rectangular 80-Hz PDC with 5-ms pulses, 40% duty cycle; 60-Hz PDC with 4-ms pulses, 25% duty cycle; 30-Hz PDC with 4-ms pulses, 12% duty cycle) and Coffelt's CPS (pulse train of three 240-Hz, 1.6-ms pulses every 15th of a second, 7.2% duty cycle)
- Test in rectangular concrete raceway with full cross-sectional electrodes to ensure homogenous field
- Experimental design: 10 fish per species per group (treatment and control), three replicates
- Water conductivity and temperature will approximate field conditions
- Post-testing: document immediate and short-term mortalities, freeze surviving individuals, necropsy for internal injuries (hemorrhaging, spinal injury, organ damage)
- Analysis: Fisher exact test using 2×2 contingency tables to test association between injuries and electrofishing exposure

**Part 2—Definitive Experiments (Spring 1994-Winter 1994/95):**

*GCES—Effects on Juvenile Humpback Chub:*
- Test multiple species (possibly humpback chub, bonytail, and/or roundtail chub) and size groups
- Experimental design: 20 fish per species per size group per experimental group, three replicates
- Test predetermined voltage gradients and exposure times for three behavioral responses: taxis, narcosis, and tetany
- Evaluate four waveform types at constant water conductivity and temperature
- Monitor survival and growth for 2-3 months post-exposure
- Random subsamples sacrificed for necropsy; remaining fish monitored for long-term effects
- Analysis: Two-way ANOVA to test effects of voltage gradients, waveforms, growth, and long-term survival

*FGFI—Effects on Razorback Sucker Embryos and Early Larvae:*
- Obtain eggs from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]
- Experimental design: 20 viable eggs or newly hatched larvae per group (treatment and control), three replicates
- Test four waveform types at 1.2 volts/cm; additional tests at 5 and 10 volts/cm using 60-Hz PDC
- Exposure time: 10 seconds for each current/waveform/voltage gradient
- Conduct separate trials at three embryonic stages: early-mid epiboly, early tail-bud, fin-fold; and before swim-up for larvae
- Monitor hatching success, larval growth, development, and survival
- Rear surviving larvae for 1-4 weeks (possibly longer)
- Analysis: Two-way ANOVA to test effects of treatments and developmental stages on egg damage, hatching success, growth, anomalies, and survival; One-way ANOVA for larval growth and survival

**Part 3—Final Report (Spring-Early Summer 1995):**
- M.S. thesis by Jack Ruppert consisting of multiple chapters, each suitable for peer-reviewed publication
- Revised according to U.S. Bureau of Reclamation requirements and submitted as final report
