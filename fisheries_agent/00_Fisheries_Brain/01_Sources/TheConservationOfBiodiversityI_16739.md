## Executive Summary

This chapter by Mario A. Ramos (Instituto Nacional de Investigaciones sobre Recursos Bioticos, INIREB, Chiapas, Mexico) argues that global biodiversity conservation is a **shared responsibility between developed and developing nations**, using Mexico as a case study. The piece surveys Mexico's conservation awareness (growth of NGOs like [[Monarca A.C.]] and [[Pronatura A.C.]]), the socioeconomic environment (population growth, urbanization, debt crisis, oil-price collapse), the political environment (fragmented environmental governance, the creation of [[SEDUE]]), and the technical/scientific environment (eroding research capacity, low prestige of taxonomy and systematics). It closes with concrete recommendations for developed nations and international organizations to support conservation in the Third World. An appended excerpt from Robert J. A. Goodland (The World Bank) introduces the **1985 World Bank Wildlands Policy**, a landmark institutional commitment to avoid financing projects that convert ecologically significant "wildlands," including wetland and aquatic habitats of conservation concern.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although not fish-centric, the paper situates conservation issues within a broader **habitat and ecosystem framework** relevant to aquatic and terrestrial biodiversity in Latin America:

- **Protected areas and biosphere reserves**: Mentions the [[Montes Azules Biosphere Reserve]] (Chiapas) and the [[Sian Ka'an Biosphere Reserve]] (Quintana Roo), the latter encompassing coastal/marine and wetland habitats explicitly cited as a model for balancing human use and natural resource sustainability.
- **Fisheries governance**: Notes that historically in Mexico, "fisheries" were administered under a separate Fisheries Ministry, distinct from pollution control (Health Ministry) and protected areas (Agriculture/Urban Development Ministries) — illustrating institutional fragmentation affecting aquatic resource management prior to consolidation under [[SEDUE]] (Urban Development and Ecology Ministry) in 1982.
- **Wildlands policy (World Bank)**: The Goodland excerpt defines "wildlands" as natural land and water areas in a state "virtually unmodified by human activity," explicitly including water areas — relevant to freshwater and wetland/aquatic habitat conservation financing. The policy's first element restricts Bank financing for projects converting wildlands of "special concern" deemed exceptionally important for biological diversity.
- **Resource extraction pressures**: Petrochemical and oil industry expansion (Mexico's principal export) is identified as a major driver of air, water, and soil pollution, with direct implications for aquatic ecosystem degradation, though no specific aquatic taxa are analyzed.
- **Sustainable-use models**: References ancient Mayan and Brazilian indigenous land/resource-use systems and contemporary projects (e.g., the [[Green Iguana Project]], INIREB captive-breeding programs for crocodiles and peccaries) as examples of balancing biodiversity conservation with human resource needs — a conceptual framework transferable to sustainable fisheries and aquatic resource management.

## Key Findings

- **Population and development pressure**: Mexico's population grew from ~19 million (1940) to ~79 million (1985); urban population increased 10-fold (4M → 41M) between 1940–1980, intensifying pressure on natural resources including water and aquatic habitats.
- **Economic crisis constraints on conservation**: Hyperinflation (105% in 1986), currency devaluation (19.95 to ~700 pesos/USD, 1976–1986), and capital flight (~US$55 billion, 1979–1982) severely limited government capacity to fund environmental/resource protection, including fisheries and aquatic habitat programs.
- **Institutional fragmentation**: Environmental and resource management (pollution, fisheries, protected areas) were historically split across multiple ministries, undermining coherent aquatic and terrestrial ecosystem governance until the 1982 creation of [[SEDUE]].
- **Scientific capacity erosion**: Real salaries for Mexican scientists dropped to 30% of 1979 levels; research budgets cut 23–27% in 1986 due to falling oil revenues, directly limiting capacity for basic research in **systematics, taxonomy, and biogeography** — fields foundational to cataloguing aquatic and marine biodiversity.
- **International legal gaps**: Mexico had not signed the [[Ramsar Convention]] (Wetlands of International Importance), the [[CITES Convention]], or the [[Bonn Convention]] (Migratory Species) at time of writing, representing significant gaps in legal protection for aquatic/wetland habitats and migratory aquatic species.
- **World Bank Wildlands Policy (1985)**: A major financing-side conservation mechanism explicitly covering "natural land **and water areas**," restricting development financing for high-value wildlands and marking a shift toward biodiversity-conscious lending by a major international financial institution — indirectly significant for future aquatic habitat protection funding.
- **Recommended shared-responsibility framework**: The paper calls for international organizations (FAO, World Wildlife Fund, Nature Conservancy, World Bank, Inter-American Development Bank) to support local capacity building, interdisciplinary research, and functional (not merely paper) protected areas — a framework applicable to aquatic/marine reserve effectiveness in Latin America.
