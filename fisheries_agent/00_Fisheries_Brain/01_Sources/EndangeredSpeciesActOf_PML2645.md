# Endangered Species Act of 1973 (Statutory Text Excerpt)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-species-act

## Executive Summary

This document is a reproduced compilation of the United States [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 (Pub. L. 93-205, 87 Stat. 884, codified at 16 U.S.C. §§ 1531 et seq.), with its amendments through 1988, as published by the Bureau of National Affairs. It is a legal text rather than a research study. It sets out congressional findings, definitions, listing procedures, federal consultation duties, state cooperation, enforcement, and funding. It covers fish, wildlife, and plants generally, so it is not specific to any single species, habitat, or ocean basin.

## Background & Description

The Act was enacted on December 28, 1973, and amended repeatedly between 1975 and 1988 (the amendment list runs from PL 94-325 to PL 100-707). Its stated purposes are to provide a means for conserving the ecosystems on which endangered and threatened species depend, and to conserve those species. The table of contents lists 18 sections, covering topics from interagency cooperation (Sec. 7) to the annual cost analysis by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Sec. 18).

Key definitions in Section 3 include:
- **Endangered species:** any species in danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of its range, excluding insects designated as pests.
- **Threatened species:** any species likely to become endangered within the foreseeable future.
- **Species:** includes subspecies and distinct population segments of vertebrate fish or wildlife that interbreed when mature.
- **Take:** to harass, harm, pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture, or collect, or to attempt such conduct.
- **Critical habitat:** specific areas with essential physical or biological features, plus, where the Secretary determines it necessary, areas outside the occupied range.
- **Conserve:** all methods needed to bring a species to the point where the Act's measures are no longer necessary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Findings (Sec. 2) state that various species of fish, wildlife, and plants have been rendered extinct through economic growth and development without adequate concern and conservation. Others have been depleted so that they are endangered or threatened. The Act also cites the United States' international commitments under treaties including the migratory bird treaties with [[Canada]] and [[Mexico]], the Migratory and Endangered Bird Treaty with [[Japan]], the [[Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species]] (CITES), the International Convention for the Northwest Atlantic Fisheries, and the International Convention for the High Seas Fisheries of the North Pacific Ocean.

Listing decisions under Section 4 must weigh five factors: habitat destruction or modification; overutilization; disease or predation; inadequacy of regulatory mechanisms; and other natural or manmade factors. Determinations must rest on the best scientific and commercial data available. Section 4 also requires five-year status reviews of all listed species and recovery plans with measurable delisting criteria.

## Key Findings

The Act's main provisions, summarized from the text:

- **Listing and critical habitat (Sec. 4):** The Secretary of the Interior (or Commerce for marine species) lists species based on the five factors above. Critical habitat is designated concurrently with listing where prudent. Petitions must receive a 90-day finding and a 12-month decision. Proposed rules require at least 90 days' notice, with a one-year deadline for final rules, extendable by six months.
- **Federal duties (Sec. 7):** Every federal agency must ensure that its actions are not likely to jeopardize listed species or adversely modify critical habitat. Consultation has a 90-day default period, and biological assessments are due within 180 days.
- **Endangered Species Committee ("God Squad") (Sec. 7(e)-(p)):** Seven members can grant exemptions from Section 7(a)(2) only by a vote of at least five. Exemptions require mitigation measures. A national security exemption is available from the Secretary of Defense.
- **State cooperation (Sec. 6):** Federal agencies cooperate with states that run adequate conservation programs. Federal cost share is capped at 75 percent, rising to 90 percent for multistate agreements. Section 6(i) created the cooperative endangered species conservation fund, funded at 5 percent of certain Federal Aid to Wildlife Restoration receipts.
- **Prohibited acts (Sec. 9):** For endangered fish and wildlife, it is unlawful to import, export, take (within U.S. waters, the territorial sea, or the high seas), possess, sell, or transport such species. Plant prohibitions are more limited and largely concern trespass and removal from federal land.
- **Exceptions (Sec. 10):** Scientific-purpose and enhancement permits require a conservation plan. Permits require findings that the taking is incidental and will not appreciably reduce the species' survival. Hardship exemptions, Alaska Native subsistence provisions, and pre-Act scrimshaw and antique article exemptions also apply.
- **Penalties (Sec. 11):** Civil penalties reach $25,000 per violation for knowing violations of major provisions, $12,000 for other regulations, and $500 for other violations. Criminal fines reach $50,000 and up to one year's imprisonment for knowing violations of major provisions. Rewards and citizen suits are also provided for.
- **Authorized funding (Sec. 15):** Interior was authorized up to $35,000,000 for fiscal 1988, rising to $40,000,000 in 1992 (figures as given in the OCR text). Commerce and Agriculture also received authorizations, along with $600,000 per year for the Endangered Species Committee.
- **Related statutes (Sec. 13 and Sec. 17):** Conforming amendments tie the Act to the Migratory Bird Conservation Act, the Land and Water Conservation Fund Act, and the Marine Mammal Protection Act. The Act does not override the more restrictive provisions of the Marine Mammal Protection Act.
- **Editor's notes:** The text includes an editor's note on Section 1010 of PL 100-478, which requires the EPA to study and educate the public about an endangered species pesticides labeling program.
