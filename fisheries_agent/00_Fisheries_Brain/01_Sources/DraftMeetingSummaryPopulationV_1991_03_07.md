## Executive Summary

This document summarizes a 1991 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service workshop convened to evaluate whether **Population Viability Analysis (PVA)** and **risk analysis** could be used to refine recovery goals for four endangered Colorado River fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Experts presented decision theory frameworks and PVA methodology, followed by a qualitative PVA case-study exercise for Colorado Squawfish. Key conclusions were that razorback sucker and bonytail chub populations are declining too rapidly for formal PVA (requiring immediate deterministic mortality reversal), while Colorado Squawfish and Little Colorado River Humpback Chub populations were stable enough to support a formal PVA. Substantial knowledge gaps were identified regarding habitat distribution, genetic stock structure, mortality sources, and population dynamics.

## Background & Description

### Target Species and Status
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): Occupies ~1,200 km of currently occupied habitat across the Yampa/Green, Upper Colorado, and San Juan subbasins. Considered stable enough for formal PVA.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): Virtually extirpated in the wild except in **Lake Mojave**, where remaining individuals are nearly 50 years old — indicating a complete recruitment failure over multiple decades.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Reproduction documented but no evidence of successful recruitment. Approximately 1,000 old individuals remain in the **Green River**; Lake Mojave individuals are also nearly 50 years old.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): Gene flow between Lower Basin and Upper Basin populations has been interrupted, fragmenting what was likely a historically connected metapopulation. Little Colorado River population considered stable enough for formal PVA.

### Historical Context
- Prior to 1979, little was known about the distribution or life history of these fishes.
- Substantial habitat and fish losses occurred before baseline scientific data were collected.
- Remnant stocks persist primarily in areas that have been relatively unimpacted by development.
- **42 species of nonnative fishes** have been introduced into the Colorado River system, with 10 considered abundant — representing a major competitive and predatory pressure on native fishes.

### Methodological Frameworks Presented
1. **Decision Theory / Risk Analysis** (Lynn Maguire, Duke University) — a 9-step process for evaluating recovery alternatives, water resource development trade-offs, including establishing objectives, defining measurement criteria, identifying uncertainty sources, and conducting sensitivity analyses.
2. **Population Viability Analysis (PVA)** (Peter Brussard, University of Nevada) — outlines the extinction process as a transition from systematic deterministic decline to stochastic-driven collapse once habitat fragmentation crosses a threshold.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Distribution
- **Currently occupied habitat**: ~1,200 km across Yampa/Green, Upper Colorado, and San Juan subbasins.
- **Currently unoccupied (historical) habitat**: Three ~100 km stretches in the Salt, Verde, and Lower Colorado rivers.
- Suitable adult squawfish habitat is characterized by wide, slow, meandering river sections >5 km long (yielding higher catch rates than narrow canyon reaches).
- Spawning success is correlated with less-altered river reaches exhibiting higher spring flows.

### Systematic (Deterministic) Mortality Factors
**Human-induced:**
- **Fishing mortality**: ~5% per year for adult Colorado Squawfish (range 5–20%), based on ~10% angler harvest rate from tagged/radio-tagged fish.
- **Flow regulation**: Altered water temperatures, stranding of larvae/young-of-year (YOY)/juveniles, cold-shock events, inundation of nursery habitat, and migration barriers.
- **Research sampling mortality**: Electroshocking, radio-tagging, and larval collection/pickling.
- **Contaminants**: Pesticide applications (e.g., Mormon cricket spraying) and **selenium** toxicity are suspected concerns.

**Natural mortality:**
- Predation by native roundtail on squawfish larvae/young.
- Predation by nonnatives — an estimated 20 species consume eggs, 10 consume larvae, 5 consume juveniles (though stomach content data are lacking).
- Disease/parasite vectoring by nonnatives (e.g., fathead minnows carry *Ich*, to which squawfish show high sensitivity).
- Food supply limitation linked to altered flow regimes, reservoirs acting as nutrient sinks, and competition with nonnatives.

### Stochastic Factors
- **Demographic stochasticity**: Considered relatively unimportant given assumed large population sizes, except at critically low numbers.
- **Genetic stochasticity**: Effective population ratio (**Ne/N**) estimated at ~0.1, based on skewed sex ratios (~15 males captured per female on spawning grounds; ~2:1 male-biased catch generally), variance in progeny numbers, and overlapping generations.
- **Environmental stochasticity**: Runoff magnitude, timing, duration, and frequency strongly influence recruitment — high spring flows benefit natives (preparing spawning substrate, flooding bottomlands) while suppressing nonnatives; low flows favor nonnatives and increase disease/crowding/predation risk for natives.
- **Catastrophic risks**: Oil spills, forest fires, sustained drought, disease epidemics, contaminant spills, and extreme flow events.

### Population Structure
- Believed **3 distinct subpopulations (stocks)** exist within the Yampa/Green system.
- Genetic interchange among stocks appears limited, based on radio-tagged fish showing fidelity to specific spawning areas.
- Evolutionary history suggests low colonization rates of new spawning areas by squawfish.

## Key Findings

1. **Species-specific PVA readiness**: Formal PVA is not feasible for razorback sucker or bonytail chub due to ongoing population decline; reversing deterministic mortality is the top priority for these species. Colorado Squawfish and Little Colorado River Humpback Chub populations are stable enough for formal PVA modeling.

2. **Population estimates (guesses) for Colorado Squawfish** by subbasin:

| Life Stage | Yampa/Green | Colorado (Upper) | San Juan | LCRB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adults | 3,000 | 500 | 50 | — |
| Sub-Adults | 7,000 | 700 | + | — |
| Age 0-1 | 50,000 | 2,000 | 100 | 1,600 |

3. **Skewed sex ratios**: Approximately 15 males captured per female on spawning grounds; roughly 2:1 male bias in general catch — raising concerns about effective breeding population size and genetic diversity (Ne/N ≈ 0.1).

4. **Extinction process model**: Extinction proceeds from systematic/deterministic decline (habitat loss, overexploitation, fragmentation) to a threshold where stochastic factors (demographic, environmental, genetic, catastrophic) accelerate collapse — the "**extinction vortex**." Recovery requires both crisis management (halting deterministic decline) and preventive management (buffering against stochastic risk).

5. **Nonnative fish impact**: 42 introduced nonnative species (10 abundant) represent significant predation, competition, and disease-vector pressure on native fish eggs, larvae, and juveniles.

6. **Critical knowledge gaps identified**, including:
   - Genetic stock structure and migration rates among squawfish subpopulations
   - Status of squawfish in Lake Powell and the Dirty Devil River
   - Proportion of mortality attributable to research sampling vs. predation vs. fishing
   - Squawfish tolerance to selenium contamination
   - Spawning site fidelity across the species' range
   - Diet composition and proportion of nonnative prey consumed

7. **Recommended actions**: Conduct qualitative PVA for all four species; pursue a contracted formal PVA for Colorado Squawfish; conduct risk analyses to prioritize recovery activities; and immediately address deterministic mortality sources for razorback sucker and bonytail chub.

8. **Conceptual framework**: The workshop proposed an iterative management loop — Rare Fish Data → Evaluation of Threats (PVA + Risk Analysis) → Evaluation of Recovery Measures → Recovery Goals — emphasizing that new information should continuously refine recovery strategy.
