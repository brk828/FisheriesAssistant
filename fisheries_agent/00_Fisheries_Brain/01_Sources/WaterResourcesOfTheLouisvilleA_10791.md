# Water Resources of the Louisville Area, Kentucky and Indiana

#hydrology #water-resources #groundwater #surface-water #ohio-river

## Executive Summary

This 1953 U.S. Geological Survey circular assesses the water resources of the Louisville metropolitan area, which comprises Jefferson County, Kentucky and parts of Clark and Floyd Counties, Indiana. The [[Ohio River]] is the dominant source, with a 25-year average flow of 75,000 mgd, while the [[Ohio River Valley Alluvium]] provides the principal ground-water supply. Present use is a small fraction of available supply: 764.6 mgd total in 1952, against a potential of roughly 2,600 mgd of river flow and about 450 mgd of ground water. The report concludes that quantity is unlikely to be limiting, but that river pollution, floods, temperature, and ground-water depletion require management.

The document is not a fish or aquatic-biology study. It contains no species-level data, so the taxonomic fields are marked N/A. The aquatic content concerns water chemistry, temperature, flow, and habitat setting.

## Background & Description

The report is one of a series on water resources of selected industrial areas, prepared by the Water Resources Division for defense planning and municipal and industrial expansion. Its authors are M. I. Rorabaugh, F. F. Schrader, and L. B. Laird.

The metropolitan area is near the center of the Ohio River drainage basin. Its three topographic zones are:
- Rough, dissected ridges in eastern Jefferson County, 700 to 750 feet above sea level, plus the "Knobs" remnant plateau.
- Gently sloping uplands in northern and central Jefferson County (the Muscatatuck regional slope).
- The Ohio River flood plain, a belt from about half a mile to 5 miles wide.

Bedrock consists of Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, and Mississippian limestones, shales, and sandstones. The Ohio River cut a deep valley into this bedrock during glacial times, and the valley was later filled with glacial outwash sand and gravel, up to 150 feet thick, under a clay blanket 5 to 40 feet thick.

Climate is continental. The mean annual temperature is 57.0°F, and average annual precipitation is 42.95 inches. The frost-free period runs from early April to late October.

Population of the tri-county area was about 580,000 in 1950, a gain of nearly 30 percent over 1940-50.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrologic cycle.** Of the nearly 43 inches of precipitation, about 15 inches becomes runoff. In the flood-plain area, about 6 inches per year percolates to the water table. Summer evapotranspiration creates a soil-moisture deficit, so recharge occurs mainly in winter.

**Shale and limestone terrain.** Shale areas have little ground water and generate high runoff, and their drainage problems cause septic systems to overflow. Limestone areas have solutional crevices, which allow rapid drainage to streams and sinkhole entry.

**Small streams.** Five major tributaries were studied: [[Harrods Creek]], [[Beargrass Creek]] (Middle and South Forks), [[Silver Creek]], [[Floyds Fork]], and [[Pond Creek]]. These drain more than two-thirds of the area. The report describes them as unreliable supply sources because flows become very low or cease in dry periods. Pond Creek is affected by acid industrial wastes, and Beargrass Creek's flow is augmented by urban leakage.

**Pollution.** Acid mine drainage from upstream, along with industrial and domestic wastes, alters the Ohio River's natural calcium-magnesium bicarbonate character. Bacterial pollution at the Louisville water intake reached 2,300,000 coliform MPN per 100 ml in September 1950.

**Temperature.** River water ranges from 32°F to about 85°F, and surface temperature averaged 59°F over 1946-52. Ground water is more thermally stable, averaging about 58°F.

## Key Findings

**Surface water**
- Ohio River average flow at Louisville (1928-52): 75,000 mgd (116,000 cfs). Maximum: 1,110,000 cfs on January 27, 1937. Minimum: 1,360 mgd (2,100 cfs) on August 12, 1930.
- Flow equaled or exceeded 3,500 mgd 99 percent of the time. The 20-year-recurrence minimum daily flow is about 2,600 mgd (4,000 cfs).
- Drainage area at Louisville: 91,170 square miles, about 45 percent of the 204,000-square-mile basin.
- Hardness of river water averaged 146 ppm in 1952, with a range of 58 to 270 ppm. Alkalinity averaged 58 ppm.
- Navigation: the channel is maintained at 9 feet. Dam 41 creates an upper pool at 420 feet above sea level. Over 18,000 boat passages were recorded at dam 41 in 1951.
- Upstream reservoirs built for flood control may raise low flows at Louisville.

**Floods**
- The 1937 flood, the highest and most destructive on record, crested at 57.15 feet at the upper gage (460.15 feet above sea level). The 1945 flood was 10 feet lower at the crest.
- Most high floods occur January to April.
- Flood walls protect Louisville, Jeffersonville, and New Albany. As of 1953, Jeffersonville was complete, Louisville was complete except for pumping stations, and New Albany was under construction.

**Ground water**
- Estimated potential: about 450 mgd. Present use in 1952 was 34.2 mgd, or 8 percent of that potential.
- Individual wells in the valley alluvium commonly yield 200 to 500 gpm.
- A critical shortage in 1943 left pumpage about 20 mgd above natural recharge. Conservation measures brought pumpage back into balance with recharge by 1946.
- Ground-water hardness: the median in sand and gravel was 475 ppm in 1952. Over limestone bedrock, the median was 642 ppm, against 470 ppm over shale. Limestone wells averaged 580 ppm.
- Ground-water temperature averages about 58°F. Along the river north of Louisville, it ranges from 52°F to 66°F.

**Water use (1952)**
- Surface water: 730 mgd. Of this, 659.1 mgd was industrial cooling, 71.4 mgd was public supply, and 85.6 mgd was chemical-plant cooling.
- Ground water: 34.1 mgd, of which 30.8 mgd was private industrial use.
- Total: 764.6 mgd. Domestic use was 18.6 mgd, and industrial and commercial use was 746.0 mgd.
- Four public systems served about 500,000 people with 74.2 mgd. Louisville Water Company's plant has a rated capacity of 80 mgd and delivered 67.9 mgd in 1952.

**Policy and management conclusions**
- The report judges that the river's flow will not limit total use. Temperature and chemical pollution are the more likely constraints.
- Ground-water development should rely on induced infiltration from the river.
- Water rights are governed by common-law riparian rights for surface water and quasi-riparian rights for ground water. The Ohio River Valley Water Sanitation Commission (1948, eight-state compact) and state agencies in Kentucky and Indiana regulate pollution.
