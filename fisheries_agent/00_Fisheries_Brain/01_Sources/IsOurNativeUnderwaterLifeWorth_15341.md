# Is Our Native Underwater Life Worth Saving?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 essay by [[Robert R. Miller]], Curator of Fishes at the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, argues against the widespread use of non-specific chemical toxicants, chiefly [[Rotenone]], in fishery management. Miller contends that "trash fish" eradication programs kill native fishes and the wider aquatic community, including insects, zooplankton, and bottom-dwelling invertebrates, with little scientific basis. He uses the 1962 [[Green River Rotenone Eradication]] in Wyoming, Utah, and Colorado as a central case. That operation ran out of control, damaged aquatic life in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], and was aimed at preparing [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] for sport fish. The essay calls for prior surveys of rare species, targeted and selective methods, consultation before chemical use, and recognition of native fishes as sport fish.

## Background & Description

- **Scope of chemical use:** Over the preceding 25 years, every U.S. State and all Canadian provinces had adopted chemical poisoning as a fishery-management tool. Between 1952 and 1962, the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], through its [[Dingell-Johnson Program]], supported poisoning of some 225,000 acres of lakes and 2,500 miles of streams. Miller states this is only a fraction of treated waters, since each State ran its own program.
- **Toxicity:** At 0.5 ppm of 5 percent rotenone, the chemical is toxic enough to kill most fishes and many other aquatic organisms (Burdick, Dean, and Harris, 1955). Death results from suffocation. The [[Gizzard Shad]] is especially sensitive, with fatal concentrations of 0.10 to 0.15 ppm.
- **Terminology:** Miller argues that calling rotenone a non-poison because it kills by suffocation is incorrect, since the dictionary defines rotenone as a poison. He also criticizes the term "trash fish," which includes native as well as introduced species, and the term "rehabilitation," which he says is used for propaganda.
- **Legitimate uses:** Miller accepts limited application in enclosed lakes, artificial ponds, and reservoirs for localized problems, such as stunting in [[Yellow Perch]] or overpopulation by exotic species.
- **Threatened native fauna:** Native fishes of the American Southwest often occupy small ranges. Since Miller began studying the region, eight species have gone extinct and thirty-one others are in jeopardy, together about 40 percent of the known native freshwater fishes of Western North America (north of Mexico).
- **Policy context:** Miller cites H.R. 2857, introduced January 25, 1963, by Representative John D. Dingell, which would require advance consultation with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and State wildlife agencies before federal programs using pesticides or chemicals for mass biological control.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Endemism:** The [[Colorado River Basin]], of which the Green River is a major tributary, has a higher percentage of endemic species than any other river in Western North America. Eighty-seven percent of its native freshwater fishes occur nowhere else.
- **Threatened species in the affected reach:** The Green River section treated with rotenone held ideal habitat for four rare species:
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): the largest minnow in the Western Hemisphere, once approaching six feet and nearly 100 pounds.
  - [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): specialized for torrentially swift water.
  - Swift-river form of the [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus delphinus*).
  - [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): the least threatened of the four.
- **Pressures on native fishes:** Overgrazing, deforestation, dams and water diversions, lowering of the water table, and competition with introduced species all threaten native fauna (Miller, 1961).
- **Insects and invertebrates:** Mayflies are important in the food cycle of many fishes. Other insect groups in the river include species at the western limit of their range and species found nowhere else. Effects on microscopic and bottom-dwelling invertebrates were largely unknown. Rotenone is known to affect zooplankton in freshwater lakes (Almquist, 1959; Kiser, Donaldson, and Olson, 1963).
- **Reservoir transition:** Flaming Gorge Reservoir began impounding on November 1, 1962, in a 91-mile section of river behind Ashley Dam. The river there is silt-laden, and the area is not a native trout habitat, although tributaries held the [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]].

## Key Findings

### The Green River Poisoning (September 1962)

- More than 20,000 gallons of emulsified rotenone preparation were applied to nearly 500 miles of river between September 4 and 8, 1962, by more than 100 men.
- The poison cost exceeded $157,000. Congress authorized funding in June 1961 after approval by the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife.
- Rotenone was introduced at 5 ppm of 5 percent rotenone at 17 of 22 stations, timed for a continuous flow of 86 hours. Stations 18 to 21 received about 4 ppm, and station 22 received 2 ppm, which is still four times the concentration needed to kill most fishes.
- The chemical was also introduced at 15 tributary stations in Wyoming and Utah.
- Miller describes the project as the most extensive eradication of its kind ever undertaken, and calls it interstate pollution.
- Managers admitted that success would mean a minimum of six years of good trout fishing.

### Ecological Damage

- Entomologist [[George F. Edmunds]] found disappointingly few aquatic insects three weeks after the project ended. Hand screens yielded no insects at all in some attempts.
- No dobsonflies or dragonflies were found. Both require more than one year to develop, so their absence could not be explained by emergence.
- Mayflies were present at less than 10 percent of normal populations, except one sand-burrowing kind that escaped the rotenone.
- Verbal reports indicated fish mortality as far as 110 river miles below the project's terminus. Miller notes the distance from Ashley Dam to the Jensen, Utah bridge is 108 river miles.
- Tens of thousands of valuable insect specimens were killed and not recovered, and some species may have been exterminated.

### Detoxification Failure

- Detoxification was attempted at Browns Park Bridge in Colorado, about 30 miles downstream from Flaming Gorge Dam and 16 miles above the Dinosaur National Monument boundary. Earlier plans had called for a station at the dam base.
- Potassium permanganate was applied from about 6 a.m. September 7 to about 5 p.m. September 11, 1962. It did not protect animals in or below Dinosaur National Monument. Authorities knew of this failure on or before September 15.
- The Utah Department of Fish and Game nonetheless published a statement claiming successful detoxification (Regenthal, 1962).
- Miller reports that no advance tests were made of potassium permanganate's effectiveness in rivers the size of the Green River.
- Rotenone reportedly traveled more than 115 miles below the last station, roughly 8 miles above Ashley Dam, and the project went ahead even though closure of the dam was delayed.
- Assurances from the Fish and Wildlife Service (letters of September 20 and October 12, 1961) said effects would not reach Colorado, which Miller says proved false.

### Recommendations and Alternatives

- Agencies should identify rare, threatened, or scientifically important species before applying toxicants.
- Agencies should notify interested persons of treatment plans.
- If non-specific toxicants must be used, breeding stock of threatened species could be removed beforehand, held during treatment, and reintroduced after the poison dissipates. This works only if habitat remains suitable.
- Development of selective compounds, such as those against [[Common Carp]], is a step in the right direction. The Fish and Wildlife Service's La Crosse, Wisconsin laboratory can perform more than 600 bioassay tests per week. The [[Sea Lamprey]] control compound (Applegate, Howell, and Smith, 1958) is cited as an example, though its selectivity depends on costly logistics.
- The American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists has two committees offering information: a Committee on Fish Conservation (chaired by Carl L. Hubbs) and a Herpetological Conservation Committee (chaired by Frederick R. Gehlbach). They compiled a list of rare, restricted, and endangered fishes, amphibians, and reptiles.
- Miller notes that the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists passed a 1961 resolution condemning the Green River poisoning.

### Sport Fishing and Native Species

- Miller argues that native fishes such as the Colorado squawfish could become prized sport fish, particularly as fishing shifts toward recreation over food or trophies. He notes the squawfish is larger than rainbow trout.
- He criticizes replacing native squawfish with [[Rainbow Trout]], citing European use of carp and other minnows as sport fish.
- Miller's stated conclusion is that fishery biologists will judge their predecessors harshly if native sport fish are eliminated by chemical eradication.
