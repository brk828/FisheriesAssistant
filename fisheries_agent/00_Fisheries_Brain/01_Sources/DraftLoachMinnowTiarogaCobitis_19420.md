# Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis) Recovery Plan (Draft, 1988)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is a 1988 draft recovery plan for the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), a small, secretive, bottom-dwelling cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico, with a formerly recorded range in Sonora, Mexico. Prepared by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) for [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region 2, the plan was listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973, as amended). It is an agency review draft, not an approved government document, and its goals are contingent on appropriations and priorities.

The species has declined from a formerly widespread, locally abundant fish to scattered tributary populations. The plan attributes decline to habitat alteration (dewatering, impoundment, grazing, sedimentation, contamination) and to invasion by non-native fishes, most notably the [[Red Shiner]]. Its primary objective is to protect existing populations, restore depleted and extirpated ones, and ensure self-sustaining populations, which would justify delisting. A stepdown outline of nine major tasks, a priority-ranked implementation schedule, and a critical habitat appendix are provided.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and morphology.** The loach minnow belongs to a unique, monotypic genus and family Cyprinidae. Described by [[Girard]] (1857) and summarized by [[Minckley]] (1973), it has an elongated, slightly compressed, ventrally flattened body; 8 dorsal and 7 anal fin rays; about 45 lateral line scales; a small, terminal, oblique mouth without barbels; and pharyngeal teeth in two rows (formula 1,4-4,1). Its olive body is blotched with dark pigment, with depigmented spots at fin bases and a basicaudal spot. Breeding males show red-orange coloration on fins and body.

**Status.** The species was not considered imperiled by [[Miller]] (1961) but its rarity was noted in later decades. It is listed as a Group 2 endangered species in New Mexico (protected under the New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act) and a Group 3 threatened species in Arizona. Neither state specifically protects occupied habitat.

**Life history (summary).**
- **Reproduction:** First spawning at age I, late winter to early spring in Aravaipa Creek and late March to early June in New Mexico. Adhesive eggs are laid under flattened rocks; eggs per rock range from fewer than 5 to more than 250 (population means of 52 to 43). Fecundity is about 150 to 250 mature ova. Mature ova average about 1.5 mm.
- **Growth:** Larvae are about 5 mm at hatching. Age-0 fish reach 30 to more than 40 mm SL by mid-summer and slightly more than 50 mm SL by year's end. Age II fish reach about 68 mm SL at most. Longevity is usually 15 to 24 months, occasionally 36.
- **Diet:** Opportunistic benthic insectivore, feeding on riffle-dwelling mayfly larvae (ephemeropterans), black fly and midge larvae (simuliids and chironomids), with stoneflies and caddisflies seasonally important.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Loach minnow is restricted almost entirely to the bottom because of its reduced gas bladder. Occupied habitat is shallow, with moderate to swift current and gravel-to-cobble substrate. Adults occupy riffles, while larvae and juveniles use shallower, slower riffle margins. In Aravaipa Creek, occurrence is associated with dense filamentous green algae at some times, but not everywhere.

**Co-occurring fishes.**
- Native: [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), desert sucker (*Pantosteus clarki*), Sonoran and desert suckers (*Catostomus insignis*), and longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*). Interaction with the dace is likely minimal; suckers graze algae and microfauna.
- Non-native: [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) feed in riffles on prey overlapping with loach minnow diets, and flathead catfish are piscivorous even when small, so direct predation is plausible.
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) occupies stream margins used by juveniles and is strongly implicated in declines, while [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) also occupies these margins.

**Distribution.** Historically recorded in Arizona from the Salt, White, Verde, Gila, San Pedro, San Francisco, Blue and Eagle creek systems and others, and in New Mexico from the Gila (East, Middle and West forks), San Francisco and Tularosa rivers. Today it is extirpated from Mexico (though surveys are incomplete), persists in limited reaches of Arizona (notably [[Aravaipa Creek]] and the Blue River drainage), and is fragmented in New Mexico. Transplants into Sonoita Creek (1968) and Sycamore/Seven Springs Wash (1970) were later extirpated.

**Reasons for decline.** The plan identifies:
- **Dewatering** from groundwater pumping, channelization, diversion and damming.
- **Impoundment**, which creates lentic habitat and alters flow, temperature and organic drift.
- **Livestock grazing**, mining, timber harvest, agriculture and development, which raise turbidity and sediment, clog substrate and introduce toxicants.
- **Non-native fishes**, which the plan argues are necessary for extirpation: all sites with suitable but unoccupied habitat hold non-native fishes.
- A notable example: wastewater from the Cananea Mine (Sonora) killed all fishes in a 100-km reach of the San Pedro River in 1977 to 1979.

The authors note that habitat alteration and non-native interactions often occurred simultaneously, making it difficult to separate their effects.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objective.** Protect existing populations, restore depleted and extirpated populations and habitats, and ensure non-endangered self-sustainability in perpetuity. Quantitative delisting criteria await more information.

**Stepdown outline (nine major tasks):**
1. **Protect existing populations:** identify and prioritize populations, designate critical habitat, enforce laws (including Section 7 consultation under the ESA), discourage detrimental land and water uses, ensure perennial flows, curtail non-native fish introductions, and acquire and protect lands and water rights.
2. **Monitor status:** establish standard monitoring sites and techniques; determine natural variation; conduct biannual (spring and autumn) population estimates; monitor community composition.
3. **Assess non-native interactions:** investigate direct (predation, displacement) and indirect interactions through field experiments and laboratory studies.
4. **Quantify habitat effects:** determine how substrate (siltation, armoring), velocity and depth, and temperature affect life-cycle completion, and their combined effects.
5. **Enhance or restore habitats** of depleted populations.
6. **Reintroduce populations** to historic range. [[Eagle Creek]] and the [[San Pedro River]] are identified as the most amenable reintroduction systems. Loach minnow occurred in Eagle Creek in 1950 (13 individuals collected). The San Pedro is the type locality, where the species and 10 other native fishes were lost over the last century.
7. **Define self-sustaining population criteria:** natural variation in absolute numbers, age-class structure, reproduction and recruitment, minimum stock size and environmental variables.
8. **Contingency captive propagation:** not currently deemed necessary, but hatchery planning is recommended in case of imminent threat.
9. **Information and education:** public outreach (local, state, national and Mexican agencies), and professional exchange through clearinghouse, meetings and peer-reviewed publication.

**Implementation schedule.** Tasks are ranked: Priority 1 (essential to prevent extinction), Priority 2 (maintain current status), Priority 3 (all other actions for full recovery). Cost estimates are provided for USFWS expenditures only; many entries are "Unknown" or "Ongoing." Examples include determining quantitative criteria for self-sustainability (approximately $10,000 per year for 3 years) and habitat improvement (Priority 3, ongoing, unknown cost). Responsible agencies include Arizona Game and Fish Department, New Mexico Department of Game and Fish, USDA Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management and Bureau of Reclamation.

**Critical habitat (proposed, 1985).** Covers about 24 km of Aravaipa Creek (Arizona), and reaches of the Blue River (about 78 km), Campbell Blue Creek (about 14 km) and San Francisco River (about 6 km) in Greenlee County, Arizona; and Dry Blue Creek (3 km), San Francisco River (15 km), Tularosa River (24 km), East Fork Gila (24 km), West Fork Gila (12 km), Middle Fork Gila (18 km) and Gila River (37 km) in New Mexico. Designation has been deferred and remains unfinalized, so protection is limited.

**Critical knowledge gaps.** The plan stresses that little is known about the species' basic ecology, that populations in Mexico and on tribal and national forest lands are unsurveyed, and that the interplay of habitat change and non-native fish invasion needs experimental resolution.
