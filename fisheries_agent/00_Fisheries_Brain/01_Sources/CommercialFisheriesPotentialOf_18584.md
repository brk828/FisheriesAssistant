# Commercial Fisheries Potential of Reservoirs - Research Proposal

#ichthyology #reservoir-ecology #commercial-fisheries #desert-ecosystems #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1972 research proposal from [[Arizona State University]]'s Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory requests three years of funding (1972-1975) to continue studies on the commercial fisheries potential of [[Salt River]] reservoirs in central Arizona. The proposal builds on six years of prior research (1966-1972) funded under [[PL 88-309]] and addresses major operational changes to the reservoir system, particularly the installation of pump-storage generation units at [[Mormon Flat Dam]] and [[Horse Mesa Dam]]. Principal Investigator [[W. L. Minckley]] proposes to monitor system-wide changes in water quality, productivity, and fish populations as the reservoirs transition from variable water levels to stabilized conditions. The study emphasizes understanding fish dispersion patterns relative to environmental gradients and developing predictive models for commercial harvest. Total funding requested: $64,935 over three years, with anticipated supplemental support from [[Salt River Project]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

## Background & Description

The [[Salt River]] chain consists of four mainstream reservoirs: [[Roosevelt Lake]], [[Apache Lake]], [[Canyon Lake]], and [[Saguaro Lake]]. Prior research (1966-1972) documented these systems during a period of extreme variability including complete drawdowns of two lower reservoirs and major hydrological events. The 1965-66 winter runoff introduced massive organic material into the system, enriching the lakes and supporting expanded fish populations. Subsequent runoff in 1967 maintained high water levels but carried minimal organic debris.

The fish assemblage is dominated by introduced species, as native western fishes are poorly adapted to reservoir conditions. The simplified food web consists of: (1) primary consumers including [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), introduced clam *Corbicula*, and [[Buffalofishes]] (genus *Ictiobus*); (2) secondary consumers including [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), catfishes (genus *Ictalurus*), sunfishes (genus *Lepomis*), and [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*). The plankton-based food web contrasts with more complex eastern reservoir systems.

Each reservoir behaves independently despite being part of a connected system. Mobile species like threadfin shad and buffalofishes show uniform growth throughout individual lakes, presumably moving to optimize feeding. Sedentary species like largemouth bass exhibit differential growth rates in different areas, reflecting local habitat quality. This behavioral dichotomy provides opportunities to use fish distributions as indicators of environmental conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Major engineering modifications necessitate continued study:

**Pump-Storage Operations**: Installation of pump-turbine generation units at [[Mormon Flat Dam]] (affecting [[Canyon Lake]] and [[Saguaro Lake]]) and [[Horse Mesa Dam]] (affecting [[Apache Lake]] and [[Canyon Lake]]) represents unprecedented manipulation of desert reservoir systems. Preliminary test data from Mormon Flat Dam reveal significant impacts:

- Suspended solids pumped back into Canyon Lake averaged 5 pounds per acre foot (51% organic), compared to 6 pounds per acre foot (75% organic) in generation water
- Water drawn selectively from Saguaro Lake thermocline (18-20°C) contained up to 45 pounds suspended solids per acre foot (60% organic)
- Eight-hour pump-storage cycling moves equivalent of 10.3 acre feet of material comparable to domestic sewage (624 pounds suspended solids per acre foot)
- Thermal loading: twice the gram calories per unit volume pumped back versus generated
- High mortality of zooplankton (70-90% fragmentation) and fishes during pump-back
- Stratification temporarily broken but re-established within 24 hours

**System-Wide Changes**: Generation capacity increases at three dams will alter downstream habitats through modified generation cycles. Stabilization of Apache and Canyon lakes (versus continued drawdown at Roosevelt and Saguaro) creates "experimental" and "control" reservoirs for comparative study. Net retention time will increase despite constant gross flow, allowing development of entirely new environmental regimes.

**Climate Variability**: Desert climate introduces major uncertainties. Drought periods cause drawdown and organic material accumulation on watersheds. Subsequent runoff events flush accumulated organics into reservoirs, creating pulses of enrichment. The timing and magnitude of these events profoundly affect productivity.

**Anticipated Changes**: Pump-storage may push Canyon Lake toward amictic (non-stratifying), tropical, eutrophic classification. Effects may transmit downstream through Saguaro Lake into the [[Salt River]] water delivery system. Stabilization will influence adjacent water tables and aquatic vegetation development.

## Key Findings

**Proposed Research Objectives**:

1. Intensively monitor input/output of Salt River system and conditions within each lake
2. Continue manipulation and analysis of existing six-year dataset
3. Publish findings in open literature
4. Study pump-storage phenomena through controlled experiments (3 weeks generation only, 2 weeks alternating pump-storage/generation, 3 weeks generation only)
5. Assess impacts of system modifications on fish populations and overall ecology

**Research Procedures**:

*Year 1*: Further analysis and publication of 1966-1972 data; intensive surveillance of pump-storage phenomena in field and laboratory

*Year 2*: Study system under operational conditions as managed by Salt River Project

*Year 3*: Data analysis and impact assessment, emphasizing fish populations and overall ecology

**Sampling Methods**:
- Grid-based random sampling of reservoir quadrats
- Chlorophyll extraction for algal biomass
- Gravimetric techniques for plankton and benthos
- Temperature, conductivity, light penetration, dissolved gases profiles
- Small-meshed gill nets for fish dispersion (vertical and horizontal)
- Tagging programs for larger species
- Sonic detection and telemetry (if feasible)
- Multivariate regression analysis and computer simulation

**Justification**: Increasing human populations will drive demand for food fish from western reservoirs. The proposed [[Central Arizona Project]] will double reservoir acreage in the Salt River basin. Current pump-storage installations provide unique opportunity to study major habitat manipulation with stabilized "experimental" reservoirs (Apache, Canyon) and variable "control" reservoirs (Roosevelt, Saguaro). Desert reservoir systems are simpler than eastern counterparts, offering advantages for developing predictive models. Management requires indices of production and fish movements for efficient exploitation.
