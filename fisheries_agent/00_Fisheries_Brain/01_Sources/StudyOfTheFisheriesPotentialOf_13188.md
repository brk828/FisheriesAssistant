# Study of the Fisheries Potential of the Virgin Islands

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1969 Special Report, Contribution Number 1 of the [[Virgin Islands Ecological Research Station]] (VIERS), documents a joint fisheries study of the American Virgin Islands conducted by the Caribbean Research Institute of the College of the Virgin Islands with support from the U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife and the U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries. It reviews prior knowledge of the region's fish and fisheries, describes the physical and biological environment, catalogs fish communities and reef-fish sampling results, surveys the commercial fishery, provides a shoreline angling guide, and examines ciguatera (fish poisoning). The authors conclude that the local fishery is characterized by a lack of change, that demand for local seafood exceeds supply, and that ciguatera is the principal barrier to developing a local inshore fishery. Recommendations address enforcement, marketing infrastructure, fisherman assistance, gear research, and deep-water snapper, shark, cephalopod and aquaculture development.

## Background & Description

The project began in August 1965 when the Caribbean Research Institute (CRI) and the Virgin Islands Ecological Research Station established operations at Lameshur Bay, St. John, with a fisheries laboratory. A combined program, designated F-4-R (BSFW) and 2-33-R (BCF), was formalized in February 1966 under the PL-88-309 program, with matching funds from Virgin Islands legislative action. Project Leader Arthur E. Dammann directed the work.

The report's introduction notes that Caribbean research history begins with Oviedo (1526, 1535, 1537) and includes early illustrators such as John White (1585) and [[Mark Catesby]] (1731–1734), who illustrated 44 West Indian fish species. Numerous expeditions contributed, including the HMS Challenger, the U.S. Fish Commission steamer Albatross (955 Caribbean stations) and the R/V Oregon (1,461 Caribbean stations). Bayer estimated about 10,000 biological stations worldwide over 100 years, 3,446 within the Caribbean proper. A 1968 FAO/UNESCO symposium in Willemstad, Curaçao, gathered more than 150 papers, yet the authors stress that basic taxonomic inventories and species biology remain incomplete.

Methods included bottom dredges, otter trawls, plankton nets, hook and line, fish traps, rotenone poisoning of an enclosed reef, [[Glass-Bottomed Transect Survey]] (over 100 nautical miles), and water quality instruments (Nansen bottles, Niskin recorders, Secchi discs, electronic temperature/salinity/conductivity meters).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical environment.** The islands lie at roughly 65°W, 18°N, in the true tropics and in the path of the trade winds and North Equatorial Current. St. Thomas, St. John and Puerto Rico share a shelf roughly 2,000 square miles in area, with about 300 miles of shelf edge and about 500 miles of shoreline; St. Croix is isolated by the Anegada Trough. The Puerto Rican Trench (27,498 feet) is noted as the deepest point in the Atlantic. Because the islands are oceanic, they lack estuaries and permanent streams, which limits shrimp and estuarine fish production.

**Temperature and salinity.** August open-ocean sea surface temperature is 27.5–28.0 °C and January 25.5–26.0 °C, with the thermocline at 50 m (August) and 75–100 m (January). Shallow lagoon temperatures ranged from 24.0 °C to 38.2 °C. Open-water salinity is about 35 ppt. In [[Mangrove Lagoon]] on St. Thomas, the highest salinity recorded was 36.4 ppt; after more than 10 inches of rain in spring 1969 the lagoon was fresh to about 32 inches, killing virtually all marine phytoplankton and persisting for more than 60 days.

**Currents and tides.** Upwelling of deep Atlantic water supplies nutrients that support reef growth. Tidal range is only a few inches per day, so intertidal flats are scarce and steep shorelines predominate. Dredging and filling have destroyed many shallow lagoons, including Jersey Bay and Mangrove Lagoon, slated for a jet airport.

**Substrate.** About 50% of the shelf is living coral heads and patches interspersed with dead rubble; the remainder is sand and rubble. Turtle grass beds are separated from reefs by a roughly 30-foot zone of unconsolidated sand maintained by grazing reef fishes.

**Plankton and productivity.** Phytoplankton peaks near the thermocline and at about 1% surface illumination (about 150 ft in August, 225–300 ft in January). Margalef (1968) reported cell counts of 20–200/ml from more than 450 "species"; primary productivity ranged from 400 g C/m² in highly fertilized areas to 20–100 g C/m² in poor central Caribbean waters. Owre (1968) recorded 302 copepod species from the area, 28 of them newly described calanoids. Hammer (1968) listed 790 benthic algal species, 28% endemic.

**Benthic reef community.** A sampled Chocolate Hole reef (120 × 75 × 30 ft) was 43% non-living and 57% living cover, including 11 soft coral species and at least 4 sponge species. Diadema urchin density there was approximately 65/m², compared with 13.4/m² reported by Randall et al. (1964) elsewhere in the Virgin Islands.

**Mary Creek reef complex.** A fringing reef on the north shore of St. John was analyzed by Stoeckle et al. (1968). Six zones were mapped: pro-reef sand, living reef, coral rubble, grass-sand-rubble, grass-sand, and sand. Rigid frame builders (Montastrea, Diploria, Acropora palmata, A. cervicornis, Millepora) form the framework; encrusting coralline algae cement the reef. Reef growth is attributed to massive corals establishing on loose sand, with the reef front facing prevailing Northeast winds and two channels supplying circulation.

**Fish communities.** Around the islands, reef fish fall into continental and insular groups (Robins, 1968); the Virgin Islands are primarily insular, with clear, constantly warm water over calcium carbonate bottoms. Upper-slope fish of the western Caribbean show a size–depth relation tied to pelagic or benthic development (Bullis and Struhsaker, 1968). Miller (1968) describes an "Antillean Barrier" separating Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico benthic fish faunas.

## Key Findings

**Species inventory.** About 1,700 fish species are recorded for the region, of which 200 are commercially fished and about 150 are considered latent resources. The project cataloged 275 species from the Virgin Islands. Randall (1968) illustrates 300 common reef fishes, at least 60 used in the fishery. Table 10 lists pelagic species; sharks are listed in Table 11.

**Seasonal pelagic patterns (Graphs 2–6, 5.5 years of data).** Blue and white marlin peak in August, when waters are warmest. Sailfish are absent in August and peak in January. Dolphin peak in April, with high numbers from late fall through winter. Wahoo and false albacore peak in cooler months. Surface temperature differences between January and August are only about 1.5–2.5 °C.

**Reef poisoning experiment (Chocolate Hole, 1968).** The enclosed reef initially held 1,201 fish (74.8 lb; 29 families, 54 genera, 82 species) plus three spiny lobsters. After pot hauls and rotenone poisoning on 11 June, a 21-day recovery and a second poisoning on 13 September yielded about 80.8 lb of fish (1,225 individuals; 30 families, 51 genera, 75 species). Forage fish recolonized rapidly; food and game fish did not. Over about six months the reef yielded 155.6 lb. Only 4 of 152 fin-clipped game fish were recovered, versus 1,247 of 4,093 tagged by Randall (1961).

**Standing crop comparison.** The authors' estimate of 0.034 kg/m² compares with 0.160 kg/m² and 0.158 kg/m² reported by Randall (1963b) for natural and artificial reefs, and 0.049 kg/m² in Bermuda (Bardach, 1959).

**Food habits.** Randall (1967) analyzed stomach contents of 5,526 specimens of 212 species from 60 families, grouped into feeding guilds (plant and detritus feeders, zooplankton feeders, sessile feeders, mollusc crushers, generalized carnivores, ectoparasite feeders and fish feeders).

**Commercial fishery (1967–68 survey).** About 400 persons were engaged in commercial fishing in 1967 (120 full-time and 280 part-time). The survey contacted 83 full-time fishermen (69%) and 70 part-time (25%), plus 34 British Virgin Islands fishermen. Pots are the principal gear: 155 of 187 fishermen used pots alone or with other gear, and 71 used pots exclusively. The average boat was a locally built 14–20 ft open boat with an outboard under 20 hp.
- Total landings: 922 short tons, of which 747 tons (81%) came from American Virgin Islands fishermen, valued at just under $1 million.
- Landings rose from 616,000 lb (1930, $49,080) to 1,495,278 lb (1967–68, $781,896): catch up about 150%, value up about 1,500%.
- Per capita local seafood consumption was about 34 lb (versus 28.8 lb in 1932).
- Local landings supplied only about 22% of commercial use (206 tons); fish comprised 16% of total fish landings used commercially.
- British Virgin Islands fishermen landed about 18% of the market in 1967–68, up from about 2% in 1929.
- Fishermen counted in 1930 and 1967 were both about 400, a relative decline of 60% as population grew to about 55,000. Only 56.3% were American Virgin Islands-born, and the average fisherman was almost 45 years old with 19 years of experience.

**Catch per unit effort.** St. Thomas fishermen showed the best B/A ratio among the American islands (2651), St. Croix 2164, St. John 802, and the British Virgin Islands 3144. St. Croix's south-shore dredging and tanker traffic were cited as reducing catches.

**Handling and marketing.** Most fish are sold whole, ungutted and unscaled, and ice is rarely used. Only one outlet reported purchasing iced fish. More than two-thirds of restaurants indicated willingness to pay more for processed, iced local fish. More than 70% of restaurants and 60% of groceries wanted more local seafood. Preferred items were red snapper, grouper, lobster, kingfish and potfish.

**Ciguatera.** The report documents 58 of 280 fish tested as toxic by bio-assay (about one in five, though the sample was deliberately biased toward large predators). Of 61 liver tests, 57 were positive. Halstead (1958) recorded 60 Caribbean outbreaks since 1601, 18 in the Virgin Islands and Puerto Rico, affecting more than 118 people with 2 deaths there; worldwide ciguatera mortality was estimated at about 12%. The mouse bio-assay proved unreliable; the fiddler crab (Uca pugnax) and mongoose were also evaluated, with no assay judged more reliable than the mouse test. Respondents considered barracuda, large jacks and groupers most dangerous.

**Shoreline fishing guide.** The report maps and describes about 40 St. Croix, 25 St. John and 25 St. Thomas shoreline sites, with access, habitat and water notes, and species notes for blue marlin (record fish of 814 and 845 lb), white marlin, sailfish, wahoo (current world record listed at 124 lb), Allison tuna, cobia, dolphin, kingfish, tarpon, bonefish and snappers.

**Recommendations (summary).**
1. Enact enforceable fishery legislation: control officers for theft, pollution and conservation; regulate shipping routes; maintain statistics.
2. Build sanitary marketing centers on each major island and run consumer education against iced and frozen fish prejudice.
3. Provide fisherman assistance: loans, a cooperative for gear, and training courses.
4. Study optimal pot design, with fish behaviour and bait tested alongside.
5. Expand seining by opening beaches, including National Park beaches.
6. Investigate deep-water snapper fisheries; trials found Black, Blackfin, Queen, Red and Silk snapper at the shelf edge.
7. Evaluate gill nets, multiple-line trolling, miniature surface longlines, and light-and-pump fishing for schooling fish.
8. Continue [[Ciguatera Research]] toward a rapid, inexpensive individual-fish test.
9. Protect green turtles, which the authors describe as near extinction.
10. Determine why lobsters are absent from St. Thomas–St. John pots.

**Conclusions.** The authors judge that the charter fleet can expand several times from a biological standpoint but should expand cautiously economically. Problems include lack of young entrants, high boat and parts costs, a small shelf (about 2,000 square miles), scattered small schools, and ciguatera. Shark (USA market), cephalopod (squid and octopus) and aquaculture ventures are proposed for investigation, with conch (Strombus gigas) and whelks (Cittarium pica) identified as candidate farming species. The authors note that no economically reliable aquaculture technique had been demonstrated as an industry in the United States.
