# Cui-ui (Chasmistes cujus) in Pyramid Lake, Nevada: Life History, Ecology and Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 review in the *Great Basin Naturalist* (Sigler, Vigg and Bres) synthesizes data from the [[Pyramid Lake Ecological Study]] (1975–1978) with agency reports and the literature to present an overview of the [[Cui-ui]], a large lacustrine sucker endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]]. The species is the last pure remaining member of the genus *Chasmistes*, has declined sharply since the diversion of [[Truckee River]] water, and is listed as endangered. The paper covers history, embryology and larval identification, morphology, growth and aging, diet, spawning migration, mortality, water-quality tolerance, lake ecology, Native American fisheries, and management recommendations.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** First described by [[Edward Drinker Cope]] (1883) as *C. cujus*. Four recent *Chasmistes* species are recognized (*C. cujus*, *C. liorus*, *C. brevirostris*, *C. muriei*, the last now extinct, known from a single collection); two further extinct species are known only from fossils.
- **Morphology:** A large, big-mouthed sucker with a ventro-terminal mouth, thin papillose lips, coarse scales (59–66 in the lateral series, which separates it from [[Tahoe Sucker]] and [[Mountain Sucker]]), a two-chambered swim bladder, and branched, broccoli-like gill rakers. Breeding males show a red to brassy side color and nuptial tubercles; females are bluish gray and grow larger.
- **Measurements:** Snyder (1918) reported specimens of 410–670 mm standard length. Robertson (1979) recorded 39 adult fish of 448–653 mm fork length in the 1978 sample.
- **Early life:** The egg is about 2 mm in diameter and swells to about 3 mm during water hardening. Hatching occurs at about 216 hours at 13 °C, with larvae 6–7 mm long. Larvae are initially threadlike and become functional fry by about 912 hours.
- **Longevity:** Lives 18 or more years, possibly about 40 (Scoppettone, unpublished report); growth is rapid for the first 5–8 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake and river setting.** [[Pyramid Lake]] lies at 1154.9 m elevation, with a surface area of 437 km², a volume of 25.4 km³, mean depth of 57.9 m, and maximum depth of 100.6 m (Harris 1970). It is monomictic and has a high total dissolved solids (TDS) load of about 5,350 mg/l during 1976–1977. Sodium is the dominant ion, and the lake is high in bicarbonate alkalinity. The [[Truckee River]] is the only significant inflow in most years.

**Plankton and food web.** Blue-green algae, chiefly *Nodularia spumigena*, dominate the phytoplankton (>74%). Chironomids are the most abundant macroinvertebrates. The zooplankton community includes *Diaptomus sicilis*, the most abundant zooplankter year-round. Cui-ui diet is poorly known. Larvae prefer *Diaptomus* and periphyton on rocks, and adults probably feed on benthic zooplankton and invertebrates near the bottom.

**Anthropogenic pressure.** The [[Newlands Reclamation Project]] diverted up to half or more of the lower Truckee flow from 1915 to 1970. Pyramid Lake dropped more than 24.4 m between 1909 and 1968, TDS rose from about 3,500 to 5,500 mg/l, and [[Winnemucca Lake]] disappeared in 1938. A delta at the Truckee mouth in the early 1930s blocked spawning migrations, and natural reproduction was very limited for about 50 years.

**Spawning and habitat.** Spawning runs typically occur from April to June, triggered by fresh-water surges. Spawning uses depths of 9–43 cm, velocities of 25–47 cm/s, and substrate that is about 60% gravel. Eggs require fresh water (<600 mg/l TDS) for water hardening, or they fail to hatch. Lake spawning near freshwater springs is possible but its success is unknown.

**Native American fisheries.** The [[Northern Paiute]] of Pyramid Lake, called *kuyuidikadi* ("eaters of cui-ui"), relied heavily on the fish for food and trade and preferred it to trout because it could be dried. Archaeological records extend to about 9,500 BP.

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** The species is endangered, and the population has not recovered since the early 1930s decline. Least abundant of the four native fishes: 0.05% by number and 0.47% by weight (Vigg 1981). The mean catch per gill-net set fell from 1.29 (1976) to 0.98 (1977), not significant (p > 0.1).
- **Fishway counts:** 13,807 adult cui-ui passed the [[Marble Bluff Fishway]] in 1982, the largest run in five years.
- **Fecundity:** Females mature in their 5th or 6th year and produce 20,000–30,000 eggs per year (Koch 1972). Mean eggs per female at Marble Bluff from 1978 to 1983 were 36,662, with 1983 exceeding 55,000 (Table 9).
- **Hatching success:** 75.2% at the PLF hatchery in 1983. Koch et al. (1979) recorded 91.6% in controls. Scoppettone et al. (1981) estimated survival to emergence from river spawning at 2.7%.
- **Age and growth:** Scales, fin rays, opercula and otoliths were compared; opercula were chosen as reliable and practical. Back-calculated fork lengths are given in Table 5 (n = 79 fish, ages I–XVII). Length-weight relationships were calculated for 139 females and 147 males. Condition factor K is 1.08–1.24 for spent fish.
- **Year-class strength:** A reasonable correlation exists between year-class strength and river flow for 5 of 12 years, and a poor correlation for 3 of 12 years. The weak 1969 class occurred in a high-water year, which was linked to lethal suspended sediment.
- **Temperature optimum:** Egg hatching and spawning are best at 13.9 °C. Cui-ui activity in the lake peaked at a threshold of 11.0 °C in 1976 and 17.6 °C in 1977.
- **Depth distribution:** Maximum densities occur in the inshore benthic zone at 0–15 m (2.01 fish/net). No cui-ui were captured at 46 m or deeper.
- **Water-quality tolerance:** Eggs are intolerant of TDS above about 525 mg/l for fertilized embryos; 90% mortality occurred at 5,897 mg/l by day 3. One-day larvae showed 20% and 13.3% mortality at 5,781 and 3,503 mg/l. Chronic 180-day tests showed reduced juvenile survival at TDS levels between 3,620 and 5,322 mg/l.
- **Mortality stages:** Mortality is divided into five stages (adults, eggs, larvae, juveniles, maturing adults). Predation by white pelicans, double-crested cormorants and California gulls is significant. Knopf and Kennedy (1980) found no evidence that pelicans fed on cui-ui.
- **Conservation measures:** Management goals include restoring a stable, naturally reproducing population and maintaining river temperatures below 13.9 °C. Other measures include restoring a stable meandering channel, artificial propagation, operating the Marble Bluff Dam and fishway, and controlling predators in the Marble Bluff impoundment. Lake TDS should not increase, and lake levels should stay within ±3 m except in high-water years.
- **Fishing ban:** All cui-ui fishing, including by tribal members, has been prohibited since 1979.
- **Research gaps:** The authors identify poorly understood areas: diet, lake spawning success, disease, parasites and the effects of TDS on juvenile survival.
