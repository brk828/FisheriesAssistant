# Schooling of River Carpsuckers and a Population Estimate

#ichthyology #marine-biology #carpiodes

## Executive Summary

This 1969 short paper by [[Davin J. Behmer]] tests whether [[River Carpsucker]] (*Carpiodes carpio*) move as separate schools in the [[Des Moines River]], Iowa. The study used a [[Mark-Recapture Sampling]] program in a 4 km reach during summer 1965, tagging 1,111 fish. Recaptures showed that most fish stayed near their tagging stations, and the data did not support schooling. The study also produced two population estimates, one of 6,932 fish and a maximum of 44,318. The author judged the first to be too low and the second too high.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[River Carpsucker]] (*Carpiodes carpio*), a freshwater catostomid-group fish, described by Rafinesque.
- **Motivation:** Size and age samples from the Des Moines River taken at the same location in consecutive weeks varied more than expected. This led to the hypothesis that carpsuckers move as schools and that different schools were sampled on each trip.
- **Study period and site:** Summer and fall 1965, in a 4 km (2.5 mile) segment between tagging stations 1 and 5, near the [[Y.M.C.A. Camp]] and [[Boone Dam]] in [[Boone County Iowa]].
- **Affiliation:** Iowa Cooperative Fishery Unit, [[Iowa State University]], with support from the [[Iowa State Conservation Commission]] and the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preference:** Tagging stations were placed in preferred carpsucker habitat, namely riffle backwaters and rocky-bottom areas. Each station covered roughly 90 to 180 m of river.
- **Hydrology:** High water limited sampling early in the season, so most marking and recapture occurred in late July and August. At low water the river averages about 10 m wide, or about 1 hectare per kilometer.
- **Size structure:** No fish under 150 mm total length were tagged. Few fish under 230 mm were present, even in late summer, which suggested negligible recruitment during sampling.
- **Water-level effects:** A tagged group from station 5 (June 8, 1965) was recaptured in October at the mouth of a creek that was flooded at both times. Water was too low between mid-June and mid-September for fish to enter that creek.
- **Historical comparison:** [[Harrison 1956]] estimated 2,840 *Carpiodes* spp. per km in an 8.8 km impoundment section of the same river during a reclamation project.

## Key Findings

**Capture and tagging methods**
- Most fish were captured by [[Electrofishing]] from a boat using a 220-volt A.C. generator. A few were taken with an experimental [[Gill Net]] or a single-wing [[Fyke Net]].
- Four tag types were used: [[Strap Tag]] (sizes 1 and 3, applied to the left opercle), [[Streamer Tag]] (attached by monofilament), a plastic disc used as a body cavity tag, and a pectoral fin clip to mark tagged fish.
- Strap tags were used on most fish because they were the most efficient method. They eroded the opercle, and size 1 tags appeared to cause less damage.
- A handling trial with 16 fish held in wading pools at the University showed no mortality over more than three months. Tag losses were 1 of 16 (a size 3 strap tag). Field tag loss was thought to be low, but not quantified.

**Movement and schooling**
- Of 102 recaptures, 75 (about 74%) had not moved from their tagging station.
- Of the 75 non-movers, 60 were recaptured within 20 days and 72 within 48 days.
- Of 27 fish that moved, 10 moved upstream and 17 downstream (Table 2). Movement distances were mostly 0.4 km or less (12 fish) or over 1.6 km (10 fish).
- Groups tagged together were recaptured together at the original station in 11 instances. Only one group of three moved together to a different station. Seven groups of two (one of three) were recaptured together at different stations.
- The author concluded there was no evidence that carpsuckers move in schools. Co-recapture at the original station more likely reflects a sedentary habit in preferred habitat. Tagging and handling could also disrupt aggregates.
- Four fish were recaptured twice. One fish was recaptured 10 km downstream the following year.
- The movement proportion is likely an underestimate, because sampling was restricted to stations and favored fish that had not moved.

**Population estimates**
- The author applied the six assumptions listed by [[Ricker 1958]] for [[Mark-Recapture Sampling]]. Five were reasonably met: equal natural mortality, equal catchability, no mark loss (small losses), all marks reported, and negligible recruitment. The assumption of random mixing of marked and unmarked fish was not met.
- Using the [[Schnabel Method]] with combined station data, the estimate was **6,932** fish longer than 150 mm in the 4 km area. This equals about 1,733 fish per km, or about 500 kg per km, based on a mean weight of 288 g from 770 fish. The author considered this an underestimate because non-random sampling and non-random mixing inflated recaptures.
- A second estimate used 269 fish fin-clipped in 1964, with 7 recaptured in 1965. The [[Bailey Modification]] of the [[Petersen Method]] gave **44,318**. Recruitment, fin regeneration, and movement likely inflated this figure, so it was taken as a maximum.

**Conclusions**
- Carpsuckers in this reach were largely sedentary and remained in preferred habitat.
- The original hypothesis, that carpsuckers move as schools, was not supported by the data.
- Variation in sample composition may be better explained by local habitat use than by schooling.
- The true population likely falls between the two estimates. The author noted that the data were not designed for population estimation.
