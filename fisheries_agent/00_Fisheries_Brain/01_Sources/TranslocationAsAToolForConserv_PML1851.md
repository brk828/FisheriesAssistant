# Translocation as a Tool for Conserving Imperiled Fishes in the Western United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This peer-reviewed paper by [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University) reviews experiences with [[Translocation]] of imperiled freshwater fishes in the western United States. It presents the political and biological hurdles of moving fishes to new or restored sites, outlines guidelines adapted from [[Williams et al. (1988)]], and reviews several [[Case Histories]]. The author argues that translocation is a necessary substitute for natural dispersal in fragmented arid landscapes, but that site selection, stock quality, monitoring and documentation are frequently inadequate. The paper was presented at a symposium in France in 1993 and accepted for the edited volume on endemic freshwater fishes of the northern Mediterranean basin.

## Background & Description

The paper defines key terms: taxon, species and population are used interchangeably; translocation and transplantation are synonyms for any movement of a taxon; and stocking into a site inside the native range is a reintroduction, while stocking outside it is an introduction (following [[Hendrickson and Brooks (1991)]]). Conservation is distinguished from [[Preservation]]: preservation prevents extinction, whereas true conservation aims to maintain evolutionary potential, and a translocated population is viable only after sustaining itself over ecological time spans of tens to thousands of years.

The indigenous freshwater fauna of western North America is declining rapidly. At least 20 species have gone extinct in the region in the past century, and about 120 of the remaining 200 taxa are of special concern. Most threatened forms belong to three groups: cyprinoids (minnows and suckers), salmonids (trouts and salmons), and cyprinodontoids (livebearers, killifishes and allies). The Endangered Species Act of 1973 provided legal protection from the 1960s onward.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat loss is central to the decline. By 1930 fifty federal dams had been built in the region, with about 40 more, the largest capable of massive power generation, built between 1930 and 1980. Groundwater pumping often far exceeded recharge, lowering water tables and causing spring failure. Artificial ponds, reservoirs and canals now provide surface water, but these lentic systems favour non-native fishes that invade or are stocked into remnant streams and springs, where they prey upon, compete with and replace native fauna.

Moyle and Sato (1991) proposed reserve criteria, which the paper adapts for transplant sites: resources sufficient for persistence, populations large enough to be self-sustaining, within-boundary replication, protection from edge effects, and replication across distant protected areas. Short-lived species (living under 3 years and maturing by their second summer) can often meet these criteria locally, whereas large, long-lived species require large habitats that are difficult to secure. Natural refugia (permanent springs) and transitory habitats historically maintained genetic exchange; dams and water mining have now severed these connections, making translocation necessary.

Barriers, both natural and artificial, protect upstream species from non-natives but increase fragmentation. Hybridization between native and non-native taxa is a major problem, with examples among salmonids, cyprinids, catostomids, cyprinodontids, poeciliids and centrarchids. Habitat modification and chemical eradication of undesired organisms have also damaged native biota, including the loss of endemic hydrobiid snails in California springs.

## Key Findings

- **Site selection** is the most complex step. Guidelines (Table 1) call for sites within the native range, authorized places, fulfilled life-history requirements, restricted dispersal potential, minimal hybridization risk, no harm to rare taxa, and secure protection.
- **Legal framework:** The 1982 ESA amendment created "experimental" categories. "Essential" experimental populations retain full protection, whereas "non-essential" populations waive most protection, leaving their habitats unprotected. The new categories increased bureaucracy; authorization could take two or more years, and final arrangements for desert pupfish and Gila topminnow on federal lands stalled for more than 5 years.
- **Non-native invasions** are documented: spread of *Cyprinella lutrensis*, *Hybognathus placitus* and *Notropis girardi* in streams west of the continental divide coincided with declines in native minnows; *Cyprinodon variegatus* hybridized with Pecos pupfish; and *Gambusia affinis* displaced native topminnows.
- **Stock selection:** Genetic data should guide choices, but these data are often lacking. Alternatives include translocating the rarest stock, the stock nearest the new site, or the most common stock. A proposed guideline keeps foreign gene contribution below 1.0% per generation.
- **Monitoring:** Early programs were often too ambitious and later reduced to insufficient effort. Monitoring goals are to determine survival, establishment, population growth and research value. Failures can be explained only with frequent enough monitoring.
- **Stocking numbers:** [[Allendorf and Ryman (1987)]] considered 25 reproducing individuals of each sex an absolute minimum for salmonids in hatcheries. Typically 30 to 200 individuals were used for short-lived species at Dexter NFH. Long-lived species need far greater numbers.
- **Razorback sucker example:** Assuming 0.1% survival of 1.5 million stocked fish (1981–1989, lower Colorado River basin) would yield 1,500 fish spread across an estimated 20,550 ha, less than one fish per surface hectare. Single females can produce at least 15 million eggs a year, and maturity occurs at 2–6 years.
- **Case histories:** Of eight critical cases, six survived. Two (Moapa Valley Spring pupfish and Amistad gambusia) went extinct despite efforts, largely through inadequate attention and human error. The Yaqui chub was moved in 1969, became the only known US population by 1976, and was spread back across its range from 1980 after land purchases and refuge establishment. The Big Bend gambusia suffered a convoluted history of mixing with mosquitofish and repeated stocking.
- **Documentation gap:** Much translocation data remains in "gray literature." Half of the Hendrickson and Brooks (1991) fish translocation data came from personal communications, agency files and unpublished reports. The author urges formal peer-reviewed documentation from the outset, voucher specimens, and standardized reporting.
- **Taxonomic uncertainty** hampered recovery of Colorado River Gila chubs for almost 30 years.
