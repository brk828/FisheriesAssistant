# Biological Problems in Water Pollution: Transactions of the 1956 Cincinnati Seminar

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-biology #bioindicators

## Executive Summary

These transactions compile papers from the first seminar on [[Biological Problems in Water Pollution]], held April 23–27, 1956, at the [[Robert A. Taft Sanitary Engineering Center]] in Cincinnati, Ohio, and edited by Clarence M. Tarzwell for the [[Public Health Service]]. Ninety persons registered, from 28 states, the District of Columbia and Canadian provinces, drawn from industry, universities, state health and conservation agencies, and federal laboratories. The seminar used panel discussions in five subject areas: bioassays, biological indicators of pollution, current investigations, [[Water Quality Criteria]] for aquatic life, and training of sanitary aquatic biologists.

The main technical content covers standardizing toxicity tests with fish and mice, a proposed [[Application Factor]] method for industrial effluents, and reviews of microbial, algal, protozoan, diatom, fungal and mollusk indicators. The consistent conclusion is that single taxa are rarely reliable indicators and that community-level analysis, with chemical and physical data, is needed.

## Background & Description

The seminar was motivated by the isolation of sanitary biologists, whose number was growing in state agencies, universities and industry. Each subject panel had a chairman and 4 to 9 panelists who spoke for 10 to 20 minutes, after which discussion from the floor took the rest of the day. Presentations were off the record. The transactions are therefore not a record of the discussions. Papers were solicited from all participants and may or may not have been presented. Tarzwell announced that the seminar would recur every three years, with the next meeting planned for 1959 and suggestions requested by June 1958.

The transactions contain three main sections:

- **Use and Value of Bioassays:** [[Burdick]] on graphical threshold derivation; the [[Public Health Service]] interim plan for a [[Paralytic Shellfish Poison]] reference standard; and [[Henderson]] on application factors for toxic wastes.
- **Use and Value of Biological Indicators of Pollution:** papers on microorganisms, bacteria ([[Coliform Group]]), protozoa, algae, diatoms, fungi, mollusks, aquatic insects, fish populations, and lake indicators.
- **Current Investigations, Criteria and Training:** state and national research reports (Wisconsin, Ontario, Western Canada, Oregon, Missouri Basin, Ohio, Great Britain), a study of the polychaete [[Capitella Capitata]], and papers on turbidity, silt, wildlife, and the training of biologists.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several recurring themes link the papers:

- **Toxicity depends on water quality.** Toxicity is generally greater at higher temperatures and lower dissolved oxygen. Metal, cyanide and ammonia toxicity depends strongly on pH, alkalinity and hardness. Synergy occurs: copper and zinc together were reported as eight times more toxic than copper alone.
- **Indicator status is context-dependent.** [[Lackey]] stresses that many protozoa and algae occur in both polluted and clean habitats, so a species' presence does not by itself indicate pollution. Relationships must be tested experimentally.
- **Organic enrichment versus toxicity.** Diatom and bacterial indicator systems were developed mainly for organic sewage. Industrial toxicants can give a different response, since a stream with high oxygen and low bacteria may still be toxic to aquatic life.
- **Sampling and identification are difficult.** Preserved specimens often cannot be identified to species, and dead diatom frustules and dead organisms can persist in collections, producing false pictures of the living community.
- **Mollusks reflect dissolved oxygen, sludge and silt.** Lung-breathing snails and sphaeriid clams tolerate septic conditions better than gill-breathing snails and unionid mussels.

## Key Findings

### Bioassays and Toxicity Testing

- **Threshold derivation (Burdick):** Curvature in toxicity graphs on log paper results from the effective range not matching the axes. Burdick proposed subtracting an assumed threshold concentration and a minimum time-to-death from the data until a straight line results. The method is described as a hypothesis that has been tested on some curves, and the author notes it "cannot yet be considered completely proved."
- **Shellfish poison standard (Public Health Service):** Purified paralytic shellfish poison, derived by Dr. E. J. Schantz at the Army Chemical Corps, serves as a tentative reference standard, with results reported in weight of poison. About 0.2 µg of purified poison equals one mouse unit. Standard solution is 100 µg/ml. Test mice weigh 19 to 21 g, and death times should fall between 5 and 7 minutes. The conversion factor (CF) should check within ±20% of the laboratory mean, and a recheck is advised if that value is consistently exceeded. Data are interpreted with [[Sommer Tables]].
- **Clam and mussel extraction:** Samples of 100 g are acidified, boiled, pH-adjusted to 4.0–4.5, and clarified before mouse inoculation at 1 ml intraperitoneally.
- **Static tests (Henderson):** Recommended tests use fathead minnows (*Pimephales promelas*) and bluegill sunfish (*Lepomis macrochirus*), each 1-1/2 to 2-1/2 inches long, in five-gallon bottles with ten fish per concentration (five fish in ten liters, duplicate). Observations run for 96 hours, with 24-, 48- and 96-hour TLm values estimated by interpolation. Dilution water should come from the receiving stream above the outfall.
- **Application factors:** Henderson's stepwise method calculates dilution required from the TLm and effluent flow. Using the example TLm of 2.4% and 10 cfs of effluent, the dilution ratio is 1:40, which requires 400 cfs of dilution water. The tentative general application factor is 10 times the 48-hour TLm. An illustrative derivation gave 2 × 3 × 2 = 12, so 400 × 12 = 4,800 cfs of dilution water. Henderson states that considerable research is needed before factors are accepted.
- **Effluent variability:** Bioassays of 24-hour composite samples over three months showed wide variation in toxicity within single industries. Samples of petrochemical wastes ranged from 2.6% to 22% (average 9.6%), and oil refinery samples gave a 48-hour TLm of 37–40%.

### Biological Indicators of Pollution

- **Microorganism identification (Cooke):** Preserved algae and fungi are often unidentifiable, and only living, cultured material allows reliable determination. Monographic revision at genus or family level is recommended.
- **Bacteria (Kabler):** Pathogens are unsuitable routine indicators because methods are laborious and harm occurs before recovery. The [[Coliform Group]] remains the best available indicator of fecal pollution. Feces contain 5 to 500 million coliforms per gram, of which about 60–95% are *Escherichia coli*. The [[Escherichia Coli]] ratio is not an index of water quality. Sewage streptococci are less numerous than coliforms, with a streptococcus-to-coliform ratio of 1/100 to 1/10, and serve best as a corroborative test. Spore-forming anaerobes (*Clostridium perfringens*) are rejected as indicators.
- **Protozoa (Lackey):** Few protozoa are restricted to one habitat type. Oxygen-limited ciliates (e.g., *Trimyema compressa*, *Enchelyomorpha vermicularis*) are notable exceptions. Lytle Creek, Ohio, yielded 167 species at five stations and Cowan Creek 89 species. The unpolluted Santa Fe River, Florida, had 332 species, 99 shared with Lytle Creek, showing that species counts alone do not indicate pollution.
- **Algae (Palmer):** Palmer examined 56 workers' reports and compiled more than 500 sewage-tolerant algal kinds, using a weighted score (2 points for very high tolerance, 1 for high). *Oscillatoria* ranked first among genera (34 authors, 57 points), followed by *Euglena* (31 authors, 53 points). Among species, *Euglena viridis* scored highest (18 authors, 33 points). Palmer warns that the lists are aids, not definitive indicators.
- **Diatoms (Patrick):** Patrick argues that diatoms respond to deleterious effluents in a manner similar to fish and insects. In a bioassay analysis of 50 industrial wastes, concentrations causing 50% diatom growth reduction and 50% fish mortality were very similar, and the diatom was less sensitive than fish in only 8% of cases. Of 354 species from ten rivers, only one occurred in all ten rivers and 202 occurred in only one. Patrick recommends the Catherwood Diatometer, a float with glass slides left about two weeks, with community structure analyzed by truncated normal curves.
- **Fungi (Cooke):** Water molds (*Saprolegniaceae*, *Leptomitus lacteus*, *Sphaerotilus natans*) are associated with organic pollution. Coprophilous fungi are rarely found in human feces. Cooke proposes Greek-based terms (lymabiont, lymaphile, lymaxene, lymaphobe) to describe fungal dependence on fecal material, linked to Kolkwitz's saprobic zones.
- **Mollusks (Ingram):** No mollusk species is a reliable pollution indicator. Unionid mussels are typically absent from septic water, while air-breathing snails (*Physa integra*, *Physa heterostropha*) and sphaeriid clams (*Musculium transversum*, *Pisidium* spp.) persist in degraded reaches. Sphaeriids survive by closing their valves, while unionids depend on fish hosts for glochidial larvae. Physa integra was found in trickling filters at Dayton, Ohio, where BOD ranged from 59 to 131 ppm and dissolved oxygen in filter effluent was 2.7–4.9 ppm.

### Current Investigations and Criteria

- **Dissolved oxygen and fish:** Kabler, Lackey and others cite fish and invertebrate tolerance thresholds; Wilson's paper addresses turbidity and silt as direct effects.
- **Training:** Panelists debated how to train sanitary aquatic biologists for field work. The emphasis was on combining taxonomy, chemistry and statistics.
- **Future work:** Tarzwell planned a follow-up seminar in 1959 with suggestions requested by June 1958.
