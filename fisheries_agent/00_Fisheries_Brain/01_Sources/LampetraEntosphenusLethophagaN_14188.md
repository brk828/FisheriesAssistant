# Pit-Klamath Brook Lamprey: A New Nonparasitic Lamprey from the Pit and Klamath Systems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #petromyzontidae

## Executive Summary

Carl L. Hubbs formally describes *[[Lampetra Lethophaga]]* (Pit-Klamath Brook Lamprey) as a new species, the dwarfed, nonparasitic derivative of the parasitic [[Pacific Lamprey]] (*Lampetra tridentata*). The species is known only from the connected drainages of the Pit River (a Sacramento River headwater) in northeastern California and the upper [[Klamath River]] system in south-central Oregon. Adults do not feed after the autumn metamorphosis, grow no further, spawn and die, and are much smaller at maturity (under 170 mm) than sea-run *L. tridentata*. The paper draws on specimens collected from 1898 to 1970 at 11 localities, and it argues that nonparasitic lampreys have arisen repeatedly from parasitic ancestors, with local diversity arranged in a mosaic pattern. The species name was previously unavailable: Bond (1961) had listed it only as "Klamath brook lamprey, *Lampetra* sp." The type series was collected at the head of Fall River in 1934.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and nomenclature.** The holotype is UMMZ 130648 and the paratypes are UMMZ 130649, all from the source of Fall River, Shasta County, California. Earlier records were misidentified as *Entosphenus tridentatus* (Rutter, 1908) and *Lampetra planeri* (Hubbs, 1925). The name *lethophaga* refers to the elimination of feeding in adults (Greek *lethe*, forgetfulness; *phagein*, to eat).

**Diagnosis.** Compared with *L. tridentata*, the new species has:
- a small oral disc (under 5% of total length) that is usually puckered;
- reduced supraoral median cusps, often weak or absent;
- lateral circumoral cusp formulae frequently reduced by one cusp from the 2-3-3-2 standard;
- fewer posterior circumorals (9 to 15);
- nonparasitic feeding and growth, with maturity below 170 mm;
- in some places, breeding in prenuptial coloration and body form.

**Morphology.** The dentition shows both reduction and increased individual variation. Bicuspid posterior circumorals are common. Myomere counts vary by stream system: Pit River 63-69 (mean 65.62), Sprague River 58-66 (mean 63.50), Crooked Creek 63-73 (mean 67.20). Proportionally, the oral disc is much smaller than in *L. tridentata* (36-49 vs. 74-96 per thousand of total length).

**Coloration.** Field-observed adults from Fall River were slaty above and brassy-silvery below, with clear waxy-yellow fins. Nuptial adults from Crooked Creek were blackish-purple with a pale cloacal region. Hubbs found no evidence supporting the pigmentary differences Bond (1961) attributed to ammocetes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Localities include the cold, clear springs and streams at the head of Fall River (about 1020 m, 11.4-13.3 °C), the Sprague and Sycan rivers (1280-1760 m), and Klamath State Fish Hatchery springs on Crooked Creek (about 1280 m, 7.8 °C). Associated fishes include [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*), Klamath endemic suckers and sculpins (*Cottus* spp.), and dace.

**Endemism.** The Pit and Klamath systems share other endemic fishes, including *[[Cottus Pitensis]]* and *Catostomus microps*. Klamath endemics include *Catostomus rimiculus* and *Cottus tenuis*. Behnke (1970) reported undescribed trout in the same region.

**Distribution pattern.** The nonparasitic lampreys of the North Pacific appear to have complementary ranges. Nonparasitic forms related to *[[Lampetra Ayresii]]* and *[[Lampetra Japonica]]* occupy other areas. The parasitic *L. tridentata* extends from central Baja California and the Bering Sea to southern Japan (Shikoku), and its nonparasitic counterpart is absent from most of this range.

**Parasitic relatives.** A dwarfed, precocious parasitic form in the Klamath system (Klamathon, Shasta River, Copco Lake) and a parasitic midget from Miller Lake, now extinct after a toxaphene treatment, both bear on how nonparasitism arose. Bond's Miller Lake observations suggest that the population lost obligate parasitism under food limitation.

**Life history.**
- Ammocetes probably spend at least four years in the larval stage (inferred from size modes; 40 ammocetes were collected in August and 95 in October).
- Metamorphosis occurs in autumn. The 114 transformers from Hat Creek (October 4, 1968) were examined, with none showing signs of feeding.
- Gonads mature as the gut atrophies. Adults were collected from February 16 to August 17.
- Breeding timing is uncertain. Hubbs proposed either a nuptial metamorphosis before spring spawning, or neotenic maturation with summer spawning.

**Sex ratio.** Overall, 87 females to 100 males (187 specimens). The ratio falls with developmental stage: 128 per 100 males among ammocetes, 79 among transformers, 72 among adults.

## Key Findings

- **Nonparasitic status confirmed.** No food was found in the guts of any adults examined, and the gonads matured as the gut atrophied. Lampetra lethophaga is therefore a nonparasitic species, the first recognized in *Entosphenus*.
- **Restricted range.** The species is known from 11 localities (5 in the Pit system, 6 in the Klamath system) and is complementary to other North Pacific nonparasitic lampreys.
- **Dentition differs.** Fewer posterior circumorals (9-15 vs. the parasitic series), reduced cusp counts, and a strong tendency toward bicuspid posterior teeth indicate a derivation from precocious Klamath populations of *L. tridentata*.
- **Intermediate specimen.** A postnuptial male from Willow Creek (176 mm), in the Lost River system, is intermediate in disc size (64 vs. 36-58 in *L. lethophaga* and 74-96 in *L. tridentata*) and in several other features. Its status remains uncertain.
- **Neoteny.** At the Fall River type locality, adults reached full maturity without developing nuptial coloration or secondary sexual characters. Hubbs notes that this condition differs from the nuptial metamorphosis seen at Crooked Creek and Sprague River, and he does not consider it a sound basis for separating populations.
- **Regional diversity.** Local differences in cusp frequencies, bicuspid frequency, myomere counts and proportions are consistent with mosaic-patterned differentiation. The author does not consider these differences sufficient for subspecific status.
- **Growth.** Lampetra lethophaga adults almost certainly do not grow after metamorphosis, whereas dwarfed *L. tridentata* roughly double in length as parasitic adults.
- **Systematic position.** Hubbs maintains nonparasitic forms at full specific rank, while acknowledging that intergradation with parasitic forms and polyphyly remain possible.
- **Conservation note.** The Miller Lake parasitic midget was lost to a toxaphene eradication operation intended to remove it. A detailed comparison with *L. lethophaga* awaited Bond and Kan's study.
