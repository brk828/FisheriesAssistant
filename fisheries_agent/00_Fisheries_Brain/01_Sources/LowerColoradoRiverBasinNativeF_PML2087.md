# Lower Colorado River Big-River Fish Management Plan and Biological Assessment Coordination

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of interagency correspondence and a draft management plan for the native "big-river" fishes of the lower [[Colorado River]] basin. The four focal species are [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. All four are federally listed as endangered. The 1994 draft plan was prepared by the [[Parker Fishery Resource Office]] of the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS). It proposes a regional strategy to stabilize and recover these fishes by circumventing recruitment failure caused largely by predation and competition from non-native fishes.

The 1995 letters and meeting agenda concern a [[Section 7 Consultation]] between the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and USFWS. Reclamation is preparing a [[Biological Assessment]] on the effects of its Lower Colorado River operations from [[Hoover Dam]] downstream. The correspondence covers species list concurrence, a proposed November 1995 completion date, and a stakeholder meeting held February 7, 1995.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] basin covers about 650,000 km², roughly one twelfth of the contiguous United States, and drains parts of seven states plus Mexico. Elevations range from below sea level in the Salton Sea basin to above 4,000 m in the Rocky Mountain headwaters. For management, the basin is split at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] into upper and lower basins. The lower basin includes the Colorado mainstem and the Little Colorado, Paria, Virgin, Bill Williams, and Gila subbasins.

Prior to impoundment, the mainstem was a slow, turbid river with seasonal flows. Flows were high and cold in spring and dropped sharply in summer. The four "big-river" fishes evolved under these conditions. Their life histories share several traits: large body size, long life, and high predation pressure in early stages.

Species status summary from the document:

- **Colorado Squawfish:** Wild populations extirpated from the lower basin. Reintroduced populations exist in the Salt and Verde rivers as "experimental, non-essential" fish.
- **Bonytail Chub:** Caught only in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. Considered at Level I (prevent extinction) in the lower basin.
- **Humpback Chub:** A viable population persists in the [[Grand Canyon]] area, mostly in the lowermost Little Colorado River.
- **Razorback Sucker:** The largest known population is in Lake Mohave. Wild individuals are sporadically captured in Grand Canyon and below Davis Dam.

Declines are attributed to habitat modification (large impoundments, cold releases, loss of natural flow variance) and predation or competition from [[Non-Native Fishes]]. Reservoirs, canals, and agency stocking programs maintain a constant supply of these predators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The draft plan identifies the main threat as **recruitment failure**. Non-native predators prey on eggs, larvae, and juveniles. Experimental work suggests that all four species can survive to maturity when isolated from other fishes during early life stages.

The plan proposes these environmental conditions for habitat:

- Isolated natural or seminatural waters that can be cleared of non-native fish with piscicides (generally at least 5.0 ha).
- Larger isolated habitats (80 ha or more) for long-term adult management and self-sustaining populations.
- Free-flowing river reaches restored in the Gila, Verde, Bill Williams, and mainstem Colorado, with barriers to invasion by non-native species.

The plan also covers the Yuma Clapper Rail. The February 1995 letter asks that it be included in the biological assessment, along with critical habitat for bonytail and razorback sucker.

## Key Findings

**Species list and consultation (1995 correspondence)**

- Reclamation's assessment focuses on the Yuma Clapper Rail, Razorback Sucker, and Bonytail Chub. USFWS was asked to propose any additional species.
- Reclamation expected it could not finish a complete biological assessment within the six-month period after species list concurrence. It planned a final version by November 1995 and asked USFWS for concurrence with that date.
- A stakeholder meeting was held February 7, 1995, in Las Vegas, NV. Attendees included water and power groups, fishery and wildlife agencies, and other public and private interests. The agenda covered background, the biological assessment schedule, the status of the lower basin plan, a Basin-States feasibility assessment, and questions.

**Plan goals and objectives (1994 draft)**

- The ultimate goal is to establish self-perpetuating populations of all four big-river species, with genetic variability reflecting extant wild populations, across portions of their historic habitats.
- Six primary objectives (Levels I–VI) are defined in Figure 1, from preventing extinction (Level I) through stabilization, recovery, reintroduction, self-sustaining status, and delisting or downlisting (Level VI).
- The plan focuses on the shared problem of early-life survival, and it primarily addresses bonytail and razorback sucker. Humpback chub is excluded from specific recommendations for now because of an ongoing Environmental Impact Statement and Glen Canyon Dam Biological Opinion provisions. Colorado Squawfish recommendations are limited to the experimental populations.

**Management actions**

- **Prevent extinction:** Identify isolated habitats. Remove non-natives and stock endangered taxa within three years, starting on refuges. Native fish reaching about 300 mm TL are used for augmentation or reintroduction.
- **Hatcheries:** [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] holds broodstocks of all big-river fishes except humpback chub. Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery, Arizona Game and Fish Department's Bubbling Ponds hatchery, and Colorado River Indian Tribes ponds are expected to join propagation efforts.
- **Fish health:** Screen natural habitats for disease before stocking. Monitor hatchery and stocked fish at least annually and before any transfer.
- **Genetics:** Maximize the number of wild-caught larvae contributing to recruitment. Use strict pedigree breeding with probability-based breeding regimens when wild larvae are unavailable. Mix wild fish with F1 broodfish to minimize inbreeding.
- **Expansion:** Plan and stock large isolated habitats within five years, mainly on refuges. Expand to floodplain habitats on other federal lands within ten years. Use reservoir filling as an opportunity to stock adults and larvae.
- **Coordination:** Create a Lower Basin Coordinator position and a single Recovery Team covering all four species. Team members should be biologists chosen for species knowledge, basin knowledge, and relevant expertise. No more than half should come from agencies that may affect the species. The Recovery Team and coordinator should meet with the Regional Director at least twice a year.
