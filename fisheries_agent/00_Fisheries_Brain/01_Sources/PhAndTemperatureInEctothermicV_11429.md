# pH and Temperature in Ectothermic Vertebrates

#ichthyology #marine-biology #acid-base-physiology #ectotherms

## Executive Summary

This review compiles arterial blood and intracellular pH data from ectothermic vertebrates to test two competing frameworks of temperature-dependent acid-base regulation: [[Alphastat Regulation]] (Reeves) and [[Constant Relative Alkalinity]] (Rahn). Across 207 pH–temperature points representing 81 species, the pooled slope is -0.016 U/°C, close to the alphastat prediction of about -0.018 U/°C. However, the authors find that blood pH falls less steeply with temperature than theory predicts in most comparisons, and that intergroup differences lie mainly in elevation (intercept), not slope. Marine fishes, reptiles, and amniotes have lower blood pH than freshwater teleosts and amphibians. The mechanisms behind these differences remain unknown.

## Background & Description

Ectothermic vertebrates show a consistent decline in body fluid pH as body temperature rises. The phenomenon was first reported in the 1920s and 1930s, then drew wide attention after [[Robin Study on Turtles]] (1962) and subsequent work by Rahn, Reeves, and colleagues.

Two theoretical frameworks dominate:

- **Constant relative alkalinity (Rahn, 1966):** Animals maintain a constant [OH⁻]/[H⁺] ratio, with blood pH held 0.4–0.6 U on the alkaline side of neutral pH (pN). Slope of about -0.017 U/°C.
- **Alphastat regulation (Reeves, 1972, 1977):** Protein buffers, principally the imidazole group of histidine, keep a constant degree of dissociation (α-imidazole). This preserves protein charge and tertiary structure. Slope of about -0.018 U/°C between 0 and 40°C.

Alphastat requires two conditions: a pH slope near -0.018 U/°C, and constant total CO₂ across the temperature range. Both have been challenged (Heisler, 1986; Cameron, 1989).

Data selection followed strict criteria. Blood samples were preferably arterial, drawn from chronically catheterized, unanesthetized, minimally disturbed animals under normoxia, with electrodes equilibrated at acclimation temperature. Heart puncture data were used only when catheterized data were unavailable. Mammals and birds were excluded because heterothermy changes their regulatory state. Intracellular pH was measured mainly by the DMO method ([[DMO Distribution Method]]), with additional red cell freeze-thaw and in vivo ³¹P NMR data. Tissues analyzed were red blood cells, skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, liver, and brain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors argue that interspecific differences in PCO₂ and [HCO₃⁻] are explained by habitat and respiratory mode (Rahn, 1966). Water-breathers reach a given blood pH with low PCO₂ and [HCO₃⁻], while air-breathers at the same pH and temperature have proportionately higher values of both.

Respiratory mode also informs proposed explanations for blood pH differences. Reptiles, the most terrestrial and committed air-breathers, have lower blood pH. The authors suggest that reduced breathing volumes may conserve water, while gill ventilation in fishes and cutaneous CO₂ loss in amphibians may shape their higher pH. These explanations are contested, since marine teleosts and elasmobranchs are also water-breathers yet have lower pH than freshwater teleosts.

Plasma sodium is another candidate. [[Burton Plasma Sodium Hypothesis]] (1994) proposed an inverse pH–[Na⁺] relationship. The authors could not reproduce this relationship in their compiled data at 15 or 25°C (slope not significantly different from zero).

## Key Findings

**Blood pH (extracellular) vs. temperature**

- Pooled slope of 207 pH–temperature points (81 species, each averaged over up to 36 studies) is -0.016 U/°C (r = 0.813). This is not significantly different from the alphastat slope of -0.018.
- Most individual species and subgroup slopes are significantly negative. Subgroup slopes range from -0.004 to -0.022 U/°C.
- Slopes did not differ significantly among major groups; elevation (intercept) did.
- Freshwater teleosts have higher blood pH than marine teleosts and elasmobranchs at the same temperature.
- Reptiles have a significantly lower blood pH than freshwater teleosts and amphibians. Lizards have the highest elevation among reptile groups but remain below freshwater teleosts and amphibians.
- Freshwater species (fishes, amphibians, reptiles) have higher intercepts than marine species (slopes not different).
- Anamniotes show a significantly higher pH intercept than amniotes (reptiles), with mammals and birds predicted to fall near 7.4–7.5 at their body temperatures.
- Air-breathing fishes were excluded from the main groupings because their acid-base status fits neither pattern. The authors note that the obligate air-breather [[Synbranchus Marmoratus]] has uniquely high pH.

**Testing the theoretical models**

- Comparisons with the ideal alphastat slope are equivocal. Within-range slope was consistent with the ideal in 16 of 24 individual species studies and 8 of 18 subgroups. A sign test found 22 of 25 species and 17 of 18 subgroups had slopes less than the ideal.
- Intracellular data agreed somewhat better: 4 of 5 tissues included the ideal slope within their 95% confidence limits.
- α-imidazole appears more tightly controlled than pH–pN, which changes more with temperature. In turtles, α-imidazole rises only from 0.80 to 0.82 over 30°C.
- The mean plasma slope across 25 species studies is -0.0144 ± 0.001, compared with the predicted -0.018.

**Intracellular pH**

- Intracellular data are sparser and more variable. Skeletal muscle has 63 data points; heart muscle has 38. The authors do not draw firm conclusions from this compartment.
- Heart muscle showed a significantly shallower slope than the alphastat ideal.
- Amniotes have lower intracellular pH intercepts than anamniotes in heart tissue and liver, but no marine–freshwater difference appears.

**CO₂ and bicarbonate**

- Air-breathers (amphibians and reptiles) show little change in plasma [HCO₃⁻] with temperature but a significant rise in PCO₂.
- Water-breathers are less consistent. Two of three species with data (including the channel catfish, using Cameron & Kormanik data) showed a significant fall in [HCO₃⁻] and rise in PCO₂.
- Freshwater teleosts as a group showed a slight but significant rise in PCO₂ with temperature. The authors conclude fishes likely use both respiratory and ionic mechanisms for temperature-dependent pH regulation.

**Sodium and interpretation**

- The authors could not confirm the [[Burton Plasma Sodium Hypothesis]]: no significant relationship between plasma pH and [Na⁺] at 15 or 25°C.
- The authors explicitly state they do not know the mechanisms behind the inter-group pH differences.

**Conclusions**

- The compiled database is offered as a resource for future investigators.
- Intergroup differences in blood pH, particularly the consistently higher pH of anamniotes than reptiles, are a new finding that calls for functional explanation.
- Deviations from the overall ectotherm slope in small taxonomic groups rest on few studies and require more data within each species' normal temperature range before they can be treated as real.
