# Grand Canyon Place Names

#gazetteer #toponymy #historical-geography #grand-canyon

## Executive Summary

*Grand Canyon Place Names* (Byrd H. Granger; illustrated by Anne Merriman Peck; University of Arizona Press, 1960, 1980 printing) is a historical gazetteer of the [[Grand Canyon]] and its surrounding plateaus, rims, rapids, springs, and side canyons. It is not a fish or aquatic-ecology study. Its main value is documenting how names were given to features by explorers, surveyors, pioneers, [[Indigenous Peoples of the Grand Canyon|Indigenous peoples]], and tourism operators. Each entry typically gives an elevation, a location code keyed to Map GC-series sheets, a pronunciation or language note where relevant, a naming rationale, and references. The river-related entries (rapids, creeks, falls, and the [[Colorado River]] itself) are the closest the text comes to aquatic habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The introduction describes the Grand Canyon as lying entirely in Arizona. Its most spectacular part, about 105 miles long, lies within [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. Depth ranges from roughly 3,000 to 6,000 feet and width from 4 to 15 miles, with the North Rim higher than the South Rim.
- **Formation:** The region was uplifted, and the [[Colorado River]] cut downward through it, exposing a geological record the text calls "scientifically second to none." Following [[Francois E. Matthes]], the text attributes wall retreat mainly to undercutting of soft shale from below, followed by spalling. Vertical fractures and faults produced side canyons and amphitheaters, while the "temples" formed from blocks of strata.
- **Exploration timeline:**
  - [[Garcia Lopez de Cardenas]], of the [[Coronado Expedition]], is credited as the first white man to see the canyon (1540).
  - Lt. Amiel W. Whipple followed the lower Colorado up to the mouth of "Big Canyon" (1854).
  - Lt. Joseph Christmas Ives reached the edge of the main gorge (1857). Dr. John S. Newberry, the expedition's geologist, gave the first detailed description.
  - [[John Wesley Powell]], a one-armed Civil War veteran, led the first boat expedition through the entire length of the canyon (1869). He made a second, more thorough survey in 1871–72 and changed the name to Grand Canyon.
  - Later namers included [[Clarence E. Dutton]] and Charles E. Walcott (c. 1880), and Matthes with Richard T. Evans (1902).
- **Naming themes:** The gazetteer groups names into several categories:
  - **Descriptive** (e.g., The Alligator, Breezy Point, Cedar Mountain, Arrowhead Terrace).
  - **Indigenous** (tribes and families such as Havasupai, Hopi, Piute, Chemehuevi, Maricopa, Pima, Yavapai, Papago, Lipan, and Zuni), plus Indigenous-language river names (e.g., Havasu, "blue water"; Kaibab, "mountain lying down").
  - **Pioneers and officials** (e.g., [[William Bass]], [[John Hance]], Louis Boucher, Ralph Cameron, Jacob Hamblin, John T. Alsap).
  - **Scientists** (e.g., Darwin Plateau, Huxley Terrace, Spencer Terrace, Lyell Butte, Marsh Butte, Cope Butte).
  - **Classical and world-religion names** (Brahma, Shiva, Vishnu, Buddha, Confucius, Cheops, Osiris, Ra, Set, Vesta, Zoroaster temples).
  - **Arthurian names** (Bedivere Point, Excalibur, Merlin Abyss, Guinevere Castle), introduced by Evans in 1902.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The gazetteer contains a substantial set of water-related features, mainly in the Colorado River corridor and its tributaries:

- **River:** The [[Colorado River]] entry traces its many names: Spanish names such as Rio de Buena Guia (Alarcon, 1540), Rio del Tison (Diaz, 1541), and Rio de los Martires (Kino); Indigenous names including Pa-ha-weap ("water deep down in the earth," Piute) and Hackatai (Havasupai); and the eventual adoption of "Colorado" from Garces' Rio Colorado.
- **Creeks and springs:** Bright Angel Creek (clear water, following 1869 flood-turbidity), Crystal Creek (extremely clear water), Havasu Creek (the largest southern tributary, "blue water"), Tapeats (Thunder River) Creek, Shinumo Creek, and many named springs such as Roaring Springs (water gushing from the wall with a "true roaring sound," then falling about 400 feet).
- **Falls:** Havasu Canyon contains five falls. Mooney Falls drops about 220 feet, Bridal Veil Falls is 170 feet high, and Beaver Falls was once frequented by beaver. Ribbon Falls deposits travertine. Cheyava Falls is intermittent.
- **Rapids:** Named rapids include Lava Falls, Sockdolager Rapids (named from the slang "knock-down blow"), Granite Rapids, Upset Rapids, Deubendorff Rapids, and Hance Rapids.
- **Travertine and geology:** Travertine deposits (calcium carbonate precipitated from water) appear in Travertine Canyon, Elves Chasm, and Ribbon Falls. Granite Gorge is described as a forty-mile gorge with pink and white granite masses. Marble Gorge is about 61 miles long, with four portages recorded in 1869.
- **Terrestrial habitat:** The Kaibab Plateau carries large forests (named as the Kaibabits by the Piute). Kaibab National Forest was created in 1908. De Motte Park is an open glade at about 8,000 feet. Thousands of wild burros once gathered at Burro Canyon for water.
- **Human–environment events:** The text records the 1911 flood in Havasupai village, when a forty-foot wall of water swept away buildings. It also records the 1956 mid-air collision of two airliners over Chuar Butte at 21,000 feet, which triggered an air-safety investigation.

## Key Findings

- **Scope and purpose:** The work is an alphabetical gazetteer of hundreds of Grand Canyon place names, each with elevation, approximate location code, naming rationale, and references. It documents naming history rather than ecology.
- **Exploration chronology:** Cardenas (1540) is the first recorded white visitor. Powell's 1869 and 1871–72 expeditions produced the majority of the surviving names and the current name "Grand Canyon."
- **River names:** The Colorado River accumulated many names from Spanish explorers and Indigenous groups before "Colorado" became standard. The gazetteer records this as a multi-century process of successive renaming.
- **Indigenous naming:** Many features honor Indigenous peoples, families, or individuals (e.g., Havasupai, Hopi, Piute, Chemehuevi, Yuma, Yunosi Point, Kwagunt Valley). Some Indigenous-language meanings are given as recorded (e.g., Havasu, "blue water"; Pa-ha-weap, "water deep down in the earth").
- **Havasupai reservation data:** The text states that a "Suppai" reservation was created on June 8, 1880, and that the present 518 acres were set aside on March 31, 1882, for the "Yavai Suppai." About 235 Havasupai were on the reservation, and the tribe held 2,540 acres in Cataract Canyon.
- **Galloway–Stone expedition (1909):** Four boats traveled from Green River, Wyoming, to Needles, California, in two months and one week, described as the fastest trip on record.
- **Coronado context:** The text describes the expedition that left on February 23, 1540, with 336 Spanish military men, 559 horses, and hundreds of Indians, reaching the Zuni villages on July 7, 1540. It calls the expedition a complete failure in its search for wealth. Coronado died on September 22, 1554.
- **Notable elevations:** Imperial Point (8,801 feet) is the highest point along either rim. Shinumo Altar rises about 600 feet above surrounding terrain.
- **Notable tragedies:** Several entries record deaths, including Mooney (1880, Mooney Falls), Hansbrough (drowned in Marble Canyon), Sturdevant and Johnson (drowned on Colorado River duty), and the Howland brothers and William Dunn (killed by Indians after leaving Powell's 1869 expedition).
- **Kolb Natural Bridge:** The bridge, reported in 1953 and visited in 1954 (after Sen. Barry Goldwater's sighting), has a span of 200 feet at its base and an arch at least 200 feet to its underside.
- **Editorial and citation status:** The gazetteer uses numbered reference codes and map designations (GC-1, GC-7, GC-8, and others). Many entries cite Shellbach File, Barnes, Kolb, and Dellenbaugh as sources. Some entries note that a name is a corruption or variant.
