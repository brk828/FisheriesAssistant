# Geochronology of Pluvial Lake Cochise, Southern Arizona, III: Pollen Statistics and Pleistocene Metastability

#palynology #paleoecology #pleistocene #pollen-statistics #playa-lakes

## Executive Summary

This paper applies correlation statistics to pollen counts from a 42-meter drill core taken from [[Pluvial Lake Cochise]] in the [[Willcox Playa]], southeastern Arizona. Correlations among 12 pollen types, based on 200-grain counts from 211 levels, were tested against the correlations expected under two [[Null Models]] (A and B) derived from the multivariate B-distribution. The authors interpret the dominant correlation pattern, in which [[Pinus]] (pine) is negatively related to most other types, as reflecting Wisconsin-age shifts between various woodland types and [[Yellow Pine Parkland]]. They argue that the record shows vegetational and climatic metastability, a conclusion they compare with the instability of the Laurentide Ice Sheet margin.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Playa lake sediments in closed basins preserve a fossil [[Pollen Pool]], a mixture of pollen from several elevational vegetation zones. The authors cite earlier work showing 900 to 1200 m of vertical displacement of vegetation zones during the Wisconsin glacial episode.
- **Core:** A 42 m core from the dry Willcox Playa, collected with a 1.5 m barrel. Recovery was estimated at 85 percent. The core was divided into five "divisions" (I to V), replacing the earlier term "zones" to avoid confusion with postglacial zones. Divisions II to IV are placed in the Wisconsin (about 1.9 to 21 m), and Division V (21 to 42 m) in the Sangamon and Illinoian.
- **Pollen categories (12):** Pinus, Quercus, Juniperus type, Picea + Abies + Pseudotsuga (PAP), Gramineae, Chenopodiaceae + Amaranthus (cheno-ams), Sarcobatus, Artemisia, high-spine Compositae, low-spine Compositae, other arboreal types, and other non-arboreal types.
- **Statistical approach:** Observed product-moment correlations were compared with constraint correlations. Coefficients were Fisher Z-transformed, and significance was tested with a t-value treated as a standardized normal variable. The authors note that the test is theoretically justified only under null model A, and that a computer experiment by [[Felix Chayes]] gave approximately normal Z values under null model B.
- **Ecological assumptions:** Modern plant communities (spruce-fir forest, pine parkland, oak-juniper woodland, chaparral, desert grassland) are assumed to have occupied the basin in the same sequence as today.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pollen transport:** Pollen from higher elevations is transported into the basin centre. Pine and Celtis are more abundant in mid-basin surface samples than near shore. Halophyte pollen (e.g., Atriplex) would indicate shoreline conditions, but the authors found very little cheno-am pollen in the relevant intervals.
- **Woodland types:** The authors propose two woodland types in Division IV. One is summer-dry (Artemisia, cheno-ams, Sarcobatus), typical of the [[Great Basin]]. The other is summer-wet (oak, grass, low-spine Compositae), more typical of the High Plains and Mexican Plateau.
- **Pine pollen morphology:** [[Ponderosa Pine]] (Pinus ponderosa) pollen is among the largest of Southwestern species, while piñon pollen is among the smallest. Bladder-length measurements from core slug 96 show more large grains in the pine zone (154 of 200 over 41 units, against 105 of 200 in the woodland zone; χ² = 26.3, 1 df, p < 0.01). This is consistent with a shift toward ponderosa pine.
- **Dry-lake phases:** Division V contains many pollen-barren levels and lower Eh measurements, suggesting that the lake was often dry and that desert grassland and Chihuahuan desert shrubs grew near the playa margin. Scattered Typha (cattail) grains in the lowest levels suggest shoreline springs.
- **Terminology:** The authors use a four-way biochore classification (forest, savanna, grassland, desert), with "savanna" covering pinon-juniper, pine-oak and ponderosa pine parkland.

## Key Findings

1. **Core-wide correlation pattern:** Pine is negatively correlated with most other types and is constraint-positively correlated only with PAP. The remaining types (cheno-ams, Artemisia, high- and low-spine Compositae) tend to be positively correlated with one another.
2. **Core slug 96 (19.96 to 20.22 m):** Continuous sampling in 6 mm cubes revealed two distinct pollen zones. Pinyon-juniper woodland with some oak and sagebrush, and a fringe of Atriplex, is replaced upward by ponderosa pine parkland (70 to 90 percent pine above level 20).
3. **Core slug 71 (pine peak at 15.54 m):** A total of 61 counts was made. A grassy woodland was replaced by pine parkland under a cooler or wetter climate, then woodland reinvaded above 15.39 m with more Artemisia and Sarcobatus.
4. **Within-zone variation:** In pine parkland levels, when pine declines the slack is taken up mainly by grass and secondarily by low-spine Compositae. Cheno-am pollen does not vary with pine, which the authors take as evidence against a fluctuating lake level.
5. **Serial correlation:** Pine counts show low serial correlation within a single zone, but high serial correlation across the whole core. This indicates that combined-zone correlations reflect vegetational shifts and within-zone correlations reflect minor fluctuations.
6. **Division IV (36 levels, about 6.9 m):** Pine is high in only three levels. The authors infer woodland along the shores of Lake Cochise, with short climatic episodes of 400 to 1200 years duration. Pine parkland occupied the lake edge at least three times.
7. **Division III (39 levels, 10.2 m):** Mean pine count is 158.9, against 120.3 in Division IV. Nine levels exceed 180. PAP (especially Abies and Pseudotsuga) correlates positively with oak and juniper, which the authors attribute to high-altitude montane species. The level at 7.44 m has an unusually low pine count (88) and high oak, juniper, Abies and Pseudotsuga values; the authors describe it as the best development of this mixed assemblage.
8. **Division II (6 levels):** Pine is very high, and 1200 pollen grains contain only 23 non-pine grains. The authors interpret this as a prolonged cold period, correlated with the late Wisconsin glacial maximum, in which spruce-fir forest was obscured by pine parkland pollen.
9. **Division V (42 levels):** Pine has its lowest values in the core. Grass has its highest. Pine shows no constraint associations with other taxa except a negative association with cheno-ams, which the authors read as lake-size effects.
10. **Age estimate:** About 70,000 to 210,000 years, based on sedimentation rates from other playa lakes. The authors estimate up to 210 possible pollen zones, or about one zonal change per 1000 years.
11. **Metastability:** At least ten episodes of pine parkland occupation of the Sulphur Spring Valley during the Wisconsin are inferred. The authors compare this instability with the Laurentide Ice Sheet margin as described by [[Flint]] (1963), and contrast it with the more stable Foraminifera and oxygen-isotope records of marine cores (Ericson et al., 1961; Emiliani, 1955).
12. **Limitations stated by authors:** Sampling intervals were too wide to resolve all fluctuations. Close-interval analyses were limited to a few slugs. Statistical methods were acknowledged as "rudimentary". Replicate cores are needed, and the authors call for small pollen sums excluding pine.
