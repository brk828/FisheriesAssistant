# Factors Influencing Supply of Major Ions to Inland Waters, with Special Reference to the Atmosphere

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Eville Gorham]] (Botany Department, University of Toronto; *Geological Society of America Bulletin*, v. 72, p. 795–840, June 1961) examines why the major ionic composition of inland waters varies so widely. It argues that the atmosphere is a more important source of dissolved ions than previously recognized, but that soil and rock weathering usually supplies the larger share. Five partially independent environmental factors (climate, geology, topography, biota, and time) interact to control the concentration and composition of precipitation, soil solutions, and lake and river waters. The author rejects the idea of a single "average fresh water" toward which all waters converge, and calls for single-factor case studies to clarify each factor's role. Although the document contains no fish data, it is a general aquatic-chemistry review and so is tagged as limnology rather than ichthyology.

## Background & Description

Four cations (calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium) and three anions (bicarbonate, sulfate, chloride) account for nearly all the dissolved ionic load of fresh water. The Conway (1942) average world fresh water has a total ionic concentration of 2.35 m equiv./l, with cations of Ca 64%, Mg 17%, Na 16%, K 3% and anions of HCO3 73%, SO4 16%, Cl 10% (Table 1).

Ionic proportions in natural waters do not mirror the crust. Finnish bedrock is largely granitic and deficient in carbonates, sulfates, and chlorides, yet Finnish river waters are relatively enriched in calcium and magnesium and depleted in potassium compared with the rock, indicating that alkaline earths are leached from soils more readily than alkalies (Table 2).

The review defines the sources of ions as the sea, land surfaces, [[Volcanoes]], [[Air Pollution]], and organic debris, transported by rain, snow, dry fallout, or gas. Ion release from weathering proceeds through solution, oxidation–reduction, hydrogen-ion action, and complex formation, and is modified by [[Ion Exchange]] and the [[Mode of Water Percolation]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate.** Arid regions produce sulfate–chloride waters, humid vegetated regions produce bicarbonate-dominated waters (Table 3: Hudson River vs. Rio Grande vs. Essequibo River). Closed-basin lakes depend on evaporation exceeding precipitation. Langbein's climatic limits are roughly 26 in. precipitation at 40 °F, 32 in. at 50 °F, and 39 in. at 60 °F. Median salinity of closed-basin lakes is near 10,000 ppm.

**Geology.** Conway's analysis suggests waters below 50 ppm salinity drain igneous and metamorphic rock; above about 200 ppm, sodium, potassium, and chloride rise sharply, linked to arid saline soils or salt beds. The English Lake District shows the range: volcanic-rock lakes are very dilute, while lakes on Carboniferous and Triassic rocks are more concentrated and calcareous.

**Topography.** In Swedish lakes the calcium/chloride ratio varies with altitude and substrate. Siljan limestone lakes reach a ratio of 7.3, glacial till lakes about 2.1, and lakes near sea level lower values, reflecting marine submergence and leaching.

**Biota.** Vegetation removes large quantities of minerals; in the cited temperate data, cycling through vegetation is often similar to or greater than atmospheric supply. Forest canopies enrich rain in potassium about 18-fold, calcium and sodium about 4- and 3-fold. Peat bogs isolated from mineral soil become acidic and calcium-deficient.

**Time.** Chemical evolution of lakes is hard to observe directly. Lakes may have been richer in calcium at the end of the Pleistocene. Drought and climatic cycles change lake salinity, and the Great Salt Lake showed a maximum salinity about twice its minimum over a century.

## Key Findings

- **Atmospheric supply is underestimated.** In the English Lake District, rain supplies almost all the chloride, sulfate, sodium, and potassium of dilute volcanic-area lakes, and more than 80% of magnesium and more than half of calcium.
- **Finland:** atmospheric input supplies about 20% of sodium, 10–20% of potassium, about 10% of magnesium, 20–40% of calcium, 30–50% of chloride, and 50–70% of sulfate of river discharge. Chloride is probably almost entirely atmospheric. Table 4 gives the Kuopio/Vuoksi and Jyväskylä/Kymijoki comparisons.
- **Mechanisms:** dry fallout and vegetation capture of salt and dust are proposed to explain chloride discrepancies. Sea spray is mostly in "giant particles" above 0.8 µ radius (about 99% of its mass).
- **Sulfate sources:** sulfuric acid from air pollution (e.g., Sudbury smelter fallout mostly within about 5–15 miles), oxidation of sulfides in soils and sediments, and possibly hydrogen sulfide from sea floor or peat.
- **Ion exchange:** bog waters become more acidic in dry weather, and magnesium/chloride ratios rise from 0.063 (wet) to 0.136 (dry). Divalent ions are more strongly adsorbed than monovalent ones.
- **Single-factor approach:** the author concludes that studies varying only one factor (such as the approach in [[Soil Science]] and plant ecology) should clarify the true composition of natural waters.
- **Rejection of the standard-water hypothesis:** the Rodhe (1949) idea of a converging "average fresh water" is considered too extreme. The Uppland lake averages resemble world averages only after statistical treatment that hides large local variation.
