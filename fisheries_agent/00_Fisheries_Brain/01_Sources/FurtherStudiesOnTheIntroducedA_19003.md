# Further Studies on the Introduced Asiatic Clam (Corbicula) in Tennessee

#ichthyology #marine-biology #corbiculidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 report from the [[Tennessee Stream Pollution Control Board]] (Tennessee Department of Public Health), authored by Ralph M. Sinclair and Billy G. Isom, documents the spread, biology, taxonomy and control of the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula*) in Tennessee. The clam, first taken in the Western Hemisphere in 1938, had spread through the Ohio River Basin and was by then found over virtually all of the Tennessee River and Lower Cumberland basins. The authors conclude that all Ohio Basin specimens belong to one species, placed in the synonymy of *Corbicula manillensis*. The clam fouls water intakes, pumps, condensers and sand and gravel operations. The report finds that chlorination during the spawning season is effective as an in-plant control, and recommends a three-week treatment at 1.0 ppm chlorine residual.

## Background & Description

The Asiatic Clam is a bivalve mollusc of the class Pelecypoda, order Heterodonta, placed in the family Corbiculidae, separate from the Sphaeriidae. It is often mistaken for juvenile mussels ("baby mussels"), but the lateral and cardinal hinge teeth distinguish it: three cardinal teeth per valve, and two lateral teeth on each side of the right valve and one on each side of the left. The shell is ovate to trigonal, heavy, concentrically sculptured, and covered with a lustrous yellow-green periostracum that erodes on large, old specimens. The largest Tennessee specimen recorded was 43 mm.

Shell form varies with environment, age and transplantation. [[Kuroda]] (1938) and [[Prashad]] (1920s) are cited to show that outline and colour are unreliable species characters. Hinge-tooth reversals occur, reported at 12.1% in Ohio Sphaeriids by [[Eggleton and Davis]], and abnormal cardinal or lateral patterns appear in Arizona, California and Tennessee samples.

**Reproduction and life history:**
- The clam is monoecious and incubatory; inner gill lamellae act as marsupia holding hundreds of developing larvae.
- It reaches sexual maturity in its first year at about 6.5 mm in Tennessee.
- Spawning season in Tennessee is July to November; the report also gives comparable seasons for Japanese, Philippine and Taiwanese species.
- Larvae (veligers, about 160–220 µm) are short-term planktotrophic, non-swimming, and released in large numbers. Benthic juveniles attach by byssus.

**Taxonomy:** The authors place the Ohio Basin population in the synonymy of *Corbicula (Corbiculina) manillensis* Philippi 1841, along with *C. leana*, *C. vicina*, *C. javanica* and part of *C. fluminea*. The authors note that [[Habe]] and [[Van Benthem Jutting]] both considered the introduced form not to be true *C. fluminea*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Substrate:** The clam occurs on sand, mud, gravel and rock. The highest densities per unit area were in sand-mud substrates (Bates), while gravel and rock offer protection from washout during high flow. The "under rock" habitat is described as unique.

**Water quality and flow:** Corbicula is typically freshwater, though some species tolerate brackish water. It survived extreme water-level fluctuation, and one lab test left 18 of over 50 specimens alive after a month in a pail. It tolerates high chlorides, metals and low dissolved oxygen, and the authors note it was absent below domestic and industrial outfalls in San Francisco Bay studies.

**Seasonal activity:** Tennessee clams remained active in winter and did not bury, unlike Japanese *C. sandai*.

**Mortalities:** Mass die-offs occurred in April 1961 (Wolf Island, Tennessee River) and in August–September 1962 at Chattanooga, where clogged traveling screens were recorded. The cause was never determined. Dead shells stay buoyant and drift downstream.

**Food and predators:** The clam feeds on phytoplankton and possesses a crystalline style like the Unionid mussels. Natural enemies include waterfowl (189 of 1,988 killed Reelfoot Lake waterfowl contained clams, 1.37% of food), flatworms (*Dugesia tigrina*), and fish including blue and channel catfish, carp, redear sunfish and [[Freshwater Drum]].

**Distribution and spread:** The clam spread from the Ohio Basin up the Tennessee and Cumberland rivers. The upstream Tennessee record was Fort Loudoun Dam (mi. 602.3). Vectors include river commerce, bait buckets, boating, ballast water, and gravel barge unloading. Its spread reflects [[Zebra Mussel]] (*Dreissena polymorpha*) patterns in Europe.

**Culture:** Clams were kept in laboratory culture for months, but veligers were not reared to the benthic stage because of protozoan predation.

## Key Findings

- **Spread:** Corbicula reached Fort Loudoun Reservoir, the upper Duck River, Richland Creek, and Old Hickory Reservoir by 1962; records extended to Cincinnati, Mobile and New Orleans.
- **Nuisance:** The clam fouls water supplies, TVA steam plants, industrial lines and sand and gravel operations. Clams in aggregate can move toward the surface when concrete is poured, leaving voids.
- **Economics:** Close to twelve sand and gravel operations on the Cumberland and Tennessee rivers; the report cites an estimate of several million dollars annual production.
- **Control:** Chlorination at 1.0 ppm residual for three weeks eradicated adult clams in an industrial plant at Chattanooga (treatment began April 1962). Veligers were not effectively killed at the New Johnsonville plant by intermittent 1 ppm dosing.
- **Bioassay (Bayer 73):** 24-hour TLm for fingerling [[Bluegill]] was 0.18 mg/L. The planktotrophic veliger TLm was 0.17 mg/L. Adult clams and snails died within 48 hours at 0.56 mg/L.
- **Chlorine veliger test:** Veligers (about 230 µm) withdrew their foot at 2 ppm and were dead within ten minutes at 50 ppm.
- **Recommendation:** Tennessee water treatment facilities with surface sources should prechlorinate to a residual of at least 0.5 ppm at the filter beds and post-chlorinate to 2 ppm, with at least 0.5 ppm at the furthest distribution point.
- **Plankton monitoring:** Periodic raw-water plankton sampling before and during spawning should guide control timing. One-gallon samples should be concentrated and examined in a Sedgwick-Rafter cell.
- **Utilization:** The clam was considered economically a liability, though possible uses noted were pollution indicators (for example Sr-90 and copper), local food, and fish bait.
- **Later addenda (1964):** Clams reached the Apalachicola and Withlacoochee rivers in Florida, and redear sunfish on Chickamauga Reservoir showed a near-exclusive diet of Corbicula.

**Conclusions:** Only one species was introduced. Continuous chlorination during the June–November spawning season is necessary to control the clam, and the clam will spread into all major Tennessee and Cumberland streams.
