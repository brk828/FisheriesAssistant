# San Bernardino and Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuges Comprehensive Management Plan Environmental Assessment

#environmental-assessment #refuge-management #endangered-species #Yaqui-fishes #wetlands #conservation #NEPA

## Executive Summary

This Environmental Assessment evaluates three management alternatives for the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge]] in southeastern Arizona for the period 1995-2015. The refuges were established to conserve threatened and endangered fish and wildlife species, particularly four native [[Yaqui Fishes]] (Yaqui chub, Yaqui topminnow, Yaqui catfish, and beautiful shiner). The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposes Alternative A (Ecosystem Approach) as the preferred alternative, emphasizing cooperative management with local communities, private landowners, and Mexico to protect water resources and restore native habitats. The Finding of No Significant Impact concludes that implementation of the Comprehensive Management Plan does not constitute a major Federal action significantly affecting the human environment.

## Background & Description

The San Bernardino and Leslie Canyon NWRs are located in Cochise County, Arizona, along the Mexican border. San Bernardino NWR lies 16 miles east of Douglas, while Leslie Canyon NWR is situated in the Swisshelm Mountains to the north. The refuges exhibit a semi-arid climate with mean annual precipitation of 12.5 inches, characterized by biseasonal patterns of winter and summer precipitation separated by spring and fall droughts. Maximum summer temperatures average above 95°F, with winter mean minimums of 40°F.

The refuges encompass eight major habitat types: Chihuahuan Desert Scrub (the most contiguous type), Desert Grassland, Mesquite Bosque, Fallow Fields, Riparian Woodland (cottonwood, willow, alder, ash, walnut), Riparian Scrub, Marshland (cienegas with permanent artesian flooding), and Aquatic habitats. Water resources are critical, supplied by springs and artesian wells enhanced by an 1887 earthquake that created a fault south of San Bernardino NWR in Mexico, forming an artesian reservoir. [[Leslie Creek]] provides essential baseflow and flushing flows for native fish survival.

The refuges support over 250 bird species (including 90 neotropical migrants), 42 mammals, 27 reptiles, 9 fishes, and 10 amphibians. San Bernardino NWR is recognized as a distinctive cultural transition zone with human occupancy exceeding 10,000 years, including the National Historic Landmark Slaughter Ranch complex and prehistoric Animas Phase/Casas Grandes pueblos.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Eight native fish species historically inhabited the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]], but only four currently occur on the refuges: [[Yaqui Chub]] (endangered), [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (endangered), [[Beautiful Shiner]] (threatened), and [[Longfin Dace]] (candidate). The [[Yaqui Catfish]] (threatened) has been extirpated from the wild in the United States but maintains populations in Mexico and is being propagated at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]. The remaining four native species ([[Yaqui Sucker]], [[Mexican Stoneroller]], [[Roundtail Chub]]) are candidate species found only in Mexico.

Non-native fish introductions (mosquitofish, bullhead, sunfish) have caused severe problems for native species, though removal efforts have been successful. The [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] (candidate) has declined due to predation by introduced [[Bullfrog]]s, which also prey on young [[Mexican Garter Snake]]s (candidate). Active bullfrog removal through trapping and gigging is ongoing. The [[San Bernardino Spring Snail]] (candidate) is limited to shaded hillside seeps at San Bernardino Spring. The [[Huachuca Water Umbel]] (candidate plant) grows in Black Draw, Oasis Pond, and Evil Twin Pond.

Water quality is excellent, with surface waters meeting state standards except for dissolved oxygen and lead. Waters are virtually absent of dissolved trace metals and near neutral or slightly alkaline (pH 7.78 ± 0.75). Although 4 of 23 organochlorine pesticides were detected in tissue samples, all values were below National Academy of Sciences criteria. The aquifer's extent and limitations remain uncertain, requiring development of a computer model with the University of Arizona Department of Hydrology.

The refuges are situated within an "Area of Ecological Concern" defined as the watersheds and groundwater basins directly associated with management of Yaqui fishes. Ecosystem sustainability depends on cooperative management with the [[Malpai Borderlands Group]], private landowners, and Mexican jurisdictions to protect the watershed through conservation easements and sound land/water use practices.

## Key Findings

**Alternative Selection**: The Service selected Alternative A (Ecosystem Approach) as the preferred alternative because it supports ecosystem-based management with emphasis on partnerships, water resource protection, native fish population enhancement, and grassland restoration to reduce woody species invasion and increase biological diversity.

**Alternative A (Preferred) - Ecosystem Approach**:
- Day-to-day management would not change significantly, but would emphasize working with local communities, private landowners, and Mexico
- Habitat management centered on water resources for threatened/endangered species
- Native grassland restoration to reduce invading woody species
- "Off-refuge" approach to environmental education due to sensitive endangered species
- Special use permits continue for birdwatching, hiking, and small game hunting
- No significant impacts to climate, minimal short-term air quality impacts from prescribed burns
- Water quantity and quality would be further protected through aquifer management strategies
- Minimal effects on geology and soils; restoration would ultimately reduce soil loss
- Increased biological diversity through habitat restoration (mesquite thinning, grassland restoration, riparian protection)
- Beneficial impacts to threatened/endangered species through active recovery programs
- Continued special use permit system; no trails or auto tour loops developed
- Full compliance with cultural resource protection laws

**Alternative B (No Action)**:
- Continue management under 1985 master plan
- Focus on intensive field work for Yaqui fishes and leopard frog management
- Public use program remains unchanged with special use permits
- Water quantity/quality could be negatively impacted long-term without management guidelines
- Soils subject to accelerated loss without grassland restoration on abandoned fields
- Biological diversity not maximized due to loss of historical habitat conditions
- Missed opportunities for cumulative enhancement and cooperative management
- Limited environmental education and public outreach
- Lack of clear cultural resource protection strategies

**Alternative C (Maximize Habitat Developments and Public Use)**:
- Assumes aquifer can support intensive development (uncertain)
- Maximum water development with new wells and pumping systems
- Intensive habitat restoration: mesquite thinning, prescribed burns, grassland restoration
- Extensive aquatic/wetland habitat development
- Significant facility development: visitor station, trails, parking, restrooms, auto loop
- Increased biological diversity from expanded habitats
- Beneficial impacts to threatened/endangered species if aquifer assumptions hold
- Significant soil disturbance from construction
- Increased visitation could negatively impact resources through trampling, littering, wildlife disturbance
- Risk of exotic fish introductions
- Increased revenues to local economy
- Greater cultural resource interpretation but increased vandalism risk

**Environmental Consequences Summary**:
- No alternative would result in significant cumulative impacts
- Climate: no impacts under any alternative
- Air quality: temporary impacts from prescribed burns (Alternatives A and C)
- Water: Alternative A provides best protection; Alternative B risks degradation; Alternative C assumes unproven aquifer capacity
- Soils: minimal impacts under A; accelerated loss under B; significant disturbance under C
- Biological diversity: maximized under A and C; not optimized under B
- Threatened/endangered species: all alternatives provide benefits, but A offers most comprehensive approach
- Public use: A maintains current low-impact levels; B unchanged; C significantly expanded with associated risks
- Socioeconomic: A provides moderate benefits; B minimal; C significant economic impacts

**Management Goals and Strategies** (10 issues addressed):
1. **Ecosystem Sustainability**: Create partnerships with Malpai Borderlands Group, work with private landowners, increase monitoring
2. **Biological Diversity**: Restore native habitats through prescribed burns, mechanical control, native plantings; preserve riparian woodlands; support conservation easements
3. **Endangered Species**: Implement Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan, prevent non-native introductions, develop cooperative agreements with Mexico, culture techniques for fish propagation, restore aplomado falcon habitat
4. **Water Management**: Protect water rights, develop water use plan, maintain wells, establish monitoring devices, create aquifer computer model, evaluate water quality
5. **Compatibility and Public Use**: Annual compatibility analyses, ensure reasonable recreation levels, monitor impacts, maintain access roads
6. **Environmental Education**: Develop outreach programs for schools and community, provide technical assistance to landowners, create interpretive trail with Johnson Museum
7. **Cultural Resources**: Ensure compliance with protection laws, utilize Regional Historical Preservation Officer, monitor visitor impacts
8. **Interagency Coordination**: Strengthen role in Malpai Borderlands Group, maintain MOUs with agencies and Mexico, establish cooperative agreements
9. **Land Protection**: Protect 24,000-acre area through conservation easements and willing-seller acquisitions, acquire mineral rights, work with 99 Bar Ranch on Leslie Creek protection
10. **Staffing and Funding**: Hire permanent biologist with fisheries/herpetology expertise, assess annual funding needs, review plan every 5 years

**NEPA Determination**: Finding of No Significant Impact issued. The goals, objectives, and strategies do not call for major developments or actions that would significantly impact the environment. No Environmental Impact Statement required. Implementation may proceed 30 days after decision notice date (May 1995). Site-specific proposals will receive additional NEPA review as needed.
