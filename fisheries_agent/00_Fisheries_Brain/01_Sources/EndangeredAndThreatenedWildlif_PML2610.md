# 12-Month Finding on a Petition to List the Lower Colorado River Basin DPS of Roundtail Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register proposed rule (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, July 7, 2009) announces a 12-month finding on a 2003 [[Center For Biological Diversity]] petition to list a distinct population segment (DPS) of [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The Service concluded that the lower basin population qualifies as a DPS, meets the discreteness and significance criteria of the [[DPS Policy]], and is a listable entity. Listing is found to be warranted but precluded by higher-priority listing actions. The DPS is added to the candidate species list and assigned a Listing Priority Number (LPN) of 9. The finding was prompted by a 2007 settlement agreement that required a new status review.

## Background & Description

- **Legal basis:** Section 4(b)(3)(B) of the Act requires a 12-month finding on petitions with substantial information. Findings are "not warranted," "warranted," or "warranted but precluded."
- **Procedural history:** In 1985 the species became a Category 2 candidate; it lost formal candidate status in 1996. A 2003 petition sought listing of a lower basin DPS and of the [[Headwater Chub]]. A May 2006 finding deemed the lower basin population not a DPS. A 2006 lawsuit by the Center for Biological Diversity led to a November 2007 settlement requiring reevaluation.
- **Taxonomy:** The population is *Gila robusta*, the same species as upper basin populations. It is not treated as a separate species or subspecies. Roundtail chub has been recognized as a species since the 1800s and is regarded as a species by the American Fisheries Society.
- **Morphology and life history:** A cyprinid (minnow) with a streamlined body, olive-gray to silvery coloration, and sometimes dark blotches. Typically 9 to 14 in (25 to 36 cm), up to 20 in (50 cm). Lifespan 5 to 7 years; spawning from age 2. Spawning occurs February through June over slow to moderate velocities. Omnivorous, feeding on invertebrates, plants, detritus, fish, and algae.
- **Thermal tolerance:** Occupied waters range from 0 to over 32 °C; upper thermal tolerance reported at 30.3 °C (86.6 °C is as printed in the source, but context indicates a typographical error); spawning recorded at 14 to 24 °C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Discreteness.** Historically, the species formed two population centers, one in the upper and one in the lower Colorado River basin, with little mixing. Mainstem Colorado River records are sparse. The populations are separated by about 275 river miles. Glen Canyon Dam now adds artificial separation, but the historical separation is the primary basis for the finding. Mitochondrial DNA evidence shows lower basin populations carry a unique, divergent lineage not found in the upper basin.

**Significance.**
- *Ecological setting:* The lower basin is dominated by monsoon and mixed monsoon-snowmelt hydrographs, with flashy floods and higher sediment, temperature, and salinity. The upper basin has snowmelt-driven hydrographs. Ecoregions differ, including the Colorado Plateau Semi-Desert and Arizona-New Mexico Mountains provinces (lower) versus Intermountain and Nevada-Utah provinces (upper).
- *Gap in range:* Lower basin populations occupy over one-third of the historical range (2 of 6 states), including the entire current range in Arizona and New Mexico.
- *Population share:* 31 lower basin populations versus 74 upper basin populations, so about 30 percent of remaining populations.
- *Area:* Lower basin populations account for about 107,300 sq mi (49 percent) of the 219,310 sq mi Colorado River Basin.
- *Historical stream length:* Up to 2,796 mi (4,500 km) historically; currently 497 to 901 mi (800 to 1,450 km).
- *Unique status:* Lower basin populations are not the only surviving natural occurrence of the species, so that DPS factor was deemed not applicable.

**Distribution and status (Voeltz 2002; Cantrell 2009).**
- Historically occupied about 2,796 mi of stream in Arizona and New Mexico.
- Current status: about 18 to 32 percent of former range occupied; roughly 60 percent of formerly occupied stream extirpated.
- Of 40 populations assessed by Voeltz (2002): 0 stable-secure, 6 stable-threatened, 13 unstable-threatened, 10 extirpated, 11 unknown.
- Updated assessment (Cantrell 2009): 1 stable-secure (Fossil Creek, restored and renovated), 8 stable-threatened, 13 unstable-threatened, 10 extirpated, 9 unknown.
- Management Areas: A (Gila, Salt, and Verde basins), B (Bill Williams basin), and C (Little Colorado River basin).

## Key Findings

1. **DPS determination:** The lower Colorado River basin roundtail chub population is discrete and significant, so it qualifies as a DPS and is a listable entity. This reverses the Service's 2006 finding.
2. **Listing status:** Listing is warranted but precluded by higher-priority actions. The DPS receives LPN 9 because threats are imminent and of moderate magnitude. It is added to the candidate list. Critical habitat decisions are deferred.
3. **Principal threats (Factor A, habitat):** Dams, water diversions, groundwater pumping, dewatering, livestock grazing, urban and rural development, road use, mining, logging, recreation, channelization, and high-intensity wildfire. Water withdrawal is a threat to most extant populations. The Big Chino Water Ranch Project and groundwater pumping in the Verde basin are highlighted.
4. **Principal threats (Factor C, nonnative species):** Predation and competition by nonnative fishes such as green sunfish, largemouth bass, channel and flathead catfish, red shiner, and rainbow trout are described as a serious, persistent threat. Nonnative species are present in every population except Fossil Creek. Nonnative removal at Fossil Creek and other sites produced increases in roundtail chub abundance.
5. **Factor B (overutilization):** Not considered a threat; roundtail chub is a permitted sport fish in Arizona, with a catch-and-release restriction in Fossil Creek.
6. **Factor D (regulatory mechanisms):** Existing regulations are inadequate. No specific federal protection exists, the species is a sensitive species on Forest Service lands, and water laws do not protect habitat or flows. Some conservation agreements (Range-wide Agreement, Arizona Agreement, New Mexico Plan) provide partial benefit but are in early implementation.
7. **Factor E (other factors):** Fragmentation, stochastic events (drought, flood, fire), and climate change increase extinction risk. Fagan et al. (2002) estimated a high local extirpation risk. Climate models predict aridity increases in the Southwest. Management Area C was judged the most threatened.
8. **Conservation actions:** Acquisition of land and water rights (for example, The Nature Conservancy's Upper Verde River Wildlife Area), instream flow rights, establishment of new populations in nonnative-free habitats (such as Ash Creek, Roundtree Canyon, and refuge ponds), and fish barriers and renovation (Fossil Creek).
9. **Sources of uncertainty:** Genetic data are sparse and difficult to interpret. Population size and trend data are limited, and many status assessments depend on only a few surveys. The Service notes that its conclusions may change as new information arrives.
10. **Priority and budget:** The FY 2009 Listing Program budget was about $98.8 million. Critical habitat subcaps constrain resources. The Service will continue to accept information and monitor the species.
