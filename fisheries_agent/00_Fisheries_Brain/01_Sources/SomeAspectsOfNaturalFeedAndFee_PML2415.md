# Feeding Habits and Trophic Relations of Tilapia galilaea and Tilapia aurea in Lake Kineret

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Pepietta Spataru and Myron Zorn of the [[Fishery and Aquaculture Research Station]] (DOR, Israel) examines the feeding ecology of two commercially important [[Tilapia]] species in [[Lake Kineret]]. The two species, [[Tilapia Galilaea]] and [[Tilapia Aurea]], together yield more than 150 tons annually. Monthly digestive-tract analyses were run over two years (1973 onward). The authors found that *T. galilaea* is a selective phytoplankton feeder that depends heavily on the dinoflagellate [[Peridinium Cinctum]], and that it also recycles algal cell contents through its feeding. *T. aurea*, by contrast, has a broad, opportunistic diet and grows more slowly in the lake. The authors attribute this to its recent colonization of the deep lake, where it has had little time to adapt to a specific food source. The paper argues that fishery management must account for these trophic differences.

## Background & Description

**Study species**
- *Tilapia galilaea* is described as endemic to Lake Kineret. The text characterises it as a phytophagous fish with a "favourite" feed item.
- *Tilapia aurea* is described as a relatively new arrival in the Kineret ecosystem. Until 1956 it was found mostly in [[Lake Hula]], a shallow, swampy lake in the Upper Galilee. It has been established in Lake Kineret through stocking since then, and its catch has risen since.

**Rationale**
The study has two stated aims:
1. Increasing fish production in the lake.
2. Biological removal of organic matter from the water through natural trophic cycles.

The authors note that the lake has a rich trophic basis of phytoplankton, zooplankton and zoobenthos. Most fish species in the lake are herbivorous, with short food chains and only a few predatory species. Natural adult mortality is low, which the authors suggest should support high yields. Yields depend on how fully the fish use this food base.

**Context of declining catches**
Despite substantial stocking of *T. galilaea* and *T. aurea* fingerlings, and of new species such as [[Hypophthalmichthys Molitrix]] and [[Mugil]] sp., Kineret fisheries declined over the three years before the study. The authors use this to justify a detailed look at feeding and trophic relationships. They also warn that any new species must be checked first to ensure it does not consume the food of established species.

**Methods**
- Feed analysis of the fish's [[Digestive Tract]] content began in summer 1973.
- Collection covered a period of two years.
- Between 15 and 30 specimens of each species were collected every month.
- Fish of different sizes and sexes were sampled at different locations and seasons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Phytoplankton and food web**
The lake's phytoplankton, zooplankton and zoobenthos were previously studied by [[Gophen]] (1972), [[Por]] and [[Eitan]] (1970), and [[Berman]] and [[Pollingher]] (1974). These studies describe a rich trophic base. The authors also cite [[Kimor]] and Pollingher (1965) on the lake's plankton.

**Peridinium and the seasonal cycle**
*Peridinium cinctum* (described as a member of the Pyrrophyta/Dinophyta group) has a cellulose wall built of plates separated by sutures, called the theca. Its cell volume is 70,000–125,000 µm³. It forms blooms in the lake and appears in large numbers for 7–8 months of the year. From January to August it makes up over 50% of the lake's total phytoplankton biomass. It is therefore a major, year-round energy source for *T. galilaea*, especially during this period.

**Digestion and nutrient recycling**
Inside the fish gut, *Peridinium* cells are broken down in stages:
- The theca splits along its plates.
- Vacuoles form and grow inside the cell.
- The protoplast is digested completely.

Some cells pass through the gut undigested. These lack a theca and are non-viable. They are quickly attacked by bacteria, so they enter the nutrient and energy cycle faster. The authors conclude that *T. galilaea* is both a consumer of *Peridinium* and a contributor to its mechanical destruction and nutrient recycling.

**Seasonal shifts in feeding**
- In March, the number of green algae falls almost to zero.
- As *Peridinium* numbers decline, *T. galilaea* consumes it only incidentally.
- At that point the fish switch to a varied and highly diverse diet.
- In autumn, the fish's diet is described as including Chrysophyta, *Peridinium* cysts and other rotifers.

**Feeding site and intensity**
*T. galilaea* feeds year-round on the phytoplankton mass. Its feeding site shifts between the water surface and the lake bottom, following the movement and aggregation of its food. Feeding intensity, measured as ingested feed as a percentage of fish weight, follows a yearly cycle (Fig. 2). The authors suggest that feeding peaks when *Peridinium* is most abundant. They attribute the decline in feeding at other times to lower total phytoplankton concentration, which reduces filtration efficiency.

## Key Findings

### 1. Diet of *Tilapia galilaea*
- *T. galilaea* is a phytophagous fish with a strong preference for *Peridinium cinctum*.
- Other algal species are also eaten. Their quality and quantity change with the season. Some of these algae reach tens of millions of cells, but they contribute a negligible share of the feed by weight.
- Its feeding is **selective** and changes little from season to season.
- Differences between adult fish of different sizes are **quantitative, not qualitative**. The ratio of food consumed to fish weight is roughly constant at any given time.
- There are no significant feeding differences between males and females.

### 2. Diet of *Tilapia aurea*
- Analysis of *T. aurea* feeding was not complete when the paper was written.
- Its diet is **non-selective and opportunistic**. It consumes food at random according to what is available in the water it occupies at a given time.
- Diet varies from fish to fish and across places and times. It includes phytoplankton, zooplankton and zoobenthos.

### 3. Growth and size
- In the lake, *T. aurea* is always smaller than *T. galilaea* of the same age. The size difference increases with age (Fig. 3).
- In a fish-pond experiment at the research station (Wohlfarth et al., private communication), *T. aurea* grew more readily than *T. galilaea*. The average growth rate of the former was 1.45 g/fish/day. The latter grew about 25% more slowly. The exact rate for the latter is illegible in the scan.

### 4. Hypotheses for the growth difference
- ***T. aurea* has not adapted to the deep lake.** Lake Kineret is deep and environmentally stable. Its food supply does not change drastically over short periods, so specific adaptation to a food item can develop. *T. galilaea*, being endemic, has done so. *T. aurea* comes from shallow, variable waters such as Lake Hula and fish ponds, where broad trophic flexibility is an advantage. In Lake Kineret it has not adapted to any particular food, and its feeding method is inefficient, which may explain its slower growth.
- **Repeated stocking limits adaptation.** Because *T. aurea* is constantly re-introduced by stocking, and most of these fish are caught before they reproduce substantially in the lake, the population has had little opportunity to adapt.
- **Variable origin of stock.** A second hypothesis is that *T. aurea* stock comes from several sources: different ponds, fingerlings from government station ponds, and natural reproduction in the lake. This could explain the high variability in feeding behaviour.

### 5. Conclusions and recommendations
1. *T. galilaea* is a phytophagous fish. Its favourite food is *Peridinium cinctum*, a bloom-forming dinoflagellate that is present for 7–8 months of the year.
2. *T. aurea* has an extremely varied diet that changes with place and time.
3. Unlike *T. galilaea*, which is adapted to the abiotic and biological conditions of Lake Kineret and grows normally, *T. aurea* has grown relatively recently into the lake. Its growth rate is slow and, according to the authors, it does not reproduce successfully there.
4. Management must consider not only short-term fishing interests but also the wider disturbance to the natural equilibrium. Any introduction of new species should be preceded by a study of the lake's biological laws.
