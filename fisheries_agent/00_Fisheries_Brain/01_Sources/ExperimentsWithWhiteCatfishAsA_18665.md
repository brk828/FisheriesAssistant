# Experiments with White Catfish as a Sport Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary
This 1965 Auburn University Agricultural Experiment Station report (E. E. Prather) describes three pond experiments (1962–1964) evaluating the [[White Catfish]] (*Ictalurus catus*) as a sport fish for small ponds, stocked alone at 2,000 and 3,000 per acre and in combination with [[Channel Catfish]] (1,600 channel : 400 white per acre). The white catfish grew well, survived well, and provided good fishing at higher stocking and feeding rates, but was harder to catch than channel catfish and was generally less preferred by anglers. The author concludes that combining the two species may extend the period of good fishing.

## Background & Description
- The white catfish was originally native to Atlantic Coastal areas of the United States from Delaware to Florida. It has been introduced into the Middle West, Nevada and California.
- It appears to prefer slow or standing waters, fresh or brackish, and has supported commercial fisheries and sport fishing in large reservoirs. Little data existed on its value in smaller ponds and lakes.
- Earlier Auburn work in 1958 ([[Prather and Swingle]], 1960) showed it spawned readily in ponds, responded to supplemental feeding, gave high production per acre, and had meat quality equal to channel catfish.
- Funding came from the Auburn Agricultural Experiment Station and the Alabama Department of Conservation Dingell-Johnson Project F-10-R.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Experimental ponds:** a 2.5-acre pond (experiments 1 and 2) and a 22-acre pond (experiment 3).
- **Co-stocked species:** [[Fathead Minnow]] (forage, 1,000 per acre), [[Largemouth Bass]] (100 fingerlings per acre, predator), [[Nile Tilapia]] (742 per acre, experiment 2) and [[Java Tilapia]] (1,000 per acre, experiment 3). [[Green Sunfish]] contaminated the first pond but were held in check by bass.
- **Management:** daily feeding of Auburn No. 2 pellets except Sundays; superphosphate fertilization (once in experiment 2; two applications of triple superphosphate in experiment 3; none in experiment 1).
- **Water quality and health:**
  - [[Microcystis]] plankton blooms became heavy in July 1963 and were controlled with four weekly treatments of 0.8 lb copper sulfate per acre.
  - Gill flukes on channel catfish (May 20, 1963) were treated with 10 ppm [[Formalin Treatment]]. Plankton die-off lowered dissolved oxygen to 1.1 ppm at 4 ft on May 23, but no fish were killed.
  - Catfish samples in experiment 2 showed no parasite problems.
- Waters were highly colored by plankton from daily feeding, which made artificial lures ineffective.

## Key Findings
**Experiment 1: 2,000 white catfish per acre (2.5 acres, 1962)**
- Feed: 3,370 lb per acre. Catfish averaged 0.56 lb by July 15 (largest 0.8 lb).
- Fishing (July 16–October 22, $1 permits, limit 5): 54 fishermen per acre caught 86 white catfish (49.8 lb), 16 bass (3.1 lb) and 1.4 lb miscellaneous fish. It took 3.1 hours per white catfish and 5.3 hours per pound.
- Fishing effort was low because the fish were small, hard to catch, and an adjacent pond with 1–2 lb channel catfish and bass fished better.
- Draining on November 15 recovered 1,642 white catfish (1,411.5 lb) per acre, giving 86.4% survival including catch.

**Experiment 2: 3,000 white catfish per acre (2.5 acres, 1963–1964)**
- Feed: 6,357 lb per acre over both years.
- 663 fishermen per acre caught 1,359 white catfish (1,703.2 lb), 49 bass (27.3 lb), 69 tilapia (25.0 lb) and 5.6 lb miscellaneous fish, for a total of 1,761.1 lb.
- Fishing was excellent in July; 55.8% of the catfish catch was taken in July and 19.2% in August.
- Fishermen caught 45.3% of stocked catfish and 32.4% was recovered at draining (September 29), a total of 77.7%.
- Draining recovered 935.6 lb white catfish, 31.8 lb bass, 62.0 lb fatheads and 63.5 lb miscellaneous fish per acre. Total production was 2,854.0 lb per acre including the catch.
- White catfish reproduced in 1964, but only 200 fingerlings per acre remained.

**Experiment 3: 1,600 channel + 400 white catfish per acre (22 acres, 1963–1964)**
- Feed: 4,164.9 lb per acre.
- 559 fishermen per acre caught 1,340.6 lb: 981.3 lb channel catfish, 264.2 lb white catfish, 44.4 lb bass, 37.7 lb tilapia and 13.0 lb miscellaneous fish. Total catch was about the same as earlier ponds stocked with 2,000 channel catfish per acre.
- Anglers caught 5 channel catfish for each white catfish, though the stocking ratio was 4:1.
- Draining (October 22, 1964) recovered per acre: 110 white catfish (143.2 lb), 84 channel catfish (147.4 lb), 81 bass (40.4 lb), 10,843 fatheads (37.5 lb) and 90.1 lb miscellaneous fish.
- White catfish spawned in spring 1964, but only 14 small ones per acre survived predation. Channel catfish apparently did not spawn.
- 76% of white catfish were accounted for (52% caught, 24% drained); 70% of channel catfish (65% caught, 5% drained).
- Effective baits for both species: large pinkworms, chicken and beef liver, catalpa worms, live minnows, cut fish, and commercial blood and cheese baits.

**Author's conclusions**
1. White catfish are harder to catch than channel catfish but provide good fishing.
2. Anglers rate their taste as equal, but most prefer channel catfish because they bite better, fight harder, and give about 5% higher dressed weight (smaller head).
3. Combining white and channel catfish appears desirable and may extend the period of good fishing, since white catfish are less readily caught.
4. White catfish spawn in these combinations, but too few young escape predation to sustain good fishing under heavy fishing pressure.
