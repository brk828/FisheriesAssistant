# A Method for Determining the Surface Areas of Various Aquatic Plants

#hydrobiology #limnology #aquatic-plants #surface-area-method

## Executive Summary

This 1962 methodological paper by J. J. Harrop and R. E. Hatt (Department of Zoology, Southampton University), published in [[Hydrobiologia]], proposes a practical way to measure the surface area of aquatic plants so that the fauna living on them can be compared quantitatively. The authors argue that surface area is the most appropriate parameter, because macrofauna mainly graze the microbial film covering plants rather than plant tissue. Their method builds on [[Wieser Adsorption Coefficient]] technique: the weight of a thin liquid film retained on a plant is measured and calibrated against surface area. Tested on a simple leaf (Hydrangea), its polythene replica, and three aquatic plants (Ranunculus fluitans, Callitriche platycarpa, Carex sp.), the method yielded strong positive linear correlations (r = 0.92 to 0.93 for the leaf and replica, reported as "excellent" for all plants).

## Background & Description

The paper is a research article in a journal that publishes hydrobiology, limnology and protistology. The text includes the journal's author instructions (language, nomenclature, summary and figure requirements), which are not research content and are not summarised further.

**Problem.** Qualitative studies of plant-associated fauna were common, but quantitative data were scarce. Earlier approaches each had limits:

- Grab samplers used by [[Whitehead 1935]] and Macan (1948/1949) were considered unreliable for plants with long filaments such as Ranunculus, Myriophyllum and Carex.
- Hand collection and filling canvas bags of known volume (Jones & Soons, unpublished; Wilcomes 1959/1960) gave only rough quantitative measures.
- [[Krecker 1939]] expressed fauna per 10 feet of stem length, which applies only to certain plants.
- [[Rosine 1955]] calculated surface area mathematically per 10 cm of stem length, but this failed for complex-habit plants such as Ranunculus fluitans.

**Rationale.** Citing [[Shelford 1918]] and [[Butcher 1933]], the authors note that macrofauna feed largely on the film of bacteria, protozoa and algae covering plants, so surface area should relate to animal numbers and biomass.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Aquatic plant beds in freshwater systems, where plant-associated invertebrates live on the surfaces of [[Submerged Aquatic Plants]].
- **Study species/plants:** [[Ranunculus Fluitans]] (finely divided, dichotomously branching leaves), [[Callitriche Platycarpa]], and [[Carex Species]], plus the Hydrangea leaf used for calibration.
- **Trophic relationship:** The macrofauna graze the epiphytic microbial film, not the plant tissue. Surface area therefore serves as a proxy for available food-producing habitat.
- **Test conditions:** Leaf measurements were made at 5 °C to limit evaporation of the test solution.

## Key Findings

1. **Core method.** A standard solution of Teepol detergent in 500 cc of water formed a continuous thin film on leaves. Each leaf was degreased in acetone, dried, weighed, dipped in the solution, shaken for 20 seconds, and reweighed. This was repeated five times and averaged.
2. **Calibration on a simple leaf.** Hydrangea leaves were compared with polythene replicas of identical shape and area. Film weight was slightly greater on the natural leaves. Correlations between surface area and film weight were 0.92 (Hydrangea) and 0.93 (polythene), both significant at p < 0.001, showing a positive linear relationship.
3. **Surface area of complex plants.**
   - *Ranunculus fluitans:* Stems and leaves were treated as cylinders. Each plant was cut into short pieces, and each piece's diameter and length were measured with a micrometer eyepiece. Cylinder areas were summed. This was done for 16 plants or plant parts.
   - *Callitriche platycarpa:* Twenty plants were measured, with stems treated as cylinders and leaves as rectangles.
   - *Carex sp.:* Twenty leaves were measured graphically.
4. **Validation across growth forms.** Scatter plots of surface area against film weight for all three plants (Fig. 2) showed "excellent" correlations. Specific coefficients for these plants are not given in the text.
5. **Practical use.** Once a regression line is established for a plant, the surface area of any similar specimen can be read from its measured film weight "with reasonable accuracy." This allows quantitative comparison of faunas among plant species.
6. **Summary.** The authors conclude that the method provides a comparable habitat parameter for small animals living on aquatic plants. The paper also includes a French-language summary (Résumé).
