# Rearing Native Colorado River Fishes in Golf Course Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 report, submitted by Paul C. Marsh of [[Arizona State University]] to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] under Order Number 1425-4-PG-30-06810, reviews efforts to rear endangered [[Big River Fishes]] in artificial, non-hatchery ponds. It draws mainly on three sites: the [[Arizona State University Research Park]] (ASURP) and the [[ASU Golf Course]] (ASUGC) in Tempe, Arizona, and the [[BMGC Ponds]] in Blythe, California, plus [[Floyd Lamb State Park]] (FLSP) in Las Vegas, Nevada. The authors conclude that [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] can spawn, grow and survive in these ponds when protected from non-native predators. The programs were successful at ASURP and ASUGC, while BMGC and FLSP showed largely failed or undocumented outcomes.

## Background & Description

- **Historical status:** Colorado River basin streams once held 36 native species (Carlson and Muth 1939). Four "big river" fishes were widespread and abundant: [[Bonytail]], humpback chub, [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. All are now federally listed as endangered (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1985).
- **Causes of decline:** Dams, water diversion, habitat alteration, chemical pollution and non-native introductions contributed. Predation on early life stages by non-native fishes is considered the primary cause of recruitment failure (Minckley and Deacon 1991).
- **Hatchery origins:** Propagation protocols were developed at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and refined at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. A reclaimed backwater near [[Lake Mohave]] first allowed successful rearing of young razorback suckers in natural conditions (Langhorst and Marsh 1986).
- **Scope:** The report summarizes successes and failures, covering growth, feeding, disease, predation, recruitment, handling, pond management and anticipated problems.

Both Arizona golf and research sites were lined, and their designs were similar to local golf courses. Only ASURP was built for a sport-fishing component, and none of the golf course ponds were intended for fishing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Non-native fauna.** Exotic fishes are now ubiquitous in the Colorado River basin. Predation on larval natives can be caused by almost any non-native species, including adults and young of species not usually considered fish-eaters. Predation effects should therefore be considered wherever recovery is expected to succeed.

- **ASURP:** [[Redbelly Tilapia]] (1,000 stocked May 1986) for vegetation control; [[Red Shiner]] invaded via the SRP canal.
- **ASUGC:** Triploid [[Grass Carp]] for vegetation control (stocked autumn 1988); red shiner and [[Mosquitofish]] present but not abundant.
- **BMGC:** Around 50 tilapia (probably *Tilapia zillii*) stocked in 1984 produced stunted cohorts (fewer than 2% of adults exceeded 10.0 cm TL). Mosquitofish were likely introduced for mosquito control.
- **FLSP:** Rainbow trout and channel catfish stocked by the Nevada Division of Wildlife for recreational fishing; goldfish, fathead minnow, redear sunfish, black bullhead and largemouth bass were also recorded.

**Water quality and conditions.** At FLSP, groundwater pumping reduced inflow so greatly that augmentation was needed. A 1988 die-off at FLSP coincided with water temperature of about 27°C, pH 9.5 and dissolved oxygen of 8.7 mg/L, with no cause identified.

## Key Findings

### Program Histories

- **ASURP (Tempe):** Stocked 1,049 juvenile [[Colorado Squawfish]] (75 mm TL), 879 [[Bonytail]] (75 mm TL) and 1,000 [[Razorback Sucker]] (80–200 mm TL) in October–November 1986. Stockings from other sources continued, including the Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery, Hassayampa River Preserve, Wickenburg, Parker and Dexter. Bonytail and razorback suckers are expected to survive 30–50 years in these lakes, forming a broodstock with measurable genetic diversity.
- **ASUGC (Tempe):** Stocked 1,500 juvenile squawfish, 1,500 juvenile bonytail and 2,000 razorback suckers in September–October 1988. Squawfish and bonytail came from Dexter; razorback suckers came from Page Springs State Fish Hatchery.
- **BMGC (Blythe):** No plan existed for native fishes. Rotenone was applied in August 1983, and 1,966 juvenile bonytail (mean TL 10.2 cm, n = 25) were stocked on 29 September 1983. About 1,000 juvenile razorback suckers were stocked in the same month. Larvae were stocked in March 1986 (about 10,000) and March 1987 (about 5,050). A fish kill on 30 July 1987 killed 427 razorback suckers, and about 1,600 were removed in March 1988. Monitoring was sporadic, with no recovery in netting or snorkeling in 1989 and no records of later sampling. The CADFG Blythe office closed in 1990.
- **FLSP (Las Vegas):** 5,000 juvenile razorback suckers (75 mm mean TL) were transferred from Dexter in spring 1984 and about the same number released into Mulberry Pond in August 1984. Catches and estimates included:
  - January 1985: 9 captured (mean 12.0 cm).
  - October 1985: 8 captured (mean 29.4 cm).
  - February 1986: 34 captured; depletion estimate 35.
  - April 1987: 4 captured.
  - July 1989: 17 captured (mean 47.7 cm).
  - 1992 mark-recapture: Petersen estimate of 264; NVDOW biologists believe about 500 is more realistic.
  - Die-offs: roughly 100 dead or removed in a September die-off (year garbled in OCR), and about 90 dead in 1991.

### Growth

Growth in length and weight of captive razorback suckers at golf course and similar facilities equals or exceeds the best hatchery growth, especially through about age 5–6. Other non-native fishes had no apparent effect on growth. Growth in ponds is comparable to wild fish (McCarthy and Minckley 1987). No growth estimates exist for natural historic conditions.

### Feeding

Juveniles and sub-adults in artificial habitats ate diets like wild fish, dominated by zooplanktonic crustaceans and aquatic insect larvae, with substantial detritus. Razorback suckers appear primarily planktivorous, though benthic feeding is important.

### Disease

No specific disease studies exist for artificial ponds. Native fishes are considered generally healthy, though [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] (a parasitic copepod) was noted on fish at ASUGC and ASURP without attributed mortality.

### Predation

No records show predation on natives at ASUGC, ASURP, BMGC or FLSP. Experiments showed that immature Odonata (dragonflies and damselflies) consume larval razorback suckers. Stream-dwelling predators, including ictalurid catfishes, impact recently stocked juveniles. Juveniles under about 30 cm TL should not be stocked where large non-native predators are established. Once fish exceed that size, they are relatively immune to most current predators.

### Recruitment

Recruitment to adult stage is common for natives reared in protected artificial ponds. Natural reproduction was not confirmed in artificial ponds except at hatchery ponds at Dexter. Reasons include few collections, short time since stocking, predation and other factors.

### Anticipated Problems and Management

- **Acquisition and monitoring:** Problems include harvest during warm periods, lack of suitable capture methods and disturbance of organic substrates causing deoxygenation.
- **Handling:** Fish should be removed from nets quickly, held in live cages or large tubs, and kept out of water for no more than about one minute.
- **Management needs:** These fish are tolerant of high temperature, turbidity, salinity and low dissolved oxygen. Aeration, circulation, make-up water and feeding are generally unnecessary.
- **Dewatering and toxicants:** Dewatering should be avoided. Herbicides, algicides and insecticides must be used cautiously.
- **Recreational fishing:** Co-management with recreational fishing is not recommended because unintentional mortality and unauthorized stockings are likely.
