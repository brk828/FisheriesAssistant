## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department completion report (Project F-7-R-9) evaluates the effectiveness of **fall planting of fingerling trout** compared to spring-only stocking at [[Luna Lake]], Arizona, during the 1966 season. Creel census data were collected biweekly (one weekday, one weekend day) across the ice-free season (~April–November). Key metrics tracked included catch-per-man-hour, average creeled fish length, angler origin, party size, and bait/method effectiveness. A significant winter die-off due to low dissolved oxygen confounded direct evaluation of the fall-stocking hypothesis. Overall, angler use and total harvest increased relative to 1965, though average fish size declined, largely attributed to a compensatory spring plant of catchable-size trout following the winter kill.

## Background & Description

- **Average total length of creeled fish (1966):** 9.1 inches (down from 10.3 inches in 1965).
- **Monthly length trends:** Average length fluctuated seasonally — 11.0 in. (April, low sample), peaking at 10.88 in. (June), declining through summer to a season low of 8.37 in. (October) as new [[Fingerling Trout]] cohorts entered the fishery.
- **Size distribution:** Most creeled fish fell within the 7–11 inch range, with the modal length classes at 8–9 inches (1,406 and 1,679 fish respectively) out of 6,538 measured fish.
- **Stocking size classes referenced:**
  - Small fingerlings: 2–3 inches (planted in May 1966).
  - Larger fingerlings: 4–5 inches, identified as more immediately valuable for the fishery.
  - Catchable-size trout: stocked directly into the fishery in early May following the die-off.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** [[Luna Lake]] is a put-and-take montane trout fishery subject to seasonal water quality stress, including a documented **winter die-off** caused by **extremely low dissolved oxygen** throughout the lake — a classic winterkill event in shallow, ice-covered eutrophic lakes.
- **Trophic/recruitment dynamics:** Fingerlings planted in May (2–3 inches) grew and recruited into the harvestable fishery by mid-summer, sustaining catch rates through the remainder of the season. This illustrates a lag between stocking and fishery recruitment tied to fingerling size at release.
- **Angler-fishery interaction:** Catch-per-man-hour served as a strong driver of total angling effort — years with higher catch rates (e.g., 1962–1963: 1.00–1.31 fish/hr) correlated with higher total fisherman-days, while poor catch years (e.g., 1959: 0.22) saw reduced use.
- **Human dimension:** Nearly half (45%) of anglers were non-residents of Arizona, underscoring the socioeconomic importance of the fishery to the local community of Alpine, AZ, and to state tourism revenue.

## Key Findings

### Catch and Effort (Table I)
- **Overall 1966 catch-per-man-hour:** 0.74 (up from 0.59 in 1965).
- Catch rate rose sharply through the season: April (0.02) → May (0.55) → October (2.17), reflecting recruitment of stocked fingerlings and reduced fishing pressure late season.
- **Total fishermen (census period):** 8,384; **Total fish harvested:** 23,475 — both increased over 1965.

### Size Composition (Table II)
- Total fish measured: 6,538 (47 additional fish not measured).
- Average length declined seasonally from spring (~10.3–10.9 in.) to fall (~8.4–9.1 in.) as smaller fingerling-origin fish entered the catch.

### Historical Trend (Table III, 1958–1966)
- Catch-per-man-hour and total angler-days varied substantially over the 9-year record, ranging from lows of 0.22 (1959) to highs of 1.31 (1963), indicating high year-to-year variability in fishery performance likely tied to stocking practices and environmental conditions (e.g., winterkill events).

### Angler Demographics (Table IV)
- **Non-residents:** 45% of total anglers — the single largest origin category.
- **Maricopa County:** 15%; **Apache County:** 14%; remaining Arizona counties each contributed minor percentages.
- **Unlicensed juvenile anglers:** 2% of total.
- **Average party size:** 2.5 anglers; **average catch per party:** 6.9 fish.

### Bait and Method Effectiveness (Table V)
- **Prepared bait:** most popular (49% of anglers), with 67% catching at least one fish.
- **Worms:** 28% of anglers, 65% success rate.
- **Artificial flies:** only 16% of anglers used flies, but had the **highest success rate at 78%**, confirming fly fishing as the most effective (though not most popular) method.
- **Artificial lures:** 7% of anglers, 65% success rate.

### Management Implications
- The confounding effect of the **winter die-off** prevented clear evaluation of fall vs. spring fingerling stocking effectiveness for 1966.
- Report reiterates that **larger fingerlings (4–5 inches)** would provide more immediate recruitment value and larger average fish size compared to smaller (2–3 inch) fingerlings.
- **No changes** to current stocking or creel census methodology were recommended based on this year's findings.
