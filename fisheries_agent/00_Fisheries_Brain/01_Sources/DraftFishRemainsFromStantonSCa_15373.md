## Executive Summary

This paper (Miller & Smith) reports on fish skeletal remains recovered by Robert C. Euler from **Stanton's Cave**, an archaeological/paleontological cave site in Marble Gorge, Grand Canyon, Arizona. The cave, situated ~120 feet above the Colorado River near Vesey's Paradise, yielded historical (largely non-fossilized) bone material representing nearly all fish species native to this reach of the [[Colorado River]]. The assemblage is dominated by [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows) — notably the taxonomically contentious [[Gila Cypha]] (humpback chub) — and [[Catostomidae]] (suckers). The find is significant for supplying rare skeletal material to help resolve long-standing uncertainty over whether *Gila cypha* constitutes a valid, distinct species within the genus *Gila*.

## Background & Description

**Diagnostic skeletal elements recovered and examined:**

- **Gila cypha (humpback chub):**
  - Incomplete neurocranium with attached [[Weberian Apparatus]] (Fig. 1), plus 9 attached vertebrae and 3 ribs (from antechamber 1 surface).
  - Left pectoral girdle (Fig. 2), tentatively assigned to *G. cypha*, distinguished by:
    1. A ventral ridge on the cleithrum (notably *absent* in *Gila elegans*).
    2. Angle formed between the anterior and dorsal arms of the girdle.
    3. Relative length ratio of the outer versus dorsal arms.
  - Parts of 3 vertebral columns (possibly *G. cypha* and/or *G. elegans*) from a pack rat's nest.

- **Ptychocheilus lucius (Colorado squawfish):**
  - Right branchiostegal bone (specimen SC62), estimated to derive from a fish approximately 4–5 feet long.

- **Catostomus discobolus (bluehead sucker):**
  - Incomplete left dentary from a specimen estimated at ~315 mm standard length (S.L.).
  - Incomplete right dentary from a specimen estimated at ~260 mm S.L.

- **Catostomus latipinnis (flannelmouth sucker):**
  - Incomplete neurocranium from a specimen estimated at ~400 mm S.L.

- **Fossil cyprinid material:**
  - 5 articulated fossil vertebrae, tentatively referred to *Gila*, representing the caudal portion of the vertebral column of a large minnow (~400 mm S.L.) — described separately as "Cyprinidae, undetermined."

**Taxonomic note:** The genus *Gila* within the middle/upper Colorado River basin comprises three morphologically compared taxa: *[[Gila Robusta]]* (roundtail chub), *[[Gila Elegans]]* (bonytail — recognized as a full species per Minckley and Deacon, 1968), and *Gila cypha* (humpback chub). Skeletal (particularly cleithral/pectoral girdle) characters are proposed as a potentially valuable, previously unexplored character system for resolving species boundaries in this group.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native fish fauna of the Grand Canyon/Marble Gorge reach of the Colorado River** historically included six species across two families:
  - Cyprinidae: *Gila cypha*, *Gila elegans* (probably present), *Ptychocheilus lucius*, and rarely *Gila robusta*.
  - Catostomidae: *Catostomus latipinnis*, *Catostomus discobolus*.
  - Only *[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]* (humpback sucker) is notably absent from the cave assemblage despite being native to the region.
- *Gila robusta* (roundtail chub) is considered probably rare in this stretch of the mainstem river; no direct records exist, though it is known to occur in at least one tributary, the [[Little Colorado River]].
- **Historical range of Gila cypha:** Type locality near the mouth of Bright Angel Creek in Grand Canyon (Miller, 1946). Subsequently documented via archaeological material as far downriver as Boulder Canyon (15 miles below Hoover Dam) and as far upriver as Flaming Gorge/Hideout Canyon on the Green River, Utah (near the Wyoming border), and near Moab, Utah on the Colorado (Grand) River.
- Possible historical occurrence up the [[San Juan River]] as far as the Goosenecks, west of Mexican Hat, Utah (speculative).
- Per Stalnaker (1971), by the time of writing, *Gila cypha* was restricted to three refugial areas:
  1. The canyon region of the lower Yampa and Green Rivers in [[Dinosaur National Monument]].
  2. The area around Lees Ferry, below Glen Canyon Dam.
  3. The Grand Canyon region.
- The cave itself functioned as a depositional site via human (Indigenous) activity and pack rat nest accumulation, preserving fish remains alongside bird remains (previously treated by Parmalee, 1969) at the archaeological site designated ARIZ. C:5:3.

## Key Findings

- Fish remains from Stanton's Cave represent **nearly all fish species native to this reach of the Colorado River**, providing a rare zooarchaeological snapshot of the pre-dam native fish community.
- Material recovered spans multiple contexts within the cave: antechamber 1 surface, pack rat nests near field stations (FS #3, FS #5, FS #14), the main room grid excavations, and a cave passageway — collected primarily in June 1969 by R.C. Euler's team (L. Powers, R. Page, and others).
- Remains are classified as **historical (non-fossilized)**, with estimated age up to ~4,000 years B.P., except for one set of five articulated fossil cyprinid vertebrae.
- The taxonomic status of *Gila cypha* as a valid full species had been questioned by taximetric analyses (Holden and Stalnaker, 1970), without resolution. This study proposes **skeletal morphology** (previously unexamined) as a promising character system for taxonomic resolution, using comparative material from *G. robusta*, *G. elegans*, and *G. cypha*.
- Diagnostic pectoral girdle features (ventral cleithral ridge, arm angles, arm length ratios) are identified as potentially useful for distinguishing *G. cypha* from *G. elegans*.
- The only expected native species absent from the assemblage is *[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]* (humpback sucker).
- Species identifications were made by R.R. Miller and G.R. Smith, Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan (1969, 1971).
