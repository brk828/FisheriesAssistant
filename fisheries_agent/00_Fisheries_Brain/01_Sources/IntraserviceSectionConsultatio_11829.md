# Intra-Service Section 7 Consultation on Colorado Squawfish Reintroduction

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #conservation-biology #reintroduction

## Executive Summary

This 1990 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biological opinion addresses a formal Section 7 consultation on the proposed reintroduction of [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) into the [[Colorado River]] mainstream between [[Headgate Rock Dam]] and [[Imperial Dam]] (approximately 130 miles) in Arizona and California. The species, listed as endangered in 1967, would be introduced as a nonessential experimental population under Section 10(j) of the Endangered Species Act. The Service concludes that stocking juvenile Colorado squawfish is not likely to jeopardize the species' continued existence and will promote conservation. The project represents a cooperative effort between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] to establish a fourth population and potentially develop a trophy fishery.

## Background & Description

[[Colorado Squawfish]] is the largest cyprinid in North America, historically reaching weights exceeding 36 kg and lengths of nearly 1.8 m, though specimens over 7 kg have been rare in recent times. The species is a voracious predator and the top native carnivore of the [[Colorado River]] system. Early common names included "white salmon," "Colorado salmon," or simply "salmon" due to its substantial size and migratory habits.

The species' evolutionary history spans the Miocene, Pliocene, and early Pleistocene epochs, marked by oscillations between pluvial and arid habitats. By the mid-Pliocene, Colorado squawfish had adapted to swift water, though fossil evidence indicates use of both lakes and rivers. Large size, great mobility, and spawning migrations represent adaptations to drier seasons when suitable spawning habitats were limited or far-removed from adult habitats.

**Life History Characteristics:**
- **Longevity**: Wild fish exceed 30 years; captive broodstock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] reach 16 years while remaining healthy and producing viable offspring
- **Habitat preferences by life stage**:
  - Young-of-the-year (up to 64 mm): shallow backwaters with little current over silt/sand bottoms
  - Juveniles (65-200 mm): shallow backwaters with minimal current
  - Sub-adults (200-400 mm): transition to deeper water with velocity
  - Adults: large-river fish in various depths and velocities over diverse substrates
- **Diet**: Zooplankton and insect larvae when young; become piscivorous at approximately 60 mm (Jacobi and Jacobi 1982 reported fish feeding at this size); diet almost exclusively fish by 200 mm total length. Adults feed on most native and introduced fishes, but are opportunistic (documented consuming jackrabbits, American coots, and Mormon crickets)
- **Thermal tolerance**: Broad range from 0°C (winter) to exceeding 35°C (summer), as evidenced by historical distribution from Yuma to upper basin

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical Decline Factors:**

The [[Colorado River]] changed dramatically during the 1900s with construction of more than 20 dams on the mainstream and tributaries since 1913. Multiple factors contributed to Colorado squawfish decline:

1. **Physical habitat modifications**:
   - Colder water temperatures directly downstream from reservoirs
   - Altered seasonal and daily discharge patterns
   - Lowered turbidity
   - Nutrient retention in reservoir plankton populations
   - Sediment trapping causing channel transformation from sand/silt to armored cobble/boulders
   - Reduced backwaters and sloughs used for nursery and resting areas
   - Replacement of natural flood/drought cycles with consistent discharges

2. **Nonnative fish introductions**: In Arizona, 28 freshwater and 3 saltwater native species have been joined by at least 60 introduced fish species since the turn of the century. Introduced fishes subjected Colorado squawfish to biological interactions to which they were poorly adapted due to previous isolation. Mortality from choking when preying on [[Channel Catfish]] has been documented.

**Current Distribution:**

At present, Colorado squawfish occur naturally in three locations, all in the Colorado River basin upstream from [[Glen Canyon Dam]]:
- [[Green River]]/[[Yampa River]] system
- Upper [[Colorado River]] mainstream
- [[San Juan River]]

A refugium/broodstock population is maintained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], Dexter, New Mexico. Experimental reestablishment efforts in the [[Verde River]] and [[Salt River]] sub-basins began in 1985, with early results encouraging.

**Project Site Description:**

The proposed reintroduction site is the Colorado River mainstream between [[Headgate Rock Dam]] (River mile 524.5) and [[Imperial Dam]] (River mile 657.2), spanning approximately 130 miles through La Paz and Yuma Counties, Arizona, and San Bernardino, Riverside, and Imperial Counties, California. [[Palo Verde Diversion Dam]] (River mile 568.8) restricts upstream fish movements but allows downstream passage. This relatively free-flowing section retains some riverine characteristics similar to the early 1900s, though modified for flood control and water supply.

The target area flows through approximately 40 miles of National Wildlife Refuge lands ([[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]).

## Key Findings

**Biological Opinion:**

The proposed stocking of juvenile Colorado squawfish into the mainstream Colorado River between Headgate Rock and Imperial Dams is **not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of Colorado squawfish** and **will promote the conservation of the species**.

**Project Design:**

- **Duration**: 10 consecutive years
- **Fish source**: Captive broodstock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (more than 400 individuals maintained; 1989 egg take exceeded 2 million eggs from 22 fish, producing more than 199,000 fry)
- **Stocking numbers**: Service will provide minimum 1.5 million fry annually (150,000 per year); [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] will rear up to 1 million fingerlings annually (100,000 per year) for stocking
- **No impact on wild stocks**: All fish from captive broodstock; no collection from wild populations
- **Goal**: Establish a trophy fishery with severely limited angler harvest until after stocked fish reach sexual maturity and have opportunity to spawn

**Recovery Plan Objectives Supported:**

The project addresses multiple Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan research/management activities:
- Refine life history/spawning and recruitment information (121)
- Assess inter-/intraspecific interactions (122)
- Develop aging techniques and determine age distribution/growth rates (123)
- Identify and assess nonnative species impacts (212)
- Identify areas for reintroduction/augmentation (321)
- Stock and monitor reintroduced populations (323)
- Assess sport fishery potential (422)

**Anticipated Incidental Take:**

Approximately 70% of yearly reintroduced fingerlings could be taken within one year of reintroduction, primarily through:
- Predation by predator fish (majority)
- Angler activity (primarily after fish reach adult size)
- Fish-eating birds

This take level is not judged to prevent adequate broodstock availability for recruitment needed to sustain a population.

**Reasonable and Prudent Measures:**

1. Authorized take not permitted until Colorado squawfish become sexually mature and have ample opportunity to reproduce
2. Angler education program including species identification and safe release techniques
3. Monitoring program to identify habitat requiring special management

**Listed Species Considerations:**

Three federally listed animal species occur in the area:
- [[Yuma Clapper Rail]]: No interaction expected (inhabits marsh areas)
- [[Bald Eagle]]: No impact expected (nesting sites not in proximity; no intensive feeding activity in stocking area)
- [[Sonoran Pronghorn]]: No impact expected (occurs considerable distances from introduction areas)

Colorado squawfish occupying [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] waters will be afforded full protection under the Endangered Species Act, requiring Section 7 consultation for refuge actions that may affect the species.

**Conservation Significance:**

If completely successful, this reestablishment would create a fourth population of Colorado squawfish in the basin, considerably reducing extinction threat. The project provides critical information on feasibility of reestablishing a top native predator in modified environments with controlled stream flows and established exotic fish communities. Innovative management as a sportfish supported by the recreational fishing community could ensure long-term species conservation.
