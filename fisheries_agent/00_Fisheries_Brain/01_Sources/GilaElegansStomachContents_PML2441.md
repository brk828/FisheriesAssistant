# Gila elegans Stomach Contents and Growth in Refuge Stock Tanks

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This 1987 ZOL 590 report by James A. Starr examines the feeding ecology and growth of the endangered [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) held in three stock tanks at the [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] in Arizona. The study was motivated by sharp population declines of native fishes in the [[Colorado River Basin]], where habitat loss, competition and predation by non-native species, and reduced recruitment are major threats. Stomach contents were analyzed from fish captured in January and February 1987. The bonytail diet was broad, including both plant and animal material, with no detectable size-related differences. Both species showed measurable growth over the study period. The author concludes that the bonytail is an opportunistic feeder, and that food selection should not limit reintroduction efforts.

## Background & Description

- **Species of concern:** The bonytail (*Gila elegans*) and razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) are native Colorado River fishes that were once abundant throughout the basin. Both are now threatened with extinction.
- **Threats cited:** Loss of suitable habitat, competition and predation by non-native species, and the effects of dam construction (including the Glen Canyon Dam reservoir), which altered natural flows and reduced recruitment.
- **Status:** The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team (1984) reported that bonytail are near extinction, and that four self-sustaining populations must be established before the species' status can be downgraded.
- **Knowledge gap:** Natural population studies were incomplete, and information on food habits was inadequate. Starr argues that diet research is needed to support a comprehensive management plan.
- **Study site:** Three stock tanks at the Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge, located roughly 20 miles north of the Mexican border area (the exact distance is partly illegible in the scan). The tanks were selected by a committee of local fish and wildlife officials and professors from Arizona State University. They were named Road Camp, Big Bertha, and Kings Tank.
- **Stocking:** Each tank was stocked in fall 1986 with approximately 1,500-plus fish (the exact count is illegible in the scan). Fish were sourced from a national fish hatchery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods:** Fish were collected with trammel nets, seines, bag seines, and gill nets. Captured fish were measured for standard length, total length, and weight, then returned to their tanks. Stomachs were removed from a subset of fish (when possible) from each tank. The entire digestive tract was preserved, and the contents were analyzed in the laboratory. Samples were stored in individual vials at Arizona State University.

**Diet composition:** Plant and animal material both occurred in the stomachs. Animal prey included aquatic insect larvae (Chironomidae and other Diptera), Planorbidae snails, cladocerans, corixids (Corixidae), Odonata, Hemiptera, and fragments of the Asian clam *Corbicula*. Seed pods were also present. Animal tissue occurred at nearly equal frequency across the size classes, with slightly higher occurrence in the larger size group. Plant material was also found in the stomachs. The percentages of stomachs containing each category are garbled in the scan, so they are not reported here. Table 1 lists stomach contents for the Buenos Aires Refuge, January–February 1987, across the Road Camp, Big Bertha, and Kings Tank sites.

**Digestion:** Analysis showed that digestion occurred quickly. Prey items were largely digested by the time fish were preserved, except when specimens were placed in formalin immediately after capture, which preserved stomach contents in a more intact state.

## Key Findings

1. **Broad diet:** *Gila elegans* feeds on a wide variety of plant and animal material. Starr concludes it is an opportunistic feeder that does not restrict its food selection.
2. **No size-related diet shift:** No differences were detected in stomach contents between bonytail above and below the study's size threshold (the threshold values are illegible in the scan).
3. **Animal prey:** Aquatic insect larvae, snails, cladocerans, corixids, and *Corbicula* fragments were the main animal prey items identified.
4. **Growth in razorback sucker:** Average weight rose from roughly 23.9 g to 64.7 g, and average total length increased from 140 mm to 166.4 mm over the study period.
5. **Growth in bonytail:** Average length increased from 76 mm to 106.8 mm, and average weight rose from 6.2 g to 10.0 g.
6. **Sample data:** At Kings Tank (31 January 1987), a seine and gill-net combined sample yielded 91 bonytail (mean total length 105.3 mm, mean weight 10.0 g). All bonytail captured across the study totaled 152 individuals (mean total length 100.9 mm, mean weight 9.5 g). Road Camp tank samples (1 February 1987) were also recorded, with mean total length 98.1 mm and mean weight 14.0 g.
7. **Conservation implication:** Because the bonytail's diet is broad and its food habits are flexible, food availability is unlikely to limit reintroduction success. Survival in an environment with abundant predators and competitors is the more pressing concern.
8. **Study limitations:** Starr notes that qualitative observations about capture methods, fish location within the tanks, and tank conditions were also collected, though these are not quantified.
