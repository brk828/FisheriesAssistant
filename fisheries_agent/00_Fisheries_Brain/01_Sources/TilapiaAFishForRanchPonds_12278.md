# Tilapia for Arizona Ranch Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

W. J. McConnell, associate professor of Wildlife Management and unit leader of the Arizona Cooperative Fishery Unit, evaluates whether a sterile hybrid tilapia from [[Malacca Tilapia Hybrid]] could support a new agricultural enterprise in southern Arizona. The hybrid, produced at the Tropical Fish Culture Research Institute in Malacca, Malaysia, is described as superior to native pond fishes in growth, cost of production and table quality. Because only males result from the cross, the fish cannot overpopulate ponds. The article presents Tucson-area pond trials from 1963 and 1964 and outlines possible commercial, recreational and family uses.

## Background & Description

- **Parent stock:** The pure African species [[Tilapia Mossambica]] is noted for rapid growth on inexpensive rations and excellent table quality, but its rapid breeding produces dense populations that suppress growth.
- **Hybrid origin:** The Malacca hybrid results from mating two closely related races. Only males are produced, so the hybrid cannot reproduce, which the author states assures complete population control for the fish culturist.
- **Acquisition:** Both parent stocks were obtained by the [[Cooperative Wildlife Research Unit]] of the [[University Of Arizona]] in July 1962.
- **Publication:** Reprinted from *Progressive Agriculture in Arizona*, Vol. XVII, No. 2, pp. 3-4 (March-April 1965), College of Agriculture, University of Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Feeding:** Tilapia supplement low-cost diets efficiently with large quantities of algae. The author states that no native pond fish can equal this combination of total production and individual growth on low-cost diets, because none use algae and cottonseed meal effectively.
- **Winter mortality:** Tilapia do not winter in relatively cold water and die off completely each winter. Each spring's planted population therefore avoids competition with older fish from the previous season, unlike most native species, which require eradication and restocking.
- **Age structure:** The author cites evidence that populations gain weight faster when composed of even-aged individuals, which explains high yields in new ponds.
- **Setting:** The desert regions of southern Arizona, where recreational fishing near population centers is scarce, are identified as the key market setting.
- **Comparison:** Tilapia hybrids are described as much superior to the [[Bluegill]] as a farm and ranch pond fish.

## Key Findings

**Pond trial results (Tucson area, 1963-1964)**
- Fry performed in ponds as they had in Malacca. No females appeared among several hundred adult hybrids examined.
- Unfed hybrids stocked as 0.75 inch fry on May 1 at 600 fry per acre reached 6 oz by September 1 and 9 oz by November. Survival in hybrid-only ponds was about 75 percent.
- With 1 inch fingerlings stocked in mid-April at 2000 per acre and fed cottonseed meal pellets at 3 percent of body weight per day, fish reached 4 oz by mid-July, 8 oz by September, and many 10-12 oz individuals by October.

**Economics**
- Hatchery fingerling production costs are at least as low as for native varieties amenable to hatchery production.
- Cottonseed pellets cost 30 to 40 percent of usual pelleted pond fish feeds.
- The author states it appears possible to raise 1000 pounds of hybrids per acre per season to harvestable size (4 to 12 oz) at 20 to 30 cents per pound of live weight, with further cost reductions possible at large scale.

**Proposed enterprises**
- **Recreational fishing sales:** Considered the most lucrative option, especially in desert regions near population centers.
- **Retail fish sales:** Tilapia flesh resembles [[Channel Catfish]] in texture and taste, which has steady retail demand. This may benefit regions remote from fresh fish sources.
- **Family and dude ranch use:** At low stocking densities requiring no feeding, the hybrid, alone or with channel catfish, is proposed as a superior pond fish for family tables and recreation.
- **Fingerling supply:** A commercial hatchery producing hybrid fingerlings, analogous to poultry chick production, could be a restricted but profitable industry.
- **Professional management:** Arizona real estate developers, country clubs and housing developments may need professional fish management services.

**Conclusion**
- The author concludes that fish culture is unlikely to become a major Arizona industry but has definite potential as a specialty enterprise based on modest, steady demand.
