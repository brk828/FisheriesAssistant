## Executive Summary
This document is a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service letter (dated August 14, 1995) from the Arizona Ecological Services State Office to Dr. W.L. Minckley, Team Leader of the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]], detailing a near-extirpation event affecting the [[Gila Topminnow]] population at Mud Springs (Site #18) in the [[Tonto National Forest]]. The population, originally established via a 1982 introduction, had persisted in cement cattle troughs from 1987–1995 but was found nearly desiccated in August 1995 due to spring box/pipeline failure combined with heavy cattle use. Only five fish (reportedly all female) were observed before the site went dry, and none were recovered during a subsequent rescue attempt. The letter outlines planned habitat renovation and a supplemental restocking effort for 1996.

## Background & Description
- No detailed morphometric data provided in this document; it is a management/status correspondence rather than a taxonomic description.
- Sex identification noted qualitatively: on-site observers (Jeff Burgess and Russ Orr) visually classified all five recovered individuals as **females**, though no confirmatory morphological criteria (e.g., gonopodium absence, body shape) were described in the text.
- Species identity confirmed as [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), a small-bodied live-bearing poeciliid characteristic of desert spring systems in the American Southwest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Artificial cement trough system fed by a natural desert spring, supplemented by a springbox and pipeline infrastructure — an anthropogenically modified microhabitat within a broader arid spring ecosystem.
- **Population history:** Fish introduced in 1982; a secondary population established itself within the trough system and was documented from 1987–1995, indicating that translocated stock can persist for multi-year periods in low-flow, human-maintained habitats.
- **Environmental stressors identified:**
  - Reduced water levels caused by a malfunctioning springbox or supply pipe.
  - Heavy cattle traffic/use degrading available aquatic habitat and likely contributing to water loss and sedimentation.
  - Minimal vegetative cover within the trough, limiting refuge availability during low-water conditions.
- **Co-occurring pressures:** Livestock grazing and water extraction represent a direct human-wildlife habitat conflict typical of desert spring conservation sites managed for multiple uses (cattle + endangered fish refugia).
- **Trophic/microhabitat notes:** The species relied on limited standing water, sediment, and sparse vegetation within a constructed trough — indicating minimal habitat complexity was sufficient for short-term persistence but insufficient during acute water-level decline.

## Key Findings
- **Population collapse event:** By August 5, 1995, the trough was nearly dry, with only a small puddle remaining; five [[Gila Topminnow]] (believed all female) were observed, suggesting a heavily skewed or unrepresentative surviving subgroup.
- **Rapid habitat decline:** Water levels dropped from ~3–4 inches (Aug 7) in the upper trough to no observable fish (Aug 8) within 48 hours, with the lower trough holding only ~1.5 inches of water.
- **Failed rescue attempt:** A coordinated search by Arizona Game and Fish Department and Tonto National Forest personnel (Lisa Bizios, Scott Zalaznik, Doug Duncan) found **no live or dead topminnow**, despite examining vegetation and bottom sediments in both trough sections — suggesting complete loss of the observed individuals (via desiccation, predation, or undetected mortality).
- **Root cause identified:** Failure/malfunction of the springbox or pipeline, compounded by heavy cattle use, prevented water levels from being maintained at historic norms.
- **Timing issue:** Site renovation, already scheduled, was determined to be approximately **six weeks too late** to prevent this population decline.
- **Planned remediation (as of 1995):**
  - New springbox and pipeline installation beginning September 1995.
  - Creation of "potholes" to capture natural spring flow for topminnow habitat, separate from cattle-only troughs.
  - A supplemental release of Gila topminnow planned for summer 1996, with source stock to be determined collaboratively by the Fish and Wildlife Service, [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], Tonto National Forest, and the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]].
- **Conservation implication:** This case illustrates the vulnerability of small, isolated introduced populations of endangered desert fishes to infrastructure failure and habitat competition with livestock, reinforcing the importance of habitat separation (cattle vs. fish refugia) in future recovery planning for the [[Gila Topminnow]] subspecies.
