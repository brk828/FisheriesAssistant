# Chromosome Study of the Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This short 1969 methods paper reports an attempt to determine the chromosome number of the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), a small desert fish whose range has shrunk to a few isolated springs and ponds. The author did not attempt to interpret the number or construct a [[Karyotype]]. Two squash-preparation methods were tested. The first, adapted from [[Scheel Squash Technique]] work on Rivulinae, produced no usable results. The second, a modified [[Lee Bone Marrow Technique]] applied to testes, produced chromosome spreads on only two of twelve slides. From one photographed fish of the [[Monkey Spring Pond]] population, the author concluded a diploid number of 44–46, with a most frequent haploid (N) count of 23. The author stressed that this sample is too small to be conclusive.

## Background & Description

The paper argues that chromosome numbers, combined with known geographic distributions, can shed light on how related fish groups expanded across the landscape. Most published fish chromosome techniques, such as the [[Goldfish Cytogenetics]] work of Ojima and colleagues (1966), were designed for much larger fish than pupfish, so the author tried a technique developed for small cyprinodontids.

*Cyprinodon macularius* was once widespread in small, widely separated desert water bodies across the lower [[Gila River Basin]] and the [[Salton Sea]] area. Its range is now restricted. Two remnant Arizona populations survive: one at [[Quitovaquito Spring]] in [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]] and one in the pond fed by [[Monkey Spring]]. The author suggests each isolated population has diverged from the historical form, so chromosome studies of each could clarify their relationships.

Breeding behavior at Monkey Spring Pond was observed from March through September. Males were described as dark sky-blue, with blackened dorsal and anal fins and little of the orange or yellow caudal pigmentation reported for fish from other areas (Miller, 1943). The author links this colour difference to the discussion of population relationships. Earlier ecological and behavioural work is cited from [[Miller 1943 Taxonomic Review]], [[Barlow Behavioral Studies]] (1958, 1961), and [[Cowles 1934 Ecology]].

A comparison species, *Cyprinodon nevadensis* ([[Amargosa Pupfish]]), was photographed during dissection to illustrate testis removal. Its testes were several millimetres longer than those of *C. macularius*, which measured only 1.5–3.0 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The desert pupfish lives in small, often thermally and chemically variable pools. The author notes that these fish respond to water temperature, water chemistry, feeding, and possibly time of day, all of which could affect cell division and therefore the chance of obtaining dividing cells. Captive fish held at [[Arizona State University]] in a compound pond may not have experienced conditions matching their native habitat. This may have reduced the number of actively dividing cells in the testes.

The author also points out that the [[Testes Tissue]] used contained little material, and that fish were sacrificed from a population considered rare and restricted. These constraints limited sample size and experimental replication.

## Key Findings

**Methods tested**

- **Scheel squash technique (modified):** Testes were expanded in diluted (hypotonic) Ringer's solution, fixed and stained with 0.3–0.4% orcein in 50% acetic acid, then squashed. Two male *C. macularius* were used, and the results were negative. The author attributes the failure to several possible factors, including synthetic rather than natural orcein, fish that were beheaded rather than narcotized, substitution of amphibian Ringer's for fish Ringer's, and the author's inexperience with squash preparations. The method was considered advantageous for small tissue samples and simple to perform, but it sometimes produced overlapping chromosome fields.
- **Modified Lee technique:** Testes were teased in 1% sodium citrate, fixed in acetic acid and methanol (1:3), and dropped onto wet, pre-cooled slides using the "blaze dry" method. The technique was applied without a mitotic inhibitor. Slides were stained with Wright's stain and mounted in Permamount. Spreads were recorded by mechanical stage coordinates and photographed with Plus-X film at a 4-second exposure.

**Sample and results**

- Twelve fish were processed to make twelve slides. Slides 1–10 were *C. macularius*; slides 11–12 were an unidentified species.
- Only two of the twelve slides showed chromosome spreads. Both had been incubated as specified in the protocol step, while a few others had not been.
- Only one slide was photographed, so the count rests on a single fish from the Monkey Spring Pond population.
- The author concluded the **diploid number is 44–46**.
- The **most frequent haploid (N) number is 23**. The author describes this as not conclusive given the small sample.
- Photographs of the spreads showed 2N = 44, 2N = 46, and N = 23 counts. The author notes that some counts involved subjective judgement, and that one or more chromosomes were sometimes missing from the field.

**Discussion and recommendations**

- Losses of cells during decanting, and cell adherence to non-siliconized glassware, were the most likely causes of empty slides.
- The males used showed no strong breeding coloration and had only slightly darker fins than females, despite the sampling occurring within the breeding season.
- Examining gill and spleen tissue, which requires a mitotic inhibitor, might give better results.
- The author expected that, with further information and experience, Scheel's squash technique would be useful.
- The chromosome images were judged comparable to, or better than, some published examples, though they lacked the distinctness of mammalian preparations.
- Future work should confirm the count with more fish from the Monkey Spring population and extend it to other populations, to support evolutionary comparisons.
