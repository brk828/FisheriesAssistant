# Lakesucker Life History, Status, and Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reviews the life history, age structure, current status, and management options for four long-lived lakesucker species of western North America: [[Cui-Ui]], [[June Sucker]], [[Shortnose Sucker]], and [[Lost River Sucker]]. All four are lake-dwelling catostomids that migrate into streams to spawn. Their populations have collapsed through water diversion, habitat alteration, water-quality decline, non-native species, and [[Hybridization]]. Age-structure analysis of opercular bones shows that these fish have persisted through long periods of failed recruitment because of exceptional longevity. Of the four species originally present, the authors conclude that one has been lost to hybridization, two are at clear risk of extinction, and only the cui-ui has had significant recent reproduction. The outlook is described as bleak.

## Background & Description

The lakesuckers are the specialized members of the genera *Chasmistes* and *Deltistes*. They were most abundant and widespread during the Pliocene and Pleistocene, when they inhabited many lakes of the [[Great Basin]] and [[Lake Bonneville]]. Desiccation since the last pluvial period has left only isolated populations. Diagnostic features include branched gill rakers and a terminal mouth in *Chasmistes*, and triangular gill rakers and a more ventral mouth in *Deltistes*. The terminal mouth is presumed to be an adaptation for feeding on zooplankton.

Shared life-history traits include:
- Late maturity, with first spawning at about 5–10 years of age
- Long lifespans
- Obligatory stream spawning in cool, fast water over gravel or cobble
- Larvae that drift quickly into lake habitats after swim-up

Opercular bones provide a reliable means of aging. Annuli form from dense, transparent winter growth and opaque summer growth, and the spacing of annuli shows when somatic growth slows at first reproduction. The method was validated for cui-ui over 8 years (see [[Opercular Aging]]). The study examined more than 1,000 cui-ui from spawning runs (1979–1987), along with samples of the other three species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Cui-ui (Pyramid Lake, NV).** Cui-ui spawned in the Truckee River, the only perennial tributary of [[Pyramid Lake]]. Spawning runs began in early April to mid-May and ended in early to mid-June, at river temperatures of 9–17 °C. Spawning occurred in fast water (mean 50 cm/s) at depths of 21–140 cm over gravel. Hatching success was highest below 17 °C. Eggs hatched in about 10 days, and larvae swam up after roughly 4 more. Historically, cui-ui spawned in the lower 70 km of the Truckee River. Radio-tagged fish now migrate less than 10 km and remain in the river for 4 to 16.5 days. The [[Newlands Project]] (completed 1906) diverts about 50% of the Truckee flow. Pyramid Lake has dropped by up to 25 m since 1906, and [[Winnemucca Lake]] dried by 1938.

**June sucker (Utah Lake, UT).** [[Utah Lake]] is operated as an irrigation reservoir. It is increasingly eutrophic and saline, and introduced fishes have largely replaced the native fauna. In 1981, common carp made up nearly 93% of fish biomass. Spawning occurs in the [[Provo River]], which has been reduced to 6.1 km of usable spawning habitat.

**Lost River and shortnose suckers (Klamath Basin, OR/CA).** [[Upper Klamath Lake]] has been affected by the [[Link Dam]] (1912), which converted it to an irrigation reservoir. Sedimentation and nutrient loading have increased, and summer blooms of *Aphanizomenon* push pH to about 10.5. Local dissolved-oxygen depletions occur when populations crash. [[Chiloquin Dam]] (1928) blocks access to spawning habitat, and the spawning reach below it is only 1.6 km long. Introduced [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] have also become abundant.

**Common threats across species.** These include:
- Water diversion and reduced downstream flows
- Degraded water quality from agricultural and municipal use
- Non-native species, including the [[Sacramento Perch]] in Pyramid Lake
- Hybridization and introgression
- Historical overfishing
- Predation by [[American White Pelican]] on spawning fish

## Key Findings

**Longevity and age structure**
- Cui-ui, June suckers, and Lost River suckers can live more than 40 years. Shortnose suckers live more than 30 years. The oldest cui-ui opercles were aged to 41 years.
- The authors argue that exceptional longevity allowed lakesuckers to persist through recruitment failures of 10–20 years.
- Radant et al. (June sucker) aged 18 fish collected in 1984; they ranged from 24 to 42 years.

**Cui-ui**
- Cui-ui first spawn at about 6 years (360–380 mm FL), but most do not spawn until 8–12 years (460–520 mm FL).
- The sex ratio shifted with age: 1.3 females per male at 13 years, 2.0 at 19 years, and 5.0 over 30 years.
- Recruitment from 1950 to 1969 was essentially absent. In the 1983 prespawning aggregate, about 92% were from the 1969 year class.
- Hatchery releases of about 8 million larvae occurred between 1980 and 1987. Since 1983, juvenile cui-ui (100–950 mm FL) have been collected, and several new age classes have appeared in the run.
- The authors state that the positive signs are "not yet indications that the cui-ui is adequately protected." Impacts of current management will not be known for another decade.

**Management actions for cui-ui**
- [[Marble Bluff Dam]] (1976) and a 5.1 km bypass channel with four fish ladders allow fish to reach the river above the delta. The channel maintains 1.1 m³/s of flow during spawning.
- Fish are trapped at the dam; 100–300 females are spawned artificially per year.
- The [[Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe]] operates a hatchery at Sutcliffe.
- Management needs include maintaining Truckee River flows, improving water quality, and resolving water-rights disputes.

**Lost River and shortnose suckers**
- Lost River suckers apparently reproduced in few recent years. In 1986, 95% of the fish sampled were 19–30 years old.
- Fecundity for Lost River suckers ranges from about 101,000 to 235,000 eggs per female.
- Shortnose suckers are the smallest of the four species. Roughly one-third of shortnose suckers in Upper Klamath Lake are hybrids, and about 2,000 suspected pure individuals were estimated in the 1985 run.
- Copco Reservoir shortnose suckers ranged from 16 to 33 years of age. The oldest recorded individual was a 33-year-old hybrid collected in 1987.
- The Klamath Basin Interagency Working Group was formed in 1986 to protect Lost River and shortnose suckers.
- The Klamath Tribe has developed a hatchery as a short-term measure.

**June sucker**
- The population is fewer than 1,000 individuals and is considered "very close to extinction." No genetically pure June suckers are thought to exist. The introgressed form was described as a new subspecies (*C. l. mccullochi*) by Miller and Smith (1981).
- Mass mortality occurred when Utah Lake was nearly drained during drought and the fish froze.
- A 1984 management plan recommended hatchery propagation. Juveniles were stocked into [[Camp Creek Reservoir]] in 1987, which has only about 2 ha of surface area.

**Recommended actions**
- Removal or modification of Chiloquin Dam is considered the most effective step for Klamath suckers. The authors estimate this could enlarge potential spawning habitat by at least a factor of fifty.
- Reducing nutrient loading and investigating the hypereutrophic condition of Upper Klamath Lake are priorities.
- Water from [[Stampede Reservoir]] should remain committed to fisheries use, as the cui-ui and [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] both depend on it.
- Intensive hatchery propagation is needed to avert extinction of June suckers.

**Conclusion**
The authors state that "of the 4 species originally present, one has disappeared through hybridization, two are clearly at risk of extinction, and only one has had significant successful reproduction in the last two decades." They warn that without commitment to preservation, lakesuckers "are truly a group in peril of vanishing slowly as we watch."
