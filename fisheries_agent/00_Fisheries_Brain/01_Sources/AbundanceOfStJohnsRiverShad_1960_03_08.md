# Abundance of St. Johns River Shad

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary
This 1960 study by Charles H. Walburg (Bureau of Commercial Fisheries) examines the population dynamics of the [[American Shad]] (*Alosa sapidissima*) in the [[St. Johns River]], Florida — the southernmost spawning population of this species on the Atlantic Coast. Part of a coastwise investigation sponsored by the [[Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission]] beginning in 1950, the research used tagging and catch/effort data (1953–1959) to estimate population size, fishing mortality, and spawning escapement. Results showed that both catch and population size generally increased during the study period while fishing effort decreased, though causal factors driving abundance fluctuations remained undetermined due to the short time series.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: [[American Shad]] (*Alosa sapidissima*), family [[Clupeidae]]
- **Size at river-egress**: Juveniles leave rivers for the ocean at 3–6 inches in length (typically in fall of their first year)
- **Fecundity**: Females produce 116,000–660,000 eggs, varying with length, weight, and age
- **Egg characteristics**: Eggs are demersal (non-adhesive), deposited loosely in water, absorb water after fertilization and sink to the river bottom, where they are transported by current
- **Incubation**: Hatching occurs in 6–8 days, dependent on water temperature
- **Age determination**: Age and spawning frequency determinable via scale analysis (per [[Cating 1953]])
- **Tagging method**: Petersen disk tags inserted directly below the dorsal fin origin; tagging mortality considered negligible when only vigorous fish were tagged

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Life history**: [[Anadromous Fish|Anadromous]] species — spends most of life at sea, returns to freshwater rivers to spawn
- **Range**: Atlantic Coast from the St. Lawrence River (Canada) to the St. Johns River (Florida) — the southern range limit
- **Spawning migration timing**: Begins as early as November in Florida, as late as June in Canada (latitudinal cline)
- **Post-spawning survival**: Populations north of [[Cape Hatteras]] survive spawning and undertake repeat (iteroparous) spawning migrations in subsequent years; populations south of Cape Hatteras (including St. Johns River) are semelparous — die after spawning
- **Juvenile habitat**: Remain in natal river until fall before entering ocean
- **Adult marine habitat**: Summer/fall in the [[Gulf of Maine]]; winters in deep water off the Middle and South Atlantic Coast
- **Sexual maturity**: Reached in 3–5 years, after which most individuals return to their natal stream (homing behavior)
- **River system**: St. Johns River flows ~285 miles, originating ~50 miles north of Lake Okeechobee, flowing north through shallow lakes to Jacksonville then east to the ocean
- **Fishing gear/zones**:
  - Anchor-gill nets: near river mouth (ocean-adjacent)
  - Drift-gill nets: Jacksonville and Palatka areas
  - Haul seines ("shad nets"): between Palatka and Welaka
  - Recreational hook-and-line: between Lakes Monroe and Harney (Dec–April, peak mid-Jan to mid-March)

## Key Findings
- **Historical catch trends**: Commercial fishing began in 1858 near Mayport. Catch records (from 1880) show a peak of 2,833,000 lbs in 1908, declining with fluctuations to a low of 203,000 lbs in 1952.
- **1958 Tagging Study**:
  - 950 shad tagged with Petersen disk tags during the haul-seine fishing season
  - 97 tags recovered by the gill-net fishery (subtracted from total, leaving 853 tags available to haul-seine fishery)
  - 108 tagged fish recovered among 344,000 lbs caught by haul seines
  - Calculated fishing rate: 12.7% (108/853)
  - Estimated run size available to haul-seine fishery: 2,709,000 lbs (95% CI: 2,223,000–3,260,000 lbs, per [[Chapman 1948]] method)
  - No tagged fish recovered outside the river, supporting assumption that downstream-moving tagged fish eventually resumed upstream spawning migration
- **Population estimates (1953–1959)**: Using [[Talbot 1954]] methodology, both total population size and catch generally increased over the study period, while fishing effort generally decreased
- **Sport fishery growth**: Began in 1942; by 1958 became the largest recreational shad fishery on the Atlantic Coast. 1958 creel census: ~65,000 fish (~175,000 lbs)
- **Sport catch trend**: Increased from 82,000 lbs (1953) to 161,000 lbs (1959)
- **Total catch trend**: Increased from 362,000 lbs (1953) to 785,000 lbs (1959)
- **Spawning escapement trend**: Increased from 627,000 lbs (1953) to 2,169,000 lbs (1959)
- **Annual fishing mortality rate**: Decreased from 37% (1953) to a low of 15% (1957), then rose to 26% (1959)
- **Management implications**: Optimum sustained yield management not yet feasible — insufficient years of population data (only 7 years) to identify causal factors (fishing, pollution, water temperature, stream flow, predation, competition) driving abundance fluctuations. Longer-term monitoring recommended to develop testable hypotheses, analogous to successful management models used for [[Pacific Halibut]] and [[Sockeye Salmon]] on the West Coast, and shad populations in the [[Hudson River]] and [[Connecticut River]].
- **Comparative context**: St. Johns River shad, unlike northern populations, are semelparous (die after spawning), differentiating management considerations from northern stocks studied by [[Talbot and Sykes 1958]].
