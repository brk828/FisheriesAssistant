# Spawning Behavior in Seven Species of Darters (Pisces: Percidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Brimleyana research paper, by [[Lawrence M. Page]], [[Michael E. Retzer]] and [[Robert A. Stiles]], documents egg-deposition behavior in darters (family Percidae) from eastern North American streams. Observations on six species, [[Percina Evides]], [[Etheostoma Duryi]], [[Etheostoma Asprigene]], [[Etheostoma Barbouri]], [[Etheostoma Aquali]] and [[Etheostoma Microlepidum]], plus more detailed data on [[Etheostoma Chlorosomum]], are presented. The authors classify darter spawning into four modes: burying, attaching, clumping and clustering eggs. Egg-deposition sites are now known for 57 darter species.

## Background & Description

Darters are small percids of freshwater streams. Their reproductive strategies vary by subgenus, and the paper focuses on the subgenus [[Nothonotus]], which is the only darter subgenus in which some species bury their eggs and others clump them.

The four spawning modes defined in the paper are:
1. **Burying:** eggs are placed in the substrate and abandoned.
2. **Attaching:** eggs are spread over a relatively large area on objects above the substrate (usually plants or rocks) and abandoned.
3. **Clumping:** eggs are massed at the interface between a slanted stone and the stream bottom and guarded until hatching.
4. **Clustering:** eggs are placed on the underside of a stone or log and guarded until hatching.

Prior work cited includes [[Winn 1958]], who discussed these behavioural modes, and [[Page In Press]], which summarizes spawning data for 48 species. Additional studies covered [[Etheostoma Longimanum]], [[Etheostoma Obeyense]] and [[Etheostoma Coosae]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Percina evides (Little River, Tennessee):** Spawning occurred at 17–20 °C over sand and gravel interspersed with cobble and boulders, in 30–60 cm of water in the upper parts of riffles, at current speeds of 0.20–0.61 m/s.
- **Etheostoma duryi (Butler and Factory creeks, Alabama and Tennessee):** Inhabits runs and pools with moderate current and rocky substrate. Spawning was observed on seven dates between early March and late May.
- **Etheostoma chlorosomum (East Fork Kaskaskia River, Illinois):** Captured in a sand-bottom pool at 22 °C, maximum depth 80 cm, near a large accumulation of logs and plant debris. The stream is mostly long pools with some gravel riffles.
- **Etheostoma asprigene (Cane Creek, Illinois):** Captured in fairly fast water below a rip-rap riffle at 20 °C. The stream is largely sluggish with clay and sand substrates.
- **Etheostoma barbouri (Pettys Fork, Kentucky):** Nests found in a large slab-rock pool, one at about 50 cm depth.
- **Etheostoma aquali (Buffalo River, Tennessee):** Four nests in a large rubble riffle; swift water at 22 °C, averaging 30 cm deep.
- **Etheostoma microlepidum (East Fork Stones River, Tennessee):** One nest in a moderately fast riffle of large gravel and small rubble, 15 cm deep, at 21 °C.

## Key Findings

- **Reproductive mode classification:** *Percina evides* is an egg-burier. *Etheostoma duryi* attaches eggs to rocks. *E. asprigene* and *E. chlorosomum* attach eggs to plants. *E. aquali* and *E. microlepidum* are egg-clumpers. *E. barbouri* is an egg-clusterer.
- **Subgenus Nothonotus:** Three species are now known to be egg-clumpers (*E. maculatum*, *E. aquali*, *E. microlepidum*) and three to be egg-buriers.
- **Species coverage:** Egg-deposition sites are known for 57 darter species, as tabulated in Table 1 in four categories (buriers, attachers, clumpers and clusterers).
- **Percina evides courtship:** Prespawning involved the female examining the substrate, followed by the male. The female's genital region pushed into the substrate during spawning, and she rested for up to three minutes afterwards. Males performed head-to-tail maneuvers that may have induced the female to turn around; this was observed in only two of four courting pairs, and a definitive explanation awaits further observation.
- **Etheostoma duryi:** Eggs are laid individually in slight depressions on rock sides or tops. Reproductive behaviour is essentially the same as that of the closely related [[Etheostoma Simoterum]]. Vibration appeared less intense than in *E. simoterum*, though this needs cinematographic verification.
- **Etheostoma chlorosomum (aquarium study):** Three males and three ripe females (40–50 mm SL) were held in a 40 L aquarium at 23 °C. Spawning began after feeding on live cladocerans and continued from 1230 to 1700 hr. Eggs (about 1 mm diameter) were laid 1 to 3 at a time and attached mainly to a twig (3 mm diameter), with others on algae, dead leaves and a log.
- **Etheostoma asprigene (aquarium study):** Five breeding fish (2 males, 3 females, 35–50 mm SL) spawned in a 40 L aquarium. Eggs were attached to glass corners 15–25 cm above the substrate and later to a crabgrass plant, but none were buried or clustered under stones. The authors conclude the species is an egg-attacher, with eggs likely attached to living or dead plants in nature.
- **Etheostoma barbouri:** Two nests were found on undersides of flat stones, one with 70 eggs (2.1 mm diameter) guarded by a male, and another with 42 eggs. This is the last of ten described *Catonotus* species confirmed as egg-clustering.
- **Etheostoma aquali:** Four nests were found in Buffalo River. One preserved nest held 551 eggs averaging 1.8 mm in diameter, with eggs at multiple developmental stages, indicating more than one spawn. In an 80 L aquarium at 24 °C, a male guarded three small clumps in the interface between the stone and gravel. Eggs appear to be laid with the female wedged between rock and substrate. Six eggs from a clump hatched after 3–4 days at 24–28 °C, with hatchlings 6.5 mm TL.
- **Etheostoma microlepidum:** One nest contained an estimated 346 eggs (2.0 mm diameter) in a multi-layer clump like those of *E. maculatum* and *E. aquali*.
- **Evolutionary inference:** Because *E. maculatum*, *E. aquali* and *E. microlepidum* are closely related, all three were predicted to be egg-clumpers, and the prediction was confirmed. The authors suggest that confirming clumping in *E. acuticeps*, *E. moorei* and *E. rubrum* would support their shared ancestry with *E. maculatum*.
- **Comparative clutch architecture:** Single-layer clusters occur in *Boleosoma*, *Catonotus* and the minnow *Pimephales*, while multi-layer clumps occur in *Nothonotus*, madtoms (*Noturus*) and some sculpins (*Cottus*). The selective factors separating these types remain unknown.
