# Artificial Propagation of Brook Trout and Rainbow Trout, with Notes on Three Other Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This revised 1939 Bureau of Fisheries document (Appendix VI to the 1923 Report of the U.S. Commissioner of Fisheries, by Glen C. Leach, Chief, Division of Fish Culture) is a practical manual on the [[Artificial Propagation]] of [[Brook Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], with shorter sections on [[Black-Spotted Trout]], [[Loch Leven Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]. It covers hatchery siting, water supply and aeration, hydraulic calculations, egg-taking, incubation, egg and fry shipping, rearing, brood stock management, distribution, and fish diseases. The methods are presented as general principles drawn from the author's experience, with details left to local conditions.

## Background & Description

The brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) is described as one of the most beautiful and widely distributed American trouts, belonging to the charr group marked by crimson spots. Its body is typically one-fourth to one-fifth of its length in depth, with about 230 scales in the lateral series, and dorsal and anal rays of 10 and 9. Color varies with sex, age, and locality; fish from clear, sandy waters are slender and silvery, while those from muddy, vegetated water are stout and dark.

The rainbow trout (*Salmo shasta*) is native to tributaries of the Sacramento River, particularly the McCloud River, California. It is described as shorter and deeper than other *Salmo* species, with 20 scale rows above and below the lateral line in the typical form, and it was introduced to eastern waters by the U.S. Fish Commission in 1880.

The document also covers the black-spotted or cutthroat trout (*Salmo lewisi*) of Yellowstone National Park, the non-native [[Loch Leven Trout]] (*Salmo levenensis*) from Scotland, and the [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo fario*), which was introduced under the name "von Behr trout."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.** The ideal brook-trout stream receives spring-fed tributaries, has gravelly bottoms, clear shallow water, a steady current, and summer temperatures not exceeding 65°F. The most favorable growth temperature is 45 to 65°F. Streams in cultivated or deforested land suffer from turbid freshets and high summer temperatures, so the best streams are found in wooded, hilly, or mountainous country.

**Water quality for hatcheries.** Spring water is preferred, ideally about 48°F at the ground surface. Lake water from a deep lake is more temperature-stable than shallow-lake water. Bog water, particularly from sphagnum bogs, is often too acid for trout. Aeration is essential, since fish extract dissolved oxygen from water passing over their gills. Water should be aerated through falls, riffles, and pans before it enters troughs.

**Volume requirements.** For 100,000 eggs in high-quality, cold, well-aerated water, the document gives 3 to 5 gallons per minute. Spring water at 48 to 52°F, aerated only by a pool, needs 5 to 8 gallons per minute. A hatching trough of 14 to 16 feet holding 25,000 to 40,000 fry needs 6 to 8 gallons per minute. Rearing ponds need considerably more water.

**Ecological impacts of introductions.** The document reports that brook trout were introduced into Michigan's Au Sable River in 1885 (20,000 fry planted) after logging destroyed the native grayling spawning beds. By 1895, about 10,000 trout were captured on spawning beds there. The document also records that brown trout had a poor reputation in brook-trout waters, with anglers and fish-culturists reporting predation and competition.

**Disease agents.** The main hatchery diseases described include whirling sickness (*Octomitus salmonis*), costiasis (*Costia necatrix*), gyrodactyliasis (*Gyrodactylus*), white spot disease, blue sac disease, and fungus. Parasitic tapeworm larvae occur in Yellowstone trout but are described as posing no real danger to consumers after cooking.

## Key Findings

- **Hatchery siting.** The best water source is deep-seated, well-protected spring water. Lake water from deep, spring-fed lakes is second. Brook and river water is generally inferior because of flooding, turbidity, and drought. Hatcheries should have 3 feet or more of fall for aeration, and a 10-foot fall is "much better still."
- **Egg size and development.** Brook trout eggs average about one-sixth of an inch in diameter, with 300 to 700 eggs per fluid ounce. Development takes about 125 days at 87°F and about 50 days at 50°F. Rainbow trout eggs run 300 to 360 per fluid ounce.
- **Spawning season.** Brook trout spawn in autumn (September in Colorado, mid-October in New York and New England). Rainbow trout hatchery spawning ran from October to January at Wytheville, Virginia, and from February to May in California.
- **Shipping.** Eyed eggs can travel longer distances, while green eggs are "tender" and can be shipped only within about 48 hours. The document recommends sphagnum moss as the best insulating medium. One egg box (12 × 15 × 4 inches) carries 80,000 to 100,000 eggs.
- **Growth.** Under favorable conditions, brook trout reach about 4 to 5 inches at one year, 7 to 8 inches at two years, and about 1 pound at three years. Rainbow trout reach 8 to 10 inches at one year, 12 to 14 inches at two years, and 16 to 18 inches at three years.
- **Fecundity.** A two-year-old rainbow trout produces 500 to 800 eggs, and a six-year-old produces 2,500 to 3,000. Black-spotted trout produce about 900 to 1,000 eggs per spawned fish, with annual egg takes from 3,000,000 to 20,000,000 at Yellowstone hatcheries.
- **Brood stock.** For rainbow trout, 15 to 25 percent of females yield eggs in their second year, about 60 percent in the third, and 80 to 90 percent thereafter. About 10 to 15 percent of mature females are barren each season.
- **Handling.** Spawn takers must avoid pressure forward of the ventral fins and avoid handling fish by the tail. Improper handling can displace eggs into the abdominal cavity, causing barrenness or death.
- **Commercial trout culture.** Eggs from commercial hatcheries sold for $1 to $1.50 per thousand in 1922. Market "thirds," "quarters," and "fifths" (3, 4, and 5 fish per pound) commanded 50 to 60 cents per pound, net.
- **Disease management.** Recommended preventives include cleanliness, careful handling, uncontaminated diets, and abundant clean water. Salt baths (about 2½ percent solution for 10 to 15 minutes) treat costiasis. Cider vinegar at roughly 1:15 is used for gyrodactyliasis. Salt and vinegar treat fungus and external parasites.
- **Native species conservation.** Propagation of Loch Leven and brown trout was discontinued because these introduced species were often detrimental to native fish, a conclusion the document attributes to multiple sources.
