# Population Differentiation and Introgressive Hybridization of the Flannelmouth Sucker and Razorback Sucker

## Executive Summary
This 1987 study used starch-gel allozyme electrophoresis to quantify genetic differentiation and introgressive hybridization between the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The primary conservation motivation was to evaluate whether the [[Dexter Fish Hatchery]] breeding stock of razorback sucker (sourced from [[Lake Mohave]]) had become disproportionately contaminated with introgressed flannelmouth sucker genes due to sampling error compounding natural hybridization. Using four species-diagnostic "marker" loci (Ck-A, G6pi-A², M-Icdh-A, S-Sod-A²), the authors found low but detectable bidirectional introgression (0–5% toward flannelmouth sucker; 0–3% toward razorback sucker). Critically, the Dexter Hatchery stock showed **equal or lower** levels of introgressed genes compared to the wild parental Lake Mohave population, indicating the hatchery propagation program had not amplified hybrid contamination and could be considered genetically sound, provided broodstock continues to be drawn from natural (not captive-reared) populations.

## Background & Description
- Study population 1a (Lake Mohave) was a **morphologically biased sample** of 18 adult razorback suckers deliberately selected because they displayed morphological traits suggestive of hybrid origin (i.e., suspected flannelmouth × razorback hybrids).
- A separate unbiased random sample of 100 juvenile razorback suckers (sample 1b) was also taken from Lake Mohave to test whether morphology-based hybrid selection biased the genetic results.
- The [[Razorback Sucker]] is distinguished by its characteristic "razorback" keel morphology, part of the "big-river" ichthyofaunal assemblage of the Colorado River basin.
- No F₁ hybrids (individuals heterozygous at all four marker loci simultaneously) were identified in any sample — detected introgression represents products of backcrossing (F₂ or later generations), not first-generation hybrids.
- Morphological identification of putative hybrids was found to be a **poor or decoupled indicator** of actual allozyme-based introgression, since the biased "hybrid-looking" sample (1a) showed low introgression levels similar to the unbiased juvenile sample (1b).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Both species are native catostomids inhabiting the [[Colorado River System]], historically allopatric or occupying differing habitat conditions, but brought into increased sympatry/contact via anthropogenic habitat modification (impoundments, flow regulation).
- [[Razorback Sucker]] is classified as a "vulnerable" species (Williams et al. 1985) with a documented range-wide decline, prompting large-scale artificial propagation centered at the [[Dexter Fish Hatchery]] (Dexter, New Mexico).
- Sampling localities for *C. latipinnis* spanned a wide geographic range: [[Virgin River]] (Utah/Arizona), [[Paria River]] (Arizona), [[Little Colorado River]] drainage (East Clear Creek, Arizona), and the [[Upper Colorado River]] (Mesa County, Colorado) — reflecting broad distribution across the basin.
- Razorback sucker samples came from [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona), [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] (California, hatched from wild-collected eggs), and the Dexter Hatchery stock.
- Hybridization between razorback sucker and Catostomus species (flannelmouth, [[Sonora Sucker]] *C. insignis*, and [[Utah Sucker]] *C. ardens*) has been documented since Hubbs and Miller (1953), with flannelmouth sucker implicated in the largest number of hybridization reports (Wick et al. 1982).
- The interaction is hypothesized to be linked to **habitat modification** rather than incomplete reproductive isolation alone; continued Colorado River system alteration could increase introgression rates over time.
- A parallel case is cited in [[Notropis cornutus]] and [[Notropis chrysocephalus]] (common and striped shiners), where similarly low-level sympatric introgression was documented (Dowling and Moore 1984).

## Key Findings

### Genetic Variability (21 presumptive loci examined)
- **Flannelmouth sucker**: 9 of 21 loci were polymorphic in at least one geographic sample. Mean heterozygosity ranged from 0.024 (Little Colorado River) to 0.048 (Paria River).
- **Razorback sucker**: 11 of 21 loci were polymorphic across samples. Mean heterozygosity ranged from 0.018 (Dexter Hatchery) to 0.034 (Lake Mohave).
- All genotype arrays conformed to Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium except the **S-Mdh-A²** locus in the Dexter Hatchery sample, which showed significant heterozygote deficiency (P < 0.001).

### Population Differentiation
- **Flannelmouth sucker** showed statistically significant geographic population structuring: F_st = 0.231 ("great" differentiation, sensu Hartl 1981), driven mainly by loci Ck-A, G3pdh-A, G6pi-A¹, G6pi-B¹, and G6pi-B².
- Nei's genetic identity among flannelmouth populations ranged from I = 0.976–1.000 (genetic distance D = 0.000–0.024).
- **Razorback sucker** native populations (Lake Mohave and Senator Wash) showed essentially no differentiation: I = 1.000, D = 0.000, F_st = 0.011 ("little" differentiation).
- Interspecific divergence between flannelmouth and razorback sucker: average D = 0.220, I = 0.802.

### Species-Diagnostic Marker Loci
Four loci were identified as nearly or fully species-specific, useful for detecting hybridization:
- **Ck-A** (creatine kinase)
- **G6pi-A²** (glucose-6-phosphate isomerase)
- **M-Icdh-A** (isocitrate dehydrogenase)
- **S-Sod-A²** (superoxide dismutase)

### Introgression Quantification (Table 5)
Using Ck-A and M-Icdh-A as introgression indicators:

| Population | Proportion of genes from flannelmouth sucker |
|---|---|
| Flannelmouth sucker (theoretical max) | 1.000 |
| Virgin River | 0.950 |
| Paria River | 1.000 |
| Little Colorado River | 1.000 |
| Upper Colorado River | 0.993 |
| Razorback sucker (theoretical max) | 0.000 |
| Lake Mohave (biased adult sample) | 0.028 |
| Lake Mohave (unbiased juvenile sample) | 0.015 |
| Senator Wash Reservoir | 0.000 |
| **Dexter Hatchery** | **0.010** |

- Introgression is **bidirectional**: gene flow occurs from flannelmouth → razorback sucker (M-Icdh-A allele favored) and razorback → flannelmouth sucker (Ck-A allele favored).
- Overall introgression levels were low: **0–5% toward flannelmouth sucker; 0–3% toward razorback sucker**.

### Hatchery Stock Assessment (Central Conservation Finding)
- The Dexter Hatchery razorback sucker stock exhibited introgression levels (0.010) **equal to or lower than** the wild parental Lake Mohave population (0.015–0.028).
- This indicates that **genetic drift/sampling error in hatchery propagation had NOT amplified introgressed flannelmouth genes**, addressing the central management concern of the study.
- Genetic variability of hatchery stock was comparable to native populations, supporting continuation of the breeding program **as long as broodstock is periodically replenished from wild populations** rather than recycled from previously hatchery-reared stock (to avoid compounding subsampling effects).
- Razorback sucker genetic variability, while below the mean for catostomid fishes generally, was similar to flannelmouth sucker levels and higher than several other abundant catostomid species (e.g., [[Moxostoma duquesnei]], [[Moxostoma pappillosum]], [[Moxostoma poecilurum]]).

### Ploidy/Genome Note
- Catostomid fishes are ancestrally **tetraploid** (Uyeno and Smith 1972), theoretically expressing duplicated loci. However, no differences in duplicate gene expression/locus number were found between flannelmouth and razorback sucker, ruling out this character as a hybrid-identification tool.

### Management Implications
1. Morphological hybrid screening is unreliable/decoupled from allozyme-confirmed introgression status.
2. Random (non-electrophoretically-screened) broodstock selection from Lake Mohave is genetically justified given the low, comparable introgression rate found in both biased and unbiased samples.
3. Continued habitat modification of the Colorado River system may elevate future introgression rates between these species.
4. Long-term hatchery sustainability requires sourcing new broodstock from wild populations rather than serial subsampling of captive-reared generations, to prevent compounding genetic drift.
