# Care of Tropical Aquarium Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquarium-science #ornamental-fish

## Executive Summary
This 1965 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service leaflet is a husbandry guide rather than a species-specific research paper. It outlines the four essential requirements for maintaining a healthy home aquarium — suitable water, sufficient oxygen, correct temperature, and correct feeding — and identifies the primary causes of aquarium failure: impure water, overcrowding, sudden temperature change, and improper feeding. The document also extends these principles to the emerging practice of marine (salt-water) aquarium keeping, noting the increased sensitivity of marine systems to waste accumulation. This leaflet functions as foundational guidance for the [[Ornamental Fish Trade]] and general aquarium science.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The leaflet frames captive care requirements as a proxy for natural ecological tolerances:

- **Water chemistry**: Most home aquarium species are sensitive to chlorinated municipal water and require dechlorination (standing 24+ hours). Deep well/artesian water is discouraged due to high mineral content; highly acid swamp water and untreated rainwater are unsuitable.
- **Thermal range**: Most popular tropical species tolerate roughly a 10°F range, with an optimal window of **72–80°F**; goldfish (Carassius auratus, [[Cyprinidae]]) prefer cooler water. Sudden shifts of more than 2°F during water changes or transfers are noted as potentially fatal — indicating narrow thermal tolerance windows typical of tropical stenothermal fishes.
- **Oxygen dynamics**: Dissolved oxygen availability is inversely related to temperature (75°F water holds more O₂ than 85°F water) and is tied to stocking density. A guideline of 25 square inches of surface area per inch of fish length is given for gas exchange. Notably, aquatic plants are explicitly stated as **not** a significant oxygen source for fish in this context, despite common assumption — surface aeration and air-water interface turnover from pumps are emphasized instead.
- **Marine vs. freshwater systems**: Marine aquaria are described as ecologically more fragile than freshwater systems, requiring stronger filtration and aeration because waste products and uneaten food are more toxic in a closed saltwater system. Marine substrates are also prone to forming anoxic "dead spaces" and bacterial pockets more readily than freshwater substrates.
- **Longevity/life history**: Under captive conditions, [[Guppy]] and fighting fish are noted to rarely exceed two years of life, and increasing susceptibility to infectious disease with age is highlighted as a general life-history trait across tropical ornamental species.

## Key Findings
- Four pillars of aquarium husbandry: (1) suitable water, (2) sufficient oxygen, (3) correct temperature, (4) correct feeding.
- Primary failure modes in captive fish-keeping: impure water, overcrowding, abrupt temperature fluctuation, and overfeeding/improper diet.
- Chlorinated tap water must be aged (≥24 hrs, open container) before use; deep well and swamp/rain waters are generally unsuitable without treatment (e.g., rock salt-amended rainwater).
- Tank geometry matters ecologically: straight-sided tanks (3–20 gal) provide better surface-area-to-volume ratios for oxygen exchange than bowls/globes.
- Contrary to popular belief, live aquarium plants are not considered a reliable oxygen source for fish in this framework; mechanical aeration is prioritized.
- Recommended thermal band for tropical species: 72–80°F, with maximum allowable rate-of-change of ~2°F during transfers/water changes.
- Feeding protocol: twice daily, limited to a 5-minute consumption window, with prompt removal of uneaten food to prevent water fouling and oxygen depletion.
- Diet composition: commercial dried foods (fish/meat meal + cereal/flour), supplemented with live foods (daphnia, tubifex worms, mosquito larvae, white worms, chopped earthworms) and fresh scraped meats (liver, fish, shrimp). Fry require microscopic foods: brine shrimp, infusoria, micro-worms, powdered egg yolk.
- Marine aquaria require more rigorous filtration/aeration than freshwater systems due to greater toxicity of accumulated wastes and higher risk of anaerobic "dead spaces" in substrate; sub-sand (under-gravel) filters were noted as a valuable innovation for both marine and freshwater setups circa 1965.
- Evaporated marine aquarium water should be replenished with fresh water only, never additional salt water, to avoid progressive salinity increase.
- Life-history note: many popular tropical species (e.g., guppies, bettas) are short-lived (~2 years) in captivity relative to other companion animals, with disease susceptibility increasing with age — supporting a management recommendation to stock young rather than adult specimens.
