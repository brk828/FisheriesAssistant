# Analysis of Long Term Survival, Natural Reproduction and Potential Hybridization of Striped Bass in Devils Lake, North Dakota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #population-genetics #hybridization-study

## Executive Summary

This study assessed the risk of [[Striped Bass]] (Morone saxatilis) transfer from [[Devils Lake]] to Canadian waters via a proposed outlet to the [[Sheyenne River]]. Approximately 13,000 striped bass fingerlings were stocked into Devils Lake in 1977 to provide a trophy fishery. The investigation determined: (1) whether any original 1977 stock remain in Devils Lake; (2) if successful reproduction has occurred; and (3) if striped bass have hybridized with [[White Bass]] (M. chrysops), introduced in 1971. Over 90,000 hours of netting effort since 1977 captured only one striped bass (1989). Three additional striped bass were caught by anglers (1988, 1992, 1993) in over one million angler hours. Electrophoretic analysis of 106 Morone specimens showed diagnostic banding patterns consistent with pure white bass populations with no evidence of hybridization. The study concludes that natural reproduction has not occurred, hybridization has not occurred, and the possibility of surviving original stock is extremely remote.

## Background & Description

[[Devils Lake]] is a saline, hypereutrophic natural lake in northeast North Dakota at elevation 1436 feet mean sea level, covering 67,235 surface acres with maximum depth of 32 feet and average depth of 16 feet. The lake is the terminus of a 3,814 square mile watershed and experiences dramatic water level fluctuations due to hydrologic and climatic conditions, ranging from nearly dry (elevation 1400.9 msl in the 1940s) to current levels around 1435 msl.

The striped bass stocking in 1977 consisted of approximately 13,000 fingerlings ranging from 1.3 to 2.5 inches, intended to provide a unique trophy fishery. Devils Lake's brackish conditions made it a logical choice for this salt-tolerant species. White bass were introduced earlier in 1971. High water levels in the 1990s prompted discussion of an outlet to lower lake elevations, raising concerns about potential biota transfer to Canada via the [[Red River]] system, ultimately flowing into [[Lake Winnipeg]], Manitoba.

The study employed multiple methodologies: review of historic netting data (1977-1995), intensive large mesh gill net sampling in 1995, examination of angler catch records ("Whopper Club"), spawning habitat assessment, and genetic analysis using both electrophoresis and DNA fingerprinting techniques.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Devils Lake's morphometry and hydrology are unsuitable for striped bass reproduction. Successful striped bass spawning requires specific conditions defined by Crance (1984) and Davin et al. (1989): lotic systems with water velocity of 7.1 to 19.3 cm/sec (18 to 49 inches/second) to keep eggs suspended, depth of 180-900 cm (6-30 feet) in egg incubation zones, and substrate of sand-sized particles or larger. Hatching occurs in 28-80 hours at 52-72°F.

Devils Lake has two major inlets: [[Channel A]] (5 miles from Dry Lake to Six Mile Bay) and [[Mauvais-Big Coulee]] (7 miles from Pelican Lake to Minnewaukan Flats/Bay). With velocities of 30.5 to 91.5 cm/sec, only 7.3 to 2.4 hours are available for egg suspension in Channel A and 10.3 to 3.4 hours in Mauvais/Big Coulee—far below the 28-80 hours required for hatching. Both inlets have mud substrate with decaying vegetation rather than the required clean gravel or rock. The upper reaches have soft organic bottoms with high biochemical oxygen demand and high sulfur dioxide content.

The inlets are intermittent coulees with low gradients (Mauvais/Big Coulee drops ~1 foot/mile; Channel A drops ~2.3 feet/mile). Peak flows occur in April-May, but striped bass would theoretically spawn in late May-June when flow velocities typically fall below 1 foot/second.

White bass in Devils Lake exhibit exceptional body condition with mean relative weights exceeding 110 (100 is considered very good). Growth rates are similar to other water bodies, with few fish living past age 6 and none found over age 8. Maximum recorded white bass size was 440 mm (17 inches) and 1,320 g (2.9 pounds), well within normal range for the species.

## Key Findings

**Netting Effort and Captures:**
- Over 90,000 hours of netting effort expended since 1977 (53,730.5 trap net hours, 2,989.5 hours with 6'x125' variegated gill nets, 3,368.5 hours with other gill nets)
- Estimated 30,000 additional hours during spawning seasons (1977-1991)
- Over 1,000 hours with large mesh gill nets in 1995 specifically targeting striped bass
- Only one striped bass captured in all netting efforts: adult weighing ~8.17 kg (18 pounds) netted near Ziebach Pass in 1989

**Angler Catches:**
- Three striped bass caught by anglers in over one million angler hours (1988-1992 creel surveys)
- First: May 22, 1988, 16.4 pounds (7.5 kg), 34.5 inches (876 mm)
- Second: May 20, 1989, 18 pounds (8.2 kg), 35 inches (889 mm)
- Third: June 11, 1993, 20.75 pounds (9.4 kg), 38.5 inches (978 mm)
- All angler-caught striped bass from eastern Main Bay near Highway 57 crossing

**White Bass Population:**
- Maximum white bass from 1991-1995 surveys: 440 mm (17 inches), 1,320 g (2.9 pounds)
- Largest white bass in "Whopper Club" records: 1.97 kg (4.3 pounds), also North Dakota state record
- Of 305 white bass submitted 1982-1990, only two exceeded 500 mm
- Growth rates: Age 1 = 127 mm, Age 3 = 267 mm, Age 6 = 362 mm
- Creel surveys documented: 224,548 white bass caught in 555,700 angler hours (1988); 64,000 caught in 249,993 hours (1991); 35,738 caught in 323,953 hours (1992)

**Genetic Analysis:**
- 106 Morone specimens collected from three sites using trap and gill nets
- Tissue (muscle, liver) and blood samples analyzed using electrophoresis at [[A.E. Wood State Fish Hatchery]], Texas
- Three diagnostic loci examined: PGI (EC 5.3.1.9), SORD (EC 1.1.1.14), EST (EC 3.1.1.2)
- Isoelectric focusing performed on polyacrylamide gels (pH 3-5) with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 stain
- All 106 samples showed banding patterns consistent with pure white bass
- No evidence of hybridization detected at any locus
- Control samples from [[Heart Butte Reservoir]] (original white bass source) showed identical patterns
- DNA analysis scheduled for completion January 1996 (expected to confirm electrophoretic results)

**Comparative Growth Data:**
- Nebraska [[Branched Oak Reservoir]] striped bass/white bass hybrids show rapid growth: Age 1 = 154 mm, Age 4 = 442 mm
- By age 4, hybrids larger than age 6 Devils Lake white bass
- No Morone specimens of hybrid size captured in Devils Lake sampling

**Spawning Habitat Assessment:**
- Channel A: 5 miles long, velocity 30.5-91.5 cm/sec, only 7.3-2.4 hours egg suspension time
- Mauvais/Big Coulee: 7 miles long, velocity 30.5-91.5 cm/sec, only 10.3-3.4 hours egg suspension time
- Required egg incubation time: 28-80 hours at 52-72°F
- Substrate: mud with decaying vegetation (required: sand, gravel, or rock)
- Peak flows April-May; striped bass spawn late May-June when flows <1 foot/second

**Conclusions:**
- Natural reproduction of striped bass in Devils Lake is "almost a certainty" not to have occurred
- No hybridization with white bass has occurred
- Possibility of surviving original 1977 stock is "extremely remote"
- Lack of necessary spawning substrate, inadequate flows for egg incubation, and absence of large Morone in samples provide strong evidence
- Study demonstrates comprehensive assessment using multiple lines of evidence: netting surveys, angler records, habitat evaluation, and genetic analysis
