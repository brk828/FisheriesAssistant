# Morphometry of the Coregonine Fishes of Great Bear Lake, N.W.T.

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coregonine

## Executive Summary

This study by W. A. Kennedy of the [[Central Fisheries Research Station]] presents morphometric measurements and meristic counts for three coregonine species from [[Great Bear Lake]]: [[Lake Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis*, n = 78), [[Cisco]] (*Leucichthys artedi*, n = 77) and [[Round Whitefish]] (*Prosopium cylindraceum*, n = 8). Within-lake variability was substantial, which casts doubt on the validity of several whitefish and cisco species previously described from the region. Differences among subsamples were attributed mainly to water temperature rather than genetics, following [[Martin 1949]]. The data more than double the morphometric information available for northwestern Canada.

## Background & Description

The fish were collected during a 1945 survey by the [[Fisheries Research Board of Canada]] to assess the lake's fisheries potential. Three coregonines were found, and no other coregonine species were reported in the lake. Availability, distribution, growth, size at maturity, sex ratio and food habits were treated separately in [[Kennedy 1949]].

Great Bear Lake lies on the Arctic Circle. Its water area is about 11,800 square miles (4,750,000 ha), or about 12,000 square miles (4,800,000 ha) including islands. Most of the lake is deeper than 400 feet (120 m), and the maximum recorded depth is 1,300 feet (400 m). The open water is clear and cold, with a sparse biota. Coregonines occur almost entirely around the periphery.

**Methods**

- **Capture:** Fish were taken by [[Gill-Net Sampling]] using 2½-, 3-, 4-, 5- and 5½-inch mesh (stretched measure). Several thousand small coregonines from seine hauls were not measured.
- **Measurements:** Standard length, head, eye, snout, body, fin and peduncle dimensions were taken following [[Dymond 1943]] methods.
- **Counts:** Dorsal and anal rays, branchiostegal rays, scales, gill rakers and [[Vertebral Count]] (including the urostyle) were recorded.
- **Preservation correction:** Of the fish, 81 whitefish, 49 ciscoes and 8 round whitefish were preserved in 5% formalin and measured later. Shrinkage factors derived from 12 ciscoes and 7 whitefish measured both fresh and preserved were used to estimate fresh dimensions. Factors were 1.00 to 1.15 for whitefish and 1.00 to 1.06 for ciscoes.
- **Analysis:** Mature and immature fish were analysed separately because growth of body parts relative to standard length often changed at maturity. Analysis used [[Allometric Regression]] on log-transformed data, analysis of variance and [[Snedecor 1946]] statistical methods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The main lake is cold and clear, and coregonines are restricted to its edges. Sampling sites were chosen because they held the only substantial whitefish or cisco concentrations reported.

**Habitat contrasts in whitefish:** Subsamples came from [[Smith Arm]], [[McTavish Arm]] and [[McVicar Arm]]. Smith and McVicar arm fish came from similar habitats: the blind ends of long, narrow arms where large rivers warmed the water compared with the open lake. McTavish Arm samples came mostly from river mouths, but the river influence was smaller and open cold water was closer. The author noted that the geographically closer McTavish and McVicar groups differed more than the distant Smith and McVicar groups, which shared habitat. This pattern was linked to [[Water Temperature Effects]].

**Habitat contrasts in ciscoes:** Ciscoes came from [[Echo Bay]] on the east shore of McTavish Arm and from [[Franklin Bay]] at the west end of [[Keith Arm]]. Franklin Bay is slightly warmer than the east end of McTavish Arm. The author linked the observed differences to this temperature gradient. No other localities with appreciable cisco concentrations were known.

## Key Findings

**1. Lake Whitefish (*Coregonus clupeaformis*)**

- Mature fish: n = 52, with regression equations, variances and standard errors given for 16 body parts. Example: head length Y = 0.97X − 0.61 (SE ± 0.016).
- Immature fish: n = 26, analysed in the same way.
- Subsamples differed significantly among themselves in some mature (head length, head depth, snout length, interorbital width, caudal peduncle depth, pectoral and pelvic fin length) and immature traits (caudal peduncle depth, body depth, body width, dorsal fin base). Growth rates did not differ significantly. The divergent McTavish Arm fish drove most of these differences.
- Meristic counts (all subsamples pooled, n = 78 unless noted):

| Character | n | Range | Mean ± SD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vertebrae | 49 | 59–64 | 61.8 |
| Dorsal rays | 78 | 12–16 | 13.7 ± 0.8 |
| Anal rays | 78 | 12–16 | 13.8 ± 1.0 |
| Branchiostegals | 78 | 8–10 | 9.2 ± 0.5 |
| Scales | 57 | 72–85 | 77.8 |
| Gill rakers | 77 | 23–31 | 27.0 ± 1.5 |

- Counts did not differ significantly among the three localities.
- Tables III and V give 95% ranges of proportionate measurements for standard lengths of 300–700 mm (mature) and 200–500 mm (immature). Fish falling outside these ranges can be considered morphologically distinct from typical Great Bear Lake fish.

**2. Cisco (*Leucichthys artedi*)**

- Mature fish: n = 77 (Table VII and VIII regressions and 95% ranges, regardless of locality).
- Sexes did not differ significantly in size of any body part for a given standard length, or in growth rate.
- Keith Arm fish differed significantly from McTavish Arm fish. McTavish Arm fish had significantly longer and deeper heads, longer maxillaries, longer and deeper caudal peduncles, deeper and wider bodies, and longer dorsal, anal, pectoral and pelvic fins.
- Vertebrae: Keith Arm averaged 59.5 (n = 71) and McTavish Arm averaged 58.7 (n = 19). This difference was significant.
- Immature fish: 11 from each arm, but length ranges (Keith Arm 250–295 mm, McTavish Arm 40–65 mm) did not permit comparison.
- Meristic counts (mature fish and 11 largest immature fish):

| Character | n | Range | Mean ± SD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vertebrae | 90 | 56–62 | 59.4 ± 1.2 |
| Dorsal rays | 88 | 12–15 | 13.3 ± 0.7 |
| Anal rays | 88 | 12–15 | 13.5 ± 0.8 |
| Branchiostegals | 88 | 7–10 | 8.5 ± 0.6 |
| Scales | 18 | 73–84 | 78.6 ± 2.8 |
| Gill rakers | 87 | 41–50 | 45.0 ± 2.0 |

**3. Round Whitefish (*Prosopium cylindraceum*)**

- Only 8 fresh specimens (standard length 251–305 mm). Each was analysed individually as proportionate measurements (Table X), since the sample was too small for statistical treatment.
- Results agreed with the four fish reported by [[Dymond 1943]] once preservation effects were considered.

**4. Taxonomic Conclusions**

- The ciscoes agree with Dymond's view that Great Bear Lake fish are *Leucichthys artedi*, making *L. lucidus* a synonym. Disagreement on fin-ray counts is likely a difference in defining a fin ray.
- The author suggested other *Leucichthys* species may also be synonyms, given the variation within a single species.
- Whitefish from other northwestern Canadian localities, and *C. nelsoni*, are not clearly distinct from Great Bear Lake *C. clupeaformis*. *C. kennicotti* and *C. nasus* are not strikingly different. The author considered that all four may be synonyms, but limited comparison material prevented firm conclusions.
- Overall, the author concluded that some previously recognized species in Canada, Alaska and Siberia may eventually be reduced to synonymy.

**5. Interpretation of Variation**

- Differences among subsamples were assumed to reflect environmental effects (water temperature) rather than genetic divergence, consistent with [[Martin 1949]].
- Individuals divergent in one character were not necessarily divergent in others, so single-trait summaries have limits.
