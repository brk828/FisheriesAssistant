# Distribution of Larval Devils Hole Pupfish (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) in Relation to Dissolved Oxygen in Devils Hole

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This final report to the [[National Park Service]] (Death Valley National Park) by Gustafson and [[James E. Deacon]] (University of Nevada, Las Vegas, April 1998) tests whether diel dissolved oxygen (DO) variation caused by algal photosynthesis affects the hatching success and distribution of larval [[Devils Hole Pupfish]]. Field work ran in April and June 1996 and May 1997 at nine upper-shelf sites, with a lower-shelf sampling area monitored by SCUBA. The authors found that pH, conductivity and temperature had little effect on hatch rate, that larvae concentrated on the inner (southern) shelf where DO variation was greatest, and that the western inner and middle sites (I-w, M-w) were the most important contributors to recruitment.

## Background & Description

The Devils Hole pupfish is a cyprinodont restricted to Devils Hole, a conical, roughly 15 m deep depression in a carbonate ridge within the [[Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge]]. It is listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and is noted as occupying the smallest habitat of any known vertebrate. Adults show a distinctive morphology attributed to long isolation and constant elevated temperature. Two refuge populations exist at Point-of-Rocks and School springs.

The pool's surface measures about 3.5 m by 22 m. A rocky upper (spawning) shelf extends north from the southern end to about 0.8 m depth, then drops into a cavern system. A deeper second shelf lies at 5 to 9 m, and a third shelf spans 12 to 19 m, with Anvil Rock at 24 m. Devils Hole has had no surface outlet for at least 10,000, and probably 50,000, years.

Early life stages are defined as follows: embryo (about 1.5 mm egg, hatching after about one week), prolarva (about 4 mm TL, yolk sac present), early postlarva, late postlarva, and prejuvenile (10 to 11 mm TL with all fin rays). Sexual dimorphism appears at about 15 to 20 mm TL.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Light and algae.** Because of the depressed topography, the surface pool receives limited direct sunlight in spring and summer and little in fall and winter. Algal production, especially on the upper shelf, varies strongly with season. Filamentous algae include [[Spirogyra]] and *Lyngbya* sp., along with diatoms. Mats were most extensive in May 1997.

**Spawning.** Spawning peaks between early April and mid-June, the period of maximal algal growth. Juveniles and postlarvae are mainly found in spring and early summer.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Earlier work reported DO fluctuating daily between about 2.0 and 2.0 ppm and reaching 6.0 to 7.0 ppm over the upper shelf during intense photosynthesis. Deacon et al. (1995) proposed that oxygen produced by algae, combined with small diel temperature variation, may be critical for egg viability.

**Temperature.** Devils Hole is consistently warm (about 33 °C). Studies of cyprinodonts show reduced hatching at constant temperatures above 30 °C, and [[Kinne and Kinne]] (1962) reported that 36 °C is near the lethal limit for pupfish.

**Water chemistry.** Conductivity was 696 to 699 µS/cm and pH about 7.1 (see [[Water Chemistry of Devils Hole]]).

## Key Findings

- **Water chemistry:** Conductivity and pH were consistent within narrow ranges (April 1996 conductivity 699 ± 6 µS/cm, range 655 to 723; June 1996 696 ± 3 µS/cm; pH 7.1 in both periods). No statistically significant differences were found among sites, shelf areas, or study periods (α = 0.05), so these parameters were judged not to influence hatch rate or recruitment.
- **Temperature:** Mean temperatures were 33.2 °C (April 1996), 33.5 °C (June 1996) and 32.6 °C (May 1997). The May 1997 range reached 31.0 to 36.0 °C, with a rise from about 32 to 36 °C in under an hour when sunlight arrived. Average temperature variation was within about 1 °C in most shelf areas in April and June 1996.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** The inner shelf showed the greatest DO variability and the outer shelf the least. Most observed larvae were found over the inner shelf where DO variation was highest, and the fewest on the outer shelf.
- **Larval distribution (prolarvae and early postlarvae):** Totals were 65 for April 1996, 34 for June 1996 and 39 for May 1997 across upper-shelf sites. The inner shelf held about 51%, the middle shelf 44% and the outer shelf 5% of the early-stage larvae in the analysis. Site I-w and M-w had the highest frequencies of early-stage larvae (30% and 20%), and sites O-w and I-M the lowest.
- **Recruitment sources:** The authors concluded that the inner shelf area, primarily site I-w, contributed the greatest number of early-stage larvae to recruitment, that the middle shelf was intermediate, and that the outer shelf contributed the least.
- **Substrate:** Sites I-w and M-w had medium to large cobble with interstices that may shelter larvae, plus Spirogyra mats and diel DO variation of at least 1.1 mg/L. Site I-c had nearly no larvae in June 1996, possibly due to a dense algal mat blocking interstices.
- **Phototaxis:** Night light attraction produced no larvae, suggesting that Devils Hole pupfish do not exhibit phototaxis. Larvae were observed resting on mesh breeder-box tops and on mesh observation platforms, and the mesh platforms proved the most effective observation method.
- **Spawning behavior:** Spawning was observed on 12 and 13 May 1997, mostly over the western inner and middle shelf, and the authors stated that these records are not complete.
- **Hypothesis:** The authors suggested that DO or temperature variation may induce arrested development during hypoxia, but this remained untested and needed further research.
- **Management implications:** Protecting the inner shelf and its western sites (I-w and M-w), which contribute most to recruitment, was highlighted as important for population management.
