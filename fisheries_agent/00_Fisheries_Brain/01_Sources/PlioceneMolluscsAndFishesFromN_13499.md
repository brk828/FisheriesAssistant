# Pliocene Freshwater Molluscs and Fishes of the Great Basin: Honey Lake and Mopung Hills

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paleontological study by D. W. Taylor and G. R. Smith, published in the [[Museum of Paleontology]] at the University of Michigan (1981), describes two fossil freshwater assemblages from northeastern California and northwestern Nevada. The [[Honey Lake]] fauna (seven molluscs, five fishes) shows strong ties to the former [[Snake River]] drainage of southern Idaho. The [[Mopung Hills]] fauna (six molluscs, four fishes) comes from a large lake that had a long independent history. The authors conclude that the Mopung Hills molluscs were lake specialists that went extinct when the lake dried or became saline, whereas the fishes survived in tributary streams and later populated the [[Lahontan Basin]].

## Background & Description

The paper addresses the evolution of drainage in the northwestern [[Great Basin]]. Fossils were collected from several localities at Honey Lake, a now-dry basin in Lassen County, California, and from the lacustrine limestone of the Mopung Hills, the western tip of the West Humboldt Range in Churchill County, Nevada.

Taxonomic work includes several new species and one new genus:

- **New molluscs:** [[Fontelicella]] melina, [[Vorticifex]] lasseni, [[Sphaerium]] nevadense, [[Valvata]] nevadensis, [[Lutrilimnea]] polyskelidis (new genus and species), [[Menetus]] carinifex, [[Physa]] humboldtiana, and [[Physa]] harpa (both species in the subgenus Costatella).
- **Renamed species:** *Valvata idahoensis* is a new name for a species previously misassigned as *V. multicarinata*.
- **Fish identifications:** Mostly open-nomenclature (cf.) assignments based on isolated jaw bones, teeth, vertebrae and opercles.

Honey Lake fossils came from three collection sites. Locality A was a light tan silt bed of about 3 ft thick at roughly 3995 ft elevation. Locality B was on the historical lakeshore, where specimens had been winnowed by the former lake. Locality C was in silts at about 4000 ft elevation, where associated skeletal remains were found in their original burial matrix. Many Honey Lake specimens appear reworked from older deposits, including some shells with lithified sandstone matrix.

The Mopung Hills fossils come from three limestone localities (A, B and C) of similar fossil content. The limestone is a coquina and shelly limestone that is locally folded and faulted, with a minimum thickness of about 75 ft and a total thickness perhaps near 150 ft. Fossils were collected in 1955 and 1957 by Taylor, and fishes in 1979 by Smith and colleagues.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Honey Lake.** Most molluscs at Honey Lake are restricted to perennial waters and could have lived in a lake or in a river or creek not subject to flood scour. The authors suggest a former perennial lake, not necessarily more than a few tens of feet deep, would have suited the molluscs. The fish evidence, particularly the presence of [[Chasmistes]] (rarely found outside lakes except during spawning runs), points to a lake larger than [[Eagle Lake]]. Modern faunas of [[Klamath Lake]] and Eagle Lake include several of the same species.

**Mopung Hills.** All recovered species belong to groups usually or always restricted to perennial waters. Deposition occurred near a lake shore. The authors speculate that the lake may have covered about 1500 square miles, including the areas of [[Carson Sink]] and Humboldt Sink. The molluscs were strictly freshwater forms, and the fishes include cool-water species, unlike the warm-water lowland fauna reported from the [[Truckee Formation]] elsewhere.

**Biogeography.** Four of eight Honey Lake mollusc species and all of the fishes show disjunct distributions that indicate former drainage links from southern Idaho across southern Oregon to northeastern California. The nearest living population of *Fontelicella* (subgenus Natricola, the "Snake River dwellers") occurs in the Abert Lake basin, Oregon. Chronology remains imprecise, and the Honey Lake fauna may postdate the Snake River's independent southern outflow.

**Age.** Honey Lake molluscs compare with the [[Glenns Ferry Formation]] (Pliocene) and the [[Bruneau Formation]] (early Pleistocene) of southern Idaho, which led to a possible Pleistocene assignment. The authors nonetheless favor a Pliocene age, largely because the *Gila* and suckers are more similar to Pliocene forms than to Recent Lahontan species. Mopung Hills is assigned to the Pliocene, a conclusion that follows from its substantial faunal difference from the lower [[Truckee Formation]] (late middle Miocene) fauna.

## Key Findings

- **Honey Lake molluscs (eight species in the pre-Lahontan deformed bed):** Only two are extinct. Most others live in the Honey Lake drainage, and all are known from the Snake River valley of Idaho except the extinct forms.
- **Honey Lake fishes (five species):** One trout ([[Rhabdofario]] cf. R. lacustris), one minnow ([[Gila]] (Gila) sp.) and two suckers ([[Catostomus]] sp. and [[Chasmistes]] cf. C. cujus). The trout and suckers resemble Glenns Ferry forms. The *Gila* is more similar to Pliocene members of the genus than to Recent *Siphateles* from the Lahontan Basin.
- ***Fontelicella melina*:** The authors' most informative mollusc for drainage connections, with a mean length/width ratio of 1.47 (95% CI 1.44–1.49) and a length/aperture length ratio of 2.16 (95% CI 2.11–2.22), based on 30 measured specimens. The lake sample of *F. hendersoni* is the least similar to *F. melina*.
- ***Vorticifex lasseni*:** A relatively large species exceeding 13 mm in length, with a mean length/width ratio of 0.98 (10 specimens). Locality B yielded about 100 specimens.
- **Mopung Hills molluscs (six species):** All but one are extinct and lack close relatives in the immediate region. The remaining living species, *Pisidium compressum*, is widespread.
- **Mopung Hills fishes (four taxa):** [[Salmo]] sp., [[Gila (Siphateles)]] sp., [[Catostomus]] cf. C. tahoensis, and [[Cottus]] cf. C. beldingi. Taken together, these are ancestral to the modern fauna of the Lahontan Basin, especially [[Pyramid Lake]].
- **Lutrilimnea polyskelidis:** A very large species, reaching about 60 mm in length, with a short spire and prominent axial ribs. It represents a new genus whose closest relatives occur in the Miocene and Pleistocene of Arizona, Idaho and Utah.
- ***Lutrilimnea ursina*:** Abundant as a Pleistocene and Holocene fossil on the shores of [[Bear Lake]] (Idaho–Utah). The youngest radiometric dates indicate it became extinct about 8000 years ago.
- **Physa harpa:** Known from 22 specimens counted for ribs, with 11–17 ribs on the last whorl. *Physa humboldtiana* shows a single ribbed type specimen among series without ribs.
- **Valvata nevadensis:** Diagnosed by 5–9 rounded spiral cords. Variation was scored in 57 specimens, compared with 65 specimens of *V. idahoensis* from the Salt Lake Formation of Idaho.
- **Interpretation of Mopung Hills:** The molluscs were specialized for a strictly lacustrine existence and became extinct when the lake desiccated or became saline. Fishes survived in tributary streams and re-occupied lakes that formed later in the basin.
- **Basin history:** The distinct faunas of Honey Lake and Mopung Hills, less than 100 miles apart, imply separate basins with a barrier between them during the Pliocene. The authors infer that the present Lahontan Basin is composite, formed from earlier separate basins that merged as topographic divides were reduced from the late Pliocene onward.
- **Parallel history:** A similar conclusion was drawn for the [[Bonneville Basin]], where Miocene to modern molluscs indicate the breakup of external drainage and the unification of several former drainages.
