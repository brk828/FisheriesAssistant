# Use of Ground Water in Arizona

#hydrogeology #groundwater #water-resources #arizona

## Executive Summary

This paper by John W. Harshbarger, presented at a 1957 symposium on [[Climate And Man In The Southwest]] (the 33rd annual meeting of the Southwestern and Rocky Mountain Division of the American Association for the Advancement of Science, Tucson, Arizona), reviews the ground-water resources of Arizona. It links the state's [[Geology Of Arizona]] to how aquifers store, transmit and yield water, describes the historical growth of [[Ground-Water Pumping]] for irrigation, and documents widespread water-table decline. The author concludes that many basins are in a depletion cycle, that withdrawals are mining a finite resource, and that management decisions require detailed geologic and hydrologic data.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** A brief overview of ground-water conditions in Arizona, emphasizing the role of geology in recharge, movement, storage and discharge, and how human use compares with the early, pre-development water table.
- **Data sources:** Mainly [[U.S. Geological Survey]] reports (e.g., Halpenny 1952; Harshbarger, Repenning and Callahan 1953), plus Arizona State Land Department records, [[Salt River Valley Water Users' Association]] data, University of Arizona Agricultural Engineering records and well-driller logs.
- **Development history:**
  - Ground water was minor before 1900; irrigation depended on surface water.
  - About 1910, artesian flow in the San Simon area led to many wells, but poor construction caused pressure loss and abandonment.
  - About 1920, surface-water irrigation waterlogged the Salt River Valley; drainage wells were pumped and reused for irrigation.
  - In 1939, the Ground Water Branch of the USGS opened an Arizona office.
  - Since 1945, post-war irrigation expansion accelerated withdrawals from alluvial reservoirs, and some farmland has been abandoned as pumping costs rose.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography and aquifers**
- The northeast lies in the Colorado Plateau province (flat-lying strata, 4,000 to 10,000 ft altitude); the southwest lies in the Basin and Range province (alluvial basins and mountain blocks). A transitional mountainous zone runs northwest across the state.
- Northern aquifers: perched water tables in Permian and younger sandstone and limestone separated by shale and claystone aquicludes. The [[Coconino Sandstone]] (up to about 900 ft thick) yields little except where fractured; St. Johns-Springerville wells reach several hundred to 2,000 gpm. The Navajo sandstone (Jurassic) is a good but low-yield aquifer, and Mesaverde (Cretaceous) sandstones supply small domestic and institutional needs.
- The [[Redwall Limestone]] (Mississippian) feeds large springs in the Grand Canyon area and along the Mogollon Rim but lies too deep for practical pumping.
- Southern basins: alluvium and lake beds of possibly less than 3,000 to more than 5,000 ft thick. Water-table depth ranges from a few feet to several hundred feet. Artesian aquifers commonly lie 700 to 1,500 ft deep; unconfined water-table aquifers are more permeable and productive.

**Hydraulic properties**
- Permeability: shale, siltstone and claystone are often below 1 gal/day/ft²; fine sandstones about 10 to 100; medium and coarse materials about 1,000; gravels with sand up to 50,000 to 60,000.
- Alluvial porosity averages about 25 percent (range 10 to 35 percent).
- Movement ranges from inches or feet per year in fine northern aquifers to several hundred feet per year in some basins.

**Recharge and discharge**
- Recharge sources: direct precipitation (negligible on the southern desert floor), surface runoff from washes and streams (the main southern source), underflow, and irrigation infiltration (up to about 15 percent of applied water in some areas).
- Natural discharge: phreatophyte transpiration, which may reach 1,375,000 acre-feet per year; evaporation; springs (from a few gallons to over 1,000 gpm); and underflow. Natural discharge has declined with drought and pumping.
- Artificial discharge: pumping averages more than 4,000,000 acre-feet per year and has grown rapidly since World War II.

**Surface water**
- Reservoirs: Verde River (Bartlett and Horseshoe, 322,000 acre-feet combined); Salt River (four dams, 1,755,000 acre-feet, with Roosevelt Dam the largest); [[Coolidge Dam]] on the upper Gila (1,200,000 acre-feet capacity; maximum storage 800,000 acre-feet since 1928).
- Average annual runoff (1921-45): Salt 586,000; Verde 456,000; upper Gila 286,000 acre-feet.
- Runoff has been above average only sporadically since the 1940s, coinciding with accelerated well pumping.

## Key Findings

1. **Basins in depletion:** Persistent water-table declines show that many Arizona basins are being depleted. The most serious declines are in the [[Salt River Valley]], the lower Santa Cruz Valley and the northern upper Santa Cruz Valley. Declines reach 140 ft in the lower Santa Cruz (Pinal County) and 150 ft in the Salt River Valley (1942-1956).
2. **Pumpage growth:** Pinal County pumpage rose from about 350,000 acre-feet (1941) to 500,000 (1942) and about 1,200,000 (1955). About one-third of Arizona's pumped ground water comes from the Santa Cruz Valley.
3. **Willcox and Douglas basins:** Development is more recent, mostly since about 1945 and intensively since 1952. Declines are less severe so far.
4. **Mining of storage:** Withdrawals are largely a mining process. Replenishment does not balance withdrawal, so the author calls for state decisions on where, and how far, ground-water mining should continue.
5. **Limits of knowledge:** Available data cannot estimate how long basins will yield water at current rates. Saturated fill may reach about 3,000 ft in some southern basins, but pumping from such depths is likely impractical.
6. **Economics:** Only well-capitalized users (municipalities, industry, and deep-well irrigators) can sustain costly pumping. Agriculture is most vulnerable, and farm abandonment has followed.
7. **Management need:** Conservation practices could extend crop irrigation for many years, but regulation must rest on full geologic and hydrologic knowledge of each basin.
