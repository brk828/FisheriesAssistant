# Mexican Scientific Research Permit Requirements for U.S. Investigators (1986)

#marine-biology #aquatic-ecology #research-permits

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of 1986 correspondence and a U.S. Embassy telegram describing how U.S. scientists must obtain [[Mexican Scientific Research Permits]] before conducting fieldwork in Mexico. It covers the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's procedural guidance, a June 1986 meeting between the [[U.S. Embassy Mexico City]] and the [[Secretariat of Foreign Relations]] (SRE), and the SRE's formal requirements for marine, land, and aircraft overflight research. No fish species or field results are reported. The material is relevant to aquatic research because it covers marine flora and fauna, fresh-water habitats, and endangered species.

## Background & Description

- **Origin:** A letter from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Albuquerque office, signed by Jack B. Woody, U.S./Mexico Project Leader), current as of February 1, 1986.
- **Telegram series:** Embassy cables (JUL 86) addressed to the State Department, with the Embassy's Science Counselor Ray Morales and Science Officer Doug Shaffer in the meeting. The cables mention a mid-June 1986 meeting with SRE North American Affairs sub-director Graciela Orozco.
- **Scope:** Mexico requires that all scientific and archaeological research by foreign researchers, including joint ventures and research invited by Mexican bodies, be processed through [[Diplomatic Notes]] and [[Diplomatic Channels]]. Research without written approval is considered illegal.
- **Trigger for the dispute:** SRE complaints about late or missing reports, and non-compliance by U.S. scientists, some involving research more than a year old. The Embassy argued that the proposed requirements would deter U.S. research in Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The requirements are split by research type:

- **[[Marine Scientific Research]]:** Covers the flora, fauna, geophysics, or oceanography of any point in Mexican waters. Vessel-based work requires receipt by the SRE at least 180 days before fieldwork. Shore-based work (beaches, islands, shorelines) also requires 180 days' notice.
- **[[Land Scientific Research]]:** Covers flora, fauna, geophysics, or archaeology on Mexican territory, including fresh-water streams, lakes, marshes, and wetlands. Requires 180 days' notice.
- **[[Aircraft Overflight Research]]:** Covers meteorology, ornithology, topography, geology, or archaeology from aircraft over land or water. Requires 180 days' notice.

Mexico is especially sensitive to research with resource-exploitation implications (minerals, metals, oil) and to [[Endangered Species]] research. It favors projects that benefit Mexico, and it wants Mexican collaboration and participation in all phases.

## Key Findings

**Procedural requirements (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service letter):**
1. Allow at least six months for processing.
2. Send two copies of the complete application to the U.S. Embassy (attn. Douglas Shaffer, Laredo, Texas mailing address). It is forwarded by diplomatic pouch to Mexico City.
3. Include CVs for each U.S. investigator, reprints of prior work, a study proposal stating objectives and importance to both countries, and a detailed itinerary.
4. List all equipment (vehicles with license and ID numbers, cameras, recorders, scientific instruments, collection gear).
5. List U.S. field participants with personal identification details. Tourist visas are valid only with a Mexican scientific or collecting permit.
6. Request Mexican participation and state any Mexican institutional cooperation and the funding source.
7. Describe the specimens to be collected, collection methods, and analysis. Firearms require prior discussion with the Embassy.
8. Provide copies of required U.S. permits, such as [[CITES]] import permits.
9. Send two copies of results to the Ministry of Foreign Relations through the Embassy when work is complete.

Mexico is described as firm on these steps, and exceptions are unlikely.

**Content requirements for marine vessel research (selected):**
- Specific, inclusive research dates, vessel name and registration, and chief scientist with institution.
- A research prospectus covering target organisms (common and scientific names), area chart, objectives, methodology, data uses, links to other projects, and data storage and availability.
- A commitment to send preliminary and final data reports (four copies) to the Mexican government through the State Department.
- Vessel description (gross tonnage, engines, equipment), captain's name, crew count, other non-scientific personnel, radio frequencies (including continuous emergency monitoring), cruise and port-call plans, scientist CVs, berths for Mexican participants, cruise coordinator, equipment list, funding sources, and funding for Mexican participants.

**Other key points:**
- Compliance does not guarantee approval. Mexico may approve or deny any request for any reason. Research outside the approved scope is illegal.
- Amendments must be requested via diplomatic note before any substantive change. Amendments may delay approval.
- Mexican-chartered vessels require only a statement that a Mexican vessel will be used, plus itinerary details.
- The SRE recommends specimen collection in duplicate, with extras given to Mexico.
- A Spanish translation of the prospectus with the initial request speeds processing, though Spanish is not required.
- The SRE wants data reported back to Mexico. Failure to report can lead to denial of future requests.
- At the June 1986 meeting, the SRE said the full requirements would be formalized into Mexican law "at some time in the near future," without a date. In the interim, the Embassy proposed circulating the synopsis to U.S. scientists.
- The SRE insisted on the 180-day advance notice for all research. The document also contains a passage referring to "120 days" (this may be an OCR or drafting inconsistency). The Embassy advised providing more lead time than the required period, since recent approvals were often granted only days before the work was to start.
- Requests were estimated at roughly six hundred, and the SRE and Embassy acknowledged the processing burden exceeded current staffing. Manpower levels were to be addressed later.
- The Embassy proposed a cover letter stating that the requirements appear excessive and could harm U.S. science in Mexico, while emphasizing that they are legally binding.
