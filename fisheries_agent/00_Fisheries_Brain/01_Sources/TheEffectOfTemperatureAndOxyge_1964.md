# The Effect of Temperature and Oxygen on the Growth Rate of the Windermere Char

## Executive Summary
This study (Swift, 1964) experimentally examined how water **temperature** and **dissolved oxygen concentration** independently affect the growth rate of the [[Windermere Char]] (*Salvelinus alpinus willughbii*), a landlocked subspecies of [[Arctic Char]] confined to Lake Windermere, England. Using constant-environment aquaria, the author determined that char grow optimally between **12°C and 16°C**, with reduced growth above and below this range. Oxygen concentration between **50% and 200% of air saturation** had no significant effect on growth rate at a given temperature, though a weak trend suggested elevated oxygen may enhance growth at higher (14°C) temperatures. These findings closely parallel prior results for the [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), suggesting that thermal physiology alone does not explain the char's narrower geographic distribution relative to trout.

## Background & Description
- Subjects: hatchery-reared char, individually identified via **fin clips**.
- Growth assessed via standard **length and weight** measurements taken before and after each experimental period.
- Fish anaesthetized with **tricaine-methanosulphonate (MS-222)** for handling, weighing, and measuring.
- No morphological anomalies or distinguishing structural traits are discussed; the paper is purely a physiological growth-rate study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range (per Sterba, 1962):** Alpine lakes, Northern Europe, Japan, Alaska, N.E. North America, Greenland, and Iceland — a far more restricted distribution than the wide-ranging [[Brown Trout]].
- The [[Windermere Char]] is a **landlocked, non-migratory population**, believed (per Friend, 1959) to have been isolated as an inbreeding colony for approximately **10,000 years**, having diverged from ancestral **migratory char** populations.
- Migratory char are currently restricted to waters **north of latitude 64°N**, implying intolerance of the warmer waters that landlocked Windermere char inhabit — suggesting the landlocked form may have undergone thermal physiological adaptation to a warmer local environment.
- Habitat implication: char are traditionally associated with **cold, well-oxygenated waters**, distinguishing their presumed niche from that of trout; however, this study's oxygen results challenge the assumption that oxygen availability alone restricts char distribution.
- Experimental husbandry: fish held in 100-liter constant-environment aquaria, 12h light/12h dark photoperiod, fed minced beef liver set in gelatine to satiation (Swift, 1960 method).

## Key Findings

### Temperature Experiments
- **Experiment 1** (4°, 8°, 12°, 16°C): Best growth at **12°C and 16°C**; worst growth at **4°C**.
  - Mean specific growth rate (weight): 5.6 (4°C), 13.2 (8°C), 16.4 (12°C), 17.1 (16°C).
- **Experiment 2** (8°, 14°, 18°C, following re-acclimation): Best growth at **14°C**; reduced growth at both 8°C and 18°C.
  - Mean specific growth rate (weight): 8.0 (8°C), 11.1 (14°C), 4.6 (18°C).
- Combined/normalized results (Fig. 1, expressed relative to 8°C = 100%) confirm an **optimum growth window of 12–16°C**, with growth rate declining at temperatures both above and below this range.
- This optimum is **very similar to that established for [[Brown Trout]]** (~12°C; Swift, 1961), undermining the hypothesis that char have a lower thermal growth optimum than trout despite their more northerly/cold-water distribution.

### Oxygen Experiments
- Conducted at two temperatures (8°C and 14°C) across three oxygen regimes: **50% air saturation, normal (100%) air saturation, and 200% air saturation**.
- **No significant difference in growth rate** was found among oxygen treatments at either fixed temperature.
- Fish at **14°C grew consistently better than fish at 8°C** across all oxygen levels, reaffirming the temperature-driven growth pattern.
- A slight, non-conclusive trend indicated that **higher oxygen concentration (200%) may modestly enhance growth at the higher temperature (14°C)** — mean weight growth rate 10.7 vs. 9.0 (normal) and 8.3 (50%) — but this pattern was not observed at 8°C.
- This lack of oxygen sensitivity within the 50–200% saturation range **mirrors findings in [[Brown Trout]]** (Swift, 1963), reinforcing that oxygen is not a primary limiting factor for growth within this range for either species.

### Broader Implications
- The similarity in thermal growth optima between char and trout suggests that **temperature-driven growth physiology alone cannot explain the char's more restricted geographic distribution**.
- The distributional divergence may instead relate to **historical isolation, competitive exclusion, or thermal tolerance limits of migratory (non-landlocked) char populations**, which remain physiologically uncharacterized in this study.
- Further comparative work on migratory char north of 64°N is identified as necessary to clarify the role of temperature physiology in shaping char biogeography.
