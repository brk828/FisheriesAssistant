# Draft Environmental Assessment: Restoration of A-7 and C-5 Backwaters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #wetland-restoration

## Executive Summary

This draft environmental assessment (EA), prepared in September 1990 by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Yuma Projects Office), evaluates a proposed restoration of two off-channel backwaters, A-7 and C-5, on the [[Colorado River]] in the [[Palo Verde Division]]. The backwaters have silted in, lost circulation, and declined as fish and wildlife habitat since their last maintenance in 1969. The [[Backwaters Committee]] of the Lower Colorado River Work Group, which includes Reclamation, USFWS, AGFD, CDFG, BLM, and CRIT, developed the proposal.

The Proposed Action would dredge channels, replace inlet culverts with [[Percolation Dike]] structures, revegetate dredge-spoil areas with native riparian species, and install [[Fish Habitat Structures]]. The alternative is No Action. The stated objectives are to restore aquatic and riparian habitat, improve the sport fishery, and reduce future maintenance.

## Background & Description

**Setting and history.** A-7 and A-7B lie on the Arizona side of the river near the Interstate 10 bridge. C-5 lies on the California side, slightly downstream. Reclamation channelized this reach in the late 1960s and dredged both backwaters in 1969. Reclamation holds maintenance responsibility under the Colorado River Front Work and Levee System Act, and the backwaters were identified as priority restoration sites.

**Physical condition.** Sediment has plugged the inlet culverts, and sandbars block the downstream outlets. The backwaters are shallow with poor circulation. A-7B is nearly dry. A-7 contains about 23,200 linear feet of channel with an average depth of 3 feet at low flows, and C-5 has about 21,700 feet with similar dimensions.

**Recent disturbance.** High flows from 1983 to 1987 drowned many cottonwood trees and drove marsh vegetation into upland areas. Fires in September 1988 (A-7) and later (C-5) killed or burned large portions of cottonwood-willow habitat.

**Proposed dredging (A-7):**
- About 11,000 linear feet at 10 feet average depth (low flows) and 60 feet average width
- About 4,000 feet of deep channel, 20 feet deep and 100 feet wide, near the inlets, outlet, and middle
- About 462,000 cubic yards removed over 5 months of continuous operation
- About 72 acres of spoil, averaging 4 feet deep
- About 8,200 feet of existing channel left undredged

**Proposed dredging (C-5):**
- About 11,500 linear feet of 10-foot-deep channel, 50 feet wide
- About 3,700 linear feet of 15-foot-deep channel with 8- and 4-foot shelves
- About 255,000 cubic yards removed over about 4 months
- About 53 acres of spoil, averaging 3 feet deep
- About 5,400 feet left undredged

Dredging is planned for early 1991, with revegetation and habitat work over the following 3 to 4 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Releases from [[Parker Dam]] drive fluctuations in the Palo Verde reach. Normal summer releases average about 10,000 cfs and winter releases about 5,000 cfs. Flood releases peaked near 35,000 cfs in 1983 and averaged about 20,000 cfs until 1988. Daily releases can vary by up to 8,000 cfs, producing stage changes of 4 feet or more in the backwaters. Short-term peaks above 15,000 cfs are predicted about every 3 years.

**Water quality.** Known problems in lower Colorado backwaters include low dissolved oxygen, high temperature, and high salinity, all linked to shallow water and poor circulation. Salinity matters more for riparian tree reproduction (cottonwood, willow, honey mesquite) than for fish. [[Selenium]] in bottom sediments exceeded the comparison baseline, though it appears to originate upstream of Davis Dam. Clams sampled from A-7 were clean for the parameters tested (Kubly and Landye 1984). A trace-metal analysis of A-7 and C-5 sediments was still in progress; results were to be added to the final EA.

**Vegetation.** The 1986 mapping shows cottonwood-willow community types (II through V), salt cedar-screwbean mesquite, salt cedar, and marsh types. Cottonwood-willow stands are not pure and often contain only as little as 10 percent cottonwood or willow. [[Salt Cedar]] is the most abundant community on the lower river and has the lowest value for wildlife.

**Wildlife.** Riparian woodland supports about 200 bird species, 13 small mammal species, 14 large mammal species, and 32 reptile and amphibian species. [[Cottonwood-Willow Community]] habitat supports the highest wildlife densities and diversity.

**Fisheries.** Fish surveys by [[Electrofishing Survey]] methods show that fishery productivity follows water depth and connection to the river. A-7 electrofishing in fall 1985 yielded 108 [[Largemouth Bass]], with all age classes represented. A survey in October 1989 yielded only 10 fish, 8 of them largemouth bass. Largemouth bass and [[Channel Catfish]] are important game species, and both have declined.

**Wetlands.** Only a few small pockets of emergent marsh and a narrow band of emergent and riparian vegetation meet federal wetland criteria. Areas flooded in 1983-1987 and classed as marsh are not wetlands because they lack hydric soils and wetland hydrology.

**Special status species.** The project area contains 18 bird species and 5 mammal species of federal or state status, grouped into six habitat categories. Federally listed species identified in informal consultation with USFWS are the [[Bald Eagle]] and [[Yuma Clapper Rail]]. The [[Razorback Sucker]] may now be extirpated from the project area, and reintroduction efforts elsewhere on the lower river have not succeeded.

**Recreation and culture.** Use is mainly day-use fishing, swimming, and canoeing, with heavy summer weekend boating and water skiing. BLM estimated almost 7,500 visits in 1989 (over 80,000 visitor hours) at A-7. No recorded cultural sites exist in either backwater, and Reclamation archaeologists concluded that none are likely.

## Key Findings

- **Purpose:** Restore aquatic and riparian habitat, improve the sport fishery, and reduce maintenance at A-7 and C-5.
- **Dredging volume:** About 717,000 cubic yards in total (462,000 at A-7; 255,000 at C-5), with about 125 acres of spoil (72 at A-7; 53 at C-5).
- **Spoil placement:** Spoil goes to low-value upland sites (bare ground, salt cedar, arrowweed) and is placed in thin layers, avoiding cottonwood, willow, and screwbean mesquite where practicable.
- **Inlet redesign:** Culverts are replaced by permeable percolation dikes, each about 100 feet long. A third dike is added at C-5, and two more are proposed within the C-5 backwater. Dikes are built to at least 2 feet below the river bottom using large rock from existing stockpiles.
- **Undredged habitat:** About 8,200 feet at A-7 and about 6,400 feet at C-5 are left shallow to provide habitat diversity. (Section II gives 5,400 feet for C-5; the discrepancy is in the source.)
- **Dredge channel width:** Restricted to 50 feet except in deep-water areas, to avoid removing shoreline vegetation. 50 feet is the minimum width the dredge can create.
- **Revegetation:** 56 acres are proposed for C-5 plus part of the A-7 spoil areas, using cottonwood, willow, honey mesquite, screwbean mesquite, and native shrubs. Wildlife value is expected to recover after trees reach 3 to 4 years of growth.
- **Fish structures:** Brush structures, rock reefs, pipe bundles, and gravel beds will be placed in deep dredged areas by AGFD, CDFG, and BLM.
- **Fisheries outcome:** Deeper, better-circulated backwaters are expected to increase fish diversity. Turbidity during dredging is expected to stay well below lethal levels.
- **Hydrology:** Permeable dikes should resist plugging and keep water circulating. Surface area will not increase, and effects on main-channel flow are minor.
- **No Action outcome:** Continued sedimentation, declining water quality and fish, marsh filling in, expanding salt cedar, and recurrent fire risk.
- **Compliance:** Informal Section 7 consultation found "no effect" for bald eagle and Yuma clapper rail, pending USFWS concurrence. EPA (404(b)(1) guidelines, April 1990), COE (June 6, 1990), EPA (June 28, 1990), and Arizona Department of Environmental Quality (August 21, 1990) were consulted. A NPDES permit and a Section 10 permit may be needed. No cultural resources are affected.
- **Mitigation:** No compensatory mitigation is proposed, because the project is expected to enhance the functional values of fish and wildlife habitat.
