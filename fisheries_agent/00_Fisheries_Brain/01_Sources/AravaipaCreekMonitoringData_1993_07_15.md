# Aravaipa Creek Fish Monitoring — June 1993

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #NativeFishConservation

## Executive Summary

This memorandum from the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), addressed to The Nature Conservancy (TNC) and the Bureau of Land Management (BLM), summarizes a two-day fish and habitat monitoring survey conducted on **[[Aravaipa Creek]]** (Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona) on **June 15–16, 1993**. Six stations were sampled — three upstream ("east end," near the [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area]]) and three downstream ("west end") of the wilderness boundary. Seining (1/8-inch and 1/4-inch mesh) was the primary capture method across runs, riffles, pools, and side-channel margins. Total catch varied widely among stations, from 452 to 2,329 fish, with catch efficiency (percentage of empty seine hauls) ranging from 0% to 47.5%. The survey documented active breeding condition in **[[Gila robusta]]** (roundtail chub), noted significant turbidity impacts from upstream road construction near Chimney Rock, and recorded heavy cattle grazing pressure at both ends of the study reach.

## Background & Description

Explicit morphological measurements of individual fish are not reported in the surviving text; the memo focuses on population-level and habitat-level data. However, notable biological condition notes include:

- **[[Gila robusta]]** (roundtail chub): One large individual was observed **"running ripe"** (actively releasing gametes, indicating peak spawning readiness). Two additional adults were noted as **tuberculate** — displaying nuptial (breeding) tubercles, a secondary sexual characteristic associated with reproductive activity in cyprinids.
- Age-class data (adult, juvenile, young-of-year categories) were recorded per station in the original fish data tables (Tables II, IV, VI, VIII, X, XII), though the specific numeric breakdowns were not recoverable from the OCR source.
- Species catalogued across stations include **[[Gila robusta]]**, **[[Rhinichthys osculus]]** (speckled dace), **[[Agosia chrysogaster]]** (longfin dace), and an unspecified **sucker** taxon (juvenile life stage noted), likely **[[Catostomus insignis]]** (Sonora sucker), a common native catostomid in this drainage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Aravaipa Creek is a perennial desert stream supporting one of the most intact native fish assemblages in the American Southwest. Habitat and environmental conditions recorded during this survey include:

- **Water temperature**: 18°C–28°C, consistent with warm-water lotic desert stream conditions in early summer.
- **Turbidity**: Moderate creek-wide turbidity overall, but **extremely high turbidity (0% visibility) at Station 2 (Chimney Rock)** due to active road construction upstream — substrate composition could not be determined at this site as a result.
- **Habitat structure**: Stations were characterized by a mix of **runs, riffles, and pools**, with substrate dominated by cobble (CO), pebble (PE), gravel (GR), sand (SA), and minor silt (SI)/rubble (RU) fractions, varying by station and habitat unit.
- **Riparian vegetation**: Dominated by **cottonwoods** and **willows**, with **filamentous green algae** present in the aquatic zone — indicative of a healthy riparian gallery forest typical of Sonoran Desert perennial streams.
- **Anthropogenic pressures**:
  - Active **road construction** above Chimney Rock, correlating with reduced fish catch and elevated turbidity at that station and the Guest House station.
  - **Heavy to moderate cattle grazing** impacts noted at both the east end and west end sampling reaches, a known stressor on riparian integrity and streambank stability in [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area]] tributaries.
  - Large numbers (>100 individuals) of small **red-spotted toads** (*Anaxyrus punctatus*, cf.) observed at Station 9 (Sycamore Tree), indicating co-occurring herpetofaunal activity in the riparian corridor.

Sampling was stratified into an **east end** (upstream of wilderness, Stations 1–3: Turkey Creek, Chimney Rock, TNC Guest House) and a **west end** (downstream of wilderness, Stations 7–9: Wagner Ranch, White's Ranch, Sycamore Tree) design, allowing comparison of native fish community status inside versus outside the protected wilderness reach.

## Key Findings

- **Total fish captured by station**:
  - Station 1 (Turkey Creek): 512 fish; 10 of 39 hauls (25.6%) empty
  - Station 2 (Chimney Rock): 537 fish; 0 of 31 hauls (0.0%) empty
  - Station 3 (Guest House): 452 fish; 2 of 32 hauls (6.3%) empty
  - Station 7 (Wagner Ranch): 583 fish; 47 of 99 hauls (47.5%) empty
  - Station 8 (White's Ranch): 1,019 fish; 21 of 106 hauls (19.8%) empty
  - Station 9 (Sycamore Tree): 2,329 fish; 9 of 104 hauls (8.7%) empty
- **Discharge (flow)** was measured at several stations: Station 2 = 2.5 cms; Station 3 = 1.7 cms; Station 7 = 2.5 cms; Stations 8 and 9 = 1.7 cms each. Flow at Station 1 was not calculated.
- **Downstream ("west end") stations generally yielded far higher total catch** (especially Station 9, with 2,329 fish) compared to upstream ("east end") stations, though this may partly reflect greater sampling effort (more seine hauls) at the west end sites.
- **Reduced catch at Chimney Rock and Guest House stations** is attributed by the field team to disturbance from nearby **road construction**, underscoring the sensitivity of native desert fish populations to sediment and turbidity disturbance.
- Documented **active spawning condition in [[Gila robusta]]** (ripe individual, tuberculate adults) confirms June as an important reproductive period for this species in the Aravaipa Creek system.
- The survey reflects a standardized, repeatable AGFD/BLM/TNC collaborative monitoring protocol (200 m seine transects, consistent mesh sizes, paired habitat and fish data tables) used to track long-term native fish status in **[[Aravaipa Creek]]**, an important refuge for imperiled Southwestern native fish taxa including [[Gila robusta]], [[Agosia chrysogaster]], [[Rhinichthys osculus]], and native catostomids.
