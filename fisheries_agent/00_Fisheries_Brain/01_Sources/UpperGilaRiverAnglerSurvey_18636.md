# Upper Gila River Angler Survey 1998

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae #centrarchidae #recreational-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This Technical Report by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Region V Fisheries Program, Technical Report 98-5-1) documents a 1998 creel survey of anglers on the Upper Gila River and three area lakes in Graham County, Arizona. The survey was motivated by a 1997 department proposal to remove harvest and possession limits on flathead and channel catfish and on largemouth and smallmouth bass. Those limits were removed on the Verde and Salt rivers but kept on the Gila River, because of public resistance and a lack of biological data on angler use. A [[Roving Creel Survey]] ran from April through October 1998 to measure angler use and harvest.

The Upper Gila River functions mainly as a catfish fishery, with peak use in April and May below irrigation diversions. The area lakes provided far more total angling hours and trips, but offered few catfish-directed opportunities. Most anglers reported that removing bag limits would have no effect or would increase their success, and few had ever filled a limit. The authors conclude that river fishing quality depends mainly on annual spring run-off and precipitation.

## Background & Description

The Gila River flows west from New Mexico to the Colorado River near Yuma. [[Coolidge Dam]] (1928) created [[San Carlos Reservoir]], which blocks upstream fish movement. The reach from Coolidge Dam to the New Mexico border is designated critical habitat for the federally endangered [[Razorback Sucker]]. The regulatory concern was therefore that angling regulations might take this species or damage its habitat.

The study area ran about 40 river miles through the [[Gila Valley]], from the San Carlos Apache Reservation boundary near Geronimo to the [[Gila Box Riparian National Conservation Area]]. Flows are dominated by irrigation diversions and returns. Twelve angler access areas were identified near bridges, canal diversions, and levee roads. Fishing was compared with three area lakes:

- [[Roper Lake]] (32 surface acres; a State Park)
- [[Dankworth Pond]] (5 surface acres)
- [[Cluff Pond 3]] (15 surface acres)

[[San Carlos Reservoir]] was excluded because of jurisdictional limits and its distinct boat-based fishery.

Survey design followed the four-step planning process of Malvestuto (1991). Survey days were stratified by month, day type (weekday or weekend), and period (early a.m. or late p.m.). Interviews covered effort, catch, harvest, residence, miles traveled, target species, and opinions on bag-limit removal. Harvest and effort were expanded using Malvestuto (1996). Nonparametric [[Kruskal-Wallis Test]]s were used because most count data were not normally distributed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Upper Gila River fish assemblage.** [[Channel Catfish]] were the dominant harvested species. [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Green Sunfish]] were also present. [[Red Shiner]] was described as exceedingly abundant in recent surveys (in press at the time). [[Threadfin Shad]] were considered probably temporary members of the river assemblage that migrate from San Carlos Reservoir during high flows. Anglers often misidentified schools of shiners as shad.

**Migration and flow.** Channel catfish move upstream to spawn and feed during elevated spring flows, and move downstream to wintering pools or reservoirs when fall and winter flows drop. Flathead catfish are primarily territorial and move only when forced by low water or competition. The authors suggest that the link between shad presence and good catfish angling more likely reflects flow regimes favorable to migratory catfish than a predator-prey relationship.

**Area lakes.** All three lakes are primarily warmwater fisheries. [[Roper Lake]] and [[Cluff Pond 3]] are also stocked with catchable [[Rainbow Trout]] in winter. Anglers at the lakes mainly targeted [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and sunfish. Catfish were rarely a target there.

**Bait use.** Live minnows could contribute non-native bait species to the fish assemblage when fish escape hooks or are released. Their use is therefore relevant to the endangered-species concern.

## Key Findings

**Angler use on the Upper Gila River**

- 76 river creels were conducted between April 15 and October 28, 1998.
- 924 access-area counts were taken (604 weekday, 320 weekend). Counts were evenly divided between early (455) and late (469) periods.
- The majority of counts (842) found no anglers. 96.9% of counts found three or fewer anglers.
- Use was highest between Ft. Thomas Bridge and Curtis Canal Diversion in April and May. Thatcher and Solomon Bridges had no anglers, and only one angler was counted at Safford Bridge.
- The Fort Thomas Diversion was the most heavily used location.
- Significant differences were found in angler counts by location, month, and period, but not by day type.
- 8,152 angler hours were estimated for the river, about 2,467 angler trips (mean trip length 2.9 to 4.3 hours by month), and an estimated 2,527 fish harvested.

**Angler characteristics and success (river)**

- The average river angler (N=158) had fished 2.38 hours, caught 1.25 fish, harvested 0.73 fish, and traveled 19 miles. Mean success rating was 1.63 on a 1 (poor) to 4 (excellent) scale.
- CPUE was 0.53 fish/hour and HPUE was 0.31 fish/hour.
- 89% of anglers resided in Graham County (140/157). Non-residents made up 1.9%.
- Of anglers' target species, 85.0% sought "any catfish" or "catfish," 11.1% sought "anything," 3.3% channel catfish, and 0.7% flathead catfish.
- Catch was 83% channel catfish (164/198), with flathead catfish, common carp, and green sunfish each making up about 5 to 6%.
- Harvest was 89.6% channel catfish (103/115). Channel catfish harvested ranged from 4.5 to 22 inches, and most fish under 10 inches were released.

**Bait and bag-limit opinions (river)**

- 73.7% of anglers (112/154) used live bait, mostly worms (88.4% of live-bait users). Only 7.8% (12/154) used live minnows.
- When asked whether removing the 6-bass and 25-catfish bag limit would increase, decrease, or not affect their success (N=124): 85.5% said no effect, 3.2% said increase, and 11.3% said decrease.
- Only 14.5% (18/124) had filled a bag limit in the previous 15 years. Only two bag limits were reported in 1998, and none were observed by survey staff.

**Area lake angling**

- 215 angler surveys were conducted: Cluff Pond 3 (N=102), Dankworth Pond (N=56), and Roper Lake (N=57). Data from additional Cluff Pond 3 surveys from concurrent management work were included.
- 797 anglers were counted: 487 at Cluff Pond 3, 73 at Dankworth Pond, and 237 at Roper Lake.
- Shore, boat, and total anglers were highest in April and May, and more were found at Cluff Pond 3 and Roper Lake.
- Estimated area-lake effort was 31,880 angler hours and 8,855 angler trips (mean trip length 3.6 hours).
- Average area-lake angler (N=166) had fished 1.8 hours, caught 0.75 fish, harvested 0.17 fish, and traveled 47 miles. CPUE was 0.42 fish/hour and HPUE was 0.10 fish/hour.
- Of anglers, 70.7% were Graham County residents (118/167). Non-residents were 1.8%.
- Target species: 55.3% "anything," 20.6% largemouth bass, 9.9% sunfish, 5.7% channel catfish, 5.0% rainbow trout, and 3.5% black crappie.
- Catch was 64.3% bluegill (81/126) and 19.0% largemouth bass. Only 29 fish were harvested, mostly sunfish (69.0%) and largemouth bass (27.6%).

**Comparison and economic contribution**

- The Upper Gila River supplied 20% (8,152 of 40,032) of total Gila Valley angler hours but 44% (2,527 of 5,715) of harvested fish.
- River anglers targeted catfish for 7,255 hours (89% of river hours). Area lakes provided only 1,913 catfish angler hours (6% of lake hours).
- Using the national average of $50 per angler trip (USFWS 1996), estimated contributions were $123,350 for the river and $442,750 for the area lakes. The authors note both are likely overestimates, with the river figure probably more so because of its high proportion of local anglers.
- River CPUE (0.53 fish/hour) exceeded the 0.50 CPUE performance goal in the department's Wildlife 2000 Strategic Plan.

**Conclusions and management recommendations**

- The authors conclude that removing bag and harvest limits on bass and catfish would not negatively affect fishing success or catfish numbers on the Upper Gila River, because limits are rarely reached.
- Restricting live bait-fish use on the Upper Gila River would have little impact on anglers or bait dealers, since only 7.8% used minnows.
- Quality fishing depends mainly on annual spring run-off and precipitation, so management options are limited. Future surveys should focus on April and May at the most-used locations.
- The authors recommend assessing and enhancing catfish populations in Cluff Pond 3, Dankworth Pond, and Roper Lake to support year-round quality fisheries during years when river flows do not produce good catfish fishing.
- Results should be widely distributed, and public input should inform future decisions.
