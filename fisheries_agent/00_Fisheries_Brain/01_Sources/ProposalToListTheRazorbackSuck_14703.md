# Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team Request to List Razorback Sucker as Threatened

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #catostomidae #conservation #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This November 3, 1980 letter from the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] formally requests threatened status listing for the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The request is based on comprehensive status assessments from both upper and lower [[Colorado River Basin]], documenting severe population decline, lack of successful recruitment, habitat loss from dam construction and channelization, and increased predation pressure from introduced species. The species was once among the most abundant fishes in the lower Colorado River but now shows no evidence of significant recruitment despite documented spawning attempts.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is an endemic fish of the [[Colorado River]] system. Historically, it was one of the most abundant fishes in the lower Colorado River. The species is a member of the sucker family ([[Catostomidae]]) and represents a unique component of the basin's native fish fauna.

In the **Upper Basin**, [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] personnel report that razorback suckers can be caught in specific areas of upper and lower reaches of the [[Green River]] when special efforts are made, but not during general surveys. They are caught less frequently than the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). Most critically, intensive study of rare endemic fish in the upper basin has shown no evidence of razorback sucker reproduction, unlike the Colorado squawfish which shows reproductive activity.

In the **Lower Basin**, the species' decline is attributed to physical and biological alterations caused by construction and operation of seven dams, channelization projects, and competition and predation by introduced game and nongame species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning Habitat Requirements**: A 1979-80 study of [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] (a pump-storage reservoir in Imperial County) documented specific spawning habitat characteristics. Spawning areas had similar slope (30%) and clean, loose gravel composition (1/4-2" diameter gravel). Spawning areas in [[Lake Mohave]] and in the upper river near [[Grand Junction, Colorado]] ([[Walter Walker Wildlife Area]]) may be similar.

The presence of annually renewed gravel bars, side channels and gravel shoreline in the lower Colorado River has decreased significantly since the dam building era. Only the Parker and Mohave divisions of the river and Lakes Mohave and Havasu have gravel substrate comparable to that used by spawning razorback suckers.

**Reservoir Operations Impact**: Reservoir operation in Senator Wash Reservoir caused water level fluctuations during February and March 1980, resulting in mortality of eggs and larvae on observed spawning areas. Dam and reservoir operations during spawning months may be a critical factor in egg and larval mortality in the lower river.

**Predation Pressure**: SCUBA observations at Senator Wash Reservoir revealed that [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) followed gravid females in spawning areas and actively fed over recent spawning sites. The presence of [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) in spawning areas was consistently recorded, though predation was not directly observed.

Illegal introductions of [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*) and [[Tilapia]] (*Tilapia* sp.) in Senator Wash Reservoir add to the predator list and enhance likelihood of distribution throughout the lower river above [[Imperial Dam]].

The abundance of [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictus olivaris*) has increased significantly from Mexico upstream to [[Parker Dam]]. A netting survey the week of June 23, 1980 resulted in first documentation of flathead catfish above Parker Dam in [[Lake Havasu]]. The acceptable size of prey items for large flathead catfish (15 lb) exceeds that of other predators except [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*). Remaining razorback sucker young-of-the-year, yearlings and even subadult fish are subject to additional predation.

## Key Findings

**Population Status and Recruitment Failure**:
- No evidence of significant recruitment has been found since the proposed threatened status by USFWS (Federal Register, April 23, 1978)
- Viable eggs and larvae were collected from two distinct spawning areas in Senator Wash Reservoir during 1979-80, but no young-of-the-year or yearlings have been found
- Two young razorback suckers (12"-15") were captured in the canal system of [[Parker Valley]] early in 1980 during annual canal drawdowns, likely originating from the lower Colorado River between Parker Dam and [[Headgate Rock Dam]]

**Threats to Population Viability**:
- Increased distribution and abundance of predator fish populations
- Planned channelization projects at Yuma, Parker, Havasu and Mohave divisions of the lower river
- Many proposed energy development projects such as the [[Hoover Dam Modification Project]]
- Significant loss of spawning habitat (gravel bars, side channels, gravel shoreline) since dam building era
- Water level fluctuations during spawning months (February-March) causing egg and larval mortality

**Recovery Team Recommendations**:
- Immediate listing of razorback sucker as threatened species
- Continued efforts by U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Colorado basin states to obtain more knowledge of this unique species
- Recognition of general lack of knowledge regarding razorback sucker life history range, habitat requirements and population status
- Recovery team commits to assist in any way possible with listing efforts

The new information substantiates that razorback suckers are attempting to reproduce, may not have much spawning habitat available that is not threatened or controlled by man, and predation on various life stages is increasing.
