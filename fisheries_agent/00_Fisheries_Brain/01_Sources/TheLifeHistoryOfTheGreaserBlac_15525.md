# Life History of the Greaser Blackfish (*Orthodon microlepidotus*) in Clear Lake, Lake County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1950 study by [[Garth I. Murphy]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] (California Division of Fish and Game) documents the life history of the [[Greaser Blackfish]], a cyprinid of [[Clear Lake]] in central California. The work covers feeding, spawning, early postlarval life, growth, and the commercial fishery. The authors conclude that the species is a valuable forage fish for warm-water game fisheries, because it is prolific, does not compete with game fish for food or spawning grounds, and its young remain in the forage size class through their first year. Commercial harvest of the species in Clear Lake was halted in 1948 to increase the lake's forage supply.

## Background & Description

- **Common names:** Called "greaser" in the San Joaquin Valley. The name "hardhead" in older literature now belongs to *Mylopharodon conocephalus*.
- **Taxonomy and range:** *Orthodon* is a monotypic genus. The species is native only to the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Drainage]] and the [[Pajaro River]] system. It was introduced to [[Carmel Reservoir]] on the Carmel River.
- **Study site:** [[Clear Lake]] lies at about 1,300 feet elevation and covers approximately 40,000 acres. It supports a typical California warm-water fish assemblage.
- **Methods:** Young and yearling fish were taken by seining with quarter-inch square-mesh nets in shoal areas. Most adults came from commercial fishermen's catches. All lengths are fork length, measured from the snout tip to the fork of the caudal fin.
- **Sex-based appearance:** During spawning, males appeared black and females olive.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and niche.** Native blackfish live in lowland shallow lakes and sluggish sloughs and avoid even moderately swift water. Unlike most native Sacramento-San Joaquin carps, the species spawns in lakes. Its feeding niche closely resembles that of the [[Gizzard Shad]] of eastern North America, a valued forage species.

**Feeding and gill rakers.** The intestinal tract is long and convoluted; a 17-inch female had an intestine 108 inches long. The authors examined stomachs and intestines of 10 adults collected in February 1949. Stomachs were empty, but the intestines held diatoms, entomostracan eggs and crustaceans, colonial and filamentous green and blue-green algae, desmids, rotifers, detritus, and occasionally fine sand. Plant and animal matter were ingested roughly in the proportions encountered, so vegetable matter predominated. Fine feeding relies on the [[Gill Rakers]]: each raker branches into multiple strands that form a broom-like straining structure, and these closely spaced brooms make an efficient filter for minute food such as diatoms and rotifers.

**Feeding by young.** Among 25 young fish (1.6 to 6.8 inches), collected June through September 1949, feeding was partly selective. Fish under 8 inches showed individual differences, with some guts dominated by Cladocera, others by green algae, midges, or detritus. Fish above 3 inches (11 specimens) fed indiscriminately, as adults do.

**Spawning.** Spawning is linked to lake temperature (see Table 1 in the source, a 1949 thermograph record). On April 13 and 14, 1949, a group of eight spawning fish was observed four miles north of Lakeport. A female worked through plant material at about three feet deep, and a male followed to fertilize the adhesive eggs. Eggs hatched in 14 days at a mean of 58°F in an aquarium; the authors note they probably hatch faster in the lake. Spawning is protracted, with spawning females taken as late as June 23, 1947, which likely explains the wide size range of young of the year.

**Early postlarval life.** Fry occupy the littoral zone, with schools readily found along shores in late April and May. They stay in shore areas through their first summer, declining in number mainly through predation. In their first winter they leave the shoals and do not return until they spawn. This was inferred from the absence of intermediate-sized fish in several hundred fine-mesh seine hauls, with one exception.

**Growth.** Growth was based on 189 sexed scale samples, with age read from scale annuli using the direct proportional method. Growth is slow in the first year, greatest in the second, considerable in the third, and relatively small after the first spawning. Females grow faster than males after the first year.

**Interaction with other species.** Murphy (1949) found young blackfish to be the most important fish food of young [[Largemouth Black Bass]] during midsummer. Bass ate blackfish far more than they ate [[Bluegill]], even though bluegill were more numerous. The authors attribute this to blackfish staying in open water, while bluegill seek cover. Blackfish spawn early, around the same time as largemouth bass, so they may buffer predation on bass fry.

## Key Findings

### Feeding
- Adults ingested plant and animal plankton plus bottom material in roughly the proportions encountered, with a preponderance of plant matter.
- Gill-raker structure lets adults strain diatoms and rotifers, which are very small.
- Young fish under 8 inches fed partly selectively; those above 3 inches fed indiscriminately.

### Reproduction and Fecundity
- Spawning occurs in April, May, and June. Eggs are adhesive and placed on plants at about three feet of depth.
- A 17.0-inch female contained approximately 350,000 maturing eggs. The species is described as very prolific.
- Most individuals first spawn at the end of their third year; a few spawn at the end of their second year.
- Most fish die after a second spawning. This was supported by the absence of twice-spawned fish in commercial catches and by numbers of dead adults in the lake after spawning.

### Growth and Age
- Scale-based mean lengths by age (fork length, inches):

- Of 54 adult females, 10 were at the end of their fourth year (age 5). Of 50 males, none had spawned more than once.

### Commercial Fishery
- Until 1948, blackfish were seined with carp, held in live cars, and trucked alive to San Francisco. Fishermen received about $0.30 per pound.
- Recorded annual catches ranged from 4,164 to 268,242 pounds. A footnote gives an approximate conversion to numbers by dividing weight by 2.
- Commercial catches consisted almost entirely of fish at the end of their fourth year. Mesh size apparently let most age-3 spawners escape.
- Taking of blackfish was prohibited in 1948 to increase the forage supply of Clear Lake.

### Management Implications
- The species is useful for warm-water fisheries. It converts lake food directly to flesh and does not compete for spawning grounds.
- Competition for space is the main risk. It could let blackfish crowd out game fish where conditions favor blackfish and disfavor game fish. Blackfish apparently overran [[Lake Hughes]] in Los Angeles County and were eliminated there, with nearby waters, by chemical treatment in 1949.
- In 1949 blackfish were stocked in [[East Park Reservoir]], Colusa County, to improve a poor forage supply. Early spawning should precede the reservoir drawdown, which probably limits bluegill reproduction, and blackfish do not depend on insects, which are scarce in fluctuating reservoirs.
- The authors suggest blackfish may be useful as forage in other waters, especially fluctuating reservoirs.
