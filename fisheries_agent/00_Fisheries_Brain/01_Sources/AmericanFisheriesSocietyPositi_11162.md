## Executive Summary

This 1992 American Fisheries Society (AFS) Position Statement addresses the genetic risks posed by **stock transfers** — the movement of individuals from one genetically distinct stock into waters inhabited by another stock of the same species. Unlike non-native species introductions (already addressed by a prior AFS position statement, Kohler and Courtenay 1986), stock transfers had received comparatively little regulatory attention despite their potential to disrupt locally adapted, [[Coadapted Gene Complex|coadapted gene complexes]] and cause **[[Outbreeding Depression]]**. The authors, writing on behalf of the AFS Genetics Section, call for the elimination of stock transfers across federal, state, tribal, and provincial fisheries management jurisdictions in North America, and outline a six-point course of action for genetic resource protection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although not framed in traditional niche terms, the paper's core ecological-evolutionary argument centers on **local adaptation**:

- Each stock's local environment (physical, chemical, biological) imposes a **unique selection regime** (Futuyma 1986), driving divergence among stocks of the same species.
- **Stock** is defined as a randomly breeding group of individuals maintaining spatial, temporal, or behavioral integrity relative to other such groups — the operational unit of genetic and management concern, potentially distinct from "population" in scale.
- Restricted gene flow among stocks (via natural spatial, temporal, or behavioral barriers) permits **genetic drift** and **[[Natural Selection|natural selection]]**-driven divergence (Mayr 1942, 1982; Barton and Charlesworth 1984).
- Disruption of natural isolating mechanisms through anthropogenic stock transfer creates opportunity for **introgressive hybridization**, potentially eroding fitness advantages accumulated over generations of local adaptation.
- Case examples cited include:
  - [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarkii*) — polytypic genetic distribution concerns (Allendorf and Leary 1988)
  - [[Rainbow Trout]] coexistence with native stocks in the Kootenai River drainage (Allendorf et al. 1980)
  - [[Lake Trout]] strain differentiation in Lake Ontario stocking programs (Krueger et al. 1989)
  - [[Chinook Salmon]] genetic population structure in the Pacific Northwest (Utter et al. 1989)
  - [[Largemouth Bass]] (northern vs. Florida subspecies) reciprocal hybrid survival/growth studies (Philipp and Whitt 1991)
  - [[Brown Trout]] genetic resource conservation in Scandinavia (Ryman 1981)

## Key Findings

- **Two components of genetic variation** are critical to long-term species survival (Wright 1978):
  1. **Intra-stock variability** — variation among individuals within a stock; the raw material for [[Natural Selection]]. Its loss reduces adaptive potential and is exacerbated by small effective population sizes, inbreeding, and genetic drift (Falconer 1981; Kapuscinski and Jacobson 1987).
  2. **Inter-stock variability** — variation among stocks, reflecting evolutionary adaptation to distinct local environments (Chakraborty and Leimar 1987).

- Species are typically composed of **multiple genetically distinct stocks** rather than single panmictic populations — the foundation of the **[[Stock Concept of Management]]** (Kutkuhn 1981; Ihssen et al. 1981; MacLean and Evans 1981).

- **Stock transfers** — moving individuals between genetically distinct stocks — risk:
  - Disrupting **coadapted gene complexes** built through generations of divergent selection (Dobzhansky 1948)
  - Causing **outbreeding depression**, a decline in fitness that may worsen across successive hybrid generations (Templeton 1986; Philipp 1991)
  - Producing unpredictable evolutionary outcomes, with restoration of pre-introduction fitness levels (if possible at all) potentially requiring many generations (Allendorf and Leary 1988)

- Three real-world scenarios were identified as drivers of unwise stock transfer practices:
  1. Centralized federal hatcheries serving geographically broad, genetically distant recipient waters
  2. Inter-agency "trading systems" exchanging species/stocks to meet production quotas
  3. Unregulated private hatchery production leading to unauthorized introductions or escapement

- Available techniques for assessing stock divergence at the time included **protein electrophoresis**, **cytogenetic analysis**, and emerging **DNA sequence divergence** methods — though these techniques may still fail to detect additional substructure driven by quantitative traits (e.g., spawning time, habitat preference) (Utter 1981; Utter et al. 1989).

- **AFS Course of Action** (six recommendations) called for agencies and the private sector to:
  1. Eliminate stock transfers within their jurisdictions via updated management practices
  2. Abolish inter-agency "trading systems" that cause stock transfer
  3. Strengthen and enforce private-sector regulations on production, sale, and transport of aquatic organisms
  4. Build consensus on stock-defining criteria and develop genetic variation databases
  5. Form species-level working groups to delineate stock boundaries, designate **genetic reserves**, and create genetically informed management plans
  6. Develop public education initiatives on stock transfer risks

- The position statement explicitly acknowledges that **no consensus yet existed** on formal criteria defining a "stock" (Waples 1991b), but argues that regulatory action should not wait for methodological refinement given the urgency of genetic resource loss.
