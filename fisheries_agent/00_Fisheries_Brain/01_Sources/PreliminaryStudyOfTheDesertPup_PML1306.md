# Desert Pupfish and Habitat at Quitobaquito Springs, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This technical report (Cooperative National Park Resources Studies Unit, Report No. 1) by [[Boyd E. Kynard]] of the University of Arizona, prepared for the [[National Park Service]] Western Region, documents a preliminary study of the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) and its habitat at Quitobaquito Pool, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona. The study ran from March 1975 to August 1976 and addressed three areas: population demography, physico-chemical conditions, and breeding habitat requirements. The pool is spring-fed, chemically stable, and generally suitable for fish, but dissolved oxygen and temperature extremes occur seasonally. Breeding is constrained by the scarcity of shallow, warm, sand- or firm-bottomed edge habitat. The report concludes with management recommendations, including restoring a warm spring outflow channel to extend the breeding season and establishing a long-term minnow-trap monitoring program.

## Background & Description

The Quitobaquito spring-fed pool is described as a unique feature of the [[Lower Sonoran Desert]]. Although it scarcely resembles the original springs and seeps, it has been altered for centuries by Native Americans and European settlers. Its fish fauna centers on the Desert Pupfish, a small cyprinodontid that is the focus of the study.

The study was conducted under a Master Memorandum of Understanding between the National Park Service and the University of Arizona, which established the [[Cooperative National Park Resources Studies Unit]] based in the Department of Biology. Work was carried out through monthly field trips from March 1975 to August 1976, with sampling dates including 20 March, 29 April, 23 May, 14 July, 28 August, 22 October, 4–5 December 1975, and 10–11 June and 15–16 July 1976.

The pool was altered in February 1962, when the National Park Service deepened the former reed-choked marsh into a pool of fairly uniform 90–100 cm depth, leaving only two shallow ledges on the northeast and east sides. Dense aquatic vascular plants, including [[Chara]] and [[Potamogeton]], covered most of the bottom by 1964.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Earlier data (Bryan 1925; Cole and Whiteside 1965; Kidd and Wade 1965) were limited. In October 1975 the spring water contained calcium 37 mg/L, magnesium 13 mg/L, sodium 210 mg/L, bicarbonate 295 mg/L, sulfate 108 mg/L, chloride 14 mg/L, nitrate 9.1 mg/L, and total solids 146 mg/L. Nitrate and phosphate were not present in pollution-level amounts. The pool pH averaged 9.0 over 12 months, is strongly buffered by bicarbonates, and was 8.7 at dawn (0510 hrs) on 10 April 1976, only 0.4 units below the afternoon value.

**Temperature and oxygen.** The spring water was about 24.5 °C at the east spring head and 22.9 °C at the observation pool, compared with 9 to 15 °C in the main pool during winter. A continuous 45-day thermograph in the northeast shallows recorded daily maxima and minima. Dissolved oxygen fell to 0.5 mg/L on 14 July 1975 in the bottom of dense vegetation, but no summer fish kills were observed in 1975 or 1976. The species' reported oxygen tolerance is 0.13 mg/L (Lowe and Heath 1969).

**Water level and turbidity.** Water level dropped 5.0 cm in the fall of 1975 and returned to its original level by July 1976. A slight algae bloom occurred from 20 May to 16 July 1976, which was absent in 1975. The spring flow had diminished greatly since Bryan's 1917 measurement of 43 gal/min.

**Population structure.** The Peterson mark-recapture estimates were 7,996 fish (22 October 1975; 95% CI 6,229 to 9,589) and 9,361 fish (10 June 1976; 95% CI 3,073 to 3,996 as reported in the text, with the total of approximately 9,361). Adults were 90.5% and 93.3% of the total in the two surveys respectively. Approximately 50% of the population was lost during winter. Fish are long-lived to a maximum of three years, and the sex ratio was 43.7:57.2 males to females in June 1976 (χ² = 3.14, P > 0.05), not significantly different from 50:50.

**Habitat use.** Before 1962 the fish used warm flowing spring water or shallow, weed-choked marsh. After the pool was deepened, fish concentrated in the north third of the pool in winter and spread over the whole pool in July. In December they were largely confined to the warmest vegetated shallows and were inactive below about 13 °C. Dissolved oxygen in dense vegetation became extremely low at night.

**Breeding.** Males establish territories along the shallow pool edge (6 inches deep). These edges offer solid root-and-earth substrate, warm temperatures, and open water. Dense vegetation was associated with low oxygen and muck substrate. Breeding males were spaced at about one per 20–30 cm along the edge. Fry were first seen on 29 April 1975 and 28 May 1976, when noon water temperature reached 24.5 °C and 28.3 °C respectively. Breeding ceased between 14 July and 28 August 1975.

## Key Findings

- **Population size:** Estimated at 7,996 fish in October 1975 and 9,361 in June 1976, with about 50% winter mortality. The cause appeared biological (food, predation) rather than physico-chemical.
- **Age and growth:** Fish reach a maximum of three years. Mean length increased rapidly in year one (31.5 mm), then slowed to 8.2 mm and 1.8 mm in later years.
- **Activity:** Pupfish are strictly diurnal. Catches in December and April peaked during the warmest part of the day, while July catches were distributed across the day with a midday dip.
- **Trap catches:** 248 fish were taken in the warmest part of the day in December, against 40 fish in the cold morning hours.
- **Habitat preference:** Shallow water over substrate or vegetation was preferred in December and April. In July, 69% of the catch was taken in deep-water traps.
- **Breeding preference:** Males chose shallow sites (about 1.5 to 10 cm deep) and firm sand over silt. No preference was shown for shelter or pool-wall cover.
- **Breeding constraint:** Warm, shallow, open breeding habitat is in short supply. The 1962 deepening of the pool reduced it.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Clear the reed-choked drainage between the observation pool and the main pool (about 30 m) and line it with concrete and sand to restore warm-water breeding access, extending the breeding season by two to three months.
  - Avoid major pool alteration until long-term survival in artificial habitat is better understood.
  - Establish annual fall minnow-trap monitoring using about 20 traps at the 1975 locations, with two dozen traps at approximately $2.50 each.
  - Monitor seasonal water quality for pesticides and nutrients from agricultural use and pumping about one mile south of the spring.
