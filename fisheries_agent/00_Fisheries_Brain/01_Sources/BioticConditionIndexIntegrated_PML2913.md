# Biotic Condition Index: Integrated Biological, Physical and Chemical Stream Parameters for Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Forest Service report, prepared under contract for the [[Intermountain Region]] by [[Robert N. Winget]] (Brigham Young University) and [[Fred A. Mangum]] (Uinta National Forest), presents the **[[Biotic Condition Index]] (BCI)**, a stream-management tool that combines aquatic macroinvertebrate community data with stream habitat and water quality parameters. The index compares an observed community tolerance value against a predicted value for the same stream conditions. The authors argue that conventional [[Dominance Diversity Indices]] are unreliable for stream management, and that the BCI supplies the biological component missing from the [[General Aquatic Wildlife System]] (GAWS) management model.

## Background & Description

- **Program context:** The Forest Service Intermountain Region was developing a stream water quality/habitat management program integrating water column, substrate, bank vegetation, bank stability, channel stability, and a biological component (macroinvertebrates) within GAWS.
- **Study phases:** Phase I synthesized ideas in "A Systematic Approach to Management by Objective for USFS Stream Fisheries." Phase II completed the work, producing the BCI paper reprinted in full.
- **Funding and support:** Forest Service contract (No. 40-84MM-9-524); [[Utah Power and Light Company]] supported the [[Aquatic Ecology Laboratory]] at Brigham Young University (1970–1979).
- **Data sources:** Files of the Forest Service Aquatic Ecology Lab (Uinta National Forest) and the Brigham Young University lab.
- **Personnel:** Winget (principal investigator), Mangum (co-principal investigator), C. Selby Herrin (data processing), and Donald K. Dunham (contracting officer representative).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Modeling philosophy.** The authors cite [[Green (1979)]], [[Skellam (1969)]], and [[Levins (1966)]] to argue that models must be fit to a specific purpose and include only the components relevant to that purpose. Linear models are poor fits for species that follow unimodal distributions along environmental gradients, which produce the so-called [[Horseshoe Effect]] in ordination.

**Habitat, water quality, and biota.** Macroinvertebrate communities respond to substrates, riparian vegetation, riffle/pool ratios, flow regimes, elevation, gradient, water temperature, upstream land use, and water quality. The authors note that what is natural for one stream is not natural for another; two unperturbed streams can differ two-fold or more in production.

**Diversity indices and natural variation.** The text describes several cases where diversity indices misled interpretation:
- High mountain headwaters in the [[Uinta Mountains]] are simplified by near-distilled water quality, not pollution.
- Desert streams in Utah with shifting sands and extreme temperature and discharge fluctuations have low diversity but natural communities.
- In the [[Plum Creek Oil Spill]] study (Masnick et al., 1976; Hoehn et al., 1974), diversity values did not reflect the spill's effects.
- Moderate pollution can raise diversity by replacing fragile taxa with hardier ones, while severe pollution reduces it.

**Study site example.** The [[West Fork Duchesne River]] stations above and below the Vat Diversion illustrate the method.

## Key Findings

**1. Model structure (study plan).**
- Raw biological and physical/chemical data from **164 station dates** were regressed to find significant relationships, drawn from over 20 streams.
- Four determinants were selected for the predictive model: **total alkalinity** (density/biomass), **sulfate** (water quality), **stream gradient** (maintenance and recovery), and **substrate composition** (microhabitat diversity).
- **54 taxa** were initially assigned tolerance quotients; this grew to a total of **371 taxa** using species associations.

**2. Correlations with number of taxa** (significant at P = 0.001 where marked; N = 100 of 164 for these tests):

| Relationship | Correlation (r) |
|---|---|
| Taxa & total alkalinity | +0.479 |
| Taxa & sulfate | −0.668 |
| Taxa & gradient (transformed) | +0.520 |
| Taxa & transformed site index | +0.701 |

The site index (assigned habitat value) explains about 49 percent of variation in taxa number (r² = 0.49). The authors state that the remaining variation reflects human-caused perturbations not modeled.

**3. Tolerance quotients.** Each taxon's TQ is the product of four component values (alkalinity, sulfate, gradient, substrate). Example: *Ephemerella grandis* scores 2 × 1 × 3 × 4 = 24. TQs range from 2 to 108. Tables 1–4 give station counts for *[[Ephemerella Grandis]]*, *[[Ephemerella Doddsi]]*, *[[Cheumatopsyche]]*, and *[[Brachycentrus]]*. Table 5 lists TQs for hundreds of taxa. Table 6 is a key to predicted community tolerance quotients (CTQp) by gradient, substrate, alkalinity, and sulfate.

**4. Index calculation.**
- Predicted community tolerance quotient (CTQp) is the mean TQ of a predicted community.
- Actual community tolerance quotient (CTQa) is the mean TQ of sampled taxa.
- **BCI = (CTQp / CTQa) × 100**, expressed as percent of predicted; it usually ranges from 45 to 100.
- Predicted values were derived from over 700 samples from 65 stations on 20 streams.

**5. Stream rating scheme.** Habitat and BCI values set a management strategy:

| Strategy | Habitat | BCI |
|---|---|---|
| Maintain high quality | High | >90 |
| Habitat improvement | High–moderate | 65–80 (or >95 as stated) |
| Habitat improvement | Low | <70 |
| Water quality improvement | High–moderate | <90 |
| Habitat and water quality improvement | Low | <70 |

The table in the source is partly garbled by OCR; the BCI thresholds above follow the most consistent reading.

**6. Management priorities.** Streams with good habitat and healthy macroinvertebrate communities receive top priority. Streams with poor habitat but healthy biota come next. The authors state that healthy macroinvertebrates indicate water quality adequate for a healthy fishery.

**7. Case study: West Fork Duchesne River.** Both stations were predicted at CTQp = 50.
- *Above Vat Diversion:* 36 taxa (spring 1977), declining to 30 (spring 1978). BCI fell from **110%** to **91%**, with a drop to **81%** by spring 1978 as recorded in the table.
- *Below Vat Diversion:* 40 taxa (March 1977), 29 by November 1977 and April 1978. BCI was **98–103%** in 1977 and fell to **87.7%** by April 1978.
- The diversity indices (Shannon-Weaver *d* and Brillouin *H*) did not reflect these changes.
- The likely cause was the 1977 drought with low flows, which may have allowed anchor ice to scour substrates. The authors flag the result as a warning requiring monitoring for 2 to 3 more years.

**8. Conclusions.** The BCI is sensitive to multiple stress types, applicable across stream types, gives a linear assessment from unstressed to highly stressed conditions, is independent of sample size when samples are representative, relies on readily available data, and integrates with existing habitat and water quality programs.

**9. Sampling method.** Benthic samples used a modified [[Surber Sampler]] (Winget, 1970) with 280-micron mesh, four samples per date per station. Field records cover location, elevation, gradient, substrate composition, riparian vegetation, land and water use, alkalinity, sulfate, clarity, and discharge.
