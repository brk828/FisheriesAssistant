# Production and Angler Harvest of Wild Brook Trout in Lawrence Creek, Wisconsin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Wisconsin Conservation Department Technical Bulletin (No. 35, 1966) by Robert L. Hunt reports the production, standing crop and angler harvest of a wild [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) population in [[Lawrence Creek]] over 1960–64, with a lifetime-production analysis of three year classes (1959–61) from emergence to extinction. Production is defined as growth by all fish in a population during a period, including growth by fish that subsequently died. Estimates were built from three annual [[Electrofishing]] population estimates, monthly growth samples (1963), and a compulsory [[Creel Census]], the only such census operating on a Wisconsin trout stream. Annual production for the whole stream was remarkably stable (83.6–96.3 lb/acre), while the upper and lower reference sections differed markedly in density and output.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** [[Lawrence Creek]] is a 3.36-mile spring-fed trout stream, with a surface drainage of 6.4 square miles and a groundwater drainage of 16.8 square miles. It lies about 40 miles south-southeast of the geographic centre of Wisconsin and holds the best wild brook trout population in Marquette County.
- **Study sections:** Sections A (3.81 acres, upper development zone) and B (3.15 acres, reference zone) were mapped in detail in 1963. Sections C and D were included with less precise data.
- **Life history terms:** Brook trout spawn in autumn; hatching is early the following year. Age group 0 emerges on February 1 and becomes age group I after 11 months, so the first "year of life" is 11 months long.
- **Context:** The study was part of a larger project on how habitat alteration (overhanging bank cover, current deflectors, 1964 in section A) affects the trout population, its food supply and angler harvest. It was funded under Dingell-Johnson project F-60-R.
- **Terminology:** The bulletin defines production, accumulated production, negative production, potential production, standing crop, biomass, angler harvest, year class and age group. Instantaneous rates of growth (g), mortality (i) and biomass change (k) are linked by k = g − i.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical habitat**
- Sections A and B were classified by bottom type, pools and permanent bank cover. Section A was about 48.8% sand and 46.7% silt (gravel about 4.5%). Section B was about 50.8% sand, 37.1% silt and 11.8% gravel. Section B had more gravel and a greater share of bottom in pools in some measures.
- Lower sections C and D have lower gradient, more long sandy flats and silt, and probably more deep pools and permanent cover than A and B.

**Hydrology and temperature**
- Annual rainfall averages 29 inches. Baseflow is about 3.5 cfs at the tributary junction, rising to about 20 cfs at the mouth. Flow is very stable between years, which the authors attribute to a large deep aquifer feeding scenic springs.
- Water temperature normally ranges from 32°F (5–10 days each winter) to about 75°F (1–3 days each summer). Weekly means ran from about 35°F to 65°F.

**Water chemistry (March 1963, before spring thaw)**
- Total alkalinity 162 ppm (as CaCO₃); calcium 43.5 ppm; magnesium 16.0 ppm; nitrate-nitrogen 1.80 ppm; phosphate-P 0.020 ppm; pH 8.0; specific conductance 272.8 µmhos at 25°C.

**Food resource**
- Unpublished data from David White covered four principal invertebrate food organisms. Average bottom-fauna biomass was about 1,196 lb/acre in section A and 1,413 lb/acre in section B in 1963, roughly 24–26 pounds of food per pound of trout. This contrasts with [[Allen (1951)]] on the Horokiwi Stream, where food appeared to limit trout production.

**Associated fish and invertebrates**
- Competitors and prey noted in the discussion include [[Sculpins]] (*Cottus perplexus*) and stonefly naiads (*Acroneuria pacifica*), used in laboratory stream work by Davis and Warren (1965).

## Key Findings

**Production and standing crop**
- Standing crops were higher in B than A in 58 of 60 consecutive months. Mean monthly biomass was 44 lb/acre in A and 60 lb/acre in B.
- Mean annual production was 48% greater in B (104 lb/acre) than in A (70 lb/acre). Maximum monthly production occurred in June in both sections in all five years.
- Production was negative in 10 of 60 months in A and 8 of 60 in B, mainly November–December during spawning.
- Whole-stream annual production varied only 15% (83.6–96.3 lb/acre; 1,007.9–1,160.5 lb total). The five-year mean was 90.5 lb/acre (1,091.2 lb).
- Age 0 and age I trout produced 81–95% of annual production.
- Annual production was about 2.1 times the April standing crop and 1.5 times the September standing crop.
- The authors attribute the B–A difference mainly to greater densities of age 0 trout (about 76% more on 1 September 1963), not to better growth.

**Lifetime production of year classes (1959–61)**

| Year class | Lifetime production (whole stream) | Angler harvest (% of lifetime production) |
|---|---|---|
| 1959 | 157.4 lb/acre (1,896.7 lb) | 9.8 lb/acre (about 6%) |
| 1960 | 68.1 lb/acre (820.3 lb) | 11.0 lb/acre (about 16%) |
| 1961 | 119.0 lb/acre (1,433.4 lb) | 18.0 lb/acre (about 15%) |

- The 1959 year class was the strongest on record (22,646 fish in September of its first year, against a 13-year mean of 10,708). The 1961 year class was also strong.
- Production was greatest in the first year of life for all three year classes.
- Actual lifetime production was less than 0.5% of "potential production" (0.39%, 0.24% and 0.45%).

**Angler harvest**
- Only one compulsory creel census was operating in Wisconsin at the time, on Lawrence Creek.
- The statewide rules were a 6-inch minimum and a bag limit of 10. The 1960 season used a 9-inch minimum and a bag of 5, with section A closed as a headwaters refuge. The 1961–64 seasons used an 8-inch minimum, a bag of 5 and fly-fishing only in sections C and D.
- 1960 had the lowest catch on record: 85 brook trout (27.3 lb) from 424 trips.
- Seasonal harvests in 1961–64 were 442–752 fish, or 8.9–14.0 lb/acre (mean 11.6 lb/acre). These equalled 10–18% of annual production (mean about 13%), with no age 0 fish creeled.
- Most of the catch was age II trout. Creel catches amounted to only 6–11% of the age I-and-older trout present when fishing began.

**Methodological conclusions**
- Year-class-based and age-group-based production estimates differ. The authors judged that a multi-generation study was needed to link production and harvest to typical age groups.
- Short studies (one or two years) gave useful approximations of annual production and harvest efficiency.
- Numerical density, not growth rate, was the main determinant of production differences among years and sections.
- Efficiency of production declined as standing crop rose.
- Production in Lawrence Creek is roughly 90 lb/acre per year and may approach a carrying capacity set by environmental conditions.

**Comparisons with other studies** (Allen 1951; Horton 1961; [[Chapman (1965)]]; [[Ricker and Foerster (1948)]]; [[Gerking (1962)]]) are used throughout to place Lawrence Creek in context.
