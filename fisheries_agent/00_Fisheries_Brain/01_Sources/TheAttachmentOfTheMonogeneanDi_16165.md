# Attachment of the Monogenean *Discocotyle sagittata* to the Gills of *Salmo trutta*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #monogenea #diclidophoroidea

## Executive Summary

This 1960 study by J. Llewellyn and I. L. Owen re-examines how the monogenean [[Discocotyle Sagittata]] attaches to the gills of [[Salmo Trutta]] (brown trout). The authors found that the parasite adopts a characteristic inclined "adhesive attitude" that is set by the direction of the host's [[Gill Ventilation]] current. The asymmetry of its posterior clamps is facultative (it can lie to either side) rather than fixed. The clamp is closed by an extrinsic muscle–tendon–[[Fair-Lead]]–hinged-jaw system, which contradicts earlier spring-system interpretations by [[Scheuring]] (1938) and [[Sproston]] (1946).

## Background & Description

The authors situate the work within a broader classification of diclidophoroidean gill trematodes. Earlier studies identified two clamping mechanisms:

- An extrinsic muscle–tendon–fair-lead–hinged-jaw system, seen in [[Plectanocotyle Gurnardi]] and [[Kuhnia Scombri]].
- An extrinsic muscle acting on a diaphragm to produce suction that closes a pair of hinged jaws, seen in Diclidophoridae.

Earlier accounts of *D. sagittata* (Leuckart, 1842; Diesing, 1850) described its clamps as operating differently. The authors also noted that the species appeared bilaterally symmetrical, unlike other gill parasites that are obligatorily asymmetrical, and so it remained an enigma.

**Materials and methods**

- Fresh trout were collected from Lakes Coniston and Windermere and from rivers in Montgomeryshire and Cardiganshire. Living material was observed at the Windermere laboratory of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] and at Penybontfawr. The shortest interval between host capture and parasite examination was 10 minutes.
- Heads and gills from trout killed by immersion in Bouin's fluid were supplied by the [[Freshwater Fisheries Laboratory]] at Pitlochry. These provided histologically fixed parasites still attached as in life.
- Sites of attachment were recorded in detail, and histological techniques followed those previously used for *Plectanocotyle*, *Kuhnia* and *Diclidophora*.

**Morphology of the adhesive apparatus**

- Four pairs of posterior clamps, plus one pair of small hooks (about 68 µm long, as printed).
- The hooks resemble the posterior hooks of the newly hatched larva and appear to persist into adulthood. This makes them a probable remnant of larval attachment organs.
- Each clamp has a fixed anterior jaw and a hinged posterior jaw, supported by sclerites (a–d), a median sclerite with a fair-lead, and a tendon. Closure is driven by intrinsic adductor muscles and stronger extrinsic muscles (e.m.d. and e.m.v.).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Infestation and microhabitat (Table 1)**

- A total of 156 adult parasites was collected from 60 fresh trout.
- In the Wales sample, 12 of 24 fish (50%) were infested, with a mean of 4.25 parasites per infested fish.
- In the Lake District sample, 24 of 36 fish (67%) were infested, with a mean of 4.375 parasites per infested fish.
- Across both areas, 36 of 60 fish (60%) were infested, with a mean of 4.33 parasites per infested fish.
- Parasites showed a preference for the first and second gill arches. Gill-arch totals were 58, 55, 26 and 17 for arches 1–4.
- Preserved Pitlochry hosts were excluded. Only 25% of 75 trout were infested, with a mean of 1.5 parasites per infested fish, and the authors attributed this to detachment during collection.

**Attachment site**

- Of 96 parasites with recorded sites, 92 were on secondary gill lamellae near the distal border of the [[Interbranchial Septum]]. The remaining four were more proximal.
- Parasites nearly always attached to one surface of a primary lamella, with the posterior end nearer the gill arch.

**Gill ventilation and orientation**

- The host's ventilation system consists of a median buccal force pump and paired lateral opercular suction pumps. The two are separated by a sieve formed by interdigitating primary lamellae.
- Water flows from the buccal to the opercular cavities for about nine-tenths of each ventilation cycle, through channels between secondary lamellae.
- Parasites distal to the septum experience an almost continuous unilateral current. By aligning obliquely to the primary lamella, the parasite reduces this resistance.
- The clamps of one side are anterior to those of the other, and the anterior end always points toward the space between the two hemibranchs of the arch.

## Key Findings

1. **Facultative asymmetry in a sample of 50 parasites (Table 2):**
   - Dorsal surface: 26; ventral surface: 24.
   - Inner hemibranch: 31; outer hemibranch: 19.
   - Inclined to the left: 28; inclined to the right: 22.
   - The authors conclude there is no marked preference for one surface or one side. Asymmetry therefore appears to be a facultative trait, not a fixed one.

2. **Size constraint:** A secondary lamella cannot hold two clamps side by side. This was consistent across all trout up to 30 cm examined. The authors suggest the size constraint and the current together produce the inclined orientation.

3. **Symmetry in dead or moribund parasites:** Parasites that detached naturally, and dead specimens, were usually bilaterally symmetrical. Earlier descriptions (Leuckart, 1842; Shaffer, 1916; Scheuring, 1938; Llewellyn, 1956b) likely used moribund or dead specimens, which would explain the reported symmetry.

4. **Two exceptions:**
   - Two parasites on the proximal primary lamella, sheltered by the interbranchial septum, were symmetrical.
   - Two parasites were asymmetrical in the reverse direction, attached to a single primary lamella's secondary lamellae at different distances from the arch.

   The authors attribute these to the weak currents within the septum's shelter.

5. **Mobility:** Scheuring's (1938) claim that the parasite moves about like a leech was not supported. No free-living fresh parasite was seen voluntarily detaching and re-attaching. Parasites forcibly detached could re-attach, usually with one side's clamps only, and sometimes in a different alignment.

6. **Clamp mechanism:** The clamp works through an extrinsic muscle–tendon–fair-lead–hinged-jaw system. Compared with [[Plectanocotyle Gurnardi]], the mechanism shows two differences:
   - The posterior-jaw sclerites are free distally in *Discocotyle*, whereas *Plectanocotyle* has a single lamellate sclerite.
   - *Plectanocotyle* has more sclerotized tissue around the fair-lead.

   The authors argue these differences make *Plectanocotyle*'s clamp mechanically more efficient, though *Discocotyle* grips firmly enough. They conclude that Scheuring's and Sproston's spring-system interpretations are wrong.

7. **Evolutionary interpretation:** Facultative asymmetry is less specialized than the obligatory unilateral asymmetry seen in *Anthocotyle*, *Gastrocotyle*, *Pseudaxine* and *Axine*. The authors suggest it may be a primitive character retained in *Discocotyle*. Attaching one clamp per secondary lamella may have favoured larger clamps rather than more clamps, a pattern different from that in Gastrocotylidae and Microcotylidae.
