# Embryonic Adaptations of Oviparous Toothcarps from Periodically Drying Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study examines how embryos of oviparous toothcarps of the subfamily [[Rivulinae]] survive the dry season in temporary waters. Nine species from six genera were compared. Most of them are [[Annual Killifish|annual killifish]] and bottom-spawning, and their eggs survive about six months of desiccation in moist mud. The author shows that embryonic development is strongly modified from the typical teleost pattern. It is interrupted by diapauses, and hatching is controlled mainly by the oxygen partial pressure of the surrounding medium. The eggs are protected by thick, filament-bearing membranes. The study concludes that bottom-spawning ([[Bottom Spawning]]) is the extreme end of a graded series of adaptations that starts from plant-spawning ([[Plant Spawning]]) species in permanent waters.

## Background & Description

The Rivulinae are small freshwater fishes, 3 to 12 cm long, found in tropical and subtropical Africa and the Americas. Their habitats range from lakes and rivers to puddles and ditches that dry out completely in dry seasons. Males are brightly coloured, while females are plain. Many species were first introduced to aquaria in the early twentieth century, and early hobbyists lost temporary-water species because the ecology was unknown.

The study group comprised:

- **Three American species** (genera [[Rivulus]], [[Cynolebias]] and [[Pterolebias]]):
  - [[Rivulus Cylindraceus]], a plant-spawner from Florida and Cuba.
  - [[Cynolebias Nigripinnis]], a bottom-spawner from the Argentine Pampa, where pools are dry from November to April.
  - [[Pterolebias Longipinnis]], a bottom-spawner from the lower Amazon region.
- **Four West African species** (genera [[Aphyosemion]] and [[Epiplatys]]):
  - [[Aphyosemion Australe]], a plant-spawner from southern Cameroon to the Ogowe delta.
  - [[Aphyosemion Calliurum]], intermediate between plant- and bottom-spawning.
  - [[Aphyosemion Arnoldi]], from the Niger delta, with bottom-spawning.
  - [[Epiplatys Chaperi]], a surface-dwelling plant-spawner from Sierra Leone to the Gold Coast.
- **Two East African species** of the genus [[Nothobranchius]]:
  - [[Nothobranchius Guentheri]], from Zanzibar and the adjacent coast, where swamp pools are dry from about May to October.
  - [[Nothobranchius Rachowi]], from the Beira area of Mozambique, where the dry season lasts about June to November.

Species were selected to include representatives of every fundamentally different biotope type.

Water chemistry and temperature are described as follows. Most habitats hold humic, acidic, soft water with a brown colour and a bottom of plant debris. Some Nothobranchius and Cynolebias pools are unvegetated, alkaline (up to pH 8) and muddy. Total hardness during the dry season can exceed 8° dGH, whereas during the rains it can be below 0.5° dGH. Temperatures range from 18 to 35 °C in equatorial areas, with annual means of 25 to 28 °C. For Cynolebias nigripinnis, a lower limit of 8 °C was recorded.

Bottom-spawning eggs lie in the surface layers of the substratum, a setting that differs sharply from open water. Decomposing plant matter rapidly consumes oxygen, and carbon dioxide accumulates. In the author's model aquarium (50 x 50 x 25 cm, with 5 cm of peat), hydrogen sulfide was detected in the surface layers, even under vigorous aeration of the overlying water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drying and rehydration.** In the lab model, 8 weeks at about 23 °C were enough for 1 to 2 cm pits of water to remain. Adult fish then died, and the eggs were left in the peat. Eggs were kept at 4 to 6 months in covered tanks, with the substrate kept "like freshly moistened tobacco." Rain was simulated with oxygen-saturated distilled water (24 l). Hatching followed within hours. The first larvae of Nothobranchius guentheri emerged after 2 hours, 40 to 50 after 4 hours, and 200 to 300 after 8 hours. Eggs were about 1 mm in diameter and larvae about 4 mm long.

**Oxygen dynamics after rain.** Figure 1 shows that dissolved oxygen fell nearly linearly over the first 12 hours after rain, while total hardness stayed low. Water pH stayed close to that of distilled or rainwater, between 5.4 and 5.8 in the first 12 hours. The fall in oxygen reflects rapid decomposition of plant matter mixed with previously preserved animal remains. At the substrate level, oxygen reached zero, while in the overlying water oxygen fell to a minimum and then recovered once suspended matter settled.

**Egg survival and the species' dry-season tolerance.**
- Eggs of [[Aphyosemion Australe]] and [[Epiplatys Chaperi]] survive a few weeks in moist, dried plant debris. The same is true of [[Rivulus Cylindraceus]].
- Eggs of [[Aphyosemion Calliurum]] and [[Aphyosemion Arnoldi]] survive several months in moist peat.
- The author concludes that all Rivulinae can probably survive at least a few weeks in such conditions.

**Local variation.** Pools are heterogeneous. Edges dry out more than hollows, shaded spots stay wetter, and eggs lie at different depths. Only embryos in suitable microhabitats develop successfully.

## Key Findings

**1. Egg membranes are specialised for desiccation.** The thick, multilayered membrane resembles plywood. Filaments differ between groups:
- Plant-spawners have long, sticky, mono- or multipolar filaments that attach eggs to vegetation.
- Bottom-spawners ([[Nothobranchius Guentheri]], [[Nothobranchius Rachowi]], [[Cynolebias Nigripinnis]], [[Pterolebias Longipinnis]]) have short, strong anchoring filaments.

Eggs of [[Nothobranchius Guentheri]] and [[Nothobranchius Rachowi]] are roughly 0.90 to 1.05 mm in diameter. Eggs of [[Rivulus Cylindraceus]] are 1.50 to 1.6 mm. The author ranks water-loss resistance from bottom-spawners (lowest loss) to plant-spawners (highest loss). Resistance to mechanical load was about 300 g for [[Nothobranchius Guentheri]] and [[Rivulus Cylindraceus]], and below 200 g for [[Aphyosemion Calliurum]] and below 150 g for [[Cynolebias Nigripinnis]].

**2. Embryonic development is interrupted by diapauses.**
- **Diapause Ib** is an early arrest, occurring from about the start of gastrulation until the embryonic shield appears. It lasts 6 days under optimal oxygen. It is ended only by oxygen penetration of the substrate, which needs about 3 days of exposure to reach stage Ib after oxygen returns.
- **Diapause II** is an arrest in the middle embryonic stage. In [[Nothobranchius Guentheri]] it ranged from 5 to 51 days. Stage II took 5 to 51 days overall, with a maximum within-batch difference of 27 days. This variability is not caused by the incubation method.
- **A third, obligatory diapause** occurs in the African species at a mid-embryonic stage.
- Hatching-competent embryos (stage V) can survive up to about 90 days in [[Nothobranchius Guentheri]], and about 4½ months overall in the experiments. In [[Aphyosemion Calliurum]], embryos survived more than two months in moist-dry conditions.

**3. Oxygen partial pressure is the key regulator.** Several findings support this:
- Stages 0 to -Ib tolerate very low oxygen. Stage Ib was arrested for 10 days under anoxia (using pyrogallol) and in oxygen below 2 mm Hg, and development resumed normally after reoxygenation.
- Blocking oxidative respiration with potassium cyanide (1/1000 mol KCN per litre) arrested development in the same way as anoxia, which suggests that oxidative respiration rather than energy supply per se controls the arrest.
- Embryos of [[Nothobranchius Guentheri]] that were raised for 10 months in water-saturated mud had all stopped developing at stage Ib. Some of these eggs were already degenerating.
- Stages III and IV tolerated at most 2 to 3 days of complete oxygen deprivation.

**4. Hatching requires a drop in oxygen partial pressure.** Hatching is triggered when oxygen supply no longer meets the embryo's basal metabolism. The critical oxygen level rises as the embryo grows, so embryos hatch once that level is exceeded. Measured hatching thresholds:
- [[Rivulus Cylindraceus]]: 42 to 100 mm Hg at 20.5 °C.
- [[Aphyosemion Australe]]: 11 to 56 mm Hg at 20.5 °C.
- [[Aphyosemion Calliurum]]: 9 to 49 mm Hg at 20.5 °C.
- [[Nothobranchius Guentheri]]: 72 to 107 mm Hg at 20.5 °C, and 4 to 17 mm Hg at 32.5 °C.

Temperature drops lift the hatching threshold (earlier hatching at higher oxygen). Hatching was also observed to be affected by carbon dioxide, but the author could not quantify its role in the field. Hardness, pH and the salinity of the water did not trigger hatching. Rainwater itself is not a trigger, and "osmotic" hatching occurs only after enzymes have softened the membrane.

**5. Hatching success depends on species and stage.**
- Bottom-spawners hatch mostly at the end of the dry season, with diapause-driven spreading of hatching over time.
- Hatching in plant-spawners is more irregular. Partial dry-hatching was seen in [[Rivulus Cylindraceus]] (up to 50%), [[Nothobranchius Guentheri]] (up to 20%) and [[Nothobranchius Rachowi]] (up to 25%).

**6. Species differ in how close they are to the bottom-spawning extreme.**
- [[Epiplatys Chaperi]] has a roughly 10-day diapause and dies from nutrient exhaustion within the egg if it fails to hatch.
- [[Aphyosemion Australe]] survives about 27 days at the terminal stage.
- [[Aphyosemion Arnoldi]] has reduced filaments, a hexagonal surface pattern, and a shorter stage-V survival time than [[Nothobranchius Guentheri]].
- [[Cynolebias Nigripinnis]] and [[Pterolebias Longipinnis]] lack diapause II. Up to 80% of [[Cynolebias Nigripinnis]] embryos were malformed, possibly because of long laboratory inbreeding.
- The author suggests a "primitive" bottom-spawning form that could survive about 5 to 7 months of drought, with diapauses Ib and II obligatory only in more extreme species.

**7. Evolutionary interpretation.** The author proposes that the transition from plant-spawning to bottom-spawning happened in steps. Eggs deposited near the water edge are first protected against drying. Bottom-spawning then spreads the eggs more evenly, reducing dependence on local conditions. Strong bottom-spawning ties species to regularly drying waters. The author argues that bottom-spawning evolved convergently in Africa and South America, and that the African species are more closely related to each other than to their South American counterparts.

**8. Comparative cases.** The same arrest-and-hatch strategy appears in other fishes and taxa: [[Fundulus Confluentus]] (eggs stranded on a Florida salt marsh), [[Galaxias Attenuatus]] (New Zealand), [[Leuresthes Tenuis]] (West Coast of North America) and [[Hypomesus Pretiosus]], as well as desiccation-tolerant invertebrates such as tardigrades and rotifers.

**Conclusion.** Embryonic survival in drying water is controlled mainly by oxygen availability, not by the moisture level of the environment. Diapause at the terminal embryonic stage delays hatching until rain returns, and the broad spread in embryo development ensures that some offspring always hatch when conditions are suitable.
