## Executive Summary

This study by Kormondy (1968) investigates decomposition (weight loss) dynamics of two dominant floating-leaved aquatic macrophytes — [[Nymphaea odorata]] (white water lily) and [[Brasenia schreiberi]] (watershield) — and pure alpha cellulose in Ellenton Bay, a [[Carolina Bay]] wetland on the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission's Savannah River Plant. Although not a fish-focused paper, it provides foundational data on detrital/organic matter turnover, a key process underpinning aquatic food webs and habitat structure that ichthyologists rely upon when characterizing wetland productivity and fish habitat quality. The work quantifies seasonal and depth-related (surface vs. substrate) differences in decomposition rates over a nine-month period, using both cumulative and initial (monthly) weight-loss methodologies, and situates these findings relative to broader questions of detritus accumulation and organic matter budgets in shallow freshwater systems.

## Background & Description

While the study is not organism-morphology-centered in a zoological sense, it details plant structural/biochemical composition relevant to decomposition resistance:

- **Nymphaea odorata** samples: single leaf with ~20–25 cm of stem; air-dry weight range 20–40 g.
- **Brasenia schreiberi** samples: 5–6 leaves each with 5–8 cm stem; air-dry weight range 8–15 g.
- **Dry-weight/water content**: Nymphaea dry weight ranged from 11.8% (March, low) to 16.8% (June, high) of air-dry weight; Brasenia ranged from 4.1% (March, low) to 9.4% (October, high).
- **Biochemical composition** (Browning 1952 method; % of dry weight):

| Species | Cellulose | Hemicellulose | Resin | Lignin | Ash |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brasenia | 29.02 | 33.40 | 11.34 | 22.40 | 3.84 |
| Nymphaea | 29.30 | 32.25 | 9.28 | 25.78 | 3.39 |

- Resin fraction: 8–10% lipid; cellulose fraction: 30–35% protein, nucleoprotein, and amino acid.
- Only ~25% of dry weight in both species is cellulose/hemicellulose; ~60% is decay-resistant resins and lignins.
- Cellulose substrate used: pure regenerated alpha cellulose (193 PUD cellophane, ~6–7% water content), 5 cm² squares, average initial weight 0.092 g.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Zonation structure of Ellenton Bay** (320 × 120 m study area): a dense, nearly monotypic zone of *Nymphaea odorata*, giving way shoreward to a ~20 m wide zone of *Brasenia schreiberi*, transitioning to *Rubus* dominance, with *Panicum* as shore vegetation. This zonation pattern is characteristic of [[Carolina Bay]] wetland ecosystems and relevant to habitat mapping for wetland-dependent fish and invertebrate assemblages.
- **Hydrology**: Water depth fluctuated seasonally — low in fall (avg. 0.75 m in Nymphaea zone, 0.5 m in Brasenia zone) and rising ~0.5 m by early spring in both zones.
- **Thermal regime**: Mean monthly surface water temperatures ranged from 12°C (fall) and 5°C (winter) to 15–20°C (spring) and 25°C (summer); bottom waters were 3–4°C cooler than surface in fall/spring and up to 8°C cooler in summer — a thermal stratification pattern relevant to fish thermal habitat selection.
- **Detritus accumulation**: Average detritus thickness was 0.28 m in the Nymphaea zone and 0.17 m in the Brasenia zone, indicating that annual decomposition does not keep pace with organic matter input — a long-term net accumulation with implications for benthic/detrital food webs.
- **Grazing herbivory**: No obvious evidence of herbivory was observed, indirectly supported by the net accumulation of undecomposed organic matter, suggesting decomposition (microbial/mechanical) rather than consumption is the dominant pathway of organic matter processing in this system.
- **Comparative ecosystem context**: Cellulose decomposition in this aquatic system was markedly slower than in an adjacent terrestrial broomsedge field (Golley 1960), where complete cellulose disintegration occurred within six months; in Ellenton Bay only 40–50% was lost after nine months, with decomposition continuing to accelerate into summer rather than ceasing during drought as in the terrestrial system.

## Key Findings

- **Nymphaea decomposition**: Cumulative and initial weight-loss data both show a high initial (onset) decomposition rate, with consistently higher loss rates at the bay bottom than at the surface; total nine-month weight loss was similar between depths, though cumulative data suggested more winter decomposition than initial-rate data supported.
- **Brasenia decomposition**: Similar cumulative loss pattern to Nymphaea; loss was rapid in fall, then greatest and sustained during spring–summer at both surface and bottom. Initial loss-rate sampling was not possible in winter because Brasenia becomes dormant and submerged, with no fresh material available.
- **Cellulose decomposition**: Loss was low in early fall and negligible during winter in both plant zones; rates increased markedly in late spring/summer, reaching ~50% loss by late summer. Unlike fresh plant material, cellulose showed little surface-vs-bottom disparity except during the spring–summer period, and its overall decomposition rate never approached the high rates seen in fresh plant tissue.
- **Mechanism inference**: Since cellulose and hemicellulose comprise only ~25% of plant dry weight, and lignins/resins (~60%, highly decay-resistant) persist, the near-complete loss of fresh plant material in nylon bags must involve more than enzymatic decomposition — likely including mechanical fragmentation, cell-wall breakdown releasing soluble/fermentable substances, and loss of fragments through bag mesh.
- **Sedimentation linkage**: Water-soluble and resistant materials released via decomposition (lignins, resins, inorganics) likely meet the "insolubility factor" for long-term preservation (per Vallentyne 1962) and become incorporated into bay sediments, forming the observed detritus layer.
- **Temperature dependence**: Elevated summer temperatures likely drove cellulose decomposition into the optimal range for mesophilic cellulolytic bacteria, explaining the marked acceleration of cellulose loss in warmer months.
- **Methodological caveats**: Weight-loss measurements were complicated by pitting/perforation of cellulose squares (precluding grid-based quantification), extraneous debris/periphyton interfering with weighing, difficulty retrieving bottom samples entangled in submerged vegetation, and estimation error in dry-weight determination for fresh plant tissue.
