# Wildlife 2000: Arizona Game and Fish Department Wildlife Program Strategic Plan (1996–2000)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sportfish-management

## Executive Summary

Wildlife 2000 is the five-year strategic plan of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] for its wildlife program, approved by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]] on December 15, 1995. It covers three subprograms: Game Management, Sportfish Management, and Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Management. The plan states that it reflects public input, is largely species-driven, and is intended as a management and ethical framework for resolving conflicts case by case rather than resolving them directly. Its objectives are quantified targets for harvest, hunter numbers, hunter days, and population levels for major game species, and it defines nine general issues with associated strategies.

## Background & Description

- **Legal mandate:** Authority is vested in the Commission and Department under Arizona Revised Statutes Title 17. Wildlife, except fish and bullfrogs in private ponds and captive wildlife, is state property (A.R.S. 17-102).
- **Mission:** To conserve, enhance, and restore Arizona's diverse wildlife resources and habitats through protection and management programs, and to provide wildlife resources and safe watercraft and off-highway vehicle recreation for present and future generations.
- **Management goal:** Provide fish and wildlife benefits and compatible public uses while avoiding adverse habitat impacts, protecting populations and public safety, and improving public understanding.
- **Planning system:** Comprises a mission statement, vision, programs and subprograms, strategic plans, five-year project narratives, annual work plans, budget prioritization, a cost accounting system, evaluation, and [[Responsive Management]].
- **Partnerships:** The Department is the only state agency with legal mandate over all Arizona wildlife. Partners include the [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[U.S. Forest Service]], as well as Tribal authorities and neighboring states and countries.
- **Plan flexibility:** The plan may be amended at any time during its five-year life, with changes presented to the public for comment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography and climate.** Arizona has two major landscape regions: the [[Colorado Plateau]] (generally above 5,000 ft, with some areas above 9,000 ft) and the [[Basin and Range Province]], which includes the Mohave, Sonoran, and Mexican Highlands. Winter precipitation comes from Pacific frontal systems, and summer precipitation from tropical moisture. Annual precipitation ranges from under five inches along the lower Colorado and Gila rivers to over 25 inches above 8,000 ft. Arizona experiences prolonged droughts, which strongly influence wildlife populations and harvest levels.

**Biodiversity.** More than 800 species of fish, amphibians, birds, reptiles, and mammals occur in Arizona. The state includes 17 hummingbird species, 42 raptor species, 28 bat species, and 13 potentially dangerous venomous reptiles. Arizona sits at the junction of four American deserts (Chihuahuan, Great Basin, Mohave, Sonoran).

**Aquatic and riparian habitat.** Almost every aquatic environment in Arizona has been modified by human use. Of the state's endemic vertebrates, 60 percent are obligate riparian dwellers, and another 10 to 20 percent are facultative riparian users. Groundwater tables have dropped dramatically since the 1800s, and some basins have subsided up to 50 feet. Water allocation is governed by the doctrine of prior appropriation.

**Land ownership.** Arizona is the sixth largest state at 113,909 square miles, with about 70 percent under federal jurisdiction. The Department has wildlife jurisdiction on all lands except Tribal lands. Approximately 12 million acres are privately owned.

**Human pressure.** The population grew from 2.7 million to 3.7 million during the 1980s, and approximately 4.04 million people lived in the state at the time of writing. Over 76 percent of residents live in Maricopa and Pima counties.

## Key Findings

### Game Management
- **Hunter trends (1994 survey of 867 respondents, 57.9% return rate):** Hunting license sales fell from 203,384 (1986) to 163,270 (1991), then rose by 23,996 (14.7%) to 187,266 by 1994. Non-resident share reached 10.4 percent in 1994, the highest in state history. Women made up 6.1 percent of hunters. Mean hunter age rose from 36.8 (1987) to 37.8 (1993), indicating declining recruitment of young hunters.
- **Hunter education:** 53.3 percent of hunters had completed a hunter education course by 1994.
- **Big game management objectives** (statewide 1994 estimates in parentheses):
  - [[Mule Deer]]: 123,000–154,000 post-hunt adults (approx. 120,000); harvest 12,500–15,000 (approx. 12,000).
  - [[White-tailed Deer]]: 87,000–95,000 post-hunt adults (82,000); harvest 5,000–6,000 (5,600).
  - [[Pronghorn]]: 7,500–9,000 post-hunt adults (7,800); harvest 600–900 (700); goal to restore historical range through transplants.
  - [[Elk]]: 25,000–30,000 post-hunt adults (29,000); harvest 7,500–12,000 (11,000).
  - [[Wild Turkey]]: harvest 1,600–2,000 (1,900 in 1994); 36,000–45,000 hunter days.
  - [[Javelina]]: 35,000–45,000 population (38,000); harvest 6,500–8,500 (6,700).
  - [[Bighorn Sheep]]: increase to 7,500 from about 6,500; harvest 100–120 (100).
  - [[Buffalo]]: 200–300 animals (about 250 on Department areas); harvest 40–60 (47).
  - [[Black Bear]]: 1.0 bear per square mile of high-quality habitat (2,500 bears); female harvest no more than 125 (total 239 in 1994).
  - [[Mountain Lion]]: harvest 250–300 (2,500 lions estimated; 4,156 permits sold).
- **Modeling:** Statewide deer, elk, and pronghorn populations are estimated using a model based on age-structured male:female ratios, harvest, and non-hunt mortality, calibrated against annual aerial and ground surveys.
- **Small game:** Objectives are based on 1985–94 data. Gambel's and scaled quail harvest was about 989,000 in 1994 (objective 524,000–1,314,000). Mourning doves harvested were about 1.1 million (objective 820,000–1,500,000). Cottontail harvest was about 141,000. Tree squirrel harvest was about 72,400. Small game populations can increase by 200 to 600 percent in a single breeding season under favorable conditions.
- **Predators and furbearers:** Trapping on state and public lands was prohibited by a 1994 state law, which will reduce harvest further. In 1994, 85 trappers took 650 coyotes and 10,000 hunters harvested 21,600.
- **Non-native game birds:** Chukar, pheasant, and valley quail were established but are limited to a few areas. No new non-native game birds will be introduced.

### Sportfish Management
- **Licenses:** In 1993, more than 319,000 residents and 57,000 non-residents bought fishing licenses. Anglers fished about 7 million angler-days annually, and mean days per angler rose from 17.7 to 22.7 per year.
- **Satisfaction:** In 1992 and 1994 surveys, 67 percent of the public rated fish management as good or excellent. Anglers' mean satisfaction was 7.0 on a 1–10 scale.
- **Supply:** 159 stream reaches (about 1,470 miles) are managed mainly for trout. Around 64 lakes (about 3,000 acres) are managed primarily for trout. About 354,800 acres of impoundments and 35,840 acres of flowing water are managed for warmwater species.
- **Demand projections:** Resident demand was estimated at 5.4 million warmwater and 2.6 million coldwater user-days by 2000.
- **Native fish conflict:** In the 1992 survey, 31 percent of anglers agreed that native fish should be managed as sportfish, 22 percent disagreed, and 47 percent were unsure. Among the general public, 65 percent wanted to preserve native fish.
- **Economic contribution:** 66 percent of surveyed anglers spent $10 or less per day; anglers spend an estimated $200 million annually (as stated in the text).
- **Objectives:** Provide recreational fishing and 70 percent angler satisfaction. Key strategies include size and bag limit reviews, watershed-based plans to reconcile sportfish and native fish, access development, toxic substance monitoring, and an "adopt a stream" program.

### Nongame and Endangered Wildlife
- **Native fishes:** Arizona has 32 native fish species (30 freshwater, 2 saltwater). Only the [[Longfin Dace]] remains abundant and widespread. The [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (up to 6 feet and 80 pounds) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] require stocking to prevent extirpation. The [[Monkey Springs Pupfish]] is extinct. [[Aravaipa Creek]] supports seven native species with virtually no non-native species.
- **Amphibians and reptiles:** Arizona has 25 native amphibian and 95 native reptile species. Eight are Wildlife of Special Concern. The [[Mohave Desert Tortoise]] was federally listed as threatened in April 1991.
- **Birds:** 350 bird species are documented as native (some sources list 541 or 290+; the document's wording is partly garbled). Of 42 raptor species, four are federally listed and 13 are Wildlife of Special Concern. Neotropical migrants show declining populations.
- **Mammals:** 134 native and 11 introduced species are recorded. Eighteen mammals are Wildlife of Special Concern and nine are federally listed.
- **Crustaceans and mollusks:** Poorly known. The [[Kanab Ambersnail]] is federally endangered. More than 40 species are under federal review.
- **Outlook:** Many common species are secure, but riparian- and water-dependent species face declines. Visitors to selected water-based recreation sites rose 38 percent between 1980 and 1990.

### Issues and Strategies (nine general issues)
1. **Planning and philosophy:** Operational plans, Total Quality Management, job-based performance evaluation, move toward ecosystem-based planning, and a Wildlife 2005 plan.
2. **Information:** Standardized inventory and monitoring, GIS and computer information systems, and research on disease and habitat.
3. **Management actions:** Data-based guidelines, watershed-based management plans, and restrictions on new non-native introductions.
4. **Habitat management and protection:** Habitat quality protocols, instream flow protection, and acquisition only from willing sellers.
5. **Partnerships:** Cooperation with agencies, private landowners, and Tribes.
6. **Laws and legal conflicts:** Legislative review and minimizing litigation.
7. **Law enforcement:** Public awareness, patrol visibility, and programs such as Operation Game Thief and Hunter Education.
8. **Wildlife recreation:** Surveys, access improvements, the hunt permit system review, and youth participation programs.
9. **Public attitudes, information, and education:** Communication, customer service, and public awareness of private property rights.

### Funding Context
- The [[Arizona Heritage Fund]], approved by voters in 1990, provides about $10 million per year from state lottery proceeds. The Department had dedicated more than $35 million to projects, and about $3.8 million had been awarded through grants.
- Hunting, fishing, and other wildlife-associated recreation had a total economic impact of more than $1.65 billion in 1991, supporting nearly 25,000 jobs.
