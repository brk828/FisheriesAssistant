# Clupeoid Fishes of the World: FAO Species Catalogue Volume 7, Part 1

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This is Part 1 of Volume 7 of the [[FAO Species Catalogue]] series, an illustrated and annotated taxonomic synopsis of the clupeoid fishes (herrings, sardines, pilchards, sprats, shads, anchovies and wolf-herrings) that enter marine fisheries. Authored by [[Peter J. P. Whitehead]] and published by the [[Food And Agriculture Organization]] in 1985, Part 1 covers three families: [[Chirocentridae]], [[Clupeidae]] and [[Pristigasteridae]]. The family [[Engraulididae]] (anchovies) is planned for a later part.

The catalogue provides keys to families, subfamilies and genera, diagnoses for genera and species, synonymy, FAO names, distribution, habitat, biology, size, fishery importance and literature for each taxon. The abstract gives the full scope as four families, 80 genera and over 300 species. The OCR of the genus count is ambiguous ("g0" in the abstract), so 80 is the most likely reading.

## Background & Description

**Scope and purpose.** The catalogue is part of the FAO Species Identification and Catalogue programme, which the document describes as developing taxonomic information for fisheries use. The author, Whitehead of the [[British Museum (Natural History)]], is described as the foremost specialist in clupeoid taxonomy. Illustrations were largely redrawn from literature and sketches, with many Indo-Pacific drawings provided by [[Thosaporn Wongratana]] (Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok).

**Morphological features.** Clupeoids are generally moderate to small fishes (about 2 to 100 cm standard length) with a single short dorsal fin, no spines in the fins, and a keeled belly with scutes. Families are separated mainly by:
- **Chirocentridae:** no belly scutes, two large canine teeth in the upper jaw, and a long, compressed body.
- **Clupeidae:** a complete series of belly scutes, a terminal mouth, supramaxillae present, and a short anal fin.
- **Pristigasteridae:** a long anal fin (at least about 30 rays) and a projecting lower jaw.

**Measurements.** Standard length (tip of snout to base of caudal fin) is the main size reference. Other key measurements include body depth, finray counts (unbranched and branched rays are counted separately), gill rakers, scutes and fin positions. Counts for scutes are written as pre-pelvic, post-pelvic and total.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeography.** Chirocentrids are pelagic inshore predators of the warmer Indo-West Pacific, from the Red Sea and east African coast to Australia and the Philippines. Clupeids occur in all seas from about 70°N to about 50°S. Many genera are shared between the Indo-West Pacific and the New World, and the document notes that some Indo-Pacific genera have been introduced into new waters.

**Feeding and life history.** Clupeids feed mainly on small planktonic animals, chiefly crustaceans, form large schools, and spawn pelagic eggs that hatch into planktonic larvae. Some are anadromous (shads), some are freshwater residents (for example, West African pristigasterids), and some are filter-feeders (for example, gizzard shads). Adult size is typically 10 to 20 cm, although a few species are larger.

**Upwelling and productivity.** Clupeoids are closely tied to nutrient-rich areas such as [[Upwelling]] zones, where seasonal plankton blooms support them. The author notes that the cooler, high-latitude upwelling areas hold the richest plankton and therefore the largest clupeoid fisheries. Tropical and subtropical seas hold more clupeoid species, but each species is less abundant, and their fisheries are multispecies.

**Fisheries and gear.** Clupeoids are caught mostly by [[Purse Seine]] and beach or boat seines, and also by gillnets, lift nets, traps and, in tropical rivers, by poisons. Their schooling habit makes them very vulnerable to these nets.

## Key Findings

**Global importance of clupeoid fisheries**
- Clupeoids are the largest suborder of non-domesticated vertebrates in world catch statistics. About 60 species are fished, and a third of them are clupeoids.
- The top 56 fish species (each over 100,000 tons in 1982) landed 40,004,027 tons. Of these, 18 were clupeoids, contributing 16,691,748 tons, or about 24% of the world fish catch.
- In 1982, 24 species were each landed at over half a million tons. Ten of these were clupeoids, together just over 21% of the total world fish catch.
- The second most exploited fish in the world in 1982 was the Japanese pilchard, [[Sardinops Melanostictus]]. Other clupeoids in the top ten included the South American pilchard [[Sardinops Sagax]], the [[Peruvian Anchoveta]] (formerly the most exploited fish), [[Clupea Harengus]] and [[Sardina Pilchardus]].
- Clupeoids are the most heavily caught group of fishes by weight, above any other single systematic group. The total is about 18.9 million tons in 1982.

**Regional patterns (1982)**
- Cool upwelling areas produced 82.6% of the total clupeoid catch, subtropical areas 9.4% (1,777,278 tons), and tropical areas 8.0% (1,516,922 tons).
- The largest catches came from the Southeast Pacific (FAO Area 87; 27.4% of all clupeoid catches) and the Northwest Pacific (FAO Area 61; 24.5%), based on the Peruvian and Japanese pilchards and the Peruvian anchoveta.
- In four FAO areas, clupeoids made up over half of the fish catch. In six more, they made up over a quarter.
- The richest species diversity is in the Indo-West Pacific, with the most species known there.

**Taxonomic results and problems**
- Over 300 species are described, but many names are synonyms, and identification is difficult in species-rich tropical genera. The genus Sardinella (and related genera) and the engraulids are shared between areas and are often misidentified.
- Many names have been proposed for few valid species. Early specimens were often not kept, so some older records cannot be verified.
- The clupeoid look is misleading: external characters often do not separate families. The author therefore relies on internal characters such as the swimbladder, inner ear and head canal system, and the Weberian apparatus.

**Chirocentridae**
- One genus, [[Chirocentrus]], with two species:
  - [[Chirocentrus Dorab]] (Indo-West Pacific), reaching about 100 cm standard length.
  - [[Chirocentrus Nudus]] (Indo-West Pacific), also to about 100 cm.
- The two species are separated by small differences in pectoral fin length and black markings on the dorsal and anal fins. The author notes that body depth variation may reflect sexual dimorphism, but this needs study.
- In southern India (Palk Bay and the Gulf of Mannar), C. nudus predominated the chirocentrid catch, and both species were recorded there. On the Orissa coast, chirocentrid catches were taken by gillnet.

**Clupeidae**
- The family covers 5 subfamilies (Dussumieriinae, Clupeinae, Pellonulinae, Alosinae, Dorosomatinae) and about 56 genera, with an estimated total of about 200 species. Most entries are for the cool-water genera, which dominate catches.
- Key genera include [[Sardinops]], [[Sardinella]], [[Opisthonema]], [[Dorosoma]], [[Clupea]], [[Brevoortia]] (menhadens) and [[Alosa]] (shads).
- [[Opisthonema Medirastre]] is a multispecies thread herring of the eastern Pacific with a filamentous last dorsal ray. It was a minor contributor to catches (about 4,049 tons in 1981, mostly from Ecuador), and it was once important as live bait for tuna fishing.
- [[Dorosoma Smithi]] (Pacific gizzard shad) is known only from river systems in northwestern Mexico, with no major fishery.
- [[Stranggomera Bentincki]] is a single eastern South Pacific species, supporting a moderate fishery in Chile.

**Pristigasteridae**
- The family is defined by a long anal fin and a projecting lower jaw. Its genera include Pellona, Neoopisthopterus, Opisthopterus, Odontognathus and Pristigaster, among others.

**Utilization and fishing methods**
- Utilization includes frozen product (about 72%), fresh (about 17%) and cured (about 12%). Reduction to fishmeal and oil is also important, especially for the Peruvian anchoveta and menhaden.
- Clupeoid products are used for human food, animal feed, fish oil, baits, and as salted or fermented products (for example, Roman fish sauce).
- The catalogue includes an overview of gear: purse seines, beach seines, boat seines, trawls, lift nets, gillnets, traps and poisons.

**Structure and references**
- Each species entry includes a scientific name and reference, synonyms, FAO names (English only), diagnostic features, distribution, habitat and biology, size, fishery statistics, local names and literature.
- The literature section is organized by FAO Area and includes key regional works (such as the FWNA and FAO Species Identification Sheets) and multi-author works cited by acronym.
