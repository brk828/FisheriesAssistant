# Loach Minnow Survey on the Fort Apache Reservation, February and June 1988

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by [[Western Technology and Engineering]] (WESTEC) for the [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]], documents a survey for the federally listed threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) in the mainstem White River and its tributaries, the East Fork White River and North Fork White River, within the Fort Apache Indian Reservation. Sampling was conducted in February 18–20 and June 6–9, 1988, as part of environmental studies for the proposed Miner Flat Dam and Carrizo Day irrigation project. A total of 25 stations were sampled, 11 in both months. Only one loach minnow was captured, in February, in the East Fork White River. In June, a breeding population was found in the East Fork, and one individual was taken in the mainstem near Carrizo Day.

## Background & Description

- **Target species:** The loach minnow is a federally listed threatened species. Its known habitat is shallow riffles in moderately large creeks and rivers, with most specimens in Mexico captured in less than six inches of water (Bob David, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, personal communication).
- **Study sponsor and permit:** The survey was sponsored by the White Mountain Apache Tribe through a contract with WESTEC; the Tribe's Game and Fish Department issued the sampling permit.
- **Personnel:** Patrick Farmer and Carlene Farmer conducted the fieldwork. Joe Ellison supervised the first two days of the February trip and served as liaison with the Tribe. Sampling methods were electrofishing (backpack electroshocker) combined with visual searching of shallow riffles; fish were preserved in alcohol and identified in the laboratory using [[Minckley Fish Keys]] (1973).
- **Prior work:** [[Propst et al. 1985]] surveyed the same streams for the loach minnow, and 25 stations were compared with that earlier survey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat conditions:** Stations were shallow riffles (often under six inches deep) with cobble/boulder substrates. Many sites carried considerable fire-related sediment following the 1987 flood. Sediment loads and turbidity increased in the North Fork during February and were high enough to hamper fish observation.
- **Flow and season:** Water depths did not change much between February and June in the mainstem and North Fork, though the East Fork became shallower. This was attributed to a prolonged runoff on the North Fork, with peak flows expected in late June.
- **Algae and periphyton:** [[Minckley]] (1973) associated loach minnows with filamentous algae beds over cobble/gravel with little sediment. No such algae beds were observed at the loach minnow stations, which the authors attributed to sediment effects.
- **Anthropogenic disturbance:** The loach minnow-occupied East Fork reach had been partially disturbed by bridge construction and flood control. Station 5 was downstream of a bridge and about 100 m downstream of an irrigation diversion.
- **Non-native species:** [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*) was found in pools at trap stations; two individuals were caught in February and represented less than one percent of the mainstem catch. Rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) were also taken in the North Fork.

## Key Findings

1. **February 1988:** 15 stations were sampled, and one loach minnow was caught in the East Fork (Station 5, roughly 50–60 cm deep). Suckers and dace were the most abundant species. Sonora sucker (*Catostomus insignis*) and speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*) were the only native species in the East Fork, and no loach minnows were caught in the North Fork or mainstem.
2. **June 1988:** 25 total stations were sampled (11 in both months). Eight loach minnows were captured at Stations 4 and 5, including breeding males and a gravid female, indicating a breeding population.
3. **Catch composition:** In the East Fork, Sonora sucker and speckled dace formed the bulk of the catch. Roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*) was taken in the mainstem and North Fork; the authors noted it was probably the subspecies *G. r. robusta*, while *G. r. grahami* was also identified.
4. **Mainstem:** A single loach minnow was captured at Station 16 in June, but this individual was about 31 mm long. The mainstem reach did not appear to support a significant population.
5. **Habitat suitability:** The loach minnow appears to associate with speckled dace, but dace can occur at sites without loach minnows. Shallow riffles sampled in this study may be dry for much of the year, and deeper runs that could not be sampled may be better habitat.
6. **Conclusions:** The East Fork appears to be the most likely stream in the study area to support loach minnows. Loach minnows may tolerate some habitat disturbance, including bank alteration and channel modification.
7. **Limitations:** Turbidity and high flows in February restricted sampling, so the search was halted after 15 sites were sampled. Sampling in June was more effective because of lower flows and clearer water.
