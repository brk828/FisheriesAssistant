# Management of Farm Fish Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Alabama Agricultural Experiment Station bulletin (Bulletin No. 254, reprinted September 1950, by H. S. Swingle and E. V. Smith) presents practical principles and recommendations for managing farm fish ponds in the Southeastern United States. Its central argument is that pond fish production is limited by food supply, which is driven by water fertility, and that fish population must be balanced with that food. Fertilization, correct species combinations ([[Bluegill Bream]] and [[Largemouth Black Bass]] as the core pair), controlled stocking, regular fishing, and weed and mosquito control are presented as the main tools for good fishing.

## Background & Description

The bulletin notes that thousands of ponds had been built in the Southeastern States for fish production, many of which either never yielded good fishing or declined within a few years. The authors attribute this largely to a lack of understanding of the principles of water-area management.

Species recommendations are summarized as follows:

- **[[Bluegill Bream]]** (*Lepomis macrochirus*): recommended for all Southeastern ponds. Most caught are 3 to 8 ounces; fish over 1 pound are seldom caught. Spawning begins April or May in Alabama, and one pair may produce 2,000 to 10,000 or more young.
- **[[Largemouth Black Bass]]** (*Micropterus salmoides*): recommended for all ponds to control overcrowding of small fish. Most caught weigh 12 ounces to 2 pounds; fish over 10 pounds are rarely caught. Eggs are laid April through June.
- **Shellcracker** (*Lepomis microlophus*): grows faster than bluegill, but is less desirable in flavor. Most caught weigh 8 to 12 ounces.
- **[[White Crappie]]** (*Pomoxis annularis*): less desirable but successfully raised; peak fishing is early spring. Most caught weigh 4 ounces to 1.5 pounds.
- **Catfish**: the [[Yellow Bullhead]] and speckled cat are the best-suited of the species tested, though less satisfactory than bluegills. Channel cats and yellow cats usually fail to reproduce in ponds and are not recommended.
- **[[Top Minnow]]** (*Gambusia* sp.): required in most Southeastern ponds by State Health Department regulation for mosquito control, and useful in new ponds because they reproduce rapidly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The bulletin frames the pond as a food web in which microscopic plants (phytoplankton) grow after fertilization, supporting small water animals and insects, which in turn feed fish. Fertility is limited by the surrounding land: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and other elements dissolved from the watershed drive plant growth, since rainfall contains little of them. Poor-land ponds are therefore unproductive.

Key environmental conditions and constraints include:

- **Water clarity**: fertilizer works only when light reaches the plants. Ponds that stay muddy cannot be fertilized economically; clay particles absorb phosphorus, and mud blocks sunlight.
- **Overflow and flood water**: ponds with heavy overflow or frequent muddy floods are poor candidates for fertilization.
- **Weeds**: submerged, emergent, and floating weeds (e.g., Najas, Elodea, Chara, spatterdock, duckweed, water hyacinth) can choke clear-water ponds, shelter small fish, and provide mosquito breeding habitat.
- **Oxygen**: fertilizing during rapid weed decay may deplete dissolved oxygen and kill fish.
- **Mosquitoes**: emergent weeds, floatage, and trash on pond edges provide breeding habitat, which reduces the effectiveness of mosquito-eating minnows.

## Key Findings

**Productivity and fertilization**
- Unfertilized Alabama ponds support 40 to 200 pounds of fish per acre; fertilized ponds support 400 to 600 pounds per acre, with fertilized ponds reported to support 4 or 5 times the weight of unfertilized ponds.
- Fertilization is described as the only practical method to materially increase the weight of fish a pond can support.
- Standard fertilizer rate: 100 pounds of 6-8-4 (N-P-K) plus 10 pounds of nitrate of soda per acre per application.
- Alternative mixture (where large amounts are needed): 40 lb sulfate of ammonia, 60 lb superphosphate (16%), 5 lb muriate of potash, and 15 lb finely ground limestone per acre.
- Application begins in early spring (usually March), repeated every 3 to 4 weeks, ending in September or October: 8 to 14 applications per year, at an annual cost of $11 to $20 per acre.
- One application sustains increased food for about 1 month; one or two applications per year give poor results.
- Muddy ponds can be cleared first with barnyard manure (1 ton per acre per application at 3-week intervals, 2 or 3 applications) or with 75 lb cottonseed meal and 25 lb superphosphate per acre at 2- to 3-week intervals.

**Growth and density**
- Well-fed fish grow rapidly; the bulletin treats very rapid growth as a sign of too few fish. Target benchmarks: bluegills about 4 ounces and bass about 1 pound within one year of stocking. Observed averages are 4 ounces in 4 years for bluegills and 1 pound in 3 years for bass.
- Overstocking produces many small, stunted fish. One illustrated comparison: 1,500 bluegills per acre gave 4.0-ounce average size after one year, while 180,000 per acre gave 0.02 ounce.
- Within about one year, a pond reaches its carrying capacity. In one experiment, reducing stock from 6,500 to 3,200 per acre raised average weight to 18 ounces; reducing to 1,300 per acre gave 3.7 ounces.
- Bluegills alone cannot be managed in ponds because of overreproduction; bass are needed to control young bream. In one illustrated case, bluegills stocked alone were overcrowded by their own young, and bream weighed only two-thirds as much when the pond was drained.

**Stocking**
- Recommended combinations include A (bream and bass), B (bream, shellcracker, and bass), C (bream, crappie, and bass), and D (bream, bullhead, and bass). Example for Combination A: fertilized ponds receive 1,500 bream fingerlings and 100 bass fingerlings or fry; unfertilized ponds receive 400 bream and 80 bass.
- Fingerlings (over one inch long) are preferred over adults, which lead to overcrowding of some species and reproductive failure in others.
- Spillways should be screened with quarter-inch mesh during stocking; fish counts should be made with a dipper.
- Old ponds (3 to 30 years old) examined by draining contained enough small fish to use all available food, so restocking is generally unnecessary.

**Management of old ponds**
- Poor fishing in old ponds most often results from low water fertility, stunting from improper stocking, or dense weeds.
- Overcrowded bluegills are corrected by adding 100 bass per acre, fertilizing, and fishing heavily for bream.
- Bass-heavy ponds with large, thin bream and undersized bass are hard to correct; heavy fishing or seining of bass, followed by fertilization, is recommended.

**Fishing**
- Approximately 50 percent of total fish weight can be removed by fishing each year.
- Fishing should begin one year after bass are added.
- Alternating periods of good and poor fishing are expected in adequately fished ponds; management aims to minimize their frequency and length.
- Recommended baits include earthworms for bream, live minnows for bass and crappie, and earthworms, crickets, minnows, and fresh fish chunks for catfish.

**Weed and mosquito control**
- Underwater weeds can be destroyed by beginning fertilization in late January or early February (100 lb 6-8-4 plus 10 lb nitrate of soda per acre, every 2 weeks, then every 3 weeks), shading the weeds with filamentous algae.
- Emergent weeds such as spatterdock, waterlilies, lotus, and watershield can be controlled by cutting leaves about every 3 weeks from early June, usually 5 or 6 cuttings for water deeper than 3 feet.
- Draining, raking, and dragging cables were tested and found costly or ineffective.
- Mosquito control depends on weed control, fertilization, and inspecting pond edges and removing floatage every 2 weeks.

**Related publications**
