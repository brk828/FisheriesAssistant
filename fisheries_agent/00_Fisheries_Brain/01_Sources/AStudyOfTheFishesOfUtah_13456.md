# A Study of the Fishes of Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Vasco M. Tanner (Brigham Young University, 1936) presents a systematic review of the native and introduced fish fauna of [[Utah]]. The paper combines a historical account of collectors and publications, identification keys to families, genera and species, illustrated descriptions, distributional records, and an extensive bibliography. The author reports 25 native species in five families and 23 introduced species. The paper also documents severe declines in native fisheries, particularly at [[Utah Lake]] and [[Bear Lake]], attributed to habitat alteration, overfishing, introductions and drought.

## Background & Description

The study aims to consolidate knowledge of Utah's native ichthyofauna for anglers, students and resource managers, and to call for continued scientific study of fisheries affected by irrigation, grazing, logging, fire and reservoir development. Earlier work was done by [[Escalante]] (1776), [[John C. Fremont]] (1844), the [[Beckwith-Gunnison Survey]] (1859) and the [[Simpson Expedition]] (1876). Major taxonomic contributions came from [[Edward Drinker Cope]] and [[Herbert C. Yarrow]] (1875), who listed 22 species in the [[Wheeler Survey]] report, and from [[David Starr Jordan]], [[Barton W. Evermann]] and [[John O. Snyder]]. Type specimens are largely held at the [[National Museum]] in Washington, D.C.

Fishes are described as vertebrates of the class Pisces. The keys were simplified for non-specialists, and the paper includes illustrated plates of external features (following [[Pratt]]) and of selected species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Bonneville Basin and Great Salt Lake drainage:** Home to most native trout, suckers, chubs and minnows. Utah Lake supported very large sucker and trout populations historically.
- **Bear Lake (Utah–Idaho):** Holds endemic species of [[Cisco]] and whitefish (*Leucichthys gemmifer*, *Prosopium spilonotus*, *P. abyssicola*). Whitefish and cisco fed mainly on plankton, including *Diaptomus*, which made up over 95 per cent of the food in 30 cisco stomachs examined in September 1930.
- **Colorado and Green River basins:** Support *Salmo pleuriticus*, *Ptychocheilus lucius* ("Colorado Salmon", up to 80 lb recorded), *Gila robusta* and *Xyrauchen texanus*.
- **Virgin River and southern Utah:** Host *Notolepidomyzon utahensis*, *Lepidomeda vittata* and *Pantosteus virescens*.
- **Threats recorded:** Diversion of spawning streams at Bear Lake; lake-level lowering that reduced food and spawning areas; overfishing with small-mesh nets; changes in food webs after introductions; and water-body changes from irrigation and reservoirs.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness:** 25 native species belonging to 5 families (Salmonidae, Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Medidae, Cottidae), and 23 introduced species, including [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and [[Mosquitofish]].
- **Native trout:** *Salmo utah* (Utah Lake Trout) was once abundant at Utah Lake. Yarrow and Henshaw (1872) reported an average length of about 14 inches and average weight of 1.5 lb, with a maximum of 3 feet and about 15.5 lb. A net haul in 1864 yielded 3,500–3,700 lb, whereas 500 lb was considered large by the 1870s. The species was rarely taken by the 1930s. The author questions the separation of *S. utah* from *S. pleuriticus*, noting possible colour variation from isolation in the Bonneville Basin.
- **Utah Lake suckers:** *Catostomus fecundus* was reported as the dominant sucker in 1881 and 1872 collections. Lateral-line scale counts from 493 specimens ranged from 60 to 74, most commonly 63–64. The author treats *C. ardens* as a synonym of *C. fecundus* and suggests *Chasmistes liorus* (June Sucker) may be nearly extirpated.
- **Drought losses (1934–36):** Utah Lake's surface area fell from a normal 93,000 acres to about 50,000 acres. Hundreds of tons of suckers and carp died in winter 1934–35 from freezing and crowding. Commercial fishing ceased, and no suckers ran up the Provo River in spring 1935. The author opposes restocking with suckers from Idaho.
- **Bear Lake endemics:** The Bear Lake cisco (*L. gemmifer*) reached good food-fish status. The Bear Lake whitefish (*P. abyssicola*) grew to 12–14 inches and was taken in gill nets for market. Suggestions included introducing [[Lake Trout]] (Mackinaw) to improve fishing.
- **Taxonomic revisions:** The author synonymises *Gila seminuda* with *G. robusta* after Snyder (1916), and considers *Notolepidomyzon utahensis* (described by Tanner, 1932, Copeia) a new Utah catostomid. The distribution of *Lepidomeda vittata* in Utah was first recorded by Tanner (1932).
- **Conservation message:** The author argues that changes in watersheds, irrigation and fishing have already caused major losses, and that continued scientific monitoring and proper regulation are needed. Many once-abundant native species are "probably gone forever."
- **Bibliography:** The paper concludes with a bibliography of over 80 works on Utah ichthyology (1853–1936), covering taxonomy, fish culture, stocking records and surveys.
