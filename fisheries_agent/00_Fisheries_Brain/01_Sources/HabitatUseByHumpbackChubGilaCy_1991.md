# Habitat Use by Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*) in the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This GCES Phase II report documents U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service (Pinetop Fishery Assistance Office) research conducted between May and October 1991 on habitat use by [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and other native fishes in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), a major tributary of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. The study combined extensive physical habitat mapping (>9,000-15,000+ point samples across multiple grid scales) with fish sampling via hoopnets, trammel nets, minnow traps, seines, and direct observation of young-of-year (YOY) fish. Results confirm the Humpback Chub as the numerically dominant species in the LCR (when accounting for a localized Speckled Dace aggregation at Atomizer Falls), with clear ontogenetic shifts in habitat use, distinct spatial distribution limits, and diel activity patterns. The report also raises significant concerns regarding lack of coordination among multiple GCES contractors working in the same sensitive habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Size-based habitat/behavior thresholds identified for YOY Humpback Chub:**
  - At **~65 mm TL**: YOY chubs shift from shallow, brightly illuminated edge habitats to deeper water habitats with cover; at this size they become susceptible to capture in the same 1/4-inch mesh hoopnets used for adults.
  - At **~75 mm TL**: circumstantial evidence suggests chubs begin upstream movement within the LCR.
- No detailed external morphometric data (fin ray counts, body proportions) were reported in this document; focus was on size-class habitat associations rather than morphology per se.
- Capture gear selectivity note: 1/4" mesh hoopnets effectively retain both adults and larger juveniles (>65 mm TL), but not smaller YOY.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use and ontogenetic shifts:**
- **YOY chubs** (post-hatch through fall): occupy shallow, brightly illuminated **edge habitats**; distributed from the Confluence (km 0) to above km 12; **diurnally active**.
- **Larger juveniles/adults** (>65 mm TL): occupy **deeper water habitats with cover**; **nocturnally active**, with peak activity from sunset to midnight, during which they move from cover into open/shallow uncovered areas to feed.
- **Adult distribution**: Confluence to the Atomizer Fall complex (km 14); no chubs captured between km 14 and Blue Springs (km 21).
- **Habitat mapping scales**: LCR mapped on 1×100 m grid (confluence to Blue Springs, 21 km); finer-resolution 1×20 m grids established in USFWS research areas (km 0-1, 2-4, 10-12).

**Co-occurring native species:**
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) — second most abundant species; unlike Humpback Chub, distributed throughout the **entire permanently flowing portion** of the LCR (km 0-21), including areas above Atomizer Falls where chubs are absent. Note: a large localized aggregation was captured near Atomizer Falls (km 14) in minnow traps, skewing overall relative abundance figures.
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] — abundant.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] — present, less abundant.
- [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Plains Killifish]] — minor components of catch.

**Non-native/exotic species:**
- [[Common Carp]] and [[Channel Catfish]] — relatively uncommon in the LCR during the study period.

## Key Findings

1. **Species dominance**: Humpback Chub is the predominant fish species in the LCR by seine and minnow-trap surveys once an anomalous, localized Speckled Dace catch near Atomizer Falls (km 14) is excluded from totals.
2. **Sampling effort**: 1,399 fish captured via minnow traps and 795 via seining (USFWS), with zero reported mortalities across both methods; totals summarized in Table 1.
3. **Ontogenetic habitat shift**: YOY Humpback Chub transition from shallow, illuminated edge habitats to deeper cover habitats at approximately 65 mm TL, coinciding with a shift from diurnal to nocturnal activity.
4. **Upstream movement hypothesis**: Circumstantial evidence suggests chub begin upstream migration within the LCR at ~75 mm TL.
5. **Spatial range limits**: Adult and YOY Humpback Chub distribution is bounded between the Confluence and the Atomizer Fall complex (km 14); no chub detected between km 14 and Blue Springs (km 21), despite Speckled Dace occupying this entire reach.
6. **Diel feeding behavior**: Nocturnal activity peaks between sunset and midnight, when chub move from sheltered deepwater areas into open and shallow uncovered feeding areas.
7. **Exotic species status**: Carp and Channel Catfish remain relatively rare in the LCR, suggesting limited establishment pressure on native fish community as of this study.
8. **Research coordination gap**: The report flags a lack of integration among multiple GCES contractors (e.g., ASU, USFWS) working simultaneously in the LCR, raising concerns about cumulative impacts on the Humpback Chub population and recommending standardized collection permits and a shared research protocol via [[ACT-GCES]].
9. **No tributary work completed**: As of this report, no research had yet been conducted on other Colorado River tributaries besides the LCR, limiting evaluation of potential secondary spawning aggregation sites for [[Humpback Chub]].
10. **Study scope tied to dam operations**: The overarching research goal links native fish habitat requirements to evaluating impacts of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on the Colorado River ecosystem in [[Grand Canyon]].
