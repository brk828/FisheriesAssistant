# Lake Mead National Recreation Area

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1950 National Park Service visitor brochure describes [[Lake Mead]], the reservoir formed by [[Hoover Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]], and the surrounding [[Lake Mead National Recreational Area]] in Nevada and Arizona. The document is not a research study. It covers the lake's size and setting, the area's geology, prehistory, plants, and wildlife, and practical information on access, accommodations, regulations, and safety. Its fish content is limited to a short list of species present in the lake and a note that some species were planted below Hoover Dam.

## Background & Description

- **Lake:** Lake Mead has about 550 miles of shoreline at high watermark and runs 115 navigable miles east from Hoover Dam. At its highest level it is 589 feet deep, holds 32,359,274 acre-feet of water, and covers 229 square miles. Silt from the muddy Colorado River settles out as the river enters the lake, leaving the water a "vivid, cool blue."
- **Naming:** The lake is named after Dr. Elwood Mead, Commissioner of the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] when Hoover Dam was built.
- **Establishment:** The recreational area was established October 13, 1936, and expanded July 18, 1947, under an interbureau agreement between the [[National Park Service]] and the Bureau of Reclamation. The Bureau administers Boulder City, Hoover Dam, and [[Davis Dam]]. The National Park Service supervises all recreational activities. The area covers about 1,899,728 acres of federally owned land and follows about 185 miles of the Colorado River.
- **Other waters:** Below Hoover Dam, [[Lake Mohave]] is impounded behind Davis Dam and is also part of the recreational area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology**
- Rock layers were deposited over more than 18,000 feet of sediment, representing most divisions of geological time.
- Paleozoic limestone and shale overlie older granite. The gorge walls rise 4,000 to 6,000 feet.
- The Grand Wash Cliffs, roughly 4,000 feet high, mark the western edge of the high plateaus carved by the river.
- Below Hoover Dam, the river runs through the narrow Black Canyon before entering the first broad valley.

**Climate and plants**
- The area lies in the arid Lower Sonoran life zone, with average annual rainfall under 5 inches.
- Perennial plants develop extensive root systems. Annuals complete their life cycle in a few weeks after sporadic rains.
- Notable plants include cacti (beavertail cactus), brittlebush, ocotillo, yucca, and Joshua trees. Cottonwood grows along the lower Virgin River, and tamarix lines the lake shores.
- Pinyon-juniper forest and some ponderosa pine grow on the higher eastern plateaus.

**Aquatic life**
- Lake Mead is the only large body of water within a radius of many miles, and it supports more than 60 species of waterfowl and wading birds.
- The fish list includes bony tail, Colorado "white salmon," [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Carp]], [[Bluegill]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Mosquito Fish]].
- Some species were planted in the Colorado River below Hoover Dam, and the brochure states they should persist in the new lake. No population data or sample sizes are given.

**Terrestrial fauna**
- About 60 mammal species have been recorded. These include Nelson bighorn sheep, Inyo mule deer, coyotes, bobcats, and many rodents.
- More than 200 bird species have been seen, including golden eagle, 12 hawk species, and 4 owl species.
- Reptiles include at least 17 lizard species, an equal number of snakes, and 2 turtle species. Six amphibian species are listed, with the spotted toad the most common.

**Human prehistory**
- The region has been inhabited for about 2,000 years. Many sites along the Colorado and Virgin Rivers were surveyed before flooding by the reservoir.
- The "Lost City," a group of pueblos in Moapa Valley, is the largest permanent dwelling site. Pueblo occupation is dated to about A.D. 800 to 1200. Earlier Basket Maker people occupied the area from the start of the Christian era until about A.D. 800.

## Key Findings

1. **Fish community:** The reservoir contains at least nine named sport and forage fish species, plus the bony tail and Colorado "white salmon." The brochure gives no abundance or survey data.
2. **Stocking:** Fish were planted below Hoover Dam to establish populations in the new lake. Outcomes are not reported.
3. **Fishing rules:** A Nevada or Arizona state fishing license is required, because each state reserves the right to require one. Fishing is permitted all day, every day. Bag limits are set for the entire area as conditions require.
4. **Boating:** More than 1,000 privately owned boats operate on the lakes. Boats must be registered with the National Park Service before launching. Houseboats are not permitted.
5. **Hunting:** Hunting is restricted. Some sections may open when the superintendent and the State Game Commission agree.
6. **Camping:** Camping is limited to designated campgrounds, with a 30-day limit per calendar year at any one campground. The free public campground at [[Boulder Beach]] has modern facilities.
7. **Safety:** Visitors are advised to leave word of their plans, stay ashore in rough water, sit still in boats, carry life preservers and flares, and swim only in designated places.
8. **Protection:** Destroying or disturbing public property, vegetation, rocks, birds, or animals is prohibited.
9. **Best season:** March through May is considered the best period for flowers and birds. Summer temperatures can reach 110°F, but low humidity makes the heat more tolerable.
10. **Access:** The area is reached by road, bus (including Greyhound service from Las Vegas), air to Las Vegas, and rail via Las Vegas or Kingman, Ariz.
