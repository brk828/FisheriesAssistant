# Evolution of Differently Scaled Populations of Aphanius anatoliae: Egg-Size Genetics and Regressive Evolution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Hellmut Grimm]] (Zoological Institute and Zoological Museum, University of Hamburg, 1979) examines the egg size (yolk diameter) of seven populations of the egg-laying toothcarp [[Aphanius Anatoliae]] in south-western Anatolia. The species varies in scale cover and body shape among populations, and earlier workers explained that variation with a [[Bastard Hypothesis]] involving hybridisation between two ancestral forms. Grimm tested egg size as an independent trait by measuring variance, performing reciprocal and backcross crosses between the populations with the largest and smallest eggs, and estimating the number of genes involved. The results do not support the hybrid origin of scale variation. Egg size appears to be polygenic and additive, and it is interpreted as an adaptive regressive change driven by [[Salinity]] and the hydrochemistry of the lake basins. Scale reduction is interpreted as a selectively neutral regressive process.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and morphology.** *Aphanius anatoliae* (Leidenfrost, 1912; Pisces, Cyprinodontidae) is an egg-laying toothcarp of south-western Anatolia. Its populations differ in several characters, most notably:

- **Scale cover.** Some populations are fully scaled. Others show extreme reduction, with ventral and dorsal areas unscaled and only the middle lateral row (MLR) retained. Some populations are uniform, while others, such as the [[Burdur Lake]] population, are highly variable.
- **Body shape.** The "[[Anatolichthys]]" form is flatter and more elongated, with a superior mouth and a "quadrangular" head. The typical *Aphanius* form is high-backed. Villwock (1964) showed that all these forms belong to one species.
- **Egg size.** Three size classes are recognised (Grimm 1979b).

**Methods.**

- Yolk diameter was measured under a microscope with a calibrated ocular micrometer, where 1 ME (measurement unit) = 0.0625 mm. Non-spherical yolks were measured as ellipsoids and converted to spherical diameters. Ten eggs per female were used.
- Body index was calculated as body height divided by total length.
- Normality was tested with the [[Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test]] (p = 0.05). Variance homogeneity was tested with the [[Levene Test]] (p = 0.001).
- Crosses were labelled with the female first. For example, "Budü F1" is a Burdur female crossed with a Düğer male.
- Only fish older than one year with a total length of at least 34 mm were analysed, because egg size in Düğer depends on age and female length.
- All fish spawned under identical conditions in 5 ‰ artificial seawater on a synthetic (Perlon) algal substrate.
- Populations were imported in 1973 and 1974.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geological history.** Lakes in the western Anatolian range formed as freshwater lakes in the Pliocene (Bertne 1971). Increasing aridity in the late Pleistocene caused evaporation, isolating populations. Lakes without outflow became saline, with high sulphate concentrations. The resulting diversity of lake sizes and depths produced a range of salinities and ion compositions, so many current habitats are species-poor extreme environments (Grimm 1976, 1979a).

**Study sites.**

| Population | Habitat and setting | Salinity | Mean body index | Mean yolk diameter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[Düğer Population]] | Freshwater tributary of Burdur Lake, about 10 km away | Freshwater | 0.232 | 24.1 ME = 1.51 mm |
| [[Burdur Lake]] population | Burdur Lake, rich in magnesium sulphate | 19 ‰ | 0.189 | 15.67 ME = 0.98 mm (65 % of Düğer) |
| [[Acigöl Populations]] | Marginal springs of Acigöl, about 25 km from Burdur, sodium sulphate | Springs 0.91–2.14 ‰ | 0.182–0.199 | 19.36 ME = 1.21 mm (80.3 % of Düğer) |

- **Acigöl.** The lake is intermittently dried in summer, leaving only a salt crust. Fish breed in more than 20 marginal springs of low salinity, because the lake itself has become too saline. The spring streams dry up about 200 m from the springs. Over 20 populations are isolated from each other and connected only during exceptionally high water levels (Kosswig 1956). The Acigöl lake salinity was reported as 109 ‰ in spring and 141 ‰ in summer, most likely 10.9 to 14.1 ‰ in the original data, while its centre is considered azoic.
- **Burdur.** The lake is at 19 ‰ and Düğer lies only about 10 km away, but gene flow between them is negligible under present conditions, as shown by their consistently different egg sizes.

**Related species.** [[Kosswigichthys Asquamatus]] from east Anatolia (Hazergölü) is a fully scaleless toothcarp living under comparable conditions. Grimm suggests that scale reduction in *A. anatoliae* may continue towards similar scaleless populations.

## Key Findings

1. **Egg-size variance is homogeneous.** Coefficients of variation were low, at most 4.2 % of the mean. Variances were homogeneous across all Acigöl populations and across all studied populations (p = 0.001). Two further well-scaled populations, from Beyşehir and Çavuşçu lakes, showed the same range of variation, so the pattern is probably species-wide. Each population is therefore presumed to be largely homozygous for egg size.

2. **Egg size is polygenic and additive.** Reciprocal crosses showed no significant difference.
   - **F1 (Burdur × Düğer):** mean 19.43 ME, SD 0.65 ME (3.3 %). The mean deviates only slightly from the theoretical value expected toward the small-egg parent (44.6 %). F1 females showed no dependence of egg size on age or length.
   - **F2:** mean 18.37 ME, shifted 32 % toward the small-egg parent, SD 0.93 ME (5.1 %). Distribution was normal.
   - **Backcross to Burdur (F2R Bu, n = 43):** mean 17.44 ME, 21 % below the expected value, SD 0.91 ME (5.2 %).
   - **Backcross to Düğer (F2R Dü, n = 39):** mean 20.44 ME, 56.5 % from the expected value, SD 1.36 ME (5.6 %). This distribution was skewed (p = 0.02).

3. **Egg size is not genetically linked to body shape.** In four of the five segregating crosses, egg size and body index were uncorrelated. The exception, the Burdur × Büdü backcross (r = 0.56, regression coefficient a = 37.6), fell short of the coefficient of at least 84 expected under linkage (significant, p = 0.005). The authors conclude there is no genetic relationship between body form and egg size.

4. **Egg size is not linked to scale reduction.** All segregating crosses had uniformly well-scaled to slightly reduced fish. No strongly reduced individuals appeared, although egg sizes varied widely. The Burdur population is highly variable in scale cover yet has uniform egg size, which excludes linkage between scale reduction and egg size.

5. **Minimum gene number.** Among 82 backcross individuals, the ratio of fish matching the homozygous parental egg size to the rest was 1:9.1. This points to at least a trihybrid inheritance (1:8). None of the 14 F2 fish matched a parental size. Because the sample is small, the estimate is only a minimum.

6. **Intermediate populations.** Acigöl egg sizes fall roughly between Düğer and Burdur, with variances no larger than in other populations. Fewer genes appear to separate large from medium eggs than separate large from very small eggs.

7. **Rejection of the Bastard Hypothesis.** The homogeneous egg-size variances, and the absence of the increased variance expected in hybrids, contradict the view of [[Kosswig]] (1954) and [[Aksiray]] (1954) that scale variation arose from hybridisation of two ancestral *Aphanius* forms. Grimm argues instead that scale variability reflects [[Regressive Evolution]] under relaxed selection, possibly after the stabilising selection that maintained full scales in freshwater disappeared.

8. **Adaptive reduction of egg size.** Freshwater populations (Düğer) still lay large eggs, while the brackish Acigöl and Burdur populations lay smaller eggs. Grimm proposes that the smaller eggs respond to osmotic stress, because a smaller egg has a higher surface-to-volume ratio and may cope better with the higher osmotic pressure. This interpretation is consistent with Eddy (1974) and with Grimm (1979b). Egg size is therefore considered an adaptive regressive process with low variability, while scale reduction is a selectively neutral regression.

9. **Proposed evolutionary history.** The author proposes that the pre-Pleistocene ancestral population had large eggs. As the lakes shrank, a shared Acigöl–Burdur population diverged from the freshwater form and reduced egg size stepwise as salinity rose. Relaxed selection then allowed scale variation to increase. Later, the faunal connection between Acigöl and Burdur was severed. Acigöl springs kept the intermediate egg size, while Burdur, rising to 19 ‰, reduced its eggs further. The present freshwater populations, especially Düğer, are relicts of the original freshwater population.

10. **Overall conclusion.** Hydrochemical conditions are held responsible for both the adaptive reduction of egg size and the neutral regression of the scale cover. The two processes ran in parallel and independently, linked causally to the chemistry of the biotopes.
