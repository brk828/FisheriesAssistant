# Active Predation of Crayfish on Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 field note by [[Minckley]] and [[Craddock]] (Department of Biology, University of Louisville) documents crayfish actively preying on fishes, a behaviour the authors note is rarely reported because crayfish are generally regarded as scavengers. The observations involve two crayfish species, [[Orconectes Nais]] and [[Cambarus Bartoni]], attacking [[White Sucker]] and [[Blacknose Dace]] that had been partly incapacitated by [[Electrofishing]] during routine collecting, or by [[Rotenone]] in Kansas. The work was done mainly in Doe Run, Kentucky, with additional observations in Kansas. The authors conclude that crayfish may play a more active role in stream and lake ecology than previously credited.

The document also contains a second, unrelated piece from *The Progressive Fish-Culturist* describing a method for handling [[Channel Catfish]] fry at a hatchery.

## Background & Description

Crayfish are traditionally described as saprophagous, meaning they feed mainly on dead material, and published accounts of them actively feeding on live fish are scarce. The authors cite Pennak (1954) for the scavenging view and Rhoades (1944) for the habitat of *C. bartoni*.

The study species were:

- **[[Orconectes Nais]]**, observed in Kansas.
- **[[Cambarus Bartoni]]**, the main predator in Kentucky. It is characteristic of the upper portion of Doe Run, where a large population occurs. Individuals reached 200 mm total length (tip of chelae to tip of appressed telson) and about 90 g in weight were common.

The prey fishes were:

- **[[White Sucker]]** (*Catostomus commersoni*).
- **[[Blacknose Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys atratulus*).
- Unidentified fishes in the Kansas pond and Otter Creek-area observations, including a bullhead that was clasped and eaten.

Field methods used 115-volt, 300- to 600-watt alternating-current generators with either "pole" electrodes or a 30-foot six-electrode seine. Crayfish reacted to the shock with a rapid, convulsive backward movement, but were never immobilized by this gear. Fish reacted in two ways: white suckers moved violently at the surface or rapidly near the bottom until they entered the electrical field, where they were immobilized. Blacknose dace almost always showed the latter response.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Recovery from shock:** Both fish species typically regained equilibrium within 3 to 5 minutes. About 20 percent of observed fish were incapacitated for up to 30 minutes.
- **Mortality:** The authors knew of no direct fish mortality caused by the equipment. However, about 10 percent of fish were in an apparently moribund condition when preserved.
- **Timing:** All incidents followed routine shocking of the area. Some pools were treated with [[Rotenone]], and the authors suggest that rotenone and shocking operations can expose stunned fish to crayfish predation.
- **Pond observations (Kansas):** In a small pond in Kansas, crayfish were observed feeding on young fishes, and a crayfish fed on a bullhead that was apparently released from a snag. Golden shiners and sunfish were present in the pond. The pond was littered with dead and dying fish within an hour of treatment. Crayfish were also seen attacking fish that had been released from the shocker's field.
- **Ledge observation:** In a Kansas creek, a crayfish waited on a ledge and attacked a small fish that swam beneath it, suggesting that crayfish use an ambush strategy.

## Key Findings

1. **Active predation is documented.** Crayfish attacked and consumed live or stunned fishes. In one Kentucky case, a white sucker was held by its pectoral fins until it was dead, and the crayfish then fed on it.
2. **Attacks followed the first observation on November 22, 1959.** In a pool about 2 feet deep, one *C. bartoni* grasped a white sucker that was regaining equilibrium. Three more *C. bartoni* joined in. The sucker was ultimately consumed.
3. **A second series was recorded on April 15, 1960.** Three *C. bartoni* were seen feeding on a blacknose dace. Three suckers were netted from the bottom; one was undamaged, one was largely eviscerated with its skin removed, and one was dead. The authors inferred that the dace had been attacked after total immobilization by the shocker.
4. **Large-scale feeding in a small pool.** In a 30-foot-diameter, 4-foot-deep pool, eight white suckers were obtained after the shocker pass. All were in good condition shortly after collection, but about 15 minutes later crayfish had been seen feeding on them, and fins and caudal peduncles were damaged.
5. **Large fish consumed.** An 18.4-inch, 340 g fish was attacked over about 16 minutes, with consistent damage to the fins and removal of the caudal region.
6. **Crayfish help clear dead fish.** The authors suggest that crayfish may contribute to the rapid removal of dead and incapacitated fishes in streams and lakes, which would make them more important ecologically than previously assumed.
7. **Chemical and sensory cues.** Crayfish appeared to detect and respond to prey movements and possibly chemical cues, though the authors caution that these mechanisms are not fully established. [[Hasler]] (1957) is cited for fish sensory biology.
8. **Acknowledgments.** The authors thank Dr. Louis A. Krumholz for reviewing the manuscript and Mr. Charles Stone for the photographs in Figure 1. The work was supported in part by the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission (Contract No. AT-(40-1)-2596).

**Second article, [[Fish Hatchery]] method:** At the Arkansas Game and Fish Commission hatchery, young [[Channel Catfish]] fry were collected from modified milk-can spawning containers. The fry adhere to the can walls. The method involves gently removing the fry from the can instead of pouring them out. The change reduces physical damage and handling stress during removal, and the fry are then washed out into tubs of water.
