# Community Ecology of Desert Anurans

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anura

*Note: the source concerns amphibians (anurans), not fish. The tag line above is retained as the required format, with the anuran taxon tag appended.*

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by Bruce D. Woodward and Sandra L. Mitchell reviews the [[Community Ecology]] of desert anurans (frogs and toads). It traces three historical stages of community ecology (natural history, emergent properties, and experimental), argues that local-scale population processes should be integrated into community studies, and contrasts desert anuran communities with better-studied eastern North American communities. The authors conclude that desert communities are depauperate, that [[Predation]] and [[Competition]] structure them depending on pond predictability, and that intraspecific mating systems can shape interspecific interactions. The chapter ends with ten numbered conclusions and a call for research on abiotic-biotic interactions in deserts.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Because anurans are, with rare exceptions, the only amphibians in deserts, the review emphasizes anurans.
- **Historical stages:** Community ecology is described as passing through a "natural history" stage (focused on large taxa and geographic areas, with adaptation as a central theme), an "emergent properties" stage (community attributes such as species number and evenness, linked to [[Island Biogeography]] debates), and an "experimental" stage (focused on biotic interactions at smaller scales).
- **Life history:** Anurans have complex life cycles in which larvae are typically restricted to water while adults move between water bodies. This means a "community" can be defined at the scale of a single pond, all ponds a population breeds in, or all local ponds.
- **Study bias:** Most experimental work uses tadpoles, because adults are widely dispersed and hard to sample, and tadpoles are well suited to manipulation.
- **Desert species:** Adults breed in ponds for a night or two, feed above ground for a month or two, and then estivate underground for several months each year (Bragg 1965; Low 1976; Blair 1976).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Eastern versus desert communities**
- Eastern communities occur in forested areas with moderate to heavy rainfall. Ten or more species commonly breed in any local area, and several species often coexist in a pond. Ponds usually persist for months, allowing sequential breeding and [[Priority Effects]].
- Desert communities are much smaller. Few species occur in any local area (around five, per Bragg 1965), and one to three species typically co-occur in a pond. Most desert ponds are highly ephemeral, and breeding usually occurs at most once a year in a given pond.
- Desert temporary ponds have high tadpole densities, few predators, and little vegetation, so tadpoles are conspicuous. Permanent desert ponds contain more predators and more hiding places.
- Desert species often take two or more years to reach reproductive size and have long lifespans. Pond drying rate is proposed as a key abiotic factor.

**Pond types and species assemblages**
- Spadefoot toads (*Scaphiopus*) and true toads (*Bufo*) mainly occupy temporary ponds in the western United States.
- Permanent water sources support a different suite of frogs (mainly *Rana* and *Bufo*).
- Intermittent streams from rocky foothills support a third group (*Rana*, *Bufo*, and *Hyla*).

**Scale and patchiness**
- Aquatic habitats are experienced in a coarse-grained manner because non-aquatic areas act as dispersal barriers. Coarse-grained species may have traits shaped by selection within their own patch, not by interactions across patches.

## Key Findings

1. **Depauperate communities:** A single desert pond contains 1 to 5 species, and any local area usually contains 10 or fewer species (Bragg 1965; Pomeroy 1981; Woodward 1981).
2. **Segregated breeding:** Each desert species usually breeds only in temporary or only in permanent ponds, though both pond types can occur within meters of each other (Woodward 1987a).
3. **Temporary pond differences:** Temporary ponds contain significantly fewer predator types and fewer individual predators than permanent ponds, but they have higher tadpole densities (Woodward 1981, 1983a, 1982b).
4. **Competition in temporary ponds:** In artificial temporary ponds, temporary-pond species had strong negative effects on the survival and/or developmental rate of *Rana pipiens* and *Bufo woodhousei*. Their likely mechanisms were exploitation competition and predation by *S. multiplicatus* tadpoles (Woodward 1982c). *Rana catesbeiana* could not colonize temporary ponds because larval development takes more than 11 months.
5. **Predation on temporary-pond tadpoles:** Most natural predators (insects, fish, snakes) consistently bias predation toward temporary-pond tadpoles. Increased movement appears to be the main mechanism (Woodward 1983a).
6. **Predictability rule:** Predation appears to dominate when tadpoles are a predictable resource (permanent ponds), while competition becomes important when tadpoles are unpredictable and densities are high (temporary ponds) (Conclusion 5).
7. **Food limitation:** Supplemental feeding increased survival only in the lowest-density *S. couchi* ponds in Texas (Newman 1987), suggesting resources are very limited in temporary ponds.
8. **Context dependence:** Species that perform well in one selective context may perform poorly in another (Woodward 1982c, 1983a; Morin 1987).
9. **Abiotic-biotic interaction:** Wilbur (1987) showed that pond duration determines which species complete metamorphosis, and that abiotic and biotic factors interact. The authors call for comparable desert studies.
10. **Mating systems:** Correlates of male mating success predict offspring growth and survival in *Scaphiopus* (Woodward 1986, 1987c), metamorphic weight in *B. woodhousei* (Mitchell 1990), and larval growth in *Hyla crucifer* (Woodward, Travis, and Mitchell 1988). Mating systems may therefore shape interspecific interactions.
11. **Female oviposition choice:** Females breed only after rains deepen a pond. Pond depth may therefore determine which species co-occur as tadpoles.
12. **Research gaps:** Direct tests of interspecific interactions in desert anurans are almost nonexistent. Adult-stage effects, [[Crustacean]] co-occurrence, and irrigation-driven predator increases remain largely unstudied.
13. **Conclusions summary:** The authors emphasize local conditions and history, broader taxonomic scope (including tadpole-insect competition, Morin et al. 1988), and the need to quantify relative effects of biotic and abiotic factors across spatial and temporal scales.
