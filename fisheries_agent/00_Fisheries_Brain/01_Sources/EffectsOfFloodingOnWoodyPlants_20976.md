# Effects of Flooding on Woody Plants at the West Sandy Dewatering Project, Kentucky Reservoir

#botany #forestry #dendrology #woody-plants

## Executive Summary

This 1955 report, reprinted from the *Journal of Forestry* (Vol. 53, No. 4), examines how flooding affects bottomland woody plants in the [[West Sandy Dewatering Project]], a malaria control diking project on the [[Kentucky Reservoir]] in [[Henry County Tennessee]]. The project was built to pump water out of a diked basin during the [[Anopheles Mosquito Control|anopheline]] breeding season, but in wet years it behaved more like an uncleared flood control reservoir with fixed maximum discharge. The authors, from the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] Malaria Control Branch, found that flooding during the growing season killed or injured much of the forest in the storage basin. They mapped survival contours for 39 woody species and grouped the basin into five flood-stress zones.

## Background & Description

The project was one of eight malaria control dewatering projects built during the construction of Kentucky Reservoir, which was impounded in September 1944. Experience showed that summertime dewatering worked in some areas but not others, depending on watershed size, inflow, and local stratigraphy. West Sandy was one of the areas that proved hard to keep dewatered.

**Physical setup**
- Earth-fill dike on West Sandy Creek, about four miles upstream from its confluence with Big Sandy River (a Tennessee River tributary).
- Dike length 3,590 ft, height about 20 ft, crest width 10 ft; outboard slope 8:1, inboard slope 3:1.
- Two-story concrete pumphouse at the south end with six electric pumps, combined capacity 250,000 gallons per minute.
- Earth spillway at the north end, 8,170 ft long and 650 ft wide.
- Scheduled dewatering area of 3,343 acres at top summer pool (elevation 359 ft). In 1944 nearly all of it was densely forested bottomland.
- Storage at elevation 359 ft is about 12,700 acre-feet. Gross dewatering rate is 1,100 acre-feet per day.
- About 38 miles of shoreline and roughly five square miles of surface area; watershed of about 144 square miles, roughly 30 times the storage basin.

**Planned annual water schedule**
1. Winter operation with Kentucky Reservoir.
2. Sharp spring drawdown.
3. Maintained dewatering through the [[Anopheline Breeding Season]].
4. Refilling in the wet winter season.

The schedule was meant to suppress mosquitoes while protecting bottomland forest. Trees also stabilized a highway fill across the lower area and the dike during high water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Operational failures.** The area was dewatered on schedule in only three of the first seven seasons. High inflows made dewatering impractical even with pumps at full capacity. Deviations from the schedule were large and long enough to favor mosquito breeding and to damage forests in the basin.

**Hydrological record.** Continuous water-level records exist from March 1945. Earlier elevations were interpolated from daily gauge readings at Kentucky Dam and Johnsonville. Counts of flooded days by contour were compiled for the project's life.

**Season definitions.** The plant growing season was set at April 1 to October 1, and the dormant season at October 1 to April 1. These dates were based on local phenology. Murray, Kentucky, about 20 miles north, has a 187-day growing season (last spring freeze April 15, first fall freeze October 19). Maximum possible flooding from September 1, 1944 to July 1, 1952 was 1,402 days for growing seasons and 1,458 days for dormant periods.

**Vertical zonation of survival.** Reconnaissance by helicopter showed clear survival contours related to flood duration. The most frequently flooded zone had no surviving woody species. The rarely flooded zone had few deaths. The middle zone had an intermediate number of affected species.

**Seedling regeneration.** Abundant seedlings germinated during dewatered periods. In 1946, TVA foresters counted about 9,000 seedlings per acre below the 349-foot contour, including blue beech, water oak, American elm, sweetgum, willow oak, blackgum, hawthorn, winged elm, and deciduous holly. A 1952 survey found no six- or seven-year-old stands between 343 and 349 feet. Recently germinated seedlings were common, however. Only willow established and survived near the 347-foot contour. The authors concluded that native or planted stock would not survive unless the area was dewatered effectively during growing seasons.

**Height advantage.** Among species of similar water tolerance, taller plants appeared to survive better. In one case, flooded buttonball died while adjacent black willow with tops above water survived. Where willow was fully submerged and buttonball was not, the reverse happened.

## Key Findings

**1. Flooding during the growing season drives woody plant mortality.**
- Periodic winter flooding had little significance for bottomland trees. Growing-season flooding was mainly responsible for observed death and injury.
- The authors caution that the data are too limited to separate the additive effects of winter and summer flooding.
- A continuous summer flood of about two weeks was enough to kill large beech specimens. In contrast, 18 days of deep winter flooding (about eight feet at elevation 361 ft in January–February 1950) had no apparent adverse effect on beech.
- Yellow-poplar and dogwood were even less tolerant of flooding than beech. Large specimens of these three species may indicate sites relatively free of growing-season flooding.
- Plantations of sweetgum, green ash, sycamore, and other bottomland species showed little or no harm from long dormant-season inundation.

**2. Tolerance of 39 woody species was ranked by the lowest healthy tree.**
- Healthy and dead lines were defined for each species using the lowest healthy individual and the lowest sickly individual with signs of life.
- Black cherry was the least tolerant species, with a healthy line at 359.4 ft and 0.6% of growing-season time flooded.
- Swamp ironwood was the most tolerant, with a healthy line at 347.5 ft and 42.9% of growing-season time flooded.
- These two survival contours are separated by about 12 vertical feet.
- Other notable entries include beech (healthy line 358.2 ft, 3.2%), yellow-poplar (357.9 ft, 4.5%), sweetgum (350.1 ft, 34.3%), water tupelo (348.6 ft, 38.4%), and black willow (347.6 ft, 42.5%).
- All woody species were killed where root crowns were flooded more than 54% of the time across all growing seasons.
- The gap between healthy and dead lines was about three feet for sweetgum. Pawpaw showed almost no sickly zone.

**3. Survival is stratified into five zones (Table 4).**

| Zone | Elevation | % time flooded (growing seasons) | Condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 343–346 ft | 54–99 | All woody species killed; snags standing, tops mostly fallen |
| II | 346–348 ft | 41–54 | High mortality; swamp ironwood, overcup oak, black willow, and sweetgum survive |
| III | 348–355 ft | 16–41 | Survivors increase with elevation up to an ironwood band near 355 ft |
| IV | 355–359 ft | 1–16 | Survival nearly complete; some dogwood, yellow-poplar, and hornbeam dead |
| V | 359–360 ft | 0–1 | Normal forest |

**4. Dewatering is needed to regenerate forest.** Seeds were plentiful, and seedlings germinated during dry spells. Subsequent adverse water levels usually prevented establishment, especially in the lower half of the basin where most timber had died.

**5. Conclusions for management.** The authors recommended using large specimens of beech, yellow-poplar, and dogwood as indicators of sites free of growing-season flooding. They also noted that the project's functional behavior as an uncleared flood control reservoir undercut both its mosquito control and forest protection goals. They summarized survival data graphically against growing-season flood percentage for each species. Growing-season flooding was the focus because surface water exerts its major adverse effects on bottomland woody components at that time.
