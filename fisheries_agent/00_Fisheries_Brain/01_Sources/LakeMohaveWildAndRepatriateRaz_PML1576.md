# Lake Mohave Wild and Repatriate Razorback Sucker Population Status Assessment 2001

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report assesses the March 2001 status of wild adult and repatriated [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], lower [[Colorado River]]. The wild adult population is estimated at 3,954 fish, confirming a dramatic decline over the past decade. Of 46,610 juveniles repatriated between 1992 and 2001, an estimated 4,523 (9.7%) remained in March 2001. The central finding is that survival is strongly linked to release size: fish larger than 35 cm at stocking were recaptured at roughly ten times the rate of fish smaller than 30 cm. The authors recommend rearing fish to at least 30 cm total length before release, even if fewer fish are stocked.

## Background & Description

The [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), formed in 1990, is a multi-agency partnership including the Arizona Game and Fish Department, Arizona State University (ASU), the USGS Biological Resources Division, the Nevada Department of Wildlife, the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the National Park Service. Its primary mission is to produce and rear native lower Colorado River fishes, especially razorback sucker.

**Rearing and release process:**
- Larvae are collected annually during the winter-spring spawning season from Lake Mohave shoreline shallows.
- Fish are reared at several facilities, including Boulder City golf course ponds, Boulder City wetland ponds, and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], and in predator-free lakeside backwaters (e.g., Yuma Cove, Davis Cove, Dandy Cove).
- Juveniles nominally reaching 30 cm are tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) tags, measured, and released.

**Monitoring:** Lake Mohave monitoring programs, initiated in 1968 by W.L. Minckley and ASU, are repeated annually at the same sites, times, and gear. Field trips occur in May, March (the "Razorback Round-up"), and November, using trammel netting and electrofishing. ASU serves as the central data repository for lower Colorado River native fish, and data are logged, entered into Excel spreadsheets, and screened for duplicate PIT tags to separate wild adults from repatriates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Razorback sucker can reproduce in the wild, but larvae cannot survive and grow to adulthood without protection from predators. The NFWG repatriation program is presented as demonstrating this need, since post-release survival is strongly size-dependent. Marsh and Brooks (1989) suggested young razorback suckers should be at least 300 mm long to be relatively safe from most aquatic predators. More than 60% of repatriates released to date (about 31,000 fish) were smaller than 300 mm, and recapture data suggest many of these did not survive. Sampling is concentrated in the March spawning season, and some repatriates had not yet reached sexual maturity and so were unavailable for recapture.

## Key Findings

**Capture data (March 2001):**
- 655 razorback suckers captured: 263 untagged and 392 PIT-tagged.
- 46 same-trip recaptures among PIT-tagged fish, leaving 346 previously tagged fish.
- Sex composition: 301 females (46%), 335 males (51%), 7 juveniles (1%), 12 indeterminate (2%).
- Of the 346 previously tagged fish, 158 were wild adults, 149 were repatriates, and 39 were field-recorded recaptures absent from the database.

**Wild adults:**
- Population estimated at 3,954 using the Schnabel annual census method (1992–2001 data), slightly lower than the 4,001 estimated from data through 2000.
- Sex ratio approximately 1:1.14 (females to males); sex of two adults was indeterminate.

**Juvenile releases (1992 to March 2001):**
- 46,610 juveniles released, of which 46,583 were PIT tagged.
- Most came from lakeside backwaters (17,794), Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery (4,116), Willow Beach NFH (11,494), Boulder City Golf Course Ponds (5,343), and Boulder City Wetlands Park (7,658).
- Yuma Cove was the most productive backwater, contributing 9,117 fish.
- 1999 was the largest release year (16,029 fish, 34% of total); 2000 released 7,152 fish.
- Release TL ranged from 96 to 563 mm; annual averages ranged from 180 mm (1993) to 394 mm (1996); the nine-year grand average was 306 mm.

**Repatriate recaptures and abundance:**
- 397 repatriates recaptured since 1992 (excluding 44 multiple and same-trip recaptures), about 0.8% of fish released.
- Estimate 1 (preferred; Schnabel single-census, 1992–2001): 4,523 repatriates (90% CI 3,092–6,946), about 22% higher than the 2000 estimate of 3,553.
- Estimate 2 (recapture ratio method): 1,530 repatriates in 2001, about 3% survivorship of 46,610 fish stocked.
- Estimate 3 (stocking and recapture through 1999): 1,250 repatriates in 1999, about 5% survivorship of 23,400 fish stocked 1992–1999.

**Size-dependent survival:**
- About 32% of recaptured repatriates (129 fish) were released at under 300 mm.
- Recaptures represent only 0.4% of fish released under 30 cm, nearly 1.4% of fish released at 30–35 cm (166 recaptures), and nearly 4% of fish released over 35 cm (102 recaptures).
- Survivorship for fish released above 35 cm is therefore about ten times that of fish released under 30 cm.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- The program has repatriated about 4,500 fish since 1992, far short of the target of 50,000 repatriates.
- The wild population has declined from probable recent-historical levels in the hundreds of thousands to fewer than 4,000.
- Young razorback suckers should be reared to a minimum total length of 30 cm before release, with larger sizes attained where practical, even if fewer fish are released.
