# The Eppalock Soil Conservation Project: Reservoir Sedimentation and Catchment Management Politics

#freshwater-ecology #reservoir-sedimentation #catchment-management

## Executive Summary

This paper re-examines the [[Eppalock Soil Conservation Project]] (ESCP), a widely cited "whole-of-catchment" erosion control scheme upstream of [[Lake Eppalock]] on the [[Campaspe River]] in central Victoria. The scheme was justified in 1959 by the [[Parliamentary Public Works Committee]] (PPWC), which predicted that enlarging the reservoir would lose a large share of its capacity to sediment. The authors argue that this prediction was inflated and that the reservoir was never seriously threatened by sedimentation, even without the catchment program. Its later success was therefore asserted rather than demonstrated. The paper also traces the evangelical, US-influenced and politically constrained origins of the soil conservation movement in Victoria, which help explain the overstated estimates.

The study is an evaluation of a water-resources and land-management project rather than an ecological survey. It contains no fish, invertebrate or water-chemistry data.

## Background & Description

- **Source:** A research article in *Australian Geographical Studies* (March 1999, 37(1):37–49) by J.A. Davis, I.D. Rutherfurd and B.L. Finlayson, from the University of Melbourne and Monash University. The front matter also includes an unrelated glacier reference list, which is omitted here.
- **Reservoir history:** A 74 000 ML storage was first proposed in the mid-1860s and abandoned in 1927 for technical and financial reasons. A smaller 1500 ML storage, the [[Eppalock Weir]], was completed in 1932. Three PPWC inquiries followed, in 1935, 1949 and 1959.
- **Enlargement:** The reservoir was enlarged in the early 1960s to 312 000 ML. Its catchment exceeds 2000 km², mostly cleared for agriculture and now dominated by dryland grazing. The ESCP covered 829 km² of it.
- **ESCP activities:** On-farm management (for example, farm water supply), soil conservation works such as gully-head control structures and diversion banks, and pasture improvement. The project ran from 1960 to 1975 and was funded by the Victorian State Government.
- **Economic evaluation:** A 1978 evaluation by Thompson reported a net value of A$2.91 million (1975–76 prices) and an internal rate of return of 24.5%. It focused on production gains and did not adequately assess reduced sedimentation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sediment sources:** [[Gully Erosion]] was identified as the most significant sediment source. Sheet and rill erosion, tunnel erosion, streambank erosion and shoreline erosion were also considered. Streambank erosion was excluded from the estimate because it was not targeted by the ESCP.
- **Gully dynamics:** Gully extension in representative sub-catchments (for example, [[Shepherds Creek]] and [[Goodalls Creek]]) was already declining by the 1959 inquiry. Aerial photographs from 1940, 1949 and 1960 showed minimal extension after 1940. Gully extension is generally expected to slow as the catchment area feeding each gully head shrinks.
- **Sediment delivery:** Broad footslopes limit sediment delivery from hillslopes to the channel network. Much gully-derived sediment left the catchment before the reservoir was built.
- **Regional comparison:** Sedimentation at [[Melton Reservoir]], west of Melbourne, was used as an analogue. Its 1916–45 sediment yield was applied directly to Lake Eppalock, although this period had some of the highest erosion rates in the state.
- **Historical erosion:** Erosion and siltation were documented in Victoria from the nineteenth century. Problems included sand drift in the [[Mallee]] (1929–30) and erosion concerns around Lake Hume (1925). Government response was slow. The [[Soil Conservation Board]] was established in 1940, and the [[Soil Conservation Authority]] followed under 1950 legislation.

## Key Findings

**Reassessing the 1959 PPWC estimate (1950–60 period):**

| Method | PPWC estimate (×10³ m³) | Revised estimate (×10³ m³) |
|---|---|---|
| Gully extension (SCA) | 6660 | Cannot be revised quantitatively; judged an overestimate |
| Melton Reservoir (SRWSC) | 5180 | 1730 (using the 1945–60 rate) |
| Eppalock Weir sedimentation | N/A | 620–1230 |

- The PPWC's 30-year projection was 22.4% capacity loss. The revised SRWSC-based estimate gives about 1.7%, which the authors describe as roughly 13 times smaller.
- The critical PPWC assumption was that sedimentation would double each decade. The authors consider this "almost certainly incorrect."

**Three independent approaches, all showing low risk:**

1. Revised SRWSC/Melton estimate: about 1.7% capacity loss over 30 years.
2. Sedimentation in the original Eppalock Weir: about 0.9% over 30 years.
3. Source-based estimate of potential erosion (gully, sheet and rill, shoreline) with sediment delivery and trap efficiency: a total of 17.94 × 10⁶ m³ deposited, with the paper stating no more than 6% of capacity lost over 50–100 years. The text reports this as "no more than 6%" and the table as a value of 57 (presumably 5.7%).

**Assessment of the ESCP:**

- Lake Eppalock's small sediment volume (surveys in 1968, 1976 and 1995) has been taken as evidence of the ESCP's success. The paper argues that the reservoir was not in danger, so the outcome cannot be credited to the program.
- The ESCP's other achievements are acknowledged: improved production, landholder participation, and its value as a training ground and trial site for soil conservation techniques.
- The authors state the ESCP is "an excellent example of 'Total Catchment Management'" but not an example of its benefit for controlling sediment delivery. See [[Whole-Of-Catchment Management]].

**Reasons for the overstated estimates:**

- **US influence:** Erosion and reservoir-sedimentation knowledge largely came from the United States. Witzig (1943) was influential in Australia, and contour-furrow design tables were adapted from US data.
- **Evangelical advocacy:** Soil conservationists such as Sam Clayton (New South Wales) and [[Harold Hanslow]] (SRWSC) used dramatic erosion statistics to promote their cause. Hudson (1985) noted that enthusiasm can lead to "weakening a good argument by exaggeration."
- **Limited process understanding:** The 1959 inquiry assumed all eroded material reached the reservoir, ignoring storage within the catchment.
- **Political context:** The [[Country Party]] under [[Albert Dunstan]] (Premier 1935–45) was accused of neglecting erosion. A 1958 Liberal election strategy sought rural votes, and the SRWSC wanted to develop the Campaspe catchment. The ESCP offered a way to sell soil conservation to farmers.
- **Changing evidence:** In 1949, G.T. Thompson (SCB) had judged the catchment in reasonably good condition, and a 1959 PPWC report recorded that it had improved. The 1959 SCA and SRWSC arguments nonetheless succeeded. The authors suggest either an error or deliberate exaggeration. Some former staff supported the second explanation, saying the real aim was to demonstrate productivity gains.
- **Scientific hook:** Sediment control may have served as a "scientific hook" for a project justified on other grounds, consistent with Nelkin's (1975) observation that policymakers often frame decisions as technical rather than political.

**Conclusions:**

- Lake Eppalock was not in danger of losing a significant share of its capacity to sedimentation, contrary to the 1959 PPWC finding.
- The authors do not single out individuals or organisations for blame, and conclude that the overall decision was justified.
- Projects should be evaluated against their underlying rationale and the factors driving them, not only their stated aims. Otherwise, assessments of natural resource management methods risk being misleading.
