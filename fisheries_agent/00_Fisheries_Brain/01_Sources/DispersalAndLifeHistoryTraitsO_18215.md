# Dispersal and Life History Traits of *Notropis girardi* Introduced into the Pecos River, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The [[Arkansas River Shiner]] (*Notropis girardi*), a cyprinid endemic to the [[Arkansas River]] drainage, was first recorded in the [[Pecos River]], New Mexico, in 1978 after an apparent release of bait fish below [[Sumner Dam]]. By 1981 the species had dispersed about 260 km downstream and by 1987 it had reached the New Mexico–Texas border. The study compared the introduced population with a native population from [[Revuelto Creek]] in the [[Canadian River]] drainage. Age structure, growth and mortality were similar between the two populations, and both showed multiple spawning peaks tied to high discharge. The authors conclude that the flow regime of the Pecos River, which mimics the variable flows of the species' former native habitat, explains much of the introduced population's success.

## Background & Description

*Notropis girardi* is a warm-water minnow that was once common across its native range in sandy-bottomed, fluctuating streams and rivers. Its distribution and abundance have declined sharply in much of that range. The authors attribute this decline mainly to altered natural flow patterns, with possible competitive or other interactions with the introduced [[Red River Shiner]] (*Notropis bairdi*). The westernmost native populations occur in the Canadian River drainage of New Mexico, where the species remains relatively common.

The study aimed to document the introduction, dispersal and life history of *N. girardi* in the Pecos River, and to compare its habitat and biology with native populations to explain its success.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Pecos River originates in north-central New Mexico, flows south across eastern New Mexico and western Texas, and enters [[Amistad Reservoir]] at its confluence with the [[Rio Grande]]. The New Mexico drainage covers 50,022 km². Several reservoirs impound the river.

**Flow regime.** Since Sumner Dam closed in 1937, mean annual discharge has declined slightly (6.7 m³/s for 1927–1936 versus 5.7 m³/s for 1938–1986), peak flows have diminished, and zero-flow days are common. Flows are generally highest in May and June and range from 0.03 to 28.4 m³/s during the May–September irrigation season. Winter flows are presumably sustained by spring input and aquifer recharge.

**Habitat.** From Sumner Dam to McMillan Reservoir the river is shallow and braided, with sandy-bottomed runs and short riffles. Downstream of McMillan Reservoir, habitat is mainly pools separated by short riffles. Water temperature ranges from 0 to 32 °C and may reach 38 °C. Conductivity ranges from 1000 to 10,000 µS/cm, and water is turbid at high flows.

**Native comparison site.** Revuelto Creek, a tributary of the Canadian River near Logan, New Mexico, is 5–15 m wide and generally less than 0.15 m deep, with shifting loose sand and some small gravel. Mean annual discharge is 1.25 m³/s, and winter low flows below 0.05 m³/s are common. Irrigation return flows supplement its natural flow.

**Native fish community.** The Pecos River supports more fish species than are typically found alongside *N. girardi* in its native range, which the authors suggest should make establishment by a non-native species more difficult.

## Key Findings

**Introduction and dispersal**

- The earliest Pecos River specimens (n = 16) were collected on 7 September 1978 just downstream of Sumner Dam. Collections from 1974–1977 contained no *N. girardi*, so the introduction most likely occurred in 1978.
- The species colonized about 260 km of the mainstream by autumn 1981 and was verified 98 km further downstream, to the New Mexico–Texas border, by 1987.
- Dispersal was downstream, presumably from a single introduction. Bait-fish trade through local dealers, which is not closely monitored, may also have introduced the species, possibly more than once.

**Abundance**

- In September 1981–February 1982, *N. girardi* was found at 14 of 17 sites, making up 6.4% of specimens. The largest collection was 472 specimens at Lake Arthur Falls (H11).
- In 1986–1987, the species was found in 21 of 24 collections at 14 of 16 localities. A total of 2213 specimens (8.8% of all specimens) was collected, with a mean of 105 per collection. The largest collection was 704 specimens at Lake Arthur Falls (B9).
- Abundance was highest in the middle reaches. The largest increase occurred in the middle 1 reach, while the middle 2 reach declined slightly. The species remained uncommon immediately below Sumner Reservoir and between McMillan and Red Bluff reservoirs, probably because of limited shallow, braided, sandy habitat and clear water.
- A tributary record was made in the Rio Felix (n = 15), a small intermittent stream. Two juvenile [[Bluntnose Shiner]] (*Notropis simus pecosensis*), a federally threatened taxon, were also collected there, which the authors link to a June 1986 flood.

**Life history**

- Age structure included Age 0 to Age III, dominated by Age 0 and Age I fish. Most prespawning specimens in March and May 1986 were Age I, and older fish were scarce by July and August, suggesting high spawning and/or overwinter mortality.
- Growth was fastest in the first summer and slowed sharply afterward. First-year growth matched values reported for laboratory-reared fish.
- Age-0 fish of 11–32 mm SL in late August indicate extended or fractional spawning. The bimodal distribution of Age I fish suggests more than one annual spawning peak. This pattern contrasts with earlier work by Cross et al. (1983), who reported a single, brief annual spawning based on uniform egg size in Kansas populations.
- Spawning in 1986 ran from mid-May through late July, with a peak between mid-June and July. Reproduction coincided with a sharp rise in discharge on 3 May 1986, when flow increased from 0.42 to 22.2 m³/s.
- Life history parameters, including growth, age structure and mortality, were similar among the Pecos River population, the Revuelto Creek population (n = 438), and native populations in Oklahoma and Kansas.

**Interpretation**

- High discharge appears to trigger spawning, keep eggs and larvae buoyant, and carry them downstream, promoting dispersal and establishment.
- Irrigation demands peak during the spawning season and are unpredictable, with high releases lasting days to weeks. These pulses mimic the variable flows of the species' former native range, which the authors identify as the main reason for the introduced population's success.
- Spring input and groundwater recharge keep the channel wet when reservoir releases are reduced, which helps the resident community survive.

**Related introduced species**

- [[Plains Minnow]] (*Hybognathus placitus*), an abundant native of Great Plains streams, was also introduced to the Pecos River and became the most abundant species there, making up more than 80% of the catch at some sites. Its success is attributed to the same high-flow conditions. Its establishment coincided with the disappearance of *H. amarus*, which had not been collected since 1968.
- The authors suggest *N. girardi* may have entered the Pecos River as an accidental bycatch in a *H. placitus* bait shipment.

**Conclusion**

The authors report that *N. girardi* is now well established in the Pecos River downstream of Sumner Reservoir. Its persistence depends on the variable flows that irrigation releases currently provide.
