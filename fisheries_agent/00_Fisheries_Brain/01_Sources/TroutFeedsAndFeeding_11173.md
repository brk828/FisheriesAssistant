# Trout Feeds and Feeding

#ichthyology #aquaculture #salmonidae #fish-nutrition #hatchery-management

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service circular (Circular 159, October 1963, with April 1964 correction) provides comprehensive guidance on feeding protocols and nutritional management for hatchery-reared trout. Authored by Arthur M. Phillips Jr., A. V. Tunison, and George C. Balzer from the [[Trout Nutrition Laboratory]] at [[Cortland, New York]], the document synthesizes decades of cooperative research (initiated 1932) among the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], [[New York State Conservation Department]], and [[Cornell University]]. It addresses feeding methods (unbound, bound, blower, cement-mixer, and pelleted diets), feeding rates based on fish size and water temperature, diet composition, ingredient selection, and production efficiency metrics. The circular emphasizes that successful trout nutrition depends equally on proper diet formulation and correct feeding practices, with metabolic rate—governed by water temperature, fish size, and activity—as the central determinant of nutritional requirements.

## Background & Description

### Institutional Context
The [[Cortland Experimental Hatchery]] established systematic trout nutrition research in 1932. This circular consolidates previous publications including Tunison's feeding tables (1936-1955) and Phillips' "Nutrition of Trout" series in the Progressive Fish-Culturist. The work represents a transition period in American aquaculture: from all-meat diets to meat-meal mixtures to manufactured pellets, with approximately 70% of National Fish Hatcheries using pellets by 1961 (Table 11).

### Trout Species Covered
The document addresses [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), providing species-specific feeding tables (Tables 1-4). Feeding rates are expressed as percentage of body weight, varying by fish size (measured as numbers per pound and length in inches) and water temperature (°F).

### Metabolic Principles
The authors establish that trout, as cold-blooded animals, exhibit basal metabolic rates directly proportional to water temperature. Body temperature may range from just above freezing to 70°F, creating "a tremendous range of normal metabolic rates." Metabolism encompasses anabolism (tissue construction) and catabolism (energy production through oxidation of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins). Factors affecting metabolic rate include:

- **Water temperature**: Higher temperatures increase metabolism and food requirements
- **Fish age/size**: Young, rapidly growing fish have higher metabolic rates than adults
- **Activity level**: Fish in swift waters require more energy than those in ponds
- **Water chemistry**: Fish transferred from high-calcium to low-calcium waters increase metabolic rate to oppose mineral losses, potentially causing death
- **Disease**: Conditions like furunculosis and kidney disease disrupt excretory function and overall metabolism

### Feeding Methods Evolution

**Unbound Diets**: Finely ground meat (5/64-inch plate) for fry, distributed via feather, spoon, or screen-bottomed dipper. Nutrient leaching is inevitable; use should be minimized.

**Bound Diets (Mush/Dough)**: E. W. Fentress discovered that 2% salt addition to pork spleen and dry meals creates rubber-like mixtures resisting water disintegration. Best binders (in order): pork spleen, beef spleen, horse spleen, beef liver, beef lungs. Meat should be slightly frozen (ice crystals present) when ground. Fed via modified potato ricer, pond ricer, compressed-air feeder, or by hand. Meat content ranges from all-meat (liver-spleen for fish 0.75-1 inch) to 25-60% dry meal mixtures (fish >1.5 inches).

**Blower Feeds**: Meat-meal mixtures (45% meat, 55% dry meals) passed through centrifugal blower, producing moist sawdust-like particles. Suitable for pond-reared fish up to 3-4 inches; inefficient for larger trout due to small particle size.

**Cement Mixer Feeds**: For fish 3 inches to brood stock size. Typical composition: 55% meat (minimum 15% beef liver), 45% dry meals, no added water. Mixed 10-15 minutes at ~17 rpm. Larger particles than blower feeds, better consumed by larger fish.

**Pelleted Foods**: Revolutionary development offering economy, ease of feeding, and preparation efficiency. Produce fish at approximately half the pounds required for conventional diets (though calorie requirements remain similar—pellets contain ~1,200 cal/lb vs. 720 cal/lb for meat-meal mixtures). Federal specifications (Table 12) define particle sizes from starter crumbles (595-420 microns) through pellets (3/32 to 3/16 inch diameter). By 1961, 70% of food at National Fish Hatcheries consisted of pellets. No published open-formula pellet qualified as complete food without meat supplementation, though many commercial pellets were successfully fed as sole diets. [[Oregon State Hatcheries]] developed moist pellets (meats and dry meals) stored frozen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Water Quality and Carrying Capacity
The document emphasizes water flow, volume, and changes per hour as critical environmental parameters. Kenneth Nichols' table (Table 5) establishes carrying capacity based on 8-12 gallons per minute in troughs. [[David C. Haskell]] (1955) developed formulas relating carrying capacity to metabolic rate:

**Permissible weight (lb/ft³) = (Weight of food × 100) / Percent fed**

Example: [[Brown Trout]] (1 inch, 53°F) carried at 1.75 lb/ft³ in troughs at [[Rome, New York Hatchery]], fed at 7.6% body weight, equals 0.13 lb food/ft³. Pond capacity for same conditions: approximately one-sixth trough capacity (0.02 lb/ft³).

### Adverse Conditions

**Insufficient Water Flow**: Metabolic waste products accumulate if not diluted. Solutions include increasing flow, reducing water volume (increasing changes/hour), or reducing food intake.

**Low Oxygen**: Common in summer with warm water, insufficient flow, and overcrowding. Critical in dirt ponds with aquatic vegetation—mortalities occur ~4 hours post-feeding when fish oxygen demand peaks during plant respiration (oxygen withdrawal vs. photosynthetic production). Management: schedule feedings to maintain photosynthesis during peak fish demand; reduce rations on dark, cloudy days; eliminate feeding if necessary.

### Temperature and Feeding Dynamics
Daily temperature fluctuations should guide feeding schedules. Feed heaviest during temperature rise (typically morning), lightest during decline. Exception: mountain-fed hatcheries where temperature drops until 10-11 AM require afternoon-heavy feeding. Seasonal patterns: spring warming accelerates metabolism, permitting feeding above table values with improved conversions; fall cooling depresses metabolism, suggesting feeding below table values for efficiency.

## Key Findings

### Feeding Rate Determination

**Standard Tables**: Deuel et al. (1952) tables (Tables 1-4) express feeding rates as percentage of body weight, varying by species, fish size (numbers/pound and length), and water temperature. Example for [[Brook Trout]] at 47°F: 7.8% body weight for 1-2 inch fish (2,542-304/lb), 6.2% for 2-3 inch fish (304-88.3/lb). Judgment required for fish at range boundaries.

**Haskell's Equation** (1959): Calculates feeding levels from conversion rates and daily length increase:

*Percent to feed = 3 × Conversion × (ΔL/L) × 100*

Where L = length (inches), ΔL = daily increase in length, Conversion = food-to-flesh ratio.

Application example (June, 308→158 fish/lb, 2.0→2.499 inches, pellet diet, 1.8 conversion):
- June 1: 4.5% body weight
- June 15: 4.0%
- June 30: 3.6%

Method best suited to constant-temperature hatcheries but applicable elsewhere if temperature-related growth changes are recognized. Table 6 permits length estimation from fish/pound data.

**Pellet Feeding Rates**: Feed at 60% of conventional diet amounts (adjusting for calorie content) or follow manufacturer's tables.

### Feeding Frequency
- **Fry starting to feed**: Small amounts hourly throughout 8-hour workday
- **1-1.5 inch fish**: Four times daily
- **2-4 inch pond fish**: Three times daily, later reduced to twice daily
- **Brood stock**: Once daily
- **Seven-day schedule**: Essential for proper husbandry

Experiments showed 24-hour feeding with extended light did not increase growth or improve conversions for fingerlings (Phillips et al., 1958). Haskell (1959) suggested possible benefit for fish >1,000/lb (<1.3 inches) but not larger fish.

### Diet Composition and Ingredients

**Fresh Meats**: Beef liver (condemned for human consumption) provides B vitamins; beef spleen serves as excellent binder with salt. Conservative culturists recommend some fresh meat in all programs, though many hatcheries succeeded with all-dry diets.

**Fresh Fish**: Fed with caution—species including whitefish, bullheads, carp, goldfish, suckers, buckeye shiners, salt-water herring, and creek chubs cause thiamin deficiency and high mortality. Cooking eliminates antithiamin factor but increases costs. Non-problematic fish species often yield high conversion rates despite low ingredient cost.

**Plant Concentrates**: Primarily carbohydrate sources (raw starch poorly digested by trout), serving as bulk and fillers. Common products: cottonseed meal (high protein), wheat middlings, red-dog flour, distiller's solubles, BY-100 (butyl alcohol byproduct). Soybean meal showed variable results in moist diets (possible taste factor); value in pellets undetermined.

**Animal Concentrates**: Fish meals best protein sources (vacuum-dried whitefish meal superior but difficult to obtain; menhaden, sardine, poultry-feed types acceptable substitutes). Dried skim milk excellent but expensive. Meat meals, meat-scrap meals, meat scrap and bone meals poor substitutes (meat-scrap meal preferred if used). Vacuum drying with low heat superior to air-over-flame methods.

**Dietary Supplements**: Only three justified additional expense in moist diets: salt (2% level, poultry dietary salt with trace elements), dried brewer's yeast, and poultry feeding oils. Antibiotics failed to promote growth or reduce conversions in trout (unlike higher animals). Highly fortified vitamin packages added to most commercial and open-formula pellets.

**Sample Formulations** (Table 9):
- Cortland #6: 12% each dried skim milk, whitefish meal, cottonseed meal, wheat flour middlings, distiller's solubles, menhaden fish meal; 3% poultry feeding oil; 30% pork spleen; 20% beef liver; 2% salt
- Pellet mixtures vary: 7-40% fish meal, 8-25% cottonseed meal, 15-24% wheat products, with vitamin A/D powder and stabilized fat

### Production Efficiency Metrics

**Conversion Rates**: Measured as pounds of food per pound of fish produced. Table 10 comparison (47°F, 20 weeks, brook trout):
- All-meat (pork spleen/beef liver/salt): 2.9 conversion, 1,204 cal/lb fish, 238.6 g protein/lb fish
- 50% pork spleen/50% dry meal: 2.9 conversion, 2,095 cal/lb fish, 359.6 g protein/lb fish
- 30% pork spleen/20% beef liver/50% dry meal: 2.7 conversion, 2,088 cal/lb fish, 334.8 g protein/lb fish
- Pellets (5 days) + meat (2 days): 2.1 conversion, 1,954 cal/lb fish
- Pellets (6 days) + meat (1 day): 2.0 conversion, 2,188 cal/lb fish

**Key Principle**: True diet efficiency comparison requires relating conversions to calorie content, not just pounds of food. Pellets require fewer pounds but similar calories per pound of fish produced.

**Production Cost**: Conversion × cost per pound of food = cost to produce one pound of fish. This metric determines economic value of ingredients.

### Quality Control and Storage

**Meat Quality**: Preserve by freezing until ground. Grind sufficient for maximum 2-day period; keep chilled until use. Trim excess fat before grinding (discard, do not feed to larger fish). Avoid rancid or spoiled products.

**Dry Meals**: Finely ground, best available grade. Store cool and dry. Purchase maximum 6-month supply due to limited hatchery storage facilities.

**Pellets**: Store maximum 3 months, preferably in cold storage. Stack on racks to prevent crushing. Use highest quality ingredients in manufacture.

**Mixed Diets**: Refrigerate (40°F) between feedings. Prepare maximum 2-day supply due to ingredient interaction and deterioration.

### Experimental Protocols

**Design Requirements**:
- Same-age fish from same egg lot
- Uniform stocking density (fish/ft³)
- Similar water flow through units
- Accurate food weighing, dedicated containers per unit
- Same percentage body weight feeding (adjust for calorie content when comparing diet types)
- Minimum duplicate, preferably triplicate replication
- Concurrent control (standard) diet under identical conditions
- Total group weighing bi-weekly (sample counts insufficient)
- Priority over other hatchery operations

**Duration**: 16-20 weeks for fingerlings in warm water (>50°F); 6+ months in cold water; full growing season for major diet changes; 2-3 years to detect pathological changes.

**Optimal Sensitivity**: Conduct during fastest growth (fingerling fish, warm water) when rapid metabolism reveals nutritional deficiencies. Results from large fish in cold winter water may not apply to fingerlings in warm summer conditions.

**Evaluation Criteria**: Growth, conversion, mortality, production cost, and (when necessary) body chemistry analysis requiring specialized personnel.

### Record-Keeping and Management

**New York State Feeding Chart** (since 1934): Daily records of weight, growth, mortality, disease treatment, fish density (weight/volume), water flow, food conversion. Multi-year accumulation enables prediction of disease timing, prevention of overcrowding, food requirement estimation, and production cost determination.

**Additional Efficiency Methods**: Tunison (1957) discussed supplementary evaluation and interpretation approaches beyond basic production charts.

**Correction Note**: April 1964 correction clarified that fish transferred from **high-calcium to low-calcium waters** (not vice versa as originally printed) increase metabolic rate to oppose mineral losses, potentially affecting growth or causing death.
