## Executive Summary

This document is a 1991 administrative letter from Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, Center for Environmental Studies) to David L. Wegner of the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, concerning logistical and methodological coordination for the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) cooperative agreement. Rather than presenting primary data, the letter details operational requirements for an upcoming field research program targeting native fishes—principally the [[Humpback Chub]] ([[Gila cypha]])—in the [[Little Colorado River]] and [[Colorado River]] (Grand Canyon reach). Key logistical concerns include PIT tag supply/inventory management, protocols restricting PIT tagging to humpback chub pending further review, establishment of field base camps, equipment storage, and radio-communications infrastructure to support summer 1991 field operations.

## Background & Description

No direct morphological data (e.g., length, weight, meristics) are reported in this correspondence. The letter does, however, reference a key tagging methodology relevant to morphological/individual-tracking studies:

- **PIT tags (Passive Integrated Transponder tags):** Used for individual identification and long-term tracking of fish. The letter specifies an anticipated operational demand of up to **1,000 PIT tags per month** for July and August 1991, with a request for an initial distribution of **at least 2,000 tags** to avoid supply shortfalls during field season.
- **Tagging protocol restriction:** PIT tagging is to be applied to the [[Humpback Chub]] only in the initial phase; deployment in "other native fishes" is deliberately withheld until (1) the desirability of tagging non-target native species is formally established, and (2) adequate tag inventory is confirmed. This implies a precautionary, resource-conscious approach to mark-recapture methodology across multiple native fish taxa in the system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary study system:** The [[Little Colorado River]], a major tributary of the [[Colorado River]] within the Grand Canyon corridor, serves as core habitat for the target fish population(s), particularly the endangered [[Humpback Chub]].
- **Field base camps:** Two research base camps are being established to support fish sampling and monitoring:
  - Mouth of Salt Trail Canyon (River Kilometer/Mile marker RK 10.75)
  - Upstream from Powell Canyon (RK 3.0)
  - Both sites required clearance from Native American tribal interests prior to occupation, indicating the ecological and cultural significance of this reach of river within tribal lands.
- **Seasonal timing:** Field operations were scheduled to commence in early July 1991, aligning fish sampling efforts with summer hydrological and thermal conditions in the Little Colorado River/Colorado River system.
- **Supporting infrastructure:** Establishment of radio communication (initially emergency-only, later routine inter-camp communication) and secure equipment storage (~290 sq ft) in Flagstaff, AZ reflects the remote, logistically challenging nature of the Grand Canyon riverine habitat where this research was conducted.

## Key Findings

- The research program was designed around intensive **PIT-tag-based mark-recapture monitoring** of the [[Humpback Chub]] population in the Little Colorado River/Colorado River (Grand Canyon) system, beginning in **July 1991**.
- Anticipated tagging effort (up to 1,000 tags/month per species-focus period) suggests a **large-scale, high-intensity sampling effort**, indicative of significant population assessment work under the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program tied to Glen Canyon Dam operations and their downstream ecological effects.
- A **precautionary tagging protocol** was adopted: expansion of PIT tagging beyond humpback chub to other native fish species was contingent on establishing formal justification and ensuring sufficient tag supply—highlighting resource-limited constraints on multi-species native fish monitoring in this system.
- Successful field logistics (base camps, storage, communications) were treated as prerequisites for research success, reflecting the operational complexity of studying fish populations in the remote, culturally-sensitive Grand Canyon/Little Colorado River corridor.
- The letter underscores institutional coordination challenges between academic researchers ([[Arizona State University]] Center for Environmental Studies) and the federal agency ([[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]]) managing the cooperative agreement, with direct implications for timely execution of the [[Humpback Chub]] research program tied to Glen Canyon Dam environmental studies.
