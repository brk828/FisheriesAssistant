# Biochemical Genetics of Endangered Colorado Pikeminnow from the Green, Yampa, Colorado and San Juan Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final draft report, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin, tests whether [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] are genetically homogeneous (panmictic) or whether differentiated populations exist. The species is an endangered large river cyprinid, listed in 1967, whose decline is linked to habitat fragmentation, blocked passage, water diversions, loss of spawning and nursery habitat, contaminants, and non-native fish introductions.

The study used [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] at 89 protein-coding loci and a [[Polymerase Chain Reaction]]-based [[Nuclear DNA Analysis]] of anonymous single-copy nuclear loci. Samples came from wild adults, juveniles and young-of-the-year (YOY) fish in the Green, Yampa, upper Colorado and San Juan rivers, plus hatchery broodfish. The authors conclude that the null hypothesis of panmixia is supported. Genetic differentiation among sampled areas was minimal, and the authors recommend conservative management.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a large river cyprinid indigenous to the Colorado River basin. Historical information comes from earlier surveys (Holden and Wick 1982; Minckley and Deacon 1991; U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1987, 1990).
- **Problem:** Population identification and status determination were difficult because the genetic structure and demographic characteristics of the species, especially fish-to-fish reproductive success, were poorly known.
- **Prior work:** Ammerman and Morizot (1989) sampled YOY fish and captive populations. Their data indicated that hatchery fish were derived from two wild populations and that the four wild populations showed genetic identity values typical of other cyprinids.
- **Stocking history:** Fish were stocked into the Colorado River mainstem (Grand Valley, early 1980s), the White River (Kenney Reservoir), and the San Juan River. Stocked fish were progeny of mixed Colorado and Green River broodfish.
- **Objectives:** Genetic identification; genetic profiles for management units; genetic status of units; identification of genetic markers; genetic data for breeding strategies and broodstock; and a management document. The study also aimed to evaluate nonlethal tissue sampling.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** Samples came from the upper mainstem Colorado River, Yampa River, Green River (including Desolation Canyon and Cataract Canyon), and San Juan River. Sampling sites were chosen by Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program biologists as places where adults, juveniles and YOY could potentially be collected in association with spawning.
- **Hatchery context:** Broodfish at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] included a Yampa River-derived population (Yampa DX-F1(74)), a Colorado/Green River population (DX-F1(81)), and a later addition of wild fish from the Colorado River.
- **Tissue handling:** Adults and juveniles were captured by trammel nets and electrofishing (1990–1993). YOY were collected in fall monitoring (1990–1991) by electroshock and seines. Skeletal muscle, fin, blood and necropsy tissues were preserved on dry ice, at -80 °C, or in 95% ethanol for DNA work.
- **Sampling constraints:** Many YOY samples were incomplete because heads were removed for otolith collection, which also removed viscera. As a result, liver-specific and brain/eye-specific loci could not be fully examined.

## Key Findings

### Methods and genetic markers
- **Allozyme loci:** 89 protein-coding loci were resolved. The authors note the locus count is somewhat uncertain because several loci have not been well studied in cyprinids. The analyses used BIOSYS-1 (Swofford and Selander 1981) on polymorphic and some monomorphic loci.
- **Nuclear DNA:** PCR-based RFLP analysis used primers for two single-copy nuclear genes, gastrin and thyroxine, derived from a bovine library. Restriction digests with 10 enzymes were applied to gastrin-like and thyroxin-like products.
- **Nuclear DNA result:** No polymorphisms were detected in individuals from the four populations. The authors interpret the sequences as highly conserved, so the nuclear method could not distinguish populations.

### Sample sizes
- Total: 745 individuals, consisting of 159 wild adults, 39 juveniles, 478 YOY (including 52 YOY reported by Ammerman and Morizot 1989) and 59 hatchery-reared fish. (Component counts sum to 735; the report gives 745 as the total.)
- Of the loci, 10 were polymorphic in at least one tissue or sample set. Three loci (GPI-2, PEPB, PGAM-2) were polymorphic in the present study but not in Ammerman and Morizot (1989). New alleles were detected at GPI-2 and TPI-2.

### Wild adults
- Allele frequencies were estimated for putatively spawning adults from the Colorado, San Juan, Yampa and Green rivers (Table 3).
- Only five of nine polymorphic loci were polymorphic in spawning adults, with the proportion of alleles ranging from 11 of 22 in the San Juan River to a higher count in Colorado River samples.
- No significant allele frequency differences were found among the four rivers.
- Significant deviations from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium occurred at PEPB and PGAM-1 in Colorado River samples, and at PGAM-2 in other samples. The authors suggest these may indicate past differentiation.
- Overall, the authors judge genetic differentiation among wild adults to be minimal.

### Juveniles
- Seven of nine loci were polymorphic; 17 of 22 identified alleles were found in juveniles.
- PGAM-2 deviated significantly from Hardy-Weinberg expectations in Cataract Canyon juveniles (N = 7).
- The mean Fst of 0.049 among juvenile samples indicates low differentiation, with sampling error a more likely explanation for variation.

### Young-of-the-year
- Allele frequencies were presented for YOY samples from the Green and Colorado rivers; no YOY were collected from the San Juan River in this study.
- Many rare alleles occurred in YOY, including most of the alleles restricted to single river systems (GPI-2*c in the Colorado system; TPI-2*b in the Green River).
- The TPI-1*A allele, found only in Green River YOY by Ammerman and Morizot (1989), was not observed in this study.
- Hardy-Weinberg deviations occurred at GPI-2 and PEPB in lower Colorado samples and at PGAM-1 and PGAM-2 in Green River samples. The authors consider this consistent with admixture of differentiated populations.
- The mean Fst of 0.046 among YOY samples is consistent with minimal genetic differentiation.

### Hatchery populations
- Five captive populations were assessed. Seven of nine informative loci were polymorphic in at least one hatchery population.
- No locus deviated significantly from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. Allele frequencies of several loci differed strongly among populations.
- The Dexter 1974 Yampa broodfish population (derived from a few adults) had GPI-2*a frequency about three times higher than in wild populations, likely due to genetic drift and over-representation of an uncommon allele.
- Hatchery samples clustered with wild adult populations on a cluster analysis (Figure 2), with the Dexter Colorado/Green DX-F1(81) and Yampa DX-F1(74) populations joining different wild clusters.
- The total of 15 polymorphic loci in hatchery broodfish provides a basis for selecting founders.

### Conclusions and management implications
- **Hypothesis test:** The null hypothesis (H0: Colorado pikeminnow are genetically homogeneous or panmictic) is supported. Ha is not supported at the present level of analysis.
- **Management units:** The authors identified genetic profiles for the Colorado and Green river management units (including the Yampa). They found the units to be genetically similar, with no useful genetic markers, and the San Juan River relatively genetically depauperate.
- **Genetic markers:** No genetic markers were identified that could be used for management purposes.
- **Breeding:** The Colorado/Green DX-F1(81) broodfish population is not representative of either river and should not be used for San Juan River stocking. The authors recommend replacing it with separate Green and Colorado River broodfish populations.
- **San Juan reintroduction:** If reintroduction is indicated, fish should come from Colorado or Green River controlled breeding programs.
- **Caution:** Because Fst values were low and the habitat may have been recently altered, the authors recommend conservative management. Panmixia may reflect recent origin, and alleles may be relicts of previously differentiated populations.

### Recommendations
- Use [[Pedigree Breeding]] strategies and genetic management programs with records of hatchery and management actions.
- Expand sampling of non-lethal tissues, including river biopsies, to include rare alleles (GPI-2*b, TPI-1*a, TPI-2*b, PGAM-2*c, and others) in broodstock.
- Combine [[Arbitrarily Primed PCR]] or [[Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA]] with isozyme mapping for higher-resolution markers.
- Maintain separate broodstock populations so a single population failure does not eliminate a unit.

### Cover correspondence (September 1998)
- A federal cover memo accompanying the revised draft notes that the authors revised the analyses with the BIOSYS-1 package and related statistics.
- Reviewers noted that the revised draft changed some sample groupings, which accounts for differences in gene frequencies between drafts.
- The memo states that the authors adopted the Nomenclature Committee of Fishes recommendation to change the common name from squawfish to pikeminnow in the text.
