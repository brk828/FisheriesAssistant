# Culture of Channel Catfish in Cages Suspended in Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictalurus-punctatus

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1969 proceedings paper by H. R. Schmittou, Auburn University Agricultural Experiment Station, reports three experiments (1967–1969) on raising [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) from fingerlings to marketable size in cages suspended in earthen ponds. The work tested stocking density, cage position, and mesh size, and it recorded observations on feeding behavior, cage materials, covers, parasites, and disease. The author concludes that the rate of water exchange through cages is the major limiting factor in production, and that bacterial disease (columnaris) may also limit culture.

## Background & Description

**Cage culture** is defined in the paper as raising fish from fingerlings to harvestable size in containers enclosed on all sides and the bottom by slats, [[Hardware Cloth]], net, or similar materials that allow free water circulation. In the study, cages were suspended with the top about 2–6 in. above the water surface and the bottom at least 1 ft. above the pond bottom.

Cage fish culture has long been used in parts of Asia (Thailand, Cambodia, Indonesia), where it was limited to riverine and sewage-stream areas because adequate feeds were lacking. The author argues that commercially formulated floating feeds now make intensive culture of channel catfish in cages feasible. Claimed advantages include use in many water types, combined culture with [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] in open water, easier observation of feeding and health, simpler disease treatment, easy harvest by lifting cages, and flexible harvest timing.

**Experimental setup:**
- Earthen ponds on Piedmont clay soils about 7 mi. north of Auburn, Alabama.
- 92 cages of 36 x 47.5 x 36 in. (1.0 m³) and 6 cages of 86 x 114 x 39.4 in. (2.89 m³).
- Cages built from 2 x 2 in. pine or spruce frames with 0.25 or 0.50 in. galvanized [[Hardware Cloth]], untreated with preservative, and floated with styrofoam or metal canisters.
- Fish sourced from the Auburn Station, the National Fish Hatchery in Marion, Alabama, and a private hatchery in Lonoke, Arkansas. Mean stocked sizes ranged from 0.028 to 0.48 lb.
- Feed: floating [[Trout Feed]] in 1.5, 4.0, or 6.0 mm sizes, fed according to a generalized feeding program and held in place by "feeding rings."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water exchange.** Water circulation through the cages emerged as the central variable. Cages with more surface exposed to wind-driven currents performed better. The author reasons that fish in interior cages, surrounded by three to five layers of hardware cloth and about 5,000 other fish, received little dilution from normal currents. Water exchange was therefore likely driven mainly by the fish themselves when wind was absent.

**Dissolved gases.** Fish kills occurred during periods of low oxygen and high free carbon dioxide. During one kill, surface oxygen was 2.0 ppm and free CO₂ was 10.0 ppm. At 3 ft., oxygen was 0.5 ppm and free CO₂ was 12.5 ppm. Fish in open water showed no distress under the same conditions. The author estimates that each open-water catfish had about 485 times the surface area of each caged fish, which likely protects open-water fish during gas stress.

**Disease and parasites.** Caged fish avoid contact with pathogen-rich bottom muds and do not reconsume feces. Parasite epizootics were not observed through 1968. The only notable parasite was *Henneguya* (Myxosporidia), found encysted in gills in one experiment. Columnaris disease, caused by *Chondrococcus columnaris*, appeared in 53 of 98 cages in 1967–1968.

**Feeding behavior as a health index.** Fish normally consumed a feed ration within about 2 minutes. Poor feeding was associated with disease, oxygen–carbon dioxide stress, recently silted water, or falling temperature.

## Key Findings

### Experiment I: Stocking Density
- Densities tested ranged up to 500 fish per m³ of cage (175–425 per m³ in earlier 1967–1968 tests).
- Fingerlings averaging 0.43 lb. were stocked at 300, 400, and 500 per cage, with two replications each (2,400 fish total), in pond S-16 from September 7 to October 17, 1968 (40 feeding days).
- Fish grew from 0.43 to 0.83 lb. in 40 days at 500 per m³. The mean gain was 0.01 lb. per fish per day, and the cage gained 4.94 lb. per day.
- Net gain per cage was 126.5 lb. (300 fish) to 197.6 lb. (500 fish). Feed conversion ranged from 1.26 to 1.34, with survival of 99.2–99.6%.
- Standing crops in the 500-fish cages were 415.2 and 420.9 lb. (highest standing crop: 421 lb./m³). Growth did not decline, so carrying capacity was apparently not reached.
- The author concludes that more than 500 fish per m³ could probably be stocked while still producing fish of 0.8 lb. or larger.
- Densities of 500 per m³ were judged within the optimum range for growing fingerlings to 0.8 lb.

### Experiment II: Cage Position
- **Test 1 (pond S-16):** Twelve 1 m³ cages formed three four-cage batteries placed in least-exposed (A), moderately exposed (B), and most-exposed (C) locations. Fish were stocked at 350 per cage (0.03 lb. each) on May 9 and harvested September 6, 1968 (120 days).
- Fish in battery A were in distress on the surface and died. Dead fish numbered 178, 325, 88, and 0 in cages 1–4 of battery A. Fish in battery C were not in distress.
- Hardware-cloth detachment allowed fish to escape from 6 of 12 cages, so no statistical analysis was possible. Performance trended from A (worst) to C (best), with B intermediate.
- **Test 2 (pond S-3):** Sixteen 1 m³ cages with 0.25 in. mesh were arranged in a four-by-four block. Positions I (corners) through IV (interior) were tested with four replications each.
- Channel catfish (250 per cage, stocked June 16–17) and white catfish (100 per cage, infected with columnaris) were held for 115 days until harvest on October 5, 1968.
- Gain ranged from 121.7 lb. (position IV) to 148.2 lb. (position I). Feed conversion ranged from 1.61 (position IV) to 1.82 (position I) in the text. Survival ranged from 69.9% to 72.3%.
- Differences in gain and survival were not significant (P = .05). Feed conversion differed significantly by Duncan's test (P = .05).
- Feed conversion was the most affected parameter. Feed was equal across positions, so less-favored cages effectively received higher feeding rates relative to growth.

### Experiment III: Mesh Size
- Eight 1 m³ cages were stocked April 10, 1969, with 500 fingerlings each (4,000 fish; mean 0.036 lb.). Four cages had 0.25 in. mesh and four had 0.50 in. mesh. Harvest was July 15, after 93 feeding days.
- Results (0.25 in. vs 0.50 in. mesh):

| Parameter | 0.25 in. mesh | 0.50 in. mesh | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net gain per cage | 49.0 lb. | 91.8 lb. | P = .05 |
| Mean fish weight | 0.145 lb. | 0.232 lb. | P = .05 |
| Survival | 89% | 94% | P = .10 |
| Feed conversion | 2.91 | 1.55 | Not tested |

- No algae grew on either mesh, so the author attributes the difference to water circulation through the mesh. The high conversion in both treatments is attributed to overfeeding, caused by overestimating standing crops.

### Production and Efficiency
- Across all 1967–1968 experiments, 10,121 lb. of fish were produced from feed totaling 12,713 lb. (abstract) or 12,718 lb. (results text), a feed conversion of 1.25. The two feed figures differ in the source.
- The lowest conversion was 1.03 (range 0.97–1.09) at 300 fish per m³ in 2.89 m³ cages. Those fish were fed 151.1 lb. over 151 days and grew from 0.027 to 0.57 lb.
- In pond S-4, cages produced about 2,000 lb. of channel catfish per acre per year in both 1967 and 1968. Open water added 200–400 lb. of other fish, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Tilapia]], [[White Catfish]], and [[Striped Bass]].
- The study cites a [[Carrying Capacity]] of about 2,400 lb. per acre for channel catfish in supplementally fed ponds (Prather and Swingle, 1960).

### Construction Materials and Costs
- Cages cost about $18 each ($12–$14 in materials).
- Untreated cages lasted two years without repair, and most were usable for a third year with minor repairs.
- Wood became waterlogged and heavy. Hardware cloth caused mouth cuts and abrasions, possibly leading to infection, and rusted at staples.

### Feeding Rings and Covers
- Early aluminum-sheet feeding rings let feed wash out as fish grew. Replacement with 12 x 20 x 16 in. topless, bottomless boxes, with about 12 in. below the water line, stopped feed loss.
- Open-topped cages produced hyperexcitable fish that injured themselves against the sides. Aluminum-sheet covers (open only over the feeding ring) ended the problem within 10 days and became standard.

### Summary Conclusions
- Water replacement rate and quality are a major limiting factor in caged channel catfish culture.
- Bacterial disease, especially columnaris, may be another major limiting factor until better treatments exist.
- Caged fish were killed by low-oxygen/high-CO₂ conditions while open-water fish were unaffected.
- The author recommends further testing of cage materials focused on cost, durability, longevity, and weight.
