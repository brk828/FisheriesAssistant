# Notes on Culturing the Largemouth Bass (Huro or Lobina Negra)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Spanish-language aquaculture guide, compiled by three biologists (María Luisa Sevilla H., Sergio Antonio Guzmán del Prado and Aurora Díaz Carrillo), describes how to culture the [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*, called "huro" or "lobina negra" in Mexico). It covers the species' natural range, feeding and reproductive biology, nest construction and spawning, larval development, and two propagation systems: an extensive pond method and an intensive hatchery method. It also addresses forage species, cannibalism control, fertilization and Mexican introductions and management measures.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *Micropterus salmoides* Lacépède, family [[Centrarchidae]].
- **Native range:** From the Great Lakes region and the Mississippi Valley to Florida, North Carolina and northwestern Mexico. The summary extends the native range from eastern Canada to the Río Bravo basin in northeastern Mexico.
- **Habitat:** Typically warm temperate lakes and slow streams with soft bottoms and dense vegetation. The species tolerates a wide range of conditions, which the authors cite as a reason it suits fish farming.
- **Diet:** Carnivorous, though it occasionally eats plants. Fry feed on [[Plankton]] (cladocerans, copepods), then on insects, larvae and molluscs. Adults are mainly piscivorous and also eat frogs, snails and crayfish.
- **Mexican context:** Numerous stockings have been made, often together with [[Bluegill]] (*mojarra de agallas azules*). Several native species serve as prey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reproduction and spawning**
- Spawning occurs mainly in spring and summer, from mid-April to July, with a pronounced peak in May.
- Egg-laying is temperature-driven: the first spawn occurs 2 to 5 days after the daily mean water temperature reaches and stays above 15.5 °C. A temporary drop in temperature triggers an additional spawning event.
- Up to three separate spawning periods may occur per season. Langlois's work on *M. dolomieu* found three egg size classes, and he concluded that a female can spawn up to 3 times a year, though some populations spawn only once.
- Males prepare nests on gravel or rocky bottoms, sometimes on coarse sand or silt, usually near logs, stumps or rocks. Preferred depths range from 25 cm to 8 m, with 1 to 2 m mentioned as a common depth range. The nest is a shallow, roughly circular depression about 60 cm in diameter, with large stones (about 10 cm) at the centre.
- Eggs are adhesive and stick to nest material. The female leaves after spawning, and the male guards the nest. One male may attract two or more females. Clutch sizes vary from 2,000 to 10,000 eggs per female, with a figure of up to 17,000 per half kilogram of fish also cited.

**Incubation and larval development**
- Egg diameter is about 2.5 mm. Incubation times reported by Webster: 2 to 3 days at 23.9 °C, about 3 to 4 days at 21.1 °C, and 9 to 10 days at 12.8 °C. The text also gives 14 days at 18 to 24 °C and general ranges of 7 days to 3 weeks.
- Fry remain in the nest while absorbing the yolk sac. They then form small schools for 3 to 4 days, and the male guards them. At 1.5 to 2.0 cm they move toward shallow shores, where they can be collected with traps or beach seines.

**Environmental requirements**
- Water temperature influences spawning, development and activity. Juveniles tolerate a wide range of temperatures, but at very high temperatures they become delicate and more susceptible to external and internal parasites.
- Mexican waters where the species does well have temperatures of 15 to 25 °C.
- Cyclops and similar natural foods reproduce best at 20 to 21 °C and do not survive above 35 °C.

**Pond management**
- Extensive ponds can be as small as 1,000 m², and the difference from intensive systems lies in stocking density rather than size.
- Spawning ponds of 2,000 to 8,000 m² are recommended. Lydell suggested 150 breeders per 4,000 m², while Davies and Wiebe suggested no more than 110 (75 females and 35 males) per 4,000 m², and noted that 15 breeders in 4,000 m² gave excellent results.
- Growth ponds are fertilized one to one and a half months before stocking. Fertilizers based on nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium promote plankton blooms, giving the water a greenish-brown colour.
- Aquatic weeds should be controlled because they harbour mosquito larvae and shelter forage fish, which can cause overpopulation and stunted bass.
- Enemies include aquatic insects and larvae, which may serve as prey, and the parasitic worm *Proteocephalus ambloplites*, which can sterilize females when abundant.

**Introductions and management in Mexico**
- The first recorded introduction was near Lake Chapala early in the twentieth century, but it apparently did not establish.
- Competition from native carnivorous fish limits populations in some waters, such as Pátzcuaro, where the species declined after an initial boom. It thrives in waters that contain only forage fish, as in Tacámbaro, Valle de Bravo and several other reservoirs.
- Introductions have also been made in Chiapas, Veracruz, Chihuahua, Michoacán, Querétaro, Baja California Sur and numerous artificial reservoirs.
- Regulations include a closed season from mid-April to mid-June, a minimum size of 20 cm, and a minimum mesh of 5 cm for commercial gillnets in inland waters.

## Key Findings

1. **Adaptability and diet:** The species tolerates diverse conditions and feeds mainly on fish as an adult. Up to about 80% of stomach content in adults was fish, with [[Mojarra]] the preferred prey when available. Insects and molluscs followed in importance.
2. **Spawning control:** Temperature thresholds (above 15.5 °C) and temperature drops govern spawning. Multiple spawns per season are possible.
3. **Nest guarding:** The male builds, cleans and guards the nest, and the species does not spawn well in tanks without suitable gravel or nest substrate.
4. **Propagation:** No artificial fertilization or incubation has been achieved. Fry are produced in ponds using natural food. The authors state that the species does not accept prepared feed readily, though prepared feed with high animal protein is accepted by juveniles of some sizes.
5. **Cannibalism:** Once bass reach about 6 to 7.5 cm they become piscivorous, so grading by size and keeping stocking densities low are key measures.
6. **Forage species:** Forage fish are essential. Native candidates include species from the Goodeidae, Poeciliidae, Atherinidae, Gobiidae, Cyprinidae, Characidae and Cyprinodontidae families. Species that disturb the bottom or compete strongly for food are unsuitable.
7. **Production and outlook:** Current stock comes from established natural populations. The authors call for a warm-water hatchery for mass production and for more ponds on agricultural production units stocked with well-chosen fish combinations.
