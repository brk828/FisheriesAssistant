# Endangered Fishes of the United States and Their Relation to Aquaculture

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This proceedings paper by [[James E Johnson]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and University of Arkansas) reviews how aquaculture affects endangered North American fishes and how hatcheries can help recover them. The author argues that the most serious threat is the accidental or deliberate introduction of non-native fishes, often spread through the bait and aquaculture trades. He presents case histories from the Southwest, describes the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] as a major refuge, and lists the practical problems of rearing rare fishes in captivity. The paper concludes that captive propagation buys time for recovery but does not replace protection of native habitat.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with a general argument about human impacts on the environment. It cites both deliberate changes (deforestation, dams, plowing of prairies) and indirect ones (pollution, acid precipitation, climate change). Drawing on [[Ehrlich and Ehrlich]], the author frames species loss through a "missing rivets" analogy: each lost species weakens the ecosystem, and the number of losses an ecosystem can absorb is unknown.

Extinction has shifted from direct overharvest, as with the passenger pigeon, to habitat destruction and "accidental extinctions." Congress passed the Endangered Species Preservation Act in 1966, then amended it in 1969 and 1973 to create the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The paper notes that more than 610 U.S. species (250 plants and 360 animals) were listed under the Act in 1991, including 86 fishes (USFWS 1991).

North America has roughly 750 to 950 native fish species (Johnson 1987), with archaic groups such as lampreys, sturgeons, gars and bowfin alongside modern minnows, darters, suckers and sunfishes. The [[American Fisheries Society]] Endangered Species Committee listed 250 taxa in 1979. The 1989 update (Williams et al.) reported 139 taxa added and 26 removed, for a total of 364 taxa needing protection. Of the 26 removed, 10 had gone extinct and 16 were delisted because better information became available. None were removed because they recovered. Habitat alteration was cited for 93 percent of listed taxa, and non-native competition or predation for 40 percent (taxa could list several causes). Johnson (1987) counted 517 native U.S. and Canadian fish taxa with some legal protection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Non-native fish introductions.** The paper identifies these as the main aquaculture-linked threat. Case histories include:

- **[[Quitobaquito Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*): an endemic subspecies in [[Quitobaquito Spring]], Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona. In 1969, illegally transported [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), likely bait-trade fish, were stocked into the spring and began to threaten the pupfish. Pupfish were held in a cattle tank while the spring was treated with [[Rotenone]], then returned after detoxification.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*C. m. macularius*): common near the [[Salton Sea]] in the 1960s. [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*), introduced through the aquarium trade in the mid-1960s, pushed pupfish into irrigation drains. In 1978 [[Redbreast Tilapia]] (*Tilapia zillii*) entered the drains from aquaculture ponds. By 1981 no pupfish could be found in the Salton Sea or the drains. The subspecies went from millions of individuals to extirpation in the U.S. in less than 20 years.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*): once Arizona's most common fish. [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), stocked by public health agencies for mosquito control, replaced topminnows in Arivaca Creek in less than two years (Miller 1961) and in some ponds in a single season (Minckley 1973). Mosquitofish evolved with many predators and have predator-avoidance behavior, while topminnows did not.
- **[[Red Shiner]]** (*Cyprinella lutrensis*): a hardy bait fish spread by "bait bucket carry." It threatens the tribe Plagopterini, including [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Rhinichthys cobitis*) and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). Woundfin also carry the [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), which is now found in nearly every U.S. state.
- **Golden shiner and rudd:** rudd (*Scardinius erythrophthalmus*) from baitfish farms have hybridized with golden shiners in several states, including Arkansas. The author judges that rudd genes will alter the golden shiner gene pool but are unlikely to endanger the species (Tave et al., submitted).

**Predatory non-natives.** [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) of the Colorado River drainage have been stocked in large numbers. Over 12 million young razorback suckers and several hundred thousand squawfish were released into the Salt and Verde rivers in Arizona from 1981 to 1990, and none are known to have survived to spawn in the wild (Tyus 1991). Channel catfish and flathead catfish, along with non-native [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), are abundant in these rivers. One 300-foot (91 m) trammel net caught 85 pounds (38 kg) of catfish (n = 8) in one night.

**Salmonids.** [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*) and [[Apache Trout]] (*O. apache*) are endemic to high desert streams of Arizona and New Mexico, above 7,500 feet (2,300 m). [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) have been stocked in some of their streams, but the major threat has been [[Rainbow Trout]] (*O. mykiss*), which hybridize with and swamp the native trout. Stocking has now officially stopped in most native trout streams, but illegal transplanting by anglers continues.

**Indirect aquaculture impacts.** Capping springs to supply hatcheries eliminates habitat for springs-dependent fishes and invertebrates. This matters especially for endemic snails of the family Hydrobiidae, which are common in isolated southwestern springs. Hatchery effluents and the spread of diseases and parasites are also concerns.

## Key Findings

- The Endangered Species Act protected 86 fish species in 1991. The AFS 1989 list included 364 taxa, about half of the roughly 750 native U.S. fish species.
- Habitat alteration (93 percent) and non-native competition or predation (40 percent) were the leading causes of listing.
- Non-native fishes, often moved through the bait and aquaculture trades, were the main aquaculture-linked threat. Cases include the Quitobaquito pupfish, Salton Sea pupfish, Gila topminnow, woundfin, and native trouts.
- Reintroductions of hatchery-raised razorback suckers and Colorado squawfish failed to establish wild spawning populations because predatory non-natives dominated the rivers.
- The Dexter NFH (New Mexico), built in the 1930s for warm-water sportfish, became the most successful refuge. It began rearing endangered fishes in 1974, with 24 taxa reared to date. None have escaped.
- Dexter's three goals are: (1) protect genetic stocks, (2) supply fish for research, and (3) propagate fish for recovery plans. Twenty species were held for gene pool protection (11 remain), nine for research (5 remain), and seven for propagation (all seven programs continue).
- Special problems of rearing rare fish include wild-origin parasites and disease, unsuitable culture techniques for river fishes, genetic bottlenecks (the author recommends more than 500 founders), the high individual value of each fish, water reuse that allows mixing and hybridization, and escape risk. Pecos River, about 1 mile away, supports about 30 native fish species, including two endangered ones.
- A "triage" system from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service sets priorities by degree of endangerment, taxonomic uniqueness and recovery potential. Twelve species were removed from Dexter over 17 years to make room for others, and none were lost from the wild afterward.
- Captive propagation is a means to recovery, not a replacement for protecting native habitat. Large-scale hatchery programs for Pacific salmon have largely failed to benefit declining populations (Hilborn 1992).
- Recommended approaches include escape-proof indoor rearing (used at the University of Arkansas and San Marcos NFH), working with native fishes in the receiving waters, and turning over small ponds to single native species in cooperation with state agencies. Six Arkansas species are listed in Table 1: Ouachita madtom, Caddo madtom, Ozark cavefish, yellowcheek darter, paleback darter and leopard darter.
- The author gives three justifications for saving endangered species: Kellert's (1985) seven social values (naturalistic, ecological, moral, scientific, aesthetic, utilitarian, cultural), an emotional "books of nature" argument, and the legal mandate under the Endangered Species Act.
- Recovery efforts have little precedent, and failures, including occasional extinctions, must be expected. Captive propagation can lower the failure rate and supply data on species needs.
