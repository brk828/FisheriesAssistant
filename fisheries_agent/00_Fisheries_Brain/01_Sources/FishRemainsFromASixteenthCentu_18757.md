# Fish Remains from a Sixteenth Century Site on Drakes Bay, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #triakidae #pleuronectidae #embiotocidae

## Executive Summary

This archaeoichthyological study by W. I. Follett, published in the 1964 Annual Report of the [[Archaeological Survey]] at the University of California, Los Angeles, examines 64 fish remains from site [[Mrn-307]], a shellmound on the east shore of [[Estero de Limantour]] in [[Drakes Bay]], Marin County, California. The site was occupied between about 1450 and 1600 A.D. and lies within the territory of the [[Coast Miwok]]. The assemblage represents six species in three families. All are good eating and can be taken by hook and line or net. The notable absence of [[Bat Stingray]] remains leads the author to suggest the site was occupied only during the winter months, when that species is absent from the estero.

## Background & Description

The remains were collected by [[Clement W. Meighan]] of the University of California, Los Angeles, and are deposited in the [[Museum of Anthropology]], University of California, Berkeley. Site Mrn-307 is about half a mile above the mouth of the estero (38°02'10" N, 122°54'19" W; see U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey Chart 5599). It is one of seven [[Shellmound]]s in the Drakes Bay region known to contain historic materials over 350 years old. The paper is a revision of a 1956 manuscript and is intended to accompany a forthcoming detailed site report by Meighan.

The collection comprises 64 remains: 24 complete, 30 incomplete and 10 fragmentary. Identifications rest on skeletal elements such as vertebrae, centra, [[Lower Pharyngeal]] teeth-bearing bones, [[Cleithrum]]s, [[Pelvic]] fins, and cranial bones. [[Table 1]] lists each specimen by UCMA catalog number, species and element. Two plates illustrate diagnostic elements of the flounder, bay blackperch and rubberlip seaperch.

### Species Represented

- **[[Leopard Shark]]** (*Triakis semifasciata*, Triakidae): 4 centra. The largest came from a shark about 122 cm total length. A modern male from Elkhorn Slough measured 135 cm and weighed 11.4 kg. Females reach 183 cm.
- **[[Southern Starry Flounder]]** (*Platichthys stellatus rugosus*, Pleuronectidae): 2 precaudal vertebrae, the larger from a fish about 63 cm long and perhaps 4 kg, and 1 first interhaemal. The species averages about 41 cm and 1 kg.
- **[[Walleye Surfperch]]** (*Hyperprosopon argenteum*, Embiotocidae): 1 lower pharyngeal from a fish about 27 cm long. The species attains 27.3 cm and is one of the best food fishes of its family.
- **[[Bay Blackperch]]** (*Embiotoca jacksoni*, Embiotocidae): 6 supraoccipitals, 3 hyomandibulars, 1 opercle, 1 urohyal, 7 cleithra, 1 postcleithrum, 1 scapula, 1 pelvic and 1 precaudal vertebra. These come from fish about 25 to 33 cm long. The vertebra's identification is doubtful. The species attains 30.5 cm and 0.6 kg and is a fair food fish.
- **[[Pileperch]]** (*Damalichthys vacca*, Embiotocidae): the largest group, including 10 lower pharyngeals, 5 cleithra, 3 postcleithra, 3 scapulae, 3 pelvics, a caudal vertebra and a rib. These come from fish about 33 to 41 cm long. The species is a common food fish of lesser quality. A San Francisco Bay specimen measured 40 cm and weighed 1 kg.
- **[[Rubberlip Seaperch]]** (*Rhacochilus toxotes*, Embiotocidae): 1 parasphenoid, 3 lower pharyngeals, 1 postcleithrum, 2 pelvics and 1 precaudal vertebra. These come from fish about 30 to 38 cm long. The species is considered the finest food fish of its family. A gravid female from Tomales Bay measured 45.7 cm and weighed 1.6 kg.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author notes that all six species commonly occur in the shallow water of bays and mud-bottomed esteros and probably enter [[Estero de Limantour]] throughout the year. Remains of the flounder, walleye surfperch and pileperch represent individuals near the maximum size known for those species. All other remains represent average-sized individuals.

The [[Bat Stingray]] (*Myliobatis californica*) is edible and was used by Native Californians. Its remains occur at several shellmounds around [[San Francisco Bay]], including Ellis Landing, West Berkeley, Emeryville, Strawberry Point and Belvedere Site 39. Elsewhere in California, remains occur at San Miguel Island, Arroyo Sequit and the McClure Site (Mrn-266) on Tomales Bay. The species enters Estero de Limantour but is absent there in winter. Stingray bones are also absent from two nearby sites: the Estero Site (Mrn-232b), about 200 yards south and 100 yards west of Mrn-307, and the Cauley Site (Mrn-242), less than two miles away. The author takes this as evidence that the Mrn-307 occupants were there only in winter, when stingrays were unavailable.

## Key Findings

1. **Assemblage composition:** 64 fish remains represent six species from three families: Triakidae (1 species), Pleuronectidae (1 species) and Embiotocidae (4 species).
2. **Edibility and capture:** All six species are good eating and are readily caught by hook and line or net. This indicates a reliable nearshore fishery.
3. **Size profile:** Remains of the [[Southern Starry Flounder]], [[Walleye Surfperch]] and [[Pileperch]] represent near-maximum-size individuals. The others are average-sized.
4. **Seasonality inference:** The absence of [[Bat Stingray]] remains, a species that enters the estero but is absent in winter, suggests the site was occupied only during the winter months. The author's wording states this as a suggestion, not a firm conclusion.
5. **Regional comparison:** Stingray remains are documented at multiple San Francisco Bay and other California shellmounds. Two nearby sites (Mrn-232b and Mrn-242) also lack them, which the author regards as supporting evidence for the seasonal interpretation.
6. **Taxonomic caveats:** Several vertebrae and ribs cannot be identified to genus and species with confidence. These are flagged as doubtful in the text.
7. **Documentation:** The specimens are deposited at the [[Museum of Anthropology]], Berkeley. A full archaeological report by Meighan was forthcoming at the time of publication.
