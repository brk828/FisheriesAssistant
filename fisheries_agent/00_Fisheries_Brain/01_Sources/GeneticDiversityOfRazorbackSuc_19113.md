# Genetic Diversity of Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) - Research Proposal and Status Review

#ichthyology #catostomidae #endangered-species #conservation-genetics #Colorado-River-basin

## Executive Summary

This document comprises a 1989 research proposal by [[Thomas E. Dowling]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] to assess genetic diversity of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) populations using restriction endonuclease analysis of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), submitted to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]]. The proposal accompanies draft materials for listing the razorback sucker as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The razorback sucker historically inhabited 3,500 miles of the [[Colorado River Basin]] but now persists in less than 25% of its range with no significant recruitment documented in 25-30 years. Major populations remain in [[Lake Mohave]] (approximately 50,000 adults), upper [[Green River]]/[[Yampa River]] (approximately 1,000 adults), and near [[Grand Junction]] (few hundred fish). All aged individuals exceed 20+ years, with many 30-40+ years old, indicating population senescence.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was described by Abbott in 1860 from the [[Colorado River]]. Adults are readily identifiable by an abrupt sharp-edged dorsal ridge (keel) behind the head and large fleshy subterminal mouth. Adults grow to 3 kg (6 pounds) and 600 mm (24 inches) total length. Juveniles less than 150 mm lack the distinct dorsal keel and are difficult to distinguish from other suckers. The species was once abundant throughout the basin, supporting a commercial fishery in southern Arizona in the early 1900s. Jordan (1891) reported them "very abundant" at [[Green River]], Utah in 1889.

Current distribution is reduced to approximately 750 miles in the upper basin and 200 miles in the lower [[Colorado River]] impounded by [[Hoover Dam]], [[Davis Dam]], and [[Parker Dam]]. The species spawns communally with several males accompanying a single female over gravel bars swept clean of silt. Spawning occurs January-April in the lower basin at 11.5-18°C and May-June in the upper basin at 10.5-18°C. Incubation time varies inversely with temperature: 19.4 days at 10°C, 11.1 days at 15°C, 6.8 days at 20°C. Hatching success is poor below 15°C. Larvae remain in gravel interstices until swim-up (approximately 4 days post-hatch at 20-21°C).

Adult razorbacks migrate considerable distances to spawn. Tyus (1987) recaptured one individual 208 km downstream from its original tagging location. Radio telemetry studies show adults utilize main channel habitats in depths 0.6-3.4 m over sand/silt substrates with velocities 0.1-0.6 m/s, selecting near-shore runs in spring and shallow waters off mid-channel sand bars in summer. Winter studies found fish preferred depths 0.6-1.4 m with velocities 0.03-0.33 m/s in slow runs, slack waters, and eddies. Temperature preference ranges 22-25°C with aversion below 14.7°C and above 27.4°C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River Basin]] has been extensively modified through construction of 15+ dams since 1910, dewatering, cooling, or impounding nearly the entire lower basin. [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] impounded approximately 500 km of former habitat and lowered water temperatures in another 105 km, preventing reproduction. Spring runoff has been reduced 28% in the [[Green River]] and 37% in the [[Colorado River]] during May-June. Mean annual discharge decreased 18% at the Green-Colorado confluence. Daily flow fluctuations at Jensen, Utah increased 400% due to peaking power operations at [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].

Predation by introduced species is identified as a major factor preventing recruitment. Heavy predation on eggs and larvae by [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and [[Redear Sunfish]] (*Lepomis microlophus*) has been documented. Larvae collected in spawning areas rarely exceed 14 mm total length in the wild, disappearing within weeks of hatching. Marsh and Langhorst (1988) demonstrated larvae survived longer and grew larger in the absence of predators. Papoulias (1986) found larvae require zooplankton densities of 10 organisms/liter within 10 days of yolk-sac absorption for survival; death occurs at 20-30 days if food is insufficient. Lake Mohave averaged only 1.5 plankters/liter, and the [[Green River]] had less than 1.0/liter during June.

Thirty-three non-native fish species have become established in the upper basin and 37 in the lower basin. Recent introductions include [[Smallmouth Bass]] in [[Lake Powell]], triploid [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), two tilapia species, [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*), and [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*). Hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) has been reported, though recent electrophoretic analyses show less than 5% incidence of flannelmouth genes in [[Lake Mohave]] populations, considered insignificant.

## Key Findings

**Proposed Research Objectives:**
1. Assess genetic differentiation between extant populations from mainstream [[Colorado River]], [[Green River]], [[Lake Mohave]], and [[Lake Powell]] (N=25 per locality)
2. Evaluate genetic diversity within populations, including possible hybridization influences
3. Determine genetic diversity in hatchery fish from [[Lake Mohave]] after more than a decade of captivity and propagation at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]

**Methodology:** Restriction endonuclease analysis of mtDNA using 5 four-base-pair recognizing enzymes (producing approximately 145 restriction sites). MtDNA to be isolated from sperm/ova by ultracentrifugation, digested with restriction enzymes, end-labeled with ³²P-dNTPs, and visualized by autoradiography following agarose and polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Population differentiation will be tested using Wright's F-statistics (F_ST) and phylogenetic analysis using PAUP. Nucleotide diversity estimates will assess within-population genetic variation.

**Population Status:**
- [[Lake Mohave]]: approximately 50,000 adults (largest remaining population)
- Upper [[Green River]]/[[Yampa River]]: 758-1,138 adults (Lanigan and Tyus 1989)
- [[Colorado River]] near [[Grand Junction]]: few hundred fish
- [[Lake Powell]]: scattered adults only
- Lower basin: essentially extirpated except [[Lake Mohave]]

**Age Structure Crisis:** Otolith examination of 70 wild razorbacks from [[Lake Mohave]] revealed ages 40-44 years, with 89% hatched prior to impoundment. No individuals younger than 20+ years found in [[Lake Mohave]] or [[Green River]] populations. McCarthy and Minckley (1987) demonstrated rapid growth of 43 mm/year (males) and 55 mm/year (females) through year 5, dropping to only a few mm/year thereafter. Tyus (1987a) reported growth of only 2.2 mm/year for 29 adults recaptured 1-8 years after tagging.

**Reproductive Failure:** Despite observed spawning in multiple locations and collection of viable fertilized eggs and larvae, no larvae larger than 14 mm have been collected in the wild (except 20 mm in an isolated, predator-free backwater that disappeared upon predator reinvasion). No evidence of natural recruitment to adult populations in 25-30 years. Hatchery propagation commenced in 1981 using [[Lake Mohave]] stock. Reintroductions started 1981-1982 in lower basin with no documented success to date. Approximately 2,000 young-of-year released into [[Green River]] from [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]].

**Habitat Alterations:** Water temperatures below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (384 km reach) and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (105 km reach) rarely exceed 15°C in summer, preventing reproduction and growth. The [[Central Utah Project]] will divert 165,000 acre-feet from [[Green River]] to [[Bonneville Basin]]; [[Two Forks Project]] will divert 45,000 acre-feet from [[Colorado River]] to East Slope. Over 75 federal consultations conducted since 1980 on projects involving water depletions in upper basin.

**Listing Recommendation:** The [[Colorado River Endangered Fishes Recovery Team]] recommended endangered listing in 1987. Draft proposed rule prepared for listing as endangered (not threatened) due to: (1) greatly reduced range (less than 25% historic), (2) apparent inability to reproduce successfully in wild, (3) extensive partitioning of range by dams, (4) extensive habitat alteration through impoundment, (5) population composed of senescent individuals. Critical habitat designation deemed "not prudent" as listing provides adequate protection through Section 7(a)(2) jeopardy standard and all significant actions require federal agency involvement.

**Budget:** Two-year study estimated at $56,715 total ($21,790 Year 1, $34,925 Year 2) including graduate student salary, equipment, expendables, ultracentrifugation service contracts, and publication costs. Assumes samples provided at no cost and availability of qualified graduate research assistant.
