# Market Perspectives of European Freshwater Aquaculture (EIFAC/EC Ad Hoc Working Party, 2001)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report from the [[EIFAC]] Ad Hoc Working Party on Market Perspectives of European Freshwater Aquaculture (Brussels, 14–16 May 2001) reviews the state of freshwater aquaculture across the EIFAC region. It focuses on marketing problems and offers strategic advice to governments, investors, consumers and producers. The sector is described as product- or producer-driven and lacking [[Vertical Integration]] between producers and consumers. The report argues that producers will stay at a disadvantage unless they form associations and co-operatives. Marketing plans are the industry's own responsibility, and the document places particular weight on the Polish case, where the collapse of the former marketing system, privatization and supermarket dominance have reshaped the market for [[Common Carp]] and [[Rainbow Trout]].

## Background & Description

The working party was sponsored by the [[European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission]] and the [[European Commission]]. It was published as [[EIFAC Occasional Paper No. 35]] by the [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] in 2001 (142 pp.). The report compiles presentations, discussions and recommendations, plus a [[SWOT Analysis]] table and five appended papers. Most of the case material concerns Poland, which faced a decline in marine fish catches and reduced quotas, giving it a reason to expand freshwater farming before accession to the [[European Union]].

Key species and products covered:
- **Common carp** (*Carp*): the dominant pond species in Poland. Average annual production in the 1980s was 25,600 t.
- **Trout**: the main growth species, with exports largely of smoked, vacuum-packed fillets.
- **Other species**: whitefish, pikeperch, pike, grayling, huchen, [[Sturgeon]], [[African Catfish]] and [[Tilapia]] were discussed as alternatives or stocking material.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Ponds provide retention and self-purification of water, along with landscape and environmental values that some scientists consider more important than their aquaculture output.
- Privatization has left pond ownership unclear and some ponds decapitalized. The lack of experienced pond owners threatens these retention functions.
- Environmental protection agencies and anglers' organizations have shifted towards animosity toward fish farms. Predation by [[Cormorant]], [[Beaver]] and [[Heron]] was a recurring problem.
- Poor economic conditions, rising feed and energy costs, and environmental complaints have made rational fish farming more difficult.
- The Baltic Sea was a stocking destination for whitefish fingerlings. Stocking material is mostly farm-bred, and the funding is largely governmental.

## Key Findings

**Structure and ownership**
- Krüger and Okoniewski (1999) estimated that privately owned pond area, including hereditary tenure, was equivalent to 79 percent of total pond surface.
- Pond fish production represents no more than 0.4 percent of the value of agricultural production, or 2 percent of total domestic meat production.
- Significant human resources exist through a professional school system and research institutes, although these institutes face survival problems.

**Trout farming**
- Trout output was projected to grow from about 11,000 t per year to 12,000 t by 2005 and 23,000 t by the end of the decade. The optimism rests on modernization, growing demand for processed products, supermarket promotion and full privatization.
- Poland exports about a third of its trout production, mainly as smoked fillets to Germany.
- Imports of frozen Norwegian salmon, processed in Poland and sold as smoked fillets, threaten trout farmers.
- Trout farming costs rose with fishmeal, oil, labour and energy prices, while prices stagnated.
- Over 2 million sea trout and salmon smolts are released each year.

**Carp market**
- Equilibrium between supply and demand was expected at about 20,000 t, with a slight decline in the second half of the decade. Maintaining it requires intensive advertising and year-round supply of fresh and processed carp.
- A wider range of fish species reduced demand for carp by almost 30 percent.
- Marketing has shifted from live fish to processed products. Processing plants and investment in equipment are needed.
- Supermarkets and middlemen dictate trade terms, which lowers prices and returns.

**Sales channels and recreation**
- A survey (Krüger, 2000) found parallel sales channels including pay-lakes (18.9 percent), agrotourism (4.5 percent) and fish-fry sales (3.6 percent). The wholesale share is garbled in the scan.
- Over 5 percent of trout farmers and almost 19 percent of carp farmers sell through put-and-take or pay-lake fisheries.
- About 700,000 registered anglers exist in Poland.
- The new product "ready to grill trout" (gutted, in aluminium foil) emerged and is now sold in all supermarkets.

**Recommendations and conclusions**
- Producers must form associations or production groups and integrate with processing industries to produce high-quality, value-added products.
- Marketing plans must be financed and carried out by the industry itself, with supportive policy frameworks.
- Certification of organic products is still largely based on private bodies.
- The overarching conclusion is captured in the slogan "Fish farmers unite!", reflecting the view that only collective organization can secure the sector's future.
