# Testimony on the Lower Colorado River Basin: Endangered Fish & Ecosystem Degradation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Poeciliidae #endangered-species #ColoradoRiver

## Executive Summary

This 1994 Congressional testimony by Dale Pontius (American Rivers) before the Senate Energy and Natural Resources Committee addresses the ecological collapse of the [[Lower Colorado River]] ecosystem due to dam construction, water diversion, and non-native species introduction. The testimony centers on four federally endangered "big river" native fish species — the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (referred to as "squawfish"), and [[Razorback Sucker]] — and the lack of a comprehensive recovery program for the lower basin, in contrast to upper basin efforts. It also discusses a 1994 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service jeopardy opinion finding that the Central Arizona Project (CAP) threatens the [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and Razorback Sucker due to CAP infrastructure facilitating invasion of non-native fish into Arizona tributaries.

## Background & Description

The source text is policy testimony and does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of the species discussed. However, taxonomic/status classifications are noted:

- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) — federally endangered; a "big river" cyprinid native to the mainstem lower Colorado River.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) — federally endangered; a "big river" cyprinid, notable elsewhere (e.g., Grand Canyon) for its distinctive dorsal hump (not elaborated in this text).
- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]** ("squawfish," *Ptychocheilus lucius*) — federally endangered; largest native cyprinid of the Colorado River system.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — federally endangered catostomid, named for its characteristic keeled/razorback dorsal ridge (not detailed in source).
- **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*) — federally threatened small-bodied cyprinid.
- **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*) — federally threatened small-bodied cyprinid.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) — federally endangered live-bearing poeciliid.

All native fish species of the [[Gila River]] basin are noted to be either federally listed (threatened/endangered) or candidates for listing — an indicator of basin-wide habitat collapse.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical hydrology**: Pre-dam Colorado River carried ~180 million tons of silt/year past Yuma, sustaining the [[Colorado River Delta]] and Upper Gulf of California ecosystem. Post-Hoover Dam, silt load dropped to 13 million tons/year.
- **Flow regime**: Historically driven by Rocky Mountain snowmelt pulses in spring, receding to near-trickle by summer, with seasonal warming — a natural hydrograph now eliminated by damming (Hoover Dam, 1935; Glen Canyon Dam, 1963).
- **Long-term flow data**: Tree-ring analysis indicates average annual flow of only 13.5 million acre-feet (maf) over 400 years, far below the 7.5 maf/basin allocation assumed in the 1922 Colorado River Compact (based on an anomalously wet period).
- **Delta/estuarine habitat**: The [[Colorado River Delta]] and Cienega de Santa Clara (a Mexican Biosphere Reserve wetland) depend on freshwater inflows, now partially sustained artificially by Welton-Mohawk Irrigation District discharge — threatened if the Yuma Desalting Plant is reactivated.
- **Refugia**: Remaining potential native fish habitat exists in free-flowing reaches of the Salt, Verde, Gila, San Pedro, and Bill Williams Rivers.
- **Primary threats to native fish**:
  - Habitat loss/alteration from damming, channelization, and riprap.
  - **Non-native/invasive fish species** introduced via CAP water conveyance infrastructure — identified as the *principal cause* of bonytail, squawfish, and razorback decline/extirpation in the lower basin.
  - Unregulated groundwater pumping depleting surface flows, particularly threatening the [[San Pedro River]] National Conservation Area.
  - Predation and competition from non-indigenous species entering via engineered water transfer systems (CAP canals linking the Colorado River to central/southeastern Arizona streams).
- **Broader ecosystem role**: The lower river corridor functions as a migratory bird flyway and refuge for species such as the endangered [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], underscoring multi-species ecosystem value beyond fish alone.

## Key Findings

1. **No comprehensive recovery program exists** for the four endangered "big river" native fish (bonytail chub, humpback chub, squawfish/Colorado pikeminnow, razorback sucker) in the *lower* Colorado basin, despite an active Recovery Implementation Program in the *upper* basin under Endangered Species Act litigation.
2. **April 15, 1994 FWS Biological Opinion**: Found CAP operations likely to jeopardize the threatened [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] and endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], because CAP facilities enable non-native Colorado River fish to invade central/southeastern Arizona streams.
3. **Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA)** to the jeopardy opinion calls for fish barriers, additional research, and monitoring — but adequacy is legally contested (at least one lawsuit filed by local groups).
4. **Habitat Conservation Plan (HCP) process** under ESA Section 10 was being developed by lower basin states/water users and FWS *without* input from scientists, Native American tribes, conservation organizations, or the public — flagged as a procedural and substantive concern by American Rivers.
5. **Gila River basin status**: All native fish species are federally listed or candidates for listing — the most severely impacted native fish assemblage discussed in the testimony. American Rivers announced a new study mapping historical/current habitat in this basin.
6. **Funding gaps**: Despite $4+ billion spent on CAP construction (with authorized purpose including "conservation and development of fish and wildlife" under the 1968 Colorado River Basin Project Act), no appropriated funds have gone to non-mitigation fish/wildlife restoration. Arizona's legislature created only a $9 million/2-year Water Protection Fund, far short of the Governor's Advisory Committee's recommended $8 million/10-year dedicated trust.
7. **Proposed CAP water reallocation**: Up to 150,000 acre-feet of unallocated CAP water recommended for fish/wildlife use on the mainstem Colorado (benefiting two federal wildlife refuges) and Arizona tributaries — precedent cited from Central Valley Project and Central Utah Project legislation.
8. **International/transboundary dimension**: Restoration assessment recommended to extend to the Gulf of California, including Mexico's newly designated Biosphere Reserve (Upper Gulf/Colorado Delta) and the Cienega de Santa Clara wetland, linked to NAFTA environmental side agreements.
9. **Bureau of Reclamation's draft water marketing regulations** (1994) for the lower basin lack adequate environmental impact provisions and do not include a proposed surcharge mechanism to fund a lower Colorado River environmental trust fund.
10. **Systemic critique**: The testimony frames non-native species introduction (via engineered water infrastructure like CAP) as the dominant proximate driver of native fish decline in the lower basin — distinguishing this mechanism from the flow-alteration/dam-blockage mechanisms more prominent in upper basin and mainstem contexts.
