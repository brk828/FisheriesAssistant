# Prominent Fishes of West Virginia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This Conservation Commission of West Virginia booklet, prepared by the Division of Education under Fish Division Chief Harry Van Meter, is an identification guide for anglers and school classes. It illustrates 50 prominent freshwater fish species, grouped by family and accompanied by brief descriptions and diagnostic features. It states that these represent less than one-third of the fishes found in state waters. A companion essay argues that West Virginia, though known for [[Stream Fishing]], is shifting toward [[Impoundment Fishing]] as streams decline from pollution and industrialization and as fishing pressure rises.

## Background & Description

The guide is written for simplicity. Each entry covers a species' size, coloration, diet, habitat preference and key identifying marks, often with a line-drawn illustration labeled with features such as fin shape, [[Lateral Line]] pattern, scale type, barbels and spines. The booklet is organized by family, with 18 families represented. Five families known from state waters are deliberately omitted: Bowfin (Amiidae), Mudminnow (Umbridae), Topminnow (Cyprinodontidae), Silverside (Atherinidae), and Stickleback (Gasterosteidae).

Back matter includes a generic "[[Hypothetical Fish Anatomy]]" sketch that labels the dorsal, anal, pectoral and pelvic fins, a conservation pledge, and a magazine subscription form for the Conservation Commission. These are ancillary and not ecological data.

Representative families and species described:

- **[[Lamprey Family]]:** [[Ohio Brook Lamprey]], a nonparasitic eel-like species up to 8 inches, with 54–60 muscle segments and two dorsal fins. Larvae burrow in stream banks for several years, and adults spawn once and die.
- **[[Paddlefish Family]]:** the sole U.S. representative, with a paddle-shaped snout. Specimens reach 6 feet. It feeds on microscopic organisms and is rarely taken by anglers.
- **[[Sturgeon Family]]:** [[Lake Sturgeon]], up to 10 feet and 200 pounds, maturing around the twentieth year. It is rare today.
- **[[Salmon Family]] (trout):** [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and [[Eastern Brook Trout]]. Brook trout are the only species that reproduce naturally in many streams, so hatchery stocking is described as essential.
- **[[Sucker Family]]:** includes [[Smallmouth Buffalo]], [[Quillback]], [[Golden Redhorse]], [[White Sucker]] and [[Hog Sucker]]. Suckers are described as among the most common state fishes, though bony and poorly regarded as food.
- **[[Minnow Family]]:** described as the largest family in state waters, dominated by small bait fishes. Includes [[Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Stoneroller]], shiners, [[Creek Chub]], [[Fallfish]] and [[Blunt Nose Minnow]].
- **[[Catfish Family]]:** [[Black Bullhead]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Brown Bullhead]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]]. These fish have sharp spines, several pairs of barbels, an adipose fin and a scaleless body, and are mostly nocturnal feeders.
- **[[Bass Family]]:** [[White Bass]], rarely fished in state waters.
- **[[Perch Family]]:** [[Yellow Perch]], [[Yellow Pikeperch]] (walleye), the [[Eastern Sauger]], and the [[Darters]].
- **[[Sunfish Family]]:** [[Smallmouth Black Bass]], [[Largemouth Black Bass]], [[Spotted Black Bass]], [[Black Crappie]], [[White Crappie]] and [[Green Sunfish]], plus [[Rock Bass]].
- **[[Drum Family]]:** [[Freshwater Drum]], the sole family member in West Virginia.
- **[[Sculpin Family]]:** [[Northern Muddler]], a scaleless, flattened-headed stream fish under 5 inches.

Other taxa mentioned in the illustrations include [[Longnose Gar]], [[Mooneye]], [[Skipjack]], [[American Eel]], [[Chain Pickerel]] and [[Muskellunge]], as well as [[Trout-Perch]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The booklet frames habitat in terms of stream type and temperature. Brook trout are described as requiring small, cold, spring-fed streams. Brown trout tolerate warmer water than other trout. Bullheads tolerate polluted waters better than most fish, while black crappie cannot withstand muddy streams as well as white crappie.

Pollution is identified as the main threat to larger streams: "pollution from one source or other has taken its toll in some of our larger streams," and industrialization makes the stream outlook "not too encouraging." Several species are described as formerly common but now rare, including lake sturgeon and paddlefish, which the guide ties to Ohio and Kanawha River systems.

Feeding ecology is noted throughout. Paddlefish eat microscopic organisms, lake sturgeon feed on bottom material, freshwater drum eat mollusks and insects, and many suckers and minnows consume vegetation and aquatic insect larvae. Darters, found in shallow riffles, feed almost entirely on small insects and larvae.

## Key Findings

- **Impoundments are the future of fishing.** The booklet argues that West Virginia is "slowly but surely turning into a 'lake fishing' state," because impoundments withstand heavier fishing pressure, are more productive, are easier to manage and rehabilitate, and yield higher creel returns with a larger share of successful anglers.
- **Recently built lakes include** Summit Lake near Richwood (42 acres), Spruce Knob Lake (26 acres), a Coopers Rock State Forest lake (6 acres), and about 18 acres of fishing water at the Bear Rock Lakes Area near Wheeling.
- **Farm ponds are numerous.** The state is estimated to have between 5,000 and 8,000 farm ponds, excluding sportsmen's club ponds, city reservoirs and commercial ponds, which are also increasing.
- **Future prospects are positive.** Several federal reservoirs are in planning, and the Conservation Commission is conducting preliminary surveys and blueprints for additional lakes as funds allow.
- **Trout stocking is essential.** Hatchery-reared trout are required to meet angler demand, with brook trout the only trout reproducing naturally in many streams.
- **Maximum sizes recorded:** paddlefish 6 feet; lake sturgeon 10 feet and 200 pounds; flathead catfish 100 pounds; channel catfish 40 pounds; freshwater drum over 50 pounds (known, though rarely above 5 pounds locally); carp 30 pounds; smallmouth buffalo 20 pounds; white sucker 6 pounds; hog sucker 2 feet; walleye 12 pounds.
- **Angling notes:** largemouth bass reports of 4 pounds are common statewide, smallmouth adults average 2–3 pounds, and brown trout of 3–4 pounds are reported by anglers.
- **Identification emphasis:** many species are separated by single features, such as the number of dorsal spines in crappies (7–8 in black, 6 in white), the number of muscle segments in Ohio brook lamprey (54–60), or the spirally wound intestine that identifies stoneroller.
