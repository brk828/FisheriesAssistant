# Fish Behavior in Flow Fields

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Teleostei

## Executive Summary

This book chapter, by Charles H. Hocutt and John E. Edinger, reviews how fishes respond to the water flows created by [[Power Plant Intake]] structures. Intake operation alters both the physical presence of a structure and the direction and velocity of water, and these cues can trigger rheotactic and schooling responses that lead to [[Impingement Mortality]] on intake screens. The chapter synthesizes laboratory and field work on [[Swimming Performance]], the physiological limits that shape it, and the engineering responses (louvers, angled screens, velocity caps and other designs) intended to guide fish away from intakes. It concludes that intake design based on average velocities is insufficient, and that site-specific flow-field data are needed.

## Background & Description

**Intake effects.** Cooling water intakes produce two major physical changes relative to undisturbed areas: the structure itself and the flow it induces. Fish are impinged against screens and die from descaling, physical damage, or suffocation when water pressure holds the gill chambers closed.

**Swimming behavior.** Fishes differ among species and life stages in their preferred water velocity and direction. Many anadromous species show countercurrent orientation and are highly sensitive to current direction, while others, such as some estuarine and coastal fishes, move with the tide. [[Stamina]] varies among species and, per Tsuyuki and Williscroft (1977), among genetically distinct stocks.

**Measurement methods.** The chapter reviews equipment used to study swimming, including [[Fish Wheel]] systems, annular troughs, straight troughs, recirculating tunnels, and fishways. Common performance measures include:
- **Sustained (cruising) speed:** maintained for 200 min or longer, with aerobic metabolism.
- **Prolonged speed:** maintained from 15 s to 200 min, supplied by aerobic and anaerobic processes.
- **Burst speed:** 15 s or less, powered mainly by anaerobic processes.
- **[[Critical Swimming Speed]]:** determined by stepwise flume velocity increases until fatigue.

**Body length and size.** Swimming speed scales with body length through the relation Y = aX^b (Brett 1965). Absolute speed therefore favors adults, while relative speeds (body lengths per second) decline with size because of hydrodynamic drag. Relative speeds usually range from 3 to 6 BL/s, with reports up to 20 BL/s for European dace.

**Body form and propulsion.** The caudal fin is the main propulsive organ. Locomotor modes are classified (after Breder 1926, with Lindsey 1978) as [[Carangiform Swimming]], anguilliform, subcarangiform, thunniform, and ostraciiform. High-aspect-ratio caudal fins are associated with fast cruising, and low-aspect-ratio forms with other modes.

**Schooling.** Organized schools may reduce hydrodynamic resistance, allowing two to six times higher endurance. Diagonal diamond configurations are more efficient than rows. Schooling also offers predator avoidance, feeding efficiency and tolerance to pollutants.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature.** Activity increases with acclimation temperature up to the [[Final Preferendum]], then declines. Sustained and prolonged performance are strongly temperature-dependent, whereas burst speeds are relatively temperature-independent. Rapid decline in swimming performance occurs above the incipient lethal level.

**Oxygen and carbon dioxide.** Oxygen demand rises logarithmically with swimming speed, while oxygen solubility falls as temperature rises. Swimming can be reduced by oxygen levels both above and below air saturation, depending on the species. Elevated [[Carbon Dioxide]] inhibits gill diffusion and lowers blood affinity for oxygen. Moss and Scott (1961) defined the critical dissolved oxygen level as the lowest concentration at which fish survive 24 h.

**Muscle physiology.** White muscle, comprising 80 to 95% of the musculature, is anaerobic and used for bursts, such as escaping intake screens. Red muscle supports sustained low-speed swimming. Strenuous exercise causes [[Lactic Acid]] buildup and glycogen depletion, and recovery can take hours. In Atlantic cod, muscle glycogen fell as much as 80% at higher velocities. In black bullheads, blood lactate rose from 5.5 to 42.3 mg/100 mL within 40 min and returned to normal after 4.2 to 4.9 h. Oxygen debt may take 8 to 19 h to repay.

**Power plant relevance.** The chapter argues that these physiological relationships can combine to cause fish kills near intakes: attraction to intake currents, prolonged swimming in front of intakes, high seasonal temperatures or recirculated cooling water, reduced oxygen or elevated carbon dioxide, and eventual fatigue and impingement.

**Impingement and velocity.** Impingement mortality depends on velocity and duration, though the relationship is complex and site-specific. Bibko et al. (1974) defined three thresholds: sustained swimming velocity, escaping swimming velocity, and impingement velocity. For striped bass (88.5 to 137.5 mm TL) these averaged 0.49, 0.61 and 0.67 m/s, respectively. Approach velocities before intake screens are almost always below 0.46 m/s and often below 0.3 m/s (Sharma and Freeman 1977; Freeman and Sharma 1977; Stupka and Sharma 1977).

## Key Findings

### Behavioral barriers and guidance systems

- **[[Louver Diverter]] systems** exploit fish sensitivity to abrupt horizontal changes in velocity and direction. Schuler (1973) reported maximum guidance of 95 to 100% with louvers spaced 2.54 cm apart, set at 20° to flow, at an approach velocity of 0.61 m/s. Louvers outperformed screens in diverting fish.
- Schuler and Larson (1975) reported 95 to 100% guidance at a 20° frame angle, 0.61 m/s approach velocity and 0.76 m/s bypass velocity, working equally well in light and darkness.
- Taft and Mussalli (1977) obtained 100% guidance for alewife, white perch, tomcod and striped bass with [[Angled Screens]] at 25° and bypass velocity 1.5 times approach velocity. Delayed mortality at 96 h was below 4%.
- Schuler (1974) found that bypass velocity should be at least 1.5 times approach velocity and probably above the sustained swimming speed of the fish. Turbulence in the bypass allowed fish to re-enter the approach channel.
- Bates and Jewett (1961) reported 90 to 98% efficiency for steelhead with vertical louvers at various approach velocities and slat spacings of 5 and 11.4 cm.
- Monan et al. (1967) raised juvenile steelhead guidance from 54 to 83% by reducing louver spacing from 40.6 to 12.1 cm.

### Velocity caps and offshore intakes

- Velocity caps add a horizontal flow that fish can perceive and avoid, which reduces entrapment of fish disoriented by vertical currents.
- Schuler and Larson (1975) found 85 to 95% higher entrapment at uncapped than capped structures, with northern anchovy. Mortality fell 60 to 70% after flows beneath the caps were made uniform.
- They identified 0.45 m/s as the optimum capped intake velocity. Even so, many capped plants entrap millions of fish per year (Sharma and Freeman 1977).
- Dorn and Helvey (1977) described six behavioral responses to intake flow: positive and negative rheotaxis, oblique and perpendicular orientation, direction reversal and "switching". Responses were species-specific.

### Mechanical and other designs

- Horizontal traveling screens may offer significant guidance potential, but mechanical problems and depth limits have delayed their development (EPA 1976).
- Inclined plane screens avoid impingement and backwash stress, but need considerable space and face design problems in estuaries with water-level fluctuation.
- The Ristroph traveling screen reportedly reduced impingement mortality by 90% for most species tested (White and Brehmer 1976). The chapter notes these results are inconclusive, and that sublethal injury could expose more individuals to latent mortality from infection.
- A porous dike intake at Mount Hope Bay, Massachusetts, has results due in 1981. Biofouling was identified as a preliminary problem.
- Water and air jet barriers are generally considered impractical. Fish pumps are in use at a Southern California Edison facility and a Detroit Edison facility on Lake Erie.

### Interpretive conclusions

- Laboratory swimming data do not correlate easily with power plant operation. Design criteria limiting velocity through screens assume fish can swim faster than intake velocity, and they ignore changes in current direction.
- The impingement coefficient (fish impinged per volume pumped) depends on population density in the zone of influence, intake design, and species susceptibility. It cannot be predicted from design alone.
- Problem species such as clupeids show strong countercurrent orientation, which argues against the assumption that concurrent-oriented fish are simply attracted into screens.
- Crowding may cause density-dependent stress, and phenotypic differences in stamina complicate prediction.
- Average velocity design is insufficient to limit impingement. Site-specific flow-field data and numerical modelling are recommended, with physical modelling considered expensive.
