# Food Habits of Carp in Northwest Iowa Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinus-carpio

## Executive Summary

This 1953 study by Tom Moen, published in the [[Iowa Academy of Science]] proceedings, examines the diet of [[Cyprinus Carpio]] (Common Carp) in northwest Iowa lakes. The author analyzed the entire digestive tract of 687 carp (407 with food) collected from 14 lakes and one pond during 1946–1949. Contrary to the popular view that carp are destructive vegetation feeders, the diet was predominantly animal material. Insect larvae, led by [[Chironomid Larvae]] (midges), were the most important food, followed by small crustaceans and snails. Plant matter was mainly detrital debris, with green fragments and seeds contributing little. Winter diets were 100 percent animal. Carp digested a full stomach in about 24 hours at 49°F or warmer, and the study concludes that carp compete strongly with other fishes for benthic food, especially in lakes with poor bottom fauna and high carp densities.

## Background & Description

Carp were introduced to the United States relatively recently and had come to dominate much of the country's fishery resources and fishery management effort. They were often blamed for fishery and game problems, and their feeding habits were central to that blame. The author notes that food and feeding relationships shape fish ecological associations and are essential for managing fishery resources (citing [[Bailey and Harrison]], 1948).

Earlier literature on carp diet was sparse and mostly general. [[Cole]] (1904) attributed the decline of wild celery and wild rice in inland marshes partly to carp. [[Forbes and Richardson]] (1920) described carp as omnivorous, taking mainly vegetable matter. [[Gerking]] (1950) found only five American references involving 399 carp. Including additional studies, the author's compilation totals 679 fish across the literature (Table 1 in the source), and the present study adds 687 more.

**Study area and sampling**
- Fish came from Spirit, East Okoboji, West Okoboji, Marble, Hottes, Little Spirit, Prairie, Diamond, Lost Island, Rush, Silver, Trumbull, High, and Storm lakes, plus the Spirit Lake Hatchery "L" pond (about 0.5 acre).
- Most lakes were shallow, glacial, and eutrophic. [[West Okoboji Lake]] exceeded 100 feet in depth; its maximum depth is listed as 132 feet in Appendix Table 9.
- Gear: [[Drag Seine]]s took most fish, with gill nets (about 10%) and traps (about 20%) contributing the rest.
- Fish were grouped as 127 young, 83 small (0.3–1.7 lb), and 477 adults (up to 25 lb).

**Methods**
- The whole digestive tract was treated as one "stomach," because carp lack a distinct stomach pocket and the anterior gut was often empty.
- Contents were measured volumetrically (cubic centimeters, moist material), then sorted under a binocular microscope. Items were recorded as percent volume and frequency of occurrence.
- Fish held more than 12 hours (summer) or 24 hours (winter) before examination were excluded to limit empty stomachs.
- Pharyngeal teeth grind food extensively, so chitinous parts of larvae were often the only identifiable remains. Caddisfly cases and some crustaceans resisted digestion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Animal food and bottom fauna**
- Lakes with little bottom fauna (0.1 cc or less per square foot) usually had little green vegetation. Carp from these lakes ate more debris, and debris and midge larvae often occurred together.
- Caddisfly larvae were confined to carp from larger lakes during open water, suggesting larger lakes offered better caddisfly habitat. Caddisfly larvae were usually associated with pondweed fragments.
- [[Hyalella Knickerbockeri]] (scud) was the only important large crustacean. It appeared mainly in larger lakes with good bottom fauna, vegetation, low turbidity, and a balanced game fish–carp ratio. [[Bailey and Harrison]] (1945) found scuds in large numbers in carp from Clear Lake when vegetation was abundant.
- Snails, chiefly [[Valvata Tricarinata]] and Amnicola species, were eaten mainly in summer and only by carp from lakes with better-than-average conditions.

**Vegetation and seasonality**
- Green fragments, mostly pondweed (*Potamogeton* spp.) leaf parts, were taken mainly by adults weighing two pounds or more. In large fish, green material reached a maximum of 28 percent of volume, averaging about 10 percent. One East Okoboji carp contributed about two-thirds of the green material in 14 September fish.
- Seeds of aquatic plants were eaten year-round, peaking in late summer, but never exceeded 2 percent of volume for any lake or period except one Spirit Lake collection.

**Winter feeding and oxygen**
- Winter feeding was lower in both volume and proportion of stomachs containing food. Crustaceans and midge larvae were of nearly equal importance.
- Carp from Hottes Lake (80 fish) and Little Spirit Lake (45 fish) taken under low oxygen (below 1.0 ppm) had completely empty stomachs.
- Feeding appeared to follow plankton pulses (inferred from observation, since plankton was not sampled), with the lowest activity in early winter.

**Egg predation and fish prey**
- Carp were not clearly shown to eat fish eggs. One April Spirit Lake carp (of 37) contained three walleye (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*) eggs.
- Moen (1950) found no walleye eggs in 51 Spirit Lake carp and nine eggs in one of two East Okoboji carp.
- [[Harrison]] (1950) found no egg predation in 87 Des Moines River carp, though fish made up about 1 percent of the diet by occurrence.

**Physical characteristics of study lakes (Appendix Table 9)**
- Spirit Lake: 5,684 acres, maximum depth 27 ft, average 15 ft.
- East Okoboji: 1,875 acres, maximum 26 ft.
- West Okoboji: 3,939 acres, maximum 132 ft.
- Rush Lake: 460 acres, maximum 4 ft; considered the poorest aquatic environment in the study.

## Key Findings

1. **Diet is mainly animal.** The diet of carp of all sizes was predominantly animal material from three groups: insect larvae, crustaceans, and mollusks.
2. **Insect larvae dominate.** Diptera larvae, mainly chironomids, ranked first across all sizes and conditions. Young carp averaging 3.5 g also ate them. Trichoptera (caddisfly) larvae ranked second among insects but only in carp from larger lakes.
3. **Crustaceans are second.** Small Entomostraca ranked second in volume. They were especially important for young and small carp year-round and for adults in winter. *Hyalella knickerbockeri* was the only significant large crustacean.
4. **Plant matter is minor.** Plant material was mostly debris, then green fragments (seldom above 10%), then seeds (under 2%).
5. **Winter diet is entirely animal.** Analysis of 167 adult carp from four Iowa lakes found 100 percent animal material, with only traces of plant matter.
6. **Consumption is high.** Animal food averaged 3.7 cc per pound of carp in open water, ranging from 0.5 cc (Rush Lake) to 9.2 cc (West Okoboji). Midge counts averaged 5,969 per stomach in open water (range 904–18,800) and 1,099 in winter (range 1–5,000).
7. **Digestion is fast.** Carp digested stomach contents in about 24 hours at 49°F or warmer. The lowest open-water temperature recorded was 48°F.
8. **Estimated population impact.** A population of 100 lb/acre would consume about 370 cc of animal food per day, or roughly 5 lb per acre per week. The author describes this as considerable competition with other fish for food, especially in lakes with poor bottom fauna and high carp densities.
9. **Egg predation is limited.** Fish egg predation was minimal and not extensive, contrary to common assumptions.
10. **Carp are not clearly harmful to vegetation.** Vegetation was a small part of the diet. The author concludes that the evidence does not support the view that carp are primarily vegetation destroyers under normal lake conditions.
