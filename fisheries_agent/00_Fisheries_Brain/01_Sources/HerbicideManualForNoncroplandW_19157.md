# Herbicide Manual for Noncropland Weeds (USDA Agriculture Handbook No. 269)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-weeds

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Department of Agriculture handbook, prepared by R. S. Dunham of the Agricultural Research Service in cooperation with the Bureau of Yards and Docks, Department of the Navy (issued March 1965), is a practical manual for chemical control of weeds on noncropland. It reviews more than 1,000 publications and draws on research from about 700 investigators. The handbook covers general weed biology, herbicide classification, hazards to people, livestock, desirable plants, equipment, and game and fish, descriptions of [[Current Herbicides]], formulation, mixing, storage, [[Application Equipment]], and application rates for specific weed sites and groups, including [[Aquatic Weeds]].

Although the handbook is not a fish study, it contains important guidance on the toxicity of herbicides to fish and aquatic life, which is the main link to aquatic ecology.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Government technical handbook (Agriculture Handbook No. 269), priced at 50 cents, with a June 1965 erratum correcting Table 2 (concentration units for emulsions, oil sprays, and suspensions).
- **Scope:** Weed control on noncropland, including storage areas, ditchbanks, roadsides, railroads, turf, woody plantings, and waterways.
- **Structure:** Sections cover general information, current herbicides, herbicide formulation and mixing, application equipment, application of herbicides, a [[Weed Species and Herbicides for Control]] table (Table 5, with annual, aquatic, biennial, perennial, and woody habits and S/I/R responses), and indexes.
- **Terminology:** Herbicides are grouped as selective or nonselective, and by mode of action as contact, translocated (growth-regulator type), or soil sterilant. Toxicity ratings follow the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act classification and [[Lethal Dose LD50]] values.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Weed biology.** Annuals produce very large seed numbers that remain viable for years in soil, so eradication is difficult. Perennials regrow from storage organs (stolons, rhizomes, bulbs, crowns, roots), so control requires depleting food reserves or destroying those organs.

**Fate in the environment.** Herbicide persistence depends on volatility, microbial decomposition, solubility, adsorption to soil colloids, soil pH and fertility, species tolerance, and application rate. Amitrole is soluble and readily leached. Monuron and diuron adsorb to clay and resist leaching. Dalapon disappears fastest in warm, humid conditions.

**Aquatic and wildlife hazards.** The handbook states that most herbicides are less dangerous than insecticides to wildlife, but arsenicals and dinitros can poison animals. A few herbicides are very toxic to fish, while many can be used safely for aquatic weed control. Safe concentrations, measured in parts per million, vary with the age, size, and species of fish, as well as water hardness. Specific warnings include:
- [[Acrolein]] is extremely toxic to fish and aquatic animals.
- [[Aromatic Solvents]] are deadly to fish and may kill all aquatic life.
- [[Copper Sulfate]] is toxic to many fish species above 1 p.p.m.; it is safe for most fish at recommended concentrations but harmful to trout.
- [[Dichlone]] may be dangerous to trout at 0.05 p.p.m.
- [[Sodium Arsenite]] is not toxic to fish at standard aquatic rates, but fish may die from oxygen loss when weed decay consumes dissolved oxygen.
- [[Dinitro Compounds]] are toxic to game and fish.
- [[Sodium Chlorate]] is toxic to fish.
- Naphtha fumes are toxic to fish and waterfowl.

The handbook advises consulting Federal or State Fish and Wildlife Services when a spraying program might endanger game and fish.

**Water quality limits.** The U.S. Public Health Service is cited as considering drinking water with over 7.5 p.p.m. copper sulfate dangerous to humans, and with over 3.0 p.p.m. copper ion as the maximum permitted in potable supplies. Water with more than 0.05 p.p.m. arsenic is described as unsafe for drinking.

## Key Findings

1. **Hazards are wide-ranging.** Herbicides can injure operators, livestock, desirable plants, equipment, and game and fish. Disposal should avoid polluting streams and water supplies; empty containers should be buried at least 18 inches deep away from water.
2. **Aquatic herbicide choices** are constrained by fish toxicity. Acrolein and aromatic solvents are highly lethal to aquatic life. Copper sulfate, endothall, diquat, and sodium arsenite have concentration-dependent aquatic use windows.
3. **Aquatic weed groups** are floating, submersed, and emersed, and each needs different treatment. Ditch and pond rates are given in p.p.m., pounds per acre-foot (1 p.p.m. = 2.7 lb per acre-foot), and gallons per cubic foot of water.
4. **Drift and runoff** are the main hazards to nearby vegetation. Recommended practices include calm weather, low pressure, coarse droplets, and buffer distances.
5. **Herbicide selection** depends on weed habit (annual, perennial, woody, aquatic), desired control duration, soil texture, and rainfall. Heavier rates are needed for perennials, clay soils, high-organic soils, and arid or humid regions with long residuals.
6. **Repeated and mixed treatments** are often more effective and economical than single heavy applications of soil sterilants.
7. **Equipment guidance** covers sprayer types, pumps, nozzles, and calibration methods, with formulas for application rates and aerial sprayer flow.

Key aquatic and broad-spectrum herbicides covered include [[Endothall]], [[Diquat]], [[2,4-D]], [[Amitrole]], [[Dalapon]], [[Simazine]], [[Monuron]], and [[Pentachlorophenol]].
