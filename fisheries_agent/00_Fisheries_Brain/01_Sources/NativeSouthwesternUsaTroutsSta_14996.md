# Native Southwestern (USA) Trouts: Status, Taxonomy, Ecology, and Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[John N. Rinne]] synthesizes over three decades of research on two endangered native trouts of the American Southwest, the [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo apache*) and the [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*). Both species were pushed into the extreme headwaters of their former range by introduced non-native salmonids and habitat loss and degradation. Both are now considered to be on a path toward secure status. The paper recommends monitoring of genetically pure populations, habitat enhancement, and development of sport fisheries as the main goals for future management. The author argues that the taxonomic debate over these fish should not delay conservation.

## Background & Description

- **Discovery and naming:** Early explorers in the late 1800s encountered "speckled" or "yellowbelly" trout in the Gila River headwaters. [[Cope and Yarrow]] (1875) first reported them as cutthroat trout (*Salmo pleuriticus*), based on three specimens collected in 1873 at Whiteriver, Arizona. [[Robert R. Miller]] described the Gila trout from Main Diamond Creek, New Mexico, in 1950, and described the Apache trout from the East Fork of the White River in 1972.
- **Conservation status:** Both species were listed as endangered by the U.S. Department of the Interior in 1967 and by the IUCN in 1969. The Gila trout is still listed by the IUCN. The Apache trout was downlisted to threatened in 1975.
- **Morphology:** The Apache trout is golden-olive-yellow with fin spotting and a yellow "cutthroat" mark under the lower jaw. The Gila trout has more and larger spots, retains parr marks in adults under 30 cm, and has a reddish lateral band like rainbow trout. Both have the cutthroat jaw mark.
- **Meristic comparison:** Table I compares snout length, fin ray counts, scale counts, and pyloric caeca among Apache, rainbow, and Gila trout. Pyloric caeca counts are the clearest separator, with Apache trout at 26–33 (range 22–40) and rainbow trout at 55–62 (range 35–70). Gila trout had 31–36 (range 32–48).
- **Taxonomy:** The genus *Salmo* in western North America remains unresolved (Behnke 1979). Apache and Gila trouts belong to the third series within the group, which also includes [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Redband Trout]] groups. The two species are more closely related to each other than to other western *Salmo*, and genetic differences between them are smaller than those between some cutthroat subspecies. Behnke (1979) suggested they might be reclassified as subspecies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Range reduction:** The Apache trout occupied less than 48 km (5%) of its original 965 km of stream habitat. The Gila trout occurred in only five headwater streams by the late 1970s.
- **Causes of decline:** Introduced trouts, interspecific competition, habitat degradation and loss, and genetic swamping through hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]] were the main drivers. Stocking history strongly shaped genetic purity. Purity was highest on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]], where stocking was limited and streams were closed to angling. It was lower on the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]], where rainbow trout were extensively stocked.
- **Headwater habitat:** Gila trout live in harsh, physically restricted headwaters with frequent flooding and drought. Streamflow in Main Diamond Creek dropped from over 1.7 m³/min to 0.45 m³/min between May and November 1969 and 1970. Gila trout tolerate water up to 27°C for short periods and gather in cool intragravel flow during droughts.
- **Thermal tolerance:** Adult Apache trout have a critical thermal maximum of 28–29°C. Fingerlings stop feeding at 20°C, lose equilibrium at 21–22°C, and die at 23°C.
- **Predation and competition:** Brown trout compete with and prey on Apache trout. Feeding chronology separation suggests competition for living space, not food, is the main mechanism.
- **Parasites:** Both native trouts have a distinct helminth fauna that differs from that of introduced brown trout.
- **Hybridization:** Hatchery-reared rainbow trout show reduced reproductive capacity, high angling mortality, and downstream movement. These factors limit introgression into headwater populations. Artificial crosses also showed that intermediate traits do not always occur, which suggests innate barriers to hybridization.

## Key Findings

- **Population history:** Gila trout numbered under 8,000 individuals in five natural and two introduced populations in the late 1970s. About 60% of them lived in Main Diamond Creek. McKnight Creek, an introduced population, held about 3,000 individuals. By comparison, late-1800s anglers in the White Mountains could take Apache trout at about 100 per hour.
- **Apache trout purity:** Apache trout are known to occur in pure form in 13 streams and in nearly pure form in another 17. Ten of the 13 pure populations are on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation.
- **Reproduction:** Spawning begins at about 8°C over gravel and pebble substrate (0.2–3.8 cm). Gila trout fecundity is low, with 76 to fewer than 200 eggs per female reported. Apache trout fecundity is about 3,000 eggs per kilogram of body weight. Fry emerge in 8–10 weeks (Gila) or 60 days (Apache).
- **Growth and biomass:** Mean biomass was 13 g/m² for Apache trout and 15 g/m² for Gila trout. Apache trout grew about 50 mm between annuli.
- **Movement:** Gila trout tend to stay put, with average movements under 100 m over eight months. Most displacement is downstream.
- **Habitat:** Pool depth was the best predictor of fish size, and six physical-hydromorphological factors explained almost half the variation in fish numbers and biomass in McKnight Creek. Log stream improvement structures created pools of higher quality than natural pools. However, these structures are costly (about $100 each in the 1960s and roughly $1,000 each at present) and lose 50–75% of their structures over 30–50 years.
- **Conservation actions:** The White Mountain Apache Tribe closed streams to angling in the late 1940s and early 1950s. The Arizona Department of Game and Fish began a hatchery program in 1963 at Sterling Springs Hatchery. About 87,000 Apache trout were reared and stocked between 1964 and 1973. Barriers failed at Ord Creek because brown and brook trout got through. Gila trout were moved to McKnight Creek in 1970 and 1972 after the native sucker population was removed with toxicant.
- **Management principles:** Removal of introduced fish and introduction of natives should be separated by at least six months, preferably one year. Antimycin reduces invertebrate food bases for about a year. Wild-to-wild transplants have worked well, avoiding hatchery-related genetic changes.
- **Recovery targets:** The Apache trout plan aims for 30 pure, self-sustaining populations. The Gila trout plan aims to replicate or enhance all five natural populations before downlisting. Gila trout culturing efforts were scheduled to increase in 1988.
- **Sport fishery potential:** Both species can reach 40–50 cm, and records suggest over 25 cm is possible with adequate habitat. Christmas Tree Lake supports a quality Apache trout fishery for a season under two weeks. Sport fishing ranked third among 34 U.S. recreational activities in 1978, with 16 million user days on National Forests. The author argues a viable sport fishery would be a major driver of conservation.
- **Recommendations:** Monitor genetically pure populations, enhance habitat with hydrologist input, keep accurate stocking records, and avoid taxonomic disputes that waste conservation effort. The author notes the trouts represent evolutionary diversity worth conserving in their native Gila basin, even as a "non-resource."
