## Executive Summary

This 1958 study by H.S. Swingle at Auburn University reports foundational experiments on growing fingerling [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) to marketable size in commercial-style earthen ponds, at a time when warmwater [[Pond Aquaculture]] was an emerging industry in the southeastern United States. The paper establishes practical protocols for parasite/disease control, introduces the **S conversion value** as a standardized metric for feed efficiency in pond systems (distinct from the traditional **C conversion value** used in flow-through systems), and evaluates the effects of feed form (pelleted vs. dry-mix), feeding rate, fertilization, stocking density, stocking season, and competition from wild fish/tadpoles on catfish yield. Spring-stocked fingerlings reached market size in an average of 228 days (S = 1.87) versus 394 days for fall-stocked fish (S = 3.0), with maximum yields of ~2,343 lbs/acre. The work directly informed early commercial catfish farming economics, dressing losses, and labor costs.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Ictalurus punctatus* (Channel Catfish), family [[Ictaluridae]].
- **Length-weight data** were tabulated (Table III) to support field estimation of standing biomass via seine sampling.
- **Stocking size**: Fingerlings stocked at average individual weights of **0.005–0.06 lbs** (~2–27 g).
- **Harvest size**: Final average individual weights ranged from **0.43 to 1.02 lbs**, depending on stocking density and duration.
- **Dressing yield**: Dressed weight averaged **61.5%** of live weight in a sample of 1,952 fish (1,222 lbs live → 751.9 lbs dressed).
  - Spring-harvested fish lost **43–45%** of live weight on dressing (attributed to presence of eggs/roe).
  - Fall-harvested fish lost **38.5–41%** of live weight on dressing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Warmwater earthen aquaculture ponds in Alabama, managed with inorganic fertilization (8-8-2 formulation) and supplemental dry/pelleted feed.
- **Thermal ecology**: Growth response was strongly temperature-dependent.
  - Optimal feeding/growth occurred when surface water temperatures exceeded **70°F** (main growing season: April–October at Auburn, AL).
  - Feeding produced little growth below **60°F**; feeding was recommended to cease below this threshold in production ponds (though a low maintenance ration was suggested for wintering brood fish and dense fingerling holding ponds to prevent nutritional deficiency and mortality).
  - Brood fish in wintering ponds were fed at 2% body weight/day, discontinued below **50°F**.
  - Densely held fingerlings were fed at 1% body weight/day, withheld only below **45°F**.
- **Interspecific interactions**:
  - **Tadpoles**: Present in large numbers in some ponds; consumed supplemental feed but were not a major dietary item for catfish and showed no clear negative effect on catfish production or S values.
  - **Wild/unwanted fish**: Infiltrated ponds despite poisoning with Noxfish (2 ppm) and gravel filtration; averaged 68.3 lbs/acre in spring-stocked ponds and 164.3 lbs/acre in fall-stocked ponds. Wild fish presence **reduced catfish production and increased S conversion values** (reduced efficiency), unlike tadpoles.
  - **Biocontrol strategy**: Stocking [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) at 100/acre in one pond (F-5) achieved 96% survival, produced 18.2 lbs/acre bass, and reduced residual wild fish to only 2.4 lbs/acre at harvest — though stocking rate was likely too high for optimal bass growth (50/acre suggested as better).
- **Parasite and disease community**: Fingerling catfish were commonly infested with:
  - External protozoan parasites
  - Gill flukes (Monogenea)
  - Tapeworms and other intestinal helminths
  - Susceptible to **Columnaris disease**, an important bacterial pathogen in transport/handling stress contexts.

## Key Findings

### Parasite & Disease Management Protocols
- **Potassium permanganate**: 10 ppm for 1 hr (brood fish); 4 ppm for 1 hr (small fingerlings) — controls gill flukes/external protozoa. Overexposure is lethal.
- **Formalin**: 15 ppm for 5–12 hrs, safe for indefinite exposure, used after permanganate treatment.
- **Acriflavine (neutral)**: 1 ppm for 5–12 hrs; compatible with concurrent formalin treatment (but not with potassium permanganate); also used during transport to inhibit bacterial growth (linked to Columnaris control).
- **Di-n-butyl tin oxide**: Incorporated into feed at 0.3%, fed at 3% body weight/day for 3 consecutive days — effective against tapeworms and intestinal parasites.
- **Survival outcomes**: Overall survival across 13 populations averaged **94.6%** (range 79.6–99.2%), indicating treatment protocols were largely effective.

### Feed Conversion Metrics
- Introduced the **S value** (uncorrected feed conversion in ponds) as distinct from the **C value** (true conversion, corrected for natural/wild food production):
  - S = Feed added ÷ Total fish produced (natural + supplemental)
  - As supplemental feeding intensity increases, S approaches C.
- Best efficiency: **S = 1.24** (Pond F-2), with an estimated true **C ≈ 1.4** after correcting for natural production.

### Feed Type and Pelleting Effects
- **Auburn No. 1 Feed**: 42% protein.
- **Auburn No. 2 Feed**: ~46% protein, 25% carbohydrate, 5% fat; composition: 35% soybean oil meal, 35% peanut cake, 15% fish meal, 15% distillers dried solubles.
- **Pelleting significantly improved efficiency**: comparable trials at 3,705 lbs feed/acre over 252 days yielded:
  - Pelleted feed: **2,363 lbs/acre**, S = 1.6
  - Dry-mix (flour) feed: **1,113 lbs/acre**, S = 3.3
- Pelleting also reduced water pollution, permitting higher safe feeding rates (30 lbs/acre/day pellets vs. 25 lbs/acre/day dry-mix, as maximum safe summer feeding rates without oxygen-depletion mortality).

### Feeding Rate Guidelines
- Recommended sliding scale (Table I) tied feeding rate to estimated standing biomass (10–1,200+ lbs/acre → 1–30 lbs feed/acre/day).
- Optimal daily feeding rates during warm months: **2–5% body weight/day**, 6 days/week; most efficient conversion occurred at **2–4%**.
- Rates above 5% caused inefficient conversion; rates below reduced growth speed and extended time-to-market.

### Fertilization
- No clear relationship found between number of fertilizer applications (2–13 applications of 100 lbs 8-8-2/acre) and total production or S values when heavy supplemental feeding was used.
- Heavy feeding alone stimulated sufficient plankton growth, suggesting fertilization is most useful only in early weeks post-stocking, not during heavy feeding periods.

### Stocking Season Effects
| Metric | Spring Stocking | Fall Stocking |
|---|---|---|
| S Conversion | 1.87 (avg) | 3.0 (avg) |
| Avg Gain (lbs/acre/day) | 7.5 | 3.2 |
| Cost feed+fertilizer (¢/lb) | 11.7¢ | 18.8¢ |
| Days to marketable size | 228 | 394 |

Spring stocking was markedly more efficient and economical, attributed to avoiding the long low-growth winter period experienced by fall-stocked fish.

### Stocking Density Effects
- Increasing stocking density (1,000 → 5,000 fingerlings/acre) increased total production and daily gain per acre but decreased individual harvest size.
- Maximum yields:
  - **2,343 lbs/acre** in 252 days at 3,000 fingerlings/acre.
  - **2,003 lbs/acre** in 188 days at 5,000 fingerlings/acre.
- Optimal stocking rate depends on economic tradeoffs between fingerling cost and per-unit dressing/processing costs (dressing cost per pound is proportionally higher for smaller fish).

### Economic Outcomes
- Dressing/packaging labor cost (at $0.50/hr): **2.16¢/fish**, **3.46¢/lb live weight**, **5.7¢/lb dressed weight**.
- Market price: 50–65¢/lb (sliding scale by volume); typical sale at 55¢/lb.
- **Spring-stocked production**: total production cost ≈ 25.4¢/lb packaged fish; net return ≈ 29.6¢/lb, or **$266.40/acre** at 2,000 fish/acre stocking.
- **Fall-stocked production**: net return ≈ 17.8¢/lb, or **~$140–240/acre**.
- Authors identify the central bottleneck for industry development as the **need for low-cost, large-scale fingerling catfish production** for stocking.
