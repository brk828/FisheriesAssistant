# Interactions Between Drying and the Hyporheic Zone of a Desert Stream

#freshwater-ecology #hyporheic-zone #stream-hydrology

## Executive Summary

This chapter examines how drying affects the [[Hyporheic Zone]] (the subsurface sediments where interstitial water exchanges with surface flow) of [[Sycamore Creek]], a spatially intermittent desert stream in south-central Arizona within the [[Sonoran Desert]]. The authors argue that climate-driven increases in intermittency will reshape not only surface flow but the whole stream ecosystem, because subsurface water can persist after the surface channel is dry. Field measurements of hydrology, hyporheic chemistry and sediment respiration, collected mainly in 1989, show that surface drying outpaces subsurface drying, that hydrologic exchange reverses direction as flow ceases, and that the stream shifts toward nitrate enrichment, oxygen depletion and heterotrophy. The authors caution that extrapolation from desert streams to mesic systems under [[Global Climate Change]] is uncertain.

## Background & Description

Global climate models predict higher temperatures and altered precipitation and runoff across much of North America, which could make permanent streams in mesic regions intermittent and make temporary desert streams more ephemeral (Schlesinger and Mitchell, 1985; Manabe et al., 1981). Intermittency is known to cause major structural and functional change in streams (Boulton and Suter, 1986; Williams, 1987). The authors propose that arid-land streams, which already show spatial and temporal discontinuities in flow, are useful natural models for these future conditions.

Surface flow reflects a balance of gains (precipitation, runoff, upstream and groundwater inflow) and losses (downstream discharge, seepage, evapotranspiration). In desert streams, winter flow is often widespread because evapotranspiration is low and snowmelt arrives from higher elevations, while summer high temperatures and low rainfall often leave channels without surface water. Water can still move through deep alluvium, so channels that look dry may carry substantial subsurface discharge. Sinks, where surface flow drains into sediments, are common in summer, and water may return to the surface downstream as sources.

Sycamore Creek lies in Maricopa County, northeast of Phoenix. Its flow is sustained mainly by high-elevation precipitation. Annual precipitation ranges from 39 to 51 cm, arriving either as intense, localized monsoon storms or as less intense, spatially extensive winter rain. The study reach (mid-stream, 640 m elevation) has low gradient and sandy alluvium that can exceed 2 m in depth. Average depth is 47 cm across a 12 km reach (Valett et al., 1990).

Earlier work by Valett et al. (1990) found that subsurface water in a Sonoran Desert stream may be three or more times the volume of the surface stream. Because water may spend more time below ground than above it, the authors set out to:
- illustrate structural and functional changes in the hyporheic zone as the stream dries;
- describe how linkages between the benthic and hyporheic subsystems change; and
- assess how these changes could affect the surface stream under increasing intermittency.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrologic exchange.** When surface flow is continuous, extensive exchange occurs between surface and hyporheic waters (Grimm and Fisher, 1984; Valett et al., 1990). Drying alters the magnitude and direction of these fluxes. Upwelling zones, where hyporheic water enters the channel, become stationary, downwelling, or restricted to discrete [[Sinks And Sources]] where surface water reappears. Persistence of surface flow depends on hyporheic volume: where sediments are small or underlain by impermeable bedrock, surface flow may be maintained (Grimm et al., 1990). In desert streams with extensive sediments, surface flow may return only after large discharge events that saturate much of the dry hyporheic sediment (Thomsen and Schumann, 1968; Graf, 1988).

**Nutrients and oxygen.** Hyporheic water is typically enriched in nutrients in both perennial streams and Sycamore Creek when surface flow is present (Dahm et al., 1987; Triska et al., 1989; Valett et al., 1990). Nitrogen limits primary production in this desert stream (Grimm and Fisher, 1986), so nitrate delivery is ecologically important.

**Metabolism.** Hyporheic respiration is a substantial part of stream metabolism. Grimm and Fisher (1984) showed that excluding it would make the stream appear autotrophic when it is in fact heterotrophic.

**Climate context.** Climate-driven drying is expected to be rapid. Streams in warmer, drier areas may keep temperate-zone morphology and sediment characteristics, and the authors note that sediment porosity, channel shape, organic matter and groundwater inputs strongly influence hyporheic function, limiting general conclusions.

## Key Findings

### Study Design and Methods

- **Hyporheic drying:** PVC wells (1 m deep) along a sandy run were monitored weekly for depth to water. Dissolved oxygen and nitrate-N were measured in well water, sampled with a hand-held pumping system.
- **Vertical hydraulic gradient (VHG):** 13 [[Minipiezometers]] (20–30 cm deep) were placed along a 150 m reach: 5 across 30 m of an upstream riffle and 8 across 120 m of a sandy run. Heads were read weekly in early mornings from July to October 1989, or until surface water left the site. Positive VHG indicates upwelling; negative VHG indicates downwelling.
- **Respiration:** Sediment cores (35 cm long, 5 cm internal diameter) were collected from 15–20 cm depth, saturated with stream water, incubated for 2 h and analyzed for oxygen loss, normalized to organic matter (Maciolek, 1962). Controls were water-only and formalin-killed cores. Three replicates were taken per site.
- **Surface–hyporheic chemistry:** Dissolved oxygen, [[Soluble Reactive Phosphorus]] (SRP) and [[Nitrate Nitrogen]] (NO3-N) were sampled at fixed upstream and downstream points before and after discontinuous flow. Differences were tested with t tests.

### Hydrologic Patterns

- Surface flow became discontinuous on **19 June**. All surface flow was gone by **8 November**.
- Surface drying was faster than hyporheic drying. On 8 November, water remained in **3 of 11 wells**. Assuming 1–2 m sediment depth and 3 m width, the authors estimate that **about 12%** of the hyporheic zone stayed saturated. They describe this as conservative, since sediments often extended beyond 3 m laterally.
- Upwelling and stationary sites shifted to downwelling as flow declined. The reversal lasted only a few days at downstream sites, where water infiltrated quickly, and up to **70 days** in upstream riffle areas.
- The rate of VHG change predicted the time to loss of surface water: **r² = 0.78, p < 0.01**.

### Hyporheic Water Chemistry

- **NO3-N** rose with distance below a sink at one site and rose and fell with the loss and return of surface water along a 400 m spatially intermittent stretch.
- **SRP** showed no consistent spatial pattern; values fell within the range previously recorded for Sycamore Creek.
- **Dissolved oxygen** showed no clear longitudinal pattern, but at fixed sites it generally declined over time. Brief increases followed small floods.

### Metabolism

- Respiratory potential ranged from **0.34 to 4.86 mg O₂ l⁻¹ hr⁻¹ g⁻¹ organic matter**.
- Water-only and formalin-killed controls showed no net oxygen decrease.
- Saturated sediments under dry reaches respired at rates comparable to those under flowing reaches (**0.91 ± 0.05** vs. **1.01 ± 0.26**, means ± SE, n = 3).
- In moist sediments from drying sections, sediment moisture was directly related to respiration (**r² = 0.77, p < 0.01**). Sediments kept metabolic capacity while wet, even at low moisture.
- Previous estimates for perennial reaches were **2.41 ± 0.13** (Grimm and Fisher, 1984) and **3.49** (Valett et al., 1990).

### Surface Stream Chemistry (Upstream vs. Downstream)

- While flow was continuous, upstream and downstream oxygen, SRP and NO3-N did not differ significantly (p > 0.05).
- Once flow became discontinuous, downstream oxygen was significantly lower (p < 0.01), while SRP and NO3-N were significantly higher (p < 0.001) on all sampling dates.
- Downstream:upstream SRP ratios were **1.21–2.00** after flow stopped. For NO3-N, ratios were **3.1–62**, with one date showing a **62-fold** increase. Under continuous flow, SRP ratios were 0.80–0.98 and NO3-N ratios 0–0.95.

### Synthesis and Implications

- With surface flow present, hyporheic oxygen rarely fell below **1–2 mg l⁻¹**. Under drying, oxygen values below **0.5 ppm** were common, and oxygen fell to **less than 5% saturation** in places.
- The authors describe a two-part shift: hyporheic nitrate accumulates downstream of sinks through in situ [[Nitrification]], while oxygen is consumed by continued respiration. Nutrient-rich, oxygen-poor water emerges at sources.
- The lack of SRP pattern was attributed to physicochemical processes such as sorption and solubility rather than biology (Grimm et al., 1981).
- Under prolonged drying, subsurface conditions may become [[Anoxia]], shifting microbial processes from aerobic to anaerobic and producing reduced nutrients such as ammonium and methane (Dahm et al., 1987; Carr, 1989).
- Upwelling sources may support large algal and invertebrate populations, although low oxygen may limit habitat near sources. Effects are expected to dissipate as water regains oxygen from primary producers.
- In increasingly intermittent streams, surface flow will shrink faster than subsurface flow, so the hyporheic zone will make up a larger share of the ecosystem. Local gains in [[Primary Production]] at upwelling points are unlikely to offset losses of benthic habitat, so the stream is expected to become more heterotrophic. Hyporheic respiration will increase relative to ecosystem respiration.
- In extreme cases, loss of all surface water would create lotic systems that function quite differently from current streams. The authors argue for expanding stream boundaries to include the hyporheic zone: at the time of drying, the stream would otherwise be treated as absent despite continuing subsurface discharge.

### Caveats and Conclusions

- Global climate models are uncertain at regional scales (Cushman and Spring, 1989), so it is difficult to predict where mesic areas will become xeric.
- Desert streams have long histories of aridity, and streams in warming regions may keep temperate geology and morphology, which may limit how far desert results apply.
- Two general expectations are identified: (1) differential drying rates will be common, amplifying the role of hyporheic processes in stream function; and (2) rising intermittency will likely increase the relative importance of [[Ecosystem Metabolism]] driven by subsurface respiration.
