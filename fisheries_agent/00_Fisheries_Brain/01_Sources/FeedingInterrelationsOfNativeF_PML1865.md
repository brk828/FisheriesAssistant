# Feeding Interrelations of Native Fishes in Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the feeding ecology of the native fish assemblage of [[Aravaipa Creek]], a perennial desert stream in south-central Arizona within the [[Sonoran Desert]]. Based on field collections and stomach analyses from 1975–1976, the authors describe what six or seven native species eat, how much their diets overlap, and how diets track changes in invertebrate availability through the year. The fish assemblage is dominated by invertebrate feeders, mainly mayfly nymphs, dipteran larvae, and caddisfly larvae. Diets overlap broadly, but the fishes partition food through differences in habitat use, feeding position, and seasonal shifts, so direct competition appears limited.

## Background & Description

Aravaipa Creek is one of the few desert streams in the region that still supports a largely intact native fish fauna. Earlier work by [[Barber and Minckley]] (1966) and [[Minckley and Barber]] (1971) described the fish community and the life history of the [[Spikedace]], and [[Minckley]] (1973) provided a general account of the ichthyofauna. This paper extends that work with quantitative data on food, food availability, and feeding interrelations.

The creek is a braided, gravel-and-sand channel with rubble riffles, shallow runs (under 6 cm deep), and deeper pools (0.5 to 1.5 m) where canyon walls or obstacles deflect flow. The study reach was a 60 m rubble-bottomed riffle with adjacent shallow sand-gravel runs and a pool formed where the channel was deflected. Mean discharge during the study period was low, with occasional flood flows.

Species examined and their common diet features:
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*): largest species (to 38.5 cm), with a large, terminal, raptorial mouth; ate the most diverse diet, including vertebrates.
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*): a small, surface-to-bottom feeder that relied heavily on mayfly nymphs and some algae.
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*): fed mainly on algae and diatoms, with a smaller share of invertebrates.
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*): mid-water and surface feeder, strongly drift-dependent.
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*): benthic riffle specialist, favoring mayfly nymphs and caddisfly larvae.
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*): bottom feeder with a diverse diet, including many small benthic taxa.
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*): herbivorous, with diets dominated by algae and few ephemeropterans.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food resources.** A total of 75 potential or actual food items were recorded from benthos, drift, and fish stomachs. Of these, 69 (93.3%) were invertebrates. Three vertebrate species were eaten only by the Roundtail Chub. Among invertebrate taxa, 36 kinds (52.2%) were aquatic insect nymphs, pupae, or non-emerging adults; 9 groups (13%) were non-insect aquatic animals; 6 groups (8.8%) were emergent adults of aquatic insects; and 18 taxa (26.1%) were of terrestrial origin.

**Benthos and drift.** Benthos yielded 36 items: 28 (77.8%) aquatic insect stages, 7 (19.4%) other aquatic invertebrates, and 3 (8.3%) non-animal categories. Drift yielded 66 taxa, including 27 aquatic insects, 5 emergent insect adults, 7 other aquatic invertebrates, and 13 terrestrial invertebrates. About 65% of drift (excluding non-animal material) came from the benthos, rising to 75% when emergent adults were included. Of 32 benthic taxa, 23 (71.4%) were found in drift. Only a few taxa, mainly Baetidae, Tricorythidae, and Chironomidae larvae, were consistently present in both benthos and drift.

**Seasonal dynamics.** Ephemeroptera and Chironomidae dominated the food base at most times. Heptageniidae and Ephemerellidae nymphs were seasonally abundant in winter and spring. Hydropsychidae caddisfly larvae increased sharply in benthos in January 1976, driven by a few large individuals rather than dense populations. Summer floods reduced mayfly density and mean nymph size, and Baetidae biomass declined. Turbellarians appeared in benthos in summer, and the Capniidae stonefly *Marcayia* occurred only in cool weather.

**Feeding interactions.** Diets overlapped substantially, but fish species generally fed on different proportions of shared items, and each species shifted its diet as food availability changed.

## Key Findings

**Methods.** Fish were collected with 9.5 m seines (6.4 mm mesh) and electrofishing gear (110 V, 1200 W, alternating current). No more than 10 individuals per species were taken every 4 hours to avoid local depletion. Only subadult and adult fish were analyzed, and stomachs were preserved in 10% formalin. Stomach contents were scored with the [[Hynes Point Method]], in which each point equals 5% of stomach volume (maximum 20 points). Benthos and drift were sampled to compare with fish diets. Drift was collected with drift nets, and data were averaged over 28-hour sampling periods to control for diel periodicity.

**Diet composition.**
- Nymphal mayflies made up 57% or more of stomach contents in 5 of 6 fish species analyzed quantitatively. The exception was *Pantosteus clarki*, which contained few ephemeropterans.
- Dipteran larvae were most common in the stomachs of the bottom-dwelling fishes *Rhinichthys osculus*, *Tiaroga cobitis*, and *Catostomus insignis*.
- Longfin Dace and Sonora Sucker had the broadest diets (38 items each in Table 1 counts), while Roundtail Chub ate 21 items and about 28.6% of its foods were not eaten by other fishes.
- Only 38.1% of Roundtail Chub food items were shared with other species; the Sonora Sucker averaged 44.3% overlap with other fishes (range 26.3–68.4%).
- Speckled Dace and Desert Sucker consumed large amounts of algae and diatoms. Speckled Dace fed on benthic algae in areas with slower flow, consistent with grazing in quiet areas near the bed.

**Drift and stomach correlations.**
- Longfin Dace: baetid drift biomass correlated with stomach occurrence (r = 0.81). Benthic standing crop did not correlate with stomach contents (r < 0.5).
- Spikedace: drift-stomach correlation was strong for mayflies, but benthic correlation was weak.
- Loach Minnow: stomach volumes correlated with benthos for some groups, but the benthic estimates were judged inadequate.
- Sonora Sucker: drifting ephemeropteran nymphs versus stomach volumes gave r = 0.80, and total dipteran larvae gave r = 0.92. The only benthic value exceeding 0.5 was total mayfly nymphs versus stomach mayflies (r = 0.58).

**Species-specific patterns.**
- *Gila robusta* (Roundtail Chub): the most generalized carnivore, feeding from bottom to surface. It ate vertebrates, terrestrial organisms, and bottom invertebrates, and was an active feeder in deeper water near cover.
- *Agosia chrysogaster* (Longfin Dace): favored open, sandy areas with moderate current in water under 15 cm deep. It fed mainly on baetid mayflies and chironomids, with less use of Leptophlebiidae and Rhithrogena, which are associated with cobble rapids.
- *Rhinichthys osculus* (Speckled Dace): fed on algae, diatoms, and small invertebrates from stream margins and quiet areas.
- *Meda fulgida* (Spikedace): fed mainly in mid-water and at the surface on drifting invertebrates, including chironomid adults. Its diet shifted toward larger drifting organisms during the summer high-flow period.
- *Tiaroga cobitis* (Loach Minnow): a riffle benthic feeder. Ephemeropteran nymphs dominated its diet during three sampling periods, and it showed selective use of trichopteran larvae in July 1975.
- *Catostomus insignis* (Sonora Sucker): ate Tricorythidae nymphs, chironomids, and summer turbellarians heavily. Its diet included many small pool-dwelling taxa.
- *Pantosteus clarki* (Desert Sucker): herbivorous, with diets dominated by filamentous algae and diatoms.

**Interpretation.**
- Because fish species shared a common invertebrate food base, the authors conclude that direct competition for food was limited. Diets diverged through differences in habitat, feeding position, and seasonal availability, which suggests resource partitioning rather than strict competition.
- Opportunistic feeding was common: fishes consumed items in proportion to their abundance, and some species fed on the same foods throughout the year.
- Diet shifts tracked changes in food availability, including the post-flood decline of mayflies, the winter increase in caddisfly larvae, and the summer rise in turbellarians.
- The authors note that high diet overlap alone does not prove competition, and that changes in prey availability may cause competition to intensify when food is scarce.

**Conclusions.** The native fishes of Aravaipa Creek form a stable assemblage that partitions a shared, seasonally variable invertebrate food base. Maintaining this partitioning depends on the stream's habitat heterogeneity and natural flow regime, and especially on the flood-driven changes in benthic and drift communities.
