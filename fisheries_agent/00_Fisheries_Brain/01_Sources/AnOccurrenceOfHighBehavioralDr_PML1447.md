# Occurrence of High Behavioral Drift for a Stream Gastropod

#malacology #freshwater-ecology #physa-gyrina

## Executive Summary

This study reports an exceptionally high daily [[Behavioral Drift]] of the freshwater snail [[Physa Gyrina]] in [[Fort Ridgely Creek]], a small second-order stream in [[Southwestern Minnesota]]. Drift reached 533,000 snails per m³/sec, which the author describes as far exceeding previously reported densities for any gastropod or most other stream invertebrates. The high drift is attributed to high snail production in upstream agricultural channels and [[Agricultural Drainage]] ditches, combined with competition for space and food. The document contains no fish data. Its focus is the benthic gastropod community and its drift behavior.

## Background & Description

The paper, by [[Paul E. Marsh]] of the Department of Entomology, Fisheries and Wildlife at the University of Minnesota, addresses a gap in [[Invertebrate Drift]] research. The author notes that major drift reviews had given little attention to gastropods and crustaceans. Since intensive drift research began, only three papers reported gastropod drift, and none reported drift at this level.

The study stream is a small, second-order system with a 17.6 km² watershed. Its headwaters have a low gradient and are heavily modified by many tributary ditches built for agricultural drainage. Near the mouth, the stream drops into a high-gradient reach of 6.0 m/km as it approaches the Minnesota River valley. Base flow at the mouth was 0.015 m³/sec.

The work was a paired comparison of benthos and drift in the ditched headwaters and the lower stream. Benthos was sampled periodically from September 1975 through May 1976. Drift was sampled on 10 dates with drift nets, and qualitative observations were also made.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Benthic dominance:** *Physa gyrina* made up an average of 16.6% of benthos numbers in the study period, making it an important element of the stream benthos.
- **Habitat:** The snails appeared most abundant in unshaded areas of the stream. Ditched headwaters apparently offered favorable conditions for population growth.
- **Drift triggers:** A major drift event followed heavy precipitation and high discharge, suggesting that flow-driven dislodgement and transport contributed to the peak.
- **Reproductive activity:** Gelatinous egg masses appeared in drift samples, indicating active reproduction at the time of drift.
- **Other factors considered:** The author considered dissolved oxygen, temperature increases, and possible herbicide or pesticide exposure from inadvertent application, but found no evidence that these caused the drift events. No other benthic taxa showed comparable mass drift.
- **Diel pattern:** Drift was not clearly periodic, but a diel periodicity was discussed as a possible form of behavioral drift.

## Key Findings

1. **Record drift density:** Daily drift reached 533,000 *Physa gyrina* per m³/sec. This density approaches the highest recorded for any stream invertebrate drift and is far above earlier gastropod reports. In biomass terms, the drift was described as the highest reported for any drifting gastropod.
2. **Two drift peaks:** A first peak occurred in spring, and a second peak in May was about fivefold higher than the first. The second peak coincided with a bloom in drift of other benthic organisms.
3. **Behavioral origin proposed:** Snails were observed floating on the water surface, which is unusual for a snail. The author argues that this is behavioral drift rather than passive dislodgement, driven by population pressure and competition for space and resources.
4. **Source attribution:** The high production in upstream agricultural channels and ditches is considered the main cause of the high drift. The stream's ditched headwaters therefore act as a source population for the lower reaches.
5. **No mortality observed:** No dead drifting snails were found, suggesting that the drifting individuals were alive.
6. **Research gap:** The author stresses that the biomass and population dynamics of this high drift still need further study, and that the role of diel periodicity needs clarification.
