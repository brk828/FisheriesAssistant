# The Red Cedar River Report II: Bioecology

#freshwater-ecology #diatoms #aquatic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1969 report by Ball, Kevern and Linton, published in the *Publications of the Museum, Michigan State University, Biological Series* (Vol. 4, No. 4, pp. 105–160), is the second in a series on the ecology of the [[Red Cedar River]], a warm-water stream in south-central Michigan. It quantifies primary and secondary production across five ecological zones: [[Aufwuchs]] (periphyton), [[Macrophytes]], and [[Bottom Fauna]]. It also tests whether production differs among zones and how it tracks pollution from a [[Williamston Sewage Treatment Plant]] and a [[Metal Plating Plant]] in Fowlerville. Fish are referenced only briefly, through earlier reports and a species checklist.

## Background & Description

The river rises in Cedar Lake and flows about 80 km northwesterly to join the Grand River at Lansing. It has twelve major tributaries and drains about 122,000 hectares (472 square miles). The study area excludes [[Sycamore Creek]], giving a study drainage of about 92,000 hectares (355 square miles). The stream is 8–24 m wide, has a mean gradient of about 0.45 m/km, and is highly buffered and alkaline. Turbidity is low but rises sharply during heavy runoff. Discharge has decreased steadily over twenty years, producing a critical summer flow. Nighttime [[Dissolved Oxygen]] occasionally falls below 8.0 ppm, and nutrient loads are excessive, although spring floods flush much of this material out.

The 48-km study reach was divided into five zones:

- **Zone V** (19.3 km, above Dietz Road): influenced by urban areas and the metal plating plant. Largely dredged, straightened, and bordered by farmland, with a silt and mud bottom.
- **Zone IV** (about 6.4 km): a slow-flowing [[Reservoir]] zone, with its impoundment occupying roughly the lower 3 km.
- **Zone III** (about 4 km, Williamston dam to Zimmer Road): receives primary sewage effluent. Bottom material is mixed.
- **Zone II** (13.7 km, Zimmer Road to Dobie Road): described as the cleanest zone, with sand, gravel, cobble, and boulder bottoms and [[Riffle-Pool Sequences]].
- **Zone I** (about 5.6 km, to the Farm Lane bridge on the MSU campus): slow flow, sand, silt, and detritus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addressed three linked questions: whether biological production differs among zones, whether [[Phosphorus Fixation]] by Aufwuchs reduces nutrient loads below the sewage outfall, and whether the standing crops of herbivorous and carnivorous insects and tubificid worms indicate water quality.

Methods included:

- **Aufwuchs:** artificial plexiglass substrata exposed for 6–18 days, with pigment and ash-free weight measurements following [[Grzenda and Brehmer Method]].
- **Macrophytes:** random 30.5 m sites, with ten 0.093 m² samples per site, dried and weighed to give production per day.
- **Bottom fauna:** [[Petersen Dredge]] samples (Ekman dredge in pools), sieved and sorted, with wet weights used as a proxy for live weight.

The Red Cedar River's history of pollution and urban growth is central to the interpretation. Organic and nutrient loads from sewage and urban runoff, together with industrial metal wastes, shaped the biota of each zone.

## Key Findings

**Aufwuchs (periphyton)**

- Diatoms dominated the Aufwuchs. Peters (1959) recognized seven sessile diatom communities, including those associated with *Navicula cryptocephala*, *Cocconeis placentula*, *Gomphonema olivaceum*, *Diatoma hiemale*, *Synedra ulna*, *Cymbella tumida*, and *Cyclotella meneghiniana*.
- Phytopigment absorbancy correlated with organism counts at r = 0.90, 0.84, 0.75 and 0.86 (P < 0.01) across four stations.
- Production peaked in June. The highest value was 2,278 mg m⁻² day⁻¹ on 22 June 1958, and rates were lowest under winter ice cover. A sudden, unexplained decline occurred in August 1957.
- Production was highest in the reservoir (Zone IV) and lowest in the most downstream zone (Zone I). Overall production was remarkably uniform across very different environments.
- Riffle production exceeded pool production, although pools were colonized more quickly. Maximum fixation occurred in intermediate velocities (30.5–91 cm s⁻¹).
- Phosphorus fixation ranged from 383.2 µg P dm⁻² day⁻¹ (June 1958) to 0.7 µg P dm⁻² day⁻¹ (January 1958). The Williamston plant's daily load of 6,790 g P would require 7.9 km of stream to deplete at the maximum rate, but about 4,380 km at the winter minimum (as reported).
- Gross and net [[Efficiency Quotient]]s were lowest in winter (0.003% and 0.0007% in January 1958) and highest in June 1958 (0.42% and 0.61%).
- Diatom N/P ratios averaged 1.04 (range 0.63–1.47), lower than values in laboratory cultures, and net nitrogen efficiency ranged from 0.029% to 0.187%.

**Macrophytes**

- [[Vallisneria]] and [[Sagittaria]] were the most common macrophytes. Growth began in mid-May and lasted about 125 days. Maximum production occurred in late June and early July.
- Macrophyte stocks expanded strikingly during the study, from sparse distribution before 1958 to large beds by 1962–1964. Possible causes include reduced discharge, urban fertilization, and highway construction that filled deeper pools.
- Macrophyte production correlated with mean annual stream velocity across zones (r = 0.92, P < 0.05), and Zone IV's low production was linked in part to heavy siltation.

**Bottom fauna and insects**

- In 1958, [[Tendipedidae]] made up 32–66% of insects by number. In 1959, 35 insect families were recorded, and the study identified the family with the greatest "relative importance" (number × weight), Tendipedidae, followed by [[Elmidae]], [[Hexagenia]] (Ephemeridae) and Tipulidae.
- A one-way ANOVA found significant differences in family composition among zones (P < 0.05). The Tukey test placed the heterogeneity between Zones II and III, which the authors attributed to sewage effects.
- Herbivorous insects, carnivorous insects and tubificid worms were analysed for 1961. Mean standing crops for the whole reach were 0.954 g wet m⁻² (herbivores), 0.373 g wet m⁻² (carnivores) and 1.458 g wet m⁻² (tubificid worms).
- Insect standing crops fell below the plating plant outfall, where tubificid worms increased. A fish kill around 14 May 1961 affected about 10 km of stream after settling ponds flooded.
- Herbivore and carnivore standing crops were reduced for two or three kilometres below the sewage outfall. Molluscs were absent below the plating plant but peaked in Zone III.
- Excessive siltation in 1961 reduced insects and unionid clams. The authors also reported critically low nighttime dissolved oxygen, which stressed many organisms.

**Other invertebrates and fish**

- Crayfish (*Orconectes propinquus* and *O. immunis*) were present in all zones and more abundant downstream. Vannote (1963) estimated 43 g m⁻² in part of Zone II.
- A check list by Meehan (1958) recorded 10 families and 32 fish species. Linton and Ball (1965) reported that more desirable fish populations declined drastically in some reaches.

**Conclusions and management implications**

- The authors judged that portions of the river had reached an advanced state of degradation. Tendipedidae dominance is usually associated with domestic enrichment.
- Waste treatment upgrades, including Fowlerville's lagoon system and East Lansing's new plant, were necessary but insufficient. The authors called for a fundamental change in waste treatment philosophy to stop discharges into streams, warning that marginal Michigan trout streams could come to resemble the Red Cedar River.
