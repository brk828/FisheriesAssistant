# Survey Findings on the Lake George and St. Johns River Fisheries Investigations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956-era report by the Florida Game and Fresh Water Fish Commission, compiled under Chief of Fisheries [[Don Luethy]], assesses the fishery of [[Lake George]] and the adjoining [[St. Johns River]] (Volusia Bar to Buffalo Bluff). It combines multiple sampling methods (haul seine, otter trawl, [[Spot Poisoning]], gill nets), creel census and angling-pressure surveys, economic valuation, submerged vegetation mapping, and a law-enforcement and public-relations review. The central management problem is the illegal "[[Monkey Fishing]]" electrofishing practice, which has produced enforcement difficulties, adverse publicity, and a shift in angling pressure from Lake George to the river. The report concludes that the fishery has not changed significantly over three years apart from rough fish and catfish increases, and recommends a [[Rough Fish Control Program]] while keeping regulations unchanged and prioritizing enforcement and public relations.

## Background & Description

The survey was initiated to provide factual information for specific management recommendations and is framed as a continuing program. The St. Johns system is described as highly productive, supporting commercial fishing and a sport fishing reputation of national significance. The report explicitly argues that sport fishing values far exceed freshwater commercial fisheries, while also calling for balanced, lawful harvest by both sectors.

Lake George (Putnam, Volusia and Marion counties, North Central Florida) is the largest lake in the St. Johns River system. It covers about 46,550 acres (the document also cites 16,588 acres in one passage, an apparent typographical inconsistency), is 9 to 11 feet deep, has roughly 55 miles of shoreline, and is fed on the western side by springs including [[Juniper Spring]], [[Silver Glen Spring]] and Salt Spring. A U.S. Corps of Engineers channel crosses the lake.

The electrofishing devices, called "monkey machines," were typically a handcrank telephone generator driven by a small DC motor powered by a six-volt automobile battery, with output passed through a condenser to two extension-cord electrodes. Output was controlled by a resistance wire; the effect on catfish was most pronounced at about 60 rpm armature speed. The Commission's experimental unit, [[Spider I]], was designed for canals and streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Vegetation:** The dominant submerged plant was [[Vallisneria americana]] (eelgrass), concentrated at about two feet depth. No definite correlation was found between plant growth areas and soil type or physical-chemical data. Hyacinth and filamentous algae were also present.
- **Water levels:** Over an eleven-year record, maximum fluctuation was 2.51 feet and the average annual fluctuation was 1.79 feet. From May 11 to July 10, 1956, levels fluctuated 1.8 feet. Practically no tidal effect occurs in Lake George.
- **Water chemistry:** Waters were described as exceptionally productive. Dissolved oxygen, CO2, pH, conductivity (salt/chloride measures), and temperatures were recorded at many stations, but the report states that complete analysis was not possible. Plankton blooms caused a minor fish kill on one occasion. Water temperatures ranged roughly from 74 to 87 °F across sampling dates.
- **Conductivity:** Operations of monkey machines near game fish habitat did not appear to affect sport fishing success, but the report warns that natural conductivity varies greatly through the year.
- **Food and behavior:** Catfish schooling was linked to concentrations of food, particularly [[Chironomidae]] larvae and adults ("blind mosquitoes") and filamentous algae. Gizzard shad are a major component of the forage and gill-net catch.
- **Invertebrates:** Freshwater shrimp (Palaemonetes), crawfish (Procambarus), blue crab (Callinectes sapidus), grapsoid crab (Rhithropanopeus harrisi), and midge larvae were recorded.

## Key Findings

**1. Haul seine (July 1956, ten hauls, 225 acres)**
- Average catch of 128 lb per surface acre; over 60% was catfish. With transient catfish excluded, the catch was 50.2 lb per acre, in good agreement with past years.
- Channel catfish made up 45.7% by number and 60.5% by weight. Gizzard shad were about 17% by number and 16.6% by weight.
- Gizzard shad share of the scale-fish catch was 42% (excluding catfish), versus 60% on the east shore and 15% on the west shore.

**2. Otter trawl (30 hauls, May 1953 vs. May 1956)**
- Marine species rose from 16% to 62% by number and from 5% to 15% by weight.
- Catfish rose from 6% to 16% by number and from 16% to 62% by weight.
- Other rough fish rose from 33% to 77% by number.
- Game fish fell from 61% to 7% by number and from 76% to 22% by weight.

**3. Spot poisoning (twelve one-acre stations, June–July 1956, 3,619 fish, 459.7 lb)**
- Fish counts were nearly equal across depth bands (A, shoreline to 2 ft: 1,152; B, 2–4 ft: 1,298; C, 4–6 ft: 1,112), but weight was dominated by deeper water (C 51%, B 41%, A 8%).
- Game fish were 36% by number and 45% by weight of the sample; rough fish 64% by number.
- Combined trawl and spot data: game fish 21% by number and 33% by weight; a ratio of 1 game fish to 4 rough fish by number, and 1 lb game to 2 lb rough by weight.
- Estimated productivity of the littoral zone was about 38 lb of fish per surface acre.

**4. Gill nets (16 sets, April–May 1956)**
- Total catch 1,050.8 lb; gizzard shad were 78% of the catch. No earlier gill-net data existed for comparison.

**5. Creel census and angling pressure**
- Lake George (June 1953 vs. June 1956): effort fell about one-third; bass per man-hour and average bass weight rose; bream per man-hour fell while average bream weight rose.
- St. Johns River: effort increased, shifting from bass to bream; bream effort increased threefold.
- June 1956 pressure: 8,442 fisherman-days on Lake George and 9,768 on the St. Johns River to Buffalo Bluff.

**6. Economic evaluation**
- At an estimated $11.00 per fisherman-day, June 1956 sport fishing was valued at $92,862 (Lake George), $107,488 (St. Johns), totaling $200,310. Annualized, the figures would be $1,114,344 and $1,289,376, or $2,403,720 combined, if June were average (the report notes this is probably not valid).
- Fishing license sales in Putnam County were $30,183.25 (1955–56) and $20,398.72 (1953–54).
- Catfish landings in Putnam County: 1,906,459 lb ($322,625) in 1953; 1,298,949 lb ($259,525) in 1954; 3,454,671 lb ($552,746) in 1955.
- Commercial fishing contributes about one-third of fisheries revenue; sport fishing about two-thirds.

**7. Electrofishing trials ([[Monkey Fishing]] vs. Spider I)**
- Monkey machines captured catfish by driving them to the surface for dip-netting. Catfish fled the energized area, and avoidance lasted as long as operations continued, depending on their frequency.
- Tables I and II (boat tests, June–July 1956) recorded 38 fish in 35.5 hours of effort (1.1 fish/hour). Warmouth, stumpknocker, bluegill and redbreast were captured, contradicting the claim that the device affected only catfish. The authors nonetheless concluded there was no evidence it captured species other than catfish in its present form.
- Spider I captured about 1,000 fish per hour, with 300 adult bluegill taken for the Welaka hatchery (one died). Its selectivity and mortality were not recorded.

**8. Law enforcement**
- March 1–June 25, 1956: five officers logged 3,509 man-hours on monkey fishing (about 35 hours per day over 87 days).
- Extra equipment of $5,126.00 was purchased in 1955–56; an all-out patrol costs at least $36.00 per day.
- Since 1953 there were thirteen monkey fishing cases in Putnam County with only two convictions. About one hundred part- or full-time operators were estimated in the area.
- Evasion techniques (jettisoning machines, transfers between boats, substituting bricks) were common.

**9. Public relations**
- Adverse newspaper and national coverage created a negative perception of the fishery, which the report says will require sustained favorable publicity to reverse. Non-resident anglers were thought likely to avoid the area.

**10. Submerged vegetation**
- Vallisneria was dominant and mainly at two feet. The Corps of Engineers' Willow Cove gauge (1936–1950) provided past water-level data.
- The report notes that the major growth areas would probably be unaffected if fluctuations stay between 2.57 and 4.36 feet m.s.l. for less than three months.

**Conclusions and Recommendations**
- The fisheries have not changed significantly in three years, except for increases in rough fish and marine species. Length-weight relations indicated good condition, so no over- or under-harvest.
- Rough fish migration is intensive, so control in Lake George alone is unlikely to be lasting.
- Sport pressure on Lake George decreased, attributed to adverse publicity, and rose on the St. Johns River.
- Monkey fishing is "of no biological significance," but its social and economic effects are serious, and enforcement remains costly and inadequate.
- Recommendation 1: a rough fish control program using chemical and electrical methods, with controlled netting, designed with sports and commercial representatives. It should be acceptable to all factions, biologically sound, and nearly or completely self-supporting. The report notes it is currently impossible because of cost and public relations.
- Recommendation 2: keep rules and regulations unchanged, and place immediate emphasis on law enforcement and public relations.
