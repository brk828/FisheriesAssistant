# The American Cyprinid Fishes of the Subgenus Luxilus (Genus Notropis)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This revision by [[Carter R. Gilbert]] (Bulletin of the Florida State Museum, 1964) treats the cyprinid subgenus *Luxilus* ([[Genus Notropis]]), a group of shiners of clear, small to medium-sized streams across temperate North America east of the Rockies. The author recognizes three species groups containing 8 species and 1 subspecies, in contrast to the 6 species and 4 subspecies of the prevailing classification. The work integrates morphology with [[Zoogeography]], ecology and clinal variation, and proposes a [[Phylogeny]] centered on Pleistocene glacial history and [[Drainage Evolution]] in the Mississippi, Ohio and Great Lakes basins. Hybridization within the group is extensively documented.

## Background & Description

The subgenus has an unstable [[Nomenclatural History]]. [[David Starr Jordan]] moved species among genera and subgenera with little apparent justification, and [[Luxilus]] and [[Hypsilepis]] were sorted into synonymy and resurrection over many decades. The author notes that the Luxilus complex shows all stages of speciation, making it a useful model of evolutionary change.

Diagnostic features of the subgenus include anal rays usually 9, pectoral rays 14 to 17, lateral-line scales usually 39 to 41, pharyngeal teeth 2,4-4,2, a large oblique terminal mouth, and dark crescent-shaped streaks on the side of the body in most species. Counts were standardized using the methods of [[Hubbs and Lagler]] (1947), with an added anterior dorso-lateral scale count, and vertebrae were determined from X-rays.

The three species groups are:
- **coccogenis group:** [[Notropis coccogenis]] and [[Notropis zonistius]] (allopatric; Tennessee and Chattahoochee systems)
- **zonatus group:** [[Notropis zonatus]] and [[Notropis pilsbryi]] (Ozark region)
- **cornutus group:** [[Notropis cornutus]], [[Notropis albeolus]], [[Notropis chrysocephalus]] (subspecies *chrysocephalus* and *isolepis*) and [[Notropis cerasinus]]

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Members of the subgenus occupy clear, gravel- to rubble-bottomed streams with alternating pools and riffles, usually at the head or foot of riffles and rarely on riffles except during spawning. Most spawn over the nests of other nest-building cyprinids such as [[Hybopsis micropogon]] and [[Semotilus atromaculatus]]. Spawning typically occurs when water reaches about 64 °F or higher, and the timing of spawning differs between species and between warm lowland and cool headwater streams.

Environmental variation is substantial. [[Notropis cornutus]] tolerates turbid, warm prairie streams and dominates headwaters; [[Notropis chrysocephalus]] is more tolerant of warm, turbid conditions and has replaced cornutus across much of the Ohio Valley. Habitat modification (warming, siltation, dam construction) is reported to have caused local replacement and loss of populations.

Biogeographic history is central to the interpretation. The subgenus is hypothesized to have evolved in the [[Mississippi Basin]] during the early to middle Pliocene. The [[Teays River]] system, the [[Laurentian River]], [[Lake Agassiz]] and other glacial lakes are invoked to explain current distributions, including the disjunct relict populations of cornutus in the lower Kanawha River (West Virginia) and the White River (Indiana).

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomy:** The author proposes 8 species and 1 subspecies, reclassifying [[Notropis zonatus pilsbryi]] as a full species (*N. pilsbryi*), *N. c. frontalis* and *N. c. chrysocephalus* as species-level units, and *N. c. isolepis* as a subspecies of *N. chrysocephalus*. [[Notropis albeolus]] is retained as a species, although the author notes it is close to the species-subspecies boundary.
- **Species limits:** Evidence for treating cornutus and chrysocephalus as species includes a mosaic distribution pattern, lack of competition-driven intergradation, post-dam disappearance of chrysocephalus, differing temperature tolerances, and an estimated separation dating to the late Pliocene.
- **Nomenclature:** The neotypes of *Cyprinus cornutus* Mitchill and *Luxilus chrysocephalus* Rafinesque were designated, and a lectotype was fixed for several names including *Hypsilepis coccogenis* Cope and *Notropis megalops albeolus* Jordan. *Notropis brimleyi* is treated as a synonym of *N. coccogenis*.
- **Diagnostic counts:** Anterior dorso-lateral scale counts were most useful for separating forms (for example, mean 21.33 in *N. cornutus* versus 14.78 in *N. c. chrysocephalus* and 13.60 in *N. c. isolepis*; N = 2,546, 1,023 and 330 respectively).
- **Hybridization:** Hybrids are frequent. The most common are *N. chrysocephalus* × *N. cornutus*, *N. c. chrysocephalus* × *N. rubellus* and *N. cornutus* × *N. rubellus*. The author lists about 25 documented hybrid combinations. The cornutus × rubellus hybrid was described as *Notropis macdonaldi* by Jordan (1889).
- **Albeolus variation:** Specimens from the New and Roanoke drainages differ from those in the Neuse, Tar and Cape Fear systems in body form and vertebral counts (usually 40 versus 39), which the author interprets as two genetic races.
- **Zoogeography:** The [[Coccogenis Group]] likely evolved in the Tennessee system; zonistius reached the Chattahoochee via stream capture. The zonatus group arose in the Ozarks, and pilsbryi's presence in the Arkansas and Red rivers is ambiguous, possibly natural relict or introduced.
- **Range limits:** Cornutus's northern limit coincides with the 65 °F July isotherm and chrysocephalus's with the 70 °F July isotherm (Radforth 1944).
- **Life history:** Outten's (1957) study shows *N. coccogenis* matures at about 2 years and about 75 mm SL, with females increasingly predominant among older fish (66.2% in year two, 76% in year three). Little life-history data exist for zonistius, albeolus and isolepis.
- **Meristic summary:** The document's tables cover fin-ray, scale, vertebral, lateral-line and anal-ray counts, proportional measurements and snout angle for each form; these were used to build the species diagnostic keys.
- **Key:** A dichotomous key separates all eight species and subspecies using pigmentation, scale counts, tubercle pattern, body form and angle of mouth to top of head.
