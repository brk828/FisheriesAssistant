# Integrated Fisheries Management Plan for the Little Colorado River Watershed

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) technical report (Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program Technical Report 146, July 2001) presents an integrated plan for managing native and sportfish together at the scale of the [[Little Colorado River]] watershed. Its central premise is that native fish conservation and sportfishing, historically managed separately and often in conflict, should be planned as one system, using watershed-scale analysis. The plan divides the watershed's waters into management units, assigns each a management emphasis (native fish, sportfish, or unknown), lists desired fish assemblages, and flags potentially incompatible species pairs. A companion [[Geographic Information System]] (GIS) habitat modeling study supports the approach. The report also documents the watershed's history of settlement, overgrazing, dam building, and fish introductions that shaped current conditions.

## Background & Description

The Arizona Game and Fish Commission was given authority over wildlife management in 1929, and [[Wildlife 2000 Strategic Plan]] directs AGFD to develop watershed-based plans that minimize conflict between sportfish and native fish. Over 80 non-native fish species have been introduced into Arizona, compared with 32 considered native. Early management largely ignored native fish, and federal legislation (NEPA 1969, Endangered Species Act 1973, Clean Water Act) later afforded legal protection to declining species. A [[Section 7 Consultation]] found that predation by stocked sportfish and their progeny threatened federally listed species.

The Little Colorado River (LCR) watershed covers about 70,160 km² on the [[Colorado Plateau]], with elevations from 3,850 m (Mt. Humphreys) to 830 m at the confluence with the Colorado River. It supports about 850,000 angler days annually, worth more than $80 million, and contains 9 native fish species, four federally listed under the ESA. Historical accounts from 1851 to 1898 describe a formerly perennial, cottonwood- and willow-lined river that later became a wide, sandy, often dry channel.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Climate and hydrology:** Elevation is the dominant climate control. Mean annual precipitation exceeds 75 cm at high elevations but falls to 15 cm or less on the plateau. Evaporation exceeds 102 cm in higher mountains. Winter snowmelt sustains perennial flow.
- **Water use:** Estimated cultural depletions average 91,480 ac-ft annually, with natural losses of 50,720 ac-ft. Many diversion dams, from early Mormon structures to about 80 reservoirs, alter stream hydrographs.
- **Overgrazing:** Cattle and sheep numbers surged in the 1880s. The Aztec Land and Cattle Company ran about 60,000 head. A drought in the 1890s killed thousands of cattle, and range deterioration followed. Riparian cottonwood stands were lost and channels widened.
- **Current condition:** Lower LCR reaches are wide, intermittent and dominated by salt cedar. Restoration is expected to take decades or longer.
- **Sport and native fish history:** Native trout were abundant in the late 1800s but were depleted by unrestricted harvest by the 1920s. Stocking of non-native trout followed from 1917 onward. Stocking of rainbow and cutthroat trout began in the 1930s through the South Fork hatchery.
- **Native species:** Little Colorado sucker (CASP), flannelmouth sucker (CALA), roundtail chub (GIRO), humpback chub (GICY), Little Colorado spinedace (LEVI), Apache trout (ONAP), bluehead sucker (PADI), speckled dace (RHOS), and razorback sucker (XYTE). Four are federally listed.
- **Non-native species:** Table 1.2 lists 25 non-native species present in the watershed, including rainbow trout, brown trout, brook trout, largemouth bass, channel catfish, common carp and fathead minnow.

## Key Findings

- **Watershed approach:** AGFD chose the LCR watershed as the first to pilot integrated, watershed-based fisheries management. Two competing approaches (Integrated Plan and Watershed Plan) were reviewed, and the final plan drew on both. A [[Five-Year Review]] cycle is planned.
- **Management units:** The plan delineates 98 lake units and 154 stream units. About 1,534.8 miles (2,468.2 km) of stream were analysed, averaging 9.9 miles per unit. Stream emphasis is 53.6% unknown, 42.0% native fish and 4.4% sportfish. Lake emphasis is 52 units (6,432 surface acres) for sportfish, 3 units (5 acres) for native fish, and 14 units (813 acres) unknown.
- **Native fish emphasis:** Of stream miles with a native fish emphasis, 73.1% are intermittent perennial, meaning actual fish habitat is probably smaller than mapped.
- **Recovery goals:** For Little Colorado spinedace, the plan maintains existing populations in East Clear Creek, Chevelon Creek, Nutrioso and Rudd Creeks, and Silver Creek, and proposes refugia and repatriation sites. Speckled dace, bluehead sucker and Little Colorado sucker are managed for no net loss, with repatriation where they were poisoned out.
- **Species conflicts:** Database searches identified many potentially incompatible pairs, such as Apache trout with rainbow trout, and Little Colorado spinedace with brook trout or smallmouth bass. Mitigation factors include stocking timing, barriers, renovation, and special regulations.
- **Habitat models:** Cell-based GIS models using Strahler stream order, link slope, elevation and stream type were built from 804 sample sites (27,949 records). Speckled dace occurred at 330 sites, the most widespread native fish. Little Colorado sucker occurred at 90 sites, and Little Colorado spinedace at 85. Apache trout and roundtail chub were too rare (26 and 8 sites) for models.
- **Sampling limits:** Sample sites were clumped and non-random, and the stream type and DEM data had known errors, so models are described as "liberal internally, but conservative externally."
- **Data gaps:** Angler needs could not be quantified at watershed scale, and angler demand was assumed to be met. Invertebrates and other aquatic taxa were poorly documented.
- **Recommendations:** Standardize collection protocols and databases, record locations as UTM coordinates, improve the intermittent-perennial hydrology layer, adopt "adopt a stream" programs, and actively participate in watershed associations such as the Verde Watershed Association. The plan is to be reviewed every five years.
