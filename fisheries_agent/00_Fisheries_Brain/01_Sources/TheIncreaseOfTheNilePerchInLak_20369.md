# Nile Perch Increase in Lake Victoria: Fishery Status and HEST/TAFIRI Research Program (1987)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centropomidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled report, produced by the [[Haplochromis Ecology Survey Team]] (HEST) and the [[Tanzanian Fisheries Research Institute]] (TAFIRI) with the Zoological Laboratory of Leiden University (1987), documents how the introduced [[Nile Perch]] (*Lates niloticus*) has reshaped the fisheries of [[Lake Victoria]]. The Nile perch, introduced in the late 1950s to early 1960s, increased exponentially from about 1980 and now supports the most important fishery in the lake. Over the same period the [[Haplochromine Cichlids]], which formerly made up more than 80% of the fish stock, declined sharply or disappeared in several areas. The authors attribute this decline mainly to predation by Nile perch rather than to fishing, and they advise caution with high, long-term investment because the ecosystem has not stabilised. The document comprises five chapters: a summary of the fishery impact, a comparison of haplochromine and Nile perch trends, a trawl survey comparison of 1969/70 and 1984/85, a description of the artisanal Nile perch fishery, and the 1987-1988 research programme.

## Background & Description

The Nile perch is a large piscivorous centropomid. Its introduction was debated through the 1950s, yet it appeared in the lake in the mid-1950s to early 1960s, with official introductions in the early 1960s. The lake's original fauna included about 17 exploited fish species or groups, with tilapiines as the main target. Haplochromine cichlids number more than 250 species and exploit nearly all lake niches.

Key species referenced:
- [[Oreochromis niloticus]] and [[Rastrineobola Argentea]] (dagaa): reported to coexist with Nile perch and sustain fisheries.
- [[Caridina Nilotica]]: a benthic shrimp that became the main food of Nile perch after haplochromines declined.
- [[Protopterus aethiopicus]], [[Bagrus docmac]], [[Clarias mossambicus]], and [[Schilbe mystus]]: formerly important table fish that declined in catch rate, with the exception of Bagrus in deep water.

Survey and fishing gear reference points include the R.V. Kiboko (105 hp trawler, 18.5 m headrope, 19 mm codend, 3 knots), used throughout the Mwanza Gulf surveys. Prior trawlers included R.V. Ibis (1969/70) and R.V. Mdiria (1972-1977).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors state that the ecosystem has changed from complex to simpler, but it is not yet stable, so firm conclusions on long-term ecological and fishery consequences cannot be drawn. The intrusion of Nile perch is described as reversing depth distribution: haplochromines are concentrated in shallow water, while Nile perch are most abundant in deeper water (16-35 m, with catches to 57 m).

Affected areas and sites:
- [[Nyanza Gulf]] (Kenya): Nile perch rose from virtually zero to more than 20,000 t per year between 1979 and 1982; by 1987 it made up nearly 100% of fish biomass.
- [[Mwanza Gulf]] (Tanzania): Nile perch increased strongly from 1982 onwards; catches were still low in 1985 but were expected to grow.
- [[Emin Pasha Gulf]]: haplochromines fell by about 80% in one year as Nile perch increased, with no fishery interference.
- [[Lake Kyoga]]: Nile perch was introduced in 1955. Its catches later dropped to about 20% of the total, while Oreochromis rose to nearly 80%.

The Nile perch diet shifted from haplochromines to Caridina shrimp. Stomachs of fish up to 70 cm contained more than 50% Caridina.

## Key Findings

**Impact on haplochromines and the ecosystem**
- Haplochromine stock, about 80-82% of total fish biomass, decreased sharply. The authors conclude that the effect of Nile perch is "orders of magnitude larger" than that of fishing.
- Haplochromine catch rate in Mwanza Gulf (R.V. Mdiria, 2-12 m) fell from 1200-2200 kg/h in the mid-1970s to 600-700 kg/h in 1982, a decline of about 120 kg/h per year.
- R.V. Kiboko catches in the Mwanza Gulf (shallow water) fell from about 400 kg/h to 200 kg/h within one year (1984-85).
- Piscivorous haplochromine species: about 45 were common in 1977-78; only about 5 remained by 1987.

**Fishery changes**
- Commercial haplochromine trawling was suspended, and the fishmeal factory no longer relied on this stock. Trawlers in Mwanza area caught an estimated 5,200 t of haplochromines per year in 1981-83.
- Fishermen have concentrated on Nile perch, which is now the most important and profitable fishery.
- Gillnet mesh sizes in the Nile perch fishery range from 6-16 inches. Improvised materials such as tyre wire, split nylon and polyethylene bag fibres are used because proper gear is lacking.
- Kenya has a small fillet export industry.

**Artisanal fishery survey (Mwanza area)**
- Of 118 canoes met, 62 (52.4%) were used by specialised Nile perch fishermen: 61 gillnet and 1 beach seine. In a sample of 80 interviewed canoes, 67 (84%) used gillnets, 5 (7.5%) beach seines and 7 (8.5%) long lines.
- Mean catch per boat landing: gillnet at Busisi 40 kg, at Juma Island 150 kg, and about 67-87 kg at other sites; the beach seine weighted mean was 237 kg, with about 900 kg from the large Semba seine; long lines averaged about 32 kg.
- Nile perch length in gill net catches ranged from 55 to 90 cm, with a mean of 65-75 cm.

**Stock status and prospects**
- Preliminary indications suggest trawl catches at depths greater than 50 m are too low for commercial fishing.
- Investors are advised to favour short-term over long-term investment because the lake's stocks are changing and future prospects are uncertain.

**Research programme 1987-1988 (HEST/TAFIRI)**
- Monitoring of six Mwanza Gulf stations (2-57 m), with the codend increased to 40 or 60 mm to better sample large Nile perch.
- Exploratory trawl surveys of Bukoba, north-east Ukerewe, Musoma/Shirati and Emin Pasha Gulf, with about 50 trawl shots per trip and roughly 20 weeks of surveys per year.
- Catch-effort data collection, a tagging program (target of about 6,000 Nile perch, with about 100 tagged per day), gillnet selectivity studies for 5-10 inch meshes, and biological studies on growth, mortality and cannibalism.
- Zooplanktivore studies (haplochromines, juvenile Nile perch and dagaa), oxygen and limnological monitoring, and studies of Caridina stock and Nile perch stomach contents.
