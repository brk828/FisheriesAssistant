# Research on Little Colorado River Humpback Chub Population, May 1988

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #population-ecology

## Executive Summary

This report documents intensive field research conducted during May 1988 on the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) population in the [[Little Colorado River]], Arizona. The study employed multiple netting methods (trammel nets, hoop nets, fyke nets, seines, drift nets) operated continuously over 28 days to mark, recapture, and monitor this endangered species. A total of 1,082 [[Humpback Chub]] were captured, with 928 individuals tagged using colored fingerling tags and 150 recaptures recorded (16.1% recapture rate). The research documented spawning condition, collected thermal regulation data on fry, examined movement patterns, and assessed the broader fish community. Four native species and five non-native species were encountered, with [[Humpback Chub]] being the most abundant native fish. Flooding prevented direct spawning observations, but extensive secondary sexual characteristics and large numbers of fry indicated active reproduction.

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) is an endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin. This study focused on the population inhabiting the [[Little Colorado River]], a critical spawning and rearing habitat. Research objectives included: (1) observing and documenting spawning activities, (2) conducting thermal regulation studies on fry, (3) performing mark-recapture studies for population estimation, and (4) collecting biological data on growth, movement, and longevity.

All [[Humpback Chub]] ≥150 mm total length were tagged with maroon or yellow colored fingerling tags inserted below the dorsal fin. Smaller individuals (<150 mm) received pelvic fin clips starting the second week of May. Morphometric data (total length to nearest mm, weight to nearest gram), sex determination, and reproductive condition (tuberculation, gravidity, milt production, breeding coloration) were recorded for all captures. Sampling occurred from the confluence with the [[Colorado River]] upstream approximately 7.2 km (4.5 miles) to above Sepapua.

The fish community included four native species: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*). Five non-native species were present: [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucus*), and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Little Colorado River]] provides critical habitat for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], serving as one of the few remaining spawning areas for this species. Water temperatures during the study ranged from 14°C to 25°C, with most captures occurring at 17-23°C. The study area extended from the confluence with the [[Colorado River]] (river mile 61.5) upstream through various habitat types including channels, pools, backwaters, and travertine formations.

Flooding during May 1988 prevented direct observation of spawning behavior, as occurred in 1987. However, the presence of tuberculate males, gravid females, and an estimated 250-300 larval fish indicated successful reproduction. Spawning appeared to occur throughout the study reach, with concentrations near the confluence and at railroad tie rock (598 m upstream).

Habitat characteristics varied considerably along the study reach. The confluence area featured complex channel morphology with islands and ledges. Upstream areas included pools, riffles, and backwaters with varying substrate types. Cladophora (green algae) was noted in several locations, providing cover and food resources.

Predation pressure from non-native [[Channel Catfish]] was documented, with one catfish stomach containing a dried juvenile [[Humpback Chub]] and another containing an 85 mm [[Speckled Dace]]. Several [[Humpback Chub]] showed catfish bite scars, particularly on the hump region. Competition and predation from non-native species represent ongoing threats to native fish populations.

## Key Findings

**Population and Sampling Effort:**
- Total of 1,890 fish captured representing 9 species
- 268 net-nights of sampling effort over 28 days (May 2-29, 1988)
- 1,082 [[Humpback Chub]] captured (57% of all fish)
- 928 [[Humpback Chub]] newly tagged; 150 recaptures (16.1% recapture rate)
- 32 [[Humpback Chub]] recaptured bearing tags from previous studies (Arizona Game and Fish, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, or earlier MNA studies)
- Only 2 mortalities directly attributable to sampling (0.18% mortality rate)

**Size and Demographics:**
- [[Humpback Chub]] total lengths ranged from 85 mm to 544 mm
- Weights ranged from 5 g to 702 g
- Large adults (>400 mm) were common, with multiple individuals >500 mm
- Substantial recruitment evident from presence of 250-300 larval/fry fish
- Size distribution suggests multiple age classes present

**Reproductive Condition:**
- Majority of adults exhibited secondary sexual characteristics
- Males showed tuberculation (light to highly tuberculate), breeding coloration (particularly in pectoral fins), and many were running milt
- Females ranged from gravid to spent to flaccid condition
- Peak spawning activity inferred to have occurred mid-May based on progression of reproductive conditions
- Only one female successfully stripped of eggs despite many gravid individuals
- Breeding colors most pronounced in males captured at railroad tie rock (598 m) and upstream locations

**Spatial Distribution:**
- [[Humpback Chub]] captured throughout study reach from confluence to 7.2 km upstream
- Highest capture rates at: confluence area, 100 m station, railroad tie rock (598 m), and 1,037 m station
- Drift net at 500 m from confluence captured multiple adults, indicating active movement
- Green gill net drifts at 5.6-7.2 km upstream captured adults in breeding condition, confirming upstream spawning areas

**Movement and Recaptures:**
- 16.1% recapture rate indicates relatively sedentary population or high site fidelity during spawning
- Some individuals recaptured multiple times at same locations
- Tag returns from previous years document multi-year survival and site fidelity
- Movement between net locations documented but most recaptures occurred at or near original capture site

**Physical Condition and Anomalies:**
- Catfish bite scars documented on multiple individuals, particularly on hump region
- Travertine scars noted on several fish
- Fin damage (abraded, torn, or missing caudal fin portions) observed
- One individual blind in left eye; another missing right eye
- Scoliosis documented in one individual
- Old wounds and scars suggest survival of predation attempts

**Thermal Ecology:**
- Estimated 250-300 larval fish collected for thermal tolerance experiments
- Larvae encountered primarily during last week of May
- Water temperatures during larval presence: 22-25°C
- Adult captures occurred across temperature range of 14-25°C

**Community Composition:**
- Native species in order of abundance: [[Humpback Chub]] (1,082), [[Speckled Dace]] (268), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (258), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (172)
- Non-native species: [[Channel Catfish]] (29), [[Fathead Minnow]] (10), [[Rainbow Trout]] (4), [[Carp]] (3), [[Golden Shiner]] (1)
- All native species showed evidence of spawning
- [[Carp]] spawning actively 500 m upstream from confluence
- Some [[Channel Catfish]] gravid; predation on native fish documented

**Methodological Notes:**
- Multiple gear types necessary to sample full size range and habitat types
- Hoop nets and fyke nets most effective for adults
- Trammel nets effective but higher mortality risk
- Seine hauls and larval nets essential for documenting recruitment
- Continuous 24-hour sampling captured diel activity patterns
- Tag retention appeared excellent with no reports of tag loss affecting recapture identification
