# Spikedace and Loach Minnow Interaction Study with Red Shiner - 1987-1988 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #threatened-species #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This document is the 1987-1988 annual report for a multi-faceted investigation contracted by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Arizona Game and Fish Department, and New Mexico Department of Fish and Game to Arizona State University. The study evaluates interactions between the invasive [[Red Shiner]] (Notropis lutrensis) and two threatened native minnows: [[Spikedace]] (Meda fulgida) and [[Loach Minnow]] (Tiaroga cobitis) in the [[Gila River]] system. Both native species are listed as threatened due to habitat modification and interactions with non-native fishes. The first-year research focused on quantifying differential microhabitat occupation and assessing potential food-resource overlap through dietary analysis.

## Background & Description

[[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] are stream-dwelling minnows endemic to the [[Gila River]] system of Arizona and New Mexico. Both species are listed as threatened by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS 1986a, b) and recognized as imperiled by Arizona (AZGFD 1985) and New Mexico (New Mexico Game Commission 1985). [[Loach Minnow]] is less well-studied than [[Spikedace]], largely due to its restricted microhabitat distribution and cryptic nature.

[[Red Shiner]] is an eastern minnow introduced to streams of the desert southwest in the early 1950s (Hubbs 1954, Miller 1961). Since initial plantings, the species has spread to colonize most low-elevation streams of the region (Minckley 1973, 1985), including habitats occupied by [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. Progressive declines in native minnows have been noted as [[Red Shiner]] invaded upstream in the [[Gila River]] basin (Minckley and Deacon 1968). However, the specific mechanisms of interaction remain undemonstrated.

Interaction between native and non-native fishes has long been implicated in declines of native species (Miller 1941, Minckley and Deacon 1968), but the nature and mechanisms of such relationships have received little quantitative attention. Predation constitutes direct negative impact, but more subtle forms of competition may also effect reductions and eventual extirpation of local populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was conducted in multiple stream systems representing different species composition scenarios:
- [[Aravaipa Creek]]: [[Spikedace]] plus [[Loach Minnow]]
- [[Sycamore Creek]]: [[Red Shiner]] only
- [[Verde River]]: [[Spikedace]] plus [[Red Shiner]]
- [[Blue River]], Arizona: [[Loach Minnow]]
- [[Gila River]], New Mexico: [[Spikedace]] plus [[Red Shiner]]

Study reaches were designated to evaluate longitudinal changes in habitat and target species composition. Physical habitat measurements included current velocity, stream depth, cover (algae, macrophytes, rock), shading (riparian vegetation, canyon wall), and substrate composition (silt, sand, gravel, cobble, boulder, bedrock, organic material).

Benthic macroinvertebrate communities were sampled qualitatively using 3 replicate kick samples, each covering approximately 1 m², with 1 x 1 m, 1.0 mm-mesh nets. Aquatic drift was assessed quantitatively from paired diel samples with 0.250 mm-mesh nets set minimally at noon and midnight, including crepuscular periods.

## Key Findings

The first year of work (March-June 1987) included:

1. **Field Studies Design**: Standardized seine sampling protocols were established to consistently sample fish communities, with identity, number, and size class (larva, juvenile, adult) recorded for all fishes encountered.

2. **Microhabitat Assessment**: Physical measurements were systematically collected at five points within each seined area to characterize microhabitat conditions where native species occur allopatric versus sympatric with [[Red Shiner]].

3. **Dietary Analysis Framework**: Specimens were preserved for length frequency and dietary analyses to assess potential food-resource overlap among the three species.

4. **Diel Feeding Patterns**: Target fish species were sampled in concert with drift netting to assess diel patterns of feeding behavior.

5. **Research Justification**: The study addresses a critical knowledge gap, as effective management toward recovery of native species cannot be implemented without understanding the relationships between [[Red Shiner]] and threatened native minnows. Although circumstantial evidence indicates [[Red Shiner]] has been responsible for declines of [[Spikedace]], research has yet to demonstrate the actual process.

6. **Conservation Context**: Both [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] have experienced habitat modification and interaction with non-native fishes as primary threats. [[Red Shiner]] has recently spread into New Mexico where substantial, uncontaminated populations of the natives persist, making this research timely for conservation management.
