# Fisheries Investigations in the Central Arizona Project Canal System, 1986–1989

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation final report (1986–1989, by Gordon Mueller, Regional Limnologist, Division of Environment, Boulder City, Nevada) documents the fishery that developed in the main canal of the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) after Colorado River water was pumped from [[Lake Havasu]]. Across the four years, about 11,000 fish of 17 species were collected or observed. Most fish entered as eggs and larvae entrained from Lake Havasu. The canal fish community was initially dominated by [[Threadfin Shad]] and sunfish, then shifted toward [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Common Carp]] and [[Bluegill]] as shad collapsed. Catch rates and biomass fell sharply as diversions increased, and the report concludes that fish can pass through pumping plants and that the canal supports limited reproduction but little recruitment.

## Background & Description

The CAP was authorized in 1968 to divert Colorado River water 521 km (324 miles) to central Arizona. Reclamation led the study with the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), Arizona State University (ASU), the Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) and the Central Arizona Water Conservation District (CAWCD). Water is lifted 251 m (824 ft) by the [[Havasu Pumping Plant]] (HPP) and conveyed through an 11.3 km (7 mile) tunnel under the Buckskin Mountains. The main conveyance is divided into the [[Hayden-Rhodes Aqueduct]], Salt-Gila Aqueduct and Tucson Aqueduct.

Reach 1 of the Hayden-Rhodes Aqueduct is the first 27.4 km (17 miles) of open canal, designed to store about 4.9 million m³ (4,000 acre-feet). Its bottom width is 7.3 m (24 ft) for the first 1,690 m, then 24.4 m (80 ft), with a maximum depth of 5.5 m (18 ft) and side slopes of 14:1. Reach 1 ends at the [[Bouse Hills Pumping Plant]] (BHPP). Full-operation discharge is 85 m³/s (3,000 ft³/s).

Diversions rose from 107,636 af in 1986 to 762,403 af in 1989 (Table 1). The average planned diversion is 1.1 million af per year. Diversions are seasonal, peaking in summer, and will change when the New Waddell reservoir is completed in 1993.

Sampling expanded from semi-annual visits to BHPP and LHPP in 1986 to a quarterly schedule in 1988 and a trimester schedule in 1989. Sampling included the pumping plant forebays and the adjacent canal reaches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish entry and passage.** Entrainment from Lake Havasu was the main source of fish. Larval entrainment averaged 1.13 larvae/m³ in 1987 and 0.88 larvae/m³ in 1988 (as reported in the Summary). Larval composition entering the canal in April–June was roughly 72–86% Clupeidae, 4–9% Cyprinidae and 10–17% Centrarchidae. Some species, including the Sonora and Desert suckers, goldfish and largemouth bass, are attributed to "bait bucket" introductions. Adult passage at HPP and HSPP was low, at about 2 fish per hour.

**Habitat.** The canal is a homogeneous, concrete-lined habitat with little cover. The report links this to rapid predator–prey collapses, similar to the bottle experiments of [[Gause Competition]].

**Water quality.** Measurements across stations recorded temperatures of 9.1–29.9°C, dissolved oxygen of 6.0–12 mg/l, pH of 7.3–10 and conductivity of 820–946 µmhos/cm. Oxygen fell to 0.57–1.27 mg/l only during the LHPP forebay dewatering.

**Invasive and nuisance biota.** [[Corbicula]] (Asiatic clam) increased in the upper canal. Reach 1 was estimated to hold about 3,900 metric tons (4,300 US tons). Aquatic weeds, including sago pondweed, curlyleaf pondweed and Cladophora, fouled trash racks and became a full-time maintenance problem in 1989.

## Key Findings

**Species and community**
- 17 species were recorded, including goldfish observed by divers but not collected. Ten species were collected in 1986–1987, four more in 1988, one in 1989 and one in 1990.
- Electrofishing provided about 72% of the catch, gill netting 10%, angling 8% and canal drainage 10%.
- Threadfin shad made up 99% of the early fish community. By 1989 shad contributed only 7.7% of the electrofishing/blocking-net sample. Adult shad disappeared from Reach 1 by fall 1987 and were not collected there again during the 30-month remainder of the study.

**Abundance decline**
- Electrofishing catch per minute peaked at 8.6 fish/min in 1987 and fell to 1.0 fish/min in 1989 (−88%). Biomass fell from 788 to 166 g/min (−79%).
- Standardized gill netting fell from 27.3 to 2.5 fish per 305 m² per 12-hour night (−91%). Biomass fell 49%.
- Reach 1 population estimates (excluding shad) dropped from 0.0126 to 0.0017 fish/m³ (−87%). Biomass fell from 8.35 to 7.06 g/m³ (−15%). In 1989 carp made up nearly 95% of the biomass.

**Striped bass**
- About 800 striped bass were taken from the system over four years. Only 15 (2%) were collected downstream of Reach 1.
- Stomach analyses (103 fish: 31 in 1987, 72 in 1989) showed a diet broader than piscivory, including snails, clams, algae, detritus and carp eggs. One fish contained about 1,400 carp eggs.
- Tagged striped bass gained weight at about +1.0% per month in 1988, while tagged largemouth bass lost about 1.7% per month.
- The report suggests thermal stress may limit adult striped bass downstream, since temperatures above about 26–29°C are lethal to adults.

**Reproduction**
- Threadfin shad, carp and sunfish spawned in the canal in 1987 and 1988. Carp showed significant larval increases between Havasu and BHPP.
- The two-year [[Ichthyoplankton]] effort collected five striped bass eggs and no striped bass larvae. Two razorback sucker larvae were also found.
- Scuba divers filmed bluegill and largemouth bass spawning in the HSPP and BHPP forebays in May 1988.

**Fish passage**
- At HSPP in April–July 1988, a net sieving one-third of discharge over 10 sets (120 hours) collected 79 fish. Of these, about 95% were dead. The estimated total passage was 237 fish, about 2 fish per hour.
- Fish under about 30 cm can pass through the pumps. A 29 cm channel catfish survived a passage test. Larger carp, striped bass and razorback suckers were also collected, which the report attributes to unauthorized stocking or to fish passing through idle turbines.

**Management and future conditions**
- Density and biomass are expected to keep declining as diversions increase. Carp, striped bass and channel catfish are expected to tolerate higher velocities best.
- Future winter diversions, with the New Waddell reservoir, may change entrainment timing and rates.
- Angling demand was estimated at 43,000 angler-days per year, worth about $170,000 per year. CAWCD opposition postponed a 1989 proposal for limited angler access. The canal is currently fenced and closed to public access, and trespass angling occurs.
- Options discussed include fish salvage during drawdowns, cage culture of striped bass, grow-out of razorback suckers and seasonal stocking of trout or catfish. Aquatic weed control and public safety must be resolved before angling is allowed.
- Reclamation planned a Resource Management Plan for CAP after fiscal year 1993.
