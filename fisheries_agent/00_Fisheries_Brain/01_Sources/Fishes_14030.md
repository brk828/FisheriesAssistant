# Virginia Freshwater Fishes: Status, Distribution and Conservation Recommendations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This chapter, from Burkhead and Jenkins' treatment of Virginia's endangered species (1991), reviews the state's freshwater and euryhaline ichthyofauna. Virginia supports 210 species (230 taxa including subspecies), of which 192 are native. The authors assess the conservation status of imperiled fishes, recommending endangered, threatened or special concern status for 36 species, and listing one extinct, six extirpated and two "status undetermined" taxa. Silt is identified as the single most pervasive threat, and the authors conclude that Virginia's fish faunas have shown little recovery over the past two decades, apart from improvements in the North Fork Holston and Clinch river systems.

## Background & Description

- **Fauna size:** 210 freshwater or euryhaline species; 230 taxa when subspecies are included. Of these, 192 are native, five probably native, and 13 clearly introduced.
- **Euryhaline and migratory fishes:** 11 euryhaline species, ten of which are diadromous: nine [[Anadromy|anadromous]] species and the [[American Eel]], which is catadromous. The [[Banded Killifish]] is the only nonmigratory euryhaline species.
- **Comparative richness:** Virginia exceeds Maryland (107 species) but is far less diverse than Tennessee (about 307 species).
- **Physiographic setting:** Drainages cross the Coastal Plain, Piedmont, Blue Ridge, Ridge and Valley, and Appalachian Plateaus, with the [[Fall Line]] marking the boundary between the Piedmont and Coastal Plain.
- **Cold-water definition:** Streams not normally exceeding 20–21°C in summer.
- **Chesapeake Bay system:** About 174 marine, 24 estuarine and 11 diadromous species, plus 77 freshwater species that enter tidal fresh or brackish water; 286 species in total.
- **Lakes:** Only two natural lakes exist, Mountain Lake and Lake Drummond, both depauperate in fishes.
- **Terminology:** "Creek" and "river" overlap biologically; creeks average about 20 m wide and rivers range from about 15 m to several kilometres in width.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeography.** Drainage fish faunas are distinctive and shaped by physiographic divides, stream piracy, and sea-level change during glaciations, which connected Chesapeake Bay tributaries into an enlarged [[Susquehanna River]] system. Many species are endemic to single drainages, and the [[Tennessee River Drainage]] is the most species-rich and most impacted region.

**Faunal decline.** Virginia has lost seven native species since [[Edward Drinker Cope]] surveyed the state in 1867. Of 217 native freshwater species and subspecies, 45 (about 21%) are imperiled, extirpated or extinct. The 1989 assessment gave the following counts:
- Endangered: 8
- Threatened: 10
- Special concern: 18
- Extirpated: 6
- Extinct: 1
- Status undetermined: 2

**Taxonomic breakdown.** Ten of 24 families are represented. The most affected are Percidae (18 species, 17 of them darters), Cyprinidae (12 minnows), Ictaluridae (four madtoms), Catostomidae (three suckers) and Centrarchidae (two sunfishes).

**Principal threats.** Causes of decline are cumulative and chronic, including:
- [[Siltation]], described as the most pervasive limiting factor; 36 of the 45 listed species are benthic.
- Turbidity, which impairs sight-feeding fishes and plant productivity.
- [[Impoundment]] and thermal alteration of tailwaters.
- Chemical spills, such as the 1967 Clinch River kill.
- Water withdrawal, channelization, [[Acid Rain]] and coal-mining effluents.

Large rivers are disproportionately affected, since they carry more pollutants and are more heavily developed.

**Chesapeake and Roanoke basins.** Sturgeon populations are depleted in Chesapeake estuaries. The Roanoke and Chowan drainages (Albemarle Sound basin) hold ten listed species and the highest endemism among Atlantic slope drainages examined.

## Key Findings

**1. Status recommendations (1990 list)**
- 36 species were recommended for endangered, threatened or special concern status, with one extinct, six extirpated, and two status undetermined.
- Changes from the 1979 list: 34 status changes in total, with 9 upgrades, 18 downgrades and 7 shifts to or from status undetermined. Eighteen species were added or deleted.
- Of 53 taxa considered in both lists, 31 remain after removing four that became extinct or extirpated; 15 of these did not change status.

**2. Notable species accounts**
- **Shortnose sturgeon** (*Acipenser brevirostrum*): federally and state endangered; not recorded in Virginia for more than 100 years and recommended as extirpated, with endangered status retained because recovery is possible.
- **Atlantic sturgeon** (*Acipenser oxyrhynchus oxyrhynchus*): downgraded from threatened to special concern. Chesapeake Bay landings fell from 329,329 kg in 1890 to 10,386 kg in 1920.
- **Paddlefish** (*Polyodon spathula*): restricted to the lower Clinch and Powell rivers; only 11 specimens recorded in Virginia from 1960 to 1985; recommended threatened.
- **Roanoke logperch** (*Percina rex*): endemic to Virginia's Roanoke and Chowan drainages; federally endangered (1989) and recommended endangered in the state.
- **Duskytail darter** (*Etheostoma* sp.): restricted to Copper Creek, one of six relict populations; upgraded to endangered.
- **Yellowfin madtom** (*Noturus flavipinnis*): isolated in Copper Creek; federally threatened, recommended endangered.
- **Turquoise shiner** (*Cyprinella monacha*): endangered; recolonising the middle North Fork Holston River after pollution abatement; the Middle Fork population was discovered in 1984.
- **Slender chub** (*Erimystax cahni*): endangered; known from only two Powell River sites; federally threatened.
- **Tennessee dace** (*Phoxinus tennesseensis*): endangered; only two isolated headwater populations; recently described (1988).
- **Speckled darter** (*Etheostoma stigmaeum jessiae*): status undetermined, possibly extirpated.
- **Spotted margined madtom** (*Noturus insignis*, undescribed subspecies): status undetermined; localised in the Dan River below the Pinnacles Hydroelectric Project, where dewatering and silt trapping affect habitat.
- **Blackbanded sunfish** (*Enneacanthus chaetodon*): endangered (state), known from only three localised Chowan drainage populations.

**3. Recovery trends**
- Recovery has occurred in the North Fork Holston and Clinch rivers following abatement of chronic chemical pollution and the absence of major fish kills for about 20 years; seven species showed clear recovery but retained their status.
- Little improvement is evident elsewhere; the authors state there is no evidence of substantial improvement in the past 20 years beyond these abatements.

**4. Recommendations**
- Long-term abatement of silt is the single most important requirement for rehabilitating Virginia's aquatic ecosystems.
- Map distributions of imperiled fishes chronologically to track decline, stasis and recovery.
- Retain the status criteria used in the assessment.
- Inventory threatened and endangered fishes every five years, and the most imperiled species (for example the slender chub and sharphead darter) annually.
- Protect critical habitats, including the North Fork Holston River and Copper Creek, and ban harvest of protected species such as the slender chub and orangefin madtom.
- Restrict bait seining and bait fishing in threatened streams such as Bear and Lick creeks.
- Control agricultural and riparian erosion, and review discharge permits in the upper James and Roanoke rivers.

**5. Data base**
- More than 10,000 collections have been recorded since 1867, about 7,800 of them from 1968 to 1989; the Middle James drainage is the least sampled.
- Records were integrated into the BOVA data bank in 1984–1985.
