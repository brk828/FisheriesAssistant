# Squeezing Out the Squawfish: Water Development and Two Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1985 *National Wildlife* article examines how dams and water diversions on the [[Colorado River]] have reduced habitat for two endangered fishes, the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the [[Humpbacked Chub]]. Both species have nearly vanished from the lower river, and conservationists fear the same fate for the upper basin's remaining roughly 500 miles of free-flowing river. The article centers on the [[Windy Gap Process]], a fee-based system used by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) that let developers fund research in place of formal jeopardy opinions. Federal officials defend the approach as a necessary experiment, while conservationists argue that approving projects without full knowledge of their effects threatens the fish.

## Background & Description

The Colorado originally ran wild and free for nearly 1,500 miles, from the Continental Divide to the Gulf of California. Completion of [[Hoover Dam]] in 1936 began a period of river regulation. Further large dams and water diversion projects followed on the lower river, reducing fluctuating flows and converting much of the lower Colorado into a series of large, placid lakes. This change drastically altered habitat for several rare fishes.

Prior to 1981, the FWS issued "jeopardy opinions" on proposed water projects that threatened an endangered species or its habitat. These opinions required developers to take specific measures to reduce threats. FWS official [[Barry Mulder]], who oversees endangered species activities in the region, recalled that in 1977 the agency realized it needed more information about the river and the fish.

The article profiles two species:

- **Colorado Squawfish:** Called the "white salmon" by early European settlers. Radio telemetry studies show it sometimes migrates several hundred miles to reach spawning grounds, described in the article as the longest migration of any freshwater fish on the North American continent. FWS scientist [[Harold Tyus]], an authority on the species, notes that it selects well-oxygenated areas, preferably whitewater canyons, for spawning, and that it declines when movement is blocked.
- **Humpbacked Chub:** Smaller than the squawfish, it remains in the river year-round. FWS scientist [[Lynn Kaeding]] describes its habitat as "deep water, turbulent river reaches that are found in very narrow canyons." Such canyons also make ideal dam sites. Researchers have located only three surviving populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The main threat is habitat alteration from cumulative water development. Dams convert free-flowing reaches into lakes and reduce flow fluctuation, while diversions reduce river volume. The article links the squawfish's decline to blocked migration and loss of spawning habitat, and links the chub's vulnerability to the same narrow canyons that suit dam construction.

The upper basin still holds about 500 miles of free-flowing river, which conservationists regard as the critical remaining habitat. FWS Associate Director [[Rolf Wallenstrom]] argues that river flow levels "have the potential to be manipulated in the long term, to benefit both water users and the fish," meaning that managed flows could serve both water users and the fish.

## Key Findings

- **Habitat loss in the lower basin:** Hoover Dam (completed 1936) and later dams and diversions converted much of the lower Colorado into placid lakes. [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpbacked Chub]] have all but vanished from this reach.
- **Threat to the upper basin:** About 500 miles of free-flowing river remain, and conservationists fear the same decline there.
- **Shift in regulatory approach:** After 1981, the FWS used the [[Windy Gap Process]], in which developers paid a fee to fund data collection, research and artificial habitat creation, in place of jeopardy opinions that could halt or delay projects.
- **Scale of approved development:** Since 1981, 33 new water-diversion schemes have been initiated or approved in the upper basin, with at least two dozen more expected to undergo review.
- **Scientific advances:** Radio telemetry showed squawfish migrations of several hundred miles, and the chub is known from only three surviving populations.
- **Conflicting views:** FWS officials, including [[Rolf Wallenstrom]], view the Windy Gap Process as a workable, experimental approach that avoids a "standstill" and possible challenges to the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Critics, including [[Bob Davison]] of the [[National Wildlife Federation]], argue that "you don't determine the ecological impact of 33 projects by first building them and then figuring out what has happened," and that every project has been approved "without full knowledge of its effects."
- **Central question:** Whether the upper Colorado allocation scheme can protect the two fishes while water development proceeds.
