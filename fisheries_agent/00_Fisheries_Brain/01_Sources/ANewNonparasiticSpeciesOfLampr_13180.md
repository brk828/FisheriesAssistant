# A New Nonparasitic Lamprey of the Genus *Entosphenus* from South Central California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #petromyzontidae

## Executive Summary

Vladykov and Kott describe *[[Entosphenus Hubbsi]]*, a new nonparasitic [[Lamprey]] species of the family [[Petromyzontidae]], from the [[Friant-Kern Canal]] east of Delano, Kern County, California. The description rests on eleven newly transformed adults and one [[Ammocoete]] (larval stage). The species is separated from its congeners by a low [[Myomeres|trunk myomere]] count, reduced [[Dentition]], only three [[Velar Tentacles]], small size, and a restricted distribution. The authors argue the species should be treated as endangered and protected. The paper is a formal taxonomic description published in the *Bulletin of the Southern California Academy of Sciences* (Volume 75, 1976).

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic context.** California petromyzontids belong to two genera: [[Lampetra Genus]] (subfamily Lampetrinae) and [[Entosphenus Genus]] (subfamily Entospheninae). Whether *Entosphenus* is a distinct genus or a subgenus of *Lampetra* was disputed among authors at the time. Three *Lampetra* species occur in western North America: the parasitic [[Lampetra Ayresii]], the nonparasitic [[Lampetra Pacifica]] (found in California), and the nonparasitic [[Lampetra Richardsoni]] (known from Oregon to British Columbia).

**Previously known Entosphenus in California and Oregon.** Three species were recognized before this study:
- [[Entosphenus Tridentatus]]: a large parasitic species (the [[Pacific Lamprey]]).
- [[Entosphenus Minimus]]: a small parasitic form, known only from Miller Lake, Oregon.
- [[Entosphenus Lethophagus]]: a nonparasitic species described by Hubbs (1971).

**Holotype and type series.**
- Holotype: CAS 35987, a male, 131 mm total length, collected about 15 February 1972 by D. P. Christenson. It is deposited in the [[California Academy of Sciences]].
- Paratypes: W333–335, W337–343 (seven males, 117–142 mm; three females, 140–141 mm), plus ammocoete W344 (160 mm), catalogued as CAS 35988.
- Total material: 11 newly transformed individuals and one ammocoete, several of which were not well preserved.

**Named in honour of** [[Carl Leavitt Hubbs]], a leading lamprey taxonomist.

**Morphological description (holotype).** The holotype is at maturity stage three (early maturity; dorsal fins separated). Body proportions in percent of total length were: disc 4.2, prebranchial 9.2, eye 1.5, branchial 10.7, trunk 54.2, tail 28.2. The holotype has four inner lateral teeth on each side of the disc, a single row of nine unicuspid posterials, a supraoral lamina with one cusp at each end (no median cusp), and five infraoral cusps. Coloration is brown on the sides and back and whitish ventrally. Caudal fin pigmentation is restricted to a narrow zone around the notochord.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and origin.** The type locality is the Friant-Kern Canal. The authors conclude it is not a normal habitat for a small nonparasitic lamprey:
- The canal is about 85 percent concrete lined.
- Flow is at least 56.6 m³ per second.

They infer that *E. hubbsi* originated in the [[Kern River System]], which was an inland drainage before the canal was built. The canal connects to the [[San Joaquin River]] through the man-made [[Millerton Lake]], but no direct connection to the sea exists. The [[Friant Dam]] (height 97.2 m) would prevent upstream passage by *E. hubbsi*.

**Co-occurring species.** The only other lamprey known from the canal near Delano is [[Lampetra Pacifica]]. Four transformed specimens (119–152 mm) of that species were collected on the west side of the canal by G. Charles Mayes on 26 January 1972.

**Biogeographic comparison.**
- [[Entosphenus Lethophagus]] is known from the Klamath River system in Oregon and northern tributaries of the Sacramento River in California.
- [[Entosphenus Minimus]] is landlocked and known only from Miller Lake, southern Oregon.
- [[Entosphenus Tridentatus]] is anadromous and ranges along the western seaboard from Alaska to Baja California.

**Conservation status.** Because of its restricted range, the authors consider *E. hubbsi* endangered in California and recommend protection, following recommendations by Miller (1972) and Vladykov (1973). They warn that delay could lead to extinction, as happened to *E. minimus* at Miller Lake.

## Key Findings

**Diagnostic characters.** The new species differs from all congeners in five ways:

1. **Trunk myomeres:** 53 in the ammocoete; 54–57 (mean 55.5) in transformed specimens. Other species range 58–73.
2. **Dentition:** reduced, with typical counts of two supraoral cusps, 1-1-1-1 inner lateral cusps per side of the disc, and 9–12 (mean 10.3) posterials.
3. **Velar tentacles:** only three (one median, the shortest, and one lateral on each side) in the three specimens examined, versus 5–18 in other species.
4. **Size:** transformed specimens are small, 117–142 mm.
5. **Distribution:** restricted to the Kern River drainage.

**Myomere comparison** (trunk myomeres, from the paper):

| Species | Myomere count | Reproductive mode |
|---|---|---|
| *E. hubbsi* (present study) | 54–57 (mean 55.5) | Nonparasitic |
| *E. lethophagus* | 63–69 (mean 65.6) in California; 58–73 (mean 66.6) in Oregon | Nonparasitic |
| *E. minimus* | 60–65 (mean 62.8) | Parasitic |
| *E. tridentatus* | 63–70 (mean 66.3) | Parasitic |

**Size and body proportions.**
- Mean total length: males 129.1 mm (eight specimens, 117–142 mm); females 140.3 mm (three specimens, 140–141 mm).
- Eye length averages 1.7 (males) and 1.8 (females), close to 1.6 in *E. lethophagus*, and lower than the 2.4 average for the parasitic species.
- Disc length (about 4.3) and prebranchial length (9.4) are very small, which the authors attribute partly to nonparasitic status and partly to the recent transformation.

**Dentition.**
- Supraoral lamina: two cusps typically (one specimen had three). Other species had two to four (*E. lethophagus*), or three (*E. minimus* and *E. tridentatus*).
- Inner laterals: typically 1-1-1-1 cusps, versus 2-2-2-2 in *E. lethophagus* and 2-3-3-2 in the parasitic species.
- Posterials: lowest in *E. hubbsi* (mean 10.3, range 9–12), versus 12.1 in *E. lethophagus*, and 17.1 in *E. tridentatus*. Bicuspid posterials were absent in *E. hubbsi*.
- Transverse lingual lamina: poorly developed in *E. hubbsi*, with cusps too weak to count.

**Velar tentacles.** Previous reports for *E. tridentatus* gave 11–18 tentacles. In this study, ten *E. tridentatus* specimens had 9–15 (mean 12.5). *E. lethophagus* and *E. minimus* had seven in single specimens, and *E. minimus* is reported to range 5–7 (per Bond, personal communication).

**Caudal fin.** Pigmentation is restricted to a narrow band around the notochord, leaving most of the fin membrane unpigmented. The caudal fin is more rounded than in other species, which are more pointed and strongly pigmented.

**Ammocoete.** The single ammocoete (160 mm) has 53 myomeres, a broad nonpigmented zone above the branchial pores, and no dark pigment on the prebranchial region.

**Conclusions.** The authors support Hubbs' (1971) call for further study of the *Entosphenus* complex. They conclude that the restricted range and small population make *E. hubbsi* an endangered species requiring immediate legal protection.
