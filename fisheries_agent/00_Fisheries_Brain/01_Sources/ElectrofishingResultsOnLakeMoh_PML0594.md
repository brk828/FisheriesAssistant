# Lake Mohave Electrofishing and Netting for Razorback Sucker and Bonytail Chub Broodstock (1981)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

Two U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service memoranda (March 1981) from the [[Parker Project Office]] describe field operations on [[Lake Mohave]] to capture endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) as broodstock for hatchery spawning. A combined work group of university, state, and federal biologists used boat electrofishing and trammel netting in January and March 1981. Fish were transported to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. The second memo reports eight bonytail chubs captured in March 1981, and the author judged the operation a success. The documents also record incidental catches of non-target species and note electrofishing settings and handling problems that may affect fish survival.

## Background & Description

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)** was the primary target of the January 1981 electrofishing effort. The first memo describes the species as spawning in groups in the coves near Airport Cove and Levi Cove. Visual surveys on January 12 found groups ranging from small aggregations up to one group of about 200 individuals.

**Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*)** was the target of the March 1981 netting effort. The memo describes it as a rare fish, with the total captures to date small enough that each individual was tracked for hatchery use.

**Equipment and methods (electrofishing):**
- The boat shocker was a [[Coffelt Vvp-10]] unit powered by a 5,000-watt generator, with a Kohler 10 hp engine.
- Two anodes on fiberglass poles extended about 3.3 m beyond the bow, with two cathodes on the boat side about one-third of the way back from the bow.
- A 40 hp Johnson outboard powered the 5 m boat. Remote controls let the operator move the boat and watch the fish.
- Reported settings: pulse width 40–60, 80–120 pulses per second, DC pulsed voltage 200–250 V, and 9–11 A.

**Equipment and methods (trammel netting):**
- Eight nets were used. Seven were 91.5 m long and 1.8 m deep with 25.4 mm square mesh. The eighth was the same depth but only 45.7 m long.
- Nets were set off points of land or along the shoreline, checked every 2–3 hours during daylight, and at least twice each night.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and location:** Survey sites included Airport Cove, Levi Cove, Cottonwood East, [[Carp Cove]] (Arizona side), and [[Six-Mile Cove]] (Nevada side), along with a point near Willow Beach. Shoreline netting was carried out in the coves and along points extending into the lake.

**Water temperatures:** Recorded values were about 11.5–11.6 °C at the January truck loading. Table 1 gives water temperatures of about 13–14.5 °C for the March netting sites, though some values are hard to read in the scanned table.

**Non-target species:** Several non-native and game fish were captured or observed, including:
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), which were the most numerous catch and were sacrificed and preserved for spawning use at Dexter and Willow Beach.
- [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Cutthroat Trout]].
- Bluegill and redear sunfish were observed in the electrical field but not netted.

Table 1 gives catch counts by species and site, but the scan is partly illegible. Carp appear most abundant, with one count reading 453.

## Key Findings

**1. Razorback Sucker capture and transport (January 1981)**
- A distribution truck arrived at Dexter National Fish Hatchery on January 14, 1981, carrying females and 58 males. Exact female count is illegible in the scan.
- The fish traveled well. The author gave the transport details in an accompanying communication.
- The night of January 14 produced additional captures, and several males were released to avoid a male-heavy sample.

**2. Electrofishing performance and handling concerns**
- The main problem was the time fish spent in the field after being shocked. Fish appeared very susceptible to shock.
- Leaving the foot switch on for more than about 15 seconds without pulsing could cause severe damage.
- The author recommended a comparative mortality study, marking one group of fish and holding them for a period of time.
- Working times were 10:20–11:30, 13:45–15:50, and 21:30–23:45 on January 13, and 19:30–23:00 on January 14. Most fish were captured after dark.

**3. Bonytail Chub capture (March 1981)**
- Nets were set in and around 6-Mile Cove and Carp Cove, with bonytails also taken from a cove on the Nevada side and a point north of the Willow Beach area.
- Eight bonytail chubs were captured between March 9 and March 12, 1981. Fish were placed in separate 24-liter containers and moved to Willow Beach hatchery with minimal stress.
- Recorded sexes: at least three and possibly four of this year's fish were males. The rest appeared to be females.

**4. Cumulative totals**
- From March 1975 through April 1980, 10 bonytail chubs were taken by net from the Arizona side of the lake. None were males.
- With this year's captures, the total bonytail chubs captured by net on the lake reached 18.

**5. Operational conclusion**
- The memo calls the operation a success and credits the cooperation of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona State University]] (Dr. W.L. Minckley supplied catch data), state biologists, and university biologists.
- The author notes that efficiency improved when fish were located and approached from the boat, and that the operation depended on coordinated effort across agencies.
