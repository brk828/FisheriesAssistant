# Biological Evaluation: Whiteriver Water Treatment Plant, Fort Apache Reservation, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This biological evaluation, prepared by the [[White Mountain Apache Game And Fish Department]] for the [[Indian Health Service]], assesses the effects of a proposed municipal water treatment plant and intake on the North Fork of the [[White River]] at Whiteriver, Arizona. Under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]], the project requires a [[Biological Assessment]] because it may affect two federally listed fish: the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and the threatened [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*).

The evaluation concludes that the project has "no effect" on Apache trout and "may adversely affect" loach minnow. A 1991 survey at 18 riffles found no loach minnow. Water withdrawal, rather than siltation or impoundment, is the main concern, particularly at extremely low flows in June and July.

## Background & Description

**Need.** Whiteriver's population was 4,792 in 1980 and is projected to reach 6,503 by 2040 ([[Whiteriver Tribal Community]]). The Indian Health Service projects potable water demand rising from 319 million gallons to 480 million gallons by 2022. The town's current supply comes from a large spring (183 million gallons per year) and nine alluvial wells. The wells are unreliable in early summer, when demand peaks.

**Project components.**
- **Intake structure:** a concrete vault on the North Fork with a 0.15-inch screened intake, a concrete channel, rock gabions, and a gate assembly. It is gravity-fed.
- **Pumps:** three 60 hp pumps placed above the high-water mark.
- **Pipeline:** a 12-inch line carrying raw water 0.7 miles to the plant.
- **Treatment plant:** built on a bench of about 6,900 m² upslope from the [[Rainbow City]] subdivision, requiring about 1,400 cubic yards of earthwork. Treatment uses coagulation, filtration, chlorination, and fluoridation.
- **Backflush management:** filter backwash flows to two abandoned sewage lagoons, where effluent settles for 37 days before entering the regional sewer system.

**Capacity.** The plant initially treats 1,400 gpm, with a maximum of 2,100 gpm. Storage is 4.22 million gallons, enough for 3.2 days at maximum production. Design life is 30 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The intake lies about six miles above the North Fork–East Fork confluence in pinyon-juniper vegetation. The land is mainly residential, with light livestock grazing and an existing access road. The three miles below the intake are heavily disturbed by road crossings, highway construction, bridges, farming, and siltation. The first half mile below the intake and the canyon in the middle of town are the least impacted reaches. The fish community is dominated by disturbance-tolerant species: [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Desert Sucker]].

**Loach minnow ecology.** Loach minnow occur only in the upper Gila River Basin. They are riffle dwellers and cannot tolerate complete dewatering. Historically they occurred in Arizona, New Mexico, and Mexico. Marsh (1988) considers them extinct in Mexico. Declines have been driven by exotic fish, siltation, impoundment, and water diversion. On the Fort Apache Reservation, a breeding population has been consistently recorded in the East Fork of the White River, while only single adults have been found in the mainstem below the forks. Predators such as [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]] pose a greater risk to fish confined to pools at low flow.

**Flow regime.** The White River has wide natural flow fluctuation, and Minckley and Meffe (1987) note that such fluctuation favors native over exotic fish in the Southwest. Flow peaks in spring from snowmelt and in late summer from rain. Lows usually occur in June and July. The river was completely dry for four days in 1963. The 7Q10 flow (the lowest weekly average expected once in ten years) is 5.2 cfs.

**Current withdrawals.** Nine alluvial wells remove about 0.6 cfs. Eight private diversions can remove up to 9 cfs, though about half return water to the stream. A tribal agriculture project removes 14 cfs, mostly from April until the early-August monsoon.

## Key Findings

### 1. Loach minnow survey (October 28–31, 1991)
- Eighteen riffles were sampled, plus one run and two pools, using a 5 m seine block net, backpack electrofishing (Coffelt shocker set to 2 amps), and substrate kicking. About 75 m² was sampled per riffle, using the method noted as successful in earlier loach minnow surveys. The North Fork flowed at 22 cfs.
- **No loach minnow were found.** Catch composition was speckled dace (68%), desert sucker (20%), and Sonora sucker (10%). [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Green Sunfish]] appeared in small numbers in backwaters and pools.
- Earlier surveys (Propst et al. 1985; Westech 1988; FWS 1988) also failed to find loach minnow in the North Fork, so the authors consider them unlikely near Whiteriver.

### 2. Impact assessment by factor
- **Siltation:** no increase expected. Construction will occur at low winter flows, and sediment removal during withdrawal is proportional to its presence in the water.
- **Impoundment:** no effect.
- **Water withdrawal:** identified as the factor most likely to affect loach minnow.
- **Species introduction:** no direct effects. Low flows could push fish into pools with predators, but predator numbers in the White River are low, so the effect is expected to be minor.
- **Interrelated/interdependent effects:** probably minor. The author does not foresee added population growth or industry from the plant.

### 3. Withdrawal rates and mitigation
- **Initial intake capacity:** 3.1 cfs. **Future capacity:** 4.7 cfs. Pumps operate in stages at 1.6, 3.1, or 4.7 cfs.
- **Initial operation:** two pumps for four hours per day (408,000 gallons per day). **Design operation:** three pumps for ten hours per day (1,260,000 gallons per day). **Theoretical maximum:** 24 hours per day (3,024,000 gallons per day). Continuous operation is considered unlikely.
- **Well closure:** seven wells (101 million gallons per year) will close, returning 0.1–0.9 cfs to the river, with the greatest return in June and July. Four wells will be abandoned, and three Orchard Wells will remain as emergency backup.
- **Net withdrawal:** never more than 3.8 cfs at the lowest river flow, and never more than 6% of the monthly mean.
- **Monthly pattern:** highest withdrawals in May through July, minimal use in winter (2–5 million gallons per month) when the spring supplies most of the town's water.

- At the 7Q10 flow of 5.2 cfs, the plant could take 73% of the flow during ten hours of pumping. That reduction could dewater substantial loach minnow habitat.
- Daytime withdrawals could reduce feeding time, but the fish would still have five or more hours to feed in summer. Nighttime withdrawals would be more likely to concentrate fish in predator-rich pools, so the project favors daytime pumping.

### 5. Conclusions
- **[[Apache Trout]]:** "no effect." Apache trout occur in the project area only rarely in winter, no recent study has found them, and winter withdrawals are small. Upstream Apache trout populations carry [[Rainbow Trout]] genetic material.
- **[[Loach Minnow]]:** "may adversely affect." The risk is judged slight because the withdrawal fraction is small and few loach minnow occur in the affected reach. The main adverse effect is withdrawal at extremely low flows in June and July.
