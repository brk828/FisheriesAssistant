# Natural Hybridization between Dorosoma and Signalosa, with a Report on the Distribution of S. petenensis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports what the authors describe as the first known natural hybrids between the [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Signalosa petenensis*) and the [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*), both collected from the [[Ohio River]]. Six hybrid specimens were compared with 25 specimens of each parental species using proportional body measurements and meristic counts. The hybrids were intermediate in most characters, but extreme in body plumpness and fin development. The paper also reviews the recent range expansion of the threadfin shad, which the authors link to reservoir construction, stocking, and human alteration of the Ohio River, and proposes that these factors contributed to the breakdown of species isolation between the two shads.

## Background & Description

Clupeiformes are nearly worldwide in distribution and include many commercially important food fishes. Before this study, no natural hybridization between clupeid species had been recorded in North America ([[Hubbs 1955 Hybridization Review]]).

**Generic status.** Both species have been placed in *Dorosoma* by some workers. The authors argue that the genera should be kept distinct until a thorough study is completed. They rely on the characters summarized by [[Miller 1950 Clupeid Review]] and [[Moore 1957 Vertebrates Survey]], and note ongoing work on pharyngeal pouch anatomy. Miller (1960) had assigned *Signalosa* subgeneric rank under *Dorosoma*.

**Taxonomy of Signalosa.** Until 1945, *Signalosa* was thought to include three species: *S. petenensis*, *S. atchafalayae*, and *S. mexicana*. Hubbs (in Hubbs & Allen, 1945) reduced the latter two to subspecies of *S. petenensis*. The authors use only the binomial until the group is revised.

**Materials.** Specimens came from the Ohio River Valley Water Sanitation Commission–University of Louisville Aquatic-Life Resources Project. Body measurements and meristic counts followed [[Hubbs & Lagler Fish Identification Methods]] and Miller (1950). Vertebrae were counted from x-rays. Specimen identifications were verified by Miller and Bailey at the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology.

**Catalogued specimens.**
- *D. cepedianum*: UL 9054 and UL 9655 (Ohio River, Louisville and Massac Creek).
- Hybrids: UL 11372 (1 specimen, near Mound City, Illinois), UL 11407 (4 specimens, Lock No. 41, Louisville), and UL 11543 (1 specimen, same locality).
- *S. petenensis*: UL 7529 (Green River), UL 10982 (Ohio River, Lock No. 41), and UL 7562 (Cumberland River).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hybrid indices.** Characters were scored on a 0–100 scale, with 0 set at the mean for *D. cepedianum* and 100 at the mean for *S. petenensis* ([[Hybrid Index]], after Hubbs, Hubbs & Johnson 1942). Most hybrid values fall near intermediate, but several characters depart from it.

**Range expansion of the threadfin shad.**
- Historically, the range was given as the Atlantic and Gulf coasts from Mexico and Florida to Lake Petén, Guatemala. Weed (1925) recorded it from Florida.
- In 1948, Tennessee Valley Authority fishery workers found the species in large Tennessee River impoundments. Small populations in the lower Tennessee River had increased after reservoirs stabilized conditions.
- Between 1948 and 1953 it was introduced into other Tennessee impoundments. Parsons (1957) later recorded it in the Cumberland System (Center Hill Reservoir) and documented stocking in Dale Hollow Reservoir in 1955.
- The species was stocked widely across the United States, including Virginia, Georgia, Pennsylvania, California, Nevada, Arizona, Kansas, and New Mexico. Reports also mention transport to Hawaii for use in tuna chumming.
- In California, stocking began in November 1953. Fish were later released into [[Lake Havasu]] and found near the [[Salton Sea]] by September 1955. Haskell (1959) described rapid "biological explosions" after stocking in western waters.
- Introductions in Kansas and probably Pennsylvania failed to survive winter. An attempt in southern Indiana in 1958 also failed.
- Downstream movement from Virginia plantings in the upper Kanawha River System established the species in West Virginia, within the Ohio Basin.

**Evidence that the species is new to the Ohio River.** This conclusion rests on negative evidence. Major surveys by Forbes & Richardson, Gerking (Indiana), Trautman (Ohio), and the Upper Mississippi River Conservation Commission did not record it in the lower Ohio or the Mississippi above the mouth of the Ohio.

**Temperature sensitivity.** Threadfin shad die when temperatures drop sharply to about 50°F or less. Transported fish suffered "heavy mortality" below 45°F. The authors suggest the species can tolerate slow cooling, or that Tennessee stocking may have selected a cold-tolerant form.

**Dams.** Lock and dam construction did not stop upstream movement. The Louisville dam, roughly 37 feet at normal pool, may still act as a barrier.

**Causes of hybridization.** Using the factors listed by [[Hubbs 1955 Hybridization Review]], the authors identify three conditions in the Ohio River: the rarity of one species, the recent invasion of another, and environmental alteration by man. Threadfin shad comprised well under 5 percent of fishes taken in 19 of the 25 collections where they occurred. Gizzard shad, by contrast, are very abundant. The authors propose that the less common threadfin shad joined spawning aggregations of gizzard shad.

## Key Findings

**Morphology: intermediate to extreme**

- **Head length:** The parental ranges overlap widely. Hybrid values are intermediate but show lower variation, similar to the threadfin shad.
- **Predorsal length:** Hybrid values are intermediate, but nape-to-dorsal-origin distance leans toward *S. petenensis* (hybrid index 79 percent). The nape-to-snout hybrid index is 50 percent.
- **Body depth:** Hybrids resemble the gizzard shad more closely. The authors describe a "plumpness" beyond either parent, which they attribute to genetic complementarity rather than strict [[Heterosis]].
- **Caudal peduncle depth:** Hybrid means match *S. petenensis*.
- **Upper jaw and mandible:** Both approximate intermediacy but lean toward *D. cepedianum*.
- **Orbit length:** Hybrids have proportionally smaller eyes than either parent, possibly because hybrids are larger on average.
- **Dorsal filament:** Hybrid filaments exceed both parents. *S. petenensis* filaments greatly exceed those of *D. cepedianum*, with almost no overlap. The authors explain this through "points of inflection" in developmental rates (Hubbs & Strawn) and additive gene effects.

**Meristic characters**

| Character | *D. cepedianum* (n = 25) | *S. petenensis* (n = 25) | Hybrids (n = 6) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lateral-line scales | 62.5 ± 2.34 (59–67) | 45.4 ± 1.63 (42–48) | Intermediate; tends toward *Signalosa* |
| Pre-pelvic scutes | 18.0 ± 0.30 (17–19) | 15.9 ± 0.53 (14–17) | Intermediate |
| Anal fin rays | 30.4 ± 1.74 (25–33) | 23.2 ± 1.33 (20–25) | Intermediate; leans toward *Dorosoma* |
| Vertebrae | 49.7 ± 0.40 (48–51) | 42.9 ± 0.58 (41–44) | 46.3 (mean) |

- Pre- and post-pelvic scute counts overlapped in the parent species. Post-pelvic counts did not differ significantly.
- Hybrid pectoral fin rays were 16 in nearly all specimens, which the authors describe as extremely high and constant compared with the parents.

**Pigmentation**
- Hybrid head and body patterns were a mixture of the two parents. Hybrids had a dark cap on the head like *Signalosa*, but a humeral spot like *Dorosoma*, and some had no humeral bar.
- Yellow pigments in fins and body resembled *Signalosa*, and were possibly more intense in the hybrids.
- Overall, the authors state that hybrids resembled *Signalosa* more closely in basic color pattern, but the two patterns were clearly blended.

**Distribution and abundance of S. petenensis**
- Threadfin shad were found in the Ohio River from about 10 miles upstream of Lock No. 41 at Louisville down to near Mound City, Illinois.
- The species was taken in 25 of 62 collections from the lower river that used gear likely to capture it.
- Counts ranged from single fish to over 10,000 young-of-year in a brief seine haul in Hardin County, Kentucky. The largest adult sample was 2,770 fish from a rotenone sample at Lock No. 53 near Grand Chain, Illinois.
- No threadfin shad were collected upstream of Louisville, which the authors used to argue against downstream movement from upstream stockings as the main source.

**Conclusions**
- The hybrids reflect a breakdown of isolating mechanisms, driven by the recent invasion of the Ohio River by threadfin shad, human-altered habitat, and the rarity of the invader.
- The authors call for further study, including anatomical work on pharyngeal pouches, to decide whether *Signalosa* and *Dorosoma* should remain separate genera.
