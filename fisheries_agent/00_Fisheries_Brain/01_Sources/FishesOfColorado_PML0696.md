# Fishes of Colorado: Cyprinid Species Accounts and Identification Key

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt comes from *Fishes of Colorado*, published in 1971 by the [[Colorado Game, Fish and Parks Division]] and co-authored by W. Harry Everhart of [[Colorado State University]] and Wayne R. Seaman of Colorado Game, Fish and Parks. The surviving pages contain a dichotomous identification key fragment and species accounts for several minnows (Cyprinidae) and a sucker-family key. Covered species include [[Common Carp]], [[Tench]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the [[Bonytail Chub]] group. The text is partly truncated by scanning damage, so some numerical details are incomplete.

## Background & Description

**Document details:** Printing code GFP-I-28, price $1.00. The book is a regional fish guide for Colorado, with a species page index (for example, Red Shiner p. 40, Fathead Minnow p. 43, Colorado Squawfish p. 44, Bonytail Chubs p. 44).

**Identification key fragment:** Couplets separate fish by lateral line shape (strongly decurved versus not decurved), head form (slender and flattened, pike-like) and dorsal fin shape. The sucker family key separates fish by a distinct notch at the mouth corner and dorsal fin form (sickle-shaped and elongate with 20 to 40 rays, or short with fewer than 19 rays).

**Species accounts:**

- **[[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio* Linnaeus):** Among the first introduced fish in Colorado, with introduction recorded in 1882. Three forms occur: fully scaled, partially scaled and scaleless ("leather carp"). Carp records are noted, and the largest reported weighed 59½ pounds, taken by Colorado Game, Fish and Parks. Carp rarely live beyond 15 years.
- **[[Tench]] (*Tinca tinca* Linnaeus):** Introduced to the [[Rio Grande Drainage]] and later spread to a county and several ponds. Tench are generally small, and the text compares their angling appeal with that of carp.
- **[[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis* Baird and Girard):** A small, omnivorous minnow. Eggs are scattered among debris, plants and other substrates; they are adhesive, and there is no parental care.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]] group (*Gila* spp. Baird and Girard):** Four members occur in Colorado: [[Rio Grande Chub]] (common in the Rio Grande drainage), [[Bonytail Chub]] (the most striking, with a reddish back in large adults and a very narrow, long caudal peduncle), [[Roundtail Chub]] (common in the Colorado River system) and [[Humpback Chub]] (very rare). Bonytails have been reported up to 18 inches and roundtails up to 15 inches. The text notes taxonomic confusion within the genus.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius* Girard):** The largest minnow in North America, with recorded weights up to 80 pounds, commonly around 15 pounds, and reported total lengths of five feet. The name *lucius* means "pike."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Carp habitat:** Thrives in warm, vegetated waters. From late May into June, carp spawn in shallow water among aquatic vegetation and debris. Young reach about five inches in their first summer and become forage for larger fish. Few predators large enough to control them remain in Colorado.
- **Carp diet and impact:** Insect larvae, nymphs, crustaceans and aquatic plants make up the diet. Large numbers can roil the water, which affects most game fish.
- **Tench habitat and feeding:** Tench are described as hardy and able to survive in conditions where many game fish cannot. Anglers fishing near the bottom catch them.
- **Red Shiner diet:** Omnivorous, eating aquatic vegetation, plankton, small insects and crustaceans.
- **Squawfish diet and life stages:** Fish up to four inches feed almost entirely on aquatic insects and small invertebrates. Fish from four inches to two feet feed mainly on other fish, with occasional insects. Adults feed mostly, if not exclusively, on fish, including trout and salmon. Lacking jaw teeth, they capture prey with large mouths and crush it with pharyngeal teeth.
- **Squawfish reproduction:** Spawns in early spring in shallows. Fertilized eggs are adhesive and attach to the bottom.
- **Management conflict:** Squawfish prey heavily on game fish, so control has been a long-standing management problem. Methods have included dynamite and chemical treatment, and the most recent is the chemical [[Squoxin]].

## Key Findings

1. **Carp:** Introduced to Colorado in 1882; the largest recorded specimen weighed 59½ pounds; carp rarely live past 15 years; females produce large numbers of eggs (the count is truncated in the source).
2. **Carp and management:** Carp disturb habitat through feeding and aquatic disturbance, and anglers are often prejudiced against them, though carp can provide sport on light tackle or when fished in cooler waters.
3. **Tench:** Introduced to the Rio Grande drainage and later spreading to additional ponds and a county; they are generally small fish that bottom-feeding anglers catch.
4. **Bonytail chub group:** Four species occur in Colorado; the Rio Grande chub is common in the Rio Grande drainage, the bonytail and roundtail in the Colorado River system, and the humpback chub is very rare.
5. **Colorado Squawfish:** The largest North American minnow, a predator on game fish, and the subject of long-term control efforts, including the chemical Squoxin.
6. **Red Shiner:** Omnivorous, with adhesive eggs and no parental care.
7. **Identification:** The included keys use lateral line form, head shape, dorsal fin form and mouth notches to separate cyprinids and catostomids.
