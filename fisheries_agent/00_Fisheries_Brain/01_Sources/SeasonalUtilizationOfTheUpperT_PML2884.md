# Connecticut Fish Fauna: Marine Additions, Estuarine Seasonality and Freshwater Records

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This compilation, edited by [[Paul M. Jacobson]] and published in 1980, brings together four studies of fish occurrence in Connecticut. The core paper documents fishes collected near the [[Millstone Point Nuclear Power Station]] in Waterford between May 1969 and December 1978. It reports 17 species not previously recorded from Connecticut marine waters. The other papers cover seasonal use of the upper [[Thames River Estuary]] by fishes, an over-wintering population of [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish) in New Haven, and additions to the freshwater ichthyofauna of the state, including introduced and adventitious marine species. The authors note that the collective work substantially advanced knowledge of fish occurrence in the Connecticut region.

## Background & Description

Documentation of Connecticut fishes dates to the 1840s, when Reverend J. H. Linsley produced the first catalogue of fishes for the region. Later state reports, including [[Saltwater Fishes of Connecticut]] (Thomson et al., 1978) and [[Freshwater Fishes of Connecticut]] (Whitworth et al., 1968), are the main regional references. Thomson et al. (1978) listed 119 marine species, and the Millstone study documented 86 of them.

The four papers were conducted independently with different objectives. Their shared result is a clearer picture of fish occurrence in Connecticut waters. The compilation includes a checklist (Appendix I) of known Connecticut fishes, coded as marine (M), freshwater (F), or introduced (I). Full species names and occurrence codes are given there.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Millstone marine survey.** The study area was about six miles of shoreline near the power station. Fish were collected by impingement at the plant intake screens, beach seines, otter trawls, gill nets and plankton tows. Impingement accounted for 36% of adults and juveniles, beach seines for 41%, otter trawls for 21% and gill nets for 2%. Plankton tows yielded all larval fishes. About 80% of the collected species are deposited at the [[Millstone Environmental Laboratory]].

**Long Island Sound as a mixing zone.** The authors describe the Sound as a "giant mixing bowl." A year-round resident population tolerates both high summer and low winter temperatures. The [[Gulf Stream]] brings warm-water southern and tropical species into the Sound in summer. Cold-water northern species arrive in winter under the influence of the [[Labrador Current]]. Summer visitors appear to predominate.

**Upper Thames Estuary.** The estuary runs about 26 km from the Quinebaug–Shetucket and Yantic rivers to Long Island Sound at New London. Its upper 5 km receive extra freshwater from a tributary and lie beyond two dams. These dams are complete barriers to upstream fish movement. Freshwater between the upper estuary and the dams was unsuitable for much of the year because of low oxygen, high temperature and pollutants.

**Campus ponds, New Haven.** The Beaver Ponds, Campus Pond and Campus Lagoon are small, connected water bodies on the Southern Connecticut State College campus. Heated water released intermittently from a college power plant keeps Campus Pond from freezing. This artificial warming allows mosquitofish to survive Connecticut winters.

## Key Findings

**Marine survey (Millstone Point, 1969–1978)**
- Collections totalled 4,061,532 adults and juveniles and 4,501,586 larvae. These counts are as printed and may be affected by OCR errors.
- Specimens comprised 54 families, 86 genera and 103 species.
- The study documented 86 of the 119 species listed by Thomson et al. (1978) and added 17 species not previously reported from Connecticut marine waters.
- Fourteen of the 17 new species were collected as adults or juveniles. Three were known only from larvae: [[Brosme Brosme]] (cusk), [[Glyptocephalus Cynoglossus]] (witch flounder) and [[Hippoglossoides Platessoides]] (American plaice).
- Of the 17 new species, eleven had known ranges extending south of Connecticut and six extended north. The authors consider most of them strays.
- The exception is [[Liparis Atlanticus]] (seasnail). It was previously regarded as a rare stray south of Cape Cod but was very common here, with 555 adults and juveniles collected. The authors conclude it has probably been present undetected along the coast.
- Radiated shanny (*Ulvaria subbifurcata*) was collected in numbers (19 adults and juveniles and 2,871 larvae, as printed). This suggests its range extends further south than previously reported.

**Rarity assessments of the 17 new species**
- *Brosme brosme* (cusk): five larvae from 1973–1977; considered very rare.
- *Melanogrammus aeglefinus* (haddock): six juveniles from impingement (May–June 1978) and one larva; considered very rare.
- *Rissola marginata* (striped cusk-eel): 26 adults and juveniles, mainly in 1976; considered a vagrant.
- *Scomberesox saurus* (Atlantic saury): six juveniles from beach seines in Jordan Cove, 1969; considered very rare.
- *Fistularia tabacaria* (cornetfish): 15 adults, 1976–1978; considered uncommon.
- *Hippocampus erectus* (lined seahorse): 12 adults and 32 post-larvae; considered uncommon.
- *Pristigenys alta* (short bigeye): five juveniles, 1973–1975; considered very rare.
- *Caranx crysos* (blue runner): 28 adults and juveniles, 1969–1977; considered uncommon.
- *Selar crumenophthalmus* (bigeye scad): seven adults and juveniles; considered very rare.
- *Trachinotus falcatus* (permit): 13 juveniles; considered very rare.
- *Vomer setapinnis* (moonfish): 41 juveniles, 1973–1976; considered uncommon.
- *Chaetodon ocellatus* (spotfin butterflyfish): one juvenile, 1978; considered very rare.
- *Mugil cephalus* (striped mullet): 374 adults and juveniles; present in every study year; considered uncommon.
- *Dactylopterus volitans* (flying gurnard): eight adults and juveniles; considered common.
- *Glyptocephalus cynoglossus* (witch flounder): eight larvae; considered very rare.
- *Hippoglossoides platessoides* (American plaice): ten larvae, 1973–1976; considered very rare.

**Upper Thames Estuary seasonality**
- Saltwater species richness was similar in spring, summer and fall, and lower in winter.
- Many more freshwater species occurred in spring and summer than in fall and winter.
- Freshwater species were more frequently present across all four seasons than saltwater species.
- Over 34% of individuals caught by gill nets in 1972–1973 and over 61% of those taken by seine in 1973 were freshwater species. The authors describe these proportions as far greater than those reported from other upper estuaries.
- The authors attribute this freshwater abundance mainly to the two dams. These restrict upstream movement and keep freshwater fish in the estuary.
- The freshwater-to-saltwater species ratio for the Thames (0.564, as printed) is the highest in the comparison table of upper estuaries.

**Mosquitofish over-wintering (New Haven)**
- Mosquitofish were first found on 12 October 1973 in Campus Pond.
- Specimens were collected from February 1976 to February 1977, and 243 fish were captured during the sampling period.
- No mosquitofish were found in the 19 selected sites in the wider West River watershed. The population appears restricted to the Beaver Ponds.
- Campus Pond never froze completely in 1976. Fish have survived at least six winters, in water kept 2–5 °C above ambient temperatures.
- Populations in Campus Lagoon and Beaver Pond are believed to be replenished by migration from Campus Pond. No successful introductions of this species have been reported elsewhere in Connecticut.

**Freshwater additions**
- *Amia calva* (bowfin) was stocked as a sport fish in lakes. Known populations were chemically treated by the state Department of Environmental Protection. A stocked population reported earlier failed to reproduce.
- *Ictalurus natalis* (yellow bullhead) was common in gill-net samples from Candlewood Lake since 1976.
- *Ictalurus punctatus* (channel catfish) is more widely distributed in the Connecticut River below Enfield Dam than previously reported.
- *Umbra limi* (central mudminnow) has been caught on the intake screens of the Connecticut Yankee Plant at Haddam since 1976.
- Adventitious marine visitors include *Bairdiella chrysura* (silver perch), *Leiostomus xanthurus* (spot), *Menticirrhus saxatilis* (northern kingfish) and *Selene vomer* (lookdown), all recorded in 1970–1973.
- *Dorosoma cepedianum* (gizzard shad), a possible anadromous species, was caught by a commercial fisherman in 1976 about 1.5 miles north of the Connecticut River mouth. It may be moving up the coast from mid-Atlantic stocks.
