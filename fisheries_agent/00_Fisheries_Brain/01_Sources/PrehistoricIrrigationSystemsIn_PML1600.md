# Prehistoric Irrigation Systems in the Salt River Valley, Arizona

#archaeology #hydrology #hohokam-irrigation

## Executive Summary

This Science article (W. Bruce Masse, 1981) reports excavation and aerial-photograph analysis of prehistoric irrigation canals built by the [[Hohokam Culture]] in the [[Salt River Valley]] near Phoenix, Arizona, between roughly A.D. 850 and 1450. Excavations in 1973 and 1978 exposed canals at [[Pueblo Grande]], and [[Herskovitz Study|Herskovitz's]] work in Tempe used aerial photographs to reveal the layout of whole systems. The paper argues that the canals reveal both construction technology and the social organization needed to run them. The document is not about fish or aquatic fauna. It also contains two unrelated items: a news story on an MIT biomedical institute and a prize nomination form.

## Background & Description

The Salt River Valley, along with other southern Arizona valleys, is known for its fertility and suitability for irrigated agriculture. Early work at [[Snaketown]] and [[Gatlin Site|Gatlin]] Ruin revealed the Hohokam canal and village network. Woodbury's 1959 trench through the Four Waters park, Pueblo Grande, gave the first cross-section of two parallel canals. Modern farming and construction have destroyed most canal remains. Early investigators in 1903 noted that most systems were already gone, and the excavator estimates that fewer than 10 km of the roughly 390 km of canals recorded in the lower Salt River survive intact.

Masse's 1976 trenching covered an area about 150 m wide and 800 m long, with more than 3 km of backhoe trench. It confirmed the two Woodbury canals and found several additional prehistoric canals, one of which was located by the [[Arizona State Museum]] in 1978. Table 1 summarizes their morphology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Canal morphology and construction**
- The **north canal** is broad and U-shaped, with bank crests about 26 m apart. A clay layer 5 to 9 cm thick in its fill was hypothesized to be a hand-applied lining to reduce seepage. Masse did not find evidence of intentional lining.
- The **south canal** is V-shaped, about 6 m wide at ground level, 4 m deep, with bank crests about 19 m apart. Its bank heights stand 2 to 3 m above the former ground surface.
- The north and south canals were originally more than 14 km and 11 km long, respectively.
- Cross-sectional shape (trapezoidal versus parabolic) varies across canals. Trapezoidal sections tend to occur in the smaller canals, with no consistent difference otherwise. Both main canals probably carried similar water volumes.

**Sediments and soils**
- Lighter deposits are mostly sands and sandy silts with little organic carbon. Darker deposits contain clays and clayey silts with more organic carbon, probably from aquatic plant growth that slowed flow, periodic burning of weeds, and charcoal flecks.
- Sediments in most canals were discontinuous over just a few meters, so depositional profiles varied sharply.
- Four canals had black or yellowish-black stains of manganese oxide, limonite, and magnetite in their basal deposits, which helped trace them.

**Palynology and vegetation**
- Pollen analysis was only partly successful because pollen in moving water is hard to interpret.
- Cheno-am species (Chenopodiaceae and Amaranthus), such as amaranth, goosefoot, and saltbush, dominated the local vegetation, typical of disturbed, moist floodplain soils.
- Cattail (Typha) pollen in most deposits suggests slow water flow even in the largest canals.
- Only three corn (Zea) grains were recovered. The authors suggest the canals mainly conveyed water to fields elsewhere.

**Flooding and drought**
- Severe floods at Pueblo Grande, including the 1980 floods, are thought to have destroyed canal segments repeatedly. Sequential remodeling, rather than contemporaneous use, is therefore likely for many canals.
- Euler et al. documented severe drought in the northern Southwest around the time of the south canal's construction. A drop in the river's flow or channel entrenchment could also have driven the need for deeper canals.

**Water distribution**
- The system uses a main canal fed from a permanent or semipermanent water source (the Salt River), with distribution canals and lateral canals spaced about 45 to 60 m apart, running perpendicular to the slope.
- This layout suits "wild flooding," in which thin sheets of water cover plots, with excess shifted downslope and lost at the system's end.

## Key Findings

1. **Two parallel canals at Pueblo Grande differ in shape but probably carried similar volumes**, and the paper argues the south canal did not function as a drain for the north canal, contrary to Woodbury's hypothesis.
2. **Woodbury's observations were partly confirmed and partly revised.** Masse found no evidence of intentional lining in the north canal, but found three distinct clay lenses where Woodbury saw discontinuous bands.
3. **Several prehistoric canals were found in the 1976 trenching area**, adding to the two known at Pueblo Grande. [[Hagenstad Canal]], the largest known pre-Classic canal in the valley, was built, remodeled twice, and abandoned within the Sedentary period, most likely between about A.D. 1000 and 1150.
4. **Canal 6 and the highest level of canal 5 appear to be part of a small-scale system built no earlier than the Classic period.** The author tentatively links these "echo canals" to the protohistoric [[Piman Indians]] (A.D. 1450 to 1850).
5. **Herskovitz's Tempe study** used aerial photographs to show scores of canal traces in fallow fields. Of 13 canals studied, five were first identified in the aerial photographs (five Classic-period canals excavated, eight others tested).
6. **Hohokam irrigation systems were organized as a hierarchy**: a main canal, subsequent branches, and the villages it served. At least ten main canals were used simultaneously in the Tempe area during the Classic period.
7. **Estimated service areas** for individual systems ranged from 1,000 to 10,000 hectares (10 to 100 km²), with the largest serving the Los Muertos, Alta Vista, and Casa Loma villages.
8. **Pollen and sediment data suggest the canals were not primarily for crops grown at Pueblo Grande**, since domestic pollen was rare. Palynology can also separate pre-1870 canals from later ones, using the presence of introduced ornamentals (pine and juniper pollen).
9. **Social implications:** The author argues that building and maintaining such systems required coordination, possibly of the "community" type. Evidence for centralized control is weak. Piman and Hohokam water management differed in that Piman villages were organized around kinship, while Hohokam settlements were linked to larger irrigation networks.
