## Executive Summary

This document is a field protocol issued by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] governing the handling, holding, and transport of wild [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), a critically endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. Rather than a primary morphological or ecological study, the text functions as an operational management guide: it establishes strict morphometric criteria for field identification of bonytail (to distinguish it from sympatric congeners such as [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]]), specifies required field equipment, and details a chain-of-custody protocol moving captured fish from river capture sites to temporary holding facilities ([[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]], [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]]) and ultimately to the permanent conservation facility at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico. The protocol reflects the rarity and conservation priority of the species — every wild individual encountered is to be retained alive for the species refugium/broodstock program.

## Background & Description

Field identification of bonytail requires strict adherence to combined length, meristic, and morphometric criteria (per Minckley 1973; measurement methods per Lagler, Bardach & Miller 1977, *Ichthyology*):

- **Minimum total length**: 300 mm
- **Fin-ray counts**: Dorsal/anal fin-ray count of 10/10 or greater
- **Caudal peduncle morphometrics** (either criterion satisfies identification):
  - a) Caudal peduncle length ÷ head length > 1.0, **or**
  - b) Head length ÷ depth of caudal peduncle > 5.0

These ratios reflect the diagnostic slender, attenuated [[Caudal Peduncle]] characteristic of *Gila elegans*, distinguishing it from other members of the genus *Gila* in the basin. Fish are to be anesthetized (MS-222 or Quinaldine) prior to any meristic counts or morphometric measurements to minimize handling stress.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution / sympatry**: Bonytail co-occurs with [[Humpback Chub]] in specific "sensitive reaches" of the Upper Colorado River Basin, and its capture is anticipated wherever recent humpback chub captures have been documented. Designated high-priority reaches include:
  - Upper Colorado River: Westwater Canyon (RM 111–125), Cataract Canyon, Moab Canyon (RM 72), Black Rocks (RM 135–136)
  - Green River: Gray Canyon (RM 146–171), Whirlpool Canyon (RM 342)
  - Yampa River: Yampa Canyon to Cross Mountain (RM 18–56)
- **Habitat associations**: Large-river canyon reaches with active mainstem flow; also uses isolated earthen ponds and groundwater-fed tanks as artificial refugia during captivity.
- **Biotic threats noted**: Susceptibility to *[[Lernaea]]* (anchor worm) parasitism, as well as abrasions, fungal infection, and secondary bacterial invasion during handling and captivity — indicating vulnerability to stress-related pathology when removed from the wild.
- **Conservation role**: The species occupies a niche of extreme rarity within its native range, prompting an ex-situ refugium strategy (temporary holding → permanent hatchery stock) rather than reliance on in-river population recovery alone.

## Key Findings

- **Zero-release policy for identified bonytail**: All wild bonytail meeting identification criteria are retained alive rather than released, reflecting the species' critically low population status circa 1989.
- **Mandatory field equipment kit** for crews sampling likely bonytail habitat: 100–150 quart cooler, portable oxygen cylinder(s) with regulator/diffuser, live net, vernier calipers, morphometric identification key, and anesthetic (MS-222 or Quinaldine).
- **Handling minimization principle**: Data collection (length, weight, photographs) is deprioritized relative to fish welfare; specimen data collection is deferred until the fish reaches a holding facility unless the fish must be released back to the river.
- **Thermal and water-quality management**: Aeration is applied only if water temperature exceeds air temperature; cooling via ice or submersion of holding containers is prescribed; water changes are minimized and only triggered by visible respiratory distress (rapid ventilation, surface gaping).
- **Prohibition on abrasive gear**: Tarred or knotted holding nets are explicitly disallowed due to risk of skin/mucous membrane abrasion, which predisposes fish to fungal and bacterial infection.
- **Multi-stage transport chain**: Capture site → cooler/field holding → 250-gallon fish distribution tank (truck-mounted) → temporary holding facility (Ouray NWR or Hotchkiss NFH, subbasin-dependent) → permanent facility at Dexter NFH within two weeks, via air or ground transport.
- **Parasite protocol**: Visible *Lernaea* must be manually removed under anesthesia; occurrence must be documented and communicated to the permanent facility to ensure follow-up treatment for potentially unremoved immature parasites.
- **Institutional coordination**: Distinct notification chains exist for the [[Green River]]/[[Yampa River]] subbasin (Vernal CRFP, contact: Harold Tyus) versus the [[Colorado River]] subbasin (Grand Junction CRFP, contact: Lynn Kaeding), with a required one-week advance notice prior to sampling trips in sensitive reaches.
- **Governance**: Protocol developed under the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], with technical consultation from species experts including Dr. W. L. Minckley and Dr. Paul Marsh (Arizona State University).
