# Vanishing Climax Communities of the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #wetland-ecology #ciénega

## Executive Summary

This special issue of *Desert Plants* (Vol. 6, No. 3, 1984; issued early 1985) presents a historical and ecological review by [[Dean A. Hendrickson]] (Arizona State University) and [[W. L. Minckley]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Arizona State University) of the **ciénega**, a spring-fed, permanently saturated wetland of southeastern Arizona. The authors argue that ciénegas are an aquatic climax community, that they have declined sharply over the past century, and that both livestock grazing and climatic change, with arroyo cutting as the main physical mechanism, contributed to their loss. The paper compiles historical accounts, cultural history, a plant checklist, and a conceptual model of stream succession, and it closes with a call to give ciénegas high conservation priority.

## Background & Description

- **Definition:** The authors apply "ciénega" to mid-elevation (1,000–2,000 m) wetlands fed by perennial springs and headwater streams. These sites have permanently saturated, highly organic, reducing soils and a depauperate flora dominated by low sedges (*Eleocharis* spp.). Historically the term also covered other marshy habitats, which the authors separate into three types: alpine/montane meadowlands (>2,000 m), ciénegas (1,000–2,000 m), and riverine marshes (<1,000 m).
- **Study area:** Arizona south of the Gila River and east of, and including, the Avra-Altar Valley. Drainages include the [[San Simon Valley]], [[Sulphur Springs Valley]], [[San Pedro Valley]], [[Santa Cruz Valley]], and the middle [[Gila River]].
- **Plant diversity:** Table 1 lists a preliminary checklist of macrophytic taxa from ciénega habitats in southeastern Arizona, drawn from literature and herbarium records. It spans stoneworts, horsetails, ferns, pondweeds, grasses, sedges, rushes, and dicots such as watercress (*Rorippa nasturtium-aquaticum*). The most important entries include [[Eleocharis Sedges]], [[Bulrush and Three-Square]], and [[Goodding Willow]].
- **Cultural history:** Four cultural forces shaped the region: prehistoric and Indigenous peoples (including the [[Hohokam]] and [[Sobaipuri]] Pima), Spanish colonization and missions (Kino from 1687 onward), Mexican ranching after 1821, and Anglo-American settlement and the cattle boom after the 1880s railway expansion.
- **Terminology:** The authors note that *ciénega* is often translated as "marsh" or "swamp," but it has a more specific meaning. One etymology, given in Mathews' *Dictionary of Americanisms*, derives it from *cien agúas* ("a hundred springs").

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Ciénegas depend on permanent, scarcely fluctuating groundwater intersecting the surface, usually where flash-flood scour risk is low. Faults along mountain fronts and impervious dikes force groundwater to the surface. Dynamic equilibrium concepts (Chorley 1962; Bull 1979) and a critical power threshold (C.P.T.) model explain where ciénegas can persist.
- **Succession:** The authors propose a successional scheme in which stable, perennial discharge leads from bare or flooded substrates through tall emergent marsh stages to a sedge-dominated ciénega climax. Ciénegas then store and slowly release water, dampening flood peaks and sustaining downstream flow.
- **Climate and paleoecology:** Pollen records from Croton Spring and Lake Cochise indicate pluvial-period vegetation shifts. Pine forests were displaced 900–1,200 m downslope during Wisconsin time (about 20,000 to 29,000 years before present). Ciénega conditions persisted for more than 4,000 years in some basins, with infrequent cycles of incision.
- **Rainfall:** Annual precipitation ranges from 13 to 49 cm, with bimodal summer and winter rainfall.
- **Fauna:** Ciénegas are refugia for relict aquatic invertebrates such as hydrobiid snails (*Tryonia*, *Fontelicella*). They also support fishes of the genus *Gila*, and the endangered Sonoran topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) occurs in only a few such sites. Riverine fishes such as the Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker were associated with the riverine marsh zone.
- **Grazing and erosion:** Overgrazing in 1891–93 coincided with a drought that caused livestock mortality of 50–75%. Cattle concentrated along water courses, trampling sponge-like organic soils and promoting drying. Arroyo cutting then followed in many basins.

## Key Findings

- **Decline:** Ciénegas and marshlands have declined greatly in Arizona in the past century. Most historic sites now survive only as remnants, artificially maintained, or are dry.
- **Historical extent:** Early accounts (Parke 1857; Bartlett 1854; Cooke 1938) describe unincised braided streams with broad marshes before about 1880–1890. Historical maps and photographs support a much wider distribution than exists today.
- **San Simon Ciénega:** Once covered over 485 ha. A 1953 BLM concrete dam now maintains artificial open water. Discharge was 9.9 × 10⁶ m³/yr in 1952. Forty-six aquatic and semiaquatic taxa were documented at Hooker Ciénega.
- **Groundwater:** Groundwater pumpage in the Willcox area rose from 2.5 × 10⁵ m³/yr (first four decades of the century) to about 3.2 × 10⁶ m³/yr (1957–1975). The Douglas Valley groundwater exploitation began in 1910.
- **Cattle numbers:** Governor Tritle's 1885 claim that tax assessments underestimated herds by at least 50% supports the authors' calculation of about 37,574 cattle grazing Cochise, Santa Cruz, Pima, and Graham counties over the five years before 1893. Recent cattle numbers were about 180,200 (1977–1981). Cattle rarely travel more than 5 km from water, so stock tanks likely shifted grazing pressure away from natural waters.
- **Climate vs. grazing:** The authors reviewed the debate over whether incision was caused by climate or grazing (Antevs, Bryan, Cooke and Reeves, Patton and Schumm). They conclude that a combination of factors, including cattle concentration along watercourses in 1891–93, drove the incision.
- **Riverine marsh and ciénega difference:** Ciénegas occur in headwaters (low-order streams), while riverine marshes occur at lower elevations along high-order, perennial channels. Riverine marshes are considered transitory, removed by floods or channel migration.
- **Restoration:** Ciénega conditions could be restored at historic sites by providing a constant water supply and reducing catastrophic flooding. Restoration may require artificial deepening, controlled grazing, or erosion-control structures placed at groundwater barriers near headwaters.
- **Conservation:** The authors call ciénegas a unique, highly endangered remnant of the natural heritage and recommend high conservation priority. Natural Area proposals were made for several sites, including [[O'Donnell Ciénega]] (managed by The Nature Conservancy with controlled burning to protect the rare orchid *Spiranthes graminea*), Babocomari Ciénega, Cook's Lake, St. David marsh, and Arivaca Ciénega.
- **Editorial conclusion:** Editor Frank S. Crosswhite notes that the special issue crystallizes the ciénega concept and demonstrates its uniqueness as an ecological unit of the Desert Southwest (see Table 2 in the paper).
