## Executive Summary

This document is a scientific rebuttal by Dr. Jim Deacon (University of Nevada–Las Vegas) to a 1998 report by the Washington County Water Conservancy District (WCWCD) concerning operation of the [[Quail Creek Reservoir Project]] on the [[Virgin River]]. The core scientific dispute centers on minimum flow requirements established to protect the endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and the [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*). Deacon argues that the 86 cubic feet per second (cfs) minimum flow stipulation in the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) [[Biological Opinion]] was based on biological research (his own work and that of student Thomas Hardy) rather than on protection of downstream water rights, as WCWCD claimed. The document synthesizes flow-recruitment relationships for woundfin and asserts that Quail Creek Project operations have contributed to, though are not the sole cause of, native fish decline in the Virgin River.

## Background & Description

The source text is a policy/water-management document and does not describe external morphology in detail. However, it references habitat-use studies distinguishing life-stage requirements:

- **Juvenile woundfin**: Maximum usable habitat (Weighted Useable Area, WUA) occurred at approximately **90 cfs** in the LaVerkin Springs-to-Washington Diversion reach (Hardy and Deacon 1982).
- **Adult woundfin**: Maximum usable habitat occurred at approximately **85 cfs** in the same reach.
- **Fry (larval) woundfin**: Habitat availability maximized near **200 cfs**, a discharge described as not occurring "naturally" in the altered river due to upstream withdrawals at LaVerkin and Hurricane diversions.

These flow-habitat relationships were derived using Instream Flow Incremental Methodology (IFIM)-style hydraulic datasets provided by USFWS and the Utah Division of Wildlife, applied to reach-specific channel geometry rather than direct morphological description of the fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Woundfin]] occupies swift, shallow, sand/gravel-bottomed reaches of the [[Virgin River]], historically including the ~12-mile segment from [[LaVerkin Springs]] to the [[Washington Fields Diversion]] — identified in the Biological Opinion as the "uppermost portion of known woundfin habitat." Key ecological findings include:

- **Flow–recruitment relationship**: Deacon (1979) found mean spring flows **below 100 cfs** were associated with poor reproductive success and poor recruitment.
- **Optimal recruitment window**: Deacon and Hardy (1984, cited as "in press" in the BO) found juvenile recruitment was most successful when mean monthly streamflow fell **between 200 and 800 cfs**.
- **Two-reach habitat framework**: Analysis divided the river into (1) a 1.5-mile segment from Pah Tempe Springs to the mouth of Ash Creek, and (2) a 12.5-mile segment from Ash Creek to the Washington Diversion — reflecting cumulative tributary inputs (LaVerkin Springs, Ash Creek) that modify mainstem flow available to woundfin habitat.
- **Seasonal flow sensitivity**: Reduction of summer/early-fall flows was flagged as especially detrimental "unless these flows are in excess of several hundred cfs," indicating strong seasonal dependence of habitat suitability tied to spawning/growth phenology.
- **Diversion timing constraint**: The operational stipulation specified that water would be diverted to the reservoir "only during periods of high flow," implicitly protecting baseflow conditions critical to fish persistence.
- **Drought interaction**: Reduced flow variability and reduced minimum flows from reservoir operation were argued to compound (not replace) natural drought effects — described as flows being "both simulated and exacerbated by water diversion" (Williams and Deacon 1998).

## Key Findings

- **Regulatory basis**: The Quail Creek Project's BLM right-of-way permit legally bound WCWCD to operate the reservoir "as presented for the analysis of impacts... by Dr. James Deacon and Thomas Hardy," making their scientific analysis the operative regulatory standard, not merely advisory.
- **86 cfs origin**: The minimum flow of 86 cfs (or natural flow, if lower) was set based on convergence between Deacon's earlier Warner Valley Project recommendation (80–100 cfs) and Hardy and Deacon's (1982) finding of near-maximal juvenile/adult habitat at 90/85 cfs — a biologically, not legally, derived figure, despite ambiguous wording in the BO referencing "downstream water rights."
- **Literature basis of the Biological Opinion**: Of eight references cited in the BO, seven were authored by Deacon and/or his students; the eighth (Miller and Hubbs) also concerned native Virgin River fishes. No water-rights literature was cited, undermining WCWCD's claim that the flow stipulation was rights-driven rather than biologically driven.
- **Scope of flow requirement**: The 86 cfs minimum was intended to apply across the **entire 12-mile reach** from LaVerkin Springs to Washington Diversion — not solely at the point of diversion — since the underlying Hardy and Deacon (1982) analysis incorporated flow contributions from Pah Tempe Springs, LaVerkin Creek, and Ash Creek, and explicitly excluded any releases from Quail Creek Reservoir itself.
- **Contributing vs. sole cause**: Deacon explicitly frames Quail Creek Project operations as a **contributing**, not sole, cause of native fish decline, citing peer-reviewed support (Deacon 1979, 1988; Gregory and Deacon 1994) and additional non-peer-reviewed technical reports (Holden and Zucker 1996; Minckley et al. 1997; Williams and Deacon 1998).
- **Competing hypothesis (Hardy et al. 1995)**: Work by Hardy and Craig Addley attributed woundfin decline primarily to drought rather than reservoir operations, but per Deacon this conclusion was not peer-reviewed nor independently corroborated.
- **Independent corroboration**: Three independent analyses (Holden and Zucker 1996; Gregory and Deacon 1994; Williams and Deacon 1998) converged on the conclusion that reduced flow variability and minimum flows from Quail Creek Diversion operation — compounded by diversions on lower LaVerkin and Ash Creeks — best explain the observed 20-year decline in woundfin population density, particularly the more severe impact of the 1989–1992 drought compared to the 1974–1977 drought under pre-dam conditions.
- **Loophole identified**: Under the operational criteria as analyzed, if natural flow throughout the reach (including at Washington Diversion) falls below 86 cfs, WCWCD is not obligated by the Biological Opinion or Environmental Assessment to release water from Quail Creek Reservoir to compensate — a critical interpretive point in the dispute.
