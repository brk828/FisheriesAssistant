# Why Fisheries Collapse and What to Do About It

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gadidae

## Executive Summary

This Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences paper by Roughgarden and Smith (1996) argues that conventional economic management of renewable fisheries drives stocks toward an unstable equilibrium, making collapse likely. The authors propose managing for [[Ecological Stability]] by setting the target stock at three-quarters of the unharvested carrying capacity (3/4 K), harvesting only when the stock is above the [[Maximum Sustainable Yield]], and using a market-based dockside tax on fish caught when the stock is below target. They illustrate the approach with the collapsed [[Newfoundland Cod Fishery]], estimating a rebuilding period of about 9 years, after which an annual harvest of about 75% of the 1981–1990 average could be sustained. The paper addresses management theory and policy design; it does not weigh the relative importance of the many causes of collapse.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** In October 1994 the [[New England Fishery Management Council]] closed Georges Bank to fishing for cod, haddock and yellowtail flounder, following a 1993 closure of the Grand Bank off Newfoundland to selected groundfish. The authors describe these as part of a worldwide overfishing condition.
- **Quota history:** From 1973 the Canadian [[Department of Fisheries and Oceans]] set annual [[Total Allowable Catch]] (TAC) limits on Newfoundland cod. Fishers did not exceed the TACs (Fig. 1), yet the fishery collapsed. Figure 1 divides history into three phases: 1960–1980, 1981–1990 and 1991–1993, with harvest highest in the first phase and collapse in the third.
- **Proposed causes cited in the literature:** political unwillingness to set adequate quotas, optimistic stock assessments, foreign poaching, unusual natural predation, climate change, subsidies, and overcapitalization after the 200-mile limit. The authors take no position on their relative importance.
- **Model:** The stock follows a logistic model, dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K) − h, with intrinsic growth rate r, carrying capacity K, and interest rate p. The example uses r = 0.25, K = 1000 and p = 0.05. Production peaks at K/2, the [[Maximum Sustainable Yield]] point.
- **Economic optimum:** Following [[Natural Resource Economics]], the optimal stock is where the slope of the production function equals the interest rate, giving N* = (K/2)(1 − p/r). In the example N* = 400 and the sustainable harvest is 60 fish per unit time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Instability at the economic optimum:** Under constant harvest, a stock at the economic optimum is unstable. Small declines drive it toward extinction, while the stock increases only when it sits above the threshold. The authors compare this to balancing a marble on a dome.
- **Environmental variability:** Interannual variation makes the production curve shift, with r and K both high in good years and low in bad years. Quota data are criticized for inaccuracy and for using censuses a year old.
- **Recommended stable target:** Setting the target at (3/4)K, midway between K/2 and K, yields a stable equilibrium under constant harvest. In the example, the stock at 750 sustains a harvest of 47 fish per unit time, and it returns to 750 after fluctuations between 250 and 1000.
- **Stability measures:** Eigenvalues show that stability increases as the target approaches K. Managing for constant effort is always at least as stable as managing for constant harvest, though the difference vanishes as the target approaches K.
- **Stock-size-dependent costs:** For Newfoundland cod, costs that vary with stock size change the economically optimal stock by only 0.4%, so they can be ignored.

## Key Findings

1. **Economic optimum is ecologically fragile.** Managing for maximum economic return places the target on the unstable side of the production hump, so a stock can slide toward collapse.
2. **Management goal:** Target a stock near (3/4)K, to the right of the hump, and harvest only when the stock is above target. This is "like keeping a marble near the base of a bowl."
3. **Cost of insurance:** The cost of "natural insurance" is the interest forgone by holding extra stock plus the lower harvest. It is affordable when r > 4p. When r = 10p, the cost is about 2% of the fishery's capital value. For Newfoundland cod the cost is about 1.77% of capital value.
4. **Simulation results:** In a 50-year simulation with random variation in r, K, stock estimates and quota execution, the best target was about 475. Targets to the right of the hump gave the highest long-term returns under risk. Simulation results are cited as showing that natural insurance raises long-term returns.
5. **Implementation via market tax:** A dockside tax is levied only on fish caught when the stock is below (3/4)K. This aligns short-term profit with long-term stability and gives a graded control.
6. **Adjusted price schedule:** The authors propose an adjusted price P_a(N) = aN^b, which makes the financially optimal stock equal the ecological target. For Newfoundland, a = 0.11 and b = 3.81 × 10⁻⁹ are derived, with P_a(3K/4) = 1.90, P_a(K/2) = 0.48 and P_a(K/4) = 0.23 (values as stated in the source).
7. **Newfoundland parameters:** r ≈ 1 per year (estimated from maximum yearly stock increase of about threefold); K ≈ 1.4 million tons (about 25 tons per km² over 550,000 km²); price ≈ C$550 per ton; p = 0.05.
8. **Economic optimum for Newfoundland:** N* ≈ 665,000 tons (market value C$366 million), sustaining an annual harvest of 349,000 tons worth C$192 million; capital value C$3.84 billion.
9. **Ecological target for Newfoundland:** 1,050,000 tons, sustaining an annual harvest of 263,000 tons worth C$144 million, with target fishing mortality of 0.25 (r/4).
10. **Rebuilding timeline:** Rebuilding to the ecological target is estimated at about 9 years of moratorium (5 years for stock maturation, 1–2 years for growth to harvestable size, and about 3 years for spawning age). The authors recommend retaining the moratorium 2 years beyond the DFO's envisioned period.
11. **Social cost:** Recovery entails social costs (unemployment, fish-processing workers, special programs). Earlier estimates cited in the source are about C$1.6 billion (1994–1996) for workers and C$2.5 billion (1994–1999) for unemployment insurance, and the authors note a second collapse would repeat these costs.
12. **Summary recommendation:** Establish a target stock at 3/4 of average unharvested abundance and levy a dockside tax on fish caught when the stock is below target.
