# Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan: Rio Yaqui Basin, United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #poeciliidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan guides the recovery of three federally listed fishes of the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]]: [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*, endangered), [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Notropis formosus*, threatened) and [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*, threatened). It also addresses the endangered Yaqui form of the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*), candidate species, and other biotic elements of the upper basin. The plan centers on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (NWR), where critical habitat has been designated. The plan's stated goal is self-sustaining populations of at least six Rio Yaqui fish species on the refuge within 10 years. Its main threats are habitat loss from arroyo cutting, groundwater pumping and dams, and predation and competition from non-native fishes, especially [[Mosquitofish]].

## Background & Description

The [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] covers about 73,000 km² of mountainous northwestern Mexico. The river flows from western Chihuahua through eastern Sonora to the [[Gulf of California]] near Ciudad Obregón. Only a small fraction of the watershed lies in the United States, in Cochise County, Arizona, and Hidalgo County, New Mexico. Historically, the Arizona fauna lived in streams, springs and [[Cienega]] wetlands of the [[Douglas Valley]] and San Bernardino Valley. The closed [[Sulphur Springs Basin]] once connected southward to the Rio Yaqui.

The plan covers the following taxa:

- **Yaqui chub**: small, deep-bodied cyprinid under 15 cm TL. Breeding males turn steel blue while females stay straw colored. The species is listed as Group II by Arizona and "vulnerable" by IUCN. The Desert Fishes Council (1985) called it "indeterminate."
- **Beautiful shiner**: small cyprinid, rarely over 75 mm TL. Breeding males are brightly colored and females are drab. Historic records in the United States are limited to Arizona and the Mimbres River, New Mexico. The fish was last found in Arizona in 1970. Its biology is poorly known.
- **Yaqui catfish**: moderately large (to 50 cm TL) forked-tail catfish. It was introduced to the upper Santa Cruz basin in the late 1800s and was extirpated by the late 1950s. Its biology is unknown.
- **Sonoran topminnow (Yaqui form)**: live-bearer, males under 25 mm TL and females 30–40 mm. The Yaqui form is endangered and shares Arizona refugia with the Gila form.
- **Candidates for listing**: [[Mexican Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma ornatum*), [[Yaqui Sucker]] (*Catostomus bernardini*), a population of which was extirpated from the United States.
- **Other biotic elements**: [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*, Rio Yaqui form, to be described as a subspecies), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*, Yaqui occurrence in the United States debated) and the endemic [[San Bernardino Ranch Snail]] (*Fontelicella* n. sp.).

The refuge was first purchased by The Nature Conservancy in 1979. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service took ownership in 1982 and bought the San Bernardino Ranch to perpetuate native Yaqui fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat diversity.** Mountain creeks are cold and clear. Most fishes live at intermediate to low elevations in springs, cienegas, creeks and moderate-sized rivers. Streams alternate riffles and pools, with undercut banks and cover from boulders and woody debris. Big rivers near the sea support marine forms that move inland.

**Refuge water sources.** Aquatic habitats include natural seeps at House and Tule ponds and Snail Spring, artesian flows, impounded waters in House, Twin and North ponds, about 2.4 km of permanent flow in [[Black Draw]], and intermittent flow in Hay Hollow Wash.

**Historical change.**
- Spanish-era and later cattle grazing and logging degraded uplands. Sawmills and mine runoff polluted streams.
- [[Arroyo Cutting]] around 1900 incised channels and turned permanent streams intermittent. Cienegas, which buffer flows, were largely destroyed. Floods in the three decades around 1900 were greater than earlier ones.
- Agricultural groundwater pumping began in Douglas Valley in 1910 and covered more than 200 km² by 1965.
- The 1887 earthquake briefly altered springs and flows.
- Three mainstream reservoirs now regulate the Mexican Rio Yaqui. Non-native fishes are less common in Mexico than in the southwestern United States, but they are present.

**Biological interactions.** Mosquitofish appeared in the Arizona basin in the 1970s. They prey on young and adults and displace native fishes. Yaqui topminnow declined in House Pond. Stocked [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], black crappie and bullhead catfish also compete with or prey on native fishes.

## Key Findings

**Extirpations and their causes**
- Habitat loss after arroyo cutting eliminated most native species from [[Black Draw]]. In 1944, Black Draw had no pools when Minckley first sampled it. Later sampling found only longfin dace and topminnow, persisting in no more than three isolated pools in the 1970s.
- Pumping in the [[Sulphur Springs Basin]] desiccated Croton Spring and Cienega, destroying longfin dace populations.
- Cattle trampling and reduced flow at Astin Spring extirpated large populations of Yaqui sucker, chub and topminnow. The spring dried in 1949 according to the main text, and in 1969 according to Appendix I.
- Capping an artesian well around 1970 destroyed a breeding run and cienega for beautiful shiner. The shiner then was forced into a pond with predatory sunfish and bass. No beautiful shiner specimen has been taken in the United States since.

**Conservation actions**
- About 200 adult Yaqui chub and 200 Yaqui topminnow were moved from Astin Spring to [[Leslie Creek]] in 1949.
- Leslie Creek chub (226 individuals) were moved to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in 1976. Hatchery stock then restored populations on the refuge.
- Proposed lakes in Leslie Canyon would have inundated Leslie Creek. Construction was disallowed for hydrologic reasons. Leslie Creek water-right claims (1,000 acre-feet/year) already exceed average runoff by about 350 acre-feet/year.
- Arizona prohibits live bait in Cochise County and restricts fish transport without a special permit.

**Recovery criteria (from the plan)**

| Species | Downlist | Delist |
|---|---|---|
| Yaqui chub | Six stable populations on the refuge, plus secure Leslie and Turkey creek populations | Secure, self-sustaining populations in historic range |
| Beautiful shiner | n/a | Five stable populations on the refuge; Guzman shiner reestablished in the Mimbres River; viable populations in Mexico |
| Yaqui catfish | n/a | Four stable populations on the refuge; viable populations in historic range |
| Yaqui topminnow | Full federal control of refuge habitat; mosquitofish and other exotics removed; populations stable | Viable populations in Mexico |

Candidate and other elements: maintain three Mexican stoneroller populations (including Rucker Canyon), four Yaqui sucker populations, four longfin dace populations (Rucker Canyon, Leslie Creek and Turkey Creek stocks), one roundtail chub population and two San Bernardino Ranch snail populations on the refuge.

**Data gaps.** Biology, life history and past and present abundance are unknown for Yaqui catfish, beautiful shiner and San Bernardino Ranch snail. Roundtail chub's former status in the United States is uncertain. Mexican populations were not surveyed until 1978.
