# Early Life History of the Gulf Menhaden (*Brevoortia patronus*) in Louisiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 paper by [[Royal D. Suttkus]] of Tulane University examines the early life history of the [[Gulf Menhaden]] (*Brevoortia patronus*) in [[Lake Pontchartrain]], Louisiana, based on sampling from July 1953 to May 1955. The study concludes that menhaden do not spawn within the lake. Instead, larvae enter the lake in winter, remain through their first summer, and emigrate in late summer to autumn. The paper also provides a detailed larval and juvenile pigmentation and meristic series, proportional body measurements compared with [[Hildebrand (1948)]], and a brief discussion of how small larvae move against currents into estuaries.

## Background & Description

- **Study context:** Material came from a cooperative study between the Tulane University Zoology Department and the Louisiana Wildlife and Fisheries Commission (Commercial Seafoods Division).
- **Species context:** The paper compares the Gulf Menhaden with the northern [[Atlantic Menhaden]] (*Brevoortia tyrannus*), whose spawning was reported by [[Kuntz and Radcliffe (1917)]] as occurring in summer, unlike the Louisiana population.
- **Larval description:** Specimens (Tulane collection Tu 7129, 8109, 9671, 9255) ranged from 18.9 mm to 58.4 mm standard length. Diagnostic features tracked through development include:
  - Melanophore patterns on the ventrolateral body, isthmus, anal fin base, and caudal peduncle.
  - A ringed gut tract that forms the ventral body margin, which the author argues Kuntz and Radcliffe misrepresented as convoluted.
  - Maxillary serrations (16 to 19 in small larvae), which disappear with growth. Blunt serrations persist to about 40 mm and are absent by 58.4 mm.
  - Ventral scutes that develop progressively from anterior to posterior, with spines appearing around 24 to 25 mm.
  - Operculal striae (one at 25.5 mm, 11 at 40.3 mm and 58.4 mm).
  - Scales appearing at about 26.8 mm.
- **Gill rakers:** Counts rose from 15 on the lower arch of the 18.9 mm specimen to more than 150 in 200 mm specimens (per Hildebrand, 1948).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning and recruitment:** Spawning is presumed (not directly observed) to occur from October to February. Larvae of 20 to 30 mm enter the lake from December through March, and first appeared in December of each winter studied.
- **Emigration:** Zero-year-class fish leave estuarine waters in August or September, with some possibly staying until October or later. The author notes this timing matches [[Westman and Nigrelli (1955)]] for *B. tyrannus* in New York and New Jersey waters. Emigration from Lake Pontchartrain is more complete than that reported for the [[Atlantic Croaker]] by [[Suttkus (1954)]].
- **Salinity influence:** The moderate November 1954 catch was possibly linked to salinities of 18 to 20 ppt during easterly and southeasterly winds.
- **Transformation period:** Metamorphosis ends at about 28 to 30 mm standard length. During this period, most body parts grow differentially; after it, growth is proportional to length. Pectoral-to-pelvic insertion distance remains proportionally constant throughout, indicating fin positions are fixed early.
- **Sampling:** Fine-meshed push nets, trawls and seines were used. A total of 184 specimens were measured for proportional data, from 13 catalogued lots collected across the lake (roughly 30°02' to 30°16' N).

## Key Findings

1. **No local spawning:** Menhaden do not spawn in Lake Pontchartrain. Larvae immigrate from outside waters each winter.
2. **Seasonal timing:** Presumed spawning runs October to February, with larval entry December to March. No summer spawning occurs, unlike northern populations.
3. **Seasonal emigration:** Young-of-year fish leave in August to October, a more complete emigration than in the Atlantic croaker.
4. **Morphological development:** Melanophore patterns, gut structure, serrations, scutes, striae and scales give a detailed developmental sequence from 18.9 to 58.4 mm.
5. **Corrected gut description:** The gut is a uniformly ringed tube, not convoluted as previously illustrated.
6. **Proportional measurements vs. Hildebrand (1948):** Lake Pontchartrain specimens fall at the lower end of Hildebrand's ranges for head depth, head length, body depth, maxillary length, caudal lobe and pectoral fin length, and show less variability, as expected from a single local population.
   - Example: body depth 33% (Hildebrand 37–42%) versus 33–41% in this study (comparison values as tabulated).
7. **Current-driven movement:** The discussion notes that the young appear able to hold position against tidal currents. Participants suggested passive transport by currents, possibly aided by streamlined body form, and compared the case with croaker larvae transported up Chesapeake Bay.
