# Coyote Predation on Aquatic Turtles

#herpetology #mammalogy #canidae #emydidae #trionychidae

## Executive Summary

This short field note, reprinted from the *Journal of Mammalogy* (Vol. 47, No. 1, 12 March 1966, p. 137), documents direct and indirect evidence of [[Coyote]] (*Canis latrans*) predation on aquatic turtles at a shallow lake in Coahuila, Mexico. The author observed one coyote capturing and mauling a [[Pond Slider]] (*Pseudemys scripta*) on 15 April 1965, and recorded remains of nine *Pseudemys* and two *Trionyx* sp. along the shoreline. The note also contrasts predation on these species with the apparent lack of predation on [[Coahuila Box Turtle]] (*Terrapene coahuila*), which is discussed as a possible result of its shell morphology. The observations were made during other research funded by NSF Grant No. GB246I-W. The author is listed as W. L. Minckley, Department of Zoology, Arizona State University, Tempe. The paper was accepted 1 June 1965.

## Background & Description

The note is a first-hand observational account, not a formal study with replicated sampling. It covers two sets of observations at the same locality:

- **15 April 1965:** A detailed sequence of a coyote hunting a [[Pond Slider]] in shallow water, followed by a shoreline survey of remains.
- **25 December 1965:** A live [[Coahuila Box Turtle]] found on its back in shallow water, with coyote tracks nearby.

The species involved are:

- **Coyote** (*Canis latrans*), the predator.
- **Pond slider** (*Pseudemys scripta*), the most frequently documented prey.
- **Softshell turtle** (*Trionyx* sp.), recorded only as remains (two individuals).
- **Coahuila box turtle** (*Terrapene coahuila*), relatively abundant in the lake, with no predation evidence in remains but one live animal found with scratched shell.

The author gives common names only as standard vernaculars; the original note uses scientific names.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lake lies in a region of gypsum geology. The shoreline was largely open white gypsum flats with scattered sedge clumps, and the bottom in the capture area was soft gypsum mud. Water at the capture site was about 15 inches deep and clear.

Key habitat observations:

- **Sedge cover:** Turtle remains were found invariably near sedge clumps, suggesting these sites are important for both turtles and their predators.
- **Burial behavior:** Mud on the coyote's face indicated that the *Pseudemys* had tried to bury itself in the bottom when attacked, a possible escape strategy that did not succeed.
- **Shell defense:** The author suggests that the ability of *Terrapene* to close its [[Plastron]] and its highly arched [[Carapace]] may make it harder for predators to open, which could explain why its remains showed no predation damage despite its abundance.
- **Predator sign:** Remains were associated with coyote scats and tracks, and the 25 December tracks were fresh, as shown by turbid water persisting in them.

## Key Findings

1. **Active hunting by coyotes:** A coyote stood watching a submerged *Pseudemys scripta* for about one minute, then entered the water, pursued the turtle, and captured it by plunging its head and forequarters beneath the surface within a few seconds.

2. **Post-capture behavior:** After dropping the still-active turtle about 3 yards from the water, the coyote pinned it between its front paws and gnawed the front of the shell, which the author says caused considerable damage to the plastron. The turtle eventually escaped.

3. **Shoreline remains:** Remains of nine *Pseudemys* and two *Trionyx* sp. were found along the shore. Two of the *Pseudemys* appeared to have been recently killed. All remains bore tooth marks and were crushed at the anterior and posterior ends.

4. **Prey selection:** The author argues that *Terrapene coahuila* would appear to be an easier prey item than *Pseudemys* or *Trionyx*, yet no predation was found on box turtle remains. The author attributes this to the species' shell closure and arched carapace, though this remains a hypothesis.

5. **Live-animal evidence:** On 25 December 1965, a live *Terrapene coahuila* was found on its back in shallow water. Its shell was severely scratched but not broken, and it was not obviously injured. The turtle was retained alive.

6. **Limitations:** The note reports no quantitative sampling, no effort or abundance estimates, and no replication beyond the two dated events. Conclusions about prey preference are therefore tentative.
