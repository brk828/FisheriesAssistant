## Executive Summary
This document is **not a primary scientific or biological research paper**; it is a legal/policy comment letter submitted by the Irrigation & Electrical Districts Association of Arizona to the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation and the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service. The letter addresses the **Final Biological and Conference Opinion (FBO)** on Lower Colorado River Operations and Maintenance (Lake Mead to the Southerly International Boundary), under [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7 consultation (Consultation No. 2-21-95-F-216). No morphological, physiological, or ecological data on fish species are presented in this text. The letter instead critiques the **scope, timing, and regulatory overreach** of the FBO relative to the developing [[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]] (MSCP) and federal habitat conservation policy (e.g., the "No Surprises" rule, Safe Harbor Agreements, and Candidate Conservation Agreements).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
While explicit ecological data are absent, the letter provides indirect context relevant to the **Lower Colorado River ecosystem**:

- The consultation area spans from **[[Lake Mead]]** to the **Southerly International Boundary** (U.S.–Mexico border), encompassing critical habitat historically associated with native Colorado River fishes (implied but not named), as well as the **[[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]** (a riparian-obligate bird species explicitly named).
- The **[[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]]** referenced in the letter is described as intending to manage habitat for **"over one hundred species"**, indicating a broader multi-taxa ecosystem management approach along the river corridor, likely including native fish such as [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and other Colorado River endemics — though these are not named in the text itself.
- The letter emphasizes that dam operations (impoundment effects at [[Lake Mead]] and downstream reaches) are the primary regulated "action" under Bureau of Reclamation discretion, implying that flow regulation, water temperature, and habitat alteration from dam operations are the ecological stressors under review — again, without specific species-level data.

## Key Findings
- The **Final Biological Opinion (FBO)** concludes that mitigation measures (Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives, RPAs) are sufficient to offset the operational effects of Bureau of Reclamation facilities on the "species studied," supporting issuance of an **incidental take permit**.
- The commenting association **supports** the FBO's core finding that operational impacts can be successfully mitigated.
- The association **objects** to the FBO extending beyond operational mitigation into de facto **species recovery planning**, arguing that:
  - Recovery Plans, not Biological Opinions, are the appropriate ESA mechanism for addressing broader species recovery (e.g., for the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]).
  - The FBO improperly preempts and constrains the still-developing **[[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]]** (MSCP), which is intended as the primary ESA compliance vehicle for the Lower Colorado River region per Interior Secretary Babbitt's policy.
  - The FBO's open-ended future obligations conflict with the Fish & Wildlife Service's proposed **"No Surprises" policy** (62 Fed. Reg. 29091–29098, May 29, 1997) and proposed rules on **Safe Harbor Agreements** and **Candidate Conservation Agreements** (62 Fed. Reg. 32189–32194, June 12, 1997), which aim to give regulated parties certainty regarding mitigation obligations.
- The letter frames the dispute as a matter of **regulatory jurisdiction and procedural sequencing** rather than disagreement over the biological adequacy of mitigation itself.
- No population counts, survey data, water quality parameters, or species-specific ecological findings are included in this correspondence — it is purely policy/legal commentary on process.
