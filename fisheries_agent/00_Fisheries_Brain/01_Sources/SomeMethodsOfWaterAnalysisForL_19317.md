# Some Methods of Water Analysis for Limnologists

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1963 Freshwater Biological Association Scientific Publication (No. 21) by F. J. H. Mackereth is a laboratory and field manual for chemical analysis of fresh waters. It consolidates methods taught in the annual Freshwater Biology course at [[The Ferry House]], covering water sampling, sample storage, glassware and reagent preparation, colorimetry, dissolved gases, conductivity, major ions, nutrients, trace metals and dissolved organic matter. The document contains no fish-specific data; its relevance to ichthyology is indirect, through water quality conditions that affect aquatic life. The manual gives both simple field techniques and more sophisticated spectrophotometric and ion-exchange procedures.

## Background & Description

- **Publication:** Freshwater Biological Association Scientific Publication No. 21, 1963, Ferry House, Windermere; based on duplicated class notes periodically revised by Mackereth.
- **Scope:** Eleven numbered sections (I–XI) plus Introduction, with a reference list and an address list of suppliers.
- **Audience:** Students and limnologists, including those working at isolated field stations where only simple apparatus is available.
- **Structure of methods:** Most entries give reagents, method, calculation and interferences. Some entries describe simple apparatus alternatives alongside [[Spectrophotometry]]-based procedures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling** (Section I)
- A weighted bottle lowered on a line, with the stopper removed by a jerk, is the simplest sampler. It cannot supply samples for dissolved gases.
- A "[[Reversed Bicycle Pump Sampler]]" can collect samples from known depths down to about 20 ft, with at least three times the bottle volume passed through before sealing.
- Commercial samplers cited include the [[Friedinger Sampler]] and [[Ruttner Sampler]].
- Integrated samples from the top 5–10 m can be taken with a weighted PVC tube. An [[Air-Lift Pump]] using nitrogen or air can yield about 20 litres in a few minutes, suitable for trace metals.
- Container materials must be chosen to avoid contamination. Brass bottles are unsuitable for copper and zinc work; phosphoric ester plasticisers and some garden hoses are problematic.

**Storage** (Section 2 of Introduction)
- Soda-glass is discouraged. Polyethylene and polypropylene are preferred.
- Phosphate concentrations can change within hours in polyethylene because of bacterial growth on the surface. Freezing with solid CO₂ and alcohol preserves samples for weeks, but this does not apply to dissolved gases or pH.
- Iodine-impregnated polythene bottles retain phosphate for at least a week.

**Dissolved gases and organic matter** (Sections II and IX)
- Oxygen is measured by the Winkler method, with a field option that uses the [[Alsterberg Bromine-Salicylate Modification]] for waters rich in organic matter.
- Percentage saturation uses temperature-dependent solubility values (Table 1) and altitude correction factors (Table 2, 0 m to 2500 m).
- Free carbon dioxide can be titrated with carbonate, or calculated from pH and alkalinity using a [[Nomogram]] (Moore 1939).

**Conductivity and ion balance** (Sections III–V)
- Conductivity is measured in reciprocal megohms/cm. In Lake District waters, 1 meq/l of ions corresponds to about 112 reciprocal megohms/cm. In [[Malham Tarn]], the corresponding value is about 90.
- The total ionic concentration from anions and cations should agree within about ±2%, providing a quality check.

**Trace contamination** (Sections 3–4 of Introduction and VIII)
- Tap water commonly contains copper and zinc from piping.
- Fingers carry sodium, chloride and heavy metals.
- Zinc contamination can come from rubber, cosmetic powders and paint on pipettes.

**Notable quantitative details (as given in the text)**
- Oxygen: 1 ml of n/80 thiosulphate equals 0.1 mg O₂. Saturation is calculated as 100 × C/Cₛ.
- Calcium, Eriochrome Black T titration: 1 ml versenate equals 0.1 mg Ca²⁺. Magnesium = (X × 0.0608 × 1000)/V mg/l.
- Ion-exchange column: 10 g resin with exchange capacity of 1 meq/g passes about 20 litres at an average of 0.5 meq/l ionic strength.
- Ammonia (spectrophotometric, Riley): extinction measured at 625 mµ in a 10 cm cell.
- Phosphate (hexanol extraction): sensitivity about 1 µg P/l, with absorbance at 730 mµ.
- Zinc (dithizone): detectable down to about 1 µg Zn/l, measured at 535 mµ.
- Persulphate organic carbon: test recoveries include complete oxidation of glucose, glycine and salicylic acid; about 95% recovery from some other compounds.

**Practical recommendations**
- Use A.R. grade reagents and run reagent blanks.
- Use quartz for trace metals and boron; use Pyrex for most other work.
- Avoid perchloric acid oxidations on a large scale without explosion precautions.
- Standardise thiosulphate and carbonate solutions regularly, as they deteriorate.
- Use nitrogen rather than air for samples from deep anaerobic zones.
- Note that B.O.D. is comparative and arbitrary (5 days, 20 °C) and does not quantify total dissolved organic matter.

**Limitations noted by the author**
- Permanganate oxidation does not fully measure organic carbon.
- Nessler tube accuracy is about ±2% under favourable conditions, and spectrophotometry is preferred where available.
- Colour matching of artificial standards varies with light source.
