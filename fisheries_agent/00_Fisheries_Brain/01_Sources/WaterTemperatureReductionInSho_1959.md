# Water Temperature Reduction in Shortened Spring Channels of Southwestern Wisconsin Trout Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #freshwater-ecology #stream-habitat

## Executive Summary
This study, conducted by the Wisconsin Conservation Department and the U.S. Forest Service as part of a Dingell-Johnson-funded stream improvement program on [[Bohemian Valley Creek]] (headwaters of [[Coon Creek]]), evaluated whether physically shortening a spring channel could lower summer water temperatures at its confluence with a trout stream. By reducing the channel length by 67% (from 563 ft to 186 ft) and rerouting the flow through a willow-shaded path, late-afternoon summer water temperatures at the spring mouth were reduced by 10–11°F. The findings support channel shortening as a viable, low-cost technique to improve thermal habitat quality for trout in [[Coldwater Stream Habitat]] management, complementing other bank stabilization and riparian shading practices.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **System type:** Small spring-fed trout stream (~20 miles long), part of the [[Coon Creek Watershed]], underlain by Lower Magnesian dolomite over Upper Cambrian sandstone — a geology producing abundant, thermally stable springs.
- **Thermal regime:** Spring water emerged from the ground at a highly stable ~48°F (±1°F), providing a critical coldwater refuge input into the warmer main stem, which reached peak temperatures of ~68°F on moderately warm days.
- **Habitat degradation factor:** Before channel modification, the spring water traveled 563 feet through an open, sun-exposed, watercress-filled pasture channel roughly parallel to the creek, allowing substantial solar heating (reaching 60°F) before it could provide cooling benefit at the confluence.
- **Habitat improvement intervention:** The channel was shortened to 186 feet (67% reduction) via bulldozer, re-routed in a straight line to the creek, seeded with a grass-clover mix to control erosion, and shaded (~90% of length) by 15–20 foot willows — enhancing the [[Riparian Shading]] effect central to [[Coldwater Stream Habitat]] preservation.
- **Seasonal effects:** The shortened channel also had an ameliorative winter effect, warming a narrow band of the creek cross-section by 5°F above the ambient 37°F near the confluence in February 1958.
- **Management context:** Part of a broader habitat improvement suite including streambank riprap stabilization, grass seeding, livestock exclusion fencing (50–80 ft buffer strips), cattle passes, and in-channel structural devices — consistent with classic [[Trout Stream Improvement]] practices of the 1950s Dingell-Johnson era.

## Key Findings
- **Channel shortening magnitude:** Spring channel reduced from 563 feet to 186 feet (67% shorter).
- **Temperature reduction:** Late afternoon peak temperatures at the spring channel mouth dropped from ~60°F (pre-diversion) to ≤50°F, averaging 49°F (post-diversion) — a **10–11°F reduction**.
- **Spring source stability:** The spring itself remained thermally stable at ~48°F year-round with minimal variation (~1°F), confirming it as a reliable coldwater source when protected from solar heating en route to the stream.
- **Mechanism of cooling:** Attributed to combined effects of (1) shorter travel distance/debris-free channel, (2) increased flow velocity, and (3) willow shading — not to reduced seepage loss, which remained unchanged.
- **Winter thermal buffering:** A 5°F localized temperature increase was detected in a 1.5-ft-wide band of the creek near the spring confluence in February, suggesting the modified channel also moderates winter cooling, benefiting overwintering trout.
- **Management implication:** Spring channel diversion is proposed as a supplementary tool to riparian shading for thermal habitat management; the authors recommend directing spring water into deep shaded pools to potentially create thermally stratified refugia for trout during extreme summer heat, warranting further study on [[Thermal Refugia]] in trout streams.
- **Methodological note:** Data comparisons relied on matched 3-day temperature recording periods before and after diversion (Aug 24–27 vs. Aug 30–Sept 2, 1957) using multi-pen thermographs measuring air, stream, and spring-channel-mouth temperatures.
