# Status and Ecology of the Virgin River Roundtail Chub, *Gila robusta seminuda* (Osteichthyes: Cyprinidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 study by [[Paul C. Marsh]] and J. N. Cross, published in the *Great Basin Naturalist*, describes the distribution, ecology and status of the [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]], a native cyprinid of the [[Virgin River]] in Utah, Arizona and Nevada. The chub occurred in about half of mainstream collections and made up less than 3% of the fauna. Its population has declined over the past 100 years because of agricultural and urban water use, reduced water quality and introduced [[Exotic Fishes]]. The authors conclude that the species is in danger of extinction and survives only in areas of perennial flow.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Gila robusta seminuda*, the most endangered of the native Virgin River fishes.
- **Context:** Southwestern native fishes have suffered local and widespread extirpations from habitat alteration. The Virgin River is one of the few remaining unrestricted streams in the Southwest, but its native ichthyofauna has been affected by agriculture and by the impoundment of the Colorado River at [[Lake Mead]].
- **Study area:** The Virgin River runs about 390 km through Utah, Arizona and Nevada. It crosses the western edge of the [[Colorado Plateau]] and the Basin and Range province, then flows south into Lake Mead. It formerly drained directly into the Colorado River before [[Hoover Dam]] was built in 1935. The climate is arid to semiarid, with high evaporation, large discharge variation and high dissolved and suspended solids.
- **Fish fauna:** Table 1 lists 17 species, of which several are native. Introduced species include [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Black Bass]], green sunfish, bluegill and black bullhead.

**Methods**
- Fish were collected from June 1973 to March 1975 with 3.0 to 6.1 m long minnow seines, with three consecutive hauls per habitat at each station.
- Environmental variables recorded at each station were air and water temperature, conductivity, current velocity, width, depth of capture, average depth and seine haul length.
- Preserved specimens were measured with dial vernier calipers, and guts were examined under a dissecting microscope. Voucher specimens are held at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use**
- The chub was taken in 62% of collections in runs, 35% in pools and 3% in riffles.
- It occurred over sand and silt in 10% of collections and over gravel and rubble in 30%.
- Mean water depth was 33 cm (SE 9.2, n = 23), and mean capture depth was 67 cm (SE 27.7, n = 23).
- Cover such as boulders, deadfalls, overhanging trees or undercut banks was present in more than half of collections.
- Mean current velocity was 0.34 m/s (SE 0.129, n = 23), ranging from 0.0 to 1.1 m/s.
- Conductivity ranged from 375 to 9,600 µmhos at 25°C, with a mean of about 1,700 µmhos.
- About 80% of collections were from unmodified habitat, 20% from slightly modified habitat, and less than 1% from extensively altered habitat.

**Distribution**
- The chub was most common in the lower mainstream, where it occurred in 17 of 23 collections. Its share of the fauna there never exceeded 11% at Station 2 or 8% at Station 3.
- In the middle reaches it was found in 3 of 11 collections, and in the upper mainstream in 8 of 11, where it made up less than 8% of the sample.
- It was never collected in the tributaries during this study. Three specimens from tributaries were found in museum collections.
- Upstream spread is limited by the highly saline LaVerkin Springs (about 15,000 µmhos).

**Threats**
- Reduced discharge during the irrigation season (200 to 250 days per year) and reduced water quality from irrigation leaching and organic waste are the main causes of decline.
- Temperatures reached 35.5°C in isolated pools in July, close to the probable lethal limit. The woundfin's lethal temperature was 34.4°C.
- Crowding in isolated pools increases predation, disease and competition. Over 500 fish of eight species were sometimes collected from one small pool.
- Introduced fishes compete with and prey on natives and may carry diseases and parasites.

## Key Findings

- **Rarity:** The chub made up less than 3% of the fauna and occurred in about half of mainstream collections.
- **Diet:** Filamentous green algae made up 90% of gut contents in the 11 guts examined (52.7 to 166.7 mm SL). Terrestrial and aquatic insects and amphipod larvae were also present. The gut is long, with a ratio of intestine to standard length of about 1, which suggests algae and detritus provide little nutrition. The authors describe the species as opportunistic. Of 307 stomachs examined by other workers, 35% were empty.
- **Reproduction:** Males from March collections had enlarged testes, while August males had reduced testes. The smallest sexable individuals were 52.7 mm and 55.7 mm SL, and the smallest collected chub was 33.5 mm SL. Spawning timing was inferred from related species.
- **Size history:** Hubbs collected 20 chubs at Littlefield, Arizona in July 1942, ranging from 27 to 248 mm SL (mean 135 mm). The largest recent specimen was 206 mm SL, and most individuals in this study were under 130 mm SL.
- **Historical decline:** The population has declined drastically over the past 100 years. Early records from the 1875 Wheeler Survey describe the chub as abundant.
- **Conservation status:** The population is in danger of extinction. It has not been collected downstream of Nevada since the mid-1960s and now survives only in perennial flow areas. More than half of its native habitat is unsuitable for long periods.
- **Management implication:** Any disruption of flow in perennial reaches is expected to cause the chub's disappearance from those reaches.
