# Gila Topminnow Extirpation Sites in Arizona: 1994-95 Field Season Results

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Technical Report, authored by David A. Weedman and Kirk L. Young (June 1995), evaluates [[Gila Topminnow]] reintroduction sites that have failed and determines which may be declared "extirpated." Since 1981, the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[U.S. Forest Service]] have reintroduced topminnows at more than 200 sites with mixed success. Sites were selected at random for evaluation, and extirpation was assessed against draft criteria from Abarca et al. (1994). Of the sites reviewed, the report recommends that the majority be declared extirpated and not restocked, while a few are flagged for augmentation or further survey.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) is a small livebearing poeciliid native to southwestern Arizona. Recovery efforts began under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Early site selection was opportunistic, choosing any available, possibly permanent water within historical range with little likelihood of land-use conflict; many failures are attributed to this approach.

Current reintroduction criteria (Abarca et al. 1994) specify:
- Drainage area ~1.0 km²; elevation <1,600 m
- Perennial, lotic, sheltered stream flow ≥0.1 m³/sec; stream gradient <3%
- Channel classification C-2 through C-5 ([[Rosgen Classification]])
- Pond surface area ≥1.2 ha; mud depth <2 m
- Little or no channelization; complex, heterogeneous habitat protected from flash flooding
- Moderate to abundant aquatic vegetation; only native, nonpredatory fishes present
- Water quality meeting [[Arizona Department of Environmental Quality]] standards; low development potential; located in the Gila River drainage

Because failed sites remain unofficially "extirpated," [[Section 7 Consultation]] obligations continue. A draft revised recovery plan proposed extirpation criteria to remove this burden, but it had not been finalized by the USFWS when this report was written. AGFD applied the draft criteria to reduce the administrative backlog.

**Provisional extirpation criteria (Abarca et al. 1994):**
1. Qualified biologists fail to collect topminnows in at least five consecutive surveys (natural populations) or three (reintroduced populations)
2. Surveys cover the majority of available habitat
3. Surveys occur at times of highest detection potential (usually June through November)
4. Mosquitofish are preserved at each survey, with museum verification
5. If the site is dry, surface waters above and below are surveyed; if none exist, provisional extirpation may be declared

Following provisional declaration, a report must be reviewed by AGFD and qualified biologists, and a final decision made by AGFD and USFWS with the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most reviewed sites are small, steep, or ephemeral. Recurrent causes of failure documented in the site accounts include:

- **Desiccation:** the dominant cause, often linked to dry stock tanks, failed artesian wells, or intermittent streams (e.g., [[Artesian Well #3]], [[Green Tanks]], [[Mesquite Spring Tank]], [[Castle Creek]]).
- **Flooding:** flash floods scouring channels, notably [[Buehman Canyon]], [[Happy Camp Spring]], [[Horse Creek]], and [[Koples Canyon]], where a 1989 flood reportedly removed all fish.
- **Elevation and temperature:** sites above the accepted range, such as [[Bear Canyon]] and [[El Pilar Tank]], where cold temperatures or extremes were suspected.
- **Predation and nonnative fishes:** [[Green Sunfish]], [[Goldfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]] were found at several sites, and [[Longfin Dace]] were common in some streams where topminnows were absent.
- **Vegetation overgrowth:** dense algae and cattails in artificial tanks, as at [[Chalky Butte Well]] and [[Artesian Well #3]].
- **Habitat isolation and steep gradients:** many sites lacked downstream connectivity, limiting dispersal.

Survey thoroughness and habitat coverage were inconsistently documented in historical records, so the authors relied on personal knowledge and communication with experienced collectors (e.g., [[James Brooks]], [[Lee Simons]], [[Brian Bagley]], [[Francisco Abarca]], [[Matt Brown]]). Surveyor qualifications were not verified; the report lists organizations and job titles only.

## Key Findings

- **Scope:** The report reviews a random set of reintroduction sites, with 23 sites qualifying for declaration of extirpation before July 1994 and additional sites qualifying during the 1994 season. Sites are grouped into three categories: extirpation reports for sites meeting criteria in 1994, sites meeting criteria before 1994, and sites not meeting criteria or recommended for further action.
- **Extirpation declared (majority of sites):** Most reviewed sites, including Buckhorn Spring, Cedar Spring, Chalky Butte Well, Corner Artesian, Holly Spring, Little Nogales Spring, Little Outfit Tank, Martin Well, Mesquite Flat Trough, Mesquite Spring Tank, Nogales Spring, Shute Springs, Alambre Tank, Artesian Wells #3 and #4, Bear Canyon, Bench Well, Castle Creek, Copper Canyon, El Pilar Tank, Government Tank, Grapevine Spring, Happy Camp Spring, Indian Spring #2, Little Nob Well, Hackard Spring, Pilot Tank, Reed Spring, Rock Spring #2, Tres Alamos Site, Unnamed Spring #0, Upper Horrell Spring, White Tank, Zig Zag Spring, and others, were recommended as extirpated and not restocked.
- **Key example, Martin Well:** Only a single topminnow specimen was found (identified in 1989, origin unknown). Nonnative fish, including [[Green Sunfish]] and mosquitofish, were recorded, and the report notes possible illegal movement from Hooned Well.
- **Key example, Alambre Tank:** Brooks (1985) reported an estimated population of 5,000, but by 1993 no topminnows were found in a 90 m² sample; 34 largemouth bass were captured. Desiccation was judged the likely cause.
- **Sites not meeting criteria, augmentation recommended:**
  - [[Horse Creek]]: formerly Category 1, believed able to persist; augmentation stocking recommended.
  - [[Humbug Creek]]: population persisted until at least 1989 despite flooding and exotics; augmentation recommended.
  - [[Indian Spring #1]]: supported a population for at least seven years; augmentation recommended.
  - [[Seven Springs]]: prone to flash flooding and heavy recreation; restocking recommended after site improvement.
  - [[Green Tanks]]: found to be suitable with enhancement; reinitiating discussions with BLM and the local rancher suggested.
  - [[Eut Spring]]: dries occasionally; water permanence to be researched before restocking.
  - [[Buehman Canyon]]: susceptible to flooding; upstream reaches to be sampled and the stream re-evaluated for possible augmentation.
  - [[Rincon Site]]: upper tinajas may be perennial; re-evaluation recommended.
- **Out-of-range sites:** [[Tres Alamos]] and [[Koples Canyon]] lie outside historical range and do not contribute to recovery; they should not be restocked.
- **Survey totals:** The report compiles a list of roughly 50 surveyors and affiliations across AGFD, USFS, BLM, USFWS, and ASU; the complete roster is in the report's appendix.
- **Literature base:** Key cited works include Abarca et al. (1994), Brooks (1985, 1986), Bagley et al. (1991), Brown and Abarca (1992), Minckley and Brooks (1985), and Simons (1987).
