# Levee Removal Project: Assessment of Floodplain Habitat Restoration in the Middle Green River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #floodplain-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Utah Division of Wildlife Resources final report (Publication 02-17, June 2002, Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program, Project CAP-6-LR) evaluates whether removing or breaching [[Levee Removal]] structures to reconnect [[Floodplain Wetlands]] with the [[Green River]] can aid recovery of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]]. Field work ran from 1996 to 1999 across twelve floodplain wetlands and six river reaches in a 67.6 km section of the middle Green River. Eight sites were breached (three in 1997, five in 1998). The report integrates chapters on physical site descriptions, primary productivity and light/nutrient limitation, invertebrates, water exchange, riparian vegetation, native fishes, and nonnative fishes, concluding with a synthesis of floodplain wetland classifications and management recommendations.

## Background & Description

The [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] (UCRRP) was established in 1988 to balance water development under the [[Colorado River Compact]] with recovery of four endangered fishes. A component of its Recovery Action Plan is restoring floodplain habitat. Flaming Gorge Dam (closed 1962) reduced mean annual peak flow from 679.9 m³/s (pre-dam) to 492.9 m³/s (post-dam), and bank stabilization plus flood levees reduced the frequency of floodplain connection. Pre-dam connection occurred about 2 out of 3 years; post-dam, only about 2 out of 7 years.

The study draws on the [[Flood Pulse Concept]], the [[River Continuum Concept]], the [[Riverine Productivity Model]], and the [[Serial Discontinuity Concept]] to frame expected floodplain contributions to river food webs. Sampling sites are described in Chapter 2, and [[Tamarisk]] covers about 14% of the reach area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Overbank flows at Jensen gauge; the 1999 connection period lasted roughly 5 to 7 weeks at individual sites, mainly May and June.
- **Sites:** Depression sites (e.g., [[The Stimrp]], [[Baeser Bend]], [[Above Brennan]], [[Johnson/J4]], [[Old Charley Wash]]) retain water to overwinter fish. Tenace sites (e.g., [[Bonanza Bridge]], [[Baser/Chew]], [[Leota/L10]], [[Old Charley Diked]]) drain with flood recession. Several sites did not function as originally classified; Bonanza Bridge and Horseshoe Bend behaved as depressions.
- **Water chemistry and light:** River turbidity averaged 218 NTU (maxima above 1000 NTU during floods). Mean water-column light in the river averaged 217 µE m⁻² s⁻¹ and the photic zone was typically under 1 m deep (5-22% of mean depth). Floodplain depressions were clearer, with mean photic zone near total depth.
- **Vegetation:** Dominant cover includes [[Tamarisk]], [[Fremont Cottonwood]], [[Cattail]] and [[Perennial Pepperweed]]. Pepperweed is a noxious exotic whose spread was a local concern.
- **Study design shifts:** The 1996 design paired sites by nonnative fish density and by depression/tenace status, but river reaches did not differ significantly in nonnative density, so the design was modified. Depressions were redefined as sites that retain water sufficient to overwinter fish.

## Key Findings

**Primary productivity and nutrients (Chapters 3-4)**
- Light, not nutrients, appears to be the main limit on pelagic primary production in the river. Nutrient bioassays showed a light-nitrogen interaction in April, May and June (e.g., June: light F = 76.43, p < 0.0001). Phosphorus had no detectable effect.
- River net primary productivity (NPP) was negative in April before the spring flood and peaked in July; floodplain NPP was far higher (mean 1769 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹ versus 138 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹ in the river, with river range -93 to 851).
- Floodplain peak productivity occurred after connection ended, so the authors concluded floodplains were not a significant direct carbon source to the river channel, although they may support riverine organisms that exploit them.

**Invertebrates (Chapter 5)**
- Thirty-nine insect taxa (seven orders) and 24 non-insect taxa were collected over three years.
- Benthic densities declined in the river from pre- to post-flood. Floodplain benthos were higher than the river after the flood.
- Zooplankton were up to eight times more abundant in floodplain outflows than in the river; benthic invertebrates were exported at very low rates, so restored wetlands acted as biomass sinks for benthos.
- Restored and natural floodplains showed no significant differences in most invertebrate taxa, except copepods, which were higher in restored sites.

**Water, carbon and invertebrate exchange (Chapter 6)**
- Three floodplains (The Stimrp, Baeser Bend, Above Brennan) were net water sinks from the channel. Above Brennan, the only site with a natural upstream breach, functioned as a flow-through site and had the highest zooplankton export.
- Floodplains were net sinks for particulate organic matter. Across the eight restored floodplains, the river transported about 1.8 × 10⁹ m³ water in the six-week period, with about 8.1 × 10⁶ m³ entering restored floodplains.
- Concluded that levee removal increases carbon transport and fish food resources, with upstream breaches recommended.

**Riparian vegetation (Chapter 7)**
- Breached levee sites were quickly colonized by native cottonwood and exotic tamarisk seedlings, plus perennial pepperweed and herbs. Cottonwood seedlings largely did not survive the first season, while tamarisk seedlings survived longer.
- Pepperweed occupied the study reach in large areas before excavation; colonized excavation areas were a tiny fraction of this. Predictive discriminant analysis correctly classified presence at 86% and absence at 74% (jackknife). Key predictors were topographic position, position along a meander wavelength, and distance to river channel.
- Authors noted that sediment deposition from vegetated breaches could reduce the sustainability of levee removal.

**Native fishes (Chapter 8)**
- Native fish use of floodplains was tied to the duration and magnitude of spring connection. Catches were higher in 1997 and 1999 (connection over 50 days) than in 1996 and 1998 (under 40 days).
- Colorado pikeminnow and flannelmouth sucker were the most abundant native species in floodplains. Razorback sucker were captured only occasionally (seven in 1997, none in 1998, and some in 1999, mostly in stocked sites). Stocked hatchery fish were present in Baeser Bend, Above Brennan and The Stimrp.
- Larval flannelmouth, razorback and bluehead suckers drifted into floodplains, but YOY survival was not documented and no native reproduction in floodplains was observed.
- Fyke nets were the most effective gear for estimating relative species composition in floodplain wetlands (rank correlation with the drained-wetland census r = 0.75, significant; trammel nets were not significant).
- Authors suggested native fish distribution in the main channel reflects habitat quality and life history more than floodplain acreage, since the restored area (≈200 ha) was small relative to the about 4,500 ha inundated at 675 m³/s.

**Nonnative fishes (Chapter 9)**
- Nonnative species made up over 99% of the catch in most floodplain sites. Across four years, over 1,109,696 nonnative fish of 14 species were captured in floodplain sites, versus about 72,000 in the river.
- Depression sites were dominated by [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Common Carp]] and [[Green Sunfish]], which reproduce in floodplains. Baser/Chew and Below Sheppard, located in the active channel, were dominated by red shiners, sand shiners and fathead minnows.
- Carp and fathead minnow larvae appeared in light traps about three to four weeks after connection; young black bullheads and green sunfish appeared only after disconnection.
- Fall 1999 biomass estimates: The Stimrp about 310,565 fish (2,190 kg); Baeser Bend about 458,474 fish; Above Brennan about 86,122 fish.
- Nonnative increases in the river were linked to draining events (Old Charley Wash beaver dam in 1997, Leota Bottom pumping in 1998, Stewart Lake draining in 1999), not to levee removal, and did not persist between seasons.
- Northern pike reproduced in Old Charley Wash in 1998 and 1999; walleye and smallmouth bass were not captured in floodplains but were present in the river.

**Synthesis and classification (Chapter 10)**
- Ideal floodplain wetland criteria: (1) configured to maximize larval entrainment; (2) cover to survive predation; (3) high productivity; (4) low nonnative fish numbers; (5) adequate water quality year-round; (6) physically self-sustaining. No sampled floodplain fully met all criteria.
- Three functional classes proposed: **Deep Depression** (larvae entrained and remain one or more seasons, then leave voluntarily or are removed); **Tenace** (larvae enter temporarily during high flow and return to the river on recession); **Managed Site** (control structures allow operation as either).
- Depression sites configured for flow-through were judged most likely to contribute to immediate recovery and long-term sustainability.

**Recommendations**
1. Continue floodplain restoration and levee removal along the Middle Green River.
2. Continue associated monitoring, focusing on early life stages of razorback sucker.
3. Prioritize depression sites (with or without control structures).
4. Configure sites for flow-through during high flow.
5. Consider nonnative fish control before inundation to benefit entrained YOY razorback suckers.
6. Use depression sites as grow-out ponds for razorback suckers, starting with small sites such as Baeser Bend; use sites with control structures that can drain fully (e.g., Stewart Lake, Leota/L10) second, given release risk.
7. Plan site management around logistics, wetland size, power lines, hydrology, control structures, recovery goals, funding and water quality.
