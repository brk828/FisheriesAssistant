# Conservation-Reliant Species: Toward a Biology-Based Definition

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This BioScience policy article (Rohlf, Carroll and Hartl, July 2014) argues that the term [[Conservation-Reliant Species]] has been defined so broadly, and so entangled with legal and policy questions under the [[Endangered Species Act]], that it has lost usefulness as a scientific concept. The authors trace the term's development from [[Doremus]] (2000) through [[Scott et al. (2005)]] and [[Goble et al. (2012)]], critique current definitions for mixing biology with law, and propose a definition based solely on the degree to which a species needs ongoing direct human intervention to persist in the wild. They argue this definition should inform, not justify, listing and delisting decisions.

## Background & Description

- **Term origin:** Doremus (2000) first addressed the concept, suggesting some species may need listing "forever" because threats such as invasive species cannot be remedied by regulation. Scott et al. (2005) coined "conservation-reliant" for species that maintain self-sustaining wild populations only if ongoing management is implemented.
- **Scott et al. (2010):** Described conservation reliance as a continuum ranging from species existing only in captivity, through species needing release of captive-reared individuals, to species needing only periodic habitat management.
- **Goble et al. (2012):** Characterized conservation-reliant species as those facing threats that can be managed but not eliminated, and later reversed earlier exclusions of species needing supplementation or frequent translocation.
- **Legal framework:** The ESA defines "conserve" to include direct interventions (habitat maintenance, live trapping, propagation, transplantation), but the goal is improvement "to the point at which such measures are no longer necessary." Regulations define recovery as the point at which species no longer need ESA protection.
- **Policy context:** The 1964 Wilderness Act and 1973 ESA reflect a preservation-oriented ethic with a clear line between humans and natural systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Threats discussed:** Climate change, invasive species, disrupted disturbance regimes (e.g., fire suppression), and pathogens (sylvatic plague) that often cannot be fully overcome, only managed.
- **Illustrative species:**
  - Gray wolves, Northern Rockies: weakly conservation-reliant; connectivity could be restored through habitat corridors.
  - Black-footed ferret: strongly conservation-reliant; requires augmentation from captive populations against plague, possibly for centuries.
  - Hawaiian endemic birds: strongly conservation-reliant; need predator/competitor control (e.g., barred owls for northern spotted owl).
  - Kirtland's warbler: at least moderately conservation-reliant; depends on prescribed burning to replace lost natural fire regime.
  - Desert tortoise: conservation-reliant under other definitions, but needs are primarily regulation of human activities.
  - Salmon: moderately conservation-reliant where dam removal is needed.
- **Human role:** Under the proposed definition, the degree of reliance depends on biology, while time frames depend on social commitment and resources.

## Key Findings

1. **Problem identified:** Current definitions are "overly inclusive," encompassing most or all ESA-listed species, which reduces their value for setting recovery priorities.
2. **Legal confusion:** Definitions mix legal criteria (existing regulatory adequacy, species-specific versus general protections, timing of regulations) with biology, which the authors say obscures real policy choices.
3. **Agency practice:** The USFWS has used conservation reliance to support delisting of species needing ongoing active management, including:
   - Wyoming gray wolf delisting proposal (USFWS 2011), justified as "a well-accepted practice."
   - Yellowstone grizzly bear delisting (USFWS 2007a), with active management and periodic translocations.
   - Black-footed ferret recovery plan (USFWS 2013a), with ongoing augmentation.
   - Kirtland's warbler (USFWS 2012a), with habitat manipulation and cowbird control "outside the purview of the Act."
4. **Shift in authors' position:** Goble et al. (2012) reversed Scott et al. (2005) by including species dependent on ongoing intervention within conservation reliance.
5. **Statutory emphasis:** The ESA's listing and delisting provisions already require evaluation of "inadequacy of existing regulatory mechanisms," showing Congress anticipated the need for species-specific protections; court and agency sources (e.g., Trout Unlimited v. Lohn, 2009) emphasize self-sustaining populations.
6. **Proposed definition:** Conservation reliance is "a spectrum representing the degree to which a species needs direct human intervention to manipulate individual members of that species at some point in their life cycle or ... in the form of ongoing physical or biological changes in its environment to persist in the wild."
7. **Two key consequences:** (a) species classified as conservation-reliant could not be delisted under current law; (b) resource investment and political will can shorten the period of reliance for weakly reliant species.
8. **Policy recommendations:**
   - Keep the biological term neutral on whether species requiring ongoing intervention may be delisted.
   - Abandon prior definitions in favor of a biology-only definition.
   - Use the concept to inform conservation prioritization.
   - Consider whether the ESA's self-sufficiency goal should be reconsidered, a question the authors explicitly leave open.
9. **Recovery prioritization:** Prioritizing recovery funds by degree of reliance could divert resources to species with greater chances of self-sufficiency, though strongly reliant species may remain listed indefinitely.
10. **Threatened status as an option:** The authors suggest the USFWS and NMFS could make greater use of the "threatened" category to tailor protections for stabilized, conservation-reliant species.
