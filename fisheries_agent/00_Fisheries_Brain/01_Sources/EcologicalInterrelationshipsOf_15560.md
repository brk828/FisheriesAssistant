# Ecological Interrelationships of Seven Native Sympatric Southwestern Fishes

## Executive Summary
This proposal, submitted by W. E. Barber (University of Alaska) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Endangered Species Program, outlines a plan to synthesize existing data on the ecological interrelationships of seven native, sympatric fish species inhabiting [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona), a tributary of the [[San Pedro River]] drainage within the [[Gila River Basin]]. Aravaipa Creek is highlighted as one of the few remaining Southwestern aquatic habitats retaining a largely unmodified, exotic-free native fish assemblage, making it a critical natural laboratory for studying pre-competition baseline ecology among native [[Southwestern Fishes|southwestern fishes]]. The study does not involve new field collection; instead, it re-analyzes samples and data gathered from June 1966–August 1967, supplemented by unpublished/underanalyzed material and subsequent theses from associated researchers (notably W. L. Minckley's group). The stated objective is to characterize niche overlap, habitat associations, and dietary/temporal interactions among the seven species to inform conservation strategy, given documented threatened/endangered status of several taxa due to habitat modification and introduction of exotic competitors such as [[Notropis lutrensis|red shiner]].

## Background & Description
The paper is ecological/proposal-based rather than morphometric, so detailed morphological descriptions are not presented. However, taxonomic composition of the assemblage is specified:

**Five Cyprinids (minnows):**
- *Meda fulgida* — [[Gila Spikedace]] (Threatened, per American Fisheries Society 1979)
- *Tiaroga cobitis* — [[Loach Minnow]] (Special Concern)
- *Gila robusta grahami* — [[Gila Chub]] (Threatened)
- *Agosia chrysogaster* — [[Longfin Dace]]
- *Rhinichthys osculus* — [[Speckled Dace]]

**Two Catostomids (suckers):**
- *Catostomus insignis* — [[Sonora Sucker]]
- *Pantosteus clarki* — [[Desert Sucker]]

Body-size/age data collection methods are noted: ~8,000 length measurements were taken across the sampling period, primarily to support age-and-growth analysis of the smaller-bodied cyprinid species. Age determination via scale analysis was attempted for some species but remained incomplete at time of writing (e.g., for *Gila*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Aravaipa Creek functions as a natural refugium free of exotic fish introductions (notably lacking *Notropis lutrensis*, which had already invaded the nearby San Francisco River and was expanding through the Gila River system). This makes it one of the last sites where baseline (pre-exotic) interspecific dynamics among native [[Cyprinidae]] and [[Catostomidae]] can be studied.
- **Sampling regime**: Monthly to bi-monthly field sampling (June 1966–August 1967) recorded fish abundance by species, water depth, temperature, bottom substrate type, time of day, and current velocity (via Pitot tube). Supplemental 24–36 hour intensive collections targeted **diel feeding periodicity**.
- **Diet**: Food habits were quantified for >1,200 fish using the points method ([[Hynes 1950]] methodology, originally developed for stickleback diet studies), allowing seasonal and diel dietary comparisons across the seven species.
- **Reproductive biology**: Data gathered on reproductive condition and fecundity across species and seasons.
- **Community structure concept — Refugia**: The creek is framed as a model refugium supporting 7 of the 12 known native fish species from the San Pedro River drainage, underscoring its conservation value.
- **Competitive dynamics (conceptual framework)**: The proposal draws heavily on the classic [[Johannes and Larkin 1961]] study of *Richardsonius balteatus* (redside shiner) vs. *Salmo gairdneri* (rainbow trout) competing for amphipods in British Columbia lakes. This example illustrates a central methodological problem: competitive interactions are often undetectable by diet-overlap snapshots taken *after* competitive exclusion/resource depletion has already occurred — because species shift to less-preferred foods once preferred resources (e.g., amphipods) are depleted, masking historical competition in stomach-content data collected post-hoc.
- **Threat context**: Desiccation of marsh habitat is cited as a major driver of decline for *[[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]]* (Gila topminnow), while introduction of exotic cyprinids (e.g., red shiner) is implicated — via inferred but poorly mechanistically understood competition — in the range contraction of *Meda fulgida* and *Tiaroga cobitis* in the Gila River basin.

## Key Findings
- **Study rationale**: No prior study had examined interrelationships among two or more sympatric *native* Southwestern fish species in the *absence* of exotic species — Aravaipa Creek offers a rare opportunity to establish this ecological baseline before exotic-driven community shifts occur.
- **Conservation status (1979, per Deacon et al./American Fisheries Society Endangered Species Committee)**:
  - *Gila robusta grahami* — Threatened
  - *Meda fulgida* — Threatened
  - *Tiaroga cobitis* — Special Concern
  - All three declines attributed to competition (with exotics) and habitat modification.
- **Methodological warning (competition detection problem)**: The [[Johannes and Larkin 1961]] amphipod case study demonstrates that dietary overlap analyses conducted after competitive exclusion has already reshaped feeding behavior can severely underestimate the historical role of competition, since species may be "forced" onto suboptimal food resources rather than feeding "by choice."
- **Study design**: Purely a data-synthesis project (no new fieldwork) — reanalysis of 1966–67 field/lab data (fish counts, physicochemical/habitat metrics, diet via points method, ~8,000 length measurements) plus incorporation of subsequent theses/reports from the Minckley research group, conducted in collaboration with Dr. W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University).
- **Anticipated outputs**: Characterization of (1) ontogenetic, diel, and seasonal changes in feeding across all seven species, and (2) habitat use patterns throughout the year — to be synthesized into an integrated account of niche overlap and interspecific interaction, delivered as a final report ~24 months post-initiation.
- **Conservation applications intended**: Findings are explicitly aimed at supporting (1) recovery of endangered/threatened species, (2) prevention of additional species becoming imperiled, (3) identification/design of natural or artificial refugia, and (4) strengthening arguments for habitat protection of Southwestern desert stream systems.
- **Broader significance**: Reinforces the pattern (per [[Minckley and Deacon 1968]]) that anthropogenic habitat modification and exotic species introduction are primary drivers of Southwestern freshwater fish decline, with ~6-7 species already extinct since the mid-1800s and numerous others imperiled, out of a regional native fauna of roughly 100 species characterized by high endemism.
