# Gila Chub Proposed Endangered Listing and Critical Habitat Designation (Federal Register, August 31, 2005)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a revised proposed rule from the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Department of the Interior) to list the [[Gila Chub]] as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and to designate about 211.9 stream miles (340.9 stream km) of [[Critical Habitat]]. It reopens the comment period, announces a [[Draft Economic Analysis]] and [[Draft Environmental Assessment]], corrects the legal descriptions of several critical habitat units, and schedules three public hearings. Public comments were due September 30, 2005. A September 2, 2005 Field Supervisor memorandum summarizes the same announcements. The document also includes a detailed critique of the draft economic analysis by an unnamed reviewer, who argues that the analysis contains factual and methodological errors.

The first page is a fragment of an unrelated [[National Highway Traffic Safety Administration]] rule on child restraint test dummies and clothing, which does not concern fish.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The Gila chub (*Gila intermedia*) is a fish historically found throughout the [[Gila River Basin]] in southern Arizona, southwestern New Mexico and northeastern Sonora, Mexico. It is listed in the rule's species table with its historic range and a status of "NA" for the existing federal listing.
- **Proposal history:** The Service first proposed the listing on August 9, 2002 (67 FR 51948), after a [[Center For Biological Diversity]] petition and a 12-month finding. The proposal designated stream channels, riparian areas, and a 300-foot buffer from the bankfull width on either side of the stream banks.
- **Ownership:** The designation covers Federal, State, tribal and private lands. Land managers named include the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]], [[Coronado National Forest]], [[Prescott National Forest]], [[Tonto National Forest]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], the [[San Carlos Apache Reservation]], and private landowners.
- **Corrected units:** After the 2002 proposal, the Service found that some unit descriptions were wrong. Corrected legal descriptions use the [[Public Land Survey System]], and total corrected critical habitat is 340.9 km (211.9 mi) in seven river units. Table 2 breaks down the length by individual landowner, with totals of 190.0 km (118.1 mi) for one landowner class and 593 km (37.2 mi) for another. Some scan values are garbled.
- **Legal basis:** Critical habitat designations require the Service to weigh economic and other relevant impacts. Under [[Section 7 Consultation]], Federal agencies must ensure that actions they fund, authorize or carry out do not destroy or adversely modify critical habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Critical habitat units (summarized):**
- Seven river units, including the Upper and Middle [[Gila River]] areas, the [[San Pedro River]] area, the [[Verde River]] area, the [[Agua Fria River]] area, the [[Lower Santa Cruz River]] area, and a unit containing O'Donnell Canyon, Turkey Creek and Post Canyon. Some scan labels are garbled.
- The Lower Santa Cruz unit includes [[Cienega Creek]] (two segments, about 12.2 km and 13.6 km), [[Mattie Canyon]], [[Empire Gulch]] and [[Sabino Canyon]].
- The Verde unit includes Walker Creek, Red Tank Draw and Spring Creek.
- The San Pedro unit includes Bass Canyon, Hot Springs Canyon, Redfield Canyon and Williamson Valley Wash. The Bonita Creek segment is 30.6 km (19.0 mi).
- The Agua Fria unit includes Little Sycamore, Sycamore, Indian, Silver, Lany and Lousy creeks.

**Other notes:**
- Cienega Creek is also designated critical habitat for the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], a species that depends on healthy riparian systems.
- Threats cited in the rule include livestock grazing, water withdrawals, wildfire, and nonnative fish, as well as the species' restricted distribution.
- Conservation actions discussed include fencing streams to exclude livestock, building fish barriers, nonnative fish control, and monitoring. Fish barriers are planned for the Blue River (Greenlee County) and Bonita Creek.

## Key Findings

**Regulatory proposal**
- The Service proposes to list the Gila chub as endangered and designate about 211.9 mi (340.9 km) of critical habitat.
- Comments submitted on the 2002 proposal need not be resubmitted. Comments on the 2005 announcements had to be received by September 30, 2005.
- Three public hearings were scheduled, with informational sessions from 3:00 to 4:30 p.m. and hearings from 6:30 to 8:00 p.m.:
  - September 13, 2005, Silver City, New Mexico
  - September 14, 2005, Thatcher, Arizona
  - September 15, 2005, Camp Verde, Arizona

**Economic analysis (as stated in the rule)**
- Forecast impacts of conservation and designation are $11.3 million to $28.1 million in constant dollars over 20 years. The scan's annualized figure is partly illegible.
- The Service certifies that the designation will not have a significant economic impact on a substantial number of small entities. Impacts are expected mainly in water management, livestock grazing, and tribal enterprises.
- Specific small-entity impacts:
  - **Vail Water Company:** annual revenues of $99,000. Replacement water costs of about $73,000 annually, if the company cannot use one of its seven wells. The analysis treats this as significant for the company.
  - **City of Safford:** annualized replacement water costs of $287,000 to $669,000 (discounted at 3 and 7 percent over 20 years). This would be significant for the city's utilities, which face an estimated 2.3 to 5.3 percent revenue impact.
  - **Ranching:** of 118 beef cattle ranching operations in affected counties, 15 small ranching operations (92 percent of 16) could see revenue reductions of 1 to 8 percent.
  - **Tribal livestock grazing:** $22,000 to $306,000 annually (seven percent discount rate), affecting about three livestock associations.
  - **Timber:** reduced lumber production of about $15,000 annually. There are 25 forestry companies in Arizona.
- Energy: the Service found no significant adverse effect on energy supply, distribution or use under Executive Order 13211.
- Unfunded mandates: the designation does not impose a legally binding duty on non-Federal entities. Its only regulatory effect is that Federal agencies must avoid destruction or adverse modification of critical habitat.

**Reviewer critique of the draft economic analysis**
The unnamed reviewer lists 16 numbered issues. The main concerns are:
1. **Factual errors and policy:** the analysis counts co-extensive costs (costs that would occur regardless of critical habitat) and uses a single baseline, which obscures the designation's own costs.
2. **Missing benefits analysis:** the analysis does not evaluate benefits, which the Endangered Species Act requires for exclusion decisions.
3. **Unsupported assumption of voluntary compliance:** the analysis assumes private landowners will voluntarily fence streams at $1,500 to $15,000 per mile, and that the San Carlos Apache Tribe will fence streams at up to $5 million.
4. **Measurement error:** the 300-foot buffer was measured as a horizontal map distance from a centerline, not from the bank as the proposed rule describes. This affects which impacts fall inside the critical habitat.
5. **Overlap and double counting:** costs tied to overlapping habitat (such as Cienega Creek) and multi-species consultations need to be divided by species.
6. **Flow data:** the 10 cubic feet per second minimum flow figure came from roundtail chub data. Bonita Creek exceeds 10 cfs only 10 percent of the time.
7. **Bonita Creek:** the reviewer disputes an estimated cost of up to $9.5 million, arguing the infiltration gallery draws subflow and lies downstream of the critical habitat.
8. **Cienega Creek well #5:** the analysis assumes pumping would reduce creek flows, a claim the reviewer says has no data support. It also estimates up to $2.4 million in replacement water costs.
9. **Fencing and existing fences:** the analysis attributes up to $51,800 in fence maintenance on O'Donnell Creek to the Gila chub, although the fences predate the listing.
10. **Timber and burning:** the analysis assumes harvestable timber in upper Bonita Creek, and it assumes tribal burning would be restricted, which the reviewer disputes.
11. **Factual errors in species information:** the reviewer lists errors about stocking history, habitat restoration, fish barrier ownership, and monitoring costs.

**Bottom line of the critique:** the reviewer argues the economic analysis overstates the costs of critical habitat designation, relies on incorrect biological data, and should be revised before the final rule.
