# The Groundwater Fauna of Italy: A Synthesis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stygofauna

## Executive Summary

This 1985 synthesis by [[Giuseppe Lucio Pesce]] (Istituto di Scienze Ambientali, University of L'Aquila), published in *[[Stygologia]]* 1(2), reviews the stygobiont and eustygophile fauna of Italian groundwaters. It traces the history of stygobiological research from 1550 to the 1980s, lists the known groundwater taxa, and proposes a subdivision of Italy into five stygofaunal provinces: Alpine, Apennine, Sardinian, Apulian and Sicilian. The author notes that the survey is likely incomplete and that large regions remain poorly sampled.

This document covers groundwater invertebrates and a single amphibian. It contains no fish species, so the fish-oriented metadata fields are not applicable to its core content.

## Background & Description

**Historical review.** The earliest record cited is from 1550, when Trissino described small eyeless shrimps from the [[Covoli di Costozza]] cave in Venetia. These were later identified as amphipods and named *Niphargus stygius costozzae* by Schellenberg (1935). Research intensified after 1950 and again after 1975, with contributions from many authors, notably [[Ruffo]], [[Kiefer]], [[Cottarelli]] and [[Argano]].

**Taxonomic coverage.** The text reviews the following groups:
- **Copepods:** the most diverse group, with harpacticoids generally richer in stygobionts than cyclopids. No stygobiont calanoids are known.
- **Ostracods:** poorly known, with about seven species listed.
- **Isopods:** six families, including [[Asellidae]], [[Stenasellidae]], [[Cirolanidae]] and [[Microcerberidae]].
- **Amphipods:** 11 genera, dominated by [[Niphargus]] (more than 20 species and subspecies).
- **Mysids, syncarids and thermosbaenaceans:** a few highly distinctive species, including [[Typhlocaris salentina]] among the decapods.
- **Gastropods:** Hydrobioid snails from several genera, mostly endemic.
- **Triclad turbellarians, water mites, oligochaetes, nematodes and amphibians.** Amphibians have only one true stygobiont, [[Proteus anguinus]].

**Colonization models.** The author proposes four routes, following Stock (1977):
1. [[Regression Model Evolution]] for ancient marine-derived stygobionts stranded during Tertiary regressions.
2. [[Active Migration Model]] for salinity-tolerant marine-derived copepods and amphipods.
3. A sea-to-surface-freshwater-to-groundwater route for some amphipods and syncarids.
4. [[Climatic Refugium Model]] for groundwater fauna derived from surface freshwater, probably in the late Pliocene and Pleistocene.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Groundwater habitats.** Italy's karst, varied geomorphology and climate create inland and coastal caves, superficial and deep phreatic networks, interstitial and hyporheic substrates, and springs.

**Provinces.** The five provinces are defined as follows:
- **[[Alpine Province]]:** north of the Po and the western Alps. It has the highest stygobiont richness, a noteworthy extent of karst (especially in the east) and many northern and Central European affinities.
- **[[Apennine Province]]:** western Tuscany and Apulia excluded. It is subdivided into north-west, central and southern regions. Central Apennines are rich and diverse, while southern Apennines are very poor in stygobionts.
- **[[Sardinian Province]]:** a typical western stygofauna with French affinities. Its karst is old (Palaeozoic and Mesozoic).
- **[[Apulian Province]]:** strongly individualized, with ancient, endemic stygobionts. It is divided into the Gargano and Salento-Murge districts, and the latter is much richer.
- **[[Sicilian Province]]:** very poorly known. Only a few confirmed stygobionts are recorded.

**Richness and gaps.** Stygobiont richness is greatest in the Alpine and Apulian provinces. Sicily and the southern Apennines are notably depauperate, though the author suspects this reflects sampling gaps. Data are adequate only for Liguria, Venetia, Tuscany, Sardinia and Apulia.

**Pollution.** Riolfatti et al. (1976) are cited as the first to examine groundwater pollution in Italy, in the context of the Adige River.

## Key Findings

- The earliest record of an aquatic subterranean animal in Italy dates to 1550 (Trissino, Covoli di Costozza).
- The author proposes five stygofaunal provinces: Alpine, Apennine, Sardinian, Apulian and Sicilian.
- Copepods are the most species-rich group. Harpacticoids are the main stygobiont copepods, and no stygobiont calanoids are known.
- Five copepod genera are listed among sea-originated groups: Halicyclops, Metacyclops, Nitocrella, Parapseudoleptomesochra and Parastenocaris.
- *Diacyclops languidoides* has numerous subspecies distributed across the provinces.
- The Alpine province has about 20 *Niphargus* species, several of them endemic.
- The Apulian province contains ancient Tethyan elements, including [[Typhlocaris salentina]], *[[Spelaeomysis bottazzii]]*, *[[Stygiomysis hydruntina]]* and *[[Monodella stygicola]]*.
- *Monodella argentarii* has a wide pan-Mediterranean distribution (west Italy, Yugoslavia, Greece).
- The Italian stygofauna is composite, combining northern and southern elements, Balkan and western affinities, and a high degree of endemism.
- The author states that the list is provisional and likely incomplete, and that the Sicilian and southern Apennine fauna are probably richer than currently known.
