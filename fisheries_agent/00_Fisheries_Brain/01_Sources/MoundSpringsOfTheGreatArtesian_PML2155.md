# Mound Springs of the Great Artesian Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #desert-springs

## Executive Summary

This note summarises a limnology chapter on the [[Mound Springs]] of the [[Great Artesian Basin]] in arid Australia. The text argues that springs act as isolated "aquatic islands" with simple, endemic biota, and that their environmental stability and physiological limits shape which species can disperse. It concludes that maintaining a number of springs in a group matters more than salvaging a single spring. Only pages 7–8 of the source are legible; pages 1–6 and 9–11 are scan debris and contain no recoverable scientific content.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Mound springs are non-marine, desert aquatic systems. Some large [[Artesian Springs]] are among the most stable non-marine aquatic environments.
- **System base:** At a comparable Californian desert spring, the system relied entirely on [[Cyanobacteria]] production with no allochthonous inputs. [[Naiman]] (1981) suspected [[Sulfur Bacteria]] are a major metabolic pathway in desert waters where organic matter accumulates. Microbial production was not measured in the GAB study.
- **Key difference:** Invertebrates were rare in the Californian system because of fish predation, whereas [[Great Artesian Basin]] springs host common endemic invertebrates, especially of the [[Lake Eyre Supergroup]], which reach very high densities.
- **Cultural and historical role:** Mound springs were the only water source in some very arid areas until recently. [[Aboriginal Australians]] used them, as shown by artefacts at almost all spring groups, and they feature in mythology. Early explorers, the [[Inland Telegraph]] and the [[Alice Springs Railway]] also relied on them (Harris 1981).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeography.** Springs are treated as islands in an arid sea, with each spring group an island group and each spring complex an archipelago. [[Fatchen]] (1984) applied [[Island Biogeography Theory]] to springs near Hermit Hill. Increasing spring area did not increase species diversity, but diversity rose with spring number. [[Hydrobiid Snails]] and crustaceans are absent or reduced in isolated springs (Ponder and Hershler 1984; Zeidler 1984).

**Temperature.**
- Upper reaches of large springs have constant temperature and chemistry. Smaller springs track near-ambient air temperature.
- Animals may face annual water temperatures from near 0°C to above 40°C (Minckley 1969).
- [[Glover]] (1982) recorded summer water temperatures reaching 40°C, a mean summer–winter range of 21°C in one spring, and differences of up to 25°C over 7 m in summer.
- Bottom water and sediment can differ by up to 10°C in summer. Shallow burrowing can lower experienced temperature by several degrees.
- Water depth, flow, vegetation and shade all create microhabitat temperature variation.

**Thermal tolerance.**
- Endemic invertebrates from Lake Eyre springs tolerated about 2 to 35–40°C (Ponder and Hershler, unpublished).
- [[Desert Goby]] tolerated 5–41°C (Glover and Sim 1979).
- Snails at Dalhousie Springs, with a constant 38°C, were active between 18 and 45°C.
- Low-temperature intolerance can create thermal barriers to dispersal, noted for the [[Dalhousie Hardyhead]] and [[Dalhousie Catfish]] (Ivantsoff and Glover 1974; Glover and Sim 1978a).

**Salinity.** Evaporation in smaller springs raises salinity in lower parts and still edges. Unacclimated tolerance limits were:
- Desert goby: 37 g l⁻¹ upper limit; 60 g l⁻¹ after acclimation.
- [[Lake Eyre Hardyhead]]: 52 g l⁻¹ upper limit; 110 g l⁻¹ after acclimation.

The fish's far higher salinity tolerance than that of endemic spring invertebrates (Ponder and Hershler 1984) may explain why fish occur across many waterbodies while invertebrates are restricted to springs.

**Desiccation.** Amphibious snails survived desiccation much better than aquatic snails: 60–90% survival after 48 h versus 100% mortality. Larger aquatic species outlasted small species and crustaceans, most of which died within one to a few hours. This suggests dispersal by non-aquatic agents.

**Flood resilience.** At Blanche Cup Spring, a high, hard mound, animal numbers were essentially unchanged after very heavy rain. Only animals at the mound base were washed away.

## Key Findings

1. **Stability with extremes:** Springs offer stable conditions in their upper reaches, yet animals must tolerate wide annual temperature ranges and salinity increases.
2. **Variability comparison:** Temporal and spatial physicochemical variation in most springs is far lower than in other desert aquatic systems (Soltz and Hirshfield 1981).
3. **Fish versus invertebrate limits:** Fish tolerate higher salinity and temperature range than endemic spring invertebrates, which helps explain their wider distribution.
4. **Dispersal barriers:** Temperature intolerance restricts dispersal of some fish and invertebrates, creating thermal barriers.
5. **Conservation priority:** Because diversity depends on spring number and isolated springs lose snails and crustaceans, it is "more important to maintain a number of springs in a group than to attempt to salvage one" (Fatchen 1984; Ponder and Hershler 1984; Zeidler 1984).
6. **Historical protection:** Early pastoralists fenced precious springs from stock damage, but artesian wells and bores introduced late in the nineteenth century changed dependence on springs.
