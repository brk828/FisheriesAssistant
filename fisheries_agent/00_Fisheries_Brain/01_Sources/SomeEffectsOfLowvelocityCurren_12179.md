# Some Effects of Low-velocity Currents on the Metabolism of Aquatic Macrophytes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-macrophytes

## Executive Summary

This 1965–1966 study by D. F. Westlake, of [[River Laboratory]] (East Stoke, Dorset), describes a prototype flow-through apparatus for measuring oxygen exchange in submerged aquatic plants under controlled water velocities. The work focuses on [[Ranunculus Pseudofluitans]], the dominant submerged plant of fast-flowing English chalk streams, and on [[Potamogeton Pectinatus]], which is common in slower waters. Flow was laminar at all tested velocities (0.02 to 0.5 cm/s). Photosynthesis rose steeply at low velocities and levelled off at higher ones. The effect was largest in metabolically active plants, reaching a sixfold increase at high oxygen concentrations for healthy shoots of *R. pseudofluitans*. The author notes these velocities are smaller than open stream flow but may be comparable with those within weed-beds.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a gap in knowledge. Earlier work had examined current effects on [[Aufwuchs Communities]] and on aquatic invertebrates, and James (1928) had studied *Fontinalis antipyretica* at only very low velocities (0.1–0.3 mm/s). No quantitative data existed for macrophyte photosynthesis or respiration at velocities of about 0.5 mm/s or below, which can occur within littoral weed-beds of lakes and rivers.

*Ranunculus pseudofluitans* (Syme) C. Cook is described as the dominant submerged plant of many fast-flowing English chalk streams. *Potamogeton pectinatus* L. is common in slower waters and had generally low photosynthetic capacity in these trials. Plants were collected from the river the day before use, and shoots about 20 cm long were bunched, dipped in paraffin wax at the cut ends, and used at a dry weight of roughly 2.3–3.5 g per experiment.

The apparatus consisted of a Perspex tube (35 × 7 cm) in a constant-temperature bath, fed from a constant-head vessel. Upstream oxygen was controlled by bubbling air or oxygen-free nitrogen. Velocity was determined from discharge and cross-sectional area at low flows, and from dye-spiral and Pitot-tube measurements when stirring developed. The design was superseded, so the author describes it mainly to discuss technical problems such as "boring" (water short-circuiting directly from inlet to outlet).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydraulics.** Piston flow held until about 300 ml/min (1.5 mm/s). At 600 ml/min the mean velocity reached about 4.2 mm/s (piston 2.7 mm/s, maximum 8.6 mm/s). Turbulence at the tube's onset was calculated at about 35 mm/s. The author states the flow was predominantly laminar, with possible entry into transition above about 500 ml/min.

**Water chemistry.** Stevenage tap water (about 400 ppm total solids) was used for most experiments at about 15 °C, typical of summer streams in southern England. The water had a pH of 7.8–8.8, with 1.6 ppm free CO₂, 220–290 ppm bicarbonate and 1.8 ppm carbonate. River waters where *R. pseudofluitans* grew had a pH of 7.5–8.3, 2–10 ppm free CO₂ and 230–270 ppm bicarbonate. The total carbon available was 45–56 ppm. Free CO₂ and bicarbonate were judged to be the key carbon sources.

**Light.** Two banks of fluorescent tubes gave a total irradiance of 0.174 gcal/cm² min (about 30,000 lux). The spectrum approximated light at 0.2 m depth within a weed-bed.

**Comparison with natural habitats.** Velocities in open stream water are commonly around 200 mm/s. Within a few millimetres of an object they fall below 50 mm/s, and within a weed-bed below 10 mm/s. Littoral lake velocities are often around 10 mm/s. The author argues that the weed-bed interior, with its low velocities and small diameters, may be laminar.

## Key Findings

- **Velocity effect on photosynthesis (*R. pseudofluitans*, low O₂):** Net photosynthesis rose sharply between 0.2 and 2 mm/s and levelled off near 5 mm/s. Extrapolation gave about a threefold increase from static conditions to 5 mm/s.
- **High-oxygen trial (Expt. DD):** Increasing velocity from static to 5 mm/s gave about a sixfold rise in photosynthesis. The author attributes differences between this and the low-oxygen experiments mainly to seasonal differences in photosynthetic capacity rather than oxygen concentration.
- **Maximum rates:** Light-saturated gross photosynthesis exceeded 10 mg O₂/g dry wt/h (about 2.8 mg C/g/h). About 15 mg/g/h was reached at maximum flow in Expt. DD.
- ***Potamogeton pectinatus* response:** A significant velocity effect appeared in only one experiment, with an increase of about 1.5 times from 0.2 mm/s to 0.4 mm/s and above. There was no further rise at higher velocities.
- **Respiration:** Respiration at high O₂ was about 2.0 mg O₂/g/h and was scarcely affected by flow. At low O₂ and low flow, respiration rose fourfold between 1 and 10 ppm O₂. Flow had no significant effect on respiration above 0.4 mm/s.
- **Oxygen inhibition:** Net photosynthesis was about 1.4 times higher at low than at near-saturation O₂. The author links this mainly to accelerated respiration, not to suppression of gross photosynthesis. A rise of 8–9 ppm O₂ was needed for a 25% decline, whereas flow-related declines of over 60% corresponded to O₂ rises of under 2 ppm.
- **Temperature coefficient:** The Q₁₀ of photosynthesis for *R. pseudofluitans* was estimated at 1.3–1.4, consistent with diffusion-limited carbon supply.
- **Light saturation:** Under static conditions in natural waters, saturation usually occurred below 10,000 lux. Under flow it may reach about 20,000 lux, and even at 30,000 lux saturation was often incomplete.
- **Mechanism:** The author proposes that metabolism is mainly limited by the transfer of carbon (or oxygen) to the leaf surface. Increased velocity thins the boundary layer and speeds this transfer. The dominance of *R. pseudofluitans* in fast, hard-water streams may reflect its high photosynthetic capacity, which requires an efficient carbon supply.
- **Methodological checks:** Steady states were reached within about 40 minutes. Shoots showed no significant deterioration over 48 h. Microbial respiration was judged negligible. Gas bubble losses were small, averaging about 0.2 mg O₂/h (1.5% of production).
- **Literature context:** The author highlights James (1928), Owens and Maris (1964), Gessner (1959) and Whitford and Schumacher (1964). Whitford (1960) had suggested an ecological effect requires velocities above 150 mm/s. The author notes this view was later modified.
