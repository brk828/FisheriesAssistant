# Conservation Status of the World's Fish Fauna: An Overview

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-biology #cypriniformes #salmonidae #acipenseridae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by [[Robert A. Leidy]] and [[Peter B. Moyle]], appears in the edited volume *[[Conservation Biology for the Coming Decade]]* (1998). It reviews the status of the world's fish fauna as an indicator of aquatic biodiversity. The authors argue that aquatic ecosystems, especially [[Freshwater Fishes]], are being lost faster than terrestrial ones, and that many fish species remain poorly known and probably undercounted as threatened. The chapter addresses six questions: why aquatic systems are vulnerable, how much diversity fishes represent, why fishes are good indicators, what traits characterize threatened species, the regional status of fish faunas, and what actions can protect them.

## Background & Description

Fishes are the most diverse group of vertebrates. More than half of all vertebrate species are fish, and about 24,600 species had been described at the time, with the total estimated to be considerably higher (the exact figure is partly illegible in the scan). These species are spread across 482 described families. Eighty percent of families (384) contain fewer than 50 species, while a few are highly speciose. The [[Cyprinidae]] (carps and minnows) has more than 2,000 species, and the chapter notes that the diversity of [[Cichlidal Fishes]] in East African lakes reflects some of the fastest evolutionary radiations on record.

Fish diversity is high partly because fishes are an ancient vertebrate lineage and occupy a wide range of aquatic environments. Most fishes are concentrated in shallow and coastal waters, reefs, and freshwater systems rather than the open ocean. Many taxa are poorly studied: the distribution, ecology, and status of most fish species remain only partly understood, even in better-known regions.

The chapter also highlights the [[IUCN Red List]] as the main global assessment of fish conservation status. The authors describe it as a major step forward but also a "snapshot in time" that is conservative for several reasons. It lumps widely distributed species that may be several taxa, ignores local and regional trends, and focuses on freshwater species with the most information, even though marine and less-studied groups may be vulnerable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors explain why aquatic environments are vulnerable to degradation. Water's solvent properties and its capacity to concentrate nutrients make aquatic systems productive, which has made them attractive for food extraction and waste disposal. Inland and nearshore marine systems receive much of the material that flows from land. Human population growth, urbanization, agriculture, and irrigation therefore degrade freshwater and coastal systems first, often with long-term effects on health and biodiversity.

Fishes matter ecologically as well as indicators of change. Three main reasons are given:

- **Diversity and sensitivity:** fish species differ in their environmental requirements, so they reflect a wide range of conditions.
- **Ecological influence:** fish alter the abundance and distribution of other organisms. For example, plankton-feeding fish can cascade through lake food webs, and algae-feeding fish alter algal communities. Some characins in the Amazon Basin may disperse seeds of flooded-forest trees.
- **Ecological linkage:** fishes link aquatic and terrestrial systems, for example through food resources that support birds and mammals.

Threats differ by habitat. Freshwater fishes face habitat fragmentation by dams, pollution, altered flow, and introduced species. Marine fishes face overexploitation, bottom trawling, cyanide fishing, coastal development, and invasive species carried in [[Ballast Water]].

## Key Findings

**Overall status**
- Freshwater fishes are being degraded and lost faster than marine fishes, although exceptions exist (for example, the enclosed [[Aral Sea]] and nearshore coral reefs).
- The authors conclude that losses in freshwater systems are probably much more severe than in surrounding terrestrial environments, and that losses are beginning in marine systems despite the oceans' large buffering capacity.
- The authors estimate that about 20% of the world's freshwater fishes are at risk of extinction, a figure they consider very conservative.
- Marine conclusions are more uncertain. No marine fish extinctions had been documented at the time, but at least 5% of marine fishes were judged at some risk.
- In regions such as California and South Africa, where several risk factors apply together, two-thirds or more of the native fish fauna may be in danger of extinction within 50 years.

**Vulnerability patterns**
- Freshwater fish losses appear highest in industrialized countries, in regions with arid or Mediterranean climates, in tropical regions with large human populations, in regions with high endemism, in small isolated water bodies (such as desert springs), and in large rivers.
- Among threatened freshwater groups are species in arid environments, species dependent on estuaries and reefs, and endemic species in very small areas such as springs or caves. Lacustrine and spring/cave fishes show the highest percentages of "extinct" and "extinct in the wild" species.
- Marine losses are most likely in enclosed seas and bays, shallow waters near large urban areas, intensively fished multispecies fisheries, and tropical reefs.
- Regions with intensively studied fish faunas have more recorded threatened species, suggesting that much loss goes unrecorded elsewhere.

**Taxonomic highlights**
- The [[Cypriniformes]] comprise about 2,700 species (roughly 11% of described fishes). The IUCN lists about 18 as threatened or extinct and 69 as data deficient. The Cyprinidae contain the largest number of listed species (n = 152).
- Salmonids are increasingly threatened in many regions, including [[Pacific Salmonids]], European salmonids, and Japanese species such as the Satsukimasu salmon and Kirikuchi char.
- Of the more than 400 species of [[Elasmobranchs]] (sharks, skates, and rays), many have declined from overfishing, and their slow growth and low fecundity make recovery difficult.
- The [[Coelacanth]] (*Latimeria chalumnae*), a living lobe-finned fish from the Comoros, is threatened by bycatch in deep-water fisheries and by collectors. Its population is likely small.
- Five sturgeon species are critically endangered. The [[European Sturgeon]] persists in only two reproductive populations, fewer than 600 and about 1,000 individuals. Causes include dam construction, pollution, and juvenile overfishing.
- Among paddlefishes, the [[Chinese Paddlefish]] faces a projected decline of at least 80% within 10 years due to habitat changes from the [[Three Gorges Dam]].
- The [[Beluga]] has declined in the Arctic basin over 50 years because of overfishing, poaching, and dams.
- The [[Aral Sea]] basin has lost sturgeon and other fishes to water diversion, canals, and pesticide pollution.

**Regional status**
- **Europe:** The British Isles have 56 native freshwater species versus 221 on the continent. Nine species there are extinct or threatened. Spain and Portugal have high endemism and many threatened species, with exotic fish introductions a major problem. Central Europe has many threatened species, and Balkan and Mediterranean rivers (Montenegro, Greece, Albania) face dam, pollution, and introduction threats. Roughly one-third of Greek freshwater fishes are threatened.
- **Africa:** [[Lake Malawi]], [[Lake Victoria]], and [[Madagascar]] have many endemic and threatened fishes. Madagascar's freshwater fauna is about 84% endemic, with 13 threatened species listed. Habitat degradation, deforestation, and introduced species, especially the Nile perch, are major drivers.
- **Asia:** Indonesia has the most threatened fish species in Asia (n = 50 listed). China, the Philippines, and Sri Lanka have many threatened endemics, including several sawfish and cyprinid species.
- **Australia:** Most freshwater fishes are restricted to a few river systems, and many are threatened by water extraction, altered flows, and exotic species.
- **Central and South America:** Many endemic fishes are threatened by pollution, dams, and introduced species. Threatened freshwater fishes include several *Rivulus* and *Poecilia* species.
- **North America:** The southwestern United States has the highest proportion of threatened fishes. California and the southeastern United States show extensive loss, with dams and water diversion major causes.
- **Oceanic islands:** St. Helena has seven threatened species, Japan 7, Taiwan 6, and Cuba 4.

**Marine fishes**
- About 78% of marine fishes live in shallow continental shelf or reef waters, where all fishes are subject to human influences.
- The IUCN listed 156 exclusively marine species as threatened, up from 20 in 1994. The authors attribute much of this increase to better information rather than a real change.
- The 1992 collapse of the Atlantic cod fishery off Newfoundland and Labrador exemplifies overexploitation. Norwegian cod declines were linked to overfishing of prey species, causing cannibalism and starvation.
- [[Cyanide Fishing]] for the aquarium and restaurant trades damages reefs. The [[Humphead Wrasse]] is listed as threatened partly because of this fishery, with one plate selling for up to $225 in Asian restaurants.
- [[Bottom Trawling]] and invasive species introduced through ballast water and shipping are growing problems, and San Francisco Bay's fauna is largely non-native.

**Recommendations**
The authors offer ten principles for global policy:
1. Religious and moral leaders should promote responsibility for conservation.
2. Recognize [[Watersheds]] as the basic unit for conservation planning.
3. Address economic systems that undervalue water and subsidize inefficient use, especially for irrigated agriculture.
4. Use [[Ecosystem Management]] as the basis for managing watersheds and landscapes.
5. Increase research, inventory, and monitoring to set conservation priorities.
6. Develop international laws to limit the introduction of non-native species, especially through ballast water and aquaculture.
7. Develop new management for marine fishes and habitats, including large-scale [[Marine Reserves]].
8. Manage fisheries to sustain all native fish stocks, with harvest limits below regeneration rates and protection of spawning aggregations.
9. Link aquatic biodiversity to habitat complexity and water quality, including dam removal and floodplain restoration.
10. Manage [[Aquaculture]] to limit escapes, disease transfer, and habitat pollution.
