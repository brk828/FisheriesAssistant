# Bonytail Chub Broodstock Collection and Transfer from Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This interagency memorandum from the Regional Fisheries office (Region 2) documents the annual collection of endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] from [[Lake Mohave]] for use as broodstock at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]. Wild numbers are extremely low, with four or fewer fish captured in a typical year. The memo reports on a May netting effort that captured 16 bonytail, of which 14 were moved to the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and later shipped to Dexter. Most of these fish died during holding, transport, or after arrival. The memo attributes the losses to a lack of a written capture and handling protocol, poor handling, and weak interagency communication. The document is undated in its heading, and parts of the OCR text are garbled, so some figures and dates are approximate.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Bonytail Chub]], a federally endangered native cyprinid of the lower Colorado River basin.
- **Rationale for collection:** Around 1987, it was proposed that bonytail caught during netting surveys in Lake Mohave be taken to Dexter. Wild-caught fish were considered survivors that could increase the genetic variation of the hatchery broodstock.
- **Founding stock:** The original Dexter broodstock comprised 10 fish. Some fish from the group at the time of transfer from Willow Beach had been released into the lake, so captured fish may be offspring of hatchery-reared fish.
- **Reproductive status:** None of the fish captured since 1987 had spawned. All had died, either at Dexter or at Willow Beach.
- **Program leads:** The survey was conducted under an [[Arizona State University]] project. Leadership included personnel from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Chris Minckley), Arizona State University (Dr. Paul Marsh), and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Kirk Young). Glenn Green of the Willow Beach Hatchery led the field crew. Roger Hammann and Phillip Spee transported fish to Dexter. George Dryer of the Fish Health Center assessed fish health.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mohave is a Colorado River reservoir where netting surveys capture bonytail. The memo describes the reservoir only as the capture site and does not report water chemistry, temperature, or habitat measurements. Hatchery holding conditions at Willow Beach were also not quantified.

Two health concerns are documented:
- **Fungal infection:** Some fish appeared fungused after capture. A treatment was applied, and a decision was made to continue holding the fish in tanks for treatment before shipment.
- **Handling stress:** Fish were captured in nets, held in a live well, and transported to the hatchery. The crew reported no stress test was performed, and handling was identified as a probable cause of loss.

## Key Findings

**1. Very small wild population.** Annual captures of bonytail from Lake Mohave have been four or fewer fish in most years.

**2. Limited broodstock value so far.** Since 1987, none of the wild-caught fish transferred to Dexter or Willow Beach have spawned, and all have died.

**3. 2nd-year capture event (May 10–13).** Netting produced 16 bonytail:
- One fish bore a fin mark. It was [[Fish Tagging|FIT tagged]] and returned to Lake Mohave.
- The other 14 were taken to Willow Beach Hatchery for holding and treatment before shipment to Dexter.

**4. Mortality during holding and transport.**
- Fish were held at Willow Beach for almost two weeks before shipping.
- One fish died during holding at Willow Beach.
- One additional fish died in transit to Dexter.
- Several more died after arrival, leaving four fish in the raceway at the time of the memo.

**5. Shipping decision.** On May 24, the Fish Health Center (George Dryer) judged the fish healthy enough for transport. Shipment to Dexter followed, with arrival reported on June 17 (date partly garbled in OCR).

**6. Root causes identified.** The memo states that the losses appear to relate to handling and not to disease alone. The principal problems identified were:
- No written protocol exists for the capture, handling, and transport of endangered fish.
- Overhandling or poor handling appears to have occurred.
- Communication between agencies was weak; for example, staff on weekend duty were not informed about the fish.

**7. Recommendations.** The memo calls for a written protocol covering capture, handling, holding, treatment, and transport of bonytail, together with clear communication procedures among the cooperating agencies.
