# Pleistocene Geology of Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pleistocene-geology

## Executive Summary

State Geological Survey of Kansas Bulletin 99 (1952), by John C. Frye and A. Byron Leonard, is a synthesis of the non-marine Pleistocene deposits underlying much of Kansas. The report reviews the geologic processes that formed these deposits (glaciation, stream sedimentation, wind deposition, weathering, erosion and pedimentation), explains the principles used to classify and correlate them, and presents the state-wide Pleistocene stratigraphy with its stages, formations and members. It also summarizes Pleistocene drainage changes and the resulting Kansas landscape. The document is a reference work rather than a fish study: it contains no fish species or fish-based data, and the aquatic material it covers is limited to freshwater and terrestrial molluscan (snail and clam) faunas used for dating.

## Background & Description

The report was prepared as background for the [[Kansas Geological Survey]] and for soil scientists, engineers and industrial users of Kansas Pleistocene materials. The Pleistocene (Quaternary) is described as the last major epoch, the interval of the "Great Ice Age." Kansas occupies a transition zone among three provinces: continental glaciation to the northeast, fluviatile and eolian deposits to the west and southwest, and the [[Ozark Province]] to the southeast.

The authors state that Pleistocene deposits contain more than 60 percent of the State's ground-water supplies and most of its sand, gravel and volcanic ash, plus important ceramic materials. The stratigraphic framework relies on criteria including [[Pearlette Volcanic Ash]], [[Molluscan Faunal Assemblages]] and [[Buried Soils]], which allow correlation across the glacial boundary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Pleistocene deposits are entirely non-marine. Key environmental processes described include:

- **Glaciation:** Ice originated in the Keewatin and Labradoran centers. Continental ice reached northeastern Kansas during the Nebraskan and Kansan glaciations; Wisconsinan ice stopped several hundred miles short of the state border. Kansan till exceeds 300 feet in places.
- **Fluviatile sedimentation:** Streams were the most extensive Pleistocene deposits, with alluviation concentrated during late glacial retreat.
- **Eolian sedimentation:** Dune sands derive from Arkansas River alluvium; loess (Peoria loess, early Wisconsinan) was carried from outwash-carrying valleys such as the Platte, Missouri and Republican rivers.
- **Weathering and soils:** More than 90 percent of Kansas's surface possesses the stability needed for soil formation. Zonal, intrazonal and azonal soils were distinguished, with only zonal and intrazonal soils useful for stratigraphy.
- **Erosion and pedimentation:** Flanking pediments in central and western Kansas were veneered with debris and have caused mapping errors in south-central Kansas.

Aquatic and freshwater context is limited to snail and clam faunas in alluvial and lacustrine sediments, used to date units such as the [[Meade Formation]], [[Blanco Formation]] and [[Sanborn Formation]].

## Key Findings

- **Stratigraphic scheme:** Kansas Pleistocene deposits are arranged in cyclic glacial-interglacial units, with five major soil-forming intervals. Stages recognized (oldest to youngest) are Nebraskan, Aftonian, Kansan, Yarmouthian, Illinoian, Sangamonian, Wisconsinan (with Iowan, Tazewellian, Bradyan, Caryan and Mankatoan Substages) and Recent.
- **Nebraskan Stage:** Includes the [[David City Formation]], [[Nebraska Till]] and the [[Blanco Formation]], which the authors place in the Nebraskan Stage based on mollusks, stratigraphic framing and physiography.
- **Kansan Stage:** The most extensive Kansas stage; includes the [[Atchison Formation]] (pro-glacial outwash), [[Kansas Till]] and [[Meade Formation]] (Grand Island and Sappa members). The Pearlette ash bed falls in the Sappa member.
- **Pearlette volcanic ash:** Petrographically distinct ash, known from more than 100 Kansas localities, serves as a single time line for interregional correlation. It is characterized by color, refractive index 1.499–1.501, sharply curved shards, iron oxide below 2 percent in most analyses, and specific gravity 2.21–2.32.
- **Yarmouthian Stage:** Recorded by the Yarmouth buried soil, placed in the earliest Yarmouthian time.
- **Illinoian Stage:** Quantitatively less important than the Kansan; represented by the Crete and Loveland members of the [[Sanborn Formation]].
- **Wisconsinan Stage:** Characterized by extensive loess deposits. The Sanborn formation's Peoria member contains the Brady soil (Bradyan Substage, interglacial).
- **Molluscan fauna:** All Pleistocene stages in Kansas contain abundant fossil mollusks; more than 100 species are listed from more than 200 localities. Five species are restricted to the Nebraskan Stage and absent from the Kansan fauna (65 species, 29 localities).
- **Drainage history:** Early Pleistocene drainage was generally southward through central and western Kansas; progressive changes produced the present stream pattern. Glacial crustal warping likely affected stream gradients.
- **Terminology:** Lithologic units are named as nouns; time and time-rock units take adjectival endings. Buried soils are named for the Age in which they formed (e.g., Sangamon soil).
- **Stated purpose:** To serve as a general data source and a starting point for more detailed regional studies. Figures, tables and appended checklists (e.g., fossil mollusk checklist) are summarized rather than reproduced here.
