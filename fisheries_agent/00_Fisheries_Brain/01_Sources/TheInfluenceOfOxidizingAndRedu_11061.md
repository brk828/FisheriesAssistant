# Influence of Oxidizing and Reducing Conditions on Element Distribution in Lake Sediments

#limnology #geochemistry #lake-sediments

## Executive Summary

This 1965 study by [[Evelyn Gorham]] and [[Dalway Swaine]] compares the elemental composition of oxidizing and reducing lake muds, glacial clays, and ferromanganese "oxidate crusts" from [[Windermere]], [[Esthwaite Water]], and [[Ullswater]] in the [[Lake District]]. Some 26 elements were analyzed semi-quantitatively by spectrography, with carbon, sulfur, phosphorus, iron, manganese, sodium and potassium measured by chemical or flame methods. The authors find that [[Redox Potential]] gradients strongly shape which elements accumulate where. Manganese is the most strongly oxidation-enriched element, while carbon, sulfur, copper, tin and nickel concentrate in organic reducing muds. Oxidate crusts vary far more in composition than muds and rival [[Marine Manganese Nodules]] in some trace metals, particularly lead and zinc. The paper concludes that no general geochemical rules hold across all ferromanganese oxidate deposits.

## Background & Description

Exchange of dissolved substances between mud and water in lakes is controlled largely by the redox potential gradient (Mortimer 1941-1942; Hutchinson 1957; Gorham 1958). The study examines how this gradient affects total sediment chemistry, a question the authors describe as poorly understood.

**Sampling and analysis:**
- Surface muds were collected in October and November 1954 with a [[Jenkin Surface Mud Sampler]], subsampled at the surface, 5 cm and 10 cm depths.
- Samples were dried at 100–110 °C and ground. Carbon was measured by dry combustion, sulfur by a method devised by F. J. H. Mackereth, and phosphorus, iron and manganese spectrophotometrically after acid digestion.
- Remaining elements were estimated semi-quantitatively by the [[Cathode Layer Arc Method]] (9 amp d-c arc). Precision was stated as about ±30%, usually better, especially at low concentrations.
- Not detected (detection limits in ppm given in the paper): As (3,000), Au, Bi (30), Cd (200), Hg (1,000), In (10), Ir, Pt, Sb (300), Tl (10). Ge (possibly ~10 ppm) appeared in three Windermere muds, and Sc (10 ppm) in two crusts.

**Sample set:** About 30 analyzed samples, including surface muds, subsurface reduced muds, a white glacial clay, a greenish gleyed clay, and roughly 10 oxidate crust samples. Crusts were found in Windermere (including the "[[Moon Crust]]" first recorded by Professor H. P. Moon) and Ullswater. The deepest Windermere site (about 60 m) and the Esthwaite Water site (about 16 m) were both sampled.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Redox and sediment color.** Surface samples were rusty or brown from ferric hydroxide and were oxidizing. Deeper samples, with black ferrous sulfide, were reducing. Mortimer's data show that the Windermere profundal mud surface stays permanently oxidized, while Esthwaite's becomes reducing each summer because of deoxygenation in its shallow, fertile basin.

**Oxidate crust formation.** Crusts appear where organic lake mud is thin or absent over basal glacial deposits, so that divalent iron and manganese reach a stable surface and oxidize there. Proposed mechanisms include:
- Supply of Fe and Mn from the water column, possibly bound to organics and metabolized by surface bacteria (Hutchinson 1957; Goldberg and Arrhenius 1958).
- Photosynthesis by algae or aquatic bryophytes raising pH and oxygen at surfaces, which could not operate in darkness (Kindle 1932, 1935; Ljunggren 1953).
- Purely chemical oxidation at some sites.

Crusts form mainly on stony or gravelly, wave-exposed shores, not on soft organic bottom muds (Kindle 1936). Freshwater crusts grow much faster than marine nodules. Goldberg and Arrhenius quote about 1 mm per 1,000 years in the ocean, whereas visible coatings may form on Nova Scotian rocks within 25 years (Kindle 1935). Ore-scraping in southern Sweden could occur about every 50 years (Ljunggren 1953, quoting Naumann).

**Organisms.** Enrichment of biophile elements (C, S, P, Mo) in crusts suggests some biological influence on accumulation. Aquatic plants in Windermere averaged Fe 1,500 ppm and Mn 1,420 ppm dry weight (Mayer and Gorham 1951).

**Source rocks and minerals.** Lead, zinc and to a lesser extent barium are much more abundant in Lake District crusts and muds than in Scottish soils or the lithosphere. Local accessory minerals rich in these metals may supply them to crusts by sorption (Smith 1960).

## Key Findings

**1. Oxidation versus reduction (oxidized vs. reduced muds)**
- Manganese is 6.5 times as abundant in oxidized surface layers as in reduced muds beneath.
- Molybdenum is about twice as plentiful in the oxidized phase, which also has over a third more Fe.
- Most other differences are under 10%, except P and Ba (about 20% enrichment, highly variable).
- Zr, Ni, S, Ga and Sn are 25–33% lower in oxidized than in reduced muds.
- Sn, Ni and S reach their highest levels in reducing subsurface muds.

**2. Sediment-type patterns (Figure 1)**
- Fe, Mn, Zn, Co, Pb, P and Mo are lowest in white glacial clay and highest in oxidate crusts.
- Sr and Ba are much higher in crusts than in other sediments. V and Y are distinctly lower.
- C, Cu, S, Ni and Sn peak in organic muds.
- Ga, Zr and La are highest in glacial clay and lowest in crusts.

**3. Variability among crusts**
- Crusts vary far more than muds. Maximum:minimum concentration ratios were 270 for Pb, 150 for Rb, >80 for Li, 50 for V, and 25 for Ga. Ratios for Fe and Mn were 7 and 9.
- Least variable elements were La (2), Co (3), Ni, Sr and C (4 each).
- Some crusts had less Fe or Mn than some oxidized muds. Crusts are cemented into recognizable aggregates, while muds are loose.

**4. Iron–manganese relationships**
- Fe and Mn are positively correlated in large crusts.
- Fe:Mn ratios of 1–2 mark the richest crusts, and ratios above 2 mark the poorest.
- The Wray Castle Boathouse fragments (sample 765) were richest in Fe, with an Fe:Mn ratio of 5.5. The authors compare this with an average oxidized-mud ratio of 6.6 in one part of the text, and 5.4 in another.
- Fe:Mn ratios were 1.1 in plants, 5.4 (oxidized) and 21 (reduced) in Windermere muds, and 80 in white glacial clay.
- Only C, S, P and Mo correlate positively with Fe and Mn in crusts, which the authors attribute to possible biological influence.
- Fe:P ratios in crusts were mostly 54–70. Muds averaged in the low twenties, likely because of organic phosphorus.

**5. Lake comparison (Esthwaite vs. Windermere)**
- Muds differ little in most elements. Esthwaite muds are notably poorer in Mn and Mo, probably because summer reduction releases them to bottom water.
- Sulfur is the element most enriched in Esthwaite. The C:S ratio is 22 in Esthwaite and 30 in Windermere, reflecting greater reduction in Esthwaite.
- Only four elements differ appreciably between the two lakes, and the other 23 are much the same.

**6. Comparison with other ferromanganese deposits (Table 3)**
- Swedish lake ores have the highest Fe, English crusts the lowest. Swedish stream ores are richest in Mn.
- Median Fe and Mn: Fennoscandian freshwater oxidates 31% Fe and 13% Mn; marine oxidates 13% Fe and 22% Mn. This inverse Fe–Mn pattern is reversed within the English freshwater set.
- Marine nodules are much richer in Mo, V, Ag, Cu, Sn, Ni and B. Co is also high in many marine oxidates.
- Pb and Zn enrichment in some English crusts is comparable to marine nodules, probably from local ore bodies.
- Goldberg's Ni–Mn and Co–Fe correlations for marine nodules are not reproduced in the freshwater crusts.

**7. Conclusions**
- No geochemical regularities hold across all ferromanganese oxidate deposits. Even the Fe–Mn inverse relationship is reversed in English freshwater samples.
- The authors recommend future work on element abundances in water, sediments and concretions within small areas, and on crusts forming directly on rocks and their ambient waters.
