# Lower Colorado River Bait Industry Survey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the summary statement of a first, rapidly executed survey of [[Bait Dealers]] along the [[Lower Colorado River]], from [[Lake Mead]] to the U.S.-Mexican Boundary. The survey was coordinated across [[California]], [[Arizona]], and [[Nevada]]. The author, W. L. Minckley of [[Arizona State University]], reproduced the compiled data by category rather than interpreting it at length. A total of 47 dealers was contacted, roughly 50 per cent of an estimated mode of about 120 dealers. The dominant bait animal was the [[Tiger Salamander]] (waterdog, *Ambystoma tigrinum*), and minnows were also commercially important. Laboratory identification of collected bait fishes is included, with a focus on the origins of [[Golden Shiner]] stocks.

## Background & Description

**Scope and method.** Verbal estimates of the number of bait dealers on the lower river ranged from 75 to 200, with a mode around 120. Field personnel collected data using a standardized [[Bait Industry Survey Data Sheet]]. The compilations cover dealer identity, states of supply, bait kinds and sales rankings, sales volumes, retail prices, income share from bait, holding facility quality, animal condition, dealer cooperation, and licensing legality.

**Bait categories.** Categories on the data sheet included:
- Small and large "dogs" (waterdogs, *Ambystoma tigrinum*)
- Small and large minnows
- [[Mudsuckers]]
- Specific fishes: [[Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Mountain Sucker]]

**Laboratory identifications.** Appendix I lists the common and scientific names of fishes collected from bait tanks on March 26, 1971 (the OCR reads "19713"). Species with particular taxonomic discussion include Golden Shiner, Red Shiner, Fathead Minnow, [[Speckled Dace]], [[Nameless Sucker]] (*Pantosteus* sp.), and [[Mosquitofish]].

**Species list from Appendix I:**
- *Cyprinus carpio* (Carp)
- *Carassius auratus* (Goldfish)
- *Notemigonus crysoleucus* (Golden Shiner)
- *Notropis lutrensis* (Red Shiner)

- *Micropterus salmoides* (Largemouth Bass)

Note: The tiger salamander (waterdog) is an amphibian, not a fish. It is included here because the survey treats it as the most important bait animal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Bait trade as a vector for fish movement.** The survey addresses the origin and movement of bait stocks. Two forms or subspecies of [[Golden Shiner]] are known from Arizona and southern California. Samples from bait tanks suggest that both forms, or a hybrid swarm of the two, remain present along the lower river. The author notes that "new forms are appearing in the open waters," implying that fish are being moved by parties not reflected in agency records.

**Provenance and legality.** The author found a general lack of illegal or unexpected species in the samples, which he interprets as evidence of "considerable competency and honesty" among dealers. The postscript cautions that a legitimate bait farmer who obtained brood stock from distant sources (such as Texas or Minnesota) would perpetuate and distribute that morphotype, so no firm conclusion can be drawn about the immediate source of sampled golden shiners.

**Regional fish populations.** The [[Virgin River]] race of *Rhinichthys osculus* and the Virgin River *Pantosteus* are relevant to the "nameless suckers" found in Nevada collections. The author suggests these suckers may derive from the [[Shoal Creek]] system, a small closed basin north of the Virgin River. No other Virgin River fishes were present in the collections.

**Mosquitofish distribution.** All mosquitofish were referred to the western subspecies, *G. a. affinis*. The eastern form is rarely found in Arizona and apparently has not established along the Colorado River.

## Key Findings

**Dealer survey**
- 47 dealers were contacted, approaching 50 per cent of the probable mode of about 120 dealers on the lower river.
- Verbal estimates of total dealers ranged from 75 to 200.
- The survey was coordinated across California, Arizona, and Nevada, with a Utah contact reported with Nevada data.

**Bait importance**
- Waterdogs (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) are the most important bait animal on the lower river, consistent with [[Espinosa and Deacon 1970]] for the [[Lake Mead]] area.
- Minnows are also a very important commodity.

**Collected fish specimens (as legible in OCR)**
- Golden Shiner: 847 specimens (Arizona), 365 (California), 283 (Nevada).
- Mountain Sucker: 222 specimens (Arizona), 211 (California), 1 (Nevada).
- Carp: 16 specimens (Arizona).
- Bluegill Sunfish: 5 specimens (Arizona), 5 (Nevada).
- Red Shiner: 6 specimens (Arizona).
- Largemouth Bass: 4 specimens (Nevada).
- Green Sunfish: 1 specimen each (Arizona, Nevada).
- Speckled Dace: 1 specimen (Nevada).
- Standard length ranges reported include 23 to 150 mm (range for all collections), 39 to 273 mm, 50 to 120 mm, 17 to 70 mm, and 25 to 45 mm. The OCR does not clearly link each range to a species.

**Golden shiner subspecies analysis**
- A total of 1,205 anal fin-ray counts were made, with sample sizes per collection from 5 to 62 fish.
- The total range was 10 to 18 anal fin-rays. The low end is quite low for the northern *N. c. auratus*, and the high end is high for the southwestern *N. c. seco*.
- An analysis of variance (by D. Portz) showed highly significant differences within the golden shiner mass. The author concludes that not all material comes from one supplier and that stocks have mixed genetic origins.

**Other taxonomic determinations**
- Red shiners were referred to the typical subspecies (*N. l. lutrensis*) because of their small size and poor condition.
- Fathead minnows were too small and poor in condition to assign to subspecies.
- The single speckled dace appears to be the Virgin River race.
- All mosquitofish were the western subspecies, *G. a. affinis*.

**Dealer conduct and licensing**
- Licensing (0 = licensed, 1 = unlicensed): 36 licensed and 7 unlicensed, with no information on 4 dealers.
- The general absence of illegal or unexpected species was interpreted as evidence of dealer competence and honesty.

**Qualitative evaluations (scale 0 = poor to 3 = excellent, all states combined)**
- Holding facilities: 1 dealer scored 0, 10 scored 1, 23 scored 2, and 9 scored 3.
- Condition of bait animals: 9 scored 1, 21 scored 2, and 13 scored 3.
- Cooperation and reliability of information: 2 scored 0, 9 scored 1, 21 scored 2, and 13 scored 3.

**Retail pricing (all states combined)**
- Small "dogs" were most often priced at $1.50–1.99 per dozen (14 dealers).
- Large "dogs" were most often priced at $1.50–1.99 per dozen (13 dealers).
- Small minnows were spread across the lower price brackets, with 10 dealers at $0.50–0.99... (per the price code, category 1 is $1.00–1.49).

**Methodological caveats**
- Several OCR-garbled tables (state-level dealer counts, income percentage distributions, and some volume tables) could not be reliably transcribed. Values here are restricted to those legible in the source.
- The author states that some questionnaire categories "may have been somewhat unrealistic," although all yielded interesting data.

**Dates and submission**
- Specimens were collected March 26, 1971.
- The report was submitted August 5, 1971. A postscript is dated August 6, 1971.
