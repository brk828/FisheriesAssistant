# San Bernardino and Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuges: Comprehensive Management Plan 1995–2015

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft Comprehensive Management Plan (1995–2015), prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region 2, sets 20-year direction for the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge]] in Cochise County, Arizona. Both refuges are core habitats for four threatened and endangered [[Yaqui Fishes]] (Yaqui chub, Yaqui topminnow, Yaqui catfish, beautiful shiner), which survive in the United States only on these refuges, apart from propagated Yaqui catfish. The plan treats the refuges as elements of a wider Area of Ecological Concern centred on the San Bernardino Valley and the Leslie Creek watershed, and it identifies groundwater depletion, including pumping in Mexico, as the principal long-term threat. It covers inventory (geology, soils, habitats, wildlife, water rights, public use, administration), legal and policy context, issue-based analysis, and goals with strategies.

## Background & Description

- **Location:** San Bernardino NWR lies about 16 miles east of Douglas, Arizona, on the Mexican border. Leslie Canyon NWR lies north of it in the Swisshelm Mountains.
- **Establishment:** San Bernardino NWR was established in 1982–1983; Leslie Canyon NWR was added in 1988 and given full refuge status in 1993. Both were established under the [[Fish and Wildlife Act of 1956]] and the [[Endangered Species Act]] to conserve listed species.
- **Land:** San Bernardino NWR covers 2,309 acres, purchased from [[The Nature Conservancy]] in 1982. Leslie Canyon NWR was expanded from 1,240 to 2,440 acres after a 1994 acquisition by The Conservation Fund.
- **Historical use:** The area was occupied for more than 10,000 years and later ranched. The [[Slaughter Ranch]] (National Historic Landmark, 180 acres designated in 1964) operated from 1884 onward. Cattle grazing, farming, pumping and channelization drained marshes, lowered the water table and converted grasslands to mesquite.
- **Area of Ecological Concern:** The San Bernardino Valley watershed is roughly 900 mi², of which 420 mi² lie in the US and 580 mi² in Mexico. The Leslie Creek watershed is roughly 80 mi².
- **Planning structure:** Part I (Inventory), Part II (Analysis, including Yaqui fish and plant/animal diversity analyses), Part III (Synthesis, goals and objectives for 10 issues), Part IV (Appendices A–F, species inventories and cultural resources policy).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and seismicity.** The San Bernardino Valley formed in the middle to late Tertiary through normal faulting. Basin fill includes stream deposits, buried lake beds and lava flows that host the San Bernardino artesian aquifer. A 1887 earthquake (reported as 7.5 on the Richter scale) reportedly altered groundwater sources, causing springs and wells to dry up and new ones to appear.

**Soils.** Eight soil series occur in the Area of Ecological Concern, six on San Bernardino NWR. Valley-floor soils such as Karro loam support mesquite bosque and riparian habitat.

**Habitats.** Eight habitat types were recognised (after [[Dirk V. Lanning]], 1981). Desert scrub covers about 39 percent (900 acres) of San Bernardino NWR and 52 percent (1,280 acres) of Leslie Canyon NWR. Desert grassland covers about 577 acres (25 percent) and 1,060 acres (43 percent) respectively. Mesquite bosque covers 508 acres (22 percent) of San Bernardino NWR. Riparian areas (forest, scrub, marsh and aquatic) cover only about 2 percent (55 acres) and 4 percent (100 acres).

**Hydrology.** Artesian water is warm, about 83–90 °F, and is used for irrigation even in winter. Well pressure has fallen from artesian lift of about 12 feet in historical photographs to a maximum of 4 feet. USGS photographs (1958 and 1979) document downcutting in cienega habitats.

**Water rights.** Leslie Creek instream flow rights were granted in April 1992. The annual total is 419.90 acre-feet. San Bernardino NWR holds groundwater registrations for 10 wells (250 AF/annum each, priority 1903) and surface claims for six springs (priority 1884). These rights do not protect artesian flow, and they offer no protection against pumping in Mexico.

**Fauna (as recorded in the document).** About 250 bird species; 42 mammal, 27 reptile, 9 fish and 10 amphibian species. Over 90 neotropical bird species use the refuges. Fish inventory details are in [[Appendix D]].

**Socioeconomic setting.** Mining employment in Cochise County fell to 4.2 percent of the workforce. Agriculture and ranching rank first in employment and product value. The [[Maquiladora Program]] has 1,925 plants employing 452,399 workers, and Agua Prieta (about 80,000 people) is across the border from Douglas (about 19,000). The regional population is projected to reach about 250,000 by 2010. The [[North American Free Trade Agreement]] is expected to increase pressure on groundwater.

## Key Findings

**Yaqui fish status (Analysis Unit 2 and Part III).**
- Yaqui chub (endangered): nine populations.
- Yaqui topminnow (endangered): 15 populations.
- Yaqui catfish (threatened): extirpated from the wild in the US; the only US population is propagated at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- Beautiful shiner (threatened): three populations on San Bernardino NWR.
- Yaqui sucker, Mexican stoneroller, roundtail chub and longfin dace are listed as Candidate Category 2.

**Threats identified.**
- Groundwater pumping, in the US and especially in Mexico.
- Non-native fishes, notably [[Mosquitofish]], which threaten topminnows and chubs. Bullhead, sunfish, largemouth bass and channel catfish are also named.
- Bullfrogs, which have reduced Chiricahua leopard frogs and affected Mexican garter snakes.
- Woody plant encroachment (mesquite, creosote, tarbush), which lowers water yield.
- Development linked to NAFTA and mining, including in the Leslie Creek basin.

**Recovery conditions.** The [[Fishes of the Rio Yaqui Recovery Plan]] (1995) requires five years of compliance before downlisting the chub and topminnow to threatened status. The conditions are eradication of non-native fishes from critical habitat, protection of the San Bernardino aquifer and Leslie Creek watershed, and protection from mining, introductions, diversion and removal.

**Management actions proposed.**
- Build a computer model of the San Bernardino Valley aquifer with the [[University of Arizona]] Department of Hydrology, and install monitoring in Mexico and on the refuges.
- Pursue a cooperative agreement with Mexico (SEDESOL, Centro Ecológico).
- Seek instream flow rights for Black Draw and conservation easements through the [[Malpai Borderlands Group]].
- Use prescribed fire every 3–5 years and mechanical control of woody plants in desert scrub, grassland and mesquite bosque.
- Establish a 24,000-acre protection area around Leslie Creek. Current owners are Bar Boot Ranch (13,000 acres), 99 Bar Ranch (7,200 acres) and BLM tracts (2,700 acres).
- Pursue a three-way land exchange (330-acre parcel for 130-acre Bartlett tract).
- Create public education programmes, including a self-guided trail at House Pond and a student stocking programme.

**Management constraints.** Staff was reduced to three permanent full-time employees after a biological technician position was lost. Approval was granted for one full-time fisheries and herpetology biologist, funded through NAFTA funds. The manager projects a minimum operating budget of $215,000, rising to $230,000–250,000 once the biologist is hired. Hunting (quail, dove, cottontail only) is allowed; fishing and trapping are prohibited.

**Planning outcomes.** The plan sets nine expected outcomes, including legal compliance, a basis for budgets and monitoring, continuity, and recognition of ecological context. It lists ten policy issues, including Ecosystem Sustainability, Endangered Species Management, Water Rights, and Interagency Coordination.

**Cultural resources.** Pueblo sites (Animas Phase/Casas Grandes and Salado, about AD 1200–1400) and the Slaughter Ranch complex are present. Management is by preservation in place, with archaeological monitoring required in Hay Hollow Wash and Black Draw.
