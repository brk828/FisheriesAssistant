# The Fishes of the Tigris and Euphrates Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by [[K. E. Banister]] in the Dr. W. Junk monograph *Euphrates and Tigris, Mesopotamian Ecology and Destiny* (Volume 38, edited by J. Illies and Julian Rzoska, 1980), reviews the freshwater ichthyofauna of the Tigris-Euphrates system. It finds that the fauna is poorly known, taxonomically unstable and largely derived from literature checklists rather than verified collections. The fauna is dominated by Ostariophysi, shows a mixed Palaearctic, Oriental and Afro-Indian composition, and has low endemicity. The author attributes this to the geologically recent and unsettled history of the basin. The chapter also includes a compiled species table and a short note on malaria in Iraq, drawn from [[T. T. Macan]] (p. 94).

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The Tigris and Euphrates originate in the highlands of Turkey, separated from Black Sea and Caspian drainages by high watersheds, and from Mediterranean drainages by low, arid watersheds in Syria and southern Turkey. Lower reaches are bounded by the [[Zagros Mountains]] to the east and the Arabian desert to the west.
- **Confluence:** Below Baghdad the rivers cross a marshy alluvial plain and join at Basrah to form the [[Shatt al-Arab]], which enters the [[Persian Gulf]]. The two rivers are treated as one zoogeographical unit.
- **Wetlands:** [[Hawr al Hammar]] covers 5200 km² at high water and 3500 km² at low water; it is shallow, eutrophic and a major freshwater fishery. [[Lake Habaniyah]] is the deepest standing water (13 m when flooded, 6 m at end of summer). The swamps more than double in area when flooded.
- **Fish use:** Inhabitants have fished these rivers for over 4000 years; the Sumerians built the earliest known fishponds in temples.
- **Checklist history:** Early lists by Khalaf (1962) and Mahdi (1962) each gave 41 primary freshwater species, with 27 in common, implying 55 species. Later compilations, notably [[Al-Nasiri and Hoda (1976)]], produced a working total of 59 nominal species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geological history.** The Mesopotamian Plain formed at the end of the Zagros orogeny (Miocene-Pliocene) and has been subsiding since. Salt-water floods have penetrated upstream within the last 2500 years, and the Hawr al Hammar marsh has a historically recent origin, with a major flood and subsidence around 636 AD.

**Faunal origins.** Kosswig (1956) proposed exchange between tropical and Palaearctic faunas beginning in the Pliocene with the retreat of the Fars (Syrian-Iranian) Sea. Uplift of the Anatolian plateau and river capture reshaped drainages. The Tigris and Euphrates probably first formed in the Pliocene.

**Zoogeographic regions.** [[De Beaufort (1951)]] placed Mesopotamia in a holarctic-oriental transition zone. [[Banarescu (1975)]] assigned the region to a poor west Asian subregion with European and Indo-Malayan affinities. Kuru (1971) recognised four elements in the Anatolian headwaters: Western Palaearctic, western Asian, south-east Asian/Indian (with African links), and a Sarmatian (proto-Black Sea) component.

**Paleoclimate.** Before about 10,000 years ago the Persian Gulf was filled with Tigris-Euphrates discharge under a wetter climate. Typhlogarra's subterranean habitat near Haditha suggests surface streams once existed where only groundwater flows today.

**Seasonal behaviour.** Al-Hamed (1966) reported that as summer water levels fall and temperatures rise, fish move upstream or into the deepest lake and marsh areas.

**Malaria note.** According to Macan, malaria in Iraq is intense in places but sporadic, depending on irrigation maintenance; rivers become breeding sites when they spill, and channels and side bays of the Shatt al-Arab were highly malarious during the study period.

## Key Findings

1. **Low endemicity and mixed origins.** The fauna is almost entirely Ostariophysi. The only non-ostariophysan fishes are *Mastacembelus mastacembelus* (Percomorphi) and the Trout (*Salmo trutta*, a non-primary species confined to Anatolian headwaters). The author attributes low endemicity to the short time since the basin was stable enough for speciation.
2. **Taxonomic instability.** Checklist errors propagate between authors. Notable cases:
   - *Aclyptosternon coum* (Sisoridae) has been listed under Siluridae, Sisoridae and Ariidae. Only one species is involved, and the generic name remains uncertain.
   - *Systomus albus* was synonymised with *Barbus luteus*, then listed separately as *Barynotus albus* beside *Barbus luteus*.
   - *Phoxinellus zeregi* and *Garra lamia* are listed in the region but are probably misidentifications (their ranges lie elsewhere).
3. **Species count.** The compiled table contains about 59 nominal species. Major families include Cyprinidae (the largest group), Cobitidae, Siluridae, Sisoridae, Bagridae, Heteropneustidae and Mastacembelidae.
4. **Recent arrival.** *Heteropneustes fossilis* (Stinging Catfish), native to Pakistan eastwards, first appeared in the Shatt al-Arab in 1960 and spread through the system, probably after a large-scale migration.
5. **Barbus.** The genus is the most speciose in the system. Its 12 recognised species split into a "European" stock (7 species, including *B. esocinus*, *B. longiceps*, *B. plebejus*) and an "Afro-Indian" stock (*B. capito*, *B. grypus*, *B. sharpeyi*). *Barbus grypus* may exceed 2 m and 100 kg, making it a contender for the largest fish in the system. The genus is probably polyphyletic.
6. **Capoeta and Cyprinion.** *Capoeta* is endemic to the region from northern Turkey to northern Afghanistan. *Cyprinion* is endemic to the Middle East between Syria and India, and its two species here lie outside the eastern species group. Hora (1956) argued from third-millennium BC paintings at Lal, Baluchistan, that *Cyprinion* has since disappeared from the region, implying a wetter past.
7. **Garra and Typhlogarra.** *Garra rufa* and *G. barriemiae* (Oman/UAE) may be closely related but are separated today by the Persian Gulf and Arabian desert. *Typhlogarra widdowsoni* is a blind, hypogean species from Haditha, with a dental pattern unlike the epigean *Garra*, so its sister group is unresolved.
8. **Catfish affinities.** The catfishes show both European (*Silurus glanis*, the southern limit of the Wels range) and southern Asian elements. The identity of *S. triostegus* and *S. chantrei* is unresolved.
9. **Mastacembelus.** *M. mastacembelus* is absent from the Orontes but once recorded from the Quweik River, suggesting a former confluence with the Euphrates. Its range is otherwise confined to the Tigris-Euphrates system.
10. **Research gaps.** Identifications largely rest on literature review. Few species have verified collections, distributions within the system are undocumented, and phylogenetic analysis is lacking. The author calls for revisionary and phylogenetic studies.
