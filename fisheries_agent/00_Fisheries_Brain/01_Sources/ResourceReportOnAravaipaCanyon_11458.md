# Aravaipa Canyon Resource Report and Management Plan

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #native-fish #riparian-ecology #land-management

## Executive Summary

This 1970 Bureau of Land Management resource report documents the natural values of [[Aravaipa Canyon]], Arizona, and proposes management criteria for its designation as a Primitive Area. The canyon contains a 15-mile perennial stream supporting seven of twelve native fish species from the [[San Pedro River]] system, including two rare and endangered species: the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). The report emphasizes that Aravaipa Creek represents "the last remaining relatively undisturbed habitat of its kind located in the desert of Arizona" and recommends restricting vehicular access, controlling livestock grazing, and protecting water quality to preserve this unique aquatic ecosystem. Public hearings in 1968 demonstrated widespread support for retaining these 5,297 acres in Federal ownership for multiple-use management prioritizing conservation values.

## Background & Description

Aravaipa Canyon is located in southeastern Arizona, accessible via approximately 11 miles of dirt road from State Highway 77 (west) or 45 miles from U.S. Highway 70 (east). The canyon features steep walls rising up to 1,000 feet above [[Aravaipa Creek]], carved through Galiuro Volcanics and Hell Hole conglomerate of middle Tertiary age. The U.S. Geological Survey calculates the drainage area as 541 square miles.

The perennial stream flows for approximately 15 miles, emerging where subsurface water is confined to a narrow channel (generally in Section 27, T.6 S., R.19 E.). Long-time residents report the stream has maintained relatively constant minimum flow. The riparian community occupies only about 90 acres of vegetated canyon bottom (300 feet average width), dominated by ash (*Fraxinus*), cottonwood (*Populus fremonti*), sycamore (*Platanus wrighti*), willow (*Salix bonplandiana*), and walnut (*Juglans major*) as overstory, with mesquite, box elder, shrub oak and hackberry as secondary species. Understory includes Bermuda grass, bristlegrass, and side-oats grama.

Four major plant communities occur: (1) riparian community along the stream; (2) southern desert shrub (paloverde-saguaro) on xeric south-facing slopes; (3) mountain shrub complex on north-facing slopes with shrub oak, algerita, and scattered pinyon-juniper; and (4) desert grassland above the canyon rim with blue grama, curly mesquite, and tobosa.

Climate is typical of southern Arizona mountain-valley complexes, with mild winters and warm to very hot summers. The area is isolated, with sparse resident population consisting mainly of ranch families. Five livestock operations lease Federal lands within the planning area, running cow-calf operations that have historically used the canyon since before 1900.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish Fauna**: Aravaipa Creek supports seven native fish species representing a "small remnant of a fast vanishing aquatic habitat type":

- [[Gila Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*)
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)
- [[Western Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) - rare and endangered
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) - rare and endangered

The report emphasizes: "No other stream in Arizona is known to support such a large number of the native fishes in the absence of substantial numbers of introduced species." The loach minnow and spikedace are considered rare and endangered by the International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources. Other suitable habitats within the [[Gila River Basin]] are "rapidly becoming non-existent, due to the activities of man."

**Avifauna**: The area serves as a haven for large numbers of birds due to year-round water availability and lush vegetation. Uncommon peripheral species from Mexican fauna include:

- [[Zone-tailed Hawk]] (*Buteo albunotatus*)
- [[Northern Grey Hawk]] (*Buteo nitidus*)
- [[Northern Black Hawk]] (*Buteogallus anthracinus*)

The [[Water Ouzel]] (*Cinclus mexicanus*) occurs due to unique habitat, and the rare [[Winter Wren]] (*Troglodytes troglodytes*) has been observed in November.

**Mammals**: The only known maternity colony of the [[Mexican Big-eared Bat]] (*Plecotus phyllotis*) occurs in a side canyon cave. This uncommon species has been recorded only a few times in Arizona, New Mexico, and Mexico. Other mammals include mule deer, white-tailed deer, javelina, mountain lion, bobcat, grey fox, raccoon, and various bat species.

**Threats to Aquatic Ecosystem**:

1. **Water Diversion**: Approximately 130 acres under cultivation depend on Aravaipa Creek surface water. An additional 100 acres in private ownership may be suitable for agricultural development. Insecticides, herbicides, or chemical fertilizers could affect water quality.

2. **Groundwater Discharge**: Mining operations near Klondyke, particularly copper ore milling, represent "the singularly most important threat to quality and quantity of water in Aravaipa Creek."

3. **Vehicular Use**: Four-wheel drive vehicles cause "churning action of tires on the highly erodable, sandy canyon bottom" destroying vegetated strips along the stream channel and furnishing moveable material for downstream transport during floods.

4. **Livestock Grazing**: Over-utilization of soil-holding vegetation may result in accelerated erosion and siltation. The Salazar operation concentrates 20-25 head yearlong in the canyon bottom, with numbers reaching 175-250 head during 7-10 day working periods in late April-May. Evidence includes "high-lined ash and mesquite trees" and lack of reproduction of desirable riparian trees (ash, cottonwood, sycamore, walnut).

5. **Exotic Species Introduction**: Introduction of exotic fish species, particularly [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), could replace native fishes.

The report states: "The water of Aravaipa Creek is the key resource of the area which has created and sustains this outstanding natural ecosystem. Because the habitat is a delicate one, its physical and biotic components and their interaction should remain essentially unaltered."

## Key Findings

**Classification and Legal Status**: Following public notice (August 14, 1968), public hearing (September 24, 1968), and widespread endorsement, the lands were formally classified for Multiple Use Management (November 23, 1968) and designated as [[Aravaipa Canyon Primitive Area]] by the Secretary of the Interior (January 16, 1969). The classification segregated 5,297.29 acres from sale, exchange, selection, and appropriation under agricultural and mining laws.

**Management Recommendations**:

1. **Access Restriction**: Vehicular access between Woods Ranch and Turkey Creek should be restricted to non-vehicular transportation. Turkey Creek Canyon road should remain open but require written BLM permission for improvements or alterations. A temporary closure order (July 6, 1970) excluded all motorized vehicles from approximately 7.5 miles of canyon due to resource damage and extreme fire danger.

2. **Grazing Management**: A grazing management plan should reduce impact on native vegetation reproduction and eliminate supplemental feeding in the canyon. Current use shows detrimental effects: "reduction in the reproduction of the riparian species" and water pollution from livestock and feeding by-products.

3. **Water Quality Protection**: BLM should work with State and county governments and land users to prevent water pollution. Stream flow records since 1919 indicate the threat of flash flooding, particularly during summer storms.

4. **Land Adjustments**: Recommendations include: (a) expanding Primitive Area to include additional public lands on north and south rims; (b) excluding Turkey Creek area and redesignating as "Recreation Lands"; (c) land exchanges with Arizona and private owners to acquire non-Federal land along canyon walls and rims; (d) acquiring public access at eastern and western canyon ends.

5. **Firearms Prohibition**: Use of firearms should be prohibited on all BLM lands in the Primitive Area for public safety and protection of non-game birds and animals.

6. **Recreation Development**: Camping areas, trails, access roads, and parking areas should be developed to accommodate and control recreation use. Development within the Primitive Area should be limited to that necessary for protection and proper administration.

**Scientific and Conservation Value**: The report emphasizes that Aravaipa Canyon represents "a small but outstanding remnant of a vegetation type that is rapidly vanishing from the Southwest, due, in large measure, to recent phreatophyte eradication along major water courses." The area provides critical habitat for species listed under the 1966 Endangered Species Preservation Act (PL 89-669), which directs the Secretary of the Interior to "preserve the habitat of such threatened species on lands under their jurisdiction."

**Recreation Potential**: The area offers opportunities for hiking, rockhounding, nature study, horseback riding, general sightseeing, primitive camping, and solitude. The mild climate encourages yearlong activity, with spring and fall providing optimal conditions. The report notes: "it would be possible to spend at least a week of leisurely walking to view the Canyon (and vicinity) without the threat of becoming bored."

**Current Use Levels**: Recreational use is light due to poor access, distance from population centers, and lack of publicity. Existing use includes sightseeing, exploration by hikers and horseback riders, four-wheel drive vehicle passage, and hunting (primarily deer and javelina). Scientific research and study by University of Arizona and Arizona State University biological sciences departments ranges from one-day field trips to detailed post-graduate studies.

The report concludes that "the protection and continued public enjoyment of the natural features occurring in Aravaipa Canyon and adjoining lands are considered to be the major consideration in land use planning," with the management plan to be developed with public aid and advice to coordinate recreation, grazing, and other uses while protecting public values.
