# Records of Changes in Color Among Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #reef-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1929 paper by [[Charles Haskins Townsend]], Director of the [[New York Aquarium]], documents the rapid, instantaneous color changes of tropical reef fishes held in captivity. Across 28 closely observed species, each could shift among a set of colorations, with three to seven distinct guises per species. The work combines written descriptions, paintings and photographs (Figs. 310–321 and Plates 1–27) to serve as a reference for identification and behavioral interpretation. Townsend argues that published color plates usually show only one phase, so they can mislead field identification. He also weighs competing explanations of reef-fish coloration proposed by [[Reighard]] and [[Longley]].

## Background & Description

Color and pattern change results from the expansion or contraction of [[Chromatophores]], pigment-bearing cells whose granules are controlled by the nervous system. The stimulus is received through the eye. Blind cave fishes and blind salamanders held at the Aquarium remained pale and lacked developed color cells, showing that vision is required for the response.

The illustrated species are mainly tropical reef fishes collected from Florida and Bermuda. The Aquarium had long been able to observe them because public aquaria make captive observation possible. The series of paintings and photographs was produced by Townsend's team, including artists Hashime Murayama, Herbert B. Tschudy, Olive Earle and Charles R. Knight. Photographers worked under difficult conditions, often at night by flashlight, and a portable tank was used to produce alarm responses.

Several species are notable in the text:
- **Shark-sucker** (*Echeneis naucrates*): clings to sharks by a sucking disk and changes from black-striped to gray or white.
- **Spadefish** (*Chaetodipterus faber*): usually silvery with five or six black cross-bands, and mimics the black angelfish when housed with it.
- **Groupers** (*Epinephelus*, *Mycteroperca*, *Promicrops*): the Nassau Grouper has up to eight phases, and the Giant Grouper, about 200 pounds, can shift from dark to pale with heavy blotches.
- **Coney** (*Bodianus fulvus*): shows many intergrading phases; Townsend concludes that the names *fulvus*, *ruber* and *punctatus* refer to one species.
- **Parrotfishes** (*Sparisoma*, *Scarus*, *Pseudoscarus*): the Red Parrotfish can flash about 16 white spots.
- **Trunkfishes, Filefishes and Triggerfishes** (*Lactophrys*, *Monacanthus*, *Balistes*): the Cowfish, Queen Triggerfish and Filefish all show multiple color guises.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Townsend contrasts aquarium observation with reef conditions. In captivity, fishes are not preyed upon, do not forage, and have monotonous backgrounds, so the meaning of their color changes is hard to determine. He identifies several likely drivers:
- **Environmental matching:** fishes shift toward banded or blotched phases on dark rockwork and toward pale phases on grayish sand.
- **Excitement:** feeding, artificial lights and aerators trigger color changes.
- **Fright and distress:** frightened fishes darken or blotch, and stopping the water flow produces a "distress phase".
- **Social and sexual signaling:** the sudden flashing of white spots by the Red Parrotfish may serve as a recognition signal in the wild.

The text also addresses how reef fish coloration might function. [[Reighard]], working at the Tortugas, concluded that conspicuousness has no biological significance and that agility gives protection, while [[Longley]] rejected warning, signal and sexual-selection hypotheses in favor of an obliterative (camouflage) function shaped by natural selection. Townsend agrees that camouflage is probable but argues that the diversity of invertebrates on reefs, observed in Polynesia across more than forty islands, makes brilliant colors match the background. Quotations from [[Wallace]] and [[Hickson]] describe the vivid corals and fishes of the Malay Archipelago and the Celebes.

Several examples suggest social and predatory context:
- A [[Gray Snapper]] learned to avoid red-dyed Atherina prey after the prey were made unpalatable, indicating color perception.
- A Spadefish moved into a tank of black angelfish turned nearly black for several weeks, then reverted with lapses, supporting mimicry.
- A Pigfish adopted the vertical bands of [[Tautog]] and rested among them, so that a casual glance did not distinguish it.
- The Bermuda Chub changed quickly between striped and spotted phases during play.

## Key Findings

- **Instant color change:** all 28 closely watched species made instantaneous shifts between colorations, and three to seven guises per species were clearly distinguished.
- **Phase-based identification problem:** standard color plates typically show only one of a species' several phases, and preservation in spirits removes color, so identification often depends on structural characters.
- **Distress phases:** stopping the water flow until fish became distressed produced additional colorations, which Townsend treats as signs of discomfort.
- **Mimicry and camouflage:** Pigfish and Spadefish provided direct experimental evidence of mimicry, and multiple species show camouflage through background matching.
- **Light and vision dependence:** color change is controlled through the eye, and blind fishes remained colorless.
- **Captive limits:** Townsend stresses that captive behavior may not reflect natural conditions, and that the true range of color phases in the wild is still unknown.
- **Research direction:** Townsend calls for long-term observation of reef fishes in natural settings, particularly during spawning and at night, to clarify the function of these color changes.
- **Taxonomic implication:** the Coney varieties (*ruber*, *punctatus*) are regarded as color phases of one species, and the author anticipates that some specific names may become synonyms as more phase records accumulate.
