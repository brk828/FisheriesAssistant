# Early Life Stages of Native Fishes in the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department final report (February 23, 1996), funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program, examines the early life history of native fishes in the lower 14.2 km of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) in Grand Canyon. The study covered larval distribution and spawning timing, drift from the LCR into the [[Colorado River]] mainstem, near-shore habitat use by young-of-year (YOY) fish, and their behavior. Four extant native species were studied: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]]. Native larvae were widely distributed in near-shore habitats, spawning ran from March to June with a peak in April, and larvae drifted mainly at night. Habitat use overlapped extensively among species, and the authors conclude that near-shore low-velocity habitats serve as important nursery areas.

## Background & Description

Early life stages of Colorado River Basin native fishes had been little studied, though they are considered critical to the life histories of these species, particularly under current conditions. Hypolimnial-release reservoirs produce cold water unfavorable for egg and larval development and reduce larval swimming ability, while nonindigenous predatory and competing fishes raise early-life mortality.

Of the eight native fish species of the Grand Canyon area, three are extirpated ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], [[Roundtail Chub]]) and one, the [[Razorback Sucker]], is extremely rare. The four extant species are known to spawn and rear in the LCR, a major tributary entering the Colorado River in Grand Canyon. Precise spawning locations in the LCR were unknown.

The report sets four objectives:
1. Determine spatio-temporal distributions of larvae in near-shore habitats.
2. Determine drift patterns of larvae and whether larvae move among near-shore habitats, including drift from the LCR to the mainstem.
3. Describe near-shore habitats used by larval fishes.
4. Describe behaviors of larval and juvenile native fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The LCR reach studied runs from [[Chute Falls]] (the upper limit for humpback chub, bluehead sucker and flannelmouth sucker) downstream to the mouth. Perennial flow originates from [[Blue Spring]] (21 km upstream) and downstream springs that provide about 6.31 m³/s at the mouth. Spring water is about 20°C, salty, CO₂-charged and calcite-saturated. Calcite precipitation forms tufaceous dams, notably the [[Atomizer Falls Complex]]. Below Big Canyon, substrates contain more boulders. Near-shore low-velocity habitats (<0.2 m/s) include vegetated margins (e.g., *Apium*, *Scirpus*, *Phragmites*) and boulder-dominated invaginations; midchannel bars and islands are rare.

**Flow and temperature.** Flow fluctuates widely from winter snowmelt and summer storms. A flood of 2830 m³/s was estimated on September 19, 1923. Spring-fed inputs moderate temperature extremes. Flow was recorded at the USGS gauge near Cameron, Arizona.

**Mainstem context.** Mainstem Colorado River water averages about 9°C cooler than the LCR mouth, and daily Glen Canyon Dam discharge fluctuations may flush larvae from near-shore habitats.

**Predation and competition.** Nonnative predators and competitors are abundant. Nonnative fishes found in drift included [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]].

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**
- Larval surveys were made on near-shore low-velocity habitats in spring–summer 1992–1994. Thirty-one longitudinal surveys were completed (12 in 1992, 11 in 1993, 8 in 1994).
- Drift was sampled with [[Drift Nets]] at two sites (10.5 and 1.9 km above the mouth) from May 1991 to July 1993, and at a third site (300 m above the mouth) in May–June 1993.
- Larval stages followed [[Snyder Larval Staging]]: protolarvae, mesolarvae and metalarvae.
- Habitat use was assessed with [[Logistic Regression]] and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Behavior was assessed with focal-animal observation.

**Larval distribution and spawning**
- No obvious longitudinal distribution patterns or specific spawning locations were detected for native protolarvae.
- Speckled dace protolarvae occurred throughout the reach and as far upstream as Blue Spring (21.0 km). Humpback chub and bluehead sucker protolarvae reached 13.5 km, and flannelmouth sucker protolarvae reached 13.0 km.
- Humpback chub spawning, back-calculated from larval length, began in March (1991–1993) and peaked in April: 70% of estimates in April 1991, 80% in April 1992, and 63% in April 1993. Most spawning ended by late May. In 1994, March (52%) and April (5%) were the main months, with sporadic January–February spawning.
- Most spawning followed peak spring runoff: 86% (1991), 96% (1992), 95% (1993) and 6% (1994).

**Drift**
- A total of 1,454 drift samples were collected. About 31% contained larval fish and 26% contained eggs.
- Of larval fish, 81% were native (38% speckled dace, 20% humpback chub, 18% bluehead sucker, 5% flannelmouth sucker), 9% nonnative and 10% too damaged to identify.
- Larvae drifted mainly May–July. Egg drift peaked in spring.
- Larvae drifted at significantly higher densities at night (K-W H = 12.14, p < 0.01). Diel periodicity was shown by humpback chub, speckled dace, bluehead sucker and channel catfish, but not by flannelmouth sucker.
- Native larvae were more abundant in near-shore drift than midchannel drift (H = 44.85, p < 0.001). Most larval fish were more abundant at 1.9 km than at 10.5 km (H = 23.90, p < 0.001), but humpback chub drifted at greater densities at 10.5 km.
- An estimated 377,115 fish drifted out of the LCR to the Colorado River between May 11 and June 26, 1993, of which 81% were native and 19% nonnative.

**Larval movement in traps**
- Larvae were caught equally across the three trap orientations (N = 2,121, H = 1.52, p = 0.467), indicating that all larval stages move into and out of near-shore slack-water habitats.
- Larvae were caught more often at 0600–1200 h than in other diel periods (H = 43.53, p < 0.05).

**Habitat use (1993)**
- Habitat was measured at 10,004 points, representing 1,377 larval and YOY fish <30 mm (462 bluehead sucker, 129 flannelmouth sucker, 258 humpback chub and 528 speckled dace).
- Logistic models could not distinguish any species from the others at better than 61.7% probability at base flow. Bluehead sucker used shallower water, humpback chub used slower velocities, deeper water and larger substrates, and speckled dace used faster velocities and were less often near cover. A significant model could not be built for flannelmouth sucker YOY.
- At high flow, only speckled dace (shallower water) differed. Cover use was concordant among species (Kendall's W = 0.907 at base flow and 0.885 at high flow).
- Older larval stages of most species shifted toward faster, deeper water and greater distance from shore at base flow. Flow, depth and distance from shore were concordant across species (Kendall's W = 0.81–1.0). Substrate was not.

**Behavior of YOY fish**
- Small humpback chub, speckled dace and flannelmouth sucker were more often benthic feeders than larger conspecifics.
- Humpback chub spent more time swimming as they grew, while speckled dace and bluehead sucker spent less.
- Smaller bluehead sucker and humpback chub were chased more often than larger ones.
- Water-column use changed with size. Humpback chub <30 mm used the bottom more, and speckled dace 31–50 mm used the surface more.

**Discussion and conclusions**
- The authors believe the four extant species spawn throughout the LCR below Chute Falls, and that humpback chub spawning may be concentrated at or above 10.5 km.
- Spawning in the LCR appears to be cued by decreasing flows after runoff, with temperature and photoperiod also likely important.
- Low larval counts in 1992 likely reflected flood mortality, flushing and reduced invertebrate food.
- Overlap in habitat use suggests little interspecific competition. Near-shore habitats may be nursery areas, but cold mainstem waters, reduced swimming ability and thermal shock may limit larval survival.
- The authors conclude that the LCR is the only productive humpback chub spawning location in Grand Canyon and that steady flows from Glen Canyon Dam could enhance survival of larval native fishes in the Colorado River.
