# Aquatic Inventory of the Bill Williams and Hassayampa Drainages, Maricopa, Yavapai, and Yuma Counties, Arizona

#ichthyology #aquatic-ecology #desert-streams #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #native-fish #water-quality #macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This technical report documents a comprehensive aquatic inventory of the [[Bill Williams River]] and [[Hassayampa River]] drainages in west-central Arizona, conducted during winter and spring 1980. The study was undertaken by the U.S. Bureau of Land Management Phoenix District Office to provide baseline data on aquatic habitat, water quality, and biological communities for management planning on public lands. The survey covered more than 205 stream-kilometers across 68 stations, encompassing federal, state, and private lands within the Harcuvar, Vulture, and Skull Valley planning units.

The drainages collectively drain 17,951 km² in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Water quality generally met state and federal standards, with exceptions near mining operations. The aquatic fauna was dominated by native species, particularly [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), which comprised 77.2% of all fish collected. A total of 20 fish species were documented, with native fishes accounting for more than 84% of collections. Macroinvertebrate communities were depauperate, dominated by chironomid dipterans, reflecting the physical instability characteristic of desert stream systems. The study provides management recommendations to enhance and maintain aquatic resources, emphasizing riparian protection, instream flow maintenance, and preservation of native fish communities.

## Background & Description

The [[Bill Williams River]] and [[Hassayampa River]] are major tributaries of the [[Colorado River]] system in west-central Arizona. The Bill Williams drainage encompasses 14,141 km², while the Hassayampa drains 3,810 km². Both systems are characterized by highly variable discharge patterns following a bimodal hydrological cycle, with winter frontal storms and summer monsoons separated by spring and autumn drought periods. Flash flooding is common and plays a critical role in maintaining channel morphology and native fish populations.

The Bill Williams River below [[Alamo Dam]] (completed 1969) flows 56 km before entering the Colorado River. The dam regulates flow for flood control, with mean annual discharge declining from 4.2 ± 4.8 m³/sec (pre-impoundment, 1929-1946) to 2.6 ± 4 m³/sec post-impoundment. The river is characterized as a broad, shallow, sand-bottomed run with numerous backwaters; mean depth is 0.2 ± 0.1 m and gradient 0.68 ± 0.44°.

Major tributaries include [[Date Creek]] (drainage area 648 km²) and [[Kirkland Creek]] (1,129 km²). Date Creek is a narrow sandy run with mean discharge of 0.066 m³/sec. Kirkland Creek exhibits greater habitat diversity with well-represented riffles, runs, pools, and backwaters; mean discharge is 0.201 m³/sec.

The [[Hassayampa River]] originates on Mt. Davis in the Bradshaw Mountains (elevation 2,256 m) and flows to the [[Gila River]] (elevation 238 m). Average discharge at Box Canyon is 0.4 ± 0.5 m³/sec (1946-1975), varying from no flow to maximum flows of 1,640 m³/sec. The drainage is characterized by low-order desert streams with mixed substrate: large substrata (boulder, rubble, cobble) in headwaters and unstabilized sand and gravel in lower reaches. Stream gradients average 1.21 ± 0.49°.

Riparian vegetation varies with elevation and includes cottonwood/willow (*Populus fremontii*/*Salix gooddingii*) galleries in upper reaches, transitioning to mesquite (*Prosopis velutina*) and saltcedar (*Tamarix chinensis*) dominance in lower areas. Non-riparian vegetation ranges from ponderosa pine and chaparral at higher elevations to creosote bush/Joshua tree and palo verde/saguaro desertscrub on the desert floor.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water Chemistry**: Water quality was generally within acceptable state (ADHS 1979) and federal (USEPA 1976) standards, with notable exceptions. Water temperature ranged from 7.0 to 23.5 °C (mean 15.6 ± 4.3 °C). pH was consistently alkaline, averaging 8.0 ± 0.5 (range 6.8-9.0). Dissolved oxygen concentrations were high, averaging 10 ± 1.7 mg/l (98.38 ± 12.28% saturation), consistently above the 6.0 mg/l minimum for fisheries.

Specific conductance varied between 295 and 1,000 µmho/cm (mean 512 ± 137 µmho/cm). Total dissolved solids (TDS) ranged from 82 to 410 mg/l (mean 241 ± 71 mg/l), below the 500 mg/l maximum recommended for surface waters but higher than the global river mean of 120 mg/l. [[Kirkland Creek]] had the highest TDS (284 ± 76 mg/l), while the Bill Williams River below Alamo Dam was most dilute (84 ± 2 mg/l).

Macronutrients showed variable patterns. Orthophosphate (PO₄-P) averaged 0.46 ± 0.49 mg/l (range 0-2.4 mg/l) and was not limiting to primary production. Nitrate-nitrogen (NO₃-N) averaged 0.44 ± 0.87 mg/l (range 0-4.5 mg/l) and was rapidly depleted in headwaters, suggesting nitrogen may be the limiting nutrient. Elevated nutrient levels near Wickenburg reflected agricultural runoff and municipal influences.

**Mining Impacts**: Significant water quality violations occurred at [[French Gulch]] below the Zonia Copper Mine. Dissolved copper reached 0.38 mg/l and manganese 31.0 mg/l, both exceeding state standards (0.05 mg/l and 10.0 mg/l, respectively). Sulfate levels reached 2,175 mg/l, far exceeding the 90 mg/l optimum for aquatic life. Bottom sediments contained copper concentrations up to 49,680 mg/l dry weight. Aquatic biota were absent at upstream stations due to synergistic copper-zinc toxicity. The abandoned [[Planet Mine]] on the Bill Williams River was previously documented with thallium concentrations of 5.1 mg/l, the highest in the Southwest.

**Macroinvertebrates**: Benthic invertebrate communities were depauperate, with overall standing crop of 90.8 ± 132.3 individuals/m² and 0.3917 ± 0.5673 g/m². A total of 23 insect taxa within six orders were collected. Dipterans dominated both in abundance (68.4%) and number of taxa (43.5%), primarily chironomid midges. Ephemeroptera and Trichoptera were next most abundant. Mean diversity (d = 3.44) was within the range (2.6-4.0) indicative of clean water systems, despite low taxa richness (4.1 ± 3.1 taxa/sample).

[[Kirkland Creek]] supported the highest invertebrate density (64.6 ± 97.9 individuals/m²), dominated by black flies (*Simulium* sp., 78.8% of collections). The Hassayampa drainage averaged 43.1 ± 36.1 individuals/m². More than 27 stations yielded no invertebrates, reflecting habitat instability from shifting sand substrates, flash flooding, and lack of substrate heterogeneity. Tributaries supported higher densities and diversity than mainstems due to greater physical stability.

**Fish Communities**: Field collections combined with museum records documented at least 20 fish species representing seven families. Native fishes comprised four species but accounted for 84.2% of total collections (6,838 of 8,109 individuals). Introduced species included 16 species and one hybrid, with 15 species present in [[Alamo Lake]].

[[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) was numerically dominant, comprising 77.2% of all collections and 100% of Date Creek fauna. This ubiquitous southwestern minnow occupied laminar runs with sandy bottoms and spawned in shallow backwaters; nests varied from 1 to 28/m² (mean 22 ± 5.8 nests/m²). Males exhibited nuptial tubercles as early as February.

[[Gila Mountain-sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*) was widely distributed in both drainages (except Bill Williams River below Alamo Dam and Date Creek), comprising 6.9% of collections. This species showed strong habitat selectivity for shallow cobble/rubble riffles with swift currents (mean velocity 0.88 ± 0.11 m/sec) and gradients >1°. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests confirmed significant distribution over depth, width, velocity, gradient, percent cover (p=0.95), and elevation (p=0.90).

[[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta robusta*) and [[Gila Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) were restricted to the Bill Williams drainage. Roundtail chub were numerous near the Kirkland-Sycamore creek confluence but absent below Alamo Dam, where they occurred prior to impoundment. Gila sucker occupied deep pools and laminar runs with overhanging vegetation.

Introduced species were largely restricted to the Bill Williams River. [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), introduced to the lower Colorado River in 1955, was second in abundance (11.1%) and dominated the Bill Williams River, where native longfin dace were never collected. This species has been implicated in native minnow declines statewide. [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis affinis*) were abundant in Bill Williams backwaters (4.1% of total catch) and have contributed to declines of native species, particularly the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]].

## Key Findings

**Population Densities and Habitat Relationships**: Overall fish standing crops were low: 1.7 ± 1.7 fish/m² and 6.0 ± 7.1 g/m², similar to other low-desert streams. [[Kirkland Creek]] supported the highest fish density (3.3 ± 3 fish/m²), likely reflecting higher benthic prey availability and habitat stability. Hassayampa tributaries (2.0 ± 1.1 fish/m², 10 ± 6.8 g/m²) exceeded mainstream densities (1.3 ± 0.8 fish/m², 5.8 ± 5.8 g/m²). The Bill Williams River below Alamo Dam had the lowest densities (0.2 ± 0.1 fish/m², 0.9 ± 0.5 g/m²).

Backwaters supported the highest fish densities (6.9 ± 13.6 fish/m²), while riffles were least productive (0.6 ± 0.5 fish/m²). Significant differences occurred between runs and riffles (p=0.95). Native fish density (1.6 ± 4.6 fish/m²) significantly exceeded introduced species (1.0 ± 2.2 fish/m²) at p<0.99. Natives significantly outnumbered non-natives in runs (p<0.99) and riffles (p<0.95), but not in pools or backwaters.

Longfin dace exhibited broad habitat tolerance, occurring in all habitat types but significantly preferring backwaters over riffles (21.0 vs. 0.81 fish/m², p=0.95). Gila mountain-sucker showed strong substrate selectivity for cobble/rubble over sand/gravel (p=0.90). [[Green Sunfish]] (*Chaenobryttus cyanellus*) were significantly distributed over depth, preferring shallow pools <0.5 m (mean depth 0.27 ± 0.10 m).

**Ecosystem Stability and Limiting Factors**: Macroinvertebrate and fish populations were limited by: (1) seasonal flow variations and flash flooding; (2) physicochemical instability; (3) scouring and siltation by fine sediments; and (4) lack of habitat diversity due to substrate homogeneity. Functional group analysis of invertebrates showed dominance of collector-gatherers, with collector-filterers (e.g., *Hydropsyche*, *Simulium*) reduced by scouring and lack of attachment sites.

Kirkland Creek's productivity reflected bottom stability (large substrate types providing permanent attachment sites) and high nutrient availability. Despite highest orthophosphate levels (0.67 ± 0.58 mg/l), nitrate-nitrogen was lowest and rapidly depleted, suggesting nitrogen limitation. The creek supported seven invertebrate taxa and the highest fish biomass (9.8 ± 16.1 g/m²).

**Impact of Flow Regulation**: [[Alamo Dam]] profoundly altered the Bill Williams River ecosystem. Post-impoundment mean discharge declined 38% and averaged 76 zero-flow days annually. The reservoir acts as a settling basin and nutrient trap, producing dilute, high-pH waters (mean pH 8.4 ± 0.2) from phytoplanktonic activity. Native fishes were completely extirpated below the dam, replaced by introduced species. The loss of flash flooding may have facilitated saltcedar establishment and prevented native fish population recovery through displacement of introduced species.

**Management Implications**: The study identified critical management needs:

1. **Riparian Protection**: Livestock grazing in riparian corridors causes bank destabilization, reduced cover, elevated water temperatures, increased erosion and sedimentation, and eutrophication from defecation. Grazing should be eliminated from riparian zones and deferred to upland terraces.

2. **Instream Flow Maintenance**: Water rights should be filed for perennial waters on public lands. Diversions, impoundments, and groundwater withdrawals should be discouraged to maintain native fish populations and riparian vegetation. Flash flooding is essential for maintaining native communities by displacing introduced species and preventing saltcedar establishment.

3. **Mining Pollution**: French Gulch violations (copper 0.38 mg/l, manganese 31.0 mg/l, sulfate 2,175 mg/l) require enforcement action. The Zonia Copper Mine lacks discharge permits; cease discharge orders should be issued by ADHS and USEPA.

4. **Native Fishery Conservation**: Both drainages should be maintained as native non-game fisheries. Introductions of non-native species for bait, sport fishing, or vector control should be prohibited. Desert streams do not satisfy game fish requirements; green sunfish and black bullhead exhibit stunting. Red shiner and mosquitofish introductions have caused native fish declines and extirpations. [[Alamo Lake]] provides the best alternative for warmwater sport fishing.

5. **Spring Surveys**: Natural springs should be surveyed for endemic aquatic invertebrates, particularly gastropods, prior to development as range improvements or wildlife water catchments, given high endemism in closed desert basins.

**Comparative Productivity**: [[Kirkland Creek]] was the most productive system for both invertebrates and fish, attributed to habitat stability (cobble/rubble substrate preventing scouring) and nutrient availability. It supported the greatest fish species richness and highest standing crops. In contrast, Date Creek, the Bill Williams River, and lower Hassayampa reaches lacked riffles and contained unstabilized sand bottoms continually swept clean, resulting in depauperate communities. Hassayampa tributaries consistently exceeded mainstream productivity due to reduced flash flood magnitude and frequency, providing more stable habitat and potential refugia.
