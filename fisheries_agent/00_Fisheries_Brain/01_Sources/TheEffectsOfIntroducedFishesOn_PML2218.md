# Effects of Introduced Fishes on Native Fishes: Arizona, Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae #Ictaluridae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by John N. Rinne of the Forestry Science Laboratory, Arizona State University, reviews how introduced fishes have reduced the native fish fauna of [[Arizona]] and the wider [[American Southwest]]. Before European settlement and landscape change, fewer than 10 native species inhabited the state's rivers and streams. Since the turn of the century to about 1970, more than three times as many species were introduced, intentionally or accidentally, for [[Sport Fishing]], bait, or [[Biological Control]]. More than half became established. Competition, predation, hybridization, and habitat alteration combined to decline native fishes, and the author judges the native fauna endangered. The paper argues that conservation must be innovative, research-based, and tied to the social value of native fishes.

## Background & Description

The native fish fauna of Arizona is depauperate compared with eastern drainages such as the [[Mississippi River]] system. Only about two species had been recorded in the state since the late 1800s, and several have become extinct. In the [[Gila River]], which drains most of Arizona, nearly one-third of the naturally occurring species belong to monotypic genera. Endemism is high in western and southwestern US fishes.

Introductions were made mainly for sport, bait, and biological control. Key groups include:

- **Salmonids:** [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Brook Trout]] now form a major part of the state's coldwater fishery.
- **Catfishes:** [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] are now widespread.
- **Centrarchids:** About 10 species, the most intensively introduced and successful family, range from the lower [[Colorado River]] to upper-elevation mountain streams.
- **Non-sport species:** [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Red Shiner]] are the most widespread. Cichlids such as tilapia occur only sporadically.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author describes four main mechanisms of interaction.

**Hybridization.** [[Rainbow Trout]] spawn in spring, in the same period as the native [[Apache Trout]], and hybridize with it. In the [[White Mountains]], fewer than 12 percent of more than 46 streams sampled between 1977 and 1987 held pure-strain Apache trout. Hybridization is a major contributing factor to decline, and stocking bans and the use of catchable-size fish have largely ended it. Apache trout were once fished at over 100 per hour in the late 1800s.

**Competition.** [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] replaced Apache trout in Ord Creek, where brook trout made up 85 percent of the number and 97 percent of the biomass of adult trout after renovation. Laboratory work suggests brook trout are more aggressive, and brown trout feed more efficiently in low light. Adult brown trout are highly piscivorous. Temperature tolerances of adult Apache trout and all three introduced trouts are the same.

**Red shiner invasion.** The [[Red Shiner]] is a generalist that dominates degraded, turbid, warm streams. Its distribution is inversely related to that of the federally threatened [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. Competition for habitat has been proposed, but the mechanism is not known. In the [[Virgin River]], red shiners replaced [[Woundfin]] after drought and dam construction reduced flows.

**Predation.** Centrarchids have largely displaced the native fauna of the lower Colorado River, probably by eating eggs and young. Channel and flathead catfish were implicated in the decline of [[Razorback Sucker]]. Field evidence suggests that about 700 juvenile razorback suckers were eaten in a 90-hour period in the [[Gila River]]. Native fishes are absent wherever these two large predators are abundant, which the author takes as strong evidence that predation is a primary cause of native fish decline. Green sunfish replaced [[Gila Chub]] in Sycamore Creek.

**Mosquitofish and Gila topminnow.** Mosquitofish, introduced for mosquito control, has replaced the [[Gila Topminnow]] and contributed to the decline of the [[Desert Pupfish]]. Shoenherr (1974) found that predation on fry is the main mechanism, with reduced adult female survival as a secondary cause. Habitat complexity and flood disturbance may allow coexistence.

## Key Findings

- Fewer than 10 native fish species originally inhabited Arizona. More than three times as many species were introduced after about 1900, and over half became established.
- Introduced species have harmed native fauna through hybridization, competition, predation, and habitat alteration. The author judges the native fish fauna endangered.
- Of native fishes in Arizona and the Southwest, 90 percent (Arizona) and 60 percent (Southwest) are listed by federal and state agencies as threatened, endangered, or of special concern.
- Predation is judged likely to be the primary cause of native fish decline in Arizona and the Southwest.
- Introductions cannot be fully reversed. Recommendations include ecosystem- or basin-level management, promoting native species for bait, sport, biological control (for example, the Gila topminnow for mosquito control), and aesthetics, research, and stringent regulation of non-native fishes.
- Aravaipa Creek and the San Bernardino Ranch and San Pedro River conservation areas are examples of habitat protection that conserves native fish diversity.
