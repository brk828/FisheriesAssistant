# Priest Lake Studies: Incubation Channels, Juvenile Production and Mysis Introduction (Project F-53-R-2, 1966)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Idaho Fish and Game Department Job Completion Report (Project F-53-R-2, period March 1, 1966 to February 28, 1967) covers six jobs on [[Priest Lake]] and [[Upper Priest Lake]] in northern Idaho. The work examined how [[Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Kokanee Salmon]] could be propagated, how juvenile trout use tributary streams, how [[Hatchery Reared Fingerling Cutthroat Trout]] contribute to the angler harvest, and whether the non-native [[Mysis Oculata Var. Relicta]] could be established in Idaho lakes. Results covered egg-to-fry survival, creel census trends, downstream trapping, and a large shrimp transplant. The report was written by Wayne Leusink, Fishery Biologist, in January 1968.

## Background & Description

The report is organized into six numbered jobs, each with an abstract, recommendations, objectives, techniques and findings:

- **Job B1:** Use of [[Incubation Channel]]s for cutthroat production, plus a creel census.
- **Job B2:** Production of kokanee for incubation channels, including a winter planting.
- **Job B3:** Production of juvenile cutthroat in Priest Lake tributaries, using an [[Inclined Screen Weir]] and [[Electrofishing]].
- **Job B4:** Survival and harvest of hatchery-reared fingerling cutthroat.
- **Job B5:** Factors affecting kokanee production in Priest Lake.
- **Job B6:** Introduction of Mysis into Idaho lakes.

Cutthroat trout (*Salmo clarki*) are the main management species. Kokanee (*Oncorhynchus nerka*) are a landlocked sockeye form that dominates the angling harvest. The report notes that a 1955–1956 survey (Bjornn) had identified a decline in cutthroat numbers, and the 1966 survey was designed to compare with it. Angler pressure is split between a fall-to-winter Dolly Varden and mackinaw trout fishery and a spring-to-summer kokanee and cutthroat fishery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning habitat:** About 80 percent of kokanee spawned on lakeshore areas and about 20 percent in the first mile of tributaries. Spawners were observed in water from 6 inches to at least 23 feet deep at a near-constant 40°F. Redds ranged from sand to stones about 3 inches in diameter.
- **Silt and drawdown hazards:** Silt deposition and substrate shifting during spring high water were identified as the greatest threat to stream-spawning kokanee. A winter drawdown caused by a dam at the lake outlet also destroyed beach-spawned eggs.
- **Feeding and competition:** Kokanee stomachs were dominated by cladocerans and copepods. Cutthroat stomachs contained mainly insects. Little competition for food was found, and competition with other species was not studied.
- **Stream fish community:** On Kalispell Creek, [[Brook Trout]] were the most abundant resident species. Dolly Varden and sculpin were also captured.
- **Water chemistry:** Water in Kootenay Lake during collection was 37°F with 11.1 ppm oxygen. Priest Lake at introduction was 37°F with 11.7 ppm oxygen.

## Key Findings

### Job B1: Incubation Channels for Cutthroat Trout
- The Kalispell Creek channel received 552,800 eyed cutthroat eggs in June 1966 (410,670; 79,000; and 63,130 eggs on June 2, 24 and 30).
- Total fry emigration from June 26 to August 2 was estimated at 230,217, an egg-to-fry survival of **41.6 percent**.
- The Hunt Creek channel was not opened in 1966 because of an egg shortage. About 696,000 fingerlings were planted there instead.
- The weir at Kalispell Creek intercepted 125 spawned-out cutthroat returning to the lake.
- **Creel census:** 9 percent of anglers were seeking cutthroat, with a catch per man hour of **0.52**. Cutthroat made up 5 percent of the total catch.
- **Recommendations:** Continue the eyed-egg policy until returning adults can be counted, continue an annual creel census, and improve the upstream weirs at Hunt and Kalispell Creeks.

### Job B2: Kokanee for Incubation Channels
- 50,218 eyed kokanee eggs, taken from September spawners, were planted in the Kalispell channel on October 11, 1966.
- About 90 percent hatched in November. Spring rains in February and March 1967 deposited nearly two inches of silt, and 20 percent of sac fry in sample bags were killed outright.
- Only about **3,900 fry** were counted out between May 3 and 10, 1967.
- Hatching required 900 to 1,100 temperature units. Fry left the channel after 1,600 to 1,700 units. Channel water ranged from 36°F to 40°F.
- **Recommendations:** Make another limited plant of early-spawning eggs in fall 1967, and study how silt and temperature affect incubation.

### Job B3: Juvenile Cutthroat in Tributaries
- The inclined screen weir, about 200 yards above the mouth of Kalispell Creek, operated from May 23 to November 30, 1966. It trapped **1,364 fish**: 619 juvenile cutthroat, 125 spent adult cutthroat, 436 brook trout, 70 Dolly Varden and 113 whitefish.
- About **78 percent** of juvenile cutthroat migrated to the lake in their second year of life.
- Of the 125 spawners, 64 were male and 62 female. All were fin-clipped, and one was recaptured in September 1966 at Reeder Creek Bay, about six miles away.
- Growth was fastest in the first year of life.
- Electrofishing in August 1966 at three stations caught 19 cutthroat, 46 brook trout, 10 Dolly Varden and 35 sculpin. Station C, near the state line, had no cutthroat fry, which suggests most lake cutthroat spawn in the lower stream.
- Fry emerged from the gravel in late July. The best rearing habitat was the reach below Station B, with heavy streamside vegetation, deep pools and sandy gravel.

### Job B4: Hatchery Fingerling Survival and Harvest
- About **26,000 (10.4 percent)** of 257,205 fingerlings planted in Upper Priest Lake in May 1966 were marked with an adipose fin-clip.
- Earlier marking rates were 4.2 percent (1964, 5,000 of 117,700) and 12.1 percent (1965, 30,000 of 248,110).
- Creel data estimated **1,263** cutthroat taken from Upper Priest Lake in 1966. Of 48 cutthroat observed in the creel, none were marked.
- Of 66 anglers checked, 23 (35 percent) were seeking cutthroat. They spent 73 hours to catch 48 fish, a catch per man hour of 0.65.
- Night electrofishing along about 6 miles of shoreline caught few trout. Water clarity and low conductivity let cutthroat avoid capture.
- **Recommendations:** Mark at least 10 percent of planted cutthroat, intensify creel census in 1967, and collect age and growth data.

### Job B5: Kokanee Production in Priest Lake
- Kokanee accounted for 75 percent of angling effort in 1966 (84 percent in 1956). Catch per hour was 1.40 by the report's abstract. Anglers spent about 49,203 hours seeking kokanee and took an estimated **68,884** fish.
- Creel mean lengths ranged from 238 mm (June) to 268 mm (September). About 90 percent of kokanee were in their fifth year of life.
- Spawning began by early November and peaked in late November. Males and females measured 294 and 281 mm. Green females averaged **542 eggs**.
- Tributary concentrations were largest in Indian and Kalispell Creeks, and no spawners were found more than one mile above the lake.
- Green eggs planted in the Kalispell channel were mostly lost to silt suffocation during spring runoff.
- **Recommendations:** Continue creel censuses and continue studies of kokanee environment and competition.

### Job B6: Introduction of Mysis Shrimp
- Roughly **850,000** Mysis were collected from Waterton Lake, Alberta, between August 10 and 22, 1966. About 800,000 were transported to Idaho, and 50,000 were planted in Canada by the Canadian government.
- Table 1 release sites: Priest Lake (110,000 on August 13 and 290,000 on August 18), Payette Lake (100,000), Pend Oreille Lake (50,000), Stanley, Redfish and Alturas Lakes (50,000 each) and Anderson Ranch Reservoir (100,000).
- Shipment losses were under 5 percent. A December 1966 collection of about 10,000 from Kootenay Lake lost about 10 percent during holding.
- In a Priest Lake test, Mysis in containers that did not touch the bottom became transparent and appeared to starve. Survivors were those in contact with bottom debris, and one individual lived until mid-June 1967. The authors concluded that Mysis need to feed on or near bottom debris.
- **Recommendations:** Make further introductions, study the chemical and physical factors that affect survival, and review the literature to guide future transplant sites.

### Overall Conclusions
- Eyed-egg incubation channels produced measurable cutthroat fry (41.6 percent survival), but winter kokanee incubation failed mainly because of silt.
- The angling trend from 1956 to 1966 could not be confirmed. Lake Pend Oreille data show annual fluctuations of 5 to 40 percent, and only one other Priest Lake estimate (1955, about 69,000 hours) exists.
