# Age and growth rate of the flannelmouth sucker, Catostomus latipinnis, and bluehead mountain sucker, Pantosteus discobolus, in the Colorado River, Grand Canyon National Park

## Executive Summary

This study (Usher, Carothers, Minckley & Jordan, ~1979-1980) investigated the age and growth of two native catostomid fishes — the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*) — in the Colorado River and its tributaries within [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. Using a novel validation of the **opercle bone aging method** for catostomids, the researchers determined that *C. latipinnis* grows faster and reaches a larger maximum size and older age (10 years) than *P. discobolus* (8 years). Both species exhibit their most rapid growth in the first year of life, followed by a steadily declining growth rate — a pattern distinct from populations in the less-altered upper Colorado River Basin. The authors attribute these regional growth differences to the cold, dam-regulated thermal regime of the mainstem Colorado River below [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

## Background & Description

- **Taxa studied:**
  - *Catostomus latipinnis* — [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], family [[Catostomidae]]
  - *Pantosteus discobolus* — [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]], family [[Catostomidae]]
- **Historical context:** Following construction of [[Hoover Dam]] (1936) and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (completed ~1963, impounding [[Lake Powell]]), the Colorado River in Grand Canyon underwent major ecological changes, including cold, clear, sediment-starved flows and invasion by exotic fish species from [[Lake Mead]].
- **Native fish decline:** Of eight native fish species historically present in the Grand Canyon mainstem, only *C. latipinnis* and *P. discobolus* remained common by the study period. Two others, *Rhinichthys osculus* (speckled dace) and *Gila cypha* ([[Humpback Chub]]), persisted mainly in undammed tributary streams.
- **Study rationale:** No prior life-history studies existed for these suckers in Grand Canyon, despite earlier work in the upper Colorado River Basin (Wiltzius 1976; McAda 1977; McDonald & Dotson 1960).
- **Sampling:** Conducted seasonally (Nov 1977–Oct 1978) via trammel nets, electrofishing, gill/trap nets, and seines in the mainstem and the first 200 m of selected tributaries.
  - *C. latipinnis*: N = 165
  - *P. discobolus*: N = 120
- **Aging method:** Opercle bone method (Le Cren 1947; McConnell 1951), validated by:
  1. Regular increase in annuli number with total length.
  2. Predictable ratio between total length growth and opercle radius growth.
  3. Agreement between length-frequency modes of young fish and back-calculated mean lengths at each annulus.
  - Back-calculations used the **Dahl-Lea direct-proportion method**: L = cR (L = body length, R = opercle radius, c = constant).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime shift:** Pre-dam riverine conditions were replaced by a perpetually cold (7–10°C) mainstem regime below Glen Canyon Dam, contrasting with warmer (9–24°C), more productive side-canyon tributary streams.
- **Habitat partitioning by age:**
  - *C. latipinnis* Age Classes 0–III occur primarily in warmer, more productive tributary ("side canyon") streams.
  - Age Classes IV–X occupy the cold mainstem, except during spawning migrations.
- **Productivity gradient:** Side-canyon streams show higher benthic productivity (unpublished MNA data), likely driving faster early growth relative to the food/temperature-limited mainstem.
- **Regional contrast:** In the less-altered upper Colorado and Green Rivers (Utah/Colorado) and lower Gunnison River, suckers show the opposite pattern — slower early growth (Age Classes I–III) but faster growth in later years (Age Classes IV–VIII) — suggesting no analogous cold/warm habitat segregation exists there.
- **Conservation implication:** *C. latipinnis* and *P. discobolus* were the only native mainstem species that had adapted successfully to the post-dam Colorado River ecosystem in Grand Canyon, underscoring the significance of understanding their life-history plasticity under regulated flow regimes. See also [[Colorado River Regulation]] and [[Native Fish Decline in the Colorado River]].

## Key Findings

### Aging Method Validation
- Opercle method successfully validated for both catostomid species for the first time.
- Strong correlation between opercle radius and total length in both species (r² = 0.985 for *C. latipinnis*; r² = 0.984 for *P. discobolus*).
- Length-frequency modes for young fish closely matched back-calculated lengths at first annulus:
  - *P. discobolus*: 70 mm (mode) vs. 69.3–74.3 mm (calculated, Age Classes I–II)
  - *C. latipinnis*: 75.0 mm (mode) vs. 77.2 mm (calculated, Age Class I)

### Flannelmouth Sucker (*C. latipinnis*)
- Age range in sample: 0–10 years.
- Oldest confirmed individual: 10-year-old female, 515 mm; largest aged individual: Age Class VIII female, 570 mm.
- Several unaged individuals (580–610 mm) likely exceeded 10 years.
- Greatest annual growth increment in Year 1 (121.6 mm), then sharp decline in Year 2 (63.5 mm), followed by a steady decline through subsequent years.
- Adult population length-frequency distribution centered around 460 mm (Age Classes VII–VIII).
- **Sexual size dimorphism:** Females significantly larger than males across most age classes, with the difference increasing with age.
- Young-of-year first appear in July/August samples.

### Bluehead Mountain Sucker (*P. discobolus*)
- Age range in sample: 0–8 years.
- Oldest individuals: two Age Class VIII females (360 mm, 318 mm); a 6-year-old female was slightly longer (361 mm); several unaged individuals reached 368–397 mm.
- Greatest annual growth increment in Year 1 (93.7 mm), sharp decline in Year 2 (34.0 mm), followed by a slow, steady decline.
- Adult population length-frequency distribution relatively evenly spread across size classes.
- **No significant sexual size dimorphism** through Age Classes I–V; a possible trend toward larger females appears in older age classes (sample size insufficient to confirm statistically).
- Young-of-year first appear in May samples.

### Regional Growth Comparisons
- **Wiltzius (1976), lower Gunnison River:**
  - *C. latipinnis* Age Class II: 44% shorter than Grand Canyon fish.
  - *C. latipinnis* Age Class VIII: 13.8% longer than Grand Canyon fish.
  - *P. discobolus*: grew ~22% faster on average than Grand Canyon population.
- **McAda (1977), upper Colorado/Green/Yampa Rivers:**
  - *C. latipinnis* Age Class II: 27% shorter than Grand Canyon fish.
  - Age Classes IV–VIII: ~13% longer than Grand Canyon fish.
- **Growth curve shape difference:** Grand Canyon populations of *C. latipinnis* peak in growth rate during Year 1, then decline steadily; upper basin populations instead peak in growth rate during Years 3–4.

### Proposed Explanation
- Differences attributed to the **dam-regulated, perpetually cold mainstem temperatures** in Grand Canyon, which suppress growth in older fish confined to the mainstem, while warmer, productive tributaries allow rapid early growth. This contrasts with the upper basin, where no comparable thermal/habitat segregation by age exists.
