# An Industry View of the Regulation of Mining Waste and Other Solid Waste Under Subtitle D of the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act

#environmental-policy #mining-regulation #RCRA #waste-management

## Executive Summary

This legal analysis examines the regulatory framework for mining waste under the [[Resource Conservation and Recovery Act]] (RCRA) Subtitle D from an industry perspective. The document traces the complex legislative and regulatory history from RCRA's 1976 inception through proposed 1989-1990 reauthorization, focusing on the [[Bevill Amendment]] and subsequent efforts to develop a tailored federal mining waste program. The authors argue for state primacy, site-specific flexibility, and avoidance of duplicative environmental controls, while analyzing competing regulatory proposals from [[Environmental Protection Agency]] (EPA), the [[Western Governors Association]] (WGA), and pending Congressional bills.

## Background & Description

The [[Resource Conservation and Recovery Act]] established two primary regulatory frameworks: Subtitle C for hazardous waste (with rigorous federal standards and enforcement) and Subtitle D for non-hazardous solid waste (emphasizing state implementation with federal guidance). Mining wastes received special consideration from RCRA's inception due to distinctive characteristics: generation in vastly larger volumes than other industrial wastes (over 99% of material handled in many mining operations is waste rather than marketable product), predominantly on-site land disposal, typically lower hazards than other industrial wastes, and location in sparsely populated, arid or semi-arid areas with limited surface water runoff and deeper water tables.

The [[Bevill Amendment]] (1980) suspended Subtitle C hazardous waste regulation for mining wastes pending comprehensive studies under RCRA Sections 8002(f) and 8002(p). EPA's 1985 Report to Congress addressed extraction and beneficiation wastes from metallic ores (antimony, bauxite, beryllium, copper, gold, iron, lead, mercury, molybdenum, nickel, rare earth metals, silver, titanium, vanadium, zinc), uranium overburden, and non-metals (asbestos, phosphate rock, oil shale). The report excluded uranium mill tailings, coal mining wastes regulated under [[Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act]] (SMCRA), mineral processing wastes, and abandoned/inactive mine sites.

EPA's July 1986 regulatory determination concluded that Subtitle C regulation was inappropriate for mining wastes, proposing instead a Subtitle D program specifically designed for mining waste characteristics. Subsequent legal challenges and rulemakings (1988-1990) progressively narrowed the Bevill exclusion for mineral processing wastes, with only 20 processing wastes retained within the exclusion pending further study by July 1990.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Mining operations occur in areas where natural mineralization may significantly impact the environment, complicating baseline assessments. The document identifies key environmental protection concerns addressed in proposed regulations:

**Groundwater Protection**: Current Part 257 criteria prohibit contamination of underground drinking water sources beyond facility boundaries, with "contaminate" defined as exceeding [[Safe Drinking Water Act]] standards for arsenic, barium, cadmium, chromium, lead, mercury, nitrate, selenium, silver, fluoride, and six pesticides. Underground drinking water sources include aquifers supplying drinking water or containing less than 10,000 mg/L total dissolved solids.

**Surface Water**: Proposed criteria would require compliance with [[Clean Water Act]] effluent limitations and state surface water quality standards, though industry argues this duplicates existing Clean Water Act programs.

**Air Quality**: Criteria would address compliance with [[Clean Air Act]] new source performance standards and air quality implementation plans, with WGA proposals focusing on fugitive emissions.

**Habitat Considerations**: Proposed regulations address facility location restrictions for flood plains, wetlands, seismic impact zones, unstable areas, areas with complex hydrogeology, fault areas, and permafrost. WGA criteria include protection of plants, fish, and wildlife.

## Key Findings

**Regulatory Classification**: Mining industry wastes fall into three categories: (1) subject to present/future Subtitle C regulation (non-mining hazardous wastes like spent solvents, six listed mineral processing hazardous wastes, mineral processing wastes exhibiting hazardous characteristics except 20 Bevill-retained wastes); (2) subject to potential Subtitle D regulation (mineral extraction and beneficiation wastes from 1985 Report, 20 Bevill-retained processing wastes, non-hazardous processing wastes); (3) excluded from both programs (non-mining wastes not regulated as hazardous, extraction/beneficiation wastes not studied in 1985 Report like sand and gravel, overburden intended for mine site return, in-situ mining materials, uranium mill tailings, exploration wastes).

**Pending Legislation**: Two RCRA reauthorization bills propose mining waste programs. The [[Baucus Bill]] (S. 1113) would establish federal permit program with 10-year terms, numerical performance standards, and "best available control technology" requirements. The [[Lukens Bill]] (H.R. 3735) would mandate state-implemented permit programs with separate regulations for industrial wastes, excluding inactive/abandoned facilities and activities regulated under other environmental statutes.

**EPA Strawman vs. WGA Proposals**: EPA's May 1988 Strawman draft emphasized comprehensive individual permits and federal design/operating criteria. WGA's December 1989 draft prioritized state primacy, continuation of existing state programs, and greater flexibility in approval mechanisms. Key differences include: WGA would cover all metallic ore/mineral industries regardless of Report to Congress coverage; WGA would regulate active leaching operations while EPA would not; WGA proposes soil criteria absent from EPA Strawman; WGA requires corrective action after every exceedance study while EPA allows exceptions.

**Industry Positions**: Mining industry supports: (1) state primacy with federal oversight focused on state plan implementation rather than individual facilities; (2) life-of-facility permits rather than 10-year terms; (3) distinction between performance criteria for new versus existing facilities; (4) flexible compliance points based on site-specific considerations; (5) avoidance of duplicative controls where Clean Water Act and Clean Air Act already regulate; (6) corrective action only for verified, statistically significant exceedances; (7) 10-year inactivity period before triggering closure requirements; (8) clear limits on financial assurance to avoid tying up excessive capital.

**Scope Issues**: Industry advocates regulating only "solid wastes" (discarded materials) under statutory authority, opposing expansion to cover ongoing processing operations like leaching. Beneficiation defined to include crushing, grinding, washing, dissolution, crystallization, filtration, sorting, sizing, drying, sintering, pelletizing, briquetting, calcining, roasting, autoclaving, chlorination, gravity concentration, magnetic separation, electrostatic separation, flotation, ion exchange, solvent extraction, electrowinning, precipitation, amalgamation, and heap/dump/vat/tank/in-situ leaching.

**Financial Assurance Concerns**: Proposed requirements for closure, postclosure, and corrective action financial assurance could substantially affect mining operation economics. Industry notes lack of currently-available insurance or surety mechanisms and difficulty quantifying risks across relatively small pool of waste units, necessitating clear program limits for workability.

**Corrective Action Debate**: EPA would allow exceptions to corrective action where: another contamination source exists and mining waste cleanup provides no significant risk reduction; remediation is technically impractical or causes unacceptable cross-media impacts; affected groundwater is not potential drinking water source with no other beneficial uses and unlikely to contaminate other waters. WGA would require corrective action after every study and cleanup to at least performance criteria levels. Industry supports EPA's more flexible approach with cost-effectiveness considerations.
