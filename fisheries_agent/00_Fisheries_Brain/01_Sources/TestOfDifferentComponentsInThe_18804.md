# Test of Different Components in the Abernathy Salmon Diet

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife Technical Paper (No. 13, August 1967) reports two years of hatchery feeding trials (1964 and 1965) on fall [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) fingerlings. The trials tested substitute components of the [[Abernathy Salmon Diet]], an all-meal diet developed at the [[Salmon-Cultural Laboratory]] to rear salmon for more than 26 weeks. The authors aimed to find an optimal, economical ingredient combination for artificial propagation.

Main conclusions: [[Turbot Meal]] and low-urea [[Dogfish Meal]] were adequate replacements for salmon carcass meal; [[Soybean Oil]] was superior to other caloric oils; dehulled cottonseed meal matched regular [[Cottonseed Meal]]; [[Dried Buttermilk]] equaled [[Dried Skim Milk]]; and storage practices strongly affected fish health.

## Background & Description

The Abernathy diet is composed of salmon carcass meal, dried skim milk, cottonseed meal, [[Wheat Germ Meal]], a vitamin package, a binder, peanut oil, and water. Earlier work at the laboratory (Combs et al., 1962; Fowler et al., 1964, 1966) produced this diet, which had sustained salmon for more than 26 weeks.

The methods followed those of Burrows et al. (1951, 1952). Fish from a single day's egg take were stocked randomly into 6-foot circular tanks at 500 grams per tank. Each diet was fed to duplicate tanks for 24 weeks, with food adjusted to fish weight biweekly. Water flowed at 7 gallons per minute at a constant 53°F.

Key methodological points:

- **Formulation:** Caloric values were 4.0 kcal/g protein (3.9 in 1965), 8.0 kcal/g fat (9.0 kcal/g vegetable oil), and 1.6 kcal/g carbohydrate. Protein was held at 25 percent (wet weight basis), and substitutions were made on an isoprotein basis.
- **Vitamins and binders:** A crystalline vitamin package, set at recommended maximum levels for chinook salmon, was added daily. It made up 2 percent of the diet along with [[Carboxymethyl Cellulose]] (CMC). Salt was used as a binder in 1964 and dropped in 1965.
- **Response measures:** Protein deposition, not total weight gain, was the growth measure. Protein utilization was calculated as protein fed divided by protein deposited. Ten fish per diet were proximately analyzed at 12 and 24 weeks.
- **Other assays:** Histopathology (five fish per key diet), blood cell counts (10 fish per diet, 1965), and paired stamina tests (100 fish per selected diet, 1965). Oxidation was tracked with the [[Thiobarbituric Acid Value]] (TBA) method. Statistics used analysis of variance at the 5 percent level.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The trials were conducted under controlled hatchery conditions, not in natural waters. Ingredient fish came from commercial producers and marine catches. Species included herring, turbot, dogfish, rockfish, sole, and tuna. Meals were prepared from whole carcasses or from fillet scraps, and their sources affected protein quality. The authors noted that species identification was assumed correct but could be in error.

Diet quality also depended on handling. The [[Rancidity]] of salmon meal, measured by TBA, produced sublethal toxicity. Nutritional requirements for vitamin E and fat were considered central to fingerling health.

## Key Findings

### 1964 Feeding Trials (21 diets)

- All 21 diets maintained fish for 24 weeks with insignificant mortality and no visible deficiency signs. Except for two diets, growth equaled or exceeded the meat control.
- **Salmon carcass meals:** The 1963 meal had a TBA of 80.0, versus 25.0 for the 1961 meal. Its rancidity reduced growth from about 4 weeks on and caused [[Hepatotoxicity]]. The 1961 meal was used as the control thereafter.
- **[[Herring Meal]]:** Protein deposition was inferior to salmon meal, and fat deposition was greater. It was a poor substitute.
- **[[Turbot Meal]]:** It was an excellent growth producer, equal to salmon meal in protein deposition, utilization, weight, and fat deposition.
- **[[Dogfish Meal]]:** Meal A performed like salmon and turbot meals. Meal B, with 1.13 percent urea versus 0.5 percent in meal A, grew significantly slower and showed poor protein utilization. Urea accounted for only 1.8 percentage points of calculated protein difference (7.7 versus 5.9 percent), so it did not fully explain the gap. A follow-up with equal dogfish meal inputs confirmed that the low-urea meal was superior.
- **Fillet-scrap meals:** [[Rockfish]] was the best of the rockfish, sole, and tuna meals, but none matched the salmon control.
- **Combination diets:** Mixing fish meals never exceeded the salmon-meal control's growth. Diets containing the high-urea dogfish meal grew significantly slower.
- **Histopathology:** The meat control showed lipoprotein deposits in the liver, linked to hard fat. Diet 3 (rancid 1963 salmon meal) showed hepatotoxic changes, including loss of cell polarity, pyknotic nuclei, and low fat and glycogen.

### 1965 Feeding Trials (21 diets)

- **Control:** The 1961 salmon meal (stored under refrigeration) and a 1964 salmon meal produced no measurable differences in growth, mortality, or hematology.
- **[[Choline Chloride]]:** Supplementation gave no measurable benefit, so it can be eliminated for simplicity and economy. Adding choline in the 16th week also failed to reduce mortality in the dried-skim-milk-deficient diet.
- **Caloric oils:** [[Soybean Oil]] produced significantly higher protein deposition and total weight than [[Peanut Oil]], corn, cottonseed, or safflower oil, and it is cheaper.
- **Vegetable meals:**
  - [[Dehulled Cottonseed]] meal (49.95 percent protein) matched regular cottonseed meal (43.81 percent). Its cost was comparable after adjusting oil, and it left fewer hulls in fish excrement.
  - [[Corn Gluten Meal]] raised mortality to 7.33 percent versus 0.56 percent in the control.
  - [[Safflower Meal]] caused significantly higher mortality, starting after 18 weeks.
  - [[Soybean Meal]] (higher-protein version) was accepted readily but still gave higher mortality, less weight, less protein deposition, and less efficient utilization.
- **[[Dried Buttermilk]]:** It was comparable to dried skim milk and gave significantly greater protein deposition, though weight differences were not measurable. Retesting was recommended.
- **Dried skim milk series:** At 0.00, 3.91, 7.82, and 11.72 percent (versus 15.63 percent in the control), weight, protein deposition, utilization, and fat deposition rose with milk content, while mortality fell. Fish on low-milk diets showed dark coloration, anorexia, and loss of equilibrium. The authors suspected an amino acid imbalance.
- **[[Wheat Germ Meal]] series:** At 2.60 and 5.21 percent (versus 7.81 percent in the control), no differences in total weight, mortality, protein deposition, or hematology were seen by 24 weeks, except diet 21 fish were larger. Blood counts showed a non-significant trend (r = 0.802; P 0.05 = 0.811). Because wheat germ is the main vitamin E source, the level was kept unchanged.
- **Storage:** Diets prepared every 3 days performed as well as daily-prepared diets. Diets prepared every 7 days produced slower late-stage growth, reduced fat deposition, and poorer stamina.
- **Room-temperature dry mix:** Fish on the premixed diet (diet 6) showed darkening from about week 20, affecting roughly 25 percent of fish. Mortality reached 1.95 percent, occurring in the last 8 weeks. Stamina performance was 10.7 versus 93.8 in the control. Meal rancidity stayed stable, so the cause remained unknown.

### Summary of Recommendations

- Use [[Turbot Meal]] or low-urea [[Dogfish Meal]] as salmon-meal substitutes; avoid rancid salmon meal.
- Adopt [[Soybean Oil]] as the caloric source to reduce cost and improve growth.
- Dehulled cottonseed meal may replace regular cottonseed meal.
- Dried buttermilk may replace dried skim milk, pending retest.
- Do not reduce dried skim milk or wheat germ levels without harm.
- Prepare diets in 3-day allotments, not 7-day batches, and refrigerate dry meals.
