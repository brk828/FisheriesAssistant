# Native Fishes of the Upper Gila River System, New Mexico

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #gila-river-basin

## Executive Summary

This 1970s survey by LaBounty and Minckley documents the historic and contemporary ichthyofauna of the upper Gila River basin in New Mexico, comparing collections from the 1930s–1972 against baseline data collected largely by Koster in the 1940s–1950s. The upper Gila is characterized as one of the **least altered native fish faunas** remaining in the [[Colorado River Basin]], yet it has undergone significant faunal turnover. Around 1900, fewer than four non-native fish species were present; by 1950 the ratio of native to non-native species was roughly 1:1, by 1960 it was 1:1.5, and by the time of this report (1972) it had shifted to approximately **1:2.3** in favor of introduced species. Of an estimated 10–13 probable native inhabitants, only 7 species persist, while 16 of 19 documented non-native introductions have become established or self-sustaining through repeated stocking. The paper attributes native fish decline to habitat modification, hybridization, competition, and predation from introduced species such as [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]].

## Background & Description

The paper is primarily faunal/distributional rather than morphometric, but it notes key taxonomic and morphological distinctions among native taxa:

- **[[Meda Fulgida]]** (spikedace) and **[[Tiaroga Cobitis]]** (loach minnow) — monotypic, endemic genera restricted to the Gila River basin; adapted to swift riffle habitats.
- **[[Salmo Gilae]]** (Gila trout) — described as an "ancient, endemic relict" species (Miller 1972b), found in isolated high-elevation tributary populations.
- **[[Salmo Apache]]** (Arizona/Apache trout) — a closely related congener, hypothesized to have historically occurred in New Mexico tributaries of the San Francisco River.
- **[[Agosia Chrysogaster]]** (longfin dace) — characteristic of low-desert streams, tolerant of warm, low-elevation conditions.
- **[[Rhinichthys Osculus Osculus]]** (speckled dace) — small-bodied cyprinid associated with cold, high-elevation, rock-bottomed streams.
- **[[Catostomus Insignis]]** (Gila coarse-scaled sucker) and **[[Pantosteus Clarki]]** (Gila mountain-sucker) — larger-bodied catostomids; Pantosteus notably penetrates to higher elevations than Catostomus and favors the swiftest water columns.
- **[[Gila Robusta Grahami]]** — a large, formerly common minnow, now apparently extirpated from the study area.
- **[[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis Occidentalis]]** (Gila topminnow) — a small livebearer, historically restricted to Frisco Hot Spring on the San Francisco River.

At least four of the five most range-restricted native forms are considered **autochthonous** to the Gila drainage, having dispersed outward to adjacent basins (Agosia chrysogaster, Gila robusta grahami, Catostomus insignis, Pantosteus clarki).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The upper Gila basin supports a gradient of habitat specialization along elevation and flow-velocity axes:

- **High-elevation, cold, rock-bottomed tributary streams (>6,000 ft)**: dominated by [[Rhinichthys Osculus Osculus]] and isolated populations of [[Salmo Gilae]].
- **Intermediate elevations, moderate-to-large streams**: [[Tiaroga Cobitis]] and [[Meda Fulgida]] locally abundant in riffle habitats; Tiaroga/Rhinichthys favor riffles, while Meda, Agosia, and larger Rhinichthys occupy downstream eddies (per Fig. 5, Turkey Creek).
- **Lowest elevations, desert-influenced reaches**: [[Agosia Chrysogaster]] predominates, and appears to be expanding upstream in recent decades.
- **Large flowing pools of the mainstream Gila**: historically supported [[Catostomus Insignis]], Meda, Agosia, [[Gila Robusta Grahami]], and large [[Pantosteus Clarki]] (e.g., near confluence with Turkey Creek).
- **Hypothetical/peripheral occurrences**: [[Ptychocheilus Lucius]] (Colorado squawfish), [[Xyrauchen Texanus]] (razorback sucker), and possibly [[Catostomus Latipinnis]] (flannelmouth sucker) may have periodically entered the lower New Mexico segments from Arizona populations during high-flow events, though direct evidence is lacking.

**Threats and interactions:**
- The extirpation of [[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis Occidentalis]] from Frisco Hot Spring is attributed to predation/competition from introduced [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish), stream incision, or anthropogenic habitat destruction.
- The decline of [[Gila Robusta Grahami]] since the early 1960s is hypothesized to result from competition or predation by introduced [[Micropterus Dolomieui]] (smallmouth bass), a pattern also observed contemporaneously in the Black River and upper Salt River, Arizona.
- [[Salmo Gilae]] and [[Salmo Apache]] populations have suffered genetic swamping via hybridization with introduced [[Salmo Gairdneri]] (rainbow trout).
- Intensive game-fish stocking near major fishing lakes correlates with localized depletion of native species.
- Speckled dace appear to have been displaced upward in elevation, possibly linked to the upstream expansion of Agosia chrysogaster.

The paper draws a stark comparative contrast with the **[[Salt River]]** at Tempe, Arizona (Table 2), where the native fauna has been almost completely replaced by introduced species since the 1890s — offered as a cautionary parallel/endpoint scenario for the upper Gila.

## Key Findings

- **Historical native fauna**: 9 confirmed (Koster, 1957) to 13 probable native fish species inhabited the upper Gila basin; only **7 remain** as of the report.
- **Extirpated species**: [[Gila Robusta Grahami]] (last confirmed record 1967, unsubstantiated) and [[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis Occidentalis]] (extirpated from Frisco Hot Spring) have disappeared from the New Mexico portion of the basin.
- **Endemism**: Two monotypic endemic genera (*Meda*, *Tiaroga*), one ancient endemic relict trout (*Salmo gilae*), and five endemic species/subspecies characterize the fauna — among the highest endemism rates in the Colorado River Basin.
- **Conservation status (1971–72)**: 3 of the remaining 7 native species were listed as "rare and endangered" by New Mexico and Arizona (per [[Miller 1972a]]).
- **Non-native introductions**: 19 documented introductions, of which 16 persist via natural reproduction or repeated stocking.
- **Shifting native:non-native ratio**: ~1:4 (1900) → 1:1 (1950) → 1:1.5 (1960) → 1:2.3 (1972), reflecting both introductions and native losses.
- **Comparative case study**: The Salt River at Tempe, Arizona, serves as a "total replacement" endpoint — native fauna essentially eliminated by 1972, with the river itself frequently dry except for canal leakage and wastewater return.
- **Trend concern**: Data from the upper Salt River system (1967–72) suggest the same native-to-non-native replacement pattern documented at Tempe may now be progressing upstream, foreshadowing risk to the upper Gila system itself.
- **Overall assessment**: Despite losses, the upper Gila River system remains one of the **least altered native ichthyofaunas** in the Colorado River Basin, underscoring its conservation significance.
