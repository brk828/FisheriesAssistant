## Executive Summary
This news article reports on a 1980s-era field expedition and subsequent hatchery mortality event involving the critically endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), one of four "big-river" fish species native to the [[Colorado River]] system. A multi-agency team of over 50 federal and state biologists, university researchers, and volunteers spent a week conducting intensive netting and electro-shocking operations on [[Lake Mohave]] in an effort to capture wild bonytail specimens for a captive breeding/genetic rescue program. Only a single wild bonytail was captured; it was transported to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, where it died within days despite initial signs of good health. The expedition also yielded substantial captures of the [[Razorback Sucker]], another endangered big-river species, providing new population and movement data. The article underscores the extreme rarity of the bonytail chub and the fragility of ex-situ conservation efforts for aging, non-reproducing wild populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Bonytail chub and razorback sucker are both classified as "big-river" fishes historically common throughout the [[Colorado River]] drainage; both now persist primarily in reservoir environments such as [[Lake Mohave]] following damming of the river.
- **Population status**: Wild bonytail numbers are described as fewer than two dozen individuals taken from the river in the prior decade; only five bonytails constitute the genetic founder pool for current captive propagation programs (Dexter National Fish Hatchery and 2–3 other Southwestern facilities).
- **Reproductive failure**: Both bonytail and razorback sucker populations in the lower Colorado River have failed to reproduce successfully in the wild for decades, attributed largely to heavy predation (likely from introduced/non-native species) and altered post-dam river conditions.
- **Capture methods**: Researchers used three 100-foot trammel nets set at rocky points around the lake (emptied every 2–3 hours in round-the-clock shifts) and electro-shocking boats to stun and net fish in shallow habitats — standard techniques for sampling large-river native fishes in reservoir settings.
- **Environmental conditions**: Lake Mohave is characterized as prone to strong, sudden windstorms (gusts >40 mph) during the sampling season, complicating field operations.

## Key Findings
- Only **one wild bonytail chub** was captured during the week-long, five-site, 50+ person expedition — highlighting the species' extreme rarity in the wild.
- The captured bonytail was airlifted to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico), a facility specializing in endangered species propagation; it appeared healthy through Friday but was found dead the following Saturday morning.
- Historical hatchery mortality context: researchers note they have "lost half the fish" handled over the years, reflecting the inherent difficulty of ex-situ management for wild-caught, aged, sensitive fish species compared to veterinary-supported warm-blooded animals.
- The genetic base for current bonytail captive breeding programs rests on just **five founding individuals**, an extremely narrow bottleneck for a recovery program.
- The expedition successfully captured, analyzed, and tagged **hundreds of razorback suckers**, yielding the first robust estimates of their Lake Mohave population size and movement patterns.
- Both bonytail and razorback populations in Lake Mohave consist of individuals that predate lower Colorado River dam construction, with some surviving up to 40 years without any confirmed successful natural recruitment.
- Lead researcher Dr. W.L. Minckley ([[Arizona State University]]), the foremost expert on Colorado River fishes at the time, characterized the loss as "not overly surprising" given the fragility of handling wild endangered fish, but affirmed the species' value as "priceless" regardless of the cost of recovery efforts.
- U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biologists (Bill Kepner, Frank Baucom) emphasized that the difficulty of the operation "points to the severity of the problem" facing the [[Bonytail Chub]] and reinforces the necessity of continued intensive recovery efforts.
- The event illustrates a recurring conservation challenge: even successful field capture of critically endangered fish can be undermined by high mortality risk during transport and captive acclimation, threatening already minimal genetic diversity in recovery programs.
