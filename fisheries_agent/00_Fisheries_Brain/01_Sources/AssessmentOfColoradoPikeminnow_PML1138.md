# Assessment of Colorado Pikeminnow and Razorback Sucker Reintroduction Programs in the Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), submitted to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] on September 30, 2004, assesses the success of [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] repatriation in the Salt and Verde Rivers of central Arizona. Since 1991, nearly 23,000 razorback suckers and over 11,000 Colorado pikeminnow were stocked. Monitoring captured 87 Colorado pikeminnow and 283 razorback suckers in the Verde River and two razorback suckers in the Salt River. Only one fish was ever recaptured, so survival and population size could not be estimated, and recruitment has not been documented. The report concludes that adult survival appears low and short-lived, and it recommends predator removal, continued stocking of fish at least 300 mm, physical and site acclimation before release, PIT tagging of all hatchery fish, and a standardized sampling protocol.

## Background & Description

Both species are native to the Colorado River basin, where they were common in the mainstem and tributaries until the early 1900s. They were extirpated from the Gila River basin by the 1970s due to dams, dewatering and non-native introductions. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed both as endangered. Reintroduction began in the early 1980s, but early stockings of small fry and fingerlings largely failed, presumably from predation by non-native fishes. From 1991 onward, hatchery fish were stocked at a minimum length of 300 mm to reduce predation.

Fish were raised at the [[Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery]] (BPSH), the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFHTC) and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (WBNFH). Razorback suckers were sourced from Lake Mohave, and grew to 300 mm in roughly two years. Colorado pikeminnow reached 300 mm in about three years. Fish were transported to the Verde River at Childs within 48 hours of harvest.

Study reaches were defined on both rivers. The Upper Salt River was split into four reaches by morphology, with canyon reaches of boulder and bedrock alternating with low-gradient alluvial reaches. The Verde River was split into three reaches, from Perkinsville to Clarkdale, Beasley Flat to Childs, and Childs to Horseshoe Lake, with the lower reach the most variable in substrate and hydrology.

Sampling used boat-mounted electrofishing, trammel and gill nets, seines, trotlines and angling. Fish were measured for total length, weighed, scanned for wire and PIT tags, and released at capture. Field methods changed over the project, so comparisons between years are difficult.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report links native fish decline to introduced species. Maddux et al. (1993) reported at least 72 introduced fish species in the Colorado River basin, roughly twice the number of natives. About 60 percent of southwestern native fishes are now listed as threatened, endangered or of special concern. In Arizona, about 80 percent of mainstream habitats have been altered physically or chemically or lost through drying.

Key threats include dams, loss of physical habitat, chemical pollution, overexploitation and non-native species. Predation by non-native centrarchids and ictalurids is a major cause of razorback sucker decline. [[Flathead Catfish]] now dominate the Salt River and may make up as much as 90 percent of relative abundance, compared with under 5 percent at the start of the program. Predation and competition from non-native fish, altered flow regimes, lost nursery habitat and the ongoing drought also limit recovery.

Barriers such as dams may block Colorado pikeminnow spawning migrations of up to 322 km. Razorback sucker larvae emerge early, which makes them highly vulnerable to predators.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking totals:** About 22,869 razorback suckers were released into the Verde River since 1991 across 30 events, nearly all at 300 mm or larger. About 11,231 Colorado pikeminnow were stocked into the Verde River from 1992, all at 300 mm or larger. Only one razorback sucker stocking occurred in the Salt River after 1990 (December 1996, 2,046 fish, mean 285 mm, from Utah).
- **Captures:** Razorback suckers captured in the Verde River peaked at 104 in 1994 and fell to 2 in 2001. Colorado pikeminnow captures fell from 30 in 1998 to four captures from 1999 onward.
- **Site fidelity:** Most native fish were captured at or near stocking sites. The exceptions were 23 pikeminnow and three razorback suckers captured in Horseshoe Reservoir.
- **Recaptures:** Only one PIT-tagged razorback sucker was recaptured (May 2002, Childs campground). It grew from 405 mm to 441 mm over roughly three years. No Colorado pikeminnow has ever been recaptured.
- **Survival and recruitment:** Survival cannot be calculated and recruitment has never been documented. Captured fish were mostly recently stocked, so the data show only short-term survival.
- **Earlier stocking:** About 12 million fingerling razorback suckers were stocked into the Verde River between 1981 and 1991 with little success.
- **Hatchery concerns:** Pond-reared fish may lack the swimming and foraging skills needed for the wild. [[Mueller et al. 2003]] reported that acclimated razorback suckers survived nearly 30 percent better than non-acclimated fish (reported as "3*%" in the scanned text; the exact figure is unreliable).
- **Recommendations:** Mechanically remove non-native predators (seasonally); stock only fish of at least 300 mm; acclimate hatchery fish to flow and in backwaters for several weeks or months before release; PIT-tag every fish before stocking and measure length and weight; follow a standardized sampling protocol; develop a program-specific database.
