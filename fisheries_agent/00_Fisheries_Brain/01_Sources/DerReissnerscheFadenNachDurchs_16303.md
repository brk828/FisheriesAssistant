# Reissner's Fiber After Spinal Cord Transection in Salmo irideus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This experimental histological study by Walter Fahrmann, published in *Zeitschrift für Zellforschung* (1963) and carried out at the [[Hydrobiologische Anstalt]] of the Max-Planck-Gesellschaft in Schlitz, examines how [[Reissner's Fiber]] (RF) behaves after the spinal cord is cut in [[Rainbow Trout]] ([[Salmo irideus]]). The author aimed to resolve contradictory reports in earlier work, particularly the disagreement between [[Olsson 1957]], who described the caudal fiber vanishing, and [[Sano and Hartmann 1959]], who saw no change. The main variable was animal age: newly hatched fish, 17 to 19 day old fish, and two-summer fish. The main result is that the caudal fiber is lost after transection in all age groups, while the behavior of the cranial fiber and the elastic recoil of the cut fiber differ strongly between juveniles and adults.

## Background & Description

The Reissner's fiber is a secretory thread formed from the secretions of the [[Subcommissural Organ]] (SCO), a periventricular ependymal structure. The fiber runs through the [[Central Canal]] of the brain and spinal cord and ends in a caudal swelling, the [[Massa Caudalis]], within the [[Caudal Ampulla]] of the [[Filum Terminale]]. The author notes that its function has remained unresolved despite decades of descriptive and experimental work.

**Study animals and ages**

Three age classes of rainbow trout were used:

1. Newly hatched fish: maximum age 15 h, mean length 1.5 to 1.6 cm.
2. Fish aged 17 and 19 days, length 2.3 to 2.7 cm.
3. Two-summer fish: about 1½ years old, length 18 to 25 cm.

**Surgical procedure**

Under [[Urethane Anesthesia]], the spinal cord was cut in the young fish by a lateral needle incision at the level of the dorsal fin. In the two-summer fish, the cut was made dorsally, just behind the dorsal fin, with scissors, and the wound was sutured. Most fish survived well, and control animals were kept alongside the operated ones.

**Fixation and staining**

Material was fixed in [[Bouin's Solution]] at set intervals. Sections (5 and 8 µm) were stained with [[Chrome Hematoxylin Staining]] (Gomori), [[Paraldehyde Fuchsin Staining]] (Gomori-Gabe), Astrablau (Miller 1957) and Azan. The RF stains rotviolet with paraldehyde fuchsin, blue-black with chrome hematoxylin, and blue with Astrablau and Azan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a laboratory study and contains no field ecology. Its ecological relevance lies in comparative neuroanatomy across aquatic vertebrates. The author cites RF studies on [[Branchiostoma]] (amphioxus), lampreys, and many fish species, and reports that the fiber shows a fairly uniform pattern across vertebrates. Species-specific and age-specific differences are expected to shape how the fiber responds to injury.

Comparative work cited includes:

- [[Esox Lucius]] (pike): Olsson (1957) reported complete caudal loss of the RF within 25 h in newly hatched fish.
- [[Tinca Vulgaris]] (tench): Sano and Hartmann (1959) found no change in the distal fiber within 24 days in adult fish.
- [[Scyliorhinus Canicula]] (dogfish): Nicholls (1912a, 1917) reported complete caudal loss by 16 days and a new ampulla in the cranial stump.
- Other taxa: Horsley (1908) found no clear change in the macaque, and the author's unpublished work on six-week-old larvae of [[Triturus Alpestris]] also showed caudal loss.

Fahrmann argues that the discrepancies among studies arise from species, age, and surgical technique. Other possible factors include compression of tissue at the cut site, nodules that anchor the fiber, and disturbed cerebrospinal fluid flow.

## Key Findings

**1. Caudal fiber loss after complete transection.** After complete cord transection, the caudal part of the RF progressively loses substance and disappears. In the 17 and 19 day fish, the fiber was lost between days 4 and 7 post-operation (p. op.). The [[Massa Caudalis]] persisted longer, and in most fish only remnants remained by 13 to 14 days p. op. Caudal fiber thinning was seen by 42 h in the newly hatched fish, with complete loss by day 10. Fahrmann concludes that caudal loss is "only a matter of time" once the supply from the SCO stops.

**2. Cranial stump response differs by age.** In the young fish, a new [[Ampulla]] with a new massa formed in the cranial stump after about 3 to 4 days. In the two-summer fish, the central canal showed only a slight ampulla-like widening that did not close fully, so the fiber could grow into the cut site.

**3. Elastic recoil in two-summer fish.** Only the two-summer fish showed strong longitudinal tension. The cut fiber snapped back toward its anchor points and contracted in both directions. At 24 h, the fiber was absent near the cut. Further cranially it was shortened by about half, with a distal diameter of 9.0 to 9.5 µm narrowing to 3.0 to 4.0 µm cranially. Control fibers were about 4.0 µm behind the SCO, 3.2 to 3.5 µm in the [[Fourth Ventricle]], and 3.0 to 3.5 µm down to the filum terminale.

**4. Maximal contraction and regrowth.** Fibers were most contracted at 7 days p. op., with diameters of 4.0 to 7.0 µm. By 14 days the cranial fiber had lengthened again and filled about half of the cranial spinal cord. It reached the operation site between days 20 and 30. Near the cut the fiber was thin (1.0 to 2.0 µm) and sometimes showed knotlike thickenings.

**5. Stable diameter in young fish.** The fiber diameter was very consistent in the approximately 3-week-old fish, at 1.0 to 1.2 µm in controls and in most operated animals up to the cut site. Disturbances occurred only where free cells accumulated in the central canal.

**6. Secretory activity of the SCO.** In two-summer fish, granule distribution in the SCO changed after surgery. Basal granule accumulations were seen at 3 days p. op., apical concentration at 7 and 14 days, and roughly equal apical and basal amounts by 50 days. The author cautiously proposes that the relaxed, severed fiber may stimulate stronger apical secretion, but notes the sample is too small to confirm this.

**7. Sample sizes.** Young-fish experiments included 33 operated fish and 9 controls (17 and 19 day group). Newly hatched fish included 14 operated fish and 3 controls. Two-summer fish included spinal cord material from 18 operated fish and 3 controls, and brain material from 8 operated fish and 3 controls. Eleven brains were examined for SCO secretion, comprising 3 controls and 8 operated fish.

**8. Interpretation.** The author concludes that the RF is a fused secretion product whose persistence depends on continuous supply from the SCO. Its elasticity differs between juveniles and adults, possibly due to a reduced secretory dependence or changes in fiber structure. The author also discusses that the RF is structurally homogeneous at the electron-microscopic level, with fine granular ground substance, and that concentric layering appears only in disturbed, swollen fibers in the caudal ampulla.

**Overall conclusion.** Caudal fiber loss is a common response across ages, but the cranial regrowth and elastic recoil are age-dependent. Fahrmann emphasizes that further work with larger samples is needed to clarify the function of the fiber.
