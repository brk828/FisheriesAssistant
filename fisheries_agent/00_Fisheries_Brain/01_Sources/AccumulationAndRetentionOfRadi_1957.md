# Accumulation and Retention of Radioactivity from Fission Products and Other Radiomaterials by Fresh-Water Organisms

## Executive Summary
This 1957 chapter by [[Louis A. Krumholz]] and [[Richard F. Foster]] synthesizes early radioecological research on how fission products and other radiomaterials are taken up, concentrated, retained, and eliminated by fresh-water organisms, with particular emphasis on fish. Drawing primarily on field data from the [[Columbia River]] (contaminated by effluent from [[Hanford Reactors]]) and [[White Oak Lake]] (contaminated by wastes from [[Oak Ridge National Laboratory]]), the authors demonstrate that radionuclide accumulation varies enormously by species, tissue type, radioelement, season, and trophic position. Key findings include extreme bioconcentration factors for elements like phosphorus-32 and strontium-90/yttrium-90 (reaching >100,000-fold in some organisms), the dominance of dietary ingestion over direct absorption as the principal uptake route in fish, and documented lethal thresholds for radiophosphorus in trout bone.

## Background & Description
The chapter addresses a critical knowledge gap identified in the mid-1950s: as nuclear power reactors were anticipated to proliferate near population centers with fresh-water disposal of liquid effluent, understanding radiomaterial behavior in aquatic ecosystems became urgent for hazard assessment. The authors note that estimates of contamination risk could be off by one to two orders of magnitude due to the complexity of biological and chemical interactions.

Three primary data sources are synthesized:
1. **The Hanford Biology Laboratories program** (General Electric Company, Richland, WA) — a long-term radiological-ecological study of the [[Columbia River]], tracking geographic distribution, food-web transfer, seasonal patterns, and biological effects of radioelements (Na²⁴, Cr⁵¹, Cu⁶⁴, P³², As⁷⁶, rare earths) released from plutonium-producing reactors.
2. **The Oak Ridge National Laboratory / Tennessee Valley Authority survey** of [[White Oak Creek]] and [[White Oak Lake]] — a three-year ecological survey (botany, limnology, vertebrate biology) examining fission product accumulation (Sr⁹⁰-Y⁹⁰, Cs¹³⁷, Ce¹⁴⁴-Pr¹⁴⁴, Ru, P³²) in the biota.
3. **Smaller academic studies**, including phosphorus-cycle work at Dalhousie University, Yale University, and Atomic Energy of Canada, and radiotracer studies of mosquitoes and frogs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Concentration factor** is defined as the ratio of radioelement concentration in an organism/tissue (µc/g) to that in the surrounding water (µc/ml), which cannot exceed the ratio of stable-element concentrations under equilibrium assumptions.
- Fresh waters vary enormously in dissolved solid content (from <5 ppm to >400 ppm), and elemental composition differs by drainage basin, making generalization across water bodies unreliable (see [[Table 1: Element Concentrations in Organisms and Rivers]]).
- **Three uptake pathways** exist for radiomaterials in aquatic organisms: (1) surface adsorption (physical process, especially significant for organisms with high surface-to-volume ratios like sponges and diatoms), (2) direct absorption from the medium (biological, dominant in plants), and (3) ingestion as food (dominant pathway in fish).
- **Trophic transfer** is the principal accumulation route in fish. Fish downstream of Hanford reactors that fed on contaminated organisms contained over 100,000 times more radiophosphorus than the surrounding water during late summer.
- **Species-specific dietary differences** drive divergent tissue-level accumulation patterns — e.g., [[Black Crappie]] versus [[Bluegill]] in White Oak Lake showed markedly different radiomaterial partitioning between soft tissue and skeleton, attributed to differing food habits.
- **Seasonal and thermal effects**: radiomaterial accumulation correlates positively with water temperature in both Columbia River and White Oak Lake organisms, though White Oak Lake fish showed an anomalous decline after August 1 despite stable temperatures, possibly indicating summer dormancy/estivation in warm-water fish species.
- **Age/growth effects**: younger, faster-growing fish accumulate proportionally more radioactivity than older, slower-growing individuals, likely reflecting more rapid anabolic metabolism; this is offset in rapidly growing organisms by biological dilution from cell division.
- **Equilibration timescales** differ by organism size: phytoplankton reach equilibrium with water radioisotope concentration in ~15 hours (for P³²), while larger animals like fish approach equilibrium far more slowly — radiophosphorus took two days to appear in fish following introduction into an acid bog lake.

### Direct Absorption Studies (Table 3 — laboratory immersion)
- **[[Goldfish]]**: Strontium concentration factor ≈150; Barium-Lanthanum ≈150; Sodium ≈30 (Prosser et al., 1945).
- **[[Guppy]]**: Calcium concentration factor ≈1,000 (estimated from Rosenthal, 1956).
- Increasing calcium concentration in ambient water was shown to *decrease* strontium uptake in goldfish — a direct example of competitive elemental inhibition.

### Uptake Mechanism in Fish
- [[Young Trout]] immersed directly in dilute Hanford reactor effluent **failed** to concentrate radiophosphorus.
- Trout **fed** organisms grown in that same effluent accumulated substantial P³² — confirming ingestion, not direct absorption, as the dominant uptake route in fish.
- 50–75% of ingested radiophosphorus is assimilated and retained by fish (unpublished Hanford data).

### Tissue-Specific Partitioning
- In [[White Oak Lake]] fish, despite most food-organism radioactivity (e.g., in larval *Chaoborus*) originating from radiophosphorus, only a small fraction of fish-body radioactivity traced to that isotope.
- Conversely, although Sr⁹⁰ contributed only a small proportion of radioactivity in plankton, ~80% of skeletal radioactivity in fish was attributable to Sr⁹⁰ — demonstrating selective tissue-targeting (bone-seeking behavior) independent of dietary proportion.
- [[Bluegill]]: higher radiomaterial concentration in soft tissue; relatively more radiophosphorus in bone.
- [[Black Crappie]]: higher radiomaterial concentration in skeleton/hard parts; relatively more radiostrontium in bone.

### Toxicity Thresholds
- D. G. Watson (Hanford Laboratories, unpublished): a bone concentration of **65 µc P³² per gram** was lethal to trout within ~6 weeks.
- A concentration of **10 µc P³² per gram** was not lethal within 12 weeks but caused measurable radiation damage.

### General Principles Established
1. Radiomaterials enter organisms via direct physiological incorporation or surface adsorption.
2. Lower organisms (bacteria, protozoa, phytoplankton) generally concentrate radioelements to higher levels than vertebrates — an inverse relationship between organismal complexity and concentration factor. Bacteria may exceed concentration factors of 1,000,000.
3. Specific tissues show strong elemental predilections: iodine → thyroid; silicon → diatom tests; calcium → mollusk shells (as carbonate) and vertebrate bone (as phosphate); phosphorus → bone and ATP in bird flight muscle.
4. Even elements at "safe" drinking-water concentrations can be bioconcentrated by aquatic organisms to harmful levels, with effects ranging from individual organism damage to population-level impacts.
5. Elimination occurs via four pathways: surface exchange, physiological excretion, moulting (in applicable taxa), and death — with released material immediately available for re-uptake by other organisms.
6. Environmental fixation matters: a large fraction of P³² added to lakes was rapidly bound in bottom sediments, becoming biologically unavailable despite phosphorus's normal metabolic importance ([[Coffin et al. 1949]]).
