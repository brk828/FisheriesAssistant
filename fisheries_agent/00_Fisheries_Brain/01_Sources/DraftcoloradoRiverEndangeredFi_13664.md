# Colorado River Endangered Fish Hatchery Feasibility Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #conservation-planning #hatchery-design

## Executive Summary

This January 1983 feasibility study by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] evaluates site selection, design requirements, and cultural methods for establishing a dedicated hatchery to propagate endangered [[Colorado River]] fishes. The study responds to recovery plan mandates calling for captive propagation of [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Target production is 500,000 fingerlings annually (3-inch size) distributed as 250,000 squawfish and 125,000 each of the two chub species. The study evaluates nine potential sites across the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] and adjacent regions, applying standardized criteria including water rights (1000 acre-feet), water quality (20-30°C temperature range, 120+ day growing season), land availability (50+ acres), and strategic location. [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] in Utah emerges as the preferred site due to its location within historical species range, proximity to stocking sites, and adequate water resources from the [[Green River]] and [[Pelican Lake]].

## Background & Description

All [[Colorado River]] endangered fish recovery plans advocate development of cultural methods for threatened and endangered species propagation. From 1979-1981, [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] personnel developed culture techniques at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in Arizona under agreement with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], producing thousands of fish for research at various laboratories and universities. The February 1982 feasibility study aims to systematically evaluate hatchery design and site selection compatible with the Region 6 [[Colorado River Endangered Fishes Conservation Plan]].

The Conservation Plan outlines strategies including: (1) providing hatchery fish for research and management studies from 1983-1996; (2) stocking to reestablish populations and increase Upper Basin stocks after 1987; and (3) developing a sport fishery for [[Colorado Squawfish]] in small reservoirs. The Plan calls for increasing present squawfish stocks through habitat management and stocking (young-of-year by 500%, juveniles and adults by 200%) and reestablishing [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] populations where feasible. Hatchery production is regarded as a fisheries management tool, not an alternative to natural habitat protection. Stocking would be limited to areas where species have been extirpated or severely diminished, provided causes of decline have been identified and corrected.

The study establishes that fish should reach 3 inches to receive and retain coded wire tags for permanent identification of lots and year classes. Under natural conditions in the Upper Basin, the growing season extends from May to October (120-150 days), but first-year squawfish grow little more than 2 inches. Hatchery-reared fish released at this size would have poor survival; however, with 11 months of hatchery growth reaching 3+ inches and late spring release after runoff, survival rates should improve substantially.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The three target species occupy different ecological niches within the [[Colorado River Basin]]. [[Colorado Squawfish]] historically ranged throughout the mainstem Colorado and major tributaries including the [[Green River]], [[Gunnison River]], and [[San Juan River]]. The species exhibits spawning migrations, making homing instinct a potential management tool. [[Bonytail Chub]] shared much of this range but has become critically rare in the Upper Basin, approaching extinction. [[Humpback Chub]] has a more restricted distribution, not historically occurring in some tributaries like the Gunnison.

Water temperature critically affects growth rates. The optimum growing temperature for [[Colorado Squawfish]] is 77°F, yielding growth rates of 0.4 mm/day. At 68°F, growth rate drops to 0.25 mm/day. Natural river temperatures in the [[Green River]] and [[Pelican Lake]] range from 32° to 81°F annually, with growing season temperatures of 68-81°F. Established warm-water pond culture methods have proven suitable for all three endangered species, with earthen one-acre ponds at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] producing larger fish than water recirculation systems at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Logan Fisheries Experiment Station]].

Cultural success varies by facility. [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico successfully cultures 15 rare and endangered southwestern fishes plus five Colorado River species ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Woundfin]]). The facility is committed to producing 200,000 razorback suckers, 100,000 squawfish, 100,000 bonytail chub, 25,000 woundfin, and smaller numbers of other species. At 50,000 fish per acre production rate, approximately 9 acres are committed to current production, with 11 acres remaining from the 20-acre total pond capacity.

Disease risks accompany fish transfers between watersheds. [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]'s open water supply from [[Lake Mohave]] has led to disease problems (Ich, tapeworm, anchorworm), though prophylactic treatments control these organisms. Facilities holding multiple species from various river systems risk introducing non-endemic fishes and unknown disease organisms when stocking into the Colorado River system.

## Key Findings

**Production Requirements and Hatchery Design:**
- Target production: 500,000 fingerlings annually at 3-inch size (250,000 [[Colorado Squawfish]], 125,000 [[Bonytail Chub]], 125,000 [[Humpback Chub]])
- Required facilities: 10-20 acres of productive pond space (one-acre earthen ponds), 20 small ponds (0.1-0.5 acres) for broodstock (100 adults/species) and experimentation, 6 raceways (6'x50') for pre-shipment holding and quarantine, 20-tank holding house with laboratory for disease work and biological studies
- Production capacity: 25,000-50,000 three-inch fingerlings per surface acre annually
- Growing period: 11 months required to reach 3-inch size for spring release
- Standard warm-water pond culture methods selected as optimal based on superior growth at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] compared to recirculation systems

**Site Selection Criteria Established:**
- Water rights: 1000 acre-feet required, dependable year-round, sufficient to refill 3 one-acre ponds in 48 hours
- Water quality: 20-30°C temperature range, 120+ day growing season, 5+ ppm dissolved oxygen, pH 7-9, relatively free of contaminants (H₂S, Mn, heavy metals, silt, disease organisms)
- Gravity supply preferred over pumping; multiple sources preferred
- Land: 50+ acres required, federal or state ownership, near-level topography with drainage, clay soil available on-site
- Location: centrally located in [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] within historical species range, within 8-hour drive of stocking sites, year-round accessible, within 50 miles of services
- Economics: most economical construction, operation, and maintenance costs while meeting management requirements

**Site Evaluations - Nine Potential Locations Assessed:**

1. **[[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]** (Arizona, [[Lake Mohave]]): Successfully cultured endangered species 1979-1981 using modified raceways with recirculated water warmed to 70-74°F. Current maximum production 100,000 fish, well below 500,000 target. Major expansion limited by restricted land availability. Distance from Upper Basin (500+ miles, 16 hours one-way) creates distribution challenges. Water temperature 54-56°F requires warming. Disease problems from open Lake Mohave water supply. Located in historical range of squawfish and bonytail but not humpback chub. Would require agreement between Region 2 and Region 6 for trout production displacement.

2. **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]** (New Mexico, [[Pecos River]] watershed): Warm-water facility with 39 earthen ponds totaling 20 acres, 55 acre-feet volume. Four wells produce 1300 gpm average (up to 2300 gpm), but water rights limited to 1353.3 acre-feet/year (830 gpm equivalent). Additional artesian well supplies 3000 gpm with pumping (318 acre-feet/year right). Constant 64°F water temperature. Very high hardness (2200 mg/L vs. 358 mg/L at Willow Beach). Currently holds/cultures 15 rare southwestern species plus 5 Colorado River species. Committed to producing 200,000 razorback suckers, 100,000 squawfish, 100,000 bonytail, plus other species. Good expansion potential with 640 acres land and ~1700 acre-feet water available, but many existing ponds leak. Distance from Upper Basin (600+ miles, 16+ hours) limits distribution. Risk of introducing non-endemic species and diseases. Not in current squawfish range, precluding homing experiments.

3. **[[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]]** (Colorado, [[North Fork Gunnison River]]): Two units (Headquarters and Chipeta) on 131.3 total acres at 5200 feet elevation. Headquarters has 32 concrete raceways supplied by springs (1600-4000 gpm, 56°F constant). Chipeta Unit had 5 earthen ponds (1-2 acres), 8 new concrete raceways, 2 earthen raceways, supplied by 9 springs (1350-2245 gpm). 1981 landslide destroyed Chipeta facilities, collection system, access road; total flow undiminished but delivery system lost. Chipeta previously grew 100 squawfish for broodstock; growth averaged 3 inches/year for sub-adults 1977-1980 (water 42-84°F). Water rights include springs (equivalent 4310 gpm/7000 acre-feet/year), two additional springs (449-898 gpm), [[North Fork Gunnison River]] (1796 gpm/2920 acre-feet), [[Le Roux Creek]] (3143 gpm/5100 acre-feet, unused due to siltation and temperature). Headquarters could produce ~100,000 fingerlings/year in rehabilitated raceways, but insufficient space for full production. **No longer considered due to landslide uncertainty and trout production commitments.**

4. **[[Logan Fisheries Experiment Station]]** (Utah, [[Logan River]]/[[Bear River]] tributary): Utah Division of Wildlife Resources research facility. 117-day growing season, 47°F average air temperature. ~42 new concrete raceways plus 14 large gravel-bottomed units and 43 smaller interconnected units. Water from ~15 artesian wells (1800-2700 gpm, 50-63°F, excellent quality). 1980-81 modified units for recirculation, reared squawfish and humpback chub from Willow Beach and Hotchkiss. Daily average recirculated temperature ~68°F (62-84°F range over 24 hours). Growth rate 0.25 mm/day at 68°F vs. optimal 0.4 mm/day at 77°F. Limiting factor: water temperature; would require deeper wells or improved recirculation with covered raceways. 8-10 hours driving to stocking sites. Outside Colorado River system, risks disease/non-endemic fish transfer between watersheds. Currently disease-free. Fish could not home to station; captive brood required.

5. **[[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]]** (Utah, [[Green River]]): 11,500 acres including ~12 miles of Green River, 3500 acres seasonal water for waterfowl. 110-day growing season, 45°F average air temperature. Water rights: 23,000 acre-feet/year from Green River, 1890 acre-feet [[Pelican Lake]], 8-inch domestic well (225 gpm). Green River water useable but requires filtration for suspended solids and fish; temperature 32-81°F (may be too warm in summer, too cold in winter); requires pumping. Pelican Lake water available by gravity (90-foot head), 68-81°F growing season, but requires filtration for sediment, fish, parasites (swimmer's itch reported); aeration needed for hydrogen sulfide; unavailable when frozen. Groundwater from Green River alluvium excellent but requires pumping and aeration; domestic well devoid of oxygen, contains manganese (Mn²⁺ up to 0.8 mg/L, potentially toxic), 52°F temperature. Substantial alluvial clay suitable for pond construction (extent unknown pending analysis). Multiple sites sufficiently large and flat; requires engineering evaluation. **Favored site:** in historical range of all three target species, on squawfish migration route (ideal for homing experiments), no risk of non-endemic species introduction, good location for Upper Basin stocking (4-8 hours, <300 miles to upper Colorado River; 6-8 hours to lower Green River), but poor for Lower Basin access (2 days, 800+ miles).

**Additional Sites to be Evaluated in FY 1983:**
- [[Page Springs State Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona)
- [[Rifle Falls State Fish Hatchery]] (Colorado) - previously considered for squawfish rearing until plan killed by state legislature in 1979
- [[Brown's Park National Wildlife Refuge]] (Colorado) - water and land on Green River
- [[Big Springs]] (Uintah Indian Reservation, Utah) - Green River drainage, substantial spring flows, fish-out ponds for trout

**Sites Eliminated:**
- [[Navajo Dam]] sites (above and below, [[San Juan River]], New Mexico) - 1981 Piper evaluation found no acceptable location; floating hatchery proposed but deemed unsuitable for endangered fish hatchery

**Critical Needs and Recommendations:**

The study identifies an urgent need for an endangered species hatchery in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. A single hatchery ([[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]) holding all reserve populations is insufficient to protect against catastrophic loss from flood, disease, vandalism, or other unexpected events. Reserve populations at multiple hatcheries are essential. Region 6 FWS currently has no warm-water hatchery facilities capable of rearing endangered Colorado River fishes and maintains no reserve populations.

**[[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] is the preferred site** based on site selection criteria, particularly its location within historical range of all three target species and position on the [[Colorado Squawfish]] migration route, enabling homing experiments. The site eliminates risks of introducing non-endemic fishes and diseases from other river systems.

**Three critical questions require early FY 1983 resolution** to focus study scope: (1) Should scope be limited to Region 6? (2) Should the hatchery be within historical range of the three endangered fishes? (3) Should state hatcheries and/or lands be considered?

**Funding and Timeline Concerns:** The $30,000 FY 1983 appropriation is insufficient for planned work, particularly without engineering involvement. Requested funds for on-site tests (soils analysis, well exploration) were not appropriated. Site selection by end of FY 1983 may lack complete engineering data, requiring future engineering input for top three sites. The [[Conservation Plan]] calls for operational hatchery in ~5 years, but this requires immediate firm commitment and adequate funding. Delay risks further stock declines; [[Bonytail Chub]] in the Upper Basin may face extinction in the interim. Wild fish needed to start the cultural program could disappear without expeditious progress.

**Cultural Methods Established:** Warm-water pond culture methods proven suitable for all three species (Hamman 1981, Inslee 1981, Miller et al. 1982). Earthen one-acre ponds produce larger fish than recirculation systems. Standard warm-water ponds selected as optimal rearing units. Fish should reach 3 inches for coded wire tag retention, enabling permanent identification of lots and year classes for research and management.
