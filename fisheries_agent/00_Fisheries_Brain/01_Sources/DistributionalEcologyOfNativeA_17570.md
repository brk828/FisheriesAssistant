# Distributional Ecology of Native and Introduced Fishes of the Pit River System, Northeastern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by [[James J. Cooper]] (Bioresources Center, Desert Research Institute) surveys fish distribution in the [[Pit River]] and its tributaries between Turner and Juniper creeks in northeastern California, based on fieldwork in summer and fall 1978. The survey found native fishes dominating the undisturbed upper river and tributaries, while introduced species dominated the altered lower river. The work also assessed the status of the rare [[Modoc Sucker]], finding hybrid suckers that suggest the species occupied Pit River and [[Willow Creek]] in the recent past. The study was conducted for the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation to evaluate whether the proposed [[Allen Camp Reservoir]] would harm habitat of the Modoc sucker or the [[Rough Sculpin]].

## Background & Description

- **Study context:** The Pit River supports native fishes of the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Basin]] plus several introduced species. Two endemics, the Modoc sucker and the rough sculpin, are rare under California Fish and Game Commission classification. The Modoc sucker is also listed as endangered by the American Fisheries Society Endangered Species Committee. Prior research focused mainly on these rare species, leaving the rest of the drainage poorly documented.
- **Study area:** Approximately 100 km northeast of Redding and 40 to 50 km southwest of Alturas. The upper river (Turner Creek to Rose Canyon Creek) flows through a narrow valley at about 1,280 m, with willow-bordered banks and meadow vegetation. The lower river crosses [[Big Valley]] (about 1,265 m elevation), where it meanders through sloughs fed by agricultural return flows. [[Ash Creek]] joins Pit River north of Bieber after crossing Big Swamp.
- **Drainage:** The Pit River basin covers about 13,468 km², originating in the Warner Mountains. About 38% (5,050 km²) lies upstream of the study area.
- **Sampling methods:**
  - Tributaries (17 May to 7 June 1978) were sampled mainly by [[Electrofishing]] with a backpack unit, using 50 m blocked reaches in three successive runs.
  - Pit River (10 to 15 July, 22 to 25 August, and 16 to 21 October 1978) was sampled with multi- and single-panel gill nets, a beach seine, and boat electrofishing.
  - A total of 52 stations was sampled: 13 on Pit River and 39 on tributaries.
- **Analysis:** [[Rank Correlation]] (Spearman's rho), Student's t-tests on arcsine-transformed data, and [[Discriminant Analysis]] (Tustat-II package) were used.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Upper river:** Steep gradient, high velocity, riffle-run-pool habitat, and boulder-gravel substrate. Cattle grazing and farming occur, but the reach is otherwise largely unimpounded. The proposed Allen Camp Reservoir would inundate much of this section.
- **Lower river:** Sluggish, silty, and turbid (Secchi depth 0.7 m), with water deeper than 1 m and warmer temperatures. Irrigation diversions such as the Gerig Diversion (about 4 m high) and the Lookout Diversion back up water for more than 16 river km.
- **Native association:** The [[Sacramento Sucker]]–[[Sacramento Squawfish]]–[[Hardhead]] association dominates the upper river and matches the sucker-hardhead-squawfish zone described by Moyle (1976) for the Sacramento and San Joaquin drainages.
- **Introduced association:** The [[Golden Shiner]]–[[Bluegill]]–[[Largemouth Bass]] association dominates the altered lower river.
- **Tributaries:** Mostly intermittent streams used as spring-runoff nursery habitat for stream-spawning species. Many dry by mid-summer. Reservoirs (Taylor, Lower Roberts, Kramer) were dominated by introduced lentic species, with the exception of native suckers and squawfish at Kramer.

## Key Findings

### Community Composition

- **Pit River:** 2,221 fish representing 14 species, 11 genera, and six families. Introduced species made up 38.5% of the river sample.
- **Tributaries:** 895 fish representing 13 species, 11 genera, and six families (seven native, six introduced). Fish were present at 23 of 39 tributary stations (59%).
- **Dominant species in the river:** Sacramento sucker (27.6% of catch), hardhead (20.8%), Sacramento squawfish (12.7%).
- **Dominant species in tributaries:** Sacramento squawfish (18.4%), Sacramento sucker (18.1%), brown bullhead, and speckled dace.
- **Most widespread:** Sacramento squawfish (12 of 13 river stations), followed by Sacramento sucker, [[Channel Catfish]], largemouth bass, and [[Brown Bullhead]] (11 stations). Five of the seven most widespread river species are non-native.

### Upper vs. Lower River

- **Upper river:** The sucker-squawfish-hardhead association made up 72.5% of the catch. Sacramento suckers averaged 64.2% of total fish weight per station. [[Channel Catfish]] averaged 23.6% of biomass, described as an apparently under-utilized sport fishery. Mean diversity was 8.2 species per station (range 8 to 9).
- **Lower river:** The golden shiner–bluegill–largemouth bass association comprised 61.9% of fish captured at seven stations. Golden shiner was dominant at three of these stations. Introduced fish were numerous but small, contributing less biomass. At one station, Sacramento suckers made up 32.7% of fish by number but 84.3% of biomass. Mean diversity was 8.1 species per station, with 5.6 introduced species on average.
- **Statistical support:** The lower-river shiner–bluegill–bass association was significantly higher than other lower-river associations (P < .001), and the upper-river sucker–squawfish–hardhead association was highly significant (P < .001).

### Tributaries

- The Sacramento sucker and squawfish were the most common association, co-occurring at most sites. Suckers occurred at 15 stations (65.2%) and squawfish at 14 (60.8%). The two species made up 64.8% of catches where found together.
- Suckers and squawfish contributed 72.1% of tributary biomass. The authors noted that electrofishing may favor large-bodied fish, so numerical dominance could partly reflect sampling bias.
- Tributary diversity was highest in the Willow Creek drainage (mean 3.3) and Widow Valley Creek drainage (mean 3.2). Diversity was positively correlated with discharge (P < .05) and was significantly lower than in Pit River stations (P < .001).

### Modoc Sucker and Hybridization

- Specimens with intermediate meristic characters were classified as **Sacramento–Modoc sucker hybrids**. They were found near the Miller Ranch on the upper river and in the headwaters of Willow Creek. Pure Modoc sucker specimens (n = 7) came from Hulbert Creek.
- Discriminant analysis used five meristic characters (lateral line scales, dorsal rays, standardized eye diameter, and scales above and below the lateral line). Sample sizes were Sacramento sucker n = 89, hybrid n = 5, and Modoc n = 7. Lateral line scales, dorsal rays, and eye diameter differed significantly among groups (P < .001). Only 8.2% of cases were misclassified.
- The Modoc sucker was not found in the surveyed habitats. The presence of hybrids suggests that either Modoc suckers recently occupied the upper river or that the gene pool has been influenced by migrants from Turner Creek, about 8 km upstream. Introgression could occur quickly in isolated populations because of high suckers' fecundity.

### Conservation Conclusions

- Of the 14 native and 12 introduced fishes Moyle (1976) listed for the Pit River system, eight native and seven introduced species were found in this survey. Daniels and Moyle (1978) listed 12 species associated with rough sculpin habitat, eight of which were captured here.
- Native fish associations in the Sacramento-San Joaquin system have often been replaced by introduced species. Coyote Creek had lost over half its native fish populations since Snyder's 1905 survey.
- The author concludes that:
  - The Modoc sucker should remain classified as rare by the State of California, and steps should be taken toward federal listing.
  - Native fish assemblages should be considered in environmental impact analyses because their ecological and evolutionary values are irreparable once lost.
  - Transplanting the Modoc sucker from genetically pure populations to suitable habitats such as [[Halls Canyon Creek]] should be considered, with habitats altered if needed to meet species requirements.
