# Redrock Canyon Gila Topminnow and Section 7 Consultation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-topminnow

## Executive Summary

This conference presentation argues that the recovery of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] in [[Redrock Canyon]] is being constrained by the way [[Section 7 Consultation]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] has been applied over roughly three decades. The author holds that Redrock Canyon is well suited to federal conservation because it is one of the few Gila topminnow populations on federal land and the only one whose whole watershed is under federal control, managed largely by the [[Coronado National Forest]] (USFS) and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS). The author contends that consultations (1982, 1990, 1992, 1999, and a pending one) protected core spring areas but did not provide for population expansion, because they ignored the species' [[Boom and Bust Life History]], upland grazing effects, and historical evidence of habitat that could be restored. The presentation closes with a call to base management decisions on environmental history and on existing information.

## Background & Description

Redrock Canyon is a small stream in south-central Arizona and a tributary of [[Sonoita Creek]] near Patagonia. Historical information suggests perennial flow through most of the watershed until about 1900. Today the stream is largely intermittent, with perennial water persisting only in several discrete "core" areas, including [[Cott Tank]] drainage, Gate Spring, Pig Camp Spring, and Falls, plus a reach near the USFS boundary.

The Gila topminnow is listed as endangered. The author notes that Redrock Canyon supports one of the most diverse remnant native fish assemblages in the [[Gila River Basin]], which includes the Gila topminnow and longfin dace, along with other native dace. Nonnative fishes are present because of stock tanks across the basin. Of the several nonnative species recorded, only [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Mosquitofish]] appear to persist. The author describes the nonnative problem as serious but not overwhelming, and probably controllable rather than removable.

Land ownership and use are central to the analysis. The watershed is relatively small and under federal control except for a 160-acre private inholding in the center of the basin and a small inholding on a stock tank at the upper end. Livestock grazing, administered by the USFS, is the predominant human use. Other uses include unimproved roads, dispersed recreation, and unauthorized border crossings from Mexico, which are increasing with Border Patrol activity. Federal actions affecting grazing, roads, and recreation are subject to consultation, which is why the author considers the watershed a good candidate for section 7 conservation.

Section 7 terminology used in the text:
- **Biological opinion (BiOp):** FWS's finding on whether a federal action will jeopardize the species.
- **Incidental take statement:** Sets the limits of take and can require project changes.
- **Jeopardy vs. nonjeopardy:** A jeopardy finding requires changes to the action. The author notes FWS biologists are encouraged to negotiate solutions that avoid it.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Boom-and-bust life history.** The Gila topminnow must expand rapidly into dry-season refugia-adjacent channels during good water years. During rare wet years it can colonize most of the basin where gradient is low and barriers are absent. Current core areas total about 2 miles of watered channel, which changes substantially from year to year. The author states that most non-core areas are too degraded to support successful long-term recolonization.

**Metapopulation structure.** Until 1999, consultations treated individual sites as separate units. The 1999 consultation was the first formal recognition of the Redrock population as a metapopulation, which means the species depends on connectivity among sites.

**Grazing and watershed effects.** Livestock grazing has modified riparian vegetation and channel form across the watershed. Exclusion produced visible recovery:
- Pig Camp Spring and Gate Spring improved markedly after exclosure. Gate Spring is a core area, while Pig Camp is a small, seasonally used area.
- The Cott Tank drainage and Falls improved dramatically after grazing removal, and downstream perennial flow was reported to extend following livestock exclusion.
- In the central (Kunde) allotment, surface water and riparian vegetation increased during cattle nonuse. Willow, which the USFS had said was unlikely to recover in intermittent reaches, formed thickets in some places after five years of nonuse.

**Dam and stock tank barriers.** A small dam on the tributary Lampshire Canyon restricts fish movement. The 1999 opinion required its removal, but it had not been removed at the time of the presentation. Stock tanks serve as nonnative source habitat. Earthen tank construction was banned in 1990.

## Key Findings

**Consultation history**

- **1982:** First formal consultation, a topminnow recovery action with a nonjeopardy finding. Small concrete deflectors were only partially successful.
- **1990 Redrock Action Plan:** Nonjeopardy with an incidental take statement. Results included:
  - Livestock exclosures on the three then-known core areas plus one small spring.
  - Closure of two stream-bottom road segments.
  - Winter-only grazing in non-exclosed mainstream areas.
  - A ban on new earthen stock tanks.
  
  The author states this improved core areas but did not provide for population expansion. Upland watershed problems were largely ignored.
- **1992 Arizona Trail:** The USFS proposed routing a segment of the trail down Redrock Canyon. FWS considered this a setback, but the USFS argued that trail impacts were too speculative for a jeopardy finding and that management could control them. An alternative route through Lampshire Canyon was rejected as too ugly, given its heavy grazing.
- **1999 consultation:** Covered all allotments in the watershed and was the first to treat the population as a metapopulation. Nonjeopardy, but with more teeth in the incidental take statement. Requirements included:
  - Expansion of the Cott Tank exclosure.
  - Stringent grazing restrictions in the perennial reach near the USFS boundary.
  - Survey of all stock tanks in the drainage and removal of nonnative species.
  - Removal of the Lampshire Canyon dam.
  - An exclosure at the tributary Oak Grove Canyon.
  
  The grazing analysis covered only three years, which the author considers a key reason the opinion was nonjeopardy rather than jeopardy.

**Status of 1999 requirements**

- The grazing restrictions near the USFS boundary were implemented and produced habitat improvement.
- The Cott Tank exclosure was completed.
- The Oak Grove exclosure was reportedly designed in a way that excluded all habitat that could be recolonized, and FWS later agreed to leave it that way under USFS pressure.
- The Lampshire Canyon dam has not been removed, and the USFS is resisting removal.
- Nonnative removal had not been conducted, though surveys were beginning.
- The private 160-acre inholding was sold. The new owner agreed to a 10-year nonuse arrangement funded by mitigation money, but the USFS did not get the agreement signed. The owner later decided to stock the allotment with cattle.

**Current consultation**

- The 1999 consultation expires in July 2002, triggering a new one.
- The USFS proposes no improvements to watershed conditions. It plans to resume grazing in the central allotment, which the author calls a significant setback.
- The only proposed improvement for the topminnow is an exclosure of the core area near the USFS boundary.
- The USFS is seeking removal or alteration of some improvements required by earlier opinions.

**Author's conclusions**

- The USFS views protection of core areas as sufficient mitigation and resists creating new core areas, metapopulation management, population expansion, restoration of the boom-and-bust cycle, and remediation of upland grazing impacts.
- FWS, in the author's assessment, will not or cannot apply enough leverage through section 7 to overcome this resistance.
- The presentation addresses the conference theme "meeting resource management information needs." The author argues the core problem is not lack of information but failure to recognize and use existing information, particularly the environmental baseline sections of biological opinions.
- Historical evidence shows the canyon was once substantially different, with enough surface flow to support diversion and irrigation. The author argues that much of this can be restored, and that the claim that restoration is impossible has already been disproven in several cases, including Cott Tank and the Kunde allotment.
- The author concludes that analyses of environmental problems and solutions should be grounded in environmental history, or the species will not be preserved.
