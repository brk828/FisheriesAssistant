# 1988 Lake Mohave Sampling for Bonytail Chub and Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #FreshwaterEcology #EndangeredSpecies #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary

This 1987 proposal from [[W.L. Minckley]] and [[Paul C. Marsh]] (Arizona State University) outlines an intensive 10-day sampling effort at [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada border) scheduled for 4–13 March 1988. The dual-purpose expedition targeted two federally significant fish species: the critically endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The primary goals were (1) to capture remaining wild adult Bonytail Chub via trammel netting for translocation to refuge/hatchery stock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], and (2) to conduct large-scale electrofishing and Floy-tagging of adult Razorback Suckers to generate population abundance, distribution, and movement data. The document reflects a coordinated multi-agency conservation response to the near-extirpation of Bonytail Chub from the wild and long-standing recruitment failure in the Razorback Sucker population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the lower [[Colorado River]] (Arizona-Nevada), supports the only known remaining wild population of Bonytail Chub in the lower Colorado River basin and the largest remaining wild population of Razorback Sucker.
- **Microhabitat use (Bonytail Chub)**: Captured historically along spits/bars near the mouths of desert washes entering the lake, particularly during windy, wave-driven spring weather. Known productive areas include the Arizona shoreline near [[Cottonwood Cove]] (1970s–1980s), and later the [[Nine-Mile Cove|Nine- and Six-Mile Coves]] area (Nevada, mid-1980s), and near [[Katherine's Landing]], Arizona (single specimen, May 1984).
- **Microhabitat use (Razorback Sucker)**: Aggregates in areas such as [[Arizona Bay]], [[Cottonwood Cove]], [[Sixmile Cove]], and above [[Katherine's Landing]]; pre-spawning and spawning period spans November through March–April.
- **Reproductive ecology**: Razorback Sucker reproduces successfully in Lake Mohave and produces viable larvae, but there has been **no evidence of substantial recruitment to the adult population in nearly four decades**. Two juveniles were captured by Arizona Game and Fish Department in the riverine reach of the lake, suggesting some small fish presence, but a dedicated July 1987 survey found no additional juvenile specimens.
- **Trophic/behavioral notes**: Existing research on Razorback Sucker (referenced but not detailed) includes age and growth, larval feeding ecology, and predator-prey relationships — implying larval mortality from predation may be a limiting factor in recruitment.
- **Conservation status context**: Both species are treated as candidates for emergency ex-situ conservation; a forthcoming revised recovery plan from [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region 2 proposed removing all captured wild Bonytail Chub to refuge/hatchery facilities due to the extreme rarity of secure wild broodstock.

## Key Findings

- **Bonytail Chub population status**: Lake Mohave is considered to hold the only remaining wild population of Bonytail Chub in the lower Colorado River basin, but past collection efforts have yielded only a handful of individuals despite sustained trammel netting.
- **Genetic/broodstock urgency**: No existing captive broodstock derived from wild-caught Lake Mohave fish had proven fertility/fecundity, underscoring urgency in acquiring additional wild adults before extinction in the wild.
- **Proposed Bonytail Chub methodology**: Trammel nets set from bottom to surface at known movement corridors (bottom morphologies) near Cottonwood Cove, Katherine's Landing (AZ), and Sixmile Coves (NV); nets tended at intervals of no more than 4 hours, ideally near-continuously, to minimize stress/mortality. On-site holding facilities, prophylactic treatment capability, and rapid-transport hatchery trucks were required contingencies. Captured fish were to be stabilized at interim facilities ([[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] or [[Page Springs Hatchery]]) prior to final transport to Dexter NFH.
- **Razorback Sucker tagging history**: University of Nevada, Las Vegas personnel tagged/fin-clipped 933 adult Razorback Suckers across Lake Mohave (Nov 1982–May 1983); only 11 recaptures occurred, with just one fish at large longer than a month, and no evaluation of tag loss was conducted.
- **Prior population estimate**: Combined recapture data (14 additional recaptures from ASU and Arizona Game and Fish Department) produced a rough adult population estimate of approximately **55,000 individuals**, but this was flagged as unreliable due to wide confidence intervals and geographically clustered marking effort.
- **1988 proposed tagging methodology**: Use of tagging guns and Floy tags (color-coded by sampling area) applied via electrofishing; goal of processing up to **800 razorback suckers per boat per day** under favorable conditions — with four boats over four nights capable of tagging **~3,200 fish**.
- **Tag-loss/survival study**: A subset of tagged fish was proposed to be held in Lake Mohave backwaters for a minimum of 4 months to evaluate tag retention and post-tagging survival under semi-natural conditions.
- **Operational design**: Electrofishing effort concentrated in known Razorback Sucker aggregation zones (Arizona Bay, Cottonwood Cove, Sixmile Coves, above Katherine's Landing); minimal effective voltage/amperage was to be used to reduce stress; boat crews structured with pilot, netter, and adequate fish-processing personnel.
- **Interagency coordination**: The effort was designed as a multi-agency collaboration involving Arizona State University, [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], [[California Department of Fish and Game]], and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], with participating agencies expected to fund their own personnel costs; ASU costs were to be reimbursed by USFWS Region 2.
- **Timeline**: Agencies were asked to confirm participation and resource commitments by 30 November 1987 ahead of the March 1988 field effort.
