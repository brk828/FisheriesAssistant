# Lake Trout Consumption and Recent Changes in the Fish Assemblage of Flaming Gorge Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study uses a [[Bioenergetics Model]] to quantify the consumption dynamics of [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*) in [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], Utah-Wyoming, and tests whether lake trout predation drove recent shifts in the forage fish community. Kokanee (*Oncorhynchus nerka*) constituted the largest share of prey biomass consumed by large lake trout. [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*) were more vulnerable to predation than [[Kokanee]] because they grow slower and remain in the vulnerable size range for a longer period. The authors conclude that kokanee will make up an even larger share of the pelagic fish assemblage in future years.

## Background & Description

Bioenergetics models have been used in freshwater food web studies to compare piscivore consumption with prey biomass and production, to forecast forage fish responses to predator changes, and to assess the relative impact of different piscivores (Stewart et al. 1981; Stewart et al. 1983). The size dependence of piscivory strongly shapes prey populations, and slow prey growth lengthens the period during which prey are vulnerable to predation (Ney and Orth 1986; Luecke et al. 1990a).

The study objective was to determine whether lake trout predation caused recent changes in forage fish populations. Purse-seine data indicated that Utah chub density declined four-fold from 1978 to 1990, while kokanee density increased after a stocking program began. Lake trout harvest rose roughly 10-fold over the same period, and by 1990 kokanee were the most abundant pelagic fish.

The reservoir was formed by impoundment of the [[Green River]] behind [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962. It is about 145 km long, covers 17,000 hectares, and has a mean depth of 34 m. Three zones differ in their fish communities:
- **Canyon Area:** deep (maximum 122 m), thermally stratified, mesotrophic to nearly oligotrophic; kokanee dominant.
- **Open Hills Area:** wider, moderately deep (maximum 61 m), windy, mesotrophic.
- **Inflow Area:** shallow (maximum 24 m), mesoeutrophic, turbid in summer; Utah chub abundant.

Other species present include [[White Sucker]], [[Redside Shiner]], and [[Rainbow Trout]], which is stocked and does not reproduce naturally. The main piscivores are lake trout and [[Smallmouth Bass]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diet and prey.** Lake trout diets were sampled from May 1990 to May 1991 using gill nets, angler and creel collections, taxidermist donations, and ice fishing. A total of 303 stomachs were analyzed. Small lake trout (400-600 mm) relied mainly on invertebrates, including [[Chironomids]], [[Crayfish]] (*Orconectes* sp.), and [[Zooplankton]] (mainly *Daphnia pulex*). Lake trout over 600 mm fed mainly on fish.

**Thermal history.** Temperatures were estimated from hydroacoustic surveys, thermistor readings at gill-net sites, and assumed seasonal depths. Modeled temperatures were 4°C in December-April, 8°C in May, 9.3°C in June-September, 9°C in October, and 6.5°C in November.

**Habitat segregation.** Juvenile Utah chub occupy warm littoral waters in summer, where lake trout are rare. In summer, adult chub occupy warm epilimnetic water while lake trout remain below the thermocline. Kokanee and lake trout overlap spatially throughout the year.

**Competing explanations.** The authors weighed alternatives. Resource competition from stocked kokanee was considered unlikely because daphnid density and size increased from 1983 to 1991 and Secchi transparency and total phosphorus were stable. Smallmouth bass were judged an unlikely cause because their diet was mostly crayfish.

## Key Findings

- **Lake trout population size:** Mark-recapture estimated 120,500 lake trout over 400 mm on 30 April 1989 (95% CI 83,700-175,300). Annual survivorship was about 65 ± 13% (SD).
- **Annual forage consumption:** Lake trout consumed an estimated 331 tonnes of forage fish per year: kokanee 196 t, Utah chub 78.5 t, and rainbow trout 58.6 t. Lake trout over 600 mm accounted for 196 t and smaller fish for 137 t. Age-classes 4-6 (586-813 mm) consumed the most.
- **Growth of lake trout:** Fish 400-600 mm grew 0.051% of body weight per day (p-value 0.54). Fish 601-800 mm grew 0.013% per day (p = 0.55). Fish over 800 mm grew 0.0086% per day (p = 0.51).
- **Prey size selection:** Lake trout did not eat prey longer than 50% of their own length. Mean prey length was 146 mm for trout under 700 mm and 294 mm for trout over 700 mm.
- **Growth contrast of prey:** Age-1 kokanee averaged 135 mm (40 g) versus 63 mm (3 g) for age-1 Utah chub. Kokanee grew through the vulnerable size window in 2-3 years, whereas Utah chub required about 7 years. Chub were therefore vulnerable nearly three times longer.
- **Trophic bottleneck:** The authors propose that lake trout under 600 mm are limited by the scarcity of suitably sized forage fish, which may reduce their growth and survival. Continued chub decline would extend this limitation.
- **Apparent competition:** Increased kokanee abundance after stocking appears to have boosted lake trout growth and survival, and the resulting higher trout density suppressed Utah chub disproportionately. This is described as apparent competition mediated by predation (Holt 1984).
- **Predicted future:** If chub continue to decline, kokanee mortality from lake trout predation is expected to rise. The authors note that lake trout–kokanee fisheries in less productive systems are unstable, though Flaming Gorge's higher productivity may allow coexistence.
