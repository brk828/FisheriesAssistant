# Refinements of Equipment and Procedures in Electro-Fishing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This short methods note by Arthur Witt Jr. and Robert S. Campbell (Missouri Wildlife Research Unit, University of Missouri) describes a boat-mounted electrofishing device, the [[Boom Seine]], and evaluates it in [[Lake of the Ozarks]], Missouri. The authors report that the device is about three times more efficient at night than during the day, and that its catch composition differs strongly from that of [[Gill Net]] and [[Hoop Net]] sets in the same water. Electroseining favored [[Centrarchidae]] (basses and sunfishes), particularly where fish moved into shallow shoreline water at night. It was weak for [[White Crappie]], [[Gizzard Shad]] and [[White Bass]], which the authors attribute to depth preference and diel behavior. Selectivity is therefore framed as a function of fish behavior and habitat as well as of gear.

## Background & Description

Electrofishing was described as a valuable and rapidly developing tool in fishery research. The note aims to (1) describe an improved electroseining rig, (2) compare nocturnal and diurnal electroseining, and (3) discuss species selectivity of the electric seine relative to nets fished in the same waters.

**Equipment design.** The boom seine is a modification of the electric stream seine of [[Funk 1949]]. Key differences from Funk's design and from [[Loeb 1957]] include:

- **Mounting:** Electrodes hang from a transverse boom at the bow of a 16-ft square-stern aluminum boat, instead of from a cable carried by wading operators.
- **Electrode array:** Four electrodes sit in a linear series on the outer boom, spaced at 3-ft intervals, with alternating polarity. Loeb's rig used two electrodes on each side of the boat, the anterior pair 12 ft forward of the posterior pair.
- **Electrodes:** Each is 12 ft long, made of ⅜-inch hollow braided metal bonding (the type used to shield ignition systems), stiffened with discarded rubber-covered cable and weighted with lead seine weights.
- **Power:** A gasoline-driven Homelite alternating-current generator rated at 1500 W and 120 V.
- **Lighting:** Three 100 W lights on clamp-on reflectors (one on the stem, one on each gunwale) for night work.
- **Safety:** A switch between the electrodes and the junction box lets the operator cut electrode current without turning off the lights.

**Operation.** One person dip-nets stunned fish from the stem. A second person rows from the stern facing forward. The seine works best moved slowly close to shore in water less than 10 ft deep, with the oarsman steering the electrodes around rocks and stumps to cover likely fish habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study setting.** Sampling took place in June 1953 along steep rubbly shores, brush-strewn banks and coves, and shallow muddy shores of Lake of the Ozarks, a Missouri impoundment. Water was fairly clear, with [[Secchi Disc]] readings averaging 18 inches. Three additional nocturnal tests were made the following October.

**Diel behavior.** The authors note that the results of electrofishing for any one species depend on its diel movements, which affect both efficiency and selectivity. Basses and sunfishes were observed moving close to shore at night and resting on the bottom in very shallow water, where they were most vulnerable to the electrodes. White crappie seek deeper water than other centrarchids and were not reached by the electrodes, and turbidity may have hampered their recovery in deeper water.

**Habitat.** Centrarchids were more abundant over rubble bottoms in shallow water. Young freshwater drum and carpsuckers were more abundant over mud bottoms, in both shallow and deep water. Gizzard shad and white bass do not commonly inhabit the immediate shoreline and were therefore less vulnerable to the electric seine.

**Comparison gear.** Gill nets (mesh given as 1-inch to 2½-inch bar measure in the scan, which reads "24-inch") were set perpendicular to shore, extending from the shoreline to 30–40 ft depth. Hoop nets were set parallel to shore at 5–25 ft depth. Nets were fished overnight, 3 or 4 days a week in 1950 and 5 days in 1953. The boom seine fished only 9 hours along the shore.

## Key Findings

**1. Nocturnal electroseining is about three times more efficient than diurnal.**
- June 1953, day: average 98 fish per hour.
- June 1953, night (same day): average 346 fish per hour.
- October 1953, night: average 305 fish per hour.
- Sampling was roughly one daylight hour and a similar length of time at night for each area, over three days in June.
- Fish shocked but not recovered were estimated to be about half of those shocked, so the catch figures are approximate.

**2. Species overlap and diel effects.**
- 21 species were captured by both day and night electrofishing.
- White bass, walleye and white crappie, all abundant in the reservoir, were taken only at night.
- The authors cite similar day–night differences in stream electrofishing (Bowman, personal communication).

**3. Selectivity relative to nets.**
The authors caution that absolute numbers are not directly comparable because effort was not constant, but relative composition shows clear differences.

| Measure | Nets (1950 / 1953) | Electrofishing (June / Oct 1953) |
|---|---|---|
| Four most abundant net species (white crappie, gizzard shad, white bass, freshwater drum) | 91% (June–Sept 1950); 78% (Oct 1953) | 17% (June 1953); 12% (Oct 1953) |
| Centrarchids (excluding white crappie) | 2% (1950); 2% (Oct 1953) | 72% (June 1953); 71% (Oct 1953) |

- Electroseining was selective against white crappie, gizzard shad and white bass.
- Electroseining was selective toward the basses and sunfishes, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Bluegill Sunfish]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Longear Sunfish]].

**4. Interpretation.**
- Selectivity reflects depth preference, diel movement and habitat use, not only gear design.
- Gear choice therefore shapes which species appear in a survey. Comparisons across gear types should account for behavior and habitat.

**5. Equipment advantages.**
- The boom seine mounts the electrodes at the bow, so one operator can cover shoreline habitat from a boat.
- The reversible electrode switch and separate light circuit improve safety and night operation.
