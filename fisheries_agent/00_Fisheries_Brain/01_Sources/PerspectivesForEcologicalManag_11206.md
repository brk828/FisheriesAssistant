# Alternatives in Regulated River Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book, edited by [[James A. Gore]] and [[Geoffrey E. Petts]] and published by CRC Press in 1989, is a multi-author volume on ecological management of regulated rivers. The supplied text is Chapter 1, "Perspectives for Ecological Management of Regulated Rivers" by Petts. It traces roughly 5,000 years of river regulation, summarizes the ecological impacts of dams, channelization and water transfers, and proposes a tiered decision framework that places [[Secondary Regulation Measures]] first. It also argues that environmental management is especially important, and difficult, in [[Third World]] settings. The chapter's focus is on rivers and their ecosystems (fish, macroinvertebrates, macrophytes, floodplain biota) rather than on any single fish species.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The chapter covers the history of river regulation, impacts of regulation, a conceptual framework, management alternatives, Third World perspectives and a conclusion.
- **Historical phases:** Regulation dates to about 3000 B.C. on the [[Nile]], [[Tigris-Euphrates]] and [[Indus River]]. Major schemes followed in Europe from about 1750. The text identifies four phases:
  1. By 1900, most large European rivers were channelized. Tulla began channelizing the Alsatian [[Rhine]] in 1817. The Tisza control, begun in 1845, drained 12.5 million ha of floodplain marsh and shortened the river by 340 km.
  2. 1900–1940: dam building expanded. The 221-m [[Hoover Dam]] (1936) impounded Lake Mead.
  3. 1950–1980: the peak of dam building. More than 700 large dams were completed each year worldwide, and China had 80,000 reservoirs by 1980.
  4. Current phase: about 500 large dams per year, with environmental issues now shaping planning. The text projects that more than 60% of world stream flow will be regulated by 2000.
- **Major dams and reservoirs (Table 1):** The highest dams include Rogun (335 m), Nurek (300 m) and Grand Dixence (285 m). The largest reservoirs include [[Bratsk Reservoir]] (169,270 × 10⁶ m³), [[Lake Nasser]]/Aswan (168,900) and [[Lake Kariba]] (160,368).
- **Interbasin transfers:** Proposed transfers include 15 × 10⁹ m³/year from the Chang Jiang (China) and about 25 × 10⁹ m³ from the Ob River to the Aral Sea region (U.S.S.R.).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Impacts of regulation (after Kitson):** Regulation acts through abstractions, augmentation, and storage and channel modification. Short-duration events include fish kills from anoxic reservoir releases and gas supersaturation below dams. Most long-term change falls into three situations:

1. **Altered flow and water quality:** Benthic algae increased after closure of the Vir Dam on the Svratka River, and submerged angiosperms spread. Life-cycle type largely determines whether macroinvertebrates tolerate regulation. Cold hypolimnial releases keep the Colorado River too cold for most native fish for 400 km below Glen Canyon Dam.
2. **Barriers to migration:** Dams disrupt anadromous fish. Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*) disappeared from the Dordogne after lower-reach dams were built between 1842 and 1904.
3. **Isolation from the floodplain:** Loss of backwaters and floodplain lakes removes spawning and refuge habitat. In the lower Mekong, fisheries provide 4.5% of GNP and 40–60% of animal protein for 30 million people. Pa Mong Dam is expected to eliminate flooding for about 700 km downstream, with a catch loss of about 2,150 t.

**Zambezi case (Table 2):** Regulation by [[Lake Kariba]] produced effects that include saltwater incursion, increased turbidity, invasive floating plants ([[Water Hyacinth]], *Salvinia molesta*), the loss of flood-dependent fish such as *Opsaradium zambezense*, and higher transmission of schistosomiasis and malaria.

**Other impacts:** Loss of lotic habitat through channel shortening, and the introduction of exotic game fish. Canal transfers have also introduced fish, for example five species via the Orange-Fish Tunnel into the Great Fish River.

**Conceptual models:**
- [[River Continuum Concept]]: Physical gradients drive biological zonation from headwaters to large rivers. Zone 2 (middle reaches) has the highest biotic diversity.
- [[Serial Discontinuity Concept]]: A dam suppresses downstream diversity in headwater reaches, but may increase it on lower reaches.
- Functional sectors (Sectors A–C) replace strict zonation for large, complex rivers.
- Transient System Model: Systems pass through reaction and adjustment phases. Full equilibrium may take tens to hundreds of years.

## Key Findings

1. **Regulation is extensive and accelerating:** Large-scale dam building and transfers have changed river hydrology across much of the world, and the full ecological effect is probably underestimated because quantitative baseline data are lacking.
2. **Impacts are cumulative and delayed:** Changes unfold in a downstream direction at different rates. Biological adjustment follows physical adjustment, which can take centuries.
3. **Management hierarchy:** The authors propose three tiers. First-order options modify project design to maintain natural dynamics. Second-order options apply compensatory or mitigation measures. Third-order options (preservation of wild rivers) are assessed only if the first two prove inefficient.
4. **Secondary regulation measures are the priority:** These include:
   - *Flow modification:* An ecological compensation flow is often set arbitrarily, for example 10% of mean annual flow in Spain. The [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] is the most widely reported tool, but it is only partly validated.
   - *Water quality control:* Selective withdrawal, reaeration and reregulation weirs.
   - *Channel design:* Ecologically sympathetic bank treatment and instream structures.
   - *Fish passage:* Passes have often failed. One Mosel River pass recorded a maximum daily run of 15,572 fish, and Volga elevators pass 1.3 and 3.9 million fish annually.
   - *Stocking and control:* Don River sturgeon stocks increased 15-fold between 1965 and 1977. Water hyacinth control and exotic fish eradication have had mixed success.
5. **Compensation and nonuse:** Compensation (fish ponds, alternative recreation) accepts impacts and can be expensive. Nonuse, or preservation of natural rivers, is justified by genetic resource protection, option value and aesthetic or scientific value.
6. **Third World priorities:** Environmental management must be balanced against food and power needs. Helpful approaches include community involvement, improving the efficiency of existing irrigation (about 50% of irrigated land is saline; only 43% of withdrawn water reaches irrigation), and basin-wide cooperation. The Zambezi Action Plan (19 projects, eight nations) is cited as a model. In Silent Valley, India, local communities halted a hydroelectric dam.
7. **Conclusion:** Ecological management cannot fully restore regulated rivers. Pollution control is often the precondition for other measures, and social and political will are required for implementation.
