# Sewage Fungus in Rivers of the United Kingdom

#microbiology #freshwater-ecology #sewage-fungus

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Curtis]] and [[Harrington]] of the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] examines [[Sewage Fungus]], the cotton-wool-like slimes of attached micro-organisms that form in rivers receiving biodegradable organic pollution. Sites were surveyed in 1968 and 1969 and compared with a 1960 questionnaire. Outbreaks were mostly limited in extent, and their severity was linked to effluents rich in readily degradable carbon. Inadequately treated or overloaded [[Domestic Sewage Effluent]] and several [[Industrial Effluent]] types were the main sources. Nutrients, dissolved oxygen and suspended solids were not strong predictors of outbreak severity. The authors conclude that adequate [[Secondary Biological Treatment]] is a reliable safeguard, with [[Tertiary Polishing Treatment]] as a backup, and that the national situation had probably not deteriorated over the previous decade.

## Background & Description

Sewage fungus is a mixed community of mostly heterotrophic organisms, including bacteria, fungi and protozoa. A filamentous matrix gives the slime its cohesion and holds non-filamentous, usually motile, organisms such as protozoa, diatoms and bacteria. The organisms are probably part of the normal microbial flora of all rivers. They form macroscopic slimes only where significant amounts of biodegradable organic nutrients are available as growth substrate. Laboratory physiology is reasonably well understood, but field occurrence and its relation to effluent type were poorly documented before this work.

Two data sources were used:
- **1960 WPRL Questionnaire:** sent to River Authorities and Conservancies in England and Wales. It returned information on **119 reported outbreaks**. Chemical data were limited to BOD, and the Laboratory did not examine the growths or sites.
- **1968–69 field survey:** **178 sites** were located and visited with the help of River Authorities in England and Wales and River Purification Boards in Scotland. This survey forms the core of the paper.

**Methods highlights:**
- Slime samples were examined for slime-forming organisms, protozoa and diatoms within 24 h of collection.
- Dissolved oxygen was measured in situ, and water temperature, flow and velocity were recorded.
- Water samples were preserved with [[Mercuric Chloride]] solution and analysed for [[Soluble Organic Carbon]], soluble phosphate, ammoniacal and other nitrogen forms, and total carbohydrate. Soluble organic carbon was preferred to [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]] (BOD) because it is more reliable when analysis is delayed.
- Slime severity was scored visually as light, moderate or heavy:
  - **Light:** sparse flocs below 5 g dry wt slime m⁻².
  - **Moderate:** intermediate values.
  - **Heavy:** thick slime covering all unscoured surfaces, at or above 100 g dry wt slime m⁻². Entrapped silt and debris can raise the total to three times this value.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Complaints (1960 questionnaire).** Complaints fell into four groups: appearance and amenity (37.5%), smell and deoxygenation (17.1%), effects of scoured flocs (13.2%) and damage to fish (17.1%). No complaint was made in 14.5% of cases.

**Community composition.** [[Sphaerotilus Natans]] and zoogloeal bacteria (see [[Zoogloea]]) were each present at more than 90% of sites and were dominant or co-dominant at more than 50%. S. natans is generally regarded as the most frequent slime-forming organism. The zoogloeal group is an unresolved taxonomic grouping that may include *Zoogloea ramigera*, *Pseudomonas* spp. and zoogloeal forms of *Sphaerotilus*. Other common constituents were:
- [[Beggiatoa Alba]], [[Carchesium Polypinum]], Flavobacterium sp., diatoms, [[Stigeoclonium Tenue]], [[Leptomitus Lacteus]] and [[Fusarium Aquaeductum]].
- [[Geotrichum Candidum]] was the commonest true fungus but was dominant at only 4.4% of sites. It has not previously been reported as a major constituent of sewage fungus.
- An unnamed filamentous [[Flavobacterium]] species forms long (>50 µm), thin (about 0.5 µm) capsulated rods with xanthophyll pigments that give a pink or orange colour to slimes.

Slime composition did not vary consistently with geographical region.

**Extent of outbreaks.** Most outbreaks were short. Data from River Authorities showed that 73.5% were less than ¼ mile long and a further 11% were ¼–1 mile. In 99% of cases the affected watercourse was 5 miles or less. The 1960 questionnaire showed the same pattern.

**Pollution sources (1968–69).** Pollution at 72% of sites was associated with industrial effluent, domestic sewage, or a combination. These three groups occurred with roughly equal frequency and produced similar proportions of heavy, moderate and light outbreaks. The most frequent sources were:
- Food and drink industries (distilleries, malting, fruit and vegetable canning): 11.2%
- Paper manufacture: 10.1%
- Textile and dyeing: 9.6%
- Piggeries: 6.7%
- Surface run-off and garage washings: 6.7%
- Refuse tips: 4.5%

In the 1960 questionnaire, domestic sewage was linked to 47.1% of outbreaks, industrial effluent to 33.3%, and combined sources to 19.5%. Food and drink industries accounted for 25.1%, with textile (7.6%) and paper (7.6%) following.

**Water quality at outbreak sites.**
- **Soluble organic carbon:** Outbreaks occurred across a wide range. Most sites had 6–20 mg l⁻¹, but 21.3% had less than 6 mg l⁻¹. Severity was not closely correlated with organic carbon, although heavy growths tended to occur in more severely polluted water.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Most sites exceeded 8 mg l⁻¹, and no slimes occurred at zero. These were daytime values, so they may not represent minima.
- **Phosphate:** Values were rarely below 0.02 or above 5.0 mg P l⁻¹. Severity was not correlated with phosphate, organic nitrogen, ammoniacal nitrogen or suspended solids.
- **Suspended solids:** 88% of sites had less than 80 mg l⁻¹.
- **Total soluble carbohydrate** (as glucose equivalents): Values ranged from below 1.1 to 30 mg l⁻¹, with most sites between 1.1 and 8.0 mg l⁻¹.

**Carbohydrate and a 1963 random survey.** In a 1963 survey of 66 randomly chosen rivers, slime occurrence rose with carbohydrate content. Rivers with 0–5 mg l⁻¹ had slime at 9.2% (54 rivers), 5–10 mg l⁻¹ at 20% (5 rivers), 15–20 mg l⁻¹ at 66% (3 rivers), and 20–30 and 30–40 mg l⁻¹ at 100% (2 and 1 rivers). One river at 46.5 mg l⁻¹ lacked slime, possibly because industrial effluents contained toxic materials.

**Single-discharge sites.** Where an unpolluted river received one effluent, the authors compared water above and below the outfall:
- **Domestic sewage (7 sites):** Organic and inorganic nutrient loadings increased.
- **Paper mills (5 sites):** Mainly organic content increased, including soluble organic carbon, organic nitrogen and soluble carbohydrate.

In both groups, daytime dissolved oxygen stayed high and outbreak severity was similar.

**Water velocity.** Outbreaks were most common at 16–60 cm s⁻¹. The distribution by velocity class was:

| Velocity (cm s⁻¹) | Sites | % of total |
|---|---|---|
| <6 | 6 | 3.5 |
| 6–15 | 31 | 17.9 |
| 16–30 | 63 | 36.4 |
| 31–60 | 58 | 33.5 |
| 61–90 | 15 | 8.7 |

The authors attribute this to high velocities preventing attachment, and to very low velocities limiting nutrient transfer except in grossly polluted water.

## Key Findings

1. **Treatment is the decisive control.** Outbreaks were strongly associated with inadequate or overloaded [[Sewage Treatment Works]]. Of 55 sewage-fungus occurrences linked to domestic sewage, most were from inadequately treated or overloaded works. Only 4 occurrences came from works with adequate secondary treatment, and all of these were light. No effluent had received tertiary treatment. Adequate secondary treatment appears to be a reliable safeguard. Tertiary "polishing" is likely to help where secondary treatment is insufficient.

2. **Carbon, not nutrients, drives growth.** Slime growth depends mainly on available carbon. The phosphate content above effluents (0.04–0.05 mg P l⁻¹) was already adequate for heavy growth, so additional phosphorus from sewage is unlikely to be important. Vitamins are probably not limiting, except that *Sphaerotilus* needs trace vitamin B₁₂.

3. **BOD and organic carbon are imperfect but useful indicators.** BOD and soluble organic carbon correlated approximately at domestic sewage sites. Heavy outbreaks were especially associated with BOD of 5–30 mg l⁻¹ (the Royal Commission's doubtful and bad classes) and organic carbon of 6–30 mg l⁻¹. Outbreaks were rare where BOD or organic carbon exceeded 30, and where they occurred they were only light or moderate, possibly because of toxic materials. The [[Royal Commission On Sewage Disposal]] (1912) had already found overlap between BOD values of its very clean and bad categories.

4. **Effluent types with degradable carbon are most implicated.** Food and drink effluents contain sugars, organic acids and amino acids. Paper effluents contain mainly starch and some soluble wood polysaccharides. Textile effluents contain starch, organic acids and esters. Domestic sewage contains sugars and organic acids before treatment.

5. **Ammoniacal nitrogen and suspended solids.** Ammoniacal nitrogen was at least 0.5 mg l⁻¹ in 75% of samples, above the [[World Health Organization]] drinking-water limit of 0.4 mg l⁻¹. In 30% of samples it exceeded 5 mg l⁻¹. Suspended solids exceeded the 80 mg l⁻¹ level recommended by the [[European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission]] for fisheries in only 12% of samples. Neither variable was clearly linked to outbreak severity.

6. **Dissolved oxygen is not a key factor.** In 89% of samples, dissolved oxygen exceeded 4 mg l⁻¹. This closely matches the 90% of river miles in the national survey by [[Owens]] and colleagues (1970). Slime organisms grow at both low and high oxygen levels, and only complete deoxygenation seems to matter. Respiration by the slime appears to be offset by reaeration. The [[National Technical Advisory Committee]] of the United States recommends more than 5 mg l⁻¹ to protect diverse aquatic communities, but no European oxygen standards existed at the time.

7. **Temporal trend.** The authors caution that the 1960 and 1968–69 results cannot be compared directly. Nevertheless, they conclude that the sewage-fungus situation in UK rivers had probably not deteriorated over the previous decade.

8. **Reported impacts.** The main complaints were aesthetic and olfactory, but damage to fish (17.1% of complaints in 1960) and deoxygenation were also important consequences.
