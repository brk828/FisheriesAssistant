# The Brown Trout in Maine

## Executive Summary
This 1954 Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Game report by Carll N. Fenderson evaluates the ecological and fisheries impact of introduced [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) in Maine, comparing it directly against the native [[Landlocked Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*). Brown trout were first introduced to Maine in 1885 (Branch Lake, Ellsworth) via eggs sourced from Loch Leven, Scotland, and propagated extensively by the U.S. Fish Commission as a perceived "cure-all" for declining native salmon fisheries. A two-year intensive study of [[Branch Lake]] (Ellsworth, Maine) — supplemented by creel census data from [[Webb Lake]] (Weld, Maine) — found that brown trout provide a poor return to anglers relative to stocking investment, tend to displace native salmon from spawning tributaries, and create an "overbalanced" population of large, uncatchable, highly cannibalistic fish that suppress overall lake productivity. Part II of the report establishes reliable morphological field characters (vomerine teeth, tail shape, adipose fin coloration) for distinguishing brown trout from landlocked salmon, addressing widespread field misidentification.

## Background & Description
Comparative analysis of distinguishing traits between [[Brown Trout]] and [[Landlocked Salmon]]:

### Reliable Characters
| Character | Brown Trout | Landlocked Salmon |
|---|---|---|
| **Vomerine Teeth** | Strongly to moderately developed, double zig-zag row; sharp, difficult to break | Weakly developed single row, deciduous, or shaft toothless |
| **Tail Shape (Spread)** | Square to fan-shaped in larger fish (>16 in); never deeply forked; rounded lobes | Usually forked, nearly square in some older fish; deeply forked in juveniles with pointed lobes |
| **Adipose Fin Color** | Fringed/spotted orange or red, fading with age | Slate grey to olive; never orange or red |

### General Coloration
- **Brown Trout**: Yellowish-brown body; large brown/black spots on sides, back, and dorsal fin, often haloed; scattered red/orange spots on sides.
- **Salmon**: Silvery sides, bluish back; black spots often X-shaped on adults; red spots typically restricted to juveniles or breeding males.

### Non-Diagnostic Characters (No Significant Statistical Difference)
- Number of branchiostegals (left side)
- Ratio of pectoral fin length to standard length
- Number of anal fin rays

### Characters with Significant but Unreliable Variability (not usable for single-specimen ID)
- Number of scales in oblique row (posterior adipose base to lateral line)
- Number of gill rakers, first arch, left side
- Ratio of maxillary length to standard length
- Ratio of caudal peduncle depth to standard length

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Spawning habitat**: Stream spawner; requires clean, cool tributary streams with rubble/gravel riffle bottoms. Redds may span 20–30 ft of stream bottom, with egg pits up to 12 inches deep.
- **Reproductive behavior**: Females excavate multiple egg pits; males defend territories pre-spawning; eggs and milt extruded simultaneously; natural fertilization success >90%. Large females may lay over 3,000 eggs.
- **Spawning season**: Late October through February, temperature/flow dependent.
- **Juvenile life history**: First 2–3 years spent in streams feeding on insects/invertebrates before moving to lake habitat and shifting to piscivorous diet.
- **Adult diet**: Strong preference for smelt (shared with native salmon); highly cannibalistic; diet composition tracks prey availability.
- **Thermal tolerance**: Adapts to warmer waters better than native salmonids, but remains fully competitive in cold-water habitats — creating direct competition with native [[Brook Trout]] and [[Landlocked Salmon]] in cold streams and lakes.
- **Interspecific dynamics**: Documented displacement of native landlocked salmon from key spawning tributaries (e.g., [[Winkumpaugh Stream]], previously a major egg source for the former Green Lake Federal Hatchery, now used exclusively by brown trout).
- **Population structure**: Tends toward "overbalanced" populations dominated by large, older, wary individuals that evade angling pressure — mirrored in California's [[Convict Lake]], described as suffering a "burden on the biological economy."

## Key Findings
1. **Introduction History**: Brown trout first introduced to Maine in 1885 at Branch Lake (~7,000 fry from Loch Leven, Scotland stock); stocked in 16 lakes between Bangor and the coast by 1900; stocking intensified in recent decades despite poor returns.
2. **Poor Angler Returns**: Michigan river study found only 0.4 brown trout caught per fish remaining at season's end, vs. 3.0 for brook trout. Maine's Belgrade pond studies found a ~5:1 ratio favoring brook trout catches over brown trout.
3. **Displacement of Native Salmon**: At Webb Lake (Weld), despite salmon stocking discontinued since 1940 and brown trout stocked continuously since 1947, salmon still comprised 72% of the cold-water gamefish catch (1953 creel census) — demonstrating the value of natural reproduction over continued brown trout stocking.
4. **Branch Lake Case Study**: Despite 752,200 brown trout stocked over the prior decade (90% of all brown trout ever stocked in the lake), the brown trout catch declined from its 1933 peak, while [[Lake Trout]] (Togue, *Cristivomer namaycush*) — established via a single 1899 introduction and sustained purely by natural reproduction — came to dominate the catch in the last 13 years of records (1929–1953 boat landing data).
5. **Management Conclusion**: Brown trout represent a poor fisheries investment; their combination with salmon in the same lake is undesirable, reducing the effectiveness of a native [[Landlocked Salmon]]–[[Lake Trout]] management combination. Recommendation: cease brown trout introductions into waters capable of supporting native salmonids; focus management on improving habitat conditions for native species.
6. **Identification Utility**: Vomerine teeth structure is the single most reliable diagnostic character between brown trout and landlocked salmon, followed by tail shape (especially in fish >16 inches) and adipose fin coloration.
