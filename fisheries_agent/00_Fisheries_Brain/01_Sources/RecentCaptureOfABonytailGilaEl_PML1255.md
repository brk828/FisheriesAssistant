# Recent Capture of a Bonytail (*Gila elegans*) and Observations on a Nearly Extinct Cyprinid from the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This short note, published in *Copeia* (1986, No. 4, pp. 1021–1023), reports the recent capture of a mature female [[Bonytail]] from the [[Black Rocks Area]] of the [[Colorado River]] in western Colorado, in July 1984. The bonytail is nearly extinct across the Colorado River basin. The authors document the fish's measurements, the expert opinions used to confirm its identity, and the other *Gila* captured at the same site. They also review historical records, the possibility of hybridization with [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]], and the causes of the species' decline. Their central conclusion is that the capture shows bonytail persist in the upper basin, although their scarcity suggests a viable population may no longer exist. Altered temperature regimes alone do not explain the decline.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), a cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River basin.
- **Status:** The aquatic ecosystems of the basin have been profoundly altered by water development, introduction of non-native fishes, poor land-use practices, and other human activities. The bonytail is now near extinction.
- **Historical abundance:** Early workers described bonytail as widespread and abundant in large rivers, including the [[Green River]], the Gunnison River, and their tributaries.
- **Recent abundance:**
  - [[Vanicek And Kramer 1969 Study|Vanicek and Kramer (1969)]] collected 67 bonytail (>200 mm TL) from the Green River during 1964–66, the last report of appreciable numbers in riverine habitat.
  - In recent years, fewer than ten presumed bonytail have been captured from the Green River.
  - About 25 bonytail have been collected from [[Lake Mohave]], a lower Colorado River reservoir. These fish are large and old and apparently not reproducing.

### Specimen Captured (July 1984)

The capture was made with a trammel net at the Black Rocks Area, a 2 km reach that is a major habitat for humpback chub. The reach is narrow, deep, and turbulent, formed where the river passes through erosion-resistant metamorphic rock. This setting differs from the broad, shallow reaches of the Gunnison and Green rivers, where bonytail were historically collected.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Identification and Hybridization Concerns

- Morphological characters do not clearly separate young bonytail from other sympatric *Gila*, so the reproductive success of Green River bonytail is unknown.
- Six ichthyologists who had worked extensively on *Gila* taxonomy reviewed the photograph and measurements. Four of five respondents identified the fish as a bonytail. The fifth judged it largely bonytail but noted a ventral mouth overhung by the snout, which may indicate some humpback chub genes.
- Two respondents mentioned a "Roman nose" or fleshy head hump, which could suggest humpback chub ancestry. Neither changed his identification, and one attributed these traits to individual variation that is especially pronounced in old fish.
- Recent artificial hybridization experiments showed that gene exchange among these *Gila* is possible (Hamman, 1981).
- Presumed bonytail × humpback chub intergrades have been reported from [[Lake Powell]], about 230 km downstream, and from the Green River (Holden and Stalnaker, 1970).

### Co-occurring Fishes at the Black Rocks Area

The trammel net that caught the bonytail also captured 602 adult-size (>250 mm TL) *Gila*. Based on gross morphology, the authors tentatively identified:

- 272 as humpback chub
- 221 as roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*)
- 69 as possible intergrades or hybrids

These tallies sum to 562 of the 602 fish, so the text may contain an OCR or reporting gap. Some humpback and roundtail chub were in reproductive condition, with expressible ova and milt.

### Age and Size

- The largest bonytail seen by Vanicek and Kramer (1969) was 388 mm TL, estimated at 7 years from scale annuli.
- Seven bonytail collected by Bozek et al. (1984) from Lake Mohave measured 475–535 mm TL.
- The largest bonytail reported by Minckley (1973) from [[Lake Havasu]] measured 499 mm TL.
- Otolith marks on large Lake Mohave bonytail suggest some fish may be more than 30 years old (W. L. Minckley, pers. comm.).
- The present specimen was therefore large and probably old.

### Causes of Decline

The decline of bonytail and other native southwestern fishes has been attributed to:

- altered discharge and temperature regimes downstream of dams and diversions
- conversion of riverine habitat to reservoir habitat
- introduction of non-native fishes
- altered water quality

Severe dewatering is an obvious detrimental effect. The only other effect demonstrated is harm from cold hypolimnetic releases from dams:

- After the 1962 closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], Vanicek and Kramer (1969) found no age-0 bonytail or roundtail chub in the Green River between the dam and the Yampa River confluence in 1964 and 1966. Those were years of sharply reduced temperatures from large hypolimnetic discharges.
- Later work by Hamman (1982) and Marsh (1985) showed that embryo survival is appreciably reduced by the low water temperatures evident below the dam, and that these conditions continue today.

The authors argue that thermal alteration alone does not explain the decline in the upper basin:

- Unlike the Green River, the Colorado River above its confluence with the Green has no large mainstream dams.
- Analyses of U.S. Geological Survey records showed that dam operation has not reduced Colorado River temperatures in the reaches where bonytail persist.
- Factors other than thermal regimes must therefore have important effects. These may include non-native species, their synergistic effects with other stressors, and changes in discharge regimes that alter spawning and early life stages.

## Key Findings

1. **Bonytail persist in the upper Colorado River basin.** A mature female was captured in July 1984 in the Black Rocks Area, western Colorado.
2. **Identity was confirmed by expert consensus.** Four of five consulted *Gila* specialists identified the fish as a bonytail. The fifth noted possible humpback chub ancestry.
3. **The population is probably not viable.** The authors state that bonytail scarcity suggests a viable population might not exist.
4. **Hybridization is a growing threat.** Few bonytail remain, which increases the likelihood that they will interbreed with more abundant humpback and roundtail chub. Intergrades have been reported from Lake Powell and the Green River.
5. **Thermal regimes are not the sole cause of decline.** Dam operations have not reduced temperatures in the upper Colorado River, so other factors, including non-native species and altered flows, are implicated.
6. **Long-lived individuals remain.** Bonytail collected from reservoirs are large and possibly more than 30 years old, but apparently not reproducing.
7. **Research needs:** The authors note that the reproductive success of Green River bonytail is unknown. Field identification is unreliable because morphology does not clearly separate young bonytail from other *Gila*.
