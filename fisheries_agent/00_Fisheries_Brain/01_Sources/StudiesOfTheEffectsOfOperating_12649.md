# Studies of the Effects of Operating the Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant on Twin Lakes, Colorado: 1980 Report of Findings

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #baseline-monitoring #pumped-storage-impacts

## Executive Summary

This report presents comprehensive limnological data collected from [[Twin Lakes]], Colorado during 1980 as part of ongoing pre-operational baseline studies prior to the August 1981 commissioning of the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]]. The study, a cooperative effort among the Bureau of Reclamation, Colorado Division of Wildlife, and Colorado State University, documents physical, chemical, and biological parameters across both the upper and lower lakes. Twin Lakes are located at 2802 m elevation in the upper Arkansas River Valley; the lower lake (736.5 ha, 27 m maximum depth) is Colorado's largest natural mountain lake, while the upper lake covers 263.4 ha with 28 m maximum depth. Data collection included temperature profiles, dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity, major ions, trace metals (copper, zinc, iron, manganese, lead), plant nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus species, silica), primary productivity using ¹⁴C methods, chlorophyll a concentrations, phytoplankton and zooplankton abundance and composition, and benthic macroinvertebrate surveys. The lakes remained ice-free for 208 days (May 15–December 9) and thermally stratified for approximately 148 days. Both lakes are characterized as oligotrophic with extremely low phosphorus concentrations (<5 µg/L), calcium bicarbonate/calcium sulfate water chemistry, and low conductivity (62–90 µS/cm). The aquatic community includes introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*), and [[Mysis Shrimp]] (*Mysis relicta*), the latter introduced in 1957 as forage. This baseline characterization will enable assessment of powerplant operational impacts through comparison with post-operational monitoring planned through 1984.

## Background & Description

[[Twin Lakes]] originated through morainic damming of Lake Creek on the eastern front of the Sawatch Range in central Colorado. The lakes have undergone significant anthropogenic modifications over the past century, including installation of outlet control works, channel dredging between the two lakes, construction of the new Twin Lakes Dam (operational use beginning late 1980), and biological introductions. The upper lake functions as a sediment catch basin, receiving turbid, heavy metal-laden runoff from Lake Creek, particularly during peak flows in June-July. Natural flows are augmented by diverted western slope water via the Fryingpan-Arkansas Project.

The study employed standardized methods across 16–18 field surveys during 1980. Physical-chemical measurements used a Hydro-lab Corporation System 8000 multiparameter probe for temperature, dissolved oxygen, conductivity, pH, and oxidation-reduction potential. Water samples from surface, mid-depth, and bottom were analyzed for major ions, trace metals (preserved with concentrated nitric acid), and plant nutrients (frozen immediately) following National Handbook procedures. Light penetration was measured with Secchi disk and limnophotometer. Primary productivity was determined using radioactive carbon (¹⁴C) uptake during peak daylight hours. Chlorophyll samples were filtered through Millipore glass pads and analyzed following Parsons and Strickland (1963) methods. Plankton were collected with closing nets (No. 20 silk, 0.076 mm mesh) via vertical hauls and preserved in 2% formalin. Benthic samples were collected with Ekman dredge, screened through U.S. Standard Series No. 30 sieve (0.589 mm opening), and preserved in 10% formalin for laboratory enumeration and biomass determination.

Newly installed Hydrolab Corporation meteorological-limnological monitoring instrumentation on anchored rafts at five stations began operation in 1980, measuring underwater parameters (temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, redox) at multiple depths and meteorological parameters (air temperature, windspeed, light) at selected stations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Twin Lakes exemplifies a high-elevation oligotrophic system strongly influenced by seasonal runoff patterns, thermal stratification dynamics, and introduced species. The lakes were ice-free for 208 days in 1980 (May 15–December 9), the shortest ice-free period in 9 years of study due to prolonged cold weather in late April-early May. Ice formation occurred December 9, the latest date recorded during the study period, due to mild early winter conditions. Thermal stratification persisted approximately 148 days (late May to late October), with peak stratification in early August when surface temperatures reached 18°C in the lower lake to 3 m depth. The epilimnion remained above 16°C from late June to early September. Fall turnover occurred in late October, later than the typical second week of October.

The upper lake experiences severe ecological constraints during runoff. Monthly inflow volumes from Lake Creek ranged from <2 million m³ (January–April) to 75 million m³ in June 1980, representing nearly 40 times the winter baseline. This concentrated June runoff brings substantial nutrient loads but also heavy turbidity and heavy metals (iron, manganese, copper, lead, zinc) from natural mineralized sources above 3600 m elevation in the South Fork watershed. The upper lake functions as a settling basin, trapping sediments and heavy metals while allowing nutrient-enriched but clearer water to flow to the lower lake. This results in the upper lake being 1–2 months behind the lower lake in biological succession following spring turnover.

Light availability varied substantially with 5-day average ambient light flux ranging from 44 g-cal/(cm²·h) in mid-July to 22 g-cal/(cm²·h) in mid-September (nearly 50% reduction from theoretical maximum due to cloud cover). Light extinction coefficients ranged from 0.26–0.44 m⁻¹ in the lower lake and 0.33–1.01 m⁻¹ in the upper lake, with maximum turbidity during peak runoff. Wind velocities averaged 10 km/h (May–November), with highest velocities (12.8 km/h average) during late October–early November coinciding with fall turnover, enhancing mixing and nutrient redistribution from sediments.

Water chemistry reflects the oligotrophic status. Total dissolved solids ranged 24–130 mg/L, lowest during peak runoff and highest during low-flow periods. Bicarbonate alkalinity was extremely low (17–24 mg/L in upper lake, 19–21 mg/L in lower lake), approximately half the world average for freshwaters. The poorly buffered water (conductivity <90 µS/cm) exhibited large pH variations (6.3–8.4) driven by biological activity and the carbonate-bicarbonate shift. Phosphorus was severely limiting with orthophosphate-P and total P concentrations always <5 µg/L, categorizing Twin Lakes as ultraoligotrophic by Likens (1975) criteria. Orthophosphate was detectable (>1 µg/L) 42% of the time in 1980 versus <1% in 1979, while total P was detectable only 15% of the time versus 30% in 1979.

Nitrogen dynamics showed clear transformation patterns. Nitrate-N concentrations were significantly higher in inflow (up to 180 µg/L) than in the lakes, decreasing progressively downstream. An estimated 15.5 × 10³ kg nitrate-N was imported via Lake Creek in 1980, with only 1.8 × 10³ kg exported, representing a net decrease of 13.7 × 10³ kg. This inorganic nitrogen was converted to organic forms, reflected in increased Total Kjeldahl Nitrogen (TKN) from 65 µg/L in inflow to 135 µg/L average in the lakes (maximum 367 µg/L in lower lake in February). Ammonia-N was most abundant during spring and fall turnover (up to 90 µg/L), generated by heterotrophic bacteria at the sediment-water interface. Silica concentrations (4.1–6.7 mg/L) were sufficient to not limit phytoplankton growth, showing no depletion during productive periods.

Heavy metal concentrations, while elevated due to natural mineralization, did not reach toxic levels in 1980. Iron concentrations were greatest in inflow, especially pre-runoff, with occasional elevated outflow concentrations due to dam construction activities. The potential exists for metal toxicity if anoxic conditions develop in the hypolimnion, converting metals to ionic forms, as occurred in late winter 1975 when the food chain was significantly reduced and required nearly 3 years to recover.

The introduced [[Mysis Relicta]] population, established in 1957 as forage for [[Lake Trout]], has profoundly altered the zooplankton community structure. Cladocerans, which comprised 20–45% of adult zooplankton in 1902–03 (Juday 1906), now constitute <1% of the population (maximum 5% in upper lake, August 1980), with only small-bodied *Bosmina* sp. persisting. Larger-bodied *Daphnia* spp., abundant in nearby waters including the newly constructed forebay, are absent from Twin Lakes due to mysis predation. This represents a classic example of trophic cascade effects from species introduction, similar to patterns documented in [[Lake Tahoe]] following mysis introduction (Goldman et al. 1979).

## Key Findings

**Physical-Chemical Regime (1980):**
- Ice-free period: 208 days (May 15–December 9), shortest in 9-year study; ice formation latest on record due to mild early winter
- Thermal stratification: ~148 days (late May–late October); peak stratification early August with surface temperatures 18°C (lower lake), 17.7°C (upper lake)
- Stratification stronger than previous years, especially in upper lake; hypolimnion remained cool (lower lake <9°C, upper lake <6°C year-round), providing optimal habitat for [[Lake Trout]] (prefer ≤10°C)
- Fall turnover occurred late October, ~2 weeks later than typical
- Average monthly water temperatures ~1°C warmer during ice-covered months than 1974–79 average due to early ice formation in fall 1979 before supercooling
- Dissolved oxygen: maximum 12 mg/L under ice (winter); minimum 1.3 mg/L (lower lake, October, ~15% saturation); concentrations <3 mg/L in lower lake hypolimnion late August–late October
- pH range: 6.3–8.4, reflecting poorly buffered water; lowest values in hypolimnion during stratification; highest during maximum stratification (September–October)
- Conductivity: 62–90 µS/cm (upper lake), 66–88 µS/cm (lower lake); 1980 averages (74 and 77 µS/cm) slightly higher than 1974–79 (68 and 74 µS/cm) but not statistically significant
- Oxidation-reduction potential: 350–550 mV at lake bottom; entire water column remained in oxidizing state throughout year (no approach to reducing conditions unlike some previous years)

**Meteorological Influences:**
- Ambient light flux: 5-day averages ranged 22–44 g-cal/(cm²·h) (June–November); maximum mid-July; extreme variability with up to 50% shading from cloud cover
- Wind velocity: 5-day averages 8–14.5 km/h (May–November); mean 10 km/h; highest velocities late October–early November during fall turnover (average 12.8 km/h), enhancing mixing
- Light extinction coefficients: lower lake 0.26–0.44 m⁻¹ (somewhat less turbid than normal during ice-free season); upper lake 0.33–1.01 m⁻¹ (maximum turbidity during runoff)
- Secchi depth: lower lake 3.0–6.3 m; upper lake 2.0–6.2 m

**Hydrologic and Chemical Inputs:**
- Total annual inflow volume via [[Lake Creek]]: close to 10-year average but more concentrated in June; May, July, August 15–50% lower than 1971–80 average
- Monthly inflow pattern: <2 million m³ (January–April) → 15 million m³ (May) → 75 million m³ (June, peak) → 25 million m³ (July) → <5 million m³ (August–September)
- Total dissolved solids: 24–130 mg/L; lowest during peak runoff, highest during low flows; generally decreased downstream indicating settling in lakes
- Bicarbonate alkalinity: upper lake 17–24 mg/L, lower lake 19–21 mg/L (extremely low, ~50% of world freshwater average)
- Phosphorus (severely limiting): orthophosphate-P and total P always <5 µg/L; orthophosphate detectable 42% of time (vs. <1% in 1979); total P detectable 15% of time (vs. 30% in 1979)
- Nitrogen budget: 15.5 × 10³ kg nitrate-N imported, 1.8 × 10³ kg exported (net decrease 13.7 × 10³ kg); inorganic nitrate converted to organic forms (TKN increased from 65 µg/L inflow to 135 µg/L in lakes, maximum 367 µg/L)
- Ammonia-N: maximum 90 µg/L; most abundant during spring and fall turnover when heterotrophic bacterial activity at sediment-water interface is mixed into water column
- Silica: 4.1–6.7 mg/L; sufficient to not limit phytoplankton; no depletion during productive periods
- Heavy metals: iron, manganese, copper, lead, zinc elevated in inflow from natural mineralized sources above 3600 m; upper lake functions as settling basin; metals remain in particulate state under oxic conditions; potential for toxicity if anoxic conditions develop in hypolimnion (as occurred in 1975)

**Primary Productivity:**
- Lower lake areal productivity: 10.6–143.5 mg C/(m²·d); mean 76.4 mg C/(m²·d); annual productivity ~22 g C/(m²·yr)
- Upper lake areal productivity: 1.6–57.9 mg C/(m²·d); mean 19.6 mg C/(m²·d); annual productivity ~9 g C/(m²·yr) (severely depressed by turbid runoff)
- Maximum instantaneous rate: 13.8 mg C/(m²·h) on November 4 (lower lake); highest rates typically occur during fall turnover when nutrients mixed into photic zone
- Productivity often greatest at depth below surface (in/near thermocline) due to light inhibition at surface and increased nutrient availability at top of thermocline
- Seasonal pattern: low winter (ice/snow cover limits light), increase after spring turnover, depression during peak runoff (upper lake), midsummer low (stratification seals nutrients in hypolimnion), fall resurgence (turnover redistributes nutrients)
- Both lakes categorized as oligotrophic based on productivity; upper lake influenced by heavy spring runoff acting as "cushion" for lower lake

**Phytoplankton:**
- Lower lake: 28–13,007 cells/L; mean 2,717 cells/L
- Upper lake: 1–997 cells/L; mean 314 cells/L (almost 9× lower than lower lake)
- Dominant taxa: *Synedra* and *Asterionella* (diatoms) most of year; *Dinobryon* (golden-brown) abundant midsummer to early winter when phosphorus most limiting
- Species shift in 1980: *Synedra* especially abundant in lower lake, apparently displacing *Asterionella* (similar nutrient requirements) and *Dinobryon* (lower phosphorus tolerance); likely related to higher epilimnion temperatures in 1980
- Other species increased in 1980: green algae *Dictyosphaerium* and *Spherocystis*, golden-brown *Mallomonas* (made up ~50% of population in upper lake during February, April, September)
- Blue-green *Oscillatoria* (enrichment indicator) not detected in 1980, positive indication of oligotrophic status
- Diatom *Tabellaria* present late June–November in both lakes but not in large concentrations

**Chlorophyll a:**
- Lower lake: 0.6–5.3 mg/m³; mean 3.0 mg/m³
- Upper lake: 0.3–7.9 mg/m³; mean 3.6 mg/m³ (17% higher than lower lake, opposite of previous years)
- Seasonal pattern: low concentrations late winter–early summer; maximum September–November when thermocline sinking exposes more bottom to turnover
- Phytoplankton concentrated in/around thermocline during stratification; dispersed throughout water column during isothermal periods
- Classification: both lakes on border between oligotrophic (0.3–3.0 mg/m³) and mesotrophic (2–15 mg/m³) by Likens (1975) criteria

**Zooplankton:**
- Lower lake: 8.6–125.8 individuals/L; mean 46 individuals/L (similar to 1979: 47 individuals/L)
- Upper lake: 1.4–65.9 individuals/L; mean 23 individuals/L (nearly 2× higher than 1979: 13 individuals/L)
- Dominant taxa: copepods (nauplii, *Cyclops*, *Diaptomus*), rotifers (*Kellicotia*, *Keratella*, *Polyarthra*, *Branchionus
