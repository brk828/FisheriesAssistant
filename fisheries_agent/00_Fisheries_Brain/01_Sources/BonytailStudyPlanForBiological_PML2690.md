# Bonytail Study Plan and Biological Investigation Protocols

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bonytail

## Executive Summary

This draft study plan sets out standardized protocols for collecting, tagging, sampling, culturing, handling, transporting, and disposing of [[Bonytail]], a threatened and endangered fish. It was prepared in response to a memorandum from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Southwest Regional Director, which required study plans to be in place before a recommendation for granting an [[Endangered Species Permit]]. Twelve agencies and institutions are named as cooperators, and the plan is to be reviewed and accepted by a designated [[Lead Station]] before any permittee begins work. The document is a draft dated 6/29/95, and it contains many blank fields (marked "_") for values still to be supplied.

## Background & Description

The plan is organized as a set of linked components:

- **Administrative checklist:** confirms planning protocols, investigator contacts, lines of communication, schedules, permits, experimental design, and [[Fish Health Center]] review.
- **Biological procedures:** governs collection, tagging, sampling, culture, handling, and transport of bonytail, with the stated aim of reducing stressors, their severity and duration, plasma-ion disturbance, and increases in metabolic rate.
- **Personnel and training:** requires minimum qualifications for collection, tagging, culture, and transport staff. Untrained individuals are classed as trainees and must work under direct supervision of a qualified biologist.
- **Fish culture and broodstock management:** covers facilities, water quality, feeding, spawning, egg and larval rearing, and health plans.
- **Fish disposal:** addresses incidental take, voucher specimens, and surplus production.
- **Fish health:** requires risk assessments for any introduction to a hatchery or reintroduction to the wild.
- **Field, tagging, genetic, and truck checklists:** equipment and readiness lists for each activity.
- **Stress physiology appendix:** explains the rationale behind the transport and handling rules.
- **Memorandum and guidelines:** assign responsibilities to investigators, lead stations, the Fish Health Center, and the Regional Endangered Species Office.

The stated purpose of the protocols is to reduce the risk of bonytail loss. Bonytail are described as "extremely sensitive to handling," and losses have occurred in the past from a lack of stress-relieving procedures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Handling and holding water.** Holding water is to contain 0.5% [[Salt]] (18.9 g per gallon), 0.26 mL/L [[Stress Coat]], 10 mg/L [[Furacin]], and 25–100 mg/L [[MS-222]]. The document gives MS-222 values of 25–100 mg/L for holding, 100 mg/L for measurements and genetic sampling, and 25 mg/L for hauling. Other limits:

- Water temperature no more than 55 °F, and kept the same or slightly lower than host water.
- Dissolved oxygen above 6.0 mg/L, maintained at saturation.
- Fish out of water for a maximum of 30 seconds, and kept wet.

**Collection.** Collection must be nonlethal. The main rules are:

- [[Trammel Net]] checks at least every four hours. Nets must be free of creosote and dried algae, and the whole net is checked for bonytail before other fish are removed. Bonytail are cut from nets rather than pulled.
- No bonytail may be taken in gill nets.
- [[Electrofishing]] is DC only, at no more than 1.0 amp, with settings based on local water quality. Field teams measure hardness, pH, conductivity, and temperature beforehand. The plan cites Snyder (1992) on electrofishing impacts.
- Rod and reel and toxicants (poisons) are prohibited.
- Holding containers must be at least 30 gallons, with density not above 100 g of fish per liter.

**Hauling.** Transport densities and conditions differ by method:

| Method | Maximum density | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Truck transport | 30 g/L | Oxygen at saturation (>6.0 mg/L); food withheld 24 h for fish under 10 g, 48 h for larger fingerlings and broodfish |
| Box and bag shipping | 30 g/L | Two bags per box, oxygen-inflated, sealed with dorking rings |
| Ice chest (cooler) | 20 g/L | Battery aerators; spare aerator and oxygen cylinders carried |

Dissolved oxygen and temperature are to be checked at least every 30 minutes during transport. The plan requires 12 hours of back-up aeration or oxygen to prevent loss during vehicle breakdown.

**Acclimatization and hatchery conditions.** Following transfer, holding water is to contain 0.5% salt for at least two days. Other hatchery conditions include:

- Water hardness 150–300 mg/L.
- pH 6.5–7.5.
- Turnover of 3–5 per hour.
- Photoperiod of about 12 h dark and 12 h light.
- Gas saturation below 102–103%.
- Disinfection by [[UV Irradiation]] or ozone where parasites are found.

**Stress physiology.** The appendix explains that severe stressors (such as overcrowding, entanglement, large temperature changes, and crushing) can kill quickly. Mild stressors acting together cause a "stress response" with increased metabolic rate and immune suppression. Deaths can then follow several days to weeks later. Anesthesia is recommended to reduce metabolic rate, but over-anesthetized fish may die.

**Broodstock and culture.** Bonytail are to be reared separately from [[Rainbow Trout]] facilities. Adult fish are held in earthen ponds or cement raceways, or in covered tanks, at a density stated as "not to exceed 10 mg/L" in the document. Broodfish are fed Biodiet with spirulina flakes, plus young-of-year rainbow trout twice annually. Feeding is 1% daily for broodfish and 10% daily for fry and fingerlings. Spawning uses hormone injection with LHRHa, and [[Cryopreservation of Sperm]] is included, with frozen sperm thawed at 100 °F. Blank fields in the spawning and incubation sections (dark/light photoperiods, temperatures, densities, and hormone doses) must be filled in before approval.

**Sampling and genetics.** Tagging and sampling are nonlethal and performed only after acclimatization. Key details:

- [[Pit Tagging]] places tags in the abdomen, medial to a pectoral fin.
- Fin clips are cauterized and treated with Betadine.
- Genetic samples include a 4 mm muscle plug from the shoulder (two if over 250 mm TL), a fin clip, one to two mL of caudal-vein blood in EDTA buffer, and gametes.
- Tissue samples are not taken from live fish below the stated minimum TL.
- Dead fish are frozen, with gonads, heart, liver, spleen, kidney, muscle, and otoliths collected, and the carcass shipped to [[Arizona State University]].

**Fish health.** Any introduction to a hatchery, or reintroduction into the wild, requires a [[Risk Assessment]] by a Fish Health Biologist. Fish are to be quarantined until that assessment is complete. Pathogen treatments may need [[INAD Exemption]]s for compounds such as nitrofurazone and praziquantel. The Regional Director has indicated these may be granted case by case for bonytail.

## Key Findings

1. **Study plans precede permits.** Permits are not to be granted until a study plan, with complete administrative and experimental design components, is approved by the lead station. Overdue final reports also block permit renewal.

2. **Strict stress-reduction standards apply.** Bonytail are very sensitive to handling. The plan prohibits photography out of water and "show & tell" handling, and it sets limits on out-of-water time (30 seconds), oxygen (above 6.0 mg/L), temperature, and density.

3. **Nonlethal methods are required.** Collection, tagging, and sampling must be nonlethal. Gill nets, rod and reel, and toxicants are prohibited. Dead fish are salvaged for tissues and voucher specimens.

4. **Training is mandatory.** Only qualified personnel may work independently with bonytail. Trainees require direct supervision. Records of each tagged fish, including length, weight, condition, tag type, and placement, must be kept.

5. **Transport requires preparation.** Detailed arrangements with receiving facilities, oxygen monitoring, back-up aeration, and written checklists are required. The most stressful phase is often loading.

6. **Surplus fish are a management liability.** Surplus bonytail are described as costly to maintain and a potential disease source. They are to be euthanized with MS-222 at 500 mg/L unless kept as voucher specimens. Surplus fish are not to be released into historical habitats.

7. **Disposal follows formal guidelines.** Incidental-take and voucher specimens go to the Arizona State University ichthyological museum, where they become university property. Disposal follows modified guidelines from ASIH, AFS, and AIFRB. Once bonytail are downlisted to threatened status, excess fish may be stocked in suitable non-recovery habitat or used for public education.

8. **Coordination is built in.** The memorandum sets duties for each party:
   - Investigators submit a plan, permit applications, progress reports, and a final report.
   - The lead station approves plans and monitors investigations.
   - The Fish Health Center manages quarantine and pathogen risk.
   - The Regional Endangered Species Office issues permits once plans are in place.

   Two weeks' advance notice is required before field collections.

9. **Several values remain undefined.** Many culture parameters (densities, temperatures, hormone doses, tank sizes, and storage periods) are blank in the draft. The plan requires them to be submitted with each permit application.
