# Diseases of Fish in the Adirondacks: A Contribution to the Life History of Clinostomum marginatum

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clinostomum

## Executive Summary

This 1914 preliminary report by [[W. M. Smallwood]], published as Technical Publication No. 1 of the [[New York State College of Forestry]] at [[Syracuse University]], examines why yellow perch and bass in Adirondack ponds become unfit for food each summer. The cause is the trematode [[Clinostomum Marginatum]], which forms whitish encysted "grubs" in fish flesh and under the skin. Smallwood compared heavily infested ponds with nearly parasite-free ponds, documented where parasites sit on the fish, examined the worms' anatomy and feeding structures, and reported early larval stages found in snails and perch muscle. The study is explicitly preliminary: the overwintering stage and the first host remain unknown.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Guides and anglers in the Adirondacks call summer perch "grubby" and consider them inedible. Smallwood found no one with firm knowledge of when the "grubs" appear or how long they last. All agreed that perch caught in winter and early spring are usually free of parasites.
- **Timing:** From the author's visits, most perch were unfit to eat by mid-June, but usually clean in late May. The condition appeared to develop over about two weeks.
- **Parasite species:** Two trematodes were found. The larger is [[Clinostomum Marginatum]], a flatworm that encysts in flesh and appears as a whitish spot through the skin. The smaller species is not named in the text.
- **Distribution in host:** On June 24, 1913, 44 perch were examined through the skin. Parasites were located on the head (38 fish), gills (5), trunk (82) and tail (78). One perch taken August 7, 1913 had over 30 fully developed cysts around the mouth cavity alone.
- **Seasonal variation:** Based on one summer's data, infection appeared lower in early August than in late June, and perch from September and October were less heavily infected than early-summer fish.
- **Adult size:** Osborne's [[Osborne 1911 Clinostomum Study]] gives adult lengths of 3–8 mm and widths of 0.9–2 mm. Smallwood's specimens were mostly 5–8 mm, with several at 10 mm, longer than Osborne's range.
- **Anatomy:** The worm has an oral sucker (0.28 × 0.25 mm) and a larger ventral sucker. It lacks a pharynx. Its intestine has contractile walls and two lateral coeca. The worm is hermaphroditic, with two testes and an ovary feeding a uterine sack.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study ponds.** Six spring-fed ponds were studied in three pairs:
- West Pine and East Pine Ponds (ridge about 300 ft wide between them)
- Copperas Pond and a small mud pond (ridge under 250 ft)
- Follensby Clear and Green Pond (strip of land about 400 ft)

East Pine, Copperas and Follensby Clear had badly infested perch for years. West Pine, the mud pond and Green Pond yielded mostly parasite-free fish. Copperas, Follensby Clear and East Pine have outlets. Bottom types, depth (from shoals to over 30 ft), and weed cover varied. Smallwood found no consistent physical factor that separated parasitized from non-parasitized ponds. Herons and kingfishers were seen equally often at both. In the shallow mud-bottomed pond and the gravelly, clear-water Green Pond, 80 to 90 per cent of perch were parasite-free.

**Springs and temperature.** Many guides link infection to warming spring water. Smallwood noted several springs, including one at the upper end of Copperas that campers used and one beside which some of the worst-infected perch were caught. Yet cold spring-fed wells on Follensby Clear did not prevent infection in nearby ponds.

**Diet.** Perch ate snails, caddis-fly larvae in sand or stick cases, dragonfly larvae and small minnows (probably [[Blunt-Nosed Minnow]]). A perch that ate an infected minnow contained a parasite in the minnow's tail.

**Predators and fate of parasites.** Several pickerel were examined. No living Clinostomum were found in their digestive tracts. The two parasites seen in the stomachs were dead.

**Winter and overwintering.** Parasite-free perch caught in winter suggest that the worms do not remain passive in perch until a bird eats them. Smallwood proposed that worms leave the fish in autumn by dissolving the cyst wall and overlying tissue. Perch sent by mail from Lake Clear (mid-September 1913) carried about ten parasites each. More than two-thirds had escaped on arrival, mostly dead. Fish kept dead for several days in summer did not show this escape, and a May 28, 1914 observation showed escape within an hour of capture.

**Host birds.** Adult worms were reported in the bittern, great blue heron, herring gull, wood stork and black-crowned night heron. Smallwood proposed examining herons, noting that the birds' legal protection might need revision if herons prove to be the main distributors.

**Larval stages (provisional).** Brownish bodies in the livers of snails (*Amnicola limosa*) were interpreted as early flatworm larvae. Three flatworms measured 0.7 mm in one snail. Later stages were 1.2 mm long and 0.2 mm wide, with intestinal tubes and round bodies in the surrounding space. Small cysts were also found in perch muscle, especially along the back. Some were 1.1 × 0.6 mm with larvae 0.72 mm long, compared with 3.5 mm cysts described by Loess. Cysts were still present on October 21.

## Key Findings

1. **Physical conditions do not explain infection.** Physiography, bottom type, depth and bird presence did not separate parasitized from parasite-free ponds. Smallwood concluded that a wide range of conditions allows the adult to mature.
2. **Infection is widespread on the fish.** Worms occur on all parts of perch, but most cysts are on the trunk and tail. Infected perch are unfit for food, and the parasite likely affects bass as well.
3. **Escape from fish is likely in autumn.** Cysts in fall-caught perch were ready to open, and worms emerged through dissolved cyst walls and skin. This suggests birds may not be required to complete the life cycle.
4. **Pickerel are not a source of reinfection.** Living parasites were absent in examined pickerel stomachs, so predators appear to kill ingested worms.
5. **Snails are a candidate first host.** Larval flatworms in *Amnicola limosa* suggest the snail is a likely early host, though which trematode they belong to is unknown.
6. **Cyst-stage development is ongoing.** Small cysts in perch muscle may remain through winter and develop into adults. Smallwood planned further study of living tissue.
7. **Key questions remain.** Overwintering stage, entry route into the fish, the number of required hosts, and the role of herons are unresolved. Smallwood described the report as preliminary and planned further field and tissue work.
