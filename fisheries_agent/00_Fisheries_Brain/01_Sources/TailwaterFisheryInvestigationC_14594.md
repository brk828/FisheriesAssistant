# Tailwater Fishery Investigation: Creel Census and Limnological Study of the Colorado River Below Glen Canyon Dam (1967-1968)

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #salmonidae #ictaluridae #tailwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This report presents the fifth segment of a six-year study investigating the tailwater fishery created by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]]. Between July 1, 1967 and June 30, 1968, creel census at [[Lee's Ferry]] documented 4,161 estimated angler trips producing 9,433 fish in 22,829 hours of effort. The fishery was dominated by [[Rainbow Trout]] (6,597 fish) and [[Channel Catfish]] (2,638 fish), with an overall catch rate of 0.39 fish per hour. Water quality parameters remained suitable for trout, though benthic organism populations were limited. Bottom fauna transplants (3,000 crayfish and 20,000 snails) were conducted to enhance food availability. A ten-day float trip from Lee's Ferry to Lake Mead revealed that the 65 miles below Lee's Ferry to the [[Little Colorado River]] contained good trout water, while most side streams supported trout populations. The study was financed primarily under Section 8 of the Colorado River Storage Project (Public Law 485).

## Background & Description

[[Lee's Ferry]] is located approximately 15 miles below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and provides the only public vehicular access to the [[Colorado River]] between the dam and [[Lake Mead]]. The fishable area extends approximately 16 miles: 3 miles accessible from shore and 14 miles accessible by boat. This tailwater fishery was created by the impoundment of Glen Canyon Dam, which fundamentally altered the river's thermal and flow regime.

During the study period, 33,800 [[Rainbow Trout]] were planted, including 3,800 catchable-size fish (9-11 inches) and 30,000 fingerlings (3.5-6 inches). Of the catchable fish, 2,500 were marked with green streamer tags to estimate return rates. The average rainbow trout in the creel weighed 0.68 pounds and measured 12.2 inches (condition factor C = 0.38). Fish collected throughout the tailwater averaged 0.51 pounds and 11.3 inches (C = 0.36).

The study employed standardized creel census techniques with road blocks and river bank checks on three weekdays and three weekend days per month. A total of 59 census days were conducted, checking 657 angler trips that produced 970 rainbow trout, 354 channel catfish, and 29 other fish for 1,353 total fish in 3,433 hours.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water Quality**: All chemical and physical parameters were satisfactory for maintaining a trout fishery. Dissolved oxygen ranged from 3-12 ppm, pH from 7.5-8.5, alkalinity from 119.7-171.1 ppm, and hardness from 257.5-359.1 ppm. Water temperature ranged from 44°F to 69°F, remaining within trout tolerance throughout most of the river. Water flows were high, averaging 11,117 cfs daily (range: 2,900-19,900 cfs), with characteristic day-night and weekday-weekend fluctuations due to hydroelectric operations.

**Biological Productivity**: Plankton population levels were satisfactory, but benthic organisms remained limited. Bottom fauna sampling revealed 0-151.2 bloodworms (midge larvae), 0-30 snails, and 0-5.5 other organisms per square foot. The station below the west diversion tunnel (Station III) showed the highest benthos density due to previous transplant efforts, with *Physa* sp. snails being most prevalent.

**Food Habits**: Stomach analysis of 528 rainbow trout revealed that zooplankton (36.1% occurrence) and algae (35.7%) dominated the diet. However, insects appeared in 12.9% of stomachs—a significant increase from 3.7% in the previous study period. Snails appeared in 0.8% of stomachs, suggesting successful establishment from transplants. The improved food availability corresponded with a 3-point increase in average condition factor.

**Species Composition**: Fifteen fish species were collected during the study. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) was the most abundant native species. Other species included [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus delphinus*), [[Kokanee]] salmon (*Oncorhynchus nerka*), and several other species.

**River Trip Findings**: The float trip from Lee's Ferry to Lake Mead (April 1-10, 1968) documented dramatic changes in water quality below the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence. Above this point, water was clear green-blue with 1-3 feet visibility; below, it became turbid chocolate-brown with only 2-4 inches visibility. Water temperature increased from 48°F at Lee's Ferry to 54°F near Lake Mead. Bottom fauna in the main Colorado River was nearly absent (0.95 midge larvae per square foot), but side streams contained abundant benthic organisms including Odonata, Plecoptera, and Coleoptera larvae.

## Key Findings

**Fishing Pressure and Success**:
- Estimated 4,161 angler trips produced 9,433 fish in 22,829 hours
- Average catch rate: 0.39 fish per hour (continuing downward trend from 1.05 in 1963-64)
- Average trip: 5.32 hours producing 2.06 fish
- 71% of anglers were male, 29% female
- 75% of angler trips originated in Arizona, with Flagstaff contributing 19%
- Boat anglers (51% of pressure) achieved higher catch rates (0.46 fish/hour) than shore anglers (0.31 fish/hour)

**Stocking Returns**:
- Marked fish return estimated at 29% for January and March 1968 plants of catchable-size trout
- One marked fish traveled 17 miles downstream in 9 days
- Previous plants showed variable returns: 61% (March 1964), 23% (October 1964), 48% (March 1967)

**Size and Condition**:
- Rainbow trout in creel: 12.2 inches, 0.68 pounds, C = 0.38
- Channel catfish in creel: 10.4 inches, 0.52 pounds, C = 0.46
- Gill-netted rainbow trout: 10.4 inches, 0.39 pounds, C = 0.35
- Spawning aggregation observed in October 1967 at diversion tunnels: fish averaged 13.1 inches, 0.62 pounds, C = 0.28

**Bottom Fauna Enhancement**:
- Two crayfish plants (500 and 2,500 *Orconectes* sp.) from [[Little Colorado River]] at Springerville
- One snail plant (20,000 *Physa* sp.) from Nelson Reservoir
- Limited success observed: one crayfish collected in gillnet, snails established at planting sites
- Snails beginning to appear in trout stomachs

**Downstream Fishery Assessment**:
- First 65 miles (Lee's Ferry to Little Colorado River): excellent trout water
- Five rainbow trout collected by angling averaged 13.0 inches, 0.68 pounds, C = 0.33
- Below Little Colorado: unsuitable for most game fish except catfish due to turbidity and temperature
- Side streams surveyed: [[Bright Angel Creek]], [[Shinumo Creek]], [[Tapeats Creek]], [[Deer Creek]], [[Havasu Creek]], [[Diamond Creek]], [[Spencer Creek]]
- Most side streams contained trout populations; [[Tapeats Creek]] was most productive (19 trout collected)
- Shinumo Creek yielded one large female rainbow trout: 18.7 inches, 2.07 pounds, C = 0.33

**Bait Preferences**:
- 76% of anglers used prepared baits (pasteurized cheese and salmon eggs most popular)
- 24% used artificial lures (Mepps-type spinners and spoons)

**Recommendations**:
1. Continue creel census to evaluate fishery potential
2. Intensify bottom fauna transplants, emphasizing snails
3. Continue large plants of 5-6 inch trout with catchable-size supplements during peak pressure
4. Evaluate planting second game fish species (cutthroat or brown trout)
5. Initiate intensified age and growth study of rainbow trout
