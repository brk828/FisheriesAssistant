# Factors Influencing Fish Distribution in Two Desert Reservoirs, Central Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Rinne, Minckley and Bersell (1981, *Hydrobiologia* 80: 31-42) examines what controls the vertical and horizontal distribution of fishes in two multi-purpose [[Desert Reservoirs]] on the Salt River, Central Arizona: [[Roosevelt Lake]] and [[Apache Lake]]. Sampling ran from summer 1970 to autumn 1971 and paired gillnet catches with temperature, [[Dissolved Oxygen]], light, [[Chlorophyll A]] and plankton/benthos data. Over 90% of the fish were confined to the upper 10 m of water. The authors conclude that no single factor dominates, but that the [[Euphotic Zone]] is the most important control, with [[Thermocline]] depth and low-oxygen zones acting as secondary modifiers.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Large mainstream impoundments in the arid U.S. Southwest were built for water storage under the Reclamation Act of 1902. Sport fishing became a major use within a multiple-use framework covering power, domestic supply, and agriculture.
- **Management history:** Stocking programs introduced [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Black Crappie]], which produced a well-known sport fishery. [[Threadfin Shad]] were added as forage, and piscivores responded with higher production (Kimsey et al., 1957).
- **Research gap:** Prior work covered commercial species, plankton, benthos and water chemistry, but sport-fish depth and horizontal distribution in relation to physicochemical and food-web factors had not been examined. The present paper addresses that gap.
- **Study reservoirs:** The two lakes share some morphology but differ strongly. Roosevelt Lake approaches Apache Lake in maximum and mean depth but has nearly six times its volume and twice its shoreline. Apache Lake is more fiord-like, is drawn down and refilled by water management, and is clearer. Roosevelt is larger and more turbid, with the turbidity concentrated at shallow inflow areas and wind-exposed surfaces.
- **Study design:** Sampling took place at fixed transects during summer stagnation (July-August), winter homeothermy (November-December), and spring warming (March-April). These reservoirs are characterized as warm, [[Monomictic Lakes]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods**
- Fish were sampled with vertical gillnets of three mesh sizes (13, 38 and 76 mm bar measure), following [[Gillnet Sampling]] designs described by Horak & Tanner (1964) and Lackey (1968). Catches were expressed as fish per m² of netting per 24 hours.
- Temperature and dissolved oxygen were profiled to 20 m at 1 m steps, then at 10 m intervals. Light penetration was measured with a submarine photometer.
- [[Benthos]] (chironomid larvae and oligochaetes) was taken by Ekman dredge. [[Zooplankton]] was collected with a Clarke-Bumpus sampler, with [[Phytoplankton]] pigments measured as chlorophyll a.
- A [[Multiple Linear Regression]] program was used to link shad and bass vertical distribution to physicochemical and biological variables.

**Physical and chemical setting**
- Temperature profiles varied substantially between the two lakes. The Apache thermocline was about half the depth of Roosevelt's.
- Dissolved oxygen curves generally followed the temperature pattern. Apache Lake had a slightly deeper zone of adequate oxygen than Roosevelt.
- Apache Lake was much more transparent. Its [[Euphotic Zone]] was roughly twice as thick as Roosevelt's, which was reduced by turbidity from inflows and wind-driven suspended sediment.

**Biological setting**
- More than 40 fish species have been recorded from the Salt River reservoirs, most of them occurring only locally (Minckley & Johnson, 1968).
- Chlorophyll a and net plankton were highest in the upper water column and at the upstream ends of the reservoirs.
- Benthic invertebrates were dominated by chironomids and tubificid oligochaetes.

**Comparative context**
The authors compare their results with [[Bull Shoals Reservoir]], [[Beaver Reservoir]], [[Keystone Reservoir]] and [[Norris Reservoir]] in the southeastern United States. In those systems, threadfin shad and bass also concentrated above roughly 10 m, and dissolved oxygen and temperature were the main limits on depth.

## Key Findings

**1. Shallow-water concentration**
- Over 90% of fish in the two desert reservoirs were found in the upper 10 m of water.
- The authors argue that fishes introduced into western reservoirs are intrinsically shallow-water, [[Littoral Zone]] inhabitants because of inherent limnological conditions typical of mid-latitude lentic systems.

**2. Relative importance of controlling factors**
- The [[Euphotic Zone]] was identified as the most important control. Its depth sat near, but deeper than, the thermocline and the zone of dissolved oxygen below 2.0 mg/l.
- Chlorophyll a and fish abundance were strongly correlated (r = 0.85), so primary production appears to be a key variable.
- Net plankton, which reflects zooplankton, correlated weakly with fish abundance (r = 0.23).
- Correlation coefficients for threadfin shad and bass numbers across sampling periods, from summer 1970 to summer 1971, were 0.36, 0.75, 0.90 and approximately 0.99. These indicate differences only in summer 1970.

**3. Species-specific patterns**
- In Roosevelt Lake, threadfin shad and largemouth bass were distributed in a bimodal vertical pattern. In Apache Lake, they showed a single peak at 5-7 m.
- Almost all individuals of the four main species were caught above the thermocline in Roosevelt Lake. In Apache, a larger share of the catch lay below the thermocline and above the 2.0 mg/l oxygen limit.
- Carp ranged deepest, and their mean capture depth increased with water depth. Carp were scattered vertically and horizontally.
- Largemouth bass shifted from shallow to deeper water in autumn, but tag returns indicated they were fairly sedentary.

**4. Horizontal distribution and seasonal movement**
- Fish were consistently more abundant in shallow areas near inflows of Roosevelt Lake and in the uppermost part of Apache Lake, in all seasons.
- Threadfin shad moved from shallow to deeper transects in autumn-winter, while bass and black crappie showed less seasonal displacement.
- Between-transect differences were significant (P < 0.05) in Roosevelt Lake during all seasons except autumn.
- Apache Lake's extreme drawdown in summer 1971 concentrated fish, and catches rose in the remaining water.

**5. Food and turbidity**
- Threadfin shad are mainly limnetic planktivores that feed on diatoms and unicellular chlorophytes, not on benthos.
- The authors state that food supply explained fish distribution poorly. Seasonal (not spatial) correspondence between food and fish was the usual pattern.
- Turbidity was higher at shallow inflow areas (P < 0.01). Over 90% of fish were netted at night, which reduces any effect of turbidity on net visibility.

**6. Conclusions**
- No single factor controls fish distribution in these reservoirs.
- The upper 10 m of water held the most fish because of the combined influence of thermocline depth, dissolved oxygen, and the euphotic zone.
- The results agree with southeastern reservoir studies. Fish introduced to western reservoirs remain shallow-water, littoral species.
