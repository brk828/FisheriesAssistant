# Distribution, Spawning and Habitat Use of the Razorback Sucker in the Green River Basin, Colorado and Utah, 1979-84

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This study, presented by [[Harold M. Tyus]] and [[William B. Harned]] for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], documents the distribution, spawning and habitat use of the [[Razorback Sucker]] in the [[Green River]] Basin from 1979 to 1984. The razorback sucker is listed as threatened by the FWS and designated as endangered by the State of Colorado, and its wild range is now largely confined to the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The authors found that adults are most abundant and most easily captured during spring spawning migrations. They identified three suspected spawning areas and located larvae downstream of them, which is evidence of successful spawning. Outside the breeding season, the fish mostly occupy lower-velocity, deeper shoreline and main-channel habitats and make only short local movements.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was once abundant and widely distributed across the Colorado River system. Its current natural range is limited mainly to the upper Colorado River Basin, where it occurs in the mainstem of the Green and upper Colorado rivers. Young individuals have rarely been found in recent years. Razorbacks also persist in mainstem reservoirs of the Lower Colorado River, where they are presumed to be non-reproducing relict populations (Minckley 1983).

The work builds on the [[Colorado River Fish Project]], begun in 1979 by the FWS and Bureau of Reclamation and expanded through 1984. Data from the [[Colorado River Fish Monitoring Project]] (1982-84), which targeted the [[Colorado Squawfish]], also yielded razorback records. A dedicated spawning survey was run from the Vernal FWS field station in 1984.

Adult razorbacks in the Green River were measured at 426 to 583 mm total length (TL). Approximately 63% fell in the 500-550 mm class and about 15% exceeded 550 mm. The share below 500 mm is illegible in the scan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Razorbacks occur in the mainstem Green River and the lower reaches of the [[Duchesne River]] and [[Yampa River]]. The main concentration lies between Sand Wash and [[Split Mountain]]. Fish were also taken in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (Island and Echo parks) and in the flatwater sections of [[Labyrinth Canyon]]. No razorbacks were captured in the whitewater canyons of the Green River Basin.

**Seasonal habitat.** Few fish were captured outside the breeding season. Radiotelemetry of one fish tracked from April to June 1980 in the Duchesne and Green rivers, which used [[Radiotelemetry]], showed the following:
- Depths of 0.6 to 2.4 m.
- Velocities of 0.1 to 0.5 m/s.
- A preference for sand/silt substrates in shoreline and main-channel habitats.
- Movement into the eddy mixing zone of the Duchesne and Green rivers during high flows, and a final observation on a submerged sand bar in August.

Captured fish also favored sand/silt bottoms in quiet water. Roughly 80% of captured razorbacks were taken where velocity was below 0.3 m/s (the scan reads "B0%").

**Spawning conditions.** Spawning fish were collected during spring runoff. Water temperatures at capture points for ripe females with strippable eggs ranged from 13.5 to 18 °C in the Green River (mean about 16 °C) and were about 15 °C in the Duchesne River. Flows declined to 15,600 cfs by June 14.

**Comparisons with other populations.** Green River razorbacks are smaller than those in other areas. Minckley (1983) reported lower-basin males up to 640 mm and females up to 740 mm TL. McAda and Wydoski (1980) reported upper-mainstem Colorado River males up to 520 mm and females up to 620 mm. Valdez et al. (1982) found an average of 554 mm and a maximum of 635 mm. The authors suggest that Green River fish are younger on average, which may indicate more successful recruitment.

## Key Findings

**Population and abundance**
- A total of 244 adult razorbacks have been collected by FWS since 1979, including 152 during the present study.
- In the basin-wide systematic program of 1979-81, only 5 razorbacks were collected. In contrast, 92 were taken in early spring samples near spawning areas during the same years.
- An intensive 1984 effort yielded 102 razorbacks in about 1.5 months.

**Spawning**
- Vernal FWS personnel captured 47 razorbacks in breeding condition (tubercles and/or ripe sex products) from 1981 to 1984.
- Three suspected spawning areas were delineated:
  1. Island and Echo parks of the Green River in Dinosaur National Monument, including the lower Yampa River.
  2. The Jensen area, from Ashley Creek to Split Mountain.
  3. The lower few miles of the Duchesne River.
- The Jensen area yielded the most ripe fish: 22 of the 33 razorbacks with ripe sex products.
- Between May 3 and June 15, 1984, 26 ripe razorbacks were collected over sand and gravel substrates.
- Ripe males averaged 502 mm TL (n=13; largest 512 mm), and ripe females averaged 541 mm TL (n=11; largest 586 mm).
- The male:female ratio on suspected spawning grounds was 1.35:1.

**Larvae**
- Razorback larvae were collected in riverine areas immediately downstream of the suspected spawning grounds in 1984. The Larval Fish Laboratory of Colorado State University identified them by comparison with cultured *Xyrauchen* larvae.
- Of 42 samples, 33 razorback larvae were found. No larvae were collected in Labyrinth Canyon.
- Successful spawning was thus confirmed in the Upper Green River.

**Movement**
- The fish stay in one general area for most of the year and make local movements of a few miles. They undertake spawning migrations in spring.
- Recaptures show some fish return to specific sites. Migration is not well understood, but the authors warn that migration must be accounted for in population abundance estimates, since concentrations in easily sampled areas during migration can inflate estimates.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Further collections of ripe adults and larvae in apparently suitable spawning habitat during spring runoff are needed to determine how widespread spawning is across the basin.
- Some spawning may occur at lower temperatures than the fish prefer during the rest of the year. A laboratory study by Bulkley and Pimentel (1983) found that hatchery-reared razorbacks avoided certain temperatures, and the Green River spawning temperatures appear lower than their preferred range.
