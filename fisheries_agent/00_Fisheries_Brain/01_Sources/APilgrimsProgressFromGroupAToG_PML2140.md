# A Sand County Almanac Revisited: Land Ethic, Biotic Integrity and Fish Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-ethics

## Executive Summary

This essay, presented as a retrospective by a California fishery biologist (Edwin P. Pister), traces how [[Aldo Leopold]]'s book *A Sand County Almanac* reshaped one manager's approach to aquatic resource management in the eastern Sierra Nevada and Death Valley regions. The author argues that management should begin with the integrity of the land, water and native biota, and only then accommodate species of economic or political interest. It recounts a career-long shift from hatchery-based, [[Utilitarian Resource Management]] toward inventories of native species, and describes conservation campaigns on the Owens River system, Lower Rock Creek and [[Death Valley Native Fishes]]. The piece closes with the claim that the land ethic has influenced major US environmental legislation.

## Background & Description

- **Author background:** Trained in limnology; in the late 1950s took a position as a fishery biologist with the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], responsible for nearly a thousand waters in the eastern Sierra-desert region, with one assistant.
- **Elevation range of the study area:** from the top of Mt. Whitney (about 4,400 m, given in the source as "meters" with the figure partly garbled) to the floor of Death Valley, nearly 100 m below sea level.
- **Management ideal:** responsibility for the perpetuation of all aquatic organisms, including fishes, amphibians, invertebrates and reptiles, and their habitats.
- **Historical context:** Management programs inherited from predecessors were built around put-and-take trout stocking and creel censuses, serving outdoor recreation demand after World War II. Licence sales were the agency's main income, and the program grew larger over time.
- **Legislative timeline noted:** The first, very inadequate, Endangered Species Act was passed in 1966, after the National Environmental Policy Act was "several years" away.
- **Key concept, A–B cleavage:** Leopold's division between people who regard the land as an integrated biota (Group B) and those who regard it primarily as a harvestable crop (Group A). Group A types are said to dominate fish and wildlife agencies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Biota under threat:** The author states that at least two, and probably five, fish species in two genera would already be extinct had the group waited for official support. Unknown numbers of invertebrate species and plants were also believed lost to habitat destruction.
- **Owens River system:** Habitat integrity and species composition were already significantly altered in the early 20th century. An initial survey found three of the fishes native to the Owens system to be endangered or of indeterminate status.
- **Death Valley native fishes:** An interagency meeting in April 1969 found that native fishes of the Death Valley hydrographic area, whose drainages originate in both California and Nevada, faced a dilemma similar to the author's.
- **Water diversions and hydropower:** The author notes that roughly 50 percent of the stream mileage downstream of Forest Service wilderness boundaries in the Owens basin had already been affected by diversions, and about 25 percent of the hydroscape no longer existed (figure as stated in the source).
- **Lower Rock Creek:** In 1959, the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power applied to divert several miles of this heavily used eastern Sierra stream into Crowley Lake for hydroelectric power. The Department of Fish and Game opposed it, and the court ruled in its favour.
- **Brown trout and golden trout:** The author's early motivation was to preserve a fishery for an exotic trout species, but he came to see the brown trout as "by no means the most important part" of the stream ecosystem.

## Key Findings

1. **Management must start with biotic integrity.** Following Leopold's land ethic, the author argues that a sound program should build from land and water integrity, to flora and fauna, and only then to species of economic interest.
2. **Biological inventories were neglected.** Agency budgets were largely spent on stocking trout and creel census work, while the native biota was poorly understood. Attempts to fund surveys met resistance from administrators.
3. **Covert action was necessary.** Because official support was delayed by a decade or more, the author and colleagues in Nevada, the National Park Service, the Bureau of Land Management and the Fish and Wildlife Service "moved underground" to protect native fishes.
4. **Policy successes:** Species recovery and non-consumptive management programmes were started; the Lower Rock Creek diversion was blocked in court; and the Forest Service adopted a compromise policy on stocking high Sierra waters outside national parks.
5. **Ethical conflict with officials:** A project official dismissed ethical objections to a small hydroelectric project on the Owens system with the remark, "When you start talking about morality and ethics, you lose me."
6. **Legislative influence claimed:** The author credits Leopold's philosophy with inspiring the National Environmental Policy Act, the Endangered Species Act, the Marine Mammal Protection Act and the Clean Water Act.
7. **Non-game species gaining recognition:** The California Natural Diversity Data Base, developed with The Nature Conservancy, is described as embryonic but encouraging. Funding proposals through licence and earmarked revenues for non-game management were also noted.
8. **Public perception is paramount.** Quoting Leopold, the author argues that government cannot sustain a policy without public support, so education and political backing are essential.
9. **Future outlook:** The author predicts that *A Sand County Almanac* will continue to be a major force shaping national environmental policy.
