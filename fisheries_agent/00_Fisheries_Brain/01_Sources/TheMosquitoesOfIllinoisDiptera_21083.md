# The Mosquitoes of Illinois (Diptera, Culicidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #culicidae

Note on scope: the source is an entomological bulletin on mosquitoes, an aquatic-insect taxon, not fish. The tags above are the required header line; the taxon tag is applied as Culicidae.

## Executive Summary

Herbert H. Ross's 1947 Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Volume 24, Article 1) provides illustrated keys, descriptions, and distribution and habitat summaries for the mosquitoes of Illinois. Of about 150 mosquito species known from the United States and Canada, 52 have been recorded in Illinois, a fauna combining northern and southern elements. The work covers the four life stages, breeding strategies, habitat preferences, seasonal succession, collecting and rearing methods, and the public-health and nuisance importance of the fauna, with the malaria vector [[Anopheles Quadrimaculatus]] as the principal medical concern in the state.

## Background & Description

The bulletin was issued in August 1947 by the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] under Chief Harlow B. Mills, as a contribution from the Section of Insect Survey. Its stated object is to improve mosquito control by supplying identification keys for species occurring in Illinois and similar climates, and by summarizing their distribution, biology, and habitat preferences.

Mosquitoes pass through four stages: the egg, the aquatic larva ("wiggler"), the aquatic pupa ("tumbler"), and the aerial adult. Adult females take blood meals, while males feed on nectar and water. Larvae breathe through air tubes or spiracular plates; [[Anopheles]] larvae lie parallel to the surface, whereas the culicine larvae feed near the bottom. The [[Chaoborinae]] (non-biting midge-like mosquitoes) and [[Culicinae]] (true mosquitoes) are distinguished by the presence of a beak in the adults.

The bulletin is organized into biology, seasonal and distribution patterns, economic importance, control considerations, collecting and preserving methods, study and rearing techniques, classification, and species accounts with keys to subfamilies, genera, species, and life stages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Egg-laying and breeding strategy.** Eggs fall into three categories: singly on water ([[Anopheles]] and [[Megarhinus]]), in floating rafts ([[Culex]], [[Culiseta]], [[Mansonia]], [[Uranotaenia]]), and singly in damp humus or above the water line ([[Aedes]], [[Psorophora]], [[Orthopodomyia]], [[Wyeomyia]]). Species laying out-of-water eggs are intermittent breeders, hatching after flooding. Annual breeders require winter freezing before development, whereas some eggs of [[Aedes]] and [[Psorophora]] can remain dormant for one or two years.

**Habitat preferences.** Species occupy running water, still water, and specialized sites. Permanent areas include marshes and shallow ponds with cattails and sedges. Temporary areas comprise winter seepage ponds, flood plain ponds, summer rain ponds, and marsh margins. Specialized habitats include [[Tree Hole Mosquitoes]] (cavities in standing or fallen trees) and [[Pitcher Plant Habitat]] (water-filled pitchers of *Sarracenia purpurea*). Semidomestic containers such as fish ponds, tin cans, and eaves troughs also support breeding.

**Seasonal succession.** Early-season [[Aedes]] (e.g., *canadensis*, *grossbecki*, *sticticus*) emerge first, with first emergence about April 1 in southern Illinois and mid-May in the north, usually about two weeks later than these dates. These are followed by [[Culiseta]] inornata and [[Culex]] apicalis, then summer species including [[Anopheles]], several [[Culex]], and *Aedes vexans* and *trivittatus*. A later group, including [[Culex Quinquefasciatus]] and *Uranotaenia sapphirina*, usually appears at least by midsummer.

**Distribution pattern.** The fauna falls into three groups: widely distributed species, northern species confined mainly to the northeastern glacial bogs and marshes, and southern species restricted largely to the southern third of the state, including the post oak flats of the Mississippi River valley.

**Flight and host behavior.** Malaria mosquitoes typically range 1 to 2 miles. *Aedes vexans* swarms may migrate 2 to 3 miles and possibly up to 15 miles, and the salt marsh mosquito *Aedes sollicitans* has been known to travel 40 to 50 miles. Biting times vary: most species bite at dusk and night, woodland species bite through the day, and *Anopheles walkeri* and *Psorophora cyanescens* bite readily in sunlight.

**Hibernation.** Most Illinois species overwinter as eggs. Some (*Wyeomyia smithii*, *Anopheles barberi*, *Orthopodomyia*) overwinter as larvae in pitcher plants or tree holes, while [[Anopheles]], [[Culex]], and *Culiseta inornata* overwinter as fertilized females in sheltered sites.

**Economic and health importance.** The nation's annual mosquito cost is put at "probably $100,000,000" from mosquito-borne disease, and close to $50,000,000 for screening, control, and depressed real estate values. Nuisance species (chiefly [[Aedes]] and [[Psorophora]], with [[Culex]] in towns) restrict recreation and can harm livestock. It is estimated that 500 mosquitoes draw one-twentieth of a pint of blood per day from an exposed animal. Malaria is endemic in southern Illinois, and [[Anopheles quadrimaculatus]] is considered the only important vector in the state. Yellow fever, filariasis, and dengue are not considered a serious threat at present.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna size:** 52 mosquito species have been taken in Illinois, out of about 150 known from the United States and Canada. The bulletin keys 10 genera in the subfamily Culicinae. The Illinois fauna combines northern and southern elements.
- **Subfamily structure:** Chaoborinae (non-biting) are represented by *Chaoborus*, *Corethrella*, and *Mochlonyx*. Eucorethra is widespread north of Illinois but unrecorded in the state.
- **Largest species:** *Psorophora ciliata* ("gallinipper") reaches a wingspread of about 15 mm, is a vicious biter, and its larvae prey on other mosquito larvae.
- **Most important medical vector:** [[Anopheles Quadrimaculatus]] is the chief malaria carrier in Illinois and the United States east of the Rocky Mountains. Five Illinois Anopheles species were recorded; *occidentalis* was included on the basis of a neighboring Iowa record.
- **Nuisance species:** *Aedes vexans* is described as the commonest Illinois mosquito in numbers and the greatest nuisance in the state, though not known to transmit human disease. *Aedes sollicitans* has invaded some cities, and at Dupo, salinity of breeding water reached three times that of ocean water.
- **Yellow fever vector:** [[Aedes Aegypti]] has been taken only at isolated points, and apparently does not persist beyond a single summer.
- **Breeding-strategy rule:** Intermittent breeders (e.g., [[Aedes]] and [[Psorophora]]) hatch after flooding, whereas annual breeders require cold-induced dormancy.
- **Control principle:** Control must be based on larval collections because many water bodies, often the largest, do not produce mosquitoes. Species identity indicates habitat type and breeding frequency (annual, intermittent, or persistent), which sets the timing of treatment.
- **Collecting and rearing:** Specimens are preserved in 80 percent alcohol; genitalia are cleared in 5 percent caustic potash; adults are mounted on card points; rearing uses paired lot-and-letter accession numbers to keep skins and adults associated.
- **Records basis:** Records came largely from Insect Survey staff collections, with extensive contributions from the U.S. Public Health Service (USPHS), the Chicago Natural History Museum (CM), and local abatement districts.
