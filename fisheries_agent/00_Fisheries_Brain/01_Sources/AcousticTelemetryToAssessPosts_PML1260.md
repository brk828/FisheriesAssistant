# Acoustic Telemetry to Assess Post-Stocking Dispersal and Mortality of Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This brief communication uses [[Acoustic Telemetry]] and scuba-based recovery to examine the fate of hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) repatriated into [[Lake Mohave]], a [[Colorado River]] impoundment on the Arizona–Nevada border. Over a 6-month study, only 3 of 19 telemetered fish (16%) remained active. Most transmitters came to rest on the reservoir bottom with no fish remains nearby, and the authors attribute the mortality most likely to [[Predation]] by non-native [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*). A parallel captive experiment found that transmitter implantation did not compromise survival, health, or reproductive development. Fish did not leave the reservoir, so the losses are attributed to mortality within the system.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is an endangered catostomid fish native to the Colorado River basin. Lake Mohave is a focal point for current [[Repatriation]] (stocking) efforts. Earlier [[Mark-Recapture]] monitoring estimated post-repatriation survival at only c. 2–6%, but those estimates had broad confidence intervals because recapture rates were low, and they assumed fish did not disperse out of the system.

The reservoir supports a sport fishery of large non-native predators, including [[Striped Bass]] over 800 mm total length. Clear water, with Secchi depths typically above 5 m and seasonally above 10 m, is created by hypolimnetic discharges from upstream dams, which favours visual predators. Piscivory by non-native fishes is well documented across the basin and is suspected as a major cause of low survival in stocked razorback suckers.

The study had four aims:
1. Determine whether fish emigrate from the reservoir.
2. Estimate the rate of post-stocking mortality.
3. Assess whether predation is a major component of that mortality.
4. Estimate [[Transmitter Retention]] and the effects of surgery and transmitter presence on survival and growth, using a captive experiment.

**Methods summary**

- **Telemetry fish:** 20 subadult fish (mean total length 381 mm) were taken from the hatchery stock at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] on 25 September 2006. All carried a 400 kHz [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag]].
- **Transmitters:** Sonotronics acoustic transmitters (7.4 g in air) were selected to keep transmitter mass below 2% of fish body mass, a common reference rule for telemetry studies.
- **Surgery:** Fish were anaesthetized with MS-222 (125 mg L⁻¹). Transmitters were inserted through a short incision anterior to the right pelvic fin and sutured. Fish received an antibiotic injection (Baytril, enrofloxacin) and were monitored for 2 days.
- **Ethics:** The protocol was approved by the Arizona State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol 05-767R).
- **Release:** Telemetry fish were released on 27 September 2006 at [[Fortune Cove]], part of a 2-day repatriation of 1,034 fish (c. 500 released on each of 26 and 27 September).
- **Tracking:** Submersible ultrasonic receivers ([[Submersible Ultrasonic Receiver]]) were placed upstream and downstream of the release site. Manual boat surveys used a directional hydrophone for the first 30 days, then bimonthly surveys across 148 GPS-referenced listening stations. Contacts were mapped in a GIS.
- **Mortality verification:** When a transmitter was immobile on re-contact, a scuba diver with an [[Underwater Diver Receiver]] attempted recovery and noted whether fish remains were present.
- **Captive experiment:** 43 fish were held from 10 January to 12 April 2007. Twenty were implanted with transmitters and 23 served as controls. Seven implanted fish were sacrificed to examine transmitter location.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and movement.** Telemetered fish stayed in the northern half of Lake Mohave. Two full-reservoir surveys confirmed that none moved past the most downstream receiver, and there was no evidence of passage through [[Davis Dam]] or any other emigration route. One fish, contacted immediately after release, was never relocated and was excluded from analysis. This loss represents c. 5% of the total and may reflect undocumented emigration, bird predation, or tag failure.

**Predation.** Sessile transmitters were found mostly in the main channel. Of 16 sessile transmitters, 14 were in the main channel (mean distance 10 km from the release site, range 2–30 km; mean depth 10 m, range 3–21 m). The other two were in shallow coves (mean 7 km from release, range 4–10 km; mean depth 4 m, range 2–5 m). Recovered transmitters showed no fish remains. Only the authors' interpretation, based on previous work, links the losses to predators, so the evidence is indirect. Predation by [[Striped Bass]] is the most likely cause because it is the only piscivore in Lake Mohave with a gape large enough to ingest fish of this size.

**Size and life history.** Fish over 400 mm total length were readily consumed in this study. Previously estimated first-year survival for a 381 mm *X. texanus* was about 40%, far above the 16% observed here over only 6 months. The species can live more than 40 years, so the losses are concerning even though the study covered a small fraction of that lifespan.

**Methodological context.** The study builds on [[Hightower et al. 2001]] telemetry approaches to mortality estimation in [[Striped Bass]] and tests assumptions behind earlier work. The authors argue that acoustic telemetry can narrow confidence intervals on survival for rare, endangered species using fewer fish and less effort than mark-recapture netting.

## Key Findings

- **Survival:** Only 3 of 19 telemetered fish (16%) remained active at the end of the 6-month study. Sixteen of 19 (84%) stopped moving after periods of activity.
- **Recovery:** 13 transmitters were recovered by scuba diving from the reservoir bottom, and no fish remains were found near any of them. Median time between first contact at the recovery location and recovery was 8 days (range 2–48). Three fish that stopped moving were not checked by scuba and were presumed dead.
- **Tracking effort:** 247 total contacts were recorded, 82 of them remotely by submersible receivers. 63 of the 82 remote contacts (about 77%) occurred after dark. Receivers were relocated on 10 occasions.
- **Dispersal:** No evidence of emigration from the reservoir, consistent with the hypothesis that stocked fish stay within Lake Mohave.
- **Cause of loss:** Rapid loss with no fish remains, combined with the large size of consumed fish and previous studies, points to predation, most likely by [[Striped Bass]].
- **Surgical effects:** In the captive study, all 43 fish (20 implanted, 23 controls) remained active and healthy for 3 months. No transmitters were shed, incisions healed, and minor suture irritation was the only issue. Both sexes showed signs of reproductive maturity, suggesting implantation did not disrupt development.
- **Transmitter retention:** Transmitters in sacrificed fish were located near the incision, with no evidence of encapsulation. Retention was high enough that recovered transmitters were treated as indicators of fish mortality.
- **Growth:** Implanted fish grew a mean of 6 mm (range 2–9 mm) and controls a mean of 5 mm (range 0–11 mm). Regressions were not significant, so growth rates could not be statistically compared.
- **Conclusion:** Stocked subadult razorback suckers faced almost certain mortality shortly after repatriation into Lake Mohave, and the authors consider the assumption that handling and tagging do not harm fish to be valid for this species.
