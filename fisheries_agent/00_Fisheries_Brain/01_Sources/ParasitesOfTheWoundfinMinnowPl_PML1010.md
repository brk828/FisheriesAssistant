# Parasites of the Woundfin Minnow and Other Endemic Fishes from the Virgin River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper surveys the parasites of the endangered [[Woundfin Minnow]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and other fishes of the [[Virgin River]] in Utah, Arizona and Nevada. Sampling took place in 1979 (February, June, September, December) and in July-August 1985 at four sites. The most common parasite was the metacercarial stage of the trematode [[Posthodiplostomum Minimum]]. The most consequential finding was the Asian fish tapeworm [[Bothriocephalus Acheilognathi]], an introduced parasite now established in the Utah segment of the river. Parasite loads were consistently higher in "disturbed" river segments affected by agricultural irrigation withdrawals and degraded water quality than in "undisturbed" segments. The authors conclude that these conditions increase the vulnerability of endemic fishes to parasitism.

The paper also contains a separate taxonomic note describing a new cactus species, [[Sclerocactus Schlesseri]], from southeastern Nevada.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The woundfin is a listed endangered cyprinid endemic to the Virgin River system. Other fishes of the drainage include the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), Virgin spinedace (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*), desert sucker (*Catostomus clarkii*) and flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*). The introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) is also present. Before this study, parasites of these fishes were poorly documented. [[Hoffman]] (1967) listed no parasites for the woundfin.

**Parasite groups discussed.**
- Digenetic trematodes, especially the strigeid metacercariae of *Posthodiplostomum minimum*, which encyst in the viscera and skin and cause "black spot."
- Pseudophyllidean cestodes, including *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*, whose adults live in the foregut and have a copepod first intermediate host.
- Ciliate ectoparasites of the genus [[Trichodina]], found on the gills.

**Study objectives.**
1. Identify the parasite species infecting woundfin and other fishes at selected sites.
2. Determine the frequency, abundance and temporal variation of parasites in woundfin during summer low flow.
3. Determine the relationship between parasitism and the immediate habitat of the host.
4. Determine the pathogenicity of the parasites to their hosts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life cycles and host effects.** Cestode life cycles in Virgin River fishes were not documented in detail. Adults are thought to develop after a fish ingests an infected copepod, since no primarily piscivorous fishes are present to act as a second intermediate host. Literature cited by the authors links cestode infection to impaired growth, nutrition, reproduction and swimming performance. Examples include [[Sockeye Salmon]] infected with *Eubothrium salvelini*, which showed reduced seawater adaptation and greater zinc sensitivity. Gut impaction and organ compression by parasites are also described.

**Introduced parasite.** The Asian fish tapeworm was introduced into the United States with imported [[Grass Carp]]. It is regarded as a dangerous parasite in European aquaculture. It is a thermophilic species and can infect many fish hosts, and in the Virgin River it apparently arrived with the red shiner in the spring of 1984.

**Disturbance gradient.** The authors used the earlier classification of [[Deacon and Hardy]] (1984), in which river reaches above the Washington and Mesquite diversions are "undisturbed" and reaches below them are "disturbed" by irrigation withdrawals. The disturbed reaches include [[Mesquite]], [[Twin Bridges]] and the [[Santa Clara River]] inflow. The undisturbed reaches include [[Beaver Dam Wash]], [[Hurricane Bridge]] and [[Berry Springs]].

- Mean and minimum flows in May-November were substantially reduced below the Washington Diversion (Bloomington) in 1985 (Table 7). A similar reduction occurred below the Mesquite Diversion (Riverside) in 1979 (Table 8).
- Water quality in disturbed reaches is reduced, largely as a result of agricultural irrigation return flows.
- In June 1985 discharge from a salt spring, [[Pah Tempe Spring]], suddenly and dramatically increased. This added to a sudden reduction in water quality throughout the Utah segment one to two months before the collections.

## Key Findings

**1. Parasite prevalence in woundfin.**
- Samples of woundfin were taken at four sites in July and August 1985 (25 fish in July and 23 in August, Table 2). Other fishes were examined for comparison.
- *Posthodiplostomum minimum* was the dominant parasite. Its prevalence was very high at several sites, reaching 100% in some samples (Table 2, OCR-garbled). Its intensity was also high at the upstream "undisturbed" sites.
- Other parasites recorded on some individuals included a nematode in the intestine, an anchor worm (*Lernaea*), and gill-infecting protozoans and crustaceans.
- No cestodes were detected in woundfin at any of the four Utah sites in 1985. The authors note that this may reflect seasonal variation, since sampling covered only late July and August.

**2. Disturbed versus undisturbed segments.**
- "Disturbed" segments (Mesquite, Twin Bridges, Santa Clara inflow) carried heavier parasite loads than "undisturbed" segments (Beaver Dam Wash, Hurricane Bridge, Berry Springs).
- Trematode metacercariae in woundfin were much more abundant in the lower, disturbed river near St. George than upstream.
- The authors hypothesize that reduced flows force fishes into slow pools, which favour snail intermediate hosts and piscivorous birds that complete the trematode life cycle. Water-quality stress may also increase parasite loads.

**3. Cestode infection in cyprinids (1979).**
- *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi* was found in all cyprinids in the lower mainstem in Arizona and Nevada. It was later identified by [[Hoffman]] and Jensen.
- The roundtail chub was infected more frequently and with more tapeworms per fish than other native species. The red shiner was infected more frequently and heavily than all native species except the roundtail chub.
- Infection frequency and density rose seasonally, with heavy loads in fall (September and December).
- Among the 17 roundtail chub examined, 3 (18%) carried Asian fish tapeworms.
- In Nevada, 37 adult Asian tapeworms were recovered from 24 of 88 red shiners (27%) collected at Mesquite on 15 or 25 August 1985 (the paper gives both dates).

**4. Pathogenicity.**
- Heavy metacercarial burdens (several hundred per fish) in the liver, kidney, heart and other viscera are considered to be a likely cause of mortality or morbidity.
- Histopathology showed encapsulated metacercariae, organ compression, hemorrhage, inflammatory response and tissue degradation, including the liver, mesenteries and intestine.
- The authors state that there is no experimental evidence that infected fish die from these infections.

**5. Ectoparasites.**
- Ciliates of the genus *Trichodina* were commonly observed on the gills of woundfin in August 1985. They have been described as causing little disease in healthy fish but can be serious in weakened ones.

**6. Management implications.**
- The authors warn that the Asian fish tapeworm poses a serious risk to the endangered woundfin and roundtail chub. Its effects on the species have been documented elsewhere in the western United States.
- Restoring stable, undiverted flows and improving water quality is likely to reduce parasite burdens. The authors propose that flow management is the most practical lever for reducing parasitism.

**7. Separate taxonomic note: *Sclerocactus schlesseri*.**
- A new cactus species, *Sclerocactus schlesseri* Heil & Welsh, was described from a Tertiary lacustrine deposit in Lincoln County, Nevada, discovered in 1981 by Dr. David Schlesser.
- It is distinguished from *S. whipplei* by narrower stems (4-8 cm versus 5-15 cm), smaller flowers (3-4 cm versus 3.5-5 cm) and densely pubescent spines.
- It is distinguished from *S. pubispinus* by larger flowers (3-4 cm versus 2.5-3.5 cm) and a longer upper central spine (3-5.5 cm versus 0.5-3.5 cm).
- The holotype is deposited at BRY (Brigham Young University).
