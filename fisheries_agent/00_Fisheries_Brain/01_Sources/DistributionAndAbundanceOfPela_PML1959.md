# Distribution and Abundance of Pelagic Fish in Lake Powell, Utah, and Lake Mead, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #Clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Gordon A. Mueller and Michael J. Horn (Western North American Naturalist, 2004) quantified the pelagic fish communities of [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Mead]], two large, nutrient-limited reservoirs on the [[Colorado River]]. Quarterly surveys from 1995 to 1998 combined vertical gill nets with a scientific echosounder across 25 monitoring sites. Pelagic biomass and density varied strongly by season, year and location, peaking in August and declining with distance downstream. Most of the reservoir area held few or no fish, and the fisheries were judged vulnerable to boom-and-bust cycles driven by low primary production.

## Background & Description

- **Reservoirs:** Lake Powell and Lake Mead are described as the two largest reservoirs (by volume) in the Western Hemisphere. Both lie in the arid southwestern United States and were built for water storage, flood control and hydroelectric power. They bracket [[Grand Canyon]], with Lake Powell roughly 400 km upstream of Lake Mead.
- **Fishery:** Both support economically important [[Striped Bass]] fisheries, with a combined annual harvest exceeding 1 million fish.
- **Gap addressed:** Little quantitative information existed on striped bass populations in nutrient-limited reservoirs, in contrast to more productive waters. The study aimed to describe the distribution and biomass of pelagic fish communities in both reservoirs.
- **Lake Powell:** Maximum surface area 65,315 ha, depth 171 m, active storage 3.33 × 10^10 m³. Principal inflows are the [[Colorado River]] (89%) and San Juan River (11%). Water flows about 385 km through Grand Canyon before entering Lake Mead.
- **Lake Mead:** Formed behind [[Hoover Dam]] in Black Canyon, about 40 km south of Las Vegas. At full pool it is about 190 m deep, covers 165,815 ha and holds 8.95 × 10^10 m³. Principal inflows are the Colorado River (~97%), Virgin River (1.7%) and Las Vegas Wash (1%), the latter carrying treated sewage effluent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Nutrient status:** Both reservoirs are oligotrophic to mildly mesotrophic. Primary production (chlorophyll-a) often declines to about 2 µg·L⁻¹ with distance from nutrient inputs, which is considered inadequate to support planktonic feeders such as threadfin shad. Water clarity often exceeds 15 m.
- **Sampling design:** 25 monitoring sites (14 in Lake Powell, 11 in Lake Mead) represented basins, main-stem canyons, side canyons and inflows. Surveys were quarterly, lasting 5 to 7 days at Lake Powell and 3 to 5 days at Lake Mead.
- **Gill netting:** Multi-mesh monofilament gill nets were suspended from floating spools to depths greater than 30 m.
- **Acoustics:** Single-beam and dual-beam scientific echosounders operated at 420 kHz (dual-beam at 420 kHz and 120 kHz configurations as reported). Biomass was estimated as weight-to-volume ratios from echo energy, and densities by echo-counting. Night surveys were reserved for inflow areas, where schooling fish were expected to disperse at night.
- **Effort:** Cumulative transect distance was 70.9 km for Lake Powell and 2,321 km (total of about 3,320 km including Lake Mead) in the study, with 384 transect files recorded across the program.

## Key Findings

- **Species composition:** Gill nets captured 449 fish. [[Striped Bass]] (57% of catch at Lake Powell/Lake Mead combined) and [[Threadfin Shad]] (12% and 80% of catches at the two reservoirs respectively) dominated, followed by [[Common Carp]], [[Walleye]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] (<1%).
- **Patchy distribution:** Each reservoir held concentrations of pelagic fish separated by wide expanses of habitat with fewer than 10 kg·ha⁻¹.
- **Peak biomass:** Lake Powell peaked at the Colorado River inflow at 709.7 kg·ha⁻¹ (±116.5); Lake Mead peaked at the Las Vegas Wash at 291.9 kg·ha⁻¹ (±158.2). These locations supported estimated densities of 124,669 fish·ha⁻¹ and 15,131 fish·ha⁻¹, respectively.
- **Total reservoir biomass:** Peaked in August 1996, at 10,852,738 ± 5,195,556 kg (about 27.6 × 10⁷ fish) for Lake Powell and 1,926,697 ± 592,991 kg (about 10.8 × 10⁷ fish) for Lake Mead.
- **Seasonal pattern:** Biomass was lowest in May. In May 1996 Lake Mead supported 65% of Lake Powell's biomass (296,736 kg vs. 453,097 kg); in May 1997 it supported 62% (101,016 kg vs. 162,262 kg). Biomass in both reservoirs was lower before spawning in May and highest in August.
- **Distance gradient:** Biomass generally decreased downstream. About 53% (n = 108) of Lake Powell's stations and 72% (n = 87) of Lake Mead's stations had biomass below 10 kg·ha⁻¹. In August 1996, biomass exceeded 20 kg·ha⁻¹ throughout Lake Powell (mean 197 kg·ha⁻¹ vs. 28 kg·ha⁻¹ in Lake Mead).
- **Annual decline:** Average density in Lake Powell fell from 26,188 to 2,605 fish·ha⁻¹ between August 1996 and 1997. Total fish numbers dropped from 276 million to 90 million. In Lake Mead, density fell from 2,394 to 1,840 fish·ha⁻¹, from 108 million to 66 million fish.
- **Variance:** Schooling fish made estimates highly variable in sparsely populated areas, so 90% confidence limits were used. Sample variance in denser areas ranged from 20% to 30%.
- **Management implications:** Fertilization experiments (including about 114,000 L of ammonium phosphate applied to 12,000 ha of Lake Mead) raised productivity only briefly and were judged economically nonviable. State agencies liberalized creel limits on striped bass to reduce emaciated fish and die-offs. The authors recommend night surveys and more transects in areas with patchy fish distribution.
- **Nutrient loading:** Nutrient loading from the Colorado River into Lake Mead has declined since Glen Canyon Dam was completed in 1964. The authors suggest pelagic communities are vulnerable to shifts in nutrient loading and predator-prey interactions.
