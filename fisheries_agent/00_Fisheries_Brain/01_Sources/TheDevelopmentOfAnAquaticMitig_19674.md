# Development of an Aquatic Mitigation Plan for the Uinta River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This technical paper, by David L. Wegner of the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] Instream Flow and Aquatic Systems Group in Fort Collins, Colorado, develops an aquatic mitigation plan for the [[Uinta River]] in Utah. The [[Central Utah Project]], including the proposed [[Uinta Dam And Reservoir]], would alter the natural flow regime and reduce the trout habitat below the dam. The study uses computer simulation of hydraulic and physical habitat in a side channel area, using the [[Incremental Methodology]] and [[Physical Habitat Simulation Model]] (PHABSIM). Seven instream habitat alternatives were evaluated for changes in [[Weighted Usable Area]] and construction cost, expressed as a [[Cost Effective Index]], and were assessed against two fishery management strategies: stocking of adult and subadult fish, and development of a reproducing population.

## Background & Description

- **Study subject:** [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), the species managed in the Uinta River fishery.
- **Problem:** Dams and diversions alter stream flow, velocity, depth, and water quality, which in turn changes the aquatic species present. The planned Uinta Dam and Reservoir would regulate water for downstream irrigation and municipal use.
- **Study design:** Three study sites were compared: one main channel area and two side channel sites. This paper analyzes Side Channel Study Site Number One. Sites were selected for the quality of existing fish habitat and their representativeness of the river. The paper notes that steep slopes, lack of pool and spawning habitat, and extreme flow fluctuation are typical of the entire upper Uinta River.
- **Prior evidence:** According to [[Mullan 1975]], inadequate stream habitat restricts the size of annual standing crops and increases winter and spring mortality of the trout population.
- **Method framework:** The [[Incremental Methodology]] has three components: (1) hydraulic simulation of the reach, (2) determination of depth, velocity, substrate, and cover by area, and (3) application of habitat evaluation criteria for each species and life stage under each flow or channel condition (Stalnaker, 1979).
- **Reach simulation:** Cross-sectional transects are each divided into 9 to 20 subsections, and each subsection is treated as an essentially separate channel for unobserved discharges.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat drivers:** Fish distribution is governed by the interaction of depth, velocity, substrate, and cover, and species select the most favorable habitat structure available.
- **Dynamic equilibrium:** A river is normally in dynamic equilibrium, so any change requires adjustment of hydraulics and of the species' responses. The paper assumes distribution and abundance respond to all flow parameters to varying degrees, not to any single one.
- **Flow regime:** The project hydrograph provides high irrigation flows from May through September, with reduced storage flows for the rest of the year. The side channel offers a possibility of regulating flows for the fishery.
- **Sediment stability:** Degradation and aggradation of bed material were analyzed using the [[Critical Tractive Force]], the condition at which bed grains begin to move. Only a flood stage over the top of the banks was found to cause severe degradation. Bed-load accumulation is not significant if excessive erosion or flow extremes are contained.
- **Broader ecosystem:** The paper notes that the food base and inherent water quality must also be examined, since carrying capacity expands only to the lowest limiting factor.
- **Simulated manipulations:** Increasing pool depths, adding weirs and pools, changing riffle substrate, varying stream flows, and adding boulders.

## Key Findings

**Alternatives tested (seven total):**

1. Original cross-sectional shape and profile, under the variable flow regime.
2. Original pools deepened by an average of one foot (0.304 m).
3. Maximum pool depth increased by three feet (0.912 m).
4. A weir placed at cross-section 1 + 40.
5. A weir at cross-section 1 + 40 combined with pool depths increased by one foot (0.304 m).
6. A boulder placed in the stream.
7. Gravel placed over the riffle at cross-section 0 + 00.

**Economic and habitat results:**

- Changes in Weighted Usable Area could not be converted directly to monetary units. Instead, construction costs were estimated from cross-sectional and profile data, and a Cost Effective Index was calculated for each alternative.
- The index is defined as: Cost Effective Index = Percent Change in Weighted Usable Area / Costs ($). Costs include operation, maintenance, and replacement, discounted over a 50-year period.
- The weir and increased pool depth alternatives produced the largest increase in winter adult habitat.
- Adding boulders and increasing pool depth was the most cost-effective way to increase adult habitat (see Figure 1).
- Adding gravel to a riffle provided the most cost-effective development of spawning habitat, the key requirement for a reproducing population.
- Comparing alternatives by Weighted Usable Area alone does not allow an even comparison; both habitat gain and cost must be weighed.

**Management implications:**

- The current stocking-based fishery requires additional winter habitat for adults.
- A reproducing population requires additional spawning, incubation, fry, and juvenile habitat.
- Trade-offs between cost and habitat gain must be weighed to decide when mitigation is achieved and enhancement begins.
- PHABSIM quantifies how flow and hydraulic changes affect available fish habitat, and identifies life stages and flow levels that may limit population survival. The [[Incremental Methodology]] gives biologists and engineers a shared basis for communication.
