# Spikedace (Meda fulgida) Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan for the [[Spikedace]], a threatened minnow endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico, was prepared by Paul C. Marsh of Arizona State University for Region 2 (Albuquerque, New Mexico). The species has been extirpated from most of its historic range and survives in only a few streams. The plan sets out recovery objectives, recovery criteria, 9 actions needed, an estimated cost of $350,000 per year over a minimum 20-year recovery period (in 1991 dollars, excluding land acquisition), and a proposed critical habitat appendix. The plan also includes a Appendix B summarizing public and agency comments and the Service's responses. The document is dated September 1991 (the cover date) and the plan itself cites 1990 as the publication year in its citation guidance.

## Background & Description

The [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) is a slender, stream-dwelling member of the minnow family ([[Cyprinidae]]). It was originally described by Girard in 1856 (cited as 1857 in the reference list) and is endemic to the Gila River system and, historically, the San Pedro River in Sonora, Mexico. Adults are small; standard length to total length is converted by SL = 0.85 TL - 0.12 (r² = 0.99, n = 100). Maximum recorded size is near 65 mm SL in Aravaipa Creek, Arizona, and 68 mm SL in the upper Gila River, New Mexico.

Spikedace were listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (proposed June 18, 1985; final rule July 1, 1985). The species is also listed as threatened by Arizona and as a state-protected species in New Mexico.

**Life history highlights**
- **Reproduction:** Males are territorial; females may spawn multiple times over a few days to several weeks. Eggs are presumed to be broadcast over substrate; no fertilized ova had been recovered. Fecundity ranges from 90 to 250 ova and correlates with length and age.
- **Growth:** Young grow rapidly in summer and autumn, reaching 35 to 40 mm SL by November. Longevity is typically one to two years; few fish reach age three, and very few survive to age four.
- **Diet:** Carnivorous, feeding mainly on aquatic and terrestrial insects drifting in the stream. Important prey include larval Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, and Chironomidae, with Plecoptera and Orthoptera taken in larger quantities by bigger fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.** Spikedace inhabit shallow, flowing water over gravel, cobble, and sand. The primary habitat is the shear zone where fast water meets slow water. Survival requires permanent flowing water; populations decline or disappear where streams become intermittent or ephemeral.

**Threats.** Dams, water diversion, watershed deterioration, livestock grazing, channelization, groundwater pumping, and introduction of non-native predatory and competitive fishes are the main causes of decline. Habitat change and non-native fishes together are considered responsible for the decline, though the relative importance of each is unresolved.

**Non-native fishes.** Ictalurid catfishes, centrarchids ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], green sunfish), and salmonids at higher elevations prey on or compete with spikedace. The [[Red Shiner]] is considered especially important: where sympatric, it occupies similar habitats, and the concurrent decline of spikedace and expansion of red shiner is described as strong circumstantial evidence of displacement.

**Co-occurring species.** The [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) is a companion species in the plan; both are addressed in parallel plans. Other native fishes mentioned include Sonora sucker, desert sucker, and roundtail chub.

**Water quality and habitat disturbance.** A wastewater spill at the Cananea Mine in Sonora killed aquatic life, including all fishes, across a 100-km downstream reach (Eberhardt 1981).

## Key Findings

**Recovery objective and criteria**
- Objective: protection of existing populations, restoration of habitat in portions of the historic range, and eventual reintroduction.
- Criteria: quantitative standards for a self-sustaining population, including absolute numbers, age-class structure, and reproduction. Delisting depends on establishing such populations.

**Recovery actions (9 listed in the plan)**
1. Protection of existing populations.
2. Monitoring of existing populations.
3. Studies of interactions between spikedace and non-native fishes.
4. Quantification of natural and effects of habitat modification.
5. Enhancement of habitat in occupied areas.
6. Reintroduction into historic range.
7. Establishment of criteria for downlisting and delisting.
8. Hatchery propagation.
9. Information and education.

**Key management recommendations**
- Maintain perennial flows, including formal agreements that prevent damming or diversion that alters natural flow regimes (e.g., a BLM instream flow application on Aravaipa Creek).
- Develop regulations that discourage transport and stocking of non-native fishes, especially red shiner, into occupied habitats.
- Restrict live bait use and bait seining in occupied reaches.
- Consider barriers, such as a proposed structure on Aravaipa Creek, to exclude non-native fishes.
- Establish a computerized database of historic and current distribution and abundance.
- Conduct biannual (spring and autumn) population estimates using standardized methods such as depletion sampling or mark-and-recapture.

**Cost and timeline**
- Estimated total cost: $350,000 per year over a minimum 20-year recovery period, in 1991 dollars, excluding land acquisition.
- Preliminary success evaluation for reintroductions five years after release, with an additional five years of monitoring before a final judgment.

**Critical habitat (Appendix A, proposed)**
- Arizona: Upper Verde River (near the Verde River confluence) and Aravaipa Creek (Graham and Pinal Counties), among others.
- New Mexico: three sections of the Gila River totaling about 73 km (Grant and Catron Counties), plus Eagle Creek recommended for designation.
- Campbell Blue Creek was retained as the correct name for the stream in the proposal.

**Public comments (Appendix B)**
- Comments from about 50 reviewers, including agencies, universities, conservation groups, and local landowners, were addressed. Many dealt with water storage, impoundments, and flood control, which the Service held are not the subject of recovery plans.
- The Service stated that no NEPA document, Regulatory Impact Analysis, or Takings Implications Assessment was required for the conceptual plan.
- Recent sampling confirmed spikedace still occupies the upper Verde River (response 19).
- The Service does not advocate experimental population designation under Section 10(j) for either fish at this time.
