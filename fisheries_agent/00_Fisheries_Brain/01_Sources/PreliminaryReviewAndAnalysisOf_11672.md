# Preliminary Review and Analysis of Central Arizona Water Conservation District Report on U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Opinion

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Poeciliidae #endangered-species-act

## Executive Summary

This document is the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]'s preliminary review (July 3, 1995) of a May 1995 rebuttal report submitted by the [[Central Arizona Water Conservation District]] (CAWCD) challenging the Service's April 20, 1994 biological opinion on the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The biological opinion concluded that CAP operations would jeopardize four listed fish species—[[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)—through introduction and spread of nonnative fish species in the [[Gila River Basin]]. The Service defends its jeopardy finding, reasonable and prudent alternatives, and consultation procedures against CAWCD's procedural and scientific challenges. The review addresses fundamental disagreements about cumulative threats, fish movement capabilities, barrier effectiveness, and the legal framework for [[Section 7 Consultation]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Background & Description

The biological opinion originated from a February 12, 1991 request by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BR) for formal consultation on "possible impacts to federally-listed endangered species for the Central Arizona Project due to the transfer of nonnative fish." The consultation scope covered the entire [[Gila River Basin]] excluding the [[Santa Cruz River Basin]]. The draft jeopardy opinion was issued May 30, 1991, with the final opinion dated April 20, 1994, after three years of extensive negotiation.

**Listed Species Status:**
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*): Threatened (listed 1986)
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*): Threatened (listed 1986)
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*): Endangered
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Proposed endangered during consultation, listed 1991

The biological opinion found jeopardy for all four species and adverse modification of critical habitat for spikedace, loach minnow, and razorback sucker. CAWCD's occupied habitat omissions included [[Eagle Creek]] and middle [[Gila River]] for spikedace; Eagle Creek, [[San Francisco River]], [[Blue River]], and [[Tularosa River]] for loach minnow; and [[Bonita Creek]] and Eagle Creek for razorback sucker.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Central Arizona Project]] is a massive water delivery system with an estimated cost of $4 billion and a 100-year operational lifespan. The project transports water through an extensive aqueduct system connecting previously fragmented portions of the Gila River Basin, creating pathways for nonnative fish dispersal.

**Nonnative Species Threat Mechanisms:**
1. **Direct introduction** through CAP canal system and turnouts
2. **Enhanced connectivity** linking fragmented basin portions
3. **Bait bucket transfer** facilitated by increased nonnative availability
4. **Movement through intermittent channels** during flood events and trailing flow declines
5. **Human-mediated transport** around physical barriers

The Service emphasizes that "dry" river channels in Arizona are only temporarily dry. Winter floods often have trailing declines lasting days to months, allowing fish with colonizing life histories to move through temporarily watered channels. Isolated perennial pools, backwaters, irrigation returns, and seepage areas scattered along channels sustain populations between flow events, enabling gradual upstream or downstream spread over years or decades.

**Critical Habitat Areas:**
- [[Aravaipa Creek]]: Critical habitat for spikedace and loach minnow
- [[Upper Gila River]]: Critical habitat for multiple species
- [[San Carlos Lake]]: Designated critical habitat for razorback sucker
- [[Upper San Pedro River]]: Area with moderate to low nonnative presence requiring protection

The environmental baseline already includes substantial nonnative fish populations from multiple sources: [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]]' [[San Carlos Irrigation Project]] (SCIP), historical gamefish stocking by the Service and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], private aquaculture escapes, unauthorized stockings, and aquarium fish releases.

## Key Findings

**Service Defense of Biological Opinion:**

1. **Cumulative Threat Principle**: The Service rejects CAWCD's "someone else's fault" argument. The presence of other threats (livestock grazing, timber harvest, groundwater pumping, existing nonnative populations) does not negate the need to protect listed species from CAP effects. Rather, existing threats increase the risk from additional CAP effects. Under Section 7 regulations, CAP effects are added to the environmental baseline and cumulative impacts to determine if the jeopardy threshold is reached.

2. **Degraded Baseline Increases Risk**: CAWCD argued that because nonnative fish are already abundant, CAP's contribution is insignificant. The Service counters that because the nonnative threat is already serious (environmental baseline), even small increases may exceed the jeopardy threshold. The biological opinion found CAP's nonnative contribution to be substantial, not small.

3. **Fish Movement Capabilities**: The Service defends its analysis of fish movement through "dry" channels. Species with colonizing life histories move rapidly during flood events and trailing flow declines. Movement occurs over years or decades through a process of colonization, persistence in isolated perennial areas, and further movement during subsequent flows. One gravid female livebearer entering during favorable conditions could establish a population causing serious losses to listed fish.

4. **Accumulating Probability**: While probability of nonnative ingress at any single CAP point may be small, the large number of potential entry points creates substantial cumulative probability over the 100-year project lifespan. The number of individuals required to establish a threatening population varies by species, habitat conditions, and timing.

5. **Electric Barrier Limitations**: The Service does not consider electric barriers completely effective or fail-safe. No effectiveness studies have been conducted on the [[Salt River Project]] canal barriers despite years of operation. At least two barriers experienced outages due to maintenance and operation difficulties, during which nonnative fish passed through. Physical barriers address only swimming fish movement, not human-mediated transport.

**Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) Cost Analysis:**

- Bureau of Reclamation estimate: $11,800,000 (0.3% of $4 billion project cost)
- CAWCD estimate: $152,056,000 (3.8% of project cost)
- Spread over 100-year lifespan: $118,000/year (BR) or $1,520,056/year (CAWCD)
- Cost discrepancies reflect different construction standards, monitoring approaches, and use of cost-share agreements

The Service considers the RPA economically feasible under either estimate. The RPA includes paired physical barriers with monitoring and nonnative removal between barriers, public education, nonnative fish management, and a nonnative management fund for research.

**Procedural and Legal Issues:**

1. **Consultation Scope**: The Service appropriately expanded scope from a single turnout to basin-wide effects as required by Section 7 regulations covering direct, indirect, interrelated, and interdependent effects.

2. **Applicant Status**: CAWCD was not granted applicant status for this consultation, though BR later granted it for the separate [[Santa Cruz River Basin]] consultation. Applicant status does not affect biological opinion validity.

3. **Species Coverage**: Earlier CAP consultations predated the 1986 listing of spikedace and loach minnow and 1990 proposed listing of razorback sucker. The 1994 opinion does not conflict with earlier opinions but expands coverage to recently listed species.

4. **Recovery Habitat Analysis**: Section 7 regulations define jeopardy as reducing "appreciably the likelihood of both survival and recovery." Analysis must consider recovery habitats regardless of critical habitat designation. Jeopardy conclusions are based on cumulative effects to all populations and habitats, not individual populations alone.

5. **Reinitiation Requirements**: CAWCD's report presents no new information revealing effects not previously considered. All issues were extensively discussed during the three-year consultation. The July 1994 information standards policy was not retroactive and the biological opinion conforms to it.

**Interrelated Effects:**

The biological opinion appropriately considers bait bucket transfer and nonnative predation as interrelated and interdependent effects of CAP. CAP does not cause these activities but increases the availability of nonnative fish for bait bucket transfer and predation on native species. By regulation, these indirect effects must be analyzed in the biological opinion.

**Literature and Best Available Science:**

The Service used all available literature on the four listed species. The literature cited section follows standard scientific practice of citing only sources directly supporting specific points, not providing comprehensive bibliographies. The five-page literature list in CAWCD's report, including recovery plans the Service authored, was not excluded from analysis but was unnecessary to cite for specific points or was redundant to other citations.
