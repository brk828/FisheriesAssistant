# Climate and Ephemeral-Stream Processes: Twentieth-Century Geomorphology and Alluvial Stratigraphy of the Little Colorado River, Arizona

#fluvial-geomorphology #alluvial-stratigraphy #tamarix

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey study by Richard Hereford examines how climate-driven discharge changes shaped the channel and flood plain of the [[Little Colorado River]] in northern Arizona during the twentieth century. The paper is a geomorphology and sedimentology study, not a fish or aquatic-biology study. Its only biological content concerns riparian plants, chiefly the nonnative [[Salt Cedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) and native [[Fremont Cottonwood]] (*Populus fremontii*).

The author argues that erosion dominated from about 1900 to the early 1940s, when the channel was broad, sandy and unvegetated. A decline in large floods and annual discharge in the 1940s and 1950s narrowed the channel, allowed salt cedar to establish, and triggered [[Flood Plain]] aggradation of 2 to 5 m between 1952 and 1978. The paper concludes that the flood plain has probably reached a critical height beyond which further vertical accretion is unlikely under the current discharge regime. It also proposes that flood-plain sediment is largely [[Allocyclic Sedimentation]] (climate-controlled), while channel deposits can be [[Autocyclic Sedimentation]].

## Background & Description

**Research problem.** Stratigraphers and geomorphologists lack consensus on how climate controls stream erosion and aggradation. Common facies models ignore climate, yet alluvial deposits are often the only record of past climate. The problem is especially acute for the late Holocene of the southwestern United States, where several hypotheses compete:

- Poor land use, such as overgrazing (Cooke and Reeves, 1976).
- Climate change (Emmett, 1974; Leopold, 1976; Euler and others, 1979).
- Random, non-climatic variation within the fluvial system (Patton and Schumm, 1981).

Previous studies largely lacked long-term discharge records. This study uses them, which is its main methodological difference.

**Study system.** The Little Colorado is an [[Ephemeral Stream]] draining about 44,000 km² of the arid to semiarid southern [[Colorado Plateau]]. Key physical facts from the text:

- Annual precipitation is under 300 mm.
- Elevation ranges from 1,200 m in the valley to 3,850 m at Humphreys Peak; median elevation is 1,830 m.
- Relief is low and bedrock is largely permeable limestone, sandstone and pyroclastic material, which promote infiltration.
- The river is dry in June, the driest month. Between 33 and 247 days per year had no measurable discharge.
- Runoff is biseasonal: winter frontal snow or rain, and July to September convective storms.
- Pinyon-juniper woodland and desert grassland cover about 70% of the basin and support livestock ranching.

**Geomorphic surfaces.** Two historic surfaces and their deposits record activity:

- The [[Cottonwood Terrace]] stands 2 to 5 m above the channel and carries large cottonwoods that germinated mostly between 1880 and 1905. It was the active flood plain until about 1905.
- The modern [[Flood Plain]] stands 2 to 4 m above the channel and 1 to 3 m below the terrace. It is dominated by salt cedar.
- Deposits are, from oldest to youngest: older channel alluvium (deposited on an erosional unconformity after 1905 to 1937), then flood-plain alluvium.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian vegetation and flood levels.** Vertical zonation of riparian plants follows flood levels, since many species cannot grow below the height of recent floods (Sigafoos, 1964; Everitt, 1968; Wolman, 1971). Salt cedar's spread depended on flood conditions. The Grand Falls germination zone matched a low-flood surface that had been deeply inundated in the 1923 and 1929 floods. Salt cedar had been present in the Little Colorado system only since 1937, despite being introduced to the western United States by 1854 and being known near Winslow by 1909. The author attributes this delay to the large floods of the early twentieth century, including five of the largest recorded floods between 1923 and 1938.

**Salt cedar as a driver or passenger.** The paper reviews a debate. Hadley (1961), Robinson (1965) and Graf (1978, 1979) credited salt cedar's spread with changing channel morphology. Everitt (1979, 1980) argued that its spread coincided with other environmental changes and that salt cedar stabilizes sediment no better than native species. The author concludes that the channel narrowing and vegetation spread were related events, both responding to reduced discharge, with salt cedar then trapping sediment during overbank flows.

**Human activity.** Two possible human drivers were examined:

- *Overgrazing* began after settlement in the 1870s and 1880s and may have affected infiltration and runoff. Early workers (Colton, 1937; Lockett and Snow, 1939; Thornthwaite and others, 1942) linked it to erosion, but Gregory (1917) attributed erosion to climate. Range-condition data are inadequate to test hydrologic effects of grazing, and Davey (1980) found moisture to be the main limit on grass productivity.
- *Water consumption and storage* can reduce floods and cause narrowing, as on the [[Platte River]] in Nebraska. The Little Colorado is largely free-flowing, its reservoirs are small, and annual discharge showed no steady decline, so the author concludes that aggradation probably occurred without significant human interference.

**Climate signals.** Climate records from Flagstaff, Winslow and Springerville, plus seven-station tree-ring width indices, were examined. Mean annual temperature was reported as 10.5 °C for 1933 to 1961 and 10.0 °C for 1962 to 1980. The author notes that the 1900s to 1940s were a period of rising Northern Hemisphere temperature and different atmospheric circulation.

**Comparable systems.** Similar erosion and aggradation sequences have been reported at [[Black Mesa]], near Santa Fe, New Mexico, in [[Chaco Canyon]], on the Green, Colorado and Fremont Rivers, and in [[Grand Canyon]] after [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closed in 1963. In Grand Canyon, regulated flows let salt cedar spread rapidly.

## Key Findings

### 1. Erosion dominated the early twentieth century

- The channel was broad, sandy and unvegetated for at least the first 40 years of the century.
- The channel was about 50% wider than at present in the late 1890s until at least the late 1930s, based on 1926 and 1934 topographic maps and 1936 aerial photographs.
- Erosion ran from about 1905 to 1937, marked by an erosional unconformity between terrace and older channel alluvium.

### 2. Discharge declined in the 1940s and 1950s

- Average annual discharge fell to about 57% of the preceding period.
- The 1942 to 1961 period is distinctive: 15 years were below the long-term median.
- Channel narrowing and flood-plain initiation occurred between 1937 and 1954.

### 3. Salt cedar invaded as flood frequency dropped

- The oldest salt cedar dates to 1937. Mature trees at Grand Falls germinated between 1947 and 1961 (eight living trees).
- The author concludes that salt cedar naturalization was delayed until large floods became less frequent in the early 1940s.

### 4. Flood-plain aggradation followed climate-linked floods

- Flood-plain alluvium accreted 2 to 5 m between 1952 and 1978.
- Seven floods between 1952 and 1978 were the main drivers of aggradation. Dated floods include January 1952, September 1969 or 1970, October 1971, October 1972 and April 1973, plus December 1978.
- The 1974 flood was the lowest annual flood recorded at the Cameron gage. The author notes it was followed by three years only slightly higher.
- Floods mostly occurred in wet years, while the older and intermediate germination horizons corresponded to the 1942 to 1956 regional drought.

### 5. Stratigraphy records vertical accretion

- Flood-plain sediment is finer than channel sand. Channel sand averages 0.8 to 1 phi coarser than levee and overbank sand.
- Flood-plain beds show ripple lamination, climbing ripples and centriclinal cross-stratification, typical of waning overbank flows.
- Three germination horizons were identified: older (1937 to 1951), intermediate (1953 to 1969 or 1970) and younger (1974 to 1977 or 1978). Intervening years were periods of deposition.
- Stratigraphic cut-and-fill relations at Tolchico, Cameron and Moenkopi Wash Northwest were attributed to flood height and levee relief, not to successive erosion and infilling.

### 6. Salt-cedar rings are usually annual

- Salt cedar is deciduous, and rings are likely annual because growth is cued by seasonal temperature.
- Young trees at Tolchico that germinated after the 1980 runoff had one ring in late 1980, two in late 1981 and three in late 1982.
- J. S. Dean (University of Arizona) found 16 samples cross-datable, though the trees are too young for unequivocal identification of ring patterns. Salt cedar may therefore be a usable [[Dendrochronology]] tool, with caveats about false and missing rings.

### 7. A geomorphic threshold changed sedimentation in 1980

- The 1980 floods were as large as earlier floods that overtopped the banks, yet in 1980 the banks were not overtopped and were eroded instead. The flood plain had effectively become a terrace.
- Vertical accretion is limited by flood-plain height. Above a critical height, floods are confined to the channel, average velocity rises, and lateral accretion and bank erosion increase. This is a [[Geomorphic Threshold]], described by Bull (1979).
- The shift from [[Vertical Accretion]] to [[Lateral Accretion]] occurred without a change in discharge, so the author describes it as autocyclic.

### 8. Flood-plain aggradation was mainly allocyclic

- Flood-plain deposits are constructional landforms controlled by discharge and climate. They are therefore mainly allocyclic (Miall, 1980).
- Further flood-plain accretion would require continuing climatic change. Channel deposits, by contrast, are reworked and record only recent events.
- The author cautions that the finding may not apply to all ephemeral streams. Baker (1977) suggests basin morphology and geology determine how sensitive a stream is to climate, and the Little Colorado may respond even to short-term fluctuations because of its size and characteristics.

### 9. Limitations and uncertainty

- Salt-cedar germination dates were not cross-dated, and the sample sizes at each locality were small, so the true nondepositional intervals may not be bracketed.
- Tables of discharge and precipitation statistics are partly illegible in the source and are not reproduced here.
- The rainfall and temperature series come from three to two stations and may not represent basin-wide climate.
