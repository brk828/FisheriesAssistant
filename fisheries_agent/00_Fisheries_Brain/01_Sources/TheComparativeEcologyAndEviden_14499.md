# Resource Partitioning in Two Pholis Gunnels from Southern British Columbia Eelgrass Beds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pholidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the ecology of two sympatric, morphologically similar gunnels, [[Pholis Laeta]] and [[Pholis Ornata]], in [[Saanich Inlet]], British Columbia. Sampling was carried out at two sites, [[Mill Bay]] and [[Patricia Bay]], between 1980 and 1983, with more than 1000 fish sampled. The authors found that microhabitat depth use, intertidal habitat use, prey type, and the location of prey taken differed between the species in fish older than one year. Prey size and activity patterns were similar. The author proposes that these differences may have facilitated coexistence, with winter segregation during breeding possibly reducing hybridization. Competition is suggested but not demonstrated.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Pholis Laeta]] and [[Pholis Ornata]] are morphologically similar. They differ mainly in pigmentation pattern, the presence or absence of an interorbital pore, and the number of dentary teeth in adults.
- **Dentition:** Small fish (<60 mm SL) of both species have similar tooth numbers. By about 140 mm SL, *P. ornata* has roughly twice as many teeth as *P. laeta* (approximately 50 versus 25).
- **Maximum lengths reported in the literature:** *P. laeta* 250 mm; *P. ornata* 305 mm. The study's fish were smaller, and the author judged it unlikely that these maxima are routinely reached in shallow Saanich Inlet water.
- **Theoretical framing:** The study draws on [[Resource Partitioning]] and [[Interspecific Competition]] theory. It notes that resource partitioning is common in nature, but that competition cannot be assumed to cause overlap reduction without experimental tests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study setting.** Both sites have subtidal [[Eelgrass Bed]] habitat (*[[Zostera Marina]]*) with similar oceanographic conditions. They differ slightly in beach slope (4.5% at Mill Bay versus 2.9% at Patricia Bay) and in intertidal small stones, which occur at Mill Bay only.

**Seasonal environment.** Surface water temperature and salinity in [[Saanich Inlet]] typically fall from 13 to 6 °C and from 29 to 25‰ between October and November (Herlinveaux 1962). These changes coincided with the fish's disappearance from eelgrass beds and may have triggered breeding behaviour.

**Geographic comparison.** *P. ornata* occurs in estuarine intertidal rocky habitat, in the absence of *P. laeta*, at Grays Harbour (WA), Tillamook Bay (OR) and Yaquina Bay (OR). *P. laeta* occupies exposed coastal habitat in those regions. The author suggests that the common, larger *P. laeta* in Saanich Inlet intertidal zones may displace *P. ornata* to deeper water in winter.

## Key Findings

### 1. Occurrence and Abundance
- Densities of both species in eelgrass were highest in summer, with *P. laeta* more common. Both were effectively absent in winter.
- Fish disappeared from the eelgrass in November 1980. This preceded by at least two months any reduction in the eelgrass standing stock, so the emigration was not caused by a loss of cover.
- *P. laeta* showed no significant depth-zone preference (P > 0.10). *P. ornata* had its largest proportion in the shallowest zone (P < 0.001).
- Young of the year of both species were captured from the eelgrass canopy, but adults usually were not.
- *P. ornata* was disproportionately captured in intertidal traps (P < 0.001).
- In winter, breeding pairs and juvenile *P. laeta* were found under intertidal rocks. Only one *P. ornata* was captured intertidally.

### 2. Diet
- Small fish of both species fed mainly on harpacticoid copepods.
- Large *P. laeta* switched to caprellid amphipods (*[[Caprella Laeviuscula]]*) and the shrimp *Hippolyte clarki*, which live on eelgrass blades.
- *P. ornata* at intermediate sizes consumed *[[Macoma]]* siphons and tanaids (*Leptochelia dubia*), and at larger sizes gammarid amphipods (*Anisogammarus pugettensis*). These are substrate-associated prey.
- Diet concordance among individual fish was significant in more *P. laeta* size groups than in *P. ornata* (Table 3 of the source).
- Prey size increased with fish size but did not differ significantly between species. Within-phenotype (WPC, 90%) and between-phenotype (BPC, 10%) components were identical in both species, so both are generalist users of a range of prey sizes.
- Diet overlap was generally high among similar-sized fish within a species and decreased as size difference increased.
- Principal component analysis separated three groups: small fish (<100 mm) of both species, large *P. laeta* (>100 mm), and large *P. ornata* (>100 mm). Two factors explained 85% of the variation.
- Both species had significantly more food in the stomach at midmorning than at night.

### 3. Activity Patterns
- Activity peaked at dawn under both constant-dark and natural-photoperiod conditions. A dusk peak occurred under photoperiod only.
- The evening peak was absent in constant dark and may not relate to feeding, given the high proportion of empty stomachs in night-caught fish.
- The author concludes that both species are likely visual daytime predators, and that activity differences are minor compared with habitat and diet differences.

### 4. Age and Growth
- *P. laeta* showed at least three cohorts. Mean length was about 81 mm SL at first annulus, 123 mm SL by the end of the second year, and up to 147 mm SL by June of the third year. A 159.1 mm specimen had six annuli.
- *P. ornata* showed two cohorts and reached about 80 mm SL at first annulus. Specimens up to 117 mm were in their second year in June 1980.
- Growth rates were similar between the species, but *P. laeta* had more large or old individuals. One-year-old *P. ornata* were larger than those in Yaquina Bay, suggesting faster growth at Saanich Inlet.

### 5. Interpretation and Conclusions
- Microhabitat, prey, and breeding location differed between the species in fish of one year and older. These differences may have facilitated coexistence.
- The differences may result from past or present [[Interspecific Competition]], or from the two species having different resource use before they came together.
- Divergent habitat use was correlated with prey microhabitat: *P. laeta*'s association with eelgrass fits its prey, and *P. ornata*'s intertidal use fits its substrate-bound prey.
- Winter breeding segregation is suggested to act as a premating isolating mechanism, reducing the possibility of [[Hybridization]].
- The author cautions that [[Niche Overlap]] alone cannot demonstrate competition. Confirming it would require experiments such as removal tests, perturbation analysis, or tests for niche overdispersion.
- Dentition may explain feeding differences. *P. ornata* may bite off muscular *Macoma* siphons, while *P. laeta* usually swallows prey whole. Further work on geographic variation is needed before character displacement can be claimed.
- Diets and habitat use are likely to vary between locations, so results may not generalize beyond Saanich Inlet.
