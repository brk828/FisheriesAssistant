# Ecosystem Repair by Headwater Erosion Control: West Turkey Creek, Chiricahua Mountains, Arizona

#aquatic-ecology #watershed-restoration #stream-hydrology #riparian-ecology #erosion-control

## Executive Summary

This 15-month study (1996-97) evaluated the ecological effects of fitted-stone erosion control structures ("gabions") installed across ~15 years on the 6,153-hectare [[El Coronado Ranch]] in the northwestern [[Chiricahua Mountains]], Arizona. The ranch, historically overgrazed, experienced desertscrub invasion, slope erosion, and arroyo cutting. Nearly 20,000 hand-built stone structures (<60 cm high, spaced 6-20 m apart) were installed to reduce erosion and promote ecosystem recovery. The study compared 12 sub-watersheds (6 with gabions, 6 without) in [[West Turkey Creek]] and [[Rock Creek]] drainages, measuring temperature, precipitation, water chemistry, stream discharge, soil moisture, and biological responses. Results demonstrate that gabion installation promotes sediment retention, channel revegetation, extended flow duration, and expansion of aquatic species into formerly ephemeral tributaries.

## Background & Description

The [[El Coronado Ranch]] spans elevations from 1,652 to 2,629 m above mean sea level, with vegetation ranging from Grassland through [[Madrean Evergreen Woodland]]/Chaparral to Mixed Conifer Forests. The area comprises igneous bedrock covered by thin, readily erodible soils on precipitous terrain (53% of 5,376 ha unsuited for livestock grazing). The primary drainage is perennial [[West Turkey Creek]], fed by a dendritic network of mostly ephemeral, high-grade channels.

Fitted-stone gabions were constructed from natural surface stone collected on-site, arranged in walls rarely exceeding 60 cm (2.0 ft) high and spaced at 6.0 to 20 m intervals across channels. Similar structures intercepted overland flow on erosion-prone hillslopes. Low structure height minimized plunge-pool development while close spacing prevented high water velocities, reducing soil erosion and transport during runoff. The failure rate was less than 1.0% over 10 years.

Twelve sub-watersheds were selected for intensive study (six with gabions, six without), ranging from 48.4 to 118.4 ha (average 83.4 ha). Nine were in the [[West Turkey Creek]] watershed and three in adjacent [[Rock Creek]]. Each was located using GPS equipment and delineated on GIS maps.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area represents a semi-arid mountain ecosystem recovering from historical overgrazing documented by Bahre (1991, 1994). Precipitation increased with elevation at approximately 3.5 cm/1,007 m, typical for the region (within the 3.3 to 4.2 cm/1,007 m range reported by Lowe 1967). Maximum temperatures were higher than anticipated year-round, especially in winter, attributable to thermometers mounted in full sun rather than shade.

Water chemistry analyses revealed soft water typical of unaltered Arizona streams. Major plant nutrients (phosphate-phosphorus, nitrite-nitrogen, nitrate-nitrogen) were typically below reliable detection levels except for PO₄-P, which was detected in 68.1% of unfiltered samples and 73.5% of filtered samples. No artificial enrichment was evident. Major ion concentrations (calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium) showed high correlation between filtered and unfiltered samples (r² ≥ 0.80 for Ca, Mg, Na; r² = 0.72 for K). Turbidities were low, typically <100 ppm even during moderately high discharges.

Biological responses included spread of hillslope grasses and vegetation onto retained sediments, extension of riparian trees into tributaries from perennial [[West Turkey Creek]], establishment of aquatic and semi-aquatic plants, and range expansion of vertebrates including native fishes and a turtle species.

## Key Findings

**Hydrological Effects:**
- [[West Turkey Creek]] (with higher percentage of gabions in watershed) retained water far longer than [[Rock Creek]] after flood events, demonstrating slower release of runoff as predicted
- Some formerly ephemeral channels developed intermittent or quasi-perennial surface flow
- Reduced "flashiness" of runoff and increased groundwater retention reflected in longer flow durations
- Discharge measurements ranged from 0.001 cfs (assigned to connected pools with seepage) to >5.0 cfs

**Soil Moisture:**
- After approximately 80 days without appreciable rainfall, soil moisture ranged from 0.58% to 32.87%
- No statistical differences among elevations within watersheds, between hillslope positions (1.0 m and 2.0 m above channel) and channel bottoms, or between watersheds with/without gabions
- Moisture was slightly higher (4.8 ± 3.7% vs. 4.0 ± 4.1%) within sediments behind gabions compared to unmodified channel bottoms
- Highest moisture values (average 18.3%, range 11.2-32.9%) occurred near seeps in channels without gabions
- In gabion-equipped channels, highest values averaged only 10.4% (range 8.5-14.7%); seeps may have been obscured by accumulated soil and debris

**Water Chemistry:**
- Nutrients other than PO₄-P typically below detection (Table 2)
- No relationship between nutrient concentrations and gabion presence/absence
- No indication of artificial enrichment in any system
- Major ion concentrations statistically identical between filtered and unfiltered samples
- Ion concentrations highest at low discharge, leveling quickly with dilution at higher flows
- No relationship between major-ion concentrations and gabion presence at any discharge level

**Biological Responses:**
- Species diversity of aquatic and semi-aquatic plants far higher in channels with gabions (based on field interpretation of collections; formal identifications pending)
- Extension of riparian tree ranges into tributaries from perennial streams
- Expansion of native fish and turtle populations into previously unsuitable habitat
- Natural revegetation of erosion scars in and along channels and slopes

**Physical Effects:**
- Visible retardation and storage of soil and organic materials above structures
- Closure and natural revegetation of erosion scars
- Structure failure rate <1.0% over 10-year period despite nearly 20,000 installations

**Recommendations:**
- Constant-recording devices must be installed for successful sub-watershed discharge assessment
- Design of inexpensive, reliable recording instrument being pursued through Unique Equipment Corp.
- Sequence of soil moisture samples following winter rains into spring drought planned for 1998 to assess drying rates

**Habitat Conservation Planning:**
- Formal Habitat Conservation Plan (HCP) and associated documents (Environmental Assessment and Implementation Plan) developed for endangered species management
- Cooperators include Arizona Game and Fish Department, [[El Coronado Ranch]], U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and U.S. Forest Service
- Documents completed public review and awaiting final approval and signature as of April 1998
