# Leeches and Their Importance in the Life Cycle of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Hirudinea #parasitology #aquatic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1951 report from the [[New Jersey Fisheries Survey]] examines the ecological role of freshwater leeches (Hirudinea) in fish populations. The authors document leech diversity, distribution, and their multifaceted interactions with fishes—as parasites, prey, competitors, and disease vectors. Field surveys identified ten leech species across New Jersey waters, with notable infestations of *Illinobdella moorei* on [[Pumpkinseed]] and [[Bluegill]] in Echo Lakes, Union County. The study emphasizes that while leeches are often considered negligible in fisheries management, their cumulative effects on fish health, egg predation, and food web competition warrant closer attention.

## Background & Description

Leeches are segmented annelids distinguished from earthworms by their "rugged" constitution, definite body segmentation (fixed number of segments), and adhesive suckers at one or both ends. Adults range from 0.5 to over 12 inches in length and exhibit diverse coloration (pale grey to black, including greens, browns, yellows, and reds). Locomotion occurs via measuring-worm-like crawling or graceful undulating swimming movements.

Feeding strategies vary: some species are predaceous on aquatic invertebrates, others are scavengers, and bloodsucking species secrete anticoagulants that prevent clotting and cause itching at bite sites. Bloodsucking leeches do not transmit human diseases but wounds may become infected due to scavenging habits. Medicinal leeches remain commercially available in U.S. cities with central European populations for treating bruises.

Over 40 freshwater leech species are identified in North America, plus terrestrial and marine forms. They inhabit shallow waters with vegetation, stones, and attachment substrates, congregating where food is abundant (inlets, outlets, lee shores). Population densities can be extraordinary: [[Lake Mendota]], Wisconsin harbored an estimated 100,000,000 leeches between shoreline and 25-foot depth; sections of the [[Illinois River]] yielded 10,000 leeches per square yard.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Leeches occupy the same shallow littoral habitats as many fish species, creating complex ecological interactions. The authors identify five major pathways through which leeches affect fish populations:

**1. Egg Predation**: Certain leech species feed on fish eggs in nests, though the significance remains poorly quantified.

**2. Prey for Fishes**: Approximately 40 fish species consume leeches, including 9 sunfish species, 6 perch species, 5 catfish species, and 4 trout species. Soft leech bodies may be rapidly digested, potentially underestimating their dietary importance. Volumetrically, large leeches may represent higher percentages of fish diets than numerical counts suggest.

**3. Fish Parasitism and Disease Transmission**: Fish-parasitic leeches transmit blood diseases analogous to mosquito-borne malaria, and serve as intermediate hosts for flatworm larvae. Meyer (1946) notes that even single leeches weaken fish vitality through blood/mucus withdrawal, and heavy infestations cause emaciation, ulcerated attachment sites, and secondary infections. Leeches commonly attach inside opercula, in mouths, or on body surfaces protected by fins. Historical epidemics include 1927 infestations on [[Redmouth Buffalo]] in Illinois River and near-total [[Yellow Perch]] mortality in Shaw Lake, Connecticut (1941).

**4. Indirect Effects via Predators**: Leeches infesting fish-eating birds and turtles (especially [[Snapping Turtle]]s) indirectly influence fish populations by affecting predator behavior.

**5. Food Competition**: Specialized "snail leeches" feed exclusively on snails and bivalves—important prey for young game and panfish. Large leeches can eliminate entire snail populations. Leeches also consume aquatic annelids, earthworms, insect larvae, and crustacea, competing directly with fish for food resources.

Leeches tolerate acidic conditions (found in pH 5.1 ponds in southern New Jersey), though lower tolerance limits remain unknown.

## Key Findings

**New Jersey Leech Fauna**: Ten species identified across northern, central, and southern regions:
- *Placobdella parasitica* and *P. rugosa*: free-living and on snapping turtles
- *Illinobdella moorei*: parasitizing [[Yellow Perch]], [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Redbreasted Sunfish]]
- *Illinobdella* sp.: on fingerling [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Catfish]] (Maurice River)
- *Cystobranchus* sp.: on seahorse in Delaware Bay
- *Macrobdella decora*, *Haemopis marmoratis*, *Erpobdella punctata*: in small streams

**Echo Lakes Case Study**: The most severe infestation documented occurred in Echo Lakes, Union County. *Illinobdella moorei* parasitized 40% of pumpkinseeds in weedless areas and 80% in water lily-dominated inlet regions. Two of four bluegills collected were infested. [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Calico Bass]], [[Golden Shiner]], and [[Brown Bullhead]] showed no parasitism. Both bluegills and pumpkinseeds were nesting during sampling. The lakes contain overpopulated, stunted pumpkinseed populations, complicating assessment of leech impacts.

**Fisheries Management Implications**: The authors argue that leeches likely play a greater role in fish ecology than traditionally recognized, particularly under certain environmental conditions. The "composite picture" of leech activity (Figure 32) illustrates simultaneous impacts as egg predators, fish prey, parasites/disease vectors, predator irritants, and food competitors. Each factor's relative importance varies with leech species composition, population density, and environmental conditions.

**Research Gaps**: Effects of leech parasitism on fish populations remain difficult to isolate, especially in systems with existing population imbalances. The extent of egg predation, disease transmission rates, and competitive impacts on fish food supplies require quantitative study. Leech tolerance to acidic waters needs further investigation.
