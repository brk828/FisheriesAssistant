# On the Survival Value of Fish Schools

#ichthyology #marine-biology #schooling-fishes

## Executive Summary

This is a theoretical and synthetic review by C. M. Breder Jr. (1967), published in *Zoologica* (New York Zoological Society), on whether fish schools have survival value. Breder argues that fish groupings are not a single kind of structure and that no one formulation can explain them all. He separates [[Obligate Schooling]] species from [[Facultative Schooling]] species and "schools" (socially maintained groups) from "aggregations" (groups drawn together by shared environmental conditions). The paper combines anecdotal observation, mathematical models, the limited experimental literature and some unpublished field notes. It does not apply the published search equations to real data. Its central conclusion is that schooling is a form of behavioral homeostasis, shaped by selection, whose protective value depends on water transparency, school geometry, sound production, predator feeding mode and school size.

## Background & Description

Breder reviews the competing approaches to the question:

- **Naturalist (anecdotal) approach:** verbal interpretation from direct observation (e.g., Breder and Halpern 1946; Springer 1957).
- **Mathematician's approach:** rigorous models of random encounters between prey and predators, notably [[Brock And Riffenburgh 1960]] and Olson (1964), which draws on [[Koopman Theory Of Search]].
- **Experimental approach:** only two reports at the time, Williams (1964) and John (1964), both on fright-induced aggregation.

Terminology is a central theme. Breder notes that "school" has been defined in dictionary terms (fish swimming together), in social terms (Spooner 1931), and in Williams's terms (persistence owed to social forces). Breder uses a measurable definition, which treats schools as groups held together by mutual orientation at standard spacing, usually about one-half to three-quarters of a body length apart (Breder 1954, 1965).

The key distinction is:

- **[[Obligate Schooling]]:** coherently polarized groups that persist unless disturbed by considerable force, and that cannot be maintained in a solitary state. Isolated obligate schoolers show erratic swimming, may exhaust themselves and die, and will school with almost any moving fish, but not with inert objects. These species are hard to keep in aquaria, which is why scombriform, carangiform and clupeiform fishes are rarely displayed in public aquaria.
- **[[Facultative Schooling]]:** groups that form under non-social influences, such as "fright schools," schools in flowing water, or temporary assemblages. These fish may ignore their companions and lose their group.

Breder also describes [[Fish Mill]] formation, a circular swimming pattern that occurs only in obligate schools.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Visibility and water transparency.** Vision is essential for school formation and maintenance. Breder quotes Brock and Riffenburgh (1960) on the optics of water, and Duntley (1952) on [[Hydrological Range]]. The contrast-based visibility limit for a 0.72-inch object is about 200 feet in clear water. Dr. Eugenie Clark estimated horizontal visibility of up to 180 feet off the coast of Yucatan. Most natural waters are far less clear.

**Geometry and [[Countershading]].** Schools tend to form spheres or balls, which minimize surface area and conspicuousness. This is a "primary" selective value that comes from simple physical constraints. Deformations are explained by the surface, the bottom, or directional travel. Breder models each fish's visual range as a slightly prolate ellipsoid, because countershading reduces the difference in visibility between looking up and looking down. A pair of fish gains little over one fish in lateral detection, because their fields of view overlap. Additional fish add visual coverage roughly in proportion to the side area presented.

**Sound production.** Most fish make little sound during locomotion. Breder suggests that schooling fishes may have been selected to suppress sound. Sounds appear mainly during sharp turns under fright (Moulton 1960), and some schooling species call only at night (Takarev 1958; Shishkova 1958; Marshall 1962). Porpoises use echolocation and are largely unaffected by fish sounds. Tavolga (personal communication) predicted that school noise would be random and partly masked by ambient noise. Breder proposes that schools with poor swimming synchronization may be an adaptation that suppresses telltale sounds.

**Near-field and far-field sound.** Breder calculates near-field ranges using the empirical sound speed equation of Albers (1960). For [[Galeichthys]] "percolator" sounds (about 20–30 °C), the near-field limit at 1000 Hz is under about one foot. The limit is about 3 feet at 300 Hz, about 3.5 feet at 200 Hz, about 8 feet at 100 Hz, and about 30 feet at 25 Hz. Directional cues therefore persist farther from the source at lower frequencies. Wavelengths are about 200 feet at 25 Hz and about 5 feet at 1000 Hz. Breder notes that the calculation excludes absolute source energy, which can be important.

**Olfaction and alarm substances.** The [[Schreckstoff Effect]] is largely confined to Cypriniformes, which are mostly freshwater and facultative schoolers. Breder regards the chemical-alarm explanation as unlikely for rapid communication, following Williams's critique.

**Ontogeny.** Some species are pelagic as larvae and later settle to the bottom. Their schooling can vanish abruptly at a post-larval stage, so the species either becomes a permanent schooler or gives up the habit.

**Predator feeding modes.** Predators such as [[Caranx]], [[Tylosurus]] and [[Sphyraena]] take one fish at a time and return until satiated. This is the least wasteful mode. [[Pomatomus]] slashes through schools, leaving many damaged fish, and Breder estimates that less than half of each destroyed fish becomes food. Prey-to-predator size ratios range from more than 20 million to 1 (see Key Findings).

**Oxygen and school size.** [[Dissolved Oxygen In Schools]] were measured in large *Mugil cephalus* schools by MacFarland and Moss (1967). Oxygen was reduced inside schools, and disruptive activity occurred where readings were lowest. This may limit school size through respiratory need.

## Key Findings

1. **Schools are not one structure.** Obligate and facultative schoolers, and schools versus aggregations, need separate treatment. The two main definitions in the literature (Williams's social-versus-non-social and Breder's measurable polarization) largely agree on which groups are schools in practice.

2. **Obligate schoolers cannot survive alone.** Isolated fish show erratic "panic" swimming, may collapse and die, and respond only to moving fish, not inert objects. Breder suggests that this drive reflects strong selection.

3. **Visibility limits protection.** Schooling is effective where visibility is restricted, but it fails at the "degenerate limit" where predators can see everything and feed continuously. In such conditions, losses are concentrated on the least healthy or most awkward individuals.

4. **Small groups are most efficient.** Adding a few fish to a small school increases its conspicuousness much more slowly than its size grows. This advantage is lost when the school becomes an absolute mass.

5. **Large schools can deter predators.** Very large schools may repel predators or disguise themselves as a single large animal (Springer 1957). Over time, predators may habituate to them, and predator numbers may concentrate, reversing the benefit.

6. **Large schools may be agglomerations.** Huge schools of *Clupea* and *Scomber* may form when smaller uniform schools merge. Size differences can then cause shearing and break-up.

7. **Size structure influences cohesion.** Hunter (1966) found that angular divergence between school members increases with size variation in *Trachurus symmetricus*. Milanovskii and Rekubratskii (1960) found that *Phoxinus* subgroups of different sizes reacted differently to threats while still behaving as one school.

8. **Hunger affects school structure.** Hunter (1966) also found that food-deprived *Trachurus symmetricus* swam farther apart than fed fish.

9. **Darkness breaks up schools.** John (1966) reported that at very low light levels, below 10⁻⁸ footcandles, schools of *Tilapia nilotica* and *Notemigonus crysoleucas* broke up. Individuals acted as mutual sources of fright, suggesting a positive repelling factor as light fades.

10. **Predator-prey size ratio determines strategy.** Predators of similar size stalk, herding requires intermediate size ratios, and predators much larger than their prey, such as *Manta* feeding on plankton, range over the water searching for patches. Breder cites a *Manta* of about 3,000 lb compared with a plankter of about 0.1 oz, and notes that the ratio is probably underestimated, possibly by 3 to 5 times.

11. **Schooling is a homeostatic mechanism.** Breder concludes that schooling and aggregation are forms of behavioral homeostasis. Schooling protects against excessive predation within limits, and reproductive output adjusts to maintain population stability.

12. **Obligate schooling species are widespread.** They tend to have wide geographic ranges, large numbers of young and migratory habits. Breder suggests that the formation and break-up of great schools may regulate gene flow within the species.

13. **Open questions remain.** The paper leaves several issues unresolved: whether mill formation is harmful, why schools vary in synchronization, what sounds schools produce at a usable volume, and how schools combine social and environmental influence in heterogeneous habitats.
