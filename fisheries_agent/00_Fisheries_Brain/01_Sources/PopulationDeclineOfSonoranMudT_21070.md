# Population Decline of Sonoran Mud Turtles at Quitobaquito Springs

#herpetology #freshwater-ecology #kinosternidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 final report by Philip C. Rosen (Arizona State University) documents a mark-recapture study of the [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon sonoriense longifemorale*) at [[Quitobaquito Springs]], [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], from September 1982 to September 1985. The population is small and declining. Estimated adults number about 67, and the total population excluding hatchlings is about 108. The population shows a severe deficit of juveniles and a complete absence of female recruitment into the adult pool for roughly six to seven years. Rosen attributes the decline mainly to low hatchling and juvenile survival, largely from avian predation in open, poorly vegetated pond margins. Projections predict extinction of females between 1998 and 2008. The report proposes phased habitat-restoration options.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Two mud turtle species were recorded at Quitobaquito. The Sonoran Mud Turtle is represented by the subspecies *K. s. longifemorale*, which is known only from the Rio Sonoita and Quitobaquito and is considered endangered in the United States. The [[Yellow Mud Turtle]] (*K. flavescens*) was recorded from only one or two specimens in 1955. Rosen found no evidence of a natural population of it at Quitobaquito.
- **Sampling effort:** The report cites 18 sampling periods and 1086 trap-days, using baited turtle traps and modified [[Minnow Traps]]. Two different totals appear in the text: 169 captures of 97 individuals in the Methods, and 177 captures of 99 individuals in the Abstract and Results. Both are reported here as given.
- **Marking and measurement:** Turtles were marked, measured, weighed, and released at capture. Ages were estimated from abdominal plastral scute growth rings (see [[Growth Ring Aging]]), and sex was estimated for juveniles from secondary sexual characteristics, which Rosen judged about 83% accurate.
- **Study area:** Quitobaquito is an isolated Lower Sonoran Desert oasis. Two main springs deliver about 30–35 gallons per minute to a 1.2-acre (0.49 ha) pond. The pond is roughly 1–1.5 m deep, with vertical banks. Dense [[Olney's Bulrush]] (*Scirpus olneyi*) forms a mat along the north end, and [[Najas]] beds grow in the open water. Mesquite bosque surrounds the pond.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predators:** The only evident potential predators of hatchlings and juveniles are [[Great Blue Heron]] and [[Belted Kingfisher]]. Both are attracted by abundant [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), which Rosen describes as highly abundant at Quitobaquito. Coyotes, skunks, and raccoons have also been recorded preying on turtles elsewhere. Large dytiscid beetles and giant water bugs (*Lethocerus*) are also a danger to small juveniles.
- **Productivity:** Gross primary production was measured at 3.3 g O₂/m²/day in May, citing Cole and Whiteside (1965).
- **Food web hypothesis:** Rosen suggests that the high pupfish density reduces food available to turtles, slowing growth and lowering clutch size. In other Arizona populations, turtle density is inversely related to growth rate.
- **Historical context:** Rio Sonoita was once a moist grassland with cienega marshes. It incised after 1891 arroyo-cutting, and groundwater pumping has since lowered water tables. Quitobaquito was modified into a reservoir and stock tank in the mid-1800s. Park Service records describe a 1970 drainage, poisoning, and bulldozing of the pond to remove introduced fish, after which a sloping shelf was built on the north end. Rosen links this disturbance and subsequent succession of the bulrush mat to the decline.
- **Habitat preference:** Captures concentrated along pond margins, especially in complex vegetated banks on the north end and in woody brush on the south half.

## Key Findings

**Population size and density**
- The best Lincoln Index estimate for adults is 71. Triple-Catch estimates are 52 (1983) and 77 (1984). The combined adult estimate is 67 ± 13 (97.5% range 41–93).
- The juvenile estimate is 41 ± 12, including 29 in the first year-class.
- Total population excluding hatchlings is 108 ± 25 (97.5% range 71–158).
- Density is 0.022 per m², or one turtle per 45 m², which equals about 220 per hectare. Table 1 lists 800–10,000 per hectare at three other Arizona sites.

**Population structure and sex ratio**
- Marked adult sex ratio is 43 males : 22 females (1.95:1). After discounting 9 young adult males, the refined ratio is 34:22 (1.55:1).
- Turtles aged 7–9 years are 17 males and 0 females. No females have been recruited into the pond for about six years.
- Turtles aged 10 years and older show a 17:22 ratio, statistically consistent with 1:1.
- Juveniles are strongly deficient at ages 2–6, and females are even more deficient at ages 3–6.

**Reproduction**
- Two X-rayed gravid females carried 3 eggs (119 mm carapace length) and 6 eggs (129 mm). Both fall within the normal size-fecundity range for Arizona.
- Females are estimated to lay about 3 clutches per year, for about 337 eggs per year in the population.
- No nests or eggshells were found, and nesting was not confirmed.

**Survivorship and demography**
- Hatchling survival from 337 eggs to 40 survivors in July–August is about 12%. Survival then rose to 43% and 50% in subsequent years. Adult annual survival is estimated at 75%, which is approximate.
- Mortality is concentrated in ages 1–3, and juvenile females suffer disproportionately high mortality in their second through fourth seasons.
- The idealized [[Life Table]] gives a generation time of 10.25 years and a net replacement rate (R₀) of 0.25. Corrected R₀ is 0.075, meaning only 7.5% of mortality is replaced each generation.
- Models predict complete female extinction by 1998 and 2008. The abstract also predicts a possible critical situation by 1990. Under the worst case, females could go extinct within six years.

**Management**
- Short-term Option: dig 3–5 channels (20–60 cm wide, 20–50 cm deep) into the bulrush mat on the north end, with pools (about 2 m × 1 m × 50–75 cm). Line them with rocks to restrain regrowth.
- Long-term Option: create a sloping shelf (6–7 m wide, 30–75 cm deep) and seed it with emergent vegetation. Consider delivering 100% of source water to the pond to expand habitat.
- Appendix I: Rosen's comments on a 40-foot experimental stream channel built in August 1985. He concludes it is sub-optimal and must meet four conditions: open canopy for primary production, bank obstructions, complex stone features, and deeper steep-sided pools with complex margins.
- Recommended monitoring: dipnetting in early spring, and trapping in late September and late October each year. Trap-based monitoring should use a range of baits (sardines, hot dogs, tuna, and dog food).

**Migration and regional conservation**
- One marked female from Rio Sonoita (marked 1983) was recaptured at Quitobaquito in May 1985. Rosen estimates that about two males and two females at Quitobaquito are immigrants.
- Migration is too infrequent to sustain the population, but augmenting it with Rio Sonoita females is possible.
- Rio Sonoita is polluted near Sonoita, and a flood between August 1983 and spring 1984 eliminated habitat downstream. Groundwater pumping may compound the risk of permanent habitat loss.

**Core conclusion**
- Rosen concludes: "The only plausible explanation for population decline and size at Quitobaquito Springs is low hatchling and juvenile survivorship." The population is in immediate danger of extinction under all reasonable assumptions.
