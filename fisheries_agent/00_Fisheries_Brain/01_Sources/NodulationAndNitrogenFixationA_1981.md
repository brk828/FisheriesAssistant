## Executive Summary

This short communication reports the first documented evidence of root nodulation and biological nitrogen fixation in **[[Desert Ironwood]]** (*Olneya tesota*), a dominant leguminous tree of the Sonoran and Colorado deserts. Seedlings of *O. tesota*, *[[Catclaw Acacia]]* (*Acacia greggi*), and *[[Palo Verde]]* (*Cercidium floridium*) were inoculated with native rhizobial soil inoculum and grown on nitrogen-free media. Only *O. tesota* formed nodules and exhibited significant acetylene reduction activity (a proxy for nitrogen fixation), while *A. greggi* and *C. floridium* showed no nodulation and background-level ethylene production. The findings contradict earlier negative reports (Martin 1948; Garcia-Moya and McKell 1970) and align with the phylogenetic placement of *Olneya* in the more advanced, typically nodulating legume subfamily **Papilionoideae**, versus the more primitive, rarely nodulating **Caesalpinioideae** to which *Cercidium* belongs.

## Background & Description

- **Nodules:** Observed only on *Olneya tesota* root systems; described as "over a centimeter wide" — notably large for a legume seedling nodule.
- **No nodules** were detected on *Acacia greggi* or *Cercidium floridium* under the experimental conditions.
- **Rhizobial isolate:** Pure rhizobia cultured from *Olneya* nodules (isolated by S. Hua, USDA Western Regional Research Center) show distinct colony/culture media morphology compared to rhizobia previously isolated from *[[Prosopis]]* (mesquite), suggesting a taxonomically or physiologically distinct symbiotic strain.
- **Plant age at assay:** Nine-month-old greenhouse-grown seedlings, raised from seed on nitrogen-free media.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** All three species are dominant leguminous trees of the Sonoran and Coloradan deserts of the southwestern United States, occupying infertile, low-nitrogen desert soils.
- **Soil inoculum source:** Composite 60 cm-deep soil samples (sand/gravel) collected beneath mature native trees at Box Canyon, a legume-rich site along Highway 195 near Indio, California; soil was only "barely moist," suggesting suboptimal conditions for rhizobial survival.
- **Physiological adaptation:** These trees photosynthesize at very low xylem water potentials (−30 to −40 bars), an adaptation distinguishing them from annual legume relatives and enabling survival in arid, nutrient-poor environments.
- **Taxonomic/symbiotic context:** Nodulation propensity tracks legume subfamily phylogeny — *Olneya* (Papilionoideae) belongs to a clade with a high frequency of nodulating taxa, whereas *Cercidium* (Caesalpinioideae) belongs to the most primitive legume subfamily, historically associated with low nodulation frequency (Allen and Allen 1961).
- Comparable nitrogen-fixing symbioses have been documented in the related desert genus **[[Prosopis]]** (13 taxa; Felker and Clark 1980), situating *Olneya* within a broader pattern of nitrogen-fixing woody legumes in arid ecosystems.

## Key Findings

- **First report** of nodulation and nitrogen fixation (via acetylene reduction) in *Olneya tesota*, overturning prior negative findings for desert legume trees in this region.
- Ethylene production (proxy for N₂ fixation via acetylene reduction assay) in five *O. tesota* seedlings: **80, 90, 460, 470, and 550 µg ethylene per plant per hour** — rates comparable to *Prosopis* and other established nitrogen-fixing legumes.
- *Acacia greggi* and *Cercidium floridium* averaged only **5.5 and 6.0 µg ethylene/plant/hour**, respectively — not significantly different from the background control level of **4.4 µg/pot/hour** (blank vermiculite pots, attributed to ethylene contamination of acetylene gas stock).
- No stress-induced ethylene was detected in plants incubated without acetylene, ruling out confounding physiological ethylene production.
- Failure of *A. greggi* to nodulate was unexpected, since only 3 of 45 surveyed *Acacia* taxa failed to nodulate in a prior broad survey (Corby 1974); authors suggest that drier soil inoculum (collected outside the spring rain window) may have limited viable rhizobial inoculum.
- Authors hypothesize that *Cercidium* may possess a **less overt, non-nodular nitrogen-fixing symbiosis** consistent with its primitive Caesalpinioideae lineage, and advocate whole-plant acetylene reduction assays (rather than nodule inspection alone) as a more reliable detection method for such cryptic symbioses.
- Rhizobial strain from *Olneya* nodules appears culturally/morphologically distinct from *Prosopis*-derived rhizobia, hinting at host-specific or lineage-specific rhizobial associations among desert legumes.
