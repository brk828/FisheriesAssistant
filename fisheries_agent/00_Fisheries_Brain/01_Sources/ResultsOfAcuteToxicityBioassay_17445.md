# Acute Toxicity of Copper Sulphate to Goldfish and Rainbow Trout in Lake Cachuma

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game Inland Fisheries Administrative Report (No. 67-4, submitted March 1967, by Robert F. Carr, Region 5) reports four [[Acute Toxicity Bioassay|acute toxicity bioassays]] on [[Goldfish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] exposed to commercial [[Copper Sulphate]] (pentahydrate) in [[Lake Cachuma]] water. The work followed a decline of the warmwater sport fishery from 1963 to 1965 and fish mortalities in spring 1965 after algae-control copper treatments by the [[Cachuma Project Water Treatment]] (CPWT). The 36-hour mortality threshold of goldfish and rainbow trout fell within the doses commonly used by the CPWT. Rainbow trout were far more sensitive than goldfish. A [[Citric Acid]] mixture was less toxic than copper sulphate alone in algae-sparse water, and a [[Peridinium]] bloom increased copper toxicity. The report recommends an independent evaluation of the treatment program, advance notice of treatments, a ban on treatments in designated spawning areas, and long-term and short-term studies.

## Background & Description

**Study context.** The [[California Department of Fish and Game]] made initial warmwater fish plantings in Lake Cachuma in 1957. The warmwater fishery was satisfactory until 1963. The first concrete evidence of a major decline was a sharp drop in gate receipts at the Santa Barbara County Park in 1965, together with angler complaints. A major fish die-off in which an estimated 500,000 fish were destroyed prompted intensified surveys in 1965. Preliminary studies reported inadequate spawning and reproduction of [[Largemouth Bass]], [[White Crappie]] and [[Bluegill]], and female bass were found retaining their eggs beyond the typical spawning ground. The CPWT began large-scale copper sulphate applications in 1962. In 1965 the Santa Barbara County Parks Department and the Department of Fish and Game started an experimental catchable-sized [[Rainbow Trout]] planting program to compensate for the warmwater decline.

**Stated purpose.** The study aims to determine which copper sulphate concentrations are harmful to game fish and to help the CPWT maintain algae control within limits safe for the fishery. The author notes that published acute toxicity data have been hard to apply as a management tool because of the lake's high alkalinity and climatic effects on water quality. Assays were run in winter and spring, so young [[Largemouth Bass]], [[White Crappie]] and [[Bluegill]] were not available. Their copper tolerance is expected to be marginal relative to goldfish, and assays on these species were planned for the future.

**Reservoir description.** Lake Cachuma lies on the [[Santa Ynez River]], about 25 miles northwest of the City of Santa Barbara. Its long axis runs northwest-southeast and it is about 6.5 miles long. It has seven large canyons with bays along the north shore. Maximum surface area is 3,250 acres and maximum depth is 190 feet. Annual water temperature variation is given as 40-75 F. The reservoir supplies irrigation, municipal water, flood control and recreation.

**Management roles.** The Santa Barbara County Parks Department manages recreation. The United States Bureau of Reclamation owns the reservoir structures. The CPWT is responsible for the quality of water metered to consumers.

**Treatment practice.** In 1965 the CPWT applied 56 tons of copper sulphate to control taste-and-odor-producing algae and weeds, in 55 treatments. Most were localized and made by boat, but several lake-wide treatments were made by plane during heavy planktonic-algal blooms. Heaviest applications were in summer. Overtreatment and fish loss have occurred in the bays during aerial treatments. Typical applications range from 0.4 to 5.8 mg/l of copper sulphate. Because copper makes up one-fourth of the copper sulphate molecule, this corresponds to a copper residual of 0.10-1.45 mg/l. The program aims for a residual of 0.3-0.5 mg/l one hour after application. Assay doses are given as mg/l of copper sulphate, from 1.0 to 56.0 mg/l.

**Terms.** The *median tolerance limit* (TLm) is the concentration at which 50% of test fish die. The *mortality threshold* (LD10 in the original, rendered "LDi9"/"LD 9" in scans) is the concentration at which 10% die.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Assays ran in December 1965 and January and April 1966 at the CPWT headquarters in Santa Barbara, following the 1962 U.S. Public Health Service *Bio-assay and Pollution Ecology* training manual.

- **Goldfish** (2-3 inches) came from Chino Fisheries Base or Pacific Goldfish Farms (Westminster). Shipping followed the same practice in both cases: 35 fish per 20-gallon can aerated from a compressed air cylinder (Chino), or 75 fish in plastic food bags in three liters of water with commercial oxygen replacing the air (Westminster).
- **Rainbow trout** (4-6 inches; 75 fish) came from Fillmore State Hatchery in 300 gallons of hatchery water, aerated and held at 61-63 F with 150 pounds of ice.
- Fish were acclimatized to Cachuma water for 3-5 days. Test tubs were 24 x 16 x 15-inch plastic containers. Goldfish were tested in 5 gallons of water, unaerated. Trout were tested in 15 gallons, constantly aerated by compressor and Venturi. Ten fish were exposed per dilution. Feeding stopped 5 days before each assay. No mortalities occurred during acclimatization.
- Behavior and survival were recorded at 15, 24 and 36 hours (trout continued to 48 hours). Water chemistry (CaCO3 alkalinity, total hardness, pH, CO2, copper residual, and water and room temperature) was measured at the start, during and at the end of each assay. Copper residual was read on 25 ml samples with a Hach photocell, with a common error of 0.1-0.2 mg/l.
- Diluent water had a Secchi transparency greater than 4 feet and fewer than 500 algal cells/ml in Assays 1, 2 and 4. Assay 3 diluent carried a moderate [[Peridinium]] bloom.

**Water chemistry and biology.** The water was alkaline, and that high alkalinity is cited as complicating the use of published toxicity data. Diluent for Assay 3 contained about 6000 Peridinium cells/ml. Peridinium became immotile and encysted within 1.5 hours of dosing at 1.0 mg/l and above. At lower doses the population decline was accompanied by a protozoa-bacteria pulse. Some Peridinium cells appeared to survive 100 hours of exposure to doses within the CPWT range. The author states that more assays are needed to clarify this.

**Protection status.** Spawning grounds were described as inadequately protected at the time. The CPWT was then beginning to notify the Department of large-scale treatments.

## Key Findings

### Assay results (copper residual in parentheses, mg/l)

**Assay No. 1 (goldfish, 36 h, algae-sparse water, copper sulphate; December 1965)**
- 15-hour TLm: 25 mg/l (6.3)
- 24-hour TLm: 15.0 (3.8); 36-hour TLm: 6.4 (1.6)
- No mortality at 3.2 (0.8) in a 24-hour exploratory test
- The 36-hour mortality threshold lies within the CPWT's commonly used range of 0.4-5.8 mg/l.

**Assay No. 2 (goldfish, citric acid mixture, algae-sparse water; December 31 to January 2)**
- Citric acid was added at a ratio of 1 part acid to 1 part copper sulphate by weight. The report describes this as a 72-hour assay.
- 15-hour TLm: 37 mg/l (9.3); 24-hour TLm: 20.0 (5.0); 36-hour TLm: 13.0 (3.2)
- No mortality at 36 hours at 5.6 (1.4)
- The mixture was less toxic than copper sulphate alone in algae-sparse water.

**Assay No. 3 (goldfish, 36 h, Peridinium bloom, copper sulphate; January 23-24)**
- Diluent from Johnson Bay contained a moderate Peridinium bloom at 6000 cells/ml.
- 15-hour TLm: 14.0 (3.5); 24-hour TLm: 6.6 (1.7); 36-hour TLm: 5.7 (1.4)
- No mortality at 36 hours at 3.2 (0.8); extrapolated mortality threshold: 7.2 (1.8)
- The 15-hour mortality threshold dropped to the margin of CPWT use levels, which the author attributes to Peridinium.

**Assay No. 4 (rainbow trout, 48 h, copper sulphate; April 5-7)**
- 15-hour TLm: 2.6 (0.7); 2-hour TLm: 2.3 (0.6); no change from 15 to 36 hours.
- Extrapolation was difficult, so four-fish exploratory doses were added at 1.8 and 3.2 mg/l. Three of four fish at 3.2 mg/l died within 36 hours. All four at 1.8 mg/l survived 48 hours.
- Two trout were seen picking up copper sulphate crystals during dissolution. The author suspects hatchery feeding habits, which may also occur in the reservoir.

### Cross-assay comparisons
- Across the assays, 15-hour mortality thresholds ranged from about 3.2 to 21.0 mg/l. Median tolerance limits at 15, 24 and 36 hours ranged from 2.3 to 37.0 mg/l.
- Rainbow trout were much more sensitive than goldfish. Their 15-hour mortality threshold was about 3.2 mg/l, 14.8 mg/l below goldfish in similar solutions. Trout TLm fell from 2.6 mg/l at 15 hours to 2.3 at 36 hours. They tolerated 1.8 mg/l for 4 days in an exploratory test.
- Goldfish 15-hour mortality thresholds were 18.0 mg/l in algae-sparse water and 7.0 mg/l in Peridinium water. The 15-hour TLm difference was 11.0 mg/l, and at 36 hours it was 0.7 mg/l.
- The copper sulphate-citric acid mixture was the least toxic treatment. Its average 36-hour goldfish tolerance was 6.6 mg/l higher than for copper sulphate alone.

### Trout copper uptake (Table 1)
- Six whole, ground trout were analyzed for total copper by atomic absorption at Geigy Chemical Corporation (Ardsley, New York). Exposure ranged from 1 hour to 13 days.
- Copper content ranged from 4 to 34 mg/l (values as given in the text). The control and the 1.0 mg/l fish were the only ones that survived the full test period, with 4 and 19 mg/l respectively.
- Two trout that died in Lake Cachuma less than 24 hours after a February 1966 treatment contained 2 mg/l or less.
- Uptake appears to be rapid and to increase with concentration. Trout can apparently accumulate considerable copper without obvious injury at trace levels. The uptake sites and mechanisms were not investigated.

### Fish behavior and toxic signs
- Both species responded within 10-15 minutes at doses of 1.0 mg/l and above. The first sign was coughing movements of the mouth and gills, followed by surface swimming around the tub edge with constant gulping. After about an hour, respiratory effort declined and loss of equilibrium set in, followed by insensitivity to stimuli.
- Trout went through these stages faster than goldfish and made greater respiratory effort in the final stages.
- At or above 32.0 mg/l, precipitate may appear on gill filaments and body surfaces, and very heavy doses cause a fungused appearance. Below 32.0 mg/l, the lack of precipitate would make field diagnosis of cause of death difficult.

### Management implications
- The author concludes there is a significant relationship between the CPWT treatment program and lake fish mortalities. Occasional mortalities and fishery depression are expected to continue unless the program is modified.
- Brief exposure of trout to a concentrated dose right after application may cause injuries that lead to eventual death.
- The goldfish threshold is marginal to the upper limit of lake treatment. An increase of 1.5 mg/l over the reported 5.8 mg/l maximum would probably cause moderate game-fish mortality within 24 hours where avoidance was difficult.

### Recommendations
1. Have an independent agency evaluate the CPWT's copper sulphate technique and its effects on Lake Cachuma water quality.
2. In the interim, use chelated copper (described in the text as a copper sulphate-citric acid mixture) to protect fish.
3. Have all county agencies that use the resource share any added cost, since benefits accrue to all users.
4. Until the problem is resolved, have the CPWT notify the Department of Fish and Game in advance of all treatments.
5. Eliminate all copper sulphate treatments from Department-designated spawning areas from June 1 to September 30.
6. Conduct a long-term bioassay on the effect of ingested copper sulphate on feeding, growth and reproduction of game species.
7. Run a short-term study of (a) avoidance reactions of game species to copper sulphate, (b) tolerance time at various doses and survival after transfer to fresh water, and (c) the main sites of copper uptake and accumulation.
