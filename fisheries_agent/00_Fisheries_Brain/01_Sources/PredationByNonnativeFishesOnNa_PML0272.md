# Predation by Nonnative Fishes on Native Fishes in the San Juan River, New Mexico and Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether [[Nonnative Species|nonnative fishes]] in the San Juan River prey on the larvae of native fishes. Researchers examined the digestive tracts of six nonnative species collected by [[Seine Sampling|seine]] at four sites in the middle reach of the river. Of 529 individuals dissected, only eight contained larval fish, a low rate of [[Larval Predation]]. The predators were [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] (the taxa with larval remains). The authors concluded that larval predation occurs but is unlikely to greatly reduce recruitment of the common native species, while the rare natives [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] may be more vulnerable.

## Background & Description

Introduced predatory fishes can reduce native fish abundance or cause local extirpation (Miller et al., 1989; Krueger and May, 1991). Studies in the American Southwest suggest predation on larvae and juveniles may limit [[Recruitment]] of native fishes (Ruppert et al., 1993; Scoppettone, 1993; Marsh and Douglas, 1997). Adults of many native catostomids in the San Juan River are too large for most nonnative fishes to eat, but their larvae are vulnerable. Many nonnative species also use the same low-velocity habitats as larval and juvenile natives, which increases the potential for negative interactions (Tyus et al., 1982; Gido and Propst, 1999).

Earlier studies disagreed on how often nonnative fishes eat larval fish. Ruppert et al. (1993) reported larval fish in about 15% of [[Red Shiner]] stomachs in the [[Colorado River]] system. Jennings and Saiki (1990) found none in red shiner stomachs in California. The authors proposed that these differences reflect site-specific food availability or predator behavior and prey vulnerability (Johnson et al., 1993).

The study's purpose was to determine whether nonnative fishes, primarily Red Shiner and Fathead Minnow, prey on larvae of native species in the San Juan River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The San Juan River supports a syntopic mix of native and nonnative fishes, which allowed direct assessment of interactions. Sampling occurred at four sites in the middle reach between Shiprock, New Mexico, and Bluff, Utah (see Gido et al., 1997, for the fish assemblage).

Sampling methods:
- **Gear:** 1.6 mm-mesh seine, daylight hours.
- **Timing:** June–July 1994 and June 1995.
- **Habitat:** Low-velocity backwaters with inundated vegetation.
- **Preservation:** 5% buffered formalin.
- **Analysis:** Digestive tracts of nonnative fish >20 mm standard length (SL) examined under a [[Stereomicroscope]]. Larvae were identified to family, and most to species. Jar contents were also checked for regurgitated prey.

To maximize detection, only collections where larval native fish were present were examined (n = 35). Water temperatures were relatively low (<18 °C) during collection, so digestion was presumed slow. Most nonnative species had not yet spawned, while larval natives were present.

## Key Findings

- **Low overall predation:** Of 529 nonnative individuals dissected, only **8** had consumed larval fish, about 1.5%.
- **Predator species:** Only *C. lutrensis*, *P. promelas* and *M. salmoides* consumed larval fish (the text names *A. melas* alongside *M. salmoides* as having the highest frequency).
- **Prey identified:** Unidentified Cyprinidae (n = 3), *Rhinichthys osculus* (n = 2), *Catostomus latipinnis* (n = 2) and *Catostomus discobolus* (n = 1).
- **Largest count:** Four larval fish were found in one *C. lutrensis*, the most of any predator. The *C. lutrensis* that ate larvae measured 110, 51, 51 and 64 mm SL.
- **No prey remains in jars:** No larval remains were found in the bottoms of collection jars.
- **Possible undercounting:** Rapid digestion or shredding by pharyngeal teeth may have hidden some predation. Shredding was most likely in *P. promelas*, which is documented to tear its prey (Dunsmoor, 1995). In most other cases there was little sign of tearing.
- **Why predation was low:** Gape size may limit small nonnative fish from eating larvae, though juvenile *A. melas* and *M. salmoides* (which have relatively large gapes) frequently ate larvae. Alternate food sources and refugia may also reduce predation (Ruppert et al., 1993; Meffe, 1985).
- **Native recruitment:** Populations of *C. latipinnis*, *C. discobolus* and *R. osculus* appear sufficient to sustain relatively large populations (D. Propst, pers. comm.), so larval predation does not appear to greatly reduce their recruitment.
- **Rare species concern:** Larvae of *P. lucius* and *X. texanus* show poor recruitment (Platania et al., 1991). The authors therefore could not discount the importance of predation for these species.
- **Indirect effects:** Predator-driven indirect effects (Mittelbach and Chesson, 1987) should also be considered in future impact assessments.
- **Recommended next step:** Future studies should determine the relative importance of larval predation across native species within this system.
