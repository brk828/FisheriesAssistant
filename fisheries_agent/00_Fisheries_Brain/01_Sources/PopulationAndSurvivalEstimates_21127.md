# Population and Survival Estimates of Humpback Chub in the Mainstem Colorado River, Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines a reviewer's critique with a draft manuscript by Ryel and Valdez (Bio/West, Inc.) on [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the mainstem [[Colorado River]] of [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. The study estimated adult abundance and survival from monthly capture-recapture sampling (October 1990 to November 1993). Most adults were concentrated in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) aggregation, with about 3,000–3,500 individuals. The reviewer judged the data valuable but the analysis and presentation unpublishable in their current form. The main objections were the reliance on closed-population models, excessive model comparison, and circular reasoning in the length-frequency section.

## Background & Description

*Gila cypha* is one of six remaining populations of an endangered species. The Grand Canyon population is separated from the other five by [[Glen Canyon Dam]], and its persistence depends on maintaining sufficient adult numbers. Nine mainstem aggregations have been identified, and the LCR aggregation is the largest, linked to LCR spawning where warmer tributary temperatures permit reproduction.

Adults (≥200 mm TL) were captured by nets or electrofishing, marked with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]], and released during 32 monthly trips. Sampling was skipped in several months, including December 1990 and August, October and December 1991. Sufficient recaptures existed for six of nine aggregations. Survival was estimated only for the LCR aggregation. Subadult (<200 mm TL) survival was inferred from shoreline [[Electrofishing]] catch rates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow and physical habitat:** Flows are regulated by Glen Canyon Dam releases for power generation, with daily peaking. Tributaries alter flows during storm runoff. Reduced turbidity and cold hypolimnetic releases are described as severely limiting spawning in the mainstem.
- **Thermal and biological stressors:** Thermal shock during transition from the warm LCR to the cold mainstem is a proposed mortality source.
- **Predators and parasites:** Non-native fishes, including [[Brown Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Rainbow Trout]], prey on subadult chub. Other mortality sources are osprey predation, starvation, and parasites.
- **Genetic and demographic thresholds:** The authors cite thresholds of fewer than 50 individuals for inbreeding concerns and about 500 individuals for maintaining genetic variability, and note that small mainstem aggregations likely depend on LCR input.

## Key Findings

### Abundance estimates

- **LCR aggregation (adults ≥200 mm TL):** Closed-model estimates placed the population at **3,000–3,500** (95% CI ±20%). Open-model means were similar: [[Open Population Models]] A = 3,080, B = 3,116, and D = 3,192.
- **Total mainstem population:** The abstract gives **3,300–3,800** adults. This is derived by adding the LCR estimate to the approximate figures for other aggregations.
- **Other aggregations:** Five aggregations with recaptures ranged from about 4–5 adults (Pumpkin Spring) to about 50–60 (30-Mile and Shinumo inflow). All were under 5% of the LCR total. Three aggregations had no recaptures.
- **Combined-period closed estimate:** Pooling all data from 1990–1993 inflated the estimate by about 1,000 fish. Correcting the Schnabel estimator for marked-fish mortality gave **3,035 adults (SE = 171, 95% CI 2,681–3,465)**.

### Survival estimates

- **Adult survival (Model B, the simplest model that fit):** Seasonal survival was **0.932 (95% CI 0.890–0.973)**, equivalent to an annual rate of **0.755 (95% CI 0.627–0.896)**.
- **Model A comparison:** Mean seasonal survival was 0.979, but its confidence intervals were wide. The geometric mean was 0.931.
- **Replacement needs:** About 204–238 adults were lost per season, or roughly 735–857 per year. Model B estimated about 238 recruits per season, which the authors describe as similar to losses.
- **Subadult survival:** Mean survival was 0.827, 0.319, and 0.102 for 1-, 6-, and 12-month periods in 1991–92. Fall 1993 showed much lower values, with a 1-month survival of 0.216. The 6- and 12-month 1993 values are not legible in the scanned text. The authors interpret the 1993 decline as density dependence, since initial catch rates were about three times higher.

### Length-frequency and recruitment

- Adults of 200–300 mm TL were far fewer than a stable size distribution (based on survival of 0.755) would predict. This held even at the upper survival confidence bound of 0.896.
- Closed estimates for >300 mm TL adults ranged from 2,172 to 2,764 (1991–1993 mean, depending on estimator). Estimates for 200–300 mm TL were highly variable and rested on very few recaptures in 1992.
- Capture probabilities were lower for 200–300 mm fish (mean p = 0.0094) than for >300 mm fish (mean p = 0.0143). The authors state this did not substantially bias the population estimate.
- The authors conclude that recruitment to the mainstem adult population is probably largely from the LCR, possibly from small adults (250–350 mm TL) leaving the LCR.

### Mortality evidence

- Brown trout: 10.4% of adults contained subadult chub (95–158 mm TL).
- Channel catfish: 1.5% of adults contained subadult chub.
- Rainbow trout: chub were found in 17% of those captured at the LCR inflow (personal communication).
- Parasites: *Lernaea cyprinacea* was found on 0.13% (8 of 6,294) of fish. The tapeworm *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi* was found in 3.6% (6 of 168) of adults flushed by stomach pump.

### Reviewer assessment

The reviewer's major points were:

1. **Closed models should be dropped.** CAPTURE closure tests have poor power, and a population cannot realistically remain closed for a full year or four years. The reviewer recommended removing Appendices 1, 3, and 4 and Tables 1, 3–7, and using open-population estimates only.
2. **Model synthesis is needed.** Because the four open models did not differ significantly, the reviewer recommended presenting only Model B, the most parsimonious.
3. **Subadult survival is poorly explained.** The conversion from electrofishing catch rates to survival is not clear.
4. **Length-frequency reasoning is circular.** The hypotheses about low 200–300 mm catches were not truly tested, and the conclusion returned to the starting premise. Survival for 200–300 mm fish was not calculated.
5. **The manuscript needs tightening.** Results and discussion overlap, and there is repetition and excess verbiage. The reviewer considered the data important and the revisions straightforward.

### Management implications

The authors suggest the estimates provide baselines for [[Adaptive Management]] monitoring in Grand Canyon. However, the 95% confidence intervals (±20–25% for the LCR) would detect only changes of roughly 30–40% or more. Monitoring would need more intensive, uniform sampling to reduce variability. Further work is needed on the relationship between mainstem and LCR populations.
