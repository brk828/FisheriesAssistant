# Amphibians and Reptiles from Meade County, Kentucky

#herpetology #amphibians #reptiles #kentucky-fauna

## Executive Summary

This herpetological compilation by [[James E Craddock]] and [[W L Minckley]] (published 1964 in *The American Midland Naturalist*) records 57 amphibian and reptile species from [[Meade County]] and adjacent northern Kentucky counties. The material was gathered between February 1959 and January 1962, mostly during fieldwork on [[Doe Run]], a karst creek draining into the [[Ohio River]]. The authors report several range extensions, document habitat use and seasonal activity, and propose three dispersal corridors that shaped the regional fauna.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Meade County lies about 30 miles southwest of [[Louisville]] and is bordered on the north by the Ohio River. The eastern half is part of the [[Pennyroyal Region]], a mature karst upland at 600–750 feet above sea level, with outlier hills rising to nearly 1,000 feet to the south.
- **Geology:** The upland is a residual surface formed in late Tertiary times. The southern limit of Pleistocene glacial advances lies well north of the Ohio River at this point.
- **Sources:** Specimens are housed in the University of Louisville Collection of Vertebrates, with type material deposited at the University of Utah. The paper compiles field notes and supplementary museum records rather than presenting a single controlled study.
- **Sampling methods:** Collections included hand captures, rotenone application in Doe Run, and museum specimens from as early as 1941.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic and riparian habitats**
- [[Cryptobranchus Alleganiensis]] (hellbender) was most common in large waters, and the Doe Run specimens may have moved in from the Ohio River.
- [[Sinkhole Ponds]] supported breeding populations of several salamanders and anurans. Examples include [[Ambystoma Opacum]] and [[Hyla Crucifer]].
- Small high-gradient streams hosted [[Desmognathus Fuscus]] and [[Eurycea Bislineata]]. Green frogs were the only ranid found along these streams.

**Terrestrial and cave habitats**
- [[Plethodon Dorsalis]] and [[Plethodon Glutinosus]] occupied steep, wooded slopes, cliff bases, and rotted logs.
- [[Eurycea Lucifuga]] was found in cave twilight zones. The authors note that habitat separation among the three Eurycea species was not clear.

**Upland and grassland reptiles**
- Open, dry habitats supported [[Cnemidophorus Sexlineatus]], [[Lampropeltis Calligaster]], and [[Sceloporus Undulatus]].
- Snake records include [[Coluber Constrictor]], [[Heterodon platyrhinos]], and [[Agkistrodon contortrix]].

**Human-driven change**
- Before impoundment, the downstream Doe Run valley was cleared of trees. Large concentrations of [[Eurycea Longicauda]] then appeared in a permanent spring brook just above the clearing, possibly after moving out of the disturbed area. No breeding was observed.

## Key Findings

**Species inventory**
- 57 species were recorded in total.
- Among the 57, the authors list 31 species in the main species accounts above. Other records include supplementary specimens from Bullitt, Jefferson, and Oldham counties.

**Range extensions and range edges**
- *Pseudotriton ruber ruber* and *P. montanus diasticus* extend known ranges westward in northern Kentucky.
- [[Plethodon Richmondi]] is at the western limit of its known range. Fifty-seven specimens were taken on the Ohio River floodplain east of Louisville in 1951.
- [[Gastrophryne Carolinensis]] is a northward range extension in Kentucky. Eight adults and five transforming young were recorded, and the authors consider these the first known specimens from along the Ohio River in north central Kentucky.
- *Cnemidophorus sexlineatus* and *Lampropeltis calligaster* occur near the eastern peripheries of their ranges.

**Abundance and ecology**
- *Desmognathus fuscus* was the most common and most variable salamander in Meade County.
- *Eurycea bislineata* was the second most abundant salamander, with 59 specimens.
- *Eurycea longicauda* was represented by 45 specimens.
- *Acris crepitans blanchardi* was the most abundant frog in the collections. *Bufo woodhousei fowleri* was second.
- Among seven stomachs of *Pseudotriton ruber* (out of 11 large specimens with contents), food items included earthworms, other salamanders, beetles, and caterpillars.
- Among the snakes, *Natrix kirtlandi* was represented by 45 specimens taken before 1940 within present-day Louisville.

**Subspecies and taxonomic notes**
- Intergradation is reported for *Pseudacris triseriata* (triseriata × feriarum), *Natrix sipedon* (sipedon × pleuralis), and possibly *Coluber constrictor* (constrictor × priapus).
- The *Desmognathus* specimens were assigned to *D. f. fuscus* rather than *D. f. welteri*, whose taxonomic status the authors consider unresolved.

**Biogeographic routes into Meade County**
- The authors identify three dispersal corridors:
  1. **Ohio River valley** from the southwest, used by *Pseudemys scripta elegans*, *Coluber constrictor priapus*, *Natrix sipedon pleuralis*, and *Rana pipiens sphenocephala*. *Natrix kirtlandi* and *Plethodon richmondi* followed it from the north and east.
  2. **The [[Knobs Region]]**, an avenue for *Pseudotriton ruber*, *P. montanus*, and *Pseudacris brachyphona* from Appalachian centers of abundance.
  3. **The open Pennyroyal uplands**, used by grassland-adapted *Lampropeltis calligaster* and *Cnemidophorus sexlineatus*.
- The [[Bluegrass Region]] appears to be avoided by many species, including *Lygosoma laterale*, *Virginia valeriae*, and *Elaphe obsoleta*. Its periphery forms the eastern range limit for several species along the Ohio River.

**Research recommendation**
- The authors suggest the Bluegrass periphery is a valuable area for studying factors that limit amphibian and reptile distributions.
