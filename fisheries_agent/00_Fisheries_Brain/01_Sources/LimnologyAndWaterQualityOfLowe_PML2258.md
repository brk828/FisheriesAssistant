# Limnology and Water Quality of Lower Lake Havasu During Preoperation and Testing of the Havasu Pumping Plant

#limnology #aquatic-ecology #water-quality #zooplankton #reservoir-ecology

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Reclamation report (Roline and Lieberman, August 1985) is the Phase I baseline limnological and water-quality dataset for [[Lake Havasu]], a [[Colorado River]] reservoir formed by [[Parker Dam]]. Sampling ran from January 1984 through June 1985, at six stations, before and during initial testing of the [[Havasu Pumping Plant]], the intake for the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The study measured physical, chemical and biological variables and modeled the potential intake of [[Bill Williams River]] water into the pumping plant. The document does not address fish; its focus is the aquatic ecosystem (plankton, benthos, water chemistry, habitat).

## Background & Description

- **Lake Havasu:** Created by Parker Dam (completed 1938), with a maximum length of about 65 km and an area of 10,100 ha. Used for water supply to the Metropolitan Water District of Southern California, flood control, power and irrigation.
- **Havasu Pumping Plant:** Located on the south shore of the Bill Williams River arm, east of Parker Dam. Testing began in early 1985. Designed capacity is 3,000 ft³/s (85 m³/s) when fully operational; a 500 ft³/s (14 m³/s) rate occurred at sporadic intervals in 1985.
- **Monitoring objectives:** Predict and document limnological changes in lower Lake Havasu caused by CAP operation. Phase I is the baseline; the document also includes Appendix A (data), Appendix B (intake modeling) and Appendix C (remote sensing mapping).
- **Station network:** Six stations were selected:
  - H-1: control station, midchannel at the entrance to the lower basin.
  - H-2: midchannel, perpendicular to the [[Colorado River Aqueduct]] intake.
  - H-3: about 100 m north of Parker Dam, representing lake discharge.
  - H-4: midchannel at the buoy line of the Bill Williams River arm, a mixing zone.
  - H-5: midbasin inside the diked area in front of the pumping plant.
  - H-6: mouth of the Bill Williams River, representing river inflow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal and chemical regime.** Lake Havasu is monomictic, with thermal stratification in summer and isothermal conditions in winter. Dissolved oxygen was 80 to 100% of saturation at all lake stations, except one exception noted in August. Bill Williams River water generally had lower dissolved oxygen. Redox potential never approached reducing conditions, suggesting that nutrients and metals in sediments would not be released under present conditions.

**Water chemistry.** Mean TDS in the main lake was about 629 mg/L. The Bill Williams River inflow had lower sulfate (130 mg/L vs. 235 mg/L elsewhere) and higher silica. TDS fell during spring river discharge. Conductivity was generally higher in the main lake than in the river inflow, and a lower-conductivity wedge of river water extended toward H-3 during high flows.

**Light and transparency.** Light extinction coefficients and Secchi depths indicated that light transmission was generally greater in the main lake than in the Bill Williams arm, where turbidity was higher. The photic zone was about 3.4 m in the main lake; the Bill Williams arm had a shallower euphotic depth (1 to 9 m range reported).

**Nutrients.** Orthophosphate at H-4 was about twice that in the main lake; during high flows, orthophosphate and total phosphorus at H-4 rose from a previous average of 11 µg/L to 30 and 32 µg/L. Nitrogen forms were similar between basins, except higher nitrate in the river inflow. Orthophosphate and total phosphorus declined over the past 11 years compared with [[Ince]] (1974). Mean total phosphorus and orthophosphate at Parker Dam in 1983–84 and 1984–85 were 12.0 and 11.4 µg/L and 2.0 and 1.6 µg/L respectively.

**Metals.** Metals were generally within accepted aquatic-life criteria, but station H-6 frequently exceeded recommended iron limits. Iron in aerated, slightly alkaline water is expected to precipitate and not produce toxicity.

**Zooplankton.** 34 species were collected. Copepods dominated the main lake, and rotifers dominated at H-6. Zooplankton density increased from H-1 (17.25/L) to H-6 (112.11/L). Copepod, cladoceran and rotifer density differed significantly between H-5 and the other lake stations. Hydraulic retention time was about 13.4 days for the entire study period in the main lake; the Bill Williams arm had much shorter residence times.

**Phytoplankton.** 30 species were identified, mainly diatoms, with [[Fragilaria crotonensis]] dominant. Maximum density was 172,000 cells/L at H-1 in January 1984. Diatoms made up 39.9 to 51.8% of composition. The Bill Williams River station was not highly productive for net phytoplankton because of turbidity.

**Chlorophyll a and trophic status.** Chlorophyll a means were 1.61 mg/m³ (H-1) to 4.00 mg/m³ (H-5), and 11.26 mg/m³ at H-6. Using [[Taylor et al. 1980]] criteria, the main lake was oligotrophic to mesotrophic and the Bill Williams arm mesotrophic to eutrophic. Most phytoplankton biomass was in the <25 µm fraction.

**Benthic fauna.** Benthic density and biomass differed between the main lake and the Bill Williams arm; oligochaetes and chironomids (midges) were the main groups. Density at H-1 and H-2 (543 organisms/m²) was not significantly different from H-4 and H-5 (605 organisms/m²). The study compared these values with other lakes worldwide.

**Discharges.** Discharge through Lake Havasu did not fluctuate radically; monthly diversions to the pumping plant ranged from about 1,358 to 1,985 ft³/s over January 1984 to June 1985.

**Currents and intake modeling.** Mean surface and bottom current velocities at H-1 and H-2 were 5.2 and 4.0 cm/s. Modeling (Appendix B) concluded that most or all Bill Williams River inflow up to the pumping rate would be drawn into the plant, and that withdrawal would extend from the surface to the bottom in spring and summer.

**Remote sensing.** Landsat-5 Thematic Mapper imagery (28 August 1984), matched to 35 field sites, produced maps of Secchi transparency, chlorophyll a and surface temperature. Predicted Secchi transparency was generally greater than 2 m in the main lake, with turbid water near the Bill Williams and Colorado River inflows.

## Key Findings

- The Bill Williams River arm and main lake differed significantly in zooplankton, chlorophyll a and trophic state; the river arm was more productive.
- Lake Havasu is mainly oligotrophic to mesotrophic; the Bill Williams arm is mesotrophic to eutrophic.
- Pumping was predicted to draw most Bill Williams River inflow into the [[Havasu Pumping Plant]], potentially replacing lake water in the diked area.
- Orthophosphate was the nutrient most likely to increase with river inflow, potentially raising primary production.
- Dissolved oxygen, redox conditions and metal concentrations did not indicate major toxicity or sediment-nutrient release.
- Recommendations: reduce or stop pumping during high Bill Williams River inflows (spring runoff or floods), and consider an "epilimnion curtain wall" at the intake channel mouth to draw hypolimnetic water during high inflow or stratification, though it may be only partly effective.
