# Historical Occurrence of Native Fish in Pima County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #native-fish #desert-aquatic-habitats

## Executive Summary

This Pima County staff report, prepared for the Sonoran Desert Conservation Plan and compiled with data from [[Wendell Minckley]], reconstructs the historical distribution of native fishes in Pima County, Arizona. Historic records document 8 native fish species within the county, four of which no longer occur under natural conditions. Of the four remaining, two are federally listed as endangered and one is a candidate for listing. The report concludes that, without a sustained conservation commitment, all native fishes in the region may become extirpated or extinct in the near future.

The accompanying cover letters (February 13, 2001 and December 20, 2000) and a workshop flyer announce a February 27, 2001 workshop on exotic aquatic species threats in eastern Pima County.

## Background & Description

The report was written by [[Pima County Flood Control District]] staff (David Scalero, Donald Ward, Neva Connolly, Julia Fonseca) and reviewed internally. It draws heavily on fish collection records supplied by [[Wendell Minckley]], included in full as Appendix A.1.

Fish science in southern Arizona began in 1904 with [[Frederic Morton Chamberlain]]'s survey of about twenty locations; he counted 16 native species statewide. Today more than half of Arizona's native fishes are extirpated or federally listed. The state total is 34 native species and subspecies (per footnote).

The report is organized into:
- **Setting**: baseline historical habitats of the [[San Pedro River]], [[Santa Cruz River]] basin, Rillito Creek, Cienega Creek, and the western drainages (including [[Quitobaquito Springs]]).
- **Status and Trends**: current fish populations and threats.
- **Discussion**: importance of aquatic habitats and native fish to restoration.
- **Appendices**: a database dump of records (Appendix A.1) and captive fish population summaries (Appendix B.1).

Native fish species profiled include:
- [[Longfin Dace]]: adaptable small stream fish, able to survive in tiny volumes of water beneath algal mats.
- [[Gila Chub]]: found in headwaters, cienegas and springs; collected from deep, vegetated pools.
- [[Roundtail Chub]]: medium to large rivers; concentrated in swift rapids.
- [[Spikedace]]: prefers shallow, moving water over gravel; federally listed.
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: one of the world's largest minnows; federally endangered; historically abundant in the San Pedro.
- [[Speckled Dace]]: shallow, swift water; historically below 5,000 ft, now most abundant at 6,000–8,000 ft.
- [[Loach Minnow]]: riffles over gravel; federally threatened.
- [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]]: tolerant of varied habitats; intolerant of dam-created lakes.
- [[Gila Topminnow]]: tolerates extreme temperatures and salinities.
- [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]]: federally endangered; restricted to small springs and ponds.
- [[Desert Pupfish]]: federally endangered; tolerates water temperatures above 95°F and salinity three times seawater.

Most native fishes are omnivorous and show adaptations to arid conditions, including the ability to recolonize formerly dry channels.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Historically, perennial streams, marshes and cienegas were far more widespread across the county's lowlands. Settlement by Angloamericans, roughly within the last century, converted this system into the dry channels seen today. Documented historical accounts describe marsh, cane and cottonwood-willow riparian systems along the San Pedro, Santa Cruz, Rillito and Cienega Creek.

Principal human pressures identified:
- **Agriculture**: diversion of streamflows since prehistoric times, intensified by Angloamerican settlement; groundwater pumping after pumping technology improved.
- **Cattle grazing**: trampling, erosion, water-quality degradation, and drinking small sources dry.
- **Mining**: groundwater pumping and tailings runoff; the loss of Gila Topminnow in Cocio Wash after 1981 mine spills is a key example.
- **Urbanization**: channelization, bank protection, groundwater depletion, and urban runoff with heavy metals.
- **Nonindigenous species**: [[Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bullfrog]], [[Crayfish]], [[Goldfish]] and [[Tamarisk]] degrade habitat or prey on native fish.

Remaining habitats are small: most lowland streams are dry, and the surviving native fish persist mainly in tributaries such as Sabino Canyon, Cienega Creek and Redfield Canyon. The west county receives little precipitation, and Quitobaquito Springs (within [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]) is the only known population of Quitobaquito Pupfish.

## Key Findings

- **Historic diversity**: 8 native fish species are documented in Pima County over the last century; 4 no longer occur naturally. Of the 4 remaining, 2 are federally endangered and 1 is a candidate for listing.
- **Statewide context**: Chamberlain counted 16 native species in 1904; 34 species and subspecies are now recognized as native to Arizona.
- **San Pedro River**: no perennial stretch in the county is confirmed to hold native fish, though about 6 species are known from upstream reaches; the Bingham Cienega Natural Preserve reach could be sampled.
- **Santa Cruz River**: 6 native species were historically recorded (Table 2). The last documented fish were in 1943 at Midvale Farms irrigation canals, where Longfin Dace and Gila Topminnow were found. Effluent flows support no native fish.
- **Rillito Creek**: Longfin Dace, Gila Chub and Gila Topminnow were recorded; Gila Topminnow last seen near Tanque Verde Guest Ranch in 1943.
- **Cienega Creek**: the most significant remaining population; Longfin Dace in the lower and upper reaches; Gila Chub and Gila Topminnow in the upper reach. Cienega Creek supports the most significant natural population of native fish remaining in the county.
- **Sabino Canyon**: Gila Chub is the last remaining native fish, threatened by non-native species.
- **Cocio Wash**: Longfin Dace and Gila Topminnow declined after the 1981 mine spill; topminnow declared extinct in the area by 1982.
- **Quitobaquito Springs**: the only known population of Quitobaquito Pupfish; the last natural population of this genus in Arizona after Desert Pupfish and Monkey Springs Pupfish disappeared.
- **Re-establishment**: most re-established populations failed. Most disappeared quickly; some lasted 5–10 years. Causes include drying, flooding, invasive species, cattle overuse, dredging and low oxygen. Small ponds and springs were inherently vulnerable.
- **Captive populations**: held at the [[Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum]], [[International Wildlife Museum]], Flowing Wells Junior High School, [[Tohono Chul Park]], and the [[Arizona Historical Society]]. Gila Topminnow populations are expanding at most sites; Desert Pupfish is more sensitive to chlorination and food availability. The [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico maintains stocks as a safeguard.
- **Nonindigenous fish**: [[Mosquitofish]] were found in Santa Cruz effluent ponds; non-native species in the Central Arizona Project canal would have to be removed before restoration.
- **Conclusion**: without serious, sustained commitment, all native fish in the region may become extirpated or extinct. County staff are working with experts to develop reintroduction programs as part of the Sonoran Desert Conservation Plan.
- **Workshop**: a February 27, 2001 one-day workshop (PAG, 177 N. Church Ave, Tucson) on exotic aquatic species threats, with RSVP to Jacki Ontiveros.
