# Hybridization in Birds and Darwin's Finch Fitness on Daphne Major

#ornithology #hybridization #geospiza

## Executive Summary

This document is a Science article by P. R. Grant and B. R. Grant (10 April 1992) on [[Hybridization]] in birds. It combines a global survey of hybridizing bird species with a long-term pedigree study of three [[Darwin's Finches]] (*Geospiza* spp.) on [[Daphne Major Island]], Galápagos. The central finding is that hybrids of the finches had higher [[Fitness]] than their parental species over several years. The authors argue this challenges conventional [[Species Concept]]s and has consequences for conservation policy. The document opens with an OCR-fragmented reference list and acknowledgments from an unrelated geoscience paper on mantle plumes; these contain no data relevant to birds or aquatic systems and are not summarized further.

## Background & Description

Hybridization is the interbreeding of species, which the authors describe as central to both taxonomic judgment and the study of speciation. Earlier estimates of bird hybridization were based on fragmentary surveys:

- [[Mayr and Short]] estimated that about 10% of 516 nonmarine bird species hybridize regularly.
- Meise concluded that 2% of recent species hybridize regularly and a further 3% occasionally.

The authors' updated global count, using a comprehensive world species list and a catalogue of hybridizing species, gives:

- **895 of 9,672 species (9.2%)** known to have produced hybrid offspring in nature, roughly one in ten.
- **Anseriformes (ducks and geese)** have the highest incidence, with 67 of 161 species (41.6%) hybridizing.
- Other high-incidence orders include Galliformes (21.5%), Trochiliformes (hummingbirds, 19.1%), and Piciformes (13.5%).
- Hybridization is unrecorded in eight of 23 orders, including Apodiformes (swifts), which has more than 100 species.

The authors stress that these figures are likely underestimates. Cryptic species are rarely studied, and many species have no congener within their range. Hybridization is also more frequent in temperate than tropical regions, though this may partly reflect uneven knowledge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study site is Daphne Major, a small volcanic island (0.34 km²) near the equator in the central [[Galápagos Archipelago]], about 8 km north of the much larger Santa Cruz Island (904 km²). The environment is seasonally arid and climatically fluctuating. Finches breed only in wet years, and the island is small enough for near-complete documentation of every nest and individual.

Three ground finch species are relevant:

- ***Geospiza fortis*** (medium ground finch, about 17 g), the most abundant resident.
- ***Geospiza scandens*** (cactus finch, about 20 g), similar in plumage and body size but differing in beak proportions and feeding habits.
- ***Geospiza fuliginosa*** (small ground finch, about 12 g), which occasionally immigrates from Santa Cruz and rarely breeds.

The island experienced a severe drought in 1977 that caused heavy finch mortality and temporary population declines. Selection also shifts with climate: large body size is favored in dry conditions and small size after very wet periods, a pattern linked to the 1982–83 El Niño event.

## Key Findings

**Long-term demographic study (1976–1991)**
- Banding began in 1973 and 1975, with a breeding study conducted every year from 1976 to 1991.
- Harmonic mean breeding population sizes were 197 *G. fortis*, 94 *G. scandens*, and 6 *G. fuliginosa*.
- Hybridization is rare but recurring. In 10 of 12 breeding years at least one *G. fortis* × *G. fuliginosa* pair formed; in half of those years *G. fortis* × *G. scandens* pairs also formed.
- Hybridizing birds made up 1.9% of breeding *G. fortis*, 0.9% of *G. scandens*, and 70.8% of *G. fuliginosa*, the last reflecting scarce conspecific mates. *G. fuliginosa* never bred with *G. scandens*.

**Reproduction**
- Hybrids bred as often as conspecifics and laid clutches of similar size. Mean clutch sizes were 3.9 eggs for *G. fortis* × *G. fuliginosa* pairs, 3.9 for *G. fortis*, and 4.0 for *G. fuliginosa* (ANOVA, P > 0.1).
- Hatching success of *G. fortis* × *G. fuliginosa* pairs was higher than that of conspecific *G. fortis* pairs (χ² = 6.89, P < 0.01).
- The only sign of hybrid inferiority was slightly lower hatching in *G. fortis* × *G. scandens* pairs, which was not significant (P > 0.1).

**Survival and fertility**
- Hybrids born in 1983 survived better than their parental contemporaries. *G. fortis* × *G. scandens* hybrids survived better than the parental species in 6 of 8 years.
- Hybrid survival in the 1987 cohort was higher than any conspecific survival over the following four years.
- Hybrids are fertile. They have backcrossed to *G. fortis* and *G. scandens*, though not to *G. fuliginosa*, and the backcrosses also bred successfully.
- No Haldane's rule effect (female heterogametic sterility) was found. Reciprocal crosses differed in hatching success (79% vs. 54%), not in the direction predicted by the rule.
- No hybrid breakdown was seen in the F₂ generation (four clutches, hatching and fledging 0.86 each).

**Overall fitness (1987 cohorts over four years)**

Fitness is the product of survival, recruitment, and breeding success. A value of 1.0 indicates numerical replacement.

| Class | Initial cohort (n) | Fitness |
|---|---|---|
| *G. fortis* × *G. scandens* | 7 | 2.868 |
| *G. fortis* × *G. fuliginosa* | 32 | 1.330 |
| *G. fortis* backcross (with *G. fuliginosa*) | 34 | 1.669 |
| *G. fortis* backcross (with *G. scandens*) | 43 | 1.148 |
| *G. fortis* | 960 | 0.844 |
| *G. scandens* | 163 | 0.780 |
| *G. fuliginosa* | 10 | 0.300 |

Both hybrid types and both backcross types exceeded 1.0, while the three parental species did not. The authors conclude that the observed outcome is higher hybrid fitness, driven mainly by higher survival.

**Interpretation**
- Proposed causes of hybrid superiority include intermediate beak sizes that exploit food more effectively, and elevated heterozygosity. The authors found no evidence of inbreeding depression in *G. fortis* after the 1977 drought.
- The hybrid advantage is not attributed to novel or disturbed environments, since Daphne has not been disturbed.
- The authors argue the three populations may be fusing, but that over the long term a selection–hybridization balance should keep them phenotypically and genetically distinct. Intermediate hybrids will be disadvantaged when large body size is again favored.
- Elsewhere in the archipelago, *G. fortis* coexists with *G. fuliginosa* on 15 islands and with *G. scandens* on 14 without apparent hybridization, though these islands have been studied less intensively.

**Conservation implications**
- Hybridizing species fall outside protection under the U.S. Endangered Species Act of 1973, according to O'Brien and Mayr.
- Rare species are the most likely to hybridize because conspecific mates are scarce. Under current "hybrid policy," the species most in need of protection could therefore lose legal protection by hybridizing.
- The authors call the evolutionary and conservation implications of hybridization "profound."
