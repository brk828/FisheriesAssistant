# Turbulence and the Periodicity of Freshwater Melosira Diatoms

#limnology #phycology #diatoms #melosira

## Executive Summary

This 1966 review, translated from Russian by J.W.G. Lund (Freshwater Biological Association, Ambleside), argues that water turbulence is a major driver of the seasonal and vertical distribution of planktonic [[Melosira Diatoms]] in lakes and reservoirs. Turbulence keeps cells suspended, while thermal stratification (a [[Thermocline]] or metalimnion) lets them sink and be lost to bottom deposits. The author extends this framework to [[Lake Baikal]], where the spring bloom of [[Melosira Baicalensis]] occurs under ice, and proposes a hypothesis involving under-ice currents and possible gas-bubble flotation. The note is partly a critical synthesis of Soviet, European and African literature and is candid about unresolved questions.

## Background & Description

- **Taxa considered:** Seven Melosira taxa (plus subspecies), including [[Melosira Italica]] (with subsp. subarctica), [[Melosira Islandica]] (with subsp. helvetica), [[Melosira Baicalensis]], [[Melosira Ambigua]], [[Melosira Granulata]], [[Melosira Nyassensis]] (with var. victoriae) and [[Melosira Agassizii]]. The author notes that taxonomy and nomenclature are unsatisfactory, and that several forms of common species exist but are not characterised.
- **Ecological grouping:**
  - *Cold-water group* (M. italica, M. islandica, M. baicalensis): temperate, sub-arctic and arctic lakes; main development from autumn to spring.
  - *Warm-water group* (M. ambigua, M. granulata): common in temperate and tropical waters, summer forms in temperate lands.
  - *Tropical group* (M. nyassensis, M. agassizii): found only in the tropics.
- **Comparative taxon:** [[Asterionella Formosa]] from Windermere is used as a model of how sinking and stratification shape depth distribution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Turbulence and sinking:** Under isothermal, calm conditions a plankton alga sinks at a relatively rapid rate. Increasing turbulence eventually randomises cell distribution, so further mixing mainly resuspends cells from deposits. Once stratified, only epilimnion cells stay mixed; cells nearer the metalimnion descend faster as turbulence falls with depth.
- **Survival in sediments:** Some Melosira species survive for long periods buried in mud, even without light and oxygen. Examples cited include M. italica (Guseva and Il'inskii 1959), M. islandica subsp. helvetica (Nipkow 1950) and M. italica subsp. subarctica (Lund 1954). Nipkow found live cells in sediment layers 4-5 years old in [[Zürichsee]].
- **Temperature limits:** A reviewer of Russian data on M. islandica suggested that about 10°C is roughly the usual limit for active multiplication. This temperature is often reached when thermal stratification arises, so the decline of the alga may partly reflect reduced turbulence.
- **Seasonal regimes differ by lake type:** Temperate English lakes are isothermal from about October to April due to their morphology and oceanic climate. Rybinsk Reservoir stratifies in winter under ice, and its main Melosira growth occurs in summer when it is turbulently mixed because of its size and shallowness.
- **Under-ice dynamics:** Earlier assumptions that currents are absent under ice are considered wrong, following Sokol'nikov (1960). Significant horizontal and vertical currents occur beneath ice.
- **Tropical lakes:** In [[Lake Nyasa]], M. nyassensis is most abundant during steady trade winds, which mix the water down to about 75 m.

## Key Findings

1. **Turbulence controls periodicity.** Melosira abundance tracks the stratification state. When the water is stratified, directly or inversely, the alga is scarce or absent. When it is isothermal, the alga reappears.
2. **Long-term English data.** Weekly estimates over 18 consecutive years in three English Lake District waters confirmed that the cold-water taxon M. italica subsp. subarctica follows this pattern (Figures 1-3).
3. **Winter ice alters the cycle.** When small English lakes froze for about 2 months (1947; 1962-63), the normal development of M. italica subsp. subarctica was altered.
4. **Artificial mixing experiment.** In 1960 Blelham Tarn was artificially overturned by air jets near the bottom. M. italica subsp. subarctica then appeared and grew in summer. Its numbers fell after mixing ceased and rose again at the normal autumn overturn.
5. **Redistribution by wind.** In summer, wind-driven resuspension and horizontal transport move threads from shallow to deep areas. Repeated over time, this impoverishes shallow zones and enriches deep ones.
6. **Warm-water species respond to mixing.** M. granulata in a London reservoir (Ridley 1962) peaked after wind-induced mixing. The second bloom was smaller because [[Silica Depletion]] had reduced silica to very low levels. A third mixing event produced no further increase.
7. **Crose Mere pattern.** In 1962 M. granulata bloomed under nearly isothermal conditions. In 1963, with more prolonged summer stratification, it was very rare and exceeded 10,000 cells per litre only in autumn, after mixing.
8. **Lake Baikal timing.** M. baicalensis growth is associated with warming to 6-8°C. The summer decline is linked to direct stratification. The measured fall rate of threads is 3-6 m per day (Skabichevskii 1929, 1960; Votintsev 1953).
9. **Internal seiche event.** Between 10 and 21 August 1926, the 0-150 m layer changed from strongly stratified to nearly unstratified, probably due to an internal seiche. Melosira abundance in that layer changed in step.
10. **Spring bloom mystery.** M. baicalensis begins multiplying under ice in February, though it is rare mid-winter. The source of the spring cells and how threads stay afloat until breakup (April to June) remain unexplained. Light increase may trigger growth, and autumn storm-distributed threads may be the seed population.
11. **Flotation hypothesis rejected in part.** Oxygen from photosynthesis was proposed by Skabichevskii (1960) as the flotation mechanism. Lund objects that no gas bubbles have been seen on cells and that small bubbles should dissolve by surface tension. Bubbles among aggregated threads could still form stable floats under ice.
12. **Under-ice transport.** Kozhova (1961) found no relationship between snow depth and Melosira abundance under ice. Lund reads this as evidence for massive horizontal transport under ice, which would move populations away from production centres.
13. **Proposed Baikal hypothesis.** Autumn storms resuspend threads, and under-ice currents keep them in suspension through winter. A warm, prolonged autumn may favour a larger spring crop (Kozhov 1955, 1962). Multiplication may be greater in littoral and shallow areas, where bubble-driven flocs could reach the surface.
14. **Tropical and warm-water taxa.** Evidence suggests that M. ambigua, M. granulata and tropical species such as M. nyassensis sink relatively rapidly and can survive long periods in darkness. Their periodicity may also depend on turbulence, though evidence is limited.
15. **Author's caution.** Lund states that the few examples should not be over-emphasised, but that the possible importance of changes in thermal stratification should not be overlooked.
