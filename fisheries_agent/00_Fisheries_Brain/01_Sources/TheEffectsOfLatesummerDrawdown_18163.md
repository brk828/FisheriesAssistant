# Late-Summer Drawdown Effects on the Fish Population of Ridge Lake, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #micropterus-salmoides

## Executive Summary

This 1954 paper by [[George W. Bennett]] of the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] reports a preliminary experiment in which [[Ridge Lake]], a small Illinois impoundment, was lowered 15 feet in September 1951 and again in 1952. The drawdown reduced lake area by 69 per cent. The author found that [[Largemouth Bass]] numbers were barely affected, while [[Bluegill]] numbers, especially small fish, fell sharply. The results suggest that a [[Drawdown]] can act differentially on the components of a fish community, and that its effects depend on the species present and the magnitude of the water-level change.

## Background & Description

The paper builds on earlier work by [[R. W. Eschmeyer]] on [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] reservoirs. Eschmeyer reported that permanent-level pools yielded poorer fishing than reservoirs with fluctuating levels, and he proposed that winter drawdowns limit rough fish by limiting their food without seriously harming game fish. Earlier studies by [[H. S. Swingle]] and [[E. V. Smith]] showed that pond standing crop is related to pond area and fertility.

Ridge Lake is an artificial impoundment of about 17 acres (originally 18 acres, reduced by silting). It has served as an outdoor laboratory for largemouth bass since it was stocked in 1941. The lake was drained in March every two years for fish censuses, and marked fish were returned after each census. Fish species present changed over time:

- Largemouth bass alone, 1941 to July 1944
- Bass and bluegills, July 1944 to May 1949
- Bass, bluegills and [[Warmouth Bass]], May 1949 to May 1951
- Bass, bluegills, warmouth bass and [[Channel Catfish]], May 1951 to March 1953

Green sunfish and black bullheads entered intermittently from a feeder stream and were removed at censuses.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The drawdowns lowered the lake 15 feet by opening a screened outlet in early September 1951 and again in 1952, after summer fishing. The level dropped in about 36 hours. Lake area fell from 17 acres to 5.25 acres (a 69 per cent reduction), and maximum depth at the outlet fell from 25 feet to 10 feet. Water remained low until December, and the basin had nearly refilled by early March. Surface water stayed above 55 °F through September and early October in both years.

The September drawdown exposed the littoral zone, which was covered with dense potamogeton and other submerged aquatics. Small fishes and aquatic invertebrates were forced into open water, or were trapped in plant mats and shallow depressions that dried within three or four days. Water temperatures in the seventies °F at drawdown likely sped digestion and food conversion, though growth would have nearly stopped after mid-October as waters cooled.

Bennett expected the drawdown to reduce invertebrate populations sharply. Reflooding would then favor fast-expanding forms such as entomostraca over large aquatic insects and crayfish. Bluegills, which feed on small organisms, might therefore find abundant food in newly flooded areas, while bass might rely on terrestrial food.

## Key Findings

**Fish population losses and survival**

- Pre-drawdown censuses (1947, 1949, 1951) were compared with the post-drawdown census of 1953 (Table 1). Poundage per acre of bass, bluegills and other fish, and the totals, were all higher before the drawdown, even after channel catfish stocked weights were subtracted.
- Total weight fell to 116.9 lb/acre in 1953 from 256.3 lb/acre (1947), 140.2 (1949) and 163.8 (1951). Bluegill weight fell to 58.8 lb/acre from 193.3 (1947), 86.9 (1949) and 105.2 (1951). Bass weight was 26.6 lb/acre in 1953 against 50.4 (1949) and 49.9 (1951).
- The 69 per cent area reduction would predict a similar weight decline. The author judged the actual decline to show partial adjustment that had not reached its limit.

**Numbers and sizes (Table 2, fish of three inches or more)**

- Largemouth bass totals ranged from 2,025 to 2,740 across the four estimates. Numbers of large bass were highest in 1948 and lowest in 1952. No loss of bass numbers could be shown.
- Bluegill totals were more than 50,000 in 1946 and 1950, and about 25,500 in 1948. In 1952 they fell to about 10,600, only 41.5 per cent of the 1948 figure. Large bluegills (5,941) outnumbered small ones (4,654) for the first time in the author's experience. Numbers of bluegills were severely reduced.
- Channel catfish made up 786 of the 1,181 "other fish" in 1952. Warmouth bass numbered 322, black bullheads 16 and green sunfish 57. A few carp (over 12 inches) were present in 1947.
- Channel catfish were stocked as predators on excess small bluegills. Of 675 fish stocked in May 1951, 143 were caught in 1951 and 138 in 1952. The author later judged the stocking a mistake because it added a variable.

**Bass reproduction**

- Bass fry were counted as schools along the shallows. Estimated fry in years without culling of small fish were low, especially after 1945 when bluegills were abundant (2,500 in 1946).
- Years with March draining and culling of small fish produced more fry: 37,000 (1947), 24,000 (1949) and 32,000 (1951).
- The estimated 34,500 fry in 1952 was similar to the culled years, even though no culling occurred. The author concluded that the 1951 drawdown was producing a culling effect on small fish. Survival to the schooling stage appeared to depend largely on predation by small fish.

**Yields and angling**

- In 1952 anglers caught 776 bass (46 per acre, 15.8 lb/acre). This was the highest count of any season, but all but 187 were 7.5 to 9.8 inches and from the 1951 year class.
- Bass yields by weight were 14.6 lb/acre (1946), 25.6 (1948) and 18.3 (1950).
- Bluegill yields were 12.7 lb/acre (1946), 45.9 (1948), 6.7 (1950) and 31.1 (1952). The 1952 catch was second only to 1948.
- Fishing pressure was 168 (1946), 320 (1948), 223 (1950) and 290 (1952) man-hours per acre.
- The 1951–1953 unaccountable loss of marked bass was 13.4 per cent, above earlier periods (11.0, 9.7 and 11.1 per cent). Poaching was suspected.

**Interpretation and recommendations**

- The author concluded that the 15-foot drawdown was too severe for the bluegill population. A 10-foot drop (about 35 per cent area reduction) might have retained more bluegills and yielded more in 1952 without seriously harming growth or bass spawning.
- Drawdown effects are expected to vary by lake and species mix, so optimum levels must be tested for each lake.
- Drawdown is limited by physical feasibility. Artificial lakes with drain outlets are suitable, while few natural lakes are. In northern shallow lakes, drawdowns risk [[Winterkill]] when refilling runoff arrives late.
- The author argued that drawdown merits further study as a management tool.
