# Mesquite Spring Renovation and Desert Pupfish Stocking Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae #endangered-species #habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Land Management report documents the 1991 renovation of [[Mesquite Spring]] in Pinal County, Arizona, and an unsuccessful attempt to re-establish a population of [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*). The spring had been originally stocked with 200 pupfish in December 1983, which initially survived and reproduced, but by July 1989 no pupfish remained. Habitat degradation from livestock use, accumulation of organic sludge, and dense [[Duck Weed]] (*Lemna* sp.) coverage created severely degraded conditions. Despite extensive renovation efforts including fencing, dredging, and substrate improvement, the October 1991 restocking failed due to critically low dissolved oxygen levels (0.60-1.0 mg/l), resulting in rapid mortality of approximately 25 of 100 released fish.

## Background & Description

[[Mesquite Spring]] is located in Pinal County, Arizona (T. 3 S., R. 11 E., Sec. 21, SE¼SE¼). The site was initially stocked with 200 [[Desert Pupfish]] in December 1983 by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. According to Bureau of Land Management Middle Gila HMP records for FY 84, the introduced population survived and reproduced, though no specific monitoring data were recorded.

By August 1989, site conditions had severely degraded compared to 1982 photographs. Livestock use had increased significantly, and a fence constructed of railroad ties and four-strand barbed wire had been replaced with steel "T" posts and welded wire mesh "hog-wire," with railroad ties left in the pond. The entire pond was covered with [[Duck Weed]] (*Lemna* sp.), and substrate consisted of a 0.30-meter-thick layer of organic ooze. Sampling in July 1989 found no pupfish present.

Renovation efforts occurred in two phases:
- March 27-28, 1991: Construction of 16 × 27 meter four-strand (smooth bottom) barbed wire fence to exclude cattle; initial dredging with backhoe to remove railroad ties and organic sludge (limited by equipment reach and water depth)
- August 29, 1991: Second dredging removed approximately 0.75 meter of benthic sludge; sand and gravel spread to depth of approximately 0.15 meter to create spawning substrate; final pond dimensions approximately 7 × 5 × 1 meter

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water quality parameters measured October 4, 1991 (Hach Water Test Kit Model FF-1A):
- Dissolved Oxygen: 0.60-1.0 mg/l
- Water Temperature: 20.5°C
- pH: 7.9
- Ammonia: 0.018 mg/l
- Carbon Dioxide: 60 mg/l
- Chloride: 68-90 mg/l
- Total Hardness: 410 mg/l
- Nitrite: 0.02 mg/l
- Nitrite N: 0.07 mg/l
- Mean depth: approximately 0.30 meter

The pond remained entirely covered with [[Duck Weed]], though water was clear beneath the surface mat. The critically low dissolved oxygen was attributed to:
1. Dense duck weed mat inhibiting atmospheric oxygen exchange
2. Oxygen depletion from bacterial decomposition of remaining organic matter

[[Desert Pupfish]] are known for extraordinary tolerance of extreme environmental conditions, including low dissolved oxygen (0.1-0.4 mg/l; Barlow 1958). However, the measured dissolved oxygen at [[Mesquite Spring]] (0.60-1.0 mg/l), while above literature minimums, was likely an order of magnitude lower than source pond conditions at [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]].

## Key Findings

**Restocking Attempt (October 10, 1991):**
- Approximately 200 [[Desert Pupfish]] seined from [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]] pond (water temperature 21°C)
- Source fish measurements: mean Total Length 35 mm, range 19-54 mm (n=30)
- Fish transported in two water-filled coolers with approximately equal numbers
- Upon release of first cooler, pupfish observed breaking surface mat of [[Duck Weed]] and gulping for oxygen
- Within approximately 10 minutes, approximately 25 pupfish laid motionless on duck weed mat, presumed dead
- Remaining pupfish in second cooler not released and returned to [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]]

**Mortality Analysis:**
The pupfish mortality was attributed to shock from drastic reduction in dissolved oxygen between source and release sites, despite the species' known tolerance for hypoxic conditions. The combination of dense [[Duck Weed]] coverage preventing atmospheric oxygen exchange and ongoing bacterial decomposition of organic matter created unsurvivable conditions.

**Management Recommendations:**
The report concluded that [[Desert Pupfish]] will not survive under current biological conditions at [[Mesquite Spring]]. Periodic monitoring within the following year was recommended to conclusively determine presence/absence of pupfish and track biological conditions at the site.
