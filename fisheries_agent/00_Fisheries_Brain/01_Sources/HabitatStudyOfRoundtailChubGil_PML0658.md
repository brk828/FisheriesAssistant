# Habitat Study of Roundtail Chub (Gila robusta) and Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis) in the Gila and San Francisco River Systems, 1979

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1979 field report by [[Ecology Audits, Inc.]] for the [[U.S. Forest Service]] assessed habitat use, distribution and basic life history of two endangered or sensitive cyprinid fishes, the [[Loach Minnow]] and the [[Roundtail Chub]], across the [[Gila River]] and [[San Francisco River]] drainages in New Mexico and one site in Arizona. Field work ran from 23 April to 12 October 1979 (120 man-days in the field, plus 30 man-days for literature review, laboratory analysis and reporting). Early-season sampling failed because of high runoff, and the December 1978 flood and heavy 1979 spring runoff had scattered and reduced both populations. October sampling, dominated by young-of-the-year, suggested recovery. The report concludes that the rivers provide adequate habitat, with water flow, water quality, elevation and food availability as the likely limiting factors.

## Background & Description

**Objectives.** The study aimed to document the distribution of both species within the Gila and San Francisco drainages, to clarify life histories, and to define habitat and instream flow requirements for habitat management planning.

**Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis).** A small, dorsoventrally flattened cyprinid reaching about 10 cm (4 in) as an adult, with a small mouth lacking barbels, olive body coloration and white spots at the fin bases. Breeding males develop tubercles on the head, pectoral fins and caudal peduncle, and bright red-orange coloration appears at the fin bases, mouth and head. Historically endemic to much of the Gila River basin in Arizona, New Mexico and Sonora, Mexico. Barber and Minckley (1966) considered it essentially extirpated from most of its historical range, with surviving populations in Aravaipa Creek, the Gila River in New Mexico and possibly the Rio San Pedro in Sonora.

**Roundtail Chub (Gila robusta).** A larger cyprinid, typically adult-sized in the study, found in deeper pools and slower water than the loach minnow.

**Taxonomic note.** The Gila robusta population in this area was referred to as *Gila robusta grahami* in some field records.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Loach minnows were associated with shallow (less than about 15 cm / 6 in), turbulent, swift riffles over coarse gravel and rubble, covered with filamentous green algae (e.g., *Spirogyra*, *Cladophora*). They rest on the bottom, supported by their pectoral fins. Roundtail chubs were found in deeper pools and slower water, and young-of-the-year of both species were concentrated in shallower, swifter water.

**Diet.** Loach minnow diet is poorly documented. Minckley (1973) reported feeding on aquatic insect larvae, especially mayflies and simuliid dipterans, with diet shifting as insect availability changes. Stomachs collected in this study were mostly empty or unidentifiable. Roundtail chubs are largely omnivorous, with stomachs mostly full of algae, plus hellgrammites, crayfish and dipteran larvae; no fish were found, although one chub was caught on a speckled dace bait.

**Reproduction.** Loach minnows spawn in late winter to early spring, with fecundity of 250 to 1,200 ova per female and first spawning in the second summer of life. Gravid females were collected 19 and 20 June 1979, with 95 and 175 ova, which the authors attributed to delayed spawning from high, cold runoff. Roundtail chub gravid females were collected 8, 9 and 10 May and 21 June 1979, with 3,090 to 15,893 ova.

**Threats.** Literature cited by the report identifies habitat loss (excessive silt, dewatering from dams and irrigation, climatic drying), introduction of exotic species such as the red shiner ([[Red Shiner]]) and the longfin dace competition, and stocking of game fish as major causes of decline. The authors note that the loach minnow could be extirpated without careful management.

**Environmental parameters (field data).**
- Water temperature: 16–27 °C (61–81 °F) at loach minnow sites; 15–27 °C (59–72 °F) at roundtail chub sites.
- pH: 7.6–8.2 for loach minnow sites (mean 7.9); 7.2–8.0 for roundtail chub (mean 7.6).
- Dissolved oxygen: 6.0–10.0 mg/L (mean 8.2) for loach minnow; 5.0–10.0 mg/L (mean 7.7) for roundtail chub.
- Water depth at capture points: 7.6–27.9 cm (3–11 in) for loach minnow; 50.8–96.5 cm (20–38 in) for roundtail chub.
- Water velocity at loach minnow capture points: 0.153–0.602 m/s (0.503–1.976 ft/s); discharge 0.072–1.343 m³/s.
- Elevation range: loach minnow about 1,500 to 2,100 m (4,900–7,100 ft); roundtail chub about 1,200 to 2,300 m (4,000–7,500 ft).

**Vegetation and habitat associations.** No clear influence of streamside vegetation on distribution was detected. Associated fishes included Sonora sucker, Gila mountain sucker, longfin dace, speckled dace, spikedace, and channel catfish.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution.** Both species occupied much of the Gila River system from the southern Gila National Forest near Red Rock to the East, Middle and West Forks. Southernmost loach minnow capture was near Bill Evans Lake; northernmost near the confluence of the Gila forks. Roundtail chub were found in the Gila system up to Beaver Creek and the Middle and West Forks, and only at Harden Cienega Canyon in the San Francisco drainage.
- **Sampling effort.** About 120 man-days in the field; sites were sampled by electroshocking, seining and rod-and-reel.
- **Population status.** Populations of both species declined after the 1978 December flood and 1979 spring runoff. Fish were scattered into mid-summer. October 1979 samples were dominated by young-of-the-year, indicating recovery.
- **Spawning delay.** Spawning in 1979 was delayed relative to historical data because of prolonged cold runoff.
- **Limiting factors.** Water depth and instream flow were identified as the most significant factors affecting distribution. Elevation, water flow and food availability may be the strongest limiting parameters. Water chemistry and streamside vegetation varied little among capture sites.
- **Conclusion.** The authors conclude that the Gila and San Francisco rivers and tributaries provide adequate habitat for both species. They recommend keeping these systems unimpounded to preserve flow and habitat.
- **Data gaps.** Age and growth could not be determined from scales. The authors state that a larger study would be needed to quantify the factors affecting distribution.
