# Angling for Answers: The Quest to Save the Colorado's Endangered Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This feature article from the *River Report* (Summer 1998) examines recovery efforts for four endangered native fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. It frames the tension between endangered species protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) and water and power development in basin states. The article describes stocking, flow reoperation, bottomland restoration and critical habitat designation as the main recovery tools, and reports the economic costs of these measures.

## Background & Description

- **Focal species:** Four native fishes at highest risk of extinction in the Colorado River Basin: razorback sucker, bonytail, humpback chub and Colorado squawfish.
- **Critical habitat:** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service ([[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], USFWS) designated 1,980 miles of the Colorado River and its tributaries as critical habitat for the four fish.
- **Distribution:** With the exception of the Colorado squawfish, the highest concentrations of the endangered fish are in the Lower Basin, although the Upper Basin is believed to contain the most natural habitat needed to sustain them.
- **Parallel concern:** The endangered [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] in the Lower Basin receives comparable attention, but the article does not cover it in depth.
- **Development context:** New Mexico has developed about 430,000 acre-feet along the [[San Juan River]]; its estimated apportionment is 727,000 acre-feet.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline and management issues**
- Dams and diversion structures, built without considering endangered species, are identified as the core problem, and water development is the main constraint on recovery.
- "Demand following" hydropower operations create daily flow fluctuations that flush larvae from warm backwaters into faster, downstream waters where predation and nutrient shortage reduce survival.
- Fluctuating peak releases erode sandbars and backwater riparian habitat below dams such as [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

**Habitat restoration**
- Bottomlands, which provide flood plain nursery habitat, are a focal point. The USFWS uses federal, state and voluntary private leases to remove dikes and seasonally flood them.
- Biologists estimate wetlands provide crucial habitat for 60 to 90 percent of all terrestrial species (as stated in the article).

## Key Findings

**1. Recovery tools in use**
- **Stocking:** Razorback larvae collected for propagation were predicted to exceed 120,000 by the end of 1998, with 1,000 young fish returned to [[Lake Mohave]]; a total of 15,000 razorbacks were expected to have been added. Tom Burke of the Bureau noted that no hatchery could match the genetic pool of Lake Mohave.
- **Fish passage:** Ladders were built at dams including the Redlands Diversion on the Gunnison River, opening over 100 miles of native spawning habitat. Future ladders at the Price-Stubbs and Government Highline Diversion dams would extend access to Rifle, Colorado.
- **Non-native fish management:** A 1996 agreement in the Upper Basin limits the timing, type and range of non-native fish stocking, many of which prey on endangered natives.
- **Flow reoperation:** [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Blue Mesa Dam]] adopted seasonal and daily target flows; daytime and evening releases now vary by no more than 25 percent. Spring releases were increased to mimic natural floods.
- **Artificial flood:** An artificial spring flood was created at Glen Canyon Dam in 1996 to restore sandbars and fish habitat, with further floods planned as sediment accumulation requires.

**2. Economic costs**
- Recovery programs cost residents, businesses and governments millions of dollars.
- Palmer estimated that altering peak flows could cost Upper Basin consumers around $15 million over five years.

**3. Development and regulatory issues**
- Turney (New Mexico state engineer) called the process "a gamble," warning that unfavorable Section 7 opinions could cost New Mexico "hundreds of thousands of acre-feet."
- Since 1988, about 400 water projects in Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, totaling at least 671,000 acre-feet, received favorable biological reviews.
- USFWS will continue to issue favorable opinions for projects with annual depletions under 1,500 acre-feet. Larger projects are reviewed case by case. A one-time depletion charge applies to projects above 100 acre-feet per year.
- Critical habitat covers nearly all of the Upper Colorado and its tributaries, plus 412 river miles of the Lower Colorado (from the Mexican border to the upper end of Lake Mead, and a section through the Grand Canyon), and 1,400 acres of riparian ecosystem for other endangered species.

**4. Policy debate**
- Under current ESA guidelines, critical habitat is designated at listing, after public comment and peer review. Miller (USFWS) argues that designation at listing often precedes full knowledge of species needs.
- Two bills (S. 1180 and H.R. 2341) proposed moving critical habitat designation to the recovery planning stage. Critics argue critical habitat is costly and duplicates listing.

**5. Monitoring**
- Only about 230 to 500 nesting pairs of the Southwestern Willow Flycatcher are estimated to remain, largely because riparian cottonwood and willow habitat has been lost.
