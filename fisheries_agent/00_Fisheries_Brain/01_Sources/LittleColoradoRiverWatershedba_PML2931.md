# Arizona Game and Fish Department: Responses to Review Comments on NGTR 146 and NGTR 169

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This June 2001 document from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) summarizes and responds to comments on two draft Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program Technical Reports: NGTR 146, an [[Integrated Fisheries Management Plan]] for the [[Little Colorado River]] watershed, and NGTR 169, *Fisheries and Watershed Management in Arizona: Looking into the Future*. Comments came from federal and state agencies, academic reviewers, interest groups (including Trout Unlimited and a Grand Canyon native fish work group) and private citizens. The response set contains roughly 171 numbered comments. Most responses either defend the original scope or describe revisions, and a single "Newly Written Common Foreword" was added to both reports to frame AGFD's future role in [[Watershed Associations]].

## Background & Description

The review covers two complementary approaches:

- **NGTR 146 (Integrated Fisheries Management Plan):** A pilot decision tool that integrates sportfish and native fish management. It uses a georeferenced fish occurrence database spanning over 40 years of collections, [[Cell-Based Habitat Modeling]] (Chapter 3), [[Habitat Analysis]] using statistical methods (Chapter 4), and a reach- and unit-based management plan (Chapter 5, Appendix A). The plan is designed for a five-year review cycle.
- **NGTR 169 (Watershed Management Strategy):** A strategic, watershed-based framework that classifies stream segments as focal, nodal, contributing, and other areas based on fish life history and connectivity. It is framed by the [[Frissell Classification Approach]] and related watershed models, and it is more conceptual than NGTR 146.

Both documents were developed by Kirk L. Young, Linda J. Allison, and Terry B. Johnson, Arizona Game and Fish Department, Phoenix.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Key ecological themes raised in the review:

- **Native fish status:** Native species were prioritized because they are listed as sensitive, threatened, or endangered. Species mentioned include [[Apache Trout]], [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (LCS, *LEVI*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*GIRO*), and [[Humpback Chub]] (*HUMP*). Native fish management relies on self-sustaining populations and stocks only as a last resort.
- **Non-native and sportfish conflict:** Non-native sportfish and other non-native species, including crayfish, are ubiquitous across much of the watershed. Control is limited to areas where success and cost-effectiveness can be justified. Removal methods such as poisoning drew criticism for non-target effects on invertebrates and other natives.
- **Habitat change:** Reviewers agreed that habitat degradation over roughly the past 150 years is a major driver of native fish decline, though the team noted it could not identify a clear link between human impacts and current distributions.
- **Hydrology and reservoirs:** Major reservoirs, ephemeral and intermittent streams, and the classification of stream permanence were flagged as problems. Dams and upstream inputs still influence downstream biota and chemistry.
- **Data limitations:** Fish collection data were uneven in space and time and collected for varied purposes. The team used them because they were the only available data.

## Key Findings

**Scope and methods**
- NGTR 146 modeling uses non-statistical cell-based habitat suitability models (HSMs), which lack predictive ability. Logistic regressions were univariate because multivariate models were unstable given unbalanced data. Goodness-of-fit tests were not run because the goal was identifying variables, not prediction.
- HSMs were developed for all native species in the four focus sub-basins, but only for species with sufficient data. Brown trout was dropped as a surrogate for Apache trout.
- Elevation was treated as continuous and stream order as categorical, because stream order was not linear in the logit.
- Lakes and reservoirs were excluded from habitat analyses because they are human-made impoundments superimposed on the channel network, though they are included in management unit planning.
- The management unit concept uses 12 criteria, of which landownership is only one.

**Management recommendations**
- Management units were classified "Unknown" in many cases; priority surveys were identified in Appendix A.
- Unit summaries cover 27 information categories and vary in detail with available data. Management goals were summarized in Tables 5.3 and 5.4 for easier use.
- Data collection standardization procedures were recommended to reduce future problems.
- Adaptive management is central, with habitat restoration followed by evaluation of whether native and non-native species can coexist.

**Strategic principles (NGTR 169)**
- Fisheries management is ineffective outside a watershed context.
- The first priority is securing the portions of the watershed crucial to maintaining current species of interest.
- Priority classification does not mean other activities must stop; prioritization sets up steps that must occur in sequence.
- Both short-term conflict resolution and long-term watershed restoration are required; reviewers were concerned about timelines.

**Responses to key criticisms**
- Criticism that the plan treated only one native species was answered by noting classification must be applied per species, then prioritized using factors such as species status and immediacy of threats.
- Criticism that the approach was unrealistic for some drainages (e.g., Verde, Upper Salt, Upper Gila) was answered by noting it is most applicable to upper watershed reaches, though upstream processes affect all reaches.
- Concerns about the lack of a conservation biology or habitat-based framework were acknowledged; the team stated the project was a pilot, not a comprehensive watershed plan.

**Revisions and commitments**
- A **Newly Written Common Foreword** was added to both reports. It recommends that AGFD participate in existing [[Watershed Associations]] rather than building new processes, involve [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] early (especially through ESA Section 7 consultation), coordinate with the [[U.S. Forest Service]] and land management agencies, and address risk assessment, cost-benefit analysis, and short-, medium-, and long-term timelines.
- Text was clarified in Chapters 2 and 4, a methods section explaining univariate analysis was added, and the Nelson Reservoir example was rewritten to show that regulated releases are hypothetical.
- The team decided not to revise NGTR 146 for most comments, citing limited time, budget, and scope.

**Overall conclusion**
The two approaches were described as complementary: NGTR 146 provides a site-level decision tool and data foundation, while NGTR 169 provides a strategic watershed framework. Reviewers broadly supported the watershed approach and the integration of native and sportfish management, but many asked for more detail on implementation, responsibilities, and timelines. AGFD acknowledged that fisheries planning is not complete and that broader collaboration is needed.
