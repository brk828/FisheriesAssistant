# Biochemical Genetics of Endangered Colorado Squawfish from the Green, Yampa, Colorado, and San Juan Rivers

#ichthyology #endangered-species #conservation-genetics #cyprinidae #population-genetics #allozyme-analysis #hatchery-management

## Executive Summary

This draft final report presents a comprehensive biochemical genetic analysis of [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a large river cyprinid listed as endangered in 1967. The study examined 833 individuals (151 wild adults, 64 juveniles, 478 young-of-year, and 140 hatchery-reared fish) from throughout the species' present range in the [[Colorado River Basin]], including the upper mainstem Colorado River, [[Yampa River]], [[Green River]], [[White River]], [[Gunnison River]], and [[San Juan River]]. Using starch gel protein electrophoresis, researchers resolved products of approximately 89 loci and identified 15 polymorphic loci. A parallel PCR-based analysis of anonymous single-copy nuclear DNA examined fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and wild populations but detected no useful polymorphisms. The study tested whether Colorado squawfish populations are genetically homogeneous (panmictic) or genetically differentiated. Results support the null hypothesis of genetic homogeneity, with only rare alleles potentially differentiating management units. The authors recommend managing Colorado squawfish as two populations (Colorado River system and Green River system) and replacing mixed hatchery stocks with separate river-specific broodfish populations maintained through strict pedigree breeding strategies.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is indigenous to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of the southwestern United States and represents a large river cyprinid that once dominated these systems. Listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1967, the species has experienced dramatic declines in numbers and range associated with habitat fragmentation, lost passage, water diversions, loss of spawning and nursery habitat, dewatering, contaminants, and nonnative fish introductions.

Recovery goals call for protection, maintenance, and establishment of self-sustaining populations throughout the historical range. However, population identification and status determination has been difficult due to limited knowledge of genetic structure and demographic characteristics, particularly fish movement and behavior associated with reproduction. Previous work by Ammerman and Morizot (1989) provided basic genetic information on two wild populations and two captive populations at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]], but did not address all suspected spawning populations or directly examine adult spawning populations.

The species exhibits complex life history characteristics. Some researchers have suggested that Colorado squawfish tend to migrate to natal spawning areas (homing behavior), which would predict genetic differentiation among populations associated with different spawning areas. Numerous reintroductions have been made into the Colorado River basin since 1980, primarily using fish derived from [[Yampa River]] broodstock, potentially complicating genetic patterns.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Colorado Squawfish]] historically occupied large river systems throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]], utilizing specific spawning areas and exhibiting potential natal homing behavior. The species' ecology is intimately tied to river flow regimes, temperature patterns, and habitat connectivity. Adults migrate to spawning areas, and young-of-year fish are typically collected in late summer or early fall below putative spawning sites.

The study examined fish from diverse river mile locations across multiple river systems:
- [[Yampa River]]: River miles 9.5 to 24.0
- [[Green River]]: River miles 33.7 to 260.5, including [[Three Fords Canyon]], [[Rabbit Valley Rapids]], and [[Mineral Bottoms]]
- [[Colorado River]]: River miles 7.7 to 211.5, including areas above and below Moab and at Fruita
- [[San Juan River]]: River miles 122.1 to 141.8
- [[White River]]: River miles 21.6 to 80.4
- [[Gunnison River]]: River mile 1.4

Habitat fragmentation and anthropogenic barriers have severely impacted population connectivity. The [[San Juan River]] population shows evidence of genetic diversity loss, probably through genetic drift accompanying population bottlenecks and reduced immigration. Water management, dam construction, and altered flow regimes have fundamentally changed the riverine ecosystems that Colorado squawfish depend upon.

Hatchery populations maintained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] include the Yampa River DX-F₁(74) broodstock (established from 8 fish captured from the Yampa River in 1974) and the Colorado/Green River DX-F₁(81) broodstock (established from 22 fish collected from Colorado River and Green River in 1978, plus 7 fish collected in 1979). These captive populations serve as genetic refugia and sources for reintroduction efforts.

## Key Findings

**Genetic Variability and Population Structure:**
- Examined 833 individual Colorado squawfish using starch gel protein electrophoresis
- Resolved products of approximately 89 loci; 15 loci were polymorphic in at least one sample
- Complete data collected for 22 alleles at 9 polymorphic loci: ES-2 (3 alleles), GR (2 alleles), GPI-1 (2 alleles), GPI-2 (4 alleles), PEPS (2 alleles), PEPB (2 alleles), TPI-1 (2 alleles), TPI-2 (3 alleles), and PGAM-2 (2 alleles)
- Three additional polymorphic loci discovered: GR, PEPB, and PGAM-2
- Two new alleles detected: GPI-2*c and TPI-2*a

**Adult Population Genetics:**
- 151 wild adults examined from Colorado, Green, San Juan, and Yampa rivers
- Five of nine polymorphic loci exhibited variability in spawning adults
- Colorado River samples showed 15 of 22 identified alleles (highest diversity)
- Uncommon alleles shared between river systems: GPI-2*d (Colorado and Yampa) and GPI-2*a (Colorado and Green)
- No clustering of alleles within river basins; alleles more or less evenly dispersed throughout collection sites
- [[San Juan River]] sample showed apparent loss of genetic diversity (very small sample size, likely reflecting scarcity)

**Juvenile and Young-of-Year Patterns:**
- 64 juveniles examined; seven loci polymorphic with 17 of 22 alleles present
- One allele (PEPS*b) found only in Colorado River juvenile sample
- 478 young-of-year examined; seven loci polymorphic
- Colorado River YOY exhibited 19 of 22 alleles; Green River YOY showed 18 of 22 alleles
- Six alleles at frequencies ≥0.10 found in both Colorado and Green river samples
- Three basin-restricted alleles all found at frequencies <0.01
- Vast majority of alleles exhibited conspicuous lack of geographic differentiation

**Hatchery Population Assessment:**
- Five captive populations assessed: Yampa River DX-F₁(74), Colorado-Green River DX-F₁(81), Yampa River F₂(91), Colorado-Green River DX-F₂(81), and Yampa River DX-F₂(87)
- Seven of nine informative loci polymorphic in at least one hatchery population
- 18 of 22 alleles represented in hatchery stocks
- Founder effects may have significantly decreased frequencies of GPI-1*a and ES-2*b alleles relative to wild populations
- Yampa River DX-F₂(91) population showed six additional polymorphic loci when liver samples were analyzed

**Basin-Specific Rare Alleles:**
- Five apparently basin-specific alleles identified:
  - Green River system: GR*b, TPI-1*a, TPI-2*a
  - Colorado River system: GPI-2*c, PEPS*b
- Only GR*b detected in adult broodfish sampled
- Distribution of rare alleles suggests that if any population genetic differentiation exists, it is between Colorado River and Green River-Yampa River systems

**Nuclear DNA Analysis:**
- PCR-based analysis using gastrin and thyroxin primers conducted on fish from Dexter hatchery and wild populations
- No genetic polymorphisms detected using gastrin-like PCR product digested with 10 restriction enzymes
- No polymorphisms detected using thyroxin-like PCR product digestions with 10 restriction enzymes
- DNA sequences associated with thyroxin and gastrin loci appear highly conserved and invariate
- Lack of variability makes examination for population differences moot using these markers

**Hypothesis Testing:**
- Null hypothesis (H₀): Colorado squawfish are genetically homogeneous or panmictic - **SUPPORTED**
- Alternative hypothesis (Hₐ): Genetically differentiated populations exist - **NOT SUPPORTED**
- Overall lack of genetic differentiation within and between river basins apparent
- Results consistent with panmixia hypothesis but do not exclude possibility of past differentiation now masked by habitat disturbance, anthropogenic barriers, or 16+ years of stocking

**Management Recommendations:**

1. **Management Units:** Define two genetic management units:
   - Colorado River system (including [[Gunnison River]])
   - Green River system (including [[Yampa River]] and [[White River]])

2. **Hatchery Broodstock Development:**
   - Replace current Colorado/Green DX-F₁(81) mixed population with separate river-specific broodfish populations
   - Develop three hatchery stocks to maximize use of existing hatchery-reared fish
   - Verify that Yampa River DX-F₂(91) year class derives from Yampa River DX-F₁(74) broodstock through biopsies
   - Establish Colorado River broodstock: characterize 20-50 fish to ensure inclusion of rare GPI-2*c and PEPS*b alleles
   - Establish Green River broodstock: genotype 20-50 fish to ensure inclusion of rare GR*b, TPI-1*a, and TPI-2*a alleles

3. **San Juan River Management:**
   - Do NOT develop separate hatchery population from [[San Juan River]] source due to lack of individual fish and genetic variability
   - If reintroduction into San Juan River is appropriate recovery strategy, use offspring from Colorado River and/or Green River broodfish
   - All reintroduced fish should result from controlled reproduction of selected individuals

4. **Genetic Management Protocols:**
   - Implement strict and stringent pedigree breeding strategies
   - Develop comprehensive genetic management programs
   - Maintain detailed records of hatchery and management actions
   - Monitor hatchery populations to ensure maintenance of genetic variability
   - Use non-lethal tissue sampling (skeletal muscle plugs, fin clips, blood) to characterize broodstock adults
   - Include liver biopsies from broodstock adults to facilitate inclusion of uncommon alleles in filial generations

5. **Future Research Priorities:**
   - Double sample sizes of young-of-year from middle reaches of Green River and Colorado River
   - Provide critical tests of genetic differentiation within and between watersheds
   - Identify with more certainty alleles found in single river basins
   - Consider AP-PCR/RAPD combined with isozyme mapping for higher resolution
   - Conduct larger, more comprehensive dissection of allele frequencies into subpopulations

**Tissue Expression and Methodology:**
- Successfully used minimally invasive tissues (skeletal muscle plugs, fin clips, blood) to examine 215 adults and juveniles
- Tissue-specific expression documented for 89 loci across liver, brain, eye, skeletal muscle, caudal fin, pectoral fin, erythrocytes, blood plasma, and gonads
- Field protocol issues: removal of fish heads for otolith "crop-sharing" resulted in loss of liver and brain/eye tissue, precluding examination of many loci and calculation of heterozygosity values for some wild populations

**Statistical Considerations:**
- Not fully sampling all populations for all loci violated basic statistical assumptions of population genetic analyses
- Decision to analyze only polymorphic loci due to limited funding
- BIOSYS-1 analyses used as empirical tools only; not valid for determining basic population genetic parameters (average heterozygosities, proportions of polymorphic loci, average number of alleles per locus)
- Very large sample sizes required for discrimination among allele frequency estimates to describe subpopulation genetic structure

**Conservation Implications:**
- Fifteen polymorphic loci guarantee high preselected heterozygosity in broodstock founders
- Geographic variability in allele frequencies suggests populations with minimal or no allele sharing
- Stocking of Colorado squawfish for more than 16 years may have masked differentiation that existed
- Information from allozyme variation sufficient to identify population structure without resorting to DNA-based technologies
- Future studies with larger sample sizes may revise results, but current data support managing for two river system populations
