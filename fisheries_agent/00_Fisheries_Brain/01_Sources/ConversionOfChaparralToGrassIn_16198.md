# Conversion of Chaparral to Grass in Central Arizona: Effects on Selected Ions in Watershed Runoff

#watershed-hydrology #water-chemistry #plant-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1975 study by D. J. Longstreth and D. T. Patten, published in [[The American Midland Naturalist]], compares runoff chemistry from two adjacent watersheds in the Mazatzal Mountains of central Arizona. Watershed D retained [[Chaparral]] vegetation after a 1959 wildfire, while Watershed C was converted to perennial [[Love Grass]] pasture and kept shrub-free with herbicides and burning. Water samples were collected over 6 months in 1971. Cation, silica and phosphate concentrations were similar between the watersheds, but the grass-covered watershed showed a marked rise in nitrate-nitrogen and supported algal growth. The authors attribute this to altered [[Nitrification]] after vegetation removal.

No fish or other aquatic fauna are reported. The document addresses plant cover, soils, and runoff chemistry only, so the note reflects that scope.

## Background & Description

- **Vegetation contrast:** Chaparral grows mainly from autumn through spring, while grasses grow from spring through late summer, in association with storms (Humphrey, 1970).
- **Study purpose:** To characterize a natural chaparral watershed and a grass-covered watershed with respect to ionic constituents in runoff.
- **Research setting:** Part of a long-term water-yield program run by the U.S. Forest Service through the Tempe, Arizona branch of the Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station.
- **Watershed C:** 38.6 ha, 1073–1311 m elevation.
- **Watershed D:** 32.6 ha, 1126–1612 m elevation.
- **Aspect and geology:** Both watersheds are mostly north-facing and steep. The parent material is granitic, from the Mazatzal Revolution, which ended the Precambrian. Soils are coarse and poorly developed.
- **Climate:** Mean annual temperature is approximately 16 °C over the 9 years before the study. Annual precipitation ranged from 40 to 120 cm since 1957, with 70% falling November through April. Snowfall is rare.
- **Disturbance history:** Chaparral covered both watersheds (about 70% cover) until an extensive wildfire in June 1959 killed most shrubs by topkill. Watershed D recovered naturally, with [[Shrub Live Oak]] dominant. Watershed C was seeded with grasses, and shrub growth was suppressed by herbicides and a controlled burn in March 1971.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Vegetation on the converted watershed:** Dominated by two love grasses, *Eragrostis chloromelas* and *E. curvula*.
- **Shrub cover:** Under 5% on Watershed C in 1969, compared with about 58% on Watershed D.
- **Water yield:** Flow was similar before the fire. After the fire and management, flow from the treated watershed was much greater than from the control.
- **Mechanism proposed for nitrogen change:** Removing chaparral alters the microclimate. Exposed soil heats up, and soil moisture rises. Together with alkaline soils, these conditions favor [[Nitrification]] (Alexander, 1961; Adams et al., 1947).
- **Possible biochemical effect:** Shrubs may release chemicals that inhibit nitrification. Earlier work on climax ecosystems supports this idea (Rice, 1964; Rice and Pancholy, 1972). Removing shrubs could therefore lift a natural restraint on nitrogen loss.
- **Context for nutrients:** The authors cite a prior clear-cut study ([[Hubbard Brook Watershed Studies]]; Bormann et al., 1968; Likens et al., 1969, 1970) showing that vegetation manipulation mainly affects nitrification and the loss of mobile nitrogen.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**
- Water samples were collected for 6 months, covering the summer and winter seasons of 1971.
- Mean values were compared with the standard t-test (Wolf, 1968).

**Cations and silica**
- Concentrations stayed relatively stable over the study period.
- Watershed C showed generally higher mean values for all cations, but the authors judged the differences to have no direct biological significance. Both watersheds had high concentrations, mainly from chemical weathering.
- Runoff was described as highly mineralized, typical of central Arizona surface waters (Cole, 1963; Kemmerer, 1965).

**Flow versus concentration**
- Cation concentrations showed little correlation with flow.
- Watershed C had much greater flow (about 4.7 cm versus 1.0 cm for Watershed D over the study) and still showed higher cation concentrations. This contradicts the dilution hypothesis.

**Nitrate-nitrogen (main difference)**
- The chaparral watershed rarely showed nitrate-nitrogen much above detection limits.
- The grass-covered watershed rose sharply in October and held at about 4 mg/L for the next 2 months.
- These levels were at least fourfold higher than those reported by Kemmerer (1965) for 12 reservoirs in the same region.
- Algal growth was substantial in grass-watershed runoff late in the study and largely absent in chaparral runoff. The authors conclude that the converted watershed's nitrate may have raised runoff fertility.

**Phosphorus**
- Total dissolved phosphorus stayed constant on both watersheds, which the authors interpret as buffering in the ecosystem.
- Available phosphate was slightly higher on Watershed C, but both values were low, so the difference was probably unimportant.
- Available phosphate on Watershed C fell as nitrate rose, possibly due to algal uptake.

**Storm response**
- An early September summer storm produced a fourfold increase in runoff over the previous sampling date. Anion concentrations rose with it, except for total phosphate on Watershed C.

**Overall conclusion**
- Replacing deep-rooted chaparral with shallow-rooted grasses appears to increase nitrate losses and runoff fertility. The mechanisms are the microclimate changes and the possible loss of nitrification inhibitors. Because this is a single 1971 season on two watersheds, the authors frame the results as indicative rather than definitive.
