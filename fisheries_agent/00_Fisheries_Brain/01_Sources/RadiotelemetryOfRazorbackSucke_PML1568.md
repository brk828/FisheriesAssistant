# Radiotelemetry of Razorback Suckers in the Gila River, Eastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study used [[Radiotelemetry]] to track hatchery-reared and wild [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) released into the [[Gila River]] in 1988. The species had been extirpated from the Gila River basin in the late 1950s, and a [[Reintroduction Programs|reintroduction program]] had begun in the early 1980s. Two release groups (14 fish in Group I, 9 fish in Group II) were tracked for up to 32 days. Fish moved both upstream and downstream, but downstream movement was significantly more frequent. Occupied habitats were shallow, slow-to-moderate-flow sand runs and pools. The authors concluded that radiotelemetry is feasible for this species and can guide monitoring, but that the 10-year program could not yet be evaluated.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is a large catostomid sucker native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Wild populations in the Gila River basin were extirpated in the late 1950s, before their life history and ecology had been studied in detail. Stocking of larvae and small juveniles was abandoned after large losses of newly planted fish to predators, and hatchery managers shifted to larger, more predator-resistant fish. Growth rates from wild and captive fish suggested that reintroduced individuals could reach adult sizes of more than 50 cm total length within several years.

Before this study, it was known that juvenile suckers moved downstream after stocking, but the movements of larger fish in the Gila River were unknown. The study was designed by [[Arizona State University]] with support from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the U.S. Bureau of Land Management, and the San Carlos Apache Tribe. The [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] supplied fish and assisted with transmitter implantation.

**Methods overview:**
- **Transmitters:** [[Smith-Root Transmitters]] (SR) or AVM internal units, weighing 11–18 g in air, operating at 40 MHz, with battery life of 6–9 months (SR) or 12–14 months (AVM). Units were coated with beeswax to prevent extrusion and surgically implanted into anaesthetized fish. Fish were also marked with external tags.
- **Receivers:** SR-40 and RF-40 receivers with whip or bi-directional loop antennas, used on foot, by canoe, raft, all-terrain vehicle, or truck, depending on access and discharge.
- **Tracking effort:** Weekly or bi-weekly tracking from the Arizona-New Mexico state line downstream 55 stream-km to the stocking site, then 38 stream-km to the [[San Jose Diversion]]. Canals and the river below the diversion were searched by motor vehicle to the delta of [[San Carlos Reservoir]], 90 stream-km downstream, and one 3-day boat search was conducted in the reservoir.
- **Habitat measurement:** At each fish location, stream width, depth, substrate, and current velocity were estimated. Where data were missed, sites were revisited within days at similar discharge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study population and release groups:**
- **Group I:** Four- to seven-year-old hatchery fish plus four wild adults, netted 20–23 May 1988 at [[Lake Mohave]]. Transmitters were implanted, and fish were released immediately at the [[Old Safford Bridge]] on 24 May 1988 (discharge 1.64 m³/s).
- **Group II:** Nine 4-year-old hatchery fish, operated on at the Dexter hatchery and implanted on 30 August 1988. One fish died the next day and another a month later. The remaining nine were released at the Old Safford Bridge on 12 October 1988 (discharge 6.23 m³/s).

**Habitat characteristics:** Occupied habitats averaged 10.5 m wide, 0.4 ± 0.3 m deep, and had a current velocity of 0.2 ± 0.17 m/s (n = 30 contacts). Dominant substrates were sand and sand/gravel mixes. Habitats used by upstream and downstream movers did not differ significantly (t-test, n = 39, p > 0.05).

**Hydrologic context:** At low flow, most of the river is diverted into irrigation canals at the San Jose Diversion and re-emerges from subsurface flow a few hundred metres to a kilometre or more downstream. This affects tracking access and may affect fish movement.

## Key Findings

1. **Movement in Group I:** All Group I fish remained within 0.8 km of the release site during the first 12 hours, with movement mainly at night. Over the following days:
   - Three of 14 fish moved upstream, averaging 7.2 km (range 0.7–23.7 km). These fish remained at large for an average of 10 days (range 1–32).
   - Eleven fish moved downstream, averaging 11.9 km (range 1.6–32.0 km). Contact was maintained for an average of 19 days (range 4–32).

2. **Directional bias:** Radiotagged razorback suckers stocked into the Gila River moved significantly more often downstream than upstream (chi-square, df = 1; the exact statistic is garbled in the scan).

3. **Habitat use:** Fish occupied primarily run and pool habitats with sand substrates. Moderate depths and slow-to-moderate velocities were typical. Habitat did not differ between upstream and downstream movers.

4. **Transmitter and fish fate:** The ultimate fate of many tagged fish remained unknown. Transmitters from dead fish were probably buried deep in substrates and could not be located. Some transmitters were recovered after being moved to depths greater than 0.5 m. The authors suspected that some fish were removed by anglers or trapped in irrigation canals.

5. **Feasibility:** The study demonstrated that radiotelemetry is feasible for razorback suckers in a large, turbid river. It also gave managers data on habitat use and movements that could inform monitoring and stocking decisions.

6. **Management recommendations:** The authors suggested stocking during fall or winter to reduce predation by terrestrial or aerial animals, and noted that radiotelemetry could be combined with conventional sampling to monitor fish in specific habitats and waters.

7. **Program evaluation:** The authors concluded that the 10-year reintroduction program could not yet be evaluated, because recaptures were limited and the program was still in progress.
