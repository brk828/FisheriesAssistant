# Conceptual Management Plan for Habitat Enhancement in Flooded Bottomlands: Escalante State Wildlife Area, Gunnison River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This conceptual management plan, prepared by Bob D. Burdick for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (June 1994), proposes restoring floodplain connectivity at two bottomland sites in the [[Escalante State Wildlife Area]] on the [[Gunnison River]], downstream of Delta, Colorado. The plan argues that loss of seasonal flooding has removed the zooplankton-rich off-channel habitat that [[Razorback Sucker]] and other [[Endangered Fishes]] need for larval survival, and that habitat restoration must accompany any reintroduction. Site 1 would be a contained, multi-compartment research and grow-out facility; Site 2 would be a backwater reconnected to the river about every third year. Cost estimates are deferred to a Fiscal Year 1995 feasibility study, with construction proposed for Fiscal Year 1996.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The Escalante SWA lies between river miles 51.0 and 53.0 on the Gunnison River, owned and managed by the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] (CDOW). The floodplain is at the terminus of a 16-mile riparian corridor and is the last major floodplain before the Gunnison joins the Colorado River near Grand Junction. Both proposed sites lie within the 100-year floodplain and form only about 88 acres of the Hamilton Tract:

- **Site 1:** a former channel cut off by a man-made dike (about 46 acres total), dominated by [[Tamarisk]] with some cattails, bulrush, and saltgrass meadows. The main channel occupied this location in 1954.
- **Site 2:** a backwater oxbow, connected to the river only during high spring flows (about 42 acres inundable).

**Historical context.** Upper-basin floodplains were once inundated annually during spring runoff. Channelization, dikes, tamarisk invasion, and water development have since reduced connectivity. The plan notes that razorback sucker were "very abundant" near Delta in 1889 and common in the lower Gunnison in the 1940s, but declined from the 1950s onward. The last documented Delta-area capture was four adults in 1981, and the species now appears extirpated upstream of the [[Redlands Diversion Dam]].

**Site selection.** Five criteria were applied: location in the historic floodplain adjacent to the river near historic habitat; natural riverine features; overlap with the 1994 adult stocking area; CDOW ownership; and inclusion in a recommended [[Chemoreception and Imprinting]] demonstration project led by Dr. Al Scholz of Eastern Washington University.

**Land management.** Acquisition began in 1955 under the Federal Aid in Fish and Wildlife Restoration Act. The Hamilton Tract was acquired in the early 1970s. A north-side dike, about 0.6 mile long, was reinforced in 1985 with [[Federal Emergency Management Agency]] funds after the 1983 and 1984 floods. CDOW's management includes mule deer, upland game, waterfowl, and public hunting and fishing access.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrologic alteration.** The plan's flow analysis draws on [[McAda and Kaeding]] (1991) and [[Cooper and Severn]] (1994c):

- Mean maximum-annual discharge declined about 47% between early development (1917–1938) and post-[[Aspinall Unit]] (1966–1988) periods.
- Mean May and June flows declined 43% and 47%. Compared with the middle-development period, May flows fell 25.1% and June flows 32.2%, and winter flows more than doubled.
- Mean total annual flow was similar between periods (about 1.8 million acre-feet), but the hydrograph was reshaped.
- Days above 10,000 cfs averaged 6 post-Aspinall versus 22 pre-Aspinall. Days above 20,000 cfs averaged 0.2 versus 2 per year.

**Geomorphic and floodplain effects.** Reduced flood frequency has simplified the channel, allowed silt to fill side channels and backwaters, and let tamarisk stabilize sand bars. Gravel-cobble spawning substrate has become armored or silted. Cottonwood regeneration has declined, and flushing of salts from floodplain soils has been reduced.

**Fish community.** A 1992–1993 USFWS survey of the Gunnison from the North Fork to Grand Junction captured 18 fish species and three catostomid hybrids. Near the SWA, three native species (flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, roundtail chub) made up about 80% of fish encountered. Non-native species included common carp, fathead minnow, red shiner, and white sucker. Green sunfish, black bullhead, and northern pike, which thrive in low-velocity habitats, may prey on young razorback sucker.

**Wildlife.** The area supports ring-necked pheasant, Gambel's quail, mallard, and wintering bald eagles, and a great blue heron rookery sits on the island between the river channels. Northern leopard frogs and bullfrogs are present.

**Conceptual ecological model.** The plan's core premise is that low-velocity, off-channel habitats drive food-web productivity for early life stages:

- **Phytoplankton:** Turbidity limits light in the upper-basin river. Flooded bottomlands and backwaters, with deposited sediment, warmer water, and sunlight penetration, offer the best conditions for phytoplankton.
- **Zooplankton:** Densities in flooded bottomlands are typically far higher than in the riverine environment. Reported Gunnison backwater means were 11 organisms per liter, versus 25 in an open-water wetland at the SWA.
- **Larval survival and the match-mismatch principle:** Larvae must find appropriately sized food soon after the yolk sac is absorbed. [[Papoulias and Minckley]] (1990) found that razorback larvae died within 10–30 days without food, and that about 20 brine shrimp nauplii per fish were needed to avoid high mortality. Zooplankton densities of about 20 per liter were found in some Green River bottomlands but rarely in backwaters and never in the river.

The plan hypothesizes that loss of bottomland food production limits razorback sucker recruitment in the upper basin, while predation and non-native fish add pressure in the lower basin.

**Selenium and trace elements.** Waterborne selenium at study sites ranged from 3 to 7 µg/L. Information bulletins from the [[National Biological Survey]] report that eggs from three female razorback suckers on the Green River contained 3.7–10.6 ppm selenium (one 1991 sample reached 28 ppm), compared with 2.8 ppm at Dexter National Fish Hatchery. Muscle-plug selenium correlated strongly with egg (r = 0.99) and milt (r = 0.87) concentrations. Laboratory 96-h LC50 tests on seven inorganic contaminants found that [[Vanadium]] and [[Zinc]] were most toxic and [[Boron]] least toxic, with no significant difference in sensitivity among the three species.

## Key Findings

**Proposed actions**

- **Site 1 (research and grow-out facility):** A 6-foot-deep "S"-shaped channel, with 1–4 foot terraces for invertebrate and zooplankton production, would be built from an inlet to an outlet. Water and fish control structures with screens would divide the site into three compartments. Stop-logs would drain 50–75% of the deep channel, with mechanical pumping needed for total drainage. Spring flows would enter through the inlet, and groundwater would sustain the deep channel through low flows.
- **Site 2 (backwater reconnection):** The oxbow berm (2–3 feet of sand/loam) would be lowered so the site connects roughly every third year, wetting for about 6–8 weeks in May and June. No water or fish control structures are planned. Cooper and Severn recommended scraping the top 12 inches of topsoil for the final grade and periodically drying the site to release nutrients.

**Research objectives at Site 1**

1. Study on-site trace-metal bioaccumulation in target species and effects on early life stages.
2. Rear larval razorback suckers to about 3 inches for PIT tagging and release.
3. Create an off-channel area restricting access by large predatory fish.
4. Provide waterfowl nesting and resting wetlands.
5. Long term: allow wild larvae and adults access during spring runoff.
6. Provide wetland habitat for waterfowl, shorebirds, and other wildlife.

**Stocking context**

- Twenty adult razorback suckers, surgically fitted with radio transmitters (expected life over 4 years), were released on 6–7 April 1994 between Roubideau Creek (river mile 50.5) and Hartland Diversion (river mile 59.9). Seven were released in backwaters near the SWA.
- The plan states that earlier stocking of millions of razorback suckers in the lower basin (1981–1987) failed without habitat modification, so stocking must be paired with habitat restoration.

**Constraints and stakeholder requirements**

- CDOW requires protection of prior investments (ditches, pipes, dikes, the Salinity Control Program) and the shelter-belt plot west of Site 1, plus continued public fishing and hunting access.
- Site 1 tamarisk removal would eliminate upland cover; construction would avoid the spring nesting season, and willow would be planted.
- A Section 404 permit from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers is required for inlet and outlet structures.
- The plan notes that the Federal Aid funding may require an amendment to cover endangered fish restoration.

**Timeline and costs**

- Cost estimates are "premature." A feasibility study by a hydraulic engineer is planned for Fiscal Year 1995.
- If feasible, NEPA compliance, permits, and a statement of work would follow in early 1995, with construction starting in Fiscal Year 1996.

**Evaluation and adaptive management**

- The five pilot sites in the program are to be evaluated for benefit, detriment, or no effect, with adaptive management refining design and operation.
- Planned monitoring includes survival and growth of stocked larvae and fingerlings with and without non-native fish, trace-element effects across life stages, seasonal invertebrate and zooplankton sampling, and radiotracking of adult razorback suckers.
- Colorado squawfish residency is also noted: one radiotagged fish was tracked from river mile 48.4 (1993) to river mile 59.1 (May 1994), passing the SWA between October 1993 and May 1994.

**Toxicity findings (Appendices B and C)**

- Irrigation-drain selenium is a concern. Eggs and milt of Green River razorback suckers showed elevated selenium, and the plan states that contaminant effects could limit recovery.
- In acute tests, the margin of uncertainty (BEC:EEC) for zinc was 85 in Ashley Creek, below the threshold of 100 that indicates high hazard. Selenite in Ashley Creek was 122. Selenate ranged to 8,333, and boron and uranium were also above the hazard threshold.
- The bulletin cautions that mixtures of contaminants may act additively or synergistically, so single-element margins may understate risk.
