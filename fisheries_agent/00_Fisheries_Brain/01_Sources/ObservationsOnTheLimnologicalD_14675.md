# Job Completion Report: Limnology and Fish Populations of Tenkiller Ferry Reservoir (1960–1961)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Oklahoma Department of Wildlife Conservation Job Completion Report (Project F-7-R-1, Oklahoma Walleye Investigations) documents eight months of [[Limnology]] monitoring on [[Tenkiller Ferry Reservoir]] (June 1960 to January 1961) and a companion survey of [[Walleye]] literature. The limnological work was intended as an index of conditions that would govern the success of walleye stocking planned for spring 1961. The author concluded that no chemical or physical limitation was present, that the reservoir has a very high productive potential, and that the walleye introduction should prove successful.

## Background & Description

- **Study reservoir:** Tenkiller Ferry Reservoir, about 12,500 surface acres, with a length of roughly 25 miles. The reservoir is fed by the [[Illinois River]] and by tributaries including Dry Creek and Caney Creek.
- **Objectives:** Collect limnological data as indices for judging the success of the 1961 walleye introductions.
- **Sampling design:** Five stations were sampled weekly from June 1960 to January 1961. Station depths ranged from 15 to 130 feet, and Station V lay in the upper reaches near Etta Bend under constant Illinois River currents.
- **Methods:** Dissolved oxygen was measured by the [[Winkler Method]]. Vertical water samples were taken with a 2000 cc [[Kemmerer Sampler]]. Temperature was read with an electrical underwater thermometer and pH with an electric meter. Plankton was collected with a Juday net (No. 25 bolting silk) and volumes were measured by the [[Frolander Plankton Volume Method]]. Identifications were verified by specialists.
- **Fish sampling:** Rotenone (emulsifiable toxicant) population samples were taken on 16 and 17 August 1960 in two coves, Whirlhole Cove (3.50 acres) and Bullet Cove (3.44 acres), which were enclosed by 1-inch mesh nets.
- **Companion document:** A separate Survey of Walleye Literature (February–June 1960) compiled roughly 25 references from the Fishery Research Laboratory library in Norman, Oklahoma, covering walleye ecology, spawning, growth, food habits, and stocking.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal stratification and circulation**
- Stratification with an established epilimnion, [[Thermocline]] and hypolimnion occurred at all stations except Station V during June through September.
- Coves deeper than about 35 feet also stratified, and oxygen in those coves approached 0.0 ppm at depth.
- Station I reached a bottom temperature of 57.2°F, a 20.9°F difference across sampling dates linked to sluice releases.
- Fall overturn began in September, producing homogeneous temperature and dissolved oxygen.
- Maximum average monthly temperature was 87.6°F (July, Station III); minimum was 39.4°F (January, Station V).

**Dissolved oxygen and pH**
- Average bottom oxygen of 0.0–3.5 ppm occurred at Stations I–IV from June to September.
- Station III recorded 0.0 ppm at about 45 feet in June through August. A nearby site was moved 300 yards west in late August, and the condition disappeared.
- pH ranged from 7.2–7.6 at the surface and from 6.9–7.2 in bottom waters (the report's values are partly OCR-garbled; the range is stated as recorded). Variation was slight through the study.

**Hydrology**
- Heavy rains in spring and mid-summer required flood and sluice releases. Peak releases occurred in May, with a maximum one-day turbine total of about 3,100 cfs.
- Sluice releases of up to 10,321 cfs (late July) pulled cold water toward the dam and altered bottom temperatures.
- Cold Illinois River inflow formed a cold wedge between 15 and 25 feet at Station IV in June.

**Plankton**
- Two production pulses occurred. The first, in June, was dominated by zooplankton. The second, in November, was dominated by phytoplankton and was denser. Station I produced 5.3 mL per vertical haul in November, and Station V stayed lowest throughout (0.1–0.3 mL).
- Distribution was homogeneous across the reservoir, with no isolated plankton communities.
- Thirty-one phytoplankton genera were recorded. The dominant taxa were [[Melosira]], [[Fragilaria]] and [[Synedra]].
- Zooplankton included five rotifer taxa (Asplanchna sp. most regular), ten cladoceran species (for example [[Daphnia pulex]] and [[Diaphanosoma brachyurum]]), and five copepod forms. The dominant copepod was [[Diaptomus reighardi]]. Chaoborus larvae were abundant.

**Fish community (rotenone samples)**
- Largemouth bass reproduction was excellent, with 187 young-of-year in Whirlhole Cove and an estimated 355 (103 per acre) in Bullet Cove.
- Gizzard shad had the highest reproduction of any species: an estimated 33,607 young-of-year in Whirlhole Cove (9,602 per acre) and 20,428 in Bullet Cove (5,938 per acre).
- Bluegill: an estimated 1,140 adults (171 lb) in Bullet Cove and 617 (31 lb) in Whirlhole Cove.
- White crappie adults: 259 (72.5 lb) in Whirlhole Cove and 113 (34 lb) in Bullet Cove. No young-of-year crappie were captured, which the author attributed to their use of deeper water.
- Smallmouth buffalo, drum (997 adults, 299 lb in Whirlhole Cove) and channel catfish were common. Commercial species were otherwise scarce.

## Key Findings

1. **Stocking conclusion:** The limnological investigations found no chemical or physical limitation to walleye introduction in 1961. The reservoir was judged to have very high productive potential.
2. **Stratification:** Thermal stratification was regular at four of five stations from June through September. Station V remained unstratified because of Illinois River currents.
3. **Oxygen limitation:** Bottom oxygen approached 0.0 ppm at depth in summer, especially in coves deeper than 35 feet. This is a constraint on deep-water habitat for walleye, and the author did not rate it as a barrier.
4. **Plankton:** Two pulses were recorded (June, zooplankton; November, phytoplankton). Distribution was homogeneous across the reservoir.
5. **Fish standing crop:** Rotenone samples showed strong reproduction in largemouth bass and gizzard shad, while adult commercial species were scarce.
6. **Growth:** The author reported that fish growth in the reservoir was above the calculated state averages. Largemouth bass averaged 7.9 in. at the end of year one and 13.1 in. at the end of year two.
7. **Literature synthesis:** Walleye literature reviewed in the companion survey emphasized spawning temperatures of roughly 38–50°F, piscivorous diets in adults, and fish-based food web dependence. Norris Reservoir (Tennessee) was a frequently cited analog.
