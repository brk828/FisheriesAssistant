# Quality of Water of the Gila River Basin Above Coolidge Dam, Arizona

#freshwater-ecology #hydrogeochemistry #water-quality

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Water-Supply Paper (No. 1104, 1950), written by John D. Hem and prepared in cooperation with the Defense Plant Corporation, reports the chemical character of surface and ground waters across the [[Gila River]] Basin above [[Coolidge Dam]]. The study area covers 12,890 square miles, about one-fifth of the entire Gila River Basin. Samples were collected between 1940 and 1944, and the abstract states that nearly 4,000 analyses are tabulated (the methods section cites more than 3,000 previously unpublished analyses).

The report documents a strong longitudinal change in water quality. Headwater flows in New Mexico are dilute, dominated by calcium and bicarbonate, while the river gains sodium, chloride and sulfate through the irrigated valleys. Dissolved solids rise sharply in [[Safford Valley]], where the river water changes from roughly 454 ppm at Solomonsville to a load-weighted increase toward [[Calva]] at the head of San Carlos Reservoir. Many shallow ground waters are too saline for domestic use, and fluoride is widespread.

The document does not present fish, invertebrate, plankton or other biological data. Its aquatic content is limited to water chemistry, flow, and springs, with brief notes on riparian vegetation.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose and funding:** Investigations began in summer 1940 as part of USGS water-resource studies, with cooperative funds from the Arizona State Land Commission, the Corps of Engineers and the Office of Indian Affairs. Work resumed in April 1943 with Defense Plant Corporation funds to study bottom-land vegetation effects on water supply in lower Safford Valley. The Safford laboratory operated until the end of 1944.
- **Physiography:** The basin is part of the Basin and Range province. Mountains rise to nearly 11,000 ft, and the river bed at the New Mexico–Arizona line is about 3,800 ft. Coolidge Dam sits at 2,310 ft.
- **Major valleys:** The [[Duncan-Virden Valley]] (about 8,000 irrigated acres in 1944), the [[Safford Valley]] (about 32,500 irrigated acres), and the [[San Simon Basin]].
- **Population:** About 40,000 people in 1944, roughly three per square mile, concentrated where irrigation or mining occurs. The largest town was Safford (about 3,500). The Clifton–Morenci mining district is a major copper producer.
- **Climate:** Valleys are warm and arid (Thatcher: mean annual temperature 62.5°F, precipitation 9.5 in.). Mountains above about 7,000 ft receive more than 20 in. annually and support pine timber.
- **Vegetation:** Desert species dominate the lowlands. [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix gallica*), introduced within the past 40 years, has formed dense thickets along the river.
- **Hydrology:** Most runoff comes from summer flash floods and unusually wet winters. The maximum annual discharge at the dam site from 1901 to 1944 was recorded in 1915. A peak of about 130,000 second-feet was recorded near the dam on January 20, 1916.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and salt sources.** The Safford, San Simon and Duncan-Virden valleys formed through Tertiary faulting and were filled with sediments, some over 1,000 ft deep. Central lake-bed deposits contain clay, silt, gypsum, salt and volcanic ash. Soluble matter from these [[Tertiary and Pleistocene Valley Fill]] deposits is leached by tributaries and side washes, while the [[Recent Alluvium]] (generally under 100 ft thick) forms the cultivated valley floor. The report concludes that much of the river's mineral load at Calva likely originates from these lake beds.

**Hard-rock sources.** Volcanic terrain upstream yields low-mineral waters. Granitic rocks in the Pinaleno and Dos Cabezas ranges contribute fluoride. Hot springs, such as the [[Clifton Hot Springs]], rise along fault zones from deep sources.

**Irrigation and return flow.** Irrigation diverts much river flow and adds salt-laden return flow and ground-water inflow. Pumping of shallow ground water for supplementary irrigation increased markedly after about 1938.

**Fish and aquatic fauna.** The report contains no fish, invertebrate or plankton data. The only biological references concern riparian vegetation (bottom-land growth, saltcedar and cottonwood-willow stands) and their effect on water use.

## Key Findings

### Surface water

- **Upper Gila (Blue Creek to U.S. Highway 666):** Low mineral content at low flow, mostly calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate. Irrigation and ground-water inflow change the river chemistry through the [[Duncan-Virden Valley]]. Ground water in the central valley reached nearly 5,000 ppm dissolved solids in one sample, and one spring in Rainville Wash contained 1,790 ppm, mostly sodium and sulfate. A nitrate maximum of 199 ppm was recorded in a shallow well near Duncan.
- **[[San Francisco River]]:** Dilute above Clifton (mainly calcium and magnesium), but the Clifton area adds sodium and chloride. The 1943–44 daily sampling showed a moderate load of sodium and chloride, with the river "rather highly mineralized" at times of low flow. The 1944 daily mean load below Clifton was 103 tons per day.
- **[[Clifton Hot Springs]]:** Ground water with about 9,000 ppm dissolved matter (mainly sodium, chloride and calcium) at about 130°F. Estimated spring flow was 2.9 second-feet in August 1941 and a minimum of 0.9 second-foot in August 1944. Computed salt loads ranged from about 24 to 70 tons per day. The apparent 1944 decrease coincided with heavy pumping at the [[Phelps Dodge Corporation]] well, suggesting that pumping could reduce salt inflow to the river.
- **[[Eagle Creek]] and [[Bonita Creek]]:** Low mineral content, usually about 300 ppm or less, mainly calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate. Bonita Creek flood sampling gave 139 ppm.
- **[[San Simon Creek]]:** Flood waters carry 500–900 ppm dissolved matter (sodium, bicarbonate, chloride and sulfate), with sediment often exceeding 10% by weight at flood stage. The high sodium percentage makes this water less suitable for irrigation.
- **Gila River at Safford Valley gauges (water year 1944):** Solomonsville averaged 454 ppm dissolved solids, Safford about 10% less, and Bylas 92 ppm. Annual dissolved-solids loads were 84,100 tons at Solomonsville, 42,200 tons at Safford and 105,000 tons at Calva, a net gain of 20,900 tons through the valley.
- **[[Seepage Studies]]:** Low-flow studies show ground-water inflow zones near Pima (about 7 miles) and between Fort Thomas and the Geronimo crossing (about 6.5 miles). In June 1944 the river at Fort Thomas carried 6,020 ppm, and the maximum sampled concentration was more than 6,000 ppm there in summer 1944. In February 1944 the Geronimo gauge reached 3,500 ppm.

### Ground water

- **Duncan-Virden Valley:** Most ground water is dilute. Concentrations above 1,000 ppm occur over about 7–8 square miles east and southeast of Duncan.
- **Safford Valley (Recent alluvium):** Concentrations range from less than 200 ppm near Black Rock Wash to more than 10,000 ppm near the river southeast of Fort Thomas. Most ground water exceeds 1,000 ppm, mainly sodium and chloride. Near Markham Wash, concentrations reached about 9,000 ppm.
- **Artesian wells:** The Mack well near Pima flowed at 1,350 gallons per minute (April 1942), at 138°F and about 3,400 ppm. A Geronimo well (600 ft deep) yielded 14,400 ppm.
- **San Simon artesian area:** Most artesian water is low in dissolved solids (200–300 ppm), with some sodium-bicarbonate waters containing up to 38 ppm fluoride. Shallow waters in the San Simon area are higher in dissolved matter and fluoride.
- **Springs:** Artesian and fault-related springs near Pima and Fort Thomas typically contain 3,000–4,000 ppm. Goodwin Spring, inside the reservation, yields less than 300 ppm.

### Unusual constituents

- **[[Fluoride]]:** The most widespread unusual constituent. Gila River above Safford showed concentrations above 1 ppm (1.3 ppm near Solomonsville; 0.7 ppm on San Francisco River), and the highest concentrations were found in ground water, especially deeper waters from lake-bed deposits. The report notes that waters above 1.5 ppm can mottle children's tooth enamel, and mottled enamel is common among basin residents.
- **[[Boron]]:** Concentrations as high as 30 ppm (as BO₂) were found near the mouth of Markham Wash in waters above 6,000 ppm dissolved solids. Surface waters were generally free of excessive borate.

### Water use and standards

- **Public Health Service limits (for common carriers):** Chloride 250 ppm, sulfate 250 ppm, magnesium 125 ppm, total dissolved solids 500 ppm ("good"), and up to 1,000 ppm where no better supply exists.
- **Domestic supplies:** Ground waters in much of Safford Valley are too saline. Safford's public supply comes from an infiltration gallery on Bonita Creek, delivering about 900,000 gallons per day. Near San Simon, few waters are low enough in fluoride for young children.
- **Industrial supplies:** Morenci relies on [[Eagle Creek]] water, which requires a 1,500-ft pumping lift. Water rights issues led to an arrangement to divert up to 14,000 acre-feet per year from [[Black River]] over the divide to Eagle Creek.
- **Irrigation classification:** Water is grouped into three classes based on specific conductance, borate, sodium percentage and chloride. Class 1 is "excellent to good," Class 2 "good to injurious," and Class 3 "injurious to unsatisfactory." Duncan-Virden surface and most ground waters are Class 1. Lower Safford Valley surface waters approach Class 2, and much pumped ground water falls within Class 2 or 3.
- **Salt balance:** If ground water pumped in 1944 (about 52,000 acre-feet at about 2 tons per acre-foot) carried about 104,000 tons of salt, that nearly equals the 105,000-ton load at Bylas. The author concludes that significant salt is probably accumulating in soils and shallow ground water, especially in the lower valley, though the 1944 load gain alone cannot prove it.

### Conclusions and recommendations

- Shallow ground-water pumping in the Safford Valley should be used with care, since much of it is more highly mineralized than is considered satisfactory for irrigation.
- Pumping at the Phelps Dodge well may reduce Clifton Hot Springs inflow to the river, and a further investigation of the Clifton area could assess whether removing that salt is practicable. The author notes that Gila River water available to Safford Valley users could improve in quality while losing only slightly in quantity.
- Future data from [[Coolidge Dam]] and [[San Carlos Reservoir]] would help clarify conditions downstream of the study area. The 1941 reservoir water was of much better quality than normal.
