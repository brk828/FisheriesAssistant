# Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub Population and Habitat Monitoring, 1983–1984

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife final report covers the transition period of 1983–1984 in the monitoring of endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the [[Colorado River]] and [[Yampa River]]. Extensive sampling in 1981–1982 gave way to a scaled-down monitoring program focused on the Yampa River. Record high runoff in 1983–1984 altered sampling schedules, habitat, and species composition, and the authors used the data to test a multi-year recapture dataset (1979–1984). That dataset supports the view that Colorado Squawfish show fidelity to river reaches and spawning areas and make long-distance migrations.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] is a large endangered cyprinid. [[Humpback Chub]] is a smaller endangered cyprinid. Both were monitored under the Colorado Division of Wildlife program, with coordination from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].
- **Study area:** The Colorado River was sampled from Grand Junction (Fifth Street Bridge) to Loma, Colorado (river mile [RM] 170–150). The Yampa River was sampled at special sites between about RM 17 and RM 108, including [[Yampa Canyon]], [[Lilly Park]], [[Juniper Springs]], [[Sunbeam]], [[Maybell]], and [[Morgan Gulch]].
- **Report context:** The work was funded by the Colorado Division of Wildlife and carried out by the Larval Fish Laboratory at Colorado State University. The authors were Wick, Hawkins, and Carlson (December 1985).
- **Monitoring history:** Tagging of endangered fish began in 1979. Tags were color-coded by river (for example, red for the Colorado, yellow for the Yampa), and a 6 × 15 mm plastic tag was attached below the dorsal fin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and temperature.** Flow and temperature governed the sampling design and the outcomes.
- A record peak discharge of 33,200 cfs occurred at Deerlodge Park on May 19, 1984. Floodwaters inundated habitats and changed riverbed geometry.
- The 1984 spring sampling was canceled because of high water. The sampling design was built on three temperature-triggered periods: runoff, spawning, and base flow.

**Habitat disruption.** High flows in 1983 breached [[Gravel Pit Habitat]] dikes on the Colorado River. Previously isolated gravel pits were connected to the main channel, which allowed introduced species to enter the river.

**Competition and predation.** The authors identified [[Largemouth Bass]], green sunfish, and mosquitofish as predators on young native fish. [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Northern Pike]] were noted as potential competitors and predators of Colorado Squawfish. A squawfish was caught with a juvenile channel catfish lodged in its throat, and the authors cited three deaths attributed to channel catfish entanglement.

**Native community.** Other native species included [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Speckled Dace]].

## Key Findings

### Colorado River, August 1983

- **Catch:** 3,570 fish were collected by seining, dip-netting, and [[Electrofishing]]. Fifteen species were recorded: five native and ten introduced.
- **Dominant species:** Roundtail Chub and Speckled Dace dominated seine catches. Flannelmouth and Bluehead suckers were most common in electrofishing catches. Red shiner, sand shiner, and fathead minnow were the most common introduced minnows in seines.
- **Comparison with 1981:** In 1981, red shiner, fathead minnow, and sand shiner dominated seine collections. The authors attributed the difference to high 1983 flows, which likely favored native reproduction, compared with low 1981 flows.
- **Timing:** Sampling in 1983 occurred 10 days later on the calendar, but 35 days earlier on a cumulative-temperature basis than in 1981.
- **Reproduction:** One young-of-year Colorado Squawfish (13 mm TL) was collected at RM 153.8, which indicated spawning in 1983 within or upstream of this segment.

### Yampa River, 1983–1984

- **1983 sampling (October 5–7):** 710 fish were collected by electrofishing, with an overall CPUE of 229 fish/hour. The highest CPUE was at Sunbeam (331 fish/hour). Three [[Northern Pike]] were collected near Maybell, along with 11 channel catfish near Sunbeam.
- **1984 sampling (August):** 1,139 fish were collected by trammel net and electrofishing. The CPUE across upper Yampa sites was 231 fish/hour.
- **1984 sampling (October 6–9):** 929 fish were collected, with an overall CPUE of 179 fish/hour. The highest site CPUE was at Lilly Park (335 fish/hour).
- **Species composition:** Bluehead, flannelmouth, and white suckers and roundtail chub were dominant. [[White Sucker]] was most abundant at upstream sites, and flannelmouth sucker at downstream sites.

### Yampa Canyon Spawning Area, 1984

- **Captures:** A total of 82 fish were collected by trammel nets in [[Yampa Canyon]] (August 2–4, 1984). Ten Colorado Squawfish were captured in the canyon, and seven of them were tuberculated, ripe males.
- **Spawning evidence:** A ripe female at RM 17.7 yielded about 50 eggs, and the number and condition of squawfish indicated continued use of the area for spawning. This was a suspected spawning site from 1981 to 1983.

### Colorado Squawfish Recaptures, 1979–1984

- **Totals:** 37 Colorado Squawfish were captured or recaptured in Division of Wildlife and cooperative studies, with 12 from the Colorado River and 25 from the Yampa River.
- **Four main patterns:**
  1. Fidelity to certain river reaches, with return to the same segments over multiple years.
  2. Fidelity to specific spawning areas (the lower 20 miles of Yampa Canyon), in successive or alternate years.
  3. Long-distance upstream and downstream migrations to spawning sites.
  4. Movement into the Yampa River from other rivers, including the Green and White rivers.
- **Movement scale:** Recorded movements ranged from 20 to 95 miles over periods of 2 to 34 months. The authors estimated that a home range for an adult in the upper Yampa could exceed 100 miles if spawning migrations were included.
- **Black Rocks area:** Eight of 12 Colorado River recaptures showed fidelity to an area of less than 4 miles. Six of these were in the lower Black Rocks area (RM 135.0–135.5).

### Humpback Chub Recaptures, 1979–1984

- **Captures:** A total of 110 Humpback Chub were captured and tagged by the Division of Wildlife or cooperative sampling, with 16 recaptured in total (8 by the Division of Wildlife, 8 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service).
- **Site fidelity:** 115 of 118 Division of Wildlife capture or recapture records were in the Black Rocks area (RM 135.5–136.5).

### Habitat Measurements, 1984

- **Most diverse habitat:** [[Lilly Park]] (RM 52.5–53.5) had the greatest habitat diversity and the highest pool frequency (8 per mile). Its gradient was 0.53%, with high rubble substrate and high sinuosity.
- **Least diverse habitat:** Sunbeam (RM 70.9–71.9) and Morgan Gulch (RM 103.4–104.4) had pool frequencies of 1 and 2 per mile, respectively. These segments had lower gradient and finer substrate.
- **Deepest pools:** The deepest pools (about 5 m) were in the [[Juniper Canyon]] segment (RM 90.3–91.3). [[Cross Mountain Canyon]] and Juniper Springs Canyon were identified as unique habitats that provide deep pool habitat during base flow.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- **Reproduction confirmed:** The 1983 young-of-year capture confirmed Colorado Squawfish reproduction on the Colorado River.
- **Monitoring design problems:** The authors identified four weaknesses in the design: trend zone areas were not accessible at all flows, temperature changes were too rapid for timely sampling, fall sampling started too late, and spawning sampling had insufficient lead time. They recommended lowering the summer spawning trigger to 19°C and starting fall sampling at 15°C in 1985.
- **Low catch concern:** Low numbers of squawfish captured in the fall were a concern, since fall had historically been productive. The authors noted that fish may have been less active or had moved elsewhere.
- **Next steps:** A habitat census of the 120-mile study section was planned for 1985, and analysis of the monitoring program was postponed until another year of data was collected.
