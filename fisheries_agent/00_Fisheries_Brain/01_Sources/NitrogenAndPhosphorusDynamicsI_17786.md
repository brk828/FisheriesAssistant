# Nitrogen and Phosphorus Dynamics in Hot Desert Streams of Southwestern U.S.A.

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #nutrient-dynamics

## Executive Summary

This 1981 study by Grimm, Fisher & Minckley (Arizona State University) examines whether [[Nitrogen Limitation]] or [[Phosphorus Limitation]] constrains primary production in [[Desert Stream Ecosystems]] of southern Arizona. Water chemistry was sampled at 52 sites on 38 streams of the [[Gila River Basin]], with uptake measured in 10 stream segments below sources. The authors conclude that nitrogen is the likely limiting nutrient in free-flowing streams, that nitrate uptake is tied to [[Periphyton]] activity, and that soluble reactive phosphate (SRP) is controlled by factors other than autotrophic uptake. ## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Small, spatially and temporally intermittent desert streams that are largely unshaded, highly productive, and autotrophic (Busch & Fisher 1981). Summer biomass-to-water ratios are high, favouring interaction between biota and dissolved nutrients.
- **Prior knowledge:** Earlier data suggested nitrogen rather than phosphorus may limit production in Southwestern deserts, but this rested on few samples from few sites.
- **Hypotheses tested:**
  1. Low N:P ratios are characteristic of streams in this region.
  2. Net autotroph production drives observed variation in nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations.
  3. Nutrient uptake at high biomass can make nutrients limit primary production.
- **Study sites:** Thirty-eight low-elevation (<1500 m) streams and segments, mostly in the Sonoran Desert, with some in a Sonoran–Chihuahuan transition zone (sites 26–36) and Interior Chaparral (sites 14, 19–22, 38, 39). Sampling was primarily in summer 1979, across spring, summer and autumn low flow, summer floods, and high winter discharge.
- **Hydrology:** Perennial flow in the Sonoran Desert typically originates in highlands outside arid zones. Summer flow often emerges as isolated segments where impermeable bedrock forces groundwater to the surface. Floods mobilize nutrients accumulated in desert soils, and sheet flow transports ions and soil-associated organic matter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** Surface waters are carbonate-dominated. Most streams had alkalinities of 2–5 meq·l⁻¹, moderate sulfate (0.1–2 meq·l⁻¹) and chloride (0.2–1 meq·l⁻¹). Conductivity averaged 420 µS·cm⁻¹ at most sites (range 240–700), and silica ranged from 4 to 12 mg·l⁻¹. Sulfate and conductivity were highest at three nearly dry streams (sites 7, 8, 9).
- **Nutrient sources:** Nitrate was the dominant inorganic nitrogen form. Source waters and flood waters were nitrate-enriched, probably from recent contact with nitrate-rich terrestrial soils.
- **Autotrophic community:** Periphyton (algae) supports high standing crops in summer. Algal cover was estimated by transect, and chlorophyll *a* and biomass were measured on triplicate samples.
- **Denitrification:** Judged unlikely to be a significant nitrogen sink in most Southwestern streams, because denitrification rates depend strongly on nitrate concentration and non-algal organic matter in sandy sediments is low (<0.1%).

## Key Findings

**Nutrient concentrations**
- Nitrate ranged from 0.07 to 105.00 µM (mean 21.86 ± 8.05, n = 52) and was significantly higher in segment sources and flood waters than elsewhere (p < 0.001).
- Ammonium ranged from 0 to 3.64 µM (mean 0.81 ± 0.30 µM, n = 42), about 6% of dissolved inorganic nitrogen. Nitrite was never detected.
- SRP ranged from 0.10 to 6.85 µM (mean 1.95 ± 0.45 µM, n = 52). It constituted 71% of total phosphorus (mean 2.81 ± 0.61 µM) and did not differ significantly between continuous streams and other sites (p > 0.1).

**N:P ratios**
- Continuously flowing streams had N:P ratios of 15 or less at all 25 sites, and most were below 6.
- At 20 of 25 free-flowing sites, nitrogen was below 3.6 µM, comparable to levels reported for nitrogen-limited systems.
- Source sites had higher but variable N:P ratios; N:P exceeded 15 at 3 of 6 exemplary sources.

**Downstream decline**
- In 23 of 25 segments, source nitrate exceeded 3.6 µM and declined downstream, and total dissolved nitrogen declined similarly.
- SRP declined with distance from source at 20 sites and stayed constant or rose slightly at the rest. Phosphorus decline was less pronounced than nitrate decline.

**Uptake**
- Nitrate uptake ranged from −0.1 to 2.7 µmol·m⁻²·h⁻¹ and was significantly correlated with periphyton abundance, whether expressed as chlorophyll *a* (r = 0.733, p < 0.01) or biomass (r = 0.760, p < 0.01).
- SRP uptake ranged from 0.140 to 0.58 µmol·m⁻²·h⁻¹ and was not correlated with periphyton abundance (p > 0.1).
- Diel study at site 5 ([[Sycamore Creek]]), three summer dates: nitrate and SRP both declined downstream at all hours, with no discernible diel pattern. Mean daily uptake was 14.9 mmol·m⁻²·d⁻¹ for nitrate and 1.63 mmol·m⁻²·d⁻¹ for SRP.

**Model comparison**
- Observed nitrate uptake correlated with predicted uptake (r = 0.827, p < 0.01, n = 12), supporting autotrophic control of nitrate.
- Observed SRP uptake did not correlate with predicted uptake (r = 0.517, p > 0.1, n = 12).

**Proposed model and conclusions**
- Water enters channels enriched in nitrate and low in SRP (N:P > 15). Nutrients are taken up as a function of net primary production along the reach.
- Stream sediments are proposed as a phosphorus source that keeps SRP from falling to limiting levels, possibly through calcium phosphate solubility and sediment sorption equilibria. This remains untested.
- Nitrogen is likely to be depleted before phosphorus, so nitrogen is the probable limiting nutrient where limitation occurs. N:P < 15 alone does not prove nitrogen limitation.
- High productivity is predicted to be restricted to segments just below points where subsurface water surfaces, unless exogenous nitrogen (e.g., nitrogen fixation) enters the system.
