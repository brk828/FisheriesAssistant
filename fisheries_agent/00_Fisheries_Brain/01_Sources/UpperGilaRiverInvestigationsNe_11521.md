# Upper Gila River Investigations: Water Resource Reconnaissance, New Mexico (1963)

#hydrology #water-resources #freshwater-ecology #groundwater

## Executive Summary

This January 1963 Bureau of Reclamation memorandum report, prepared by the Region 3 office in [[Boulder City, Nevada]], gives a preliminary reconnaissance of the water resource potential of the [[Upper Gila River Basin]] in New Mexico. It excludes the Upper San Simon Valley. The study inventories surface and ground-water supplies and uses, appraises present and future water needs, and examines possible development plans for optimum use of available water.

The work was funded by the State of New Mexico under Contract No. 14-06-300-1198 (August 2, 1961) with the [[New Mexico Interstate Stream Commission]]. The contract required the study to consider potential uses within the physically available supply, without regard to existing water rights, court decrees, or litigation. The report therefore does not affect any rights under existing decrees or prior appropriations, and does not anticipate the outcome of the pending [[Arizona vs. California]] case before the U.S. Supreme Court.

The central conclusion is that the Upper Gila supply is substantially greater than present average annual use in New Mexico. Localized deficiencies exist because of limited regulatory storage.

The document is not a fish or biological study. It contains no species-level data. Aquatic content is limited to general stream and water-quality description.

## Background & Description

**Scope and structure.** The report covers the Gila River area and the San Francisco River area, plus adjacent lands such as [[Lordsburg Mesa]] and [[Duck Creek]]. Its main chapters address:

- **Chapter I – The Area:** physiography, history, and development (agriculture, urban, livestock, mining, recreation, flood control).
- **Chapter II – Problems and Needs:** by river area.
- **Chapter III – Lands and Soils:** drainage, salinity and alkalinity, and soils.
- **Chapter IV – Water Supply:** surface water, sediment, floods, yield-capacity at reservoir sites, future agricultural requirements, and ground water.
- **Chapter V – Possible Plans of Development:** multiple-purpose dams, hydroelectric and pumped-storage potential, single-purpose dams, irrigation service areas, municipal and industrial service, recreation and fish and wildlife, benefits, payment ability, and cost allocation.

**Tables and drawings.** Seven tables and five drawings are listed. Key tables include arable lands (Table 1), streamflow records for the Gila and San Francisco rivers to [[Clifton, Arizona]] for 1928–1960 (Table 2), estimated sediment deposition at five damsites (Table 3), storage and distribution facilities (Table 4), and crop yields and land use for the Gila River and San Francisco River areas (Tables 5 and 6). Table 7 gives farm-size data for Catron, Grant, and Hidalgo counties.

**Analytical methods.** Consumptive use was computed with the [[Blaney-Criddle Method]], with effective precipitation deducted. Yield-capacity was derived from mass diagrams of historic and estimated annual runoff.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrologic setting.** The basin consists of alluviated valleys formed by structural and erosional processes in bedrock. Pre-Cambrian rocks (granite, gneiss, schist, quartzite) lie mainly south of the Gila River between Lordsburg and Tyrone. Tertiary and Quaternary rocks are volcanic and occur across the Upper Gila, San Francisco, and Duck Creek areas.

**Streamflow character.**
- Upper Gila tributaries below Hooker Dam carry high-peak, short-duration, silt-laden flows, averaging an estimated 30–35 acre-feet per square mile of drainage area, with wide variation in extreme years.
- Summer stormflows above Virden carry considerable silt from side tributaries.
- The San Francisco River is perennial at the Glenwood gage but has been recorded dry at Alma. Over one-third of its flow occurs in March and April.

**Water quality (general).**
- Surface water above Virden has low mineral content, mostly calcium and bicarbonate. Mineral content rises through Duncan–Virden Valley because of return flows and ground-water inflow.
- San Francisco River water near Clifton is highly mineralized at low flow (calcium and magnesium bicarbonate). Dissolved solids reach 200–300 ppm at higher flows. Blue River (the largest tributary) contains about 350 ppm at low flow.

**Ground water.**
- Alluvial water is unconfined, and wells are commonly under 100 feet deep.
- Water depths are 5–50 feet in flood-plain alluvium and over 100 to almost 700 feet in bedrock and the Gila Conglomerate.
- Water quality is good to excellent for most domestic and crop uses, with specific conductance of about 250–750 micromhos. Isolated poor-quality sources occur, such as Frisco Hot Springs.

**Consumptive use and native vegetation.** The report identifies nonbeneficial consumption by undesirable native vegetation along stream channels as a major regional problem, and notes ongoing research into its evaluation and eradication.

## Key Findings

**1. Surface water (Gila River, Duncan–Virden Valley)**
- Diversions have averaged about 21,000 acre-feet per year and are subject to the Gila Decree since January 1, 1936. This is less than 3 acre-feet per acre.
- Above Virden, an estimated 20,000–30,000 acre-feet are diverted annually, against average consumptive use of about 12,000 acre-feet.
- At the [[Hooker Dam]] site, 50 percent of flow is 70 cfs or less. In June, about 80 percent of flow is below 70 cfs.
- Gross gain from the damsite to the Red Rock gage has averaged 30,000 acre-feet annually since 1928. A gross loss of about 2,700 acre-feet occurs between Red Rock and Virden.

**2. Water balance, Gila River (1928–1960 averages, 1,000 acre-feet)**

| Virden to State line | Gaged inflow 116.9; cropped use 4.6; noncropped 2.8; channel evaporation 0.8; intervening inflow 2.9; subsurface outflow 7.2; flow at State line 104.4; totals 119.8 |

- Cropped irrigation averaged about 2,700 acres in Virden Valley (consumptive use 4,600 acre-feet) and about 7,400 acres in the upper valleys (11,300 acre-feet).
- Noncropped consumptive use (towns, farmsteads, rights-of-way, native vegetation) averages about 6,400 acre-feet. Channel evaporation averages about 1,700 acre-feet.
- Above Hooker Dam, 287 acres are irrigated, with consumptive use of about 450 acre-feet.

**3. Floods (Gila River)**
- The September 29, 1941 flood was the largest since 1881 (per USGS Water Supply Paper 1313).
- The notable 1916 flood below Clifton was largely contributed by the San Francisco River.
- Spillway design used unit runoff rates from the Corps of Engineers' enveloping curve.

**4. San Francisco River**
- Gaged flow near Glenwood averaged 45,400 acre-feet annually (1928–1960). Estimated flow at the State line is about 51,800 acre-feet.
- Whitewater Creek tributary flow is estimated at over 10,000 acre-feet annually.
- Irrigated acreage above Glenwood averaged about 2,800 acres, with cropped consumptive use of about 2,600 acre-feet and noncropped use of about 4,000 acre-feet. Another 340 acres are irrigated on Mule Creek, below Glenwood.
- The 1916 floods at Clifton had two peaks of over 90,000 cfs each (January 19 and October 14).

**5. Irrigation efficiency and future requirements**
- Present farm irrigation efficiency in the Upper Gila area is perhaps 50 percent or less.
- Under firm, regulated supply, ultimate efficiencies could approach 70 percent.
- Farm delivery requirement is about 2.94 acre-feet per acre. With lined canals and laterals, diversion would be about 3.5 acre-feet per acre.
- Peak demand falls in June, about 22 percent of the annual total. Return flows and canal seepage are about one-quarter of total diversion.
- Projected crop mix (Red Rock and Cliff-Gila): alfalfa 40–47%, permanent pasture 30–35%, small grain 5–8%, corn 7–10%, sorghum 3%, cotton up to 11% (Red Rock only), and orchard 1% (Red Rock only).

**6. Ground-water development**
- Irrigation wells are typically 12- or 16-inch cased, with 5–30 hp pumps. Capacities range from 200 to 2,000 gpm, and the average well produces above 1,000 gpm.
- Domestic and stock wells are commonly dug, or drilled with 6- to 10-inch casing.
- Recharge is mainly from rainfall and seepage from the Gila, San Francisco, and Duck Creek. Withdrawals are considered annually recharged, so development is limited mainly by effective storage and seasonal flow.
- No long-term water-level decline is believed to exist, though seasonal and long-term records are unavailable.

**7. Overall conclusion**
- Historic outflow from the area indicates a surplus supply under present development, although localized deficiencies exist.
- Planning options include [[Hooker Dam]] and [[Conner Dam]] (multiple-purpose, with hydroelectric potential), [[Miller Ranch Dam]], [[Spurgeon Mesa Dam]], and [[Quail Springs Dam]] on the San Francisco River, plus pumped-storage and single-purpose options.
- The report explicitly states that it is preliminary, a reconnaissance study, and not a determination of legal rights to construct structures or divert water. It anticipates a future comprehensive study that would consider water rights and possible exchanges under the [[Central Arizona Project]].
