# Pollen Analysis of Prehistoric Human Feces: A New Approach to Ethnobotany

#palynology #paleoethnobotany #cleomaceae

## Executive Summary

This paper (Martin and Sharrock, *American Antiquity*, 1964) reports the first systematic pollen analysis of prehistoric human and nonhuman feces from dry alcoves in the [[Glen Canyon]] region of southern Utah. Pollen was extracted and counted from 54 fecal samples across 14 archaeological sites. The authors find that economic plant pollen, especially [[Zea Mays]] (corn), [[Cleome]] (beeweed), [[Cucurbita]] (squash), and [[Opuntia]] (prickly pear), is abundant in human feces and appears to reflect the Pueblo diet. Pollen counts, however, vary so widely between samples that they are of little use for ordinary stratigraphic or vegetation-change interpretation. The main conclusion is that fecal pollen analysis can reveal prehistoric diet and the past environment.

## Background & Description

**Context and motivation.** Pollen had been recovered from Pleistocene ground-sloth and pack-rat dung in [[Rampart Cave]] and from carnivore feces in U-Bar Cave, New Mexico. The authors state that no earlier description of pollen extracted from prehistoric human feces had been published. They argue that pollen exines are not destroyed by vertebrate digestion, so both human and nonhuman feces from alcoves may hold abundant pollen.

**Limits of pollen analysis.** The authors note that pollen stratigraphy is still in an early stage in the arid Southwest. Fecal counts add complications: differences in pollen production among plant species, redeposition and reflotation of pollen, limits of light-microscope identification, and seasonality. A fecal sample reflects a single season's diet rather than an average of several years, as lacustrine or alluvial samples do. Ceremonial or medicinal use of plants can also affect counts.

**Study sites.** Of the 54 fecal samples, 53 come from archaeological sites in three left-bank tributaries of the Colorado River: [[Moqui Canyon]] (2 sites), [[Lake Canyon]] (9 sites), and [[Slickrock Canyon]] (2 sites). One sample comes from [[Benchmark Cave]] in the river gorge at river mile 61.5. Sites date mainly to the Basketmaker and Pueblo I–III periods, with some historic reuse by Utes and possibly Navajos. Several sites were heavily vandalized, and some river-accessed sites had been visited often in recent years, so modern origin of some samples cannot be fully excluded.

**Identification of samples.** Byers and Martin classified most samples as human on the basis of size and texture; a few may be quids (partially chewed plant bundles). Samples with little or no economic pollen were assigned nonhuman origin. Of 36 non-human samples in the tables, 12 were analyzed (samples 1–6 resembled canid dung; samples 7–12 resembled horse dung).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Plant communities.** Ecological surveys grouped local vegetation into three categories: streamside (82% cover), terrace (51% cover), and hillside (18% cover). Dominant species included shadscale, sandbar willow, Gambel oak, four-wing saltbush, rabbitbrush, ephedra, tamarisk, and blackbrush.
- **Canyon hydrology.** Lake and Moqui canyons were relatively wet before alluvial dissection began around 1880. Sand deposits stored runoff and fed a riparian habitat of willow, poplar, cattail, and sedge.
- **Slickrock Canyon.** This canyon has about 140 recorded plant species, the largest number from any tributary canyon or the mainstem. Its arable land is limited, and its alluvium was never dissected.
- **Natural pollen rain.** Wind-pollinated plants (Chenopodiaceae, oak, ephedra, grasses, sagebrush) dominate local pollen. Insect-pollinated plants (blackbrush, beeweed, cactus, mallows) are scarce or absent in sediments. Conifer and alder pollen arrives by [[Long-Distance Pollen Transport]] from montane forests many miles away. Surface soil at Mesa Verde contains 0.25% spruce pollen, and surface soil under creosote bushes near Panamint Playa contains 40% pine pollen.
- **Seasonality.** Juniper pollen falls heavily in late winter or early spring, pine in early summer, and composites in late summer or fall. Each is overrepresented in its season of release.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sample size**
- Pollen counts of 100–400 grains (generally near 200) were made on 24 human and 12 animal dung samples, totaling about 9,000 identifications.
- About 1 cc of fecal material was processed with a KOH boil followed by bleach or acetolysis; hydrofluoric acid was used to remove silicates.
- In one bleach batch, overly harsh treatment destroyed pollen.
- Replicate counts of sample 6 (Table I) gave 194 and 189 *Cleome* grains out of 200. A third count excluding *Cleome* showed that *Cucurbita* and *Zea* made up 75% of the remaining pollen.

**Economic plant pollen in human feces**
- Of the 24 samples judged human, *Zea* pollen appeared in 14, *Cleome* in 12, and *Opuntia* in 4. *Cucurbita* appeared in five.
- *Zea* exceeded 50% of the 200-grain count in samples 3 and 10 (Table I).
- *Cucurbita moschata* made up 99% of squash pollen in sample 8 (Table I), 91.5% in sample 1, and 95% in sample 7. *C. pepo* type pollen is smaller and more spiny.
- *Cleome* is more than twice as numerous as any other pollen type in the counts. The authors suggest that some of this abundance reflects sampling, since replicate counts were made from scats rich in *Cleome*.
- Economic pollen is scarce or absent in nonhuman feces, and three human samples (Table I sample 5; Table III samples 2 and 5) lack obvious economic pollen.

**Interpretation of economic plants**
- *Zea* pollen is distinctive (usually over 90 µm in dry feces), but its natural abundance in cornfields is only 0.25–1.0%. The authors suggest that high *Zea* counts most likely come from chewing corn-leaf quids dusted with tassel pollen, or from green-corn preparations.
- *Cleome* is insect-pollinated, so it is rare in alluvial or lacustrine sediments. Ethnobotanical sources describe its use as a food, pottery pigment, and medicine. Some groups dried and stored it, which could explain its presence in feces at any season.
- *Cucurbita* pollen probably entered diets through eating squash blossoms, which were cooked in fat or meat.
- High *Opuntia* (*Platyopuntia*) frequencies in samples 3 (Table III) and 2 (Table II) suggest eating cactus flowers. Cactus pads are abundant at Benchmark Cave, but its feces lack cactus pollen, perhaps because vegetative parts are not dusted with pollen.

**Other notable pollen records**
- Sample 4 (Table III) contained 92% *Populus* pollen, probably from deliberately ingested cottonwood catkins, which are an early spring green.
- Conifer pollen (juniper, pine, spruce, fir) is mostly accidental. Sample 1 (Table III) has a high juniper count, which may reflect Hopi juniper tea.
- Sample 7 (Table I) has a large, scabrate grass pollen type (45–55 µm). Large grass pollen was the dominant type in sample 8 (Table I) and sample 5 (Table II).

**Variability and stratigraphy**
- Dominant pollen types differ greatly among samples, so the authors judge the counts "all but useless" for ordinary stratigraphic purposes or for reconstructing vegetation change.
- The authors note that the main regional change is an increase in tree pollen (pine, juniper) after the 13th century. Fecal counts cannot reliably capture such trends.
- Canyon-to-canyon differences are not statistically supported. Even the absence of *Cucurbita* in Lake Canyon could be a sampling error.

**Conclusions**
- Economic plant pollen is the most valuable archaeological signal. Corn and squash appear in excavated remains at Lake and Moqui canyons, but beeweed is the most numerous pollen type in the feces.
- The authors conclude that fecal pollen analysis can reveal prehistoric diet and past environment, and that direct experimental data linking diet to fecal pollen content are needed.
- Prehistoric Pueblo origin is judged likely for samples with cultivated-plant pollen, but the field collector remains the best judge of age.
