# Seasonal Changes in the Food Ingested by Aquatic Insect Larvae and Nymphs in Two Oregon Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #aquatic-entomology

## Executive Summary

This 1963 *Ecology* report by Chapman and Demory examines the diet and seasonal feeding shifts of aquatic insect larvae/nymphs in two small, heavily shaded Oregon Coast Range streams (Deer Creek and Needle Branch, both tributaries of Drift Creek/Alsea River). The study's ultimate purpose was to trace energy flow up to [[Coho Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*), the dominant stream carnivore, by determining whether the insects it preys upon derive their energy from **autochthonous** (aquatic algal) or **allochthonous** (terrestrial detrital) sources, and whether this balance shifts seasonally. Insects were sampled roughly monthly over ~17 months, and gut contents were classified as algae, leaf fragments, amorphous detritus, or animal matter. The study found strong seasonal algal-feeding peaks in spring (coinciding with peak light availability before canopy leaf-out) and a switch to detritus feeding in summer/fall (correlating with leaf-fall and canopy closure). These insect-level findings were directly linked to [[Coho Salmon]] diet studies (Demory 1961), showing that terrestrial energy inputs dominate coho production in these small, densely shaded streams.

## Background & Description

This paper does not describe fish morphology directly, but characterizes insect prey taxa relevant to salmonid diet studies:

- **Sampling protocol**: Insects collected via fine-mesh net sweeps of ~1 ft² of disturbed bottom at randomized stations (20 on Deer Creek, 10 on Needle Branch); minimum insect size captured ≈ 1 mm.
- **Body size ranges** (mm, base of cerci to tip of snout) varied widely by taxon, e.g.:
  - *Baetis* sp.: not measured (juveniles too small/variable)
  - *Ephemerella* sp.: 3–6 mm
  - *Epeorus* sp.: 1–12 mm
  - *Peltoperla brevis*: 2–3 mm
  - *Glossosoma* sp.: 3–7 mm
  - *Micrasema* sp.: 1–6 mm
  - Elmidae larvae: 2–7 mm
- **Gut dissection method**: Insects decapitated on microscope slides; thoracic gut contents expressed and examined under compound microscope; contents scored as % algae vs. % detritus (leaf fragments + amorphous detritus), with grit as a detritus indicator.
- Diatom frustules used as a marker to distinguish live/recently-dead algal material from true terrestrial detritus, since frustules persist long after cell death ([[Diatom Frustule Persistence]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study system**: Two small (815-acre and 230-acre drainage) Coast Range streams under ~110-year-old Douglas-fir (*Pseudotsuga menziesii*) canopy, with dense understory of red alder (*Alnus rubra*) and salmonberry (*Rubus spectabilis*) heavily shading the streambed. Light beneath canopy at midday was reduced to as little as 1–35% of open-meadow levels, with strong seasonal variation due to deciduous leaf-out.

**Primary producers**: Algal assemblage dominated by diatoms (*Navicula*, *Gomphonema*, *Amphora*, *Cocconeis*, *Pinnularia*), with lesser green algae (*Protoderma viride*), blue-green algae (*Chamaesiphon*, *Oscillatoria*, *Entophysalis rivularis*), red algae (*Batrachospermum*), and yellow-green algae (*Vaucheria*). Aquatic mosses/liverworts (*Chiloscyphus*, *Riccardia*, *Scapania*, *Eurhynchium*) occupy stable substrates.

**Trophic structure**:
- **Herbivore/algal feeders**: *Peltoperla brevis*, [[Glossosoma]], [[Micrasema]] (moss/liverwort specialist), stone-cased Limnephilidae, Elmidae larvae, adult *Optioservus quadrimaculatus* — these fed on algae (mainly diatoms) when available.
- **Detritivores**: *Paraleptophlebia*, *Nemoura*, wood-cased Limnephilidae, *Lepidostoma*, *Dixa*, *Simulium*, Hydrobaeninae — dependent on allochthonous leaf/plant material.
- **Facultative/seasonal switchers**: *Baetis*, *Cynigmula*, *Epeorus*, stone-cased Limnephilidae, Hydrobaeninae — shifted from algal-dominant diets in spring to detritus-dominant diets in summer/fall.
- **Carnivores**: *Alloperla*, *Acroneuria californica*, *A. pacifica*, *Rhyacophila*, Psychomyiidae.

**Habitat-driven feeding**: Microhabitat strongly predicted diet — *Glossosoma* fed on rock-top algae (no detritus present), *Micrasema* restricted to moss/liverwort patches, while *Nemoura*, *Lepidostoma*, and wood-cased Limnephilidae occupied slow-water depositional zones rich in leaf detritus. *Paraleptophlebia* showed negative phototaxis, hiding under substrate by day (detritus-feeding) and foraging on exposed surfaces at night (algal-feeding) — a clear diel behavioral control on diet ([[Negative Phototaxis in Paraleptophlebia]]).

**Link to [[Coho Salmon]] ecology**: Demory (1961) found juvenile coho salmon in Deer Creek derived ~34% of diet (dry weight) from terrestrial insects, ~62% from aquatic insects, ~4% unidentified. Cross-referencing with aquatic insect gut data, Chapman (1961) estimated **~66% of total coho energy intake was allochthonous** (terrestrial-derived, directly or via detritivorous aquatic insects), only ~7% autochthonous (algal-derived), and ~26% unresolved. Coho in the smallest, most densely shaded stream (Needle Branch) showed the highest reliance on terrestrial organisms (40%) versus Deer Creek (21%) and Flynn Creek (29%), supporting a stream-size/canopy-density gradient in terrestrial energy dependence relevant to [[Salmonid Trophic Ecology]] and [[Allochthonous Energy Input in Headwater Streams]].

## Key Findings

1. **Seasonal algal production peak**: Highest oxygen production (mean 3.1 mg O₂/ft²/hr) in Needle Branch substrate occurred in winter/spring, opposite to the summer-peak pattern reported for exposed calcareous English streams (Butcher 1946), reflecting the unique light regime created by deciduous canopy phenology (greater sub-canopy light in winter/spring when trees are leafless).

2. **Diet composition tables** document mean % algae vs. detritus and % of individuals containing algae across 23–24 taxa in Deer Creek and Needle Branch respectively, covering Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, Trichoptera, Coleoptera, and Diptera.

3. **Diel feeding plasticity in *Paraleptophlebia***: Nighttime-collected nymphs (Needle Branch, Sept 7 1960) showed 22% mean algal gut content vs. much lower daytime values; a Deer Creek night sample (Jan 27 1961) showed 4% algae vs. 0% in the prior day's sample — direct evidence of facultative, behaviorally-mediated diet shifts.

4. **Energy pathway synthesis**: Roughly 52% of aquatic insect food (as coho prey) was allochthonous detritus, 12% autochthonous algae, and 36% unassignable — feeding into whole-system energy budget estimates for [[Coho Salmon]] production.

5. **Stream-size gradient hypothesis**: The authors infer that as coastal streams widen downstream, canopy cover decreases, algal/autochthonous production increases, and dependence of both aquatic insects and salmonids on terrestrial energy inputs should decline — a testable prediction for broader [[Stream Continuum Concept]]-type comparisons.

6. **Comparative literature context**: Contrasts with Jones (1949, 1950) South Wales calcareous streams, which supported richer autotroph communities and less allochthonous dependence, reinforcing that canopy shading and substrate stability are key drivers of the algal vs. detrital energy balance in stream food webs.
