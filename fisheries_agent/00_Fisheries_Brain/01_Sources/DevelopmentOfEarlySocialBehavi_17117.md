# Development of Early Social Behaviour in Rainbow Trout (*Salmo gairdneri*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study by [[Cole and Noakes]] (1980) examines how early social behaviour of [[Rainbow Trout]] develops and how [[Water Current]] and [[Fish Density]] modify it. Agonistic behaviour appears only once alevins become free-swimming. It starts as simple attacks and gains more complex threat patterns with age. Fast water and low density favour station-holding and apparently territorial behaviour. High density increases agonistic interactions, especially in fast water. The authors argue that the onset of agonistic behaviour reflects an ontogenetic motivational change rather than a sensory or motor change at emergence.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*), chosen because hatchery stocks are readily available and its ecology, physiology and nutrition are well documented. Its behaviour, especially early social behaviour, was poorly studied. The species is important as a sport fish, in bioassays, in [[Aquaculture]], and as a standard comparative model.
- **Life history context:** As typical of salmonids, young rainbow trout emerge from the gravel of a stream after a long embryonic period and become free-swimming alevins in open water. The authors note that early life history affects both ecological production and later adult behaviour.
- **Research aims:** The study examined (1) the onset of agonistic responses, (2) the proportions of threat versus attack at different ages, and (3) the effects of fish density and water flow on individual agonistic behaviour. Earlier work had suggested that threat becomes relatively more frequent in older fish and that density and flow significantly affect salmonid social behaviour.
- **Preliminary tests:** Embryos showed no agonistic behaviour before free-swimming. Photoperiod, substrate type and fish stock did not change the onset of agonistic responses (Cole, 1976).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental set-up**
- Embryos were kept in running well water at 10 ± 0.5 °C in darkness, simulating natural [[Redd]] conditions, and transferred to observation tanks 35 days after fertilization.
- Tanks were transparent plexiglass, 150 × 58 × 46 cm, split into two equal sections by an opaque baffle. Sections were designated **fast current** (upstream) and **slow current** (downstream). Fish were confined to their sections by 2.2 mm nylon mesh screens.
- Tank bottoms were bare (no gravel) to maximize visibility.
- Flow measurements (dye and potassium permanganate filming) showed that surface velocity in fast sections was 10–15 cm/s and bottom velocity about 7 cm/s. Slow sections had uniform flow below 1 cm/s with no turbulence.
- Lighting was a 12 h light / 12 h dark photoperiod. Fish were fed pulverized dry food to satiation three times daily.

**Population characteristics**
- Groups of 5, 15, 25 and 35 fish were placed in both current regimes (eight conditions in total).
- At the end of observations, mean total length was 29 mm (65 days post-fertilization; 160 fish total).

**Behavioural observation methods**
- Focal animal sampling ([[Focal Animal Sampling]]) was started when 50% of alevins were free-swimming. Each day, four fish were randomly selected in each of the eight sections and observed for 5 min each, between 09:30 and 13:00, for 30 days (32 fish observed daily).
- Eight modal action patterns plus fish interaction and interaction distance were recorded. Agonistic patterns included [[Head Down Display]], bite, [[Dorsal Fin Erection]], pursuit and flight. Neutral patterns included yawn, snap and stationary behaviour. Stationary behaviour was linked to holding a territory.
- Analysis used multivariate ANOVA (repeated measures), Duncan's multiple range test and trend tests. The significance threshold was P < 0.01, chosen to reduce Type II errors.

## Key Findings

**1. Onset of agonistic behaviour**
- Agonistic behaviour began only after free-swimming. Threat components (head down, dorsal fin erection) appeared later than active aggression components (bite, pursuit).
- Mean ages of first observation (days since 50% free-swimming, Table I): stationary, snap, pursuit and flight at 1 day; yawn 1.4; bite 1.4 (SD 0.5); dorsal fin erection 3.1 (SD 1.0); head down 8.3 (SD 8.1).
- Fish density and water current had no consistent effect on onset times.

**2. Effect of water current (mainly at low density)**
- At density 5, durations of fish interaction, pursuit and flight were significantly shorter in fast current (P < 0.01). Stationary behaviour was longer in fast current.
- At densities 5 and 15, stationary behaviour, flight, fish interaction, yawn and snap were less frequent in fast current (P < 0.01).
- At density 35, bite was less frequent in fast current.
- Interpretation: fish in fast current were less mobile and showed less agonistic behaviour at low density. At higher density, current had little apparent effect.

**3. Effect of fish density**
- In fast current, the step from density 15 to 25 was critical. Most action patterns, including neutral ones, were more frequent at density 25. Durations of dorsal fin erection, pursuit and fish interaction increased, while stationary duration decreased (P < 0.01).
- In slow current, the effect was less uniform. Only stationary behaviour, snap and flight were more frequent at density 25 than 15.
- The percentage of interaction time spent in threat (dorsal fin erection) increased from 20.0% at density 15 to 33.9% at density 25 (Table VI).
- Interaction distances were significantly smaller in fast than slow current at densities 5 and 15 (P < 0.01). In slow current they decreased significantly from density 5 to 15 and from 15 to 25.

**4. Effect of age (days 1–30)**
- Fish interaction, pursuit, flight and dorsal fin erection became more frequent with age (P < 0.01). Durations of fish interaction and dorsal fin erection increased, while stationary duration decreased.
- Neutral patterns (yawn, snap, stationary behaviour) showed no significant change in frequency or duration with age (P > 0.01).
- Share of interaction time in pursuit/flight fell from 94.5% (days 1–3) to 73.9% (days 28–30), while threat rose. The number of interaction events per fish rose from 1.2 to 2.0.
- Overall, agonistic behaviour changed from simple attacks early to threat-based displays later.

**5. Principal conclusions**
- **Developmental shift:** Agonistic behaviour begins with straightforward attacks and becomes more complex with age, consistent with other fish species.
- **Motivational trigger:** Because embryos in full view of each other showed no agonistic behaviour before emergence, the authors infer that a motivational change, not a sensory or motor one, accompanies free-swimming. This is in line with Dill's (1977) proposal of a single underlying motivation for agonistic and ingestive behaviour.
- **Territoriality and flow:** Increased water flow promotes station-holding and territoriality. The authors propose that territories function as [[Feeding Territory]] sites, consistent with a sit-and-wait strategy on stream drift. Greater movement in slow flow may therefore be adaptive, helping fish find better feeding sites.
- **Density override:** High density tends to override territoriality regardless of flow, possibly because the number of potential intruders makes defence inefficient.
- **Comparison with other studies:** Chiszar et al. (1975) reported 28 aggressive responses per fish per hour. Jenkins (1969) estimated 1–5 per fish per hour. The present study found about 18–40, depending on flow and age. The authors argue that differences in flow, density and age explain much of the variation between studies. Chiszar et al. had no water current, whereas Jenkins observed fish in streams.
- **Dispersal:** The authors note that aggression may disperse young fish in some salmonids, as suggested by [[Chapman]] (1962), but that [[Miller]] (1970) found it was not significant for young brook charr. The authors leave this question open.
- **Open questions:** Whether the shift from attacks to threats reflects ontogeny or learning remains unresolved. The authors call for further study, including individual marking techniques (McNicol and Noakes, 1979).

**Document note:** The source includes a French résumé and a German Kurzfassung (abstract). The German version adds that the observed shift from open aggression to threat within the first 30 days fits the species' known ecology, in which the balance between aggression and threat changes during development in fast-flowing rocky river beds.
