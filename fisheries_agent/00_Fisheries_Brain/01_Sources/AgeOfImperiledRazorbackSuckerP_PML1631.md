# Age of Impounded Razorback Sucker from Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona State University master's thesis (Michael Scott McCarthy, 1985) tests how well different bony structures estimate the age of wild [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) from [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the lower [[Colorado River]]. The species is disappearing across its range, and recruitment into the Lake Mohave population appears low or nonexistent. Of seven structures examined, only [[Sagittal Otolith]] sections gave reliable ages for large adults. Wild fish captured in 1980–1993 were 24 to 44 years old, and 88.6% hatched before or during the closure of [[Davis Dam]] and the filling of Lake Mohave. The author concludes that if the pattern of disappearance seen in other reservoirs (40–50 years after impoundment) holds, the population should disappear before the year 2000.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin. Historically it ranged from the Green River in Wyoming to the Colorado River Delta in Sonora and Baja California Norte, Mexico. It was an important food fish for Native Americans and early settlers.
- **Decline:** Recent records in the lower basin are infrequent and mostly of large adults. Small fish are sporadic and geographically scattered. The species vanished from [[Gila River]] system impoundments in the 1950s, about 40 years after major dams were built, and has become increasingly rare in mainstem reservoirs.
- **Lake Mohave stock:** This is the last major population of the species in the lower river. Minckley (1983) reported large size and presumed great age: females averaged 50 cm and males 45 cm standard length (SL) in 1981. Larvae were produced each year, but young fish disappeared before reaching 18 mm total length (TL). No fish under 37 cm SL were caught in [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] electrofishing from 1963 to 1979. High rates of blindness and tumors also suggested old individuals.
- **Study aims:** (1) compare the value of different structures for age determination; (2) test the hypothesis of substantial age in the Lake Mohave population; (3) describe individual growth patterns.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reservoir history:** [[Davis Dam]] was built between 1942 and 1951 and Lake Mohave filled in 1954. The author proposed that the stock originated before or during this period.
- **Otolith growth:** Razorback Sucker otoliths are made of [[Vaterite]], a rare crystalline form of calcium carbonate in teleosts. Growth is seasonal, with faster accretion in summer and slower accretion in winter. Otoliths are more conservative than other bones under stress such as food shortage, exertion, or low pH, so they retain a record that other structures may lose.
- **Habitat suitability:** Lake Mohave is a warm, monomictic lower-river reservoir. The author argues these conditions favor annulus formation, as the pre-impoundment Colorado River likely did because of its strong seasonal variation.
- **Feeding and habitat:** The protrusible subterminal mouth and closely spaced gill rakers suggest facultative planktivory. Rapid growth in a filling reservoir may reflect access to planktonic food. The species may be better adapted to backwaters and oxbows of the unmodified river than to mainstem habitats.
- **Management context:** The species has been propagated at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and reintroduced to suitable lower-basin habitats, so measures to prevent extinction are available.

## Key Findings

**Sample.** The study used 84 wild-caught adults, 45 hatchery-reared known-age progeny, and 14 wild and 5 hatchery fish for structure comparisons. Sources included 55 specimens collected in May 1980 and January 1981, 9 broodfish from Dexter National Fish Hatchery, 4 fish from the Arizona Game and Fish Department, and 16 fish from the California Department of Fish and Game. Four fish lacked sex data and were excluded from analyses other than aging.

**Structure suitability.**
- Scales from wild fish were small, irregular, often eroded or regenerated, and had uniform circuli. Annuli were obscure and inconsistent, so scales were unreliable (questionable to useless) for wild fish.
- Opercles, cleithra, branchiostegal rays, and vertebrae in large fish were too dense and thickened to read accurately, which tended to underestimate age.
- Fin ray sections had annular marks, but counts often differed between lobes and did not match other structures.
- Sectioned otoliths gave clear annular growth in 83.3% of 84 fish examined. Breakage during sectioning caused 2.4% of the unreadable cases, aberrant margin growth caused 10.7%, and off-focus sectioning caused 3.6%.
- Hatchery fish: annuli were visible in all seven structures, and only sectioned otoliths were reliable for wild adults.

**Age structure.**
- A total of 70 readable otolith samples were analyzed. All intact, readable sagittae of wild fish had 23 or more distinct marks.
- The oldest fish was 44 otolith years old, and the youngest was 24 years old at capture in 1980.
- 62 of the 70 fish (88.6%) hatched before or during the filling of Lake Mohave, between 1937 and 1958.

**Growth.**
- Sagitta radius and SL at capture were highly correlated (r = 0.973 for log10-transformed data), which allowed back-calculation of length.
- Females grew faster and reached larger sizes than males. The sexual dimorphism in size was established early and persisted throughout life.
- Females averaged 140 ± 4 mm SL at the first annulus and 276 ± 7 mm at the fifth. Males averaged 103 ± 4 mm and 214 ± 9 mm, respectively.
- About 27% of SL at capture was reached in the first year, and about 50% by the fifth year. Growth slowed to a few millimeters per year after the fifth or sixth year.
- Growth to the first annulus was the same before and after impoundment. Growth to the fifth annulus differed significantly (p < 0.05) between fish hatched before and after closure of Davis Dam, for both sexes. This suggests that lentic conditions may have enhanced growth.

**Longevity and maturity.**
- Maximum age is unknown. Other records support long lives, including a 22-year-old male from the lower Colorado River (collected 1976) and a 17-annulus, 60 cm fish (collected 1971).
- Consistently small growth increments in large adults were reported in the upper basin: one tagged adult showed no detectable growth over 1.5 years, and another grew 8.0 mm over 3.5 years.
- Sexual maturity at the Dexter National Fish Hatchery occurs at about 2 years in males and 3 years in females.
- The longest Lake Mohave specimen was an unweighed female of 57.5 cm SL (73.5 cm TL). The heaviest recorded from Lake Mohave was a 4.99 kg female.

**Conclusions.**
- Minckley's (1983) hypothesis of substantial age in the Lake Mohave stock is supported.
- No detectable recruitment has occurred into the population since about 1959, and little since 1954. The reasons remain unknown.
- Unless this trend is reversed, extirpation could occur before the year 2000.

**Recommendations.** The author notes that propagation and reintroduction are feasible. Ongoing research on predation, nutritional ecology, movements, habitat selection, and life history is intended to identify the factors that prevent a successful life cycle for this species.
