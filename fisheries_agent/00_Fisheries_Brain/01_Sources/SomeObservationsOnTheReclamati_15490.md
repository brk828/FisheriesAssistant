# Observations on the Reclamation of a Good, a Marginal, and a Poor Trout Stream in Massachusetts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #stream-ecology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1956 report by W. L. Minckley and James W. Mullan documents experimental reclamation of three Massachusetts streams using [[Rotenone]] in 1955, followed by evaluation one year later. The streams represented a gradient of trout habitat quality: [[Glendale Brook]] (good), [[Lawrence Brook]] (marginal), and [[Wading River]] (poor). Following rotenone treatment, streams were stocked with fingerling [[Brook Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] (2-3 inches). Results demonstrated that fingerling survival and growth were highest in the good trout stream, intermediate in the marginal stream, and non-existent in the poor stream. The study suggests that population manipulation via rotenone may improve trout carrying capacity in suitable habitats by reducing competition from warm-water species, though complete eradication proved difficult and recolonization occurred variably across sites.

## Background & Description

### Study Context
The investigation was conducted as part of Federal Aid in Fish Restoration funds under Dingell-Johnson Projects F-1-R-1, 2, and 4 Massachusetts. While pond reclamation for trout had become accepted practice, stream reclamation principles had been only limitedly applied and evaluated. The authors acknowledge the data are "highly inconclusive" and that "accident more than design shaped the work in the beginning."

### Stream Characteristics

**[[Glendale Brook]], Middlefield (Good Trout Stream)**
- Length: approximately 5 miles
- Elevation: 1,600 feet (source) to 814 feet (mouth)
- Summer water temperatures: 45-68°F (rarely reaching 68°F)
- Average summer flow: 2-3 cubic feet per second
- Water color: clear/white
- Habitat: steep gradient headwaters through forested land over rubble/boulder bedrock; meadow-like saddle in lower reaches with ponded pools (7-8 feet maximum depth); terminates in steep bedrock cascade (200-foot drop) before entering [[Middle Branch Westfield River]]
- Natural native [[Brook Trout]] stream, never previously stocked
- Sparingly fished

**[[Lawrence Brook]], Royalston (Marginal Trout Stream)**
- Length: 7.75 miles
- Elevation: 1,000 feet (source in New Hampshire impoundment) to 640 feet (mouth)
- Summer water temperatures: high 70s to low 80s daytime, mid-to-high 60s at night
- Average summer flow: 2-3 cubic feet per second
- Water color: dark brown
- pH: 6.7
- Oxygen levels (September 5, 1956): 5.7 ppm (upper wooded reaches), 4.3 ppm (meadow-marsh), 8.2 ppm (below cascades)
- Source: large warm-water impoundment (~200 acres) in New Hampshire
- [[Doane Falls]] (impassable natural cascades) near confluence with [[East Branch Tully River]]
- Limited reputation for occasionally producing large brook trout (14-18 inches)
- Lightly fished; 1953 stocking return: 36% brook trout, 20% brown trout

**[[Wading River]], Foxboro/Mansfield/Norton (Poor Trout Stream)**
- Length: approximately 11 miles
- Elevation: 160 feet (source) to 60 feet (junction with [[Taunton River]])
- Summer water temperatures: low-to-high 70s or low 80s
- Water color: dark brown
- Flow: sluggish over most course
- Bottom: rubble, gravel, sand, mud, silt
- Drains numerous warm-water ponds and swamps
- Classified as "put-and-take" trout stream
- 1955 tag return: 31% overall (estimated to represent ~75% of actual catch)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Pre-Reclamation Fish Communities

**Glendale Brook (Station 1, June 20, 1952):**
- 53 [[White Sucker]]s (2.0-9.9 inches)
- 1 [[Brook Trout]] (6.3 inches)
- 1 [[Brown Bullhead]] (3.7 inches)
- Several hundred [[Blacknosed Dace]], [[Common Shiner]]s, [[Creek Chub]]s

**Glendale Brook (Station 1, August 8, 1955 - at reclamation):**
- 306 white suckers (2.0-10.9 inches, 15.8 lbs)
- 14 brook trout (5.0-7.4 inches, 1.3 lbs)
- 245 creek chubs (6.2 lbs)
- 302 blacknosed dace (1.5 lbs)
- 7 brown bullheads (5.0-6.9 inches)
- 371 common shiners (3.5 lbs)

**Lawrence Brook (Station 1, July 6, 1953):**
- 5 trout
- Large numbers of [[Chain Pickerel]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Fallfish]], [[Redfin Pickerel]], [[Chubsucker]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bridle Shiner]], [[Blacknose Dace]]

**Wading River (Station, July 15, 1955):**
- 57.3 pounds of fish total
- 5 [[Brown Trout]] (1.3 lbs)
- Dominated by warm-water species including chain pickerel, yellow perch, golden shiner, pumpkinseed, fallfish, white sucker

### Reclamation Methodology

**Glendale Brook (August 8, 1955):**
- Emulsifiable rotenone applied at all logical points
- No spraying of each individual pool
- Stocked August 15, 1955: 2,500 brook trout fingerlings (2-3 inches) + 2,500 rainbow trout fingerlings (2-3 inches)

**Lawrence Brook (August 18, 1955):**
- Emulsifiable rotenone applied
- No attempt at complete kill due to ease of recontamination from New Hampshire pond
- Rotenone applied at all road crossings and tributaries
- Downstream dispersal controlled by U.S. Corps of Engineers closing gate at [[Tully Dam]] flood control reservoir
- Heavy rain from tropical hurricane began same day, causing severe flooding by following day
- Stocked September 2, 1955: 19,000 brook trout fingerlings (2-3 inches)
- Additional 1956 stockings: 4,000 rainbow trout (6-12 inches, April 26); 1,500 brown trout (6-9 inches, June 7)
- Late June 1956: warm-water pond in New Hampshire drained, flushing indigenous fish population downstream

**Wading River (mid-July 1955):**
- Emulsifiable rotenone introduced at three locations (headwater, middle, lower reaches)
- No attempt at complete/permanent reclamation due to ease of recontamination from swamps and headwater impoundments
- Stocked August 17, 1955: 25,000 brook trout fingerlings (3 inches)
- 1956 stocking: 3,550 brook trout (6-12 inches)

### Environmental Challenges

A tropical hurricane struck on August 19, 1955, causing severe flooding throughout Massachusetts. This affected [[Glendale Brook]] and [[Wading River]], both stocked before the flood (4 and 2 days prior, respectively). [[Lawrence Brook]] was stocked after flood conditions subsided. High water temperatures at time of Wading River stocking caused immediate mortality. The hurricane flooding eliminated planned fish pickups for population comparison.

## Key Findings

### Fingerling Survival and Growth

**Glendale Brook (Good Stream):**
- One year post-stocking (August 8-9, 1956), electrical shocking surveys revealed substantial fingerling survival
- Station 1 (1,200 feet): 48 rainbow trout (4.5-9.4 inches), 11 brook trout (6.0-8.9 inches), 1 brown bullhead, 1 blacknosed dace
- Station 2 (800 feet): 13 rainbow trout (4.5-8.4 inches), 32 brook trout (2.5-6.4 inches), 22 blacknosed dace (1-2 inches)
- Station 3 (tributary, 200 feet): 4 rainbow trout (4.5-7.4 inches), 18 brook trout (3.5-7.4 inches)
- Station 4 (headwater, 400 feet): 7 brook trout (4.0-7.9 inches), 40 blacknosed dace
- Total from 2,400 feet: 134 trout
- Crude estimate by gross expansion: >1,000 trout for entire stream (>20% survival)
- Authors note this survival "well above that reported in the literature for fingerling trout planted in non-reclaimed streams"

**Growth rates in Glendale Brook exceeded regional averages:**
- Brook trout from fingerling plant (69 sampled): 19 fish 2.0-3.9 inches, 40 fish 4.0-5.9 inches, 19 fish 6.0-8.9 inches
- Regional average for Age-Group I: 4.7 inches (study fish exceeded this)
- Rainbow trout (65 sampled): 20 fish 4.0-5.9 inches, 35 fish 6.0-9.9 inches
- Rainbow growth exceeded brook trout growth

**Lawrence Brook (Marginal Stream):**
- Fingerling survival "not impressive": only 21 brook trout from 19,000 fingerlings collected (36% of total stream area sampled)
- However, heavy angling pressure may have reduced apparent survival
- Growth exceeded regional averages: 4 fish 3.0-5.9 inches, 17 fish 6.0-8.9 inches (regional Age-Group I average: 4.4 inches)
- Angler reports indicated stream "more heavily fished in 1956 than in previous seasons" with "quality of fishing the best in the memory of man"
- Some anglers reported fishing was "too good" with limits too easy

**Wading River (Poor Stream):**
- No fingerling survival detected
- Station sampled July 31, 1956 (one year post-reclamation): 0 trout recovered
- Possible explanations: high water temperature mortality at stocking; species composition change (1956 stocked only brook trout vs. mixed species in 1955); brook trout known to disappear 3-6 weeks post-stocking in Massachusetts

### Trout Carrying Capacity Changes

**Glendale Brook:**
- Station 1: 1 trout (1952 electrical shocking) vs. 14 trout (1955 rotenone pickup) vs. 59 trout (1956 electrical shocking)
- Station 2: 50 trout (1955 rotenone) vs. 45 trout (1956 electrical shocking, less effective method)
- Data indicate "improvement in the ability...to support a greater weight of trout than before reclamation"

**Lawrence Brook:**
- Numerical increase: 28 trout total from four stations (1953) vs. 88 trout (1956) - three-fold increase
- Weight increase (two smaller stations): 1.5 lbs (1953) vs. 7.3 lbs (1956) - five-fold increase
- Confounding factors: increased stocking (2,500 catchables in 1953 vs. 5,500 in 1956, plus fingerlings); greater angler harvest in 1956; abnormal conditions from pond drainage and subsequent warm-water fish reintroduction

**Wading River:**
- Negative results: 5 brown trout/1.3 lbs (1955) vs. 0 trout (1956)
- Total fish biomass: 57.3 lbs (1955) vs. 7.4 lbs (1956)

### Degree of Kill and Recolonization

**Completeness of Kill:**
- Only [[Glendale Brook]] considered suitable for complete kill attempt, but this was not achieved
- Intermittent headwater pools harbor fish and serve to recontaminate streams
- Authors suggest complete kill might be possible if reclaimed during stream rise or by spraying every individual pool at low flow

**Recolonization Rates (one year post-treatment):**
- **Glendale Brook:** [[Blacknosed Dace]] staged successful comeback; one small bullhead also present; other species remained scarce
- **Lawrence Brook:** Prior to headwater pond flushing, appeared complete kill achieved; after flushing, most species reintroduced but standing crop of [[Chain Pickerel]], [[White Sucker]]s, etc. "well below that originally found"
- **Wading River:** Quantitative measurement showed replacement at only 13% of former standing crop (7.4 lbs vs. 57.3 lbs)

### Management Implications

The authors conclude that "population manipulation in streams with rotenone would appear to warrant consideration due to the relative inexpensiveness of the technique." They note that "the difference between a trout and a non-trout stream represents a delicate balance between trout and their environment" and that "tipping the equilibrium in some streams by the elimination of hordes of warm water fish might conceivably make for better living conditions for trout."

The study demonstrates that reclamation effectiveness is highly dependent on inherent stream quality. Good trout habitat showed dramatic improvements in fingerling survival and growth; marginal habitat showed intermediate results complicated by multiple variables; poor habitat showed no benefit. The technique appears most promising for streams with suitable physical and chemical characteristics but degraded by competition from non-salmonid species.
