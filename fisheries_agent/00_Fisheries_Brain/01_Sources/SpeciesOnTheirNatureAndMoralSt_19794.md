# Species and Ecosystems as Living Entities: On Their Nature and Moral Standing

#philosophy-of-biology #environmental-ethics #ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This *Journal of Natural History* opinion piece (1995, vol. 29, pp. 843–849) by [[L. E. Johnson]], a philosopher writing from Flinders University, argues that [[Species]], [[Ecosystems]], symbiotic entities such as [[Lichen]], colonial organisms such as [[Siphonophores]], and the [[Biosphere]] are living entities in their own right. Johnson contends that they therefore have identity, interests and [[Moral Standing]] that cannot be reduced to the sum of their individual organisms. The article is a philosophical essay. It contains no fish data, no sampling, no specimen counts and no statistics. It has no species-specific content.

## Background & Description

Johnson opens by asking why the extinction of a species is morally troubling, given that a species appears to be only an aggregate of individual organisms. He argues that this view rests on a mistaken, "clockwork" picture of reality, in which the world is composed of discrete things with properties. This picture has produced major advances, traced to [[Galileo]], [[Descartes]] and [[Newton]], and to the [[Hypothetico-Deductive Method]]. Johnson holds that it has a problematic side: it assumes discrete units, and it is shaped by Western noun-centred language.

His alternative is a [[Process Ontology]]. A living being is "a living system, a continuing process" and not a physical object. Johnson compares this to a word-processing program, which needs a computer to run but is not the computer. Living entities are characterised by:

- organised self-identity maintained against environmental disorder;
- use of a through-flow of energy to counter entropy, at least for a time (see [[Second Law of Thermodynamics]]);
- maintenance of a range of favourable states, which Johnson prefers to call [[Homeorrhesis]] rather than [[Homeostasis]], because the range varies over time.

Johnson draws on [[David Hull]] (1981), who argued that species evolve, split and go extinct, whereas classes cannot. He also answers [[Richard Dawkins]] (1986, *The Blind Watchmaker*), who denied that species have characteristics of their own. Johnson grants Dawkins's points about selection but argues that a species can still have traits and interests in its own right, through individual organisms without being their sum.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Johnson gives several ecological examples:

- **Predation:** In the absence of predators, which remove the old, ill, defective and supernumerary, some species can do badly. A species has an interest in being preyed upon, or in whatever else sustains its viability.
- **Fire:** Certain ecosystems have an interest in periodic fires, which is distinct from the interests of individual organisms.
- **Genetic diversity:** A species-level trait such as genetic diversity cannot be attributed to individual organisms, even though it is maintained through them.
- **Bees and pollinators:** Genotypes cooperate across organisms, as when insects pollinate flowers, and the stinging response of bees can cost the individual its life while still favouring its genotype.
- **Whaling:** Harm to whales must be weighed against possible loss of the species and disruption of marine ecosystems, which Johnson describes as having importance "on a different and much greater scale of magnitude."
- **Native species removal:** Johnson argues against removing native species on the grounds that an ecosystem may be stable without them. He compares this to lobotomy, which makes patients more manageable at too high a cost.

Johnson does not address freshwater or marine fish, fisheries or any particular habitat in detail.

## Key Findings

1. **Species and ecosystems have moral standing.** Johnson argues that these entities have identity and interests in their own right. Their moral significance goes beyond the aggregate welfare of their members.

2. **Biocentric ethic.** Johnson advocates a [[Biocentric Ethic]]: the interests of all beings that have interests are morally considerable, in proportion to those interests. Sentience and rationality are not the sole basis for moral weight. Interests extend to a "living in-depth organism" whose rationality and sentience are only part of its conscious surface.

3. **Individual moral weight varies.** An individual dandelion or cockroach has minimal interests. Primates and cetaceans have a high level of interests.

4. **Selection and interests.** Johnson argues that selection acts on genes, but that genotypes are selected according to how well they mesh with other genotypes in organisms, species and ecosystems. Species and ecosystems are therefore not simply collections of individuals, even though selection does not directly act on those levels.

5. **Rejection of discrete-unit thinking.** Johnson rejects the "where-do-you-draw-the-line?" objection. He argues that the indeterminate boundaries of species and ecosystems do not undermine their reality, because living entities are processes and not discrete things.

6. **Practical recommendation.** Johnson advises that managers and scientists should think in terms of [[Ecosystem Integrity]] and life processes, not just habitats or individual species. He suggests asking: What will be the effects of our actions on the whole system? Can we control or reverse them? Are they necessary and sufficiently justified? He describes respect for living processes as perhaps "the most practical thing we can do."

**Limitations:** The article is a philosophical opinion piece based on argument and examples, not on empirical research. It presents no data, and its ecological examples are illustrative. Johnson states that he is a philosopher and "cannot claim to be a biologist," and he leaves the detailed application of his ideas to biological scientists.
