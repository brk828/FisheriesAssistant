# Colorado River Squawfish (Ptychocheilus lucius) in the San Juan River, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This short miscellany of natural history notes contains one substantive fish record and two bird notes. The main item, by William J. Koster, reports the first known specimens of the [[Colorado River Squawfish]] from the San Juan portion of the [[Colorado River Basin]], documented by two fish captured in 1959 near Rosa, New Mexico. The note adds size, colouration, diet, angling lore and a list of associated fishes, and warns that the population's future is uncertain because [[Navajo Dam]] will flood the reach. The remaining two notes concern birds, one a specimen from Mexico and one an Acorn Woodpecker record from Oklahoma, and are not aquatic.

## Background & Description

Hearsay reports of the Colorado River Squawfish in northwestern New Mexico, recorded in Koster's 1957 *Guide to the Fishes of New Mexico*, were confirmed when Mr. Milton Seibel of Arboles, Colorado, supplied two specimens. The species is described as the largest North American Cyprinidae, which is why the note records additional detail on it.

- **Specimen 1:** adult male, 415 mm standard length, taken June 1959. Stomach empty. Fresh-frozen; lacked orange spots seen in life on the female.
- **Specimen 2:** adult female, 590 mm standard length, captured August 29, 1959, during Koster's visit. Stomach contained parts of a half-grown [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*).

In life, the female was silvery olive on the snout, opercle and dorsal surfaces, yellowish along the sides, and whitish ventrally. Fins were pale olive suffused with pink. An orange spot about the size of the eye was present on each cheek, and a third spot sat just above the vent on the left side. The iris was golden around the pupil and silvery elsewhere.

Local anglers distinguish this species as "salmon" from the smaller "whitefish" (*Gila robusta*, [[Bonytail]]). Seibel reports that "salmon" are usually caught by people fishing for [[Channel Catfish]]. Anglers catch no more than two or three per year, and the largest taken in the area weighed twenty pounds. Because they fight well, they are usually released unharmed, whereas "whitefish" are typically left on the bank.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling site.** Both fish were taken from the same pool of the San Juan River about 3 miles below Rosa, Rio Arriba County. The pool is about 600 feet long, 100 feet wide and 3 to 4 feet deep, with a rocky bluff 15 to 20 feet high on one side and a sloping floodplain meadow on the other. The female was taken in moderately strong current near the end of an adjacent riffle, which is described as 75 feet wide and 214 feet deep as printed (the depth figure may be a scan error for 2¼ feet). The bottom of this section is composed of rounded, firmly embedded gravel and rubble, which the author notes offers relatively little shelter for fish.

**Distribution.** Per Seibel's reports, the species has been caught in the San Juan River from the [[Navajo Dam]] site, just below the mouth of the Los Piños River, to the mouth of the Las Piedras River in extreme southern Colorado. It has not been recorded in the Los Piños, Las Piedras or the San Juan above the Las Piedras junction.

**Flow conditions.** During the visit, the San Juan was high and heavily silt-laden from recent rain. By the end of the week, the stream had cleared to a more normal state.

**Associated fauna.** The fish fauna of this reach is poorly sampled but includes:
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
- Bluehead mountain sucker (*Pantosteus delphinus*)
- Bonytail (*Gila robusta*)
- Dusky dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- Fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*)
- Channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*)
- Blue catfish (*Ictalurus furcatus*)
- Sculpin (*Cottus bairdi*)

Seibel also reports black bullheads and a further species whose description suggests [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). In total, eight species are confirmed from the reach, with two more reported by angler description.

**Threats.** Navajo Dam, then under construction, was to flood the entire river segment from which the squawfish had been reported, placing the local population's future in doubt.

## Key Findings

1. **First San Juan records:** Two specimens (one male, 415 mm SL; one female, 590 mm SL) confirm the [[Colorado River Squawfish]] in the San Juan portion of the Colorado River Basin, previously known only from hearsay.
2. **Diet:** The female had eaten a half-grown flannelmouth sucker, and the male's stomach was empty.
3. **Habitat:** Both fish came from a deep pool with a rocky bluff margin, near a strong-current riffle with gravel and rubble substrate and limited cover.
4. **Range:** Reports indicate occurrence from the Navajo Dam site to the Las Piedras River mouth, but not in the tributaries Los Piños and Las Piedras.
5. **Abundance:** Anglers catch two or three per year at most; the largest recorded weight was twenty pounds.
6. **Conservation concern:** Impending inundation by [[Navajo Dam]] threatens the only known population segment in the San Juan.
7. **Associated fauna:** Eight fish species are confirmed in the reach, and two more are reported locally.

**Non-aquatic notes in the same issue:**
- **Sutton note:** A specimen (a nominate-race bird, wing 119 mm) collected by [[George Miksch Sutton]] at about 10,000 feet near Las Vigas, Veracruz, Mexico, on April 4, 1939, is now held at the University of Oklahoma.
- **Acorn Woodpecker note:** An adult female [[Acorn Woodpecker]] (*Melanerpes formicivorus*) was observed on January 16, 1960, at the [[Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge]], Comanche County, Oklahoma. A specimen from the Northwestern State College collection, previously identified as a Lewis's Woodpecker, was re-identified as an Acorn Woodpecker. It was taken in Woods County, Oklahoma, in 1908.
