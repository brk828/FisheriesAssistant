# Recovery Implementation Program Recovery Action Plan (RIPRAP) for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document, issued by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region 6, Denver) in revised form dated March 6, 1998 (original October 15, 1993), contains two parts: the Section 7 Consultation, Sufficient Progress, and Historic Projects Agreement (Part One) and the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] Recovery Action Plan, or RIPRAP (Part Two). Together they set out how the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP) serves as a reasonable and prudent alternative under the [[Endangered Species Act]] for water projects affecting four endangered Colorado River fishes in the Upper Basin. The plan lists recovery actions, schedules, and budgets for the next several fiscal years, with an estimated total budget of approximately $100 million for FY 1998–FY 2004. The Agreement excludes the San Juan River and applies only to the four listed species.

## Background & Description

The RIPRAP addresses four endangered fishes, each with a species-specific recovery goal:

- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): primary goal is natural, self-sustaining populations and their habitat.
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): primary goal is natural, self-sustaining populations and their habitat.
- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*): immediate goal is to prevent extinction, given its critical population status in the Upper Basin.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): first priority is to prevent extinction in the wild, because successful recruitment of young fish has been limited.

The overall goal is recovery and delisting of all four species in the Upper Basin, consistent with the [[Recovery Plans]] developed under Section 4(f) of the ESA. The document is organized into a General Recovery Program Support Action Plan, Green River and Colorado River subbasin action plans, a budget section (Section 5.0), and an appendix on [[Critical Habitat]].

Recovery elements used throughout the action plans are: (I) protect instream flows; (II) restore habitat; (III) reduce negative impacts of nonnative fishes and sportfish management; (IV) conserve genetic integrity and augment or restore populations; (V) monitor populations and habitat and conduct research; (VI) increase public awareness; and (VII) provide program planning and support. Actions that produce a measurable population response, habitat improvement, legal flow protection, or reduction in immediate extinction risk are flagged with ">" and given highest priority; actions contributing to avoiding adverse modification of critical habitat are flagged with "*".

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Passage barriers** have confined the fishes to about 20 percent of their former range. Blockage by dams and diversions is suggested as a major cause of [[Colorado Squawfish]] decline.
- **Flow alteration** from upstream impoundments reduced peak spring flows and fish access to [[Flooded Bottomlands]], which hold large numbers of zooplankton and benthic organisms and are used by razorback suckers and squawfish for feeding and possibly spawning. Upper Colorado floodplains are now channelized by levees, dikes, rip-rap, and tamarisk.
- **Cold water** released from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] is presumed unsuitable for warmwater species and may explain their disappearance in the reach to the Yampa confluence.
- **Contaminants** including selenium, petroleum derivatives, heavy metals, and uranium have been identified, with suspected contaminant hot spots. The Section 7 Agreement excludes historic discharges of trace elements, heavy metals, and pesticides from the RIP's offset obligations.
- **Nonnative fishes:** 52 fish species occur in the upper basin, of which only 13 are native. Competition and predation by nonnatives such as [[Northern Pike]] is widely assumed to have contributed to native declines, though direct evidence is difficult to obtain. Northern pike were reported to consume 100 or more squawfish per year per individual, and young squawfish made up 5% of pike diet.
- **Genetic management** follows a population-as-unit framework, with refugia and paired breeding matrices used to maintain variability.

## Key Findings

**Section 7 Agreement (Part One)**

1. The RIP provides reasonable and prudent alternatives to avoid jeopardy and adverse modification of critical habitat for depletion impacts of new projects and for historic project impacts (excluding pollutant discharges).
2. The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] determines whether progress is "sufficient." Evaluation is done separately for the Colorado and Green River subbasins.
3. If progress is insufficient, biological opinions specify which RIPRAP actions must be completed to avoid jeopardy. The RIP's responsibility rests with participants collectively, not individual project proponents.
4. The RIPRAP is adaptive, reviewed annually, and updated by September 30 each year or before the annual work plan is adopted, whichever comes first.
5. Historic projects predating January 22, 1988 may be offset if needed for recovery. Post-1988 depletion increases are subject to a depletion charge.
6. [[Bureau of Reclamation]] projects incur no depletion charge while the Bureau continues contributing to the RIP annual budget.

**Funding (Section 1.4)**

- Annual operating budget of approximately $3 million, adjusted for inflation (about $24 million through FY 2004), contributed by Reclamation, the FWS, and the states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming.
- Depletion fees of $13.81 per acre-foot as of October 1, 1997, could yield $1 million through 2003.
- Operation and maintenance of refugia, hatcheries, and fish passage: about $7 million through FY 2004.
- Capital projects: about $69 million through FY 2004 for water acquisition, fish passage, hatcheries, and bottomland restoration.

**Subbasin priorities (Section 3.0)**

- **Green River:** Highest priority for squawfish recovery; supports the largest known riverine population of razorback sucker. Recovery focuses on refining [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations, with a revised biological opinion due October 1998. Stewart Lake and Ashley Creek selenium contamination is under separate cleanup.
- **Yampa and Little Snake Rivers:** The Yampa contains one of two confirmed squawfish spawning areas in the Upper Basin. The Little Snake supplies about 28 percent of Yampa flow and 60 percent of its sediment. Colorado filed for a junior instream flow right in December 1995; 48 statements of opposition were filed.
- **Duchesne River:** Squawfish and razorback suckers use the lower 15 miles, particularly during spring runoff. Initial flow recommendations were set in 1995.
- **White River:** Taylor Draw Dam (1984) blocked squawfish migration to the upper river. Interim flow recommendations are due by April 1998.
- **Colorado River (Palisade to Lake Powell):** Holds important populations of all four fishes. The 15-mile reach above the Gunnison confluence is a recovery focal point, with squawfish catch rates about double other areas. A 581 cfs instream flow right for July–September in this reach received a final decree in 1997. Reclamation has supplied up to 20,000 acre-feet from [[Ruedi Reservoir]] since 1990.
- **Gunnison River:** The 10-foot Redlands diversion was fitted with a fish ladder completed in 1996. Experimental razorback stocking began in 1995.
- **Dolores River:** Recovery actions are limited to preventing escapement of nonnative sportfish from McPhee Reservoir.

**Critical habitat (Appendix)**

- Final critical habitat rule was published March 21, 1994, effective April 20, 1994.
- Primary constituent elements are water (quantity and quality), physical habitat, and biological environment (food, predation, competition).
- Modifications added adjudication of CWCB instream flow filings, interim Ruedi Reservoir water for the 15-mile reach, floodplain restoration and an issue paper on floodplain protection, entrainment structures at Government Highline (Roller Dam), and selective removal of northern pike from the Yampa and Gunnison rivers and possibly Paonia Reservoir.
