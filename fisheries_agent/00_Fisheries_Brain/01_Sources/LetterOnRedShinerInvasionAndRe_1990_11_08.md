## Executive Summary

This letter documents the outcomes of a November 8, 1990 meeting of the Desert Fishes Recovery Team, convened to address the invasion of [[Aravaipa Creek]] by non-native [[Red Shiner]] and to review a draft recovery plan for [[Desert Pupfish]]. The primary concern centers on the threat posed by red shiner to seven native fish species in Aravaipa Creek, with particular alarm over impacts to the federally listed [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. Both listed species are now restricted to only four streams in the Gila River drainage — the Upper Gila, Upper Verde, Eagle Creek, and Aravaipa Creek — all of which are now contaminated by non-native fish. The Team, despite disagreement over the urgency of full stream renovation, agreed on five minimum management actions: fish barrier construction, intensified monitoring, establishment of a captive refugium, genetic characterization of remaining stocks, and conditional consideration of stream renovation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native community**: [[Aravaipa Creek]] supports **seven native fish species**, of which the [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] are the most vulnerable to the ongoing invasion.
- **Invasive pressure**: [[Red Shiner]] is identified as a serious threat to at least four of the seven native species; its upstream range expansion into the Canyon reach is the central management crisis.
- **Restricted range**: [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] populations are now confined to only **four streams** in the Gila River basin:
  - Upper Gila River
  - Upper Verde River
  - Eagle Creek
  - Aravaipa Creek
  - All four streams are now contaminated with non-native fish, indicating a basin-wide invasion crisis, not a localized one.
- **Population significance**: Aravaipa Creek is estimated to harbor **25% of the total existing population** of both loach minnow and spikedace, making it a critical stronghold for basin-wide recovery.
- **Compounding stressors**: Both listed species face simultaneous pressure from **habitat deterioration** and **exotic fish invasions**, suggesting habitat degradation may be facilitating invasive establishment.
- **Missed management window**: A fish barrier constructed below the Canyon prior to mid-summer 1990 could have prevented red shiner from reaching proposed critical habitat for loach minnow and spikedace; this opportunity was lost, escalating the need for intensive intervention.

## Key Findings

The Desert Fishes Recovery Team agreed on **five minimum management actions**:

1. **Fish Barrier Construction**
   - One or more barriers needed upstream of the Canyon (near [[Turkey Creek]] narrows) to block further upstream red shiner movement, contingent on census results confirming shiners have not already passed the Canyon; urgency may necessitate a temporary structure.
   - One or more barriers also needed downstream of the Canyon for effective fish fauna management throughout the creek.
   - Barriers serve a dual purpose: preventing invasive re-entry and preventing native fish from re-entering contaminated segments after floods or renovation events.

2. **Intensified Monitoring**
   - Fish fauna monitoring must begin no later than **March 15, 1991**, on a **monthly basis**, continuing until the scope of the invasion and viable solutions are identified.
   - Sampling protocols will be evaluated and adjusted continuously.
   - Monitoring and management commitment is expected to be **indefinite**.

3. **Refugium Establishment**
   - A captive refugium must be established for [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] while wild populations remain relatively abundant.
   - Species-specific culture techniques must be developed to maintain captive populations and support future restocking efforts.

4. **Genetic Stock Assessment**
   - Genetics of fish from all four remaining loach minnow and spikedace populations must be determined to preserve genetic diversity in case stream renovation becomes necessary.

5. **Conditional Stream Renovation**
   - Full stream renovation remains an option if native fish populations decline beyond the threshold of natural recovery.
   - The Recovery Team was split on timing: some advocated immediate renovation, others favored a "wait and see" approach given the drastic and disruptive nature of renovation.

**Additional Notes:**
- External support, including from **The Nature Conservancy**, has been offered in the form of funding and personnel; the Team recommends exploring all available assistance avenues.
- The letter frames the situation as a race against time, emphasizing that further delay risks irreversible deterioration of native fish fauna in [[Aravaipa Creek]].
