## Executive Summary

This paper describes the design, construction, and field application of a lightweight, transportable electric fish shocker developed for a two-man fish population study on [[Clear Lake]], Iowa. Unlike bulkier standard [[Electrofishing]] rigs of the era, this system was engineered for rapid removal and reinstallation on a small 16-foot aluminum boat, enabling flexible use across diverse lake habitats including dense vegetation, docks, boulder-strewn reefs, and open turbid water. The apparatus emphasizes durability, low maintenance, operational safety, and efficient fish capture using a single T-shaped boom and jointed copper-coated electrodes.

## Background & Description

*(Note: This source concerns sampling equipment engineering rather than organismal morphology. Relevant technical specifications are itemized below in lieu of biological morphology.)*

- **Power Source**: 150-volt alternating-current generator, 750 watts output.
- **Boat Platform**: 16-foot aluminum lapstrake boat, powered by a 10-horsepower outboard motor.
- **Boom Structure**:
  - Base piece: two-by-four, secured across the boat through the oarlocks using stove bolts and wingnuts; projected 6 inches on each side for light attachment.
  - Main boom: 17-foot two-by-four bolted at right angles to the base, extending over the bow.
  - Crossarm: 8-foot two-by-two bolted to the main boom's end, forming a T-shape.
  - Secured at bow via a 10-inch turnbuckle connecting a screw eye and the boat's mooring ring.
- **Electrodes**:
  - Constructed from four 18-inch sections of copper-coated iron ground rod (1/2 inch diameter).
  - Sections linked end-to-end with galvanized chain, fastened via nuts and bolts.
  - Electrolysis mitigated by soldering No. 12 copper wire parallel to chain links.
  - Two No. 10 copper wires permanently affixed to the boom transmitted power from generator to electrodes.
- **Design advantages**: Jointed electrodes folded for storage/transport, resisted tangling in floating and emergent vegetation, and maintained a near-vertical orientation in the water despite forward boat movement.
- **Auxiliary equipment**: Above- and below-water lights attached to the base board ends for night operations; 8-foot wood-handled dip nets; storage tubs positioned in the boat bottom and behind the bowman's seat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

This section is reframed to describe the **habitat sampling contexts** addressed by the shocker rather than a species' ecological role:

- **Target Environment**: [[Clear Lake]], Iowa — a shallow, turbid freshwater lake (Secchi-disc readings less than 24 inches).
- **Habitat Types Sampled**:
  - Heavy submerged and emergent vegetation
  - Dock structures
  - Boulder-covered reefs
  - Irregular, rocky bottom substrates
  - Open deep water
- **Operational Adaptations by Habitat**:
  - In vegetated/dock areas: one crewman waded and pushed the boat at walking pace for maximum maneuverability.
  - In deep or hazardous substrate areas (reefs, irregular bottoms): boat propelled by outboard motor, sacrificing some maneuverability for safety.
  - Turbidity influenced shocking efficacy — daytime sampling outperformed nighttime sampling due to poor visibility; above-water lights were used in turbid conditions, below-water lights in clearer conditions.
- **Fish Capture Behavior**: Stunned fish sometimes floated up out of reach of the bowman when maneuverability was reduced (motor-powered mode), affecting capture efficiency for late-floating individuals.

## Key Findings

- A **single-boom electrode configuration** minimized interference with netting operations compared to dual-boom designs, improving capture efficiency.
- **Wingnut and turnbuckle fastenings** allowed the entire boom and electrode assembly to be removed or installed by a single crew member, addressing the need for gear interchangeability on a multi-purpose sampling boat.
- The **jointed copper-coated ground rod electrodes** proved highly durable across two full field seasons in varied habitats (vegetation, rocky substrate), requiring minimal repair.
- Electrode weight ensured **near-vertical orientation** in the water column even during boat movement, improving consistency of the electrical field for shocking.
- **Diurnal sampling outperformed nocturnal sampling** in Clear Lake, likely attributable to high turbidity reducing net-crew visibility of stunned fish at night.
- Configurable lighting (above- vs. below-water) allowed adaptation of the shocker for night operations depending on local water clarity.
- Multiple **safety mechanisms** were integrated:
  - Bowman's seated position with feet below seat level prevented forward falls during sudden stops.
  - A waterproof, lever-activated kill switch near the bowman allowed immediate disconnection of electrode power.
  - A pull-type plug at the generator enabled the motor operator to cut all power quickly.
  - Standard use of rubber waders, rubber gloves, and wood-handled nets throughout shocking operations for operator protection.
- This design represents an early example of **transportable, crew-efficient [[Electrofishing]] gear**, influencing subsequent small-boat sampling equipment in freshwater fisheries research.
