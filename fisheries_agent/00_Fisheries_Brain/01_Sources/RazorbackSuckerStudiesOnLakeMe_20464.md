# Lake Mead Razorback Sucker Studies: 1996-1997 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report from [[BIO/WEST Inc]] for the [[Southern Nevada Water Authority]] (SNWA) and the [[Nevada Division of Wildlife]] (NDOW) covers the first year (October 1996 to June 1997) of a multi-year study of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mead]]. Unlike other lower Colorado River reservoirs, where populations are dominated by old adults with little recruitment, Lake Mead held a relatively young population. The study aimed to estimate population size, habitat use, life history, and spawning locations using [[Trammel Netting]], [[Sonic Telemetry]], and [[Larval Sampling]].

Population estimates from the [[Capture Program]] and [[Schnabel Estimator]] rose from an initial figure of about 200 adults to roughly 400 to 450 adults, with about 400 at [[Las Vegas Bay]] and 50 to 60 at [[Echo Bay]]. Spawning occurred from mid-February to early May, with peak activity in March and April. Echo Bay produced large larval numbers (4,966 larvae collected at five sites), while Las Vegas Bay yielded few. Of 21 sonic-tagged adults, only 7 remained consistently active through June 1997, raising concerns about [[Tag Expulsion]] and mortality.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is one of four large-river fishes endemic to the Colorado River Basin and is listed as Endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1991). Its decline is linked to mainstem dams, altered flows, water withdrawal, and predation and competition by [[Non-Native Fishes]]. Reservoir populations, such as [[Lake Mohave]], typically consist of aging adults with little recruitment, disappearing about 40 to 50 years after reservoir creation.

[[Lake Mead]] was formed in 1935 when [[Hoover Dam]] closed. Razorback suckers were common in the 1950s and 1960s, declined in the 1970s, and were not collected by NDOW or the Arizona Game and Fish Department from 1980 to 1989. In 1990, NDOW received reports from local guides that fish persisted at Las Vegas Bay and Echo Bay. Subsequent NDOW surveys from 1990 to 1996 captured adults at these sites, most of them PIT-tagged.

Fish collected in the 1990s ranged from 366 mm to 765 mm total length (TL), and the Lake Mead fish showed few signs of the blindness, lesions, and tumors seen in Lake Mohave fish, suggesting a younger population. Two hypotheses were proposed for its origin: (1) fish entering from the Grand Canyon after [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closed in 1963 and cooled the river, and (2) survival and recruitment of fish spawned in the lake by the original population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas.** [[Las Vegas Bay]] includes the marina, the inflow of [[Las Vegas Wash]] (200 to 250 cfs of urban runoff, treated effluent, industrial discharge, and aquifer flow), and the [[Blackbird Point]] spawning area. Depths range from over 80 feet near the marina to 5 to 10 feet in the back of the bay. [[Echo Bay]] lies about 50 miles from Las Vegas Bay, has no permanent inflow (Echo Wash flows only in storms), and has depths of 60 to 80 feet near the marina and 5 to 10 feet at the back.

**Lake level.** Lake elevation rose from about 1190 to 1200 feet during the study, flooding salt cedar (*Tamarix* sp.), willow, bulrush, and cattail, and creating extensive riparian and wetland habitat. Lake level affects spawning depth; the Blackbird Point spawning area was 30 to 50 feet deep in 1992 and 1993 at about 1170 to 1180 feet.

**Spawning habitat.** Echo Bay spawning occurred in shallow water (10 to 20 feet) in sheltered coves near the back of the bay. Las Vegas Bay spawning occurred deeper (40 to 80 feet) mid-bay, where currents from winds and the Las Vegas Wash inflow likely dispersed larvae.

**Movement.** Pre-spawning movement (November to February) involved tagged fish leaving and returning to spawning sites. Las Vegas Bay fish stayed within about 5 miles of the collection site. Echo Bay fish ranged up to 15 miles north to Muddy Bay near the Muddy River mouth, with frequent use of Rogers Bay. The two groups did not overlap in distribution.

## Key Findings

**Population size**
- Initial estimate (NDOW data, 1990 to 1996): about 200 adults, with a conservative figure of 200 used by the Service to limit sonic tagging to 10 percent (20 fish).
- Revised estimate with 1996-1997 data: about 400 adults at Las Vegas Bay (300 to 400 individuals), 50 to 60 at Echo Bay, and 400 to 450 total.
- Confidence intervals remained wide, and additional mark-recapture data were recommended.

**Catch and sampling**
- 61 adults caught (28 at Las Vegas Bay; 33 at Echo Bay) over 134 trammel net nights (95 Las Vegas Bay; 39 Echo Bay).
- Catch rates: 0.29 fish per net night at Las Vegas Bay and 0.85 at Echo Bay. Highest monthly rate occurred in April.
- 21 adults were sonic-tagged (16 with 14-month tags, 4 with 48-month tags).
- Roughly 5,000 larvae were collected (4,966 total in Echo Bay crappie-light sampling), with 3,193 at Site 1 and 1,273 at Site 2.

**Spawning**
- Spawning ran mid-February to early May, peaking in March and April.
- Ripe females were first captured February 25 at Blackbird Point and April 20 at Echo Bay.
- Crappie-light larval sampling at Echo Bay had a peak catch of 9.90 larvae per minute (March 12) and 14.50 per minute (April 4). Larval light traps performed poorly and were discontinued.
- No juvenile razorback suckers were collected, despite seine, minnow trap, hoop/fyke, and gill net sampling.

**Growth**
- Recaptured wild fish grew an average of about 9.6 mm TL per year (range 1.1 to 22.4 mm; 11 fish).
- Including all 13 recaptures, the authors estimated roughly 10 mm per year. This is about three times the 2 to 3 mm per year reported for the Green River and other populations.
- Stocked fish grew about twice as fast as wild fish.

**Tag performance**
- Only 7 of 21 sonic-tagged adults remained consistently active through June 1997; 3 at Echo Bay and 4 at Las Vegas Bay.
- Possible causes included tag expulsion (one tag, 2264, was recovered by SCUBA lying in silt), mortality, movement out of the search area, and signal masking by vegetation or rock. A hatchery study of San Juan River fish reported 30 to 50 percent tag expulsion.
- Recaptured tagged fish showed sutures intact and incisions healing.

**Recommendations for year two**
- Continue monthly trammel netting and sonic tracking.
- Implant 10 additional sonic tags (for a total of 31).
- Continue larval sampling during spawning and juvenile sampling at both sites.
- SNWA funding was expected to support these activities at a level similar to 1996-1997.
