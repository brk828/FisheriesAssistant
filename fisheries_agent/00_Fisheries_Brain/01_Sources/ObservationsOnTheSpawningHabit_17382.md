# Spawning Habits and Fry Production of Centrarchid Fishes in Deep Lake, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1938 field study by W. F. Carbine of the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]] examined spawning behavior and fry production of four centrarchid fishes in [[Deep Lake]], a 14.9-acre lake with no inlet or outlet in [[Oakland County]], [[Michigan]]. Observations ran from May 28 to August 15, 1938. The study counted 648 nests and collected fry from 32 of them, with counts made by direct enumeration. Estimated minimum fry production was 8,338,000 for the whole lake, dominated by [[Bluegill]] (6,610,000). The author states that the data for [[Rock Bass]], [[Common Sunfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] are inadequate and should be treated as comparative only.

## Background & Description

Fisheries management needs accurate figures on eggs, fry and adults produced by natural reproduction, and on losses at each life stage, to predict returns to anglers. The author notes that earlier work left several questions open, including how many females spawn in one nest, whether eggs are laid in one or several spawnings, egg output by female size and age, natural fertilization and survival rates, size and age at maturity, and the effects of temperature, sunlight and wave action.

The study covered four species: [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Common Sunfish]] (*Lepomis gibbosus*), [[Rock Bass]] (*Ambloplites rupestris*), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*; written as *Huro salmoides* in the source). Other fishes in the lake were [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), yellow bullhead (*Ameiurus natalis*) and mud pickerel (*Esox vermiculatus*). The author reports that no other fishes, including small cyprinids or darters, were present.

Deep Lake is roughly oval, about 1,230 ft by 660 ft, with two basins: a western basin 61 ft deep and an eastern basin 51 ft deep, separated by an area averaging about 45 ft. Shoals are narrow. The northern, southern and eastern shoals (10 to 62 ft wide) are mainly sand with patches of gravel, rock, roots and vegetation. The western shoal (18 to 70 ft wide) is mainly fibrous peat over sand and gravel.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Observations were made from a rowboat, mostly in early morning and late afternoon when the water was calmer. Wind and wave action hampered work. Polaroid glasses reduced surface glare, and many nocturnal observations were made with an automobile spotlight and storage battery, since fish were less easily frightened at night. Nests were marked with colored glass marbles, though some fish removed or buried them. Species identity was usually confirmed only when the guarding male was present, because nest form did not reliably separate species.

**Fry collection and counting.** Newly hatched golden fry were siphoned from nests with a glass tube of 0.5 in. inside diameter. Collection took 1 to 4.5 hours per nest, and each nest was revisited at least twice. Rival small sunfish and bluegills preyed on fry, and disturbed or unguarded nests were often unusable. Samples of fry, sand and water (sometimes over 20 gallons per nest) were strained through a plankton net. Fry were then separated from sand by [[Calcium Chloride Flotation]], a modification of Beak (1937). The author judged this method unsuitable where samples contained inert organic material. Counts were made by actual enumeration, since volume and weight estimates were distorted by sand and debris.

**Recovery efficiency.** In one critical sample from a nest with more than 500 cc of sand, 9,781 fry were recovered by repeated water washings, 2,282 after calcium chloride treatment, and 28 by hand picking, for a total of 12,091. Water washing and calcium chloride together recovered 99.8% of fry from that nest. Two additional samples gave 99.8% and 99.7%.

**Nest sites and bottom types.** All four species used sand, gravel, roots and muck, singly or in combination. Largemouth Bass and Rock Bass built single, mostly poorly defined nests, usually at 1 to 3.5 ft. Rock Bass nests were often placed near a stump, rock or weed clump. [[Bluegill]] and [[Common Sunfish]] nested in [[Colonial Nesting]] groups, sometimes at up to 5 ft. Bluegill nests on sand or gravel were about 18 to 24 in. in diameter. Nests on muck were larger and irregular. Fish sometimes took over nests built by other species, and nests were reused within a season. The author suggests the nests were taken over only after the original owners had finished and the fry had left. Because the shoals were narrow, the author could not tell whether fish actively preferred one bottom type. He suggests that spawners may use whatever bottom type is most available.

**Timing.** Spawning was prolonged, which the author attributes to batch spawning by individual females and to staggered ripening among females. Rock Bass showed three spawning cycles. Water temperature alone did not appear to explain the extended season.

**Depth.** Nests in Deep Lake were shallower (1 to 5 ft) than those reported from many other waters (up to 15 ft). The author suggests bottom slope may limit nesting depth, since fish can fan nests more easily over gentle slopes.

**Data discrepancies in the source:**
- The text says 33 Largemouth Bass nests and 58 Rock Bass nests were counted. Table 3 lists 4 and 9 nests examined, and the Summary totals 648 nests, which matches the text counts (33 + 58 + 188 + 369).
- The text gives the Largemouth Bass average as 4,375 across five nests. Table 3 and the Summary give 4,977.
- Table 2 lists five Largemouth Bass nests, but the 21,967 value for nest 2 falls outside the stated maximum of 11,457.
- The author states that the Rock Bass, Common Sunfish and Largemouth Bass figures are inadequate and are included only for comparison.

**Key results:**
- Bluegill produced the most fry per nest (average 17,914, from 17 nests). The author suggests this variation reflects multiple females spawning in some nests.
- Rock Bass produced the fewest fry per nest (average 796). The author suggests a single female per nest is likely, but this cannot be confirmed without egg counts.
- The Summary records 648 nests counted and fry collected from 32 nests of the four species.
- Minimum fry production was estimated by multiplying the average fry per nest by the number of nests observed, then dividing by the lake's 14.9 acres. Figures are rounded to the nearest hundred.
- The author judged the calcium chloride method approximately 99.8% efficient for recovering fry from sand.

**Comparison with other studies:**
- [[Coggeshall]] (1924) reported 11,257 to 224,000 bluegill fry per nest in Winona Lake, averaging 86,631 across four nests. That study estimated 18,467 spawning females and about 36,000 adults, and found only 2,400 nests the same year. Coggeshall attributed the gap to non-functional males. The author notes the weight-based fry counts used there are open to criticism.
- Ulrey, Risk and Scott (1938) counted bluegill ovaries. Two-year-olds averaged 3,820 eggs, three-year-olds 9,264, and four-year-olds 19,169. Deep Lake ovary counts were to be published later.

**Limitations and open questions:** The author notes that nest counts are minimums because of weather interruptions and marble removal by fish. The study did not estimate adult male and female numbers. Whether nests are used by one or several females remains unresolved for Largemouth Bass and Common Sunfish. The author also states that the temperature-only explanation for spawning timing is insufficient.
