# Bonytail and Razorback Sucker Survey of Lake Mohave (1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This cooperative 1989 survey of [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada) sampled [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) with four camps working different reaches of the lake. Objectives were to obtain bonytail broodstock for [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], recapture adult razorback suckers tagged in earlier efforts, tag additional fish, and summarize movement, abundance, growth and stock status. Only one bonytail was caught. Razorback suckers were recaptured and tagged in quantity, and the authors estimated the adult population in the tens of thousands. Catch statistics suggested a stable stock, but there was no evidence of recruitment to the adult population, so the authors warned that a decline could be rapid and hard to detect.

## Background & Description

- **Bonytail:** Sampling in March 1989 yielded a single bonytail, taken by [[Trammel Netting]] at [[Cottonwood Cove]] and transferred to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and then to Dexter. The fish was lost from an outdoor pond before drainage in April 1989. ASU personnel separately collected 5 bonytail in May 1989 from the same area, which reportedly were doing well at Dexter.
- **Razorback sucker:** A long-lived catostomid historically concentrated along the eastern shoreline of [[Arizona Bay]]. The 1989 work built on tagging programs running since the early 1980s. More than 2,000 fish were marked over the previous decade. Earlier tags included green floy and Carlin tags, yellow floy tags, and hole-punched fin marks.
- **Participating agencies:** U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Bureau of Land Management, U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, Nevada and Arizona state fish and wildlife agencies, Arizona State University, Northern Arizona University, University of Arizona, University of Nevada Las Vegas, and private individuals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area:** Four camps worked [[Little Basin]] (including Arizona Bay), upper Cottonwood Basin ([[Carp Cove]] area), upper Cottonwood Basin ([[Six-Mile Coves]] area, Nevada), and lower Cottonwood Basin ([[Arrowhead Cove]] area). Methods were [[Trammel Netting]], [[Electrofishing]] (boat-mounted) and [[Beach Seining]] along Arizona Bay.

**Community composition (trammel netting, March 1989):** 908 fish were collected. [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) dominated with 378 fish (41.6%). [[Rainbow Trout]] was next most abundant, concentrated in Little Basin. [[Largemouth Bass]] was notably scarce in lower Cottonwood Basin. Striped bass were targeted by gill netting in Eldorado Canyon because they were feeding on newly stocked catchable trout. Razorback suckers were only a minor share of trammel catches but were the target of seining and electrofishing.

**Catch effort (electrofishing):** 577 razorback suckers were captured in 435.5 minutes. Catch per unit effort (CPE) was highest in Little Basin and Six-Mile Coves, intermediate at Carp Cove, and lowest in lower Cottonwood Basin. The authors attributed much of the variation to sampling efficiency and habitat differences.

**Habitat:** Sampling efficiency varied with depth and structure, and spawning aggregations occur along shorelines in November to March.

## Key Findings

**Bonytail**
- One wild bonytail was collected (March 1989); the 6 fish held at Dexter were reduced to 5 in the wild-caught group reported by ASU.

**Razorback sucker catches and tagging**
- Beach seining at Arizona Bay captured 111 razorback suckers.
- Electrofishing captured 577 razorback suckers in 435.5 minutes.
- Roughly 810–820 razorback suckers were tagged in March 1989 (the OCR text is partly garbled). Recaptures from the same operation and earlier tagging programs were also recorded.
- Only one of the 1981 Carp Cove tagged fish was ever recaptured. Fish tagged by UNLV in 1983 produced few recaptures, which the authors attributed to low numbers.

**Movement**
- Short-term (days to weeks): most recaptures were at or near the release site. Of 19 fish recaptured 0.8–6 days after tagging in March 1988, 16 were at or near their capture site.
- Long-term (about 1 year): 24 of the fish tagged in March 1988 were recaptured. About half were outside their original tagging area. Fish from Carp Cove moved up to 6.4 km across the basin or downlake more than 25 km.
- Of 44 fish recaptured after a year or more, 15 (34%) were in the same area as initial capture, and most others were not.
- Documented moves included downlake averages of about 9 km (5–13 km, n=7) and uplake averages of about 18 km (n=8).

**Population abundance**
- Multiple estimation methods (Petersen single-census and multiple-census Schnabel, Schnabel-type and Schumacher formulas) gave results the authors considered statistically indistinguishable in the multiple-census approach.
- The reported overall estimates ranged from about 100,000 to 115,000 fish, with a best estimate near 60,000. Confidence intervals were wide.
- The authors stated that the estimates were likely conservative, because they represented only the available spawning stock, and that there is no population estimate without violated assumptions.

**Growth and size**
- Mean total length of adults: females 63.9 cm (1988) and 61.4 cm (1989) in trammel nets; males 56.9 cm and 57.2 cm.
- The smallest female collected was 44.6 cm TL (ripe), from Six-Mile Coves. It may have been young (otoliths suggest such fish could be about 4 years old) or slow-growing and much older.
- Tagged fish showed mean net length change of about +0.1 cm over 74–437 days. Growth is too slow to measure directly, possibly around 1 mm per year.
- Otolith analyses (McCarthy and Minckley 1987) found fish averaging over 32 years of age and individuals up to 44 years old, so the species is very long-lived.

**Stock status**
- Catch per unit effort and proportion of razorbacks in catch increased from the 1975–1981 period (mean CPE 2.69, n=8) to 1983–1989 (mean CPE 5.24, n=19), but without a clear upward trend.
- The authors concluded the population had remained stable over the past decade.
- No juvenile razorback suckers have been caught in about 15 years. A single seine capture of 4 small fish in 1987 is the only juvenile record since the 1950s, and one small female caught in 1989 may or may not have been young.
- Recruitment could not be confirmed, so the authors judged that a rapid decline was possible.

**Errors and data quality**
- Tag loss was thought to be small but unquantified.
- Length measurement errors (different techniques, rounding to the nearest cm, untrained personnel) produced implausible recapture differences, including one of 7.5 cm, that compromised parts of the dataset.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Continued tagging and recapture were recommended for several more years to refine the population estimate.
- The authors stated that close monitoring should continue, since a substantial decline might go unnoticed until it was far advanced. Management action would then need immediate implementation.
