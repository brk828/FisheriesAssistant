# Evaluation of an Electronic Method of Measuring Hematocrits of Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-physiology #hematology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1966 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service publication evaluates the **YSI Model 30 Electronic Hematocrit**, a portable conductivity-based instrument, as an alternative or complement to the traditional **microhematocrit (centrifuge) technique** for assessing fish blood [[Hematocrit]] values. The study, conducted at the Fish Control Laboratory in La Crosse, Wisconsin, tested reproducibility and comparability of the two methods across twelve fish species, and examined how blood [[Electrolyte Concentration]] and [[Serum Protein]] levels influence electronic conductivity readings. The authors conclude that while the electronic method is rapid and portable, it consistently underestimates hematocrit relative to the centrifuge method, requires species-specific correction factors, and loses linear reliability at hematocrits below 25%. It may nonetheless serve as a rapid field-screening tool for detecting anemia in fish.

## Background & Description

While this is primarily a methodological/physiological paper rather than a morphological description, several blood cell characteristics relevant to instrument performance are noted:

- **Erythrocyte size**: Fish red blood cells are generally **larger and differently shaped** compared to mammalian erythrocytes (citing Smith 1952; Hawk et al. 1954; Mott 1957).
- **Surface-area-to-volume ratio**: Larger erythrocytes are hypothesized to have **less surface area per unit volume**, which may reduce the insulating effect within the electronic hematocrit's glass cell — functionally mimicking a lower cell count and contributing to systematically low electronic readings.
- **Erythrocyte membrane**: The instrument's operating principle relies on the **insulating characteristics of the erythrocyte membrane**, which separates conductive intracellular fluid from conductive serum.
- **Erythrocyte behavior in vitro**: Red cells of salmonids were observed to **coagulate in sodium chloride solutions lacking anticoagulant** (Wolf 1959); residual heparin in this study likely prevented such coagulation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

This study is laboratory/physiological in nature rather than ecological, but notes relevant environmental and biological context:

- **Specimen sourcing**: Salmonids ([[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Lake Trout]]) and [[Goldfish]] were obtained from state/national hatcheries; other species ([[Shovelnose Sturgeon]], [[Spotted Sucker]], [[Northern Redhorse]], [[Brown Bullhead]], [[White Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Walleye]]) were wild-collected from the **Mississippi River**.
- **Acclimation**: All fish were held in **flowing well water** at the Fish Control Laboratory for a minimum of two months prior to testing, standardizing physiological condition.
- **Osmoregulatory context**: Blood freezing points of freshwater teleosts range from **-0.49° to -0.67°C**, corresponding to salines of approximately 0.80–1.15% NaCl (Black, 1957) — relevant to interpreting how blood [[Osmoregulation]] and electrolyte balance affect conductivity-based hematocrit readings.
- **Biological variability drivers**: Serum protein levels in fish are noted to vary with **sex, reproductive condition, diet, disease, and oxygen depletion** (Booke, 1964), factors with direct ecological/physiological relevance to field application of the method.

## Key Findings

### Reproducibility
- Centrifuge (microhematocrit) method was **more reproducible** than the electronic method across 7 species tested (Shovelnose Sturgeon, Spotted Sucker, Northern Redhorse, Brown Bullhead, White Bass, Largemouth Bass, Walleye).
- Electronic hematocrits were **consistently lower** than centrifuge values, with greater standard deviations.
- Differences between methods were statistically significant (p < 0.01) for all species tested.

### Comparability Across Species
- Mean centrifuge hematocrit values: Rainbow Trout 40.4%, Brown Trout 33.0%, Brook Trout 37.1%, Lake Trout 35.7%, Goldfish 42.8%.
- Electronic hematocrits were **20–60% lower** than centrifuge values depending on species.
- **Regression-based correction** was found preferable to a fixed mean-difference correction, since the discrepancy between methods scales with hematocrit magnitude.
- [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] shared statistically similar regression correction factors (overlapping 95% CI), suggesting a shared correction factor is feasible for these salmonids; other species required separate corrections.

### Low Hematocrit / Anemia Detection
- Below a centrifuge hematocrit of approximately 25%, electronic readings **did not change proportionately** and could not be reliably corrected via regression.
- Simulated anemia (via plasma dilution of Rainbow Trout erythrocytes) produced electronic readings of **4–6 units** at centrifuge hematocrits between 10–25%, with readings increasing again below 10%.
- Despite unreliability for precise quantification, low electronic readings (4–6) may serve as a **gross, rapid indicator of anemia** in the field.

### Time-Dependent Conductivity Drift
- Repeated ("second") readings on the same sample showed conductivity **rising to a peak within 3–7 minutes**, then declining — attributed to erythrocyte **sedimentation** and uneven distribution between electrodes.
- Second readings produced **smaller correction factors and lower standard errors** than first readings but required pre-heparinized capillary tubes, sacrificing the speed advantage of the method.

### Effects of Electrolytes and Protein
- Electronic hematocrit was **inversely proportional** to sodium chloride (NaCl) concentration and **directly proportional** to protein concentration (Rainbow Trout and Carp tested).
- Across NaCl concentrations of 0.65–1.00%, electrical readings deviated from **+4.5 to -27.5 hematocrit units** relative to centrifuge values.
- Protein effect was strongest at high salinity (1% NaCl: +7.0 to +9.5 unit increase) and weaker at low salinity (0.65% NaCl: +2.5 to +5.5 unit increase).
- Species differences in conductivity (Rainbow Trout vs. Carp) were relatively minor.
- Results diverged from human blood findings of Kernen et al. (1961), possibly due to differences in **erythrocyte size** between fish and mammals, or methodological differences in cell suspension preparation.

### Practical/Instrumental Notes
- The YSI Model 30 Electronic Hematocrit is portable (2.8 lbs), battery-powered (6V mercury cell), temperature-compensated (50–105°F), and requires only approximately 0.02 cc of blood.
- Cleaning the glass measurement cell between samples (rinsing, acetone drying) was time-consuming; residual dried/clotted blood altered effective sample volume and introduced error.
- Instrument may have secondary utility for **estimating blood electrolyte/plasma conductivity levels** independent of hematocrit, based on correspondence between plasma conductivity and known saline concentrations (approximately 0.86% NaCl equivalent).

### Conclusions
- The electronic method is a **useful complement**, not a full replacement, for centrifuge-based hematocrit determination in fish.
- Best suited for hematocrits >25%, with appropriate species-specific correction.
- Confounding variables — [[Serum Protein]], [[Electrolyte Concentration]], and erythrocyte size — must be considered when interpreting field-collected electronic hematocrit data.
- Potential value as a **rapid anemia screening tool** in field conditions where centrifugation is impractical.
