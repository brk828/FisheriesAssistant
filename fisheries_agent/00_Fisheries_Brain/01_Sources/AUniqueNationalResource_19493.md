# National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine: Institutional Overview

#science-policy #research-governance #national-academies

## Executive Summary

This 1994 booklet describes the private, non-profit institution created by Congress in 1863 to advise the federal government on science and technology. The institution now comprises four organizations: the [[National Academy of Sciences]] (NAS), the [[National Academy of Engineering]] (NAE), the [[Institute of Medicine]] (IOM), and the [[National Research Council]] (NRC). Its work is produced largely by volunteer expert committees, operates independently of its sponsors, and is funded mostly by federal agencies. The document is institutional promotion and procedural description, not research. It contains no fish, aquatic species, or ecological data. The only marine-related content is a single fast-track study on [[Atlantic Bluefin Tuna]] stocks.

## Background & Description

**Founding and charter.** The [[Act of Incorporation (1863)]], signed by [[Abraham Lincoln]] on March 3, 1863, named 50 charter members and established service to the nation as the institution's dominant purpose. The Academy was required to advise government departments on request, without compensation beyond actual expenses.

**Expansion.** During World War I, the limited membership (about 150 at that time) could not meet demand for advice. In 1916 the NAS created the NRC at the request of President Wilson to draw in specialists from the wider scientific community. Later additions were:
- NAE, established in 1964 under the NAS charter.
- IOM, formed in 1967 and becoming an Academy institution in 1970.

**Membership.** Members are elected by peers. Current counts are about 1,800 NAS members, about 1,700 NAE members, and about 1,000 IOM members. The three bodies also elect foreign associates. The booklet states that more than 4,000 members are selected across the institution.

**Executive orders.** Presidential orders from [[Woodrow Wilson]] (post-WWI), [[Dwight Eisenhower]] (1956), and [[George Bush]] (January 19, 1993, Executive Order 12832) affirmed and updated the NRC charter. The 1993 order allows non-competitive acquisition of services by the Academy when it is the only source with the necessary expertise, independence, and objectivity.

## Key Findings

**1. Operating model**
- About 645 committees involving approximately 6,400 volunteer individuals work during a single year.
- Committee members come from academe (about 63%), industry (23%), non-profit institutions (8%), and government at all levels (6%).
- Projects are funded by federal agencies (about 85% of work) and by private foundations, corporations, state and local governments, and endowment income (about 15%).
- Industry contributions with a potential financial interest in an outcome may not exceed 50% of a project's total cost.
- No fees beyond actual project costs are charged.

**2. Study types and timelines**
- *Consensus reports* are the most common product. Complex studies usually take 18 to 24 months.
- *Fast-track studies* can be completed in as little as four months, but not all projects qualify.
- *Surveys of the disciplines* identify research priorities looking a decade ahead.
- *Oversight panels* review federal programs. Examples include the long-running review of the [[National Institute of Standards and Technology]] (begun 1959), panels advising [[NASA]] after the [[Space Shuttle Challenger]] explosion, and the review of the [[Partnership for a New Generation of Vehicles]] (PNGV), which aimed for fuel efficiencies up to three times those of contemporary vehicles.
- *Meetings and roundtables* include the [[Frontiers of Science]] symposium series (begun 1989, for scientists aged 45 and under) and the [[Government-University-Industry Research Roundtable]] (established 1984).

**3. Administrative programs**
- The [[Radiation Effects Research Foundation]] has conducted health follow-up of more than 100,000 atomic-bomb survivors from Hiroshima and Nagasaki since 1947.
- The Office of Scientific and Engineering Personnel administers fellowships and associateships. Associates spend up to three years in federal laboratories.
- The [[Transportation Research Board]] oversees transportation research and two cooperative programs for highways and transit.

**4. Education and international activities**
- The NRC coordinated development of national standards for K-12 science education.
- The [[National Science Resources Center]], a joint venture with the [[Smithsonian Institution]], promotes hands-on elementary science curricula.
- The 1993 [[Population Summit of the World's Scientific Academies]] in New Delhi produced a joint statement signed by 60 academies calling for urgent action on population growth.
- International capacity-building work has included counterpart groups in China, India, Indonesia, Russia, Germany, and Japan.

**5. Independence and process safeguards**
- Projects are initiated by Congress, federal agencies, private sponsors, or internally by institutional volunteers.
- Executive committee review asks whether a study is amenable to science analysis, is of national importance, and needs the institution's unique capabilities.
- Committee selection is the sole responsibility of the institution. Sponsors may suggest members but do not choose them.
- Deliberative sessions are closed to protect independence. Sponsors may not oversee projects and have no role in report review.
- Every report undergoes anonymous review by experts who did not write it, overseen by the Report Review Committee.
- The institution is not a federal agency and is not subject to the [[Freedom of Information Act]] or the [[Federal Advisory Committee Act]].

**6. Notable outcomes cited**
- The 1986 AIDS study, funded from endowment income, is credited with prompting accelerated federal action.
- Recommendations from the 1983 risk assessment report were adopted by the [[Environmental Protection Agency]].
- The 1988 [[Human Genome]] report provided a blueprint for the Human Genome Project.
- The IOM's 1992 emerging infections report led to a federal disease surveillance program.
- The Committee on Human Rights has contributed to the release of hundreds of imprisoned scientists, engineers, and health professionals.
