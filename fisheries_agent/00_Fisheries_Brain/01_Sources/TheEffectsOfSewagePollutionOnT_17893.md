# Effects of Sewage Pollution on the Fish Population of a Midwestern Stream

#ichthyology #aquatic-ecology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how partially treated domestic sewage from the Wilmington, Ohio treatment plant affects the fish community of [[Lytle Creek]], a small stream in Clinton County, Ohio. Monthly seine collections over roughly two years (May 1950 to July 1952) recorded 11,235 fish from 38 species. Fish numbers and species richness rose steadily with distance downstream from the outfall. No fish were found at the outfall or in the septic zone, while the clean-water station downstream supported 36 species. The authors conclude that no single species is a reliable pollution indicator, that darters and black bass are the most pollution-sensitive taxa, and that dissolved oxygen is only one of several factors controlling fish survival in polluted streams.

## Background & Description

The authors' earlier work on [[Lytle Creek]] addressed the physical, chemical and invertebrate effects of organic pollution (Gaufin and Tarzwell, 1952). This paper focuses on fish species composition, relative abundance and distribution.

**Stream characteristics**
- Located about 45 miles northeast of [[Cincinnati]], flowing into Todd's Fork of the [[Little Miami River]] system.
- Permanent stream about 11 miles long, 3 to 35 feet wide at low water, draining about 27 square miles.
- Average gradient of 25 feet per mile. Stream runs through Wisconsin glacial till, with a short Ordovician limestone section about 3 miles from the mouth.
- Principal flow sources are agricultural drainage and the effluent of the [[Wilmington Sewage Treatment Plant]].

**Pollution source**
- The plant serves about 7,000 inhabitants of a non-industrial community.
- Treatment is by [[Chemical Precipitation]], which removes most solids and cuts [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]] by about 50 percent.
- Effluent contains little or no dissolved oxygen. At normal low flows it makes up 80 to 90 percent of stream volume, producing septic conditions below the outlet.
- Lytle Creek was chosen because it has a single major pollution source, no permanent tributaries below the outfall, and a wide range of pollution conditions.

**Sampling stations** (miles above mouth)
- 8.7 (upper clean-water zone), 7.6 (intermittent pollution), 7.2 (sewage outlet), 6.5 (septic zone), 5.2 and 4.2 (recovery zone), 2.8 (lower recovery zone), 1.0 (clean-water zone).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods**
- Monthly collections from May 1950 to July 1951, omitting December 1950 and January 1951 (ice or flood). Collections resumed February 1952 and ended July 1952, giving 20 monthly collections.
- Two seines with 14-inch-square mesh: a straight seine (15 ft by 5 ft) and a bag seine (35 ft by 6 ft). Both pools and riffles were sampled to capture species with different habitat preferences.
- Effort was roughly equal across stations, but stations 5.2 and 4.2 required longer searches because fish were so scarce.
- The authors describe the collections as only roughly quantitative and not suited to rigorous statistics. They argue that the large differences between stations make sampling error relatively unimportant.

**Hydrology and water chemistry**
- Winter and spring flows averaged about 6 to 7 cubic feet per second, with floods of 40 cubic feet per second or more. Late summer and fall flow dropped to about 1 cubic foot per second.
- 24-hour series recorded dissolved oxygen, pH and temperature hourly at several stations (summarized in Table 1).
- Summer water temperatures reached 87°F in the pollution zone.
- Nocturnal oxygen fell low in the recovery zone, while daytime maxima reached about 18 ppm.

**Benthic context**
- Tolerant insects, especially Diptera, were abundant in the septic zone (Gaufin and Tarzwell, 1952).
- In winter, sewage fungi, bacteria and protozoans spread across the stream bottom to station 2.8, possibly crowding out invertebrate food.

## Key Findings

**Overall community**
- Total catch: 11,235 fish, 6 families, 38 species across the 20 monthly collections.
- Species present and numbers rose progressively downstream from the outfall (Figure 1, Table 3).

- The abstract reports the lower recovery-zone station as 4.4 miles below the effluent, while the body text names it Station 2.8.
- Station 1.0 had 21 species on two occasions (March 1951, February 1952) but only 8 in July 1950. The authors could not determine whether this reflected sucker spawning migrations or incomplete captures.

**Pollution indicators**
- No single species can serve as a positive pollution indicator. The [[Creek Chub]] was the most common fish in polluted zones, but it was also abundant in clean downstream reaches.
- Several species tolerated pollution, but none showed a preference for polluted water.
- Few species in a poor station are not enough to conclude poor habitat. Unpolluted reference stations must be sampled to establish the normal population.
- Total species number and relative abundance are the most useful measures of stream condition.

**Sensitive taxa: darters and black bass**
- [[Darters]] made up 38 percent of the population at station 8.7, 4.7 percent at 7.6, 1.9 percent at 2.8 and 1.2 percent at 1.0. Six darter species were found at 1.0.
- [[Black Bass]] were absent from stations 5.2 and 4.2, present in small numbers at 2.8 (4 fish), and abundant at 1.0 (72 fish). Spawning apparently succeeded at 1.0 in spring 1951.
- Darters and bass are sensitive and disappear first under pollution. Their presence indicates that organic pollution is not a major limiting factor, although their scattered distribution limits their usefulness as indicators.
- The authors recommend using total fish species counts as the main indicator of pollution degree.

**Dissolved oxygen and distribution**
- Winter: flows above 6 cubic feet per second, low temperatures and near-saturated oxygen at all stations. Chemical criteria would suggest no harmful pollution, yet fish did not recolonize the areas they avoided in summer. Stations 5.2 and 4.2 had no fish in February and March 1952.
- Summer: low flow and warm water created persistent pollution zones. Oxygen was an important factor here.
- Station 2.8 supported a mixed fauna of over 30 species despite night-time oxygen falling well below 5 ppm for several hours. In May and August 1951 oxygen reached 1.6 ppm there, yet fish were abundant.
- Station 4.2 had a minimum nocturnal oxygen of 1.0 ppm in May 1951 but produced its best collection of the series that month.
- The authors suggest fish can survive severe night-time oxygen declines if daytime levels are adequate. A minimum oxygen level is still necessary, but mixed fish communities occurred where oxygen often stayed below 5 ppm for several hours.

**Conclusions**
- Sewage pollution produces clear zones: septic (no fish), recovery (sparse and sporadic fish) and clean-water (diverse fish).
- Dissolved oxygen is only one of several factors affecting fish survival in polluted streams. Food supply, temperature, flow and seasonal movement also matter.
- The authors caution that the number of species in unpolluted streams varies widely with local physical and chemical conditions, so comparisons need local reference data.
