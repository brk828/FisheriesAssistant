# Bylas Springs Gila Topminnow Habitat Renovation Project (Spring S1)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This USFWS project report (June 29, 2000) documents the renovation of Spring S1, known as "Tusidugihalen" ("Hot Spring"), at [[Bylas Springs]] on the [[San Carlos Apache Reservation]]. The goal was to restore habitat for the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] by removing invasive [[Mosquitofish]] and reintroducing topminnows from the nearby S3 population. The work was a partnership led by the [[Arizona Fishery Resources Office]] (AZFRO) of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], with the San Carlos Apache Tribe and other agencies. The project budget was $30,000. Site work began March 27, 2000, and monitoring was planned for the following year.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) is a small poeciliid fish endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico. It also occurs in the Ríos Gila and Concepción in Sonora, Mexico. It was once among the most abundant fishes in the lower Colorado River watershed. The [[Endangered Species Listing]] for the species dates to 1967. Population declines were attributed to predation by the introduced [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish) and to habitat degradation.

Bylas Springs is a complex of three small springs (S1, S2, S3) adjacent to the Gila River. The Bylas population is one of 10 remaining natural populations in the United States. The [[Gila Topminnow Revised Recovery Plan]] (1998) identifies it as the genetic source for all future reintroductions in the Gila River basin above [[Coolidge Dam]].

**Population history at Bylas Springs:**
- **Discovery (1968):** Gila topminnow were first documented at the springs.
- **1980:** A Gila River flood allowed mosquitofish to invade S1 and S3. Later renovation attempts failed.
- **S2:** Extirpated by low water and habitat loss from invasive [[Salt Cedar]]. It was renovated in 1996, and by that year S3 had been completely taken over by mosquitofish.
- **April 29, 1998:** Topminnows from S1 were moved to S3, where the population was thriving.
- **2000:** Topminnows at S3 were barely holding on, with mosquitofish making up more than 99% of the fish community.

**Historical habitat at S1:** Elders of the San Carlos Apache Cultural Advisory Council described the riparian area as dense with grass and large mesquites, with a large area of standing water above the spring. No salt cedar (*Tamarix chinensis*) or rushes (*Scirpus* spp.) were present.

**Project objectives (seven):**
1. Remove fencing around the spring head and fish barrier.
2. Remove introduced plants, mainly salt cedar.
3. Construct an additional fish barrier against mosquitofish reinvasion from the Gila River.
4. Plant native vegetation along disturbed stream sections.
5. Renovate the spring to remove mosquitofish.
6. Reintroduce Gila topminnow from S3 stocks.
7. Monitor the topminnow population and system response.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Gila topminnow at Bylas Springs were found along the margins of shallow pools and in deeply incised spring heads. Each habitat type was maintained by relatively high disturbance regimes. The report links this to the historical account of more standing water at S1 and to habitat-use data from recent [[Yaqui Topminnow]] studies (Maes 1995).

The main threats were:
- **Predation and competition** from [[Gambusia Affinis]], which reinvaded after flooding and survived earlier renovation attempts.
- **Habitat loss** from invasive [[Salt Cedar]], which contributed to low water conditions.

The project used an [[Ecosystem Approach]] and [[Riparian Restoration]] to address both threats, with the aim of creating a self-sustaining refuge.

## Key Findings

**Site work at S1 (March–April 2000):**
- **Salt cedar removal:** Trees were cut by chainsaw and chemically treated. The herbicide Arsenal was applied directly to the stumps immediately after cutting. Over 17 days of cutting, 792 stumps were counted and 700 linear yards of riparian corridor were cleared. Twelve western diamondback rattlesnakes ([[Western Diamondback Rattlesnake]]) were also recorded during clearing.
- **Brush disposal:** Brush piles were burned with help from San Carlos Apache fire crews.
- **Fish barrier:** The stream was diverted and a backhoe removed vegetation and sediment upstream of the failed barrier site. The new barrier combined:
  - 3'×3'×12' gabion baskets as wing walls, stabilizing 108 feet of bank on both sides
  - 54 feet of 4'×8' cement wall extending the weir above the high-water mark
  - A 13.5'×21' cement apron to stop percolation under the wing walls
- **Piscicide treatment:** After the barrier was completed, the stream was returned to its original channel and treated to remove mosquitofish. Backpack sprayers were set at 50-yard intervals along the stream. The toxicant used is noted in the report but its name is not legible in the source.
- **Revegetation:** More than 100 cottonwood and willow poles collected from the San Carlos River basin were planted along the S1 stream course.

**Status and next steps:**
- As of the report date, S1 was fishless. A survey was scheduled for fall 2000 to confirm the renovation's success before topminnows were reintroduced from S3.
- Monitoring will be conducted quarterly for the first year, measuring population dynamics, population integrity, and water quality. The [[San Carlos Apache Recreation and Wildlife Department]] will hold the data, which is treated as proprietary.
- Partners included the Arizona Game and Fish Department ([[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]), Bureau of Indian Affairs, Water Technology Inc., and the EPA Region 9 grant program.
