# Lake Mohave Sampling, March 1997

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #razorback-sucker #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This report summarizes a fish monitoring survey conducted by Arizona State University's Center for Environmental Studies in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada border) during 16–22 March 1997. The primary objective was to monitor the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) population, with secondary interest in stocked [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). Sampling employed six 91-m trammel nets deployed across depths of 1–10 m in the Cottonwood East, Carp Cove, and Bill Gayes Cove areas, plus three 150-ft trammel nets set in the Yuma Cove backwater targeting stocked juveniles. A total of nine fish species were collected, and capture-recapture data were used to assess stocking success and population status of the two focal native species.

## Background & Description
- **Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**: Adults captured ranged in total length (TL) from **472–671 mm**. One juvenile from Yuma Cove measured **221 mm**, well below the releasable size threshold of 250 mm.
- **Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*)**: One adult captured near Bill Gayes Cove (size not specified in text, tagged individual). Two juveniles from Yuma Cove measured **143 mm** and **152 mm**, both below releasable size.
- Size-based distinctions were used operationally to separate "releasable" (>250 mm) versus non-releasable juvenile [[Native Colorado River Fish|native fish]], with non-releasable individuals returned untagged to their capture location.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat use**: Native catostomids and cyprinids ([[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]]) were found in main-lake embayments (Cottonwood East, Carp Cove, Bill Gayes Cove) as adults, while juveniles of both species occupied the **Yuma Cove backwater**, consistent with the known importance of low-velocity backwater habitats as nursery areas for early life stages of Colorado River native fishes.
- **Community composition**: The broader fish assemblage sampled was dominated by non-native species — [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) — reflecting the heavily modified, reservoir-based fish community typical of the lower [[Colorado River]] system, where native species persist alongside an introduced sportfish and forage fish assemblage.
- **Depth distribution**: Trammel nets set at 1–10 m depths captured adult native suckers and chub, indicating use of near-shore to moderate-depth littoral zones.
- **Sampling effort**: Total effort equaled 60.06 units (1 unit = 100 m² of netting fished for 24 hours), providing a standardized basis for catch-per-unit-effort (CPE) calculations reported in Table 1.

## Key Findings
- **34 adult razorback suckers** were captured (TL 472–671 mm); **22 (65%)** were previously tagged recaptures, indicating a substantial portion of the adult population is part of an ongoing monitoring/tagging program.
- Recapture histories showed razorback suckers originally **stocked in 1992 (n=1)** and **1995 (n=3)**, demonstrating multi-year survival and persistence of stocked cohorts in [[Lake Mohave]].
- Only **one bonytail chub** was captured in the main lake (near Bill Gayes Cove), originally tagged in **1992**, suggesting very low detection/survival rates or low abundance of this species relative to razorback sucker in the open lake.
- **Yuma Cove backwater** sampling yielded 1 razorback sucker (221 mm) and 2 bonytail chub (143, 152 mm) — all sub-releasable size juveniles, supporting the cove's function as a nursery/rearing habitat for stocked native fish.
- Nine total fish species were documented in the lake-wide catch, underscoring continued co-occurrence of endangered native [[Catostomidae|catostomids]] and [[Cyprinidae|cyprinids]] with an established non-native fish community.
- Detailed individual capture-history data (Table 2) were present in the original source but were rendered largely illegible/non-extractable due to OCR degradation; only qualitative recapture statistics (tag years, recapture counts) could be reliably extracted from the surrounding narrative text.
