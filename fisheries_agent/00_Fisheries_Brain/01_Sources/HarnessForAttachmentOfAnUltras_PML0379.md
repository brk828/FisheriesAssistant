# Inelastic Harness for Attaching Ultrasonic Transmitters to Red Drum

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This short methods note describes a simple inelastic harness developed for externally attaching an [[Ultrasonic Transmitter]] to the caudal peduncle of [[Red Drum]] (*Sciaenops ocellatus*). The authors, from the [[Whitney Marine Laboratory]] (University of Florida at Marineland), reported that existing attachment procedures were not entirely satisfactory for their planned migration studies. The harness uses a large plastic cable tie, soft tubing cushioning, and small cable ties to hold a [[Smith Root SR 69]] transmitter. Tests in a fiberglass tank, a half-acre pond, and the Intracoastal Waterway showed the fish behaved normally, fed, and were tracked for extended periods, with the harness and transmitter staying in place. The authors argue the method is broadly useful for tracking studies of large fish with rigid tails.

## Background & Description

Small ultrasonic transmitters are increasingly used to study fish movement and behavior in the field. Attachment methods reviewed include stomach insertion ([[Henderson et al. 1966]]), dorsal musculature anchoring, surgical implantation into the peritoneal cavity ([[Hart and Summerfelt 1975]]), and other techniques ([[Ohsumi 1969]]). The suitability of any method depends on fish species and study objectives.

The harness has three components:
1. An inelastic plastic cable tie (about 190 mm long) sized to encircle the caudal peduncle.
2. Sections of soft Tygon tubing (6-mm OD) and soft rubber tubing (12-mm OD, 1.5-mm wall) threaded over the tie as a cushion to minimize abrasion and chafing.
3. Small plastic cable ties to affix the transmitter to the large tie.

The large tie is tightened until snug but unable to slip over the tail; over-tightening that compresses the peduncle requires cutting and replacing the tie. The transmitter sits on the dorsal surface with its axis at a right angle to the fish's long axis. Harnesses are preconstructed so that field adjustments only involve adding or removing tubing, and a harness can be attached in under 5 minutes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Four red drum were used in the trials. Fish were captured by hook and line at Matanzas Inlet and held in captivity for about two months before testing. The Intracoastal Waterway trial illustrates the tidal environment in which the method was applied. Attachment and tracking were compared against earlier methods, including [[Saddle-Type Attachment]] and elastic harnesses ([[Ichihara 1971]]).

## Key Findings

- **Attachment speed and handling:** The procedure takes only a few minutes between capture and release, reducing handling stress.
- **Physiological effects:** No bleeding occurred, and trauma, damage, or weakening of the fish was minimal.
- **Retention:** The harness kept transmitters attached for the full battery life of the transmitters used (SR 69 and SR 69A; lifetimes of 10 and 60 days).
- **Captive behavior:** In a fiberglass tank, two red drum (2.5 and 3.5 kg, reported as 25 and 35 kg in OCR) with dummy transmitters fed normally within 30 minutes. A third fish (about 3 kg) with an active transmitter in the half-acre pond moved actively, fed, and schooled with other fish. It was not snagged on pilings or other obstacles. The harness and inactive transmitter remained attached for more than one month, and the fish continued feeding and behaving normally.
- **Field tracking:** A fourth fish (about 3.2 kg) released into the Intracoastal Waterway on 17 January 1976 was tracked continuously for 8 hours from a boat. In the first 1.5 h it moved about 1.1 km south, against the tidal flow; in the remaining time it moved 1.2 km north, again against the tide. It entered nearly every creek mouth encountered and at nightfall was in a deep hole about 140 m up a creek 100 m from the release point. On day three it was found about 2 km south of release. Tracking ended when the receiver malfunctioned.
- **Comparative assessment:** Elastic harnesses encircling the caudal peduncle caused progressive deterioration of the tail region. The inelastic harness did not, and the authors expect it to suit any large fish with a rigid tail that is markedly broader than the caudal peduncle.
- **Limitations:** The harness was tested only on red drum. The authors note it has not been validated on other species.
