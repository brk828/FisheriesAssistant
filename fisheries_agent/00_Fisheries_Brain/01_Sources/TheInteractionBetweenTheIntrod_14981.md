# Behavioral Interactions Between Introduced Eastern Mosquitofish and Native Iberian Toothcarps

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #Cyprinodontidae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study examines behavioral mechanisms through which the introduced [[Eastern Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia holbrooki*) negatively affects two endangered native Iberian cyprinodontoids: [[Spanish Toothcarp]] (*Aphanius iberus*) and [[Valencia Toothcarp]] (*Valencia hispanica*). Experiments conducted across three seasonal periods (January, April, July 1996) at the Centro de Experimentación Piscícola de El Palmar, Valencia, Spain, revealed that mosquitofish presence significantly increased aggression received by both native species, reduced feeding rates, and disrupted reproductive behavior. Effects varied with water temperature, fish size, reproductive condition, and species-specific habitat preferences. *V. hispanica* showed more pronounced negative responses than *A. iberus*, potentially due to greater vertical habitat overlap with mosquitofish. The study provides first direct experimental evidence of behavioral interference mechanisms contributing to the decline of these endangered species.

## Background & Description

The [[Eastern Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia holbrooki*) was artificially introduced worldwide for mosquito control but has been implicated in declines of numerous native fish species. In the Iberian Peninsula, two endemic cyprinodontoids have experienced severe population reductions coinciding with mosquitofish expansion: *Aphanius iberus* (Cyprinodontidae) and *Valencia hispanica* (Valenciidae). Both species are sexually dimorphic, with females typically larger than males. *A. iberus* demonstrates greater euryhalinity than *V. hispanica*, tolerating salinities that exclude mosquitofish.

The experimental subjects came from captive breeding stocks maintained in semi-natural outdoor ponds at El Palmar. Fish were held in 96-L aquaria for at least one week before experiments, fed live *Daphnia* spp. and commercial food ad libitum, then starved 24 hours prior to trials. Experiments used 28-L aquaria (45 × 22 × 28 cm) with gravel substrate and controlled temperature matching outdoor conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study encompassed three seasonal series representing different thermal regimes: S1 (January, 14°C), S2 (April, 22°C), and S3 (July, 26°C). This temperature range reflects natural conditions these species experience. Habitat degradation (marshland draining, eutrophication, water extraction, pollution, flow alteration) has occurred alongside mosquitofish expansion in Spain, making it difficult to separate effects of habitat loss from species interactions.

*A. iberus* populations persist in more saline environments that provide refuge from mosquitofish, which cannot tolerate high salinity. Spatial segregation within coastal marsh systems shows mosquitofish dominating freshwater habitats while *A. iberus* occupies saline zones. *V. hispanica* is restricted to freshwater groundwater outflows within littoral marshes where mosquitofish are absent, making it more vulnerable.

Vertical habitat segregation was observed in experimental aquaria: *A. iberus* occupied lower water column positions (mean 9.4 ± 1.1 cm from bottom) compared to mosquitofish (13.3 ± 1.2 cm) and *V. hispanica* (14.2 ± 1.6 cm). This vertical partitioning may reduce interaction frequency between *A. iberus* and mosquitofish.

## Key Findings

**Adult *A. iberus* Responses:**
- Received significantly more aggression (chases and nips) in mosquitofish presence, with >90% of aggressive acts performed by mosquitofish
- Greatest aggression increase occurred in July without live prey (M/S3/NF treatment)
- No significant effect on feeding rates (number of *Daphnia* captured)
- Seasonal increase in prey consumption: S1 to S3 (F₂,₁₂₉ = 12.54, P < 0.0001)
- Males performed more orientations and aggressive acts than females, peaking in April during reproductive period
- Low water temperatures (January) produced near-complete absence of agonistic behavior

**Adult *V. hispanica* Responses:**
- Received significantly more aggression from mosquitofish (>95% of aggressive acts)
- Peak aggression in April without prey and with mosquitofish present
- Performed more aggression themselves in July with mosquitofish and prey (81% directed toward mosquitofish)
- Significant reduction in feeding rates with mosquitofish present (F₃,₁₂₉ = 6.13, P < 0.0007)
- Seasonal increase in prey consumption from January to July (F₁,₇₉ = 36.58, P < 0.0001)
- Courtship behavior (orientations) disrupted by presence of additional fish, replaced by aggression
- More pronounced negative effects than *A. iberus*, attributed to greater vertical habitat overlap with mosquitofish

**Young-of-the-Year (YOY) Responses:**

*A. iberus* YOY:
- Received highest aggression from adult mosquitofish (significant vs. other treatments, P < 0.025)
- Suppressed aggressive behavior in presence of larger fish (adult mosquitofish, adult conspecifics, *V. hispanica* YOY)
- Reduced feeding rates with adult conspecifics and adult mosquitofish present (P < 0.017 to P < 0.005)
- Predation by mosquitofish observed (trials discontinued and replaced)
- Adult *V. hispanica* consumed all *A. iberus* YOY in mixed trials (treatment excluded from analysis)

*V. hispanica* YOY:
- Increased aggression with conspecific YOY (reciprocal interactions)
- Received more aggression from adult mosquitofish and adult conspecifics
- Reduced feeding rates with all additional fish present except adult *A. iberus* (P < 0.024)
- No predation by mosquitofish observed (size difference: *V. hispanica* YOY ~50% larger than *A. iberus* YOY)

**Modulating Factors:**
- **Temperature**: Low temperatures (14°C) suppressed all agonistic behavior; effects intensified at higher temperatures
- **Food availability**: Live prey presence reduced aggression and orientations; fish allocated time to feeding
- **Relative size**: Mosquitofish aggression intensity related to size advantage over native species
- **Reproductive condition**: Courtship behaviors peaked in April, coinciding with reproductive activity
- **Vertical segregation**: *A. iberus* preference for lower water column reduced encounter rates with mosquitofish

**Conservation Implications:**
The study identifies multiple behavioral mechanisms potentially contributing to native species decline: increased agonistic interactions causing stress and fin damage, reduced feeding efficiency through exploitation and interference competition, disruption of reproductive behavior, and direct predation on small juveniles. *V. hispanica* appears more vulnerable due to greater habitat overlap with mosquitofish and restriction to freshwater habitats that have suffered greater human impact. *A. iberus* benefits from euryhalinity allowing refuge in saline environments and vertical habitat segregation reducing interaction frequency.
