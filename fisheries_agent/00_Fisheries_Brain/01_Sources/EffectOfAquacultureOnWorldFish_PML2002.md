# Effect of Aquaculture on World Fish Supplies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture #fisheries

## Executive Summary

This review article, published in *Nature* (vol. 405, 29 June 2000), examines whether global fish and shellfish farming ([[Aquaculture]]) enhances or diminishes the world's available fish supply. The authors conclude that aquaculture still adds to world fish supplies on balance, but only for some practices. Carnivorous fish and shrimp farming depend on large inputs of wild-caught small pelagic fish for [[Fish Meal]] and [[Fish Oil]], while herbivorous and filter-feeding species (carp, molluscs) provide a net gain. The authors argue that sustaining this contribution requires reducing wild fish inputs in feed, adopting ecologically sound management, and enforcing coastal protection policies. They find no evidence supporting the argument that aquaculture restores wild populations.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Marine and freshwater finfish, bivalves and crustaceans, which together make up about three-quarters of global aquaculture production by weight. Seaweed production is excluded from the main analysis.
- **Time frame:** Trends over roughly the past 10–15 years, a period of growing ecological and economic integration between [[Capture Fisheries]] and aquaculture.
- **Definition:** Aquaculture is distinguished from capture fisheries by ownership of stock and deliberate husbandry. Interventions range from extensive (predator exclusion) to semi-intensive (food enhancement) to intensive (full nutritional provision).
- **Diversity:** More than 220 species are farmed, from giant clams (nutrition from symbiotic algae) and filter-feeding mussels to herbivorous carp and carnivorous salmon.
- **Production systems:**
  - Molluscs: seabed, suspended nets or ropes, relying on ambient plankton.
  - Marine finfish: floating net cages nearshore, fed entirely on formulated feeds.
  - Freshwater finfish: ponds, often integrated into agricultural systems.
  - Shrimp: coastal ponds.
- **Subsectors:** Commercial intensive farms producing high-value commodities for global markets, and family or cooperative extensive farms producing low-value species for subsistence or local markets. These boundaries are blurring in Asia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Shared ecosystem services:** Ocean fisheries and aquaculture compete for habitat and nursery areas, feed and seed (larvae) supplies, and waste assimilation.
- **Biological links:** Exotic species invasions and pathogen transmission (for example, white spot virus in shrimp).
- **Habitat impacts:** Shrimp and salmon farming can damage ocean and coastal resources through habitat destruction, waste disposal and mangrove conversion. Mangrove-associated shrimp fisheries are cited as dependent on intact mangrove habitat.
- **Primary production:** About 8% of total aquatic primary production (137,000 Mt dry weight) is needed to sustain capture fisheries, seaweed collection and aquaculture. The share ranges from 2% in the open ocean to 24–35% in freshwater, shelf and upwelling systems.
- **Freshwater scarcity:** Future limits on freshwater could constrain herbivorous carp and tilapia farming and push development toward marine systems.
- **Sector geography:** Asia produces roughly 90% of global aquaculture output, and China alone more than two-thirds. Europe, North America and Japan produce just over one-tenth but consume most internationally traded farmed seafood.

## Key Findings

**Growth and supply**
- Global farmed fish and shellfish production more than doubled in weight and value between 1987 and 1997.
- Farmed fish supplies totalled 29 Mt in 1997, compared with 10 Mt a decade earlier.
- Farmed fish currently accounts for over one-quarter of fish directly consumed by humans.
- Capture fisheries landings have plateaued at roughly 95 Mt per year, and most ocean stocks are now over- or fully fished.
- Between 1986 and 1997, 4 of the top 5 and 8 of the top 20 capture species were used in feed production. These are mainly small pelagic fishes (anchoveta, Chilean jack mackerel, Atlantic herring, chub mackerel, Japanese anchovy, round sardinella, Atlantic mackerel and European anchovy).

**Feed dependence**
- Intensive and semi-intensive systems use 2–5 times more fish protein as [[Fish Meal]] than the farmed product supplies.
- For the ten most commonly farmed fish types, an average of 1.9 kg of wild fish is needed per kilogram of fish raised on compound feeds. Only catfish, milkfish and carp require less fish input than harvest.
- Carnivorous species require 2.5–5 times as much fish biomass as feed as the amount produced.
- The share of fish meal used for farmed fish rose from 10% (1988) to 17% (1994) and 33% (1997).
- About 80% of carp and 65% of tilapia are farmed without modern compound feeds.
- Compound feeds for omnivorous tilapia often contain about 15% fish meal, above required levels.

**Fish in the wider food chain**
- Of about 96 Mt of capture landings, 65 Mt of whole fish and 1 Mt of seaweeds go to humans. The remaining 30 Mt of fish catch, plus 2 Mt of processing scraps, feed fish meal production.
- About 10 Mt of fish meal goes to aquaculture feeds, and about 22 Mt to chicken, pig and other animal feeds.
- After ocean capture for feed, the net volume of fish reaching human consumption via aquaculture is at most 19 Mt.
- Poultry and swine are the largest consumers of fish meal by volume, but aquaculture feeds contain a much higher fish meal share than the 2–3% typical of livestock feeds.

**Market effects**
- Salmon farming has not reduced wild capture despite 30–50% declines in international prices for four of five wild salmon species in the 1990s. Global salmon catches rose 27% between 1988 and 1997.
- Wild hake and haddock catches remained relatively stable despite growth in tilapia and other substitute farmed fish.
- High fixed fleet costs, inelastic labour and fishing subsidies (about 20–25% of gross revenue) may prevent aquaculture from reducing short-term catches.

**Efficiency debate**
- A 10% energy transfer between trophic levels implies about 10 units of food for a predatory fish versus 2–5 units for a farmed fish. The authors note this comparison is debated because marine energy flows are poorly documented.
- Regardless of efficiency ratios, relying on expanding catches of small pelagic fish for feed would be ecologically disastrous and would constrain aquaculture's own growth.

**Solutions proposed**
- **Feed substitution:** Research focuses on oilseeds (especially soybean), meat by-products and microbial proteins. Complete fish meal replacement faces barriers in carnivorous species because of amino-acid balance and digestibility problems.
- **Omega-3 concerns:** Replacing fish oil with vegetable oil may raise the human dietary n-6:n-3 fatty acid ratio, which is already considered too high.
- **Integrated systems:** Polyculture of silver, grass, common and bighead carp uses different pond niches. Seaweed (e.g., *Gracilaria chilensis*) and mussels grown on salmon farm effluent reduce nutrient loads, and the seaweed revenue can offset infrastructure costs.
- **Policy:** Regulating pond siting in mangroves, setting effluent limits, enforcing biosafety for imported stock, mandating wastewater treatment, and removing subsidies that favour wild fishing. The authors argue that development banks and governments should play leading roles.

**Conclusion**
- Aquaculture adds to global fish supplies only if current fish meal and fish oil trends are reversed and coastal environmental degradation is controlled.
- Without a shared public and private vision, an expanded industry threatens both ocean fisheries and itself.
