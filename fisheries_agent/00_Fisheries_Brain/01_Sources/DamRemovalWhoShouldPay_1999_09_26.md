## Executive Summary

This 1999 *Seattle Times* article examines the socioeconomic and political dimensions of dam removal projects intended to restore [[Pacific Salmon]] runs in Washington State. It contrasts two case studies: the **Condit Dam** on the White Salmon River (privately funded removal by PacifiCorp, ~$17 million) and the **Elwha River dams** (Elwha Dam and Glines Canyon Dam) on the Olympic Peninsula, whose removal was projected to cost taxpayers ~$120 million. While not a primary fish biology paper, the article provides critical ecological context regarding the impact of unpassable hydroelectric dams on anadromous salmon populations and the regulatory/political forces shaping habitat restoration efforts.

## Background & Description

No direct morphological data on fish specimens is presented in this source. However, the article implies the biological requirement of [[Anadromous Fish Migration]] pathways — specifically the absence of fish passage structures (fish ladders) at the dams in question — as the central mechanism of population collapse. Both the Condit and Elwha dams were built without fish passageways, structurally blocking upstream migration to spawning habitat for the entire history of their operation (Condit: built early 1900s; Elwha dams: since 1912).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** Freshwater river systems used by anadromous salmonids for upstream spawning migration, situated within the Elwha River (Olympic Peninsula, draining to the Strait of Juan de Fuca) and the White Salmon River (Columbia River Gorge tributary).
- **Migratory corridor obstruction:** Both dam systems (Elwha Dam, Glines Canyon Dam, and Condit Dam) are described as complete barriers to migration, having "never had a passageway for fish," effectively severing access to upstream spawning and rearing habitat since construction.
- **Protected area overlap:** The Glines Canyon Dam lies within [[Olympic National Park]], intersecting salmon habitat with a federally protected wilderness ecosystem.
- **Anthropogenic stressors:** Hydroelectric power generation (each dam generates less than 1% of the output of a standard Columbia River dam), municipal water supply diversion (Port Angeles draws water downstream of the Elwha dams), and industrial power use (Daishowa America paper mill draws 45% of its power from the Elwha dams) represent competing human uses layered onto the salmon habitat.
- **Restoration stakeholders:** The [[Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe]] is identified as a key stakeholder advocating for dam removal and salmon run restoration, citing cumulative ecological and cultural losses dating to 1912.

## Key Findings

- **Total migration blockage:** Both the Condit Dam and the two Elwha River dams (Elwha Dam and Glines Canyon Dam) lack fish passage infrastructure, and are cited as having directly "destroyed the salmon runs" in their respective river systems upon construction in the early 20th century.
- **Regulatory driver — FERC relicensing:** The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission's (FERC) refusal to relicense Condit Dam without a $30 million environmental upgrade (including fish ladder installation) was the key trigger prompting PacifiCorp to instead choose dam removal ($17 million) as the more cost-effective route to compliance — directly linking fish-passage regulation to dam removal economics.
- **Comparative removal costs:** Condit Dam removal was projected to cost the public $0 (fully funded by PacifiCorp), whereas Elwha dam removal was projected at ~$120 million in public funds, including $29.5 million to purchase the dams from Fort James (formerly James River Corporation) and ~$90 million for demolition and restoration.
- **Changing regulatory climate:** The disparity is attributed to a shift in FERC's regulatory posture between the "Reagan-era" 1980s/early 1990s (lenient toward dam owners) and the environmental regulatory climate of the late 1990s (stricter enforcement of fish-passage and environmental compliance standards).
- **Restoration scale context:** The article foreshadows a larger emerging policy debate over removing four dams on the Snake River in Eastern Washington to aid salmon recovery — a case where public ownership would eliminate the compensation dispute seen in the Elwha case.
- **Ecological cost externalized to Tribes:** The [[Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe]] representative noted that while the dam-owning company was compensated ($29.5 million), no compensation mechanism existed for the loss of salmon runs experienced by the tribe and broader public since 1912, highlighting an equity gap in restoration economics.
