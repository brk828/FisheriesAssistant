# A Nonlethal Technique to Recover Gut Contents of Roundtail Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This methods study tests a [[Stomach Pumping]] technique that recovers gut contents from live [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) without killing them. The authors use roundtail chub as a surrogate for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*), which are endemic to the [[Colorado River]] and cannot easily be sacrificed under permit. Forty adults were collected by [[Electrofishing]]. Twenty were stomach-pumped and twenty served as handled controls. Necropsy showed the method evacuated 100% of gut contents with no apparent damage to the [[Pharyngeal Teeth]], esophagus, or intestinal lining. The authors recommend the method as a safe, efficient, nonlethal procedure for the Gila chubs, with caveats on light pressure and early termination.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Roundtail chub, a large cyprinid of the [[Gila Genus]]. The endangered humpback and bonytail chubs share a nearly identical gut configuration, mouth parts, and pharyngeal tooth structure and counts (Holden 1968).
- **Conservation problem:** Food habit studies usually require killing large numbers of fish. In small or threatened populations this can deplete stocks, and permits to sacrifice endangered species are difficult to obtain. Understanding food habits is important for identifying factors that limit [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] survival (Valdez and Clemmer 1982).
- **Prior use of stomach pumping:** The technique has been applied without injury to salmonids, centrarchids, ictalurids, percids, and esocids (Seaburg and Moyle 1964; Swenson and Smith 1973; Meehan and Miller 1978). Its use on large cyprinids such as the Colorado River chubs had not been reported.
- **Apparatus:** The pump was based on the [[Gengerke Modification]] of the [[Seaburg Stomach Sampler]]. It consisted of two 50-cm lengths of flexible plastic tubing attached to a handheld, valved rubber bulb (a commercial in-line outboard motor primer). Tubing was 5 mm or 10 mm outside diameter, matched to fish size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** The study area was the mainstem Colorado River near the Colorado-Utah state line, sampled August 5–6, 1992.
- **Diet:** Roundtail chub in this study mainly ate insects, with some algae and small organic debris. The authors argue these foods are easily flushed from the intestine, which supports the method's suitability for Colorado River chubs.
- **Gut anatomy:** The genus *Gila* has a simple, S-shaped intestine and no [[Pyloric Sphincter]]. This allows material to pass freely through the intestinal tract, so contents can be flushed from the [[Anal Vent]] rather than the mouth.
- **Tooth replacement:** Cyprinid pharyngeal teeth are shed and replaced continuously (Harder 1975), which is relevant to interpreting loose teeth found during dissection.
- **Permits and funding:** The work was funded by the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (Contract 0-CS-40-09110) in cooperation with [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]], under permits from the Colorado Division of Wildlife.

## Key Findings

**Sample and design**
- 40 adult roundtail chub were collected by boat electrofishing (120 V DC, 8 A, 20% pulse width, 40 pulses/s).
- Test group: 20 randomly selected adults, mean 279 mm total length (TL). The reported range is partly illegible in the scan.
- Control group: 20 randomly selected adults, mean 260 mm TL, range 219–347 mm. Controls were handled identically but not pumped.
- Fish were euthanized with [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222) after testing. The anesthetic was not used for sedation before pumping.

**Method**
- The outlet tube was inserted about 10–20 mm into the esophagus to limit backflushing.
- One biologist held the fish head-up with the vent over a whirl-pack, while the other inserted the tube and slowly squeezed the bulb.
- Visual checks confirmed the tube passed the pharyngeal teeth correctly.
- Dissection followed a standard cranium cut and a transverse cut to the gill arch bases. Pharyngeal teeth were counted, checked for alignment, and inspected for loosening or breakage. Volumes of flushed material and residual gut were measured in a graduated cylinder.

**Efficiency**
- Food was recovered from 8 of 20 test fish. Traces of algae or unidentifiable debris were found in 8 fish, and 4 contained no food.
- Most flushed material was insects, some mixed with algae.
- Pump efficiency was 100% (by volume), based on residual gut examination after flushing.

**Physical effects**
- Material was expelled from the vent with relatively little water pressure, suggesting little physical stress to the intestine.
- Visual inspection showed no lesions, contusions, or deformations in the gut tract.
- No broken or damaged pharyngeal teeth were found in test fish.
- Loose pharyngeal teeth occurred in 7 control fish and 9 test fish. Their presence and the tooth buds seen beside them indicate natural [[Tooth Shedding And Ankylosis]] rather than pump damage.
- A knife test on sacrificed fish showed that teeth could be broken but not loosened by light force, far beyond what a small plastic tube could apply.
- Fish were not held long after pumping. However, the lack of visible gut or tooth damage and the absence of stress or disorientation suggested no long-term ill effects, though these were not formally evaluated.
- Roundtail chub were docile during pumping, so anesthesia was not needed. The authors suggest the same may hold for other chubs.

**Recommendations**
- The method is recommended for adult humpback and bonytail chubs, and is expected to be safe, efficient, and nonlethal for Colorado River chubs when used as described.
- Use only light pressure on the bulb. A large algal bolus or food item could block the intestine, and forcing it could injure the fish.
- If no food is evacuated after two or three gentle flushes, stop and release the fish.
- Tubing of appropriate size should be matched to fish size.
- The authors note that longer-term survival and condition effects were not tested and should be evaluated before routine use.
