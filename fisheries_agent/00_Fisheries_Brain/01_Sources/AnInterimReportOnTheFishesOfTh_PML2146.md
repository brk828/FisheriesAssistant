# Inventory of the Fishes of the New Mexico Portion of the San Juan River, 1987

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 interim report, prepared by Steven P. Platania and Kevin R. Bestgen (Department of Biology, University of New Mexico) for the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] under a U.S. Bureau of Reclamation contract, documents a 1987 inventory of fishes in the New Mexico reach of the [[San Juan River]]. The survey targeted five natives of the [[Colorado River Basin]] that are federally listed or candidates for listing: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Only Colorado Squawfish and Roundtail Chub were captured, and the report confirms that both still occur and likely reproduce in New Mexico waters. The authors frame the 1987 work as essentially an inventory and recommend at least two more field seasons of sampling.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** About 96 km (66 miles) of river from Farmington to the Colorado–New Mexico border, flowing across a low-relief intermontane plateau. Riparian vegetation is mainly cottonwood and willow, with introduced saltcedar and Russian olive. Land use is largely irrigated agriculture and grazing.
- **Regional context:** The Upper Colorado River Basin holds 55 fish taxa reported, of which 13 are native and eight are endemic. Four are federally listed as endangered, and two are candidates for listing. Little prior work had targeted the San Juan sub-basin, which was assumed to hold few rare fishes.
- **Study objectives:** (a) determine the presence and relative abundance of target species between Farmington and the Colorado border; (b) describe fish habitat and assess its suitability for target species; (c) describe the fish community; and (d) formulate recommendations for future recovery management.
- **Historical records:** The first known San Juan collection in New Mexico was at Aztec in 1934. A Colorado Squawfish was documented near Rosa in 1960, and [[Navajo Reservoir]] pre-impoundment surveys in 1961 were the first major attempt to document the New Mexico fauna. Sublette's 1976 survey (34 stations) captured Roundtail Chub only, and a 1977 survey repeated this pattern.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting and hydrology**
- Mean annual discharge at Farmington (1913–1985) was reported at 67.8 m³/s. Tributary inputs from the [[Animas River]] and [[La Plata River]] contribute heavy sediment loads, and spring runoff is pronounced.
- Five diversion structures lie within the study area, ranging from earthen dikes to concrete weirs. Some were passable only at certain flows, and the report notes this created access and coordination problems for sampling.
- Conductivity ranged from 375 to 920 µmhos during the August and October trips.

**Habitat (1987 trips)**
- Upstream of [[Shiprock]], the river was largely confined to a single channel, with swift runs and riffles dominant in May and cobble bars exposed at lower flows.
- Downstream of Shiprock, the channel braided into up to four channels and gradient and current velocity decreased. Backwaters were more common here, along with undercut banks and debris jams.
- Backwaters were classed as shallow, ephemeral, slow-water areas adjacent to the river. They were sampled by seining in primary and secondary types.

**Anthropogenic impacts**
- Construction of Navajo Dam altered flow and temperature regimes. The report argues that this, along with the introduction of non-native species, probably drove declines in native populations, especially in the reach nearest the reservoir.
- A 1962 pre-impoundment [[Rotenone Eradication]] program killed large numbers of fish from the Colorado–New Mexico border downstream, including Colorado Squawfish.
- Later localized fish kills were linked to water release patterns from Navajo Dam.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Six trips were made between March and October 1987: three reconnaissance trips and three sampling trips.
- Three inflatable rafts (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) were used. The primary collecting method was [[Electrofishing]] from a raft and a boat, supplemented by [[Seine Sampling]] of backwaters in October.
- Fish were identified, counted, weighed, measured and released. Bluehead and flannelmouth suckers and target species were tagged. Plans were modified in the field because of high water, equipment failures and safety concerns, and these deviations are noted in the report.

**Electrofishing results (98 collections, May–October 1987)**
- A total of 4,879 specimens representing 13 species and one hybrid were collected.
- The most common native fishes were [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. Together they made up the bulk of the catch and were present in nearly all collections.
- Common Carp and Channel Catfish were the most common non-natives. Electrofishing catch rates were highest for Flannelmouth Sucker (about 74 fish/hour) and Bluehead Sucker (about 49 fish/hour).
- Brown Trout were collected, but mainly in the upper reach.
- Electrofishing in May produced far fewer specimens, which the authors attribute to high runoff and low sampling efficiency.

**Target species**
- Colorado Squawfish: two adults and two young-of-year (YOY) were captured. The two adults were taken in May 1987 and August 1987, and one YOY and another individual were collected in backwaters and a side channel in October 1987.
- Roundtail Chub: one subadult and three YOY were captured.
- Humpback Chub, Bonytail and Razorback Sucker were not collected. The report notes this does not exclude their presence, since Razorback Sucker identification requires a much more extensive early spring survey.

**Backwater seine results (75 collections, October 1987)**
- A total of 26,793 specimens were collected. The sample was dominated by non-native species, with Red Shiner the most abundant and present in all but two collections. Fathead Minnow was the second most common, and Mosquitofish was the most widespread.
- Native Speckled Dace was the most abundant native species in seine samples.
- Roundtail Chub were collected from three backwaters (single YOY specimens from each). Colorado Squawfish YOY were taken from two backwaters, one of them 3.0 river miles above the Animas confluence and one 0.5 miles below.

**Historical and comparative findings**
- The 1987 results agree with earlier surveys that Roundtail Chub and Colorado Squawfish persist in the San Juan, but the study's comparison with previous ichthyofauna lists shows many introduced species in the 1987 fauna were not documented in earlier surveys.
- Introduced species (e.g., Common Carp, Red Shiner, Channel Catfish) have been present for at least 25 years but are apparently less common than in the upper basin.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The report concludes that Colorado Squawfish and Roundtail Chub at least occasionally inhabit and probably reproduce in the New Mexico portion of the San Juan River. Further inventory is needed to define habitat use and population size.
- Recommended sampling schedule: at least two more spring/summer and one fall inventory, covering the entire study area.
- Most likely Colorado Squawfish habitat is between Shiprock and the Colorado border. Substantial effort should focus on the reach between the backwaters near Fruitland and the state line.
- Backwater sampling should be expanded. Each backwater should be characterized only by general dimensions, connectivity, dominant substrate, location relative to channel, water temperature, conductivity and dissolved oxygen.
- Electrofishing protocols should be standardized, with a complementary river-channel physical habitat survey conducted for each five-mile reach.
