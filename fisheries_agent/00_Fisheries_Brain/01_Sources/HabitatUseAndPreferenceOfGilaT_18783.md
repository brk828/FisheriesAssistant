# Habitat Use and Preference of Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #endangered-species #habitat-ecology #desert-aquatic-systems

## Executive Summary

This Master's thesis by Robert Eugene Forrest (University of Arizona, 1992) quantifies microhabitat use and preference of the federally endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) through field observations in [[Cienega Creek]], Arizona, and controlled laboratory experiments. The study employed visual observation of fish aggregations, measurement of physical habitat variables (depth, velocity, substrate, temperature), and experimental trials in outdoor pools and aquaria. Results demonstrate that topminnow prefer stream margins with calm, shallow water containing aquatic vegetation, consistently occupy surface waters 1-2°C warmer than surrounding areas, and show strong positive responses to cover. Experimental work revealed that groups of fish behaved differently than solitary individuals, with groups showing consistent cover preference while single fish did not. The research provides quantitative habitat data to inform recovery efforts for this endangered species, which has declined to only 8 natural populations in the United States due to habitat loss and competition with exotic [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] is a small live-bearing fish in the family [[Poeciliidae]], endemic to the [[Gila River]] system of Arizona, New Mexico, and northern Sonora, Mexico. Sexual dimorphism is pronounced: males reach 20-25 mm standard length with a modified anal fin (gonopodium) and blacken during breeding; females reach 25-35 mm, develop a blackened peritroct when breeding, and are tan above with a dark lateral band. Sexual maturity occurs within 5-12 months. Females store viable sperm for several months; gestation lasts approximately 21 days with brood sizes of 15-20 (up to 90) young born alive.

Historically, Gila topminnow were the most abundant "low desert" fish in the [[Colorado River Basin]] prior to 1940, occurring throughout low-elevation (<1,800 m) aquatic habitats including permanent springs, marshes, cienegas, and permanent and intermittent streams. The species was listed as endangered in 1967. By 1990, only 8 natural populations remained in the United States: 7 in the [[Santa Cruz River]] system (Redrock Canyon, Cottonwood Spring, Monkey Spring, [[Sonoita Creek]], [[Cienega Creek]], Sharp Spring, upper Santa Cruz River) and 1 at Bylas Springs (Gila River tributary). Despite over 340 introduction attempts at more than 100 sites since 1982, only 17% of introduced populations persisted through 1989.

The decline is attributed to: (1) drastic loss of aquatic habitats since the late 1800s through water diversion, groundwater pumping, and channel modification; (2) introduction of exotic [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) beginning in 1926, which cause fin damage, direct predation on juveniles, and competitive displacement; and (3) predation by introduced species such as [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Cienega Creek]] in Pima County, Arizona, supports the largest natural population of Gila topminnow in Arizona. The stream is a shallow, meandering system with deeply incised pools and quick-moving runs, containing over 16 km of occupied habitat. Native [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*) and [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) also occur; no exotic fish species have been documented.

Macrohabitat surveys (June 1991) revealed that marsh was the most common habitat type (49.9% by area) but pools supported the highest relative abundance of topminnow (49.6% of fish), followed by glides (24.6%) and marsh (16.4%). Riffles, rapids, backwaters and other fast-water habitats contained few topminnow.

Microhabitat data were collected at 6 sites (2 backwaters, 2 pools, 2 glides) in July 1991 and May 1992. Topminnow formed large aggregations (50-500 fish) along stream margins, under algal mats, in emergent vegetation, and in shallow areas. When disturbed, fish fled to deeper water or opposite-bank cover but returned to margins within minutes. Visual observation from the bank proved adequate for documenting habitat use.

Water temperature strongly influenced topminnow distribution and diel movements. In early morning when stream margins were 16-17°C, no topminnow occupied a backwater; by afternoon when temperatures reached 26-29°C, approximately 500 fish were present. Topminnow occupied the stream center in early morning and evening when it was 1-2°C warmer than margins, then moved to margins as they warmed during the day. This pattern reversed in evening as margins cooled.

Previous authors noted topminnow occur in diverse habitats but prefer shallow, warm, quiet waters with dense vegetation. However, quantitative microhabitat data were lacking, and some reintroduction failures suggested incomplete understanding of habitat requirements.

## Key Findings

**Microhabitat Use and Preference (Field Observations):**

**Depth:** Topminnow were observed over depths of 1-60 cm but showed no consistent preference for shallow water across all sites. Statistical comparisons (Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests) showed significant differences between used and available depths at some sites but not others. However, topminnow were nearly always observed at the water surface regardless of total depth. Large numbers of young fish consistently occupied the shallowest areas (3-5 cm) along margins.

**Velocity:** Topminnow strongly avoided measurable water velocity. Only 3 of 6 sites had measurable flow; topminnow used areas with 0-0.10 m/s velocity but preferred areas with no measurable velocity (designated "trace" when current was visible but <0.01 m/s). Stream margins with thick emergent and submergent vegetation reduced velocity to near zero in occupied areas.

**Substrate:** Topminnow were observed over substrates ranging from plant material and silt to cobble. Chi-squared tests showed significant substrate selection at only 3 of 6 sites, with no consistent pattern. Site 1: small gravel selected; Site 2a: silt, small/large gravel, and cobble selected; Site 6: silt and sand selected. Despite statistical results, plant material occurred in a large percentage of occupied areas and appeared important for providing vertical and surface concealment, feeding substrate, and breeding territories.

**Temperature:** Water temperature was 1-2°C higher in areas occupied by topminnow compared to adjacent unused areas. Topminnow exhibited clear diel movement patterns driven by temperature: occupying stream center when it was warmest (early morning/evening), then moving to margins as they warmed during the day. Backwaters warmed rapidly and were heavily used during afternoon but abandoned by dawn.

**Experimental Results (Outdoor Pools, February-March and June-July 1992):**

Groups of 10 topminnow (5 males, 5 females) were tested in 1.7-m diameter pools with half the surface area containing cover.

**Cover Preference:** Topminnow showed strong, consistent preference for cover over open water across all cover types and seasons:
- **Styrofoam sheets** (surface cover only): 77% of observations on covered side in winter (P<0.001 morning and afternoon); in summer, preference significant only in afternoon (P<0.05), not morning (P>0.2). Of fish on covered side, significantly more were concealed under cover in winter morning (P<0.001) but not afternoon; in summer, fewer were concealed in morning (P<0.001) with no difference in afternoon.
- **Plastic strips** (vertical and surface cover): 84% of observations on covered side overall (P<0.001 all times/seasons); higher preference in winter (94%) than summer (73%). Significantly more fish concealed within strips than near them (P<0.001 all times/seasons). Fish were virtually invisible within strips and moved with them when disturbed.
- **Floating algae** (surface cover): 75% of observations on covered side (P<0.001 all times/seasons). Significantly more fish concealed under algae than near it in winter (P<0.001 morning and afternoon) and summer afternoon (P<0.05), but not summer morning.

**Response strength:** Plastic strips (providing both vertical and horizontal cover) elicited the strongest response, followed by floating algae, then styrofoam sheets. Significantly higher percentage of fish associated with floating algae in morning than afternoon in winter (P<0.025) and with plastic strips and floating algae in summer (P<0.005).

**Depth/Water Column Preference:** In pools with three depth zones (10.2, 25.4, 55.8 cm), topminnow strongly selected the upper 1/3 of the water column regardless of total depth (P<0.001, two-way ANOVA). No significant preference among the three horizontal sections (P>0.10). Surface water averaged 1.5°C warmer than middle third in morning, 1.3°C warmer in afternoon; 3.5°C warmer than bottom third in morning, 5.1°C warmer in afternoon. When disturbed, fish briefly moved to lower third but returned to surface within minutes.

**Experimental Results (Aquaria, April-August 1992):**

Single topminnow (tested separately, 5 females and 5 males per condition) were placed in 38-liter aquaria divided by transparent barriers with 5 circular openings allowing movement between conditions. Fish were observed every 30 minutes for 6 hours on two consecutive days.

**Key findings:**
- **Strong preference for calm vs. turbulent water:** Both sexes showed highly significant preference for calm water (females 94:6, males 96:4, P<0.05).
- **No consistent cover preference:** Fish showed no significant preference for cover over no cover in 4 of 6 tests. Plastic strips were significantly preferred over other conditions in only 1 of 6 tests.
- **Sex differences:** Males and females responded similarly in only 4 of 7 condition pairs. Responses to floating algae and water cress were inconsistent.
- **Contrast with group behavior:** Single fish did not exhibit the strong cover preference observed in groups in outdoor pools.

**Comparison of Methods:**

The author found critical differences between individual and group behavior. Groups in outdoor pools consistently selected cover (all types) over open water, matching field observations. Single fish in aquaria showed inconsistent cover responses differing by sex. The author suggests topminnow, which naturally occur in large aggregations, respond to environmental conditions as groups rather than solitary individuals. Additional factors: (1) fish in outdoor pools experienced regular disturbance from experimenters and predatory birds, potentially strengthening cover association; (2) fish in aquaria were protected from visual disturbance by blinds; (3) aquaria size meant fish were always in close proximity to cover.

**Management Implications:**

Current recovery efforts emphasize introductions/reintroductions within historic range, but only a small percentage of populations persist beyond a few years. The author advocates shifting from "survival philosophy" (introducing fish into declining habitats) to "recovery philosophy" (defining and providing habitat optima).

**Habitat requirements identified:**
- Large reaches of heterogeneous stream environments (not isolated springs or short stream segments)
- Shallow stream margins connected to deeper waters
- Extensive emergent and submergent vegetation for cover
- Areas with calm water (velocity <0.10 m/s, preferably unmeasurable)
- Areas capable of warming (backwaters, shallow margins)
- Substrate may not be critical, but further study needed

**Recommendations:**
1. Manage for environments similar to [[Cienega Creek]]: long stream reaches with heterogeneous macrohabitats that ensure availability of suitable microhabitats despite seasonal dynamics (flooding, scouring, drought, vegetation changes)
2. Conduct routine monitoring during summer and winter to determine if low temperatures cause mortality or merely inactivity
3. Monitor all sites for [[Mosquitofish]] and eradicate if present
4. Assess cienega-type habitats (not evaluated in this study due to difficulty observing fish)
5. Conduct studies to determine specific tolerances and preferences for temperature, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, and water quality
6. Future habitat preference experiments should use groups rather than individual fish

**Statistical Methods:**

Kolmogorov-Smirnov 2-sample tests compared depth distributions between used and available areas. Chi-squared goodness-of-fit tests compared substrate distributions (categories pooled to achieve n>5). Student's t-tests compared fish distributions across experimental conditions. Two-way ANOVA tested depth/position preferences in pools. Chi-squared tests (Fry 1958) compared time spent in aquarium halves against expected distributions. Paired t-tests compared morning vs. afternoon cover use.

**Sample Sizes:**

Field: 6 sites sampled in 1991 and 1992 (2 sites replaced due to scouring). Transects spaced 0.25-2 m apart depending on site length; measurements every 20 cm across transects. Outdoor pools: 8 trials per cover type in winter, 8 in summer; 8 depth trials in summer; 10 fish per trial, observed 12 times over 1 hour. Aquaria: 5 females and 5 males per condition (7 conditions tested); 12 observations per fish over 2 days.
