# Draft 90-Day Finding for Desert Fishes Council Petition to List 31 Animal Species in Nevada and California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrobiidae #goodeidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae #conservation-policy #endangered-species-act

## Executive Summary

On March 25, 1991, the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] received a petition from the [[Desert Fishes Council]] to list as endangered 11 species of fishes, one amphibian ([[Amargosa Toad]]), and 20 species of aquatic invertebrates (primarily springsnails and aquatic beetles) from southern Nevada and southeastern California. The petition actually contained 30 valid species after accounting for duplicates and taxonomic clarifications. This memorandum constitutes the 90-day finding under section 4(b)(3)(A) of the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. All petitioned species were designated as Category 2 candidate species as of November 21, 1991. The petition was signed by Mr. Phil Pister, Executive Secretary of the Desert Fishes Council, an international conservation organization dedicated to preserving native species and aquatic habitats of North American deserts.

## Background & Description

The [[Desert Fishes Council]] is an international conservation organization whose primary purpose is to preserve native species and aquatic habitats of North American deserts. Membership is composed primarily of biologists in research and management from State and Federal agencies, universities, and private organizations. The petition cited numerous publications documenting biotic and abiotic threats to aquatic ecosystems in western North America, including habitat deterioration and decline in numbers and populations of the petitioned species (Miller 1961, La Rivers 1962, Minckley and Deacon 1968, Courtenay and Robins 1975, Deacon et al. 1964, Dudley and Larson 1976, Williams et al. 1985, Courtenay et al. 1985, Williams and Sada 1985, Miller et al. 1989, Schoenherr 1981).

Twenty-seven of the petitioned species are endemic to restricted aquatic habitats within southern Nevada. Two species occur at several localities in Nevada ([[Ash Meadows]] and [[Oasis Valley]]) and in several springs in the [[Death Valley]] area, [[Inyo County]], California. One species ([[Death Valley Agabus Diving Beetle]]) is known only from Carson Slough in Inyo County, but may also occur in nearby Ash Meadows, [[Nye County]], Nevada.

The petitioned species exhibit high degrees of local endemism. In [[Ash Meadows]], nine of 11 described species of snails (Family [[Hydrobiidae]]) are endemic to the area, and three of those are restricted to single springs (Hershler and Sada 1987). Both the [[Devils Hole Warm Spring Riffle Beetle]] and the [[Amargosa Naucorid Bug]] are also restricted to single springs within [[Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge]] (AMNWR).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Distribution by Geographic Area:**

**A. [[Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge]]** (southern [[Nye County]], Nevada): Contains 13 petitioned invertebrate species, including multiple endemic springsnails (Family [[Hydrobiidae]]) distributed across approximately 20 springs. Nine springsnail species are endemic to Ash Meadows, with three restricted to single springs. Historical distribution is uncertain because most aquatic habitats were extensively modified for agricultural uses during the late 1960s and early 1970s for a cattle/alfalfa ranch.

**B. [[Amargosa River]] and adjacent habitats** (Nevada and California): Contains [[Oasis Valley Speckled Dace]], [[Amargosa Toad]] (presently known only from riparian areas along 0.5 mile of the Amargosa River near Beatty, Nevada, and Crystal Spring approximately 1 mile north of Beatty), [[Oasis Valley Springsnail]], [[Death Valley Agabus Diving Beetle]], and [[Amargosa Tryonia Snail]]. The Amargosa River channel has been channelized through the town of Beatty, and portions are used by all-terrain and four-wheel-drive vehicles for recreation.

**C. [[Pahranagat Valley]] and [[White River Valley]]** (southern [[White Pine County]], northeastern [[Nye County]], and central [[Lincoln County]], Nevada): Contains [[White River Desert Sucker]], [[White River Speckled Dace]], [[Preston White River Springfish]], [[Pahranagat Speckled Dace]], [[Pahranagat Pebblesnail]], [[Grated Tryonia Snail]], and [[Moapa Warm Spring Riffle Beetle]]. Surveys in 1990 and 1991 documented extirpation of several species from multiple springs. The federally endangered [[White River Spinedace]] (Lepidomeda albivallis) occurs at only one locality (North Flag Spring) with an estimated total population of 50 adult individuals.

**D. [[Moapa River]]** (northern [[Clark County]], Nevada): Contains [[Moapa White River Springfish]], [[Moapa River Roundtail Chub]], [[Moapa Speckled Dace]], [[Moapa Pebblesnail]], [[Moapa Warm Spring Riffle Beetle]], [[Grated Tryonia Snail]], and [[Oasis Valley Springsnail]]. Modification of springs and outflows for domestic and agricultural uses has greatly reduced historical habitat. One spring and associated marsh containing [[Moapa Dace]] (federally listed endangered) was eliminated when the outflow was completely contained in a pipeline.

**E. [[Meadow Valley Wash]]** (western [[Lincoln County]], Nevada): Contains [[Meadow Valley Wash Desert Sucker]] and [[Meadow Valley Wash Speckled Dace]] in perennial stream portions from Ursine downstream to Elgin. Meadow Valley Wash above Condor Canyon was dammed to create Echo Canyon Reservoir, which is stocked with [[Rainbow Trout]] (Oncorhynchus mykiss) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (Micropterus salmoides). Water is diverted from the channel to irrigate alfalfa at many points, and the channel is dry in many places during irrigation season.

**F. [[Railroad Valley]]** (northeastern [[Nye County]], Nevada): Contains [[Railroad Valley Tui Chub]] in Bull Creek headwater springs, Duckwater Creek, Blue Eagle Spring, Butterfield Spring, and several artesian wells. Status has not been checked recently, with at least one landowner refusing permission to check status. Groundwater pumping occurs for alfalfa irrigation and oil exploration activities.

**Threats to Aquatic Ecosystems:**

Abiotic factors detrimentally affecting petitioned species include groundwater pumping and the impoundment, diversion, and alteration of surface waters for municipal, agricultural, and industrial purposes. Almost all springs and outflows in [[Ash Meadows]] were altered and channelized during the late 1960s and early 1970s. Pumping directly from Jackrabbit and Forest springs caused extirpation of the [[Ash Meadows Amargosa Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon nevadensis mionectes) and [[Ash Meadows Speckled Dace]] (Rhinichthys osculus nevadensis). Nearby groundwater pumping caused the water level in [[Devils Hole]] to drop (Dudley and Larson 1976, Williams and Sada 1985, Dettinger 1989), threatening survival of the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon diabolis).

Biotic factors include predation and/or competition from introduced species such as crayfish (Procambarus clarkii), snails (Melanoides tuberculatus), salt cedar (Tamarix sp.), sailfin mollies (Poecilia latipinna), mosquitofish (Gambusia affinis), bullfrogs (Rana catesbeiana), guppies (Poecilia reticulata), convict cichlids (Cichlisoma nigrofasciatum), softshell turtles (Tryonix sp.), pondweed (Vallisneria sp.), channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus), rainbow trout, and largemouth bass. These introduced species have adversely affected native species throughout the region (Williams et al. 1985, Miller et al. 1989, Landye 1973).

Both biotic and abiotic factors have contributed to extinctions of the [[Ash Meadows Poolfish]] (Empetrichthys merriami), [[Pahranagat Spinedace]] (Lepidomeda altivelis), Raycraft Ranch poolfish (Empetrichthys latos concavus), Pahrump Ranch poolfish (Empetrichthys latos pahrump), and [[Las Vegas Dace]] (Rhinichthys deaconi). Extirpation of several petitioned species from some habitats where they historically occurred has also been documented (Courtenay et al. 1985).

## Key Findings

1. **Petition Validity**: The petition clearly identified itself as such and contained the name, signature, address, and telephone number of the petitioner. The actual number of valid species contained in the petition was 30 (the grated tryonia snail was listed twice, and the Oasis Valley springsnail as listed included the "virile amargosa snail").

2. **Candidate Species Status**: All petitioned species were designated as Category 2 candidate species in the November 21, 1991, Animal Notice of Review (56 FR 225). Category 2 candidate species are those for which the Service has received some information to indicate that proposing to list as endangered or threatened may be appropriate.

3. **Substantial Information Finding**: The Service finds that the petitioner has presented substantial information indicating that listing the 30 species identified in the petition may be warranted. This decision is based on scientific and commercial information contained or referenced in the petition, and on other information available to the Service at this time.

4. **Endemic Species Vulnerability**: The restricted range and small population size of most petitioned species means they may be particularly vulnerable to extirpation or extinction. Several petitioned fish species have been identified by the Endangered Species Committee of the [[American Fisheries Society]] as being of special concern (Deacon et al. 1979, Williams et al. 1989).

5. **Recovery Plan Coverage**: All petitioned candidate species within [[Ash Meadows]] are included in the approved Recovery Plan for the Endangered and Threatened Species of Ash Meadows, Nevada. However, until pertinent recovery tasks are actually implemented, present status of the petitioned species in Ash Meadows will remain unchanged.

6. **Groundwater Threats**: Inclusion of many species within AMNWR has increased protection from surface-disturbing activities, but may provide little or no protection from depletion of the carbonate groundwater aquifer which supplies springflow throughout the habitats. A relationship between spring discharge and effects of distant pumping on the carbonate-rock aquifer may be difficult to establish. Long-term effects of such pumping on spring flows may not show up for years, by which time depleted spring flows would not recover in the near-term even if all pumping ceased.

7. **Water Rights Applications**: Recent water rights applications to the [[Nevada State Engineer]] by the [[Las Vegas Valley Water District]] for 146 separate ground water/surface water sites totaling approximately 850,000 acre-feet per year have increased the potential threats to aquatic habitats throughout the geographical area in which the petitioned species occur.

8. **Devils Hole Water Level Decline**: Recent analysis of data obtained from USGS and [[National Park Service]] indicates that water surface elevation in [[Devils Hole]] has been declining since August 1989 (Lehman and Atkins 1991). The authors concluded that the decline was "undoubtedly caused by water usages in areas tributary to Devil's Hole," and that current water usage may have to be decreased in the future to maintain water levels at the court-ordered elevation.

9. **State Agency Concerns**: The [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] expressed concerns about the petition, noting that basic documentation in support of listing was in many cases of poor quality or non-existent. NDOW staff indicated that the petition was not coordinated with other individuals working towards recovery and preservation of native fishes, and that the Department's Native Fish Program was neither consulted nor informed about the pending action.

10. **Next Steps**: A notice announcing this finding and initiating a status review for publication in the Federal Register will be submitted through the normal clearance route.
