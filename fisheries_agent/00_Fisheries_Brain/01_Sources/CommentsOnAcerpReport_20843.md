# Phelps Dodge Comments on the ACERP Streams and Rivers Draft and Arizona Stream Water Regulation

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #water-quality-regulation

## Executive Summary

This document bundles three items: a 5 October 1994 letter from [[Phelps Dodge Corporation]] counsel Jerry O. Worsham II to the [[Arizona Department of Environmental Quality]] (ADEQ), and two excerpts from the draft [[Arizona Comparative Risk Project]] (ACERP) report on streams and rivers by Minckley and Kubly (14 September 1994). The letter disputes the draft's claim that mining at Morenci caused toxic outflows that "decimated fishes" in Chase Creek and the San Francisco River, and asks that the example be deleted. The report excerpts establish a stream baseline, outline Arizona's water regulatory structure, and describe natural and anthropogenic sources of toxic stream pollution, including mining, spills and historical mining periods. The material is primarily policy and historical in nature; it contains few quantitative ecological data on fish populations.

## Background & Description

**Document type and parties.** The letter (ref. JDW:lmm, with attachments) responds to a draft report reviewed for ADEQ by Ms. Pat Mariella. Worsham states that other reviewers would likely submit further comments.

**Stream baseline.** The report uses digitized hydrographic maps at 1:100,000 scale. The state contains about 150,000 miles of streams (AZDEQ 1992), of which an estimated 17,537 stream miles are classed as 13,907 ephemeral and 3,630 perennial. The remainder are described as intermittent. The authors use the perennial estimate as the environmental baseline for judging human effects on stream ecosystems.

**Regulatory framework.**
- The [[Arizona Environmental Quality Act]] (AEQA) took effect 13 August 1986. It created ADEQ, effective 1 July 1987, which centralized most environmental responsibility.
- ADEQ's water pollution control program has eight components: water quality planning; standards setting, monitoring and assessment; plan review, permits and inspections; site mitigation and enforcement; point source control; nonpoint source control; clean lakes; and wetland/riparian areas.
- The [[Arizona Department of Water Resources]] (ADWR) issues water-use permits or certificates. Uses predating 1919 are recognized through appropriation rights. Disputes are settled by courts, including general adjudications.
- [[NPDES Permits]], required under the [[Clean Water Act]], are administered jointly by the EPA and ADEQ but issued and enforced by EPA. Discharge limits derive from Arizona surface water quality standards, with some stricter federal modifications.
- Numeric standards exist for 126 organic compounds, 19 inorganic species (phosphorus, nitrogen, nitrates, ammonia, cyanide, fluoride and 13 metals), and fecal coliform, pH, delta temperature, turbidity and dissolved oxygen. Rule R18-11-405.B prohibits discharges that violate surface water standards, including discharges to groundwater that could pollute gaining streams.
- The [[Water Quality Assurance Revolving Fund]] (WQARF) is Arizona's [[Superfund]] equivalent, modeled on [[CERCLA]] and administered by ADEQ. Liability falls on owners or operators at the time of disposal, during the release, or while the substance was on site.

**Mining history.** The report describes four mining periods in Arizona: aboriginal, Spanish conquistador, American prospector and modern-day. Aboriginal mining existed by A.D. 1000 and ran to about the mid-16th century. Modern mining grew after 1900, driven by large-scale low-grade mining and the flotation process for sulfide ores. Arizona produces more than 30 mineral commodities and is especially known for copper.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Natural sources of impairment.** Natural ore deposits, soils and alluvium release toxics to streams. Elevated natural background levels of arsenic, boron, selenium and total dissolved solids occur in Arizona streams and watersheds.

**Accidental spills.**
- ADEQ and the Arizona Game and Fish Department have repeatedly investigated fish kills within and downstream of the Phoenix Metropolitan Area (unpublished data).
- In summer 1974, a helicopter applicator left hoses connected after shutting off a pump, siphoning pesticide into the Mainline Canal. The spill killed fishes from upstream of Yuma, Arizona to the USA–Mexico boundary and likely farther downstream (Minckley 1979).
- An oil spill from a tanker truck crash on the upper Yampa River, Colorado was thought to have prevented reproduction by downstream fishes (Woolf 1989).
- Concern exists over a similar spill near Cameron, Arizona, which threatens the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in the Little Colorado River (USBR 1989).

**Mining impacts.**
- Past mining has polluted streams with suspended sediment, toxic heavy metals and acids. Effects depend on mining method, mineral toxicity, extraction efficiency and how wastes, haul roads, tailings and stockpiles were managed.
- Surface mining can alter landscapes through overburden stripping, vegetation removal, road building and stream channelization.
- Abandoned mines continue to pollute after extraction ends. Reclamation is rarely equivalent to restoration to prior ecosystem state, and many abandoned mines are not covered by current law.
- Pinto Creek is cited as a classic example of periodic toxicity from mine-tailings ponds that continued despite known risks (Lewis 1977).
- The San Pedro River has suffered repeated severe pollution from releases at Cananea, Mexico (Jackson et al. 1988).
- In 1993, floods exposed toxic material from a long-closed smelter along Aravaipa Creek near Klondyke. Downstream concentrations increased at the [[George Whittell Reserve]] (The Nature Conservancy) and the Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area. The source text marks the affected constituent as unspecified ("[of what?]").

**Mining regulation.**
- Under ADEQ's aquifer protection permit (APP) program, discharging facilities such as leaching operations, impoundments and solid waste areas must obtain individual APPs. They must use Best Available Demonstrated Control Technology (BADCT) and meet aquifer quality standards based on federal drinking water standards.
- Mines discharging to "waters of the United States" need NPDES permits for process and contact stormwater discharges, plus dredge-and-fill permits for wetlands and other waters. The CWA also requires SPCC plans for large oil storage and reporting of oil and hazardous spills.
- "Waters of the United States" has been interpreted broadly to include non-navigable tributaries, intermittent streambeds and normally dry arroyos (e.g., *United States v. Phelps Dodge Corp.*, 1975). As a result, Arizona mines may need NPDES permits even in remote desert areas.
- NPDES permittees must not cause receiving waters to exceed water quality standards.

**Economic context.** The report cites the copper industry's 1993 economic impact in Arizona: $5.7 billion in total economic benefit, more than double the value of 1993 copper mine production, and more than 58,000 jobs from combined direct and indirect contributions. Total jobs were almost five times direct copper employment. Other Arizona businesses received the largest share, with $3.8 billion in sales revenue forming two-thirds of the industry's total impact (Learning 1994).

## Key Findings

1. **Dispute over historical fish kills.** Phelps Dodge argues that the draft's statement that historical toxic outflows from Chase Creek and the San Francisco River "decimated fishes" and affected crops is unsupported. It cites Lindgren (1975), Rampe et al. (1981), Witcher (1981) and Watt (1956). Its position is that natural hot springs and mineralized groundwater, not mining, caused the poor water quality, with iron, sulfate, TDS, sodium and calcium chloride, fluoride, copper, arsenic and low pH noted. The company requests deletion of the example and correction of the report.
2. **Baseline definition.** Only about 3,630 of roughly 17,537 estimated stream miles (about 21%) are perennial, and the report uses these as its environmental baseline.
3. **Regulatory structure.** ADEQ, ADWR, EPA (for NPDES) and WQARF form an overlapping framework. Numeric standards cover 126 organic compounds and 19 inorganic species, plus several physical and chemical parameters.
4. **Natural and anthropogenic toxics.** Natural ores and soils, accidental spills, historical mining tailings and abandoned mines all contribute to stream toxicity, with documented fish kills and species threats.
5. **Scope of federal jurisdiction.** Broad "waters of the United States" interpretations extend NPDES requirements to intermittent and dry arroyos, a point of significant regulatory consequence for mining.
6. **Economic significance.** The copper industry's 1993 economic impact is substantial, which frames the tension between economic benefit and environmental legacy in the report.
