# Foraging Behaviors and the Interaction of Alewife and Bloater in Lake Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #alosa #coregonus

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by [[Larry B. Crowder]] and [[Fred P. Binkowski]] examines how [[Alewife]] (*Alosa pseudoharengus*) and [[Bloater]] (*Coregonus hoyi*), two common planktivores of [[Lake Michigan]], forage on [[Mysis Relicta]]. The authors measured handling times and probabilities of capture in laboratory tanks, combined these with data from [[Janssen 1976]] on filter-feeding alewives, and built [[Cost Curves]] to compare feeding modes. They argue that feeding mode shifts (filtering, gulping, particulate feeding) follow prey size and density, and that the relative foraging advantage of alewife and bloater changes with zooplankton size. This shifting competitive balance is offered as a partial explanation for the observed population dynamics of the two species.

## Background & Description

**Study species**
- **Alewife** is a planktivore that can use three feeding modes: [[Filter Feeding]], [[Gulping]] and [[Particulate Feeding]].
- **Bloater** can only gulp and particulate feed. The authors note that bloaters are probably more efficient than alewives at feeding near the bottom.

**Background context**
- Alewives became abundant in Lake Michigan after the decline of native piscivores, including [[Lake Charr]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*), caused by overfishing and [[Sea Lamprey]] predation.
- Average zooplankton size fell under intense alewife planktivory.
- The decline of native planktivores, including bloater, has been attributed to competition, though [[Crowder 1980]] suggested predation as an alternative explanation.
- A later bloater rebound and a habitat shift by alewives suggest the interaction is more complex than simple competitive dominance.

**Study prey**
- [[Mysis Relicta]] is the largest common prey in the diets of both species. The authors estimate that the energetic benefit per Mysis may be up to 1000 times that of [[Daphnia]] (Janssen & Brandt 1980).

**Experimental setup**
- Fish were held for 6 months at 15–17 °C before trials. Mysis were collected at night and kept at 4 °C.
- Bloaters were tested individually in 330 L flow-through aquaria with sand substrate. Alewives were held in groups of 2–6 fish of similar size.
- Tanks ran on a 12 h light : 12 h dark photoperiod at 16 °C.
- Fish used in live-Mysis trials were trained for at least one month. Each fish was tested over 7–8 days, and only the last four days were analysed to limit training bias.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Prey–predator relationships**
- Feeding mode appears to depend on prey size relative to predator size, prey density, and the predator's ability to switch modes. [[Durbin 1979]] reports particulate feeders typically eat prey at a prey:predator size ratio of 1:20 to 1:200, while filter feeders eat prey at 1:150 to 1:20,000.
- In darkness, filtering may become more profitable because the visual field shrinks and encounter rates drop (Vinyard & O'Brien 1976).

**Mysis behaviour**
- In the presence of bloaters, actively swimming Mysis were quickly captured. Survivors settled motionless on the bottom, mostly within 3 cm of the tank edge.
- In the presence of alewives, Mysis dropped to the bottom and stayed there, where they were nearly safe from capture. They were actively moving along the bottom, not sitting still.
- In fish-free tanks, Mysis became more active and moved higher in the water column over time. Relatively few sat still on the bottom after the first half hour.

**Competitive balance**
- Adult alewives and bloaters eat different foods and use different habitats, so they are unlikely to compete directly.
- Young-of-year (YOY) alewives and bloaters use similar foods and habitats, so they may compete strongly.
- Small prey favour alewives, which can filter them efficiently. Large zooplankton favour bloater young, which can use bottom-oriented prey.

## Key Findings

**Handling times**
- Bloater handling times fit a power function with exponent 0.60 (regression HT = 0.50 PPR^0.6, n = 31, r² = 0.82). PPR is the prey-to-predator weight ratio × 10⁴.
- YOY alewife particulate-feeding handling times were highly variable. Smoothing across 10 PPR groups gave a regression (HT = 0.54 PPR^0.?, n = 10, r = 0.95) that was a better predictor than the raw fit (r = 0.47).
- Combining alewife data with Janssen (1976) across all three feeding modes gave HT = 0.76 PPR^0.? (n = 19, r = 0.99).
- Minimum handling time was 0.4 s for alewives, based on YOY alewives on Daphnia (average 3.5 s for the first 10 prey, about 0.35 s per prey) and Janssen's data (0.46 s).
- Minimum handling time for bloaters was set at 0.75 s, borrowed from a congener (*C. artedii*) at high Daphnia density. Bloaters would not feed on Daphnia in the lab over a 3-week period.
- Bloaters consumed the first 6 of 25 Mysis in an average of 1.3–1.5 s. Alewives caught six Mysis in under 10 minutes on only 2 of 13 days, with an average of 122.6 s per Mysis.

**Feeding mode and energetics (Janssen 1976 data)**
- Filtering was seen only in the largest alewives (about 30 g). Medium alewives (12.7–22.5 g) gulped, and small alewives (8.0–12.9 g) took individual prey.
- Feeding mode shifts occurred at prey-predator weight ratios where filtering became cheaper per unit biomass eaten. The authors interpret this as biomass maximization per unit time.
- Alewives' cost per unit of mass was much lower for small prey when filtering was included, so small prey were only cheap when they were dense enough to trigger filtering.
- Cost curves suggest alewives have lower costs on prey below about 0.3 mg (roughly a 1.7 mm Daphnia). Bloaters have equal or slightly lower costs on larger prey.
- Field filtering by 50–70 mm alewives was observed at dusk. Prey were *Cyclops bicuspidatus thomasi* at about 0.05 mg wet weight.

**Probability of capture (Table 3)**
- Midwater capture probability averaged 0.72 for YOY alewives and 0.89 for bloaters.
- Bloater capture probability was lower for Mysis on the bottom than in midwater. Larger bloaters had a higher capture probability in both zones.
- In nearly three weeks of observations, alewives captured only a few bottom-resting Mysis. About one-third of their bottom attacks succeeded.

**Interpretation and hypothesis**
- The ability to filter gives alewives a competitive advantage when small prey are abundant, as they were in the mid-1960s.
- When zooplankton are large, bloater young do not suffer this disadvantage. Large bloaters can also consume bottom-dwelling prey that alewives cannot reach.
- The authors propose a **shifting competitive balance** between alewife and bloater. This may explain the declining bloater recruitment in the early 1960s, and the later bloater increase after salmonid stocking and recovery of zooplankton size.
- Stocked [[Salmonid Predators]] may keep planktivore densities low enough to maintain large zooplankton. Under those conditions, alewives and bloaters should be able to coexist.
- Weather may also affect year-class strength, but the authors note these mechanisms are poorly documented in Lake Michigan.

**Limitations noted by the authors**
- Pursuit times and probability of capture were not included in cost curve calculations, because pursuit was hard to measure in the lab and capture probability was measured only for Mysis.
- Actual energetic costs of filtering versus particulate feeding are poorly known. If filtering costs more, small prey would need to be denser than the model predicts before fish switch to filtering.
- Experiments used small laboratory tanks, and bloater gulping could not be quantified.
