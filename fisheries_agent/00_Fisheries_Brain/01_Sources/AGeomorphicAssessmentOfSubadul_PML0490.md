# A Geomorphic Assessment of Subadult Humpback Chub Habitat in the Colorado River through Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This thesis by Yvette K. Converse (Utah State University, 1996; Major Professor Charles P. Hawkins) examines habitat use by subadult [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) along 24 km of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. The study tested three objectives: (1) geomorphic differences among reaches and shoreline types, (2) associations between subadult densities and those geomorphic differences, and (3) how the post-dam [[Flow Regulation]] regime has altered physical habitat. Subadults were most consistently associated with high cover, and vegetated, talus and debris-fan shorelines held the highest densities. The author argues that reduced habitat quality under artificially high base flows may limit subadult survival.

## Background & Description

**Species and status.** Humpback chub were listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1967 and were described scientifically in 1945 from a specimen taken in Grand Canyon. Early surveys confounded them with other *Gila* species, so pre-1970 locality records are unreliable. Of more than 70 humpback chub studies, only 21 appear in peer-reviewed journals; most are agency or progress reports (Appendix A).

**Life history.** Optimal spawning and growth temperature is about 20 °C. Adults spawn on the descending spring hydrograph over gravels. Larvae swim upstream and adhere to bottom surfaces, dispersing one to three months after emergence. Fish mature at about 200 mm total length in two to three years and may live 20 to 30 years. They feed mainly on macroinvertebrates and algae. Adults use large eddies and deep pools, while subadults primarily occupy shoreline areas.

**Population structure.** The Grand Canyon population has two aggregates: a main-channel resident group and a group that spawns in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), where spawning occurs March to June. The Grand Canyon population is the largest and most stable of six known populations.

**Regulatory context.** Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] requires biological opinions for federal actions affecting listed species. In 1978 the USFWS judged [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations likely to jeopardize the species. This led to the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES), and in August 1991 to Interim Flow Operating Criteria.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The reach begins at the LCR confluence (RM 61.4) and ends at Hance Rapid (RM 76.5). It is divided into three geomorphic reaches set by lithology:
- **Reach 1** (Tapeats Sandstone and Escalante Creek member of the Dox Formation): resistant rock forms a narrow, deep corridor.
- **Reach 2** (Ochoa Point, Comanche Point and Solomon Temple members of the Dox Formation): erodible shaley strata form a wider, shallower channel.
- **Reach 3** (Escalante Creek member, Shinumo Quartzite and Hakatai Shale): a narrow corridor again. Reach 3 was added midway through the study, so shoreline habitat data came only from Reaches 1 and 2.

**Hydrology.** Glen Canyon Dam, about 25 km upstream of Lee's Ferry, regulates flow. Pre-dam flows ranged from about 60 m³/s in winter to over 3000 m³/s in floods. From 1962 to 1991, unrestricted ramping kept discharge between 60 and 850 m³/s. Interim Flows (from August 1991) set minimum and maximum releases of 225 m³/s and 550 m³/s, with ramp limits of 70 m³/s/hr up and 45 m³/s/hr down. Water from the dam is 7 to 11 °C. The [[Paria River]] adds sediment mainly during floods. The LCR is a spring-fed, calcium carbonate–enriched tributary with a 10-year flood of 425 m³/s.

**Geomorphology.** Grand Canyon's river drops over 500 m across 450 km, with half the drop in rapids that make up about 9% of the length. Debris fans at tributary mouths create the rapids and eddy complexes.

**Shoreline types.** Six types were recognized: bedrock, cobble, debris fan, sand, talus and vegetation (defined in Table 1 of the source). Vegetation was mainly overhanging [[Tamarisk]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) that stabilized sand after flow regulation began in 1962.

## Key Findings

- **Geomorphic differences.** Reaches 1 and 3 were geomorphically similar and differed from Reach 2. The width-to-depth ratio in Reach 2 was nearly twice that of the other reaches. Rifle area was 4% in Reach 1, 6% in Reach 3 and 1 to 3 to 4 times greater in Reach 2 (reported as 1% in the text; treat as the source states it). Shoreline type effects on depth, velocity and cover were significant (Wilks' λ = 0.198, F = 8.09, P < 0.001), and a significant reach × shoreline interaction (P = 0.003) showed that shoreline differences were not consistent between reaches.
- **Bedrock contrast.** Mean depth on Reach 1 bedrock shorelines (2.39 m) was about twice that of other types (0.30 to 1.0 m). In Reach 2, depths were uniform (0.25 to 0.60 m). Cover along bedrock was high in Reach 2 but low in Reach 1.
- **Sampling effort.** Of 664 electrofishing samples, 173 had concurrent habitat measurements and were used for direct habitat analyses. A total of 69 longitudinal transects were measured (40 in Reach 1, 29 in Reach 2).
- **Cover association.** Discriminant Functions Analysis showed fish-occupied locations had more cover (43% vs. 33%; univariate P < 0.001). Depth (2.61 vs. 2.67 m) and velocity (0.11 vs. 0.13 m/s) did not differ appreciably. Cover appeared to override depth and velocity in channel margins.
- **Density patterns.** Overall densities were highest on vegetated shorelines, followed by talus and debris fans. Bedrock, cobble and sand had low densities. Reach-level estimates were 50 fish per 10 h in Reach 1, 33 in Reach 2 and 92 in Reach 3 (the source gives 92 for Reach 3). The reach × shoreline interaction was significant (P = 0.003 for the model, with the density-related ANOVA reported as significant).
- **Limited explanatory power.** Geomorphology explained little of the total variation in subadult densities (R² = 12%).
- **Discharge effects.** Mean depth and velocity increased with discharge, while mean cover decreased. The pattern varied by shoreline type. Cover on bedrock, talus and debris-fan shorelines fell with discharge, whereas cover on cobble, sand and vegetation remained constant or rose slightly (MSLR, discharge effect Wilks' λ = 0.539, P < 0.001).
- **Flow regime change.** Pre-dam flows were below 140 m³/s 20% of the time; post-dam flows below 140 m³/s occurred only 3% of the time, and below 225 m³/s only 12%. Base flows were, by the author's estimate, two to five times higher than pre-dam levels.
- **Three hypotheses for vegetated shoreline use.** Subadults may use vegetated shorelines because (1) flow regulation has made natural shorelines marginal, (2) pre-dam habitats such as backwaters no longer form or persist, or (3) vegetation provides better conditions than natural shorelines did.
- **Loss of backwaters.** Sand deposits are less extensive and structurally simpler since dam closure. A few permanent [[Backwater]] habitats hold high densities of young native fish, but fish occurrence there depends on water conditions.
- **Thermal constraints.** Historical temperatures ranged from about 7 to 18 °C. Water at the LCR now averages 9 to 11 °C. Bulkley et al. (1982) reported impaired swimming and growth at 5 to 15 °C. The author suggests these thermal limits, together with elevated base flows, probably limit subadult growth and survival.
- **Recommendations.** The author recommends (1) controlled research on high base-flow effects on growth and survival, (2) an operating criterion for [[Glen Canyon Dam]] that includes low releases during critical subadult periods, and (3) management that accounts for compounded effects of temperature and discharge.
- **Limitations.** Diet and food availability were not measured. The author notes that the 2.5 m channel-margin width may be too broad to detect depth and velocity associations with small fish.
