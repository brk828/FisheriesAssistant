# Food Supply and Fecundity in Brown Trout *Salmo trutta*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

T. B. Bagenal tested whether the amount of food a [[Brown Trout]] receives affects its fecundity, the number of eggs it produces. The study used two tank experiments (1966 and 1967) and a small field experiment in a stream. Fish fed more food matured in higher proportions, grew faster, and carried more eggs. The 1967 tank results also indicated that well-fed fish had smaller eggs by dry weight, and that the ovaries of some well-fed fish held more water. The field experiment was too small and too disrupted to test egg output, though it did support the maturity result. The paper notes that stress from competition for food may be the indirect cause of lower fecundity.

## Background & Description

Fecundity studies in fish usually relate egg number to length, weight or age, and they show wide variation among apparently similar females. Food intake was a commonly proposed explanation, but the literature search found only one experimental test: [[Scott 1962]] on [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). Scott started with 5000 yearlings. After three years, only 1618 survived, 7% of them were mature, and 40 females could be analysed across five feeding groups. Scott reported that 60 to 70% of females in the wild would be mature at the comparable age.

Bagenal repeated the work with brown trout, keeping two lots of fish for nine months up to spawning on consistently different rations. Experiment 1 began in 1966 with 200 hatchery fish from the [[Pickering Hatchery]]. Experiment 2 began in January 1967 with about 400 fish. Fish were fed a commercial trout food (47% protein, 49% fat, 29.7% carbohydrate, 1.5% fibre, 10.49% ash, 65% water). Rations were set from manufacturer recommendations, with the full ration at 10°C equal to 8.80 g/kg of fish weight.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tank system.** Circular tanks (1.83 m diameter, 63 cm deep at the centre) were supplied with [[Windermere]] lake water at about 200 gal/h, creating a continuous circular flow. Water supply failures caused problems in both years. In 1966, 26 trout died from lack of oxygen between 11 and 18 August. In 1967, a power failure on the night of 6 October killed 161 fish from tanks 6 and 7 and 12 fish from tanks 8 and 9.

**Disease.** Fish in Experiment 1 died from infection with *Pseudomonas fluorescens* and *Aeromonas liquefaciens*. In Experiment 2, an outbreak of [[Ulcerative Dermal Necrosis]] (U.D.N.) caused many deaths in October and early November 1967, and all remaining fish died before the end of 1967. This disease limited the breeding work.

**Aggression and sorting.** In Experiment 2, the more aggressive fish in each tank took more than their share of food, so the planned replicate tanks no longer received equal treatment. Fish were sorted by size: under 25.5 cm into tank 6, over 25.5 cm into tank 7, under 24.5 cm into tank 8, and larger fish into tank 9. Because of this, the tanks could no longer be treated as replicates.

**Field experiment.** In [[Black Brows Beck]], Lancashire, a stream divided into 12 sections of about 9 m each was stocked with stripped mature female trout from Rydal Beck. The densities were 3, 6 and 12 fish per section, introduced on 30 November 1965 and recovered by electrofishing on 25 October 1966. Of 84 introduced fish, only 13 were recovered. Herons (*Ardea cinerea*) were seen fishing the area and probably took some fish.

## Key Findings

### Tank experiments: growth and maturity

- **Experiment 1 growth:** Fish from tank 5 (full ration) were significantly longer and heavier than those from tank 1 (about half ration). Mature female adjusted weight at 28 cm was significantly greater in tank 5 at the 5% level but not at the 1% level.
- **Experiment 2 growth:** Well-fed tanks 6 and 7 grew substantially. Tanks 8 and 9 (one-third ration) increased in length but gained little weight.
- **Maturity (Table IX):** Tank 5 had 63 of 63 fish mature. Tank 1 had 55 of 89 mature. Tank 6 had 51 of 66, tank 7 had 65 of 85, tank 8 had 22 of 50, and tank 9 had 26 of 65. Figure 2 suggests a direct relationship between food received and percentage of fish maturing.

### Fecundity

Mean fecundity (number of eggs) for mature females (Table X):

| Tank | Mature females | Mean length (cm) | Mean fecundity | Eggs per g total weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (1966, half ration) | 40 | 27.26 | 690 | 21.56 |
| 5 (1966, full ration) | 42 | 29.16 | 791 | 18.70 |
| 6 (1967, full ration) | 43 | 28.39 | 1056 | 23.68 |
| 7 (1967, full ration) | 52 | 29.81 | 1064 | 22.12 |
| 8 (1967, one-third) | 16 | 25.46 | 495 | 23.46 |
| 9 (1967, one-third) | 20 | 25.28 | 573 | 25.13 |

- Fecundity was correlated with gonad weight (r = 0.90 in tank 1) and dry egg weight, and it was more strongly correlated with total weight than with length, which Bagenal attributes to ovary weight.
- In 1967, after adjusting for length, weight and gonad weight, tanks 6 and 7 had more eggs than tanks 8 and 9. Expected fecundity for a 27.4 cm fish was 956 (tank 6), 835 (tank 7), 583 (tank 8) and 685 (tank 9).
- In 1966, the adjusted means for tanks 1 and 5 did not differ significantly. The 1966 regression slope was steeper, which Bagenal attributes to unsorted competition among starved fish.

### Egg size and water content

- Well-fed fish had more eggs at a given dry egg weight. At the grand mean dry egg weight, the fully-fed fish had 870 eggs and the poorly fed fish had 793. Eggs of well-fed fish were smaller by dry weight.
- For a given dry egg weight, fish from tank 6 had significantly heavier gonads than fish from tank 7. This suggests higher water content. Bagenal interpreted higher water content as a sign of riper fish and earlier spawning.

### Breeding and method checks

- Only two females from tank 6 and six from tank 8 could be stripped and fertilized. There were no significant differences in egg size, water, fat or total nitrogen content. Eggs from both groups eyed and hatched on the same days.
- Egg counts from 10 replicate weighings (20 eggs each) in three fish gave coefficients of variation of 0.68%, 0.98% and 2.54%. Simpson's plaice estimate was 4.78%.
- Atretic (resorbing) eggs were very few and could not explain the fecundity differences.

### Field experiment results

- Recovered fish: 9 immature and 4 mature. Mean fecundity at the 3-fish density was 55 and at the 6-fish density was 74. The paper reports individual counts of 47 and 64 eggs (3-fish density) and 38 and 111 eggs (6-fish density), with no significant difference in fecundity.
- Density had a marked effect on whether fish matured.

### Conclusions and discussion

1. More better-fed fish matured.
2. Better-fed fish grew faster.
3. Better-fed fish produced significantly more eggs, even after allowing for their larger size.
4. Better-fed fish produced smaller eggs by dry weight.
5. In two of the three better-fed tanks, eggs had higher water content, which may indicate earlier spawning.

Only the maturity result was supported by the field experiment. Bagenal suggests that competition-induced stress may have reduced fecundity indirectly through changed [[Endocrine Activity]]. He argues that egg size should be measured by dry weight or chemical composition, not wet weight, because wet weight may only reflect spawning time. He also cites [[Svardson 1949]] on a balance between egg number and egg size selected by natural selection. Bagenal notes that this balance applies within a population, since mountain-stream trout lay fewer and smaller eggs than lowland trout of the same species.
