# Endangered Species Subpermit Authorization: Dr. W.L. Minckley et al. (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Cyprinodontidae #Poeciliidae #Salmonidae #ConservationPermitting

## Executive Summary

This document is a regulatory/administrative permit authorization rather than a primary research paper, but it encodes significant conservation and taxonomic data on imperiled southwestern United States freshwater fishes. Issued by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region 2, Albuquerque) to [[W.L. Minckley]] and collaborators (including [[Michael E. Douglas]], [[Thomas E. Dowling]], [[Paul C. Marsh]], and graduate/technical staff) at [[Arizona State University]], the letter authorizes "take," captive holding, propagation, and research activities on a suite of federally listed and special-status fishes native to the [[Gila River Basin]] and [[Colorado River Basin]] in Arizona and New Mexico. The permit reflects the applied conservation genetics and captive propagation research program associated with the Minckley/Douglas/Marsh lab, a key group in [[Southwestern Native Fish Conservation]].

## Background & Description

The letter does not provide direct morphological descriptions, but the taxonomic list implicitly documents species-level and subspecies-level diversity within several genera undergoing active systematic study at the time (reflecting ASU's work on chub complex taxonomy):

- **Genus *Gila*** (chubs) — multiple congeners and subspecies distinguished for management: [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*), [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*), [[Sonora Chub]] (*Gila ditaenia*), [[Chihuahua Chub]] (*Gila nigrescens*).
- **Genus *Oncorhynchus* [=*Salmo*]** — [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*) and [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*), reflecting the reclassification of North American inland trout from *Salmo* to *Oncorhynchus*.
- **Subspecies-level distinction** in [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*) versus [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*), indicating recognized intraspecific divergence tied to isolated spring/oasis habitats.
- [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, now *Ptychocheilus lucius* / [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]) represent monotypic or near-monotypic large-river cyprinid lineages.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), family Catostomidae, is notable for its distinctive dorsal "razorback" keel, an adaptation hypothesized to relate to hydrodynamic stability in swift riverine currents.
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*, incl. Yaqui form) is a small live-bearing poeciliid.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The species covered occupy a range of specialized desert aquatic habitats within the arid Southwest:

- **Large-river mainstem specialists**: [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] are adapted to historically swift, turbid, warm-water reaches of the Colorado River system; the letter specifically references extant **Lake Mohave** wild razorback sucker populations as a source for larvae, eggs, and milt collection.
- **Headwater/tributary specialists**: [[Gila Trout]] and [[Apache Trout]] inhabit cold, high-elevation headwater streams in the Gila and White Mountains, respectively.
- **Isolated spring/oasis endemics**: [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] is restricted to a single spring system (Quitobaquito Springs, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument), exemplifying extreme habitat specialization and vulnerability.
- **Small-stream cyprinids**: [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], and [[Sonora Chub]] occupy small to medium perennial desert streams, and are managed under **50 CFR 17.44 Special Rules** rather than standard ES permits.
- **Captive refugia and non-essential experimental populations**: The permit explicitly authorizes take of [[Colorado Squawfish]] from "captive stocks, refugia stocks, and non-essential experimental populations," reflecting the Endangered Species Act's use of designated experimental populations to reduce regulatory constraints on recovery actions.
- The document underscores the broader ecological context of the [[Desert Fishes Council]] research tradition, in which fragmented, spring-fed, and regulated river habitats necessitate ex situ propagation (e.g., at ASU and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]) as a core recovery tool.

## Key Findings

- **Multi-species permit structure**: A single blanket subpermit (PRT-676811) authorized take, holding, and propagation of **9 primary listed taxa** (Gila trout, bonytail chub, Virgin River chub, humpback chub, Yaqui chub, woundfin, desert pupfish, Quitobaquito pupfish, Gila topminnow) plus separately conditioned authorizations for **Colorado squawfish** and **razorback sucker**, through December 1993.
- **Quantitative take limits** were specified per locality (e.g., 5–20 individuals/locality depending on species), reflecting population-specific harvest ceilings designed to balance research need against conservation risk.
- **Razorback sucker propagation protocol**: Up to 20 ripe females could be strip-spawned (without hormone induction) in Lake Mohave, with milt collected from males; broodstock had to be released unharmed post-spawning. A mortality threshold of **10 individuals** triggered mandatory suspension of all operations and required notification to the FWS Endangered Species Division within 5 working days — an early example of adaptive, precautionary permitting conditions.
- **Larval collection provision**: Razorback sucker larvae could be collected directly from wild Lake Mohave populations and transported to ASU for rearing, supporting research on recruitment bottlenecks — a long-standing concern for this species given near-total absence of natural recruitment in reservoir populations.
- **Regulatory tiering under the ESA**: Species with "Special Rules" status under 50 CFR 17.44 (loach minnow, spikedace, Little Colorado spinedace, Sonora chub, beautiful shiner, Apache trout, Chihuahua chub) were explicitly excluded from the subpermit, requiring only valid state permits — illustrating a two-tiered federal regulatory approach for threatened vs. endangered designations.
- **Jurisdictional limits**: The Region 2 subpermit covered only Arizona and New Mexico; separate subpermits were required from Region 1 (Oregon) for Nevada work and Region 6 (Colorado) for Utah work, reflecting the fragmented, multi-agency jurisdictional management of the Colorado River Basin's endangered fish fauna.
- **Chihuahua chub special provision**: New Mexico Game and Fish requested a **moratorium on wild-population take** in 1993, redirecting research specimen needs to captive stock held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] — highlighting state-federal coordination in managing especially vulnerable populations.
- **Institutional context**: The permit names key figures associated with Arizona State University's desert fishes conservation genetics program, underscoring the collaborative, multi-investigator nature of Southwestern endangered fish recovery efforts in the early 1990s.
