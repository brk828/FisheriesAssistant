# Inventory of Fish Species and the Aquatic Environment of Sixteen Backwaters of the Imperial Division of the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game report (Administrative Report No. 75-3, December 1975, by Gary W. Ponder, Region 5, Inland Fisheries) inventories sixteen backwaters in the [[Imperial Division]], a 63-km (39-mile) reach of the [[Colorado River]] at the eastern edge of Imperial County. Physical, chemical, biological and fish inventory data were collected in 1970 and 1971. The report documents heavy regulation of the river, sediment-driven backwater decline, and the high habitat value of the division's backwaters, and it ranks the backwaters by fisheries value to guide management.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] is one of the most intensively managed streams in the world. Upstream storage reservoirs such as [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Havasu]] and [[Lake Powell]] have converted the historic high winter-spring flows into high summer flows to supply irrigation, municipal water and hydroelectric power. Channelization and dredging have altered much of the river, and the endemic fishes that required spring flows for spawning and protection from predators have declined drastically in the California reach. Several species are extinct and others are listed as state or federal endangered species.

The Imperial Division is the only remaining largely unaltered section of the river in California, apart from [[Topock Gorge]]. It is a low desert floodplain, less than 1.6 km (1 mile) wide, bounded by the [[Chocolate Mountains]] in California and the Trigo Mountains in Arizona. The division is largely roadless, and most of its land is public, much of it within [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]. [[Imperial Dam]], completed in 1938, created most of its major backwaters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and flow.** At an average summer flow of 283 m³/sec (10,000 cfs), the division holds about 2,163 ha (5,500 acres) of water. The main channel covers about 1,306 ha (3,226 acres), and major backwaters over 8 ha (20 acres) cover 1,108 ha (2,739 acres). Flows at Adobe Ruins fall to about 99 m³/sec (3,500 cfs) in January and 326 m³/sec (11,500 cfs) in July. Daily fluctuations of up to 1 m (3 ft) in backwaters with large inlets and outlets can strand fish and damage egg survival. Sudden flow drops after thunderstorms can strand fish or dry shallow backwaters.

**Sediment and succession.** The main channel substrate is mainly silt and sand, and backwater bottoms are largely silt and detritus. Degradation of the upper division lowers the water table, draining backwaters, while aggradation deposits sediment. Emergent plants such as [[Cattail]] and [[Carrizo Cane]] then encroach to complete the decline. [[Imperial Dam]] filled its reservoir with sediment, leaving a storage capacity of only about 1.05 km³ (851,000 acre-feet) of the original.

**Climate and water quality.** Climate is low desert: average precipitation is 8.59 cm (3.38 in) per year at Yuma, and evaporation reaches 244 cm (96 in) per year. Shallow backwater temperatures often exceed 32 °C (90 °F) in July and August. Dissolved oxygen was zero in one stagnant backwater, indicating high BOD.

**Vegetation.** Common submergent plants include [[Spiny Naiad]], [[Sago Pondweed]], [[Water Milfoil]] and [[Coontail]]. Emergents include cattails, bulrush, carrizo cane, and riparian shrubs and trees such as [[Salt Cedar]], [[Mesquite]], arrowweed and cottonwood.

**Methods.** Sixteen backwaters were surveyed for depth (multiple transects, contour maps), turbidity (Secchi disc), temperature, total dissolved solids, dissolved oxygen (Winkler/Alsterberg modification), chlorinity, hardness and alkalinity. Biological sampling used a 61-m (200-ft) plankton tow, Ekman dredge samples and bottom sediment sieving. Fish were collected with overnight monofilament gill nets (0.9 to 8.9 cm mesh) and fyke nets. Acreages came from 1971 aerial photographs.

## Key Findings

- **Backwater decline:** The upper Imperial Division is a zone of streambed degradation, and its backwaters are draining and filling with emergent vegetation.
- **Sixteen backwaters surveyed:** Backwater sizes range from about 2.2 ha (5.5 acres) for the smallest, such as Backwater #11, to about 50.8 ha (127 acres) for [[Taylor Lake]].
- **Shallow, poor-quality backwaters:** [[Velian Lake]] (Backwater #2) is very shallow, has dissolved oxygen at zero, and held only four [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia*), indicating that poor water quality and high temperatures prevent game fish populations.
- **Common fish species:** Gill net catches were dominated by [[Common Carp]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Striped Bass]]. Carp dominated by weight and threadfin shad by number in several backwaters.
- **Striped bass spawning:** [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] (Backwater #14), a constructed reservoir with no aquatic plants, supported striped bass that had spawned there; the report describes the 1967 and 1968 year classes.
- **Best fisheries habitat:** Backwaters with a single inlet-outlet channel on the downstream end and deep holding areas for fish during temperature extremes, such as Senator Wash Reservoir, Squaw Lake and Backwater #10, were judged the most valuable.
- **Management recommendations:** Priority should go to the upper-division backwaters near Velian Lake, where channel degradation and low winter flows reduce water levels. Dredges and heavy equipment would damage the wilderness setting, so the report suggests controlled explosives to deepen potholes, as used successfully at [[Mono Lake]] and near Bishop. Improved backwaters would benefit waterfowl as well as fish.
- **Ranking:** The report ranks the backwaters by assets and problems (for example, Senator Lake had a shallow area and poor circulation; Squaw Lake had good exchange and deep water). A full ranking table appears in the appendix.
- **Appendix:** An appendix lists the locations (township, range and section) and surface areas of all sixteen backwaters, ranging from 1.0 ha (2.5 acres) to 220.5 ha (545 acres) for [[Devil Lake]] and [[Ferguson Lake]].
