# Critical Habitat Designation for Threatened Spikedace (Meda fulgida)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #threatened-species #critical-habitat #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] published a final rule on March 8, 1994, designating critical habitat for the threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. The spikedace was listed as threatened on July 1, 1986, but critical habitat designation was deferred pending economic analysis. This final rule designates approximately 154 kilometers (95 miles) of critical habitat across three river systems: the [[Gila River]] in New Mexico, the [[Verde River]] in Arizona, and [[Aravaipa Creek]] in Arizona. The designation triggers mandatory consultation under Section 7 of the Act for Federal actions that may affect these areas.

## Background & Description

The [[Spikedace]] is a small, slim cyprinid fish less than 80 millimeters (3 inches) long, characterized by very silvery sides and spines in the dorsal and pelvic fins. It is the only species in the genus *Meda*. First collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro in Arizona and described by Girard in 1856, the spikedace historically occupied approximately 2,600 km (1,600 mi) of river habitat throughout the Verde, Agua Fria, Salt, San Pedro, San Francisco, and upper Gila River systems. The species inhabits moderate to large perennial streams in both mainstreams and moderate-gradient tributaries up to 1,800–1,900 meters (5,900–6,200 feet) elevation.

Spikedace occupy shallow riffles with sand, gravel, and rubble substrates with moderate to swift currents, as well as swift pools over sand or gravel substrates. Recurrent flooding is critical for maintaining habitat and provides a competitive advantage over non-native fish species. Due to habitat destruction and competition/predation by non-native fish, the species' range and abundance have been severely reduced to approximately 31 km (19 mi) in [[Aravaipa Creek]], 108 km (67 mi) in the upper [[Gila River]], 57 km (35 mi) in the [[Verde River]], and 40 km (25 mi) in [[Eagle Creek]]—only 9 percent of historic range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The spikedace is listed as threatened in both Arizona and New Mexico state lists. The species was petitioned for listing by the [[American Fisheries Society]] (March 14, 1985) and the [[Desert Fishes Council]] (March 18, 1985). Land ownership along designated critical habitat is mixed: [[Bureau of Land Management]] administers portions as wilderness or Areas of Critical Environmental Concern; [[The Nature Conservancy]] manages sections as nature preserves; [[U.S. Forest Service]] administers National Forest lands including wilderness areas; and private landowners hold scattered parcels.

Primary threats include habitat destruction, water development projects, non-native fish species (particularly the [[Red Shiner]] *Cyprinella lutrensis*), altered flow regimes, sedimentation, water quality degradation, and riparian vegetation loss. Genetic studies indicate that the three remaining populations (Gila, Verde, Aravaipa) are genetically distinctive, making maintenance of these widely separated populations critical for species survival. The [[Verde River]] population is the most genetically distinct, possibly to subspecific or specific level, and occupies habitat with relatively stable thermal and hydrologic regimes.

## Key Findings

**Critical Habitat Designation:**
- **Total designated:** Approximately 154 km (95 mi) across three systems
- **Aravaipa Creek:** 24 km (15 mi) from N½ of SW¼ sec. 26, T.6S., R.17E. upstream to W½ of NE¼ sec. 35, T.6S., R.19E.
- **Verde River:** 57 km (35 mi) from 1 km below confluence with [[Sycamore Creek]] upstream to [[Sullivan Lake]]
- **Gila River:** 73 km (45 mi) total in three sections: (1) 50 km from mouth of Middle Box canyon upstream to confluence with [[Mogollon Creek]]; (2) 11.5 km up West Fork from confluence with East Fork to west boundary sec. 22, T.12S., R.14W.; (3) 11.5 km up Middle Fork from mouth to confluence with Big Bear Canyon

**Primary Constituent Elements:**
- Permanent, flowing, unpolluted water
- Adult habitat: slow to swift flow velocities (0–100 cm/second) in shallow water (3–38 cm deep) with shear zones, sheet flow areas, and eddies
- Juvenile habitat: slow to moderate flow (0–60 cm/second) in shallow water (3–70 cm deep) with moderate instream cover
- Larval habitat: slow to moderate flow (0–30 cm/second) in shallow water (3–30 cm deep) with abundant instream cover
- Sand, gravel, and cobble substrates with low to moderate fine sediment and embeddedness
- Pool, riffle, run, and backwater components
- Low stream gradient (generally 0.5–1.5 percent)
- Water temperatures 1–30°C (35–85°F) with natural variation
- Abundant aquatic insect food base
- Periodic flooding and natural, unregulated hydrograph
- Few or no predatory/competitive non-native species
- Healthy, intact riparian community
- Moderate to high bank stability

**Public Comment Summary:**
- 112 letters received from 109 parties; 78 in support, 21 opposed, 13 neutral or providing economic information
- Three public hearings held (Silver City NM, Safford AZ, Phoenix AZ) with 107 attendees and 33 statements
- Major concerns: water development projects, flood control, economic impacts, non-native fish threats, recreational fishing
- One change from proposal: [[Sycamore Creek]] removed due to erroneous presence data

**Economic Analysis:**
- Maximum identifiable added costs: $150,000 (shared with [[Loach Minnow]] critical habitat, 84 km overlap)
- Primary impacts: FEMA cost-shares for irrigation diversion reconstruction, fencing/water developments for cattle grazing on National Forest, preventive measures at recreation sites
- Local cost-share: $8,412 for FEMA-eligible projects
- No significant economic effect on substantial number of small entities under [[Regulatory Flexibility Act]]
- No areas excluded from designation based on economic effects

**Conservation Measures:**
- Section 7(a)(2) of the Act requires Federal agencies to ensure actions do not jeopardize species or destroy/adversely modify critical habitat
- Federal consultation required for activities that may affect designated areas
- Activities potentially adversely affecting critical habitat include: groundwater pumping, impoundment, water diversions, vegetation manipulation, timber harvest, livestock grazing, mining, urban development, channelization, chemical/biological pollutant release, non-native fish introduction/stocking

**Management Context:**
- [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]] managed by BLM for natural resource protection
- [[Gila Wilderness]], Lower Gila River Bird Habitat Management Area, and Gila River Research Natural Area on U.S. Forest Service lands
- [[Gila Cliff Dwellings National Monument]] along 1 km of critical habitat
- Future revision may add currently occupied areas not included in 1985 proposal and unoccupied areas vital for recovery
