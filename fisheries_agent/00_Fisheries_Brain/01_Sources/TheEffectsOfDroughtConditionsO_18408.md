# Effects of Drought on the Fish and Bottom Organisms of Two Small Oxbow Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 study by Andreas A. Paloumpis (Iowa State College, Ames) examines how a severe [[Drought]] in 1955 affected the fish and macroscopic [[Bottom Fauna]] of two small [[Oxbow Pond]]s on [[Squaw Creek]] in [[Boone County Iowa]]. The south pond was rotenone-treated in July 1955 to remove fish that had entered during a flood, then dried completely by early October. The north pond shrank to one pool and was then frozen solid during a severe winter. The author concludes that fish and macroscopic bottom fauna were apparently eliminated in both ponds. Repopulation depends on survival of organisms in Squaw Creek.

## Background & Description

The two oxbows formed in 1952 when Squaw Creek was straightened during bridge construction. They are separated from the creek by an earthen fill and from each other by a road grade, but they connect through creek overflow during floods.

- **South pond:** about 1 acre in surface area, average depth 3 feet. Cattle use two watering areas here.
- **North pond:** about 2 acres, average depth 4 feet. Cattle have no easy access.
- **Water supply:** run-off, seepage, and flooding from the creek.
- **Substrate:** mainly silt, with sand and gravel in isolated areas. The north pond contained many tree stumps and dead trunks, with bottoms covered in dead leaves.

The fish community was dominated by [[Common Carp]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Bluntnose Minnow]], and [[Fathead Minnow]]. Other species recorded included [[Common White Sucker]], [[Western Golden Shiner]], [[Northern Creek Chub]], [[Central Bigmouth Shiner]], [[Stone Cat]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Orange-spotted Sunfish]], [[White Crappie]], and [[Central Johnny Darter]]. In total, 13 species are listed.

On July 14, 1955, creek water entered the ponds. By July 19 the water had receded and the ponds were disconnected. The south pond was rotenoned on July 19 to remove fish that had entered during the flood, which freed the bottom fauna from fish predation during the drought. Rain in July and August did not restore the water level. By September the area was in severe drought.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Bottom fauna**

- Bottom organisms were fairly abundant in November 1954 but declined over the summer, probably due to insect emergence.
- Rotenone did not visibly affect the bottom fauna. The July 21 sample, taken after treatment, was nearly identical to July 18 before treatment. No dead organisms were found in unpreserved July 21 samples.
- Oligochaetes made up 34.1% of samples by number and 11.5% by volume. No concentration during the drought was observed.
- Dipterous larvae (chironomids, ceratopogonids, and [[Chaoborus Punctipennis]]) rose from 50.3% of samples by number at normal water levels to 92.4% during the drought. This is the clearest evidence of concentration in the remaining water.
- [[Mayfly Nymph]]s (*[[Hexagenia Limbata]]* and *Leptophlebia* sp.) were present in November 1954. A large emergence of *H. limbata* occurred May 24–26, 1955. No nymphs were taken after the May 28 rotenone treatment, though the pond surface was covered with exuviae. Only three *Leptophlebia* nymphs were found in September 1955.
- Dragonfly nymphs (Libellulidae) were absent at normal levels but totaled 15 in the seven samples taken on September 13, 1955.
- Notonectids (*Notonecta undulata*) and corixids (*Arctocorisa* sp.) were abundant in September and were not counted in Table 1.
- Nematodes and clam shrimps (Conchostraca) were uncommon and gave no drought-related conclusions.

**Water chemistry**

- On October 27, 1955, the north pool was 150 × 20 feet with an average depth of 9–12 inches. The water was black, the mud smelled oily, and fish were gulping air at the surface.
- [[Dissolved Oxygen]] was 0.2 ppm on October 27, and again on October 31 and November 8.

**Winter conditions**

- By November 17 a 3-inch ice cover had formed. It reached 6–9 inches by November 28.
- By December 28 the north pond was frozen to the bottom, and the mud was frozen to more than 9 inches.
- A thin ice layer on November 8 may have protected dying fish from predators.
- Spring water returned in early November as springs refilled the pond, and both oxbows were covered with about 12 inches of water by March 1, 1956.

## Key Findings

**South pond (drying and [[Rotenone Treatment]])**

- The pond was dry by the first week of October 1955 and within two weeks no macroscopic organisms were found in mud samples taken to 12 inches.
- Fish stranded in isolated pools died. No fish over 6.0 inches total length were found, probably because predators, including herons and raccoons, had removed larger fish earlier.

**North pond (fish die-off and winterkill)**

- On November 8, 1955, dead white suckers, creek chubs, bluntnose minnows, fathead minnows, bullheads, orange-spotted sunfish, and green sunfish were found on the bottom. Two carp (15.0 inches) were recovered from the surface.
- On November 12, Ekman dredge samples from the remaining pool still contained live bullheads.
- Ice-entrapment counts from 13 holes cut in the frozen pond (18 × 18 inches) were:
  - **Upper 2 inches of ice:** no fish.
  - **Intermediate zone:** 105 bullheads, 7 central bigmouth shiners, 4 fathead minnows.
  - **Lower zone (including frozen mud):** 6 bigmouth shiners, 4 bluntnose minnows, 25 fathead minnows.
- Two explanations are offered for the vertical separation. Minnows may have died first, or bullheads, which gulp air, may have displaced minnows from the surface area under the ice.
- A frozen mud sample thawed in the laboratory contained only dead macroscopic organisms, including chironomid larvae.

**Post-winter sampling (March 1956)**

- Fifteen seine hauls in the north pond collected no live fish. They yielded 55 dead fish: 45 bullheads, 9 fathead minnows, and 1 orange-spotted sunfish.
- Eighteen bottom samples from the north pond and ten from the south pond contained no live macroscopic bottom forms.
- Leeches (*Erpobdella* sp.) appeared in north pond samples on November 9, 1955 (at least five per 10-sample series), with five in the November 10 series and none on November 11. Their significance was not discussed.

**Conclusions**

- The author describes the destruction of fish and macroscopic bottom fauna in both ponds as "apparently complete."
- Fish restocking depends on Squaw Creek overflow during high water and on fish survival in the creek. Preliminary spring data suggest low fish survival, but this has not been quantified.
- Bottom fauna repopulation does not depend on overflow. It depends on survival in the creek, and many taxa have adult stages that can colonize isolated pools.
