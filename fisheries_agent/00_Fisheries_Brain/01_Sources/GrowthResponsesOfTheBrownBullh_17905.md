# Growth Responses of the Brown Bullhead (*Ictalurus nebulosus*) to Temperature

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Allen Keast]] (Department of Biology, Queen's University, Kingston, Ontario) examines how temperature controls feeding and growth in the [[Brown Bullhead]] (*[[Ictalurus Nebulosus]]*). Two age classes, year 0 and year 2 fish, were held for 8 weeks at six constant temperatures (5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 °C) under controlled feeding. Laboratory results were then compared with field growth in [[Lake Opinicon]], a small mesotrophic lake in eastern Ontario, along with seasonal water temperatures and prey biomass.

Ration increased with temperature in both size classes. Instantaneous growth peaked at 20–30 °C. Field growth was highest from June to August, which coincided with peak water temperatures rather than peak prey biomass. Field growth rates were comparable to laboratory values, which the author interpreted as evidence that food was not limiting in Lake Opinicon and that bullheads reached their potential growth for the length of the growing season.

## Background & Description

The brown bullhead is a North American freshwater catfish in the family [[Ictaluridae]]. It is nocturnal (Crawshaw 1975a), and its diet in Lake Opinicon has been studied in detail (Keast 1985). The species occupies the northern part of its geographic range in the study area (Scott and Crossman 1973). Two subspecies are recognised, divided on a north–south axis.

Temperature is treated as a controlling factor for metabolism and growth in fish (Fry 1971; Brett 1979; Brett and Groves 1979). It is also treated as an ecological resource (Magnuson et al. 1979; Crowder and Magnuson 1983). Behavioural thermoregulation and [[Optimal Foraging Theory]] both predict that fish maximise net energy after maintenance costs, and temperature changes the energy left over for growth. Most earlier work on temperature and growth was done in the laboratory. Few studies have linked those results to natural seasonal temperature and prey abundance (e.g., Cox and Coutant 1981; Boehlert and Yoklavich 1983).

### Methods Overview

**Laboratory experiment**
- Fish were seine-netted in late September to early October, at the end of the growing season.
- Year 0 fish (total length 49–78 mm) and year 2 fish (total length 125–149 mm) were used.
- Tanks were 99.5 L continuous-flow units with a 25 cm water depth. Each temperature series was duplicated.
- Initial biomass was 31–42 g wet weight for year 0 and 102–140 g for year 2.
- Stocking densities were 8–10 small fish and 3–5 large fish per tank.
- Water was Kingston municipal supply, dechlorinated by charcoal filtration (67 mg CaCO₃/L, pH 7.2).
- Acclimation followed the [[Fry Acclimation Formula]]: temperature was changed by 1 °C per day or by 1 °C every 3 days. Fish were then held for 1 week at the test temperature before growth was recorded.
- The trial ran for 8 weeks, from November to January.
- Food was commercial redworms (Oligochaeta, 3–6 cm), with 83% water content and 5.07 kcal/g ash-free dry weight. Feeding was in excess. Food consumed was calculated from the difference between offered and recovered worms, corrected for feces.
- Wet weights and total lengths were recorded at the start, the end, and every 2 weeks in between.

**Field sampling**
- Four cohorts were sampled by seine net from Lake Opinicon: September 10 to October 10, May 25 to June 2, July 2 to July 9, and August 2 to August 9.
- Age was determined from dorsal spines, following Martzolf (1955).
- Water temperature was logged 1 m below the surface at 1.5 m depth, beneath shoreline trees in Cow Island Bay.
- Prey were sampled with [[Push Net Sampling]]: forty 0.75 m² samples were taken monthly at depths of 1.3–1.7 m. Variation was tested with the [[Friedmann Test]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Opinicon.** The lake is small and mesotrophic, with a surface area of 7.87 km². Annual water temperatures range from 0 to 28 °C. Water temperature rose from 10 °C to 18 °C between May and June 1, reached 26 °C by July, fluctuated between 21 and 26 °C from July to September, and then fell from 24 °C to 15 °C by October 1.

**Prey community.** The main prey were [[Chironomid Larvae]], Trichoptera larvae, Ephemeroptera and Anisoptera nymphs, isopods, amphipods, and molluscs. The chironomid assemblage contained at least 120 species (Dr. D. Oliver, personal communication). Isopods (*Asellus intermedius* and *A. racovitzai*) and amphipods (*[[Hyalella Azteca]]*) were present at moderate biomass through summer, then increased markedly in September. *H. azteca* has three summer generations, while many mayflies and dragonflies have one generation per year.

**Thermal preference.** In a 9–30 °C gradient, bullheads preferred 26 °C (Crawshaw and Hammel 1974). Their final thermal preferendum was 29–31 °C (Crawshaw 1975b). Based on these values, the species is classed as a "temperate eurytherm" (Hohanson 1977).

## Key Findings

### Laboratory Results

- **Ration:** Food consumption per fish per day rose significantly with temperature in both size classes (p < 0.05).
  - Small fish ate similar amounts at 10 and 15 °C, and at 25 and 30 °C.
  - Large fish ate similar amounts at 5, 10 and 15 °C, and at 25 and 30 °C.
  - Ration increased with fish size at every temperature except 5 °C.
- **Instantaneous weight growth:** Growth was lowest at the coldest temperatures and was highest at 20–30 °C for large fish and at 30 °C for small fish.
  - Small fish grew at a similar rate at 15 and 20 °C.
  - Large fish grew at similar rates at 5 and 10 °C, and again at 20, 25 and 30 °C.
  - Small fish grew faster than large fish at every temperature except 20 °C.
- **Instantaneous length growth:**
  - Growth in length was not significantly different from zero for large fish at 5 and 10 °C.
  - Growth in length was not significantly different from zero for small fish at 10 °C.
  - At 5 °C, large fish grew faster in length than small fish. Small fish grew faster at all other temperatures.
- **[[Conversion Efficiency]]:**
  - Small fish fluctuated between roughly 10% and 13% with no clear link to temperature.
  - Large fish were most efficient at 15, 20 and 25 °C.
  - The author describes efficiency as high across a broad range, with no clearly definable optimum.
- **Survival:** No fish died in any of the 24 tanks. Some year 2 fish at 20, 25 and 30 °C developed *Saprolignia* infection and were treated with chloromycetin. Those replicates were removed because treatment may have affected growth.

### Field Results

- **Seasonal growth:** Growth differed significantly among seasons for both year classes (Friedmann test, χ² = 18.500, p < 0.001). Growth was negligible from September to May and was greatest from late May to August.
- **Instantaneous growth (percent total length per day, from Table 2):**

| Sept 25 – May 29 | 0.03 | 0.03 | 0.02 | 0.01 |
| Sept 25 – Oct 31 | 0.11 | 0.10 | 0.08 | 0.04 |
| May 29 – July 5 | 0.55 | 0.32 | 0.34 | 0.20 |
| July 5 – Aug 5 | 0.20 | 0.38 | 0.29 | — |

  Growth declined with cohort age. The length–frequency pattern stayed stable through the year, so no size-dependent mortality was detected.
- **Prey biomass:** Total prey biomass differed significantly across the season (χ² = 40.416, p < 0.001). Biomass peaked in late May and in September and was lowest in June and July.
  - The June decline is attributed to spring–early summer insect emergence and possibly fish predation.
  - The September increase is attributed to late-summer chironomid, amphipod and other hatches, and to reduced fish predation as temperatures fell.
  - Ephemeroptera nymph biomass peaked in late May and September. Trichoptera biomass peaked in late May.

### Synthesis and Interpretation

- Field growth was greatest from late May to mid-September (20–25 °C). This matched the laboratory optimum for growth and conversion efficiency.
- Field growth rates were comparable to laboratory values. The author concludes that food was not limiting, and that bullheads achieved their potential growth for the available season length.
- Two qualifiers apply. First, prey data reflect standing crop, not production. Second, fish ration requirements should be higher in midsummer, when metabolic rates are higher. The author argues that prey biomass fell in midsummer but never dropped low enough to impede growth.

### Implications for Range Expansion

- Lake Opinicon lies in the northern part of the species' range. Food was most abundant at temperatures below the species' optimal range.
- The author asks whether the species has colonised northward beyond its optimal temperature range, and proposes two possible limits: (i) a mismatch between peak food availability and optimal feeding temperatures, and (ii) too short a period of optimal temperatures to reach minimum growth.
- Southern latitudes have longer seasons and higher water temperatures. These could extend the growing period and raise relative growth rates. Latitudinal genetic variation in thermal responses is unknown for the brown bullhead, but has been shown for [[Largemouth Bass]] (Phillip et al. 1983).
