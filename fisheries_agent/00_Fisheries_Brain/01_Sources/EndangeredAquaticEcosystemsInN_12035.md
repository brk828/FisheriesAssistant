# Endangered Aquatic Ecosystems in North American Deserts with a List of Vanishing Fishes of the Region

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 paper by the Endangered Species Committee of the [[Desert Fishes Council]] (Williams, Bowman, Brooks, Echelle, Edwards, Hendrickson and Landye) identifies endangered aquatic ecosystems across the [[Great Basin Desert|Great Basin]], [[Mojave Desert|Mohave]], [[Sonoran Desert|Sonoran]] and [[Chihuahuan Desert]] regions of the United States and Mexico. The authors list 164 fishes of conservation concern in the abstract (182 in the full table, including 18 recently extinct) and identify 15 ecosystems that each support at least four rare fishes. Habitat degradation, groundwater pumping, impoundment and exotic species introductions are the principal threats. The authors conclude that protecting intact habitats and whole communities, rather than individual species alone, offers the best prospect for long-term survival.

## Background & Description

The paper responds to a long-recognized but previously unquantified decline of aquatic life in North American deserts. Desert fish faunas are depauperate and poorly known, and many Mexican fishes and most invertebrates remain undescribed. The study area covers the Great Basin, Mohave, Sonoran and Chihuahuan Deserts plus adjacent basins such as the Upper Colorado and Upper Klamath.

**Methods:**
- Ecosystem boundaries were drawn using the distribution of native fishes, since basins often hold endemic assemblages.
- An ecosystem qualified only if it supported at least four rare fishes in recent times.
- Status categories followed [[International Union for Conservation of Nature|IUCN]] definitions: Endangered (E), Vulnerable (V), Rare (R), Indeterminate (I). Extinct taxa were included to document the full native fauna.

**Key counts from the rare fish list:**
- 182 fishes in 12 families; Cyprinidae dominates with 84 fishes (46%).
- 46 endangered, 52 vulnerable, 46 rare, 20 indeterminate, 18 recently extinct.
- 52 fishes (29%) are undescribed.

**Ecosystems identified (15):** [[Cuatro Ciénegas]] (Coahuila), [[Gila River]], [[Rio Grande]], [[Pecos River]], [[Railroad Valley]], [[Colorado River]], [[Green River]], [[Pahranagat Valley]], [[Parras Basin]], [[La Media Luna]], [[Ash Meadows]], [[Upper White River]], [[Moapa River]], [[Rio Yaqui]] and [[Upper Klamath Basin]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat types and drivers of decline:**
- Desert springs, cienegas and thermal pools host high endemism, often with each spring or small basin holding its own fish or snail.
- Groundwater pumping for agriculture and cities lowered water tables and caused spring failures, notably at Comanche Springs, Leon Springs and Devils Hole.
- Dams and reservoirs (e.g., [[Amistad Reservoir]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]], [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]) inundated habitat, altered temperature and flow, and blocked migrations.
- Exotic species (e.g., [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Tilapia]], [[Oriental Snail]]) caused predation, competition and hybridization.
- The authors note that the Colorado River basin depletions rose from 0.3 to 4.7 billion cubic meters annually at Lee's Ferry (1896–1975), and that about 27% of average flow at Lee's Ferry had been depleted by 1980.

**Invertebrates and other taxa:** The paper also covers endemic hydrobiid snails, isopods, amphipods, crayfish, mussels and reptiles. Cuatro Ciénegas alone has at least ten endemic hydrobiid snails and four endemic reptiles.

## Key Findings

- **Scale of loss:** 164 fishes (abstract) or 182 fishes (Table 1) of concern; 46 endangered, 18 recently extinct. Fifteen ecosystems support 83 vanishing fishes.
- **Endemism:** Endemism is high, and apart from the Colorado and Green Rivers, little overlap occurs among fish distributions.
- **Cuatro Ciénegas (Coahuila):** About 1,200 km² bolson with 12 endemic fishes (summary list), a polymorphic cichlid species flock (*Cichlasoma minckleyi*, classed Indeterminate), and many endemic snails and crustaceans. The authors urge national park status and unmodified preservation.
- **Gila River system:** Historically held 12 fishes of concern. Many big-river fishes (e.g., [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Desert Pupfish]]) have been extirpated. [[Aravaipa Creek]] holds seven native fishes and is a key refugium.
- **Rio Grande:** Amistad Reservoir inundated the lower Devils River and Pecos habitats. The Amistad gambusia and the phantom and Rio Grande bluntnose shiners are extinct. Big Bend gambusia survives only through laboratory culture and reintroductions.
- **Pecos River:** Groundwater overdraft caused the failure of Comanche Springs (about 102,000 L/min in the early 1900s) and Leon Springs (about 34,000 L/min), neither producing measurable flow by 1962.
- **Colorado and Green Rivers:** Humpback chub, bonytail, Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker persist mainly in isolated reaches. Bonytail in the pure form survives only in Lakes Havasu and Mohave, with adults estimated at 32–39 years old. Cumulative upper-basin depletions reached 5,472 cfs by 1980.
- **Pahranagat Valley:** Five endemic fishes and two invertebrates; the Pahranagat spinedace is extinct; the Pahranagat roundtail chub numbered only about 37–250 individuals (1979–1981).
- **Parras Basin:** Stumptooth minnow and Parras pupfish are extinct (last collected 1880 and 1903); remaining Gila are in imminent danger.
- **La Media Luna (San Luis Potosí):** Area of about 1 km² with five endemic fishes, including monotypic genera *Cualac* and *Ataeniobius*; introduced cichlids and poeciliids pose the main threat.
- **Ash Meadows (Nevada):** Ash Meadows poolfish is extinct. Devils Hole pupfish varied seasonally between about 150 and 400 individuals. In 1983, The Nature Conservancy acquired most private land, later transferred to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].
- **Moapa River:** The Moapa dace (*Moapa coriacea*) numbered about 3,000 (1984–85 estimate), restricted to 3 springs and 3 km of rheocrenes. The Moapa National Wildlife Refuge was established in 1979.
- **Upper White River:** Lund Town Spring is the nearly pristine refugium for three or four rare fishes.
- **Rio Yaqui:** Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*) is very rare; the San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge may offer refugia.
- **Upper Klamath Basin:** Once supported hundreds of thousands to millions of suckers in the late 1890s. Chiloquin Dam on the Sprague River hinders spawning runs and promotes hybridization.
- **Conservation priority:** The authors argue that preserving natural habitats and whole communities gives better long-term survival than artificial propagation alone. They cite Cross and King (1983) on genetic divergence in hatchery Atlantic salmon, and the change in captive Devils Hole pupfish.
- **Recommendations:** Protect remaining refugia (e.g., Devils Hole, Aravaipa Creek, Lund Town Spring, Little Warm Spring), restore flows, control exotics, expand the Moapa refuge, improve Chiloquin Dam fish passage, and fund taxonomic and invertebrate surveys.
