# Effects of Impoundments on Freshwater Mussels in the Black Warrior and Tombigbee Rivers, Alabama

#malacology #unionidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1992 study by Williams, Fuller and Grace, published in the *Bulletin of the Alabama Museum of Natural History* (No. 13), compares freshwater mussel (Unionidae) assemblages in an unimpounded upper [[Tombigbee River]] reach with those in impounded sections of the [[Black Warrior River]] and Tombigbee. The unimpounded reach supported 30 species, while the impounded segments together yielded 19. The authors attribute this loss mainly to habitat alteration caused by [[Dam Impoundment]]s, which they describe as the most important factor restructuring the [[Mobile River Basin]] ecosystem. Of 54 unionid species known from the two rivers, 11 (20.4%) were listed or under review for listing as endangered. The [[Asian Clam]] was present but not abundant and showed no detected impact on native mussels.

## Background & Description

**Historical records.** Mussels from the Black Warrior and Tombigbee were first collected in the early 1800s. Reported richness has changed over time: Lewis (1876) listed 24 species from the Black Warrior system, Hinkley (1904) recorded 29 from the Black Warrior and Mulberry River, and van der Schalie (1939a) reported 50 species from the Tombigbee near [[Epes, Alabama]] and Columbus, Mississippi, of which about 40 were then considered valid.

**Navigation infrastructure.** Low navigation locks and dams built from the early 1900s were replaced by larger dams for hydropower and a 9-foot navigation channel from Birmingham to Mobile. Table 2 summarizes four major structures in the study area:

| Lock and Dam | Year completed | Dam height (ft) | Reservoir area (acres) |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Demopolis Lock And Dam]] | 1954 | 58 | 10,000 |
| [[Warrior Lock And Dam]] | 1957 | 71 | 7,800 |
| William Bacon Oliver | 1939 | 49 | Primarily within original banks |
| [[Gainesville Lock And Dam]] | 1976 | 71 | 6,400 |

The Gainesville structure impounded the unimpounded reach used here as baseline, and the Demopolis Dam also impounds the lowermost Black Warrior.

**Study area.** The Black Warrior and Tombigbee are the westernmost rivers of the Mobile basin. The Black Warrior's upper headwaters drain Paleozoic formations of the Cumberland Plateau, with steep gradients and rocky shoals. Downstream of Tuscaloosa and across the Tombigbee, rivers lie on Cretaceous formations of the upper Coastal Plain, with a gradient of about one foot per mile and sand, gravel and mud substrates. The region receives an average of 52 inches of precipitation annually, with summer daytime highs near 30°C and frost-free periods of 200 to 250 days.

**Sampling methods.** Field sampling took place from 1972 to 1975 at 20 stations:
- **Unimpounded Tombigbee (T1–T6):** six stations between Gainesville and Warsaw, Alabama, sampled by hand, including large riverine mammal middens on shoals.
- **Impounded Tombigbee (T7–T12):** six stations between Epes and Gainesville, sampled by SCUBA diving and by hand.
- **Impounded Black Warrior (W1–W8):** eight stations below Oliver Dam, behind and below Warrior Dam, and below the Warrior Lock cutoff, also sampled by diving in deeper water.

Specimens were deposited in the Museum of Zoology at The Ohio State University. The species checklist (Table 1) was compiled from published records and museum collections at the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia, the Florida Museum of Natural History and the National Museum of Natural History. Names follow Turgeon et al. (1988).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat contrast.** Unimpounded stations were gravel shoals and riffles with moderate to swift current. Impounded Tombigbee habitat had slow to non-existent current over sand, mud or chalk and marl bedrock. The impounded Black Warrior was more heterogeneous, with varying substrate and flow.

**Reproductive disruption.** Citing Fuller (1974), the authors identify disruption of reproduction as the most significant impact of dams on bivalves. Impoundments alter fish communities and can eliminate glochidial (larval) host fish. Even where infection succeeds, impounded substrates and water quality may not support juvenile mussels.

**Sediment and substrate.** Siltation from agriculture, sand and gravel mining and coal mining affects both river systems. Ellis (1936) reported about 90% adult mortality for several species under one-fourth to one inch of silt in laboratory tests. Scruggs (1960) found that the most productive mussel substrates in Wheeler Reservoir were mixtures of rubble, gravel and sand, while clean, tightly packed sand or sand–mud mixtures were least productive.

**Regulated flow.** Irregular discharge from Warrior Dam is a major adverse factor below the dam. Badly eroded shells at station W6 may reflect sediment movement linked to releases from upstream reservoirs.

**Regional comparison.** The authors cite the Muscle Shoals area of the Tennessee River, once the most diverse mussel fauna in North America (about 70 species in 31 genera), which lost roughly 50% of its species to TVA impoundments (Ortmann, 1924; van der Schalie, 1939b; Stansbery, 1964; Isom, 1969).

**Invasive species.** The [[Asian Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*) is considered an exotic competitor for space and energy. It was probably introduced to the Mobile basin in the late 1950s and was abundant in the Mobile River in 1962. In this survey it was present at 12 of 20 stations, but at low density, and the authors detected no damage to native mussels.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness declined with impoundment.** The unimpounded Tombigbee held 30 species, versus 10 in the impounded Tombigbee and 18 in the impounded Black Warrior (19 combined across impounded segments). Station richness in the unimpounded reach ranged from 14 (T2) to 29 (T5).
- **Most common unimpounded species:** *Ellipsaria lineolata*, *Fusconaia ebena*, [[Obliquaria Reflexa]], *Pleurobema marshalli* and *Quadrula asperata*.
- **Twelve unimpounded species were absent from all impounded stations,** including *Ellipsaria lineolata*, *Epioblasma penita*, *Lampsilis ornata*, *Pleurobema decisum*, *P. marshalli*, *P. taitianum*, *Quadrula metanevra*, *Q. stapes*, *Strophitus subvexus*, *Truncilla donaciformis* and *Villosa vibex*.
- **Most productive impounded stations:** W6 (below Warrior Dam, at Hall Shoals) yielded 13 species, the highest of any impounded station. The highest impounded Tombigbee station (T10) yielded eight. Among impounded stations, 11 of 19 species (57.9%) occurred at only one or two sites.
- **Most widespread impounded species:** *Obliquaria reflexa*, present at five of 14 impounded stations. *Fusconaia ebena*, *Leptodea fragilis* and *Lampsilis teres* were each at four stations.
- **Epes loss estimate:** Van der Schalie (1939a) reported 34 species from Epes (31 recognized today). Assuming the 10 species found at other impounded Tombigbee stations also occur there, the authors estimate a 67.7% loss of the preimpoundment fauna. Station T12 at Epes, sampled by diving, yielded no mussels.
- **Species of concern:** [[Medionidus Macglameriae]] was known from two type specimens at Epes, was not collected here, has not been recorded since 1935 and is presumed extinct. Hartfield and Jones (1989b) found low recruitment, with juveniles in only nine of 25 species along 46 transects in the Gainesville bendway.
- **Conservation status:** Of 54 known species, 11 (20.4%) are listed or under consideration. Six are listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1991): *Epioblasma penita*, *Pleurobema curtum*, *P. marshalli*, *P. taitianum*, *Potamilus inflatus* and *Quadrula stapes*. Five are under review: *Epioblasma metastriata*, *Lampsilis altilis*, *L. perovalis*, *Pleurobema decisum* and *P. rubellum*.
- **Species richness of the basins:** 54 species are known from both rivers; 50 from the upper Tombigbee, 48 from the Black Warrior and 44 from both. Ten occur in only one basin, and 21 are endemic to the Mobile basin.
- **Potamilus inflatus:** Absent from most recent Tombigbee collections, possibly due to silt from agricultural practices. The species was found at W6 and W7 in the Black Warrior, suggesting tolerance of some impoundment conditions.
- **Corbicula fluminea:** Present in small numbers and not considered a threat to native mussels in this survey.
- **Recommendations:** The authors call for immediate attention to protect and recover the listed and candidate mussels. They also note that baseline data from the unimpounded Tombigbee are important because the Gainesville reach was impounded in 1976.
