# Consequences of Biomanipulation for Fish and Fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Circular No. 952 (Lammens, 2001) reviews [[Biomanipulation]] by fish reduction in shallow lakes and reservoirs of Europe and North America. The central aim of biomanipulation is not to change the fish community itself but to shift the aquatic ecosystem from an [[Algal Dominance]] state to a [[Macrophyte Dominance]] state. The document covers fish removal techniques, the intensity of fishing, stocking of [[Piscivorous Fish]], 17 case studies, and the effects on commercial fishers and anglers. It concludes that biomanipulation is one option among restoration tools, requires sustained effort, and works best when combined with [[Nutrient Control]].

## Background & Description

Biomanipulation was first applied in small lakes in the 1970s and in large lakes from the 1990s. [[Shapiro and Wright]] (1984) defined it as "the deliberate exploitation of the interactions between the components of the aquatic ecosystem in order to reduce the algal biomass." Early work by Hrbáček et al. (1961) and Brooks & Dodson (1965) showed that planktivorous fish shape zooplankton communities and, indirectly, phytoplankton.

Thinking moved from bottom-up control (phosphate reduction) to top-down effects and [[Alternative Stable States]] (Scheffer, 1993; Jeppesen et al., 1991). Lakes can settle either in a turbid, algae-dominated state with high fish biomass or in a clear, macrophyte-dominated state with a different fish community. Among the manageable variables, the document lists nutrients, fish and water depth.

Eutrophication typically shifted European fish communities from pike and perch as main predators toward roach and bream, with pikeperch as the dominant predator. This shift favoured commercial fishing for pikeperch, while anglers moved from a mix of tench, crucian carp, rudd and pike toward mainly bream, roach and pikeperch.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods of fish removal** (Section 2):
- **Draining or pumping out:** most efficient for water bodies of a few hectares connected to a drainage system (e.g., Lake Zwemlust, Great Linford, Maltanski Reservoir).
- **Seining:** the most common method, used when stocks must fall by more than 50%. Dutch seines reached 600 m.
- **Trawling:** used mainly in Scandinavian lakes; less effective, requiring several years.
- **Gill-nets, fyke nets, combined methods:** used for fine-tuning and for winter or spawning-season fishing.
- **Poisoning:** rotenone was the most common agent (Norway, USA, Poland), but it was not permitted in the UK, and public acceptance was a barrier.

**Intensity of fishing:** Rapid reduction (>70% of total biomass) is preferred because it gives an immediate clear-water phase, especially where Daphnia recover quickly. Slow reduction is used for lakes over 100 ha, for difficult fishing, or where public resistance exists. In Finland, almost 100 lakes were biomanipulated this way.

**Stocking piscivorous fish:** Pike and pikeperch (or walleye) are the most common stocked species. Stocking works only when catch restrictions for piscivores are introduced. Mortality of fish under 10 cm is very high, so stocking is most effective with predators over 10 cm, though rearing costs are high.

**Habitat drivers:** Restoration measures (nutrient reduction, water-level fluctuation, morphological change, fish reduction, predator stocking) shift the habitat from turbid open water to clear, vegetated water. The fish community then changes largely in response to habitat, with fishing playing a smaller role.

**Case studies (17 sites, 9 locations):**
- **Netherlands:** Lake Zwemlust (1.5 ha, 100% reduction, drained); Lake Breukeleveen (180 ha, 60% reduction, failed due to immigration); Lake Wolderwijd (2650 ha, 75% reduction, cleared in 1991, stonewort >50% cover by 1999); Noorddiep and Bleiswijkse Zoom; Frisian Lakes (~10,000 ha, 25–30% annual bream removal).
- **Germany:** Bautzen Reservoir (533 ha, predator stocking, biomass fell to ca. 100 kg/ha after 10 years); Lake Haussee (136 ha, ca. 25% removed, predators >20% of total after 10 years).
- **Poland:** Maltański Reservoir (63 ha; repeated draining, the second phase reversed the first); Lake Wirbel (11 ha, reverted after no vegetation developed).
- **Sweden:** Lake Finjasjön (~1000 ha, trawled, fish biomass reduced to ca. 50 kg/ha).
- **Finland:** Lake Vesijärvi (Enonselkä: 2600 ha, 1989–1994 removal; Paimela Bay: 390 ha, 445 kg/ha removed over 1992–1995); Lake Köyliönjärvi (1250 ha, 414 to ca. 80 kg/ha); Lake Tuusulanjärvi (59.5 ha, ca. 101 kg/ha removed); Lake Enäjärvi (508 ha).
- **Denmark:** At least 16 lakes; in most, 80–100% of fish biomass was removed.
- **Norway:** Lake Haugatjern (rotenone; algae decreased fourfold); Lake Asklundvatn (rotenone, trout stocked); Lake Vikvatn (ca. 460 ha, rotenone, algae stayed low).
- **United Kingdom:** Great Linford (ca. 37 ha, nature conservation for ducks); Ormsby Broad (54 ha, Norfolk Broads).
- **United States:** Round Lake (first US lake), Lake Mendota (4000 ha, walleye and pike stocked), Lake Christina (1619 ha, rotenone in 1987; still clear after 12 years), Lake Denham (Florida, 104 ha, ca. 425 kg/ha removed), Como Lake (29 ha, Minnesota, anglers' catch rose 4–5 fold).

## Key Findings

- **Biomanipulation goal:** shift the ecosystem from algal to macrophyte domination; the fish community change is a means, not the end.
- **Typical outcome:** lower fish biomass, more diverse community, with bream and roach giving way to tench and rudd (Europe), and predators (pike, perch) increasing.
- **Removal strategy:** rapid, complete removal works best in small lakes; slow, sustained removal is needed for large lakes.
- **Stocking requirement:** piscivore stocking succeeds only with catch restrictions, minimum size limits, and careful handling.
- **Sustainability:** many projects reverted to the old state when vegetation failed to develop (Maltański, Wirbel) or when immigration was high (Breukeleveen). Vegetation development stabilises the new state.
- **Fishery benefits:** commercial fishers gain from being paid to remove fish and from eel and pikeperch catches. Anglers initially resist, but gain from more predators, provided regulations protect piscivores.
- **Cost-benefit:** feasible when benefits are economic (drinking water, commercial fishing, land value in Finland). Recreational and ecological benefits are hard to express in economic terms.
- **Decision criteria:** (1) severity of eutrophication, (2) chance of success (lake size and depth are critical; small lakes are easier than those over 1000 ha), (3) effect on present users, (4) support from users with legal rights.
- **Overall conclusion:** biomanipulation should be considered within the whole ecosystem, is only one restoration option, and is most effective when combined with [[Nutrient Reduction]] and sustained effort.
