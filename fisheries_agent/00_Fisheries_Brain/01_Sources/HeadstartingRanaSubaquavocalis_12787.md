# Ramsey Canyon Leopard Frog Head-Starting and Amphibian Conservation Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ranidae

## Executive Summary

This article, written by [[Michael J. Demlong]] of the [[Phoenix Zoo]]'s Living Collections, describes a head-starting program for the [[Ramsey Canyon Leopard Frog]] (*Rana subaquavocalis*), a critically endangered anuran from the Huachuca Mountains of southeastern Arizona. A 1996 census found only about 60 adults, making it one of the rarest frogs in the world. In 1995 a cooperative conservation team removed about half of the known egg masses from wild spawning sites, reared larvae at the Nature Conservancy preserve, the Phoenix Zoo and the Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum, and released 141 metamorphs and 206 larvae in October 1995. The author frames captive propagation as a temporary measure unless the causes of decline are identified. The piece also reviews wider amphibian declines and the shift from single-species to ecosystem-based conservation. The document also includes several unrelated commercial advertisements (infrared heaters, reptile books, CD-ROMs, feeder insects, magazine subscriptions), which are not summarized here.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Ramsey Canyon Leopard Frog]] (*Rana subaquavocalis*), a member of the [[Rana Pipiens Complex]], described by [[Platz]] (1993). Platz noted it calls underwater.
- **Range:** Restricted to a radius of about 6 km in the Huachuca Mountains near the Arizona-Mexico border, making it the most restricted range of any ranid frog species (Platz, 1995).
- **Breeding habitat:** Primarily in artificially made impoundments, none larger than half an acre.
- **Population:** Approximately 60 adults in 1996, down from fewer than 50 in 1995, a 50-percent decline within seven years of the earlier census.
- **Threats:** Small population size, limited distribution, exotic predaceous fish, introduced [[American Bullfrog]] (*R. catesbeiana*) and habitat loss.
- **Status and protection:** A candidate for federal listing as endangered or threatened by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]]. All native leopard frogs are protected by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]]. A conservation agreement (memorandum of understanding) among several agencies and a local rancher provides further protection.
- **Broader context:** The article notes declines of other anurans in the United States, including the [[Puerto Rican Crested Toad]], arroyo toad, Wyoming toad, western toad, Houston toad, cascades frog and Tarahumara frog. Hypothesized causes include UVB radiation, habitat loss and fragmentation, pollution, exotic species, disease and other human-related factors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat conditions:** Wild ponds in Ramsey Canyon are cool, woodland habitats; Phoenix is hot Sonoran Desert. Winter 1995 and spring/summer 1996 were the driest seasons recorded in Arizona in almost a century, drying some streams and ponds, desiccating egg masses and eliminating habitat.
- **Water temperature at release:** Air and water temperatures at Ramsey Canyon were about 50 and 55 °F (10 and 12.7 °C).
- **Predation:** Egg masses were protected from aquatic invertebrates and conspecifics in a galvanized livestock tank 4 ft (1.2 m) in diameter and 23 in (0.6 m) deep, and in mesh cages or plastic strawberry baskets in the wild pond.
- **Larval biology:** Smaller metamorphs show higher mortality and lower reproductive success (Berven and Gill, 1983; Berven, 1990). Overcrowding can reduce growth rate and size at metamorphosis (Reques and Tejedo, 1995).
- **Health issues:** Larval bloating, possibly linked to high ammonia (documented in [[Foothill Yellow-Legged Frog]] by Malfatti, 1996), and a nonlethal kinked-tail deformity in about 5 percent of zoo larvae, which resolved at transformation.
- **Conservation philosophy:** The article contrasts the earlier "ark" approach of zoos with a holistic [[Ecosystem Approach To Conservation]].

## Key Findings

- **Census decline:** Adult numbers fell from fewer than 50 (1995) to about 60 counted in the 1996 census context, with a documented 50-percent decline in seven years; low recruitment prompted intervention.
- **Head-starting intervention (spring 1995):** About half of the known egg masses from two wild spawning sites were removed and reared in captivity, and some larvae were fostered to the Phoenix Zoo and Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum.
- **Transport method:** Larvae of 1 to 3 weeks were shipped in prewashed bags with creek water, 15 to 20 larvae per bag, on crushed ice in insulated coolers, with metamorphs (about 1.2 in, 40 mm snout-to-vent) packed five per bag and ballooned with air. All animals arrived after a four-hour drive without incident.
- **Captive rearing:** Larvae were kept in 10-gallon aquaria, then 3 ft diameter kiddy pools at densities of about 20 to 30 per pool, fed mainly boiled spinach with other greens, and later commercially reared crickets. Water was changed by siphoning about 50 percent every other day, with aged tap water.
- **Mortality:** Captive mortality was 4 percent (one of about 25 deaths noted) at the Phoenix Zoo, similar to rates for other ranids (e.g., Rana yavapaiensis) and captive amphibians such as the Puerto Rican crested toad. Causes were three mechanical accidents and one unknown bloating death.
- **Release (October 30, 1995):** 141 metamorphs and 206 larvae were released at the Ramsey Canyon preserve after a seven-day acclimation in an environmental chamber at Arizona State University-West, which simulated wild temperatures of 70 to 55 °F (21.1 to 12.7 °C) at 50 percent humidity with a 10-hour light cycle.
- **Post-release results:** Up to 15 released metamorphs (identified by toe marks) survived the drought and first hibernation. In September 1996 about 70 more captive-reared metamorphs were released at Garden Canyon.
- **Effectiveness uncertain:** Success will not be clear until after the next spring and possibly many springs later. The author questions whether captive rearing is viable, since no research has shown head-started herpetofauna do better than wild-raised animals (Chiszar et al., 1993), and concludes that returning frogs without addressing causes of decline is "a temporary solution at best."
- **Lessons learned:** The project raised public awareness, built stronger links among zoo, government and conservation agencies, and offered a possible pilot for other anuran recovery programs.
- **Future plans (1997):** Building an in situ conservation center or additional ex situ rearing facilities, modifying artificial ponds into more permanent water sources, removing introduced bullfrogs, and identifying suitable release localities.
