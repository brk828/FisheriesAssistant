# The Effect of Torrential Rains on the Reproductive Cycle of *Rhinichthys osculus* in the Chiricahua Mountains, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #rhinichthys

## Executive Summary

This study examines how [[Hydrologic Regime]] controls the reproductive timing of the [[Speckled Dace]] (*[[Rhinichthys Osculus]]*) in the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] of Arizona. Under normal precipitation, the species spawns in two discrete peaks, one in early spring and one in late summer. Under drought, overcrowded and undernourished populations largely fail to reproduce. The author concludes that the reproductive period is regulated primarily by the [[Solar Cycle]] (daylength), while fresh water supplied by [[Flash Floods]] or snowmelt is the most influential trigger. The author notes that the results are limited by the drought conditions of 1960–1961 and rest partly on sketchy evidence.

## Background & Description

The work is one part of a broader study of fish ecology and life history in the Chiricahua Mountains. It focuses on reproductive habits of the Speckled Dace in relation to hydrologic and environmental conditions. The study was supported by [[National Science Foundation]] Grant G 11334 and carried out at the [[Southwestern Research Station]] at Portal, Arizona, of the American Museum of Natural History. Field assistance came from Frankland M. Gorham (1960) and C. J. Cole and Thomas Ryan (1961).

The species occupies the streams of the eastern slope of the Chiricahua Mountains, notably the three branches of [[Cave Creek]]: [[Main Cave Creek]], [[North Fork]], [[South Fork]], plus [[East Turkey Creek]]. Distribution generally follows the presence of water. The distribution map reflects August 1961, after a year of drought, so it is more restricted than it would be under normal rainfall.

Field observations were made in the week of 9 April 1960, the week of 23 April 1961, and throughout June, July, and August of 1960 and 1961. Fish were captured at least weekly to determine their reproductive state.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Streams flow continuously from November through May, and the three Cave Creek branches are usually isolated by the end of April. In spring 1960, following heavy winter snow, the branches merged and flowed at 3 to 5 cubic feet per second. By mid-June the streams had receded to small isolated pools. About half of annual precipitation falls in autumn and winter, and the other half in July and August, largely as local cloudbursts. Flash floods are irregular in timing and location; one stream may flood repeatedly while another does not.

**Climate.** Precipitation from January 1960 to August 1961 was well below normal, and the waters receded continuously. Rains began on 1 July and continued through 8 July in 1960, and began in late July 1961, continuing into September with heavier and more durable cloud cover than in 1960.

**Temperature.** Daily minimum–maximum stream temperatures in April 1960 were 8 to 15 °C. Temperatures in the year-round window of spawning were judged too low outside roughly April to October.

**Comparative range.** Thermal springs at [[Kendall Warm Springs]] (Wyoming) show spawning from April into September, as reported by Simon (1946), in the subspecies *Rhinichthys osculus thermalis*. Koster (1952) reported early spring and summer spawning in [[Agosia Chrysogaster]] and sometimes [[Pantosteus Plebeius]] in New Mexico.

## Key Findings

### 1. Bimodal reproductive cycle
- With normal precipitation, spawning forms discrete peaks in early spring and late summer, with little activity between.
- The northern range of the species shows a single spawning period in late spring or early summer, depending on latitude and altitude.
- The author argues that the ancestral condition was probably continuous spawning, and that the bimodal pattern is a derived, secondary condition shaped by local seasonal conditions.

### 2. Spring spawning
- On 12 April 1960, a collection of adults was moved to a gravel-bottomed pond at a constant 18 °C. Males were ripe and females were swollen with eggs, but only 1 of 17 females was ripe.
- Spawning began in the pond on 17 April 1960.
- In spring 1960, spawning was intense; juveniles were abundant in all streams after 1 June.
- Spring spawning was triggered by the combination of flowing water, increasing daylength, and rising temperatures.

### 3. Late-summer spawning
- The 8 July 1960 flash flood failed to induce spawning, although it created a durable flow in South Fork. Spawning there began in late July 1960 and continued through 10 August in Section 3. Spawning ceased on 27 July in Section 4 when flow stopped.
- A flash flood on 15 August 1961 initiated major spawning in Main Cave Creek within 5 days. Within 3 days, ripe males were abundant and ripe females common.
- Decreasing daylength, declining temperatures, and flowing water alone were judged ineffective; late-summer spawning "requires extraordinary inducements."

### 4. Drought effects
- Under drought, overcrowded and emaciated populations fail to spawn. The South Fork population in 1961 was crowded into a few pools in very poor condition and showed no spawning.
- Spring 1961 spawning was sparse: in Main Cave Creek, a collection of 40 adults included only 2 ripe males and one likely spawning female; South Fork had only 4 ripe males.
- Juvenile abundance dropped sharply; an estimated 60 spring-hatch juveniles were found between the Southwestern Research Station and Section 10 in 1961, versus several thousand per 100-ft section in 1960.

### 5. Transplant experiment
- On 26 June 1960, 265 fish (about 50 adults) from Main Cave Creek were moved to Section 14 of East Turkey Creek, and 220 (about 50 adults) to Section 15.
- Early-July rains raised flow to about ¾ cubic foot per second. Some spawning occurred in both sections, evidenced by about 40 juveniles in each.
- This showed that Main Cave Creek fish were capable of late-summer spawning.

### 6. Nest structure and behavior
- Nests were usually a few inches to a foot in diameter, in quiet pool areas at depths of 1 to 4 inches. Only 3 of 10 nests were in rapids.
- Initially, dominant males defended sites against intruders. Later, as many as 60 males occupied one nest and hierarchy disappeared.
- Males cleaned the stones with their mouths, producing a silt-free site. A female approached, was met by a swarm of males, and spawned after repeated visits.
- Activity on a nest lasted 4 to 5 days, after which it shifted to a new nest, sometimes nearby.

### 7. Eggs and development
- Eggs were located on the undersides of stones and in the interstices of finer gravel below, never on upper surfaces.
- Larvae remained in the gravel for 7 to 9 days (OCR-uncertain) before becoming free-swimming.
- In the laboratory at 18 to 19 °C, eggs hatched in 6 days. Eggs also hatched successfully at 24 °C. Parent fish came from a 18–19 °C range in South Fork.

### 8. Maturity and fecundity
- Females generally mature at 2 years, but some 2-year-old fish remain immature. The percentage of mature 2-year-olds varies annually; the value for 1960 is printed illegibly in the source.
- Egg counts increased with female length, from 174 eggs at 47 mm to 514 eggs in the largest fish listed, with 482 eggs at 75 mm. The two fish of 71 and 75 mm were 3 years old; the others were 2 years old.

### 9. Regulating factors
- Water availability and the solar cycle are the main controls. A fresh supply of water at the right time appears to exert more influence than any other factor; early freshets are unimportant if they are not followed by others.
- The major 1960 spring spawn followed a heavy snowmelt, while the lower spring 1961 spawn followed low water.
- The author cites Mont A. Cazier (personal communication) that large-scale late-summer spawning follows flash floods, as in summer 1959.

### 10. Summary of conclusions
1. The reproductive cycle is a bimodal pattern driven by a combination of factors, with the most prominent being cumulative water supply, freshets, or flash floods, and temperature.
2. Undernourished, overcrowded populations do not spawn at any time.
3. If summer freshets arrive too early in the season, or not at all, late-summer spawning does not occur, although the frequency of ripe males increases.
4. A single flash flood at the right time will initiate a major spawning effort.
5. After a dry winter, streams in spring are at their lowest, so spring spawning is limited.
6. The lack of spawning in the intervening period suggests that high temperatures may inhibit spawning, and that decreasing daylength is a sub-threshold stimulus.
