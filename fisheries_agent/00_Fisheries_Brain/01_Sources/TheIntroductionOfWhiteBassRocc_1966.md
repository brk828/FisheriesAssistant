## Executive Summary
This 1966 report documents the first introduction of **White Bass** (*Roccus chrysops*, now classified as *Morone chrysops*) into California waters. Following a 1964 feasibility assessment by Chadwick and von Geldern, which concluded that White Bass could enhance recreational angling in large warmwater reservoirs by improving utilization of open-water habitats, the California Department of Fish and Game executed two translocation events into [[Nacimiento Reservoir]], San Luis Obispo County. The introductions involved fingerlings from Nebraska and reproductively mature adults from Oklahoma, with the latter release timed and positioned to encourage natural spawning.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Roccus chrysops* (White Bass), synonymous with contemporary classification under [[Morone chrysops]]
- **Fingerling size class**: 4 to 6 inches (first plant, November 1965)
- **Adult size/sex data**: 64 adults consisting of 28 males and 41 females (second plant, February 1966)
- **Condition at release**: All fish reported in "excellent condition" with negligible transport mortality and no observed post-planting mortality, suggesting the species tolerates air transport and translocation stress well.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Target habitat**: Large warmwater reservoirs, specifically identified for enhancing utilization of **open-water (limnetic) zones** — a niche less exploited by existing California warmwater sportfish at the time.
- **Release site**: [[Nacimiento Reservoir]] (San Luis Obispo County, California)
- **Spawning strategy**: The adult cohort was deliberately released near the inflowing **Nacimiento River**, indicating researchers' intent to exploit the species' known riverine/tributary spawning behavior typical of *Roccus* (Moronidae) life history.
- **Source ecosystems**:
  - [[Lake McConaughy]], Keith County, Nebraska (fingerling source)
  - [[Tenkiller Reservoir]], Cherokee and Sequoyah counties, Oklahoma (adult source)
- **Intended ecological role**: Introduction as a pelagic/open-water predatory sportfish to fill a perceived vacant niche in California reservoir ecosystems, supporting recreational fisheries management goals.

## Key Findings
1. **First introduction event**: ~160 fingerlings (4–6 in.) transported via commercial airline from Nebraska and planted in Nacimiento Reservoir on November 17, 1965 — the first-ever plant of this species in California.
2. **Second introduction event**: 64 sexually mature adults (28 males, 41 females) transported via California Department of Fish and Game Beechcraft aircraft from Oklahoma and released on February 17, 1966, strategically placed near river inflow to promote spawning.
3. **Transport viability**: Both fingerling and adult life stages tolerated interstate air transport with negligible mortality, supporting feasibility of long-distance fish translocation programs.
4. **Management rationale**: The introduction was grounded in a prior formal desirability assessment ([[Chadwick and von Geldern 1964]]) rather than ad hoc stocking, reflecting a deliberate, science-informed approach to non-native species introduction.
5. **Interagency cooperation**: The project relied on coordination across three state wildlife agencies — California Department of Fish and Game, [[Nebraska Game, Forestry and Parks Commission]], and [[Oklahoma Department of Wildlife Conservation]] — illustrating interstate collaborative fisheries management practices of the era.
6. **No immediate adverse outcomes**: No post-planting mortality was observed at the time of reporting, though long-term establishment and ecological effects were not yet assessed in this brief report.
