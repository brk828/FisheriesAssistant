# Razorback Sucker Recruitment Studies in Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This correspondence from [[Paul B Holden]] of [[BIO/WEST]] to [[Glen Gould]] at the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (May 12, 1997, with a follow-up fax dated May 13, 1997) responds to [[Reasonable and Prudent Alternative 10]] (RPA #10), issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], on recruitment of [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mead]]. The letter contrasts two hypotheses for the origin of the Lake Mead fish, proposes field and analytical studies to test them, and outlines a funding and timeline framework for developing a recovery plan before May 15, 2001.

## Background & Description

The letter disputes the assumption that Lake Mead razorback suckers were recruited either in the lake or in the Colorado River above it. The author and [[Jon Sjoberg]] have proposed other origins in earlier reports and presentations:

- **Hypothesis 1 (migration from Grand Canyon):** Adult fish left the [[Grand Canyon]] in the late 1960s as [[Lake Powell]] filled, when the canyon changed from warm and turbid to cold and clear. These fish, about 20 to 30+ years old, would have colonized [[Echo Bay]] and [[Las Vegas Bay]]. Older razorbacks grow little, while younger fish grow significantly, so measured growth could test this idea. Recaptured fish showed growth between 1992-94 and 1996-97, based on only a few fish.
- **Hypothesis 2 (recruitment from original stock):** Fish recruited at both bays came from the original razorback stock, which was fairly plentiful in the lake from the late 1930s to the early 1970s. Recruitment may still occur occasionally. The FWS appears to follow this hypothesis in RPA #10.

The study was run under contract with [[Southern Nevada Water Authority]] (SNWA) funding, with [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] (NDOW) involvement. Field methods mentioned include [[Trammel Netting]], [[PIT Tagging]], and [[Sonic Telemetry]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake level and cover:** Lake level was rising rapidly in 1997. The author suggests that high water flooding riparian vegetation may provide cover that young razorbacks otherwise lack. Work at [[Lake Mohave]], in the upper basin, in laboratory studies, and with other sucker species suggests young razorbacks need rich habitat with abundant cover for good survival, and use it to avoid [[Predation]].
- **Predators:** Minnow trapping at Echo Bay caught several yearling [[Sunfish]], identified as the primary suspected predators of razorbacks.
- **Population structure:** Recent captures at both bays suggest "resident" fish that stay in the local area most of the year and "nonresident" fish that move in mainly to spawn, arriving later in the season (April and perhaps May). Echo Bay and Las Vegas Bay appear to be two distinct groups with no overlap in the areas used, based on sonic-tagged fish locations.
- **Life stages:** Larval razorbacks have been confirmed at both bays. Intensive searches for juveniles were planned. Larval suckers digest quickly in predator stomachs, so this method has had limited success with larvae.

## Key Findings

1. **Competing origin hypotheses:** The Grand Canyon migration hypothesis is testable through growth measurements. The original-stock recruitment hypothesis is the one underlying RPA #10.
2. **Growth evidence:** Growth was documented in a small sample of recaptured fish from 1992-94 to 1996-97.
3. **Cover hypothesis:** Recruitment success may depend on lake level and flooded vegetation, which provide cover. The plan is to compare juvenile captures in 1997 at Echo Bay (and possibly Las Vegas Bay) with the following two to three years, when lake levels will likely differ.
4. **Aging study:** More than 50 adult razorbacks were handled in the first seven months of the study. Scale samples could help determine whether recruitment came in one pulse or several. Scales are considered reliable mainly for younger fish; fish 15 or more years old can be separated from younger ones. Proposed scale reading of fish captured in 1997-1999 would be compared with reservoir operation history, water year type, and weather.
5. **Population structure:** Echo Bay and Las Vegas Bay appear to be two distinct populations, with resident and nonresident fish present in both.
6. **Sampling gaps:** Adult sampling has not yet covered other parts of the lake because most effort went to the two bays.
7. **Predator identification:** The sampling would also describe the fish fauna of both bays and identify potential predators of razorbacks. Stomach pumping or preservation of predators was proposed to look for juvenile suckers.
8. **Funding and timeline:** No funding request was submitted yet. Additional Reclamation funds would allow juvenile searches, expanded trammel netting, scale collection for aging, and more sonic tracking, and would free SNWA funds for other work. Studies would need to be completed by June 2000, leaving a year for plan development, review, and approval, so the RPA #10 plan could be finalized with Arizona and Nevada before the May 15, 2001 deadline.
9. **Collaboration:** The author proposed a cooperative study. Contacts suggested for state agency discussions were Tom Lyles and Larry Riley of Arizona. The letter was copied to Zane Marshall.
