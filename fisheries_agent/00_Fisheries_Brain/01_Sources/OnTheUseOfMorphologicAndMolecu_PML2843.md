# Red Wolf Species Status: Hybrid Origin Versus Distinct Taxon

#mammalogy #canidae #canis-rufus

## Executive Summary

This commentary by [[Robert K. Wayne]] (Institute of Zoology, London) responds to [[Dowling et al. (1992)]], who argued that the [[Red Wolf]] (*Canis rufus*) is a distinct taxon that merits protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Wayne contends that the red wolf's status is not supported by mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or morphological evidence. He argues that its mtDNA lineages are shared with [[Coyote]] and [[Gray Wolf]] populations, that its morphology lies intermediate between gray wolves and coyotes, and that hybrids should rank below distinct endangered species in conservation priority.

## Background & Description

The debate centres on how to define species status. Dowling et al. relied on the [[Phylogenetic Species Concept]], which defines species by uniquely shared derived characters. Wayne notes that Dowling et al. did not address the [[Biological Species Concept]], which defines species as reproductively isolated groups. Wayne argues that no direct method exists for measuring genetic variation in the genes that govern interfertility, so reproductive isolation must be inferred indirectly from morphological or molecular divergence between closely related sympatric forms. Selection may complicate that inference.

Key taxa and literature referenced include:
- **Red wolf (*Canis rufus*)**: proposed by Dowling et al. as a separate taxon; described by [[Nowak (1979)]] as descended from an early Pleistocene form (about 1.5 million years ago) that predates modern gray wolves.
- **Gray wolf (*Canis lupus*)** and **coyote (*Canis latrans*)**: the sympatric canids against which red wolf genetics and morphology are compared.
- **Mitochondrial DNA**: the molecular marker used in the main genetic analyses (e.g., [[Wayne and Jenks (1991)]], [[Lehman et al. (1991)]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hybridization**: mtDNA genotypes in red wolves that are identical to those in coyotes indicate widespread interspecific gene flow. Wayne argues that gray wolf-like genotypes in red wolves, including one otherwise found only in Mexican wolves and another common in northern gray wolves, likely reflect past gene flow between northern and southern wolf populations and red wolves.
- **Lack of barriers**: Wayne states that there were no clear ecological or biogeographic barriers separating red wolves from nearby gray wolf populations before European colonization, which makes a long-persisting subspecies difficult to justify.
- **Hybrid zones**: [[Hybrid Zone]] studies cited (Barton and Hewitt 1989) indicate that hybrid zones in mammals are generally small and restricted, unlike in plants, birds and lower vertebrates.
- **Eastern Canadian canids**: intermediate-sized canids from eastern Canada have been described since the 1970s (Kolenosky and Standfield 1975; Schmitz and Kolenosky 1985). Previous authors attributed their intermediate form to coyote-gray wolf hybridization or to changes in prey size and abundance.
- **Human-induced change**: Wayne notes that hybridization likely reflects human-induced habitat change, and that comparable hybrids occur in other systems (coyote-wolf hybrids in eastern Canada, gray wolf-domestic dog hybrids in Italy, domestic dog-dingo hybrids in Australia).

## Key Findings

1. **Genetic evidence does not support a distinct red wolf lineage.** Wayne reports that mtDNA results show no phylogenetically distinct red wolf genotypes, only genotypes very similar or identical to those of gray wolves and coyotes. He also notes that the sequence divergence between red wolves and gray wolves is less than that among genotypes within single gray wolf populations (Wayne et al., in press).
2. **Subspecies criteria are not met.** Under recent definitions requiring uniquely shared traits (Avise and Ball 1990; Dizon et al. 1992), a mixture of gray wolf genotypes in red wolves indicates they are not a gray wolf subspecies.
3. **Morphology is intermediate, not distinct.** In the most comprehensive morphological analysis (Nowak 1979), the red wolf lies intermediate between gray wolves and coyotes. Nearly all measured features on which red wolves differ from gray wolves approach the coyote condition, and available red wolf specimens almost bridge the proximal ends of the other two species. Figure 1 (multivariate analysis of 15 cranial measurements) shows that suspected hybrids overlap with red wolf positions.
4. **Phenetic similarity is not enough.** Wayne argues that the morphological distinction of red wolves rests on phenetic similarity rather than discrete character states, and that this is circular with regard to species status. He concludes that discrete, uniquely shared character differences are needed before red wolves can be defined as a separate taxon.
5. **Conservation priority should favour distinct species.** Wayne argues that the [[Endangered Species Act]] is vague and that hybrids between closely related forms should not receive the same priority as distinct species. Given limited resources, taxa should be ordered by phylogenetic distinctiveness, with hybrids ranked lower. Preserving hybrids should not come at the expense of the genetic integrity of distinct endangered species.
6. **Unresolved question.** Wayne acknowledges that if the red wolf is confirmed as a hybrid, the case for its protection, and for protecting similar hybrids elsewhere, must be reconsidered with additional morphological and molecular data.
