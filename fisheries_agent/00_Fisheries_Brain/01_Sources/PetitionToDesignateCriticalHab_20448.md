# Petition to Designate Critical Habitat for the Gila Trout (Oncorhynchus gilae gilae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 petition, filed by David Hogan, the [[Greater Gila Biodiversity Project]], the Southwest Center for Biological Diversity and the Biodiversity Legal Foundation, asks the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) to designate [[Critical Habitat]] for the [[Gila Trout]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The petitioners argue that designation is prudent, determinable and essential for recovery. They build on the 1992 review by Propst, Stefferud and Turner, which recommended reclaiming and managing entire sub-drainages rather than isolated headwater reaches.

The petition identifies [[Livestock Grazing]] and [[Fire Suppression]] as the main habitat threats, alongside hybridization with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]]. It recommends critical habitat for sixteen watersheds (nine in New Mexico, seven in Arizona), along with restoration measures such as rest from grazing, beaver reintroduction and controlled burns.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The Gila trout was first recorded by Snyder (1915), then described as *Salmo gilae* by Miller in 1950 from Main Diamond Creek. Behnke (1992) treated the [[Gila Trout]] and [[Apache Trout]] as two subspecies of one species, *Oncorhynchus gilae*. Electrophoretic data from Loudenslager et al. (1986) gave a genetic similarity index of 0.93 between Apache and Gila trout, 0.86 between Gila and rainbow trout, and 0.72 between the Gila/Apache group and cutthroat trout.

**Status.** The USFWS listed the subspecies as endangered in March 1967. The petition states that it faces "almost certain extinction" without habitat restoration. Population estimates show a decline:

| Year | Estimated population |
|---|---|
| Historic (pre-settlement) | More than 100,000 (estimate) |
| 1950 | About 6,367 (five extant populations) |
| 1980 | About 10,175 (average estimate) |
| 1993 | About 2,610 in the wild |

If the McKenna and Little Creek populations prove hybridized, the 1993 total could be as low as 1,660.

**Life history.** Key biological details from the petition and the cited literature:
- Spawning occurs April to June when water is at least 8 °C (Rinne 1980). Females build redds in gravel, usually near cover.
- Age at maturity varies by stream. In McKnight Creek, females mature at age III (minimum 172 mm TL). In Main Diamond Creek, they mature at age IV or V (minimum 133 mm TL).
- Mean fecundity was 143 eggs per female in Main Diamond Creek and 335 in McKnight Creek. The maximum observed was 686.
- Diet shifts seasonally. Simuliid larvae dominate in February, ephemeropteran nymphs in May, and terrestrial insects in October.
- Critical thermal maxima were 28.25 °C and 25.57 °C at acclimation temperatures of 10 °C and 20 °C (Lee and Rinne 1980).

**Distribution.** The subspecies has been eliminated from nearly all of its historic range. All relict and reintroduced populations are now within the [[Gila National Forest]]. Arizona populations are gone: Gap Creek, stocked in 1974, persisted only until 1990 and has been absent since 1991. The petition gives a detailed review of disputed historic records in the Agua Fria, Verde, San Francisco and Mimbres drainages, and of [[Gila River]] tributaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.** The petition argues that pre-settlement conditions were cooler, slower and less silty than today. Historic [[Beaver]] dams and dense riparian vegetation created abundant deep pools, shaded ponds and riffles. Current occupied habitat consists of narrow, shallow, cobble-dominated runs, and the species appears intolerant of flash flooding.

**Threats.**
- **Livestock grazing.** The petition states that grazing has degraded riparian and aquatic habitat, causing downcutting, lower water tables, siltation and loss of pools. It cites the EPA report *Managing Change* (Chaney et al. 1993). It also says more than 60,000 acres of degraded land lie within allotments in watersheds supporting Gila trout, and that nearly all occupied watersheds are in grazing allotments rated "unsatisfactory".
- **Fire suppression.** Roughly eighty years of suppression have allowed fuels to build. The petition cites a pre-settlement natural fire interval of at least once every ten years in ponderosa pine forest. In 1989 the [[Divide Fire]] scoured [[Main Diamond Creek]] and [[South Diamond Creek]].
- **Beaver elimination.** Beaver were trapped heavily in the 1820s and 1830s, and the petition argues that losing them reduced water tables, riparian cover and pool habitat.
- **Hybridization and non-native salmonids.** The petition calls hybridization, together with grazing, one of the two principal threats. Stocking of rainbow and cutthroat trout began in earnest in the early 1900s, and the surveys that might have protected pure stock did not begin until 1950.
- **Isolation.** Small, isolated populations face extirpation through inbreeding, flood and drought. The petition relies on metapopulation theory (Levins 1969, 1970), Lande (1987) and Shaffer (1987).

## Key Findings

- **Critical habitat is prudent and determinable.** The petitioners argue that the two principal threats, hybridization and habitat destruction, are linked to watershed condition. They also argue that the primary constituent elements listed under 50 CFR 424.12 are well documented.
- **Watershed-scale protection is needed.** Citing Propst et al. (1992), the petition calls for "entire sub-drainages with multiple tributaries" to be reclaimed and managed, and says the 1988–1989 losses demonstrate that isolated headwater reclamation is inadequate.
- **Loss of populations in 1988–1989.** Floods and the Divide Fire reduced or eliminated several populations. The Main Diamond Creek population was lost (about 566 fish were evacuated before the fire), and South Diamond Creek was reduced by more than 95 percent. McKnight Creek lost more than 95 percent of its population in 1988.
- **Population status.** Stable populations at Iron Creek, Spruce Creek and Mogollon Creek were noted, along with the genetic-purity concerns for McKenna and Little Creeks. Black Canyon held fewer than 300 fish (Stefferud, pers. comm. 1993).
- **Recommended critical habitat.** The petition seeks protection for ten pure populations (Table 2), eleven populations of undetermined purity (Table 3), and sixteen watersheds: nine in New Mexico (including [[Black Canyon Watershed]], [[Negrito Creek]], [[Mogollon Creek]], [[Mineral Creek]] and Main Diamond Creek) and seven in Arizona (East Fork Verde River, West Clear Creek, [[Eagle Creek]], Wet Beaver Creek, Sycamore Creek, [[Oak Creek]] and [[Fossil Creek]]).
- **Reintroduction sites.** Eight additional New Mexico streams are listed as possible reintroduction sites (Table 4).
- **Agency failures alleged.** The petition argues that the USFWS did not designate critical habitat, despite a 1993 letter stating it did "not plan" to do so, and that the Forest Service has continued grazing where the USFWS had earlier concluded consultation would produce a jeopardy finding. It also argues that the Gila National Forest's Diamond Bar Allotment Management Plan did not address Gila trout recovery.
- **Recommended actions.** The petition recommends removing livestock from unsatisfactory allotments, reintroducing beaver, using controlled burns, salvaging native fish during stream renovation, and avoiding hybrid waters for new stocking.
