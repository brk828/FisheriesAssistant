# Gila Taxonomy Project Workshop Highlights and Recovery Program Status Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum (June 1996, FY96) transmits the highlights of a February 29–March 1, 1996 workshop reviewing the [[Gila Taxonomy Project]], which was funded through the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Program]]. The project examined the *Gila* complex of [[Humpback Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], and [[Bonytail]] using three data sets: [[Morphometrics]], [[Allozymes]], and [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA). Morphological evidence supports three distinct species, and the workshop recommended further allozyme analyses and the integration of all three data sets by December 1997. The group also addressed whether [[Bonytail Reintroduction]] should proceed before the taxonomy is resolved; opinion was divided, with a conservative, experimental approach favored.

## Background & Description

The memorandum, from Dick Wydoski (Propagation and Life History Coordinator), is a status report for interested parties. The Gila Taxonomy Project covered *Gila* specimens from the Gulf of California to the known Upper Basin range. Its components were:

- **Morphology:** completed for Upper Basin *Gila* and published in *Copeia* 1995(3): 636–649.
- **Allozymes:** a report is in preparation.
- **mtDNA:** ongoing, with a target completion date of March 31, 1997.

Workshop attendees included fishery geneticists and taxonomists (e.g., Fred Allendorf, Brooks Burr, Tom Dowling, Mike Douglas, Don Morizot, Dave Philipp, Wayne Starnes, Holt Williamson), Biology Committee members (Gary Burton, Larry Crist, Leo Lentsch, Frank Pfeifer), and Wydoski as recorder. Morphology-based identification guidance from [[Douglas et al. 1989]] (Copeia 1989(3): 653–661) remains the reference protocol for separating the chubs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document frames the *Gila* question in terms of the endangered status of bonytail and humpback chub and the possibility that recent hybridization, possibly linked to altered river habitats, threatens the genetic integrity of the three forms. Key study areas named in the document include [[Cataract Canyon]], [[Desolation-Gray Canyon]], [[Westwater Canyon]], [[Black Rocks Canyon]], [[Yampa Canyon]], and [[Grand Canyon]]. Fish stocked into Upper Basin waters become part of the system, and hybridization could not be reversed if reproductive isolation or behavioral mechanisms broke down. Stocked endangered fishes have historically dispersed downstream, and upstream movement is also possible.

## Key Findings

**Morphology**
- Morphometric data support the hypothesis that the *Gila* complex in the Colorado River system contains three distinct species.
- Fish from Cataract Canyon and Desolation-Gray Canyon differ morphologically from other humpback chub populations and are more similar to one another than to allopatric conspecifics.
- Phenotypically identified humpback chub were intermediate between roundtail and humpback chub but resembled roundtail more closely.

**mtDNA**
- Ancient introgressive hybridization appears characteristic of *Gila* in the Upper Colorado River Basin.
- Preliminary restriction-endonuclease analyses showed that Upper Basin roundtail chub carry *G. cypha* mtDNA.

**Allozymes**
- 85 loci were examined; 24 were used in the data set, and 12 of these were polymorphic.
- 7–8 additional loci that may be polymorphic were not analyzed; Don Morizot was to identify them.
- [[Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium]] testing found 8 of 12 Upper Basin samples deviating significantly at one or more loci. Westwater and Black Rocks Canyon samples each deviated at 5 loci.
- Deviations reflected heterozygote deficiencies, attributed to possible year-class allele frequency variation, reproductive isolation, or sampling error.
- Wayne Starnes had already run Hardy-Weinberg and cluster analyses at 12 loci, but the full data set will be re-analyzed once new data are added.
- Discrepancies in roundtail allozyme scores from the Colorado River near Rifle, CO, prompted a recommendation to verify all scores.

**Recommended Analyses**
- Test the three-species hypothesis using Hardy-Weinberg tests (entire samples and each species) and [[Linkage Disequilibrium]] analyses of polymorphic loci.
- Identify each specimen by morphology before genetic analysis.
- Assess geographic structure using Cavalli-Sforza chord distance, Nei-Rogers distance, Wagner distance, and [[Cluster Analysis]] (UPGMA).
- Use allozyme data from the five Upper Basin sites containing both roundtail and humpback chub.
- Use a minimum of 14–15 total polymorphic loci.

**Management Products and Timelines**
- The work group unanimously supported integrating the three data sets, which requires no new field data under adaptive management.
- The end product is a report with direct management implications, including recommendations for maintaining [[Genetic Integrity]] of three distinct species.
- Target dates: additional allozyme analyses by March 31, 1997 (Task 1); integration and interpretation by December 30–31, 1997 (Task 2), requiring about 8 months.
- Dick Wydoski will prepare FY 1997 Program Guidance, and a scope-of-work will follow committee approval.
- Suggested analysts were Tom Dowling, Mike Douglas, Bruce DeMarais, and Wayne Starnes. Starnes lacks computing resources and is moving to North Carolina; Morizot lacks time. A fishery geneticist with adequate availability must therefore be found.

**Field Identification**
- A [[Gila Identification Manual]] with photographs and field protocols was recommended for biologists new to the Recovery Program. Photographs are held by the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], and BIOWEST, Inc. (at the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] office in Salt Lake City). Mike Douglas holds videotapes of specimens.
- Specimens that do not clearly fit one species should be examined genetically (allozymes and mtDNA).

**Bonytail Reintroduction**
- Asked whether reintroduction can proceed without complete taxonomic analyses, opinion was generally "yes" if conservative and experimental. Two participants (D. Philipp, M. Douglas) favored immediate reintroduction, two (F. Allendorf, W. Starnes) favored deferral, and the rest were uncommitted.
- Recommended safeguards: small numbers stocked where hybridization risk is minimal; baseline genetic analyses of young *Gila* near stocking sites and of stocked bonytail; and a genetic monitoring program covering upstream and downstream spawning.
- Laboratory crosses of the three species through the F2 generation, including backcrosses, using allopatric "pure" stocks, were recommended. T. Dowling suggested completing this work before stocking.

**Original Management Questions**
The workshop agenda listed key questions, including whether roundtail, bonytail, and humpback chub are distinct species; whether intermediate forms are more common now than in past decades; patterns of gene flow; regional differences among stocks; definitive criteria to identify species and hybrids; whether non-lethal methods (morphometrics, protein or DNA markers from superficial tissue, chromosomes) can distinguish forms; criteria for larval and juvenile fish; and the effective population size needed to maintain genetic diversity in wild and broodstock fish.
