# Razorback Sucker: First Five Years and Reintroduction Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report reviews the first five years (1981–1985) of a proposed ten-year [[Razorback Sucker]] reintroduction program in Arizona. Under a [[Memorandum of Understanding]] signed in 1981 between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], nearly 9.5 million razorback suckers were stocked into historic mainstem and tributary habitats of the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]]. Post-stocking monitoring recovered very few fish, and predation by introduced catfishes was identified as a major cause of loss. The authors conclude that management changes are needed to improve recovery prospects.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its decline prompted the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to begin recovery efforts. The 1981 MOU committed the Service to producing up to 100,000 razorback suckers annually for ten years, while the Arizona Game and Fish Department assisted with stocking and monitoring.

Fish were produced at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH) and stocked as fry, as 75–150 mm total length (TL) fingerlings, as one-year-old fish (about 390 mm TL), and as wild adults from [[Lake Mohave]]. Some DNFH fry were moved to the [[Page Springs Hatchery]] (PSH) in 1984–85 for rearing and stocking in the Verde River drainage as fingerlings.

The source's per-drainage breakdown is partly garbled by OCR. The totals above are the clearest figures. Fry were largely stocked into the Salt River and selected tributaries to compare fry and fingerling stocking success.

**Monitoring methods:** Surveys used seines, hoop nets, gill nets, trammel nets, and backpack, raft, or boat electrofishing ([[Electrofishing]]; [[Trammel Net]]). Surveys covered areas near and downstream of release sites. In spring 1985, mainstem reservoirs on each river were sampled for fish that might have survived since 1981, which would be several hundred millimeters long and exhibit pre-spawning shoreline aggregation.

**Tagging:** Fingerlings were nose-tagged ([[Nose Tagging]]), but tagging was discontinued after 1984. Unmarked fry stocked in the Salt River in 1982, and unmarked fingerlings stocked by the AGFD in the Verde River, made it impossible to track the program's marked cohorts.

**Predation and dispersal:** A mark-recapture study of catfish in one stocking area, together with stomach content analysis, showed that stocked razorback suckers move mainly downstream at night and are heavily preyed upon by introduced catfishes ([[Predation by Introduced Catfish]]; [[Mark-Recapture Study]]). Potentially most stocked fish could be eaten within the first few kilometers of downstream movement. Predation by both [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]] was described as extremely high.

## Key Findings

- **Low recapture rates:** River and tributary surveys from 1981 to 1984 recovered no stocked razorback suckers. In 1985, several fish were recaptured:
  - An unverified 430 mm adult caught by an angler on the Verde River near Camp Verde.
  - A 353 mm TL razorback collected by USFWS personnel on the lower Gila River near Phoenix.
  - 16 specimens from lower Carrizo and Cedar creeks (Salt River drainage).
  - 60 razorback fingerlings from backwaters along Bonita Creek, a Gila River tributary.
- **Reservoir surveys:** Intensive netting and electrofishing in reservoirs recovered no razorback suckers.
- **Expected low recapture:** Given that a single female can produce about 100,000 ova, and that natural recruitment in the drainage is extensive, the authors state that nearly 9 million stocked fish is not an exceptionally high number. This is especially true because fry survival is expected to be very low, and most fish stocked were fry. The survival figure in the source is garbled by OCR.
- **Predation is the key threat:** Introduced catfish are the main source of mortality for stocked fish, which points to predator control as a priority.
- **Proposed management changes:**
  - Stock larger fish into mainstem habitats.
  - Stock higher in the drainage, where predator densities are lower.
  - Stock during winter.
  - Remove or deplete introduced predators from discrete reaches around stocking sites, allowing stocked fish time to acclimate before predators become abundant. This must be coordinated with the AGFD to avoid conflict with sport fishing interests.
  - Continue rearing at AGFD hatchery facilities to offset the reduced number of fish needed when larger fish are reared at DNFH.
- **Future research:** Studies at DNFH will evaluate the condition and behavior of hatchery-reared razorback suckers in relation to riverine conditions and predation by non-native fishes.
