# Fish Distribution Study of the Blue and San Francisco Rivers, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study by Tim Hurst and Dr. W. L. Minckley documents fish distributions in the Blue and San Francisco Rivers of southeastern Arizona, based on collections made from June 1994 to July 1995. The authors argue that western North American fishes, which are generalists adapted to high-variance flow regimes, are being displaced by introduced eastern species that thrive in the stabilized, connected habitats created by dams and flow regulation. The Blue River, a relatively unaltered stream, supports five abundant native species and few non-natives. The San Francisco River, which is deeper, more altered, and more accessible, is dominated by non-native predators and competitors. The analysis combines species abundance descriptions, interaction graphs, correlation statistics, and [[Geographic Information System]] mapping. The main conclusion is that local conditions, rather than elevation, control fish distributions, and that larger streams hold more species.

## Background & Description

**Regional context.** Western North American freshwater fishes are far less diverse than eastern ones, with roughly 200 species in the West compared with as many as 600 in the East. Citing Minckley and Deacon (1991), the authors note that almost half of all fish species listed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service as threatened or endangered, or under consideration for listing, occur west of the [[Continental Divide]]. Eastern fishes are described as specialists that tolerate competition, whereas western fishes are generalists with narrow niches and weak competitive ability.

**Study area.** The [[Blue River]] originates in New Mexico, flows through canyons, receives many tributaries, and joins the [[San Francisco River]] near its mouth. The [[San Francisco River]] rises in Arizona, passes into New Mexico, and returns to Arizona in Greenlee County near the mining towns of Clifton and Morenci. The Blue River is described as remote and largely unaltered. The San Francisco River has deeper pools, is less remote, and is more heavily altered by humans.

**Methods.** Collectors, including Paul Marsh, Brian Bagley, Tim Hurst, Terry Inman, Glen Knowles, and others from the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest office, sampled from Raspberry Creek on the Blue River to the western Forest Service border. Sampling used a Smith-Root model 15-A gasoline-powered [[Backpack Electrofisher]] and, rarely, a 6 by 4 ft, 1/8-inch mesh nylon seine. Species counts and habitat data were recorded at each locality. The analysis included species abundance descriptions, three tables and graphs of potential interactions, correlation coefficients, and GIS mapping in Arc/Info and ArcView.

**Species accounts.** The authors describe five abundant native species and several non-natives.

*Native species:*
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*): small, usually under 100 mm, found from sandy desert streams to cool conifer-zone streams. It is a nocturnal, opportunistic omnivore. It is listed as a Candidate 2 species.
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*): 45 to 63 mm, found in headwater streams at roughly 2,000 to 3,000 m. It is described as the only native fish found in all seven freshwater drainages of the western United States.
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*): a large sucker reaching about 800 mm, found at 300 to over 2,000 m. Juveniles occupy shallow swift water and adults move to deeper, calmer water. It hybridizes with desert suckers.
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarkii*): 100 to 280 mm, found in riffles and near boulders. It feeds more on vegetation than the Sonora sucker.
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*): a small, short-lived fish of gravel riffles. It is listed as Threatened.

*Historically present native trout:*
- [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*): historically present in the region but not recently collected in the Blue River. It is listed as Endangered.
- [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*): historically found in the Blue River. It is listed as Endangered.

*Non-native species:*
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*): a widely introduced sport fish that hybridizes with native trout.
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*): an adaptable bait fish linked to the decline of native fishes.
- [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*): a large, predatory sport fish that is the dominant predator in parts of the San Francisco River.
- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*): an omnivorous sport and commercial fish that has reduced native populations in the San Francisco River.
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*): a widely introduced food fish that occurs in waters below 2,000 m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime and habitat connectivity.** The authors' central argument is that flow variability structures western fish communities. Western fishes are adapted to high-variance systems with alternating flooding and low flow. Introduced eastern fishes cannot tolerate high-flow periods and are often flushed downstream. Dams and flow stabilization have removed that disturbance and created connected, stable habitat that favors non-native species. The Blue River's frequent flooding is said to prevent non-native persistence, whereas the [[Lower Colorado River]], described as one of the most altered streams in North America, supports large non-native populations.

**Competition and displacement.** The text describes non-native fishes outcompeting natives for food and space, a major reason native western fishes are near extinction. Several species accounts identify specific interactions:
- Red shiners are believed to reduce longfin dace numbers through competition and to outcompete juvenile natives in marginal habitats.
- Rainbow trout and red shiners are believed to compete with speckled dace for food.
- Loach minnows are believed to compete directly with desert suckers and speckled dace.
- Common carp are described as broadly tolerant and likely to influence most species they co-occur with.

**Threats to native fishes.** Declines are attributed to dams and irrigation projects that remove normal flow variance and create habitat for non-natives, as well as livestock grazing and four-wheel-drive vehicle use that damage riffle habitat. Warming streams are also cited as a threat to Gila trout. The loach minnow now occupies only about 15% of its historic range, with about 2,220 of roughly 2,600 km of historically occupied stream no longer supporting the species.

**Study limitations.** The authors caution that electrofishing effort differed among localities, so abundances at some sites may be underestimated. They also note that correlation coefficients in this western system may appear weak. They chose to interpret them more conservatively than they would in a more competitive habitat.

## Key Findings

- **Native fish richness:** Five native species were found in abundance in the Blue River system: longfin dace, speckled dace, Sonora sucker, desert sucker, and loach minnow.
- **Non-native dominance:** Non-native fishes were abundant in the San Francisco River, particularly in deeper pools, where common carp and flathead catfish were collected or observed in large numbers.
- **Elevation is not a strong control:** For speckled dace, the catch-per-unit-effort (CPE) versus elevation regression gave R² = 0.005, indicating no meaningful correlation. The authors conclude that elevation changes within the drainages did not significantly control speckled dace distribution.
- **Desert sucker and elevation:** The desert sucker CPE versus elevation regression gave R² = 0.033, a weak relationship. Desert suckers were more abundant at higher elevations within the sampled range.
- **Speckled dace dominance:** A regression of total CPE on speckled dace CPE gave R² = 0.884. Speckled dace made up the largest share of total catch.
- **Stream size and diversity:** A regression of stream rank by size against total species number gave R² = 0.378. Larger streams held more species because they offer more habitat and space.
- **Longitudinal pattern:** Fish biodiversity varied along the stream course. The authors describe it as consistent with lower diversity in headwaters and downstream reaches and higher diversity in the middle portion of the streams sampled. Speckled dace were found throughout the reach, and longfin dace penetrated the whole study area.
- **Hypothesis support:** The authors state that the hypothesis of longitudinal zonation in Arizona fishes, with a broad transitional zone between low-desert and upland faunal elements, was clearly supported.
- **Primary control:** Local conditions were identified as the main factor affecting fish distributions. No consistent pattern of environmental change with altitude was found.
- **Historic and absent species:** Several species were reported in earlier collections or surveys but not in recent collections. These include [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], spikedace, mosquitofish, flathead minnow, brown trout, and roundtail chub.
- **Historic collections:** The appendix contains site-by-site collection records from the Blue River, the San Francisco River, and tributaries such as Harden Creek, Coleman Creek, and Campbell Blue Creek. Records include counts for speckled dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker, longfin dace, channel catfish, and flathead catfish. These records are not reproduced here in full.
- **Conservation priority:** The authors emphasize that western fishes deserve high conservation priority because of low regional diversity and high extinction risk. Non-native species are identified as the leading threat to native fishes.
