# Effects of Nonnative Fish upon Stream Communities on the Coronado National Forest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Forest Service-funded report (final version, February 2002, by David H. Hall and Robert J. Steidl, [[University of Arizona]]) documents the first phase of a restoration project in the [[Santa Catalina Mountains]] of southern Arizona. The study compares desert stream canyons with and without [[Green Sunfish]], a nonnative centrarchid, to assess how the invader affects native fishes, frogs, turtles, snakes and aquatic macroinvertebrates. Sites were sampled in a [[BACI Design]] across four periods in 2002 (early May, late June, August, September). Sites with nonnative fish had markedly lower macroinvertebrate and tree frog tadpole densities and no leopard frog tadpoles, while turtle and garter snake patterns were less clear. The report also lays out a schedule for [[Piscicide Treatment]] of nonnative fish and post-treatment monitoring through 2004, with a final report due in 2005.

## Background & Description

**Context.** Nonnative species have contributed to the loss of native aquatic fauna and altered ecosystem structure and function ([[Biological Invasions]]). Effects are especially severe in the riparian zones of the southwestern United States, which occupy a small share of the landscape. The authors cite a recent successful removal of green sunfish from [[Sabino Creek]] using piscicides. Afterward, native [[Gila Chub]] and [[Lowland Leopard Frog]] began reestablishing there, which motivated similar work in other canyons with nonnative fish.

**Study goals.** The project's objectives were to:
1. Map all sites and document seasonal changes in stream lengths and numbers of pools.
2. Determine presence/absence and relative abundance of nonnative fish, leopard frogs, tree frogs, garter snakes, [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] and aquatic macroinvertebrates (Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, Odonata, Trichoptera, Hemiptera, Coleoptera and others).

Species of concern also included [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Longfin Dace]].

**Study sites.** Five canyons and one stock tank were sampled:

| Site | Type | Nonnative fish status | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Paige Creek]] | Creek, cobble/gravel, run-riffle | Green sunfish present (treatment site) | Drainage area 61.4 km²; dense emergent vegetation (cattails, sedges) and shading by sycamore, cottonwood and willow |
| [[Paige Well]] | Stock tank, about 900 m² in drought | Green sunfish and [[Yellow Bullhead]] | Knotgrass stand; shaded by willow and cottonwood |
| [[Romero Canyon]] | Steep bedrock/boulder canyon | Fish only in Lower Romero (treatment site) | Drainage area 18.3 km²; 1.75 m natural falls isolates Upper Romero; 5 km reach surveyed |
| [[Cargodera Canyon]] | Steep bedrock/boulder canyon | Free of sunfish (control) | Drainage area 9.5 km² |
| [[Montrose Canyon]] | Steep bedrock/boulder canyon | Free of sunfish (control) | Drainage area 10.5 km² |
| [[Buckhorn Spring]] | Steep bedrock/boulder canyon | Free of sunfish (control) | Drainage area 4.7 km² |

Romero Canyon was split into Lower (with nonnative fish) and Upper (without) sections. The falls prevent fish from moving upstream. Cargodera, Montrose and Buckhorn served as spatial and temporal controls.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and habitat.** Most canyons are steep and drain bedrock, boulders and cobble, with cascades, runs, step pools and tinajas (small bedrock pools). Pools have little vegetation or soil bank development. Drought conditions were severe during summer and fall. Paige Creek's perennial section shrank to a 1-km reach plus a 300-m reach upstream from Clopton ranch, and Romero Creek was intermittent with most standing water in more than 60 perennial pools.

**Sampling methods.** The team used relative density (mean number of organisms caught or mean capture rate, number caught per hour) to compare sites. Each stream had 3 to 12 pools sampled, depending on drought. The methods included:
- **Baited-hoop traps** (2.5 cm mesh) for Sonoran mud turtles and large fish (>79 mm standard length).
- **Minnow traps** (two per pool) for frog larvae, small fish (<79 mm SL) and hatchling turtles.
- **Dip-net sampling** of macroinvertebrates across bank, mid-column and bottom areas, each netted for 1 m.
- **Time-constrained visual-encounter surveys** of 100-m reaches for adult frogs and snakes, recording weather and time of day.
- **Angler counts** at the three fish sites to track [[Sport Fishing]].

## Key Findings

**Nonnative fish distribution.** Green sunfish were found in Paige Creek, Paige Well and Lower Romero Canyon. Substantial numbers were present in the Hell's Gate reach of Paige Creek, which was surveyed in early May to check a 2000 Arizona Game and Fish Department report. Yellow bullhead occurred in Paige Well and in middle sections of Lower Romero. No native fish were found at any site. Some isolated pools in Paige Creek had no fish, but these were often adjacent to pools that did.

**Sunfish densities.** Green sunfish densities were similar across sites and sampling periods. Paige Creek had the highest densities, followed by Lower Romero, and Paige Well had the lowest.

**Macroinvertebrates.** Macroinvertebrate densities were three to six times higher at sites without fish. Densities tracked seasonal flow changes in Paige Creek. Lower Romero had consistently low densities despite a flow regime similar to Paige Creek. The authors found it surprising that Paige Creek, which has more nonnative predators, also had more prey, possibly because its greater cover offers refuge from predation.

**Sonoran mud turtles.** Relative densities were apparently unaffected by fish presence. The proportion of juvenile turtles was about 10% higher at sites without fish (χ² = 8.7, P < 0.03). Hatchlings were trapped at sites without fish (n = 5) and at sites with fish (n = 3). No hatchlings were captured in Lower Romero, while four were captured in Upper Romero, despite equal effort. Size distributions also differed between Upper Romero (n = 78) and Lower Romero (n = 52). The authors suggest competition with sunfish, rather than predation, may explain the difference, since hatchling turtles are known to evade predators like [[Largemouth Bass]] through antipredator behavior.

**Leopard frogs.** Sites with fish had no leopard frog tadpoles and few adults. Adults appeared at fish sites only in August and September, and all were found in Lower Romero near the fish barrier. The authors propose that dispersing frogs from upper Romero or Montrose may pass through fish areas during the summer monsoon, but they likely do not breed successfully there.

**Tree frogs.** Tree frog tadpole densities were lower at fish sites in every month except September. Adult tree frog densities were consistently lower at fish sites.

**Garter snakes.** No consistent density differences were found between sites with and without fish.

**Sport fishing.** Sport fishing was observed once in Romero Canyon, by a single angler. None was seen at Paige Creek, though tackle was found around Paige Well.

**Conclusion and implications.** The authors describe the association between nonnative fish and reduced native aquatic fauna as a strong pattern. Whether fish cause these patterns will be tested after treatment. The authors expect densities of native aquatic fauna to increase once nonnative fish are removed.

**Future work and schedule.**
- Pre-treatment monitoring continues through May 2002 at Romero, Paige Creek and Paige Well.
- Piscicide treatment of those three sites occurs in mid- to late June 2002, timed to low summer flow.
- Post-treatment sampling occurs in late June, late July and early August.
- Bear Canyon and [[Canada del Oro]] are proposed for future treatment. Pre-treatment monitoring starts in May 2002, with treatment in late June 2003.
- All sites are sampled in early May, late June, early August and late September of each year, with monitoring continuing through 2004.
- The final report and presentations are due in 2005.
