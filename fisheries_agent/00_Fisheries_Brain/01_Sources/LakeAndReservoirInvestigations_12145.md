# Lake and Reservoir Investigations: Arrowrock and Lucky Peak Reservoirs, Idaho (1965–1968)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #reservoir-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Federal Aid to Fish Restoration report documents population dynamics, species composition, limnological conditions, and management strategies for [[Arrowrock Reservoir]] and [[Lucky Peak Reservoir]] on the [[Boise River]], Idaho, from July 1965 through February 1968. Extensive gill netting revealed that the fish population in [[Arrowrock Reservoir]] remained stable in 1965 and 1966 but changed drastically in 1967 following an extreme drawdown in fall 1966. Catch per net-hour decreased from 6.8 in 1966 to 1.8 in 1967. The percentage of rough fish dropped from 93% in 1966 to 66% in 1967, while [[Rainbow Trout]] increased from 3% to 18% due to continued hatchery stocking. [[Coho Salmon]] fingerlings introduced in April 1967 grew to nearly eight inches by September, comprising 16% of total catch. [[Lucky Peak Reservoir]] maintained a stable fish population since 1964. Limnological investigations found dissolved oxygen, temperature profiles, and zooplankton abundance suitable for game fish production except during the 1966 Arrowrock drawdown, which caused oxygen deficiency and fish mortality. Food habit studies showed suckers fed primarily on zooplankton while trout, squawfish, and yellow perch were primarily piscivorous.

## Background & Description

[[Arrowrock Reservoir]] and [[Lucky Peak Reservoir]] are managed impoundments on the [[Boise River]] system in Idaho. Arrowrock is fed by the [[Middle Fork Boise River]] and [[South Fork Boise River]], with minor tributaries including [[Willow Creek]], [[Rattlesnake Creek]], and [[Cottonwood Creek]]. [[Lucky Peak Reservoir]] receives inflow from Arrowrock outflow and [[Mores Creek]]. Both reservoirs are subject to variable water levels due to irrigation and water supply demands.

The fish community in both reservoirs includes game species—[[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Dolly Varden]] (*Salvelinus malma*), [[Mountain Whitefish]] (*Prosopium williamsoni*), [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), and [[Coho Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*)—and nongame species including [[Largescale Sucker]] (*Catostomus macrocheilus*), [[Bridgelip Sucker]] (*Catostomus columbianus*), [[Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus oregonensis*), [[Longnose Dace]] (*Rhinichthys cataractae*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Tui Chub]] (*Siphateles bicolor*), [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*), [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus*), [[Chiselmouth]] (*Acrocheilus alutaceus*), and sculpins (*Cottus* sp.).

Management historically involved large plants of rainbow trout fry and fingerlings. By 1967, stocking shifted to catchable-size rainbow trout and fingerling coho salmon in both reservoirs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Arrowrock Reservoir Storage Volumes (acre-feet):**
- 1965: Maximum 291,600; Minimum 79,800
- 1966: Maximum 286,000; Minimum 940
- 1967: Maximum 283,500; Minimum 1,120

The extreme drawdown in fall 1966 to 940 acre-feet caused significant ecological disruption. Dissolved oxygen concentrations dropped below 3 ppm at low pool in October 1966, resulting in a documented fish kill. Most surviving fish migrated through the bottom outlet into [[Lucky Peak Reservoir]].

**Water Temperature:** Arrowrock stratifies beginning in April and becomes nearly homothermal by October. Maximum peak surface temperature was 24°C (76°F). Lucky Peak remains nearly homothermal year-round due to high inflow and outflow, with maximum surface temperature of 21°C (71°F).

**Water Chemistry:** Both reservoirs exhibit similar water quality—pH near neutral (6.8–7.5), low total dissolved solids (5.9–104.0 ppm), low ortho-phosphates (0.10–1.10 ppm), and low alkalinity (24–68 ppm CaCO₃) and hardness (24–40 ppm CaCO₃).

**Zooplankton:** [[Daphnia]] was the most abundant zooplankter in both reservoirs, present in concentrations exceeding 10 organisms per liter from May to September, with peak abundance in May and June. During low pool periods, zooplankton populations nearly disappeared.

**Tributary Spawning:** The [[Middle Fork Boise River]] and [[South Fork Boise River]] are the primary spawning areas for Arrowrock. Explosive sampling of the Middle Fork in July–August 1967 found predominantly large adult suckers (15 largescale, 26 bridgelip, 1 mountain sucker) and only 2 rainbow trout (both hatchery fish) among 168 total fish. [[Mores Creek]], the main tributary to Lucky Peak, contains rainbow trout, whitefish, and rough fish, with known spawning runs of suckers and squawfish.

## Key Findings

**Species Composition Changes in Arrowrock Reservoir:**

Gill nets were fished for 828 total hours across 1965–1967. In 1965, catch per net-hour was 3.2 (1,212 fish in 378 net-hours). In 1966, catch per net-hour increased to 6.8 (1,743 fish in 256 net-hours), with suckers comprising 64%, redside shiners 32%, squawfish 4%, yellow perch 4%, and rainbow trout 3%. In 1967, following the extreme drawdown, catch per net-hour dropped to 1.8 (345 fish in 194 net-hours). Species composition shifted dramatically: suckers 49%, rainbow trout 18%, coho salmon 16%, redside shiners 13%, squawfish 4%, yellow perch <1%.

**Pre-Drawdown Concentration (1967):** As water levels receded in summer 1967, fish concentrated near the dam. Gill nets near the dam caught salmonids almost exclusively, while nets in the upper South Fork arm caught predominantly suckers. By August 18, catch rates reached 4.87 fish per net-hour near the dam, with rainbow trout and coho comprising 63% of the catch.

**Post-Drawdown Recovery:** Gill netting during reservoir refill (October–November 1967) yielded only 3 fish in 53.5 net-hours (0.06 fish per net-hour): 1 bridgelip sucker, 1 Dolly Varden, 1 redside shiner. No fish kill was observed at low pool in 1967, indicating most fish migrated through the dam into Lucky Peak.

**Lucky Peak Reservoir Stability:**

Comparison of 1962 explosive sampling (Webb 1964) and 1964/1967 gill netting showed increased squawfish abundance. In 1962, estimated composition was suckers 43%, redside shiners 42%, squawfish 11%. By 1967, gill net catches showed squawfish 36%, suckers 48%, redside shiners 12%. Coho salmon fingerlings planted in April 1967 comprised 2% of catches by fall.

**Food Habits:**

Analysis of 356 fish stomachs from Arrowrock in 1966 revealed distinct feeding strategies:
- [[Largescale Sucker]]: 76% zooplankton, 8% insects, 0% fish, 16% miscellaneous
- [[Bridgelip Sucker]]: 89% zooplankton, 1% insects, 1% fish, 9% miscellaneous
- [[Squawfish]]: 13% zooplankton, 1% insects, 84% fish, 2% miscellaneous
- [[Rainbow Trout]]: 15% zooplankton, 8% insects, 71% fish, 6% miscellaneous
- [[Yellow Perch]]: 0% zooplankton, 0% insects, 60% fish, 40% miscellaneous
- [[Chiselmouth]]: 100% zooplankton

In Lucky Peak (1967), analysis of 16 rainbow trout and 15 coho salmon showed both species fed primarily on zooplankton (54% and 69% respectively), with fish comprising 25% and 14% of diet.

**Fish Condition:**

Mid-August 1967 condition factors (K) for Arrowrock fish: 23 rainbow trout averaged 226 mm length, 137 g weight, K=1.2; 37 coho averaged 169 mm, 48 g, K=1.0. These values indicate good fish condition despite the recent drawdown.

**Stocking History:**

Arrowrock received variable plantings from 1950–1967, ranging from 50,100 rainbow fry (1950) to 234,500 fingerlings (1959). In 1967, only 6,000 catchable rainbow and 60,000 coho fingerlings were planted. Lucky Peak received similarly variable plantings from 1954–1967, with 1967 stocking of 32,600 catchable rainbow and 40,000 coho fingerlings.

**Management Recommendations:**

Two alternative strategies proposed:

1. **Annual Drawdown Strategy:** Draw Arrowrock to approximately 1,000 acre-feet annually to reduce rough fish populations. Plankton populations rebound rapidly, providing abundant food for fingerling trout or coho planted each spring. Fish would grow to 8–9 inches by fall and drift into Lucky Peak as water volume decreases, with higher survival rates than current 2–3 inch fingerlings. Requires large-scale mark-and-recovery testing.

2. **High Winter Pool Strategy:** Maintain high water levels through winter to support catchable-size trout stocking, though high costs and low angling pressure make this questionable.

**Limnological Conditions:**

Dissolved oxygen remained near saturation in both reservoirs except during the fall 1966 Arrowrock drawdown. Lucky Peak maintained near-saturation oxygen levels at all times in 1966–1967. Temperature profiles remained suitable for coldwater fish production throughout the study period. Chemical analyses confirmed water quality adequate for fish survival and growth. Recommendation made to discontinue limnological investigations as sufficient baseline data collected.
