# The Kentucky Naturalist, Vol. I, No. 1 (Summer 1946)

#ecology #botany #geology

## Executive Summary

This inaugural issue of *The Kentucky Naturalist*, journal of the Kentucky Society of Natural History (Louisville), sets out the Society's aims and reports on natural history in Kentucky. Its main research content is Harvey B. Lovell's survey of plant succession and wildlife at the [[Otter Creek Recreational Area]], ten years after the land was retired from farming, and William M. Clay's account of the [[Falls of Elk Lick]], a travertine deposit in a limestone gorge. The issue also includes society news, an exhibit call for natural history photographs, a wildlife conference notice, a quiz on common names, and a book review of a freshwater fish guide. Fish are addressed only in that review and in the quiz; the issue is otherwise concerned with plants, birds, geology and society activities.

## Background & Description

The journal is published quarterly by the Society from Box 81, University of Louisville, at $1 a year (35 cents per single copy). Its editor states that it was created to give members an active outlet, with popular notes and articles here and more technical papers reserved for the *Annals of Kentucky Natural History*, a series begun in 1941 and due to resume at irregular intervals. The next issue was to focus on [[Mammoth Cave National Park]].

The contents are:
- Cover photograph, "Frozen Waterfall," by Carl C. Cornett
- "Some Successional Changes in the Biota at Otter Creek Area" by Harvey B. Lovell (p. 3)
- "The Falls of Elk Lick" by William M. Clay (p. 6)
- News and Notes (p. 8) and Notices and Reviews (p. 12)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Otter Creek Recreational Area.** The area was selected in 1934 and largely acquired in 1935 with submarginal land funds. It had been under [[National Park Service]] management for about ten years when Lovell surveyed it. Eroded gullies were stabilised with stakes, stones, sticks, grass and planted [[Black Locust]]. By spring 1946 very little new erosion was seen, though no eroded area was yet fully vegetated. Mowing of the old fields stopped in 1942.

- **Pioneer areas:** Dense shrub stands included coralberry, sassafras, dwarf sumac, persimmon, black locust and red cedar, plus patches of *Rubus*, *Smilax* and wild roses. The tallest red cedars, black locusts and persimmons were about 15 feet high. Open patches held broom sedge, mountain mints and weedy composites. Woody plants were nearly absent on ridges and eroded ground. A fence line was dominated by sassafras (50%), dogwood (20%), red cedar (12%), sumac (5%) and cherry (5%). Sink holes, numerous across the uplands, acted as focal points for woody colonisation.
- **Wooded areas:** Remnant stands along the Ohio River bluffs, Otter Creek and the [[Tall Trees Camp]] ridge were judged to be approaching, but not yet at, a subclimax stage. In a sample on the Tall Trees ridge the forest was [[Oak-Hickory Forest]]: oaks 60%, hickories 20%, with beech 8%, maple 4%, tulip-tree 4% and walnut 3%. Oaks had trunks 12–18 inches in diameter and were 60–70 feet tall. Beech regeneration was noted.
- **Wildlife:** Field Sparrow, Prairie Warbler and bobwhite were numerous in pioneer areas, with Bachman's Sparrow in eroded ground and Northern Yellowthroat around ponds. Woodland birds included Kentucky Warbler, Hooded Warbler and Summer Tanager. Rabbits and squirrels were very plentiful.

**Falls of Elk Lick.** The site is in eastern Fayette County, under a mile from a busy highway and near the [[Palisades of the Kentucky River]]. A spring-fed stream plunges about 60 feet into a forested gorge. The 60-foot calcareous tufa (travertine) apron below the brink is the main feature, more notable than the falls themselves. The origin of the deposit is uncertain. Proposed causes include CO₂ loss from turbulent spray, CO₂ uptake by mosses and plants, and evaporation during a drier climate. Clay suggests a three-stage history: gorge cutting, travertine deposition, and present disintegration with upstream retreat of the cliff. Exposed rocks are the Tyrone limestone ("Kentucky River Marble"), the Oregon limestone ("Birdseye Limestone") and the Camp Nelson formation, all Ordovician.

**Kentucky River Fault.** The fault crosses the river nine times in about 40 miles. The river alternates between the up-thrown and down-thrown blocks, which explains the narrow palisade valley on the up-thrown block and the broader, gentler valley on the down-thrown side.

## Key Findings

- **Erosion control works on former farmland.** Simple obstructions and planted black locust had largely halted erosion at Otter Creek after about ten years, though vegetation cover was still incomplete.
- **Succession is progressing but immature.** Pioneer shrub and tree stands were 15 feet tall after ten years. Canopy closure had not yet occurred in the former fields, while the older wooded areas were approaching subclimax. Lovell plans to re-survey after another ten years.
- **Sink holes drive woody colonisation.** These depressions carried the first woody growth, including wild cherry, dogwood, sumac and wild grape.
- **Upland forest composition.** The Tall Trees ridge oak-hickory stand is dominated by oaks, with beech regenerating.
- **Falls of Elk Lick is an uncommon travertine formation.** Its origin is unresolved, and the deposit is currently disintegrating, a pattern the author links to climatic cycles.
- **Ordovician strata and Kentucky River Fault.** The local geology is accessible for field study, with good fault exposures along the highway near Clay's Ferry.
- **Fish content is limited.** The only fish material is a review of *Representative North American Fresh-Water Fishes* by John T. Nichols (Macmillan, 1942, 128 pp., 60 illustrations, $1.25). It covers 60 of the several hundred North American freshwater species, chosen as among the most often observed, and is recommended for anglers and beginners. Nichols was Curator of Recent Fishes at the [[American Museum of Natural History]].
- **Society activities announced:**
  - A geology paper by R. H. Stouder on September 19, 1946.
  - A botany talk by Dr. P. A. Davies on [[Charles Wilkins Short]] on October 17, 1946.
  - A photography exhibit with four classes (inanimate, animate wild, captive, scenery). Entries were limited to 15 transparencies, prints had to be at least 8×10 inches, the entry fee was 50 cents, and the deadline was November 13, 1946.
  - A wildlife conference at Otter Creek, with field trips, lectures and an astronomy session.
- **Name-quiz:** A 10-question quiz on misleading common names (for example, a "fish" that is not a true fish) is scored at 10 points per correct answer, with 60 passing and 80 good. The answer key was on page 11, which is not included in this text.
