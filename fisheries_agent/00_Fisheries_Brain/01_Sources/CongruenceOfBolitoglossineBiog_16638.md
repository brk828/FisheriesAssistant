# Paleotransport on Displaced Suspect Terranes: Bolitoglossine Salamander Biogeography

#herpetology #biogeography #plethodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 *Cladistics* paper proposes a vicariance hypothesis for New World biogeography. Plethodontid salamanders, particularly the tribe [[Bolitoglossini]], were carried to their present positions on crustal fragments (tectonostratigraphic or [[Suspect Terranes]]) that originated on an ancestral landmass and were later accreted onto North, Central and South America. The author argues that this model explains congruence between salamander phylogeny, distribution and geology more parsimoniously than earlier models based on active dispersal across land bridges. The hypothesis is presented as testable, and if corroborated it would give geologists a new way to analyze terrane relationships and require biologists to reassess New World historical biogeography.

## Background & Description

The family [[Plethodontidae]] consists of terrestrial, lungless salamanders. It is thought to have arisen from an [[Ambystomatid Salamanders|ambystomatid]] lineage in the Appalachian region of North America in the Mid-Mesozoic or earlier. The tribe Bolitoglossini diverged by the Late Cretaceous or the beginning of the Tertiary.

- **Supergenera:** Three monophyletic bolitoglossine supergenera are recognized. [[Batrachoseps]] and [[Bolitoglossa]] form a sister group, and together they are sister to [[Hydromantes]], which is known only from California and southern Europe.
- **Standard distribution model:** Earlier work explained the present range through Arcto-Tertiary dispersal to western North America, southward spread to Middle America, and a Pliocene entry into South America across the [[Panamanian Isthmus]].
- **Endemism:** Tropical bolitoglossine diversity is organized into [[Faunal Regions]] (areas of endemism), which were previously attributed to Tertiary aridification isolating upland populations.
- **Terminology:** The paper uses "[[Suspect Terranes]]" for allochthonous crustal fragments, and "[[Terrane Riders]]" for biotas carried on them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Terrane geology.** The North American craton has grown by accreting continental fragments, island arcs and ocean-floor pieces onto its western edge. Most of California, large parts of Nevada, Oregon, Idaho, Washington, British Columbia and virtually all of Alaska were accreted from the Paleozoic to as recently as the Late-Middle Miocene. Mexico retains native cratonic material only in northeast Sonora and Chihuahua. The remainder was accreted from the Late Paleozoic to the Late Cretaceous. Terranes south of Mexico, extending into northwestern Colombia and Venezuela, were accreted partly as recently as the Late Miocene.

**Key tectonic elements:**
- [[Maya Terrane]]: rifted from the southern United States margin during Middle to Late Jurassic opening of the Gulf of Mexico, arriving at its present position by latest Jurassic.
- [[Chortis Block]]: likely adjoined southern Mexico throughout the Mesozoic.
- [[Costa Rica-Panama Terrane]]: sutured onto Chortis by the Eocene and attached to the Caribbean plate. Central America has moved as much as 1400 km eastward relative to the continents since about 38 Ma.
- [[Paleomagnetism]] indicates that many terranes were displaced northward from low paleolatitudes, but it cannot resolve paleolongitude.

**Habitat and sedentism.** Batrachoseps occupies a wide range of habitats, from Alaskan forests to Mojave Desert canyon springs and tropical forest on Volcán de Colima. Intervening areas of apparently suitable habitat lack the genus, which the author takes as evidence of isolation.

**Sedentism.** Detailed home-range data show that bolitoglossines are very sedentary:
- *Batrachoseps attenuatus* rarely moved more than 41.5 m during a 2-year study (Hendrickson, 1954).
- *Bolitoglossa subpalmata* has an average home-range radius of less than 5 m (Vial, 1968).

The author argues that this low dispersal ability supports long-term fidelity to terranes.

## Key Findings

**1. Paleogeography and phylogeny agree.** An area cladogram from plethodontid phylogeny ([[Cladistics]], Wake 1966; Elias and Wake 1983) is largely compatible with an area cladogram built from geologic data. Under the model, the principal split between a southern Mexico–Guatemala lineage and a Central and South American group corresponds to the eastward displacement of Central America.

**2. Terrane-linked taxa with documented paleolatitudes:**
- Upper Cretaceous strata of [[Salinia]], home to two semispecies of *B. pacificus*, were deposited at 21° ± 7° paleolatitude, about 2,500 km south of present position.
- [[Stanley Mountain Terrane]] (*B. nigriventris*) was at 6° ± 5° paleolatitude 90 Ma.
- Salinia and Stanley Mountain collided by 72 Ma and accreted as a composite terrane by the early Eocene. They have since undergone up to 300 km of additional northwest displacement.
- [[Franciscan Complex]] limestone on which *B. attenuatus* lives was deposited 96 Ma at 17° ± 7° South paleolatitude.
- [[Alexander Terrane]] (*B. caudatus*) shows Jurassic southern-hemisphere paleolatitudes and Mid-Cretaceous equatorial positions.

**3. Strong congruence between taxa and terranes.** Only one bolitoglossine taxon (*B. campi*, Inyo Mountains) occurs on indigenous North American cratonic rock. Diversity and endemism are generally lower on the South American craton than on terranes accreted to it. Single taxa in *Batrachoseps* are associated with single or composite accreted terranes.

**4. Older divergence times.** The author argues that biochemical age estimates are systematically low. Cited estimates include:
- *Hydromantes* origin about 50 Ma (immunological; Wake et al., 1978)
- *Batrachoseps*/*Bolitoglossa* split before 40 Ma (electrophoretic; Yanev, 1980)
- tropical bolitoglossine genera up to 60 Ma (immunological; Maxson and Wake, 1981)

The author suggests that the low estimates may reflect an inappropriate molecular divergence rate constant.

**5. South American colonization.** The model explains why South American lineages are older than the Panamanian isthmus. Previous work placed the terrestrial corridor at no more than 7 Ma. Under this model, bolitoglossines arrived in South America on accreted terranes, such as the Costa Rica–Panama arc and terranes accreted to Colombia and Venezuela. This made allopatric divergence possible before the isthmus formed.

**6. Reinterpretation of "island hoppers."** Fossil "invasions" of South America by land snails, tortoises, rodents, primates, procyonids and ground sloths may represent biotas carried on accreted terranes rather than independent dispersals. Webb's (1978) remark that many island hoppers "arrived on the same boat" is reinterpreted as referring to terrane docking.

**7. Dispersal required.** The author concludes that the amount of dispersal required is far less than in earlier scenarios. Imperfect matches between salamander areas and geologic areas imply some dispersal, especially in *Bolitoglossa*.

**8. Limitations.** Many suspect terranes are largely marine, and submergence would have caused extinction of terrestrial biota. The author notes that bolitoglossines on the California Channel Islands, on islands near Central America, and in small desert habitat islands show that salamanders may have survived such a history. Survival on small islands would still imply some dispersal onto former submarine substrates.

**9. Testability.** The author notes that the hypothesis would be falsified if geologic studies invalidated the paleogeographic model, or if phylogenies of other groups failed to produce congruent cladograms. Other groups with congruent distributions, such as *Viburnum* (Donoghue, 1983, unpublished analyses) and some western North American fishes (Minckley et al., in press), are cited as potential tests. Xenodontine snakes, placed by [[Savage Historical Units|Savage (1982)]] in a separate Central American unit, are cited as a group that would not fit the model.

**10. Implications.** If corroborated, the model would give a testable geochronological scale for calibrating molecular clocks and would require reassessment of Croizat-style and Savage-style biogeographic scenarios. The author also calls for more phylogenies and better geologic resolution of inter-terrane relationships.
