## Executive Summary

This document is the 1967 Annual Report of the Arizona Cooperative Fishery Unit (University of Arizona, Tucson), led by Dr. William J. McConnell with Assistant Unit Leader Charles D. Ziebell. Rather than describing a single species, the report summarizes an active freshwater fisheries research program encompassing **production limnology**, **fish life histories**, and the development of **fisheries in reclaimed wastewater**. Key research threads included the ecology of [[Channel Catfish]] in small impoundments, effects of natural plant tannins/phenolics on aquatic organism growth, sonic fish-tagging technology, exotic species management (grass carp, Tilapia), and multiple graduate theses on plankton dynamics, litter leachate effects, carbohydrate-based food chain manipulation, and interspecific competition among centrarchids. The Unit also advanced infrastructure, including a 3/4-acre off-campus experimental pond facility with twelve 6,000-gallon tanks.

## Background & Description

The report is administrative/ecological in nature and does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of species. However, several taxa are referenced in the context of experimental fish populations:

- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*) — subject of population sampling and internal sonic tagging studies; tag designed for a service life of at least 100 days, implying a body cavity suitable for internal implantation.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Salmo gairdneri*, Richardson) — used in growth trials in experimental tanks and reclaimed wastewater ponds.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus salmoides*, Lacepede) — used in carbohydrate food-chain and competition/predation studies.
- **Malacca [[Tilapia]] hybrid** — used in aquaculture/pond fishery development and carbohydrate growth trials.
- **[[Chinese Grass Carp]]** — evaluated for aquatic weed control efficacy, implying herbivorous feeding morphology suited to macrophyte consumption.

No quantitative morphometric data (length, weight, meristics) are reported in this administrative summary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Unit's research spanned several ecological themes relevant to Arizona's small, warm-water impoundments and reservoirs:

- **Trophic dynamics and primary production**: Phillip Stewart's thesis found plankton photosynthesis closely tied to phosphate (PO₄) and alkalinity of inflow water, and zooplankton biomass linked to photosynthesis plus nitrate (NO₃) correction — but not to benthic photosynthesis, indicating pelagic-benthic decoupling in zooplankton food supply.
- **Allochthonous organic inputs**: William Seawell's study on [[Yellow Pine]] litter leachates in small impoundments identified both negative effects (alkalinity reduction, increased color, high biochemical oxygen demand) and positive effects (biogenic salt and organic matter addition), concluding that slow watershed leaching benefits fisheries more than direct litter import into lakes.
- **Carbohydrate-driven food chains**: George Adams tested crude beet molasses and corn dextrose as energy subsidies for the food chain supporting [[Rainbow Trout]], Tilapia hybrids, and [[Largemouth Bass]]; carbohydrate additions boosted phytoplankton biomass and bacterial activity but did not translate into significant fish growth gains.
- **Interspecific competition and predation**: Richard Biggins examined food-web interactions among sunfishes (green and bluegill), black crappie, and largemouth bass in Peña Blanca Lake, Nogales, AZ, using trap nets, gill nets, and electrofishing.
- **Zooplankton production drivers**: Eric Bergersen's ongoing project investigated trace metals (via atomic absorption spectrophotometry), bacterial populations (phase-contrast counts), and plant nitrogen content (Kjeldahl method) as factors influencing zooplankton production beyond primary productivity alone, across four Arizona lakes.
- **Evaporation control impacts**: Steve Lewis assessed how floating mechanical evaporation barriers affect light penetration, photosynthesis, oxygen levels, and fish growth in experimental ponds.
- **Reclaimed wastewater fisheries**: Robert Hallock's project evaluated growth, condition, and survival of channel catfish, trout, and Tilapia fingerlings stocked in ponds filled with tertiary-treated municipal reclaimed wastewater, monitoring oxygen, pH, zooplankton, and benthos.
- **Non-game/game fish interactions**: Daniel Hopson's new project (initiated Sept. 1967) was designed to test how numerically dominant small non-game fish (e.g., golden shiners, green sunfish) affect growth of young game fish (rainbow trout, largemouth bass) using graded species-composition experiments in 6,000-gallon pools.
- **Natural phenolic compounds**: The Unit Leader's own research examined effects of plant tannins and related phenolic compounds (mixed tannins, flavonoids, phenolic acids, hydroxycinnamic acids) — contributed via watershed litter — on the growth of aquatic organisms, attempting identification or characterization by molecular size and hydroxyl group reactivity.

## Key Findings

- **Zooplankton–nutrient linkage**: Zooplankton biomass in mountain impoundments correlated with plankton photosynthesis (adjusted for NO₃) or with a composite index of inflow PO₄, NO₃, and alkalinity — but showed no relationship to benthic photosynthesis ([[Phillip Stewart]] thesis, completed Jan. 1967).
- **Pine litter leachates are net-negative for fisheries**: Despite contributing some nutrients and organic matter, [[Yellow Pine]] needle leachates' inhibitory effects (lowered alkalinity, elevated color/oxygen demand) outweighed trophic benefits; slow watershed-mediated leaching is preferable to direct litter deposition into lakes ([[William Seawell]] thesis, completed June 1967).
- **Carbohydrate supplementation insufficient to boost fish growth**: Although molasses and dextrose additions increased phytoplankton biomass and bacterial activity (higher plate counts, altered pH/oxygen extremes, increased turbidity), they did not significantly enhance growth rates of rainbow trout, Tilapia hybrids, or largemouth bass — though scheduled additions timed to bacterial pulses were suggested as a potential management tool ([[George Adams]] thesis, completed Aug. 1967).
- **Technological development**: A compact sonic internal fish tag with ≥100-day service life was developed in collaboration with Sensory Systems, Inc., alongside a sonic remote recovery device for unmarked fish traps/gear — advancing methods for tracking [[Channel Catfish]] populations in small impoundments.
- **Exotic species applications**: Field trials confirmed practical use of [[Chinese Grass Carp]] for aquatic weed control and supported development of pond-based Tilapia fisheries as part of the Unit's sport fishery extension program.
- **Infrastructure growth**: Completion of a 3/4-acre off-campus research facility (1966) with a climate-controlled wet laboratory, workshop, and twelve 6,000-gallon experimental tanks (expandable) substantially increased the Unit's experimental capacity.
- **Academic output**: Three M.S. theses were completed in 1967 (Stewart, Seawell, Adams); five new or continuing graduate projects were active, spanning limnology, aquaculture, and fish behavior/competition studies. No formal Unit publications were issued during the reporting year.
- **Training/enrollment**: 75–78 students were enrolled in Unit-affiliated seminars taught by the Leader and Assistant Leader; the Unit supervised numerous undergraduate and graduate fishery students, awarding three M.S. degrees involving a fishery major.
