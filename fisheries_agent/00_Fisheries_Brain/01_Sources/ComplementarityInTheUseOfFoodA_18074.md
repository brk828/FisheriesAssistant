# Complementarity in the Use of Food and Thermal Habitat by Lake Michigan Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae #clupeidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1981 study by [[Crowder]], [[Magnuson]] and [[Brandt]] tests whether competition structures the [[Lake Michigan]] fish community by comparing diets and thermal habitat use among five common species. Bottom trawls off Grand Haven, Michigan, were taken 7–13 September 1977 (N = 68 hauls). The authors report complementarity: species with similar diets tended to use different thermal habitats, and vice versa. Native species that declined during the invasion of exotic [[Alewife]] and [[Rainbow Smelt]] had feeding and habitat requirements similar to the exotics. The authors conclude that competition is likely important, though the data alone cannot prove it.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** The Lake Michigan fish community changed greatly over the past 50 years. Rainbow smelt invaded in the 1920s, and [[Sea Lamprey]] (first reported 1936) decimated large piscivores such as [[Lake Trout]], which allowed alewife and smelt to proliferate.
- **Native declines:** [[Emerald Shiner]], [[Lake Herring]] and five species of deepwater cisco became rare. Proposed causes include competition for food with alewife and smelt, and predation on early life stages.
- **Hypothesis:** If competition structures the community, species with similar diets should partition habitat, and species with similar habitats should differ in diet. This pattern is termed complementarity (Schoener 1974).
- **Prior work:** [[Brandt et al. 1980]] documented thermal habitat partitioning among the six most abundant species, including [[Deepwater Sculpin]].
- **Study species:** YOY and adult [[Alewife]] (treated as separate units because their habitats differed significantly), [[Rainbow Smelt]], [[Spottail Shiner]], [[Trout-perch]] and [[Yellow Perch]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design**
- Fish were collected from the zone where the [[Thermocline]] meets the lake bottom, using 15-min tows with a semiballoon otter trawl (7.6 m headrope, 2 cm stretch mesh body, 0.6 cm cod-end liner).
- A thermistor on the headrope sorted the 68 hauls into nine 2 °C thermal strata.
- Day samples: N = 28. Night samples: N = 40.
- Up to 50 individuals per species per haul were frozen on dry ice and later measured and dissected.
- A total of 1161 stomachs from five common species were examined.
- For alewife, 6.8% of stomach volume was subsampled. For other species, total contents were counted.

**Analytical approach**
- Diets were expressed as proportion by number, with rare prey reported as frequency of occurrence.
- Similarity was measured with [[Proportional Similarity Index]] (Schoener 1970; Hurlbert 1978), where C = Σ min(P_xi, P_yi).
- Daytime (05:30–19:30) data were emphasized because alewife and possibly smelt migrate off the bottom at night, which can mislead interpretation.
- The authors corrected a calculation error in the day catch-per-unit-effort for adult rainbow smelt.

**Diet composition (summary of Tables 1–2)**
- Adult alewife, YOY alewife and smelt fed mainly on [[Daphnia]] and copepods (day and night).
- Spottail shiner fed on Eurycercus and chydorid cladocerans, plus insects by day and fish eggs at night.
- Trout-perch ate mainly chironomids by day and chironomids plus Pontoporeia at night.
- Yellow perch ate mainly Pontoporeia by day and Eurycercus at night.

**Limitations noted by the authors**
- Thermal habitat was assessed only by bottom trawling.
- Prey availability was not measured directly.
- Few juvenile fish were caught, so larval and juvenile interactions cannot be assessed.
- Results are from a late-summer, post-spawning snapshot and should not be extrapolated to other seasons.

## Key Findings

**Diet similarity (daytime)**
- Overall similarity ranged from C = 0 to C = 0.95.
- Adult alewife and rainbow smelt had the most similar diets.
- Exotic–native pairs showed almost no overlap.
- Pairs among yellow perch, spottail shiner and trout-perch ranged from C = 0.24 to 0.38.
- YOY alewife overlapped adult alewife at C = 0.32 and rainbow smelt at C = 0.28.

**Thermal habitat similarity (daytime)**
- Overlap ranged from C = 0.03 to 0.87, with no pair at zero.
- Highest overlaps: spottail shiner–yellow perch (C = 0.81) and YOY alewife–spottail shiner (C = 0.81).
- YOY alewife had low overlap with rainbow smelt (C = 0.03) and adult alewife (C = 0.08).
- All other pairs had intermediate overlap (C = 0.15–0.61).

**Complementarity patterns**
- Adult alewife and rainbow smelt: similar diets, segregated by thermal habitat.
- YOY alewife: segregated from adults and smelt by habitat, with diet more similar than habitat use.
- YOY alewife, spottail shiner and yellow perch: similar thermal habitat, segregated by food.
- Trout-perch: moderate overlap in diet and habitat with spottail shiner and yellow perch, possibly experiencing diffuse competition (MacArthur 1972).
- Exotics tended to segregate by habitat; natives tended to segregate by food.

**Historical implications**
- The three native species examined showed no significant diet overlap with alewife or smelt.
- Emerald shiner, young lake herring and young deepwater ciscoes fed almost entirely on microzooplankton, overlapping exotics, and declined most sharply.
- Yellow perch declined somewhat, possibly through food competition or predation by alewife on perch larvae.
- Trout-perch and spottail shiner were relatively unaffected, consistent with their distinct diets.

**Conclusions**
- Partitioning data are insufficient to prove competition was important (Sale 1979), but the authors argue it likely structured the community.
- Behaviorally flexible habitat choice may allow rapid resource partitioning in response to competition.
- Further work needs prey-availability data, laboratory preference studies, and more experimental field designs.
