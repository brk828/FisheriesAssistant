# Keys to Native and Introduced Fishes of Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This paper, published in the *Journal of the Arizona Academy of Science* (1971, vol. 6, no. 3, pp. 183–188), presents a set of artificial dichotomous keys to the fishes of [[Arizona]]. Author W. L. Minckley of [[Arizona State University]] compiled the keys from classroom use, original collection data and earlier published reports. The keys are described as a preview of a more comprehensive state fish work. They cover native fishes and the many introduced species that now dominate the state's freshwater fauna, which are marked with an asterisk. The keys run through 85 numbered couplets, from sturgeons through perches and sunfishes, and use common names from the [[American Fisheries Society]] checklist (1960) where possible.

## Background & Description

The keys were built for teaching and field identification. The author states that they will be expanded later with literature citations, explanations of taxonomic decisions, and more data on recently introduced or hypothetical species.

Key conventions:
- **Non-native species** are marked with an asterisk (*). The text notes that non-native fishes dominate the present Arizona ichthyofauna.
- **Footnotes** cover species not yet established, single records, and experimental introductions. Early introductions that left no established population, or that were recorded only as bait, are generally left out.
- **Diagnostic characters** are mostly meristic and morphometric: fin-ray and spine counts, lateral-line scale counts, gill raker and pharyngeal tooth counts, pyloric caeca numbers, intestinal length, and body proportions.

Funding and support came from the Arizona State University Faculty Research Committee and Department of Zoology, the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], the National Science Foundation, the Sport Fishing Institute, The Nature Conservancy, and the U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries. The author credits colleagues and graduate and undergraduate students for help with the keys.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The keyed fauna covers several connected freshwater systems:
- **[[Colorado River]] system**, including the lower river, the [[Virgin River]] (Nevada), and [[Lake Powell]] (Utah), just upstream of Arizona.
- **[[Little Colorado River]]**, which supports endemic spinedace and sucker forms.
- **[[Gila River Basin]]**, home to many native *Gila* chubs, trout and topminnows.
- **[[Rio Yaqui Basin]]**, with Yaqui chub, Yaqui catfish, Yaqui shiner and Yaqui sucker records.
- **Man-made waters**, such as [[Painted Rock Reservoir]], canals near Winterhaven (California), the Page Springs Hatchery, and a borrow pit near Buckeye.

Notes on establishment:
- Two sciaenid genera (*Cynoscion* and *Bairdiella*) and a pomadasyid (*Anisotremus davidsoni*) were introduced into a borrow pit at Painted Rock Reservoir but did not establish populations.
- [[White Perch]] was introduced near Buckeye in the late 1960s and appears to have disappeared without reproducing. [[Sacramento Perch]] was also introduced there without apparent reproduction.
- A single [[Walking Catfish]] (*Clarias batrachus*) was caught in a canal near Winterhaven, California, in early 1970.
- The [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idellus*) was held in experimental ponds near Tucson and at Page Springs, but was not known from open waters.
- *Gila atraria* was acclimated in the upper Colorado River, taken in Lake Powell, and used as bait along the lower Colorado. The author judged it most likely established at least locally in Arizona.
- *Notropis venustus* was introduced into the Virgin River, Nevada.

## Key Findings

- **Structure:** The key runs through 85 numbered couplets. It begins with the heterocercal sturgeon (Acipenseridae) and then moves through salmonids, catfish, minnows, suckers and livebearers, ending with the perches, sunfishes and sculpins.
- **Introduced fauna:** Many of the keyed taxa are non-native. Examples include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Common Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Guppy]], [[Green Swordtail]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]] and several [[Black Bullhead]]-type catfish.
- **Native desert and endemic species of conservation interest:**
  - [[Gila Trout]] and [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo gilae* and the "Apache trout" form), separated by scale counts, body depth and pelvic fin length.
  - [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), which can have as few as 56 lateral-line scales in restricted populations.
  - [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Colorado Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), separated by skull shape, predorsal hump and fin-ray counts.
  - [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*).
  - [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*) and [[Sonora Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. sonoriensis*), separated by basin, snout and mouth position.
  - Spinedace (*Lepidomeda vittata* and *L. m. mollispinis*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*).
- **Diagnostic taxonomic points:** Pyloric caeca counts and upper jaw length separate rainbow trout from *Gila*-type trout. Pharyngeal teeth and gill raker counts separate carp from goldfish. Gonopodial structure separates the livebearing genera.
- **Footnotes on uncertain status:** Not all listed species are confirmed residents. Several (e.g., *Clarias batrachus*, *Ctenopharyngodon idellus*, *Cynoscion*, *Bairdiella*, *Roccus americanus*, *Archoplites interruptus*) are noted as introduced but not established.
- **Purpose:** The keys are a provisional working tool. The author plans a fuller state fish work with citations and explanations of taxonomic decisions.
