# Monitoring Study of Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) Reintroduced into the Lower Colorado River in 1988

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared by Daniel R. Langhorst for the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (contract FC-7494, report dated January 1989), documents the 1988 monitoring of hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] stocked into the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The study tracked growth, survival, dispersal, and predation of stocked juveniles in three enclosed backwater coves at [[Goose Flats Backwater]], in ponds at [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]], and in the main channel. Protected ponds produced strong growth, while the enclosed coves lost most of their fish to dispersal, predation, and falling water levels. The author concludes that reestablishment with present techniques is unlikely to succeed and recommends concentrating on long-term pond rearing to a larger stocking size.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River]] drainage of western North America. Historic collections place it in larger streams from Wyoming to Sonora, Mexico, and it was common in the lower Colorado River basin until the early 1950s. Since then, its numbers have declined sharply, and it has been extirpated from most lower-basin habitats.

In 1986, the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) agreed on a 10-year reintroduction program. USFWS produced fish at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico), and the state reared juveniles at the [[Nillland Native Fish Ponds]] (Imperial Warmwater Fish Hatchery) to refine rearing methods for larger stocking sizes. About 1.4 million larvae (10–18 mm TL) were stocked at six lower-river sites in 1986, but five subsequent monitoring efforts failed to recapture any fish.

Earlier work (Langhorst 1988) found that larvae stayed near stocking sites for about two weeks and reached about 14 mm before disappearing. Juveniles moved out of backwaters toward the main channel at night and were no longer captured after one week. [[Bluegill]] were the main consumers of larvae, while [[Largemouth Bass]] accounted for all recorded predation on juveniles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area runs along the Arizona-California border from Headgate Rock Dam to Imperial Dam and lies in the Sonoran Desertscrub–Lower Colorado River subdivision. The floodplain is broad, with sand and silt soils and rocky slopes at the mountain fronts. Rainfall is bimodal, and average annual precipitation at Blythe is about 8 cm.

Human modification is extensive: dams, reservoirs, water diversion, channel dredging, and bank stabilization. Riparian vegetation is dominated by non-native saltcedar, with scattered native cottonwood and willow. Emergent plants include giant bulrush, cattail, and common reed, and submergent beds include pondweeds, watermilfoil, and spiny naiad. The aquatic fauna is dominated by established non-native fishes, including [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], [[Tilapia]], and centrarchids, while native species, including the razorback sucker, are largely extirpated.

Flow regulation by [[Parker Dam]] strongly controlled backwater conditions. Releases were about 365 m³/s in late August and then declined through the autumn, causing cove depths to fall in proportion. Covers that were 2.5 m deep in July 1988 were reduced to shallow drying puddles by January 1989.

## Key Findings

**Study design and stocking**

- About 41,280 larvae (about 10 mm TL) were transported from Dexter in March 1988 to six ponds (0.10 and 0.20 ha) at [[Nillland Native Fish Ponds]] for rearing.
- Larvae were stocked into Cibola and Imperial NWR ponds (about 3,000 and 8,250 juveniles, 7 June 1988), the [[Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond]], enclosed coves at Goose Flats Backwater, and the main channel (October and December 1988).
- Three coves (0.40, 0.25, and 0.29 ha) were isolated with block netting from 27 June to 7 July 1988. Each received about 1,000 juveniles on 28 July 1988.
- Site selection criteria included depth of at least 2 m, rooted vegetation for cover, benthic organic debris for foraging, and protection from human interference.

**Growth and survival**

- Cove fish grew slowly and suffered heavy losses. Inventories on 28 October and 26 October–1 November found 394 razorbacks in Cove 2 and 290 in Cove 3, with markedly fewer in Cove 1.
- Mean growth in the coves was about 23 mm per month, so the study estimated a minimum of 1.5 years of growout to reach 400 mm TL.
- Pond growth was much faster. Fish stocked at Cibola and Imperial ponds on 7 June 1988 (mean 84 mm TL) averaged 208 mm and 215 mm TL by mid-September, roughly 35 mm per month over six months.
- Fish reared at Nillland for seven months and removed on 20 October 1988 averaged 182 mm TL (range 124–256 mm), about 27 mm per month (the conclusions cite 187 mm).
- Causes of cove losses included beaver damage to net barriers (Coves 2 and 3), stress mortality from transfer (dead fish found up to two weeks after stocking), predation by herons and egrets, and predation by large fish that escaped capture.

**Dispersal after barrier removal (21 November 1988)**

- One day after removal, 118 razorbacks were recaptured, and only 19 (16%) were found outside the coves, mostly in the channel sections of the backwater. One fish from Cove 2 moved about 1,000 m upstream.
- One week after removal, 21 razorbacks were recaptured, of which five (24%) were outside the coves. Two Cove 2 fish moved about 1,400 m and 1,800 m up the backwater channels.
- Four weeks after removal, only one razorback (Cove 3 mark) was captured, as dense water milfoil beds had died back with the onset of winter.
- Earlier work in 1987 showed a similar pattern: abundant fish one day after release, sharply reduced at one week, and undetectable at two weeks.
- No fish from the 23 September 1988 stocking of 1,114 floy-tagged golf-course-pond fish were recovered in a 1.3-hour survey of the backwater.

**Predation**

- Trammel and gill net sets (6–21 July) removed few large predators, mainly channel catfish, flathead catfish, and largemouth bass. Rotenone could not be used because permits were not in place.
- On 21 November 1988, nine largemouth bass and two channel catfish were collected from the backwater on day one. One bass contained a 146 mm razorback. At one week, two of five bass contained razorbacks (150 and 160 mm TL).
- Predation was reportedly similar to 1987, when largemouth bass were the only predators found to contain razorbacks (4 of 46 stomachs).
- In the main channel (13–15 December 1988, River Miles 88–62), flathead catfish were the most abundant predator in dense inundated riparian vegetation. None contained razorbacks, though other prey was found.

**Main channel recovery**

- One stocked razorback (217 mm TL, right pelvic fin clip) was recovered near River Mile 85.5 and probably came from the 7 December 1988 stocking, about 5.5 km upstream of the recapture point.
- The Parker Division reach, sampled in October and November 1988, yielded no razorbacks after about three hours of electrofishing. About 200,000 juveniles had been stocked into the upper reach in 1987.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- The authors judge it unlikely that razorback sucker can be reestablished in the lower Colorado River with current techniques.
- Protected ponds (Cibola, Imperial, Blythe golf course, and Arizona/Buenos Aires facilities) produced the most successful growth and survival.
- Recommended actions include discontinuing backwater cove rearing because of water-level instability, rearing fish to at least 400 mm TL before release, securing long-term operation of pond facilities, and dividing surviving fish among the available ponds.
- Scheduled hatchery work: the 1988 pond fish would reach 400 mm by about June 1989, and the next larval stocking (roughly 30,000–40,000) was planned for the 20 ha pond in March 1989.
