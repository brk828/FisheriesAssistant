# Multivariate Discrimination of Colorado Plateau Gila spp.

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1998 *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* paper by Douglas, Miller and Minckley tests whether the big-river chubs of the [[Colorado Plateau]] can be told apart using museum specimens. The authors apply discriminant analysis to meristic and morphometric data for [[Gila Robusta]], [[Gila Cypha]] and [[Gila Elegans]], and classify juveniles and putative hybrids with a field-usable formula. The three species are phenotypically distinguishable, and a five-character field function identifies adults at about 94% better than chance. Juveniles of *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* are poorly classified, and the hybrid test is inconclusive because of small sample sizes.

## Background & Description

*Gila* is a cyprinid genus endemic to western North America that shows wide morphological and meristic variation within and among species. Proposed causes include ecotypy/ecophenotypy, hybridization, ontogeny, character selection and weak analyses. Fishery managers long dismissed these native chubs as undesirable, which delayed taxonomic work. Dam construction, diversion and beheading of [[Colorado River Basin]] rivers have pushed formerly allopatric forms into sympatry, increasing hybridization risk. Because the species are endangered, further study is difficult.

Three hypotheses were tested:
- **H1:** *G. robusta*, *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* are morphologically indistinguishable.
- **H2:** Characters that discriminate adults also discriminate juveniles (<180 mm SL).
- **H3:** Putative hybrids are intermediate and cannot be assigned to one parental species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study is grounded in the history of [[Colorado Plateau]] river management. Rivers such as the [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]] have been sampled in earlier work, and the Black Rocks site in Colorado was used in comparative field identification. Early collections from the Green River illustrate the problem: the same material was recorded as "bonytails" by one author and as 13 humpbacks and 36 bonytails by another. Such inconsistency reflects both real phenotypic overlap and the lack of diagnostic characters that field biologists could apply.

Hybridization is a recognized concern. *Gila seminuda* is treated as a hybrid species of *G. robusta* × *G. elegans* ancestry (DeMarais et al. 1992). Holden and Stalnaker (1970) argued that intergrades bridge *G. cypha* and *G. elegans*; their explanations include incomplete speciation with introgression, one variable species, or two species with intergrades. Smith et al. (1979) later argued that most of those intergrades were actually *G. cypha*.

## Key Findings

### Methods and Data
- **Specimens (museum, to 1978):** *G. robusta* 81 adults and 106 juveniles; *G. cypha* 58 adults and 30 juveniles; *G. elegans* 28 adults and 6 juveniles; 17 putative hybrids; 3 *G. seminuda*.
- **Characters:** 25 morphometric and 10 meristic variables per specimen. A field subset used 19 morphometric and 3 meristic characters, all measurable with calipers.
- **Missing data:** 1.76% (*G. robusta*), 0.98% (*G. cypha*) and 1.83% (*G. elegans*), estimated by regression.
- **Size correction:** Log ratios to standard length. The authors state this suits field use, requiring only a caliper and calculator.
- **Analyses:** Nonparametric discriminant analysis with jackknife error estimates, then parametric discriminant analysis using five field characters, Kappa statistics and canonical variate analysis.

### Adult Discrimination
- **Normality:** Only one meristic count was normally distributed in each species. Morphometric covariance matrices were heterogeneous, so a pooled matrix was used.
- ***G. robusta* vs *G. elegans*:** 100% separated by dorsal rays and gill rakers (meristic), or by caudal peduncle depth (morphometric and field).
- ***G. cypha* vs *G. elegans*:** Hardest pair. Meristic 98.8% (85/86); morphometric 96.5% (83/86); field 95.3% (82/86), with 89.3% of *G. elegans* correct in the field set.
- ***G. robusta* vs *G. cypha*:** Meristic 95.7% (133/139); morphometric 99.3% (138/139); field 99.3%.
- **All three species together:** Meristic 92.2% (154/167); morphometric 95.6% (131/137); field 96.3% (132/137). *G. elegans* was correct in 89.3% (25/28) of field cases.
- **Five-character field function:** Five misidentified individuals (two *G. cypha* as *G. elegans*, three *G. elegans* as *G. cypha*). Kappa = 0.9417 (Z = 19.463; P < 0.00001). Canonical variate 1 explained 87.4% of variation and variate 2 explained 12.6%. Only the first two axes were significant.
- **Equal-sample test (N = 28 per species, within-group covariance):** Three individuals misclassified.

Note that the abstract's "over 95% of all adults" for meristic characters refers to the pairwise comparisons; the three-species meristic result is 92.2%.

### Juveniles
- **Overall:** 88% (126/142) correctly classified.
- ***G. robusta*:** 97.2% (103/106).
- ***G. cypha*:** 60.0% (18/30). Twelve were assigned to *G. robusta*.
- ***G. elegans*:** 66.7% (4/6). Two were assigned to *G. robusta*.
- **Interpretation:** The high overall figure is driven by the 75% of juveniles that are *G. robusta*. The authors suggest the occiput and nuchal hump have not yet developed enough to be diagnostic.

### Putative Hybrids
- ***G. robusta* × *G. cypha* (N = 10):** 60% (6) assigned to *G. robusta*, 40% (4) to *G. cypha*.
- ***G. cypha* × *G. elegans* (N = 4):** 50% (2) assigned to each parent.
- ***G. seminuda* (N = 3):** 66.7% (2) assigned to *G. robusta*, 33.3% (1) to *G. cypha*.

### Hypothesis Outcomes
- **H1 rejected:** The three species are phenotypically distinguishable. Misclassified individuals were rarely shared among data subsets, which suggests several characters independently discriminate.
- **H2 not rejected:** Juvenile assignment is mixed. It is reliable for *G. robusta* but marginal for *G. cypha* and *G. elegans*. The authors suggest composites of quantitative characters may help.
- **H3 not rejected:** Hybrids were assigned to one parent or the other, so intermediacy could not be excluded. The authors note that the paucity of hybrids weakened the test.

### Management Implications
- The field-usable subset retains most discriminatory power, so managers can identify adults with calipers.
- Character selection, adequate analysis and "the art of seeing well" are identified as the key factors in separating these species.
- Caudal peduncle depth, dorsal and anal rays, and head and snout measurements recur across studies as the most useful characters.
- Juveniles of *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* need further work before field identification is reliable.
