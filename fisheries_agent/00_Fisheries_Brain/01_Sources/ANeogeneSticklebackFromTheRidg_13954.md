# A Neogene Stickleback from the Ridge Formation of California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gasterosteidae

## Executive Summary

This paleoichthyological paper by [[Lore Rose David]] (California Institute of Technology, 1945) describes *[[Pungitius Haynesi]]* n. sp., a small fossil stickleback represented by a single type specimen from dark-gray shale of the [[Ridge Formation]] near [[Piru Creek]], California. It is the first identifiable macrofossil from this formation, which the author considers to be upper Miocene or Pliocene in age. The fish is most closely related to the living [[Pungitius Pungitius]] (nine-spined stickleback), and the author concludes that it represents a freshwater variety that is unlikely to be older than middle Miocene.

## Background & Description

**Discovery and material.** The specimen was collected by E. B. Haynes of Fillmore, California, and donated to the [[California Institute of Technology]], Pasadena. Because wartime restrictions prevented further collecting, the holotype (Calif. Inst. Tech. Coll. Vert. Pale. No. 10347) is the only fish available for study. The author notes that more material may exist at the locality.

**Locality and geology.** The fossil came from a dark-gray silty shale in an embankment at the south approach to the first bridge across Piru Creek, on the old route of the Ridge Route (U.S. Highway 99). The locality (Calif. Inst. Tech. Vert. Pale. Loc. 389) lies in the U.S.G.S. Tejon quadrangle. The strata belong to the Ridge Formation, which is described in detail in the [[Ridge Basin]] studies of Eaton (1939). Eaton's Division II, a thick unit of a few hundred feet of shale, was interpreted as brackish-water and lacustrine and tentatively assigned to the Pliocene. Earlier work by Clements (1937) briefly mentioned these deposits.

**Morphology of the holotype.**
- Length: 50.5 mm, including a broken urostyle; caudal fin missing.
- Elongate body with a slender head and small eye; body narrows rapidly to a narrow, constricted caudal peduncle.
- Head 3.4 times in body length; body depth about 5 times in body length.
- Snout slightly longer than the eye, 4 times in head length; mouth long for the genus (3 times in head length), almost closed, and directed diagonally forward and upward.
- Vertebrae: 15 + 19 = 34.
- Dorsal fin formula: first dorsal uncertain (middle portion disturbed); second dorsal with a spine 3.3 times in head length, followed by soft rays (recorded as D 2 < I, 10 in the OCR-affected text).
- Anal fin: I, 8, inserted slightly behind the second dorsal.
- Pelvic spines missing; about 12 or more pectoral rays, tips broken.
- Dermal skeleton absent except for shields associated with the dorsal spines and girdles, suggesting a feeble dermal armor.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Depositional environment.** The occurrence of this stickleback is taken to indicate that the sediment accumulated in fresh water, consistent with earlier interpretations of the strata. The author notes that sticklebacks migrate to shallow water to spawn in early summer and retreat to deeper pools in autumn, a habitat in which the fossil may have been preserved.

**Global distribution of sticklebacks.** Sticklebacks are restricted to the Northern Hemisphere, ranging across Eurasia, into northern Asia and Japan, and across North America from Alaska to California and from Nova Scotia to Cape Cod. Two species dominate most of this range: [[Gasterosteus Aculeatus]] (common stickleback) and Pungitius pungitius. Both occur in marine, brackish, and freshwater habitats, and dermal armor varies with habitat, being strongest in northern seas and weakest in southern seas and fresh water.

**Modern California fauna.** Pungitius pungitius does not occur in western North America today. Modern California populations of the common stickleback are represented by named varieties, including forms recorded from the Santa Clara River and Santa Ana River. The author suggests that Pungitius may once have had a wider distribution than Gasterosteus in late Tertiary times.

**Other fossil sticklebacks.** Only a few are known:
- [[Gasterosteus Doryssus]] from the [[Lahontan Beds]] of Nevada (Pleistocene), which is related to the common stickleback and is not closely related to *P. haynesi*.
- A Pleistocene form from Canada (Dawson, 1894) related to Gasterosteus aculeatus.
- A Neogene form from western Siberia (Shtylko, 1934).

## Key Findings

1. **New species.** *Pungitius haynesi* is described as a new Neogene stickleback and is the only fossil stickleback known from California.

2. **Close relationship to the nine-spined stickleback.** Head and body structure and vertebral count (34) agree with *P. pungitius*. Comparison with two Recent specimens from [[Lake Nipigon]], Ontario (donated by [[Carl L. Hubbs]]), showed that one agreed in vertebral count (15 + 19 = 34), while the other had 15 + 20 = 35. Differences include a slightly longer body and a more posterior insertion of the second dorsal fin, which the author treats as within the range of the living form.

3. **Age.** The specimen is not likely to be older than middle Miocene, and its presence in the Pliocene is judged more likely. The Ridge Formation is described as upper Miocene or Pliocene, and the author notes that the geologic age cannot yet be determined definitively.

4. **Freshwater origin.** The fish indicates a freshwater depositional setting. The author concludes that *P. haynesi* represents a freshwater variety of the *P. pungitius* lineage.

5. **Extinction.** The species probably disappeared from California at a later time, possibly due to climatic change.

6. **Evolutionary implications.** The wide modern distribution of sticklebacks across the Northern Hemisphere suggests they have existed for a long time, and the principal species of the Gasterosteidae may have been established by Miocene time.

7. **Need for further material.** The author stresses that more fossil specimens with better-preserved dorsal spines are needed to verify the relationship to the living nine-spined stickleback.
