# Chemical Vegetation Control Manual for Fish and Wildlife Management Programs

#fisheries-management #aquatic-ecology #vegetation-control #environmental-policy

## Executive Summary

This 1968 manual from the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] provides comprehensive guidance on herbicide use for vegetation control in fish and wildlife management programs. Issued by the [[U.S. Department of the Interior]] under Secretary [[Stewart L. Udall]], the document establishes strict policies requiring that pesticide use be "fully consistent with the protection of the entire environment" and that "where there is a reasonable doubt regarding the environmental effects...no use should be made." The manual covers 16 major herbicide classes including phenoxy compounds (2,4-D, MCPA, 2,4,5-T, silvex), copper sulfate, dalapon, diquat, endothall, and others. It provides detailed application rates, toxicity data, and species-specific control recommendations for hundreds of terrestrial and aquatic plant species. Notably, no herbicide except [[Copper Sulfate]] was approved for use in potable water or flowing natural streams, and no residue tolerances for fish or shellfish had been established by the [[Food and Drug Administration]] for any herbicide at the time of publication.

## Background & Description

The manual was developed by Thomas E. DeVaney of the Division of Pesticide Registration and issued as Resource Publication 48 in January 1968. It serves as the primary guideline for Bureau personnel and cooperators in translating departmental policy into practical pest plant control solutions. The document explicitly restricts several chemical classes from Bureau use, including arsenicals, chlorinated hydrocarbons, dinitro compounds, and amitrole, due to "undue hazard to public health or to resources other than fish and wildlife."

The manual emphasizes that all Federal programs or programs supported by Federal funds must have prior approval by the [[Federal Committee on Pest Control]], with no pesticides exempt from this requirement. Maximum application rates are established for specific chemicals on Bureau lands: 2,4-D limited to 8 lbs. acid equivalent per acre per year for broadcast applications (40 lbs. for spot treatments), silvex to 8 lbs. per acre per year, diquat to 3 lbs. active ingredient per acre per year on land and 15 lbs. on water areas, and phenylurea compounds (diuron, simazine, atrazine) to 4 lbs. per acre per year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The manual addresses both terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems, with particular attention to impacts on fish populations and water quality. For [[Copper Sulfate]], the most widely used algicide, toxicity data shows dramatic variation by species and water chemistry. Trout tolerate only 0.14 ppm while [[Smallmouth Bass]] can withstand 2.00 ppm. Water hardness critically affects toxicity: in soft water (15 ppm hardness), [[Bluegill]] experienced 100% mortality at 0.8 ppm copper sulfate, while in hard water (132 ppm hardness) 70 ppm was required for complete mortality. The 48-hour LD₅₀ for bluegills ranged from 0.6 ppm in very soft water to 45.0 ppm in very hard water (1544 ppm alkalinity).

The document acknowledges that water-bloom algae, while sometimes problematic, are "usually desirable in shallow lakes as the turbidity they create tends to shade out undesirable vegetation, thereby maintaining a better habitat for fish." However, excessive algal blooms in fertile agricultural areas or waters receiving sewage effluents can cause fish kills through nighttime oxygen depletion, as algae consume oxygen during dark periods despite producing it during photosynthesis.

For aquatic vegetation control, the manual provides species-specific recommendations. [[Coontail]] (*Ceratophyllum demersum*), [[Water Stargrass]] (*Heteranthera dubia*), and [[Watermilfoil]] (*Myriophyllum* spp.) can be controlled with iso-octyl ester of 2,4-D at 20 lbs. acid equivalent per acre during vigorous growth. [[Spatterdock]] (*Nuphar luteum*) requires 6-8 lbs. per acre with repeat applications 4-6 weeks later. [[Alligatorweed]] (*Alternanthera philoxeroides*), described as "a serious problem for fish and waterfowl management" and "difficult to control," responds best to silvex propylene glycol butyl ether ester at 8 lbs. per acre with repeat treatments every 4-6 weeks.

## Key Findings

**Policy Framework**: The Department established that contemplated pesticide use must account for "both known and possible environmental effect" with the guiding rule that "where there is a reasonable doubt regarding the environmental effects...no use should be made." This precautionary principle preceded modern environmental legislation.

**Herbicide Restrictions**: Except for copper sulfate, no herbicide was approved for use in potable water or flowing natural streams. No residue tolerances for fish or shellfish had been established by FDA for any herbicide, meaning fish and shellfish from treated waters could not be sold for human consumption.

**Copper Sulfate Toxicity**: Application rates for algae control (0.05-10.0 ppm depending on species) often approached or exceeded fish tolerance levels, particularly for sensitive species like trout (0.14 ppm maximum). Water hardness dramatically affected toxicity, with soft water increasing copper sulfate toxicity by up to 75-fold compared to very hard water.

**Formulation Matters**: For phenoxy compounds, the choice of formulation (ester vs. salt) critically affected both efficacy and aquatic toxicity. Low-volatile esters (butoxyethanol, iso-octyl, propylene glycol butyl ether) were equally or more effective than volatile esters and presented lower drift hazards. Specific 2,4-D derivatives considered hazardous to fish included butyl ester, isopropyl ester, butoxyethanol ester, ethyl ester, and several amine salts.

**Newer Alternatives**: Endothall and diquat were identified as "far less toxic to fish" than copper sulfate for controlling some aquatic plant species, representing emerging alternatives to older, more hazardous chemicals. However, the dimethylcocoamine derivative of endothall was "more than 100 times as toxic to fish" than sodium or potassium salts, making salt selection imperative.

**Application Rates**: The manual provides precise application rates for 200+ plant species across three categories: annual/perennial herbaceous plants (60+ species), woody vegetation (80+ species), and aquatic vegetation (60+ species). Rates varied from 0.5 ppm for [[Curlyleaf Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton crispus*) with silvex to 30 lbs. acid equivalent per acre for [[Spatterdock]] control with 2,4-D granules.

**Ecological Considerations**: The manual emphasizes that "vegetation control and all habitat management programs should always be governed by ecological principles" and that control programs "should be ethical and defensible," citing the need to maintain "the integrity of plant and animal communities" and recognize "the material and spiritual needs of other men."

**Water Chemistry Effects**: Temperature, hardness, turbidity, and carbon dioxide content all affected herbicide toxicity to aquatic organisms. Effectiveness of some herbicides was "greatly reduced when the temperature is below 40° F." Diquat was "readily absorbed on clay particles and should not be used in muddy water."
