# Spawning and Movements of Razorback Sucker in the Green River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines spawning timing, movements, and habitat use of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the upper [[Green River]] and lower [[Yampa River]] of Utah and Colorado, using electrofishing, [[Capture-Recapture]] records, and [[Radiotelemetry]] from 1987 to 1989. Ripe fish were captured from mid-April to early June, mainly during rising and peak spring flows at water temperatures averaging about 14°C. Some individuals undertook spawning migrations of 30 to 106 km, while others were local or undetected. The authors conclude that the species is in peril because of low numbers of spawning adults, [[Hybridization]] with other catostomids, little or no recruitment, and habitat loss.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is one of several catostomid fishes endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Historically abundant enough to be harvested commercially, it is now threatened with extinction. It was proposed for listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and is protected by six western states (Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming).

Natural populations persist in some reservoirs of the lower Colorado River, the upper Colorado River near Grand Junction, sporadically in the lower San Juan River, and in the Green and Yampa rivers. The authors describe the Green River population as small but the largest extant riverine population of the species. Stocks are composed of old individuals, and recruitment is minimal or nonexistent across the basin.

Sampling used electrofishing in the upper Green River (km 396.8 to 552.0) and lower Yampa River (km 0 to 4), supplemented by trammel netting in the lower 72 km of the Yampa. Sex and spawning condition were determined by expression of milt or eggs. Fish with breeding tubercles but no expressible products were classed as unripe. New captures were tagged with Carlin-type tags, weighed, and measured. Radio-implanted fish were tracked by radio receivers. Movements were classed as "local" (remaining within a spawning reach) or "migrations" (between rivers or spawning reaches). Water temperature, depth, velocity, substrate, and habitat type were recorded at each capture point.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning habitat.** Ripe fish were captured in runs associated with submerged bars of cobble, gravel, and sand. Mean bar depth was 0.63 m (n = 118, SD = 0.15) and mean water velocity was 0.74 m/s (n = 40, SD = 0.21). Simultaneous capture of ripe males and females indicated use of specific riffles for spawning, although spawning behavior was not observed because of high turbidity. Females showed abrasions on the caudal peduncle, which the authors interpret as substrate preparation before egg deposition.

**Flooded lowlands.** Three ripe fish were captured in quiet, low-velocity habitats of sand-silt and vegetated substrate, including [[Old Charley Wash]] and the mouths of tributaries such as [[Ashley Creek]] and the [[Duchesne River]]. The authors suggest that flooded lowlands and lower tributary reaches serve as resting and feeding areas during the breeding season.

**Temperature and flow.** Spawning occurred during increasing and peak spring flows. Mean water temperature was 13.8°C at the [[Yampa Site]] (range 10.5 to 16°C) and 14.1°C at the [[Jensen Site]] (range 9 to 17°C). In 1986, 47% of ripe or tuberculate fish were captured in flooded lowlands at mean temperatures of about 19°C, compared with 15.8°C in the main channel. Cited laboratory work reports adult preferences of 22 to 25°C and avoidance of 8 to 15°C.

**Co-occurring species.** Ripe [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), along with putative razorback × flannelmouth hybrids, were captured in the same riffles. Flannelmouth and bluehead suckers were captured over longer periods (45 and 42 days) and spawning of all three species overlapped between mid-April and late May.

## Key Findings

**Sample and population structure**
- 230 adult razorback suckers were captured from 1987 to 1989 (125 newly tagged). Seventy fish were recaptured on 105 occasions, and 24 of these were recaptured more than once.
- 194 fish (84%) were ripe: 144 males (74%) and 50 females (26%). Six further fish were tuberculate but not ripe.
- Total length ranged from 405 to 597 mm. Males averaged 507 mm TL (SD = 23.76) and 1,370 g. Females averaged 547 mm TL (n = 45, SD = 20.56) and 1,686 g (n = 43, SD = 296.98).
- No razorback suckers smaller than 405 mm were captured, which the authors take as evidence that recruitment is low or absent.
- Combined with 1981 to 1986 data, 58% of 530 adult captures were ripe or tuberculate. Breeding males outnumbered breeding females by about 2.5:1.0, compared with 1.6:1.0 reported earlier.

**Breeding behavior and timing**
- Breeding tubercles appeared on males shortly before readiness to spawn and faded soon after spawning. Heavily tuberculate males recaptured 11 to 35 days later were no longer ripe.
- Recaptured males were ripe for 2 to 28 days and females for 2 to 15 days (about 8 days on average).
- Ripe females were captured over an average of 27 days and males over 34 days. The spawning period was probably earlier than observed, because ripe fish were caught on the first sampling day in 1987 and 1989.
- Spawning extended into June in high-runoff years (1984 and 1986).

**Site fidelity and capture locations**
- The Jensen site (km 486.4 to 504.0) yielded 177 breeding captures (about 91%). The Yampa site (lower 5 km) yielded 14 (about 7%).
- Twenty-seven breeding fish were recaptured in the same spawning reach in different years. Six Jensen fish were recaptured at that site in three different years. One male captured in 1981 was recaptured at the same location in 1988 while ripe.

**Movements**
- Eight radiotagged fish (58 recaptures) remained within restricted reaches and were classed as local. Most used the Jensen site.
- Seven radiotagged migratory fish (12 recaptures) moved 52.8 to 190.4 km.
- Thirteen fish moved between the Jensen site and Ouray, with a mean one-way movement of 94.4 km (range 54 to 106 km). Nine of these were collected ripe during the movement period.
- Four fish moved between Jensen and the Yampa site (mean one-way 49.6 km, range 30.4 to 60.8 km) through [[Split Mountain Canyon]].
- Overall, spawning migrations of 30 to 106 km were detected in 17 fish. Movements of other individuals were more limited or undetected.

**Hybridization and co-occurring suckers**
- Putative razorback × flannelmouth hybrids were intermediate in skull, mouth, and nape morphology. They were found in Yampa Canyon (km 0 to 72) and the upper Green River (km 478.4 to 499.2). The most upstream suspected hybrid was 67.2 km above the Green River confluence.
- In 1989, flannelmouth and bluehead suckers were each about eight times more numerous than razorback suckers at the Yampa site. Flannelmouth suckers were twice as abundant as razorback suckers and four times as abundant as bluehead suckers at the Jensen site.

**Conservation concerns**
- The authors identify four main threats: low numbers of reproducing adults, lack of recruitment, habitat loss, and predation by non-native fish. Habitat loss includes impoundment, dikes, altered spring flooding, and loss of access to floodplain habitat.
- Hybridization is likely enhanced by the abundance of flannelmouth suckers, the scarcity of razorback suckers, and their spawning overlap. Its effects on razorback decline are not well understood.
- The authors suggest that fidelity to specific spawning riffles implies more than one stock in the Green River basin. If so, reintroduction programs should consider maintaining separate genetic strains.
- Longevity of perhaps 40 years means that adults may persist long after recruitment has failed, so the authors call for more immediate recovery measures.
