# A Comparative Study of the Weberian Apparatus in the Genera Dionda and Notropis (Cyprinidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 Master of Science thesis by Morgan Emery Sisk, submitted to [[Oklahoma State University]], compares the bony elements of the [[Weberian Apparatus]] in three species of [[Dionda]] and six species of [[Notropis]], plus one hybrid combination. The author set out to test whether this small, internal skeletal complex could help clarify the relationship between the two genera, which are superficially similar and were traditionally separated mainly by gut length.

The study concludes that the apparatus is highly variable within species and only weakly differentiated between genera. Its taxonomic value is therefore judged "inconclusive." The ossicles are described as having no taxonomic value, while the [[Neural Complex]] shape and a spur on the third neural pedicle show the clearest, though still tentative, differences between the genera.

## Background & Description

The [[Weberian Apparatus]] is a chain of modified anterior vertebrae and small ossicles connecting the [[Swim Bladder]] to the inner ear. It is characteristic of the [[Ostariophysi]], a group defined by Jordan (1929) as fishes "with the anterior vertebrae modified to connect with the air-bladder and inclosing an organ of hearing." Its function remained undetermined; proposed roles include sensing changes in atmospheric pressure, aiding hearing, and acting as a hydrostatic organ. The author notes that recent experiments indicate it may serve one or more of these functions.

The apparatus has two parts:

- **[[Pars Sustentaculum]]:** the first four modified vertebrae, which are not fused to one another in these minnows.
- **[[Pars Auditum]]:** four ossicles (claustrum, scaphium, intercalarium, tripus) and the interossicular ligament.

The thesis reviews a long history of work, from [[Ernst Heinrich Weber]] (1820) through [[Chranilov]] (1926–1930), [[Watson]] (1939), and [[Krumholz]] (1943), with several competing nomenclatures for the ossicles. The author follows Bridge and Haddon (1889) because it avoids confusion with the mammalian middle-ear bones.

Most specimens came from the [[Oklahoma State University]] fish collection. Three *D. diaboli* were donated by [[Clark Hubbs]]. Fresh material was collected from Oklahoma localities including Lake Texoma, Blue River, Little River, Red River, and several creeks and rivers in Cherokee, Delaware, and Sequoyah counties.

Preparation involved several techniques:

- **[[Alizarin Red-S Staining]]:** a modification of Hollister's (1934) in toto method. Viscera, eyes, and scales were removed, then specimens soaked 72 hours in 2% potassium hydroxide, stained until cherry red, and left 72–96 additional hours. This was the most successful method.
- **Potassium hydroxide maceration:** 72 hours in 2% KOH loosened muscle tissue in larger fish without disarticulating the ossicles.
- **Brine fixation:** fixing live fish in strong brine (1/2 lb table salt per pint of water) disarticulated the apparatus within 48 hours at temperatures above 26 °C, though ossicles could still be salvaged and stained.
- **Preserved material:** dissection of formalin-fixed, alcohol-preserved specimens failed, as the tissue became too hard.

Illustrations were made in charcoal with a camera lucida.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study covers freshwater minnows from Oklahoma and Texas streams and rivers. The author's comparisons of body form link the apparatus to ecology: *Dionda* are slender-bodied, whereas most notropids are laterally compressed and deeper-bodied. *Notropis percobromus* is an exception, approaching the slender *Dionda* form, and its neural complex is correspondingly lower and more horizontally elongate. The author suggests that neural complex shape may reflect general body shape rather than phylogeny.

The thesis also notes that ossicle variation may relate to age. Larger, older specimens show a longer anterior ramus of the tripus, a thicker distal intercalarium, and ossification within the interossicular ligament. In one large *N. cornutus* (153 mm standard length), ossification appeared at two points in the ligament, which the author suggests could eventually impair ossicle function in very old fish.

## Key Findings

**Structure of the apparatus**

- The apparatus in *Notropis* and *Dionda* includes the first four modified vertebrae, none fused to the others.
- The overall apparatus is similar across both genera but highly variable within all species examined.
- Lateral projections of the first and second centra are reinterpreted as **pleurapophyses** (modified pleural ribs fused to the centra), following Hyman (1942).
- The ossa suspensoria are described as independent outgrowths of the fourth pleural ribs, not part of the fourth centrum. This conflicts with earlier interpretations, including [[Watson]] (1939) and [[Nelson]] (1948).

**Differences between the genera**

1. The [[Pars Sustentaculum]] of *Notropis* is stouter and heavier.
2. A spur-like process on the third neural pedicle is present in *Notropis* (six species) and absent in *Dionda*, where only a small ridge remains.
3. The [[Neural Complex]] is usually vertically elongate and cup-shaped (wine-glass profile) in *Notropis*, but horizontally elongate and boat-shaped in *Dionda*.

The author judges the neural complex differences to be of dubious taxonomic value. They may represent a gradient linked to body shape.

**Taxonomic value**

- The [[Weberian Ossicles]] are of no taxonomic value. They are highly variable within species and often differ between left and right sides of a single specimen.
- The [[Tripus]] is of no taxonomic value for any of the nine species. Fenestration varies even within a species.
- The intercalarium is the most variable ossicle across all species.
- Overall, the author concludes that the apparatus offers inconclusive evidence about the taxonomic relationship of *Dionda* and *Notropis*. It does indicate that the genera are closely related.

**Neural complex within Notropis**

- Considerable variation exists among notropid species. The author notes that the subgenus *Cyprinellus* (*N. lutrensis*, *N. venustus*, *N. whipplei*) has morphologically distinct neural complexes, even though *N. lutrensis* and *N. venustus* are closely related and interbreed freely (Hubbs and Strawn, 1956).
- Specific variation in *Dionda* is negligible.

**Hybrid specimens**

- The six presumed *N. lutrensis* × *N. venustus* hybrids had neural complexes almost identical to *N. venustus*, with no intermediacy. The author suggests either dominance by *N. venustus* or that some specimens may not be hybrids.

**Notochord and vertebral remnants**

- A cartilage-like ring between centra resembles an intervertebral disc. In some specimens, a cartilaginous slip extended through a centrum, indicating incomplete notochord ossification.

**Neural complex origin (tentative)**

- The neural complex appears to represent the neural spines of the second and third vertebrae plus fused interneural bones, anterior to the fourth neural spine. This is a tentative hypothesis pending embryological study.

**Suggestions for further study**

1. An embryological study of the apparatus in two or three cyprinid genera.
2. Critical examination of the neural complex in all *Notropis* species.
3. Further variation studies in the large genus *Notropis*.
4. A comparison of *Dionda* with *Hybognathus*, which have been considered congeneric.
