#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cichlidae

## Executive Summary
This study by Vives (1994) investigates parental preference behavior in free-swimming larval **convict cichlids** ([[Cichlasoma Nigrofasciatum]]), a biparental New World cichlid native to Central America. Using 14 breeding pairs (both 'gold' and wild-type color morphs) across 21 broods and 69 experimental trials, the author tested whether groups of 2-day-old free-swimming fry preferentially approached and remained near their mother or father when the parents were sequestered in a three-compartment test tank. The study examined the influence of (1) color morph, (2) rearing in the presence vs. absence of fry predators, and (3) the duration of parent-offspring separation prior to testing. Overall, 68% of fry groups made a clear choice, with a strong maternal bias (74% of choices) and no detectable effect of color morph or parental body size. Separation time strongly interacted with rearing condition to determine both the likelihood of making a choice and the latency to choice, with predator-reared fry showing faster and more persistent approach responses.

## Background & Description
- **Sexual size dimorphism**: Males were consistently larger than females.
  - Male standard length: mean 6.15 cm (SD = 0.85; range 4.85–7.75 cm; N = 21)
  - Female standard length: mean 4.99 cm (SD = 0.66; range 3.9–6.5 cm; N = 21)
  - Mean pairwise size difference: 1.24 cm (SD = 0.64; range 0.25–2.7 cm), males always larger.
- **Color morphs**: Both 'gold' and wild-type pigmentation morphs were used; these are known to differ behaviorally in related contexts (e.g., adoption rates), but showed **no significant difference** in parent-choice outcomes in this study.
- **Developmental timeline** (relevant to morphological/ontogenetic staging):
  - Egg hatching: 2–3 days post-spawning (mean 2.5 days, SD = 0.6)
  - Yolk-sac absorption / onset of free-swimming and exogenous feeding: 3–5 days post-hatch (mean 3.9 days, SD = 0.7)
  - Test subjects: fry at 2 days free-swimming, chosen because mobility and exogenous feeding are well established at this stage, increasing vulnerability to predation and behavioral responsiveness to parental models.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range**: Pacific slope of Guatemala to Costa Rica; Atlantic slope of Costa Rica and Nicaragua.
- **Reproductive ecology**: [[Convict Cichlid]] pairs are cavity spawners, using natural cavities or self-excavated cavities (McKaye 1977). This is typical of many New World [[Cichlidae]] with **biparental care**.
- **Division of parental labor**: The mother remains proximal to the free-swimming fry school, while the father patrols and defends the territory and fry distally — a pattern consistent across Central American cichlids and central to the study's interpretation of maternal choice bias.
- **Predation pressure**: Fry straying from the parental school are vulnerable to predation by conspecific juveniles/adults and heterospecific fish (e.g., other cichlids). Predator identity in experimental rearing tanks included adult/juvenile convict cichlids, *Tilapia mariae*, and *Cichlasoma bimaculatum*.
- **Schooling/shoaling behavior**: Parents act as a focal/organizing point for the fry school; fry displaced from the school face elevated predation risk, consistent with Hamilton's (1971) "selfish herd" geometry.
- **Comparative context**: Similar parent-recognition dynamics have been documented in other cichlids, including [[Etroplus Maculatus]], [[Herotilapia Multispinosa]], [[Hemichromis Bimaculatus]], [[Nannacara Anomala]], and [[Cichlasoma Citrinellum]] (Midas cichlid).

## Key Findings
- **Choice frequency**: 47 of 69 fry groups (68%) made a definitive choice for one parent, significantly more than expected by chance (χ² = 9.06, df = 1, P < 0.005).
- **No color-morph or size effect**: Gold vs. wild-type parent/offspring pairs did not differ in choice outcomes (χ² = 0.768, df = 2, P = 0.69); parental size also had no significant effect on choice occurrence (χ² = 0.904, df = 1, P > 0.25) or latency (Spearman r_s = –0.11).
- **Maternal bias**: Among groups making a choice, females were selected ~74% of the time (35 of 47), nearly three times as often as males (χ² = 11.26, df = 1, P < 0.005) — likely reflecting the mother's closer proximal association with fry during normal care behavior.
- **Separation time effects**: The duration fry were separated from parents before testing significantly affected whether a choice was made (χ² = 9.236, df = 3, P = 0.027), with a non-linear pattern (not a simple decay function).
- **Predator × separation-time interaction**:
  - At <60 min separation, nearly all groups chose regardless of predator-rearing history.
  - At 61–120 min separation, predator-reared fry still reliably chose a parent (10/11 trials), whereas non-predator-reared fry made **no** choices (Fisher exact test, P = 0.00003) — a striking divergence.
  - At >121 min, rearing condition had no detectable effect.
- **Reaction time (latency to choice)**: Predator-reared fry groups showed significantly shorter latencies to choice than non-predator-reared groups (Wilcoxon rank-sum, one-tailed, P = 0.045), suggesting a stronger and more persistent approach response when predation risk was experienced during rearing.
- **Paternal choices concentrated under predation risk**: Of 12 total paternal choices, 10 came from predator-reared groups, potentially reflecting closer male-fry association (and thus familiarity) in predator-present rearing tanks.
- **Individual pair consistency**: Data from five pairs that reared multiple broods showed within-pair consistency in choice patterns (e.g., one pair — "Pair E" — always chose the father when a choice was made), suggesting brood-rearing style consistency within a pair rather than a purely heritable trait.
- **Mechanistic interpretation**: The study argues fry may respond to generic "femaleness"/"maleness" cues rather than specific parental recognition per se, consistent with prior chemosensory work by [[Barnett 1982]] and [[Barnett 1986]] on *C. citrinellum*.
- **Methodological contribution**: Unlike prior studies (e.g., Barnett 1982, 1986) that excluded "no choice" trials from analysis, this study explicitly incorporates and analyzes the substantial fraction (32%) of non-choosing groups, revealing the importance of separation time and rearing condition as confounding variables in parent-recognition experiments.
