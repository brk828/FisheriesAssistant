# Status, Distribution, Ecology and Conservation of the Rare Southwestern (USA) Trouts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary
This is a brief manuscript, circulated for internal review in January 1990 by J. N. Rinne of the USDA Forest Service, Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station. It was prepared as a poster paper for the International Symposium on the Biology and Conservation of Rare Fish (London, July 1990) and for publication in the Journal of Fish Biology. The reviewers' cover letter asks for further abbreviation, because the text is scheduled to occupy only about 2 published pages. The paper summarizes the taxonomy, description, distribution, habitat, biology and conservation status of two rare native trouts of the southwestern USA, the [[Apache Trout]] and the [[Gila Trout]]. Both are now confined to small headwater streams after habitat loss, hybridization and competition from introduced salmonids. Both were listed as endangered, and recovery teams and plans have been developed.

## Background & Description
**Taxonomy.** Early explorers found native "speckled" or "yellowbelly" trout in the headwaters of the [[Gila River]] in Arizona and New Mexico. They were first reported in 1875 as a variety of [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo pleuriticus*), based on three specimens collected in 1873 from the "White River," Arizona. About 75 years later R. R. Miller described the Gila trout (*Salmo gilae*) from Main Diamond Creek, New Mexico. All native trout of the [[White Mountains]] were provisionally included under that name. Miller later described the Apache trout (*Salmo apache*) from the East Fork of the White River. Osteological studies (1988) placed these Pacific trouts in the genus *Oncorhynchus*, together with the salmons. The Gila and Apache trouts are more closely related to each other than to other western North American *Oncorhynchus*.

**Apache trout.**
- Golden-olive-yellow coloration, with spotting on the fins and a yellow "cutthroat" mark under the lower jaw.
- Purple and pink tints may appear, but not in a lateral band as in [[Rainbow Trout]].
- Body is deeper and laterally compressed, with uniformly spaced, medium-sized, roundish spots.
- Large dorsal fin; the dorsal, pelvic and anal fins have a conspicuous cream or yellowish tip.

**Gila trout.**
- Typically has more and larger spots than the Apache trout, retains parr marks as adults, and is more iridescent gold.
- Spots are small and diffuse, generally confined above the lateral line, and the adipose fin is well spotted.
- Fins have white to yellowish tips, and adults have a faint salmon-pink lateral band.
- It also has the golden or yellowish "cutthroat" mark.
- Considerable variation in color and spotting reflects natural variation plus introgression from rainbow trout and possibly cutthroat trout genes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
**Habitat.** Because of habitat loss and hybridization, pure-strain Apache and Gila trout occupy only the extreme headwaters of drainages. These streams have highly variable conditions of flooding, drought and wildfire. Population size varies greatly, yet densities and biomass are comparatively high in these harsh streams (Apache trout about 13 g per square meter; Gila trout about 14 g per square meter, as reported in the text).

**Threats.**
- Hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]].
- Competition with introduced [[Brown Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]].
- Habitat degradation from logging, livestock grazing, road building, damming and water diversion.
- Some populations were lost entirely.

**Associated management.** More than 4,000 habitat improvement structures were built in Arizona and New Mexico between the 1930s and mid-1960s.

## Key Findings
**Distribution and status**
- Apache trout once occupied the headwaters of the [[Little Colorado River]], [[Salt River]] and [[San Francisco River]]. In the late 1800s it could be caught at about 100 fish per hour.
- Pure Apache trout populations now occur, naturally or through introduction, in about a dozen streams, mostly on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]].
- Apache trout occupy less than 48 km (30 mi) of an original 965 km (597 mi) of stream habitat (about 5%, as given in the OCR text). It was listed as endangered by the U.S. Department of the Interior and by the [[IUCN]] in the 1960s (the scan shows 1967 for both).
- The Gila trout's original range is poorly defined because of a lack of pre-1950 collections. In 1896 it extended downstream to Cliff, New Mexico, where one could catch about one per minute.
- By the late 1960s nearly all of the total Gila trout population resided in [[Main Diamond Creek]], the type locality. By the mid-1980s an introduced population in McKnight Creek (Mimbres River headwaters) was the second largest.
- Gila trout are now naturally restricted to five extreme headwater streams above barrier falls or dry streambeds. They were listed as endangered in 1967.
- Gila trout numbers fluctuated about 10-fold over 2-3 years in Main Diamond and McKnight creeks, owing to flooding, drought and wildfire.

**Biology: Apache trout** (Big Bonito Creek, Fort Apache Indian Reservation)
- Spawned on receding flows at about 7 °C (44 °F) over gravel-pebble substrate.
- Females matured at about 15 cm (6 in), at age 3.
- Fecundity was estimated at about 2,000 eggs per kilogram of body weight.
- Eggs hatched in about 18 days (scan unclear), fry emerged in 60 days, and fry moved downstream at night.
- Thermal tolerance of fingerlings: feeding ceases at 20 °C (68 °F), equilibrium is lost at about 21-22 °C, and death occurs at about 22 °C (scan unclear).
- Fish reach large size in lakes (about 40-50 cm) but are generally small (under about 28 cm) in headwater streams.

**Biology: Gila trout**
- Spawning onset is determined more by rising water temperature than by receding flows.
- Redd construction began at about 9 °C in 6 to 13 cm of water, near the banks and within 5 m of cover, over gravel of 0.2-3.8 cm.
- Spawning started earlier in lower-elevation streams.
- Fry emerged in 8-10 weeks and used riffles, apparently avoiding pools because of competition and possible cannibalism by adults.
- Short-term tolerance of water up to about 27 °C (scan unclear).
- Mark-recapture work shows the species is mostly sedentary, with average movements mostly downstream and under 100 m.

**Conservation history**
- The [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]] led early Apache trout conservation. The Arizona Game and Fish Department began a hatchery program in the 1960s, and by 1964 the Tribe had adopted a plan for chemical stream reclamation and fish barriers against upstream rainbow trout movement.
- An Apache trout recovery team was organized in 1975 and a recovery plan completed in 1979/1980 (scan unclear). The main goal is to establish 30 pure, self-sustaining populations and restore non-threatened status.
- Threatened status has allowed limited sport angling since 1975. [[Christmas Tree Lake]] offers a quality fishery with a large mean size, and catchability is very high. A sustained sport fishery is seen as a driver of conservation.
- Gila trout conservation began with the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish in 1921, which also built a hatchery in the [[Gila Wilderness]]. A recovery team formed in the mid-1970s, and the goal is to replicate all five naturally occurring populations before downlisting to "threatened." Gila trout are also to be assessed as a sport species.
- Both trouts should be conserved for their uniqueness. They evolved in the Gila and Verde River headwaters and survive only with constant vigilance, though considerable progress has been made in two decades.
