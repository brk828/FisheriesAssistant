# The Fish of Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1950s-era educational pamphlet, written by William L. Reavley and published by the [[Colorado Game and Fish Department]] (Educational Pamphlet Number Three, Colorado Conservation Magazine), explains the problems that affect fishing in Colorado and describes the principal game and rough fish of the state. It is organized in three parts: high-country trout management, warm-water fish management, and the roles of research and hatchery operations. Its central message is that sport fishing depends on active public understanding and support for wise management of watersheds, water supplies and fish populations.

## Background & Description

The pamphlet is addressed to the "new generation of Coloradoans." It notes that more people in an expanding population make preserving fishing as an outdoor sport harder over time. The text is a general educational publication rather than a formal research report, and it contains no new sampling data beyond the figures cited in its examples.

Colorado is described as the highest state in the nation. The source states that the state has 1,143 mountains reaching 10,000 feet or higher, and that Colorado claims 75 per cent of the area in the United States above 10,000 feet. Six major rivers begin in the state: the Colorado, [[Rio Grande]], Arkansas, South Platte, North Platte and Republican. The source also estimates there are 300,000 fishermen in Colorado.

Fish described in the pamphlet fall into two groups:

- **High-country (cold-water) species:** [[Native Trout]] (cutthroat), [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Grayling]], [[Suckers]] and [[Minnows]].
- **Warm-water species:** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[White Bass]], [[Walleyed Pike]], [[Freshwater Drum]], [[Carp]], plus bluegill and crappie, which are discussed as examples of overpopulation problems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Carrying capacity and watersheds.** Water quality is judged by the pounds of fish a water can support per acre, which the source treats as a definite limit. Trout need shelter, food and places to rear young. Human land use reduces this capacity. Grazing, timber cutting, mining, recreation, and forest fires that remove soil-protecting plants all cause erosion, which can fill trout streams with soil and rock. Vegetation, in the form of mulch and growing plants, protects streams from this sediment.

**Water use and pollution.** Growing domestic, industrial and agricultural demand leaves less water for fish. Dams alter fish habitat and create management problems. Sewage and mining and manufacturing wastes are described as serious threats to fishing waters.

**Warm-water reservoirs.** Most eastern-slope reservoirs have fluctuating water levels. This limits fish food production and can interrupt spawning or prevent eggs from hatching. Overpopulation is a recurring issue where bluegill and crappie reproduce rapidly. Carp are described as hardy enough to survive conditions fatal to game fish and to become dominant in many waters.

**Ice fishing.** Most lakes below 7,000 feet are open to ice fishing in winter.

## Key Findings

**High-country species**

- **[[Native Trout]]:** The only trout in Colorado when white settlers arrived; named for the red marking on the lower jaw. It spawns from March into July, depending on water temperature, and is being crowded out of its former range. The source says it can still give excellent fishing, especially in lakes.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]:** Almost half of the trout caught in Colorado are rainbow. More rainbow are raised and planted than any other trout. The steelhead is described as a rainbow that runs to the ocean.
- **[[Mountain Whitefish]]:** Found in the White, Elk and Yampa rivers of northwestern Colorado. It spawns in the fall, lays more eggs per female than trout of equal size, and can survive heavy fishing without artificial planting. Winter whitefish fishing is growing in popularity.
- **[[Grayling]]:** Native to Montana tributaries of the Missouri River. Colorado raises some and made a recent plant in Grand Lake. Grayling decline when brown, rainbow or brook trout are present. Their large, bright dorsal fin identifies them.
- **[[Suckers]]:** Often given a poor reputation. Claims that they eat large numbers of trout eggs are described as unproven. In lakes they can compete with trout for food.
- **[[Minnows]]:** The family [[Cyprinidae]] includes chubs, dace, shiners, squawfish, carp and minnows. The [[Colorado River Squawfish]] can reach 80 pounds. Minnows are described as important forage for game fish.

**Warm-water species**

- **[[Largemouth Bass]]:** Introduced from eastern and southern native ranges; prefers sluggish, weedy water. Females can carry as many as 10,000 eggs. Bass are stocked to reduce pan fish populations, although the source notes that bass prefer insects and crustaceans to fish.
- **[[White Bass]]:** First planted in Colorado in 1948 at [[John Martin Reservoir]]; now found in Adobe Creek Reservoir, Bonny Reservoir and Loveland Lake. Can reach four pounds.
- **[[Walleyed Pike]]:** A recent import from the perch family, occasionally caught at up to 25 pounds. Larger walleyes eat mostly fish, so they are introduced to control overpopulation. Still-fishing with minnows on the bottom gives the best results in Colorado.
- **[[Freshwater Drum]]:** Introduced into [[Bonny Dam]] in 1951. Spawns about mid-June at 15 inches or more, usually reaching spawning size within two or three years. Weights of 60 pounds have been recorded.
- **[[Carp]]:** Cultivated for food for more than 4,000 years in China and for hundreds of years in Europe; introduced to the US in the 19th century. Carp grow to about fifty pounds, are used as mink feed and as cooked feed for trout at hatcheries.

**Management and research**

- **Overpopulation control:** Year-round seasons and large bag limits may help, but fish can become too small to attract anglers. Predatory species such as walleye are one alternative. Rough fish such as carp are removed by seining or by [[Derrising]], a poisoning process that suffocates fish.
- **Hatchery production:** The Department operates 25 hatcheries and rearing units. In 1951, 293 tons, or 9,600,000 individual fish, were raised and released. Eggs hatch in about 50 days at 50°F, and a six-inch fish takes about a year to grow.
- **[[Put-and-Take Stocking]]:** Heavily fished waters that nature cannot support are stocked, and hatchery fish are also used to establish species in new or fishless waters. The Department receives no general tax money, and its fish-raising costs exceed license revenue. The source warns that license fees may have to rise unless cheaper methods are found.
- **Research value:** At [[Parvin Lake]], a fenced lake with a single gate, fish marked with jaw tags or fin clips showed that smaller 2-inch trout gave better returns than 7- to 10-inch trout. The source argues that cheaper small fish can be planted in lakes, while larger fish go to streams. An annual [[Creel Census]] guides regulation changes.
- **Created waters:** A cooperative project with the Silverton Fish and Game Club took three years and $6,000, with $1,000 from the club, to create [[Andrews Lake]].
- **Farm ponds:** Built with the U.S. Soil Conservation Service to increase fishable water, though water storage projects still support most eastern-slope game fish.

**Conclusion:** The pamphlet concludes that good fishing depends on watershed protection, sound water-use planning, continued research and public support for management. It argues that each water, warm or cold, needs its own survey.
