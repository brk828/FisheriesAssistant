# DDT Residues in Fish, Birds, and Bats of New Mexico and Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This Environment-style research note reviews [[DDT]] and its breakdown products ([[DDD]] and [[DDE]]) in wildlife from New Mexico and Arizona, years after the 1972 ban on general use of DDT in the United States. Using U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) national monitoring data, unpublished FWS studies, and other published work, the authors find that residues remain elevated in several areas. Fish, duck muscle, songbirds, starlings and [[Carlsbad Caverns]] free-tailed bats all show contamination at levels that may threaten wildlife and exceed human-consumption standards. The authors conclude that the source of the DDT is still unclear, whether local legacy residues, wintering areas in Latin America, or recent input, and call for research to identify it.

## Background & Description

The authors summarize four data sources: (1) FWS national surveys of contaminants in freshwater fish ([[Common Carp]] and others), starlings and hunter-killed [[Mallard]] ducks; (2) unpublished FWS studies of pesticides in fish and songbirds; (3) other published studies; and (4) a study of organochlorine residues in duck samples from areas previously shown to be contaminated.

Several concepts frame the analysis:

- **Breakdown pathway:** DDT breaks down first to DDD, then to DDE, so the presence of DDT or DDD can point to recent inputs, while DDE alone suggests older, persistent residues.
- **Migration:** Most sampled wildlife is migratory, so residues may come from other regions, including [[Latin America]] wintering grounds.
- **Interference:** [[Toxaphene]] can produce a chromatographic peak at the same position as DDT, which prevented a definitive DDT analysis in some Arizona fish.
- **Standards:** The [[Food and Drug Administration]] (FDA) action level for total DDTs in fish is 5 ppm wet weight, and the tolerance for total DDTs in animal fat is 5.0 ppm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas.** Contamination was studied across several watersheds: the lower [[Rio Grande]], the lower [[Pecos River]], the [[Gila River]], the Verde River, the Little Colorado River, San Juan County, the Canadian River, and the Guadalupe Mountains region of New Mexico. Reservoir sites included [[Painted Rock Reservoir]] (Arizona), [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]] and [[Red Bluff Lake]] (both on the Pecos/Rio Grande system).

**Possible sources of DDT.**
- Legacy residues from agricultural use before the 1972 ban.
- Residues carried by migratory wildlife from wintering areas.
- Recent local input, including a 55-gallon drum labelled as DDT found at a Centennial airfield in 1980, and sales of [[Dicofol]] in Arizona. The authors note that commercial dicofol can contain DDT and DDE as impurities, and that Arizona sales totaled 24,000 kg of active ingredient in 1980 and 48,500 kg in 1981.
- Waste dumps. Toxic waste dumps exist at Artesia and Carlsbad, New Mexico, but the authors consider the contribution of such dumps to local DDE contamination unlikely.

**Predator risk.** In experimental studies, reproductively harmful [[Eggshell Thinning]] was produced by only 3 ppm DDE in the diet of mallards, black ducks, American kestrels and screech owls. The authors reason that predatory birds feeding heavily on contaminated starlings or fish could suffer reproductive problems. DDE-induced reproductive impairment has also been documented in the endangered [[Peregrine Falcon]] in northern New Mexico and Colorado.

## Key Findings

**1. Fish residues exceed the FDA standard.** In 1980, fish from Painted Rock Reservoir and the Gila River near Buckeye, Arizona, were analyzed:
- Painted Rock Reservoir: DDE of 9.56 ppm in [[Channel Catfish]], 6.86 ppm in [[Common Carp]], 7.53 ppm in [[Largemouth Bass]] and 0.65 ppm in [[Mozambique Tilapia]]. All four samples contained up to 0.91 ppm DDD. DDT could not be confirmed because toxaphene at 3.13 ppm in the catfish interfered with the analysis.
- Gila River carp: DDE of 6.68 ppm and 5.78 ppm. Both contained DDD at 0.20 ppm and toxaphene at 3.53 and 3.81 ppm.

The authors state that these data indicate that many fish exceed the 5 ppm FDA standard.

**2. Duck muscle in Arizona is far more contaminated than in New Mexico.** Duck muscle samples from the 1981–82 hunting season were analyzed individually:
- Arizona: eight samples exceeded the FDA tolerance for DDE. Gila River duck DDE levels were high enough to exceed the tolerance by a large margin.
- New Mexico: only one of 15 samples exceeded the tolerance.
- Comparison: muscle from a site of massive DDT contamination near a former manufacturer contained less residue than the Gila River ducks, but the site's soil and sediment concentrations were far higher.

**3. Regional hot spots in duck wings (1979–80 survey).**
- Arizona: four samples from the eastern Gila and Verde rivers near Buckeye contained DDE at 5.95, 4.92, 2.42 and 1.?0 ppm (value partly illegible in source), and two contained DDT (0.01 and 0.03 ppm). Little Colorado and lower Gila samples were below 0.80 ppm with no DDT.
- New Mexico: the highest DDE (3.02 and 0.45 ppm) came from the lower Rio Grande, and one group contained DDT at 0.02 ppm. The lower Pecos River had 0.32 ppm DDE. Pooled samples from San Juan County, the northern and middle Rio Grande and the Canadian River were all below 0.25 ppm with no DDT.

**4. Songbirds and starlings.**
- Starlings collected in 1979 from a Roswell, New Mexico, area and a Goodyear, Arizona, area had the highest DDE values in the nation. Starling DDE levels increased in both New Mexico sites between 1976 and 1979.
- Songbirds from the Guadalupe Mountains had DDE in Say's phoebe pools at 7.1 ppm and in mourning dove pools at 5.3 ppm. Other songbirds, including whip-poor-wills (3.5 ppm) and violet-green swallows (6.65 ppm), also had high residues.
- Songbirds from the Lincoln National Forest included mourning doves at 5.38 ppm. Tree swallows at Los Alamos (3.87 ppm) and cliff swallows at Taos (3.68 ppm) also exceeded 3 ppm.
- DDT and DDD were not reported in the songbird samples from 1980 and the Guadalupe surveys. The authors consider the clustering of contaminated birds in the Guadalupe region as evidence against an explanation based purely on wintering-ground exposure.

**5. Reservoir sediment and biota monitoring (1970–1980).** Elephant Butte Reservoir and Red Bluff Lake were monitored in most years from 1970 to 1974, in 1976, and in 1978 and 1980:
- Maximum DDE at Elephant Butte was 0.23 ppm, DDD 0.14 ppm and DDT 0.09 ppm. At Red Bluff Lake, the maximum DDE was 0.92 ppm.
- DDT and DDD were absent in 1973 (the year after the ban) and reappeared in later years. The authors interpret this as reduced DDT input during the first year or two after the ban, followed by continuing but smaller input than before the ban.

**6. Carlsbad Caverns bat colony.** The summer population of Mexican free-tailed bats (*Tadarida brasiliensis*) at [[Carlsbad Caverns]] declined from an estimated 8.7 million in 1936 to 200,000 in 1973.
- Fledgling residues were high in 1974, up to 300 ppm in a reference group and up to 460 ppm in an exercised group.
- In laboratory tests, some bats that were fed no food and exercised to simulate fall migration died, because DDE was mobilized from stored fat in lethal amounts.
- All bats contained DDT, with a maximum of 0.91 ppm in carcasses.
- Bats from colonies in cotton-growing regions of Arizona, Texas and California had residues one-fifth to one-third as large, although cotton accounted for over 80 percent of domestic DDT use.

**7. Conclusions and recommendations.** The authors conclude that serious damage is likely to the Carlsbad bat colony and to predatory birds in New Mexico and Arizona. They recommend:
- Intensive residue sampling of wildlife in and around heavy pesticide-use areas and near toxic waste dumps.
- Blood sampling of songbirds before and after migration to test input from wintering areas.
- Research to identify the geographic extent and source of current DDT input, and to determine whether predatory species are affected.
- Possible administrative action on current pesticide-use practices in these areas, if the source is found to be local.
