# Quitobaquito Pupfish Population Study: Pesticides, Refugia, and Population Recovery (1975–1978)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pupfish

## Executive Summary

This final report, submitted August 1, 1979, by [[Boyd E. Kynard]] (University of Arizona, Fisheries Science) to the [[National Park Service]], summarizes three phases of work on the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] at [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]] from 1975 to 1978. The study monitored the physico-chemical environment of [[Quitobaquito Spring]] and [[Williams Spring]], estimated population size and age structure, tested fish for [[Pesticide Contamination]], and established refugia. The population crashed in 1976, recovered to roughly 8,000 fish by fall 1978, and the study attributes the crash to pesticides, probably drifting from agriculture south of the border. Several refugia were established, but the report concludes that the monument's refugia remain vulnerable.

## Background & Description

The Quitobaquito pupfish is a small desert fish confined to a few spring-fed habitats on the monument. The report notes that the [[Sonoyta River]] population in Mexico, located only about [[Sonoyta River]] area miles south of Quitobaquito, is the most important population for the species' genetic and taxonomic status, and it may already be extirpated.

The study's four objectives were:

1. Water quality of Quitobaquito, Williams Spring, and candidate refugia.
2. Pupfish population size, age structure, spawning, and survival.
3. Determination of [[Methyl-Parathion]] and [[Organochlorine Pesticides]] levels in Quitobaquito pupfish.
4. Establishment of two pupfish refugia on and near the monument.

The species' status was reported to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Endangered Species Office in Albuquerque, New Mexico, on November 7, 1978, with a recommendation to list it as threatened.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Quitobaquito pool and Williams Spring water quality was essentially unchanged from earlier years and remained excellent for pupfish. Flow at Quitobaquito Spring measured 31.5 gal/min (12-17-77) and 30.0 gal/min (6-21-78). Water in [[Bates Well]] and [[Finley Tank]] differed from Quitobaquito but stayed within pupfish tolerance limits. Williams Spring was surveyed but judged unsuitable as a refugium.

**Habitat threats.** Agricultural development in the Sonoyta River valley, including concrete irrigation canals and cotton fields, increased surface and subsurface water withdrawal. The report warns that this development could expose Quitobaquito and Williams Spring to windborne pesticides. Dripping Spring was considered unsuitable because its shade limits photosynthesis and spawning temperatures and its surface area is only a few square feet.

**Fragility of isolated populations.** The [[Gachado Tank]] refugium illustrates the risk of populations that depend on hauled water. In October 1975 the tank was drained and restocked, but it dried up within five months because water was not delivered often enough, killing all pupfish.

## Key Findings

### Pesticide Contamination
- Methyl-parathion was detected in a fish sample in December 1976, but the sample was too small for quantification. Laboratory contamination was also considered.
- Samples collected in November 1977 and analyzed at the University of Arizona Pesticide Residue Laboratory found methyl-parathion at approximately **0.03 ppm** in Quitobaquito fish. An OCR-degraded value for Sonoyta River fish is not reliably readable in the source.
- Sonoyta River fish also had large peaks of p,p'-DDE, p,p'-DDD, and p,p'-DDT, with the highest levels in this population. Quitobaquito fish also contained detectable p,p'-DDE, DDD, and DDT.
- All water, sediment, and plant samples contained major sulfur and phosphorus peaks, but no parathion or paraoxon peaks, so these were not associated with any pesticide.
- The authors conclude that methyl-parathion was the most likely cause of the 1976 population decline. No other natural or man-made factor was correlated with the crash.

### Population Size and Age Structure
- Population estimate by [[Peterson Mark-Recapture Estimate]] on October 11, 1977: about **4,560 fish** (95% confidence interval 2,391–5,205). Markable-size fish (≥21 mm total length) were the majority; sub-markable fish were the rest.
- A total of 311 fish were fin-clipped and released. Total population was measured for length in a sample of **n = 360** fish.
- Fall 1978 estimate: about **8,000 fish**, a roughly 100% increase over fall 1977.
- Total population on October 22, 1975, before the crash, was **7,986 fish**. By fall 1978 the population was similar to the pre-crash level.
- Before the 1976 crash, markable-size fish made up only about 1/3 of the population. By 1977 the age structure shifted toward larger fish, with individuals of Age I and II present.
- Spawning in spring 1978 was successful, and males were seen along the pond shoreline in numbers similar to 1975.

### Refugia Establishment
- **[[Gachado Tank]]** (October 1975): drained, restocked with 50 adults and 110 subadults, and failed within five months because water delivery was irregular.
- **[[Finley Tank]]** (Research Ranch, Elgin, Arizona): a pond of about 200,000 gallons, roughly 40 × 20 m, average depth 2.3 m. It was treated with powdered rotenone in June 1978 to remove resident bass and sunfish. Stocking was delayed until November 1978, too late for spawning, so the population entered winter at stocking levels of about 300–600 fish. Water temperatures in winter were cooler than at Quitobaquito, but the site was chosen because the Research Ranch is committed to preservation.
- **[[Bates Well]]** (February 1978): stock tanks on the monument were stocked with 350 pupfish, plants, and insects from Quitobaquito. Fish were reproducing in spring 1978. The report warns this refugium is small, probably supporting fewer than 500 fish, and vulnerable to catastrophe.
- **State Historic Museum population** (July 1978): a museum population was established from the observation pool at the University of Arizona. The report notes that this population showed signs of inbreeding depression.
- Only about one-half or less of the Quitobaquito population is expected to survive to spawn in spring. Population estimates were not made in spring 1978 because of heavy visitation.

### Management Recommendations
- Establish additional refugia on the monument, possibly a larger pond at Bates Well with an area similar to Quitobaquito.
- Re-establish pupfish in the spring-head at Quitobaquito, where water flows out of the ground and surviving fish could repopulate the pool after a pesticide kill. Clearing reeds from the spring-head and lower drainage was recommended to restore natural spawning conditions.
- Continue annual fall population estimates and age-structure studies.
- Initiate pesticide monitoring through the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service contaminant laboratory in Columbia, Missouri.
- Check all refugia populations periodically, with the Finley Tank refugium checked first in 1979.
