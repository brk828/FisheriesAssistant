# Glen Canyon National Recreation Area Fish Management Plan April 1996

#ichthyology #fish-management #native-species #endangered-species #reservoir-ecology #Colorado-River #conservation

## Executive Summary

This Fish Management Plan (FMP) establishes a 5-year cooperative framework among the [[National Park Service]], [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]], and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] for managing fisheries within [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]]. The plan addresses the complex ecological challenges created by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Lake Powell]], which have drastically altered aquatic habitats and affected both upstream and downstream fish communities. The FMP aims to balance two primary objectives: protecting and restoring native fish populations (particularly four endangered Colorado River species) while maintaining quality recreational sport fishing opportunities. The plan covers multiple habitat types including flowing rivers, reservoir inflows, Lake Powell proper, the [[Lees Ferry]] tailwater, and perennial streams, each requiring distinct management approaches. Implementation occurs through annual workplan meetings, coordinated permitting, and data sharing among signatory agencies.

## Background & Description

[[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]] encompasses [[Lake Powell]], the second-largest reservoir in the United States by volume after [[Lake Mead]], with nearly 2,000 miles of shoreline extending 180 miles in length. The reservoir was created by closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963, impounding waters that span the Arizona-Utah border and drain a watershed of 111,700 square miles. The area includes flowing portions of the [[Colorado River]], [[Dirty Devil River]], [[Escalante River]], [[Paria River]], and [[San Juan River]].

Pre-impoundment, the Colorado River through Glen Canyon was characterized by extreme seasonal variation: massive spring floods (80,000-300,000 cfs) alternating with low base flows (3,000 cfs), warm summer temperatures near 80°F, and near-freezing winter conditions. This harsh environment supported specially adapted native fishes including [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*).

Post-dam conditions created a clear, deep, thermally stratified reservoir reaching 561 feet deep at full pool. Water releases from deep penstocks (elevation 3,470 feet) maintain constant 46°F temperatures in the tailwater. The dam eliminated spring floods, increased summer/winter flows, and created new habitat types: productive inflow zones where rivers meet the reservoir, a warm meromictic lake with oligotrophic characteristics, and a cold tailwater supporting [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reservoir exhibits a trophic gradient from nutrient-rich, turbid inflows to clear, nutrient-poor waters near the dam. [[Lake Powell]] is meromictic with high thermal and chemical stratification; below approximately 150 feet, the hypolimnion remains consistently stable. The thermocline changes seasonally, and hydraulic processes can deliver spring runoff plumes at mid-depth extending the entire lake length.

Nonnative species now dominate the fish community. [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) were introduced in 1968 and spread reservoir-wide by 1970, becoming the primary forage base. [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) stocked 1974-1979 exhibited unprecedented natural reproduction and became the most numerous sport fish. A classic predator-prey cycle developed: striped bass decimate shad populations, leading to bass starvation; reduced bass predation allows shad recovery; recovered shad support rapid bass growth and reproduction, repeating the cycle.

Other established nonnatives include [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), [[Walleye]] (*Sander vitreus*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), and various sunfishes. The [[Lees Ferry]] tailwater supports a "blue ribbon" rainbow trout fishery with over 100,000 Age II+ fish maintained through natural reproduction (target 50%) and supplemental stocking.

Native fish populations have declined severely. [[Bonytail]] are believed functionally extinct in the upper basin; none captured in Lake Powell since 1985. [[Colorado Squawfish]] have not been reported in the lake since 1977, though young-of-year are found in inflow areas. Only three [[Humpback Chub]] were captured in Lake Powell 1971-1991. [[Razorback Sucker]] numbers are at all-time lows; 1987-1988 surveys captured 12-14 individuals in the San Juan inflow, with 11 removed for broodstock. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] persist in low numbers without documented reproductive recruitment in the reservoir.

Biocontaminants pose concerns. Selenium concentrations in plankton from Wahweap Bay and Warm Creek ranged 5.3-14.6 μg/g dry weight; striped bass fillets contained 12.7-19.9 μg/g dry weight selenium. Food chain organisms with selenium ≥3 μg/g dry weight are potentially detrimental to consumers.

## Key Findings

**Management Structure and Authority:**
- The plan establishes formal coordination among three agencies with overlapping jurisdictions: NPS (federal land manager), UDWR (primary Lake Powell fisheries manager since 1970), and AGFD (lead on Lees Ferry tailwater)
- Annual workplan meetings each December review progress, approve upcoming activities, coordinate permitting, and ensure conformance with FMP objectives
- The plan addresses conflicts between state and federal laws/regulations affecting fishery resources
- Implementation requires permits from UDWR, AGFD, NPS, and USFWS as appropriate for threatened/endangered species work

**Habitat-Specific Management Goals:**

*Flowing Rivers:* Protect, restore and recover native fish populations, particularly four endangered species. Objectives include understanding habitat requirements, ensuring optimum instream flows, conserving genetic integrity, identifying recruitment limitations, establishing monitoring benchmarks, controlling nonnative predators, and promoting sport fish harvest.

*Inflows:* Protect and restore native fish populations in these artificially created, highly productive transition zones. Critical habitat designated on Colorado River to North Wash and San Juan River to Neskahi Canyon. Objectives address native fish survival, dam operation impacts, habitat quantification at varying lake levels, predation assessment, and evaluation of migration barriers. Young-of-year Colorado squawfish frequent the Colorado inflow but appear to suffer heavy predation; 101 captured April 1993 but only 3 in August post-runoff survey.

*Lake Powell:* Maintain a stable, balanced sport fishery. Target catch rate 0.5 fish/hour (currently 0.3); annual harvest ~300,000 fish from ~2 million angler hours. Specific size targets: largemouth bass 12" (8-15"), smallmouth bass 11" (7-14"), striped bass 18" (12-19"), walleye 14" (10-16"). Critical objective: stabilize striped bass-threadfin shad predator-prey cycle to prevent wild population fluctuations. Maintain static or rising lake levels April 1-June 15 for spawning. Optimize striped bass harvest (no creel limit) to reduce population pressure.

*Lees Ferry Tailwater:* Maintain "blue ribbon" trout fishery while protecting native flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and speckled dace. Target ≥100,000 Age II+ rainbow trout with 50% natural reproduction; Age III fish achieving 18" length with relative weight ≥0.80. Slot limit protects 16-22" fish. Maintain continuously inundated Cladophora and invertebrates at ≥5,000 cfs discharge. Protect flannelmouth sucker spawning at Paria River confluence.

*Perennial Streams:* Protect and preserve native species in streams above full pool elevation (3,700 feet), particularly isolated aquatic ecosystems. Conduct inventories and prevent nonnative introductions.

**Critical Issues Identified:**
1. Effects of dam operations on fisheries poorly understood; biological input not consistently incorporated into decision-making
2. Impacts from raising spillway gates 4.5 feet (proposed in Glen Canyon Dam EIS) not evaluated
3. Upstream dam operations (Flaming Gorge, Navajo) affect Glen Canyon as receiving system
4. Reasons for continued native fish declines need identification and mitigation
5. General lack of information on native species status, habitat needs, and nonnative competition/predation effects
6. NPS not formally involved in Recovery Implementation Programs despite critical habitat within NRA
7. Invading organisms, parasites and disease threaten aquatic systems
8. Need for coordinated federal involvement in promoting recreational fishing

**Stocking History:**
Extensive stocking 1963-1995 established sport fishery. Rainbow trout: 13+ million stocked 1963-1977 reservoir-wide. Largemouth bass: 2.9 million stocked 1963-1965. Striped bass: 682,000+ stocked 1974-1979 (Wahweap Creek, Bullfrog). Smallmouth bass: 236,000+ stocked 1980-1988 throughout reservoir. Threadfin shad: 1,500 adults plus 200,000 eggs/fry 1968-1969. Lees Ferry tailwater: rainbow trout stocking 1964-1992, ranging 13,000-171,000 annually, including kamloops strain.

**Education and Outreach:**
Programs include NPS interpretive evening programs, UDWR toll-free fishing hotline (1-800-ASK-FISH) with weekly updates, displays and brochures at visitor centers and launch ramps, video development for promotion and education, and partnerships with schools for curriculum development and cooperative education opportunities.

**Plan Implementation:**
Five-year plan reviewed annually, renewable by written agreement. Annual workplans developed with 30-day advance distribution of proposals. Approvals by UDWR Lake Powell Project Manager, AGFD Fisheries Branch Supervisor, and NPS Chief of Resource Management. Data sharing agreements among agencies for fishery, aquatic resources, visitor demographics, and use/protection data.
