# Is Gambusia holbrooki a Major Threat to Native Freshwater Fishes of South-Western Australia?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study, presented at the 5th Indo-Pacific Fish Conference (Nouméa, 1997) and published in 1999, asks whether the introduced [[Gambusia Holbrooki]] (mosquitofish) threatens native freshwater fishes in south-western Australia. The authors combined controlled tank experiments, field sampling of native fish fin damage, a comparison of a rehabilitated wetland with a natural one, and a survey of 163 [[Lentic Waterbodies]]. They found that the mosquitofish inflicts fin damage on native fishes, most strongly on juveniles, and that native species persisted alongside it mainly where physical cover was present. The authors conclude that the species is unlikely to cause extinctions but is a real threat to native populations in cover-free lentic systems, and they recommend habitat restoration and tighter controls on deliberate and accidental releases.

## Background & Description

- **Focal introduced species:** [[Gambusia Holbrooki]] (Poeciliidae). Described as the most widely distributed freshwater fish in Australia and a successful colonist of disturbed and rehabilitated lentic habitats. It was introduced as a biological control agent for mosquitoes.
- **Native focal species:** [[Edelia Vittata]] (western pygmy perch), a species of similar size and diet to the mosquitofish, widespread across south-western Australia.
- **Other native fishes discussed:** [[Galaxias Occidentalis]] (western galaxias), [[Bostockia Porosa]] (nightfish), [[Nannatherina Balstoni]] (Balston's pygmy perch), salamanderfish (*Lepidogalaxias salamandroides*), black-stripe minnow (*Galaxiella nigrostriata*), mud minnow (*Galaxiella munda*), spotted minnow (*Galaxias maculatus*), trout minnow (*Galaxias truttaceus*), and freshwater catfish (*Tandanus bostocki*). Of the 10 native species, eight are endemic. Apart from the catfish, none typically exceeds 140 mm total length.
- **Other introduced species:** [[Perca Fluviatilis]] (redfin perch), a large piscivore, is also established in the region. Approximately 20 introduced freshwater fish species (five families) are self-maintaining in Australia.
- **Prior knowledge:** Earlier authors suggested that introduced fishes cause declines through competition, predation, and disease. Evidence that mosquitofish harass similar-sized fishes was largely anecdotal. Only [[Pseudomugil Signifer]] (blue-eye) had been shown to suffer reduced growth and reproduction under mosquitofish presence, in controlled conditions.
- **Habitat change:** Less than 100 years of agriculture and urbanisation have removed many freshwater habitats. Deforestation, wetland drainage, and resulting changes in inflow, salinity, siltation and eutrophication continue to degrade habitats in the lower south-west.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat types:** Ephemeral pools (for example, on the [[Peat Flats]] along the south coast) and permanent lakes and pools. Some small species are restricted to ephemeral pools, while others occur in all waterbody types.
- **Role of cover:** Physical cover (submerged aquatic plants, sunken timber, boulders, and fallen branches) emerged as the key habitat variable. It shelters native fish from the mosquitofish and supports invertebrates that native fish eat.
- **Seasonality:** Native fishes breed from winter to late spring, when mosquitofish numbers are low. Small pools shrink during summer and autumn, when densities of mosquitofish are high and competition for food and space is likely.
- **Density contrast:** In south-western Australia, mosquitofish densities are often at least one order of magnitude greater than those of small native species.
- **Dispersal routes:** Human-mediated spread includes deliberate releases for mosquito control and accidental release by anglers using the fish as bait.

## Key Findings

### 1. Controlled tank experiment (agonistic effects)
- Fin damage to *Edelia vittata* was directly related to the density of *Gambusia holbrooki* in each tank. A linear regression indicated a highly significant relationship (p < 0.005).
- The caudal fin was the most frequently and most severely damaged. Fins were damaged in some individuals across all fin types, except the first dorsal fin.
- On average, 23% of *E. vittata* died in tanks that also held *G. holbrooki*, compared with 3% in tanks without them. Most deaths occurred within the first two weeks.
- About 80% of damaged fins showed parasitic and/or fungal infection, whereas undamaged fins showed little or none. The exact cause of death was not determined.

### 2. Wild populations: fin damage in native fishes
- Of 1,322 native fish examined for caudal fin damage, 219 (16.6%) showed no damage, 507 (38.4%) minor damage, 333 (25.2%) moderate damage, and 263 (19.9%) major damage.
- Fin damage was more frequent and more severe in 0+ (young-of-year) fish than in older fish. Older fish often showed scarred or deformed fins, suggesting past rather than recent damage.
- *Bostockia porosa* juveniles were especially susceptible; most 0+ individuals in the major-damage category had little caudal fin left.
- Within the sampled fish from the RGC system, fin damage was significantly and inversely related to total length (p < 0.05).
- Fin damage was common in native fish where mosquitofish were present and absent from waterbodies without them.

### 3. Rehabilitated versus natural wetlands
- Sampling used collapsible fish traps (25 × 25 × 40 cm), with at least four traps per habitat type, baited and left for one hour.
- *E. vittata* was captured at sites with cover, whether or not mosquitofish were present, and at sites without cover that held mosquitofish. It was never captured at sites lacking cover where mosquitofish were present.
- Relative abundance (fish per trap per hour) ranged from a mean minimum of 1.9 at Red Lake sites with only minimal cover (small boulders) to a mean maximum of 14 at dense macrophyte and fallen-branch sites.
- ANOVA showed a highly significant difference between habitat types (p < 0.001), attributed to higher abundances at complex-cover sites in Red Lake.

### 4. Habitat survey of 163 lentic waterbodies
- Native fish were caught in 97 of the 163 waterbodies.
- Of 82 permanent waterbodies, 55 (67%) contained native fishes. Of 81 ephemeral waterbodies, 42 (52%) did.
- Mosquitofish were captured only in permanent waterbodies (30 of 82, or 37%).
- Except for one pool, native fishes were found alongside mosquitofish only where cover was present. In the exception, only three mosquitofish were caught.
- A pool in the RGC system that had lacked cover, and that held many mosquitofish, later developed dense aquatic plant growth and was colonised by *E. vittata*.

### 5. Threat assessment and conclusions
- The authors conclude that mosquitofish are unlikely to drive native fish populations to extinction in lotic systems or in lentic systems with cover. They are likely to reduce population size in these habitats.
- The species is a potential threat to native populations in cover-free lentic systems. These include degraded, rehabilitated and artificial wetlands, as well as the natural pools of the southern peat flats.
- In the peat flats, the authors judge the threat low unless large numbers of mosquitofish are deliberately released. Most such pools are naturally cover-free for much of the year, so adding snags would alter their natural morphology.
- Management recommendations: add emergent and submerged plants, timber, snags and hay to degraded and artificial wetlands. Hay bales placed in newly established pools at the [[RGC Wetland Centre]] supported a diverse invertebrate community within three weeks.
- Discourage deliberate releases for [[Mosquito Control]] and accidental release by anglers. Inform local authorities and anglers of the effects of mosquitofish on the region's unique and depauperate freshwater fish fauna.
- The authors note that once established, mosquitofish are extremely difficult to eradicate.
