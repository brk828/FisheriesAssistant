# Cyprinid Fishes of the Genus Gila in the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic and ecological study re-examines the western chubs of the genus [[Gila]] (Cyprinidae) in the lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. The author analyzed roughly 1,800 specimens from the lower and middle Colorado basins and northern Mexico, combining morphometric, meristic, habitat and drainage-history data. The study concludes that the chubs of the region are not best explained by the "ecotype" concept. It recognizes three species, with [[Gila Robusta]] split into subspecies, and elevates [[Gila Intermedia]] to full species status. It also restores [[Gila Elegans]] as a valid species and ties the present distribution of these fishes to late Cenozoic drainage reorganization and to recent habitat loss.

## Background & Description

Western chubs of the genus Gila have a long and confused taxonomic history. Their names have been reshuffled across six generic names and more than a dozen specific synonyms, mainly because of poor understanding of geographic variation and frequent geographic isolation (allopatry). The study aimed to define the distribution of these forms, test the temporal and spatial stability of morphometric, meristic and habitat characters, and determine whether an ecotypic interpretation is applicable.

Morphological data included body length, six morphometric measurements (interorbital width, head width, head length, caudal peduncle depth and length, anal fin-base length), and the head length/caudal peduncle depth ratio. Counts of dorsal, anal and pelvic fin-rays and lateral-line scales were also recorded. Data were standardized to thousandths of standard length and tested with analysis of variance (p < 0.05).

Key diagnostic characters separate the three species:

- **[[Gila Elegans]] (bonytail):** slender, streamlined body with a thin, pencil-like caudal peduncle; head length/peduncle depth usually 5.0 or greater; 10 or more dorsal fin-rays; 38 to 50 vertebrae.
- **[[Gila Intermedia]] (Gila chub):** chubby, dark body with large, thick, broadly imbricate scales; fewer than 80 lateral-line scales; 7 to 8 dorsal fin-rays; head/peduncle ratio usually 3.0 or less; 38 to 45 vertebrae.
- **[[Gila Robusta]] (Colorado River chub):** thick but not chubby body with small, thin scales; lateral-line scales usually more than 80; 9 dorsal fin-rays; head/peduncle ratio usually 3.0 or more; 43 to 49 vertebrae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Present chub distribution reflects major changes to surface water over the last 100 years. [[Arroyo Cutting]] has lowered water tables and drained marshy habitats, while groundwater pumping and major and minor dams have dried streams. The author treats these changes as the background against which population patterns must be interpreted.

Habitat data show a broad spectrum for the chubs:

- **[[Gila Robusta]]** occupies nearly all habitat types, from the warm, strongly flowing [[Phoenix Canal]] system (about 300 m above sea level) and the turbulent [[Salt River]], to cold high-gradient White and Black rivers (about 2,500 m), to small pool-riffle tributaries. Its morphology stays fairly uniform across broad areas, with no clear habitat preference.
- **The [[Gila Robusta Grahami]] subspecies** has been taken in small, warm desert streams such as [[Aravaipa Creek]] (mean gradient 7.0 m/km, mostly riffles), in large, swift [[Fossil Creek]] (a drop of nearly 500 m over an 18 km descent to the [[Verde River]]), and in high mountain tributaries (2,500 to 3,000 m).
- **[[Gila Intermedia]]** is largely southern and lives mainly in lentic habitats such as marshes, brooks and impoundments. It occurs in the larger [[San Carlos River]], rock pools of Fish Creek Canyon, pool-riffles of [[Cave Creek]], and an artificial habitat, the Buckeye Canal. It prefers quiet water.

The author argues that these habitat patterns are inconsistent with an ecotypic model. The three forms are essentially allopatric, and their morphological stability persists across very different environments, which is unlikely if the animals were as plastic as ecotypic responses would require.

## Key Findings

**Species-level conclusions**

- [[Gila Elegans]] is restored to valid species status (previously synonymized with G. robusta by Ellis, 1914). It is now essentially restricted to the Colorado River above [[Lake Mohave]] and is extinct in the Gila River system. Recent Arizona Game and Fish Department collections indicate it persists in the upper lower basin but is rare.
- [[Gila Intermedia]] is elevated to full species and is endemic to the Gila River basin, most abundant in the southeastern portion. The name *Gila gibbosa* has priority, but is unavailable, so *Tiaroga intermedia* is applied under the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature.
- [[Gila Robusta]] is recognized as a single species with three subspecies or ecotypes: *G. r. robusta* (throughout the basin in larger streams, including the Verde mainstream and Salt River above [[Roosevelt Lake]]), *G. r. grahami* (restricted to Verde tributaries, the upper Gila River and Aravaipa Creek), and *G. r. jordani* (the Pluvial White River, Nevada). A further form, *G. r. semidnuda*, is noted from the Virgin River.

**Diagnostic statistics (Gila intermedia vs. G. robusta)**

- 97% of 390 *G. intermedia* specimens have fewer than 80 lateral-line scales (77% fewer than 75). In contrast, 80% of 670 *G. robusta* specimens have 80 or more, and 96% have more than 75.
- Strongly inscribed basal radii occur in 63% of about 70 *G. intermedia*, but in only 14.6% of 137 *G. robusta*, where they are weakly developed.
- Dorsal fin-rays are 7 or 8 in 94% of *G. intermedia*, and 9 or more in 83% of *G. robusta*.
- Head/peduncle depth ratio exceeds 3.0 in 87% of *G. robusta* and falls below 3.0 in 83% of *G. intermedia*.

**Subspecies differences (G. r. grahami vs. G. r. robusta)**

- *G. r. grahami* has 70 to 90 lateral scales in 98% of specimens, with 82% between 75 and 85. *G. r. robusta* has 80 or more in 97%.
- Dorsal fin-rays are 9 in 99% of *G. r. robusta*, while about 18% of *G. r. grahami* have 8.
- Anal fin-rays are 9 in 87% of *G. r. robusta*. *G. r. grahami* shows an approximately 50:50 ratio of 8 and 9.
- Blotching is common in *G. r. grahami* but rarely developed in *G. r. robusta*.

**Stability and variation**

- Morphometric and meristic characters remained stable over time at several localities, including East Verde River, Monkey Springs, Cave Creek, Wet Beaver Creek and Gila River headwaters (table 1). Sample sizes per year ranged from 4 to 50.
- Allometric growth was minimal and did not change overall trends, and no meristic size effects were evident.
- Morphological variation was very high within the Verde River system and in tributaries, but the Verde mainstream is morphometrically uniform. The Monkey Springs population is distinctive over many years.

**Drainage history and origins**

- The author proposes a four-stage sequence: (1) an early form of *G. robusta* entered the lower basin from the north during early drainage integration with the [[Colorado Plateau]]; (2) *G. intermedia* or its ancestor invaded from the south, inhabiting waters south and west of the [[Mogollon Highlands]]; (3) completed integration of the Gila basin allowed a second invasion of *G. robusta*; (4) ecological displacement and intergradation produced current distributions.
- Chubs referable to *Gila* are known from the mid-Miocene onward, and *G. robusta*-like species from the late Pliocene.
- Late Pliocene lacustrine and fluvial deposits exceeding 600 m, along with the [[Bouse Embayment]] and volcanic damming, isolated populations of chubs. Cutting of dams in the Pleistocene reintegrated the system and allowed downstream *G. robusta* to displace isolated upstream populations of *G. r. grahami*.
- Relatives of *G. intermedia* occur on the [[Mexican Plateau]], including *G. ditaenia*, *G. nigrescens* and *G. pandora*. This supports a southern origin for *G. intermedia*.

**Conservation and status**

- *G. intermedia* is known only from isolated habitats in central and southern Arizona. The Gila River system has lost its native chub fauna in several reaches, and some populations (e.g., [[Queen Creek]]) are likely extinct.
- The study's findings are based on material that supports the case for maintaining distinct taxa and protecting the remaining isolated populations from dams, pumping and stream alteration.
