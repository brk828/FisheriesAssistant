# Fish Collection from the Rivers of Central and Northern Mexico (1894)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1894 report by [[Albert J. Woolman]] documents a small collection of 24 freshwater fish species from headwater streams sampled between [[El Paso del Norte]], Texas, and [[Orizaba]], Veracruz, during a 1891 expedition connected with studies of [[Mount Orizaba]]. Six species and one genus (*Evarra*) were new to science. The fauna is dominated by minnows (Cyprinidae) and killifishes (Cyprinodontidae), reflecting the isolated, seasonally variable river systems of the Mexican plateau. Specimens were distributed to the U.S. National Museum, Stanford University, Indiana University and the British Museum.

## Background & Description

**Scope and methods.** In the summer of 1891 the author joined a party led by Dr. J. T. Scovell, with assistance from Mr. Ulysses O. Cox. Collections were made by seine and hook in mountain streams. Because all sampling took place in headwaters, every form collected is a strictly freshwater species.

**Taxonomic composition.** The 24 species belong to seven families:
- Cyprinidae: nearly 50% of the species from south of the Rio Grande
- Cyprinodontidae: about 30%
- Remaining ~20% split among five families, each with one species except Percidae (two *Etheostoma* species)

The Ictaluridae (listed as Siluridae), Catostomidae, Characidae and Atherinidae are each represented by a single species.

**Diagnostic traits noted.** The author highlights:
- Uniform pharyngeal dentition in Cyprinidae, usually 0, 4–4, 0
- Scales of Mexican species generally smaller than those of related northern species
- Greater variability and richness of color in Mexican forms

**Classified list (summary).** The checklist arranges species by order and family (Nematognathi, Eventognathi, Haplomi, Percesoces, Acanthopteri). Of the most important entries:
- Siluridae: *Ictalurus punctatus* (Rio Grande), *Ameiurus dugesi* (Rio Lerma), *Leptops olivaris* (Rio Grande)
- Catostomidae: *Moxostoma congestum* (Rio Grande), *M. austrinum* (Rio Lerma)
- Cyprinidae: 15 species, including *Notropis orca*, *N. aztecus*, *N. chihuahua*, *Couesius adustus*, *Evarra eigenmanni*
- Cyprinodontidae: *Gambusia nobilis*, *G. infans*, *Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus*, *Cyprinodon eximius*, *C. elegans*, *Characodon variatus*
- Atherinidae: *Chirostoma jordani*
- Percidae: *Etheostoma micropterus*, *E. australe*

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rio Grande at El Paso del Norte.** Above the waterworks, the stream has shallow rippled rocky reaches that make seining difficult. Few species were taken. The channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*) was very abundant, averaging about 10 inches. Flathead catfish were uncommon, and the sucker *Moxostoma congestum* was abundant.

**Rio de los Conchos at Chihuahua.** The river bed is more than half a mile wide but carries very little water because of minimal rainfall. Water is supplied by an aqueduct from mountains about 10 miles away and is dammed about 2 miles below the city for irrigation. The pond above the dam holds muddy, vegetated, quiet water filled with small minnows and cyprinodonts, and a shallow ripple with darters. *Campostoma ornatum* was among the most abundant species here.

**Rio Lerma at Salamanca, Guanajuato.** The Lerma is a major tributary of the Pacific. It flows west about 80–90 miles to [[Lake Chapala]], then northwest to the sea as the Rio Grande de Santiago. At Salamanca the river is about 75 feet wide and 3–8 feet deep, with a swift, muddy current during the sampling season and gravel-and-stone shallows. It drains small plateau lakes and sits about 6,000 feet above sea level. *Ameiurus dugesi* was abundant, and *Algansea dugesi* was the most common food fish, locally called "whitefish" and reaching about 15 inches.

**Canals of the City of Mexico.** Heavy rains had flooded the lakes and canals. Dense algae and aquatic plants made seining laborious. Only three species were collected, and fresh fish in local markets came mostly from the coast. Notable here is *Notropis aztecus*, described as new from the canal system.

**Rio Blanco at Orizaba.** This headwater stream rises from large springs north of Orizaba and falls more than 4,000 feet over about 120 km of rapids and waterfalls before reaching the Gulf of Mexico. The author argues that the fish must have inhabited these waters for a long time, since they could not have ascended from the lowlands. Only one species, *Pseudoxiphophorus bimaculatus*, was taken, and it was very abundant in mill races and quiet nooks.

## Key Findings

- **Six new species and one new genus were described:**
  - *Notropis orca* (Rio Grande basin)
  - *Couesius adustus* (Rio Conchos)
  - *Notropis aztecus* (City of Mexico canals)
  - *Evarra eigenmanni* (gen. and sp. nov., City of Mexico canals; allied to *Tiaroga*, *Phenacobius* and *Agosia*)
  - *Gambusia infans* (Rio Lerma area)
  - *Chirostoma jordani* (City of Mexico and Salamanca)
- **Species identity questions:** The author suggests *Algansea tincella* may be a synonym of *A. dugesi*, and that *Hybognathus melanops* may match *Dionda melanops* of Girard. Gambusia from the Rio Conchos is distinguished from *G. affinis* by higher scale counts (36–38 vs. 30–33) and an unbarred caudal fin.
- ***Pseudoxiphophorus* validity:** The two forms described by Heckel (*P. bimaculatus* and *P. reticulatus*) intergrade in the Orizaba material. The author considers them probably one variable species, and questions whether the genus can be separated from *Gambusia* on dorsal-fin length alone.
- ***Characodon*:** The author's specimens agree closely with *C. ferrugineus* Bean, but he doubts Bean's later synonymy of that species with *C. variatus*, citing differences in age and sex that he could not confirm.
- **Regional patterns:** The Mexican fauna is characterized by isolated basins, high seasonal variability in discharge, uniform cyprinid dentition, small scales and bright coloration. Duplicate specimens were distributed to several museums on two continents.
