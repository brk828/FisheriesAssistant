# Instream Flow Incremental Methodology Analysis of the Gila River, Upper Gila Water Supply Study, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], applies the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFG4a/PHABSIM family) to the upper Gila River in New Mexico. It evaluates the aquatic impacts of proposed Direct Pumping/Offstream Storage alternatives for the [[Upper Gila Water Supply Study]], which was an adjunct to the [[Central Arizona Project]]. The report sets monthly minimum instream flow recommendations to protect the native fishery, focusing on the Federally listed [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] as indicator species.

## Background & Description

The Upper Gila Water Supply Study was authorized under the Colorado River Basin Project Act (Public Law 90-537, 1968). It allowed New Mexico a consumptive use not exceeding an annual average of 18,000 acre-feet over any ten consecutive years, including reservoir evaporation, beyond existing water rights. The Bureau's primary aim was to capture and store upper Gila River water for municipal and industrial uses in Grant County. Withdrawals could alter over 200 miles of free-flowing river in New Mexico and Arizona above [[San Carlos Reservoir]]. Project life was expected to be 50 years.

Early mainstem dam and groundwater options were dropped, and the scope narrowed to Direct Pumping/Offstream Storage alternatives. The study area runs from the southern boundary of the [[Gila Wilderness]] to Redrock, New Mexico. The river is divided into five river segments, each with one representative reach:

- **I. Hooker Reservoir** (below Turkey Creek): analysis not completed after the alternative was dropped.
- **II. Nature Conservancy** (below Mogollon Creek confluence): 274-ft sample reach, slope 0.007.
- **III. Forest Service Bird Management Area** (lower-to-middle Cliff-Gila Valley): 1,176-ft reach, slope 0.016.
- **IV. Middle Box** (south of Telegraph Mountain): 685-ft reach, slope 0.025.
- **V. Redrock** (about 3/4 mile upstream of NM Highway 464 bridge): 1,406-ft reach, slope 0.016.

The Gila River in New Mexico supports one of the largest native fish assemblages in the lower Colorado River basin, attributed to its relatively unmodified condition and uncontrolled flows. Spikedace and loach minnow are Federally listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]], with proposed critical habitat. Roundtail chub is protected by New Mexico state law. Introduced predators and competitors, including [[Red Shiner]] and [[Channel Catfish]], threaten native species, and the fishery below the Middle Box near Redrock is dominated by introduced fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Flows follow a short high-volume runoff, with peaks during spring snowmelt and late-summer rains, followed by long low-flow periods. Table 1 gives monthly statistics from 1928–85. At the Gila gage (No. 09430500), median monthly flows ranged from 47 to 207 cfs. At the Redrock gage (No. 09431500), medians ranged from 37 to 293 cfs, and maximum flows reached 2,200 cfs.

**Land use.** Human activities include dispersed recreation, fire suppression, cattle grazing, and limited irrigation and mining diversion. Watershed use was expected to remain stable.

**Method rationale.** The Methodology assumes carrying capacity is limited by events such as flood, drought, and land use that constrain habitat in critical periods. Sensitive indicator species are used as surrogates, on the premise that protecting them protects the wider fish community. Habitat is expressed as [[Weighted Usable Area]] (WUA, in square feet per 1,000 feet of stream) for each life stage.

**Flood ecology.** Floods near two orders of magnitude above mean annual flow shift fish faunas toward native dominance. Floods below one order of magnitude had no detected effect. Native fishes are considered adapted to periodic floods, which renew habitat and suppress introduced species.

## Key Findings

**Habitat values by reach (Table 2, WUA peaks).**
- The Bird Management Area reach (III) provides the greatest habitat value for all four native fishes.
- The Middle Box reach (IV) provides the least.
- The Nature Conservancy reach (II) provides moderate habitat.
- The Redrock reach (V) frequently shows high habitat values, but native populations there are constrained by non-native predators and competitors and by dewatering for irrigation.

**Field and modeling effort.**
- Microhabitat data for four native species (spikedace, loach minnow, Sonora sucker, desert sucker) were collected from June 1983 to June 1984 using electrofishing, seining, and rock-underside sampling.
- Fifty-six habitat preference curves were developed from over 2,800 data points. Fish sample sizes ranged from 40 to 571 per life stage (Appendix B, Table 2).
- Some spikedace and loach minnow curves rested on fewer points than targeted. The authors judged them reliable because of narrow habitat requirements. Sucker curves were considered less transferable.

**Redrock spikedace analysis (Tables 3–4).**
- Adult spikedace habitat peaks at flows well below the 20-year median flows.
- Spawning spikedace habitat peaks at flows well above the median.
- Spawning is considered the most critical requisite from March through May. Adult habitat matters most for the rest of the year.
- The authors concluded that reproductive capacity is critical to the long-term survival of the Redrock population, which depends partly on upstream larval drift.

**Recommended minimum monthly instantaneous flows (cfs).**
- *Below the infiltration gallery at the mouth of Mangas Creek (Table 5):* 150 in Jan, Feb, Aug–Dec; 250 in Mar–May; 45 in Jun; 70 in Jul.
- *Below Ira Canyon, head of the Middle Box (Table 6):* 60 in Jan–Feb; 212 in Mar–May; 40 in Jun; 70 in Jul–Dec. This applies if the galleries are relocated downstream.

**Flood and flushing flows.**
- Periodic floods near 14,000 cfs are needed for long-term maintenance of native fishes in the Cliff-Gila Valley.
- Flushing flows of about 5,000–6,000 cfs every 2–3 years are needed to maintain spawning substrate quality. These are equivalent to the 2-year flood peak, following Estes (1985) and Osborn (1982).
- The one-in-two-year floods could be significantly affected by direct pumping.

**Introduced species.** The [[Red Shiner]] was absent from the New Mexico Gila before 1978, reached Redrock by 1980, and has remained uncommon in the lower Cliff-Gila Valley since 1981. The authors expected its population to stay small if habitat and flow remain unchanged.

**Conservation conclusions.**
- Native fish persistence depends on preserving the few remaining unregulated reaches.
- Regulated systems should be operated to simulate natural flood and temperature regimes.
- Sonora and desert suckers, which have broad microhabitat requirements, are expected to be protected by flows that maintain spikedace and loach minnow.
