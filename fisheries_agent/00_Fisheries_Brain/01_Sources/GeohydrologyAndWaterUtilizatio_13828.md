# Geohydrology and Water Utilization in the Willcox Basin, Graham and Cochise Counties, Arizona

#hydrogeology #groundwater #water-resources #arizona

## Executive Summary

This USGS Water-Supply Paper (1859-F), prepared with the [[Arizona State Land Department]], assesses the [[Groundwater]] system of the [[Willcox Basin]], a closed interior-drainage basin of about 1,500 square miles in northern [[Sulphur Springs Valley]]. Its valley floor covers about 950 square miles. The study finds that [[Irrigation Pumping]] since the 1910s, and especially after 1953, has pulled groundwater storage well below natural [[Groundwater Recharge]]. In 1963 pumpage reached about 180,000 acre-feet per year against estimated natural recharge of 54,000 to 75,000 acre-feet per year. Water levels have fallen steadily, and the main cones of depression are expanding.

The document does not address fish, invertebrates, plankton, or aquatic habitat. Its subject is groundwater hydrology and water-resource management.

## Background & Description

The report was written by S. G. Brown and H. H. Schumann. Its field data were collected mainly from 1945 to 1960, and the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] has measured water levels since 1946. The study was launched in July 1959 to determine the occurrence, movement, availability, and chemical quality of groundwater, with emphasis on development after 1953. Earlier work included [[Meinzer and Kelton (1913)]] and [[Jones and Cushman (1947)]]. Companion chemical data appear in Hydrologic Investigations Atlas HA-214 ([[Kister et al. (1966)]]).

**Physiography and climate.** The basin lies between the Pinaleño, Dos Cabezas, and Chiricahua Mountains on the east and the Galiuro, Winchester, Little Dragoon, and Dragoon Mountains on the west. Its central feature is the [[Willcox Playa]], a barren, wind-deflated flat bounded by beach ridges from ancient [[Lake Cochise]]. Summers are hot, with intense convective thunderstorms from July to September, and winters are cool with light frontal rains and snow. Average annual pan evaporation at Willcox is reported as 84.59 inches (OCR-garbled in the source), more than seven times average annual precipitation.

**Well-numbering system.** Wells follow the [[Bureau of Land Management Well-Numbering System]], in which the first digits give township, range, and section, and the letters give quarter-section tracts. For example, (D-4-5)19caa is in the NE¼NE¼SW¼ of section 19, T. 4 S., R. 5 E.

**Geology.** The basin formed in middle and late Tertiary time through large-scale normal faulting and mountain uplift. Its rock units fall into two groups:

- **Rocks of the mountain blocks** (Precambrian through Tertiary): granite, rhyolite, andesite, gneiss, schist, limestone, shale, sandstone, and quartzite. These yield only small amounts of water, mainly from fractured or weathered zones.
- **Rocks of the basin** (Tertiary and Quaternary alluvium):
  - [[Moderately Consolidated Alluvium]] (tilted up to 29° and carbonate-cemented, with rhyolitic and andesitic clasts) and [[Poorly Consolidated Alluvium]]. Both yield little water, and the two are hard to separate in drillers' logs.
  - [[Unconsolidated Alluvium]], which holds the principal aquifer. It has two facies: [[Stream Deposits]] (lenticular sand and gravel, the main aquifer) and [[Lake-Bed Deposits]] (black clay, which acts as a confining layer and creates local artesian conditions near the playa).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The basin is an interior-drainage system. Water reaching the playa leaves only by evaporation and transpiration. Groundwater-dependent vegetation is the main ecological link:

- [[Mesquite]] (*Prosopis*) was mapped over about 90,000 acres in 1911. Dense growth on about 22,000 acres where depth to water was under 25 feet used about 22,000 acre-feet per year. Mesquite becomes a poorly developed xerophyte where water is deeper than 25 feet.
- [[Saltgrass]] transpired an estimated 20,000 acre-feet per year near the playa, according to a U.S. Department of Agriculture estimate (1941).
- Land clearing removed some phreatophytes, and lowered water tables reduced the remaining evapotranspiration.

Surface water is scarce. Streamflow comes from summer thunderstorms, winter rains, and snowmelt. Most runoff from the mountain fronts disappears through seepage and evaporation before reaching the playa. Only two gaging stations exist: Grant Creek near Fort Grant (12.5 square miles, August–September 1920 only) and West Turkey Creek near Light (about 19 square miles, August 1919 to September 1925). West Turkey Creek averaged 5,500 acre-feet per year, about 290 acre-feet per square mile per year.

Recharge comes almost entirely from [[Stream Infiltration]] along the mountain fronts. Direct precipitation on the valley floor contributes little or nothing, and irrigation return flow is recycling, not true recharge.

## Key Findings

**1. Predevelopment balance.** Before large-scale pumping, the aquifer was roughly in equilibrium. Water moved from mountain-front recharge zones toward the playa, where it was lost to evapotranspiration:

| Discharge component (1910–11) | Acre-feet per year |
|---|---|
| Mesquite transpiration | 22,000 |
| Saltgrass transpiration | 20,000 |
| Evaporation from shallow groundwater | 33,000 |
| **Total** | **75,000** |

Calculated underflow to the playa area (Stewart 11,000; Kansas Settlement 25,000; Pearce-Cochise 18,000) totals 54,000 acre-feet per year. The authors conclude that natural recharge lay between 54,000 and 75,000 acre-feet per year. The abstract gives the underflow figure as 34,000 acre-feet per year, which conflicts with the body text.

**2. Aquifer properties.**
- Stewart area: median specific capacity was 20 gpm per foot of drawdown per 100 feet of saturated material. The upper and lower quartile range was 39.5 gpm per foot.
- Kansas Settlement area: median specific capacity was 7.4 gpm per foot per 100 feet. This aquifer is less permeable but more homogeneous. Its upper-to-lower quartile range was only 7.5 gpm per foot.
- Transmissibility estimates: Stewart 160,000 gpd/ft; North Kansas Settlement 140,000; South Kansas Settlement 42,000; Pearce-Cochise (listed as 55,000 in the OCR, which is uncertain). The abstract gives an overall range of 58,000 to 160,000 gpd/ft.
- An aquifer test on industrial well (D-15-26) 23dcc, which taps only consolidated alluvium, gave transmissibility of about 6,000 gpd/ft.

**3. Pumping history.**
- About 4,000 acre-feet were pumped in 1928, when roughly 25 plants irrigated about 2,000 acres in the Stewart area.
- About 10,000 acre-feet were pumped in 1945.
- About 180,000 acre-feet were pumped in 1963.
- Cumulative irrigation pumpage through 1963 was about 1,860,000 acre-feet: Kansas Settlement 990,000, Stewart 680,000 (690,000 in the abstract), and Pearce-Cochise 190,000.

**4. Water-level declines.** Average declines from spring 1958 to spring 1964 (table in source):

| Area | Pumpage (acre-feet) | Average (ft) | Max (ft) | Min (ft) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stewart | 531,000 | −29 | −64 | −11 |
| Pearce-Cochise | — | −19 | −30 | +5 |
| Kansas Settlement | — | −74 | −127 | +14 |

Maximum annual declines of 1962–63 were more than 10 feet per year in the Kansas Settlement area and 7 feet per year in Stewart. The abstract cites 19 feet per year for Kansas Settlement, a discrepancy with the conclusions.

**5. Overdraft.** In 1963 pumpage exceeded natural recharge by about 105,000 to 126,000 acre-feet per year. Much of the water pumped is mined from storage. Cones of depression around Willcox and Stewart, and around the Kansas Settlement area, have coalesced. Flow now runs toward pumping centers, and in some places it has reversed.

**6. Water-level behavior.**
- Cyclic pumping produces seasonal drawdown. Levels are lowest at the end of the irrigation season and recover partly during winter.
- Fluctuations in the Stewart recorder well (D-13-24) 16bbb grew from about 2 feet in 1948 to nearly 30 feet in 1960, as annual pumpage rose from about 14,000 acre-feet.
- Some Kansas Settlement wells rose 14 feet since 1952, probably from upward circulation in wells perforated along their full length or from infiltration of tailwater.

**7. Precipitation context.** Precipitation was below average in 1899–1904, 1909–13, 1921–25, and 1941–63. Above-average periods occurred in 1904–09, 1913–21, and 1925–41. The authors argue the water-level changes reflect pumping rather than rainfall-driven recharge.

**8. Management implication.** Additional pumping anywhere in the basin will accelerate water-level declines in that area and in neighboring areas. Development in lightly used areas such as Sierra Bonita Ranch and the North Playa area will increase declines there, because drawdown from the Stewart cone already extends into both.
