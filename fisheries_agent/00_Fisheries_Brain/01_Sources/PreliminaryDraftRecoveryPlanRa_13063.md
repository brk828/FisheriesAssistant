# Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) Draft Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft recovery plan, prepared for [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] Region 6 by Harold M. Tyus of the [[Center For Limnology]] (University of Colorado at Boulder) and dated January 30, 1996, addresses the [[Razorback Sucker]], an endemic, monotypic catostomid of mainstream [[Colorado River Basin]] rivers. The species was listed as endangered on October 23, 1991 (56 FR 54957), and critical habitat was designated on March 21, 1994 (59 FR 13374). Remnant populations persist in [[Lake Mohave]] (largest extant population), and in the [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]] near Vernal, Utah (the two largest riverine populations).

The plan frames recovery within an [[Aquatic Ecosystem Recovery Plan]] for the four listed big-river fishes of the Colorado River, and is written as a strategic rather than tactical document. Principal threats are nonnative fish predation and competition, flow alteration by dams and diversions, habitat fragmentation, and loss of floodplain and backwater habitats, with recruitment failure identified as the central demographic problem.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and history:** The razorback sucker belongs to the family Catostomidae (subfamily Catostominae) and is the only species in the genus *Xyrauchen* (placed in tribe Catostomini, one of four genera in that tribe per Nelson 1994). It was first described in 1860 by Abbott as *Catostomus texanus*, later treated as *C. cypho* by Lockington (1880), and placed in *Xyrauchen* by Eigenmann and Kirsch (Kirsch 1889). Native names include "tsa'xnap" and "suxyex." Fossils assigned to *Xyrauchen* come from Pliocene deposits, with a Pleistocene specimen from near the Salton Sea, California.

**Morphology:** Adults may reach about 1 m total length and 5–6 kg, though most are smaller. Females are larger than males and average 470–740 mm in Lake Mohave (Minckley 1983). The species is distinguished by a pronounced bony keel behind the occiput formed from enlarged interneural bones. Meristics reported: dorsal fin rays usually 14–15 (range 12–17), anal fin rays 7, total vertebrae 45–47, lateral line scales 68–87, and gill rakers 44–50 on the first arch. Breeding males show dark dorsal coloration and bright yellow to orange lateral and ventral surfaces, with tubercles more pronounced than in females.

**Diet:** Suckers are mainly detritivores and herbivores. Riverine adults consume benthos (Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Chironomidae), algae, and detritus; reservoir adults are chiefly planktivorous (Cladocera), with occasional benthic feeding.

**Longevity and growth:** McCarthy and Minckley (1987) estimated ages of 24–44 years using otoliths. Growth is rapid for about the first six years and slow to nonexistent in older fish. Tyus (1988) recorded a mean adult growth of 2.2 mm/year for 39 recaptured fish.

**Related taxa mentioned:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) are the other listed Big River fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic range:** The species was once widely distributed and abundant throughout mainstream rivers of the Colorado River basin, including Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming, and the Mexican states of Baja California Norte and Sonora. In the lower basin it occurred from the delta upstream to Lee's Ferry, Arizona.

**Present status:** Scattered individuals persist, but the only extant population is in [[Lake Mohave]]. Minckley et al. (1991) estimated about 60,000 adults there; Marsh (1995) reported a roughly 60% decline to about 25,000 fish in 1995. In the upper basin, fewer than 1,000 adults were estimated to inhabit the upper Green River basin (Lanigan and Tyus 1989). Larval fish have been reported in the upper basin in the last 10 years, but no significant recruitment to any population has been documented.

**Life history and habitat:**
- *Reproduction:* In the lower basin, spawning occurs January through April in reservoirs at water temperatures of 11.5–18°C, over gravel, cobble, or mixed substrates; in Lake Mohave fish were observed spawning at depths up to 5 m, mostly in less than 2 m. In the Green River, spawning occurs mid-April through May (Tyus 1987; Tyus and Karp 1990), with 40 of 42 running ripe adults captured between May 24 and June 17 in the Grand Valley (Osmundson and Kaeding 1990), associated with water temperatures of about 14°C (range 9–17°C).
- *Adult habitat:* Radiotelemetry in the Green and Duchesne rivers showed fish occupying depths of 0.6–3.4 m over sand or silt at velocities of 0.3–0.4 m/s. Winter use involved slow runs, slack water, and eddies at 0.6–1.4 m depth.
- *Movements:* Lake Mohave fish traveled 4–26 km; spawning migrations of 30.4–106.0 km (one way) were recorded in the Green River near Jensen, Utah, and in the lower Yampa River.
- *Temperature preference:* Adults preferred 22–25°C, avoided temperatures below 14.7°C and above 27.4°C (Bulkley and Pimentel 1983).
- *Larvae:* Larvae remain near shore after hatching in Lake Mohave and disappear within weeks; laboratory work (Paulin et al. 1989) indicates 2-week-old larvae actively enter the drift at night.
- *Juveniles:* Presumed to require quiet, warm, shallow backwaters, eddies, and flooded bottomlands; juveniles have been very rarely collected since the early 1950s in Lake Mohave.

**Genetics:** Lake Mohave stocks had greater mtDNA diversity than upper basin stocks (14 vs. 4 haplotypes in the Green River and 1 in the Colorado River; Dowling and Minckley 1993). Allozyme studies of Lake Mohave brood fish at Dexter National Fish Hatchery showed introgression rates no higher than other catostomids (Buth 1987).

**Reasons for decline:**
- *Nonnative fishes:* More than 50 nonnative species have been introduced into the basin since the late 1800s. Green sunfish, common carp, flathead and channel catfish, and others prey on eggs and larvae. Karp and Tyus (1990) reported consumption of 90% of larvae by green sunfish in 4-minute trials (red shiner 50%, redside shiner 10%). In Lake Mohave, larvae in a predator-free environment grew to 30 mm while those exposed to predation were truncated at 10–12 mm (Brooks 1985; Langhorst 1987; Marsh and Brooks 1989).
- *Flow alteration:* Peak discharges were reduced about 50% since 1942, while base flows increased 21% (Fradkin 1984). Over 20 major dams were built on the mainstem, from Hoover Dam (1930s) to Glen Canyon Dam (closed 1963). Floodplain inundation was almost eliminated.
- *Barriers:* Burdick and Kaeding (1990) identified seven barriers above Glen Canyon Dam; the lower basin includes more than 15 mainstream dams.
- *Water quality:* Cooler tailwater releases and reduced sediment load. Selenium concentrations of 25–45 µg/g dry weight were found in two razorback tissue samples at Stewart Lake Waterfowl Management Area (Stephens et al. 1988). Young razorbacks exposed to some agricultural drainage water to the Green River showed 30–50% mortality (Waddell, pers. comm., 1993).
- *Habitat loss:* Tamarisk invasion reduced channel width by an average 25% in Canyonlands National Park (Graf 1978). Channel degradation disconnected wetlands from the river (Lyons 1989).
- *Hybridization:* Not considered a major threat at present; many old reports may reflect confusion with females, which have a reduced keel.
- *Disease:* No evidence that disease or parasites are a major factor in endangerment (Flagg 1980).

**Critical habitat:** Fifteen river reaches (RZ1–RZ15) covering about 49% of historic habitat (1724 mi) were designated in the Colorado River basin, spanning the Green, Yampa, Duchesne, Colorado, White, Gunnison, San Juan, Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers. Primary constituent elements are water (quantity and quality), physical habitat (spawning, nursery, feeding, and corridors within the 100-year floodplain), and the biological environment (food supply and interspecific interactions).

**Conservation context:** The state of Arizona and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service launched a reintroduction program in the lower basin in 1981; more than 12 million larval and juvenile fish were stocked from 1981 to 1990, with little success and no new populations established. The [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]] (1987) and San Juan River program provide the implementation framework, and lower basin coordination is under development.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objective:** Protect and expand three existing populations (Lake Mohave, Green River, Yampa River) and establish four new ones from remnant stocks or reintroductions.

**Recovery goals (Part II):**
- *Short term:* Prevent extinction. Considered attained when the decline of extant stocks in Lake Mohave and the lower Yampa and Green rivers has been reversed.
- *Downlisting to threatened:* Requires (1) recovery of the Lake Mohave, Green, and Yampa populations, reaching equilibrium at 2,000 or more adult fish each over a sustained period, and (2) recovery or establishment of two additional populations, one in the upper and one in the lower recovery unit.
- *Delisting:* Possible after downlisting, once two additional populations are recovered or established and protected (a total of four new or recovered populations beyond the three extant ones).

**Recovery units:** A Lower Basin Razorback Sucker Recovery Unit (Lake Mohave, a Class III preserve under Moyle and Sato 1991) and an Upper Basin Recovery Unit (Green and lower Yampa rivers, a Class II preserve).

**Priority actions (Actions Needed):**
1. Reverse decline and stabilize existing Lake Mohave, Green River, and Yampa populations.
2. Protect habitats from further degradation.
3. Restore habitats to make them compatible with recovery goals.
4. Maintain existing genomes in hatchery refugia (Dexter National Fish Hatchery, Ouray National Wildlife Refuge) and increase diversity as necessary.
5. Augment or reestablish populations in critical habitat.

**Control of nonnative fishes:** Cease stocking nonnative fishes in critical or upstream habitats, and control nonnatives in priority recovery areas.

**Candidate reestablishment sites (upper basin):** Gunnison River near Delta, Colorado; Grand Valley of the Colorado River; and the Yampa/Green confluence. Recovery areas should be about 50 river miles long.

**Recovery timeline and costs:**
- Populations stabilized and immediate extinction threat avoided by the year 2000.
- Downlisting possibly by 2010.
- Delisting possibly as early as 2020, if criteria are met.
