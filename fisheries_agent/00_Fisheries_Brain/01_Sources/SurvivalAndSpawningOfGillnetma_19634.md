# Survival and Spawning of Gill-Net-Marked Red Salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Research Report (No. 40), by Philip R. Nelson and Carl E. Abegglen, measures whether [[Gill Net]] marks reduce the survival and spawning success of [[Red Salmon]]. In 1953, 6,253 salmon were tagged at a trap and gill-net site in [[Karluk Lagoon]], [[Kodiak Island]], [[Alaska]], with roughly half gill-net marked and half unmarked controls. Most excess mortality of net-marked fish occurred shortly after release, between the lagoon and the [[Karluk Weir]]. The authors estimate that 10 to 20 percent of gill-net-marked fish escaping commercial nets die from webbing injuries. Travel time, spawning condition and the link between mark severity and survival showed no significant effect, although handling during tagging appeared to harm females more than males in one group.

## Background & Description

Salmon in Alaska are caught commercially by traps, purse seines, gill nets and lines, with gill nets the most common source of gear marks. In [[Bristol Bay]], gill nets are the only gear used for red salmon. Gill-net-marked fish made up 6.3 percent of the annual escapement through the [[Brooks River]] weir over the previous nine years, under 2 percent at the Karluk River, and about 5 percent on the [[Columbia River]] according to Hansen and others (1950). Earlier studies (Schaefer 1951; Hansen and others 1950) found no evidence that gill-net injury reduced recovery of tagged fish, but they did not measure mortality from the point of netting to the spawning grounds. The gill-net fishery took an average of 24 percent (about 15 million fish) of the Alaska salmon catch from 1943 to 1952, which motivated the experiment.

The trap was set about 100 feet from shore in a channel with flows of 1 to 2 feet per second. Fish were led into compartment 1, where some were tagged as controls and others were netted in a suspended 5⅜-inch stretched-mesh gill net. Fish that passed through the net or backed out were also captured and tagged. Petersen disk tags were fixed beneath the dorsal fin, using colour combinations that identified each group (A: removed from net; B: passed through net; C: backed out of net) and their control counterparts. The tagging ran from June 16 to August 15, 1953.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study took place in a small, clear, shallow watershed, which made it possible to count tagged fish at the [[Karluk Weir]], about 23 miles upstream, and to recover dead fish on small streams. Algae and debris on the net webbing made it impossible later in the season to keep the leads on the bottom. The authors also noted that commercial nets are usually sited in salt water and fished under tidal conditions, and that commercial nets are longer and deeper, so the experiment may not perfectly reflect field conditions.

## Key Findings

- **Tagging totals:** 6,253 salmon were tagged. Net-marked fish numbered 198 (June 12 to July 7) plus 2,916 (July 8 to August 15), and controls 196 plus 2,943.
- **Lagoon mortality:** 110 tagged dead fish were recovered in the lagoon, 79 (72%) net-marked and 31 (28%) controls, all unspawned.
- **Differential mortality by group:** Between the lagoon and the weir, excess mortality of net-marked fish was 19.5% for Group A, 13.1% for Group B and 0% for Group C. Between the weir and the spawning grounds, no significant differential mortality was detected in any group.
- **Weir passage (Group A):** 63.8% of controls and 44.3% of net-marked fish passed through the weir.
- **Travel time:** Mean time from tagging to recovery was 43.8 days for controls and 42.2 days for net-marked fish; migration curves were closely superimposed.
- **Sex effects:** The sex ratio changed significantly only among net-marked Group A fish, where females survived less well than males. Tagging and net marks had no significant sex-specific effect in Groups B and C.
- **Spawning condition:** No significant difference between net-marked and control fish in the spawning condition of either sex. Overall spawned percentages were 85.7% of control females and 90.6% of net-marked females (with unknown males excluded from the second value; see report tables).
- **Healing:** Gill-net marks generally healed. In Group B, 68.5% of net-marked fish reexamined on the spawning grounds had marks that had disappeared or were barely distinguishable. No relationship was found between mark severity (first, second or third degree) and survival.
- **Estimated field mortality:** 10 to 20 percent of gill-net-marked fish escaping commercial nets are estimated to die from webbing injuries. In a hypothetical escapement of 100,000 salmon with 6.3% net-marked, this equals 700 to 1,575 fish, or 0.7 to 1.6% of the seasonal escapement.
- **Conclusion:** Gill-net-marked fish that survive are not delayed in migration, spawning is not impaired, and losses are probably of minor importance at the seasonal scale, though local segments of the run may suffer more during peak fishing (up to 20% net-marked in some weeks at Karluk and Brooks weirs, and up to 45% in a weekly period at [[Hells Gate]]).
