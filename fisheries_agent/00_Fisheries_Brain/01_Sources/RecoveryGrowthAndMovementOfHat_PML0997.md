# Recovery, Growth, and Movement of Hatchery-Reared Landlocked Atlantic Salmon at Long Pond, Maine

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Game study followed three annual stockings of fin-clipped, hatchery-reared [[Landlocked Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*) made in [[Long Pond]], [[Hancock County]], between 1952 and 1954. The study used [[Creel Census]] data, [[Scale Analysis]] for growth, and two-way [[Fish Trapping]] in the pond outlet from 1952 to 1958.

Anglers returned an estimated 4.0 percent of the stocked fish over the four years following the final stocking (1955–1958). Hatchery-reared salmon made up over 90 percent of the salmon catch during the census period. Growth and exploitation differed between year-classes, and only one salmon from the stocked groups was documented as continuing to sea. The author concludes that planting hatchery salmon is a desirable management tool for Maine lakes that lack natural spawning habitat.

## Background & Description

**Study rationale.** Before this work, little quantitative information existed on the recovery by anglers, survival, growth, and movement of landlocked salmon stocked in Maine lakes. The project was partly financed by [[Dingell-Johnson Act]] funds as Project F-2-R, Maine.

**Study lake.** Long Pond covers 897 acres at an elevation of 59 feet. Its maximum and average depths are 120 and 39 feet. Key characteristics:

- Surface temperatures rarely exceed 72°F; hypolimnion temperatures usually range from 47°F to 50°F.
- Dissolved oxygen exceeds 2 ppm at all depths.
- The water is uncolored and slightly acidic, and the pond meets the requirements for [[Oligotrophic Lakes]].
- Salmon were first introduced about 1895 and [[Smallmouth Bass]] about 1940. [[Brook Trout]] are native to the drainage.
- Other species present include chain pickerel, [[Alewife]] (anadromous), American smelt, white sucker, golden shiner, northern redbelly dace, and American eel.

**Drainage.** Of the 11 tributaries, seven have no value for salmonid spawning, three provide limited trout habitat, and only Great Brook offers a small amount of salmon spawning and nursery area. The outlet runs about 1.75 miles to [[Somes Sound]]. Four dams (3 to 6 feet high, each with a fishway) occur in the outlet. The uppermost regulates Long Pond water levels, and the lower dams impound Somes Pond (104 acres) and small village ponds for fire prevention.

**Representativeness.** The author considers Long Pond representative of at least 10 lakes managed for salmon in northeastern Maine. These lakes share good water quality, smelt populations as the main forage fish, coexisting warm- and cold-water game species, and a shortage of salmon spawning and nursery area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking and handling.** Three stockings were made at Long Pond between 1952 and 1954 at three widely separated locations, with about half of the 1952 and 1955 groups placed near the mouth of Great Brook. All fish were age-group I+ at stocking. Fish were anesthetized with [[Urethane]] for [[Fin Clipping]], held at the hatchery for 1 to 3 days, and no post-clipping mortality was noted. A 1948 planting of 15,000 advanced salmon fry was judged unlikely to have affected the experimental stockings made more than four years later.

**Creel census design.** Complete ice- and open-water creel censuses ran in 1954. From 1955 to 1958, annual catches were estimated from sample catch per unit effort and total effort estimates. Censuses were personal-interview, day-long surveys. Ice fishermen were contacted at fishing sites, and open-water anglers were checked at the pond's two public boat landings. About 85 to 90 percent of effort on census days was recorded. Effort-error tolerances were set so that open-water estimates would fall within 3 line hours of the true average daily effort at the 95 percent confidence level, using a [[Statistical Sampling Method]] suggested by Dr. Douglas Robson of Cornell University.

**Trapping.** Two fish traps in fishways caught fish moving in either direction. The upper trap, at the junction of the lake and outlet, operated from 1952 to 1958. A lower trap at the head-of-tide junction with Somes Sound began in October 1953. The lower trap was not operated during a 2- to 4-week period in June and early July, when spent alewives were migrating in large numbers. Dam spillways were screened to prevent undetected downstream migration.

**Growth methods.** Scales were taken from the left side of each fish between the lateral line and the dorsal fin origin and read with a projector at 45 diameters. Earlier lengths were back-calculated with the Dahl-Lea proportional formula, using a correction factor of 1.0 inch derived from a regression of scale radius on total length for all Long Pond salmon.

## Key Findings

### Recovery by anglers

- Three stocking groups (1952, 1953, 1954) totaled over 9,000 fish, with about 3,000 fall-stocked salmon per group.
- Estimated returns were 5.0 percent (1952), 0.76 percent (1953), and 6.1 percent (1954).
- The overall estimated return was 4.0 percent for the 1955–1958 period.
- Hatchery-reared fish made up over 90 percent of the total salmon catch during the census period.

### Fishing quality and effort

- **Open water:** Hatchery-reared salmon were caught at 0.060 fish per line hour from 1955 to 1957, compared with 0.0047 fish per line hour for wild salmon in the same years. Open-water catch per unit effort was 0.041 for the 1952 group in its earliest years, and the author notes that later year-class CPUE values were 0.0031, 0.0044, and 0.008 for 1954–1958 (per the text, OCR-affected).
- **Ice fishing:** Ice fishermen expended the majority of total effort (reported as 58.0 percent in the abstract) and took 14.8 percent of the salmon catch.
- Long Pond catch rates compare favorably with those for salmon at other Maine waters, though the author notes that the yields differ from those of larger, well-known salmon lakes.

### Survival and exploitation

- Survival rates (Ricker method) included 0.170 for the 1954 group from age III to IV. Survival for the 1952 group increased between age IV and V, which the author judges probably real because that group grew to large size early.
- The 1954 group was exploited abruptly, with almost its entire catch taken during the fall of 1956 and the spring of 1957. The 1952 group was exploited over a full three-year period.
- The decline in survival with age for the 1952 group is attributed to fish nearing the end of their normal life span.

### Growth and density effects

- The 1952 group, stocked at low existing population density, showed high initial survival but subsequently poor growth.
- The 1953 group, stocked at high density, apparently suffered large mortality.
- The author concludes that growth and survival differences between hatchery-origin groups can arise within small, uniform lakes.

### Movement

- Of over 9,000 salmon stocked, a small fraction moved down the outlet during the six years after first stocking. Only one of these salmon is documented as continuing to sea.
- Downstream movement through the pond-outlet trap included 26 adult salmon. Upstream movement included 8 juvenile and 13 adult hatchery-reared fish.
- Juvenile movements occurred soon after stocking, while adults were captured during fall spawning migrations.
- The author concludes that [[Fish Ladder]] or outlet trap installations to prevent emigration of stocked salmon are unnecessary. This is supported by Wilkie (1959), who showed that most fish return to home waters after spawning.
- The failure of stocked salmon to use the outlet for spawning is taken to support the hypothesis that landlocked salmon return to natal streams, even within small drainages.

### Management implications

- A planting program is a desirable management technique for maintaining landlocked salmon fisheries in Maine lakes lacking habitat for natural reproduction.
- Small, well-dispersed fisheries can provide more recreational opportunities than concentrated fisheries on large, widely separated waters, because landlocked salmon have a limited North American distribution and high sporting value.
- The study was being repeated at Long Pond using about 6,000 fish per year rather than a larger number, with additional studies recommended on other lakes.
- Approximately 1,000 fin-clipped salmon were to be stocked in the Long Pond outlet, with equal numbers planned for subsequent years. If outlet trapping shows a spawning run develops, stocking plans for salmon lakes should include suitable inlets or outlets where runs are not yet established.
