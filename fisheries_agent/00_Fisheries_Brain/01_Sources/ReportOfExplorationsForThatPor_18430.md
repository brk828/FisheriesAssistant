# Railroad Route Explorations near the 32nd Parallel (1854)

#hydrology #freshwater-ecology #railroad-survey

## Executive Summary

This 1854 report by [[John G. Parke]], Lieutenant of the Corps of Topographical Engineers, documents a survey for a railroad route to the Pacific between the [[Rio Grande]] (near [[Dona Ana]]) and the [[Gila River]] (at the Pimas villages). The survey was authorized under War Department instructions from [[Jefferson Davis]] (Secretary of War) and was partly conducted in Mexican territory under a Mexican government permit. The party left San Diego on January 24, 1854, numbered 56 people, and followed the Southern emigrant road east to the Gila, then traversed the desert to [[Mesilla Valley]], arriving on March 13, 1854.

The report's central conclusion is that a railroad grade is feasible along much of the route, with easy gradients (a maximum of about 50 feet per mile on the main stretch), but that the chief obstacle is water scarcity. Only nine localities were found to have permanent water along the line, and long jornadas of 50 to 70 miles lacked any water.

## Background & Description

**Authorship and purpose.** The report, dated August 22, 1854, was written to the Secretary of War. Its stated purpose was to assess the practicability of a railroad through the region by documenting terrain, water, timber, building stone, and gradients. The instructions required barometric levelling along the line, with altitude readings at every point important to the road profile.

**Organization of the party.** Key members were:
- [[Henry Custer]], assistant and topographer
- [[Dr. A. L. Heermann]], physician and naturalist
- [[George Stoneman]], First Dragoons, commanding a 28-man escort, who also served as quartermaster and commissary (18 men employed in those duties; 5 more carried barometers and tripods)

**Route.** The party followed the emigrant road from San Diego via Warner's rancho, across the Colorado Desert, to [[Fort Yuma]], then up the Gila to the Pimas villages (reached February 13, 1854, 390 miles from San Diego). From there the survey passed through [[Tucson]], the [[San Pedro River]] valley, the [[Playa de los Pimas]], and [[Puerto del Dado]], then followed [[Cook's Trail]] to the [[Ojo de Vaca]], [[Rio Mimbres]], and [[Cook's Spring]] before reaching the Rio Grande.

**Instruments and methods.** Altitudes were measured with mercury and cistern barometers, corrected for hourly variation using tables by [[L. Blodget]] of the [[Smithsonian Institution]]. Distances were measured with a viameter attached to a wheel, and bearings with a prismatic compass. The chronometer was damaged early in the survey, so latitudes and longitudes were borrowed from [[Major W. H. Emory]] of the [[Mexican Boundary Survey]].

**Political context.** A hostile filibustering expedition against the Mexican province of Sonora had departed from California. The War Department worried that this might create danger for the party, and it instructed Parke to abandon the survey if serious interference was likely. The party proceeded after the expedition's threat was assessed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and vegetation.** The landscape is arid, with extensive desert plains, rugged mountains, and volcanic mesas. Vegetation includes mesquite, larrea (creosote), artemisia, cactus (including *cereus giganteus*), bunch-grass, grama grass, salt grass, and cottonwood along streams. Timber is scarce. Cottonwood, the only tree large enough for sills, grows in just four places: the Gila, Tucson, the Mimbres, and the Rio Bravo. Pine occurs in the mountain cañadas, and cedar and dwarf evergreen oak are found in sheltered nooks.

**Water resources.** Water was the dominant constraint. The report identified nine localities with permanent water:

| No. | Locality | Character |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tucson | Clear running stream; disappears a few hundred yards below town |
| 2 | Cienega de los Pimas | Fine springs; water sinks into sand |
| 3 | Rio San Pedro | Turbid stream flowing to the Gila |
| 4 | Spring near camp 22 (Puerto del Dado) | Cold, palatable, very limited |
| 5 | Valle de Sauz | Clear but slightly brackish stream spreading into marsh |
| 6 | Hole near camp 25 | Likely a blind drain of surface water |
| 7 | Ojo de Vaca | Slightly sulphureous spring forming a marsh |
| 8 | Rio Mimbres | Clear, cold mountain stream; sinks into plain below |
| 9 | Cook's Spring | Pond-like, sulphureous water |

Streams generally vanished into sand soon after leaving the mountains. Seasonal rain pools also provided temporary water. At Tucson, the rainy season starts in April and lasts three or four months.

**Hydrology observations.** Tucson's stream was about 1 foot deep and 6 feet wide, with water at 62°F while the air was 32°F at sunrise. The San Pedro was about 18 inches deep and 12 feet wide, flowing 12 feet below banks that were nearly vertical and miry.

**Wildlife and human presence.** The report mentions the [[Pima and Maricopa Indians]], who were friendly, and the [[Apache Indians]], who visited camps and were encountered in the mountains. It also notes large herds of stock driven to the California market, which had stripped grass near watering points.

## Key Findings

**1. Route and feasibility**
- The main surveyed line (red full line) had a maximum grade of 50 feet per mile along the 97-mile stretch from camp 10 to camp 15, with the steepest section (19 miles) between camps 14 and 15.
- A shorter alternate (blue line) through the Cienega de los Pimas and along the San Pedro could reduce grades to 38 feet per mile to the summit, with a maximum of 61 feet per mile on the San Pedro bottom.
- Crossing the [[Puerto del Dado]] required a 60-foot summit cut, and the descent totalled about 700 feet over 4.7 miles plus 181 feet over a further 7 miles.
- Maximum grades on the Cook's Trail section were 78.7 feet per mile at the camp 25 summit and 89 feet at the Rio Mimbres. The report noted both could be reduced by slight southward deviations.
- The highest point suggested for an alternate northern detour was 4,862 feet at Camp Castro near Mount Graham.

**2. Water is the main constraint**
- Jornadas of 38 miles, about 70 miles, and about 60 miles without water were encountered.
- Animals were severely exhausted by these stretches, and the party had to travel by night.
- The report recommended testing whether water could be obtained by digging wells, because this would be critical for railroad planning and for emigrant safety.

**3. Timber and stone**
- Cottonwood was available only at four locations (Gila, Tucson, Mimbres, Rio Bravo).
- Stone suitable for construction included granite, secondary limestone, sandstone, and gypsum near the Rio Bravo.

**4. Meteorite discovery**
- Two large iron meteorites were used as anvils at Tucson, found in a canyon of the Santa Rita Mountains about 25 to 30 miles south. They weighed about 1,200 pounds (annular) and about 1,000 pounds (prismatic).
- [[Charles U. Shepard]] analysed a sample on July 31, 1854. He identified nickel, phosphorus, magnesia, and iron, and reported that silver was likely present, with a specific gravity of 6.66. The mass is finely amygdaloidal, with an earthy white mineral resembling that in the Bishopsville (S.C.) meteoric stone. The description of a porphyritic structure after etching matches the reported observations.

**5. Fort Webster incident**
- On March 8, 1854, men sent to [[Fort Webster]] found the post abandoned and largely burned, with fresh Indian tracks and smoking embers, suggesting the attack had occurred within days. The party became more cautious as a result.

**6. Altitude data**
- Barometric readings were reduced to sea level, assuming 30.050 inches and 64°F. Blodget estimated the greatest probable error in absolute elevation at 100 feet, with grade errors considered negligible.
- A camp near Mount Graham was given at 4,862 feet, and a point labelled "B" on the profile was at about 5,177 feet.

**7. Concluding recommendations**
- The survey concluded that a railroad was practicable along or near the route. The party did not pursue the northern Texas extension, because it would have exceeded the appropriation and Captain Pope was already surveying that line.
- The party returned to the United States via San Antonio and Indianola, Texas.
