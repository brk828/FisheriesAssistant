## Executive Summary

This study, conducted by the Fisheries Research Division at the Auburn University Agricultural Experiment Station, examined how [[Water Hyacinth]] (*Eichhornia crassipes*) and inorganic fertilization interact to affect fish-food organism abundance and fish production in experimental ponds. [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) and [[Redear Sunfish]] (*Lepomis microlophus*) were co-stocked across five pond treatments varying in fertilization (none, 0-8-0 NPK, 8-8-0 NPK) and hyacinth presence. The central finding is a treatment-dependent, and even reversing, effect of hyacinth on fish standing crop: hyacinth had no measurable effect on fish biomass in unfertilized ponds, but suppressed fish standing crop by at least 50% in fertilized ponds — likely due to competitive nutrient partitioning between the floating macrophyte and the phytoplankton-based food chain that supports invertebrate prey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat matrix:** Fifteen experimental ponds partitioned into five triplicate treatment groups:
  1. Unfertilized, no hyacinth (control)
  2. Unfertilized, with hyacinth
  3. 0-8-0 (NPK) fertilized, no hyacinth
  4. 8-8-0 (NPK) fertilized, no hyacinth
  5. 8-8-0 (NPK) fertilized, with hyacinth
- Hyacinths were spatially confined to **one-ninth of the pond surface area**, limiting direct competitive footprint while still enabling measurable ecological effects.
- **Stocking:** Bluegill and redear sunfish were stocked together randomly at **9,880 fingerlings/hectare** (note: an earlier FAO Fish Culture Bulletin, 1969, 2(1):5 erroneously reported this as 1,620/ha).
- **Trophic structure:** [[Water Hyacinth]] root systems provided substrate for invertebrate colonization (snails, [[Odonate Nymphs]], amphipods, midge larvae), which in turn served as prey for the stocked [[Centrarchidae]] species — establishing a habitat-mediated trophic link between macrophyte structure and fish diet.
- **Competitive dynamic:** Fertilization was intended to stimulate both hyacinth growth and planktonic/food-organism productivity, but the two apparently competed for the same nutrient pool (N and P), creating a trade-off between macrophyte biomass and the phytoplankton-zooplankton-invertebrate food chain supporting fish growth.

## Key Findings

- **Invertebrate abundance by treatment:**
  - Fish-food organism numbers and dry weights on hyacinth roots were **greater in unfertilized (control) ponds** than in fertilized ponds.
  - [[Snails]] and [[Odonate Nymphs]] were dominant taxa in control ponds but were largely absent/non-dominant in fertilized ponds.
  - Dry weights of [[Amphipods]] and [[Midge Larvae]] did **not differ significantly** between control ponds and either fertilizer treatment.
  - Fertilized ponds with hyacinth showed **low invertebrate numbers and biomass**, attributed to intense predation pressure from sunfish rather than to low primary productivity.

- **Fish standing crop outcomes:**
  - In **unfertilized ponds**, mean fish standing crop was approximately **equal** between hyacinth and non-hyacinth ponds — hyacinth presence alone did not depress fish production absent fertilization.
  - In **fertilized ponds**, mean fish standing crop was **at least twice as large in non-hyacinth ponds** compared to hyacinth-containing ponds.
  - Overall fish production was **greater in fertilized+hyacinth ponds than in unfertilized (control) hyacinth ponds**, indicating fertilization still benefited fish yield even with hyacinth present, but far less than fertilization without hyacinth.

- **Proposed mechanism:** The reduction in fish production observed in fertilized hyacinth ponds is attributed to **nutrient competition between water hyacinth and the planktonic food chain** — hyacinth likely sequestered fertilizer-derived nutrients that would otherwise have fueled phytoplankton and downstream invertebrate/fish production.

- **Management implication:** Uncontrolled or unmanaged [[Water Hyacinth]] coverage in fertilized aquaculture ponds may substantially reduce sport-fish (bluegill/redear sunfish) production efficiency, despite providing structurally favorable invertebrate habitat under nutrient-poor (unfertilized) conditions.
