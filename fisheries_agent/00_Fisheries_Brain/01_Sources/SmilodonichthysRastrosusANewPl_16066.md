# Smilodonichthys Rastrosus: A New Pliocene Salmonid Fish from Western United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 Bulletin of the [[University of Oregon Museum of Natural History]] (No. 18), by Ted M. Cavender and Robert Rush Miller, formally describes *Smilodonichthys rastrosus*, a new genus and species of extinct [[Salmonidae]] from Pliocene deposits of California and Oregon. The taxon is based chiefly on the holotype, a large articulated skull (UO 26799) from the [[Gateway Locality]], Oregon. The authors place it in the subfamily Salmoninae on the basis of overall morphology and note closest resemblance to [[Oncorhynchus]], yet they also document a combination of traits shared with the coregonine [[Stenodus]] and with the feeding-related specialization of the coregonines. The name refers to its enlarged premaxillary "sabre" teeth and its very numerous gill rakers. The authors estimate a standard length of about 1900 mm.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic placement.** The family Salmonidae in western North America comprises upwards of 30 species in seven genera across three subfamilies (Norden, 1961). The authors describe *S. rastrosus* as a new genus because of its high degree of morphological distinction. Its osteology suggests a phyletic relationship closest to Oncorhynchus, but no living species of that genus approaches the fossil in feeding-mechanism specialization.

**Material.** The species is known from fossils collected over about fifty years. The holotype is UO 26799, a skull with jaws and anterior gill arches, collected from Gateway in summer 1964. The paratype (UO F3335) is a partial skeleton of a very large individual, somewhat larger than the holotype, including neurocranial bones, complete mandibles and premaxillae with large breeding teeth, and approximately 40 vertebrae. Additional material includes UMMP and UCMP specimens from Oregon and California, mostly premaxillary teeth, vertebral centra and jaw fragments.

**Diagnostic features.**
- More than 100 gill rakers on the first branchial arch in the adult (upwards of 110 recorded).
- Jaws and palate without feeding teeth; only one massive breeding tooth on each premaxilla and small teeth on the dentaries.
- Lower jaw very deep at the coronoid process (greatest depth three times in length).
- Maxilla toothless; supramaxilla exceptionally large, reaching 80% of the oral border of the maxilla.
- Parasphenoid keeled posteriorly; vomer diamond-shaped without a tooth-bearing platform.
- Interopercle with a pronounced dorsal extension.
- Shallow, small posttemporal fossa.
- Urohyal about 3/4 as long as the lower jaw.
- Vertebral centra ornamented with fine longitudinal ridges.

**Dimensions.** The neurocranium measures 307 mm (dorsal length) and 316 mm (ventral length). The right mandible measures 264–265 mm, and the holotype urohyal 215 mm. The premaxillary breeding tooth base is about 35 mm wide and 50 mm long. Using head-length-to-standard-length scaling from *Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*, the authors estimate the head length at 462 mm and the standard length at about 1900 mm.

**Etymology.** *Smilodonichthys* refers to the sabre-toothed cat genus *Smilodon*, alluding to the paired large anterior teeth; *rastrosus* is Latin for "raker," alluding to the numerous gill rakers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Localities and age.** Fossils occur at four main sites, two in each state, plus minor sites along the [[Columbia River]] in Oregon:

- **Gateway Locality (UO loc 2250), Oregon:** Coarse boulder and gravel facies of the "Torrential Beds" of the Madras Formation, at about 2,250 ft elevation. The age is uncertain, no older than Barstovian and no younger than Hemphillian, with a strong possibility of Hemphillian (Middle Pliocene) age. The beds contain reworked Barstovian (Late Miocene) vertebrates.
- **Worden Locality, Oregon:** Abandoned railroad cut in tuffaceous sandstone of possibly the Yonna Formation (Pliocene). Land mammals and cyprinid and catostomid fishes co-occur.
- **Pinole Locality, California:** Pinole Tuff, dated by potassium-argon to 5.2 million years (Evernden et al., 1964). Associated with a fragmentary mammalian fauna.
- **Turlock Locality, California:** Mehrten Formation at Turlock Lake. Land mammals indicate a Late Hemphillian (Late Middle Pliocene) river-border fauna, closely related in time to Pinole.

**Feeding ecology.** The numerous, long, attenuated, toothless gill rakers, closely spaced and overlapped, suggest feeding on small pelagic organisms such as those present in the North Pacific Ocean. The authors conclude that, given this and the fossil's geographic location, the species fed in the ocean during its life history. Comparisons with planktivorous coregonines and scombroids support this interpretation.

**Possible anadromy.** The unusually cavernous dermal bone structure, which resembles that of anadromous *Oncorhynchus*, may correlate with anadromous, fat-storing behaviour, though the authors present this as a possibility. Breeding teeth were a secondary sex character associated with upstream spawning migration in some salmonines.

**Lack of kype.** Individuals believed to be spawning males showed no modification of the symphysial area, indicating that no kype (hooked lower jaw) developed in this species.

## Key Findings

- *Smilodonichthys rastrosus* is a new extinct salmonid genus and species known from the Pliocene of California and Oregon. The holotype is a large articulated skull.
- It is the only known salmonid with more than 100 gill rakers on the first arch (upwards of 110), exceeding the previous maximum of about 78 in *Coregonus muksun* and about 42 in *Oncorhynchus*.
- The toothless maxilla and enormous single premaxillary breeding teeth distinguish it from all other salmonines. The supramaxilla is exceptionally large, and no other known teleost has a supramaxilla of comparable size.
- Branchiostegal ray count is 29 in the holotype (14 left, 15 right), a number reached only in Salmoninae and similar to *Oncorhynchus* and *Salvelinus*.
- Skull, jaw and palate features show a mix of salmonine and coregonine traits. The authors note affinities with [[Stenodus]], suggesting shared primitive characters between the Coregoninae and Salmoninae.
- The feeding apparatus points to planktivory or small pelagic prey in the ocean, a conclusion supported by the Pacific location.
- Age is constrained to Late Miocene to Middle Pliocene; the Pinole Tuff gives 5.2 million years, and the Turlock fauna indicates Late Hemphillian.
- The Worden Locality also yields an extinct *Oncorhynchus*-like species, indicating that *Oncorhynchus* is at least as old as Middle Pliocene, contrary to the Pleistocene origin suggested by Neave (1958).
- The authors conclude that the species lacks a kype and that its cavernous bone structure may indicate anadromy.
