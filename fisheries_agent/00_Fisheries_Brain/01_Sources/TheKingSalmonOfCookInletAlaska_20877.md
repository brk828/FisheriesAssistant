# The King Salmon of Cook Inlet, Alaska

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Bureau of Commercial Fisheries) by Robert M. Yancey and Fredrik V. Thorsteinson, published as Special Scientific Report–Fisheries No. 440, investigates the decline of [[King Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) runs in [[Cook Inlet]], Alaska. Field work was conducted during the 1958 and 1959 seasons. The authors conclude that the decline is real rather than a product of restrictive regulations, and that [[Personal-Use Fishery]] and [[Sport Fishery]] harvests are important factors affecting runs. Biological sampling documented ten age groups, a significant length-fecundity relationship, and a run pattern in which schools split into ever-smaller tributaries. Escapement surveys were only partly successful because of [[Glacial Turbidity]], the large number of small streams, and the vast area involved.

## Background & Description

King salmon were historically one of the most important salmon species in the Cook Inlet area. Russians traded them to the English as early as 1787, and Native Alaskans and early settlers relied on them heavily for food. By the 1950s, runs had declined severely between 1953 and 1959. Salmon passed through a commercial and personal-use (subsistence) fishery in salt water, then a personal-use and sport fishery in fresh water, a "fourfold exploitation" that prompted the study.

The study had four objectives:
1. Review the commercial catch and regulations.
2. Determine the magnitude, location, and catch of personal-use and sport fisheries.
3. Collect and analyze biological statistics.
4. Determine the distribution, timing, and relative magnitude of escapement.

Commercial catches were primarily canned. The canned pack, reported consistently since 1923, rose steadily to a record in 1951 and declined after 1954. The [[Commercial Fishery]] used [[Set Gill Net]] and [[Drift Gill Net]] gear, and traps were banned by the State of Alaska in 1959.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Migration and habitat:** Fish migrated from fresh water to the ocean in their second year and returned after 2, 3, or 4 years at sea. Spawning occurred in tributaries of the Cook Inlet drainage, including clear tributaries where fish could be seen.
- **Turbidity:** Many glacial streams are too turbid for visual detection of salmon, which complicated escapement surveys and made the Glacial Turbidity of main stems a key limitation.
- **Tidal and water conditions:** Extreme tidal ranges, rough water, and glacial silt in salt-water areas prevented sport fishing there.
- **Largest system:** The [[Susitna River Basin]] (about 19,000 square miles) drains the largest king salmon runs in the area, with a combined mainstem and tributary length of about 750 miles.
- **Spawning elevation:** Spawning areas were observed at 2,500 to 3,000 feet. All king salmon streams in the district lie north of Anchor Point.
- **Latitude trend:** King salmon mature at greater ages farther north, consistent with the Cook Inlet dominant age span of 4 to 6 years.

## Key Findings

**Decline and regulations**
- The canned pack peaked at 59,488 cases in 1951 and declined from 1955 to 1959 (15,034 cases in 1955).
- Commercial gear was limited (set nets maximum 35 fathoms, 105 fathoms aggregate per fisherman; drift nets maximum 150 fathoms; mesh 8 1/2 inches during May 25 to June 30).
- Weekly closed periods increased from 36 hours before 1949 to 120 hours from 1953 to 1959. Because 1953 and 1954 packs were achieved under the same regulations as the later, lower packs, the authors conclude that shortened seasons alone cannot explain the decline.
- Catch per set net day fell after 1956 despite relatively stable effort, supporting a real decline.
- Set gill nets took 71.5 percent of the 1959 catch, drift gill nets 17.2 percent, and traps 11.2 percent.

**Personal-use and sport fisheries**
- The 1958 personal-use catch was estimated at 3,025 fish (951 cases), about one-sixth of the 1958 commercial pack.
- Personal-use nets were not registered and catches were not reported, so their effect on runs is unknown.
- The sport fishery is conducted only in fresh water. On the Anchor River, an estimated 3,000 anglers spent 15,000 man-hours in 1954. Kenai Peninsula streams recorded 20,450 anglers in 1955 (83,322 man-days, combined with Anchor River).

**Biology**
- Ten age groups were found in the commercial catch; age groups 4, 5, and 6 occurred most frequently. Age group 6 was dominant in 1958 and age group 5 in 1959.
- Males averaged smaller than females. Mean male length was 809 mm in 1958 and 724 mm in 1959; mean female length was 887 mm and 845 mm, respectively.
- Male length increased through the season in both years. Analysis of variance showed significant differences among fishing periods (male F = 9.804; female F = 3.285).
- In 1959, fecundity ranged from 4,242 to 13,619 eggs (mean 8,517) in 60 females measuring 667 to 1,005 mm. The linear regression of eggs on length was significant (r = 0.8105). The authors state the average egg count appears to be the highest recorded on the eastern Pacific shore.
- Scale sampling was affected by regeneration: 41 percent of 1958 scales were unusable versus 11 percent in 1959, when multiple scales were collected per fish.

**Distribution, timing, and escapement**
- Five suspected glacial spawning streams on the northwest shore were tested: no adults were captured, but fingerlings were taken in the Katnu, McArthur, and Chakachatna rivers.
- King salmon were seen in the Deshka River as early as late May and in the Yentna and Skwentna systems by July 7.
- The spawning run behavior involves schools splitting into smaller groups at successively smaller tributaries, sometimes ending with spawning units of only two or three pairs. The authors suggest this pattern may be linked to the decline.
- Relative escapement magnitude could not be estimated for Cook Inlet or between systems.

**Summary of conclusions**
- The commercial catch declined despite shortened seasons and gear restrictions.
- Peak catches occur earlier near the latitude of Anchor Point, with a possible later run into Kenai Peninsula streams south of Boulder Point.
- Smaller average size in 1959 is associated with a shift toward younger age-group dominance.
