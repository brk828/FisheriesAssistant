# Disappearance of endrin from fish and other materials of a pond environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ecotoxicology #Centrarchidae #Percidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 field study by W.R. Bridges documents an [[Endrin]] pesticide fish-kill in a 4-acre pond near Mead, Weld County, Colorado, following aerial application of the organochlorine insecticide to an adjacent beet field in June 1959. The pond received endrin drift, killing large numbers of [[Yellow Perch]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Bluegill]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Common Carp]]. Despite the acute mortality event, chemical residue analyses of water, mud, vegetation, and fish tissue over a 3-month period showed relatively rapid environmental clearance of endrin, and a substantial portion of the fish population survived — suggesting the acute field toxicity of endrin was lower than predicted from prior laboratory bioassays (Iyatomi et al. 1958; Henderson et al. 1959). The study is notable as an early example of environmental [[Pesticide Residue Dynamics]] tracking across multiple compartments of a lentic ecosystem.

## Background & Description

This paper is a residue/toxicology study rather than a morphological description; no anatomical or morphometric data on the fish species are reported. The species assemblage collected for analysis and observation included:

- **[[Yellow Perch]]** (*Perca flavescens*) — Percidae
- **[[Pumpkinseed]]** (*Lepomis gibbosus*) — Centrarchidae
- **[[Bluegill]]** (*Lepomis macrochirus*) — Centrarchidae
- **[[Black Crappie]]** (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) — Centrarchidae
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus salmoides*) — Centrarchidae
- **[[Common Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*) — Cyprinidae
- **[[Fathead Minnow]]** (*Pimephales promelas*) — Cyprinidae (bioassay live-car fish)
- **[[Black Bullhead]]** (*Ictalurus melas*) — Ictaluridae (bioassay live-car fish)
- **[[Green Sunfish]]** (*Lepomis cyanellus*) — Centrarchidae (bioassay live-car fish)
- **[[Goldfish]]** (*Carassius auratus*) and **[[Guppy]]** (*Lebistes reticulatus*) — cited from laboratory toxicity comparisons (Henderson et al. 1959), not collected from the pond.

No size, weight, or anatomical detail is given beyond number of individuals sampled per date.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: A ~4-acre pond near Mead, Colorado; max depth 7 ft, average depth 3 ft; soft mud substrate; dense [[Aquatic Vegetation]] cover (*Utricularia* sp. and *Ceratophyllum* sp.) persisting through late fall.
- **Water chemistry**: pH 9.1 (notably high/alkaline), phenolphthalein alkalinity 35 ppm, total alkalinity 147 ppm, summer afternoon temperatures ~75°F. The elevated pH (vs. maximum 8.2 tested by Henderson et al. 1959) is proposed as a possible factor moderating endrin's field toxicity.
- **Contamination pathway**: Aerial application of endrin (6 oz active ingredient/acre, 19.5% emulsifiable concentrate) to an adjacent beet field on June 21, 1959, with pond exposure attributed to spray drift, likely from an overflight of the northwest section during application.
- **Trophic/exposure pathways for bioassay fish**: Live-car fish (fathead minnow, black bullhead, green sunfish) placed in the pond on July 23 accumulated measurable endrin despite non-detectable levels in open water, implicating exposure via:
  - Direct contact with contaminated mud/vegetation in which live-car bottoms were embedded.
  - Ingestion of turbid, sediment-suspended water stirred by wind action.
  - Trophic transfer via feeding on aquatic invertebrates carrying residues (paralleling separate DDT/forest-spray observations in trout).
- **Environmental compartments tracked**: water, mud, vegetation, fish tissue — establishing a cross-compartment picture of contaminant fate in a lentic freshwater ecosystem, relevant to [[Bioaccumulation]] and [[Sediment-Bound Contaminants]] research.

## Key Findings

- **Water**: Endrin detected at 0.04 ppm (June 25) declining to non-detectable by ~1 month post-spray (July 24, "0.00" ppm); peak reported concentration equivalent to ~40 ppb four days after spraying.
- **Mud**: Endrin persisted far longer than in water — detected as late as August 21 (0.35 ppm) and dropping to non-detectable by September 4–21, roughly **2 months** post-contamination.
- **Vegetation**: Residues present through early August (0.37 ppm on Aug 4) but none detected after August 4, indicating vegetation-bound endrin cleared within ~6 weeks.
- **Fish tissue**: Appreciable endrin levels detected in fish tissue up to ~2 months post-spray (August 17: 1.00 ppm in bluegill, 0.58 ppm in pumpkinseed); by <3 months (September–October) residues were only trace or non-detectable.
- **General clearance order**: Water cleared fastest (~1 month), followed by vegetation (~6 weeks), with mud as the longest-persisting reservoir (~2 months), consistent with sediment acting as a depot for [[Organochlorine Pesticides]].
- **Bioassay live-cars**: No mortality among fathead minnow, black bullhead, and green sunfish during a 4-day exposure (July 23–27) despite tissue accumulation of endrin, despite water endrin being non-detectable — supporting non-waterborne (sediment/particulate/dietary) uptake routes.
- **Acute toxicity discrepancy**: Laboratory-derived 96-hr TLm values from Henderson et al. (1959) ranged from 0.66 ppb (bluegill) to 2.1 ppb (goldfish) — implying that field water concentrations (up to 40 ppb) should have been lethal, yet substantial fish survival was observed in the pond, including anglers catching fish within a month of the spill.
- **Proposed explanation for reduced field toxicity**: Possible poor toxicant distribution/mixing, and/or elevated pond pH (9.1, higher than the 8.2 maximum tested in lab studies), or unidentified water-quality interactions moderating endrin bioavailability/toxicity under field conditions — a departure from lab-based [[Acute Toxicity Testing]] assumptions.
- **Species-specific residue patterns**: [[Pumpkinseed]] samples, tracked across the longest time series (July–October), showed a clear decline from 0.58 ppm to trace/non-detectable, offering the best resolved species-level residue decay curve in the study.
