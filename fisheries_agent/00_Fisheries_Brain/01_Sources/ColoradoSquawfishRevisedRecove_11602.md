# Colorado Squawfish Revised Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This revised recovery plan, prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 6), addresses the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the largest cyprinid in North America and the top native carnivore of the [[Colorado River]] system. The species was listed as endangered on March 11, 1967, and the original recovery plan was approved on March 16, 1978. Its population has declined from historic levels because of physical alteration of the river (dams, dewatering, channelization, habitat fragmentation) and biological pressures from [[Nonnative Fishes]].

The plan sets delisting as the recovery objective, managed by [[Recovery Area]]. Natural populations are now restricted to the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], with hatchery-reared fish stocked into the Salt and Verde Rivers in Arizona. The plan lists five major actions: population monitoring and life-history research; management and habitat protection; reintroduction into historic range; communication and information sharing; and development of biological criteria for downlisting and delisting. Recovery is coordinated with the [[Recovery Implementation Program]], estimated at $53 million over 15 years, with an estimated recovery date of 2003 for the Upper Basin.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and size.** The species is the largest of four living members of *Ptychocheilus*, placed in the minnow family Cyprinidae. Maximum weights above 36 kg (79 lb) and lengths near 1.8 m (71 in.) have been recorded, but fish above 7 kg (15 lb) have been rare in recent times. Early settlers called it "white salmon" or "Colorado salmon." The other living congeners, the [[Sacramento Squawfish]], [[Northern Squawfish]] and [[Umpqua Squawfish]], remain common in their native waters, unlike the Colorado species.

**Evolution.** Fossil and paleoclimatic evidence (Miocene through early Pleistocene) indicates repeated shifts between pluvial and arid habitats. The species was adapted to swift water by the mid-Pliocene. Large size, mobility and spawning migrations were adaptive in drier seasons but may now contribute to its decline.

**Legal status.** Listed as endangered on March 11, 1967 (Federal Register), with full protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] from January 4, 1974. California, Arizona, New Mexico, Utah and Colorado also protect the species under state law.

**Morphology.** An elongated, pike-like fish with a large, nearly horizontal mouth and long, slender pharyngeal teeth (tooth formula 2,5-4,2) for grasping prey. Anal and dorsal fins each have nine principal rays; 80-95 lateral-line scales. Adults are countershaded (dark olive back, lighter sides, white belly). Young are silvery with a dark wedge-shaped spot at the caudal fin base.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Historically abundant in the mainstem Colorado River, most major tributaries and the Colorado delta in Mexico, with records across Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming and Mexico. Present natural populations are confined to the Upper Basin. The Green River subbasin below the Yampa confluence, the Yampa, and the White River hold the most fish; the Green River subbasin is considered the highest priority site for recovery. A small reproducing population persists in the [[San Juan River]].

**Habitat and temperature.** Young-of-the-year and juveniles use shallow backwaters with little current over silt and sand; adults use a range of depths and velocities, moving into backwaters and flooded riparian areas during peak runoff. Adults show little winter movement (15 of 20 radio-tagged fish moved less than 5 km between October and March in the upper Green River). Laboratory preferred temperatures for juveniles and adults were about 21.5-26.6 °C. Optimal embryo development occurred near 20 °C.

**Spawning and migration.** Gonads ripen during spring runoff (May-June). Spawning begins near 21 °C and occurs between late June and mid-August. Adults may migrate up to 320 km (200 mi) to spawning grounds. Major spawning sites are the lower 32 km (20 mi) of Yampa Canyon and [[Gray Canyon]] of the Green River. Spawning is suspected in Labyrinth Canyon. Homing and fidelity to spawning sites are indicated.

**Diet.** Young fish feed mainly on zooplankton and insect larvae but become predatory early (fish as small as 30 mm have been documented eating fish). Juvenile diet is about 86% fish, mainly the introduced [[Red Shiner]]. Adults are almost exclusively piscivorous.

**Growth.** Tagged adults in the Green River averaged 10.2 mm/year. Back-calculated lengths from 182 fish ranged from 44 mm at age I to 600 mm at age X. Growth is strongly temperature-dependent; fish in warm grow-out ponds reached 185-304 mm in one year, versus about four years in the river.

**Reasons for decline.** The Colorado River was historically a highly variable, silt-laden river that has since been transformed by more than 20 dams built since 1913. Key pressures include:
- Cold tailwater releases below reservoirs, altered discharge and reduced turbidity and nutrients.
- Channelization and loss of backwaters used for nursery and resting areas.
- Competition and predation by nonnative fishes, which outnumber native species (42 of 55 fish species in the Upper Basin were introduced).
- Habitat fragmentation by dams and diversions (e.g., Flaming Gorge, Taylor Draw), restricting migration.
- Rotenone treatment in 1962 at Flaming Gorge that killed squawfish, plus later eradication programs in the 1960s.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** The species is endangered; natural populations persist only in the Upper Basin, and the Lower Basin population was extirpated, with a last adult taken from Lake Mohave in 1967.
- **Recovery areas:** Three areas are defined: the Green/Upper Colorado subbasins (treated as one area), the [[San Juan Subbasin]], and the Lower Basin. Downlisting to threatened applies once self-sustaining populations exist in specified reaches (e.g., Green River from the Colorado confluence to the Yampa confluence, lower 220 km of the Yampa, lower 240 km of the White River, and the Colorado from Lake Powell to Palisade).
- **Delisting criteria:** Require downlisting criteria to be met, reduction of fragmentation threats, legal protection of essential habitat, flows and water quality, and removal of other significant threats. Lower Basin delisting depends on reestablishing populations in the Salt or Verde Rivers.
- **Stocking:** Over 96,000 fingerlings and 442 larger fish were stocked in the Salt and Verde Rivers in 1985; 31,750 fingerlings in 1987; and 20,000 and 89,303 fingerlings in 1988 at the Salt and Verde sites, respectively. In 1988, 57 recaptures were made on the Verde and six on the Salt. Stocking into [[Kenney Reservoir]] (about 17,000 in 1988, 33,000 in 1989, 36,000 in 1990) forms an experimental sportfishery.
- **Population monitoring:** In the San Juan River 1987-1989 surveys, eight adults and nineteen young-of-the-year were captured.
- **Flow dependence:** Higher spring flows benefit squawfish and disadvantage introduced fishes. Lowest larval growth and abundance occurred in 1983-84, linked to abnormally high summer flows from Flaming Gorge Dam.
- **Genetics:** Stocking in the San Juan or Upper Basin must await genetic documentation of distinct populations.
- **Funding and schedule:** The Recovery Program was projected at $2.3 million per year, with a $10 million water rights fund and a $5 million facilities request. Private water projects contribute $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion. The Implementation Schedule (Part III) lists tasks with priorities, responsible agencies and FY-01 to FY-03 cost estimates.
- **Public review:** Two public comment periods (1989 and 1991) yielded 30 letters, with over 250 press releases sent to print media.
