# Preliminary Ecological Survey of the Northern Sierra del Carmen, Coahuila, Mexico (April 1–10, 1945)

#ecology #terrestrial-ecology #mammalogy #ornithology #herpetology

## Executive Summary

In April 1945 a ten-day field party crossed the [[Rio Grande]] into the northern [[Sierra del Carmen]] of [[Coahuila]], Mexico, to complete an ecological survey of the adjacent [[Big Bend National Park]]. The survey recorded vegetation, mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians, and compared them with the American side. Its central conclusions are that the Mexican landscape is a northward extension of Mexican biota, that its grasslands were far less grazed than those in Big Bend, and that [[Mearns Quail]] and [[Mexican Black Bear]] persisted south of the border after apparently disappearing from the park. The document is a terrestrial field survey. It reports no fish, and aquatic information is limited to springs, tinajas and river margins.

## Background & Description

The expedition was a cooperative effort by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[National Park Service]], the Texas Cooperative Wildlife Research Unit, the Chicago Natural History Museum and the American Wildlife Institute. Its members were W. B. McDougall, C. C. Presnall, K. P. Schmidt and W. P. Taylor. Access and local guidance came from Major General Miguel Gonzales, owner of roughly 300,000 acres of [[Rancho El Jardin]] in northern Coahuila. The party assembled at Marathon, Texas, on March 31, 1945, and travelled by horse and pack train, with eight men and five pack animals. Their base was [[Campo Central]], at about 5,300–5,400 ft, roughly 15 miles southeast of Boquillas. Specimens were collected under field numbers (A679–A731) and deposited with the expedition.

The authors framed the survey as a test of whether the [[Chisos Mountains]] and the wider Big Bend are biogeographic outliers of Mexican flora and fauna. The field notes are detailed and often include altitude, locality and date.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography and vegetation.** The Sierra del Carmen is an igneous mass with a serrated ridge, locally called the [[Sierra Fronteriza]]. It rises to Picacho del Centinela, with the ridge at about 8,100 ft. The surrounding limestone escarpments carry [[Desert Scrub]] up to about 2,500–3,000 ft, with [[Creosote Bush]], [[Lecheguilla]] and [[Sotol]]. Above that lies rolling grassland dominated by [[Blue Grama]], with [[Giant Dagger]] (*Yucca carnerosana*) in large numbers. Woodland begins near 5,500 ft and gives way to [[Ponderosa Pine]], Graves oak, Douglas fir and Mexican white pine at higher elevations. The most significant finding was the presence of eastern [[Basswood]] (*Tilia americana*) growing alongside western ponderosa pine.

**Water.** The area is unusually well watered for the region. The survey heard of at least 16 springs on the flanks of the Sierra Fronteriza. Springs on the north and east slopes drain toward the Rio Grande near Stillwell Crossing, and those on the southwest slopes drain to Arroyo de Jaboncillo. Springs are seldom more than a mile apart. The [[Tinajas]] (rock water pockets) and springs provided the only surface water on the trip. Tornillo Creek was impassable from flash flooding on March 31.

**Land use and fire.** The survey contrasts the two sides of the river. Early in the century the Mexican lands were probably as overgrazed as the lands later included in Big Bend. In recent years, however, the Big Bend lands were more heavily misused, while most of the Gonzales ranch had scarcely been grazed for ten years. Goats and horses were heavy near camp. Many giant daggers had burned repeatedly, which the authors linked to the dominance of blue grama, since it tolerates both [[Overgrazing]] and [[Fire Ecology|burning]] better than taller grasses.

## Key Findings

**Biogeography.** The Sierra del Carmen contains a plant community not found in Big Bend, dominated by fir and Mexican white pine. It also has basswood, an eastern deciduous species, near western ponderosa pine. The authors describe the region as a northern outpost of the Mexican flora. Giant dagger country appears to exceed the area of [[Dagger Flat]] in the park. Several characteristic Big Bend plants, including [[Juniperus Flaccida]], *Hechtia scariosa* and *Euphorbia antisyphilitica*, were more common on the Mexican side.

**Mammals.** The survey listed 21 mammal taxa from specimens, observations and reports. Notable records include:
- **New Mexico Black Bear** (*Ursus americanus amblyceps*). Bear sign (dug sotols, cut dagger leaves, droppings) was found, along with brush platforms reported as cub resting sites. The authors considered the species nearly or completely gone from the Chisos.
- **Mountain Lion** (*Felis concolor stanleyana*). Common, based on a skin from the ranch, fresh tracks including a kitten track, and local reports.
- **White-tailed Deer** (*Odocoileus virginianus carminis*). Two specimens from Campo Central were described as "very nearly topotypical." The type locality of the subspecies is the Sierra del Carmen. Deer were scarcer than expected, which the authors attributed to regular hunting and to greater predation by bears and wolves. Big Bend deer are fully protected.
- **Mule Deer** (*Odocoileus hemionus crooki*) and **Texan Peccary** (*Pecari angulatus angulatus*) were recorded from skulls or reports only, with the peccary described as very scarce.
- Other mammals recorded include Gray Wolf, Texas Coyote, Arizona Gray Fox, Plateau Bobcat, Texan Ringtailed Cat, Fulvous Harvest Mouse, White-throated Wood Rat and Black-tailed Jack Rabbit. Mammal records are provisional where no specimen was taken, such as the skunk and pocket gopher.

**Birds.** About 60 bird taxa were recorded. Key entries include:
- **Mearns Quail** (*Cyrtonyx montezuma mearnsi*). A pair was seen on a well-grassed slope at Campo Central, digging grass bulblets. The species is absent from the American side and was considered extirpated in Big Bend.
- **Arizona Scaled Quail** (*Callipepla squamata pallida*) occurred in coveys of 8 to 18 in foothill grassland.
- **Rio Grande Wild Turkey** (*Meleagris gallopavo intermedia*) was reported as extremely scarce near the ranch. Flocks of up to 200 were reported near Hacienda de Babia.
- **Western White-winged Dove** (*Zenaida asiatica mearnsi*). A nest with one young bird was found in a desert willow at Noria de Gilberto on April 10.
- The survey also includes records of Mexican Black Hawk, Olive Warbler, Painted Redstart and Red-backed Junco at high elevations.

**Reptiles and amphibians.** The early season and cool nights limited the herpetofauna. Seven reptile taxa were collected, including Great Plains Swift, Couch's Swift, Rock Swift, Collared Swift, Stejneger's Lizard (*Uta stansburiana stejnegeri*), Desert Racerunner, Western Spotted Garter Snake and [[Brownsville Racer]] (*Coluber constrictor stejnegeri*). The Brownsville Racer record is notable because the species had been known only from the lower Rio Grande. A rattlesnake (*Crotalus* sp.) was seen but not captured. Two amphibians were taken: [[Desert Toad]] (*Bufo punctatus*), calling in a chorus at the mouth of Tornillo Creek, and [[Rio Grande Leopard Frog]] (*Rana pipiens berlandieri*), abundant in spring at Campo Central.

**Fishes and aquatic fauna.** No fish, aquatic invertebrates or water-quality measurements are reported. The only aquatic-related observations are the Belted Kingfisher on the Rio Grande at Boquillas and the frog and toad records noted above.

**Management implications.** The authors concluded that the Sierra del Carmen could again support Mearns Quail and Mexican Black Bear if protected in the meantime. They noted that with adequate protection white-tailed deer would soon outnumber the land's carrying capacity, so the question would be how to manage them rather than how to restore them. Their overall judgment was that the Mexican grasslands were the best-preserved examples of [[Chihuahuan Desert]] grassland of the period.
