# Toxicity of Fluoride to Rainbow Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 paper from the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] (Herbert and Shurben) tests whether [[Fluoride Toxicity|fluoride]] from [[Water Fluoridation]] that passes through [[Sewage Effluent]] could harm fish in rivers. The authors used laboratory bioassays on yearling [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) in water of three hardness levels. Their method avoided the buildup of metabolic waste products, which they considered a weakness of earlier work by [[Neuhold And Sigler]]. They found that fluoride toxicity depends on [[Water Hardness]] and that clear [[Threshold Concentration|thresholds]] exist. Their conclusion is that the 1.0 p.p.m. F expected in undiluted effluent is unlikely to harm a trout fishery, even in very soft water.

## Background & Description

Where water supplies are fluoridated, the usual target is 1 p.p.m. F. Sewage effluent from these districts could therefore carry up to this concentration into rivers. The authors note that few extensive studies of fish survival in fluoride solutions existed before this work.

Neuhold and Sigler (1960) tested two species:

- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*): 50% mortality within 20 days at 75–91 p.p.m. F in very soft water (calcium 0.5–4.5 p.p.m., magnesium 0.0–0.3 p.p.m.). Their probit fit implied that 1.0 p.p.m. F would kill no more than about 0.1% of a similar population, so the authors considered it virtually non-toxic to carp.
- Rainbow trout: 50% kill within 20 days at 3.6 p.p.m. F. Their data implied that about 3–4% of a population might die within three weeks at 1.0 p.p.m. F in very soft water.
- Trout eggs were more resistant to fluoride than grown fish.

Neuhold and Sigler kept fish in the same solutions for the full 20 days. The present authors argued that accumulated waste products could alter fluoride toxicity, so they used a renewal method (see Key Findings).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry as a modifier.** Hardness changed fluoride behaviour in solution. In the hardest water a precipitate formed after [[Sodium Fluoride]] was added, and in intermediate-hardness water a persistent opalescence appeared. Total fluoride measured by filtration and analysis was close to the nominal dose (within 5%). The dissolved fraction, however, varied greatly from day to day. The authors concluded that dissolved fluoride was less toxic in the two harder waters than in the softest.

**Dilution in rivers.** Effluent fluoride would normally be diluted by river flow. The authors applied a fishery-protection criterion: a continuous discharge should kill no more than 5% of a sensitive species such as trout within three months when diluted only by dry-weather river flow.

**Natural mortality.** The authors compared the calculated losses with the normal [[Natural Mortality]] of trout populations, which is much larger than the losses predicted at 1.0 p.p.m. F.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design**
- Ten yearling rainbow trout, averaging about 10 cm, were tested at each fluoride concentration.
- Each fish was held in 40-litre aerated solutions, kept near air saturation for dissolved oxygen. Fish were moved daily to freshly prepared solutions 15–30 minutes after feeding. This prevented accumulation of metabolic products.
- Fish were acclimatised for 6 days before exposure. Trials ran for a maximum of 21 days per water type.
- Soft water was dilution water (12 p.p.m. as CaCO₃) from [[Lake Trawsfynydd]]. Test waters also included 45 p.p.m. (a mix of lake and borehole water) and 320 p.p.m. (borehole water from the laboratory).

**Soft-water results (12 p.p.m. hardness)**
- Survival times at each lethal concentration fitted a logarithmic-normal distribution, allowing interpolation of times for any percentage kill using [[Probit Analysis]].
- At lower concentrations, survival curves showed [[Biological Truncation]]: some of the most resistant fish survived to the end of the trial. Truncation occurred at lower percentages as concentration fell, which the authors took as evidence of thresholds that are higher for resistant fish than for sensitive ones.
- Plotting log survival time against log concentration (Fig. 2) showed a near-straight relation at higher concentrations. Below a certain level, curves turned sharply upward, so a small drop in concentration produced a very large increase in survival time.
- Neuhold and Sigler's data also suggested a threshold: their last recorded mortality occurred at 218 hours within 480-hour experiments.
- Threshold concentrations at 21 days were 8.5 p.p.m. F for 50% mortality and 4.0 p.p.m. F for 5% mortality.

**Harder waters**
- Threshold behaviour also appeared at 45 and 320 p.p.m. hardness.
- Table 1 (as tabulated) shows the following: at 45 p.p.m. hardness, 113 p.p.m. F and above killed 100% of fish within 21 days, while 75 p.p.m. F killed none. At 320 p.p.m. hardness, 150 p.p.m. F killed 90%, and 100 p.p.m. F killed none. The table values are difficult to read in the scan and should be checked against the original.
- Dissolved fluoride concentrations at the thresholds in harder water were higher than the soft-water threshold, confirming lower toxicity in harder water.

**Conclusions and risk assessment**
- About 4 p.p.m. F causes 5% mortality within three weeks. The curve shape suggests that losses would not rise much by three months at that concentration.
- The highest expected effluent concentration, 1 p.p.m. F, is predicted to kill about 0.01% of a trout population of similar resistance. The authors describe this as negligible compared with natural mortality.
- Their conclusion: even in very soft water, undiluted effluent at the highest expected fluoride concentration is unlikely to harm a trout fishery. In harder water, or where effluent is diluted by several times its own volume of river water, the authors judged the risk to fisheries from fluoridation of public supplies to be virtually nil.
