# Influence of Las Vegas Wash Density Current on Nutrient Availability and Phytoplankton Growth in Lake Mead

#phytoplankton #limnology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This limnological study by Baker and Paulson examines how the saline, nutrient-rich [[Las Vegas Wash]] inflow forms a [[Density Current]] in [[Las Vegas Bay]] of [[Lake Mead]] and controls nutrient supply to [[Phytoplankton]]. Sampling ran monthly from October 1977 to September 1978. The wash water's high [[Total Dissolved Solids]] (TDS) and cool temperature let it sink as an underflow in winter, sit as an interflow in the metalimnion during summer and fall, and mix in spring. This transport pattern delivers phosphorus into the euphotic zone and produces inner-bay productivity that is strongly nitrogen-limited. The authors conclude that the density current, not simple nutrient loading, governs the bay's trophic response. They also warn that planned desalination of the wash could raise nutrient availability and create nuisance algal conditions.

## Background & Description

Density currents form in reservoirs when temperature or salinity differences separate inflowing and receiving waters. Earlier work by [[Anderson and Pritchard]] on Lake Mead documented underflow in winter, overflow in spring and interflow in summer and fall. Later studies recognized that density currents also transport heat and nutrients ([[Carmack et al.]], [[Gloss et al.]]).

**Study area.** Lake Mead was formed in 1935 by [[Hoover Dam]] and occupies a 183 km reach of the [[Colorado River]] on the Nevada-Arizona border. It is the second reservoir in the series after [[Lake Powell]] (formed 1953). At full capacity it reaches a maximum depth of 180 m and a volume of 36×10⁹ m³, the largest reservoir by volume in the United States. Annual precipitation is less than 12.7 cm, and the Colorado River supplies 98% of inflow. The major basins are Gregg, Temple, Virgin and Boulder. The Las Vegas Wash drains into Las Vegas Bay, an embayment of Boulder Basin.

**Las Vegas Wash.** The wash is the natural drainage of the Las Vegas Valley. Two secondary sewage treatment plants about 17 km upstream contribute over 95% of its flow (2.26 m³/s). Ammonia is high because of sewage effluent and ammonia stripping. Nitrate and TDS exceed 100 mg/L and 20,000 mg/L, respectively, from groundwater inputs and contaminated groundwater from old evaporation ponds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nutrient and salinity loading (Table 1 and 2 summaries).** The wash contributes less than 1% of Lake Mead's inflow volume, but it adds a disproportionate nutrient load. Its phosphorus loading exceeds that from the Colorado River. Inorganic nitrogen makes up about 15% of the total load. The Colorado River inflow is poor in phosphorus (very high N:P ratio of about 82.8:1), whereas the wash is phosphorus-rich (low N:P). The U.S. Water and Power Resources Service identified the wash as a major salinity source for the lower Colorado River and planned to reduce its salinity by about 50%.

**Study design.** Stations in the inner, middle and outer parts of Las Vegas Bay and in Boulder Basin were sampled about monthly over 3-4 day periods. Analyses included:
- Ammonia (phenol-hypochlorite method), nitrate (hydrazine reduction), phosphate and total phosphorus (ascorbic acid method)
- Chlorophyll-a from composite surface, 3 m and 5 m samples
- In situ ¹⁴C primary productivity at 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 10 and 15 m
- Temperature, oxygen, pH and conductivity (Hydrolab Model 11A)

Conductivity isopleths traced the density current because of the high TDS of the wash.

**Seasonal dynamics.**
- **Winter:** The lake was isothermal, so the cold, dense wash formed an underflow throughout Las Vegas Bay. In January and February it extended as an underflow into Boulder Basin along the old stream channel.
- **Stratification:** Once thermal stratification developed, the wash (warmer than the hypolimnion) formed an interflow along the metalimnion.
- **Fall (October):** The interflow ran out to the bay mouth at about 22 m. Overturn was just beginning and the thermocline was sharp.
- **Spring (April):** Substantial mixing occurred because the warming wash reduced its density difference with the lake.
- **Summer:** Interflow persisted in the metalimnion, with broad stratification.

## Key Findings

1. **Density currents control nutrient delivery.** The vertical position and mixing of the wash current determined nutrient availability in the euphotic zone. Phosphate rose to 57 µg/L in the inner bay during summer, where mixing was greatest.
2. **Productivity gradient.** Phytoplankton productivity and chlorophyll-a were highest in the inner bay and declined progressively toward Boulder Basin.
3. **Nitrogen limitation.** Growth in Las Vegas Bay was nitrogen-limited, with N:P ratios mostly below the approximate 10:1 needed for growth (Golterman 1975). Nitrate was completely stripped from the epilimnion in summer, and winter nitrate exceeded 200 µg/L after fall overturn.
4. **Phosphorus accumulation.** Nitrogen deficiency led to excess phosphorus accumulating in the inner bay. This produced an apparent but spurious correlation between phosphate and productivity/chlorophyll-a.
5. **Shift in limitation.** Boulder Basin has become more nitrogen-limited, while the upper basins (Virgin, Temple and Gregg) remain phosphorus-limited.
6. **Lower-than-expected response.** Goldman's application of the Vollenweider-Dillon phosphorus loading model predicted loading many times above the "dangerous" rate, but observed growth was much lower. The scan garbles the exact multiplier, so the figure is unverified. The authors attribute the gap to density-current-driven dynamics.
7. **Trophic status.** The inner Las Vegas Bay was classified as slightly eutrophic, while the rest of the bay and Boulder Basin were mesotrophic to oligotrophic (Paulson et al. 1980). Hypolimnetic discharge at Hoover Dam also reduces nutrient retention.
8. **Desalination risk.** A planned bypass pipeline around the wash system would reduce salinity by about 50% and raise inflow temperature by 2-6°C. Under present conditions the wash cools about 3°C between the treatment plants and 1.6 km above the lake (spring 18.6 to 15.5°C). Under the proposed scenario, a 3°C temperature increase combined with a 50% salinity reduction would produce surface flows in spring.
9. **Projected ecological effects.** Desalination would enhance mixing in the inner bay and increase nutrient availability, so nuisance algal blooms could develop in Las Vegas Bay. The authors conclude that desalination may be detrimental to water quality.
