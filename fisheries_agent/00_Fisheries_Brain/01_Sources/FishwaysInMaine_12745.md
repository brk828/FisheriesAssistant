# Fishways in Maine

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-passage

## Executive Summary

This 1956 pamphlet, Pamphlet No. 5 of the [[Inland Fisheries and Game Department]] (Maine), sets out the Department's policy on [[Fishway]] (fish ladder) construction, operation and function. Written by Chief Engineer [[Laurence Decker]], it explains why fishways are needed to restore fish migrations blocked by dams, how the Commissioner decides whether to require one, which fishway types the Department recommends, and what an engineering investigation must cover. It also includes hydraulic flow tables for engineers and fishery biologists (Appendix B) and photographs and schematic sketches (Appendix A).

## Background & Description

Fishways are hydraulic structures built in streams to help fish get past natural or man-made obstacles during migration. The document presents them as one of the most important tools in managing Maine's inland fisheries.

The Fishery Research Division found that many dams block fish moving to and from spawning and nursery areas. Key examples include:

- [[Atlantic Salmon]] moving up Maine coastal rivers in spring to spawn in rubble-bottomed riffles.
- [[Alewife]] moving up rivers into lakes and ponds.
- [[Landlocked Salmon]] and [[Brook Trout]] leaving lakes and ponds in autumn to spawn in tributaries or outlet streams.

Without access to spawning areas and a route back, most or all natural reproduction can be lost.

**Decision process.** When biologists find that a dam seriously impairs desirable game fish, the Department Engineer surveys the site and prepares a design and cost estimate. The Engineer and biologists report to the Commissioner, who weighs whether the benefit justifies the cost. If it does, the Commissioner can legally order the dam owner to install a fishway. The owner may supply a satisfactory design, but usually the Engineering Division prepares it.

**Design factors.** Design depends on the migrating fish, stream flow, and the dam's type, use and height, as well as the fishway's structure. Water used by a fishway is effectively lost from the usable supply, much like a leak, so water economy matters. Because the dam owner pays, cost is also a major concern. Concrete fishways cost more initially than timber fishways but last longer and cost less to maintain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Migration behavior.** Upstream migrants push against the current until they reach spawning grounds or an impassable barrier. They usually hold as close to the obstruction as velocity and turbulence allow, then search for a way through or around it. The entrance should therefore sit as near this terminal point as possible:

- If placed too far below the dam, fish often bypass it, because attraction flow is nearly always weaker than at the dam.
- If placed too close to the dam's foot, excessive turbulence and swift water can keep fish from finding it.

**Flow and attraction.** On large rivers, larger flows are needed to give stronger attraction at the entrance, which in turn requires larger pools to dissipate the extra energy. Too much flow creates turbulence that fish cannot handle, and too little may fail to draw them to the entrance.

**Site conditions.** Many dams sit on streams with large water-level fluctuations, or on lake outlets subject to heavy drawdown. Fishway performance must hold across these changing levels.

**Structure and debris.** Fishways must withstand weathering and the impact of logs and ice. Debris (sticks, logs) can block orifices and pool passages, so trash racks and periodic cleaning are required. Many dams in Maine wilderness areas are timber, and timber fishways are used where needed.

## Key Findings

### Basic fishway requirements

The pamphlet lists seven general requirements that apply to all game fish in Maine waters. Larger species may need larger pools, and species approach obstacles differently:

1. The entrance location, flow quantity and flow character must let fish find the fishway easily.
2. Flow must be controllable within set limits despite headwater and tailwater fluctuation.
3. Pools must be large enough to limit turbulence and provide swimming and resting space.
4. Hydraulic conditions must let an average fish move between pools without excessive effort. These depend on drop per pool, pool size, flow, and the size, location and type of weirs and orifices.
5. The structure must withstand weathering, logs and ice.
6. It must be economical to build, maintain and operate, including its water use.
7. It must be accessible, protected from debris, and able to be shut off so pools can be drained, cleaned, inspected and repaired.

### Recommended fishway types

- **[[Overfall Type Fishway]]:** Water passes from pool to pool mainly over notched weirs or chutes, with small drainage orifices near the baffle bottoms. Notches may be [[Rectangular Weir]], triangular ([[V-Notch Weir]]) or [[Cipoletti Weir]] (trapezoidal). Chutes are no longer than half a pool's length and carry water without free fall. The drop per pool is kept at a maximum of one foot.
- **[[Submerged Orifice Fishway]]:** Flow passes entirely through submerged orifices near the bottom of the baffles, which may be vertical or inclined about 45 degrees. With both ends submerged, the level difference between pools stays roughly uniform and balances automatically as stream level changes. It needs no manual adjustment but must be kept clear of sticks and debris.
- **[[Combination Fishway]]:** Submerged orifice pools, called [[Balancing Pool]]s, are placed at the upper end, with overfall weirs below. The number of balancing pools equals twice the headwater fluctuation in feet. In the worked example, a 3-foot fluctuation requires six balancing pools. The example design uses 10 x 12 inch balancing orifices, 12 inch Cipoletti weirs and 6 x 8 inch drain orifices in the lower pools. A trash rack sits at the upstream exit, a wood gate over the first pool orifice allows shutoff in winter or for repairs, and the floor must keep at least two feet of water at all times.
- **[[Denil Fishway]]:** A straight channel with closely spaced baffles sloping upstream, attached to the floor and sides. Water entering the spaces between baffles creates cross-currents that dissipate energy. It performs well under normal level fluctuations but needs much more water and is more complex. Only one had been built in Maine as of 1956. Tests in other states showed high efficiency, and the Department hoped for more installations.

### Pool size guidelines

Minimum pool sizes by maximum flow (from the pamphlet's table):

| Max. flow | Min. pool size | Min. pool depth | Fish served |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 to 3 cfs | 5' x 5' | (OCR unclear; about 4') | Trout, alewives |
| 4 to 5 cfs | 6' x 6' | 4' | Trout, alewives, lake salmon |
| 6 to 7 cfs | 6' x 8' | 5' | Salmon, trout |
| 8 to 20 cfs | 8' x 8' or larger | 6' | Atlantic salmon |

Resting pools, twice the size of the above, are required every fifteenth pool. Pools in the standard designs range from 5 x 5 ft to 8 x 8 ft, with a drop of no more than one foot per pool.

### Engineering investigation

Each site needs a careful study before design or cost estimation. The checklist covers:

- Stream data: width, depth, velocity and volume, headwater and tailwater fluctuation, gradient, bottom type.
- Dam data: type, material, condition, use, height, length, spillway length, gates, sluiceways and penstocks, and how water is normally spilled.
- Entrance and exit: best location considering attraction, cost, effect on the dam, accessibility, and protection from ice and logs. Also required are tailwater elevations at the entrance and headwater elevations at the exit, plus maximum and minimum head.
- Design parameters: desired drop per pool, flow range, number and arrangement of pools, pool size and depth, weir and orifice types, materials, flow control, compensation for headwater fluctuation (gates, drop planks, or submerged balancing pools), trash rack type, and ice/debris protection.
- Economics: the most economical practical design and long-term maintenance cost.

### Flow tables (Appendix B)

The appendix gives four reference tables and their formulas:

- **Sharp-edged submerged orifices** (Table 1, eight sizes from 6 x 6 in to 18 x 24 in, heads 2 to 24 in): Q = CA√(2gH), with C = 0.625 and g = 32, Q in cu. ft./sec. Values rise by 50% for rounded-edge orifices.
- **Rectangular weirs with complete contraction** (Table 2, crest lengths 1' to 6'): Q = 3.33(L − 0.2H)H^1.5.
- **Suppressed rectangular weirs** (Table 3, in gallons per minute): Q = 1494.5LH^1.5.
- **Cipoletti weirs** (Table 4, 12, 15, 18 and 24 in crests): Q = 3.367LH^1.5, in cu. ft./sec.
- **V-notch weirs** (Table 5, factors of H^2.5 for 90° and 60° notches): general formula Q = 2.5 tan(θ/2) H^2.5, where 90° gives Q = 2.5H^2.5 and 60° gives Q = 1.443H^2.5.

Table values are reproduced from the source; some OCR digits were garbled in scanning, so verify against the original before engineering use.

### Overall conclusion

The pamphlet's main conclusion is that properly designed and installed fishways, matched to stream characteristics and fish behavior, effectively restore passage for migratory game fish. Each site requires a thorough engineering and biological study, and the Department directs their construction when the fishery benefit justifies the cost.
