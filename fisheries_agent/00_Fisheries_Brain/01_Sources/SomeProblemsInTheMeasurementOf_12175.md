# Some Problems in the Measurement of Radiation Under Water: A Review

#limnology #photobiology #underwater-radiation

## Executive Summary

This 1965 review by D. F. Westlake of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] (River Laboratory, Wareham, Dorset) summarizes agreed practice for measuring radiation under water in ecological studies. It addresses terminology, units, optical density, instrument design, and the optical errors that arise when surface and submerged photometers are compared. The paper is not about fish. It is a methods and terminology review for limnology and aquatic photobiology.

## Background & Description

The review responds to confusion in ecological radiation studies. Earlier reviews by [[Sauberer]] and [[Strickland]] had moved towards standard procedures, but differences persisted between European and American practice. The paper focuses on four topics:

1. The confused use of "absorption", "extinction" and "attenuation".
2. The uses of optical density.
3. Calculating the spectral distribution of visible energy under water from readings taken with selective photocells and colour filters.
4. Optical problems when comparing an aerial photometer with an underwater photometer.

**Spectral ranges**
- Full solar spectrum: about 300 mμ (ultraviolet) to about 3,000 mμ (infrared). Most energy lies between 400 and 1,000 mμ.
- [[Visible Light]]: 380 to about 780 mμ, about half of total energy.
- [[Photosynthetically Active Radiation]]: taken as 390 to 710 mμ (±10 mμ), about 0.46 to 0.48 of total energy.

**Units**
- Irradiance (energy rate per unit area) should be expressed in energy units. The author recommends W/m² (J/m² s) under the metre-kilogram-second system, but notes that many ecologists prefer the gram calorie per square centimetre per second (gcal/cm² s) because meteorological data use it.
- The [[Langley]] (1 ly/min = 1 gcal/cm² min) was recommended, but the author considers it an extra term that loses information.
- Table 1 gives conversion factors among J/m² s, ergs/cm² s, gcal/cm² min and lux (illuminance). For example, 1 gcal/cm² min is about 6.98 × 10² W/m² (as reported in the source table).
- [[Craig]] proposed quanta per square centimetre per second. The author accepts this for biophysics and biochemistry, but not for ecological energy budgets.

**Photometric units**
- Lux, foot-candle and metre-candle describe white-light illuminance as seen by the human eye. They are unsuitable for other organisms or coloured light.
- Table 2 shows that the energy equivalent of 1 lux varies widely with source. For daylight the values are 6 × 10⁻⁴ (visible) and 13 × 10⁻⁶ gcal/cm² min (total). For tungsten filament lamps they are 6.8 × 10⁻⁵ to 60–80 × 10⁻⁶ gcal/cm² min (total).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Transmission processes**
Irradiance in water falls with distance from three sources:
- **Divergence**: geometric loss. It is negligible in "limiting" situations such as sunlight or sky radiation.
- **Deflection**: rays scattered away from the detector. It is negligible in limiting situations.
- **Transformation**: conversion of radiant energy to heat by water, solutes or particles. It is the true loss mechanism.

**Bouguer-Beer relations**
- Ideal conditions (parallel monochromatic beam, normal incidence, non-scattering medium) follow i = i₀(log base)⁻ᵃᴸ.
- Natural radiation is diffuse and polychromatic, and in homogeneous (mixed) water behaves analogously. It is described with a combined coefficient E′ related to depth (Equation 2a–c).
- For natural radiation under water, the mean direct path length is reported as about 1.2 times the depth, though it varies with sun height and the mix of sunlight and skylight.
- Semi-logarithmic plots of irradiance against depth in mixed water are close to straight lines. Changes in slope usually indicate that the water is not mixed.

**Terminology**
The author documents at least five ways of distinguishing absorption from extinction:
- By logarithmic base (for example, Strickland uses base e for absorption and base 10 for extinction).
- By medium type (Sauberer: absorption in pure liquids, extinction in scattering media).
- By distance factor (path length versus depth).
- By radiation type and detector directionality (Sverdrup, Johnson and Fleming).
- No distinction (some authors use the terms interchangeably).

The author endorses replacing extinction with [[Attenuation]]. Under the proposed scheme:
- [[Absorption]] is loss by transformation, related to path length, giving true Bouguer-Beer coefficients.
- Attenuation is observed loss with depth, combining absorption and scattering, and is used for empirical depth coefficients.

The base of the logarithm must always be stated.

**Optical density**
- [[Optical Density]] D = log₁₀(I₀/I_t) (Equation 9a). It is proportional to the attenuation coefficient, concentration and depth.
- Optical densities are additive across components. For natural waters, D_T = D_w + D_s + D_p (water, solutes and suspended particles).
- Pure-water absorption (D_w) can be calculated from tabulated absorption coefficients. D_s and D_p can then be found by subtraction.
- Vertical attenuation studies in weed beds have been used to estimate water-weed concentration.

## Key Findings

**Instrumentation and calibration**
- Absolute irradiance is best measured above the surface using heat-based instruments such as the [[Kipp Solarimeter]], Epply pyrheliometer and Schenk pyranometer. Underwater, simpler relative instruments are used. Absolute underwater readings are possible only to about 5–10 m in clear water.
- Relative readings are usually taken with [[Selenium Photocell]]s (barrier-layer type). These are sensitive mainly between 300 and 700 mμ, with a peak at 500–600 mμ. Their spectral response therefore affects readings in coloured water.
- Calibration in absolute terms is difficult because factors vary with sky conditions and need frequent checking.

**Spectral calibration method**
- Sauberer recommends at least three, preferably seven, colour filters for PAR studies. Equivalent glasses (Chance) and cells (EEL) are similar to his recommended Schott and Lange equipment.
- The author sets out a worked calculation. A filter with 56% transmission at 400 mμ and a cell with relative sensitivity 0.62 gives a combined response of 0.35.
- Window, opal and filter transmissions are combined when high accuracy is needed (example: window 91.5%, opal 40%, filter 56%, combined T = 20.5%).
- The method weights responses by the relative energy distribution of the source. Results are expressed as percentages of the total photosynthetically available energy remaining at each depth. For a blue filter transmitting x% of a waveband containing y% of the energy, xy% of the total remains.

**Surface and submerged photometer errors**
- Immersion alters photometer optics. Photometers matched in air may not match when one is submerged. Semi-logarithmic plots of uncorrected readings show an apparent steeper slope in the first 10 cm.
- The [[Opal Diffusing Disc]] corrects the cosine response of the photometer. A clear glass window does not.
- Dry rough opals follow the cosine law better than polished opals. Wetting largely removes this advantage.
- Worked example (Fig. 3): dry opal emergent light 60%, submerged opal 47%. The submerged photometer reads 22% less than the surface photometer (78 versus 100). The implied correction factor is 1.19 (93/78).
- Theoretically the factor can range from 0.92 to 1.78, depending on refractive index and opal albedo.
- The [[Immersion Effect]] can be measured with a blackened funnel holding at least 0.9 R of pure water above the photometer. Surface reflection can be calculated from Fresnel's law.
- Slope-based estimates of attenuation are possible without a correction factor, provided the first reading is deep enough for the immersion effect to become constant. The line intercept below 100% gives a measure of the immersion effect.

**Other instruments and methods**
- Transmittance meters measure beam attenuation in situ and reveal stratification of colour and turbidity. Laboratory spectrophotometers are unreliable for turbid water because of settling and scattered-energy losses.
- The [[Secchi Disc]] is widely used for transparency. Results have general significance only when a standard technique is strictly followed.
- Integrating irradiance over time is difficult. The Siemens electrolytic recorder was the most popular method, and a capacitor discharge counter has been used underwater.

**Overall conclusions**
- Use energy units with spectral detail, not photometric units, for ecological irradiance.
- Use [[Absorption]] and [[Attenuation]] with the qualifications set out above. State the logarithm base.
- Use optical density for combining component transmissions.
- Correct for immersion effects, or use slope-based methods, when comparing surface and underwater photometers.
