# Refuge Management for the Threatened Railroad Valley Springfish in Nevada

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Goodeidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study documents an 11-year (1978–1989) case history of a refuge (introduced) population of the [[Railroad Valley Springfish]] (*Crenichthys nevadae*) at [[Chimney Hot Springs]], Nevada, established to buffer the species against extinction risk from habitat degradation at its native spring localities. Approximately 100 fish were transplanted from [[Big Spring]] in 1978 into artificial pools constructed along the springs' outflow. The population thrived until a stochastic spring-failure event dried the habitat in summer 1981, extirpating the population. A second introduction (50 fish) in 1982 reestablished the species, which grew to an estimated 1,881 adults by 1985. In 1988, human-caused removal of small rock dams altered pool hydrology, raising water temperatures above the species' thermal tolerance and forcing most fish to disperse downstream outside the refuge boundary; cattle trespass compounded habitat damage. Restoration of the rock dams in 1989 allowed partial recovery (836 adults). The authors argue that refuge populations of threatened fishes require continuous, active maintenance of water supply and habitat structure, frequent monitoring, and protection from stochastic environmental events (drought, seismic activity) and anthropogenic disturbance (vandalism, livestock trespass) to ensure long-term persistence.

## Background & Description

- **Total length (TL):** Subsample of 267 trapped adult fish on 4 May 1985 ranged from **29–65 mm** (mean 41.2 mm).
- **Juveniles/young-of-year:** Observed at approximately **12 mm** TL along shallow pool margins, too small to be captured in minnow traps.
- **Population age structure:** Presence of multiple size classes and observed young suggested an established population comprising **perhaps three generations** by 1985.
- **Marking method:** Fish were distinguished by pool of origin using different **caudal fin clips** to track inter-pool movement.
- Species is a member of [[Goodeidae]] (livebearing goodeids), notable among North American desert fishes for **extreme thermal tolerance**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Type:** Endemic to isolated thermal desert springs in [[Railroad Valley]], Nevada — a classic Great Basin relictual aquatic system analogous to habitats of the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] and [[Gila Topminnow]].

**Native Range:** Seven thermal springs across two disjunct areas:
- Duckwater area (northern valley): [[Big Warm Spring]], [[Little Warm Spring]]
- Lockes Ranch (~43 km south of Duckwater): North, Hay Corral, Reynolds 1, Reynolds 2, and Big springs

**Thermal Tolerance (a defining ecological trait):**
- Coolest water temperature at which fish were still common: **36.0°C** (pool 1 margin, 1985)
- Fish captured and survived in traps at **37.5°C**
- Fish observed briefly entering water as hot as **40.2°C**
- Highest temperature recorded in native habitat: **38°C** (Big Spring)
- Upper lethal/avoidance threshold demonstrated in 1988: pools at 43–57°C forced complete evacuation/mortality
- Fish exhibited **behavioral thermoregulation**, moving between pools of differing temperature and burrowing into cooler substrate (as low as 30.7–35.8°C in substrate versus 33.8–38.4°C in overlying water) during peak daytime heating.

**Refuge Habitat Structure:** Three artificial, interconnected pools along the outflow of Chimney Hot Springs' main spring, cooling progressively downstream:
- Source spring discharge: ~66°C
- Pool 1 inflow: 50–55°C
- Pool 2 inflow: 35–40°C
- Pool 3 inflow: 23–31°C
- Springwater chemistry: specific conductance 640–763 µS/cm; pH 7.4–7.5
- Small rock dams were critical to impounding water and allowing sufficient cooling; their removal in 1988 caused lethal overheating.

**Threats to Native Populations:**
- Channelization and diversion of springflows
- Livestock grazing/trampling of riparian and spring habitat
- Invasive species: [[Guppies]] (*Poecilia reticulata*) and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), the latter escaped from an aquaculture facility at Big Warm Spring and spread through the native spring system, displacing springfish
- Oil field development in Railroad Valley (Eagle Springs, Trap Spring, Bacon Flat fields) raising concerns about groundwater/aquifer impacts

**Co-occurring Introduced Refuge Sites:** [[Sodaville Hot Spring]] (Mineral County) and [[Old Dugan Ranch]] (Hot Creek Canyon, Nye County), both established by Nevada Department of Wildlife as additional refuge populations.

## Key Findings

1. **Successful initial establishment:** ~100 fish transplanted from Big Spring to Chimney Hot Springs on 25 October 1978 dispersed and thrived throughout the three artificial pools.

2. **Stochastic extirpation event (1981):** A natural decline in spring discharge (possibly linked to drought or seismic activity along the valley's fault system) caused the habitat to dry completely by late July/early August 1981, extirpating the refuge population. Nearby native springs also dried during this period, suggesting a regional hydrological event.

3. **Successful reintroduction (1982):** A second stocking of 50 fish reestablished a self-sustaining population after springflow stabilized.

4. **Robust population growth (1985):** Petersen mark-recapture estimate yielded **1,881 adults** (95% CI: 1,647–2,115). Catch rates varied strongly by pool: 1.5, 17.7, and 7.1 fish/trap-hour in pools 1, 2, and 3 respectively — indicating pool 1 (hottest) supported far fewer fish than cooler pools 2 and 3.

5. **Behavioral thermoregulation confirmed:** Fish shifted among pools (e.g., occupying only pools 2 and 3 in 1986–1987 when pool 1 became too hot) and sought cooler subsurface substrate, demonstrating fine-scale thermal habitat selection.

6. **Anthropogenic disturbance (1988):** Cattle breached the exclosure fence on two occasions (2 March and 29 May 1988), trampling habitat and degrading water quality. Concurrently, removal of rock dams (likely by trespass cattle or unauthorized human interference) eliminated pool impoundment, causing water temperatures to spike to 57.0°C (pool 1), 45.2°C (pool 2), and 43.1°C (pool 3) — all exceeding the species' survivable range.

7. **Mass dispersal/near-extirpation (1988):** No live fish were found in the pools on 25 June 1988; survivors fled up to **490 m downstream**, with the majority found **>289 m** downstream, outside the refuge fence, where erosion-created vertical drops prevented their return upstream.

8. **Partial recovery (1989):** Reconstruction of rock dams restored suitable temperatures; population rebounded to an estimated **836 adults** (95% CI: 689–983) by 28 June 1989, with catch rates of 0.8, 7.1, and 3.6 fish/trap-hour in pools 1–3.

9. **Comparative context:** Similar water-delivery vulnerabilities have jeopardized refuge populations of the [[Gila Topminnow]] (Arizona) and the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (Hoover Dam Refugium), reinforcing that refuges dependent on manual or vulnerable water-control infrastructure have poor long-term prognosis for rare fish conservation.

10. **Stochastic risk generalization:** Documented cases of refuge failure in other rare desert fishes — [[Mohave Tui Chub]] (flood-caused failures at Barstow Way Station and Piute Creek) and [[Woundfin]] (flood destroyed Hassayampa River population, 1972) — support the conclusion that refuge populations should only be considered "established" after surviving at least one extreme stochastic environmental event with successful recruitment.

11. **Management recommendations:**
    - Conduct **annual monitoring** of population and habitat at all Railroad Valley springfish refuge sites (Chimney Hot Springs, Sodaville, Old Dugan Ranch).
    - Assess **genetic variability** between native (Duckwater vs. Lockes Ranch) and introduced populations to detect founder effects, genetic drift, or bottleneck impacts from the 1988 decline.
    - Establish a **separate refuge population** specifically representing the genetically distinct Duckwater-area fish, given ongoing threats (habitat loss, invasive species competition) to that source population.
    - Continue Bureau of Land Management efforts to prevent cattle trespass and maintain rock-dam pool structures to buffer thermal extremes.
    - Recognize that **long-term survival cannot be guaranteed** even under active management, due to inherent stochastic environmental variation.
