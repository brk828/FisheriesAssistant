# Re-establishing Lost Wildlife: Inter-State Trade in Restored Species

#wildlife-conservation #reintroduction #mammalia #aves

## Executive Summary

This 1985 newspaper feature, by Larry Green, describes a growing [[Wildlife Translocation]] trade among U.S. states and with Canada. States exchange animals such as [[River Otter]], [[Wild Turkey]] and [[Pine Marten]] to rebuild populations of species lost to habitat loss, development, overtrapping, pollution and [[Pesticide Impacts]]. The article covers the rise of taxpayer-funded [[Non-Game Species]] restoration programs, the ecological and aesthetic rationale for reintroduction, and local opposition. It is journalistic and contains no original research data, so the figures below are reported as stated by the sources quoted.

## Background & Description

The article reports that states have accelerated programs to return wildlife to habitats they were forced to leave. Examples cited include [[Bighorn Sheep]] in Oregon, [[Beaver]] in Ohio and [[Peregrine Falcon]] in California.

Trades reported in the article include:
- Wisconsin and Colorado: river otters for pine martens.
- Michigan: 145 wild turkeys bartered for 29 moose from Canada.
- Minnesota: black bears and ruffed grouse for wild turkeys, and fishers for a species "to be named later."
- Iowa: 240 wild turkeys for 120 river otters.
- Montana: surplus grizzly bears offered for exchange, with no takers.

Other species mentioned in the trade include [[Snowshoe Hare]], elk, ruffed grouse and trumpeter swans. Officials compared the trading to baseball cards. When states cannot trade, biologists obtain eggs or young from breeding programs and zoos.

Species profiles from the article:
- **Peregrine falcon:** Nearly wiped out in the 1950s by DDT. Eggs sell for up to $2,000 each. Reintroduction is under way in cities such as St. Louis, Boston and Baltimore, and in rural Minnesota. Michigan's last record was in 1957.
- **River otter:** Eliminated early in the century by overtrapping and water pollution. Iowa's experiment showed otters could survive in the Des Moines River, where they were last seen in 1913.
- **Trumpeter swan:** The largest waterfowl native to North America. Nearly disappeared in the late 1800s through hunting for food and plumage. Restoration is intended partly to displace the non-native [[Mute Swan]].
- **Mute swan:** Described as aggressive. It drives out other species and uproots vegetation. A boater drowned last year after a mute swan attack, according to Michigan's Robert Hess.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Reintroduction is motivated by aesthetic, environmental and ecological reasons. Officials argue that species once native to an area should be restored, because "critters kept the pests down and nature in balance." Restoring a species is also described as protecting habitat for other species: "if you save a habitat for one you save a habitat for both," said Carrol L. Henderson.

The article links certain species to water quality. Otters are described as indicators of "good water quality." Michigan's fisher releases were meant to help control a rapidly expanding [[Porcupine]] population.

Non-native or unwanted species also feature. Mute swans are described as displacing muskrat and damaging vegetation. Michigan is studying the possibility of releasing [[Wolverine]] in the Upper Peninsula, though some question whether the species ever occurred there.

Local opposition is part of the context. Wyoming ranchers forced a delay in plans to restore [[Gray Wolf]] to [[Yellowstone National Park]]. Eight timber wolves released in Michigan a few years earlier were killed by local residents.

## Key Findings

- **Growth of state programs:** Over the eight years before 1985, the number of states with taxpayer-supported non-game restoration programs rose from none to 33. Funding comes largely from state residents who divert part of their state income tax refunds to these programs. The article describes this as "millions of dollars."
- **Michigan's activity:** In November 1985, Michigan released 50 pine martens and 10 fishers. Its first trumpeter swan release was scheduled for the following spring, and it is studying peregrine falcon releases.
- **Species-specific outcomes:** Peregrine falcon numbers were reduced by DDT, and the species is now being reintroduced to urban and rural sites. River otters were restored in Iowa after a summer experiment. Trumpeter swans are being restored across the Midwest.
- **Policy approach:** Minnesota's wildlife official said that "if we give a state anything we expect something in return," which reflects the reciprocal nature of the trades.
- **Unresolved questions:** The feasibility of a wolverine release in Michigan remains uncertain because its historical presence in the state is unclear.
