# Effects of Five Diets on the Growth and Mortality of Fry to Fingerling Channel Catfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictalurus-punctatus

## Executive Summary

This preliminary hatchery study tested five formulated and commercial diets (A to E) on [[Channel Catfish]] (*[[Ictalurus punctatus]]*) fry from the swim-up stage to fingerling size, during July 22 to September 16, 1957. The work was run by the [[Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Commission]] to support production of fingerlings for stocking [[Farm Ponds]] and state lakes. Diets were compared on livability, weight gain, feed efficiency and cost per pound of fish produced. The main results were that trout-chow-only diets had poorer survival, that a high-protein dry-ingredient diet (Diet D) gave the best weight gain, and that Diet B gave the lowest cost per pound of gain.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Produce large numbers of channel catfish fingerlings. Kansas workers had propagated the species artificially since the early 1920s.
- **Historical contributors:** The paper credits John Murphree, Seth L. Way and others. Way designed a channel catfish incubator now used by state and private hatcheries.
- **Rationale:** The author found no literature giving the dietary requirements for rearing channel catfish fry to fingerling size, so feeding trials with available feeds were necessary.
- **Report type:** A preliminary report published in the *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science* (Vol. 61, No. 3, 1958) by Joseph L. Gray, formerly Fish Culturist with the commission.
- **Page 1 content:** The first page is a fragment of an unrelated reference list on forage plants, grazing and carbohydrate reserves (for example, works by Graber, McCarty and Hanson). It is not part of the catfish study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rearing system**
- Troughs were made of 20-gauge galvanized tin: four troughs of about 7 ft 11.5 in (Nos. 1, 2, 3, 5), one of 8 ft 1.25 in (No. 4), all about 6 in water depth.
- Water flowed by gravity from a rearing pond, which drew from the [[Ninnescah River]] supplying the whole hatchery. Each trough received about 10 gallons per minute (0.022 CFS) through a gate valve.
- Fry absorbed their yolk sacs over 5 to 7 days before feeding began. They then rose to the surface to take floating feed.

**Stock and crowding**
- Roughly 28,365 unstocked fry (6.1 lb) remained after the 1957 spawning season. This was too few to fill a rearing pond, which normally holds 100,000 fry.
- Starting stocks were 9,765 fry (2.1 lb) in Trough 1, and 9,300 fry (2.0 lb) each in Troughs 2 and 3.
- Crowding forced fry transfers to other troughs at the ends of weeks 2, 3 and 4. Surplus fry were moved to rearing pond No. 48 in week 5.

**Water temperature**
- Weekly maxima ran from about 81°F to 90°F, and minima from about 68°F to 80°F.
- The author judged the optimum to be from the mid-70s to the upper 80s °F. Warmer water made the fry more voracious.

**Feeding behaviour**
- Fry were fed four times daily (9:00 a.m., 1:00, 5:00 and 10:00 p.m.). Feed floated, so uneaten amounts could be measured.
- Fry were most active at night. Feed collected on the outlet screens during the day and was devoured as darkness approached. The 10:00 p.m. feeding was the most intense.

**Beef liver**
- Fresh beef liver (1 lb per week, Fridays) sank rather than floated. Troughs receiving liver had screens clogged with fecal waste for two days afterward.

## Key Findings

### Diets tested

| Diet | Composition (plus 1 lb beef liver weekly unless noted) |
|---|---|
| A | 100% [[Purina Trout Chow]] (liver) |
| B | 50% [[Dry Buttermilk]], 50% [[Meat and Bone Scraps]] (liver) |
| C | 100% Purina Trout Chow (no liver) |
| D | 50% Trout Chow, 25% Dry Buttermilk, 25% Meat and Bone Scraps (liver) |
| E | 50% Dry Buttermilk, 50% [[Digester Tankage]] (liver) |

Feed costs per 100 lb were about $4.44 (digester tankage), $5.00 (meat and bone scraps), $7.00 (dry buttermilk), $10.00 (beef liver) and $11.00 (trout chow). Crude protein was about 60% for digester tankage, 50% for meat and bone scraps, 31% for dry buttermilk, 25% for trout chow and 20.2% for beef liver.

### Mortality (see [[Mortality Rate]])

- **All seven weeks, Diets A, B and C:** Mortality was A 4.51% (127 dead), B 3.67% (95 dead), C 5.15% (129 dead). Differences were significant (χ² = 6.337, df = 2, 0.04 < P < 0.05).
- Pooling the two trout-chow diets (A and C) against the rest was significant (χ² = 5.827, P < 0.05). Pooling the two liver diets (A and B) against the rest was not (χ² = 3.226, 0.05 < P < 0.10). The author concluded that poor survival came from the trout chow itself, not from the lack of liver.
- **Weeks 3 to 7, Diets A to D:** Mortality was A 1.79%, B 1.93%, C 2.17%, D 2.09%. No significant difference was found (χ² = 1.809, P ≈ 0.72 to 0.73 range as reported).
- **Weeks 4 to 7, all five diets:** Mortality was A 1.64%, B 0.61%, C 1.76%, D 1.33%, E 0.33%. The difference was highly significant (χ² = 38.590, df = 4, P < 0.001). Pooling trout-chow diets against the high-protein diets was also highly significant (χ² = 34.682, P < 0.001), so high-protein diets gave better livability from week 4 onward.

### Weight gain (see [[Randomized Complete Block Design]])

Weeks were used as blocks to remove the normal week-to-week growth variation.

- **All seven weeks, Diets A, B and C:** No significant difference (F = 0.324, not significant).
- **Weeks 3 to 7, four diets:** Mean gains were A 1.80 lb, C 1.90 lb, B 2.08 lb and D 3.60 lb. Diet D gave significantly higher gain (F = 44.037, P < 0.001; [[Duncan's Multiple Range Test]] P < 0.01).
- **Weeks 4 to 7, five diets:** Mean gains were E 1.55 lb, A 1.62 lb, C 1.75 lb, B 1.78 lb and D 3.52 lb. Diet D was again significantly superior (F = 36.000, P < 0.001; Duncan P < 0.05).

### Feed efficiency

No significant differences in [[Feed Conversion Efficiency]] were found among diets in any of the three time periods.

### Cost per pound of gain (see [[Cost Per Pound Gain]])

- **All seven weeks:** Mean costs were B $0.26, A $0.38 and C $0.51 per lb of gain. Diet B was significantly cheaper than A or C, and A was significantly cheaper than C (F = 11.231, P < 0.01).
- **Weeks 3 to 7:** Mean costs were B $0.31, D $0.36, A $0.46 and C $0.62. Diet C was significantly the most expensive. Diets B and D did not differ significantly.
- **Weeks 4 to 7:** Mean costs were B $0.35, D $0.41, A $0.51, E $0.54 and C $0.69. Diet B was significantly cheaper than E or C. Diet D was significantly cheaper than C. Diets A, E and C did not differ significantly.

### Conclusions (as stated by the author)

1. Livability was relatively poor on a diet of trout chow alone.
2. Mortality did not differ among Diets A to D from weeks 3 to 7.
3. From weeks 4 to 7, diets with high protein content gave significantly better livability.
4. Weight gains did not differ among Diets A, B and C over the full seven weeks.
5. From weeks 3 to 7, Diet D gave better gain than A, B or C. It also did so from weeks 4 to 7.
6. Feed efficiency did not differ among diets.
7. Diet B had the lowest cost per pound of gain. Diet A was cheaper than Diet C throughout.
8. Diet C had the highest cost per pound of gain from weeks 3 to 7.

### Limitations

The study was described as preliminary. Fry were moved between troughs for crowding, which changed stocking densities during the trial. Body chemistry was not analysed.
