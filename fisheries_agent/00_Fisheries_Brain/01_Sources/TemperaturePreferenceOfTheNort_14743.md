# Temperature Preference of the Northern Redbelly Dace (*Phoxinus eos*)

## Executive Summary
This study by Stauffer, Melisky, and Hocutt (1980) examined the thermal preference behavior of the [[Northern Redbelly Dace]] (*Phoxinus eos*), an important freshwater forage fish, using a horizontal thermal gradient apparatus. Fish were collected in Michigan and acclimated to six temperatures (6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 33°C). The relationship between acclimation temperature (A) and preferred temperature (P) was modeled quadratically: **P = 1.152A − 0.017A² + 7.03** (R² = 0.53, N = 720). Solving for the point where P = A yielded a **final temperature preferendum of 25.3°C**. This work contributes to the broader understanding of [[Temperature Preference]] behavior in forage fishes, which is critical for assessing thermal effluent impacts under environmental regulations, and complements physiological data on [[Thermal Tolerance]] in cyprinids.

## Background & Description
The source text does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of *Phoxinus eos*. The paper is behavioral/physiological in focus. Key biological notes include:
- Classified as a minnow, genus *Chrosomus*/*Phoxinus* (Cyprinidae)
- Recognized as a forage fish species of ecological importance (Scott & Crossman, 1973)

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Geographic range**: Nova Scotia south to Pennsylvania, west to Colorado and British Columbia (Scott & Crossman, 1973)
- **Habitat**: Cold-water tributary streams; specimens collected from Spratt Creek, a tributary of the [[Thunder Bay System]], Lake Huron
- **Trophic role**: Important forage fish species, occupying a prey position in freshwater food webs; ichthyofauna (predator species) often reside at the thermal preferenda of their prey rather than their own optimal temperatures (Fairbanks et al. 1971; Neill & Magnuson 1974)
- **Thermal ecology context**: Understanding forage fish thermal preferences is critical to interpreting the distribution of predatory sport/commercial fish species, since predator habitat selection is frequently constrained by prey thermal preference rather than the predator's own preferendum
- Related comparative species include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Yellow Perch]], all previously studied for thermal preference but representing sport/commercial rather than forage fish

## Key Findings
- **Acclimation-Preference Relationship**: At low acclimation temperatures (6–18°C), dace preferred temperatures *higher* than acclimation temperature; at 24°C acclimation, preferred temperature matched acclimation (24°C); at high acclimation temperatures (30–33°C), preferred temperature was *lower* than acclimation temperature.
- **Best-fit model**: Quadratic regression outperformed linear and cubic models:
  $$P = 1.152A - 0.017A^2 + 7.03$$
  explaining 53% of variance in preferred temperature (N = 720 observations).
- **Final Temperature Preferendum**: Calculated at **25.3°C** (per Fry's 1947 method, solving for P = A).
- **Lethal thermal limits**: 
  - Tyler (1966) reported 50% mortality in fish acclimated to 24°C when exposed to 33°C under summer conditions.
  - In the current study, all specimens died when acclimation was attempted above 33°C (even at a gradual rate of 0.5°C/day), suggesting an upper acclimation limit near this threshold.
  - Tyler (1966) also found seasonal variation in lethal temperature tolerance — summer-collected dace exhibited higher lethal temperatures than winter-collected dace, even at equivalent acclimation temperatures.
- **Seasonal effects on preference**: Melsky & Gift (1971) previously found some species show higher temperature preference during falling (autumn) vs. rising (spring) temperature regimes; however, Cherry et al. (1977) found this seasonal difference disappeared for 6 of 9 species when acclimation periods were extended in the lab — suggesting acclimation duration may override seasonal collection effects.
- **Methodological note**: Preferred temperature was determined using the thermistor probe nearest the fish's head, based on Mueller's (1977) finding that head thermal history determines preference behavior, not whole-body position in the gradient.
- **Applied significance**: Establishing thermal preferenda for forage species like *P. eos* supports more accurate environmental impact assessments related to thermal effluent discharge, addressing a documented gap in knowledge about forage fish thermal criteria relative to well-studied sport/commercial species (Gift 1977).
