# Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center Annual Report, Fiscal Year 1994

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (Dexter NFH & TC), Dexter, New Mexico, covers Fiscal Year 1994. The station maintained, cultured and distributed imperiled fishes of the American Southwest and northwestern Mexico, with 14 threatened or endangered species held during the year. Major activities included spawning of [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] for cooperating agencies; cryopreservation and genetic survey work; continued [[Woundfin]] and [[Chihuahua Chub]] recovery support; and unsuccessful attempts to spawn [[Yaqui Catfish]] and [[Warner Sucker]]. The report also details the station's water-rights transition after artesian well failure, pond lining, construction of a new Wet Lab, staffing changes, funding requests for FY 95 and FY 96, and the station's public outreach.

## Background & Description

The station sits on 640 acres in the Pecos River valley of southeastern New Mexico, about 200 miles southeast of Albuquerque. It was established under the White Act of 1931 and began operations in 1932. Its mission was broadened after the 1973 [[Endangered Species Act]] assigned the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] responsibility for imperiled fishes. Three original goals were set: establish a refuge for critically imperiled fishes, implement studies and culture development, and produce fish for reintroduction. Station objectives were later expanded to seven, including maintaining genetically sound broodstocks, supplying live and preserved specimens, sharing expertise, and publishing findings.

Dexter was designated a Technology Center in FY 91, one of only six in the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. In FY 93 a geneticist, Dr. J. Holt Williamson, was hired and reported for duty on December 2, 1993.

Physical and water characteristics reported:
- Elevation about 3,500 ft; average rainfall about 12 inches; growing season 180–200 days.
- Five shallow aquifer wells (150 ft deep) with a combined pumping capacity of 2,500 gpm.
- Well water at a constant 64°F, alkaline, pH 7.5–8.5, total hardness averaging 2,100 mg/l, and total dissolved solids of 3,500 mg/l.
- Culture facilities: four 6' x 40' raceways, ten 3' x 10' fiberglass circular tanks, and 45 earthen ponds totaling 22 surface acres. Thirteen ponds were lined with Hypalon in FY 93, and ten more were lined with polypropylene in FY 94.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report frames the station's work around the decline of Southwestern freshwater ecosystems. Dams and diversions have altered many rivers in this arid region, and native fishes have declined in numbers and range, often unintentionally. Of the roughly 500 freshwater fish species in the United States, 101 are listed as threatened or endangered, and 31 of these occur in Region 2. Nearly all species held at Dexter are native to New Mexico or adjacent states and Mexico (Chihuahua and Sonora), which the report describes as a unique concentration of rare fishes at one locality.

Water rights are a central concern. The station holds shallow groundwater rights dated 1931–1933 totaling 1,795 acre-feet per year, plus artesian rights of 318 acre-feet. The artesian well (1,000 ft deep) collapsed in FY 91, and a water trade was arranged with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], under which the Bureau purchased 390 acre-feet of shallow water in exchange for the artesian right. The final usable quantity was not yet known. Pending state lawsuits involving Texas and the Mescalero Apache Tribe could threaten junior rights dated after the mid-1940s.

Ecosystem and recovery team participation included the Upper/Middle Rio Grande Ecosystem Team (including the Mimbres River and Guzman basins), the Upper and Lower Colorado River basins, the San Juan, Pecos, and Gila/Salt/Verde systems, and several recovery teams (for example, [[Apache Trout]] and [[Gila Trout]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] and [[Woundfin]] teams, and the Warner Sucker and Upper Klamath Basin Sucker working groups). The station reported limited financial capacity to participate fully in these teams.

## Key Findings

**Station operations and personnel**
- The FY 94 staff comprised eight PFT employees, including a Center Director, Assistant Director, Geneticist, Fishery Biologist, two Maintenance Men, a Biological Technician and an Office Assistant.
- Longtime employee Troy Winham retired May 3, 1994. Dr. Williamson's position was filled, and the vacancy created by Winham's retirement was not refilled because of buyout costs.

**Genetics, captive breeding and cryopreservation**
- Genetic management guidelines were initiated for Region 2 endangered fishes, modeled on Region 5 guidelines.
- [[Bonytail Chub]]: 28 unique family lots were created from paired matings of 28 males and 28 females, following genetic management guidelines.
- [[Cryopreservation]] protocols and a sperm repository were developed for [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]], with sperm from about 50 Lake Mohave razorback suckers collected and stored at Louisiana State University.
- Genetic surveys covered [[Gila Trout]] and [[Apache Trout]] (a $25,000 contract with Genetic Analysis, Smithville, Texas) and basin-wide surveys of razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish using allozyme and DNA analyses.

**Razorback Sucker**
- 17 females were spawned, yielding about 1.48 million eggs (mean 87,268 per female), with 178,545 swimup fry from the first group at 12.68% survival.
- A second spawn of 5 females and 10 males on April 8, 1994 produced 424,195 eggs and 74,908 swimup fry (17.6% survival).
- 1,289 adults weighing a combined 1,624 pounds were stocked in the Salt River, Arizona.
- Because of pond-lining work, no 1994 year class was reared for growout.

**Colorado Squawfish**
- Four females (1974 year class) were spawned on June 1, 1994, yielding 327,233 eggs (mean 81,808 per female) fertilized with eight males.
- Due to hatchery demolition, spawning was conducted in outdoor raceways. No 1994 year class was reared.

**Bonytail Chub**
- Spawning on April 27, 1994 with 28 females and 28 males yielded 1,245,496 eggs (mean 44,482 per female). Swimup fry totaled 254,500 (reported 30.88% egg-to-swimup survival).
- About 400 domestic brood fish and about 10,000 1994 year-class fish were held for FY 95 stocking in Lake Mohave.

**Warner Sucker**
- Twenty fish were held after Lernaea (anchor worm) infestation, which was controlled by hand-picking. All 20 fish spawned in May 1994 and eight females were injected with CG; no fertilization occurred.
- All 20 fish died during summer 1994 with no clear cause. The report suspects high pond temperatures near 90°F, and states that no stressed or dead fish were observed.

**Woundfin**
- In May 1993 a 0.2-acre Hypalon-lined pond was stocked with Dexter-produced fish that were two years old. Spawning occurred and many fry were observed.
- Recovery in August and October 1993 yielded 80 adults and 4,000 young-of-year (3,859 recovered in October); 3,208 fingerlings were distributed in FY 94.
- Dr. Steven Vives (Georgia Southern University) received 100 young-of-year fish for temperature and photoperiod experiments. The station held 997 fish (115 '92 year class adults, 323 '93 year class adults, 559 '94 year class juveniles).

**Chihuahua Chub**
- 450 fish averaging 5–6 inches were stocked in McKnight Creek (Mimbres River) in October 1992. Spring 1993 sampling indicated they overwintered well and survived a high-magnitude flood.
- No spawning or stocking occurred in FY 93 or FY 94; stockings were expected FY 95–FY 99. 149 adults were held. The captive population derives from only ten fish collected in 1979; the report recommends adding wild stock.

**Pahranagat Roundtail Chub and Virgin River Roundtail Chub**
- Pahranagat roundtail chub (held for Region 1): a leaking pond led to losses; 10 females produced 51,350 eggs in April 1993, and 21,800 fry were stocked. The station ended FY 94 with 101 fish.
- Virgin River roundtail chub: no spawning was intentionally carried out; 94 adults were moved for overwintering. The species may be removed from inventory in 1995 because of recovery.

**Yaqui Catfish**
- Of 201 surviving wild-collected fish tested by morphometric and electrophoretic analysis, 152 were pure Yaqui catfish, 30 were hybrid intergrades with [[Channel Catfish]], 5 were hybrid backcrosses, and 14 were undetermined.
- At least 112 pure fish are held; 108 are the minimum required for return to Mexico. 31 pure fish were delivered to the Universidad Autonoma de Nuevo Leon in June 1992.
- Spawning trials in 1993 (paired pens, raceways, and open ponds with HCG and carp pituitary) and 1994 (open pond at Dexter; Uvalde NFH trials at various temperatures) produced no spawns of Yaqui catfish. Channel catfish at Uvalde spawned at 35–48 hours after injection.
- Collecting trips to the Rio Bavispe drainage of Sonora were made in August–September and October 1994 to plan broader genetic sampling.

**Desert Pupfish, Leon Springs Pupfish, Gila Topminnow, Big Bend Gambusia**
- Desert pupfish and Leon Springs pupfish maintained good populations, with about 3,000 of each transferred to winter ponds. Leon Springs pupfish were supplied to EPA Gulf Breeze, Florida, and 25 to the University of Arkansas.
- The Sharps Spring Gila topminnow stock (over 3,000 fish) was held for reintroduction. 25 were provided to the University of Arkansas.
- Big Bend gambusia overwintered poorly outdoors; about 3,000 were moved to a new pond and about 250 were kept indoors in aquaria.

**Fish health**
- Yaqui catfish carried a low level of yellow grub, which is manually removed; the parasite cannot complete its life cycle at the station because the intermediate snail host is absent.
- The chief problem is recurrent introduction of anchor worm (Lernaea) on fish from the wild. The report recommends close health review of new introductions until FDA drug and chemical regulations are clarified.

**Funding, facilities and maintenance**
- Congress appropriated about $1.4 million in FY 93 for design, planning and construction of the new Wet Lab, administrative complex, genetics lab and water quality lab. The Wet Lab was awarded April 1994; the old administrative building was demolished July 18, 1994, and staff relocated to temporary offices.
- Maintenance Management System accomplishments in FY 94 totaled $208,013, including well and pump maintenance, a new drain line, tank replacement, asbestos removal, re-roofing, and an upgrade of the raceway power system.
- Remediation of an old underground fuel tank was initiated after contamination was found; a FY 95 contract was awarded to complete the work.
- FY 96 funding request: $520,000 and 9.5 FTEs for permanent operations, with a genetics laboratory start-up request of $125,000 and about $1.0–2.0 million sought for remaining development.

**Outreach and public relations**
- Just over 2,500 visitors toured the station, including school and civic groups. Three National Fishing Week events drew 229 children and 128 adults.
- Two high-school mentorship students worked with staff for nine months.
- A vandalism incident in October 1993 destroyed a visitor-center aquarium and took Yaqui catfish; no arrest was made.
