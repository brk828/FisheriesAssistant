## Executive Summary

The document is a 1999 pre-proposal to the W.M. Keck Foundation requesting seed funding to establish the **Keck Biodiversity Observatory** at Arizona State University (ASU), linking a field station ([[Sierra Ancha Field Station]]) with a new centralized molecular biology laboratory. The stated goal is to merge "macro" (ecological/evolutionary/field-based) and "micro" (molecular/genetic) approaches to understand how habitat fragmentation — both natural (geological, evolutionary timescale) and anthropogenic (urban, ecological timescale) — drives extinction and speciation dynamics that structure biodiversity. The proposal explicitly leverages the NSF-funded [[Central Arizona-Phoenix Long-Term Ecological Research]] (CAP LTER) program, one of only two urban LTER sites in the US and the only one in an arid environment, and highlights the [[Sierra Ancha Experimental Forest]] watershed as a natural, minimally disturbed "source" area supplying water to the Phoenix metropolitan area — thereby providing a natural-to-urban aquatic/ecological gradient for comparative study. A second, unrelated section of the document proposes a separate "Molecular Photonics Initiative" laboratory for biotechnical microsystems (nanofabrication, biomimetic photosynthetic devices), which contains no aquatic or ichthyological content.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although no specific fish taxa are discussed, the document frames a broader aquatic/terrestrial ecosystem context relevant to habitat and biodiversity study design:

- **[[Sierra Ancha Experimental Forest]]**: elevation range 3,500–7,200 ft, approximately 110 miles northeast of ASU; geologically and biologically diverse terrain (canyons, cliffs, mountains, alluvial valleys) supporting flora and fauna representative of multiple North American life zones.
- **Aquatic connectivity gradient**: The Sierra Ancha watershed supplies most of the water to the Phoenix metropolitan area, creating what the authors describe as "an aquatic gradient and connectivity from a natural 'source' area to one with increasing human activities" — i.e., a spatial continuum from undisturbed headwaters to a heavily urbanized downstream system ([[Central Arizona-Phoenix Long-Term Ecological Research]]).
- **Long-term monitoring baseline**: U.S. Forest Service data since the 1930s on weather and stream flow provide a long-term environmental dataset potentially relevant to hydrological and aquatic habitat change in the watershed.
- **Habitat fragmentation framework**: The proposal's conceptual model treats fragmentation (natural, e.g., mountain uplift or sea-level change; and anthropogenic, e.g., urbanization) as the key driver structuring population isolation, genetic divergence, and biodiversity outcomes across terrestrial and — by extension — aquatic systems within the watershed gradient.
- **Undisturbed control site function**: The Sierra Ancha Forest and Station are framed as a natural "control" against which effects of urban/suburban expansion in the [[Central Arizona-Phoenix Long-Term Ecological Research|CAP LTER]] study area can be compared, applicable to both terrestrial and aquatic biodiversity assessments.
