# Mitochondrial DNA Variability in Endangered Fishes: Razorback Sucker Hatchery Stocks and Sonoran Topminnow Lineages

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #poeciliopsis

## Executive Summary

This document is a two-paper compilation from *Conservation Biology* (Volume 10, No. 1, February 1996). The first paper (pp. 120–127; Dowling, Minckley, Marsh and Goldstein) examines mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) diversity in the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]*) in a source population at [[Lake Mohave]] and in three hatchery year classes. It concludes that the 1990 hatchery year class lost diversity and recommends that wild-caught larvae, not hatchery culture, be used to conserve genetic variation. The second paper (pp. 128–135; Quattro, Leberg, Douglas and Vrijenhoek) reports mtDNA variation in the endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*[[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]]*), finding no variation in the Gila River basin but substantial divergence in a population from the upper Yaqui River.

## Background & Description

### Razorback Sucker

The razorback sucker is a large, long-lived catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of western North America. Its populations collapsed largely from [[Recruitment Failure]] linked to human impacts, including habitat modification, water management and nonnative species. Adults are thought to average 25 to more than 40 years of age, and juveniles were virtually unknown when the paper was written. Federal listing as endangered occurred in 1991 (USFWS 1991).

Hatchery culture of the species began in 1981 at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center. The authors argue that hatchery programs designed for harvested sport fish, which select for rapid growth and high survivorship, conflict with the [[Captive Breeding]] goal of retaining [[Genetic Diversity]] for reintroduced fish.

### Sonoran Topminnow

The Sonoran topminnow is an endangered fish of the Sonoran Desert. The authors state that restoration of the species should rest on an understanding of evolutionary relationships among remaining natural populations. The species remains locally abundant in Sonora, Mexico, but its status in Arizona is the focus of the study.

### Methodological Background

mtDNA is maternally inherited and evolves rapidly in many vertebrates, making it a useful marker for population structure and maternal lineages. Both studies used [[Restriction Endonuclease Analysis]] to characterize mtDNA haplotypes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Razorback sucker habitat and history.** [[Lake Mohave]] is a mainstem impoundment of the Colorado River, created in the early 1950s by closure of Davis Dam. It has an upstream riverine reach about 60 km long grading into a lower basin about 48 km long (maximum width 6.4 km). The reservoir has no direct tributaries that supported razorback sucker populations before impoundment. Fish are concentrated in the upper part of the lower basin and become vulnerable to capture when aggregated near shore for spawning from late autumn through spring. The species made up 1.0–30% of net catches in spawning areas over the past 20 years.

**Population status.** The source population was estimated at approximately 55,000 individuals during sampling (1987–1989), presumably descended from residents present at impoundment. Estimates indicate fewer than 23,300 adults remained in 1993. Recruitment has been negligible since an initial population explosion, and no verified natural recruits were found among nearly 12,500 fish handled since 1974. The authors note that reservoir stocks elsewhere in the lower Colorado basin disappeared about 40 years after impoundment, suggesting that the Lake Mohave population could collapse at any time.

**Sonoran topminnow range.** Sampling covered Gila River basin populations in Arizona and several river systems in Sonora. A population inhabiting the upper reaches of the Río Yaqui in southeastern Arizona was genetically distinct from all others examined.

## Key Findings

### Razorback Sucker mtDNA Diversity (Dowling et al.)

- **Sample base:** 56 individuals analyzed with eight restriction enzymes, yielding about 160 fragments and 27 haplotypes. The source comprised 25 wild-caught fish from Lake Mohave (captured 1987–1989). Hatchery tissue came from three year classes produced at Dexter National Fish Hatchery: 1987 (n = 10), 1989 (n = 11) and 1990 (n = 10, sampled in 1990).
- **Source diversity:** The Lake Mohave sample had 17 haplotypes in 25 individuals, with 11 of 17 unique to a single fish. Haplotype diversity (h) was 0.97, which the authors describe as high compared with other vertebrates.
- **Hatchery year classes:**

| Year class | Haplotypes (individuals) | Haplotype diversity (h) |
|---|---|---|
| 1987 | 6 (10) | 0.89 |
| 1989 | 7 (11) | 0.91 |
| 1990 | 4 (10) | 0.71 |

- **Statistical test:** Resampling of the Lake Mohave sample (1000 replicates of 10 individuals) gave a mean of 0.91 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.80–0.98. Only the 1990 year class differed significantly from the source (only one of 1000 resamples gave a lower value). The authors note the test is conservative because the source sample is small.
- **Unique haplotypes:** The 1987 and 1989 classes carried four and six haplotypes not found in the source, while 1990 carried one.
- **Effective number of females:** Estimated at 23.8 (1987), 32.2 (1989) and 6.9 (1990), all appreciably smaller than the actual numbers of females used (55, 14 and 17, respectively).
- **Survivorship:** Survival from fertilized ova to swim-up was 11.1% in 1990 (n = 17), below the minimum of 22.5% (mean 40.1%, maximum 71.4%, n = 590 females) recorded for the seven preceding year classes.
- **Simulation:** Modeling showed that h is relatively insensitive to [[Broodstock Size]], levelling off near 0.95 at about 20 individuals, whereas the number of alleles increases with broodstock size. The authors therefore consider h a poor indicator of genetic change in hatchery stocks and the allele count a better measure.
- **Likely causes:** Reduced diversity in 1990 is attributed to an interaction between the small number of contributing females and offspring viability, possibly including inbreeding among related broodstock. Record-keeping problems, including mixed unmarked wild and hatchery fish, prevented a clear genealogy.

### Management Recommendations (Dowling et al.)

The authors compared three options for preserving genetic diversity:

1. **Standard hatchery propagation:** Not recommended as a primary strategy, because it trades innate genetic variability for numbers. Hatchery culture should nonetheless be maintained as a reserve broodstock.
2. **Natural spawning in predator-free bays:** Adults held in protected bays could spawn naturally, using different adults each year to maintain variability. Fewer fish are produced per year.
3. **Capture and rearing of wild larvae (preferred):** Reservoir-spawned larvae are collected and reared in protected sites until they reach a size likely to survive, which the authors expect will "almost certainly preserve the most variability."

Specific recommendations include replacing the current [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] broodstock with wild-caught individuals, not releasing current broodstock progeny to replace the Lake Mohave population, expanding the bay-culture program, and rearing as close to Lake Mohave as possible. The authors urge that all three options be kept open. They warn that delay may be fatal because the old wild adults are dying and the population is in precipitous decline.

### Sonoran Topminnow mtDNA Variation (Quattro et al.)

- **Gila River basin (Arizona):** No mtDNA diversity was detected within or among samples.
- **Sonoran river systems:** Considerable variation was found within and among populations from several river systems in Sonora.
- **Upper Yaqui River (Arizona):** A population inhabiting the upper reaches of the Río Yaqui in southeastern Arizona showed substantial divergence from all other populations examined.
- **Implications:** The authors discuss how this highly divergent population should inform the management of the topminnow in Arizona. They call for more detailed genetic and morphological studies to determine the distributional limits and specific status of this highly divergent form.

### Overall Conclusions

Both studies emphasize that conservation of endangered fishes must rest on the genetic structure of remaining natural populations. The razorback sucker study shows that production-oriented hatchery practices can erode mtDNA diversity, even when the number of fish produced is large. The topminnow study shows that a single divergent lineage can exist within a species' range and must be recognized in management.
