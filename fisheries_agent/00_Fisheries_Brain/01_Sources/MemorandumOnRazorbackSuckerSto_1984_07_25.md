## Executive Summary
This field memorandum documents a coordinated survey and stocking operation for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) conducted along the Gila River drainage in Arizona in July 1984. Personnel from [[Arizona Game and Fish]], [[Arizona State University]], and the [[Bureau of Land Management]] sampled multiple sites — [[Bonita Creek]], the [[Gila River]] near Safford-Morenci Bridge, [[Guthrie]], [[Apache Grove]], and [[Eagle Creek]] — to characterize baseline fish community composition prior to and during stocking of hatchery-reared razorback suckers. Notably, [[Gila robusta]] (roundtail chub) was recorded in the Gila River near Bonita Creek for the first time in at least 20 years. The memo also records rapid downstream displacement of stocked razorback suckers coinciding with a major flood pulse that destabilized the riverbed, and incidental reproductive data on flathead catfish.

## Background & Description
While this memo is primarily an operational field log rather than a morphometric study, several taxon-specific field observations are recorded:

- **Flathead Catfish** (*Pylodictis olivaris*): Individuals ranged from <0.5 lb to >14 lb, including a 12-inch specimen and several >5 lb fish. Gonadal inspection of four mature females found **three with fully developed ovaries "ready to spawn"** and one with undeveloped ovaries, indicating asynchronous reproductive timing within the population.
- **Channel Catfish** (*Ictalurus punctatus*): Recorded as relatively rare compared to flathead catfish at most sites, with catches ranging up to 14 lbs at some locations.
- **Common Carp** (*Cyprinus carpio*): Young-of-year (YOY) carp were unusually abundant across multiple reaches, roughly co-dominant with red shiners in seine catches.
- **Sonoran Sucker** (*Catostomus insignis*): Found preferentially in deep water along shaded cliff faces; generally low in abundance relative to cyprinids.
- **Desert Mountain-sucker** (*Pantosteus clarkii*): Captured only incidentally in trammel netting at Eagle Creek.

Biological samples (stomachs, gonads, spines) were collected from all captured catfish for downstream laboratory analysis (age, diet, reproductive state).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The survey captured a **snapshot of native and non-native fish assemblage structure** across a hydrologically dynamic desert river system, with strong site-to-site and day-to-day variability driven by flow and substrate conditions:

- **Bonita Creek / Gila River confluence area**: Mixed assemblage of YOY carp, red shiners (co-dominant), rare [[Longfin Dace]], rare [[Mosquitofish]] in channel (more common in backwaters), rare channel catfish, uncommon flathead catfish, and rare Sonoran sucker. First documented occurrence of *Gila robusta* in ~20 years signals a possible recovery or recolonization event.
- **Guthrie and Apache Grove sites**: Fish fauna very consistent with Bonita Creek reach; Apache Grove distinguished by presence of Sonoran suckers in deep, shaded cliff-pool microhabitat.
- **Eagle Creek (below Phelps-Dodge pumping plant)**: Turbid, high, rising flows following heavy rain. Trammel netting and gillnetting yielded large flathead and channel catfish, carp, Sonoran sucker, and desert mountain-sucker. Seining was less effective due to swift water and rough substrate; captured single red shiner, sparse longfin dace, channel catfish, carp, and Sonoran sucker.
- **Post-flood community shift**: Following a river rise of ~1.5 m between 18–23 July, fish fauna at the stocking sites showed a **substantial reduction in carp and red shiner abundance**, a **relative increase in longfin dace**, catfish becoming rare, mosquitofish disappearing from the channel (retained in backwaters), and Sonoran sucker becoming rare but still present — indicating rapid community reorganization in response to bottom destabilization and pool infilling ("sanding in").
- **Stocking-site hydraulics**: Newly stocked razorback suckers exhibited strong, rapid **downstream drift/movement** behavior immediately post-release, with no upstream movement observed — likely a passive or active response to elevated flow velocity and an unstable, resettling substrate.

## Key Findings
- **First Gila robusta record in ~20 years** in the Gila River near the mouth of Bonita Creek, suggesting a notable range/occurrence change for this native cyprinid.
- **Razorback sucker stocking events** occurred at Apache Grove (~0700 hrs) and the Safford-Morenci Bridge site (1015–1030 hrs) on 23 July 1984.
- **Rapid post-stocking downstream displacement**: Fish stocked at Apache Grove moved ~400 m downstream within 45 minutes; fish stocked at Safford-Morenci Bridge moved ~500 m downstream by 1300 hours (exact timing uncertain due to logistic delays). No upstream movement was detected at either site.
- **Major flood pulse** raised river levels ~1.5 m between 18 and 23 July, destabilizing the bottom substrate (soft, mobile) and filling pools with sediment ("sanded in"), directly altering fish community composition at the stocking sites.
- **Flathead catfish reproductive data**: 3 of 4 mature females examined had fully developed ovaries ready to spawn, suggesting the survey period coincided with an active spawning window for this species in the Gila River system.
- **Differential sampling effectiveness**: Seining was highly effective in slow, stable-bottom reaches (e.g., Bonita Creek confluence) but substantially less effective in swift, rough-bottomed reaches (e.g., Eagle Creek), necessitating supplemental trammel netting and monofilament gillnetting.
- **Multi-agency field collaboration** among [[Arizona Game and Fish]], [[Arizona State University]], and [[Bureau of Land Management]] personnel was central to executing both the baseline fish surveys and the razorback sucker release, reflecting a coordinated recovery effort for this federally significant [[Catostomidae]] species.
