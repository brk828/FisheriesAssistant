## Executive Summary

This document is a 1997 policy correspondence (not a primary research paper) between retired U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) biologist George Divine and Bureau of Reclamation (BR) officials, critiquing the Final Biological and Conference Opinion (BO) governing [[Lower Colorado River]] operations under the Endangered Species Act. While primarily administrative, the letters contain substantive population and management data on two federally listed native fish — the [[Razorback Sucker]] and the [[Bonytail Chub]] — within the context of the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (formed 1990) and the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (LCRMSCP). The author argues that many "Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives" (RPAs) in the BO are biologically unrealistic, historically regressive (attempting to recreate 1950s river conditions), and administratively overreaching, and instead advocates a "no net loss" interim strategy paired with active, ongoing head-starting/reintroduction programs.

## Background & Description

Limited direct morphometric data are given, but the letters specify size-classes used as management benchmarks:

- **Razorback Sucker ([[Razorback Sucker]])**: Stocking and monitoring targets reference **12-inch (~300 mm) sub-adults/adults** as the size released back into [[Lake Mohave]] following captive rearing. Fingerlings are tracked at **~100 mm** prior to grow-out to the ~300 mm release stage. Some released adults were **radio-tagged** at ~300 mm for spawning-aggregation monitoring.
- **Bonytail Chub ([[Bonytail Chub]])**: Target reintroduction size is **10–12 inch (sub-adult)** fish, produced via captive broodstock programs.
- Both species are referenced as long-lived, with wild/reared Razorback Suckers noted to be capable of living **approximately 40 years** in the wild post-release.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Both target species occupy the mainstem Lower Colorado River and associated impoundments — principally [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Havasu]], and [[Lake Mead]] — with additional reintroduction efforts noted for the [[San Juan River]].
- **Recruitment dynamics**: The letter identifies a critical ecological distinction between **spawning success** and **recruitment to adulthood**. It states that Razorback Suckers (and likely Bonytail Chub) "successfully spawn in Lake Mohave now" — recruitment to the adult stage, not spawning itself, is described as the limiting factor for population recovery.
- **Historical baseline**: Successful natural recruitment of Razorback Sucker is tied to a specific historical event — the **1954 closure of Davis Dam** and the subsequent multi-year filling of Lake Mohave, which the author explains created a temporary pulse of expanded aquatic productivity typical of newly filling reservoirs (a limnological phenomenon), not a permanently reproducible steady-state condition.
- **Invasive species pressure**: The Colorado River ecosystem is described as physically modified and colonized by **more than 30 non-native fish species**, complicating any management goal of restoring native-fish-dominated habitat conditions.
- **Riparian linkage**: The [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], though not a fish, is treated as an ecologically and administratively linked indicator species within the same BO, tying river/floodplain hydrology and channelization management to riparian habitat condition.

## Key Findings

- **Native Fish Work Group (1990–present)**: Formed to reverse the decline of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave; original goal was to **replace the aging ~50,000-fish Razorback Sucker population by 2000**. After 6 years, fewer than **10,000 twelve-inch Razorback Suckers** had been released — the program's stated goal had not yet been met, though "positive evidence" of returning young fish at spawning aggregations was reported.
- **Updated 1997 population data**: By the time of writing, **more than 5,000 advanced fingerling / ~300 mm radio-tagged Razorback Suckers** had been released into Lake Mohave, with **~40,000 fingerlings (~100 mm)** on hand, projecting **15,000–20,000 additional 300 mm fish** returnable to Lake Mohave during 1997.
- **Bonytail Chub production commitment**: In 1994, FWS committed to producing **25,000 ten-to-twelve-inch Bonytail Chub annually for five years** for introduction into Lake Mohave; this target was expected to be first met in 1997.
- **Reintroduction feasibility critique**: The author calculates that under ideal conditions, **50,000 Razorback Sucker fingerlings could be produced in two years**, but that this scale of reintroduction into the mainstem below Parker Dam was not a stakeholder priority, rendering an associated RPA biologically unrealistic as written.
- **Mini-ecosystem provision**: One RPA required construction of **600 acres of "mini-ecosystem" habitat over 5 years** intended as refugia for self-sustaining populations of Razorback Sucker, Bonytail Chub, and other native fish — described as the most credible/"doable" provision with environmental-group support.
- **Historical recruitment critique**: A central scientific/management argument of the letter is that the BO's mandate to "manage Lake Mohave water levels to provide the same conditions that allowed for successful recruitment in the 1950s" conflates a **transient reservoir-filling productivity pulse** (post-1954 Davis Dam closure) with a repeatable or restorable steady-state management target — deemed biologically and logistically unreasonable (would require draining and refilling the reservoir).
- **Monitoring methodology**: Annual **February/March Razorback Sucker "roundups"** are cited as the basis for confirming that stocked and wild fish co-occur on spawning beds and contribute fertilized eggs and swim-up larvae, though larval-to-adult recruitment remains unconfirmed as self-sustaining.
- **Overall management stance**: The author recommends a **"no net loss"** interim conservation standard for Razorback Sucker, Bonytail Chub, and Southwestern Willow Flycatcher during the 5-year consultation period, deferring aggressive recovery targets to a cooperative 50-year [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] rather than unilateral Biological Opinion mandates.
