# Insecticide Pollution of North Alabama Streams: Bottom Fauna and Fish Populations After the 1950 Cotton Dusting Season

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This Alabama Department of Conservation Fisheries Report (Number 2, March 1955), by fisheries biologists [[A. D. Hooper]] and [[J. M. Hester]], assesses the damage to streams in North Alabama caused by agricultural insecticides applied to cotton during the 1950 [[Boll Weevil]] infestation. Heavy rains washed excess [[Cotton Insecticides]] into streams. Bottom fauna were sampled in 36 streams in 1953, and fish populations were surveyed in six of them in late summer 1953 using [[Rotenone]]. All six fish populations were judged undesirable. Game fish were scarce, carnivorous fish were often too small to measure, and most streams supported little fish biomass. The authors recommended restocking polluted streams with bass or other carnivores, ideally in late summer, and called for further research on insecticide effects.

## Background & Description

**Pollution event.** In summer 1950, 80 to 95 percent of the cotton acreage in Jackson, Limestone, Madison, Marshall, and Morgan Counties was treated with insecticides. Blount, Cullman, and Franklin Counties also had large treated areas. Because of heavy rain, applications often exceeded recommended amounts, and high concentrations reached streams.

**Insecticide inventory.** Young and Nicholson (1950) reported that five North Alabama counties used 19,391,910 pounds of dusting insecticides on cotton. Toxaphene (20% formulation) made up 61 percent of the total. The remainder was a mixture of [[BHC]] (3%) and [[DDT]] (5%) with other ingredients (3%), [[Aldrin]] (2.5%, 4%), and calcium arsenate with other ingredients (1%).

**Study design.** The work had two parts:
- **Bottom fauna survey:** Bottom samples were taken from 36 streams in eight North Alabama counties in January to April 1953. One to three collection points were randomly placed on each stream, and tributaries were sampled where possible. An [[Eckman Dredge]] was used when feasible. Rocky bottoms forced hand or bucket sampling, so the data are qualitative.
- **Fish population survey:** Six streams in three counties were sampled in August and September 1953. Areas were treated with 5 percent emulsifiable rotenone, and all killed fish were collected, identified, counted, measured, and weighed.

No control streams were available, because no unaffected comparison streams could be found.

**Laboratory and pond toxicity data (from the literature cited).** [[Lawrence]] (1950) found that:
- Toxaphene at 0.05 ppm killed [[Bluegill]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] fingerlings in aquaria within 36 hours, but the same concentration in an earthen pond killed neither within five weeks.
- DDT at 0.05 ppm killed many bluegill fingerlings and yearling bass in a pond, but this was no longer toxic to bluegill after 30 days. At 0.5 ppm, DDT killed all bass and bluegill and remained toxic seven weeks later.
- BHC at 0.18 ppm did not kill fish in a pond, but in aquaria 0.1 ppm killed 50 percent of bluegill and bass fingerlings.

[[Doudoroff]], Katz, and Tarzwell (1951) ranked toxicity to fish as toxaphene > aldrin > DDT > BHC. Soils from cotton fields sampled one to two months after dusting were toxic to goldfish, indicating that drainage could make streams toxic.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Bottom fauna.** [[Oligochaeta]] (aquatic earthworms) were found in all 36 streams. [[Chironomidae]] (midge larvae) occurred in 26 streams. Other taxa appeared in some streams: Ephemeroptera (mayfly nymphs), Anisoptera and Zygoptera (dragonfly and damselfly nymphs), Amphipoda (scuds), Isopoda (sowbugs), Tabanidae (horsefly larvae), Coleoptera (aquatic beetles), Hirudinea (leeches), Plecoptera (stoneflies), Trichoptera (caddisflies), Hexagenia (burrowing mayflies), and Chaoborus (phantom midge larvae). Two or more taxa were present in 31 streams, but no stream supported a great variety of organisms.

**Fish community structure.** The authors used the [[Swingle Ratios]] (Swingle, 1950), which compare forage fish weight to carnivore weight (F/C), the pounds of forage fish per pound of carnivore (Y/C), and the balance index (A value). Swingle considered populations unbalanced if A fell outside 33 to 90, F/C outside the printed range (partly illegible in the scan), or Y/C outside 0.02 to 4.8. Because Swingle's work was done in warm-water Alabama conditions, it was judged applicable here.

**Timing and sampling biases.** Late-summer sampling occurred at low flow, and rotenone probably killed fish downstream of the sample areas. Both factors may have inflated the apparent fish per acre figures.

## Key Findings

**Fish population results (six streams, 1953):**

| Stream | Fish per acre (lb) | Notable composition |
|---|---|---|
| Dry Creek | 112 | Carp 3.3%; shad and suckers ~27%; game fish 28% |
| Paint Rock River | 292 | Drum, buffalo, and shad 78.3%; game fish 0.5% |
| Piney Creek | 30 | Bream 51.4%; no other game fish; catfish and suckers ~6% |
| Sugar Creek | Not clearly legible | Shad and suckers dominant; game fish 2.5% |
| Indian Creek | 148 | Game fish 5.7%; carp, minnows, and suckers over 92% |
| Mountain Fork Creek | 58 | Minnows ~52%; crappie, green sunfish, and rock bass only; 3.3% |

*Note:* Piney and Mountain Fork Creek had the lowest yields. Carnivore size was too small to compute F/C and Y/C in three of six streams.

**Main conclusions:**
1. **Bottom fauna:** Survival of bottom organisms in most streams suggested that stream bottoms no longer held toxic insecticide levels by 1953. Insecticides may have been flushed out by floods and normal flow, buried in silt, or lost toxicity through chemical reaction.
2. **Fish populations:** None of the six streams supported a desirable population. Game fish were under 3.5 percent by weight in 50 percent of streams.
3. **Carnivore deficit:** The authors attributed the lack of carnivores to late-summer 1950 pollution. Carnivores had finished spawning, while surviving forage fish could spawn heavily and overcrowd streams. This may have blocked carnivore spawning the following season.
4. **Differential toxicity:** If carnivores die at lower concentrations than rough fish, this could explain low game fish percentages. Mountain Fork and Piney Creeks may also have been polluted in 1951 and 1952.

**Recommendations:**
- Restock polluted streams, especially with [[Largemouth Bass]] or other carnivores, which would be beneficial.
- Stock in late summer after the dusting season. If fish are unavailable then, stock carnivores the following spring, when hatchery supplies are better.
- Conduct studies on the immediate effects of organic insecticides on stream fauna and on their residual effects on fish and food organisms.

**Summary of results:**
- 80 to 95 percent of cotton acreage in five counties was treated, using 19,391,910 pounds of insecticides in 1950.
- Thirty-six streams were sampled for bottom fauna, and six were sampled for fish populations.
- No stream supported a desirable fish population.
- In 50 percent of streams, game fish made up less than 3.5 percent of the total weight.

**Limitations:** The study had no control streams. Bottom samples were qualitative, and several figures in the scanned document are partly illegible. The authors also acknowledged that the pesticide-to-fish link was inferred rather than directly measured.
