# Conservation of the Black Bass Resource of the Lake Ontario-St. Lawrence Region

#ichthyology #marine-biology #black-bass

## Executive Summary

This New York Conservation Department document presents a preliminary survey of the [[Smallmouth Bass]] (black bass) resource in [[Lake Ontario]] and the [[St. Lawrence River]], prepared by the Bureau of Biological Survey under [[Dr. John R. Greeley]]. The survey treats the region as the largest and most important smallmouth bass water in the State, with fishing that has largely held up. It frames bass management as a problem akin to crop management, balancing carrying capacity, harvest, and replacement. The author describes the study as a working basis for discussion, not a final solution. The main recommendations are to reduce the daily bag limit from 10 to 6 bass per person and remove the boat limit, to keep other regulations unchanged for now, to control competing fish species such as [[Rock Bass]], and to coordinate New York and Ontario management of the shared fishery.

## Background & Description

- **Study subject:** [[Smallmouth Bass]], referred to in the text as small-mouth black bass or simply bass. No scientific name is given in the document.
- **Study region:** Largest and most productive New York bass grounds lie in Lake Ontario between the Henderson Harbor and Cape Vincent regions, extending down the St. Lawrence into the Chippewa Bay area. These waters are jointly controlled by New York and Ontario.
- **Lake size:** Lake Ontario is stated as 7,300 square miles. If bass areas are conservatively estimated at 5 per cent of the area, that is 365 square miles, described as nearly twice the entire water area of the [[Finger Lakes]].
- **Local infrastructure:** 9 state campsites, cottages, and other accommodations. Guides work out of [[Henderson Harbor]], [[Chaumont Bay|Chaumont]], Cape Vincent, [[Alexandria Bay]], and Clayton. Most anglers use motor boats; rowboats and shore angling are also used.
- **Context:** The formerly valuable [[Whitefish]] resource of Lake Ontario is described as reduced to a fraction of its earlier abundance, which informs the Foreword's emphasis on planning while bass remain healthy.
- **Sponsoring body:** [[New York Conservation Department]], under Commissioner Lithgow Osborne.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Bass production** is limited to comparatively shallow water. Lake Ontario has few shoals, so bass grounds are less extensive than casual observers assume.
- **Carrying capacity:** Bass grounds are too extensive for habitat modification to matter much, although some of the best fishing occurs around structures such as [[Lighthouse Foundations]]. Competition from other species is a recognized factor limiting production.
- **Competing species:** [[Rock Bass]] occur on virtually all grounds and compete with bass for [[Crayfish]] and minnow food. Rock bass are only lightly harvested, and the author suspects conditions favor their overabundance. Other abundant, under-harvested species include [[Common Sunfish]] and [[Yellow Perch]]. [[Carp]] and [[Eels]] are also noted as not removed in sufficient numbers.
- **Spawning:** Stream spawning occurs from May into July and is readily observed in creeks. Lake spawning is later; unspent and breeding bass were taken on shoals off [[Galloo Island]] in late July. Probable June breeding areas include the head of Chaumont Bay, [[Eel Bay]], Bucks Bay, and Flynns Bay. Probable July spawning areas are on Lake Ontario shoals.
- **Growth:** Scale analysis indicates good first-year growth, poor to fair growth in years two through three (often four), and excellent growth in years five and six. Small fish below the legal size of 10 inches are in poorer condition than fish of similar size in certain other waters, while larger fish are described as exceptionally fat.
- **Competition in juveniles:** Juvenile bass are often found at high population densities. Small bass were abundant on the "gas buoy" shoals off Galloo Island in late July, where they might limit one another's food supply.
- **Environmental conditions:** The open lake is often too rough for fishing, and some St. Lawrence rapids are inaccessible. These conditions favor bass escapement and allow the resource to withstand a longer season.

## Key Findings

**Status of the resource**
- The region's bass supply has held up well overall, though some local areas show decreased production.
- Angler license returns for the entire State, 1929 to 1938, were: 276,056; 286,280; 263,654; 886,161 (as printed, possibly a scan error for 386,161); 449,927; 493,358; 613,921; 648,213; 883,287; 921,683. The author notes there is no catch data specific to the region and no check on fish size, so "better fishing" may be a dangerous assumption.
- Bass average small. Three-pound fish are considered good, and few fish older than 6 years were found in angling samples. A bass is said to reach legal size at about three years and may live 10 years or more if not killed.

**Causes of limitation**
- Angling pressure is the most probable cause of the scarcity of older bass.
- Poor juvenile growth is considered a more likely "weak link" in replacement than conditions affecting the smallest fish.
- Competition from other fish on the same grounds limits bass production.
- Stocking results are of questionable value. Hatchery operations may be unnecessary given the adequacy of natural reproduction, and further expansion of planting cannot be assumed.

**Management principles**
- Handling of the resource should sustain the highest level of utilization. Total catch must be balanced against the resource's capacity to replace removed fish.
- Carrying capacity, harvesting, and replacement are the essential factors to consider. Every bass caught must be replaced.
- Control of competing species, including rock bass, carp, eels, and others, may raise carrying capacity for bass.

**Regulatory recommendations**

| Proposed change | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Increase size limit | No change now; consider if 10 to 12 inch bass become scarce |
| Close nursery areas | No change now; consider after detailed surveys if small fish become critically short |
| Shorten season (June 16 to July 1 opening) | No change now; a July 1 opening would hurt local hotels, guides, and tourism, though it might let more bass spawn |
| Closed areas for adult stock | No change now; requires detailed knowledge of bass habits |
| Daily limit | Reduce from 10 to 6 bass per person and remove the 25-bass boat limit; state-wide change to 6 per person suggested, since other areas allow 8 |

- The author notes that a 6-bass limit would reduce catches for typical parties. For example, a party of three could take 18 fish instead of 25. Average party size is closer to three than to five.
- The June opening is considered too early by some, but the author suggests it is not critical given the region's large size and natural escapement factors.

**Conclusions and plans**
- Consider the entire fisheries management plan for the water unit as the most likely method of improving bass fishing.
- Coordinate New York and Ontario in an effective joint management plan for the St. Lawrence River and Lake Ontario fisheries.
- Further studies are needed on reproduction rates, year-class strength, growth, competition among size groups, control of competing species, and the balance of catch by size.
- Prompt measures should be taken if definite signs of deterioration appear. The department asks for cooperation from sportsmen, guides, and other stakeholders.
