# *Fontelicella* Gregg & Taylor, a New Genus of West American Freshwater Snails

#malacology #hydrobiidae #freshwater-snails

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper by [[Gregg and Taylor]] (1965, *Malacologia* 3(1): 103-110) establishes the new genus [[Fontelicella]] within the subfamily [[Hydrobiinae]] of the family [[Hydrobiidae]], a group of small freshwater prosobranch snails in western North America. The genus is divided into three new subgenera: [[Fontelicella (s.s.)]] (type *F. californiensis*, with 8 species, Pliocene to Recent, in the western U.S. and Baja California), [[Natricola]] (3 species in the Snake River drainage of Idaho and Wyoming and the Harney Lake Basin of Oregon), and [[Microamnicola]] (1 species in the Amargosa River drainage of Nevada and California). The authors diagnose the genus by shell, embryonic shell, verge, radula, and egg characters, and compare it with [[Cincinnatia Integra]], the most similar adequately described species, from eastern North America. A central claim is that the arrangement of melanin and calcareous granules on the head is a useful, previously neglected source of specific characters.

## Background & Description

Hydrobiid snails have small, characterless shells and have been little collected or studied in western North America, so their classification at species and family level was poorly resolved. The authors surveyed western American species to understand their histories, distribution, and fossil record, and published the genus name so that other papers in progress could use it. The genus name derives from Latin *fons* (spring) and *helix* (snail), meaning "little spring snail," reflecting the group's characteristic habitat.

**Generic diagnosis (summary):**
- **Shell:** 2.5-8 mm long in adults; 3-6 whorls; rimate or imperforate; narrowly elongate to conic or globose; aperture 20-40% of shell length; no sculpture beyond minute growth striae.
- **Embryonic shell:** helicoid, umbilicate, about 1.5 whorls.
- **Verge:** bifid, with a narrow, melanin-pigmented free penis and a wider, virtually unpigmented, ductless accessory process on the left. The accessory process usually bears a terminal glandular lobe, a subterminal constriction, and additional glandular areas (ridges, lobules, or papules).
- **[[Radula]] formula:** 15-30 : 17-24 : 3-6-1-2-3 : 3-7-1-3-7, with 7 teeth per row (central, lateral, first and second marginals, and so on).
- **Eggs:** laid singly in smooth, unornamented capsules 0.3-0.5 mm in diameter, free on the substratum or appressed to a firm surface.

**Type species:** *Fontelicella californiensis* Gregg & Taylor, new species.

**Nomenclatural remarks:** The most commonly applied names for these species were *Paludestrina longinqua* (Gould) and *P. stearnsiana* Pilsbry. *Amnicola longinqua* is based on fossil shells and may be composite. The original habitats of *F. stearnsiana* near Oakland have been destroyed by urban growth, so no topotype material can be collected. The authors therefore chose as type a species whose type locality is likely to remain available for future study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat:** Species of *Fontelicella* inhabit perennial springs, seeps, small streams, large springs, rivers, and lakes, typically on mud, sand, gravel, rocks, sticks, or among aquatic plants. *F. hendersoni* is the only known species from a warm spring habitat.

**Locomotion and behavior:** Shells are carried free of the substratum with the apex tilted upward and posteriorly, at about 30-45° to the long axis of the foot. Snails crawl relatively rapidly and smoothly, without the stepwise gait seen in the Pomatiopsinae. Tentacles diverge at 70-100° (90° commonly) and frequently touch the substratum. The snout is pressed to the substrate and moves side to side while crawling.

**Feeding:** Worn radular teeth, direct observation, and fecal pellets indicate that snails rasp hard surfaces and pick up fine particles. The authors suggest that these snails selectively take detritus from soft mud or graze microorganisms from stones, dead wood, and decaying leaves.

**Mantle cavity and feeding currents:** The ctenidium has about 14-30 triangular lamellae. The inhalant current on the left is stronger than the exhalant current on the right. Ciliated epithelium moves rejected particles to the hind end of the foot, and it can carry a fecal pellet out of the mantle cavity when the snail is inverted.

**Fecal pellets:** Ovoid or spindle-shaped and arranged lengthwise in the rectum, unlike the transversely arranged cigar-shaped pellets of [[Pleuroceridae]]. Representative sizes are 0.12 × 0.28 mm (*F. hendersoni*) and 0.11 × 0.25 mm (*F. californiensis*). They are made of vegetable fibres and tiny rock particles.

**Phototaxis:** Under the microscope, *F. hendersoni* showed a positive tropism to heat or light from a lamp. No other species studied showed this response.

**Distribution:** The genus is the most widespread and speciose group of west American Hydrobiidae. Its range covers the Great Basin and Pacific drainages of California, Nevada, southeastern Oregon, southeastern Idaho, Utah, Arizona, and Baja California. Fossil records extend to the middle and possibly early Pliocene.

**Pigmentation and head granules:** The body is gray to deep purple-black from variable melanin granules, with no other pigment known in the genus. This contrasts with [[Lithoglyphus]], which also has yellow pigment granules. Calcareous granules are scattered through the head-foot mass and are concentrated behind the eyes. Dense white granulose patches near the eyes are characteristic of the genus. The authors state that the pattern of pigmentation is diagnostic for nearly all species, consistent with [[Muus]] (1963), who found pattern differences among three Danish *Hydrobia* species.

## Key Findings

- **New genus and subgenera:** *Fontelicella* Gregg & Taylor is erected within Hydrobiinae, with three new subgenera:
  - **[[Fontelicella (s.s.)]]:** 8 species, Pliocene to Recent, western U.S. and Baja California. Shell 2.5-5 mm, 3-4 whorls. Habitat is small springs, seeps, or streams.
  - **[[Natricola]]:** 3 species (*F. hendersoni*, *F. idahoensis*, *F. robusta*) in the [[Snake River Drainage]] and Harney Lake Basin. Shell 4-8 mm, 4-6 whorls. Accessory process about twice as long as the free penis.
  - **[[Microamnicola]]:** 1 species (*F. micrococcus*) in the [[Amargosa River Drainage]]. Shell 1.5-1.7 mm, about 3.5 whorls. Accessory process lacks terminal glandular lobe and ornamentation.
- **Type species:** *F. californiensis* was described from Campo Creek, San Diego County, California, and its type is held at the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology (catalog no. 220000). It is the species most characteristic of perennial springs and small mountain streams, occurring in both Pacific and interior drainages.
- **Closest relative:** [[Cincinnatia Integra]] (eastern U.S.) shares a single-ducted verge, a relatively large accessory process, and a small penis. It differs in verge detail, radula, pigmentation, shell, and foot structure. Foot is distinctly auriculate in *Fontelicella* but not in *Cincinnatia*, and the snout is the most heavily pigmented part of the body in *Fontelicella*.
- **Diagnostic characters:** Verge ornamentation (glandular areas on the accessory process) is the main basis for species separation. Head pigmentation and granule patterns are emphasized as a neglected but useful set of taxonomic characters for future Hydrobiidae work.
- **Shell-only assignments:** Fossil species *Amnicola longinqua*, *A. micra*, *A. pilsbryana*, and *Hydrobia truckeensis*, and the anatomically unknown *Paludestrina cedrosensis* and *Pomatiopsis intermedia*, are assigned on shell characters alone, a judgment based on the authors' knowledge of the anatomy of nearly all described western American Hydrobiidae.
- **Ecological conclusions:** The genus is largely confined to springs and small streams, with feeding behavior centered on detritus and surface microorganisms. The warm-spring species *F. hendersoni* differs in phototactic response.
- **Stated purpose:** The paper validates the genus name so that related papers in progress can use it, and it provides the framework for linking the fossil record to modern biology.
