# Native Fish Management Below Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 management position paper addresses the decline of native fishes in the [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]] after the closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963. The authors argue that dam operations altered hydrology, water temperature and sediment supply, and that these changes, together with [[Non-Native Fishes]], degraded habitat for native species. They recommend an integrated set of operational changes: a seasonal flow pattern, a [[Multi-Level Intake Structure]] to warm releases, and [[Sediment Augmentation]]. The paper is written to support decision-makers facing an impasse over the choice of a preferred operating alternative.

## Background & Description

The document is a Native Fish Management memorandum dated September 9, 1993. It is organized in layers. A brief opening section gives operational recommendations for managers. A longer background section supports the authors' reasoning, drawing on the scientific literature, unpublished ongoing studies, and the authors' combined field experience.

The focal taxa are five native fishes of the Colorado River basin:

- [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), formerly called "Colorado squawfish," listed as endangered.
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), endangered; reproduces in only a single tributary, and its mainstem distribution is restricted.
- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), endangered.
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*).
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), endangered; extremely rare in the reach and not believed to reproduce there.

The authors note that other native fishes also suffered reduced or eliminated mainstem reproduction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pre-dam versus post-dam conditions.** Closure of Glen Canyon Dam drastically altered the hydrological, physical and biological functions of the downstream riverine ecosystem. The main changes were:

- **Flow:** A pronounced seasonal pattern of flow variation was replaced by daily discharge fluctuations.
- **Sediment:** Tens of millions of tons of sediment were trapped annually in [[Lake Powell]], reducing sediment transport through Glen and Grand Canyons.
- **Temperature:** Hypolimnetic releases from Lake Powell reduced the annual range and mean of water temperatures. Perennially cold water limited or prevented successful mainstem reproduction by native fishes.
- **Organic matter:** Allochthonous particulate organic matter inputs from the upstream system ceased, altering relationships between the mainstem and its tributaries.

Together these changes reduced the diversity and reliability of instream habitats. Most non-native species present before 1963 persisted after closure, and massive new sportfish stocking programs were started in Lake Powell. The authors state that the presence of non-native fishes has both contributed to and is a descriptor of this habitat degradation.

**Management objectives.** The authors list four objectives for enhancing native fish populations in Grand Canyon:

1. Achieve successful reproduction in the Colorado River and its tributaries.
2. Increase survival of early life stages.
3. Increase recruitment to adult, reproducing segments of populations.
4. Minimize detrimental effects (competition, predation) of non-native fishes.

They add that full restoration of the native fauna would require reintroduction of species extirpated since closure.

## Key Findings

### 1. Hydrology in the absence of temperature modification
- Replace daily fluctuations with seasonal discharge patterns. Beginning in March, flows should rise above autumn-winter lows.
- Spring flows should peak as early as practicable (near 30 May or June) at not less than 5,000 cfs, or at yearly maxima in years with sufficient water. The authors suggest high, not necessarily maximum, flows during the main reproductive period.
- Spring peaks use [[Controlled Floods]] to exploit negative effects of floods on non-native fishes. Flood effects are expected to suppress non-native populations, following Mockley (1991) and related work cited in the text.
- Descending limbs should decline in monthly steps, with return to base flow by September. Base flows should be held between 5,000 and 10,000 cfs through February.
- Future annual water deliveries are assumed to vary about a legally mandated volume (the exact figure is illegible in the scan), with most deviations above that amount.
- Continued release of cold water will largely preclude native fish reproduction in the mainstem and may limit potential benefits of flow changes.

### 2. Hydrology with temperature modification
- The authors call temperature modification the only way to alleviate cold-water limits on mainstem reproduction. They recommend a [[Multi-Level Intake Structure]] (MLIS) to control release temperature.
- Release temperatures should rise gradually from hypolimnetic levels beginning in March or the earliest feasible spring month, reaching approximately 15 °C from epilimnetic releases in June through September or October.
- Temperatures should return rapidly to hypolimnetic levels in November through February.
- The specific temperature pattern should vary somewhat among years to mimic natural variability.
- Projected downstream warming: about 2 °C additional at the [[Little Colorado River]], and nearly 7 °C at [[Diamond Creek]] (386 km downstream). Releases of 15 °C in June and July should reach 19–20 °C, the authors state, which is optimal for spawning, incubation and rearing, in the mainstem beginning about 200 km below the dam.
- Warmer water would benefit trout growth in tributary fisheries.

### 3. Risks of temperature modification
The authors identify potential effects on aquatic and riparian components:
- Changes in algal species composition and productivity.
- Changes in invertebrate composition and productivity.
- Invasion by additional non-native fishes from Lake Powell.
- Strengthening of existing non-native populations.
- Increased incidence of fish diseases and parasites.

The authors consider invasion and establishment of non-native fishes the greatest concern. They argue that other life-history requirements of these species could limit invasion, and that periodic floods would act as a major control agent.

### 4. Sediment augmentation
- Introduce sediment in the highest spring flows, when it can be most easily moved and distributed to higher nearshore habitats.
- Augmentation would restore the historical role of sediment in forming and maintaining habitats and keep a sediment balance in Grand Canyon.
- Sediment supply is needed to use controlled floods to control non-native fishes and to create and maintain nearshore rearing habitat.
- The quantity, particle size distribution and delivery method are unknown. The authors judge the previously proposed pipeline elaborate, costly and environmentally questionable.
- Sediment should include clays as well as sand. Turbidity from fine sediments provides cover for native fishes and can limit non-native predators. Silts and clays are also the main source of phosphorus, which appears to limit primary productivity in the Colorado River in Grand Canyon.

### 5. Working hypothesis
The authors propose, and recommend testing, that sediment augmentation combined with temperature modification would allow timing of controlled floods or diel fluctuations to be manipulated to favor native over non-native fishes. They expect that the system's flexibility would also support multiple uses for which Glen Canyon Dam was built.

### 6. Background status
The background section notes a scarcity of detailed pre-dam habitat descriptions for the mainstem. Historic habitat conditions are therefore inferred from relevant information rather than measured directly.
