# Genetic Management Guidelines for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft planning document, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) Region 6 and dated November 1993, provides genetic management guidelines for four endangered big-river fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. It functions as a conceptual framework, decision-support tool, and reference for planning, implementing, and evaluating captive breeding, reintroduction, and natural propagation within the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (Recovery Program). The guidelines apply a 13-step, medical-model framework (diagnosis, prognosis, treatment, monitoring) to genetic management. The document is explicitly described as a working, first draft that is expected to be revised with field input.

## Background & Description

**Legal framework.** The [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of 1973, as amended (1978, 1982, 1988), mandates recovery of listed species and requires federal agencies to ensure their actions do not jeopardize listed species or destroy critical habitat (Section 7). The Service administers the Act, prepares recovery plans (Section 4), encourages State cooperation (Section 6), and regulates take (Section 9).

**Target species.** Three cyprinids (minnows) and one catostomid (sucker) are endemic to the Colorado River Basin and listed as endangered. All four were once broadly distributed from Mexico to Wyoming but have declined sharply. The document attributes decline mainly to water development (dams, flow alteration, dewatering, diversions) and to introduction of nonnative species.

**Document structure.** The guidelines are organized into five sections:
- Section One: Recovery Program background and setting.
- Section Two: Genetic principles, concepts, and issues (including species concepts, population genetics, and conservation unit criteria).
- Section Three: Planning framework based on the medical model (13 steps).
- Section Four: Natural propagation guidance.
- Section Five: Captive propagation guidance.

The document also includes a reference list, a glossary, and appendices (Roman-numeral designations, e.g., Appendix IV on gene pool maintenance breeding).

**Core genetic concepts.** Section Two reviews DNA structure and replication, Mendelian and quantitative inheritance, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, effective population size (Ne = 4NmNf/(Nm+Nf)), inbreeding coefficient (F), heritability, and selection programs. It emphasizes that genetic diversity is the basis of adaptation and evolutionary potential, and that genetic markers (allozymes, mtDNA, RFLPs, sequencing) are tools for identifying and characterizing populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat loss as primary driver.** The Service identifies habitat alteration as the major contributor to endangered species decline. Specific threats include dam construction, altered flow regimes, channel geomorphology changes, dewatering, watershed chemistry changes from agriculture, ranching, and mining, and introduction of nonnative fish.
- **Water development history.** Key milestones include the 1922 Colorado River Compact, the 1948 Upper Colorado River Compact, and the [[Colorado River Storage Project Act]] (1956), which authorized large mainstem dams including Lake Powell, Flaming Gorge, Navajo, and the three Aspinall Project reservoirs on the Gunnison River.
- **Population structure models.** Two contrasting models are discussed: an "island" (spring-head) model, in which variation lies mostly among isolated populations, and a "stream hierarchy" model, in which variation is structured by river distance and discharge. The four target species are considered to fit the stream hierarchy model, and the two systems require different management.
- **Conservation unit concepts.** The document reviews the Evolutionarily Significant Unit (ESU), distinct population segment, stock, and genetic conservation unit (GCU), and the phylogeographic decision matrix of Dizon et al. (1992).

## Key Findings

**Program status and purpose**
- The Recovery Program was formalized by a Cooperative Agreement signed in January 1988 after four years of negotiation. Its goal is to recover and delist the four species within 15 years (1988 to 2003) while allowing water development to proceed.
- The Recovery Program is intended to serve as the "reasonable and prudent alternative" for Section 7 consultations.
- The 1987 "Blue Book" defined five recovery elements, later modified in the 1993 draft RIPRAP to seven: (I) protect instream flows, (II) restore habitat, (III) reduce nonnative fish impacts, (IV) conserve genetic integrity and augment or restore populations, (V) monitor and research, (VI) increase public awareness, and (VII) program planning and support.
- Depletion offsets under the Cooperative Agreement were set at a one-time contribution of $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion, applied case by case.

**Genetic management goals (Williamson, 1990)**
1. Avoid immediate population extinction.
2. Protect, maintain, and restore naturally sustaining populations and their habitat.
3. Maintain genetic diversity among and within populations.
4. Maintain capacity for population adaptation.
5. Maintain evolutionary potential for long-term survival and speciation.

**Guiding principles**
- The fish population is the fundamental unit of genetic management.
- Natural, naturally sustaining wild populations have the highest recovery value and program priority; captive propagation and stocking are less preferred unless populations face imminent extinction.
- Stocking without sufficient habitat presents genetic risk and may jeopardize wild populations; all reintroductions should be judicious, cautious, and experimental, with stocking guidelines (Williamson 1992a) applied.
- Stocking decisions should be made before genetic characterization only with extreme caution, since stocking is irreversible and can obscure population identity.
- A "no selection" gene pool maintenance program is recommended for captive breeding to avoid altering the donor population.

**Planning documents**
- The Genetic Management Guidelines provide the "why and how" conceptual guidance.
- The Genetic Management Plan is the operational document ("what, when, where, by whom, for how much, and for how long"), updated annually.
- The Annual Propagation Operations Plan derives from the Genetic Management Plan.
- The draft Coordinated Hatchery Facility Plan (April 1993) assessed facility needs against immediate, short-term, and long-term fish needs.
- A Genetics Panel and a Propagation Subcommittee are proposed to advise the Propagation Coordinator and the Biology Committee.

**Worked example (Yampa River razorback sucker)**
- Biological status is critical, with imminent danger of extinction; habitat status is unknown; genetic risk is low to moderate.
- The tactical plan proposes capturing 10 wild adults (5 males, 4 females) in FY 1995 and breeding them in a factorial matrix, with a goal of total N = 50 (25 males, 25 females) for artificial genetic refuges.
- Proposed facilities are a primary refuge at Ouray NWR, Utah, and a secondary refuge at Craig, Colorado.
- Reintroduction is contingent on addressing habitat issues in the Yampa and Green Rivers.

**Research needs (Utter 1981, as cited)**
- Whether hatchery fish can retain all characteristics of founding populations.
- The estimated genetic impact of hatchery fish on target and peripheral native populations.
- Whether domesticated fish retain the ability to revert to the wild state.
- Whether captive propagation alters the target population's characteristics enough to call its ESA status into question.

**Conservation unit strategies**
- Utter (1981) recommends a stepwise sampling sequence that builds from background data to successively finer-scale genetic sampling.
- Currens et al. (in press) define a GCU as the most inclusive genetic, geographic, and temporal group whose management will not reduce the genetic diversity of smaller, less-inclusive groups.
- Dizon et al. (1992) propose a four-category decision matrix (Categories I to IV) based on genetic divergence and geographic partitioning, with Category I most likely to qualify as an ESU and Category IV least likely.

**Document status**
- The authors describe the document as a draft, "not a finished product," that is intended to remain flexible, adaptive, and open to revision by all programmatic levels, with substantial field-personnel input.
