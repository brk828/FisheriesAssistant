## Executive Summary

This document is a 1984 letter from the Director of the California Department of Fish and Game (CDFG) to Dr. W. L. Minckley, Chairman of the [[Desert Fishes Council]], addressing concerns about United Energy Corporation's (UEC) proposed aquaculture operation near [[Yermo, California]]. The core issue is the risk of escape of exotic aquaculture species — notably [[Tilapia]] — into the [[Mojave River]] system, a sensitive desert aquatic habitat. While acknowledging the legitimacy of concerns raised by the Desert Fishes Council, CDFG concluded that the risk of exotic species establishment was "remote" and elected to support the proposal under a framework of 16 regulatory conditions, balancing state aquaculture development mandates against native desert fish conservation.

## Background & Description

No morphological data on specific fish species are provided in this document. This is a policy/regulatory letter rather than a biological survey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Exotic species risk**: [[Tilapia]] populations are explicitly noted as already established in wild waters in the region, with CDFG expressing concern about further range extension of these established feral populations.
- **Habitat context**: The aquaculture facility's ecological risk was evaluated based on:
  - Immediate terrain surrounding the facility
  - Distance from the facility to the [[Mojave River]] floodplain
  - Elevation of the site relative to the river
  - Physical/security containment measures preventing escape
- **Receiving ecosystem**: The [[Mojave River]] floodplain represents a desert lotic/floodplain habitat presumably supporting native desert fish fauna of concern to the Desert Fishes Council, though no specific native species are named in this letter.
- **Invasion pathway concern**: The primary ecological risk identified is escape of non-native aquaculture stock (tilapia and other unspecified "exotic fishes") into public waters, potentially competing with or displacing native desert fish species adapted to the Mojave basin's unique hydrology.

## Key Findings

- CDFG determined the risk of exotic fish escape from the UEC facility to be sufficiently remote to justify supporting the aquaculture registration.
- The California Fish and Game Commission adopted **16 conditions** (August 3, 1984) as part of UEC's certificate of aquaculture registration, expanding upon **14 conditions** originally proposed in CDFG's letter to the Commission.
- Additional conditions beyond the original 14 include:
  - UEC must reimburse CDFG for monitoring activities related to the facility.
  - UEC must fund any clean-up/recapture operations required if fish escape into public waters.
- On-site inspection by regional fisheries personnel factored heavily into the risk assessment, evaluating terrain, floodplain proximity, elevation, and security infrastructure.
- CDFG frames its support as consistent with legislative mandates to promote aquaculture growth in California, while asserting this can be achieved "without compromising fish and wildlife resources."
- The letter reflects a broader regulatory tension between **economic development via aquaculture** and **conservation of native desert fish species**, a recurring theme in [[Desert Fishes Council]] advocacy, particularly given the known vulnerability of Mojave Desert aquatic ecosystems to introduced species such as tilapia.
