# Aspects of the Thermal Ecology of *Cyprinodon elegans* and *Gambusia nobilis*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinodontidae #Poeciliidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1972 research memorandum/report, prepared by Baylor University biologists for the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department, presents rapid-turnaround (5-day) field and laboratory data on the thermal ecology of two critically restricted desert spring fishes: the [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon elegans*) and the [[Pecos Gambusia]] (*Gambusia nobilis*), both endemic to spring systems at [[Balmorhea State Park]], Texas. The study measured critical thermal maxima/minima (CWMax/CWMin) and diel thermal preferences to inform the design of a proposed **pool preserve** intended to protect these species from habitat loss, exotic fish introduction, and groundwater pumping. The wild pupfish population was estimated at only ~1,025 individuals total, with the gambusia population roughly estimated at 5,000 in Texas. Findings directly shaped specific engineering and management recommendations for the preserve.

## Background & Description

- **Sexual size dimorphism** differs markedly between the two species:
  - *Cyprinodon elegans*: males and females show **little difference in size**, and correspondingly **little difference in thermal tolerance**.
  - *Gambusia nobilis*: pronounced sexual size dimorphism — **females are substantially larger than males**, and this correlates with females tolerating **higher CWMax and lower CWMin** than males (i.e., broader thermal tolerance in the larger sex).
- Behavioral endpoints used as tolerance proxies:
  - **CWMax** (critical water maximum): fish hangs vertically/diagonally, motionless.
  - **CWMin** (critical water minimum): fish cannot right itself when tipped over.
- Reported values are **water temperature adjacent to the fish**, not internal body temperature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Core habitat**: A concrete irrigation canal system connecting [[Phantom Spring]] and [[San Solomon Spring]] within [[Balmorhea State Park]]; ambient spring temperatures normally range **22–26°C** (White et al. 1941), with recorded outflow extremes of **13–29°C** at the surface (1955, 1971–72).
- **Population distribution**:
  - *C. elegans*: ~1,000 adults in Phantom Spring waters (30 Sept 1972); ~25 in San Solomon Spring waters; only ~100 of the ~1,025 total individuals occur within park boundaries proper.
  - *G. nobilis*: broader range including the [[Leon Creek]] drainage north of Ft. Stockton, the same spring systems, and at least four localities in southeastern New Mexico; Texas population conservatively estimated at **5,000 individuals**.
- **Microhabitat use / territoriality**: Adult pupfish were territorially confined to deeper (25–27°C) water in the Phantom Spring "hole," while juveniles occupied shallow (1–5 cm), warmer (27–29°C) downstream margins — indicating **age-based spatial and thermal segregation** linked to territorial behavior.
- **Habitat structure dependency**: Both species rely on **submerged aquatic vegetation** for cover; vegetation density fluctuated with water level changes (ca. 1–30 cm), directly affecting habitat availability.
- **Threats noted**: exotic/predatory and competitor fish introductions, groundwater pumping lowering spring flows, human swimming activity, and algicide chemical use in San Solomon Spring — motivating the isolated pool preserve design (see [[Balmorhea Pool Preserve Design]]).

## Key Findings

**Thermal Tolerance (Table 1 summary):**

| Species/Sex | CWMax (°C, mean ± SE) | CWMin (°C, mean ± SE) |
|---|---|---|
| *C. elegans* – A.M. | 39.66 ± 0.129 | — |
| *C. elegans* – P.M. | 40.31 ± 0.124 | -3.11 ± 0.277 |
| *G. nobilis* – males | 38.11 ± 0.152 | 6.57 ± 0.153 |
| *G. nobilis* – females | 39.34 ± 0.172 | 4.39 ± 0.178 |

- *C. elegans* tolerates **significantly higher and lower** water temperatures than female *G. nobilis* (t = 3.62, P < 0.01), indicating pupfish possess the **broadest overall thermal tolerance range** of the two species.
- **Diel shift in tolerance**: Pupfish tested in the **afternoon (3–5 PM)** tolerated significantly higher CWMax than those tested in the **morning (9–11 AM)**, suggesting acclimatory or circadian physiological flexibility.
- Female *G. nobilis* tolerate significantly higher CWMax and lower CWMin than males (broader tolerance in larger sex).

**Thermal Preference (Table 2 summary):**
- Both species preferred **21–25°C** water in the morning, shifting to **26–30°C** in the afternoon — consistent with the diel shift observed in tolerance data.
- Overall pooled preference for both species centered on **21–25°C**, matching the 24–25°C collection-site temperature.
- When both species were tested together in the afternoon, most individuals of both species aggregated in the **26–30°C** range, corroborating single-species trial results.

**Management/Conservation Implications:**
- The pronounced thermal tolerance range and diel preference shifts, combined with observed territorial spatial displacement (adults vs. juveniles), directly informed **pool preserve design recommendations**, including:
  1. Minimum 0.5 ha, elongate, bowl-shaped pool (20 cm–1 m depth gradient) with controlled underground inflow/outflow (preferably from **Phantom Spring**, avoiding San Solomon due to exotic fish, human swimming, and algicide use).
  2. Natural substratum and native submergent/emergent vegetation plantings to provide the spatial heterogeneity required by territorial pupfish.
  3. Full perimeter fencing with controlled public "pull-out" viewing points, riprap edges planted with native salt grass.
  4. Strict exclusion of non-native fishes, with weekly visual and periodic seine-haul census monitoring.
  5. Siting near park headquarters for both protective oversight and gravity-fed water supply.
- The report underscores the value of the preserve for both **species preservation** and **public interpretive education** on relictual Southwestern fish faunas and threats from groundwater depletion and introduced species (see [[Southwestern Relictual Fish Fauna]]).
