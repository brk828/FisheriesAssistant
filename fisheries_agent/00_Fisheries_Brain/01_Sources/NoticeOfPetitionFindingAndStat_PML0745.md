# Southwestern Willow Flycatcher Status Review and Petition Finding (1992)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #tyrannidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service notice, dated September 4, 1992 and published in the *Federal Register* (Vol. 57, No. 170, September 1, 1992), announces a 90-day petition finding and a status review for the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*). A petition to list the subspecies as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) was found to contain substantial information indicating listing may be warranted. The subspecies had already been elevated to Category 1 candidate status in November 1991. The Service requested additional data from researchers, agencies, and the public, and committed to a final finding by January 30, 1993. The document is a federal regulatory notice, not a research paper. Although it concerns a songbird rather than a fish, it describes riparian habitats that support aquatic ecosystems.

## Background & Description

- **Subject:** *Empidonax traillii extimus*, a riparian-nesting passerine of the southwestern United States, and its potential listing under the ESA.
- **Regulatory history:** Category 2 status in January 1989 (54 FR 7 notice); elevated to Category 1 on November 21, 1991 (56 FR 58804).
- **Process:** Under 50 CFR 424.14(b), the Service must conduct a status review and, within 12 months of receiving the petition, decide whether listing is not warranted, warranted, or warranted but precluded by higher-priority listing actions.
- **Information sought:** Distribution, abundance and trends; habitat use; threats under the five ESA listing factors (habitat destruction and modification; overutilization; disease or predation; inadequacy of regulatory mechanisms; other natural or manmade factors); and beneficial management practices.
- **Contact:** Field Supervisor, Phoenix Field Office (Attn: Timothy Tibbitts, primary author). Comments are to be submitted within the period stated in the enclosed notice.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range and state-level status**
- **California:** Once a common breeder in coastal southern California and the lower Colorado River. Now a small, widely disjunct remnant. Only two nesting groups were stable or increasing in recent years: one on private land (threats from livestock grazing virtually eliminated) and one at Camp Pendleton. The Kern River group declined in 1991. About six other groups, each with six or fewer pairs, are known.
- **Arizona:** Extirpated from much of its former range, including the lower Colorado River. Breeding is confined to three areas in the Grand Canyon, which had declined to two pairs by 1991. Riparian habitat loss has been extensive, and most willow flycatcher habitat is now gone.
- **New Mexico:** Breeding populations are generally west of the Rio Grande. The range has not been reduced, but numbers have likely declined. Most remaining nesting birds may occur in New Mexico. A group of 15 pairs was lost to rising water at Elephant Butte Reservoir.
- **Texas:** Eastern range edge in western Texas. Data on current population levels and trends are lacking.
- **Utah:** Northern breeding limit in southern Utah. Records are sparse and boundaries with the *E. t. adastus* subspecies are poorly defined.

**Documented habitat losses**
- Wetland losses between 1780 and the 1980s by state: California 91%, Nevada 52%, Utah 30%, Arizona 36%, New Mexico 33%, Texas 53%.
- Losses of up to 50% of riparian habitat in Arizona, attributed to urban encroachment, water diversion, and surface mining.

**Threat factors discussed by petitioners**
- **Brown-headed cowbird parasitism:** Reduces nest success and fledging rates. Cowbird expansion is linked to habitat fragmentation.
- **Livestock grazing:** Trampling of nests, loss of riparian vegetation structure, and cattle attraction to linear riparian habitats. Flycatcher numbers rose about 100% over five years after grazing was reduced.
- **Water impoundment:** Dams alter downstream hydrology and inundate habitat. Replacement habitat along reservoir shorelines (often tamarisk) is unstable.
- **Tamarisk invasion:** Replaces native cottonwood-willow habitat, which is associated with declines in the yellow-billed cuckoo, summer tanager, and northern oriole. The subspecies does not nest in tamarisk at low or high elevations.
- **Predation:** Great-tailed grackles prey heavily on eggs and young. Predation near forest edges increases with habitat fragmentation.
- **Pesticides, logging, and tropical deforestation:** The petitioners presented no specific documentation. The Service will investigate these during the status review.
- **Regulatory mechanisms:** No conservation plans or habitat restoration projects existed on federal lands. The only protective measures were on private and military land, and the subspecies is protected under the [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]].

## Key Findings

1. **Petition finding:** The Service found that the petition presented substantial information indicating that listing the southwestern willow flycatcher may be warranted, based on documented population declines due to habitat loss and modification, brood parasitism, predation, and inadequate regulatory mechanisms.
2. **Status:** The subspecies was elevated to Category 1 in November 1991. A decision on listing is due by January 30, 1993.
3. **Decline:** The population is "clearly much smaller now than 50 years ago," with continued downward trends reported.
4. **Emergency listing:** The Service will consider a request for emergency listing and for designation of critical habitat. A proposed emergency rule would be published if emergency listing were determined to be necessary.
5. **Data needs:** The Service requested additional information on distribution, abundance, trends, habitat use, and threats, and on the effects of tropical deforestation, logging, and pesticides, which the petitioners did not document.
6. **Management:** Few beneficial measures were in place, with the exception of private conservation land and Camp Pendleton, where Least Bell's vireo management also benefited the flycatcher.
