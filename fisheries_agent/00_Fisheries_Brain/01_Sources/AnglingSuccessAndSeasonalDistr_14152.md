## Executive Summary
This 1959 report by I.B. Byrd (Alabama Department of Conservation) presents an 8-year (1950–1958) analysis of angling success and seasonal catch distribution across 12 state-owned public fishing lakes totaling 841 acres in Alabama. The lakes were stocked with [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]] following management protocols developed by H.S. Swingle, and periodically balanced via rotenone poisoning. Over 4,286 acre-years of fishing, 695,282 fisherman-trips yielded 2,530,182 fish weighing 746,598 lbs, demonstrating that properly fertilized and managed artificial lakes can sustain high, stable fish yields year-round without requiring draining or restocking — countering the prevailing assumption that such lakes inevitably decline after an initial "boom" period.

## Background & Description
The paper does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of the study species, as its focus is fisheries yield and management rather than taxonomy. Key species involved:
- **[[Bluegill]]** (*Lepomis macrochirus*) — stocked as fingerlings (2–3 inches) in fall.
- **[[Redear Sunfish]]** (*Lepomis microlophus*) — co-stocked with bluegill; together comprised the bulk of catch by weight.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus salmoides*) — stocked as fingerlings (1–2 inches) in spring, roughly one year after bream stocking.
- **[[Bullhead Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus natalis*, *I. nebulosus marmoratus*) — incidental/surviving population from pre-stocking rotenone treatments, notably persistent in Lamar and Pike Lakes.
- **[[Crappie]]** (*Pomoxis annularis*, *P. nigromaculatus*) — unofficially introduced (likely via unauthorized stocking) in Cullman and Crenshaw Lakes during year 5, appearing in catches by year 6.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat construction**: Lakes ranged from 32–250 acres, built on carefully selected sites for fertilization and population management, following the Swingle pond-management paradigm (fertilization, balance monitoring, rotenone-based population control).
- **Stocking sequence & timing**: Bluegill/redear sunfish stocked ~18–19 months before opening; largemouth bass stocked ~13–14 months before opening (bass introduced in spring, ~1 year after bream in fall). Lakes opened to fishing 12–13 months after bass stocking, typically in June/July.
- **Trophic/competitive interactions**:
  - Bullhead populations competed with bluegill/redear sunfish for resources — Pike Lake's high bullhead catch (67.0 lbs/acre) coincided with suppressed bream yields.
  - Crappie appeared to competitively suppress in Cullman Lake but established more successfully in Crenshaw Lake (6.1 lbs/acre annual catch), and their presence correlated with a slight decline in bass catch in year 8 (competition inferred).
  - Sodium arsenite treatment (4 ppm As₂O₃) used to control the summer alga *[[Pithophora]]* in Marengo, Clay, and Butler Lakes caused declines in bluegill/redear sunfish catches in subsequent years, consistent with findings by Lawrence (1957) on arsenite toxicity to fish populations.
- **Seasonal distribution**: Fishing effort and total catch rose from January to a peak in May–June, then declined gradually through December, with lowest activity in November–January. Bass catch was more evenly distributed across the year (peaks in April, May, June, September) than bream, whose catch was sharply concentrated April–September.
- **Angling pressure**: No size limits were imposed; creel limits were 20 bluegill/redear sunfish and 6 largemouth bass per fisherman-trip. Average trip duration ≈ 3.5 hours.

## Key Findings
1. **Sustained yield across years**: Excluding an inflated first-year catch (223.9 lbs/acre, reflecting two years' cumulative production), annual catch per acre remained stable (144.5–179.0 lbs/acre) through year 8, refuting the belief that bass-bluegill lakes become unproductive after year one.
2. **8-year cumulative statistics**: 4,286 acre-years of fishing produced 2,530,182 fish (746,598 lbs) from 695,282 fisherman-trips; average annual yield = 162.2 trips/acre and 174.2 lbs/acre (590 fish/acre).
3. **Species catch composition**: Bluegill + redear sunfish averaged 187.1 lbs/acre/year; bass 29.5 lbs/acre/year; bullhead 7.0 lbs/acre/year; crappie 0.6 lbs/acre/year.
4. **Opening-day catch dynamics**: A pronounced initial catch spike occurred upon first opening due to naive, unfished populations — first-day catch averaged 26.9 lbs/acre (up to 58 lbs/acre in one lake), representing ~34% of week-1 catch, ~26% of month-1 catch, and ~12% of year-1 catch. Catch declined sharply within 7 days and leveled off after ~4 weeks; ~56% of opening-year catch occurred in the first 3 months.
5. **Catch-per-effort seasonality**: While total catch and fisherman numbers peaked in spring/summer, catch-per-fisherman-trip was higher in fall/winter, attributed to a shift toward more experienced ("specialist") anglers during off-peak seasons and reduced fishing pressure allowing better individual success, especially for bass.
6. **Bullhead decline over time**: Average bullhead catch fell from 11.6 lbs/acre (year 1) to 1.5 lbs/acre (year 8), suggesting a die-off or replacement dynamic within the managed community.
7. **Economics**: Average annual gross income was $143.00/acre ($81 from fishing permits at $0.50/day, $33 from boat permits at $1.00/day, $29 from concessions), with a net return of ~$12.00/acre/year after operational costs. Construction costs averaged $480/acre; recovering this investment would require raising permit fees to $0.75–$1.00.
8. **Comparative context**: This yield (174.2 lbs/acre/year) exceeded most contemporary reports from other managed/unmanaged lakes (cf. [[Bennett 1954]], [[Eschmeyer 1935]], [[Pelton 1950]], [[Thompson and Hutson 1951]]) and compared favorably with fertilized experimental ponds (Swingle 1945, 1952). Discussion participants estimated unmanaged impoundments would yield only 1/4 to 1/5 of this production.
9. **Bass cropping rate**: External discussant (R. Stroud) estimated the ~30 lbs/acre bass harvest represented a 40–45% cropping ratio relative to regional standing-crop estimates (~60–65 lbs/acre in managed Southeastern ponds) — a notably high sustained-yield ratio.
