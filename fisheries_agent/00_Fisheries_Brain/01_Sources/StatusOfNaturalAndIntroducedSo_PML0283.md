# Status of Natural and Introduced Sonoran Topminnow (Poeciliopsis occidentalis) Populations in Arizona Through 1985

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 report by James E. Brooks of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] summarizes monitoring of natural and introduced populations of the [[Sonoran Topminnow]], a small live-bearing freshwater fish listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The report tests whether habitat variables can separate successful from unsuccessful [[Topminnow Introduction Sites]] and proposes site-selection criteria. It also reviews the condition of natural populations threatened by [[Mosquitofish]] invasion and recommends the species be considered for downlisting to threatened status.

## Background & Description

- The Sonoran topminnow is endemic to the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. Two forms occur in Arizona: *P. o. occidentalis* (Gila River drainage) and *P. o. sonoriensis* (Rio Yaqui drainage). Both are federally listed as endangered.
- Both subspecies declined sharply because of exploitive land and water use during colonization of the arid region, and archaic water-use practices continued despite shrinking surface water.
- The [[Recovery Plan]] (USFWS 1983) calls for protecting natural populations and expanding distribution through introductions.
- A 1983 [[Memorandum of Understanding]] among USFWS, the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and the [[U.S. Forest Service]] allowed introductions of the Gila form on Forest Service land, with the condition that livestock water uses not be interrupted. In 1984, the [[Bureau of Land Management]] agreed to fully protected introduction sites while other experimental, nonessential sites were designated under Section 10j of the ESA.
- Stocking in 1983 placed fish in 21 sites on Forest Service lands. Three introductions were made on BLM lands in 1984, and 1985 introductions totaled 14 (Table 1). Fish were moved by hatchery truck, ice chests, or oxygenated sealed bags, with temperature acclimation before release.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site evaluation methods.** Presence or absence of topminnow determined whether each introduction was successful. Population size was not estimated. Habitat variables recorded included water source, presence of snails, bank stability, other fish species, bank vegetation density and canopy cover, substrate composition, cover type, habitat type, stream gradient (measured with a hand level), and physico-chemical parameters (dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature, conductivity) taken once per site.

**Statistical approach.** [[T-Test Analysis]] compared habitat variables among natural, successful, and unsuccessful sites (p < 0.05). [[Linear Discriminant Analysis]] (SPSS) identified variables distinguishing successful from unsuccessful introductions.

**Habitat context.** Introduction sites occur in upper Sonoran desert scrub to pinyon-juniper communities. Drainages are typically narrow, with steep gradients. Most natural populations occur in oak woodland and grassland with gradients usually below 2%.

**Threats.** Invasive [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) remain the main threat, along with flooding, desiccation, livestock use, and illegal stocking of nonnative game and forage fish into stock tanks.

## Key Findings

**Introduction results (1985 monitoring)**
- 71 introduction sites were monitored, dated 1976 through 1984 on Forest Service, BLM and private lands. Of these, 49 (59%) were occupied by topminnow and 22 were classified as unsuccessful.
- 30 successful introductions were at least three years old, meeting the downlisting criterion.
- Sycamore Spring's name was corrected, and Cottonwood Artesian was reclassified and excluded from statistical analyses. Tucker Box, reported unsuccessful, was resampled in 1986 after reports of topminnow; only longfin dace and fathead minnow were collected.
- Mesquite Tank was drained by unauthorized persons, but topminnow were later found in two bedrock pools about one kilometer downstream.

**Natural populations**
- All known natural populations were monitored in 1985. Bylas and Middle Springs and Salt Creek, on the San Carlos Apache Reservation, were the most changed sites.
- Mosquitofish invaded Bylas Springs and Salt Creek in 1979–80 after floods removed an alluvial fan that had served as a barrier. Concrete barriers were built in winter 1983–84 and Bylas Springs and Salt Creek were renovated with antimycin in April and June 1984. About 200 topminnow were restocked in Bylas Springs in July 1984. Salt Creek was not restocked and remained fishless.
- In December 1985, mosquitofish had reinvaded Bylas Springs through flow around the barrier. Middle Spring's population was intact.
- Redrock Canyon: largemouth bass and mosquitofish were found in stock tanks, and the stock tanks are a source of nonnative fish.
- Sharp Spring maintains a fragile balance between topminnow and mosquitofish, with flooding allowing topminnow to persist.
- Monkey Spring, Cottonwood Spring and Cienega Creek were unchanged from earlier reports. Santa Cruz River and Sonoita Creek populations continue despite nonnative fish.

**Statistical results**
- Stream gradients did not differ among site types. Unsuccessful sites had smaller drainage areas, contrary to earlier predictions.
- Successful and unsuccessful sites contained significantly more bedrock than natural sites. Emergent aquatic vegetation was substantially less common at successful and unsuccessful sites.
- A discriminant function built from 55 introduction sites (canonical correlation 0.85) explained 72% of variance and correctly classified about 90% of sites used to build it. The authors caution that this is highly biased and needs validation.
- Testing 1982 classification functions on 1983 introductions correctly classified only 50% of sites, so the earlier functions were of little value.

**Genetic considerations**
- Most introductions used stock from Monkey Spring, which [[Vrijenhoek et al. 1985]] criticized for low genetic variability. They recommended a heterozygous source such as Sharp Spring.
- Broodstock from Sharp Spring (September 1985) and Heron Spring (April 1986) produced about 5,000 fish by August 1986 at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].

**Downlisting and management**
- The recovery plan requires at least 20 introduced populations to persist at least three years within historic range. As of 1985, 30 met this, so downlisting from endangered to threatened should be pursued, with caution about long-term persistence during drought, land-use changes, and nonnative fish introductions.
- Recommended site selection criteria (Table 11) include drainage area under 10 km², elevation under 1,500 m, stream flow under 0.1 m³/s, gradient under 3%, gradual surrounding topography, silt-sand-gravel substrate, abundant aquatic vegetation, diverse spring-stream or cienega habitat, stable perennial water, and native fish only, within the Gila River drainage.
- Overall, the author concludes that topminnow are more adaptable generalists than previously assumed, provided habitat stability is maintained and predatory nonnative fish are absent.
