# Protected Fishes of the United States and Canada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-status #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This American Fisheries Society (AFS) monograph by [[James E. Johnson]], with editorial support from Beth D. McAleer and funding from The Standard Oil Company, catalogs native fishes of the United States and Canada that receive legal protection or are of Special Concern. The listing uses only two categories: **Protected** (legal security under endangered species legislation) and **Special Concern** (declining, but either more secure or insufficiently known to warrant full protection). The compilation includes 517 taxa (described and undescribed species and subspecies), of which 418 described species (56% of the roughly 743 native freshwater species) receive some protection and 235 species (32%) are fully protected. The work is explicitly designed to show the extent of protection and the direction of endangered species legislation, and it recommends that [[Deacon Et Al. 1979]] remain the authoritative source for biological listing recommendations.

## Background & Description

The publication was prepared under the AFS Endangered Species Committee, building on earlier compendia by [[Miller 1972]] and Deacon et al. (1979). Miller (1972) used Endangered, Rare, Depleted, and Indeterminate categories, and documented legal protection of nongame fishes in only five states. Deacon et al. (1979) took a continent-wide biological approach, placing 251 taxa in Threatened, Endangered, or Special Concern categories, of which 202 occurred in the United States and Canada. Following the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, terminology was standardized, which led the present work to return to Miller's format and purpose while adopting the two-category system.

The text notes that protection decisions often involve non-biological criteria, so Threatened and Endangered designations can appear inconsistent. Examples cited include the Threatened [[Leopard Darter]] and Endangered [[Fountain Darter]] offering little difference in protection, and the Endangered [[Okaloosa Darter]] and [[Shortnose Sturgeon]] facing less immediate danger than some Threatened taxa.

Tables in the document list taxa by family (Table 1, with color plate references), by state and province (Table 2), and compare recommendations across years (Table 3). Nomenclature follows Robins et al. (1980). The full checklists are summarized below rather than reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Native North American freshwater fishes are noted for high numerical richness and diversity, but many waters have been altered by agricultural and industrial development. Habitat change is identified as the main driver of declines (Miller 1961; Pister 1974; Ono et al. 1983). Desert and southwestern systems feature heavily in the lists, including desert springs, spring-fed pools, and fragmented river systems such as the [[Colorado River Basin]] and [[Great Lakes]] region.

Several listed taxa are not globally endangered but are rare at the edges of their ranges. The text cites the [[Johnny Darter]] in eastern Colorado as an example, where loss would not threaten the species overall but would diminish the state's native fauna. Cave-dwelling and spring-restricted taxa, such as the [[Ozark Cavefish]] and [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], face especially narrow distributions.

## Key Findings

- **Scale of listing:** 517 taxa are recorded as receiving some protection in the US and Canada, with 418 described species (56% of native freshwater fauna) and 235 species (32%) fully protected.
- **Regional coverage:** Only Alabama, Alaska, Hawaii, and Louisiana (states), and six Canadian provinces/territories, reported no Protected or Special Concern fishes. Thirty-eight states protect nongame fishes.
- **Change over time (Table 3):** In 1972, about 55% of the 202 taxa recommended by Deacon et al. (1979) already had some protection. By 1986, over 90% had at least partial protection (Special Concern), and 126 taxa (62%) had full legal protection.
- **Family patterns:** Cyprinidae (167 taxa) and Percidae (106 taxa) contribute the largest numbers, followed by Salmonidae (43), Cyprinodontidae (34), and Catostomidae (40). Cyprinidae also has the most Protected entries among families in Table 3 (54 in 1986).
- **Notable entries:** The [[Blue Pike]] (Stizostedion vitreum glaucum) is listed as biologically extinct, though it still appears in state lists. The [[Shortnose Sturgeon]] is listed Protected in many states, and the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] are Protected across several western states.
- **State totals:** The United States total is 73 Protected and 126 Special Concern. Tennessee (61 Protected, 5 Special Concern) and Ohio (40 Protected) are among the most heavily protected states.
- **Author's conclusion:** The protection statistics are "surprising and somewhat disquieting," and the listing is intended as a tool for agencies and for monitoring the direction of endangered species policy rather than as a replacement for biological assessments.
