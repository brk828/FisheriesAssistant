# San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program (1995)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the February 1995 compilation of the [[San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP), comprising the Program Document, the [[Cooperative Agreement]] (executed 1992), the [[Long Range Implementation Plan]] (LRP, February 1995), and a [[Side By Side Analysis]] comparing the San Juan RIP with the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]]. The Program aims to recover the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] while allowing water development to proceed in compliance with federal, state, tribal and interstate law. It is built on a 15-year horizon and a seven-year research effort funded through the Animas-La Plata Project (ALP) Biological Opinion. The estimated annual budget is $1,980,000, with a stated shortfall of $160,000.

## Background & Description

The San Juan River is the second largest of three sub-basins of the Upper Colorado River basin, draining about 38,000 square miles across parts of Colorado, New Mexico, Arizona and Utah. Historically the basin supported at least eight and possibly nine native fish species, including [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Mottled Sculpin]] and [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]]. Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker and bonytail chub are federally listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

Key historical facts reported:
- Colorado squawfish: first confirmed records in 1936 (three juveniles, Alcove Canyon, Utah).
- Razorback sucker: confirmed in the basin in 1976 (two adults, floodplain pond near Bluff, Utah).
- Bonytail chub: occurrence uncertain; known only from skeletal remains in middens and questionable specimens.
- At least 40 non-native fish species introduced to the Upper Colorado basin since the late 1800s; 23 reported in the San Juan sub-basin.

Hydrology and development: mean annual discharge near Bluff (1914–1986) was 2,574 cfs, with recorded highs of 69,986 cfs and lows of 0 cfs. [[Navajo Dam]] (completed 1962) reduced spring peaks, raised base flows and lowered and stabilized water temperatures. Net depletion of San Juan flow was reported as up to 657,200 acre-feet annually, or 632,200 acre-feet at Bluff after return flows from Dolores River imports. Six diversion structures exist on the New Mexico mainstem, and the Hogback diversion was reconstructed in September 1987 and now diverts all low flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water quality concerns include naturally elevated [[Selenium]] (concentrations rising from under 1 µg/l near Archuleta to 4 µg/l at Bluff), irrigation return flows, oil and gas contamination, and [[Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons]] found in fish tissue (naphthalene, benzo(a)pyrene, phenanthrene, sampled 1990–1991). Fish health surveys noted widespread external abnormalities and bacterial infections, presumably stress-related.

Non-native species are a major pressure. Common carp and channel catfish dominate warmwater main-channel reaches, while [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] are the most common in low-velocity habitats. The document states the high flows of 1987 suppressed channel catfish reproduction, illustrating flow-based control. Chemical control was judged largely infeasible.

Native fish community context: the native fish fauna evolved under extreme seasonal flow variability, and most species are long-lived with distinct life-stage niches. Only four of the eight verified native species remain comparatively common in the drainage.

## Key Findings

**Population status (as reported):**
- A small reproducing population of Colorado squawfish persists. Since 1987, 21 adults have been captured, and young-of-year fish were collected in 1987, 1988 and 1990. Spawning is believed to occur in July, possibly August.
- Only one razorback sucker was collected in the river since 1987, although several were captured in the San Juan inflow area of Lake Powell, many in spawning condition, with no recruitment documented.
- No bonytail have been captured since systematic study began in 1987.

**Program structure and goals:**
- Goal 1: conserve Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker consistent with ESA recovery goals.
- Goal 2: proceed with water development in compliance with law, compacts, Supreme Court decrees and trust responsibilities to the Southern Ute, Ute Mountain Ute, Jicarilla Apache and Navajo.
- Three committees: Coordination, Biology, and Navajo Dam Operating.
- Participation is voluntary; two-thirds majority votes govern most decisions.

**Research and recovery elements (Section 3):**
- Research on distribution, reproduction, recruitment, flow-habitat relationships and habitat modelling.
- Genetic characterization of populations and evaluation of refugia and augmentation.
- Habitat protection, including reoperation of Navajo Dam to mimic a natural hydrograph, contingent on research showing benefit.
- Water quality baseline and contaminant effects research.
- Non-native control through stocking restrictions (warmwater stocking halted; salmonid stocking limited), bait-minnow regulation and enforcement.

**Funding (Table 6.1):** total annual budget of $1,980,000 with a suggested funding total of $1,820,000, leaving a $160,000 shortfall. Major contributors include the Bureau of Reclamation ($670,000 including in-kind), the Bureau of Indian Affairs ($175,000 direct plus $400,000 in-kind), and the Fish and Wildlife Service ($200,000). Capital needs were estimated at $5 million.

**Compliance and progress:** a "sufficient progress" standard lets the Service offset jeopardy for minor depletions, with the aggregate of minor depletions capped at 3,000 acre-feet annually during the 7-year research period. Navajo Dam operations, legal protection of flows, indications of population improvement and water quality improvement are identified as the most important progress elements.

**Side by side comparison:** the San Juan RIP addresses water quantity, water quality and physical habitat, and includes native fish community management. The Upper Colorado RIP (established 1988) focuses on depletions, applies a depletion charge ($12.71/acre-foot in FY95), and excludes direct impacts from new projects. Both programs were recognised by the Service as potential reasonable and prudent alternatives for jeopardy and adverse modification determinations.
