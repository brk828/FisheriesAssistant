# Assessment of Selenium Exposure in Larval Razorback Sucker and Evaluation of Laboratory-Based Predictions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared by Daniel W. Beyers and Cris Sodergren of the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (Project CAP-6 SE, dated 10 January 2001), tests whether predictions from an earlier [[Food-Chain Exposure Experiment]] hold when [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae are exposed to naturally occurring [[Selenium]] in surface waters. Water was collected from three localities on the [[Colorado River]] near Grand Junction, Colorado.

Larvae aged 27 days post-hatch were exposed for 28 days to site waters and to food organisms cultured in those waters. The study separated [[Dissolved Exposure]] from [[Dietary Exposure]] using a factorial design. The highest dietary and whole-body selenium concentrations were 21.8 µg/g and 42.0 µg/g. Existing guidelines predict adverse effects above 3 µg/g dietary or 4 µg/g whole-body selenium, so these levels exceed the guidelines. No adverse effects of selenium were detected. A negative growth effect from dietary exposure to site-water constituents was attributed to [[Co-Contaminants]] rather than selenium. The authors caution that absence of detected effects does not mean wild populations are unaffected.

## Background & Description

**Species and contaminant.** Selenium is a metalloid present in geologic formations and surface waters of the Colorado River Basin. It is an essential micronutrient, but at elevated concentrations it is toxic. Toxicity arises because selenium substitutes for sulfur in amino acids, altering protein structure and function (Maier and Knight 1994; [[Lemly Guidelines]] cited therein). Organic forms, such as selenoamino acids, are more bioavailable and bioaccumulate more rapidly than inorganic forms.

**Population status.** Razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) populations are presumed to have declined in the Upper Colorado River Basin. Causes include loss of physical habitat, altered temperature regimes, interactions with non-native fishes, and degraded water quality. Selenium concentrations in some nursery habitats exceed the USEPA (1987) freshwater aquatic life criterion of 5 µg/L. Elevated selenium has also been measured in larval and adult fish (Hamilton and Waddell 1994; Waddell and May 1995; Hamilton et al. 2000).

**Prior work.** A previous [[Food-Chain Exposure Experiment]] (Beyers and Sodergren 1999, 2000) used laboratory-formulated waters with selenium gradients (<1 to 190 µg/L) and food organisms (<0.702 to 9.24 µg/g). It found no adverse effects on survival or growth in any treatment. The present study tests whether those predictions hold under natural conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Test waters.** Five test waters were used:

- **Control:** Fort Collins municipal tap water, aerated and heated to 18 °C, then adjusted to "very hard" water using USEPA (1991) procedures.
- **[[De Beque]] (DB):** presumed uncontaminated backwater.
- **[[Orchard Mesa]] (OM):** moderately contaminated backwater.
- **[[North Pond]] diluted 50% (NPD):** North Pond water mixed 1:1 with control water, simulating a 50% contaminant reduction.
- **[[North Pond]] (NP):** highly contaminated pond.

Water was collected on 28 April, 10 May, and 17 May 1999.

**Water chemistry.** Alkalinity, hardness, and conductivity rose sharply from control to North Pond. Mean alkalinity was 187 mg/L (control), 182 mg/L (DB), 308 mg/L (OM), and 180 mg/L (NP). Mean conductivity was 341 µS/cm (control), 1,234 µS/cm (DB), and 3,718 µS/cm (NP). Dissolved oxygen in beakers ranged from 6.3 to 7.5 mg/L. Full cation and anion tables appear in the appendix and were not used for statistical testing.

**Food chain.** The food chain was built from three trophic levels grown in site water:

- [[Chlorella Vulgaris]] (freshwater algae)
- [[Brachionus Calyciflorus]] (rotifer)
- Razorback sucker larvae

Site waters were pasteurized at 70 °C for 1 h for algae and rotifer cultures only. Larvae were exposed to unfiltered, untreated water.

**Sediment.** The authors note that sediment interactions, which may affect selenium bioavailability in the field, were not included.

## Key Findings

**Selenium concentrations**
- Water selenium ranged from <1 µg/L (control and DB) to 20.3 µg/L (NP).
- Algae selenium reached 3.74 µg/g. Rotifer selenium ranged from <0.702 to 21.8 µg/g.
- Fish exposed to the control diet contained 2.34 to 14.4 µg/g whole-body selenium. Fish exposed to the corresponding site diet contained 5.45 to 42.0 µg/g.

**Survival**
- Survival ranged from 92.5% to 100%.
- Survival was 100% in five of nine treatments, including all North Pond treatments.
- No consistent relationship with selenium exposure was found. Lower survival elsewhere was attributed to spontaneous mortality.
- Statistical testing of survival was not performed because of insufficient variation.

**Growth**
- Dissolved site-water constituents had a **positive** effect on fish mass (p < 0.0001) and total length (significant). Fish in site waters with control diet grew larger than controls.
- Dietary exposure to site-water constituents had a **negative** effect. Fish on site diets averaged 14% smaller than controls (p < 0.0001 for mass; p = 0.0012 for total length).
- The two effects were of similar magnitude, so net growth in site treatments was approximately equal to controls.

**Correlation analysis (Pearson)**
- Dissolved selenium and whole-body concentration: r = 0.699, p < 0.0001 (strong).
- Dissolved selenium and fish wet weight: r = 0.334, p = 0.0347 (weak, positive).
- Dissolved selenium and total length: r = 0.200, p = 0.2156 (not significant).
- Whole-body concentration and weight: r = −0.149, p = 0.3573.
- Whole-body concentration and length: r = −0.079, p = 0.6279.

**Interpretation of growth effects**
- The positive dissolved-exposure effect may reflect essential trace elements absent from laboratory control water, or reduced osmoregulatory cost in higher-solute waters (Williamson et al. 1993).
- The negative dietary effect was not linked to selenium. Reasons included:
  - No dose-response pattern in survival or growth despite strong selenium gradients.
  - A growth reduction at De Beque, where selenium was below thresholds.
  - Consistency with [[Ogle and Knight]] (1989), in which a diet containing 20.3 µg/g selenium affected fathead minnow growth, while the 21.8 µg/g diet in this study did not affect razorback sucker.

**Comparison with predictions**
- Predicted toxic thresholds were 2 µg/L in water and 3 µg/g in diet (Lemly 1993a; NIWQP 1993). Whole-body threshold was 4 µg/g.
- These water and diet thresholds were exceeded in OM, NPD, and NP. The whole-body threshold was exceeded in all four site waters.
- No corresponding reductions in survival or growth were observed.
- The authors suggest the guidelines include safety margins, do not account for other life stages, and rely on refined selenium forms rather than naturally cycling selenium.

**Biological significance**
- The authors conclude that biologically significant effects are unlikely in nursery habitats at or below 20.3 µg/L water and 21.8 µg/g diet selenium.
- The authors also argue that using 27-day-old larvae instead of 10-day-old fish introduced only minor bias, since first feeding occurs at about 10–12 days and larval sensitivity to dissolved selenium is similar across ages (Hamilton 1995).

**Sensitive life stages and risks**
- **Young-of-year over winter:** Selenium increases metabolic demand during winter (Lemly 1993b), which may reduce overwinter survival. This stage warrants further study.
- **Reproductive females:** Selenium transfers from adults to eggs. Maternal transfer could cause cumulative effects if embryos and larvae are exposed in nursery habitats. A separate reproductive study was in preparation (Hamilton et al. 1999).

**Recommendations**
1. Quantify dietary selenium exposure in nursery habitats by sampling prey when larvae are present.
2. Evaluate overwinter survival of young-of-year fish under selenium exposure.
3. Predict selenium bioaccumulation in wild adults and link it to movements using [[Radio Telemetry]].
4. Conduct mesocosm studies with young-of-year fish under realistic selenium cycling, habitat, and food conditions.
5. Consider reproductive investigations depending on findings from ongoing studies.

**Overall conclusion**
No adverse effects of selenium were observed in larval razorback sucker over 28 days, even at whole-body concentrations up to 42.0 µg/g. The authors stress that this does not show populations are unaffected, since pre-exposure via maternal transfer and sensitive life stages were not tested.
