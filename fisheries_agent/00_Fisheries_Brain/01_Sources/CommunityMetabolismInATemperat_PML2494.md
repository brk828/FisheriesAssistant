# Community Metabolism in a Temperate Cold Spring

#aquatic-ecology #community-ecology #chironomidae

## Executive Summary

This study quantifies [[Community Metabolism]] and energy flow through the invertebrate community of [[Root Spring]], a small, constant-temperature [[Limnocrene]] on [[Intervale Farm]] in [[Concord Massachusetts]]. The author argues that small cold springs are nearly ideal natural systems for ecosystem energetics because their chemical and physical conditions are stable and their biota is species-poor. Field sampling ran from June 1953 to November 1954, with the energy analysis period set at November 1953 through October 1954. The study combines population sampling, growth and mortality estimates, respiration measurements and food-assimilation experiments to build an energy budget for the whole community. Detritus, mainly apple leaves, was the main food source, and most energy passed through herbivores. The author also concludes that transformation to heat was low in carnivores such as planarians, which lose much energy as mucus.

## Background & Description

**Rationale.** The author notes that community metabolism is usually expressed in energy or biomass units, and that a flow-of-energy approach is more informative than a cycle-of-matter approach. Community energy flow is traced from primary production through each trophic level, with energy transformed to heat at each step. Because heat losses cannot be measured directly, they are calculated from respiration rates using the oxycalorific coefficient of [[Lindeman Trophic Efficiency|Ivlev (1934)]]. Several key terms are defined:

- **Assimilation:** energy taken in and absorbed by a population.
- **Respiration:** energy transformed to heat for work.
- **Net production:** assimilation minus respiration, the energy used for growth and reproduction.
- **Gross production:** total energy fixed before respiratory losses.

**Study site.** Root Spring is a limnocrene about 2 m in diameter, 10–20 cm deep, with a mud bottom where most organisms live. It has no true plankton. The spring is fed from the foot of a glacial till bank and forms a springbrook. Earlier work on Florida springs by [[Odum]] and on temperature-varying springs by Dudley is cited as background.

**Scope.** Over 40 animal species were identified. Only a few occurred in numbers large enough to matter to the energy balance, and these are treated individually before being combined into a community budget.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions.** Water temperature was near 7–10 °C and stable through the year, close to mean annual air temperature for the region. Dissolved oxygen was high, and the author judged that it did not limit the fauna. Carbon dioxide and oxygen saturation are mentioned, with CO2 possibly excluding some invertebrate species.

**Flora.** The flora was almost entirely benthic algae and diatoms. Diatoms dominated in winter and early spring, and filamentous green algae such as *Stigeoclonium* and *Spirogyra* peaked in late spring before declining. Duckweed (Lemna) covered the edges but was washed out by spring rains and contributed little. The algae supplied some energy to animals, but the main food source was plant debris, mostly apple leaves, that collected on the bottom.

**Fauna and feeding groups.**
- *Detritus and algae feeders:* [[Limnodrilus hofmeisteri]] (oligochaete), [[Calopsectra dicipiens]] (chironomid midge), [[Asellus militaris]] (isopod), [[Crangonyx gracilis]] (amphipod), [[Physa]] (snail).
- *Mixed feeders:* [[Anatopynia dyari]] (chironomid, herbivorous but also predatory), which obtained about two-thirds of its food from plant sources.
- *Predators:* [[Phagocata morgani]] (planarian), the most important carnivore.
- *Herbivorous caddisflies:* [[Frenesia difficilis]] and *Limnophilus* sp., which fed on leaves and roots and were largely from the adjacent springbrook.

**Community composition.** Table lists were compiled for algae, protozoa, rotifers, nematodes, annelids, mollusks, crustaceans, insects, and mites. Insects, especially Diptera and Trichoptera, were the most numerous large groups. Only a handful of taxa contributed significantly to energy flow.

## Key Findings

**Population dynamics**
- *Calopsectra* had no larvae during winter. Its standing crop peaked in August at 87.6 kcal/m², after which pupation and adult emergence reduced the population to zero.
- Adult emergence was 63% female and 47% male by the reported counts (based on 1,500 adults), and about two-thirds of females laid eggs in the spring they emerged, averaging 250 eggs each.
- Larval mortality was estimated at 30% for May–June, 15% for July–August, and about 87% for the egg-to-hatch stage.
- *Limnodrilus* reproduced throughout the year and showed a sharp drop in November 1954, probably caused by hurricanes that disturbed the spring.

**Energy flow**
- *Calopsectra* respiration and transformation to heat were measured for the larval and pupal stages. Energy was lost at each metamorphic step, with adults holding 1.44 times as many calories as the emerging adult.
- *Anatopynia* respired 208.5 kcal, about 84% of its assimilated energy, and passed 30.7 kcal to other populations.
- *Limnodrilus* transformed 1.22 ± 0.35 cal per cal of fresh weight per month to heat, and assimilated 64.1 kcal over the year.
- *Phagocata* lost much energy as mucus, nearly equal to its body calorie content each month (0.94 ± 0.10 cal mucus/cal per month). Only about 12% of its assimilated energy was transformed to heat, and its net production efficiency was about 87%. The author suggests this low heat loss is an adaptation to high mucus losses.
- Caddisfly larvae had a calorific value of about 0.98 cal/mg fresh weight, and their respiration was estimated from comparable work on *Limnophilus* species.

**Community energy budget**
- The main energy input was organic debris (about 2,350 kcal/m²/yr), with minor inputs from photosynthesis and immigrating caddisfly larvae.
- About 71% of the energy in the debit side of the budget was transformed to heat. Deposition accounted for a further large share, and adult insect emigration for about 1%.
- The community's efficiency of net production was calculated from the balance, and the author notes that it was comparable to temperate communities.
- Assimilation by macroscopic primary consumers was about five times higher than in comparable temperate-latitude equatic communities. The author attributes this to the stable temperature, which keeps animals active all year.
- Microorganisms respired about 1,150 kcal/m²/yr and were grazed by bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. Most microorganisms were feeding on animal remains.

**Mortality and predation**
- Non-predatory mortality was estimated at about 20% of the herbivore trophic level, which the author considers lower than in most communities.
- The most conspicuous herbivore, *Calopsectra*, was only third in importance in terms of energy flow. The author suggests that the relative importance of species in a community depends on energy flow rather than size or biomass.

**Conclusions**
- Constant-temperature springs provide a good system for studying community energetics, because environmental variation is low and species are few.
- Herbivores and carnivores differ in energy use, with carnivores such as planarians transforming less energy to heat because of mucus production.
- A detailed energy-flow analysis gives a better picture of the relative importance of animal populations than population size or biomass alone.
