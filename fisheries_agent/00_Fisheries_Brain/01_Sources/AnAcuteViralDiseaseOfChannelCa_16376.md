# An Acute Viral Disease of Channel Catfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 Technical Paper from the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] reports the first demonstrated viral disease of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*). Field and laboratory work by Fijan, Wellborn, and Naftel of [[Auburn University]] linked a new virus, the [[Channel Catfish Virus]] (CCV), to acute mortality in hatchery fish during 1968. The authors named the resulting clinical picture [[Channel Catfish Virus Disease]] (CCVD). The virus was shown to be heat-labile and smaller than 0.22 micron, and it was isolated in cell culture, where it produced [[Syncytial Cells]] that the authors considered diagnostic. The paper also documents how [[Columnaris Disease]] and secondary bacterial infections masked the viral signs, and it urges wider diagnostic testing across the expanding [[Catfish Farming]] industry.

## Background & Description

**Context.** Commercial channel catfish production in the United States grew from its start in 1960 to an estimated 36,000,000 pounds in 1968. By then it exceeded the total production of all other cultured fish species in the country. Earlier reviews of [[Catfish Diseases]] (Meyer, 1968a) listed bacterial and parasitic agents but no viral disease. Viral causes had been suspected in several recent outbreaks, but none had been diagnosed.

**Clinical picture.** Affected fry and fingerlings showed a combination of external, internal, and behavioral signs:
- External: hemorrhagic fins and abdomen, ascites, pale or hemorrhagic gills, and exophthalmos.
- Internal: pale, enlarged kidneys; hemorrhages in muscle, liver, kidney, and spleen; a mucoid-filled stomach; and yellowish edematous fluid in the abdominal cavity.
- Behavioral: spiral swimming and hanging vertically with the head at the surface.

**Study scope.** The paper covers three well-documented case histories, field transmission trials, laboratory bioassays on nine fish samples, virus isolation in cell culture, and a discussion of diagnosis. It is a technical report, not a survey of the wider aquatic community.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stress and water conditions.** Stress preceded most epizootics. Several outbreaks followed periods of low [[Dissolved Oxygen]], including a pump failure in case history 1. Water temperatures during the outbreaks were high, generally 22–30 °C. Temperature also affected disease expression: at 30 °C the incubation period was shorter and fish died within hours of showing behavioral signs, while at 20 °C incubation lasted up to 10 days.

**Transmission and spread.** Experiments showed the disease was contagious among channel catfish. Shipments and mixing of fish during rapid farm expansion were seen as likely routes of spread. The authors also noted that the virus is capable of being carried in natural waters, so pond water was pumped onto sandy soil and ponds were limed to limit dispersion.

**Species specificity.** In field tests, goldfish, bluegill, redear sunfish, and striped bass stocked with infected water did not die, and in cell culture the virus replicated only in brown bullhead cells. The authors note the "[[Brown Bullhead]]" line as a sensitive host system.

**Co-occurring pathogens.** [[Aeromonas Liquefaciens]], *Pseudomonas fluorescens*, gill-disease myxobacteria, *Trichodina* sp., and *Cleidodiscus pricei* were found in various outbreaks. Parasites were ruled out as the cause of the mortality in some epizootics.

## Key Findings

### Case Histories (1968)

- **Case history 1 (central Alabama, commercial farm):** Pond 5 was stocked with 100,000 advanced sac-fry on July 6. Heavy mortality followed on July 25. About 500 of the largest fish (40–50 mm) died a week after a pump failure. Terramycin at 4 g/100 lb of fish did not reduce deaths. The pond was drained on August 17, and about 10% of the stocked fish were found alive.
- **Case history 2 ([[Mammoth Springs National Fish Hatchery]], Arkansas):** 41,000 fingerlings (25–38 mm) were moved to raceway 1 on July 16. About 450 died by July 18, and approximately 22,000 had died by July 22. Terramycin was ineffective, and all fish in raceway 1 were dead by July 25. Aureomycin was then fed in raceway 3, but losses rose sharply 24–48 hours after treatment began.
- **Case history 3 ([[Frankfort National Fish Hatchery]], Kentucky):** Deaths in pond 31 began on August 19. About 5,000 fish were dead by August 23 at 29 °C. Losses ended after about 5 days as temperature fell to 23 °C. The pond was drained on September 5, and 21,600 of 48,000 stocked fish were recovered.

### Field Experiments (Mammoth Springs)

- **Experiment 1 (contagion):** Healthy fish placed in livecages in an affected raceway developed CCVD. Group I (25–38 mm) showed signs within 78 hours and all died within 6 days. Group II (50–75 mm) began dying 72 hours after exposure, and 22 of 25 were dead by day 7.
- **Experiment 2 (disinfection and host range):** Raceway 1 was disinfected with 20 ppm chlorine. Channel catfish in raceway 3, which received its water, still developed disease, with 15 deaths by August 30. None of the goldfish, bluegill, redear sunfish, or striped bass died.

### Laboratory Findings

- **Samples:** Nine channel catfish samples (I–IX) from Alabama, Arkansas, Illinois, Mississippi, and Texas were tested. Sample IX from Texas produced disease in fish, and virus was isolated from it.
- **Filterability:** Results from samples I, II, III-B, IV, and IX indicated an infective, heat-labile agent smaller than 0.22 micron. Infectivity was lost after heating at 60 °C for 1 hour. Samples I and III-A killed fish with columnaris-type signs only.
- **Communicability:** Ten fish from sample I and eight from sample II placed in aquaria at 25–28 °C all died within 15 days, with the first death at 6 days. In a second aquarium at 21–23 °C, all fish from sample I died within 5 days.
- **Columnaris effect:** Oxytetracycline (12.5 mg/L) changed the picture in one tank. Untreated fish showed columnaris signs only, while treated fish showed CCVD.

### Virus Isolation and Characterization

- **Cell culture:** Primary channel catfish ovary cultures and the brown bullhead (BB) line developed cytopathic effect (CPE). RTG-2, FHM, and BF-2 cells did not. CPE in primary cultures appeared in 2–4 days, and in BB cultures in 3–7 days.
- **Syncytial cells:** The virus induced giant multinucleate (syncytial) cells. The authors judged this diagnostically important because no other known fish virus produced them.
- **Passage stability:** The isolate was passaged through 11 consecutive cell culture passages, and CPE remained constant through them.
- **Titers:** In the first passage, 5.6 × 10^? LD50 per mL was reported (the exponent is illegible in the scan). Titers were 1 × 10^? TCID50/mL for primary cultures and 1 × 10^? TCID50/mL for BB cells (exponents illegible in the scan). Primary cultures responded more quickly and sensitively than BB cells.
- **Postulates:** The virus was reisolated from a dead fish inoculated with a 2.4 × 10^? dilution, fulfilling [[Rivers Postulates]].

### Diagnostic Recommendations and Conclusions

- **Clinical signs:** A vertical posture with the head at the surface, seen in 20–50% of fish in all epizootics, was considered a specific sign of CCVD. Erratic swimming, exophthalmia, dropsy, and hemorrhage also occur in other acute viral diseases of fish, including [[Infectious Pancreatic Necrosis]] and [[Viral Hemorrhagic Septicemia]].
- **Diagnostic protocol:** Incubate inoculated animals and cell cultures at 25–30 °C. Use only channel catfish fry and fingerlings as test animals. Add antibiotics to test water to prevent secondary bacterial infection. Examine suspect columnaris cases for CCVD by splitting slightly affected fish between high-dose broad-spectrum antibiotic water and untreated water. Persistent mortality and CCVD signs in the treated group would indicate a viral cause.
- **Geographic concern:** CCVD was diagnosed in channel catfish from Alabama, Arkansas, Kentucky, and Texas. The authors argued that the isolation of the virus from four widely separated localities indicated a serious epizootic situation. They warned that the entire catfish farming area could be at risk and that further study was urgently needed.
