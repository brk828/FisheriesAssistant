# A River Under Siege: The Etowah River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-mussels

## Executive Summary

The article argues that the [[Etowah River]] in north Georgia appears healthy on the surface but is in serious ecological decline. Surveys of its fish and mussel fauna show that 35 of about 50 historic mussel species have disappeared, along with seven fish species, and seven more fish species are imperiled. The authors identify [[Silt Pollution]] and bed load as the most pervasive degrading forces, with [[Impoundment]] by [[Allatoona Dam]] (1955) as a major blow. They also warn that urban growth from [[Atlanta]] threatens the river within roughly a decade. The article closes by calling for citizen involvement, local conservation groups, and sustained stewardship, noting that riverine ecosystems can recover.

## Background & Description

- **Authors:** Noel M. Burkhead and James D. Williams (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, National Fisheries Research Center, Gainesville, Florida) and Byron J. Freeman (Institute of Ecology, University of Georgia). The piece appeared in *Georgia Wildlife*, Summer 1992.
- **Core message:** Conservation biologists struggle to alert the public about environmental problems without seeming extremist. The river's pastoral beauty masks decline, a problem of "benign perception."
- **River course:** Headwater tributaries drain the southern fringe of the [[Blue Ridge]] as cool, clear, rocky streams. The river slows, warms, and acquires sandy bottoms in the [[Piedmont]]. [[Allatoona Reservoir]], a flood control and hydroelectric impoundment completed in 1955, lies mid-system. At Rome, the Etowah joins the Oostanaula River to form the [[Coosa River]].
- **Land use:** Mostly rural and agricultural, with farming, quarrying, and timbering as the main economic activities. No major cities sit on the banks.
- **Regional context:** The authors state that not one pristine river remains in the United States. Many southeastern rivers (e.g., the Altamaha, Chattahoochee, Coosa, Tennessee, and Cumberland) are severely degraded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Freshwater mussels**
- Mussels are filter feeders that remove plankton from water. Some live 50 to 60 years, making them the longest-lived freshwater invertebrates.
- They stabilize stream bottoms, feed fishes such as river redhorse and freshwater drum, and are eaten by otters, muskrats, raccoons, and wading birds.
- Mussels depend on fish hosts. Their parasitic larval stage, the [[Glochidium]], attaches to fish gills or fins for several weeks before becoming free-living juveniles. Declines in host fish therefore add to mussel losses.
- North America has the world's greatest freshwater mussel diversity. More than 200 of the 300 U.S. species occur in the southeast, and almost 20 percent of the nation's mussel fauna once lived in the Etowah.

**Fishes**
- Fishes have generally fared better than mussels, but the authors estimate the Etowah once held 96 native species, nearly one-fifth of southeastern U.S. species.
- Small-bodied species such as darters and madtoms are ecologically sensitive. Their presence usually indicates good environmental quality.
- [[Cherokee Darter]] and [[Etowah Darter]] are endemic to the Etowah system.

**Degrading forces**
- [[Silt Pollution]] and [[Bed Load]] are the most pervasive pollutants. They smother stream bottoms, bury fish eggs, reduce photosynthesis, and disrupt the food-chain base.
- Soil erosion from fields plowed to stream edges, construction sites, bridges, and exposed road banks drives silt inputs.
- Impoundment alters flow, temperature, dissolved oxygen, and sediment load, eliminating mussels that evolved in clear, free-flowing water.
- Other threats include [[Water Withdrawal]] for urban supply (including a proposed impoundment on Yellow Creek), pesticide runoff from golf courses, poorly sited landfills, inadequate sewage facilities, and [[Urban Sprawl]].
- Pesticides tested on adults may be toxic to larval forms, and pollutants may have cumulative effects greater than their individual effects.

## Key Findings

**Mussel decline**
- Native Americans (1000 to 1700 A.D.) in the basin had a fauna of about 50 mussel species. Excavated Indian mounds in Bartow County yielded 24 species used as food and in ritual.
- Only 15 species remain, about 30 percent of the former fauna.
- At least 35 of the 50 historic species have disappeared.
- Of the 15 remaining species, four were federally proposed for endangered status (Coosa moccasinshell, southern clubshell, triangular kidneyshell, upland combshell) and two for threatened status (Alabama moccasinshell, fine-lined pocketbook). These six have not been found in the Etowah for many years.

**Fish decline**
- Seven species have disappeared: [[Lake Sturgeon]], [[Blue Shiner]], river redhorse, coldwater darter, goldline darter, [[Trispot Darter]], and [[Walleye]].
- Seven more are imperiled: frecklebelly madtom, [[Amber Darter]], Cherokee darter, Etowah darter, freckled darter, holiday darter, and rock darter.
- Losses total about 7 percent of the river's fish species, with another 7 percent in jeopardy.
- The amber darter is federally endangered and survives only in the main channel above Allatoona Reservoir (and in parts of the Conasauga River). The blue shiner and goldline darter are declining and were being proposed for federal threatened listing.
- All seven extirpated fishes except the walleye were on the proposed Georgia protected list.

**Timeline of impacts**
- Massive land clearing in the mid-to-late 1800s began siltation. Erosion control and crop rotation were not widely practiced until the mid-1930s.
- [[Allatoona Dam]] (1955) was described as the second serious blow. It caused most reservoir mussel populations to die, and downstream populations were lost due to altered flow patterns.

**Causal argument**
- The authors compare the loss of species to missing rivets from an airplane: the system's functioning depends on its parts. They cite [[Aldo Leopold]]'s advice to "save all the parts."

**Recommendations**
- Maintain vegetated buffers along streambanks to absorb erosion from sheet flow.
- Enforce silt controls and keep them in working order. The authors observed silt screens overwhelmed or placed perpendicular to the stream bed.
- Monitor construction near streams more carefully.
- Pursue water conservation before building new supply impoundments.
- Form local groups such as a proposed "Friends of the Etowah River" to give citizens a unified voice in projects affecting the river.

**Outlook**
- Riverine ecosystems have inherent resiliency, but recovery may take decades. Streams improved markedly after the [[Clean Water Act]] of 1973, and the authors argue further measures are needed.
- The authors predict Atlanta will overtake the Etowah within about a decade. They urge conservation planning now to protect the river for future generations, citing the Iroquois principle of considering effects on the seventh generation.
