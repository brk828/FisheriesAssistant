# Fish Populations Following a Drought in the Neosho and Marais des Cygnes Rivers of Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Ictaluridae #freshwater-fish-populations

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by James Everett Deacon, published by the [[University of Kansas Museum of Natural History]], documents how fish populations in the [[Neosho River]] and [[Marais des Cygnes River]] in Kansas recovered after the severe drought of the early-to-mid 1950s. Sampling from 1957 through 1959 at six main-stem stations used electrofishing, seines, gill nets, rotenone and staining with dyes. The author reports a shift from pond- and intermittent-adapted fauna toward species of permanently flowing water, and a rapid rebound of [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Freshwater Drum]] driven by a strong 1957 year-class. The study also reports that some riffle-dwelling and stream-restricted species recovered slowly.

## Background & Description

The study addresses whether fish communities can readjust to continuous flow after intermittent conditions caused by the most severe drought in Kansas history. The introduction notes that average annual drought losses in Kansas (1930s and 1950s, about $75,000,000 in 1951 prices) exceeded average annual flood losses ($35,000,000) from 1926 to 1953. It also notes that 1951 was the wettest year on record, followed by the driest five-year period, with the driest year in 1956. In 1956-57 the city of Chanute recirculated treated sewage for potable supply.

Study rivers:
- **[[Neosho River]]**: rises in the Flint Hills and flows 281 miles in Kansas to the Arkansas River. Six stations were sampled, including the [[Upper Neosho Station]] near Council Grove, a 0.5-mile study reach that dried almost completely in 1956.
- **[[Marais des Cygnes River]]**: a tributary of the Missouri River with a more sluggish character, more mud bottom and fewer riffles. Stations were at Pomona (upper), Ottawa (middle) and the Marais des Cygnes Wildlife Refuge (lower).

Drainage areas and flow tables (Tables 1-4) show high variability; for example, the Neosho near Council Grove (250 sq mi) recorded a minimum flow of 0 cubic feet per second in several water years.

Methods included:
- Electrofishing (600-watt and 1500-watt units; night and day collections)
- Seines (4 to 60 ft)
- Gill nets (125 ft)
- Trap, hoop and fyke nets (abandoned as ineffective)
- Sodium cyanide (used infrequently)
- Rotenone (small pools, to check other methods)
- Bismarck Brown Y stain (1:20,000) for mark-recapture

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat drivers.** Fish distribution tracked habitat type. Riffle specialists, such as the [[Neosho Madtom]], [[Gravel Chub]] and [[Slender-headed Darter]], were slow to recover. Pool-dwelling and pond-associated species, such as [[Black Bullhead]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and crappies, declined once flow resumed.

**Reproduction and crowding.** The author proposes that during drought, crowding in residual pools reduced growth and reproduction. Reduced turbidity in pools exposed spawning sites and increased predation on young fish. When flow returned in 1957, expanded habitat allowed a large hatch of channel catfish and flatheads, and fish grew faster than in crowded conditions.

**Refugia.** Some stream-restricted species (such as [[Blunt-faced Shiner]], [[Mimic Shiner]], [[Spotted Bass]] and [[Fan-tailed Darter]]) probably persisted in mainstem pools during drought and then declined as tributaries re-filled.

**Mobility.** Mark-recapture data showed that [[Red Shiner]] and blunt-nosed minnows were highly mobile, while [[Fat-headed Minnow]], [[Stoneroller]], young channel catfish, [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Long-eared Sunfish]] were sedentary.

**Food.** Riffle insects were abundant throughout the study, and the author concludes food did not limit fish recovery on riffles.

## Key Findings

- **Drought losses:** Average annual drought losses in Kansas exceeded flood losses over 1926-1953. Drought losses were $75,000,000 (1951 prices) per year, versus $35,000,000 for floods.
- **Sampling scope:** Six main-stem stations were sampled (three on each river) from 1957 to 1959, with an annotated list covering a large number of species (the author lists about 70 species across both rivers).
- **Channel catfish recovery:** A large 1957 year-class followed severe reductions during the drought. Young-of-the-year reached about 9 inches by September 1959, and 1957 young were expected to contribute to the fishery in 1960-61. The author notes that Kansas channel catfish rarely live beyond seven years.
- **Flathead catfish:** Declined only slightly from 1957 to 1959. Males mature at 15-18 inches and females at 18-20 inches. The 1957 year-class reached about 11 inches by autumn 1959. Flatheads take fish and crayfish as they grow, and extended spawning in holes in clay banks.
- **Freshwater drum:** Abundance declined from 1957 to 1959, but average size increased because of a dominant 1957 year-class. Drum mature at about 12 inches.
- **Long-nosed gar:** Abundant at lower Neosho and lower Marais des Cygnes stations; numbers increased with continuous flow. Maturity at approximately 20 inches. Long lifespan and air-gulping were noted as probably ensuring survival through adverse conditions.
- **Habitat-based groups of recovery:**
  - Increased with permanent flow: gar, river carpsucker, gravel chub, mountain minnow, parrot minnow, Neosho madtom, stoneroller.
  - Decreased (clear-tributary and pond species): rosy-faced shiner, mimic shiner, spotted bass, freckled madtom, black bullhead, crappies, largemouth.
- **Neosho versus Marais des Cygnes:** The Neosho had a more diversified fauna (gravel bottom, riffles, greater gradient). The Marais des Cygnes was sluggish, with more mud bottom, and favored gizzard shad, carp, black bullhead and white crappie.
- **Endemic species:** The Neosho madtom, an undescribed species endemic to the Neosho River, was common in gravel riffles by 1959 after being absent in 1957.
- **Long-term concern:** The author reports that [[Carpiodes velifer]] has had only one Kansas record since 1924 (from the lower Neosho station in 1958). Several restricted-habitat species have disappeared or become restricted in the Wakarusa system, implying long-term environmental change.
- **Management recommendations:** Young channel catfish from years like 1957 could stock ponds without reducing adult stream populations. Suckers and carp are abundant but largely unused because current regulations preclude effective capture methods.
