## Executive Summary

This 1986 proposal letter, authored by Paul C. Marsh (ASU Center for Environmental Studies) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS), requests stocking of three imperiled native Southwestern fishes—[[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Bonytail]]—into artificial ponds at the ASU Research Park in Tempe, Arizona. The ponds, totaling ~20 surface acres, were originally designed to support a recreational fishery of introduced [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Channel Catfish]], with [[Tilapia]] stocked for aquatic vegetation control. The proposal frames these ponds as a controlled, accessible experimental system to study interactions between native and introduced fish species, addressing a critical knowledge gap in native fish recovery and management efforts across the Colorado River Basin.

## Background & Description

The document does not provide detailed morphological descriptions but specifies requested stocking sizes for native species, indicating consideration of life-stage-specific vulnerability and survivorship in mixed-species environments:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): ~5,000 individuals requested, approximately 3 inches in length (juvenile stage).
- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*): ~5,000 individuals requested, "any size" — suggesting flexibility in available hatchery stock and interest in testing multiple size classes against predation/competition pressure.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): ~15,000 individuals requested, 3–6 inches or larger — the largest requested cohort, likely reflecting either greater hatchery availability or a priority focus of the ongoing reintroduction program.

No morphological data are given for the introduced species (bass, bluegill, catfish, tilapia), as these are common, well-characterized sport and utility fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pond habitat**: Three artificial, canal-fed ponds (~20 surface acres combined) at the ASU Research Park, Tempe, Arizona. Direct connection to the local canal system creates an open pathway for additional, uncontrolled fish invasion beyond the initially stocked species.
- **Introduced species roles**:
  - [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]: recreational sport fishery targets, also serve as predator/competitor guild analogous to introduced species natives face in the wild.
  - [[Channel Catfish]]: recreational fishery species.
  - [[Tilapia]]: stocked specifically for aquatic vegetation/weed control (biological control function), not for sport.
- **Native species context**: The proposed native fishes are all endemic to the Colorado River Basin and are central to ongoing endangered species recovery efforts:
  - [[Razorback Sucker]]: subject of an active reintroduction program in Arizona; species interaction with introduced fishes is a major unresolved management question.
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Bonytail]]: species for which recovery plans were in development at the time, with introduced-fish impact assessment identified as a forthcoming focal issue.
- **Rationale for pond use as surrogate habitat**: Natural field habitats for these natives are described as large, remote, and difficult to access, with low-density introduced populations that are hard to detect; this makes controlled pond systems a valuable proxy for studying native-introduced fish interactions under realistic co-occurrence conditions.

## Key Findings

- **Central research question**: The potential negative impact of introduced fishes (bass, bluegill, catfish, tilapia) on native Colorado River Basin fishes ([[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]]) is a major unresolved issue in Southwestern native fish management.
- **Field study limitations**: Prior attempts to study these interactions in natural habitats have been hindered by: (1) large/remote/inaccessible habitat, (2) natural populations confined to artificial habitats, (3) low detectability of small introduced populations, and (4) limited funding, personnel, and equipment relative to the geographic scope required.
- **Proposed solution**: Use of the ASU Research Park ponds as a proximal, accessible, semi-controlled experimental site to observe native-introduced species interactions, leveraging existing introduced fish populations and canal-derived colonizers.
- **Stated benefits of the proposal**:
  1. Proximity to ASU campus enables efficient researcher and student access compared to remote field sites.
  2. Co-occurrence with introduced fishes mirrors wild conditions faced by natives, enabling novel ecological data collection.
  3. Surviving adult natives could supplement the existing captive gene pool for conservation breeding programs.
  4. High visibility site offers public education potential, supporting broader native species recovery and awareness goals.
