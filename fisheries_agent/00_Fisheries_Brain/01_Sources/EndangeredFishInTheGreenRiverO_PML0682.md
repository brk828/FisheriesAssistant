# Inorganic Contaminants in Irrigation Water and Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Research Information Bulletin (1994) evaluates whether inorganic contaminants in irrigation water threaten three endangered fishes of the [[Green River]] in Utah: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail]]. The work followed a Department of the Interior [[National Irrigation Water Quality Program]] study that found elevated [[Boron]], [[Selenium]], and [[Zinc]] in water, sediments, and biota in the [[Ashley Creek]]–[[Stewart Lake]] area. Laboratory 96-hour acute toxicity tests on seven inorganic contaminants showed that sensitivity did not differ significantly among the three species. Comparing toxicity values with measured field concentrations, the authors found a high potential hazard from selenite and zinc in Ashley Creek. They also warned that mixtures of contaminants below individual toxic thresholds could act additively or synergistically.

## Background & Description

The three species are endangered fishes native to the Colorado River system. The middle Green River provides important remnant habitat for them. Young-of-the-year Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker use shallow embayments at and below the mouth of Ashley Creek and the Stewart Lake outlet as nursery habitat. Irrigation return flows concentrate in these areas. Habitat use by young Bonytail is poorly known, but they probably use similar nursery habitat.

The study did not establish a direct link between the contaminants and effects on endangered fish in the field. Its purpose was to assess the hazard of inorganic contaminants in irrigation water to these species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Test conditions.** Toxicity tests used water mimicking the middle Green River near Jensen, Utah. Reported parameters include hardness of 197 ppm, alkalinity of 107 ppm, sodium of 49 ppm, chloride of 28 ppm, and sulfate of 159 ppm. Calcium was also reported.

**Test organisms.** Fish were obtained as eggs from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico. Wild fish were not used. Three life stages were tested for each species: swim-up, 1-gram, and 2-gram juveniles.

**Field hazard framework.** The authors used the margin of uncertainty, the ratio of the biological effects concentration (96-hour acute toxicity value) to the expected environmental concentration (maximum waterborne concentration). Margins below 100 indicate a high potential for environmental hazard, and margins above 1,000 generally indicate a low potential. Field concentrations came from the DOI irrigation drain water study on the middle Green River. No lithium data were available for field comparison.

**Regulatory context.** The [[Endangered Species Act]] requires a high degree of confidence, approaching 100 percent, that listed species will not be adversely affected. This standard shapes how the authors interpret single-contaminant margins.

**Population status.** No recruitment of Bonytail has been found in the Green River, and recruitment of Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker has been limited. The authors note that physical, chemical, and biological changes in recent decades have hindered recovery. Toxic effects from [[Irrigation Drainage]] may be another limiting factor.

## Key Findings

1. **Toxicity ranking.** Across all species and life stages, the overall order of toxicity from most to least toxic was: [[Vanadium]] = [[Zinc]] > selenite > lithium = uranium > selenate > boron. Significance was set at P = 0.05.

2. **No species difference.** Sensitivity to the seven inorganic contaminants did not differ significantly among the three species. No single life stage was consistently more sensitive than the others.

3. **Life-stage patterns.** Boron, lithium, selenite, and zinc were most toxic to the swim-up stage. Vanadium was most toxic to juveniles. Uranium produced identical results across all species and life stages.

4. **Margins of uncertainty.** Using the most sensitive species and life stage as the biological effects concentration, margins ranged from 85 for zinc in Ashley Creek to 8,333 for selenium as selenate. Waterborne concentrations of selenite and zinc in Ashley Creek were judged to pose a high potential hazard to these endangered fish.

5. **Mixture concern.** Larval Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish use backwaters and tributary mouths, where they encounter mixtures of inorganic contaminants. Concentrations of individual contaminants may fall below toxic thresholds yet still interact additively or synergistically. Single-contaminant hazard assessments may therefore underestimate risk.

6. **Management implication.** Given the recovery limitations and the Endangered Species Act's confidence standard, the authors urged cautious interpretation of single-contaminant hazard. Irrigation-derived contaminants are a plausible additional constraint on recovery.
