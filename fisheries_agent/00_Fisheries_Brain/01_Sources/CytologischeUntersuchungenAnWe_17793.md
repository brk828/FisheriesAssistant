# Cytological Studies of West African Killifishes: Aphyosemion and Roloffia (Cyprinodontidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This German-language cytogenetic study examines the chromosome numbers and karyotype structures of 12 species (about 20 populations) of West African killifishes in the genera [[Aphyosemion]] and [[Roloffia]], both within the subfamily [[Rivulinae]]. The karyotypes are extremely heterogeneous, with haploid numbers from n = 13 to n = 23. The author argues that the two mutation models proposed by Scheel, [[Centric Fusion]] and [[Tandem Fusion]], cannot explain this diversity. He concludes that structural mutations that leave the centromere number unchanged, especially [[Pericentric Inversion]], must also have shaped karyotype evolution. The study also reviews the taxonomic division between the two genera and their relationship to American rivulines.

## Background & Description

- **Genera:** [[Aphyosemion]] Myers, 1924 and [[Roloffia]] Stenholt Clausen, 1966 are species-rich groups of small fishes, typically 5–8 cm, with a maximum of about 15 cm. Males are brightly colored and often have elongated fin filaments; females are drab and brownish-grey. Females have an oviduct pouch around the urogenital opening.
- **Aphyosemion subgenera (Myers):** [[Aphyosemion]] s. str. (dorsal fin rays 8–12, dorsal fin set behind the anal fin), [[Fundulopanchax]] (dorsal rays 10–16, dorsal fin set at or before the anal fin, heavier gular region), and [[Callopanchax]] (dorsal rays 17–19, fins set further forward and opposite each other).
- **Generic split (Stenholt Clausen, 1966):** Species east of the [[Dahomey Gap]] retain the name Aphyosemion, while western species are placed in Roloffia. The only exception is *A. spurelli*, which is a synonym of *A. walkeri* according to Scheel (1968b). Distinguishing features include the trapezoidal versus triangular arrangement of cephalic lateral-line neuromasts, scale structures, and adhesive organs on fin rays.
- **Chromosome-based separation (Scheel, 1970):** Aphyosemion (sensu Stenholt Clausen) has n = 9–20, while Roloffia has n = 19–23. The author's own data for Roloffia give n = 20–23.
- **Bivittatum group:** *A. bivittatum* is treated as a superspecies with a relatively constant phenotype, and Stenholt Clausen ranked it as a subgenus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

These fishes are freshwater rivulines, widely kept as aquarium fishes. The sampled populations came from Sierra Leone (Roloffia), Gabon (*A. bivittatum* at East Bata; *A. australe*, probably from the Ogowe Delta, based on the aquarium trade name "Kap Lopez"), Cameroon (Mboumboula, near Duala), and Nigeria (Ijebu-Ode; *A. gulare* near Lagos). Several samples came from aquarium trade or breeder stocks, so exact collection localities are often uncertain.

The Dahomey Gap, a savanna belt separating the Guinean and Congolese rainforest blocks, is the biogeographic boundary used to separate the two genera. The author notes that the West African rivulines show greater karyotypic variability than most other teleost groups, with the salmonids as the only comparable case.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Meiosis: orcein-acetic acid squash technique (Karse, 1961), with distilled water replacing Ringer's solution for hypotonic treatment and 1% sodium citrate for 10–20 minutes in some species. Permanent slides were inferior, so fresh preparations were used and remained usable for about two weeks.
- Mitosis: gill cells following McPhail & Jones (1966), with 0.1–0.15 ml of 0.05% colchicine injected 3–6 hours before preparation. Fin cell cultures followed Lueken & Foerster (1969) in some species.
- Sampling: at least 50 metaphase plates were counted per population, and the best were photographed.

**Karyotype observations**
- *A. australe* shows ring, cross, and rod tetrads in diakinesis, with large and smaller ring tetrads and large rod-shaped and V-shaped elements.
- In *A. bivittatum*, metacentric chromosomes occur only in the n = 13 population (6 metacentrics) and the n = 20 population (2 metacentrics). The n = 18 and n = 19 populations have none, although the n = 20 population does. The very long acrocentric chromosomes in all populations show a secondary constriction near the middle.
- *R. roloffi* populations show no meiotic or mitotic differences, and mitotic metaphases show only acrocentric chromosomes.
- *R. occidentalis toddi* ("blue variant of *A. sjoestedti*") is cytologically identical to *R. occidentalis occidentalis*.
- *R. chaytori* populations, collected far apart, show no cytological differences. Four chromosomes are enlarged relative to the others.
- Tetrad types (rod, cross, and ring) vary among species, with ring tetrads dominating in *R. brueningi*.

**Evaluation of the fundamental-number (NF) hypothesis**
- Post (1965) and Kaur & Srivastava (1965) found that n = 24 is the most common haploid number among freshwater teleosts (52% of species in Post's survey). Post interpreted it as ancestral and derived other numbers by centric fusion.
- Scheel (1968b) instead proposed n = 25 as the ancestral rivuline number, derived by aneuploidy from n = 24, and used centric fusion and tandem fusion to reach the recent karyotypes. He calculated 10 NF values (14, 15, 18–25) across 37 populations of 32 species or superspecies.
- The author argues this approach fails. The *A. bivittatum* populations (n = 13 to 20) cannot be reduced to a single NF, and the NF hypothesis yields no useful result for the group.
- The number n = 24 is absent from *Roloffia* and *Aphyosemion*, but it occurs in 9 of 11 American rivuline species examined (Figure 1).

**Proposed mutation mechanisms**
- Centric fusion reduces chromosome number while keeping the number of arms constant. Tandem fusion reduces both chromosome number and arm number.
- Mutations that alter chromosome structure without changing centromere number are needed to explain the data. [[Pericentric Inversion]] is the main candidate: it can convert metacentric chromosomes into acrocentric ones (and vice versa) without changing the chromosome number. Such an inversion was documented in Muridae by Matthey (1964).
- The secondary constriction in the long acrocentric chromosomes of *Aphyosemion* may be evidence of a previous pericentric inversion.
- Other proposed mechanisms include tandem fusion between an acrocentric and a metacentric chromosome, and retention of small metacentric chromosomes produced by centric or tandem fusion.
- The author notes that aneuploidy, which Scheel invoked, is unlikely because it usually disrupts gene balance.

**Conclusions**
- The West African rivulines have highly variable karyotypes that cannot be reduced to a common fundamental number by current models.
- Further detailed studies of chromosome fine structure and meiotic pairing are needed to explain the evolution of these karyotypes.
