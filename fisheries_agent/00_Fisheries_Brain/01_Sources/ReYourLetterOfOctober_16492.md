# Re: your letter of 30 October 1989

## Executive Summary
This document is a 1989 professional correspondence from Dr. W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University, Department of Zoology) to Edward M. Hallenbeck of the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation. The letter responds to a proposal to develop a feasibility study for native fish rearing facilities on **[[Lake Mohave]]**, located on the Colorado River. Minckley strongly endorses immediate action, citing prior research indicating that **minimally prepared backwater habitats** are effective for producing large numbers of **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*). He extends this expectation to the **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*), though this specific technique had not yet been field-tested for that species at the Lake Mohave site. The letter functions as an expert endorsement urging rapid implementation of conservation infrastructure, specifically the construction of physical barriers to isolate rearing habitats.

- **[[Bonytail]]**: Referenced only by common name; a member of the endangered "big-river" Colorado River cyprinid guild (not described in this text).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Strategy**: The core ecological recommendation centers on the use of **backwater habitats** — low-energy, off-channel environments connected to or adjacent to the main reservoir/river body.
- **Habitat Preparation**: Minckley specifies that these backwaters need only be "minimally prepared," suggesting that natural or semi-natural productivity (rather than intensive hatchery infrastructure) is sufficient to support recruitment.
- **Barrier Systems**: Recommends construction of at least one, possibly two, physical barriers. These barriers likely serve to:
  - Exclude predatory non-native fishes (a known major threat to razorback sucker recruitment in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]).
  - Contain reared native fish stocks within controlled backwater zones.
- **Species Comparison**: While razorback sucker production in backwaters is described as an established, research-supported outcome, bonytail rearing in this specific habitat type is framed as a plausible but **unverified extrapolation**, since no on-site trials had occurred at Lake Mohave prior to this letter.

## Key Findings
1. **Proven Technique**: Existing research (pre-1989) demonstrated that minimally prepared backwater habitats can successfully produce large numbers of **[[Razorback Sucker]]**.
2. **Untested Extension**: The applicability of this backwater-rearing method to **[[Bonytail]]** at Lake Mohave remained an open question requiring field validation.
3. **Management Urgency**: Dr. Minckley explicitly urged the Bureau of Reclamation to minimize bureaucratic delay and proceed rapidly with barrier construction and habitat preparation.
4. **Infrastructure Requirement**: The primary physical intervention needed was the construction of one to two barriers to create isolated/controlled backwater rearing environments.
5. **Institutional Collaboration**: The letter documents an early collaborative link between academic ichthyological expertise (ASU Zoology Department) and federal water management (U.S. Bureau of Reclamation) in native fish conservation efforts on the Colorado River system.
6. **Historical Context**: This correspondence represents an early management-planning step in what would become long-term native fish conservation and augmentation programs for endangered Colorado River fishes at **[[Lake Mohave]]**.
