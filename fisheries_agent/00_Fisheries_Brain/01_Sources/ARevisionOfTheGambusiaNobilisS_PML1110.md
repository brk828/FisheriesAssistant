# A revision of the Gambusia nobilis species group with descriptions of three new species and notes on their variation, ecology, and evolution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gambusia

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic revision by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and a co-author (surname truncated in the scan) reviews the [[Gambusia Nobilis]] species group, a set of freshwater livebearers from Texas and northeastern Mexico. The authors recognize six species, three of which are described as new: [[Gambusia Geiseri]], [[Gambusia Hurtadoi]], and [[Gambusia Alvarezi]]. The study combines morphometric and meristic comparisons, gonopodial (male copulatory organ) structure, laboratory rearing, and field surveys of springs and irrigation systems.

Key conclusions concern taxonomic limits, the reliability of published localities, and conservation. One population of [[Gambusia Gaigei]] is extinct and another is near extinction, and the authors recommend a rescue effort for the surviving population. The disjunct distributions are linked to past irrigation diversions, drought, and competition with [[Gambusia Affinis]].

## Background & Description

Earlier taxonomy was unsettled. [[Hubbs 1929 Revision]] recognized four species in the group, including [[Gambusia Senilis]], and assigned a new name, G. geiseri, to a species previously called affinis. Hubbs (1926) had divided the genus *Gambusia* into subgenera (*Gambusia*, *Arthrophallus*, and *Schizophallus*). Later work questioned this arrangement. Krumholtz (1948) reported unpublished evidence from Hubbs and Walker, and from Haskins and Rosen, that the nominal species *affinis* and *holbrookii* regularly integrade and should be combined. [[Rosen and Gordon 1951]] grouped the tribe Gambusiini into five gonopodial "species groups," but these groups corresponded to three *Gambusia* subgenera and two other gambusiine genera.

Diagnostic characters of the *nobilis* group include:

- Enlarged, modified distal spines on ray 3 of the gonopodium, with terminal hooks.
- Dorsal ray counts usually 7 to 9, sometimes 10.
- Pigment features: a dusky, dark-margined caudal fin, dusky dorsal fin markings in females, and a dark streak from eye to caudal base in some forms.

The authors chose to say little about proportional body measurements because laboratory experiments showed that body proportions shift with environment. Measurements and counts follow [[Hubbs and Lagler 1947]], and the key separates wild adults using gonopodial elbow position, the form of ray 4a, dorsal ray number, and color pattern (crescentic lateral spots, predorsal streaks, and spotting on the caudal fin).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The species occupy warm springs, spring-fed pools, and rivers, many of which were altered by irrigation. Study sites include springs in [[Big Bend National Park]], the Rio San Marcos and Comal Creek in Texas, the Rio Soto la Marina drainage, and localities in Chihuahua and Coahuila. Local informants reported post-1900 changes in water connections caused by irrigation, and the disappearance of at least one river (Rio Sauz).

The authors attribute the isolation and distributional gaps among populations to:

- **Drought**, which fragmented aquatic habitat.
- **Competition** with [[Gambusia Affinis]], a related and widespread species.
- **Irrigation diversions**, which explain the disjunct distribution of several populations.

Water temperature varies seasonally in at least one spring, and dorsal ray number correlates with temperature in two species.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomy and species status**
- Six species are recognized in the *nobilis* group. Three are described as new: *G. geiseri*, *G. hurtadoi*, and *G. alvarezi*.
- Five of the six species are maintained in the laboratory and retain their morphological distinctions.
- The western records of *G. geiseri* are considered the result of introduction.
- Two published localities each for *G. nobilis* and *G. senilis* are doubted.
- A spotted race of *G. senilis* is recognized, and the authors describe its distribution as tied to past irrigation diversions.
- Three forms are suspected to be sex-reversed or to have delayed transformation. Their gonopodia resemble those of related species and genera.

**Morphological variation**
- Laboratory-raised fish differ substantially in body proportions from wild-caught fish of the same population. Laboratory stocks tend to have deeper bodies, and the authors judge these differences environmental rather than genetic.
- Color patterns, dorsal ray counts, and gonopodial structure are treated as more reliable characters than body proportions.
- Dorsal ray number varies with water temperature in two species.

**Distribution and conservation**
- Two populations of *G. gaigei* are described: one is extinct and the other is near extinction. The authors recommend an attempt to save the surviving population.
- The distribution of the group is explained by drought, competition with *G. affinis*, and irrigation-driven changes to water connections.
- Among the six species, the authors describe one as having a lower survival prospect than the others, and some species are abundant in clear spring-fed habitats but occur infrequently in other habitats.

**Methods and collections**
- Specimens were collected in Texas and Mexico, including the December 1954 and January 1955 collecting trip, with permits from Mexican authorities.
- Specimens are catalogued in museums including the University of Texas Natural History Collections (TNHC), University of Michigan Museum of Zoology (UMMZ), Museum of Comparative Zoology (MCZ), Stanford Natural History Collection (SU), U.S. National Museum (USNM), and the Mexican collection (ENCB).
- Figures show the distal gonopodial tips of *G. geiseri*, *G. nobilis*, and *G. senilis*, and the anal fin rays of laboratory-raised *G. hurtadoi*.
