# Ecology of the Aquatic Box Turtle, *Terrapene coahuila*, in Northern Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #emydidae

## Executive Summary

This study reports a field ecology program on the [[Coahuilan Box Turtle]] (*Terrapene coahuila*) conducted between December 1964 and November 1967 in the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] of northern Mexico. The species is endemic to an intermontane desert basin of about 800 km², where it lives principally in small, spring-fed marshes. Unlike its terrestrial congeners, it is the only aquatic member of the genus *Terrapene*, and its ecology more closely resembles that of aquatic emydids such as [[Painted Turtle]] (*Chrysemys picta*) and [[Slider Turtle]] (*Pseudemys scripta*). The author documents year-round activity, extended mating and egg-laying seasons, relatively high population density (about 60 adults per acre), omnivorous insect-and-plant feeding, and restricted intermarsh movement. The turtle is judged rare under IUCN criteria, with drainage and collecting identified as the main threats.

## Background & Description

*Terrapene coahuila* is a semi-aquatic box turtle described by Schmidt and Owens (1944). Earlier work by Webb, Minckley, and Craddock (1963) surveyed its habitats, habits, and distribution, but data on food, reproduction, and populations were lacking. The genus *Terrapene* includes 4 species and 11 subspecies, and *T. coahuila* is ecologically and evolutionarily unusual within it. The study was based on 87 field days and on examination of 59 preserved specimens, supported by [[Mark-Recapture Methods]] and laboratory dissections. The author was supported by National Science Foundation grants to [[W. L. Minckley]] and collected under a Mexican scientific permit.

Basin endemics include *T. coahuila*, [[Pseudemys scripta taylori]], and [[Trionyx ater]]; *Trionyx spiniferus emoryi* also occurs there. The basin also shows high endemism among snails, crustaceans, and fishes (Minckley 1969).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geography and habitat.** The Cuatro Ciénegas basin (ca. 30 × 40 km) lies on the [[Mesa del Norte]] within the [[Chihuahuan Desert]], at about 740 m elevation, with surrounding mountains 1500–3000 m high. Annual rainfall is 0–200 mm. The intensive study area lay about 9.5 km SW of the town of Cuatro Ciénegas, at the bajada of Sierra de San Marcos.

**Marsh communities.** Marshes are distinct aquatic communities bordered abruptly by saline grassland (*Distichlis* spp.) and shrubs (*Allenrolfea occidentalis*, *Prosopis juliflora*). *Eleocharis rostellata* provides about 70% of vegetative cover, supplemented by *Chara* mats. Of 11 marshes in the study area, 7 bore conspicuous *Baccharis glutinosa* on their margins. Marshes are oriented north-south, fed by springs from the south, and no marsh dried completely during the study. Total marsh area in the main study area was 11,019 m² (2.72 acres); individual marshes ranged from 56 m² to 8,745 m².

**Thermal environment.** Water in sinkholes (posos) averaged about 33.5 °C and remained above 30 °C year-round. Mud beneath the surface can be about 5 °C cooler than surface water, providing refuge from midday heat. Water in marsh 8 reached 38.8 °C in October 1966.

**Threats.** Canal construction drained the Posos de la Becerra marsh complex in December 1964, reducing it from about 10 km² to under 0.2 km². Grass burning, horse and mule grazing, and specimen collecting are additional pressures.

**Associated fauna.** Fishes in marshes included *Gambusia marshi*, *G. longispinis*, *Cyprinodon bifasciatus*, and *Cichlasoma* spp. The author notes that the turtle is the largest reptile in many of these marshes.

## Key Findings

**Activity and thermal biology**
- Active year-round except during brief environmental extremes; activity is restricted in winter (estimated December–February).
- Cloacal temperatures closely tracked water temperatures. Voluntary limits in marshes were 14.8 °C (January, minimum) and 33.5 °C (April, maximum).
- Summer activity is concentrated at dawn, dusk, and night; midday inactivity is common.
- Basking was documented on 21 December 1965, when land-caught turtles were up to 14.0 °C above air temperature.
- Field heat-tolerance experiment: distress at about 35.0 °C; no turtles recorded in marshes with water above about 37.8 °C.
- Minimum effective temperature for movement is estimated at about 12.0 °C.

**Reproduction**
- Mating recorded from September to June, most frequently in March and April; copulations also documented in October, November, and December, usually in shallow water.
- Ovulation begins around early April; egg laying runs from May to early September.
- Clutches of 2 or 3 eggs predominate; overall mean clutch size 2.3 (range 1–4).
- About 53% of July–August females were judged capable of a second clutch and 35% of a third.
- Mean annual reproductive potential for the third-clutch group was 6.8 eggs/female per season.
- Mean egg mass was 5.66 g, lighter than eggs of northern *T. carolina* and *T. ornata*.
- Sexual maturity: males at mean carapace length 109.5 ± 2.2 mm (smallest mature 93.1 mm); females at 101.6 ± 2.1 mm (range 90.7–147.5 mm); males mature at smaller size than females.

**Feeding**
- Diet is opportunistic and omnivorous. Of 45 stomachs with food, insects made up 50.7% and plant material 45.6% of volume.
- Stratiomyid fly larvae were the most frequent animal food (about 64% of stomachs).
- *Eleocharis rostellata* occurred in 64.4% of stomachs.
- Fish made up only 0.5% of volume; fishes were ignored when available alive.

**Movement and population**
- 169 turtles were marked; 36% were recaptured 271 times.
- Mean distance between successive captures was 12.8 m; females (61% of movements) were not significantly different from males.
- About 21% of recaptured turtles moved between marshes, probably overland.
- Population density was about 60 adults per acre (148/ha) across the main marsh area, comparable to *Chrysemys picta* and *Pseudemys scripta* and higher than *T. carolina* and *T. ornata*.
- Density estimates from three mark-recapture methods ranged from 53.7 to 62.9 turtles per acre (e.g., Schnabel estimate 164 individuals; 95% CI 135–208).
- Sex ratio was 1.00 male : 1.34 female overall (not significantly different from 1:1 in any monthly division except a marginal August deviation, p > 0.05).

**Conservation**
- Drainage of Posos de la Becerra may have eliminated about 3,700–7,400 turtles.
- The species is classed as rare under IUCN definitions, and potentially endangered.
- The author recommended: assessing irrigation projects near prime habitat; restricting canal construction that drains marshes; and legislating collection limits to scientific purposes with official permission.
- More than 100 preserved specimens were already held in U.S. museums, reducing the need for further collecting.
