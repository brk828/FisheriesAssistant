# Status of the Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) in the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 review by W. L. [[Minckley]] (Arizona State University) assesses the status of the [[Razorback Sucker]], an endemic "big-river" fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The lower basin population is reduced to scattered individuals outside [[Lake Mohave]], where large adults persist but no recruitment has been detected since the mid-1950s. Predation by introduced fishes is judged the most important factor in the species' decline, with habitat alteration from dams, diversions and water development as underlying causes. The paper also reviews the historical distribution, sampling results, age and growth, sexual dimorphism, fecundity, disease, and early captive propagation efforts at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The species was described by Abbott (1861) as *Xyrauchen texanus*; it is one of four endemic "big-river" fishes of the Colorado basin, along with [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]]. As of 1983 it had not been listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] despite its decline.
- **Morphology and sexual dimorphism:** Males have breeding tubercles on the head, anal fin and caudal fin, plus a dark-pink ventral coloration during spawning (January through March). Females are larger than males; based on five females, four criteria (pelvic and anal fin length, urogenital papillus shape, and tubercles) differed significantly between sexes at the 0.05 level.
- **Measurement conventions:** Lengths were given as total length (TL), with standard length (SL = 0.788 ± 0.012 TL in 110 fish, 42–69 cm TL) used for morphometric comparisons and fecundity.
- **Study context:** Field work took place in 1975, 1977 and 1979–82 across Lake Mohave, below Hoover Dam, Lake Mead, and Havasu. Voucher specimens are housed at the Arizona State University Collection of Fishes (ASU).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Mohave:** Created in 1950 behind [[Davis Dam]], this reservoir is inhabited mostly by introduced fishes; only two native species (razorback sucker and bonytail) persist as a small fraction of the fauna. Carp dominated trammel and gill-net catches in summer.
- **Habitat use:** Largest catches came in water up to 5.0 m deep over gravel or cobble bottoms. Razorbacks were strongly tethered to bottom habitats and did not appear inshore.
- **Historical range:** Records extended throughout the mainstream Colorado, the lower Gila (near Yuma, Phoenix and Casa Grande), the Verde, Salt, and upper San Pedro rivers, and the Salton Sea, where it was extirpated by rising salinity by 1929.
- **Reservoir persistence:** Large fish persisted for decades in reservoirs including [[Roosevelt Lake]] (c. 1930s–1950) and Saguaro Lake (to c. 1949, drained 1950s), but the species appeared to be disappearing from Lake Mead and Lake Havasu.
- **Threats:** Dewatering, channelization, impoundment, cold hypolimnetic releases from high dams, predation by introduced carp, channel catfish, centrarchids and mosquitofish, and parasite and disease impacts.

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** Lake Mohave remains the stronghold, with razorbacks comprising about **12.5%** of all fishes taken by trammel netting from 1974–82; no recruitment was detected in that period despite observed spawning. Elsewhere, the species is reduced to scattered individuals.
- **Age and growth:** Growth averaged about 0.5 cm per year since 1964. Females ranged 47–74 cm TL and males 37–81 cm. Scale-based age estimates were at least 11–14 years, with maxima over 20 years; the author estimates current Lake Mohave fish hatched during reservoir filling in the early 1950s and are nearly 30 years old.
- **Fecundity:** In five ripe females (39.1–57.0 cm SL), total ova ranged from 74,600 to 144,000 (mean 100,800). Relative fecundity was uniform, at 1,680 to 1,908 ova/cm SL (mean 1,812), which the author considers evidence that fish are reproductively capable.
- **Sex ratios:** Offshore trammel samples were female-biased (e.g., 0.29–0.86 females in 1975), while spawning aggregations were male-biased.
- **Disease and injury:** Injury and disease incidence was high. Many individuals were blind in one or both eyes, and some had eye orbits overgrown by epidermis. Bacterial infections (*Aeromonas hydrophila*), *Myxosoma* sp., and the parasitic copepod *Lernaea* sp. were recorded. Bacterial infections were highest during and after breeding.
- **Hybridization:** Hybrids with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] were captured in 1975 and 1982, all large (>50 cm TL).
- **Predation:** The authors consider predation on early life stages by introduced fishes the major cause of decline. Razorbacks are naive to predators, and reservoir-breeding fish suffer egg and larval predation by carp and centrarchids.
- **Propagation:** Captive breeding started in 1974 at Willow Beach. Fish were moved to Dexter National Fish Hatchery in 1981, which produced about 20,000 fingerlings (~8 cm TL). More than 1.8 million larvae were produced in 1982, and stocking occurred in Arizona streams (e.g., Gila, Salt, Verde and Oak Creek).
- **Conservation outlook:** Successful reintroduction could create riverine stocks, especially in stream reaches where non-native fishes are rare. The author argues that reservoirs can sustain persistent adult populations without successful recruitment, and that interactions between native and introduced species must be managed for recovery.
