## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary scientific study but a formal comment letter submitted by the Colorado River Indian Tribes (CRIT) Water Resources Department in response to a Biological and Conference Opinion issued regarding U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (BOR) maintenance activities on the Lower Colorado River, from Lake Mead to the Southerly International Border. The letter addresses jurisdictional, management, and scientific concerns tied to the protection of endangered native fish species, focusing heavily on the role of entrainment at diversions/canals and the dominant threat posed by non-native predatory fish. While it lacks direct morphological or population data, it provides valuable context on the applied conservation and regulatory framework governing native fish protection in this reach of the [[Colorado River]].

## Background & Description

Likely referenced (but unnamed) species endemic to this reach of the Colorado River Basin include members of the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and [[Humpback Chub]] complex, all of which are federally listed and commonly subject to Biological Opinions in this region.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat scope**: The Opinion under discussion covers the Lower Colorado River mainstem from [[Lake Mead]] to the Southerly International Border, including designated critical habitat for listed native fish.
- **Anthropogenic pressures identified**:
  - **Entrainment risk**: Native fish (and non-native predatory fish) are subject to entrainment at BOR-managed diversions, canal systems, and power plant intakes.
  - **Irrigation infrastructure**: The CRIT Irrigation System, diverting water at Headgate Rock Dam, is highlighted as a zone where entrained fish may be affected, though CRIT disputes BOR jurisdiction over this internal tribal infrastructure.
  - **Non-native predatory fish**: Identified in the letter as the "single most important factor causing endangerment of the native fish," implying a niche-level competitive/predatory displacement dynamic between introduced and native species in the river system.
- **Trust and jurisdictional boundaries**: The ecological management boundary is legally and administratively divided between BOR-controlled river/critical habitat zones and CRIT-controlled Reservation water deliveries, creating a fragmented management landscape for the shared aquatic ecosystem.

## Key Findings

- CRIT asserts that BOR's regulatory jurisdiction should end at the point of water diversion into the CRIT Irrigation System (Headgate Rock Dam headworks), and that statements in the Opinion extending oversight to Reservation-controlled water delivery and entrainment exceed BOR's legal authority.
- CRIT argues that a **Tribal management plan** should be recognized as an equally valid alternative to a federal management plan for addressing entrainment and native fish protection, invoking the BOR's trust responsibility to fund Tribally-led studies and management.
- The letter objects to language that could require **shutdown of power plants or closure of diversion gates** as an incidental take mitigation measure, calling for explicit assurances against such operational shutdowns.
- CRIT contends that the Reasonable and Prudent Measures (RPMs) in the Opinion are **misdirected**, focusing on entrainment control and power plant intake protection — measures that affect only a small percentage of fish loss — while failing to address the dominant threat: **non-native predatory fish**.
- The letter calls for the Opinion's priorities to be restructured to either actively pursue **non-native predator eradication/control** or to formally acknowledge that native species will remain functionally restricted to isolated refugia/preserves under current management strategy.
- CRIT emphasizes its own long-standing conservation practices along the river, citing efforts to rehabilitate Reservation lands to support both native and non-native wildlife, positioning Tribal stewardship as complementary to (or exceeding) federal conservation efforts on the [[Lower Colorado River]].
