# Preferred Temperature of Yearling Colorado Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #physeteridae

## Background & Description

**Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)** is the largest native minnow in North America. It was historically widespread in the mainstem Colorado and [[Green River]] and large tributaries such as the Gila, San Juan, Gunnison and [[Yampa River]]. Construction of large dams from the 1930s through the 1960s is presumed to have caused a rapid decline, and the species is now listed as endangered. It persists only in the upper basin, where dam and land/water-use effects are weaker.

The study tested **yearling** fish hatched in July 1979 from hatchery-reared adults at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona). Fish were transferred to the Utah Water Research Laboratory in July 1980 and measured 7.5 to 10 cm total length.

**Sperm whale (*Physeter catodon*)** is a deep-diving toothed whale. Historically it was taken by whalers in the Gulf of Mexico during the 18th and 19th centuries. Sightings have been rare since, with only six reported in the previous 30 years (four off the Mississippi Delta, two in the Florida Straits) and none recently from the western Gulf.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature as a master factor.** The authors cite [[Brett]] (1971), who called temperature the "ecological master factor" among abiotic variables. Because preferred temperatures reflect thermal optima for biological processes, reducing the water volume within a species' preferred range can reduce usable habitat. The proposed mechanism for squawfish decline is that dam operations altered annual temperature cycles.

**Preference concepts.** Fish in a thermal gradient tend to concentrate in a narrow temperature band, a behavior called [[Behavioral Thermoregulation]]. Acute preference, measured within about two hours, is strongly shaped by prior acclimation. After about 24 hours, fish move to a species-specific [[Final Preferendum]], as defined by [[Fry]] (1947), where preferred temperature equals acclimation temperature.

**Dams and cold tailwaters.** Laboratory data suggest that squawfish and other endemic species would avoid cold reservoir tailwaters. The authors propose this avoidance partly explains the disappearance of native fishes from the Green River below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], where native Colorado fishes have not been found from the dam to the warmer Yampa River confluence since the dam closed. Summer water temperatures across much of the drainage have also declined from historical values.

**Sperm whale habitat.** Most sightings were in the western Gulf of Mexico, in deep water near the edge of the continental shelf. Aerial surveys tracked sea surface temperature with an infrared radiometer, but the paper's text on environmental correlates is truncated in this excerpt.

## Key Findings

### Colorado squawfish thermal preference

- **Design:** Fish were acclimated to 14, 20 and 26 °C and tested in a horizontal [[Thermal Gradient Trough]] (5 m × 25 cm × 5–10 cm deep). Twenty fish were tested per acclimation temperature. Fish were held at 20 °C and moved by 1 °C per day to their acclimation temperature.
- **Acute preferenda (means of individual modes):**
  - 14 °C acclimated: **21.9 °C** (± 0.74 SE)
  - 20 °C acclimated: **27.6 °C** (± 0.44 SE)
  - 26 °C acclimated: **23.7 °C** (± 0.64 SE)
- **Significance:** ANOVA on individual modes gave P = 0.01. Fisher's LSD showed all three acclimation groups differed from one another (P = 0.01 or 0.05).
- **Final preferendum:** Estimated visually from the intersection of the acute-preferendum curve with the line of equality, at **25 °C**.
- **Data quality:** Individual fish were screened for positional bias against control runs. Only three of sixty individuals were rejected. Most fish selected temperature rather than trough position. Controls showed a clear end-of-tank bias.
- **Comparison with other endemics:** Estimated final preferenda were 25 °C for adult squawfish, 23–24 °C for [[Razorback Sucker]] and 24 °C for [[Humpback Chub]].
- **Conclusion:** Thermal preference data support the view that altered temperature regimes below dams reduce suitable habitat for endangered endemic fishes.

### Sperm whale distribution in the Gulf of Mexico

- **Survey effort:** Aerial surveys were flown off Corpus Christi, Brownsville, Marsh Island and Naples, from August 1979 to March 1981, on a roughly bimonthly schedule. Each bimonthly transect series totaled 2,664 km, extending up to 222 km offshore. Surveys were supplemented by [[Research Vessel Gyre]] logbook entries and photographs from a sportfishing boat near the Mississippi River Delta.
- **Sightings:** Sperm whales were recorded on 17 occasions. The authors counted 47 adults or subadults and 12 calves, in groups of 1 to 14 animals. Aerial surveys yielded 35 sightings and shipboard cruises yielded 24 more individuals, consistent with the total of 59.
- **Distribution:** Most sightings occurred in the western Gulf, in deep water near the shelf edge. The authors note that the 18th–19th century whaling record contrasts with the scarcity of 20th-century sightings.
- **Purpose:** The data were reported to improve understanding of the species' distribution and status in the Gulf, where information is scarce.
