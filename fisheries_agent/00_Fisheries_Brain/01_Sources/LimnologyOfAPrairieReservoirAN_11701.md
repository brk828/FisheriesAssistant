# Limnology of a Prairie Reservoir: A Newly Created Impoundment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #reservoir-limnology

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation technical report (R-92-14, October 1992) by Davine Lieberman documents a two-year limnological study (April 1989 to May 1991) of [[Calamus Reservoir]], a young impoundment created by [[Calamus Dam]] on the Calamus River in the Sand Hills of central Nebraska. The reservoir reached full pool in 1988, and the study captured its early high-productivity phase. Under [[Carlson Trophic State Index]] scoring, the reservoir is classified as eutrophic (mean TSI = 65.07). Summer oxygen depletion near the dam and episodic un-ionized ammonia exceedances were recorded. The report also includes an appended [[Creel Survey]] by the [[Nebraska Game and Parks Commission]], which documents a shift from a northern pike fishery to a walleye-dominated fishery. The authors conclude that long-term monitoring is needed before management decisions are based on the reservoir's productivity.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Calamus Dam (completed 1985) forms a reservoir of about 2,083 ha at full pool, 16 km long and 2.4 km wide, with 58 km of shoreline and maximum depth near 23 m at the dam. It is part of the [[Pick-Sloan Missouri Basin]] North Loup Irrigation Project, which irrigates about 21,040 ha (the creel appendix gives 21,457 ha).
- **Hydrology:** The Calamus River is about 88% groundwater-fed. Mean inflow was 6.7 m³/s. Full-pool flushing time was 211 days, falling to 105 days at maximum drawdown. Annual drawdown averages about 5.6 m.
- **Reservoir aging:** The report frames the reservoir as a new impoundment in the "trophic upsurge" phase, which may last 5 to 7 years. It contrasts this with natural lake succession and notes that most reservoirs are less than 60 years old.
- **Sand Hills context:** Sand Hills lakes are commonly eutrophic, phosphorus-limited and weakly stratified, with low Secchi transparency.
- **Hatchery:** The [[Calamus State Fish Hatchery]], downstream of the dam, began production in fall 1991 and plans cool-water species such as northern pike, muskellunge, tiger muskie and walleye.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting and stratification.** The reservoir is monomictic and generally well mixed. Stratification is extremely weak, driven by prevailing winds and fluctuating levels. Mean inflow temperature was 13.7 °C, and inflow temperatures did not exceed 25 °C in summer.

**Oxygen dynamics.** Surface and inflow oxygen stayed above 93% saturation. Bottom-water depletion occurred near the dam. Decomposition of inundated sod, trees and soil, together with zooplankton grazing, was proposed as the cause. Hydrogen sulfide odor was noted at the lower station in August 1989. The authors compare this to Sherman Reservoir and to [[R. B. Russell Reservoir]] in Georgia, which showed severe first-year hypolimnetic anoxia.

**Nutrients and water quality.** Total phosphorus was highest in the inflow and decreased down the reservoir through sedimentation. Total Kjeldahl nitrogen was high throughout and increased toward the dam. Nitrate was unusually low for a diatom-dominated system, and the authors treat the nitrogen-limitation interpretation with caution. Water is soft (alkalinity 52 to 61 mg/L as CaCO₃). Specific conductance was 131 to 146 µS/cm. Redox potential was 340 to 358 mV.

**Macrophytes and benthos.** Aquatic plant growth is limited by water-level fluctuation, and the littoral zone is poorly developed. Benthic density was low (mean 119 to 172 individuals per m² across reservoir stations), dominated by [[Chironomidae]] and oligochaetes. Sandy substrate supports few organisms.

**Fishery.** Initial fish included largemouth bass, bluegill, yellow perch and northern pike. Walleye, wipers, crappie, alewife and yellow perch were later stocked. The creel survey indicates the fishery's transition from northern pike to walleye and channel catfish, with bluegill and panfish declining due to lack of littoral habitat.

## Key Findings

**Trophic status**
- Mean [[Carlson Trophic State Index]] = 65.07 (eutrophic). Components: TSI Secchi 61.36, TSI total phosphorus 66.04, TSI chlorophyll a 67.80.
- Mean chlorophyll a ranged from 31.0 to 42.2 mg/m³ across reservoir stations. Secchi depth was below 2.0 m. Total phosphorus was above 0.050 mg/L at most stations.
- Most Sand Hills lakes are eutrophic. Calamus Reservoir has higher chlorophyll and lower total phosphorus than most comparison reservoirs (Table 17).

**Dissolved oxygen and ammonia**
- Summer bottom oxygen fell as low as 0.04 mg/L near the dam in August 1990. In August 1989, the lower station fell to 1.8 mg/L at the bottom and 4.1 mg/L at 7 m.
- Un-ionized ammonia exceeded [[EPA Freshwater Aquatic Life Criteria]] at the upper station (April and June 1989) and at the lower station (April and August 1989, June 1990, January 1991).

**Phytoplankton and zooplankton**
- 175 phytoplankton species were identified. [[Diatoms]] made up 99% of reservoir biovolume. The dominant taxa were [[Stephanodiscus]], Melosira and Fragilaria. The blue-green [[Anabaena]] dominated in November 1990.
- 28 zooplankton species were identified. Rotifers made up 59 to 77% of zooplankton. Copepods were second except at the upper station, where cladocerans ranked second. Peak mid-station density was 184.4 individuals/L.

**Management conclusions**
- The authors state that results reflect only two years and the reservoir's 4th and 5th years of age. Productivity is expected to decline as the reservoir ages.
- Future monitoring should track bottom-water oxygen, un-ionized ammonia, chlorophyll a and trophic indices.
- Fishery yield in early reservoir years should not be used as a basis for long-term management (citing [[Kimmel and Groeger]] 1986).

**Creel survey (Appendix B)**
- The creel survey covered 1 July 1989 to 30 June 1991. Estimated total effort was 37,184 angler hours (the total table is partly garbled in the scan; figures are reported as given).
- The northern pike harvest fell from about 10.3 fish per day to 2.0 per day. Walleye became the preferred species, with roughly 40% of anglers seeking walleye.
- Channel catfish harvest rose from 163 fish (TP-3) to 1,588 fish (TP-6). Panfish harvest remained very low.
- The creel was a baseline, and a seven-fold increase in fish harvest was projected based on [[Jenkins and Morris]] (1979) predictions.
- The economic travel cost analysis (Appendix B, Segment 3) gave a consumer surplus of $47.66 per trip for all anglers, based on 3,266 calculated trips.
- The authors note that R² values (0.043 to 0.616) are low and that the data need further refinement.
