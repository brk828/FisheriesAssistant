# Fish Culture and Stocking in Colorado, 1872–1978

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife report (Division Report No. 12, June 1985, by William J. Wiltzius) is a historical account of private, federal and state fish culture and the stocking of fishes in Colorado from 1872 to 1978. It was written to document the introduction and stocking of exotic fishes into the Colorado River system for ongoing legal proceedings, and it fills a gap because pisciculture history in Colorado had never been adequately reported. The author compiled hatchery histories, legislation, sportsmen's group records, annual stocking totals and earliest-known introduction dates for many species. The document does not present a new field study; its data are archival and largely drawn from newspapers, Fish Commissioner reports and federal records.

## Background & Description

Colorado's snowmelt-fed rivers historically held robust native cutthroat trout populations: the [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] (South Platte and Arkansas drainages), the [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], the [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]] and the [[Yellowfin Cutthroat Trout]] (known only from Twin Lakes near Leadville, and now believed extinct). Nomenclature follows Behnke (1979). Of 89 fish taxa (including subspecies) expected in Colorado, 54 were native, 2 were potential residents and 33 had been introduced, with more introduced since.

Pisciculture in Colorado began privately. [[James M. Broadwell]] reared trout near the South Platte River from 1870 and hatched 10,000 eggs from Boscobel, Wisconsin, in 1872, probably the first [[Eastern Brook Trout]] introduction to the state. Territorial laws of 1870 banned explosives and poisons for taking fish, and the 1872 and 1874 acts encouraged private culture. The [[Fish Breeders Association]] formed in Denver on July 1, 1874. A State Fish Commissioner position was created in 1876 and filled by [[Wilson E. Sisty]] in 1877. The first state hatchery opened near Henderson (the "Denver Hatchery") in late 1881. Federal involvement began with the 1871 creation of the U.S. Fish Commissioner position under [[Spencer F. Baird]], and the [[Leadville Federal Hatchery]] opened in 1889–1890.

Historic figures profiled include [[Gordon Land]], described by the author as Colorado's most influential 19th-century pisciculturist; [[William A. Bell]] at Manitou Park; [[William H. Cushman]] at Green Lake; [[Colonel George De La Vergne]]; [[General John Pierce]]; [[John Law]]; [[W. T. Kirkpatrick]]; and [[William Radcliffe]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report documents major habitat degradation. Blasting with dynamite, unscreened irrigation ditches, sawdust discharge, mine tailings and smelter chemicals, and silt from irrigated fields all reduced trout populations. By 1881 blasting had nearly destroyed trout in the Roaring Fork and reduced them in the Eagle and Williams Fork tributaries. Irrigation ditches grew from less than 500 miles before 1866 to about 6,000 miles by 1889. Enforcement was weak: deputies were unpaid, and sportsmen's associations such as the [[Colorado Game and Fish Protective Association]] (formed May 1882) were formed to assist enforcement and lobby for legislation.

Hatchery production relied on Colorado's abundant spring water. Hatcheries were often sited at springs near Henderson, Gunnison, Durango and Leadville. Early state feed included frozen carp seined by commercial fishers, later replaced by a dry pellet diet developed in the 1950s. The report also describes the aquarium railroad car of the U.S. Fish Commission and the later shift from wooden to concrete and steel facilities.

## Key Findings

- **Earliest documented introductions:** brook trout from Wisconsin eggs (Broadwell, 1872); Chinook salmon (Cushman, 1874); German carp (Sisty, 1879); Atlantic salmon (1881); rainbow trout from California eggs at the federal McCloud River station (1882, 10,000 eggs shipped to Sisty).
- **Brook trout:** [[Dr. William A. Bell]] obtained 100,000 eggs in each of 1874 and 1875 from New York for Manitou Park, a venture he said cost him over $5,000.
- **Rainbow trout:** first introduced from California in 1882, and became the most abundantly stocked trout in Colorado. Widespread distribution followed state and federal egg-collecting along the Gunnison River, Emerald Lake and the Grand Mesa lakes in the early 1900s.
- **Legislation:** 1870 territorial ban on explosives and poisons; 1872 provision allowing seine-free taking of trout for propagation; 1874 act protecting private propagation waters, with fines of $50 to $200; 1879 sawdust and closed-season revisions.
- **State appropriations:** Fish Commissioner expenses rose from $100 to $1,000 in 1879. The 1881 hatchery appropriation was $2,750 for site and buildings plus $2,500 for eggs and operations.
- **State hatcheries:** Table 8 lists 60+ state hatchery or production units from 1881 onward. Of 15 operating in 1916, only six were state-owned (Denver, Durango, Steamboat Springs, Glenwood Springs, Del Norte, Buena Vista), with the rest privately owned or leased.
- **Stocking totals:** state salmonid distribution reached about 19.1 million (1914) and 22.3 million (1915); state cold- and warmwater totals reached 31.5 million coldwater and 23.9 million warmwater in 1969.
- **Forest Service:** distributed 3.5–8.5 million fish per year in 1920–1939 (Table 6), with figures given without species or hatchery detail after 1928.
- **Nomenclature:** early "catfish", "pickerel" and "wall-eyed pike" records often denoted bullheads, sauger or other species, and mixed-species lots were sometimes distributed.
- **Conclusion:** the author notes that many stocking records are unreliable for individual species because state and federal distributions may double-count the same fish, and no stocking data are updated beyond 1978.
