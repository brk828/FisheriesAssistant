# An Epidemic of Leeches on Fishes in Rock River

#parasitology #hirudinea #piscicolidae #freshwater-ecology #commercial-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This bulletin documents an unprecedented epidemic of the fish leech [[Piscicola punctata]] on [[Red-Mouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinella*) in [[Rock River]], Illinois during the winter of 1925-1926. The infestation was remarkable both for its intensity (up to 50+ leeches per fish, averaging ~20) and its novelty—fishermen reported no similar occurrence in the previous half-century. The epidemic exhibited strong seasonal patterns, beginning in January when water temperatures were 0-1°C and ending abruptly in late March-April when temperatures rose above 4°C. A smaller recurrence in winter 1926-1927 suggested the phenomenon might not persist. The infestation affected commercial fish yields and market quality, as infested fish showed raw, bleeding wounds and inflamed flesh where leeches attached.

## Background & Description

[[Piscicola punctata]] Verrill is described as the commonest freshwater fish leech in North America, typically found in ponds and lakes of northern states and the Mississippi Valley. However, prior to this epidemic it had not been collected during two years of continuous aquatic biological work on [[Rock River]], despite handling thousands of [[Red-Mouth Buffalo]] and collecting hundreds of bottom fauna samples.

**Morphological Description:**
- Size: 15-25 mm (extensible), diameter 2-3 mm
- Narrow neck-like constriction behind anterior sucker
- Posterior sucker wider than body when expanded, 3-4× diameter of anterior sucker
- One pair of eyes with conspicuous bar-like pigment cups placed obliquely
- Two smaller posterior pigment spots resembling second eye pair
- Coloration: Translucent greenish ground-color when empty, densely covered with minute black specks on dorsal and ventral surfaces
- Eight or more pale lateral spots connected by pale stripe
- When blood-filled, appears speckled brownish

The leech feeds on both blood and mucus from fish hosts. Specimens were identified by Professor J. Percy Moore.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Host Specificity:**
The epidemic primarily affected [[Red-Mouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinella*), with 38 of 45 fish (84%) infested in the first documented haul (February 25, 1926). Over the epidemic period, 2,831 red-mouth buffalo averaging 2 pounds were examined in a 20-mile stretch near [[Rockford]].

Secondary hosts with lighter infestations included:
- [[Small-Mouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*)
- [[Common Red-Horse]] (*Moxostoma aureolum*) - less affected despite greater abundance
- [[Mongrel Buffalo]], [[European Carp]], [[Common River Carp]] (quillback), [[Common Sucker]] - small numbers

**Attachment Sites:**
Leeches preferentially attached in:
- Axils of fins
- Around anus (where cuticle is thin)
- Sites of injury (missing scales, cuts)
- Leeches at penetration sites were filled with blood and mucus; others contained only mucus or were empty

**Geographic Distribution:**
The epidemic was documented at multiple [[Rock River]] locations: [[Rockford]], [[Oregon]], and [[Sterling]]. Fish in sloughs and backwaters showed heavier infestations (>100 leeches per fish) than those in main channel (~20 per fish). No evidence of similar epidemics in other Illinois streams ([[Illinois River]], [[Mississippi River]], [[Wabash River]]) during the same period.

**Seasonal Temperature Relationships:**
The epidemic showed strict temperature dependence:

| Period | Water Temp (°C) | Leech Status |
|--------|----------------|--------------|
| November 1925 | >1°C | Absent (0/1,481 fish) |
| January 1926 | 0-1°C | Infestation begins |
| February-March 1926 | 0-1°C | Peak epidemic |
| March 18-24, 1926 | Rise to 6.7°C | Decline begins |
| April 17, 1926 | ~12°C | 2 leeches on 51 fish |
| April 30, 1926 | ~12°C | Last leech observed |
| November 30, 1926 | <1°C | Reappearance (reduced numbers) |
| March 22, 1927 | 4.4°C | Rapid disappearance |

**Life History Observations:**
- Leeches found on [[Elodea]] vegetation in [[Long Slough]] on April 29 appeared empty and active (contrast to sluggish, blood-filled state on fish)
- Young leeches (8-10 mm) first appeared November 30, 1926, were translucent and apparently fed only on mucus
- Rapid growth to adult size (20-30 mm) during January
- Larger individuals fed on both blood and mucus
- Moore (1912) reports stalked cocoons attached to water plant stems

**Color Variant:**
A provisionally identified color variant of *P. punctata* was found on [[Small-Mouth Black Bass]] and [[Wall-Eyed Pike]]:
- Smaller than buffalo leech
- Dark brown instead of iridescent pale green
- Occurred almost exclusively on head (50 found on mouth/branchial region of 1.25 lb bass)
- More active, closely applied to host throughout body length
- Fed only on mucus (no blood observed)
- Curled tightly when preserved (vs. straight in buffalo leech)
- Same seasonal pattern as buffalo leech

## Key Findings

1. **Epidemic Characteristics:** An unprecedented epidemic of *Piscicola punctata* occurred on [[Red-Mouth Buffalo]] in [[Rock River]] during winter 1925-1926, with 84% of fish infested and average loads of 20 leeches per fish (range 1-50+, up to >100 in backwaters). This represented the first such occurrence in living memory of local fishermen (>50 years).

2. **Strict Seasonal Pattern:** The epidemic was confined to periods when water temperature was 0-1°C (January-March), with leeches appearing after freeze-over and disappearing rapidly when temperatures rose above 4°C. This pattern repeated in winter 1926-1927 but at much reduced intensity (~10% of previous year's numbers).

3. **Host Condition Effects:** "Thin" fish bore more leeches than "fat" ones, though causality was uncertain. Attachment sites showed raw, bleeding wounds surrounded by inflamed flesh. Fungous infections were observed in April in wounds made by leeches.

4. **Commercial Impact:** The epidemic affected fish yield and market quality. Fishermen removed leeches before sale; many thin, heavily infested buffalo were discarded. Boat bottoms handling a few hundred pounds of fish were "almost completely covered" with leeches. The mere presence of leeches was "repulsive to buyers."

5. **Post-Epidemic Absence:** Despite >100 collections in various river habitats following the epidemic, not a single *P. punctata* individual was found, suggesting the species may return to rarity in [[Rock River]] fauna.

6. **Comparison with Other Piscicola Species:** Unlike *P. punctata* in Rock River, other species show different patterns:
   - *[[Piscicola milneri]]* parasitizes [[Whitefish]] year-round in [[Lake Michigan]] and [[Lake Nipigon]], found "by hundreds" on hosts
   - *[[Piscicola geometra]]* (European) attacks fish at all seasons, transmits blood parasite *Trypanoplasma cyprini*, and is a well-known pest in fish culture

7. **Ecological Uniqueness:** This represents the only known instance where *P. punctata* assumed pest proportions. The species is typically rare in Rock River and had not been collected during two years of intensive prior sampling (1923-1925).

8. **Prognosis:** Given the unprecedented nature, reduced recurrence in 1926-1927, and absence of obvious predisposing conditions, the author concluded *P. punctata* would likely not continue as a significant pest and would return to rarity in the river fauna.
