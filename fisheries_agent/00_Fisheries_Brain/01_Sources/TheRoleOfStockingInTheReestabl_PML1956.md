# Flannelmouth Sucker Recovery and Razorback Sucker Stocking Failure on the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This USGS Open-File Report (03-258) by Gordon Mueller evaluates native fish communities of the lower Colorado River between [[Davis Dam]] and [[Lake Havasu]] (about 60 km of river) from 1999 to 2002. The study began as an assessment of the status and stocking augmentation of [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] but was reduced and refocused after funding cuts of about 60%. The river has been channelized, dammed and heavily invaded by nonnative species, and native fishes have declined to remnant populations. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], stocked once in 1976, has reproduced successfully and expanded, whereas stocked razorback suckers and bonytail show poor survival (below roughly 12%) and no detectable recruitment. The author attributes the contrast mainly to habitat preference and predation, and to differences between wild and hatchery-reared fish. Recommendations focus on site acclimation and physical conditioning before stocking, predator removal, refuge ponds, and expanded monitoring.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River, once the fifth largest river in the USA by volume, now seldom reaches the sea. Dams, diversions, levees and dredging have converted much of the lower river into a canal-like channel, and reservoirs cover 36% of the historic channel. More than 50 nonnative fish species have been introduced to the basin. By 1930 most native species had become rare, and wild [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and bonytail are reported as extirpated downstream of Glen Canyon Dam.

The study area is the 60 km reach from the Davis Dam tailrace to Catfish Bay near Lake Havasu. Surveys were conducted from December through May, when both sucker species spawn, using trammel nets, passive integrated transponder (PIT) tagging, light trapping for larvae, and a standardized boat-based visual survey method. Sampling was centered on Laughlin, Nevada and Park Moabi, California.

The report contains five chapters: (1) fish surveys; (2) telemetry studies; (3) a stocking risk assessment; (4) a bibliography of big river fishes; and (5) the role of stocking in management and recovery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat partitioning:** The upper 40 km is dominated by channel habitat (tailwater, dredged channel), whereas the lower 40 km is dominated by backwaters and reservoir influence, with large cattail marshes and off-channel lagoons such as [[Laughlin Lagoon]].
- **Predator communities:** Nonnative predators (largemouth bass, striped bass, smallmouth bass, bullhead, channel catfish) were typically more than twice as abundant in the lower reach, where razorback suckers occur. Flannelmouth suckers in the upper reach faced fewer predators.
- **Anthropogenic pressures:** Laughlin Lagoon was dredged and deepened in 1999, increasing entrainment risk for suckers. Planned marina construction and a new bridge threaten known spawning areas.
- **Hydrology:** Daily hydropower fluctuations at Davis Dam cause stage changes that affect backwater habitat and the distribution of fish.
- **Water temperature:** Larval light-trap data recorded temperatures of about 11-14 °C during the March-April emergence period.

## Key Findings

**Community composition**
- More than 8,000 fish were collected over four years; 19 species were recorded (3 native, 16 nonnative).
- Native suckers made up 40% of the catch in the Laughlin area. Overall, 238 razorback suckers, 1,355 flannelmouth suckers and 1 bonytail were captured.
- Dominant species by average percent composition were common carp, largemouth bass and flannelmouth sucker.
- Visual surveys (25 surveys, 2,172 fish counted, August 2000) estimated 64.4 fish/ha; common carp made up 56.3% of counts.

**Flannelmouth Sucker**
- Flannelmouth sucker is the only native species thriving in the study area. The population is estimated at about 4,129 fish (density 8.6/ha over 490 ha).
- Mark-recapture estimates ranged from 2,209 to 3,051 fish across 2002 census periods (maximum estimate 2,998).
- Age ranged up to 14 years (the oldest sampled fish was 14 years; a 39-year-old fish was reported elsewhere). Growth declined from 4.51 mm/month for fish of 450-475 mm to 0.35 mm/month for the largest fish.
- Spawning was observed from March to April at depths greater than 2 m, and larvae were found at Laughlin Lagoon. Ripe fish were also captured from October to June, indicating a prolonged spawning season.
- Recaptured females showed high site fidelity; over 60% returned to their capture point at least 12 months later.

**Razorback Sucker**
- Catch was relatively low. An estimated 1,350 razorback suckers were present in the study area (density 0.06/ha), and about 30,000 large (>25 cm) fish had been stocked in Lake Havasu since 1995.
- Initial survival of stocked fish is estimated at roughly 2% to below 12%, with a population estimate of 1,600 to 3,600 fish.
- Larvae were found in 2000, 2001 and 2002 at Park Moabi and Blankenship Bend, indicating spawning, but no recruitment was detected.
- Wild-origin fish were nearly absent; only three fish over 600 mm could be considered wild.

**Bonytail**
- Only one bonytail was captured, near Bullhead City. Several bonytail have been stocked in Lake Havasu, but few have been recovered.

**Telemetry and physiology**
- Telemetry failed to track resident fish (mostly due to signal loss in deep or turbid water). Among 8 test groups (15 fish each), razorback dispersal was greatest in the Green River site (mean 69.5 km/month).
- Flow-conditioned fish dispersed less (mean 1.89 km/month) than predisposed fish (7.73 km/month), a statistically significant difference (Wilcoxon, P<0.05).
- Modde (FWS, oral communication) reported nearly three times the return rate for razorback suckers held in backwaters for one year compared with those released directly into the river.

**Stocking assessment**
- A GIS-based stocking risk model (Broderick and Callahan, BOR, 2000) ranked the reach by survival risk. Lake Mohave ranked highest (6), while the reach below Davis Dam ranked lowest.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The contrasting outcomes of flannelmouth and razorback suckers show that habitat preference and predator communities are primary factors in recruitment.
- Recommended actions include:
  1. Expand the Lake Havasu roundup to monitor bonytail (April sampling).
  2. Mechanically remove predators from Park Moabi Lagoon during spawning periods.
  3. Stock only native fish of at least 30 cm, since survival is strongly size-dependent.
  4. Physically condition and site-acclimate hatchery fish in backwaters for several weeks to months before release.
  5. Build at least a dozen small (<5 ha) refuge ponds.
  6. Refine the GIS stocking risk model.
  7. Investigate the migratory behavior of subadult suckers.
- The author concludes that complete predator removal is neither technically nor politically viable, and that refuge communities offer the only practical route to self-sustaining native populations.
