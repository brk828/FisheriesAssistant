# Problems and Constraints in Managing Rivers With Variable Flow Regimes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter argues that ecological theory, management guidance and water legislation for rivers were largely built on perennial, temperate streams, and that uncritical extrapolation of those principles to intermittent and ephemeral rivers can be misleading. Flow variability (flood pulses and drying phases) structures biota and ecosystem processes in these systems. Regulation that removes variability, through dams, weirs, inter-basin transfers and groundwater abstraction, produces documented ecological decline. The authors call for a specialist policy for regulated intermittent rivers that protects the dry phase, uses flow regime rather than hydrograph, and integrates whole-system management. The chapter is a draft (January 1998) for the volume *Global Perspectives on River Conservation*.

## Background & Description

The chapter is written by [[A. J. Boulton]], [[F. Sheldon]], [[M.C. Thoms]] and [[E.H. Stanley]], affiliated with institutions in Australia and the United States. It addresses the gap between perennial-river science and the management of rivers whose natural state includes flooding and drying.

Key concepts defined in the text:

- **Stream classes (by flow predictability and duration):**
  - Ephemeral: flows briefly (<1 month), irregularly, after rain.
  - Intermittent/temporary: flows for longer periods (>1–3 months), with an annual dry period.
  - Semi-permanent: flows most of the year, ceasing in dry weather (<3 months), drying to pools.
  - Permanent: perennial flow, ceasing only in rare extreme droughts.
- **Three scales of hydrological behaviour** (after [[Walker Et Al 1995]]): the flood pulse, the flow history (sequence of pulses) and the flow regime (long-term statistical generalisation).
- **Flow regime analysis:** [[Puckridge Et Al]] analysed 20-year hydrographs for 52 large rivers and found independent measures of variability (timing, duration, magnitude, rates of rise and fall, frequency). Dryland rivers such as Cooper Creek and the Diamantina were the most distinctive.
- **Coefficients of variation** of annual flows in dryland rivers are, on average, 467% greater than in humid and temperate regions, per [[Davies Et Al 1994]].

Figures and tables (flood pulse aspects, flow regime examples for the Mekong, Mississippi, Columbia, Darling and Cooper Creek, and a schematic of flow variability effects) are referenced but not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical extremes.** Amplitudes in drying pools far exceed those in permanent streams. As rivers dry, conductivity rises through evapoconcentration, water temperatures can exceed 30°C, dissolved oxygen falls, and pH can drop to 4.5 in some receding pools with high leaf leachate.

**Biota.** Species numbers of plants, invertebrates and fish are generally lower in intermittent streams than in nearby permanent streams of similar size. Invertebrates lacking desiccation-tolerant stages or good recolonising ability are eliminated by drying. Most riverine fish cannot tolerate drying and are restricted to permanent streams, although some use intermittent channels for spawning (see [[Erman And Hawthorne 1976]]). Emergent macrophytes tolerate water-level fluctuation better than submerged or floating plants.

**Hyporheic exchange.** In many gravel and sand-bed intermittent streams, subsurface flow represents a substantial share of total discharge. During drying, downwelling dominates, microbial respiration shifts hyporheic metabolism toward anaerobic processes, and the hyporheic zone can act as a refuge for surface organisms (see [[Hyporheic Zone]]).

**Floodplain and geomorphology.** Variable flows maintain channel complexity through "benches" that provide habitat and store organic matter. Floodplain lakes and billabongs can dry for years or decades, and reflooding at different frequencies produces diverse biota from desiccation-resistant sediment stages. In three central Australian rivers, invertebrate assemblages were structured by flooding frequency, unlike the less-variable [[River Murray]].

**Climate and geography.** Timing of flow depends on catchment geography: Mojave Desert streams flow in spring from snowmelt, while Chihuahuan Desert streams respond to summer convective storms. Southwestern US flows are influenced by [[El Nino Southern Oscillation]]. A 10% decrease in mean annual precipitation may reduce surface runoff by 30–50% (Dahm and Molles, 1992).

**Temperate analogues.** The chapter applies the same logic to UK chalk streams and upland streams. Drought (1988–1992), worsened by groundwater abstraction, caused severe impacts on upland invertebrates, but recovery was rapid.

## Key Findings

**1. Theory and policy are biased toward perennial rivers.** Ecosystem theories (e.g., the [[River Continuum Concept]]) and management guidance reflect temperate, permanent systems, and need modification for variable-flow rivers.

**2. Flow variability is ecologically essential.** Drying and flooding structure assemblages and processes, and the dry phase is as significant as flooding. Dryland persistence of many species probably depends on maintained intermittency, though data are limited because pre-regulation baselines are often missing.

**3. Regulation removes variability with measurable consequences.**
- Barwon-Darling River: water diversions equal about 60% of long-term flow, and up to 70% of daily flows can be abstracted during individual flood pulses. Since 1965, flows became more predictable and constant, and many in-channel benches disappeared.
- Murray-Darling Basin: about 80% of total flow is diverted for off-stream use, and major rivers experience "drought" flows in 60% of years, compared with 5% under natural flows (Cullen et al., 1996).
- Colorado River, Grand Canyon: after Glen Canyon Dam (1963), sand bars shrank and vegetation encroached. A "controlled flood" in March 1996 released about 900 million cubic metres over 7 days to rebuild habitat. Its success was still being monitored.

**4. Effluent-dominated intermittent streams pose a policy dilemma.** Where effluent forms most of the dry-season flow, water-quality standards have sometimes been relaxed because a sustained, low-quality flow is seen as preferable to a dry channel. The chapter challenges the assumptions that ephemeral streams lack viable communities and that effluent yields net ecological benefit.

**5. Water-quality and biological indices can mislead.** In Chessman's (1995) invertebrate index, of 15 most pollution-insensitive families, only three were commonly found in relatively pristine temperate intermittent streams. Fifteen "tolerant" families were largely common in intermittent streams. Intermittency acts on the biota much as pollution does in permanent streams, so the index may misclassify intermittent rivers.

**6. Environmental flows and scientific panels.** Seven scientific panels had been conducted in the Murray-Darling Basin by 1997. The Barwon-Darling panel recommended maintaining the natural frequency, duration and intensity of drought. Models often perform poorly in the low-flow band, and hydrological data are inadequate.

**7. Artificial floods may be ineffectual.** At the Oanob Dam (Namibia), the 1993 artificial flood peaked at 15 m³/s and totalled under 5 million m³, travelling under 30 km. Pre-regulation average annual flood discharges exceeded 150 m³/s. The artificial flood also carried almost no organic material and little silt or nutrients, so "water alone may not be enough."

**8. Water policy problems.** Western perceptions treat a dry riverbed as a problem. The "use it or lose it" rule in western US states encourages consumption. Per capita water use in Arizona and New Mexico is almost twice the national average. Australian COAG reforms propose market trading of water rights, while the Murray-Darling "Cap" (set at 1993/94 development levels) is politically contentious and criticised by some ecologists as too generous.

**9. Recommended policy elements.** The authors propose a specialist policy for regulated intermittent rivers that addresses:
1. The importance of the dry phase.
2. Maintenance of irregularity, based on pre-regulation variability where data allow.
3. Assessment based on flow regime, not hydrograph.
4. Integrated, whole-system flow management, not allocations for a few species.
5. The link between water quality and quantity, including the difference between artificial and natural floods.
6. Maintenance of flow variability to sustain habitat diversity over large time and space scales.
7. Explicit recognition of public perception of intermittency as a "problem," with educational programmes.

**10. Prognosis.** Intermittent and ephemeral streams are globally widespread and often neglected in competition for water. Successful management must be pro-active, based on natural flow regimes, and built into the licence agreements of water users. Each release should be treated as a large-scale experiment for adaptive management.
