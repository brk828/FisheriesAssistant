# Subsidence and Fissuring in Arizona

#hydrogeology #groundwater-depletion #land-subsidence

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Water Commission Project Bulletin (No. 24, August 1980), adapted from [[Wesley Peirce]]'s article in the [[Bureau of Geology and Mineral Technology]] *Fieldnotes* (June 1979), describes land subsidence and earth fissuring caused largely by [[Groundwater Withdrawal]]. Subsidence is quietly lowering hundreds, perhaps thousands, of square miles of Arizona's land surface, and fissures are widespread. Both threaten infrastructure such as [[Interstate 10]] and the [[Southern Pacific Railroad]]. The bulletin focuses on the [[Casa Grande]] region, the [[Picacho Fault]] zone and several urban areas, and concludes that subsidence and fissuring will continue as long as water is withdrawn in large quantities.

## Background & Description

The document is a short technical newsletter item rather than a formal study. Peirce is principal geologist for the State of Arizona at the [[Arizona Bureau of Geology and Mineral Technology]] at the [[University of Arizona]] in [[Tucson]].

- **Key mapping sources:** [[Laney]], [[Raymond]] and [[Winikka]] (1978) produced maps and survey profiles of water-level declines, land subsidence and earth fissures in south-central Arizona for the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] (USGS), the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and the Arizona Department of Transportation.
- **Lead researcher:** [[Thomas Holzer]] (USGS) studies subsidence and fissuring across the Southwest, from Texas to California, with particular interest in predicting future fissures in the United States.
- **Subsidence mechanism:** Groundwater occupies pore spaces in soft sediments. When it is removed, the fine-grained sediments compact, and the land surface lowers. The magnitude depends largely on the volume of sediment dewatered by pumping.
- **Monitoring gap:** Much subsidence in Arizona probably remains undocumented, because few survey networks periodically measure land-level changes.
- **Editors and contact:** Phil Briggs, Jim DeCook, Ken Foster and Dick Haney. Copies of the 1978 referenced maps were available free from the [[Arizona Water Commission]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geologic setting.** The [[Basin and Range Province]] covers the southwestern half of Arizona. Alternating valleys and mountain ranges create thick sequences of soft, water-bearing sediments, often storing large amounts of groundwater more than 1,000 feet below the surface. More than 90 percent of Arizona's population lives in these basins. The groundwater, combined with rich soils and long growing seasons, supports about 95 percent of the state's agriculture.

**Valley-range discontinuity.** Hard-rock ranges separate the basins laterally. This valley-to-range contrast strongly influences where subsidence and fissuring occur. The authors describe its effect as impossible to overemphasize (Vuich and Peirce 1973).

**Water use scale.** In 1977 alone, enough water was pumped from Arizona's ground to cover a football field with a water column "more than 1,000 miles high," as the text states. Groundwater is integral to the earth's foundation, so its large-scale removal causes the materials to shrink or compact.

**Regional case studies:**

| Area | Subsidence | Groundwater decline |
|---|---|---|
| [[Picacho Basin]] (around [[Eloy]]) | At least 120 sq mi subsided 7.0–12.5 ft since 1952; maximum average rate 0.5 ft/yr | About 200 to just over 300 ft (1923–1977) |
| [[Stanfield]] (12–15 mi west of Casa Grande) | About 7 mi square subsided 8.0–12.0 ft | Up to 450 ft |
| [[Phoenix]] region ([[Scottsdale]], Arizona Canal) | Some subsidence documented by [[Schumann]] (1974); susceptibility likely | Up to 300 ft |
| [[Sun City]] | Not yet determined | 200 to 300 ft |
| [[Tucson]] | Officials fear subsidence may be imminent | More than 100 ft in some areas, and increasing |

**Urban and infrastructure concerns.** Tucson draws most of its water from groundwater in the surrounding valley. Some proposals suggest reducing long-range subsidence risk by pumping more water from an undeveloped basin to the west. A cooperative USGS–Tucson program will measure water-level trends, install recorders in selected wells, remeasure established benchmarks and add leveling stations before subsidence begins.

## Key Findings

**1. Subsidence is widespread and largely unmeasured.** Hundreds to perhaps thousands of square miles are subsiding, but only a few regions are well monitored, and [[Groundwater Decline]] data are essential for anticipating subsidence.

**2. Earth fissures cluster on buried hard-rock irregularities.** Fissures are believed to form above buried bedrock irregularities that control soft-sediment thickness along basin margins. Geophysical methods can locate these features and may guide alternative [[Central Arizona Project]] aqueduct alignments to Tucson.

**3. Fissuring is extensive.** Fissures occur in [[Cochise County]], [[Pinal County]] and [[Maricopa County]]. There are at least 15 fissure areas and more than 100 individual fissures in Pinal and Maricopa counties alone.

**4. The first documented Picacho fissure appeared in 1927.** An approximately 1,200-foot-long fissure formed on September 12, 1927, after a heavy thunderstorm. It was described by [[R.J. Conrad]] (1929).

**5. The Picacho zone is a fault-like feature caused by groundwater extraction.** The zone is about 9 miles long, with a consistent vertical offset of up to 1.5 feet, the west (valley) side down relative to the east (mountain) side. [[Holzer, Davis and Lofgren]] (1979) conclude that the feature, called the [[Picacho Fault]], formed after 1961 from groundwater extraction and poorly understood subsurface processes, not earthquakes. The absence of documented earthquakes since 1961 supports this.

**6. Reported earthquakes were probably atmospheric shocks.** Reports of earthquakes are attributed to mistaken atmospherically induced sounds rather than earth-generated vibrations.

**7. Vertical-offset fissures pose greater engineering risks.** Picacho-type scarps are more damaging than common simple tension (pull-apart) fissures. The Picacho zone crosses I-10 and passes beneath the Southern Pacific Railroad, requiring periodic repairs. Recent resurfacing has temporarily subdued the visible offset.

**8. A buried fault may control the Picacho pattern.** Holzer (1978) speculated that an old buried fault that does not reach the surface cuts soft sediments and influences the dewatering pattern, contributing to stress buildup beneath the surface fault.

**9. Subsidence and fissuring are human-induced readjustments.** The authors argue that these processes are precipitated by human disruption of a natural balance that took millennia to form, and that "so long as we withdraw water in such great quantities, subsidence and fissuring will continue."
