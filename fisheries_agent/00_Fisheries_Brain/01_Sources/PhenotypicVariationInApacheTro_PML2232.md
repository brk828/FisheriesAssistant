# Phenotypic Variation in Apache Trout Relative to Co-occurrence with Introduced Salmonids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This manuscript, prepared by John N. Rinne and W. L. Minckley (USDA Forest Service, Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, and Arizona State University), re-evaluates the morphological variation of [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo apache*) and tests whether [[Hybridization]] with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) has altered its phenotype and distribution. Using meristic and morphometric data from more than 650 collected specimens, plus type material and comparative samples, the authors found that observed variability exceeds the original type description, but that the variation does not trend toward intermediate character states expected from hybridization. Introgression is judged to have occurred on [[National Forest Lands]], while stocks on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] are far less contaminated.

The intended audience is fishery researchers and managers. The reviewer form attached to the document (RM 1630-6) is largely blank, with a single handwritten date, so no formal review recommendation is recorded.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** Apache trout occur naturally in the White Mountains of east-central Arizona. It was described in 1972 by [[Miller]] and listed as endangered by the U.S. Department of the Interior in 1975. A recovery team and plan (USDI, 1979) followed.
- **Threat:** The historic range was drastically reduced by habitat alteration and interspecific interactions, including apparent hybridization with non-native trouts. A key recovery goal was to restore the species to former habitats using fish of known genetic purity.
- **Congeneric candidates:** Three other salmonids were considered as potential hybridizing partners:
  - [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. clarki*) were stocked in the early 1900s but have disappeared from the region and are rare in Arizona; the authors excluded them after finding no indicative characters.
  - [[Gila Trout]] (*S. gilae*) is native to the Verde, Agua Fria and Gila headwaters. Transfer to the San Francisco drainage occurred in 1905, and some evidence of hybridization exists, but the authors judged it unlikely to have been widely introduced within the range of *S. apache*.
  - Rainbow trout (*S. gairdneri*) have been introduced to Arizona since the late 1800s and stocked widely across the White Mountains. Records of origin were not maintained until recently.
- **Taxonomic history:** Earlier workers (Cope and Yarrow, 1875; Jordan, 1891; Evermann and Rutter, 1898) assigned the fish to *S. clarki* or its subspecies, and Miller (1950, 1961) tentatively identified East Fork White River trout as *S. gilae* before the 1972 description.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Headwater and high-elevation streams of the White Mountains. Native range extends to elevations as low as about 1,800 m, with headwaters above about 2,100 m. Relief is high, and natural falls and barriers isolate headwaters from upstream fish movement.
- **Stocking history:** The first non-native trout introductions were recorded as 1917 (eastern brook trout), followed by rainbow trout, with a hatchery at Springerville built in 1921. Stocking by the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) occurred on the Reservation (1933–1939 and after 1958) and on National Forest lands in the 1950s–1960s.
- **Tribal closure:** The White Mountain Apache Tribe closed headwater streams to stocking and angling as early as 1940, which protected high-elevation populations.
- **Habitat alteration:** The Little Colorado River basin, a large part of the probable historic range, was heavily impounded. Warm-water fishes and many ponds and reservoirs now occur there, and early habitat change likely excluded *S. apache* before non-native salmonids arrived.
- **Management constraints:** Most streams slated for Apache trout restoration require treatment with ichthyotoxin to remove non-native trout, which is logistically difficult. Unauthorized stocking is a management problem that can only be addressed through public education.

## Key Findings

- **Morphological variability:** Ranges for scales, pyloric caeca, and snout length (SNL) were wider than those reported by Miller (1972). Snout length of 29 of 111 fish (26.1%) fell outside Miller's limits, and adipose fin length (ADFL) of 42 of 111 specimens (38.7%) lay outside his reported limits. Vertebral counts corresponded well across datasets.
- **Taxonomic separation:** Principal components analysis (PCA) separated type material of *S. apache* clearly from *S. gairdneri*. The first two components explained 69.9% of variance in the type comparison and 58.8% in the Reservation comparison.
- **Discriminant analysis (DFA):** DFA definitively separated types from *S. gairdneri*. Reservation fish aligned strongly with *S. apache*, and 100% were identified as the native species. On National Forest lands, 8 fish were classed as *S. gairdneri* and the remaining 209 as *S. apache*, with ranks nearer intermediacy.
- **Transplants:** Transplanted *S. apache* stocks resembled National Forest specimens, suggesting contamination or introgression in those lines.
- **Distribution impact:** An estimated 37% of the area above 2,100 m (and 43% above 1,800 m) now holds fish of hybrid origin. Of 717 km of streams above 2,100 m (809 km above 1,800 m), the authors estimate 65.9%, 82% and 80.2% (values as reported in the document) now support *S. gairdneri*, other introduced salmonids, or introgressed *S. apache*.
- **Conclusions:** Pure *S. apache* persists most frequently on the Reservation, where headwater closures and barriers protected populations for over 30 years. Introgressive hybridization probably occurred on National Forest lands, but contamination on the Reservation is far less severe, if detectable at all. Selection against the non-native genome may be strong, particularly for catchable-sized rainbow trout that have low reproductive success.
- **Management implications:** Hatchery genetic quality control is essential. The authors identify stream stocking by non-natives and illegitimate stocking as the two major management problems.
