# Growth and Mortality of Zebra Fish (*Brachydanio rerio*) at Two Temperatures and Two Diets

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 study by [[J. F. Craig]] and [[J. M. Fletcher]] tested whether temperature and diet energy content, acting on physiological processes, set the growth and mortality rates of juvenile [[Zebra Fish]] that then carry into adulthood. Twelve glass tanks, each stocked with 100 fish, were held for 112 days at 24 °C or 28 °C and fed either a standard flaked diet (metabolizable energy 13.45 kJ g⁻¹, Type A) or a cellulose-added diet (8.71 kJ g⁻¹, Type B). The highest mortality occurred at 28 °C on the high-energy diet, and the fastest growth occurred at 24 °C on the high-energy diet. Growth and mortality were positively linked overall, but the relation broke down under thermal stress.

## Background & Description

The study builds on earlier work by [[Craig]] (1979, 1980, 1982) on [[European Perch]] (*Perca fluviatilis*) cohorts in [[Windermere]]. That work proposed that warmer temperatures and rich food speed growth and physiological ageing, while cold and poor food slow growth and extend life expectancy. The authors note that predation in natural populations often masks these controlling factors, so a laboratory design was used.

The working hypothesis was that temperature and diet control physiological processes, which set juvenile growth and mortality rates, and that these rates then persist into the adult phase.

Experimental setup:
- Twelve aquaria (62 × 28 × 34 cm, 53 L), filtered and sterilized Windermere lake water, undergravel filtration, heaters, and 12 h light/12 h dark cycle.
- Tanks 1–3 and 7–9 at 28 °C; tanks 4–6 and 10–12 at 24 °C.
- Tanks 1–6 received cellulose-added food (Type B); tanks 7–12 received the normal food (Type A), in triplicate.
- Fish were about 35 days old at acclimatization on 18 February 1982, and were sampled on day 112 (9 June 1982).
- Food ration was set at about 3.5–3.9% of mean body weight per day, with energy partitioned against standard metabolism (Q₀).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Non-ionized ammonia, nitrite and nitrate did not reach toxic levels in any tank, including tank 3. Tank 3 nonetheless showed a rapid rise in total ammonium nitrogen and a pH drop, which the authors attributed to an accelerated increase in ammonia-producing bacteria. This tank suffered a sudden mortality (40 deaths between days 98 and 111) that was not explained by water chemistry alone.

**Physical conditions.** Mean illumination was 45.7 ± 2.3 lx (range 34.4–56.0 lx). Mean dissolved oxygen was 8.8 ± 0.3 mg L⁻¹ (range 7.0–11.8 mg L⁻¹). On day 33 a voltage surge caused a temperature drop to about 20 °C overnight; temperatures were restored within 12 h.

**Food composition.** Type A food contained (% dry basis as reported) approximately 8.1% total nitrogen and 1.2% fat; Type B contained approximately 6.6% total nitrogen, 3.7% holocellulose and 1.5% fat, reflecting the cellulose addition.

**Competition.** Aggression and dominance were most evident in high-energy-diet tanks, where large fish dominated and smaller fish "wasted away." The authors note that the bulky cellulose diet may have reduced food competition by keeping fish fuller for longer.

## Key Findings

**Mortality (instantaneous daily rate, Z):**
- Highest mortality: tanks 7, 8, 9 (28 °C, Type A). Z ranged from 0.0080 to 0.0133.
- Second highest: tanks 10, 11, 12 (24 °C, Type A), Z 0.0022 to 0.0056.
- Lowest: tanks 1, 2 (28 °C, Type B) and 4, 5, 6 (24 °C, Type B), Z 0.0004 to 0.0013.
- Tank 3 died at two rates: Z = 0.0001 (days 1–97, 2 deaths) and Z = 0.0361 (days 98–111, 40 deaths).
- All regressions were significant (P < 0.001). Between-group differences were far larger than within-group differences.

**Growth:**
- Fastest growth (instantaneous rate G, J J⁻¹ day⁻¹) occurred at 24 °C on Type A (tanks 10–12). Slowest growth occurred at 28 °C on Type A.
- Fish in tanks 10–12 were heavier and had higher energy density than other groups (Table III).
- Day 112 dry weights and energy values differed significantly between groups but not generally within groups (Table IV).
- Gross efficiency was highest at 24 °C on both diets; partial efficiency was highest at 24 or 28 °C on the low-energy diet. Both efficiencies and growth were lowest at 28 °C on the high-energy diet.

**Length–weight condition:** A pooled slope b = 3.3214 was used, with tank-specific intercepts a. Estimated weights of a 30 mm fish ranged from 210 mg (tank 3, lowest) to 287 mg (tank 11, highest). The condition index agreed with the random-sample energy analysis.

**Carbon–energy conversion:** Calorific value (Y, J mg⁻¹) against carbon (X, mgC mg⁻¹) was Y = 2.9777 + 40.2712X (P < 0.001). The mean conversion factor was 46.2 ± 0.4 J (mgC)⁻¹, close to the 46 J (mgC)⁻¹ used for invertebrates by [[Salonen et al. (1976)]] and similar to the value for Protozoa from [[Finlay & Uhlig (1981)]].

**Growth–mortality link:** Across tanks, mortality and growth were correlated (P < 0.05) when the 28 °C, Type A tanks were excluded: Z = −0.0038 + 0.7894G (r = 0.7852, 6 d.f.). Net biomass increase (G − Z) was similar across the 24 °C tanks.

**Conclusions:**
- Temperature and diet energy interact to affect conversion efficiency, growth and mortality. Elevated temperature combined with a high-energy diet was the most stressful condition.
- Growth and mortality were linked through a physiological ageing process, but only within tolerable thermal limits. Beyond an optimum, stress breaks this linkage.
- The authors suggest that the "optimum temperature" for a body function can shift with energy intake, following [[Elliot (1981)]].
- Perch fry, with higher energy content than benthos, were considered critical for perch growth and mortality in Windermere (Craig, 1979).
