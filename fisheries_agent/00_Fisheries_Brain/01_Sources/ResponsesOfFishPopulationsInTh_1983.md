# Responses of Fish Populations in the North Fork of the Feather River, California, to Treatments With Fish Toxicants

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #stream-ecology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae #Centrarchidae #Cottidae #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by Moyle, Vondracek, and Grossman evaluated the long-term (10–11 year) effects of rotenone poisoning treatments on fish assemblages in two reaches of the [[North Fork Feather River]], California. Both reaches had been treated twice, a decade apart (1966/1977 lower reach; 1971/1981 upper reach), allowing a rare before/after-recovery comparison. The key finding was that **nongame fish populations (suckers, squawfish, hardhead) rapidly and fully recovered** from poisoning within a decade, while **wild [[Rainbow Trout]] populations showed no clear evidence of population increases attributable to nongame fish removal**. Differences in trout success between the two reaches were best explained by **habitat quality and the 1976–1977 California drought**, not interspecific competition. The authors concluded that habitat improvement, not toxicant-based nongame fish removal, is the more effective management strategy for sustaining wild trout fisheries in streams.

## Background & Description

The [[North Fork Feather River]] is a heavily hydro-modified tributary of the Sacramento River system, managed by Pacific Gas & Electric (PG&E) with multiple impoundments, tunnels, and penstocks that have eliminated natural flood/low-flow extremes. Two river reaches were studied:

- **Lower reach**: ~56 km below Rock Creek Dam plus the East Branch; characterized by long, deep pools separated by boulder-strewn riffles; highly regulated flows; poisoned in 1966 and again in 1977.
- **Upper reach**: 35 km between [[Almanor Reservoir]] and a fish barrier near Gansner Bar; roughly equal mix of riffles, runs, and pools; flows augmented by natural runoff giving more natural variability; poisoned in 1971 and again in 1981.

Resident species common to both reaches:
- [[Sacramento Sucker]] (*Catostomus occidentalis*)
- [[Sacramento Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus grandis*)
- [[Hardhead]] (*Mylopharodon conocephalus*)
- [[Riffle Sculpin]] (*Cottus gulosus*)
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*)
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*, introduced/exotic)
- [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*, stocked)

Occasional stragglers from Almanor Reservoir included Sacramento perch, common carp, and wakasagi.

**Methods**: Fish were collected from blocked/netted test sections after rotenone application, measured (fork and standard length), weighed, and scale-aged. Standing crop (kg/hectare), age structure, and back-calculated growth (Lee's back-calculation from scales) were compared across treatment years (1966, 1967, 1977 for the lower reach; 1981 for the upper reach).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regulation**: PG&E-mandated minimum flows differ substantially between reaches (lower reach: ~153–170 m³/min typical; upper reach: 102–238 m³/min plus natural runoff), producing more stable but flow-limited conditions downstream and more naturally fluctuating conditions upstream.
- **1976–1977 Drought**: A severe drought drastically reduced summer flows in the lower reach (mean August flow dropped from a typical ~17 to ~8 m³/min) and elevated water temperatures above 20°C on many days, approaching lethal limits for [[Rainbow Trout]] (per [[Jobling 1981]] thermal tolerance data). The upper reach (Gansner Bar) was comparatively buffered from drought-driven temperature/flow extremes.
- **Thermal regime differences**: Lower reach summer temperatures normally 16–20°C but exceeded 20–23°C during 1977 drought; upper reach ran 18–23°C even in normal years, but did not depart much from baseline during the drought.
- **Habitat structure**: The lower reach's long deep pools favored warmwater-tolerant [[Sacramento Squawfish]], [[Hardhead]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]], while disadvantaging trout, which forage more efficiently on drifting invertebrates in faster-flowing water (per [[Tippets and Moyle 1978]]). The upper reach's riffle-run-pool balance favored trout growth and survival.
- **Anthropogenic pressures**: Both reaches experienced repeated hatchery trout stocking and angler harvest; the upper reach nonetheless sustained a self-recruiting wild trout population despite this pressure, while the lower reach trout population remained persistently small and apparently dependent on stocking.
- **Fish barrier effect**: An artificial barrier below the upper reach study section limited squawfish/hardhead recruitment from downstream, contributing to their low abundance there (juveniles only were found).

## Key Findings

### 1. Nongame Fish Recovery
- Nongame fish (mainly [[Sacramento Sucker]]) comprised ~80% of standing crop biomass in both reaches, confirming rapid, essentially complete recolonization/recovery within 10–11 years of prior poisoning.
- Lower reach total standing crop in 1977 (~255 kg/ha) was comparable to the upper reach in 1981 (~270 kg/ha).
- Species rank-order abundance (suckers > squawfish > hardhead > trout) was consistent between 1966 and 1977 in the lower reach, indicating full assemblage-level recovery, though sucker biomass was about half in 1977 vs. 1966 (attributed to drought effects).
- Chi-square analysis confirmed statistically significant (P < 0.05) shifts in species numbers/biomass between 1966 and 1967 post-treatment, consistent with rapid spawning/recruitment or recolonization from unpoisoned areas.

### 2. Game Fish (Trout) Response
- No evidence that wild trout populations increased substantially 10–11 years post-poisoning in either reach.
- **Lower reach**: Trout numbers/biomass were nearly identical in 1966 vs. 1977 despite continued hatchery stocking; only 29 trout >15 cm were collected (≈41 fish/km), placing this reach in the **lower 32%** of California trout streams (per [[Gerstung 1973]] standards).
- **Upper reach (1981)**: 30 trout ≥15 cm were collected (≈353 fish/km, excluding fish removed earlier by CDFG electrofishing rescue), placing it in the **top 17%** of California trout streams.
- Brown trout failed to establish in the lower reach by 1977 despite earlier stocking success in 1967 — indicating unsuitable habitat.
- No hatchery-origin trout (based on body/fin morphology) were found in the 1981 upper-reach sample, indicating a genuinely wild, self-sustaining population; a similar check was not possible for 1977 fish.

### 3. Growth & Age Structure — Evidence on Competition
- **Sacramento Sucker**: Lower reach population (1977) was dominated by large, old fish (6–8 years; 1969–1971 year classes) with a skewed age structure attributed to the earlier 1967 treatment plus reduced recruitment. Upper reach (1981) showed a full, more natural age range (0+ to 9+) dominated by young-of-year. Condition factors differed significantly (lower reach K=0.96 vs. upper reach K=1.30; P<0.001), suggesting intraspecific competition/density stress in the lower reach.
- **Rainbow Trout**: Upper reach fish grew rapidly (mean lengths: YOY 92 mm, yearlings 199 mm, age-2 320 mm) — comparable to or better than other Sierra Nevada streams. Growth in the lower reach appeared similarly patterned but based on small sample sizes.
- **Sacramento Squawfish**: Large population in lower reach (up to 435 mm, ages to 8+), but growth was **slower** than at seven other Sacramento system sites. In the upper reach, only 36 YOY (mean 70 mm) and 15 yearlings (mean 124 mm) were found; larger adults were reportedly restricted upstream of the fish barrier.
- **Hardhead**: Lower reach supported 144 individuals (ages 0+ to 10+, 35–475 mm); growth was markedly slower than reported for the American River population ([[Reeves 1964]]: 460 mm by age 6 vs. 345 mm in this study). Only 3 YOY hardhead were found in the upper reach.
- **Smallmouth Bass**: Absent from the upper reach; 63 individuals found in the lower reach (mostly YOY and yearlings), with slow growth compared to other California populations — suggesting a newly established, suboptimal population possibly benefiting from reduced squawfish competition (similar niche/diet).
- **Riffle Sculpin**: Likely undercounted in both reaches due to collection bias (cryptic, sink to bottom). Length data suggested unusually favorable conditions, with individuals exceeding previously recorded maximum sizes (>90 mm SL) for the species.

### 4. Drought & Habitat as Primary Drivers (Not Competition)
- The authors concluded **habitat differences combined with the 1976–1977 drought**, not competitive exclusion by nongame fish, explained the contrast between reaches.
- Drought conditions (low flow, high temperature) in the lower reach likely stressed trout and suckers while favoring warmwater-tolerant squawfish, hardhead, and smallmouth bass — species that successfully spawned during this period.
- The persistence of a robust wild trout population in the upper reach *despite* large coexisting sucker and sculpin populations, angling pressure, and hatchery stocking directly undermines the assumption that nongame fish depress trout via competition.

### 5. Management Implications
- Toxicant treatments produced only short-term (likely <4–6 years) apparent improvements in trout fisheries, largely due to post-treatment hatchery stocking rather than a genuine competitive release.
- The paper's central recommendation: **habitat improvement (flow and temperature regime management)** is a more effective and durable strategy than repeated poisoning operations for sustaining wild trout fisheries in regulated streams like the [[North Fork Feather River]].
- Cautions against assuming nongame fish abundance (e.g., [[Sacramento Sucker]]) automatically implies competitive suppression of trout — findings here show this assumption failed a real-world test in the upper reach.

### Related/Adjacent Study Noted in Source Text
The same journal issue includes a separate paper (Gilderhus & Burress, 1983) on the piscicide **GD-174** [2-(digeranylamino)-ethanol] as a selective toxicant for common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), which found the compound largely ineffective and inconsistent across 25 pond trials, leading to discontinuation of its development at the National Fishery Research Laboratory. This is tangential to the Feather River study but reflects the broader era's emphasis on chemical fish population control methods discussed in [[Eschmeyer 1975]].
