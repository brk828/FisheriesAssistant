# Laboratory Studies on Antimycin A as a Fish Toxicant

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-toxicology

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife report (Berger, Lennon, and Hogan, February 1969) documents laboratory and simulated-field studies of [[Antimycin A]], a teleocidal antibiotic formulated as a piscicide. The work tested liquid and sand formulations against 31 freshwater species across egg, fry, fingerling, juvenile, and adult stages, under varying [[Temperature]], [[Water Hardness]], [[Water PH|pH]], and [[Turbidity]]. The results contributed to the registration of antimycin in the [[Fintrol-5]] formulation as a fish toxicant in the United States and Canada in 1966. The authors conclude that the compound is potent, stable in storage, detoxifiable with [[Potassium Permanganate]], and suited to general, partial, or selective fish control.

## Background & Description

- **Compound:** [[Antimycin A]] crystals, 96 to 98 percent pure, supplied by the Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation and Ayerst Laboratories. Stock solutions were 10 mg in 100 ml of acetone or ethanol, remaining stable for months in cool, dark storage but renewed biweekly in this work.
- **Fintrol-5:** A sand formulation of 1 percent antimycin A, 24 percent [[Carbowax]] (polyethylene glycol 6000), and 75 percent 40-mesh sand. It releases toxicant as particles sink, designed for depths of 0 to 5 feet.
- **Fintrol-15:** An experimental sand formulation (reported as 26 mg antimycin per gram) intended for depths to 15 feet. Fintrol-30 for greater depths was under consideration.
- **Background:** Antimycin's early promise was noted by Derse and Strong (1963) and Loeb (1964). It was patented as a piscicide by Strong and Derse in 1964, and Walker, Lennon, and Berger (1964) made preliminary toxicity observations.
- **Methods:** Most static bioassays used reconstituted deionized water with a total hardness of 40 ppm. Responses were expressed as ECₓ values (concentrations in ppb killing 0, 50, or 100 percent of fish within 24 to 96 hours), analyzed by [[Probit Analysis]] with 95 percent confidence intervals. Specialized methods included [[Column Test]]s (an 8-foot plexiglass column), [[Simulated Field Trials]] in 0.01-acre concrete pools, and [[Effective Contact Time]] trials.
- **Test organisms:** The 31 fish species were mostly from national and state hatcheries, with some wild specimens. Fish were quarantined and pre-tested before use.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality effects**

- **Temperature:** Fish were more susceptible at warmer temperatures, with twofold to fivefold differences in EC values between 7 and 22 °C. Antimycin remained toxic under ice (5 to 6 inches), though somewhat higher concentrations may be needed in very cold water.
- **Hardness:** The toxicant was slightly less effective in harder water. For example, 0.06 ppb killed all rainbow trout at 20 ppm hardness but none at 360 ppm.
- **pH:** pH had the strongest influence. Goldfish 96-hour EC₉₉ values were 0.20 ppb at pH 5, 1.10 ppb at pH 8, and 60 ppb at pH 10. Antimycin was more active and persisted longer at pH 5 to 8 than at pH 9 to 10. High pH accelerated degradation, which the authors suggest could be advantageous in some situations.
- **Turbidity:** Suspended clay at 1,000 ppm slightly reduced toxicity to rainbow trout (24, 48, and 96-hour EC values of 0.40, 0.14, and 0.10 ppb versus controls at 0.16, 0.10, and 0.05 ppb). In the field, turbidity delayed mortality rather than preventing it.

**Degradation and detoxification**

- Antimycin degraded more rapidly in hard, alkaline water than in soft, acid water. Degradation products were not toxic to fish.
- Dry heat at 200 °C for 60 minutes or longer destroyed toxicity. Heating for 15 or 30 minutes did not.
- Potassium permanganate at 300 ppb detoxified 5 ppb of antimycin within 6 hours.

**Non-target and environmental observations**

- Naiads of damselflies and aquatic plants in treated pools were unharmed.
- Fish were not repelled or excited by the toxicant, which the authors considered important for selective control.
- The compound is colorless and odorless, making it suitable for stream reclamation.
- Related studies in the discussion documented effects on sea lamprey larvae (9 ppb within 21 hours), spot (48-hour EC₅₀ of 0.23 ppb), grass carp (killed by 3 ppb), and tilapia (killed by 5 ppb).

## Key Findings

**Overall toxicity**

- Of the 31 species tested, 24 succumbed to 5 ppb or less. Only certain catfishes survived 25 ppb.
- Trouts were the most sensitive and catfishes the least. Less than 1 ppb killed all individuals of 14 species at one or more temperatures.
- Twenty-seven of 31 species were eliminated by 10 ppb. Shortnose gar, bowfin, and channel catfish required up to 25 ppb, and white catfish, flathead catfish, and black bullheads required up to 200 ppb.

**Life stages**

- **Eggs:** Rainbow trout eggs exposed to 0.5 ppb or more of acetone-antimycin did not survive to the eyed stage. Eggs exposed to 0.1 ppb had heavy swim-up mortality, with 7 percent surviving to 67 days (control: 55 percent). Brief exposures (30 to 120 minutes) at 100 ppb halted development, but 1 ppb for a short exposure had no significant effect on hatching. Goldfish eggs were fully killed by 2-hour exposures to 7.5 and 10 ppb. Channel catfish eggs were far more resistant, with over 90 percent surviving 2-hour exposures at 100 ppb or less.
- **Fry:** Bowfin became more resistant with age, with 96-hour ECs of 0.13, 0.24, and 0.35 ppb at 3, 6, and 8 weeks. Rainbow trout sac fry and fingerlings responded similarly (ECs of about 0.03 to 0.05 ppb at 12 °C).
- **Juveniles and adults:** In 1,000-gallon vinyl pools, 5 ppb or less killed all goldfish, carp, fathead minnows, bigmouth buffalo, green sunfish, pumpkinseed, bluegill, and largemouth bass within 144 hours. Adult black bullheads required 160 to 200 ppb.

**Effective contact time**

- Brief exposures suffice. At 10 ppb, a 1-hour exposure was lethal to green sunfish and bluegill, and 4 hours was lethal to carp, longear sunfish, and black crappie. Black bullheads required 3 to 4 hours at 500 ppb.

**Sand formulations**

- Fintrol-5 96-hour ECs were 0.04 ppb for rainbow trout and 0.20 ppb for carp, closely matching acetone-antimycin. Fintrol-15 values (0.07 ppb and 0.15 ppb) compared favorably.
- Column tests showed uniform release within the first 5 feet, with some further release from bottom sand at 8 feet.

**Simulated field trials**

- In 0.01-acre pools, 1 ppb of sand-formulated antimycin killed all rainbow trout and 86 percent of bluegills. Two ppb killed all spotted suckers and green sunfish. Four ppb killed all carp, and 10 ppb eradicated all 12 species tested.
- Caged rainbow trout showed the toxicant degraded to a safe level within 8 days in all pools.
- In cold (3.4 to 5.5 °C) pools, 5 ppb killed brown trout, carp, bluegill, and largemouth bass in clear water within 2 to 7 days. In turbid water, death took 7 to 12 days.
- Five and 10 ppb of Fintrol-5 disappeared within 8 and 13 days in clear water, and within 9 and 17 days in turbid water.

**Injected antimycin**

- Rainbow trout died at 0.02 mg/kg intraperitoneally (all within 24 hours). Carp were intermediate, with all dying at 0.40 mg/kg or more within 72 hours. Black bullheads needed at least 20 mg/kg, with complete kills at 30 mg/kg or more.

**Stability**

- Acetone-antimycin stock solutions retained potency over two years in cool, dark storage. Sand formulations appeared stable if kept cool and dry, but this was not measured specifically.

**Conclusions**

1. Antimycin is toxic to fish in water or when injected, across all life stages from egg to adult.
2. Stored crystals and Carbowax-sand formulations are stable and irreversibly toxic, and can be detoxified with potassium permanganate.
3. Degradation is faster in hard, alkaline water. Degradation products are non-toxic.
4. Purity, formulation integrity, and lack of repellency favor general, partial, or selective fish control. Possible uses include spawning areas, catfish production ponds, littoral zones, and stream reclamation.
