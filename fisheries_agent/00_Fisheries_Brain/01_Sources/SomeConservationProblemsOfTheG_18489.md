# Some Conservation Problems of the Great Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 Illinois Natural History Survey publication, Biological Notes No. 3, by [[Harlow B. Mills]] and W. L. Minckley, examines the conservation problems of the [[Great Lakes]]. It argues that the lakes' fisheries are complex, interdependent biological systems whose decline cannot be attributed with confidence to any single cause. The paper documents a sharp fall in commercial yield, a shift from prized species to "rough" fish, and the disappearance of [[Lake Trout]] in several lakes. It reviews candidate causes, including overfishing, introduced species such as the [[Sea Lamprey]], [[Alewife]] and [[Smelt]], pollution, shoreline modification, and water diversions. The author concludes that cause-and-effect relationships remain poorly understood and calls for adaptive, broadly framed regulation and more specialists.

## Background & Description

The author, a chief of the Illinois Natural History Survey, opens with a philosophical framing of conservation as a field that is compartmentalized across mathematics, physics, chemistry and biology, with biology posing the greatest number of interacting variables. The paper was adapted from an address given at a 1952 symposium on the natural setting and engineering problems of the Great Lakes in Chicago.

The text recalls [[Stephen A. Forbes]] (1925), whose classic essay "The Lake as a Microcosm" describes a lake as an interdependent system in which any change affecting one species influences the whole assemblage. The paper draws on [[Van Oosten]] (1949), [[Langlois]] (1945, 1951, 1952) and [[Hile]] (1949, 1951) for much of its statistical and interpretive base.

The Great Lakes fishery is described as commercially and socially significant. The lakes are said to produce about 55 percent of the total commercial catch excluding marine fisheries, and to support a $12,000,000 fishing industry along with allied industries. Sport fishing is important to many localities. Nearly 53 million people, about 40 percent of the U.S. population, live in states fronting the lakes, and about 14 million (roughly 10 percent) live in fronting counties.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food web interdependence.** The paper uses a simplified chain (bacteria and diatoms, protozoans, small crustaceans, aquatic insects, fishes, humans) to illustrate that removing one link, such as microcrustaceans, would depress fish populations while releasing protozoa and bacteria. See [[Food Chain]].

**Shallow-water habitat.** Shores and shallows are described as disproportionately important. Warmer shallow waters support plankton growth and aquatic plants, which provide food and shelter for fry and fingerlings. All catchable fishes pass through juvenile stages in these areas.

**Pollution.** Three kinds are identified: domestic sewage, which adds nutrients and removes dissolved oxygen; industrial effluent, which can injure lake life; and silt from agricultural watersheds, which alters the bottom. The author warns that dilution may fail in bays or enclosed areas, which can become "cesspools." See [[Sewage Pollution]] and [[Silt Deposition]].

**Stream spawning.** Per Langlois (1945), pickerel and muskellunge once ascended the Huron, Maumee and Sandusky rivers to spawn. Chemical wastes and greasy sludge created barriers to movement. Silt at river mouths (Raisin, Maumee, Portage, Sandusky) eliminated rooted vegetation, removed breeding grounds and food for waterfowl, and increased wave action. This accelerated [[Shore Line Erosion]] by about 2 feet per year in some areas. A clay bluff at the Ohio-Pennsylvania line was cut back 10 to 20 feet in one storm on November 2, 1942.

**Water diversions and levels.** The out-diversion at Chicago is limited to 3,100 cubic feet per second (1,500 c.f.s. direct flow plus 1,600 c.f.s. domestic pumpage). Since 1937, waters from the Hudson Bay drainage have been diverted into Lake Superior: 1,400 c.f.s. by way of Long Lac and 3,600 c.f.s. from Lake Nipigon from 1943, a total of 5,000 c.f.s. Langlois (1952) suggests this may have raised Lake Erie by 3 inches. Lake Erie's extreme fluctuation was a 7-foot rise at one end and an equal drop at the other, a 14-foot differential. Tides in Lake Michigan amount to about 5 centimeters. See [[Diversion of Waters]] and [[Water Level Fluctuations]].

**Canals.** The [[Chicago Drainage Canal]], [[Welland Canal]], Erie Barge Canal, Trent Canal and several Ohio and Indiana canals connect the lakes to other systems. The author notes they may not have changed levels but each carries potential to alter the physical environment.

## Key Findings

- **Commercial decline.** Average annual yield was about 101 million pounds for 1879–1918. Since then it has fluctuated around 79 million pounds, a decrease of nearly one-fourth. The 1940–1947 average was about 75 million pounds. Yield fell more than 22 percent after 1918 despite a larger fleet, better gear and expanded grounds.
- **Shift to rough fish.** The proportion of rough fish in the catch rose sharply between 1879–1903 and 1936–1945: Superior from 20 to 80 percent, Michigan 9 to 28 percent, Huron 17 to 29 percent, Erie 10 to 32 percent, and Ontario 38 to 60 percent. Van Oosten (1949) attributes the greater share of this change to loss of fine fish rather than increased rough fish production.
- **Causes are uncertain.** Candidate causes include overfishing, infectious disease (the smelt), sea lamprey predation, competition from smelt or alewife, pollution, and unknown environmental change. The author stresses that the cause-and-effect links are not established.
- **Alewife.** Entered the upper lakes through the Welland Canal after its opening on April 20, 1931 (official opening August 6, 1931). A single Lake Erie specimen was reported on September 23, 1931, and the species reached Lake Huron two years later. It is not abundant above Niagara. Its effect on other species is unknown.
- **Smelt.** Introduced to Crystal Lake, Benzie County, Michigan, in 1913 to feed lake trout, then spread to Lakes Michigan and Huron. Catch averaged a little over 1 million pounds yearly during 1931–1939 and over 3.6 million pounds in 1940–1943. It collapsed to 4,500 pounds in 1944, then recovered to 786,000 pounds in 1947. The cause of the collapse is not known; Van Oosten suspects a bacterial or viral disease.
- **Sea lamprey and lake trout.** The lamprey is described as a low vertebrate, entirely parasitic after its larval stage, and probably introduced to the upper lakes through the Welland Canal. Lake trout catches collapsed: Lake Huron averaged 1,685,000 pounds (1895–1939) and fell to 1,000 pounds in 1949 and 65 pounds in 1950. Lake Michigan averaged 6.5 million pounds (1940–1944), then 342,000 pounds (1949) and 54,000 pounds (1950). Lake Superior remained relatively stable, at just under 3 million pounds in 1949 and nearly 3.2 million in 1950 (U.S. waters).
- **Doubt about lamprey causation.** Lampreys show host preferences (dogfish most readily) and attack many species without a clear link to their population changes. Attacks are not necessarily fatal to trout. Lake Superior, the least affected lake, still has the best trout fishery. The author suggests the lamprey may be one decimating factor rather than the decimating factor.
- **Shoreline and water-level policy.** Water level changes are described as predictable and therefore manageable, not "acts of God." The author criticizes fixed legislation, such as the 3,100 c.f.s. Chicago limit, as "arbitrary," and recommends giving an authority the responsibility of adjusting regulations to changing conditions.
- **Research gap.** The author concludes that pristine conditions are unknown, statistical baselines are limited, and the number of specialists available to unravel cause and effect is "woefully small."
