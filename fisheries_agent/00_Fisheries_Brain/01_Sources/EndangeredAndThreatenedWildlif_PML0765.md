# Bald Eagle Reclassification Proposal (1994)

#ornithology #marine-biology #haliaeetus-leucocephalus

## Executive Summary

This 1994 [[Federal Register]] proposed rule from the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] (FWS) would reclassify the [[Bald Eagle]] from endangered to threatened across most of the [[Lower 48 States]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The bald eagle would stay endangered in the southwestern United States and be listed as endangered in [[Mexico]]. The proposal rests on sustained population growth since the 1960s and 1970s, with breeding numbers rising from 417 active nests in 1969 to about 4,000 occupied breeding areas in 1993, and on the fact that four of five [[Recovery Plan]] regions had met their threatened-status goals. The FWS was also considering delisting in parts of the range but did not propose it at that time.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The bald eagle (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*) is a large, powerful raptor with a white head and tail, and is the national bird of the United States. The document describes it as a bird of aquatic habitats, feeding mainly on fish, with waterfowl and other prey species also taken.
- **Protective history:**
  - The [[Bald Eagle Protection Act]] (1940) prohibited take, possession, sale, and trade of bald eagles.
  - The eagle was listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1978 as endangered in most of the lower 48 States, except Michigan, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Washington, and Oregon, where it was designated threatened (43 FR 6233).
  - The [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]] also protects the species.
- **Decline causes:** Historical declines were driven by [[DDT]] and other persistent organochlorines, which caused eggshell thinning and reproductive failure through their metabolite [[DDE]], along with shooting, habitat loss, and human disturbance.
- **Recovery structure:** The FWS divided the lower 48 States' bald eagles into five [[Recovery Regions]], each with its own recovery plan and team. Plans were approved as follows:
  - [[Chesapeake Bay Recovery Region]]: 1982, revised 1990
  - [[Pacific Recovery Region]]: 1986
  - [[Southeastern Recovery Region]]: 1984, revised 1989
  - [[Northern States Recovery Region]]: 1983
  - [[Southwestern Recovery Region]]: 1982
  - The Northern and Southwestern plans were under active revision.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and diet**
- Bald eagles are associated with aquatic ecosystems throughout most of their range, including estuaries, large lakes, reservoirs, and rivers.
- Nests are usually in large trees near water, and nesting never occurs farther than 3 km (2 miles) from water.
- Fish dominate the typical diet, supplemented by waterfowl and other prey, along with carrion when readily available.
- Wild bald eagles commonly live about 20 years, and up to 50 years in captivity.

**Threats identified in the proposal**
- **Habitat loss and disturbance:** Habitat loss is described as a major challenge to recovery. Shoreline development, recreation, and human disturbance remain threats, particularly around nests and in the Southeastern and Northern regions.
- **Contaminants:** Organochlorines and [[Lead Poisoning]] from ingested lead shot and sinkers are still concerns, and mercury and other pollutants affect some populations.
- **Power lines:** Electrocution on power poles is a continuing mortality source.
- **Small, isolated populations:** The [[Southwestern Recovery Region]] population is small and isolated, with an estimated minimum annual adult mortality of 16–22%.
- **Shooting and poisoning:** These are persistent, though the document describes shooting as a lesser threat in some areas.
- **Disease:** Disease is not considered a significant population-level threat, although diseases such as avian cholera, aspergillosis, and botulism affect individual birds. Only 2.7% of bald eagles submitted to the National Wildlife Health Research Center from 1985 to 1990 died from infectious disease.

## Key Findings

### Population trends
- The 1969 National Survey reported only **417 active nests** in the lower 48 States, with an average of **0.59 young per active nest**.
- By **1993**, about **4,000 occupied breeding areas** were reported, with an estimated average of **0.93 young per occupied territory**.
- Occupied breeding areas rose **409%** compared with 1974 (791 territories), and **32%** since 1990 (3,020 territories).
- The species was doubling its breeding population every 6–7 years.

### Regional status and proposed action

| Recovery Region | Status summary (from document) | Service proposal |
|---|---|---|
| Chesapeake Bay | Threatened goals met; delisting goals (300–400 pairs) not met | Reclassify to threatened |
| Northern States | 1,602 occupied areas in 21 States (1993), 0.95 young per area; delisting goals met for occupied areas | Reclassify to threatened |
| Pacific | 1,066 occupied areas (1993), 0.86 young per area; threatened goals met; about 20 of 37 management zones met goals | Reclassify to threatened in California (except the 10-mile strip along the Colorado River), Idaho, Montana, Nevada, and Wyoming |
| Southeastern | 982 occupied areas (1993); 68% of nests successful; 7 of 11 state goals met | Reclassify to threatened |
| Southwestern | 29 occupied areas (1993), 27 young produced; threatened criteria fully met by range expansion | Retain as endangered |

- **Southwestern Region:** Breeding has expanded beyond the Salt and Verde River systems into the Gila and Bill Williams systems in Arizona and the Rio Grande in New Mexico. The document notes that the 1995 entry for Arizona reported 27 occupied territories and 2 for New Mexico (29 total for the region).
- **Pacific Region:** Delisting goals include at least 800 nesting pairs, 1.0 fledged young per pair, and at least 65% success per occupied site.

### Two-population segmentation
- The FWS recognized the **Southwestern population** as distinct, based on evidence of reproductive isolation. It would remain endangered, the adjacent **Mexico** population would be listed as endangered, and the rest of the lower 48 States' population would be reclassified as threatened.
- The FWS also states that a complete reclassification to threatened for all eagles south of Canada is possible in the final rule.

### Regulatory effects
- Under the proposed reclassification, prohibitions in 50 CFR 17.41(a) would apply to the threatened population, and prohibitions in §§17.21 and 17.22 would continue for the endangered population.
- A [[Special Rule]] (§17.41(a)) would allow permits for exhibition, education, selected research, and other special purposes.
- [[Section 7]] consultation requirements would still apply, and the proposal does not reduce [[Section 9]] protections on non-federal land.
- No changes to other federal protections, including the Bald Eagle Protection Act and Migratory Bird Treaty Act, would result.

### Public comment
- The 1990 [[Advance Notice Of Proposed Rulemaking]] (ANPR) drew responses from 4 federal agencies, 22 state agencies, 23 citizen groups, and 140 individuals.
- Seven state agencies favored reclassification or retention as threatened, and the remaining 15 recommended against delisting or reclassification.
- Many commenters objected to reclassification, citing concern about habitat loss, contaminants, reduced federal protection, and possible economic pressure on eagle habitat.
- The FWS stated that a [[Population Viability Analysis]] was unnecessary for this decision, because the recovery plan goals for the Chesapeake, Northern, Southeastern, and Pacific regions were already met.
- Public comments were requested by **October 11, 1994**, and requests for a public hearing had to be received by **August 26, 1994** (45 days after publication).

### Conclusions
- The FWS finds that the bald eagle no longer meets the [[Endangered Species Act]] definition of endangered across most of its lower 48 range, and that it is likely to become endangered in the foreseeable future only if current threats are not managed.
- The proposal is based on the principle that recovery goals were conservative and met the Act's definition of threatened.
- The FWS is not proposing to delist the bald eagle in the lower 48 States at this time.
