# Missourians to Help Shape Future of Columbia Bottom

#conservation #habitat-management #floodplain-ecology #Missouri-River #wetlands

## Executive Summary

The [[Missouri Department of Conservation]] acquired the 4,318-acre [[Columbia Bottom Conservation Area]] at the confluence of the [[Missouri River]] and [[Mississippi River]] from the City of Saint Louis in September 1997. This article from August 1998 describes the agency's public engagement process to develop a management plan for what will become the largest natural area near Saint Louis. The property features 800 acres of forest and a 110-acre island, representing some of the last remaining wildlife habitat along the Missouri River in Missouri. By 1980, more than 90 percent of the river's floodplain forests, prairies and wetlands had been converted to agricultural uses, making this conservation area particularly significant for fish and wildlife.

## Background & Description

[[Columbia Bottom Conservation Area]] is a 4,318-acre property located at the confluence of the Missouri and Mississippi Rivers near Saint Louis, Missouri. The site has a complex history: it was originally the location of a small town known as Columbia (later Saint Vrain) that appeared on plat maps from the 1800s but disappeared by 1870. In 1940, the City of Saint Louis purchased the land as a potential airport site, allowing several farming families to continue agricultural operations.

At the time of acquisition, the property consisted mostly of farmland, with six active farming operations producing crops until July 1999. The bottomland includes 800 acres of forest and a 110-acre island. Most of the area is protected from minor floods by a flood protection levee, though major floods in 1993 and 1995 overtopped this levee, leaving sand deposits and flood debris.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Missouri River]] floodplain has experienced dramatic habitat loss over the past century. Between 1890 and 1980, cultivation of the floodplain increased 43-fold. Floodplain forest that once occupied 76 percent of the land between Saint Louis and Kansas City occupied only 13 percent by 1972 and continued declining. By 1980, more than 90 percent of the river's floodplain forests, prairies and wetlands had been converted for agricultural uses, primarily corn and soybeans.

The rate of conversion accelerated after World War II when the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] began intensive efforts to stabilize the river's banks to support commercial barge traffic. Less than 10 percent of riverside lands remain inundated when flood waters rise, eliminating spawning opportunities for fish species that depend on floodplain inundation for reproduction.

The planned management approach envisions creating a mosaic of shallow wetlands, bottomland forests, bottomland prairies and cropland to attract resident and migratory wildlife. The area's proximity to Saint Louis (30-minute drive) makes it exceptionally rare as an accessible natural area near a major urban center.

## Key Findings

**Management Planning Process:**
- The Missouri Department of Conservation assembled a planning team including representatives from all Department units to develop a comprehensive management plan
- Public input was actively solicited through multiple channels: written comments, phone calls, faxes, email, public hearings, and presentations to local groups
- A conceptual plan was being developed for public comment, with the final plan to take effect after farming agreements expired in July 1999

**Habitat Management Goals:**
- Create an urban conservation area featuring a mosaic of shallow wetlands, bottomland forests, bottomland prairies and cropland
- Preserve habitat qualities for [[Missouri River]] wildlife
- Maintain the 800 acres of existing forest and 110-acre island
- Allow natural flooding processes to support fish spawning in floodplain areas

**Public Access and Recreation:**
- Limited public use began June 9, 1998
- Initial activities included hiking, biking on designated trails, fishing in both the Missouri and Mississippi Rivers, wildlife viewing, and outdoor photography
- Managed archery deer hunt planned for fall 1998
- Future opportunities under consideration: improved fishing and hunting access, expanded biking and hiking trails, wildlife viewing, frogging, nature study, self-guided auto tour
- Planned interpretive site at the river confluence
- Boat access to the Missouri River to be provided

**Visitor Use Patterns:**
- Steady stream of public use observed during summer 1998
- Hiking, biking and river fishing identified as most popular activities by wildlife management biologist Tom Leifield

**Conservation Significance:**
- Represents one of few safe havens for fish and wildlife along the Missouri River
- Provides rare public access to experience the river in a region where most riverside lands are privately owned
- Described by Scott Faber ([[American Rivers]]) as rarely found natural area within 30 minutes of a major city
- Expected to benefit both resident and migratory wildlife species
