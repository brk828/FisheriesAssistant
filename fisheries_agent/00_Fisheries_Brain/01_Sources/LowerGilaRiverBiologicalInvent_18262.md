# Lower Gila River Biological Inventory

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This interagency inventory (1973–1974) documents the biological resources of the lower [[Gila River]] valley in southwestern New Mexico, from the Gila Wilderness boundary to the Arizona state line, a reach of about 67 river miles. The study prioritized the [[Riparian Vegetation]] that makes the valley unique, and related wildlife and aquatic groups (stream flow, fish, herpetofauna, birds, mammals) to it. The inventory found a declining riparian resource with little woody reproduction, a native fish assemblage in decline relative to introduced species, and stream flows that are poorly protected under New Mexico water law. The report recommends riparian management alternatives, flow stabilization, and periodic flooding to sustain native aquatic life.

## Background & Description

The study was undertaken by an interagency group formed in 1973 comprising the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]], the [[U.S. Forest Service]], the [[Soil Conservation Service]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Bureau of Land Management]], and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. Academic contributors included researchers from Prescott College, New Mexico State University, the Museum of Northern Arizona, and Western New Mexico University. The work was motivated by the [[Endangered Species Preservation Act of 1966]] and the need to identify habitat for endangered species.

The study area is a rolling valley floor seldom wider than 500 feet, with two narrow, steep-walled gorges and terraces widening to nearly half a mile. Elevation falls from about 4,700 ft to 3,700 ft. Land ownership is mixed: about 17 miles cross BLM National Resource Lands, 19 miles cross the [[Gila National Forest]], 30 miles cross private land, and under one mile is state land. Agriculture, livestock grazing, water diversion, and natural floods and droughts have substantially altered the valley.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian vegetation.** The riparian type occupies the flood plain and is dominated by [[Fremont Cottonwood]], [[Arizona Sycamore]], and willows, with understory baccharis, forbs, and grasses. The existing riparian type totals 2,572 acres; roughly half is private or state land, 2% BLM, and 21% National Forest land. Dominance shifts along the river: diversity is greatest upstream and lowest downstream, and BLM lands in some reaches are dominated by [[Mesquite]].

**Hydrology.** The [[Gila River]] is an erratic, silt-laden stream with wide seasonal and annual flow fluctuation. Gauging records show an average discharge of 130 cfs near Gila, 194 cfs near Redrock, and 172 cfs near Virden, with extremes ranging from 1 cfs to 41,700 cfs. Surface and ground water are closely linked, and more water leaves New Mexico than is used within the basin. Under the [[Upper Gila Water Decree]], New Mexico law does not require minimum flows, and fish and wildlife uses rank below agricultural and industrial uses.

**Fish habitat and water quality.** Eleven sampling stations were established from Turkey Creek to the state line. In October 1973, water quality at three stations showed temperatures of 52–65 °F, pH 7.5–7.7, and total dissolved solids of 236–307 mg/L. Peak sediment loads reached 26,000 ppm.

**Herpetofauna, birds, mammals.** The valley supports high diversity, including about 97 breeding bird species and 251 species listed in total. Herpetofauna are represented by roughly 1 salamander, 8 toads, 3 frogs, 4 turtles, 24 lizards, and 32 snakes. Mammals include species that depend on riparian habitat and deer, bighorn sheep, and collared peccary that feed there.

## Key Findings

**Riparian condition is declining.**
- Of 2,572 acres, condition ratings for ground cover were low on 69% of area and low for vigor on 56%.
- Older trees predominate; sprouts, seedlings, and saplings are scarce.
- Riparian acreage decreased gradually between 1935 and 1973. The Mangus site lost most vegetation (48 acres in 1935 to 3 acres in 1973).

**Causes of decline include:**
1. Palatability of woody species to livestock and wildlife in sprout and sapling stages.
2. Complex germination requirements of cottonwoods and sycamore, which depend on flooding and recession.
3. Periodic extreme floods (notably October 1972) that uproot trees and deposit sediment.
4. Wide flow fluctuations and diversions that reduce flow and cause drying.

**Management alternatives proposed:** doing nothing (not recommended), excluding livestock from potential riparian areas, deferring grazing until saplings are established, stabilizing flows, acquiring riparian lands, artificial planting, or combinations.

**Fish inventory results.**
- Native species (about 74% of individuals sampled) include [[Longfin Dace]] (283 individuals, 27.66%), [[Sonora Sucker]] (137), [[Desert Sucker]] (136), and [[Loach Minnow]] (91, listed as threatened). [[Spikedace]] (46) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (24) were also recorded.
- Introduced species (about 26%) include [[Channel Catfish]] (126, 12.32%), smallmouth bass (27), rainbow trout (24), and mosquito fish (27).
- The historical native assemblage has declined as introduced species have increased. Floods appear to have created new habitat and produced many young-of-year fish.

**Stream flow policy concern.** Because claimed water rights exceed available supply, future adjudication may further degrade stream ecosystems. A guaranteed minimum flow was recommended to protect aquatic systems and recharge groundwater that supports riparian vegetation.

**Herpetofauna and birds.** The Gila River is described as a unique herpetofaunal area and as the most important avifauna refuge in the Colorado River drainage. Notable species include [[Mexican Duck]] (endangered), [[Peregrine Falcon]] (endangered), [[Northern Black Hawk]], [[Zone-tailed Hawk]], and [[Gila Woodpecker]]. The report also notes conflicts between recreationists and birds during breeding season and recommends coordinated landowner management.

**Mammals.** Mule deer populations rose over the past decade, generating depredation complaints on alfalfa and cotton fields. A 1973 [[Scent Station Survey]] estimated coyote and fox indices along two Gila River lines (coyote indices 140 and 153; gray fox 104 and 40; kit fox 0 and 28).

**Overall conclusion.** The valley's riparian and aquatic resources depend on periodic flooding and adequate flows. Without coordinated management, the report concludes that riparian types will continue to deteriorate and native fish will decline further.
