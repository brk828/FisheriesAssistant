# Coachella Canal Study: Fish, Amphibian, Reptile and Wildlife Resources

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1974 report, prepared for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] under a cooperative agreement with the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], inventories fish, reptile, amphibian, mammal and bird resources, and recreational consumptive use, along the 49-mile [[Coachella Canal]] and adjacent seepage areas in Imperial and Riverside Counties. The canal was slated for concrete lining, and the study was intended to establish baseline resource data. Fish were sampled by electrofishing and gill netting; the dominant gamefish were [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]], and four [[Endangered Species]] fish were noted as not abundant in the basin. Wildlife surveys covered rodents, bats, furbearers, birds (235 species recorded or listed), and reptiles and amphibians, with [[Mourning Dove]] habitat and hunter use also assessed. The authors note limits on time, data completeness and sampling methods.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Coachella Canal and a strip of land between the Coachella and Imperial canals (west), and a strip between Siphon Seven (north) and the All-American Canal turnout (south). Four vegetative communities were delineated: [[Creosote-Scrub]], [[Riparian Habitat]], [[Marsh Habitat]] and agricultural land (not vegetatively evaluated).
- **Objectives:** (1) determine fishery resources including reptiles and amphibians; (2) determine wildlife resources including small mammals, birds and rare or endangered species; (3) assist in mapping key wetland areas; (4) determine recreational consumptive use.
- **Limitations:** Short field season, incomplete knowledge of many species, and surveys conducted in a single month (October 1974) that represent only one season.
- **Report structure:** Vegetation, wildlife (mammals, birds, waterfowl, hunter use), fisheries, reptiles and amphibians, and appendices with species checklists and survey data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation.** Surveys used [[Line-Point Transect]] methods (14 transects: 9 creosote-scrub, 3 riparian, 2 marsh in the source text's tally; the document lists 9 creosote, 13 riparian, 7 marsh in one passage, so counts should be verified against the scan). Dominant canal-bank plants included [[Eurasian Watermilfoil]] and sago pondweed in the canal, and tamarisk, mesquite and cattail in riparian and marsh zones. Vegetation checklists are summarized in Appendices C and D.

**Mammals.** Recorded or expected species included [[Burro Deer]] (less than 1 per square mile in creosote-scrub, under 6 per square mile in riparian areas), [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]] (occasional visitors, not dependent on the study area), feral burros, coyote, bobcat, kit fox, and several rodents. Rodent trapping (September-October 1974, 2,108 trap-nights) found Merriam's kangaroo rat, desert pocket mouse, cactus mouse and deer mouse, with the hispid cotton rat found in marsh and riparian habitats. Spotlight surveys covered 93 transects over roughly 933 acres of creosote-scrub, riparian and marsh.

**Birds.** The study recorded 235 bird species overall (the Species Check List appendix covers loons to sparrows), with 119 observed during October 1974. Transect counts of breeding and wintering birds were reported by habitat type. Rare or endangered species that may occur include the black rail, American peregrine falcon and California yellow-billed cuckoo.

**Waterfowl.** Salton Sea waterfowl census data for January 1974 (Table 10) are included. The [[Salton Sea]] is important wintering habitat along the Pacific Flyway.

**Recreational use.** Hunter effort was estimated from a 1972 hunter survey (about 48% response). Mourning dove is the most heavily hunted game bird in Imperial County, and quail and cottontail are also hunted.

**Reptiles and amphibians.** Drift fence surveys (October 1974) captured 40 lizards, 2 snakes and 5 amphibians, and 12 reptile and 4 amphibian species were captured or observed during the study. The desert tortoise is classed as "depleted" by the California Department of Fish and Game. Possession limits apply to several reptiles, including the desert iguana and the spiny softshell turtle.

**Seepage ponds and canal.** Canal banks support aquatic plants and irregularities that provide cover; seepage ponds are isolated by marsh and riparian vegetation and are very favorable breeding areas for bullfrogs.

## Key Findings

- **Fish community (electrofishing and gill netting, 1974):** Largemouth bass dominated sampled canal sections, with abundant young-of-year indicating good reproduction. Channel catfish (eleven sampled, three young-of-year) and flathead catfish (multiple age classes, indicating a population expanding since its 1962 introduction to the lower Colorado) were also present. Centrarchids (bluegill, green sunfish, crappie, redear sunfish) were scarce, which the authors attribute partly to bass predation and the high canal flows.
- **Population estimate:** The minimum estimate was 1,500 to 3,000 fish per mile; electrofishing was said to give relative abundance, not total population.
- **Angler use:** Estimated at roughly 900 angler-use days per year, derived from Imperial Irrigation District creel data and reduced by one-third for access problems.
- **Endangered fish:** Roundtail chub, bonytail, Colorado squawfish and humpback sucker are listed endangered; a young humpback sucker was found in the East Highline Canal and another in 1974, suggesting possible spawning in the lined canals.
- **Fish species list:** Thirty-plus species are known to occur in the waters that feed the canal (Table 3), including threadfin shad, rainbow trout, carp, mosquitofish and tilapia; rainbow trout cannot survive year-round in the canal's warm water.
- **Seepage ponds:** Five ponds were sampled; only one contained gamefish (largemouth bass), and mosquitofish occurred in most. Bullfrogs were abundant across seepage areas, with significant tadpole numbers. Rotenone-type treatment (HTH) was used on some streams.
- **Herpetofauna:** Drift fences captured mainly side-blotched lizards, and zebra-tailed and Great Basin whiptail lizards were abundant. No rare or endangered reptiles or amphibians were found in the study area.
- **Mammals:** Burro deer densities were low; the study area did not support a sizeable deer population.
- **Mourning dove habitat:** The report documents steady decline of natural riparian and mesquite habitat in Imperial County since 1950 and a rise in citrus and agricultural crops, a trend expected to continue to reduce dove nesting success.
- **Conclusion:** The authors state that the canal is a valuable game fishery and that the study provides baseline data, but that more seasons of data would be needed for full evaluation.
