# Gila Topminnow Habitat Use and Preference: 1992 Annual Report and 1993 Permit Request

#ichthyology #aquatic-ecology #endangered-species #gila-topminnow

## Executive Summary

This document is a December 1992 cover letter and annual report submitted by [[William J. Matter]] and Robert Forrest of the [[University of Arizona]] to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Regional Director John G. Rogers). It covers activities under [[Endangered Species Subpermit]] PRT-676811 for the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] and requests permission to continue work in 1993. The study measures habitat use and preference of the species, funded by the [[Tonto National Forest]] and conducted through the [[Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit]] and the School of Renewable Natural Resources. The 1992 field work involved visual site surveys in [[Cienega Creek]], and laboratory trials used a captive population. The 1993 proposal would test a new trap design intended to capture fish alive from marshy habitats where abundance is hard to estimate.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] is a small freshwater fish listed as endangered. The source document does not give its scientific name, family, or detailed morphology. The research has two phases:

1. **Field measurement:** detailed records of areas used by topminnow at several field sites.
2. **Laboratory experiments:** tests of fish response to controlled environmental conditions.

The research team holds topminnow in a self-sustaining captive population at the USFWS–AZCFWRU fisheries compound. The population was reported at roughly 700 to 1,000 fish (OCR text reads "70O to l-rOO0"). The captive stock supplies fish for the laboratory trials and for planned outdoor test pools.

The letter is signed by William J. Matter, Associate Professor of Wildlife and Fisheries Science, and is copied to the Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit and the School of Renewable Natural Resources. Enclosures were included with the letter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and field methods (May 1992):**
- Two trips were made to [[Cienega Creek]], Arizona, in May 1992.
- Investigators visually located areas with abundant topminnow and recorded environmental conditions: water depth, velocity, temperature, and substrate type.
- Six sites were sampled, classified as 2 pools, 2 backwaters, and 2 glides.
- No topminnow were captured or killed during the field visits.

**Habitat cover tested in the laboratory:**
- Captive fish were allowed to "choose" among cover types: floating algae mats, vascular plants, floating styrofoam sheets, and plastic strips.
- Fish were also given a choice of water depths.
- Time spent in each condition was recorded as an index of preference.

**Fish welfare:**
- Fish were returned alive to a holding pool at the Arizona cooperative unit fisheries compound after each trial.
- Only 3 fish died during the tests.

**Methodological constraint:** The document states that estimating the relative numbers of fish in marshy areas has been hindered. Visual counts are impossible there, and most collection methods are impractical. This gap limits assessment of how important these sites are to topminnow in nature.

## Key Findings

**Completed work (1992):**
- Two field trips to Cienega Creek in May 1992 surveyed six sites (2 pools, 2 backwaters, 2 glides).
- Habitat variables measured included water depth, velocity, temperature, and substrate type.
- Laboratory preference trials used cover types and water depths, with time-in-condition as the index.
- Mortality during testing was low: 3 fish died.
- The captive population was reported at about 700 to 1,000 fish.

**Requests for 1993:**
1. **Continue holding a captive population** of Gila topminnow for laboratory tests.
2. **Test the effectiveness of small minnow traps** (approximately 1/4 in diameter, per the OCR text) for capturing live topminnow.
   - Known numbers of captive fish would be placed in small above-ground outdoor pools with varied cover that simulates marshy Cienega Creek habitat.
   - Capture efficiency of the proposed "mini-minnow traps" would be measured.
3. **Field deployment (conditional):** If an effective design is found, it would be tested in marshy areas of Cienega Creek.

**Stated significance:** Successful trap development would allow researchers to estimate relative fish numbers in marshy sites, which would advance understanding of [[Habitat Preference]] and habitat use in this endangered species.

**Limitations of the document:** It gives no sample sizes for the laboratory trials, no quantitative preference results, and no statistical analysis. Results are described only qualitatively.
