# Razorback Sucker Recovery and Otolith Crystalline Structure

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #catostomidae #endangered-species #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This document describes recovery efforts for the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of western North America. The species, once abundant and used as food by indigenous peoples, has experienced severe population declines. The document outlines a recovery program including culture and stocking initiatives, reintroduction strategies, and habitat management. Additionally, it reports a significant finding regarding otolith crystalline structure: all examined razorback sucker otoliths were composed entirely of vaterite, a rare and unstable polymorph of calcium carbonate, rather than the typical aragonite found in most teleost fishes.

## Background & Description

[[Xyrauchen texanus]] is a monotypic genus endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of western North America, with historical range extending from Wyoming, USA, to northwest Mexico. The species was historically abundant and served as an important food source for indigenous peoples in the region. Historical records document the species as "rather common" in various locations including the [[San Pedro River]] (Chamberlain 1904), abundant at Yuma and points below Gila Bend, and present in the Mexican portion of the Colorado River.

The razorback sucker is a long-lived western fish species. Wild-caught specimens examined were 9 years of age (McCarthy and Minckley 1988). The species exhibits specific spawning behavior, with spawning occurring from mid-April to early June. Adults have been documented moving considerable distances for reproduction, with one radiotagged individual moving 11 km in the [[Green River]] from a tagging locality to the [[Duchesne River]], a suspected spawning ground, in April 1981.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The razorback sucker inhabits diverse aquatic habitats within the Colorado River system, including main river channels, tributary mouths, flooded backwaters, reservoirs, and oxbow lakes. In the [[Green River]] and [[Duchesne River]], individuals were found in depths ranging to 3.4 m. Movement patterns indicate that some individuals remain in specific areas while others move between main channels and tributary mouths or flooded backwaters.

Historical distribution included populations at [[Lake Havasu]] (documented since 1942), Topock Marsh upstream from Ehrenberg, Arizona (1943), and areas between Laguna Dam and various points along the lower Colorado River. The species was collected from the Verde River and documented in general surveys from 1883 to 1987 between Laguna Dam and other locations.

The species faces multiple threats related to habitat modification in the vast and complex Colorado River system. Sampling challenges in turbulent river environments have complicated population assessments. The document emphasizes that few regional experts are involved in recovery efforts, publication outlets are limited, and data management across agencies (Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) requires improvement.

## Key Findings

**Recovery Program Development:**
- A comprehensive recovery program was formulated based on biological data and emergency criteria
- Recovery options include culture and stocking programs, with detailed implementation plans
- Reintroduction strategies were developed as primary recovery approaches
- Future actions and recovery criteria were established for this long-lived species
- Broodstock programs were initiated, including "Mohave brood" and "Concern brood" operations
- Hatchery propagation was conducted at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], Dexter, New Mexico

**Otolith Crystalline Structure Discovery:**
- Nineteen otoliths from wild-caught (9 years of age) and hatchery-reared young-of-the-year and three-year-old razorback suckers were analyzed
- X-ray powder diffraction using Debye-Scherrer Camera with copper K-alpha radiation revealed all otoliths were composed entirely of **vaterite**
- This finding is significant because vaterite is rarely found in nature and is unstable, typically undergoing progressive crystalline transformation (vaterite → aragonite → calcite) in the presence of water at room temperature and pressure
- Most teleost fish otoliths are composed of aragonite; vaterite has been reported only in primitive fishes (bichir, bowfin, gars, sturgeons) and some hatchery salmonids
- Wild specimens were trammel netted from [[Lake Mohave]], AZ-NV; hatchery fish were provided by Dexter National Fish Hatchery
- Otoliths were excised, cleared of soft tissues, dried, and ground for analysis
- Crystalline d-spacing analysis confirmed vaterite composition

**Ecological and Management Implications:**
- The dominance of vaterite may be influenced by environmental factors surrounding the otolith, including high pH or presence of certain amino acids (glycine, serine, glutamate)
- Crystalline structure of otoliths may be taxon-specific, suggesting this is a characteristic feature of [[Catostomidae]] or specifically *Xyrauchen*
- Otolin protein (molecular weight ~150,000) is uniform across taxonomic groups, but crystalline structure varies
- The finding has implications for age determination and population studies using otolith analysis

**Conservation Status:**
- The species has declined from historical abundance to imperiled status
- Multiple agencies are involved in recovery efforts, including coordination between state and federal offices
- Data management and information sharing among agencies require improvement
- The work was supported by Arizona State University and the Office of Endangered Species, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Albuquerque, NM
