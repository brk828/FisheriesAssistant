# Micropopulations in Fish and Their Effects on Differentiation and Speciation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by W. Villwock examines how small (micro-) populations of fish influence morphological differentiation and [[Speciation]]. It argues that small populations can produce striking local differences, but that microgeographic separation alone does not guarantee true speciation. The chapter develops two main case studies: the bittersalt-lake [[Aphanius]] populations of [[Lake Aci-Gol]] in Turkey, and the [[Cichlidae]] "explosive" species flocks of East African rift lakes. It concludes that [[Intralacustrine Speciation]] is a plausible route for many cichlid radiations, supported by the mating-behaviour hypothesis of [[Kosswig Hypothesis]] and by mitochondrial DNA evidence for monophyletic origin in [[Lake Victoria]] cichlids.

## Background & Description

Villwock notes that few fish examples have been studied in detail for the effects of small population size on differentiation and speciation, even though small founder numbers are known to drive rapid spread. A cited example is three pairs of muskrats (*Ondatra zibethicus*, a mammal) introduced in 1905 from Canada to Bohemia, which spread across central Europe in under 50 years. The author distinguishes this from speciation proper: introduced species that do not give rise to new species. Rosenthal (1993) is cited for some thousand fin-fish transplant cases documented in total.

The chapter focuses on processes by which population size interacts with differentiation of characters, "used in its broadest sense," and speciation mechanisms.

### Anatolian Aphanius (Cyprinodontidae)

[[Kosswig]] (1956) is credited as the first to report explicitly on macro- and micropopulations in fish, using endemic cyprinodonts from [[Lake Aci-Gol]], a bittersalt lake in southwest Turkey. In summer the lake dries to crystalline sodium sulfate, confining fish to brooks and springs. In winter the centre becomes a hypersaline swamp.

The populations are so morphologically different that an ichthyologist unfamiliar with the biota could mistake them for two species in two genera. They fall into three groups based on body shape, indices and scale formation:

- Populations 1–3: small brooks of slowly running freshwater, with normal scalation and [[Aphanius]]-like body shape.
- Populations 4a–7: living permanently in contact with concentrated bittersalt water, with almost complete scale reduction. These were proposed as a separate genus, [[Anatolichthys]] (Kosswig and Sözer 1945).
- Remaining populations: exposed to fluctuating conditions, showing "mixed" characters.

Each group comprises from very few hundred to many ten thousands of specimens.

### East African Cichlids (Cichlidae)

Around 200 to 300 cichlid species are endemic to East African Rift Valley lakes. The best-studied groups are the [[Haplochromis]] ("haplochromines") of [[Lake Victoria]] and the [[Tropheus]] ("Mbuna") of [[Lake Tanganyika]], [[Lake Malawi]] and Lake Nabugabo. Greenwood (1964) coined the term "explosive speciation" for very rapid evolution of many closely related species in one geographic area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Environmental drivers in Aci-Gol.** Water chemistry governs the populations. Strong fountains, rainfall and spring snowmelt alternate between freshwater and sodium sulfate conditions. Scale reduction and body-shape differences are linked to these physiological responses. Since postglacial times the climate has become progressively more arid, so the old lake converted into a swamp with a surrounding chain of springs, ponds and brooks. The Aci-Gol forms are thought to have arisen stepwise during that transition. A "mixed" macropopulation still exists in [[Lake Burdur]], and may serve as a model for isolating mechanisms when historic climate changes split populations.

**Parallels in the New World.** Desert water remnants in the southwestern USA (Death Valley system, Nevada) and northern Mexico harbour *Cyprinodon* species, which show adaptations to water conditions very similar to those of Old World *Aphanius*, with comparable speciation phenomena (Corcillius 1979).

**Other lakes.** Intralacustrine speciation is also accepted for many groups in [[Lake Ohrid]], where [[Salmo letnica]] is dividing into four distinct species *in statu nascendi* by forming four reproductive units. [[Lake Titicaca]] (Peru/Bolivia) is suggested as another case, with endemic [[Orestias]] species. [[Lake Baikal]] is cited as having undergone intralacustrine speciation.

**Habitat segregation in cichlid lakes.** Differences in bottom structure, coastline (rocky versus sandy or muddy), depth and submerged plants can separate populations within large lakes. Non-adapted specimens are expected to lose out, ending gene flow.

## Key Findings

**1. Small size does not automatically produce species.** Aci-Gol populations differ strongly in external characters, but they remain interfertile in hybrids. Villwock concludes that microgeographic separation alone does not lead to valid speciation, even where Mendelian inheritance of the differing characters is demonstrated (Villwock 1958; Aksiray and Villwock 1962; Villwock 1963).

**2. Inheritance and environment.** The characters involved are under polygenic control and Mendelian heredity. They are correlated with physiological responses to water conditions, and their expression depends on population size and how the involved genes are distributed. The common ancestor was probably a panmictic "hybrid swarm."

**3. Intralacustrine speciation is a statistical outcome.** Given enough habitat niches with matching feeding or reproductive adaptations and associated behaviour, differing populations should develop quickly. Occasional interbreeding by unspecialised individuals is unlikely to prevent isolation, because non-fitting mating patterns cause them to be rejected. Cross-breeding experiments by Crapon de Caprona and Fritzsch (1984) are cited as partial support.

**4. Three segregation strategies in old cichlid lakes:**
- Homogamy (assortative pairing) between certain pairs of cichlids.
- Female selection of distinctly coloured males of the same species.
- Differently developed feeding habits combined with corresponding colour varieties.

**5. Feeding specialisation.** Greenwood's work shows that Haplochromis adaptations in dentition, jaw form and digestive physiology allow species to exploit nearly every food source in the lake. Figures contrast generalised insectivores, snail-eaters, egg-and-larvae eaters (taken from brooding females' mouths) and piscivores. Pharyngeal bone series run from generalised bicuspid to specialised molariform dentition. Later workers confirmed these patterns (Axelrod 1976; Witte and Witte-Maas 1981; Van Oijen 1982; Hoogerhoud et al. 1983; Brichard 1992).

**6. Colour morphs and geography.** *Tropheus* colour varieties are segregated into separate lake areas, as in *T. moori* and *T. duboisi* forms in Lake Tanganyika. Female preference for particular colour morphs could produce speciation, provided it is heritable or imprinted. Villwock notes that controlled studies with sufficient numbers are lacking.

**7. Egg-model mating markers.** Haplochromine males bear egg-like spots on the anal fin. The female mistakes these for her own eggs, pecks at them, and triggers sperm release, fertilising eggs in her mouth. Such precise behavioural cues function like colour morphs in segregating populations (Greenwood 1965; Brichard 1992).

**8. Homogamy and genetics.** Cichlid pairs are often outwardly indistinguishable, and the selective agent is unknown. Kosswig (1947, 1963a) proposed that homogamy divides each species into many small genetic units, each sharing mating genes, so that each "species" is a set of smaller real species. Villwock finds many aspects "highly probable" but does not regard the hypothesis as proven. Individually marked-animal studies are still lacking.

**9. Geographic versus intralacustrine explanations.** Greenwood and others attribute much segregation in Lake Victoria to separation of lagoons and shallows. Villwock argues that neither geographic nor intralacustrine theory alone explains all observed segregation. In large, deep lakes such as Tanganyika and Malawi, ecological and ethological differences are seen as the main drivers, with segregation dating back about 200,000 years. Owen et al. (1990) report that Malawi's Mbuna on recently inundated rocky habitats show that intralacustrine speciation has outweighed speciation in separate water bodies.

**10. Phylogenetic conclusion.** The idea that most African cichlid groups arose polyphyletically through allopatric speciation seems false for Lake Victoria and Lake Malawi cichlids. Meyer et al. (1990) use mitochondrial DNA sequences to suggest a monophyletic origin from one species flock that split through selective breeding, that is, through intralacustrine processes.
