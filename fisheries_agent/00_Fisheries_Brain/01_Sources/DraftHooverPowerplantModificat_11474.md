# Hoover Powerplant Modification: Draft Environmental Impact Statement

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation Draft Environmental Impact Statement (DEIS) evaluates increasing the peaking generating capacity of [[Hoover Dam]] and Powerplant on the Arizona-Nevada border. Three build alternatives and a no-action alternative are compared. The proposed action (Alternative 1) adds a 500 MW [[Surface Powerhouse]] on the Arizona side, raising total capacity to 2,300 MW. Alternative 2 replaces units A8 and A9 with one 350 MW unit (net gain 260 MW, total 2,060 MW). Alternative 3 builds a 500 MW [[Underground Powerhouse]] to the same total of 2,300 MW. The project adds peaking capacity but no additional energy.

The principal environmental concerns are increased water velocities, which could reduce periphyton (algae) productivity below the dam, and larger weekly water-level fluctuations on [[Lake Mohave]], which could reduce the largemouth bass and sunfish fishery and razorback sucker spawning success. The document concludes that no endangered or threatened species would be jeopardized, and that the project would have no cumulative or synergistic effects on the Colorado River Basin. The document is intended to support a Section 404(r) exemption under the Clean Water Act and compliance with Executive Orders 11988 and 11990 and Section 10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act.

## Background & Description

[[Hoover Dam]] was built under the Boulder Canyon Project Act of 1928, with construction from 1931 to 1935 and first commercial power in 1937. The plant has an installed capacity of about 1,340 MW from 17 generating units. A routine uprating program, scheduled for completion in the late 1980s, is replacing windings and turbine runners in 14 units and will raise nameplate capacity to about 1,800 MW at a rated head of 490 feet. Power is marketed by [[Western Area Power Administration]].

Public Law 94-156 (1975) authorized feasibility studies of the powerplant. The stated purpose is to determine the optimum peaking capacity that can be added within constraints of water operations, environment, recreation, and marketing. The document uses a bucket-and-spigot analogy to explain that peaking capacity changes the rate of release, not the total energy available.

The Black Canyon section below the dam is roughly 18 to 20 miles long, with volcanic walls about 500 feet apart rising about 700 feet above the riverbed. Lake Mead, the largest U.S. reservoir by capacity (28.5 million acre-feet), lies upstream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and aquatic productivity.** The first 3 miles below the dam are the most productive river reach, due to light, velocity, and a rocky armored substrate that supports [[Periphyton]] (dominated by *Cladophora glomerata*, with diatoms *Diatoma vulgare* and *Melosira varians*). Benthic invertebrates are low in diversity, dominated by the amphipod *Hyalella* and chironomid larvae. Daily release cycles expose periphyton and invertebrates to air.

**Temperature and the warm/cold interface.** Lake Mead's hypolimnion stays near 54°F year-round. Releases from [[Hoover Dam]] are cold, and in summer they underflow warmer Lake Mohave surface water (60-80°F), creating a [[Warm-Cold Water Interface]]. The interface location varies with releases and lake elevation, from just below Willow Beach (mile 12.5) to Eldorado Canyon (mile 24). It is the main threat to the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], located 12 miles below the dam, whose trout rely on river water at 54.5°F.

**Fishery.** About 30 fish species are recorded in Lake Mead and Lake Mohave. Native species include the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]] (endangered), humpback chub, and Colorado River squawfish. Trout are stocked because natural reproduction does not occur, and [[Largemouth Bass]] and sunfish support the warm-water fishery. Striped bass were introduced to Lake Mead and have been reported in Lake Mohave, where they may prey on shad and trout. Trout, carp, and razorback suckers are the species most affected in Black Canyon.

**Terrestrial habitat.** Riparian vegetation in Black Canyon is limited, with about 8 acres of mostly [[Salt Cedar]] between the dam and the hatchery. White Rock Canyon, a desert wash of about 242 surface acres, is the proposed borrow site and supports dense small mammal populations (67 animals per acre in July 1980). Double-crested cormorants nest on cliffs about 100 yards below the dam.

**Special status species.** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service reviewed the peregrine falcon, bald eagle, Yuma clapper rail, Devil's Hole pupfish, and bonytail chub. Only the bonytail was found to possibly be affected. Trelease's beavertail cactus was not found in the project area, and the proposal to list it was withdrawn.

**Recreation and socioeconomics.** Lake Mohave supported about 1.65 to 1.82 million visitors in 1979-1980. Willow Beach receives roughly 12 to 20 percent of total use. The region's population (Clark County) grew 69 percent from 1970 to 1980, to 461,816 in the 1980 advance census. Construction would bring 420 peak workers in 1989, 129 of whom would relocate with 164 family members.

**Archaeology.** Hoover Dam is on the National Register of Historic Places. Willow Beach No. 2 is eroding and may be affected by higher water levels, while Cholla Rock Shelter is not affected.

## Key Findings

**Alternatives and capacity**

- Alternative 1 (proposed): 500 MW [[Surface Powerhouse]], total 2,300 MW, cost about $302 million, five-year construction, 9.2 MW capacity loss from cofferdam head loss.
- Alternative 2: Replace units A8 and A9 (90 MW) with one 350 MW unit, net gain 260 MW, total 2,060 MW, about $232 million, 43 years of construction loss noted in Table 2 (text states 4.5 years), 99.2 MW temporary loss.
- Alternative 3: 500 MW [[Underground Powerhouse]], total 2,300 MW, cost about $286 million, no cofferdam, five years, no capacity loss from head.
- No action: uprating only, total 1,800 MW, maximum release 49,000 ft³/s.
- Eliminated alternatives include load management, time-of-day rates, nuclear, coal, combustion turbines, combined-cycle plants, pumped storage, geothermal, solar, wind, and hydrogen. Hualapai (Bridge Canyon) Dam and Glen Canyon and Davis Dam upgrades were also rejected.

**Water and biota impacts (Alternative 1)**

- Velocities below the dam rise from 1.9 mph (existing, mean) to 2.8 mph (mean at 647 ft lake level, 62,000 ft³/s).
- Daily fluctuation at the dam: about 16 ft at lake elevation 647 and 24 ft at 630, falling to 1.5 ft and 6 ft at Willow Beach.
- Cofferdam velocity reaches 8.2 mph, with stable substrate at 12 inches.
- Periphyton productivity may decline in the first 3 miles, with restabilization in about 6 months.
- Downstream temperature varies between 54°F and 57°F, judged to have no discernible effect on aquatic life.
- The interface could reach the hatchery on one to four May or June weekends, with possible loss of some or all hatchery fish.

**Lake Mohave fishery**

- Weekly lake fluctuation rises by about 1 ft per week (up 3 ft, down 2 ft during spring and summer), which may cause an estimated 24 percent reduction in Lake Mohave productivity for bass and sunfish.
- Razorback sucker spawning could be reduced; Bureau studies indicate up to 30 percent of razorback spawning effort exposed.
- Largemouth bass nests succeed 50-58 percent in Lake Mead studies, and about 57 percent when built deeper than 2 ft.

**Terrestrial and special status**

- 34 of 242 acres of desert wash vegetation in White Rock Canyon would be destroyed under Alternative 1 (8 acres under Alternative 2; 5 acres under Alternative 3).
- The FWS concluded the bonytail chub would not be jeopardized; the species is not reproducing in Lake Mohave, and 42,500 hatchery-raised young were introduced in November 1981.
- Razorback sucker egg mortality reached 100 percent in 54°F river water in hatchery tests.

**Recreation and socioeconomics**

- Recreation in sections 12 and 13 of Lake Mohave could decline by 40-80 percent under extreme conditions.
- Peak employment of 420 workers in 1989 would add a population impact of 1,935 to Clark County (Alternative 1).
- Key informants in Henderson and Boulder City rated the project's social and economic impact as neutral overall.

**Compliance and conclusions**

- The EIS is intended to qualify for a Section 404(r) exemption, satisfy Section 10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act, and meet floodplain and wetland requirements.
- No endangered or threatened species would be jeopardized, and no cumulative or synergistic effects on the Colorado River Basin are identified.
- Alternative 1 was used for feasibility design. Alternative 3 may be more cost-effective, and would probably be selected if advance planning confirms it.
