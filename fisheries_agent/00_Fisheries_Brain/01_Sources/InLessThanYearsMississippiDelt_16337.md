# Developing Resistance to Pesticides in Mississippi Delta Wildlife

#ichthyology #marine-biology #live-bearing-fish #herpetology #ecotoxicology

## Executive Summary

This 1963 article by [[Denzel E. Ferguson]], Associate Professor of Zoology at [[Mississippi State University]], reports laboratory and field evidence that vertebrates can develop [[Pesticide Resistance]] within roughly 20 years of heavy insecticide use. Work on [[Cricket Frog]] and [[Mosquito Fish]] populations from the [[Mississippi Delta]] cotton region shows resistance to several pesticides, some more toxic than [[DDT]] and some in use for under 10 years. The article is a rebuttal to the claims in [[Silent Spring]] by [[Rachel Carson]], which predicted that vertebrates could not adapt to pesticides on any practical timescale. Ferguson argues that resistance is a real, heritable, evolutionary process, but he cautions that it is no license for indiscriminate pesticide use.

## Background & Description

The article opens by noting the public and legislative attention Carson's book received, including a special advisory committee appointed by President Kennedy and testimony before several Congressional committees. Ferguson accepts that chemicals have sometimes been used irresponsibly. He disputes Carson's view that vertebrates are defenseless against pesticides. He also points out that the Delta, which has been "drenched with organic insecticides for nearly 20 years," still supports good populations of quail, turkey, waterfowl, deer and fish.

The article is a reprinted popular-science piece (from *Agricultural Chemicals*, September 1963), not a formal research paper. It reports the department's ongoing work and summarizes results without full methods, sample sizes or statistical tests for most findings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mechanism of resistance:** Pre-existing individuals with an enzyme system capable of detoxifying DDT survived exposure while non-resistant animals died. Each application removed more susceptible individuals, so the population became dominated by descendants of a few resistant animals. Ferguson describes this as selection on natural variation, not as pesticides creating resistance.
- **Cross-resistance:** Because most insecticides fall into a few chemical groups, resistance to one compound often conferred resistance to others in the same group, and sometimes to compounds in other groups. Ferguson notes this was well known in insects but had not previously been documented in vertebrates.
- **Bird and nest studies:** Birds from DDT-treated fields contained little DDT but large amounts of [[DDE]], a non-toxic metabolite. Ferguson could not determine whether birds converted DDT to DDE themselves or obtained it from resistant insect prey.
- **Ecosystem balance:** Ferguson suggests that a new equilibrium may form after each application, although he doubts that resistant populations are as well adapted as their susceptible predecessors.
- **Genetic limits:** Small, locally confined populations may lack the genetic variability needed to adapt, so resistance may not be available to every species.

## Key Findings

**Cricket frogs (Delta vs. reference sites)**
- Frogs from heavily treated cotton-field margins tolerated DDT concentrations that caused 100% mortality in frogs with no prior DDT contact.
- These DDT-resistant frogs were also quite resistant to [[Aldrin]].

**Mosquito fish**
- Fish from pesticide-treated Delta sites were resistant to nine compounds: [[DDT]], DDD, [[Endrin]], [[Dieldrin]], heptachlor, aldrin, [[Toxaphene]], [[Lindane]] and methyl parathion.
- Several of these compounds are considerably more toxic than DDT, some have been in use for less than 10 years, and some were never used in cotton insect control.
- In some cases nearly 100 times as much insecticide was needed to produce equivalent mortality in Delta fish compared with fish from near State College, Mississippi.

**Laboratory selection**
- In one trial, 49 of 50 non-resistant fish died within 12 hours of exposure to a particular insecticide concentration; the lone survivor lived for a week without symptoms.
- A project was underway to produce resistant laboratory strains by saving survivors of repeated insecticide treatments.

**Genetic basis**
- Resistant fish maintained a constant level of tolerance after being reared for four generations without insecticide exposure, which Ferguson presents as confirmation of a genetic basis.

**Birds and mammals**
- Over 120 bird nests were studied in treated and untreated Delta fields. No significant differences were found in eggs laid, eggs hatched, young reared or other reproductive statistics.
- Preliminary work suggested resistant birds and mammals probably exist. Ferguson cites a Canadian DDT-resistant mouse strain and English rat populations resistant to [[Warfarin]].

**Conclusions and policy position**
- Ferguson states that resistance does not give humans meaningful protection, because natural selection would require unacceptable human mortality and because human food and water must stay protected until the biological effects of insecticides are better understood.
- Resistance does not justify larger application rates. Larger doses did not significantly improve control in resistant insect populations, and DDT use in the Delta had already been curtailed because of resistant insects.
- Ferguson concludes that natural protective mechanisms, combined with "a common sense approach to use of pesticides," should prevent a silent spring.

**Caveats**
- The article gives few sample sizes, concentrations or statistical tests for most fish and frog results, and it does not give scientific names for the species.
- Ferguson acknowledges that the long-term significance of resistance remains unresolved.
