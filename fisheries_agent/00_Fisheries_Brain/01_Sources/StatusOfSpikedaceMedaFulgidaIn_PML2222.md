# Status of Spikedace (Meda fulgida) in the Verde River, 1999: Implications for Management and Research

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service paper by John N. Rinne reviews six years of study of the [[Spikedace]] population in the upper [[Verde River]], Arizona. The federally listed threatened minnow has declined from common to non-detection since 1997. Calculated probabilities of extirpation from the upper Verde ranged from 0.832 to 0.873, meaning roughly 8 to 9 chances in 10. The author links the decline to the interaction of the [[Hydrograph]] (absence of significant floods) and dominant [[Nonnative Fishes]], and notes that the removal of [[Livestock Grazing]] from the riparian corridor complicates causal attribution. The paper recommends intensified monitoring, suppression of nonnative fishes, identification of refugia streams, culture techniques, and a coordinated regional research approach with the upper [[Gila River]].

## Background & Description

The spikedace is a slender, laterally compressed cyprinid with an olive-tan to brown, often mottled dorsum, silvery sides, and whitish venter. Breeding males turn golden on dorsal and lateral surfaces. It is a diminutive, short-lived, stream-dwelling minnow endemic to the Gila River Basin. Historically it was widespread and locally abundant in the San Pedro (type locality), Salt, Verde, Agua Fria, San Francisco and Gila rivers. It now occurs at intermediate elevations (1,070–1,830 m) in the upper Gila River (New Mexico), [[Aravaipa Creek]], Eagle Creek and the upper Verde River.

Spawning occurs April to June, driven by streamflow and water temperature. Eggs are adhesive and attach to sand-to-gravel substrates. Most biological knowledge comes from Aravaipa Creek and the Gila-Cliff reach, with much of the literature being survey and monitoring work rather than biology.

The paper's objectives were to: (1) document current temporal-spatial distribution and abundance in the upper Verde; (2) evaluate factors affecting its status; and (3) recommend short- and long-term management and research actions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The upper Verde (above Sycamore Creek) is free-flowing, has low baseflow (0.57 m³/sec), a single perennial tributary (Granite Creek), and experiences periodic extreme floods. Spikedace are adapted to southwestern flow variability, but populations here track alternating floods and droughts.

Nonnative fishes include introduced [[Red Shiner]] and predatory species such as [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Yellow Bullhead]]. Their proportional increase has reduced native dominance.

Habitat use: spikedace occupy shallow lentic areas (<1 m) over gravel and pebble substrates, often concentrated where two riffles converge to form eddying currents. In larger rivers they favour riffles with velocities of 30–50 cm/sec and gradients of 0.5–10%, over pebble-cobble-boulder substrates.

Livestock grazing has been suggested to harm spikedace habitat, but the author notes no published accounts document this in the upper Verde or elsewhere in southwestern riparian systems, apart from a withdrawn draft biological opinion.

## Key Findings

- **Decline:** Spikedace were common in 1994 (about 6–7% of the native fish component in 1994 and 1996 samples) but were absent from all seven established sites by spring 1997. Samples in 1997, 1998 and spring 1999 recorded none at the most upstream reaches.
- **Extirpation probability:** Using the [[Grogan and Boreman Method]] with USFS data (1994 start, last collection 1997), P = 0.873. Using the Arizona State University dataset (from 1980, last collection 1997), P = 0.832.
- **Flow effects:** Native fishes responded positively to flooding. After three years (1996–98) without significant flows, native fishes fell to under 30% of the community, and nonnatives comprised over 70%.
- **Nonnative interaction:** Nonnative fishes appear to out-compete and prey upon natives. Predation may be the primary driver of replacement. The author concludes that flow and nonnatives jointly determine community composition, and that the interaction is not yet resolvable with current data.
- **Grazing:** Livestock were eliminated from the river corridor in spring 1997 after a decade of AUM reductions. Riparian vegetation increased sharply, while native fishes and spikedace did not respond positively. Nonnatives increased alongside vegetation. The author states these correlations are untested statistically.
- **Management priorities:** Nonnative fish suppression is considered the highest priority. Waiting for a flood is judged less reliable, though suppression combined with natural flood reduction may be useful. Removal of grazing or limits on sport fishing are described as "cosmetic" by comparison.
- **Genetics:** Translocation from other river systems is not recommended, because Verde spikedace have been isolated since the 1930s dam construction (Horseshoe and Bartlett) and are likely genetically distinct (Tibbets and Dowling 1996).
- **Three immediate actions:** (1) intensify survey and monitoring; (2) locate and evaluate refugia streams in the Verde Basin; (3) develop culture techniques, to be in place before the next flood.
- **Gila comparison:** Following Hurricane Linda flooding in September 1997, spikedace were among the most common native species in the upper Gila (spring 1999). Of over 6,000 fish collected March–July 1999, only 10% were nonnative. This provides a natural comparison with the Verde, where nonnatives dominate.
- **Regional approach:** The author calls for a coordinated, regional (not forest-by-forest) research program with State agencies, examining flood disturbance and fish community succession across the Southwest.
