# The Use of Artificial Migration Barriers in the Conservation of Resident Stream Salmonids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Matthew R. Sloat]] (Montana Cooperative Fishery Research Unit, Montana State University, 1999) evaluates the use of [[Artificial Migration Barriers]] to conserve resident stream salmonids, particularly [[Cutthroat Trout]] populations threatened by non-native [[Brook Trout]]. Barriers prevent upstream colonization by non-native fish and are widely used, yet the published literature on their implementation and ecological consequences is limited relative to that use. The paper outlines barrier design criteria, reviews barrier types, and critically evaluates [[Management by Isolation]]. The central conclusion is that barriers can be the only practical means of excluding invaders, but isolation fragments populations, reduces gene flow, and raises extinction risk, so barriers are unlikely to guarantee long-term persistence unless they are paired with protection of whole sub-drainages.

## Background & Description

The native inland fish fauna of North America is experiencing widespread extinction and population loss, attributed mainly to habitat loss and the spread of exotic species. Predation, competition and hybridization with non-natives commonly reduce native fish ranges. Natural barriers such as waterfalls sometimes protect native populations, but where they are absent, managers construct artificial barriers. Two problems must be addressed when restoring native salmonids: removing non-native trout (by [[Chemical Treatment]] or mechanical removal) and preventing their re-entry, which is usually solved with a barrier.

Refuge-building for native fishes is relatively recent. In 1967 the Nevada Fish and Game Commission built a small barrier to protect [[Moorman Springfish]] at Hot Creek Preserve. In 1968, [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] were stocked into Black Hollow Creek, Colorado, after chemical removal of brook char and construction of a barrier dam. Use of barriers expanded from about 1970 onward, protecting many western cutthroat taxa and [[Gila Trout]], [[Apache Trout]] and [[Golden Trout]].

**Barrier design**
- [[Powers and Orsborn]] (1985) found that passage depends on fish swimming ability, horizontal and vertical jump distances, and the angle to the barrier crest.
- Swimming is described as cruising, sustained and darting (burst) speed (Bell 1986). Leaping data are mostly from anadromous salmonids; resident stream fish are less documented.
- Kruse et al. (1997) and Dunham et al. (1996) treated vertical structures of about 1.5 m as dispersal barriers for resident cutthroat trout, although cutthroat can surmount larger heights depending on structure geometry. In Wyoming, artificial barriers are typically at least 1 m high with a downstream apron over 2 m.
- A launch pool below a barrier strongly affects passage. [[Stuart]] (1962) found leaping conditions ideal at a fall-to-pool-depth ratio of 1:1.25, so effective barriers should eliminate downstream launch pools.
- Barriers must span both channel and floodplain. [[Rosgen Stream Classification]] ratings (Rosgen and Fittante 1986) rank A channels as excellent sites, B channels as excellent to poor depending on entrenchment, and C and D channels as unsuitable because low banks cannot create adequate fall heights.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Barrier success depends on how fish and habitat interact. Water velocity, depth and turbulence, and obstruction height all influence passage. Barriers may fail during extreme flows, and high-water bypass channels can let fish circumvent them.

Trout need spawning, nursery or rearing, adult and overwintering habitat (Behnke 1992). Some move extensively to reach critical habitat, for example downstream into deeper water to overwinter (Cunjak and Power 1986). Because movement needs are site-specific, managers must know the seasonal migration patterns of a local population before placing a barrier. Larger-scale habitat geometry is increasingly seen as important to fish persistence (Rieman and McIntyre 1995), and headwater populations depend on connection with surrounding populations for immigration and recolonization. Habitat arrangement can also change through fire, succession, land management or climate change.

Genetic and demographic risks follow from isolation. Small isolated populations face higher extinction risk from stochastic events, and reduced movement limits genetic exchange. Restricted movement can also produce genetically distinct stocks.

## Key Findings

**Barrier types**
- **[[Gabion Barriers]]**: rock-filled wire baskets with upstream apron, downstream apron or splash pad, bank gabions and a filter cloth. Novotny and Binns (1990) provided construction guidelines. Rock must be sized so that silt fills interstitial spaces; a Nameless Creek barrier failed when oversized (>6–9 in) rock left passage gaps. A large golden trout gabion barrier on the South Fork Kern River cost $100,000 plus $40,000 maintenance. A 30 ft gabion structure was estimated at about $1,200 excluding rock and labor.
- **[[Culvert Barriers]]**: poorly designed road culverts often block fish unintentionally. Intentional barriers use drops of 0.50–0.75 m onto concrete splash pads that prevent resting pools. They are inexpensive and benefit from routine road maintenance, but roads increase angler access and illegal fish transfers.
- **[[Wood Crib Barriers]]**: log or treated-wood cribs backfilled with rock, creating a 1–2 m drop. Lifespan is unknown. A 20 ft structure in White's Gulch, Montana, cost about $20,000. Managers treated it as temporary.
- **[[Bedrock Enhancement]]**: stone-concrete barriers built on natural bedrock, 2–3 m high, with masonry costs from $1,000 (Lee Valley Creek) to $25,000 (Iron Creek). Masonry is highly permanent but depends on local geology.
- **[[Bedrock Alteration]]**: blasting bedrock, for example about 30 m³ removed at Canones Creek, New Mexico, for Rio Grande cutthroat trout. Costs are low, but suitable sites are rare.

**Failures and risks**
- Young et al. (1996) reported barriers in 20 Colorado River cutthroat streams breached by non-native trout, due to introductions, poor design or maintenance.
- Thompson and Rahel (1998) found brook trout passing through interstitial spaces in an apparently intact gabion.
- Avery (1978) reported a Wisconsin culvert barrier washed out in spring flows.
- Illegal introductions by anglers are described as the most insidious threat.
- Propst et al. (1992) reported extirpation of 3 of 11 isolated Gila trout headwater populations after floods, droughts or fires.
- Dunham et al. (1996) described a "ratchet-like" extinction pattern in fragmented Lahontan cutthroat systems.
- Shepard et al. (1998) found that the proportion of westslope cutthroat making moves of 0.5 km or longer was negatively correlated with isolation.
- Allendorf and Phelps (1980) showed that artificial selection and genetic drift reduced polymorphism and heterozygosity in a hatchery westslope cutthroat stock within 14 years.

**Conclusions**
- Young et al. (1996) reported that 21 of 27 indigenous, genetically pure Colorado River cutthroat populations were located above a barrier, suggesting barriers are often the only feasible protection.
- Isolated populations often reflect extinction without recolonization. Kruse et al. (1997) found natural populations generally absent above barriers, while translocated populations could survive there.
- Sloat concludes that barriers are often successful in the short term but may not secure long-term persistence in stochastic headwater habitats.
- Larger, hydrologically diverse sub-drainages provide greater security than small isolated streams, so the paper recommends reclaiming entire sub-drainages. This requires a long-term agency commitment.
