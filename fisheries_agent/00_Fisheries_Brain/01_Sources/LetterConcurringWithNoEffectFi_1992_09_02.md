# Letter Concurring with No Effect Finding for Hicks/Pikes Peak Grazing Allotments

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-conservation #Poeciliidae #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1992 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) letter to the U.S. Forest Service concurring with a "no effect" determination under the Endangered Species Act for the proposed Allotment Management Plan (AMP) governing the Hicks/Pikes Peak livestock grazing allotments (Globe Ranger District, Tonto National Forest, Arizona). The consultation addresses potential impacts of livestock grazing on four federally protected species: the [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] (as an experimental nonessential population), and the [[Bald Eagle]]. While overall grazing impacts were determined to be negligible for three species, the letter documents a significant conservation event: the extirpation of a Gila topminnow population at Sycamore Spring due to infrastructure failure, and outlines a mitigation plan involving translocation to four replacement sites.

## Background & Description

The source document is a regulatory/administrative letter and does not provide morphological, meristic, or anatomical data for any of the referenced species. No descriptions of body form, fin structure, coloration, or size are included in the text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*):
  - Historically occupied **Sycamore Spring**, a spring-fed habitat requiring an artificial water delivery system to remain viable.
  - Population at Sycamore Spring was **extirpated** due to a damaged water system, highlighting the species' vulnerability to habitat maintenance failures in artificially sustained refugia.
  - Four replacement translocation sites were identified as **natural watercourses with natural water supply**, considered ecologically preferable to human-maintained habitats because they do not depend on continuous mechanical/infrastructural upkeep.

- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*):
  - Associated with the **Salt River channel** within the allotment.
  - Most of the channel is described as **inaccessible to livestock**, limiting potential grazing-related degradation.
  - Locations with both livestock accessibility and potential **spawning habitat** were evaluated and determined to have negligible overlap/impact, forming the basis of the no-effect finding.

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):
  - Referenced only as an **experimental nonessential population** under ESA classification; no specific habitat or ecological data provided in this allotment context.

- **[[Bald Eagle]]** (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*):
  - Nesting ecology centers on **cliff-nesting** behavior at the **Pinal Creek** and **Redmond** nest sites, reducing direct exposure to grazing-related ground disturbance.
  - Riparian large tree regeneration was identified as an important secondary habitat factor for long-term foraging/perching opportunities near nest sites, indicating an indirect ecological linkage between grazing management and riparian vegetation structure.

## Key Findings

- **No Effect Determinations**: USFWS concurred with the Forest Service's Biological Evaluation that the Hicks/Pikes Peak grazing AMP would have **no effect** on Gila topminnow, razorback sucker, and bald eagle, and on the experimental population of Colorado squawfish.
- **Bald Eagle Mitigation**: Recommended monitoring of **riparian tree regeneration trends** near eagle nest sites to support long-term nesting habitat quality, despite the no-effect finding.
- **Razorback Sucker Habitat Accessibility**: The primary rationale for the no-effect finding was the **limited physical accessibility** of spawning-capable river channel segments to livestock.
- **Gila Topminnow Extirpation Event**: A population loss occurred at **Sycamore Spring** due to failure of an artificial water delivery system, prompting an amendment to the original Biological Evaluation.
- **Translocation Strategy & Success Rate**: USFWS cited a **20–25% success rate** for Gila topminnow transplant efforts, framing the identification of **four new transplant sites** as a reasonable strategy likely to yield **at least one** viable long-term population.
- **Habitat Preference for Conservation Sites**: Natural watercourses with natural water supplies were explicitly favored over sites requiring ongoing human maintenance, reflecting a broader management principle in [[Endangered Species Recovery Planning]] for reducing long-term reliance on artificial infrastructure.
- **Interagency Cooperation**: The letter frames the Sycamore Spring replacement effort as a model for improved cooperation between the U.S. Forest Service (Globe Ranger District) and USFWS in the recovery of the Gila topminnow.
