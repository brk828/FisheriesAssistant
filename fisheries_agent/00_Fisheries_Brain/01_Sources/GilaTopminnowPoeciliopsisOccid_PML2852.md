# Gila Topminnow (Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis) Revised Recovery Plan (Working Draft, July 1998)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This working draft revises the 1984 recovery plan for the [[Gila Topminnow]], *Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*, a federally endangered livebearer listed in 1967 with no critical habitat designated. The plan covers only the U.S. portion of the range; the [[Yaqui Topminnow]] is handled in a separate [[Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan]] (1994). Only 13 natural localities remain in Arizona. Main threats are habitat destruction and predation or competition from the nonnative [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). Delisting is considered not feasible in the foreseeable future. The short-term goal is preventing extirpation from Arizona and reintroducing the fish into suitable habitat within its former range; downlisting may be possible.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and morphology.** The species was described as *Heterandria occidentalis* by Baird and Girard (1853) and redescribed by Hubbs and Miller (1941). It belongs to the genus *Poeciliopsis*, which contains 19 known species. Sexual dimorphism is marked: mature males are often black on the dorsal region with orange at the gonopodium base and bright yellow fin edges, while females remain drab. Adult males rarely exceed 25 mm standard length (SL); females average 30 to 45 mm SL, and can reach 50 mm.

**Subspecies.** The two subspecies are separated by snout length, mouth position and the lateral band in females. The Gila form has a short snout and subsuperior mouth; the Yaqui form (*sonoriensis*) has a longer snout and superior mouth.

**Distinction from Mosquitofish.** Gila topminnows have a dark, sub-orbital bar, do not turn black during breeding, have a relatively longer gonopodium, and have weak spatulate teeth. Mosquitofish have strong conical teeth reflecting a more carnivorous diet.

**Distribution.** Historically widespread in the Gila River drainage below about 1,900 m, and possibly into the lower Colorado River. Records from the Santa Cruz River are abundant, with additional populations in Sonoita, Cienega and Sabino creeks. Populations also occur in the Rio de la Concepcion and Sonora in northern Sonora, Mexico. The species is the only poeciliid native to the Gila basin.

**Reintroductions.** More than 200 Gila topminnow reintroductions or dispersals have occurred at 175 wild locations; a total of 350-plus documented stockings are reported. Twelve captive populations persisted in 1997.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat tolerances.** Gila topminnows prefer shallow, warm, fairly quiet water but tolerate a wide range. Reported ranges include temperatures from near freezing under ice to 37 °C, with a maximum tolerance of 43 °C for brief periods (Heath 1962); pH 6.6 to 8.9; dissolved oxygen 2.2 to 11 mg/L; and fresh to hypersaline water. Fish can survive almost total loss of water by burrowing into mud for 1-2 days. They are usually found in the upper 1/3 of the water column, and young favor the warmest, shallowest areas.

**Diet and feeding.** The species is an opportunistic omnivore with a gut length 1.5-2 times SL. Primary food items include detritus, vegetation, amphipods, ostracods and insect larvae; fish are rarely eaten.

**Reproduction.** The fish are viviparous, with internal fertilization via spermatophores. Females store sperm for several months and can produce up to ten broods. Brood size ranges from 1 to 31, averaging 5-15. Sexual maturity is reached as early as 2 months or as late as 11 months after birth. Breeding occurs mainly January through August. Dominant territorial males turn black, while subordinate "sneaker" males mate opportunistically.

**Hybrid form.** The all-female hybrid *P. monacha-occidentalis* is a sexual parasite that requires sperm of the parent species and reproduces by hybridogenesis.

**Mosquitofish interactions.** Mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) tolerate similar conditions and produce more broods per year (3-4 in warm climates) without superfetation. Replacement of topminnows appears to be driven mainly by predation and aggressive competition for space. Mosquitofish are more tolerant of flooding than topminnows; however, habitats that flood regularly, such as the Santa Cruz River, have allowed topminnows to coexist with mosquitofish for more than 30 years. Elimination of reintroduced populations by mosquitofish can take less than 2 years to 3 years.

**Habitat destruction.** Heavy overgrazing, wood cutting and the 1891-1893 drought caused widespread loss of vegetation and arroyo cutting. Groundwater pumping, water diversions and dams further dewatered reaches and favored nonnative fishes.

**Genetics.** Mitochondrial DNA shows greater diversity in Mexican populations than in Gila basin populations, which show little within-basin variation. Parker and others concluded that Monkey Spring is a separate evolutionarily significant unit, while Sharp Spring, Bylas Spring and Cienega Creek are management units. The plan recommends keeping each existing natural population separate until further research clarifies these relationships.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** 13 natural localities persist in Arizona; 15 potential localities are considered when Sheehy Spring and North Fork of Ash Creek (possibly extirpated) are included. Eleven localities lie in the Santa Cruz River system.
- **Primary threats:** Habitat alteration and destruction, plus introduction of mosquitofish. Of 2,000-plus reintroduction-related losses, 51% of failures since 1985 were attributed to desiccation, 20% to flooding, 20% to unknown causes, 2% to mosquitofish and 7% to miscellaneous factors.
- **Reintroduction success:** Despite extensive stocking, only about 8% of reintroduced populations became established.
- **Recovery criteria:** Downlisting is considered when (1) extirpation is prevented by protecting natural populations and maintaining captive stocks; (2) each natural population is replicated at two Level 2 and three Level 3 sites, with Level 2 populations established after 10 years and Level 3 after 5 years; and (3) monitoring and genetic protocols are implemented.
- **Viable population definition:** At least 500 overwintering adults (or existing numbers, whichever is greater), all age classes represented, and reliable annual recruitment.
- **Extirpation criteria:** A reintroduced population is declared extirpated only after at least three consecutive surveys by qualified biologists fail to find topminnows, covering most available habitat, during April through November.
- **Actions needed:** (1) protect natural populations and habitats; (2) reestablish populations throughout historic range; (3) monitor populations; (4) implement a genetic protocol; (5) maintain captive populations; (6) study life history and nonnative interactions; (7) educate the public and managers.
- **Mosquitofish policy:** The plan calls for prohibiting introduction or release of mosquitofish into occupied or recovery areas and stricter regulation of baitfish use.
- **Cost projections:** The cost table gives placeholder values only ("xx.0") for seven needs over ten years, so no actual budget figures are provided.
- **Implementation priorities:** Priority 1 covers actions essential to prevent extinction; Priority 2 prevents significant decline; Priority 3 provides for full recovery.
