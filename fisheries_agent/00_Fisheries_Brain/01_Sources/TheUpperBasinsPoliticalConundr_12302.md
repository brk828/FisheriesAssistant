# The Upper Basins' Political Conundrum: A Deal is Not a Deal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-ecology

Note on scope: this document is a water-policy paper, not a fish study. Its only fish content is passing reference to endangered native fish under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and a coldwater trout fishery below McPhee Dam. The taxonomic tags above are a required format default and do not reflect the document's subject matter.

## Executive Summary

J. William McDonald, an attorney with the U.S. Department of the Interior, prepared this paper (first edition May 1997; second edition July 1997) for the [[Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission]]. It asks whether the Commission should address the grievance of upper basin states that federal water projects authorized under historical interstate "deals" were not built as promised, while lower basin projects were. The paper uses the [[Colorado River Basin]] and [[Missouri River Basin]] as case studies. Its conclusion is that the Commission should not try to broker new upper/lower basin deals or redistribute project benefits. Equity questions about federal projects belong to Congress and the states. The Commission should instead take up the broader conflict between federal environmental law and state-law water rights and compact allocations. The author also argues that political agreements are not binding contracts, because a future Congress can amend, defund, or repeal them.

## Background & Description

**Scope and author.** The paper reflects the author's views only, not the Commission's. It excludes Native American trust obligations, which were assigned to other contributors. The author was formerly Director of the Colorado Water Conservation Board (1979–1990) and Assistant Commissioner for Resources Management at the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (1990–1994).

**Institutional setting.** The paper describes western water law as a contrast with the eastern [[Riparian Doctrine]]. Western states adopted the [[Prior Appropriation Doctrine]] ("first in time is first in right"). Interstate rivers are apportioned three ways: litigation before the U.S. Supreme Court under equitable apportionment, interstate compacts with congressional consent, and a single act of Congress.

**Key legal milestones:**
- *Kansas v. Colorado* (1907): established equitable apportionment.
- *Wyoming v. Colorado* (1922): applied prior appropriation across state lines on the Laramie River.
- *Arizona v. California* (1963, decree 1964): upheld congressional power to apportion interstate streams.
- The [[Boulder Canyon Project Act]] (1928) is the only instance of Congress itself allocating interstate river water.

**Federal reclamation.** The [[Reclamation Act of 1902]] created the federal program. By 1939 its structure had settled into interest-free, 40–50 year repayment, non-reimbursable fish and wildlife and recreation costs, and an "ability to pay" mechanism that shifted irrigation costs onto hydropower users. The paper cites more than $16 billion invested, about 10 million irrigated acres, roughly 60 billion kWh generated annually, and about 80 million recreation visitors per year.

**Flood control and navigation.** The Corps of Engineers built navigation and flood projects that were largely federally funded until a 1936 law required local cost contributions for flood control.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper treats environmental factors mainly as a constraint on development, not as ecology in its own right:

- **Hydrology.** Western rivers depend on snowmelt, with most runoff falling in April–July. Reservoirs are needed to store water for later use.
- **Colorado River.** Average undepleted flow at Lee Ferry is about 15 million acre-feet per year. The paper also notes the 1922–1996 average virgin flow was only 14.2 million acre-feet, against a 16.8 million acre-feet estimate used in the early 1920s.
- **Missouri River.** Natural flows are about 65 million acre-feet per year at the mouth, more than four times the Colorado.
- **Salinity.** Drainage from the Wellton-Mohawk district raised salinity sharply in 1961, which led to the [[Yuma Desalting Plant]] and a basin salinity control program.
- **Endangered fish.** The [[Endangered Species Act]] constrains operations. Examples include instream flow requirements for endangered fish downstream of Ruedi Reservoir and the San Juan River species issue affecting the [[Animas-La Plata Project]].
- **Environmental law.** The [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (1969) and the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] (1992) forced re-evaluation of projects and revised operating criteria for Glen Canyon Dam.

## Key Findings

**1. Lower basins largely got their projects; upper basins did not.**
- Colorado River: all lower-basin projects authorized 1928–1968 were completed. The CAP can deliver a little over 2 million acre-feet per year, though deliveries are expected to stay near 1.5 million. Arizona, California, and Nevada now consume about 7.5 million acre-feet per year from the mainstem.
- Upper Division: the four CRSP storage units were built, but nine of the 25 authorized participating projects were not. Among the deferred projects are La Barge, Savery-Pot Hook, Fruitland Mesa, West Divide, and San Miguel. The Animas-La Plata Project had not started construction.
- Missouri River: the six mainstem dams hold about 73.5 million acre-feet. More than 1.65 million acres were inundated in the Dakotas and Montana. Less than 10% of promised full-service irrigation acreage (518,356 of 4,760,400 acres) was developed.

**2. Why upper basin projects stalled.** The paper identifies seven factors:
1. Authorization outpaced appropriations.
2. Appropriations needed sustained political support over many years.
3. Federal budgets became tighter from the 1970s on.
4. Non-federal up-front cost sharing was added.
5. Rising discount rates and cost escalation made projects economically unjustified (benefit-cost ratio below 1:1).
6. Feasibility planning was incomplete, leading to problems such as the Oahe Unit drainage issues.
7. Environmental concern grew sharply from the 1970s.

**3. Five realities of political agreements.** The paper concludes that:
- Statutes do not bind future Congresses.
- Authorization requires appropriations, which are not guaranteed.
- Agreements last only as long as their champions remain in power.
- Circumstances change beyond the parties' control.
- Changed circumstances prompt re-litigation of settled decisions.

Consequently, the "deals" were never legally enforceable, and Congress did not break them.

**4. Recommendations to the Commission.**
- Do not attempt to fashion "Pick-Sloan Plan II" or "Son of CRSP." Redistribution is a political task for Congress and the states.
- Be aware of upper basin perceptions of inequity without adopting them as findings.
- Treat the distribution of benefits from existing projects (e.g., Missouri mainstem recreation, operations) as the more realistic area for negotiation.
- Recognize that past deals may have to be reopened and renegotiated, and that future agreements should preserve flexibility and security.
- Give priority to the conflict between federal environmental authorities and state-law water rights and compact entitlements.

**5. Contemporary examples cited.** The Garrison Diversion reformulation (1986) was later scaled back by the Bush Administration despite a complex negotiated deal. The Animas-La Plata Project, authorized in 1968, was reaffirmed in 1988 but remained unbuilt. The paper warns that new deals face the same vulnerabilities as old ones.

**Appendix A (summary).** The paper's Appendix A lists about 90 Pick-Sloan "future" and "reauthorized" units with acres to be irrigated, reservoir and power investment, and benefit-cost ratios. Most ratios fall well below 1.0. Units such as Garrison Diversion and Oahe are footnoted with the reasons they were halted.
