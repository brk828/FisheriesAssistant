# Toxaphene as a Selective Fish Poison in House Lake, Michigan (1958)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 field study by Kiyoshi G. Fukano and Frank F. Hooper of the [[Michigan]] Department of Conservation tested whether a low concentration of the insecticide [[Toxaphene]] (chlorinated camphene) could remove small fish from a lake while sparing larger individuals. The hypothesis arose from observations at the Sand Lakes, where small fish died before large ones at low doses. A single treatment of 0.005 p.p.m. (5 parts per billion) was applied to [[House Lake]] on July 25, 1957. Gill netting and seining before and after treatment, plus direct observation, indicated heavy mortality among small sunfish and survival of larger fish. The authors concluded that this concentration could reduce small-fish populations in hard-water lakes without greatly affecting large fish, and they planned further tests on lakes of different size, bottom type and water hardness.

## Background & Description

**Study site and prior trials.** Five lakes known as the Sand Lakes, in Grand Traverse County in northern Michigan, were treated in October 1956 with 0.010 to 0.025 p.p.m. of emulsifiable toxaphene concentrate. While collecting dead and dying fish, the authors noted that small fish died before large ones at these low concentrations. This suggested a lower dose might eliminate small fish while leaving larger individuals unharmed.

To test this, two lakes were treated at concentrations near the 10-day median tolerance limit for [[Goldfish]] (taken from [[Doudoroff]] et al., 1953):

- **[[Roots Lake]]** (Grand Traverse County): 15.6 acres, maximum depth 18 feet, bottom of marl, sand and pulpy peat with few submerged plants. A concentration of 0.002 p.p.m. applied to the epilimnion failed to kill fish.
- **[[House Lake]]** (Gladwin County): 4 acres. The [[Littoral Zone]] was covered with snags and deadheads with sparse aquatic plants. The bottom was sand and pulpy peat. Water was hard (methyl orange alkalinity 130 p.p.m.). This lake was the focus of the paper.

**Application method.** The concentrate was diluted about a hundredfold, then dripped from a speeding outboard-powered boat. The dose was calculated for the [[Epilimnion]] volume only.

**Sampling methods.**
- [[Gill Netting]]: five gill nets of mixed mesh sizes (1.5 to 3 inches stretched), set overnight in 5 to 15 feet of water, at the same localities and depths before and after treatment (July 24 and August 1).
- [[Seine Netting]]: twenty hauls with a 30-foot straight seine (1.25-inch bar mesh) on each of four days: July 24 and 25 (before) and August 1 and 2 (one week after).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses a size-selective response to a chemical piscicide. The authors' hypothesis was that small fish are more sensitive than large ones, so a sub-lethal-for-adults dose could control recruitment-stage or small-bodied fish. House Lake's hard water and littoral snag habitat are the conditions under which the authors judged the result applicable, and they explicitly noted that results might differ with lake size, bottom configuration and water hardness.

The fish community in House Lake was dominated by [[Bluegill]] and sunfish, with smaller numbers of [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Rock Bass]] and [[Carp]] noted in the sampling.

## Key Findings

**Gill netting (large fish)**
- Before treatment: 26 bluegill, averaging 4.3 inches.
- One week after treatment (same netting effort): 29 bluegill averaging 4.7 inches, 4 yellow perch averaging 6.2 inches, and 1 largemouth bass measuring 9.9 inches.
- No dead large fish (largemouth bass, bluegill or carp) were found during frequent post-treatment observations.

**Direct observations**
- Day after treatment: centrarchids 0.5 to 1 inch long found dead and dying along the east shore.
- One week after: numerous dead sunfish up to 2.5 inches long along the shore; a few surviving small bluegill showed distress symptoms.
- Two weeks after: no dying fish observed; healthy largemouth bass and large bluegill seen along the shore.
- September 13: several largemouth bass up to 14 inches long and 3 pumpkinseed about 3 inches long observed.

**Conclusions and plans**
- The authors concluded that 5 p.p.m.-billion (0.005 p.p.m.) of toxaphene can reduce the small-fish population of hard-water lakes without greatly affecting large fish.
- Further tests were planned to determine whether results can be duplicated on lakes differing in size, bottom configuration and water hardness.

**Limitations.** The evidence comes from a single treatment of one small lake (4 acres), with one pre-treatment and one post-treatment netting period, and a small number of large fish captured (for example, one largemouth bass). The Roots Lake trial at 0.002 p.p.m. showed no kill, and the Sand Lakes results were observational. The paper is a preliminary report rather than a replicated study.
