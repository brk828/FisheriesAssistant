# Vertebral Variation in the Emerald Shiner and Ethological Isolation in Xenotoca

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Goodeidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two papers. The first, a 1976 study, examines vertebral numbers in the [[Emerald Shiner]] (*Notropis atherinoides*) along the [[Ohio River]]. Counts from 1,038 x-rayed specimens show a consistent upstream-to-downstream increase, the reverse of the latitudinal pattern predicted by [[Jordan's Rule]]. The authors tentatively attribute this to slower warming of lower-river habitats. The second item is only an abstract and introduction (the text is truncated). It reports [[Premating Isolating Mechanisms]] between sympatric and allopatric populations of two goodeid livebearers, *Xenotoca eiseni* and *X. melanosoma*.

## Background & Description

### Vertebral variation and Jordan's Rule
- Jordan (1891) noted that many fish families have fewer vertebrae nearer the tropics. He could not identify an adequate cause, but predicted more vertebrae in cold, deep, dark and fresh waters.
- Gill (1864, 1889) claimed priority for this generalization over [[Günther]] (1862), who had noted it only in the Labridae. Gill explicitly denied that it was a law, calling it "true only in a general sense."
- The 1976 authors clarify that Jordan's Rule concerns cold-water or northern representatives of certain groups. It should not be confused with Jordan's Law, which concerns closely related forms in adjacent areas.

### Study species
*Notropis atherinoides* was selected because:
1. It was the most abundant fish in the Ohio River, making up 57.8% of a catch of 741,438 specimens in 341 collections (1957–1959).
2. It was usually present in large numbers at all locations.
3. Several thousand specimens from most river areas were preserved.
4. A substantial biological literature exists for comparison.

### Goodeid livebearers
- The Goodeidae comprises about 35 species of cyprinodontiform freshwater fishes restricted to the [[Mesa Central]] of Mexico.
- Goodeids have true viviparity. In all but one species, yolk is exhausted early, and embryos are nourished by the female through anal rosette or ribbon-like structures called [[Trophotaeniae]]. These are analogous to mammalian placentae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Ohio River setting
- The river forms at Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania (about 40°25′N) and flows about 981 miles southwest to the Mississippi at Cairo, Illinois (about 36°55′N).
- It is the only North American river where River Mile 0 lies at its source. Elsewhere, Mile 0 is at the mouth.
- The river is wide, shallow and turbid. Its discharge exceeds that of the Mississippi and Missouri combined.
- Its series of navigation locks and dams makes it resemble a chain of impoundments more than a continuous system.
- It receives runoff from agricultural and forested land, plus industrial and municipal effluents from small towns and several large metropolitan areas.

### Temperature and development
- Earlier work (Hubbs 1922, 1926; Gabriel 1944; Taning 1952; Seymour 1959) linked lower developmental temperatures to more vertebrae, though Taning and Seymour described V-shaped responses.
- Laboratory studies are hard to match to natural hatching conditions (Barlow 1961). The authors nonetheless regard Hubbs' (1922) idea that retarded development at lower temperatures yields more vertebrae as "irrefutable evidence."
- Other factors reported to affect vertebral number include light (MacCrimmon and Kwain 1969), low oxygen (Seymour 1959), and salinity, pH and dissolved gases (Fowler 1970; Barlow 1961).

## Key Findings

### Emerald shiner (Resu et al. 1976)
- **Sampling:** 11 locations at approximately 100-mile intervals (Location 0 at Emsworth Lock and Dam, Pennsylvania, through Location 10 at the mouth of Cache River, Illinois). Locations were 56.1 to 123.3 miles apart. Most samples were 100 specimens from a single collection.
- **Specimens:** 1,038 x-rayed specimens. Only 10 were too anomalous or unclear to count. Counts followed Bailey and Gosline (1955), with the weberian apparatus counted as the first four vertebrae and the urostyle included.
- **Range and mean:** Counts ranged from 36 to 42, with an overall mean of 39.58 (SD 0.53).
- **Gradient:** There was a consistent, gradual increase from source to mouth. Mean counts by location ran from 39.01 (SD 0.77, Location 0) to 40.15 (SD 0.67, Location 10). Analysis of variance was highly significant, and Duncan's multiple range test and the sum of squares simultaneous test (Gabriel 1964) identified the significant differences.
- **Low counts:** Four individuals, from Locations 0, 1 and 4, had 36 vertebrae. The authors state this is fewer than any value reported in the literature for the species.
- **Reversal of expectation:** The pattern is opposite to the usual trend of more vertebrae at higher latitudes.
- **Comparison with other studies:**
  - Hubbs (1922) recorded 39–44 vertebrae in a Chicago population (about 41°55′N), but used a different counting method.
  - Bailey and Allum (1962) reported means from 37.3 (Spring Creek, Kansas) to 40.5 (Big Stone Lake, South Dakota).
  - Flittner (unpublished dissertation) reported means from 40.05 to 41.05 from Lake Erie to Siskiwit Lake, Isle Royale.
  - Campbell and MacCrimmon (1970) reported a mean of 40.07 (range 37–41) in Lake Simcoe, Ontario.
- **Proposed explanation:** Lower-river habitats, especially impoundments, may warm more slowly than the upper river. Emerald shiners spawn in the mainstem over a protracted season that starts in late spring, so lower-river embryos may develop in colder water. Photoperiod may also shape the onset of breeding.
- **Contrast with spotted bass:** Bryan (1969) found fewer vertebrae in [[Spotted Bass]] from the lower Ohio River, which he attributed to warmer water. The authors suggest the bass spawn mainly in tributaries, so their embryos may not be exposed to mainstem temperatures.
- **Recommendation:** Future studies should record physical and chemical conditions at spawning. The authors could not correlate spawning temperature with individual counts.

### Goodeid isolation (Fitzsimons, abstract and introduction only)
- Fertile laboratory hybrids were readily produced from *Xenotoca eiseni* and *X. melanosoma* in forced crosses when no conspecific mate was available. No natural hybrids are known.
- In choice crosses, sympatric stocks never hybridized. Allopatric fish frequently mismated.
- Ethological tests showed differences in courtship behavior and discrimination ability in sympatric fish. These were absent in allopatric conspecific stocks and in two populations of the congener *X. variata*.
- The author interprets these differences as evidence for well-developed premating isolation in sympatry.
- Hybrids showed reduced survival and reduced ability to compete for mates, which the author considers essential for divergence in sympatry.
- The text ends before methods, results or full conclusions are given.
