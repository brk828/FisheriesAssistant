# Reactions of Aquarium Carp to Food and Flavors, and Lethal Oxygen Levels for the Northern Common Shiner

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a fragment of the *New York Fish and Game Journal* (Vol. 7, No. 1, January 1960) and contains three parts: a partial literature-cited list on waterfowl, a study of feeding and flavor perception in aquarium [[Common Carp]] by [[Howard A. Loeb]], and the opening of a study by [[Aquatic Biologist]] A. L. Cooper on [[Lethal Oxygen Concentration]]s for the [[Northern Common Shiner]]. The carp study is the most complete. It tested 115 flavors and many foods on blind carp and describes a stereotyped, multi-sense feeding sequence. The shiner study reports mean lethal [[Dissolved Oxygen]] values of 0.54 to 1.23 p.p.m. across five temperatures. The shiner text is cut off before its results.

## Background & Description

**Waterfowl literature (page 1).** Only a fragment of the reference list survives, with entries on waterfowl surveys in the [[Atlantic Flyway]], duck hunting seasons in New York, and the [[Canvasback]] (Stewart et al., 1958, *Journal of Wildlife Management*). These references are not analysed further in the surviving text.

**Carp feeding and flavor study.** Loeb's work was carried out at the State Fish Laboratory, Livingston Manor, under a Federal Aid in Fish and Wildlife Restoration project. Its aim was to find attractants for a poison bait for [[Carp Control]]. The paper frames [[Taste Perception]] and [[Olfaction]] in fish using Hasler (1957). Taste and smell are defined as chemically induced sensations relayed to the central nervous system from the olfactory sacs and from gustatory nerve endings in the mouth, barbels and body surfaces. The paper notes that the [[Vagal Lobe]] and [[Facial Lobe]] of carp are well developed and that this is correlated with feeding habits (Evans, 1952).

Methods:
- Subjects were carp from two sources: stunted fish of 1 to 4 lb (good feeders) and fat fish of 5 to 10 lb (many would not feed).
- Tests used 350 to 550 gallon temperature-controlled aquarium tanks ([[Aquarium Testing]]) viewed through plate glass from the side.
- Normal fish were used first. Visual cues were found to dominate taste and smell, so blind fish were used for most tests.
- Blind test fish weighed 1 to 3 lb. At least six fish were used per test, with at least 24 hours between tests.
- Water-soluble flavors were dissolved in a pint of water and poured into the inlet stream. Oil-soluble flavors were emulsified in a blender or in polyoxyethylene sorbitan monooleate.

**Shiner oxygen study.** The second paper was motivated by [[Fish Kill]]s of minnows caused by depletion of dissolved oxygen from organic wastes. Little literature existed for New York stream species. The northern common shiner was chosen because it was thought to be moderately sensitive to low oxygen. Fish were seined from a short, largely unpolluted section of Flint Creek near Gorham, Ontario County, from 1952 to 1956. Most were in age group I.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flavor and feeding ecology.** Carp are primarily bottom feeders in clear water and take some food in mid-water or at the surface. They have a pharyngeal grinding mill and no true stomach, so they probably feed often and in small amounts. Carp fry feed mainly on zooplankton by day and mostly by sight ([[Alikunhi]], 1958). The paper suggests this may explain the scarcity of young carp in many turbid waters where adults are abundant.

**Temperature effects.** Feeding depends on [[Water Temperature]]. Normal adults fed actively at 65° to 90° F and were less active at 90° to 95° F. Seizing of food, with little eating, occurred at 95° to 102° F, and discomfort was evident. Feeding was sluggish at 45° F and many fish stopped feeding there. Blind fish stopped feeding and reacting to extracts at about 50° to 55° F. The author suggests carp are at a disadvantage in turbid, cold water.

**Oxygen and water quality.** Shiner tests were run at 55°, 65°, 70°, 75° and 80° F. Stream temperatures were rising in spring and falling in autumn, so the timing of seining varied by test temperature. This work supports the use of [[Lethal Oxygen Concentration]] data in [[Stream Pollution]] investigations.

## Key Findings

**Carp chemoreception (Loeb, 1960):**
- 115 substances were tested. Carp detected all oil-soluble flavors (brief jaw snapping) but none produced a positive feeding reaction. Most were ignored after first detection.
- Carp detected all water-soluble extracts except pure ethyl alcohol and salt, which were not detected. The author suggests concentrations may have been too low.
- Positive feeding reactions with water-soluble extracts included beef bouillon, instant coffee, earthworm extract, liver extract, fenugreek and lovage (both maple flavor components), and tobacco-related extracts. Imitation maple flavor and molasses were also listed among triggering agents. Maple syrup gave a vague reaction. Sardine-particle oils gave positive reactions in olive and soybean oil.
- Blind fish reacted to all triggering substances, showing that olfaction is not required. The sensitivity of taste was extreme: blind fish detected one part of liver extract in 180 million parts of water.
- Many substances often assumed to be attractive, such as anise, produced only indifference.
- Carp accepted almost all foods regardless of colour or coating. Foods that could not be crushed by the [[Pharyngeal Teeth]], such as beans and split peas, were rejected. Fish pellets became the preferred food; dough baits were accepted for several days before all fish refused them.
- Live prey was attractive. Blind fish captured crayfish by a quick head movement with suction after chance contact.

**Feeding sequence:**
- Feeding follows a five-stage sequence: appetitive movement; triggering by sight, touch, vibration, taste or odour; further appetitive behaviour and active search; grasping and eating; and eating until satiation.
- Normal fish fed mainly by sight, with touch providing coordination. Blind fish relied on taste, sound and touch, and learned to link a person with vibration.
- Blind fish could not follow a flavor trail. They gathered where a flavor was strongest, largely by chance.

**Sympathetic activity among senses:**
- Visual stimulation appeared to lower the temperature threshold at which taste and odour could be detected in carp. This was consistent with an analogous effect reported in rats.
- Loss of one sense did not sharpen the others. The author concludes that remaining senses lose some capacity when one is lost.

**Northern common shiner (Cooper):**
- Mean lethal oxygen levels ranged from 0.54 to 1.23 p.p.m. across 55° to 80° F. The values gave a straight-line curve on semi-logarithmic paper.
- Fish were acclimated to test water for 8 to about 30 days (minimum holding time 8 days, maximum about 30 days). Most were acclimated to test temperature for more than 7 days, with a minimum of 4 days and a maximum of 26 days.
- The test method enclosed each fish in a closed container and let its respiration reduce the dissolved oxygen. Residual oxygen was measured when the fish lay on its side with barely perceptible gill movement.
