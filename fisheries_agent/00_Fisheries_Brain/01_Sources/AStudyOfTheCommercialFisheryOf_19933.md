# A Study of the Commercial Fishery of Lake Texoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #ictaluridae #commercial-fishery

## Executive Summary

This 1957 University of Oklahoma Master of Science thesis by Alfred Houser, conducted under the [[Oklahoma Fish and Game Council]], examines the commercial fishery of [[Lake Texoma]] during July to December 1952. Commercial fishing was framed as a management tool for controlling [[Rough Fish]] populations that compete with [[Sport Fishing]] interests. Fifty-five fishermen harvested 32,176 fish weighing 256,302 pounds, valued at $44,096. Gill nets produced 84.0 percent of the catch weight. The bigmouth buffalo dominated the harvest, and the game-to-rough fish ratio in gill nets was 1 to 87. The author concludes that the fishery warrants continuation under an authority able to regulate it using scientific data.

## Background & Description

The document opens with a prefatory section on the [[Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory]] and supporting agencies, followed by acknowledgements. The study was financed by the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] and was submitted as a thesis in August 1957 at the University of Oklahoma Graduate College.

The introduction frames a long-standing conflict between commercial and sport fishermen. The author argues the two interests are not inherently antagonistic, and that many public complaints about commercial netting rest on misidentified catches and misunderstood regulations. Legislative history is also summarized:

- **1931 law (House Bill No. 451):** Permitted limited commercial fishing under a $25.00 annual license, with a law enforcement officer required for net operations and no interstate sale allowed. Trotline fishing was less restricted.
- **1951 law:** Allowed out-of-state sales, removed or reduced mesh restrictions, and replaced the required officer with a bonded fisherman.
- **1953 law:** Reclassified fork-tail catfishes (Ictalurus) as game fish regardless of size, against Game and Fish Department recommendations.
- **1954:** Commercial fishing on all federal lakes was temporarily prohibited.

Sport fishing license sales rose from 121,720 (1938-39) to 409,728 (1951-52), and Oklahoma gained roughly 188,086 acres of new major-lake water since 1938.

The study site, Lake Texoma (Denison Reservoir), sits below the confluence of the Red and Washita Rivers. The dam was completed in 1944. Fieldwork ran from June 1952 to February 1953, based at the [[University of Oklahoma Biological Station]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author describes a recurring reservoir pattern: excellent early catches of game fish, followed by decline as rough-fish populations rise. Cited precedents include removal programs at Bass Lake, Indiana (Ricker and Gottschalk, 1941), East Okoboji Lake, Iowa (Rose and Moen, 1953), Lake Eustis and Lake Harris, Florida (Dequine), and Lake Mattamuskeet, North Carolina (Cahoon, 1953). The author notes that attributing game fish decline solely to rough fish would be poor science, though rough fish are probably among the most significant causes.

Lake Texoma's fish community was dominated by rough species. Methods were tied to location: commercial fishing concentrated in the upper Red and Washita arms, and the lake level fell to 609.5 feet in August 1952, isolating the area above the Cumberland Drainage Cut for the rest of the study. Silt and water mold on lines reduced trotline catches over time.

The commercial species list (Table 1) includes carp, three buffalo species, freshwater drum, flathead, blue and channel catfish, river carpsucker, blue sucker, and longnose and alligator gar. The author notes that gizzard shad and goldeye had no market, and that the blue sucker was rare in the lake.

## Key Findings

**Gear and effort**
- Maximum of 55 commercial fishermen; 41 interviewed, of whom 21 earned 100 percent of 1952 income from Lake Texoma. Equipment valued at $19,451 for those 41.
- Gill nets were the dominant gear (typically 200 yards long, 4-inch mesh). Trotlines (unbaited snag-lines, often 100 or more hooks per line) and hoop nets (carp and buffalo designs) were also used.
- The author judged that nets of 3-inch mesh or smaller were not economically feasible, and that no smaller-mesh nets were found in use.

**Catch**
- Total aggregate catch: 32,176 fish, 256,302 pounds (average 7.9 lb per fish; 8.96 lb per fish in the summary).
- Gill nets: 215,452 lb (84.0% of weight), trotlines 16,866 lb (6.6%), hoop nets 23,984 lb (9.4%).
- Bigmouth buffalo: 20,364 fish, 181,570 lb, the most important species by numbers, weight and value.
- Flathead catfish: 1,548 fish, 24,351 lb, the largest fish caught (average 15.7 lb).
- Estimated rough-fish removal exceeded 700,000 pounds in 1952.

**Economics**
- Total calculated wholesale value: $44,087 to $44,097 (report gives both figures in different tables).
- Gill-net income: $34,947.10 (79.3%); trotline: $7,219.10 (16.3%); hoop net: $1,930.80 (4.4%).
- Bigmouth buffalo provided $25,506.26 (57.8% of value).
- Fishermen received one-half the wholesale value; the remainder covered operating costs.

**Oldham Fisheries**
- The largest single operation, led by A. W. Oldham of Mansfield, Louisiana, employed a daily average of eight fishermen. It landed 6,726 fish (68,398 lb) using 6,241 gill-net days, 26% of the total catch.

**Game fish bycatch**
- Across 6,241 Oldham gill-net days: 82 crappie, 1 black bass, 10 undersize blue catfish and 3 undersize channel catfish. The ratio of rough to game fish was about 87 to 1.
- Gill nets also destroyed 1,956 gar and 768 turtles.

**Experimental fishing**
- Results from the author's own fishing were comparable to the commercial catch. Trotlines (110 days, 4 lines of 100 hooks) caught 29 fish averaging 11.4 lb; the largest was a 48-lb flathead.
- A 55-day, 4-net gill-net effort (220 net-days) caught 76 fish weighing 719 lb, mostly bigmouth buffalo.

**Policy conclusions**
- Commercial fishing was judged a valuable management tool, and continuation was recommended, under an authority able to supervise and regulate using scientific data.
- The 1953 reclassification of blue and channel catfish as game removed their commercial value, reducing income and in some cases ending fishermen's livelihoods.
- Proposed controls included 2-inch minimum mesh for hoop nets, 3-inch minimum mesh for gill and trammel nets, and state-issued identification tags on commercial gear, modeled on Texas practice.
- Calls for more research on population dynamics of the lake's fish.
