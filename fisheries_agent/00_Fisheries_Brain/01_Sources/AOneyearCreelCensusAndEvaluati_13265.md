# Creel Census and Evaluation of the Republican River, Nebraska and Kansas, 1951

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by the [[Missouri River Basin Studies]] of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Billings, Montana, January 9, 1952), describes a one-year creel census and economic evaluation of a 40-mile reach of the lower [[Republican River]] along the Nebraska–Kansas state line. The study estimated fishing utilization, yield, and value during 1951. The fishery is dominated by [[Channel Catfish]], and the river is described as a moderate-sized, silt-laden, warm-water system. Fishing pressure was high (about 1,505 fisherman-hours per mile), while the rate of catch was low (0.09 fish per fisherman-hour). The study estimated the fishery's total value at $31,500 for 1951, or $733 per mile of stream.

## Background & Description

The Republican River forms at the confluence of the Arikaree River and the North Fork of the Republican River in eastern Colorado and southwestern Nebraska. It flows east and joins the Smoky Hill River near Junction City, Kansas, to form the Kansas (Kaw) River, which enters the [[Missouri River]] at Kansas City, Missouri. The study area ran from the [[Superior-Courtland Diversion Dam]] at Guide Rock, Nebraska, to the South Republic Bridge at Republic, Kansas, plus 3 miles of the Superior Power Canal.

Key physical characteristics:

- **Valley and channel:** The valley is about 30 miles wide on average. The river bed ranges from about 250 to 675 feet wide. The river is about 95 percent sand and 5 percent silt. Extensive sand bars appear at low stage.
- **Drainage:** About 85 percent (22,500 square miles) of the basin lies above the study area. Soils are Peorian loess and Pleistocene sands and gravels, and sheet and wind erosion contribute silt.
- **Riparian cover:** About 80 percent of the shoreline is wooded (cottonwood, willow, elm, box elder, ash, mulberry). About 10 percent is cropland and 10 percent pasture or untilled land.
- **Flow:** Flows are highly variable. The 1951 maximum was 18,600 second-feet and the mean was 1,715 second-feet, about twice the 1932–1950 average of 857 second-feet. Historical flows have ranged from zero to 225,000 second-feet.
- **Climate:** Normal annual precipitation is about 26 inches. Precipitation in 1951 at Superior was 39 inches. Mean temperatures range from about 25°F in winter to 75°F in summer.
- **Human use:** Land is primarily agricultural. The only recreation site within the study area is a small camping and picnic ground at [[Pawnee Lake]], which has about 30 acres of water. Access is largely limited to two dam sites and seven bridges. The population within a 25-mile radius is about 55,000 (1950 census).

Two dams in the study area were central to the investigation. The [[Superior Power Diversion Dam]] is an eight-foot-high structure that diverts water into a canal about 3 miles long. The [[Superior-Courtland Diversion Dam]] is a Bureau of Reclamation irrigation structure about 500 feet long. A third structure, the [[Harlan County Dam]] about 60 miles west of Guide Rock, was under construction. It was planned to be in operation in 1952 and to form a reservoir of over 13,000 surface acres.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Analyses of samples from July 15, 1951 (Appendix A) show relatively hard, slightly alkaline water. Specific conductance was about 276 micromhos at 25°C, which the report considers within the desirable range for aquatic life. Dissolved oxygen was not measured, but it was assumed adequate, and no oxygen-related fish losses were reported.

**Turbidity.** Turbidity was not measured directly. Observations with a mercury-tipped thermometer, however, suggested readings well above 1,000 ppm. The report identifies turbidity as the most important factor affecting aquatic life. All fish species recorded for the study area are considered to have relatively high turbidity tolerance.

**Temperature.** Daytime surface temperatures varied from 5°F to 76°F in late April, and daily fluctuations of 10°F were common in summer. The maximum recorded temperature was 86°F in August. Temperatures stayed consistently above 75°F, the level considered requisite for catfish spawning, only from the last week of July. The unusually cool summer, heavy rain, and high water kept temperatures low.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Aquatic plants were almost entirely absent apart from small patches of bulrush and horsetail on the banks. The report attributes this to turbidity and to shifting sandy bottoms caused by water-level fluctuations.

**Other fauna.** Fish-eating birds were present in small numbers, including [[Great Blue Heron]], kingfishers, little green herons, black-crowned night herons, and double-crested cormorants. Beavers, frogs, and crayfish were observed, but no systematic data were collected on non-fish aquatic animals.

**Fish community.** Eight species appeared in creels. The report lists 31 additional species recorded for the Republican River (Appendix B, about 40 in total, including sturgeons, gars, shads, trouts, suckers, minnows, catfishes, topminnows, perches, and sunfishes). Key species include:

- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus lacustris punctatus*), the dominant species in the creel
- [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pilodictis olivaris*), a migratory, spawning-run fish
- [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*)
- [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus platorynchus*)
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), an introduced species
- Black bullhead (*Ameiurus melas*) and carpsucker (*Carpiodes* spp.)

**Management.** Management consisted of stocking catfish, bullheads, and crappies (Appendix C; roughly a dozen plantings from 1940 to 1951, mostly 500 to 2,000 fish, with one 13,500-fish bullhead or catfish record) and enforcing fishing regulations. Both states limit fishing to hook and line. The limit is 25 hooks per person in Kansas and 15 in Nebraska. Both states set a 12-inch minimum size for catfish, and fishing is allowed year-round.

## Key Findings

### Fishing Effort and Yield
- **Effort:** About 17,500 fisherman-days (65,000 fisherman-hours or 89,000 pole-hours) were expended in 1951. This equals about 1,505 fisherman-hours per mile of stream.
- **Catch:** About 6,550 fish weighing about 13,000 pounds were caught. That is about 152 fish and 300 pounds per mile.
- **Rate of catch:** The overall rate was 0.09 fish per fisherman-hour (0.06 per pole-hour). It ranged from 0.07 in May to 0.13 in September. Set-line fishermen averaged 0.07, slightly below the overall rate. The report notes this contradicts the common belief that set lines catch the most fish.
- **Average fish weight:** About 1.99 pounds.
- **Section differences:** Section II had more than five times the yield per mile of either of the other two sections.

### Species Composition
- **By number:** Channel catfish made up about 76 percent, flathead catfish about 8 percent, carp about 9 percent, freshwater drum about 4 percent, shovelnose sturgeon about 2 percent, and black bullhead about 1 percent. One yellow pikeperch was also caught.
- **By weight:** Channel catfish made up about 63 percent and flathead catfish about 23 percent. Catfishes together made up about 83 percent of the catch by number and 86 percent by weight.
- **Flathead catfish:** These were assumed to be migratory spawning fish. They entered the catch in late May, peaked around July 1, and then declined. Ten of 95 recorded specimens were taken above the Superior Power Diversion Dam, so the dam did not fully halt upstream movement. The Superior Power Canal, with a 20-foot drop and no fish passage, was considered an effective barrier.
- **Channel catfish sizes:** A non-selective sample of 649 fish (Chart 3) showed a roughly normal length distribution, with a proportionate share of older fish. The average length was about 16 inches in the total sample, and the average length of flathead catfish was about 22 inches.

### Fishermen and Access
- **Parties:** 2,352 parties (1.6 fishermen per party) were contacted. Including parties seen but not contacted (570) and estimated parties (about 640), the total known and estimated was 3,566. Of these, 66 percent were contacted.
- **Methods:** About 90 percent fished from shore, 9 percent used set lines, and 1 percent fished from boats. The overall average was 1.38 rods or lines per fisherman.
- **Radius of influence:** 77 percent of parties came from less than 25 miles away. About 1,500 parties came from Superior, Nebraska (population 3,220), less than a mile from the river. Only 1.2 percent spent more than one day on a trip.
- **Demographics:** Of 3,872 persons contacted, 82 percent were men, 10 percent were children, and 8 percent were women. Non-resident parties were under 1 percent.

### Economic Value
- **Transportation:** Round-trip mileage averaged 15.6 miles per person per day, or $1.09 at seven cents per mile.
- **Trip expenditures:** These averaged $0.17 per person per day. Seventy percent of fishermen made no purchases.
- **Total creel-based cost:** $1.26 per person per day.
- **Investment and annual costs:** These were not collected directly. The report borrowed about $0.5 per person per day from the [[Lake Maloney]] study, which used similar equipment and license fees.
- **Combined daily expenditure:** About $1.80 per person per day.
- **Total value:** About $31,500 for 1951, or $733 per mile of stream.
- **Cost per pound of fish:** The results section gives about $2.42, while the summary gives $2.12. Table 7 is partly illegible in the source, so this discrepancy cannot be fully resolved from the text.

### Comparisons and Interpretation
- The report judged utilization relatively intensive compared with other midwestern waters. It attributed this largely to the lack of other fisheries within roughly 50 miles.
- Despite high utilization, the report did not conclude that the fishery was overfished. Anglers were reasonably satisfied, and 1951's high water reduced fish concentration and availability. It noted that normal low-water years concentrate fish.
- The report cautions that the 1951 fishing-pressure chart has limited value for other years because of unusual weather and flow.
- Future impacts were expected: the [[Harlan County Dam]] reservoir was expected to draw some anglers away from the study area, and controlled releases were expected to reduce extreme flow fluctuations.

### Corrections
