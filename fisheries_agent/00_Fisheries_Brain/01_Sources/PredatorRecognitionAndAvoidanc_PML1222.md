# Predator Recognition and Avoidance by Larval Razorback Sucker and Northern Hog Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study compared predator recognition and avoidance in larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], and larval [[Northern Hog Sucker]] (*Hypentelium nigricans*), a species from the predator-rich [[Mississippi River Drainage]]. The authors hypothesized that razorback suckers, evolved in predator-poor waters, are less predator-wary and may explain the poor success of reintroductions. Larvae were tested against [[Largemouth Bass]] predators in a partitioned aquarium across ages of roughly 7 to 65 days. Northern hog suckers avoided predators earlier and more completely, while razorback suckers improved more slowly. The authors conclude that predation on young razorback suckers is a probable barrier to recovery, and recommend delaying stocking to about 9 months after hatching and considering chemical renovation of reintroduction streams to remove nonnative fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Razorback sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): endemic to the Colorado River basin of western North America. Once abundant, it declined sharply after the introduction of nonnative fishes. It was listed as endangered in 1991 (USFWS 1991) after more than a decade of consideration, and is protected by state law in every state where it occurs.
- **Northern hog sucker** (*Hypentelium nigricans*): a catostomid from the Mississippi River drainage, used as a comparison species representing a predator-rich community.
- The Colorado River drainage holds about 32 fish species, most confined to small, isolated waters, with roughly 12 big-water mainstream species. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is the only obligate piscivore of the system, though roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*) may also consume fish.
- The Mississippi River drainage contains more than 260 native fish species that evolved under high predator contact.
- Many fishes introduced into the Colorado basin are piscivorous game species from predator-rich environments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hypothesis:** razorback suckers, endemic to predator-poor waters, are inherently less predator-wary than ecologically equivalent Mississippi species. This historical difference in predator exposure may explain failed reintroductions.
- **Rearing and conditions:** Larvae were obtained from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico, and reared in indoor aerated pools. Water temperature in rearing and test aquaria ranged from 15 to 18 °C, with a 12 h light:12 h dark photoperiod. Northern hog sucker eggs were collected from Clear Creek, Washington County, Arkansas, on March 27, 1991, and incubated at 17 °C.
- **Test apparatus:** A 75-L aquarium divided into three 25 × 30 × 30 cm chambers by clear plastic dividers. Outer chambers held the predator; the middle chamber held larvae. A line marked "near" and "away" sides relative to the predator. The aquarium was covered with black plastic to reduce visual interference.
- **Predators:** Two [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), 10 and 14 cm total length, conditioned to the aquarium.
- **Context for management:** Reintroduction programs in the Colorado basin and the [[Upper Salt River]] in Arizona are relevant, since nonnative fishes there are a likely cause of stocked-fish mortality.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sample sizes**
- Razorback sucker: 40 trials in 1990 and again in 1991, from ages 10 to 65 days (1991 testing began March 14 and ended May 8; 1990 testing began March 24 and ended May 7).
- Northern hog sucker: 26 trials in 1991, from 7 to 35 days old (April 14 to May 12, 1991).
- Two test types: single larva with one predator, and groups of five larvae. Responses were counted over 5-minute intervals, and each individual was typically used only once. Limited retesting of hog suckers occurred because only about 200 fish were available.
- Statistics: Wilcoxon scores test (no side effect, P > 0.36); general linear model analysis of covariance (SAS Institute 1988).

**Behavioral responses**
- All larvae of both species in the near side of the chamber reacted to predator attacks, typically with a startle response followed by fleeing or hiding.
- Hiding in gravel crevices was more frequent and occurred at a younger age in northern hog suckers. Razorback suckers occasionally hid, and older larvae (>30 days) sometimes used cover.

**Single-larva results**
- By day 30, razorback suckers spent about 60% of their time on the away side, rising to more than 80% by the end of testing.
- Northern hog suckers showed positive avoidance from their first test day (7 days old) and by day 32 spent all of their time away from the predator.
- Slopes for 1990 and 1991 razorback suckers did not differ (P = 0.93). Intercepts for 1991 razorback sucker and northern hog sucker differed (P = 0.004), indicating that hog suckers avoided predators better from the start.

**Group-larva results**
- Group avoidance improved with age, but more slowly than in single larvae, especially for northern hog suckers. Intercepts and slopes differed between single and group northern hog suckers (P = 0.0001 and P = 0.04). Razorback single and group tests did not differ significantly in 1990 or 1991.
- Fleeing larvae in groups often oriented toward non-fleeing larvae and did not complete their escape to the away side, reducing the avoidance response. Groups of northern hog suckers were less avoidant than single individuals.

**Predator avoidance projections**
- Extrapolation of single razorback sucker regression lines suggests 100% avoidance at 70 to 90 days. Group tests suggest 250 to 280 days before total avoidance.

**Interpretation and recommendations**
- Results contrast with [[Pelagic Schooling Fishes]] such as northern anchovy and Pacific sardine, whose youngest larvae responded to predators at only 9 to 20%.
- The authors conclude that northern hog suckers, from a predator-rich community, avoid predators better than razorback suckers from a predator-poor community.
- Management recommendations: stock razorback suckers at least 9 months after hatching to reduce predation, and consider chemical renovation of streams such as the upper Salt River to remove nonnative fishes. There are currently no large streams in the historic range that lack nonnative fishes.
