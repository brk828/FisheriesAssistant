# Biological Survey of Streams and Lakes of Apache and Crook National Forests, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trout

## Executive Summary

This 1935 report by M. J. Madsen, Temporary Assistant Biologist for the [[Bureau Of Fisheries]] (U.S. Department of Commerce), describes a biological, chemical and physical survey of streams and lakes in the [[Apache National Forest]] and [[Crook National Forest]] in Arizona. Field work took place in July and August 1934. The survey was meant to guide the choice of species, size and number of fish to stock, and to identify habitat improvements and problems needing further study. It is explicitly a reconnaissance, not a complete study of any water body.

The survey concludes that the mountain streams are generally cold and suited to trout, but are small, rapid and short of natural food and spawning gravel in places. The report recommends systematic stocking of permanent streams, improvement work focused on heavily fished waters, and stocking of the [[Greer Lakes]]. Most other lakes were judged unsuitable for fish.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The stated aims were to (1) assess biological, chemical and physical conditions; (2) use these data to choose the species, size and number of fish to plant; (3) show how environmental changes could raise fish populations; and (4) flag problems needing more intensive study.

**Personnel and timing.** The party included M. J. Madsen (biologist in charge), Paul Klingenberg and Harry G. Moseley (associate biologists), and Tim Hudspeth (camp attendant). They worked in the Apache Forest from August 4 to August 16, 1934, and in the Crook Forest from July 21 to July 30, 1934. The Arizona State Fish and Game Commission supplied game agents, and the Forest Service supplied maps.

**Survey methods.**
- *Stations:* 200 yards of stream, or the area of a lake reachable from an anchored boat.
- *Physical:* widths and depths by rule and steel tape; altitude and gradient from U.S. Geological Survey maps; water and air temperatures with pocket Fahrenheit thermometers.
- *Chemical (field):* pH by colorimetric tubes with Bromthymol blue; free carbon dioxide by phenolphthalein titration against N/44 sodium hydroxide; [[Dissolved Oxygen]] by the Winkler method; alkalinity by titration with 0.02 N sulfuric acid.
- *Biological:* fish sampled with seines, dip nets, wire traps, angling and observation; aquatic plants rated dense, thin or scarce; bottom foods taken with a square-foot sampling net (streams) or an [[Ekman Dredge]] (lakes).
- *Pool grading:* pools graded for size, type and frequency, then combined into an overall grade of A (excellent), B (average) or C (poor). See [[Pool Grading]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Apache National Forest
- Located in east-central Arizona, about 30 miles south of Springerville. Covers about 800 square miles.
- Elevation ranges from 11,000 ft (Bald Mountain) to 4,500 ft along the [[San Francisco River]].
- Headwaters of the [[Little Colorado River]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Blue River]] and [[Black River]] rise here. The report says these headwaters suit trout and should be regulated to keep those conditions.
- Water was below normal in 1934 because of drought. Pools and riffles were fair in drought-affected streams and good in permanent ones.

**Physical.** Streams are generally rapid, with an average gradient of about 250 ft per mile and a maximum not over 750 ft per mile (OCR-corrected). Temperatures were low and never above 77°F.

**Chemical.** Waters are typical of high-mountain streams draining dark soils and rocks. Chemical analyses were limited to a few streams; the Blue River was not analyzed because it was inaccessible.

**Biological.**
- *Plants:* scarce in the larger fluctuating streams, but abundant in small spring runs.
- *Food:* [[Caddisflies]] and [[Mayflies]] dominated samples, followed by [[Stoneflies]] and miscellaneous two-winged flies (Diptera). Terrestrial insects that fell into the water were an important summer food for trout, but they were not measured.
- *Fish:* few species. [[Rainbow Trout]] were the most numerous game fish, largely from hatchery stocking. [[Cutthroat Trout]] were found only occasionally and probably descend from the original stock of the Colorado River. Suckers and [[Bonytail]] were most abundant in lower reaches. Dace were also present.
- *Spawning and health:* natural spawning looked good in permanent streams. Natural enemies did no serious damage, and no fish showed disease or parasites.

**Lakes.** Most small natural lakes are shallow, freeze to the bottom in winter, lack permanent inlets, and have little food, so none were considered fishery candidates. The [[Alpine Reservoir]] is a New Mexico-controlled storage reservoir with no fish and no permanent inflow; it has been drained for three years.

### Crook National Forest
The Crook Forest has several divisions. Only the [[Graham Mountain Division]] and the [[Clifton Division]] offer fishery possibilities. Safford is the central base for Forest Service operations.

**Graham Mountain Division.**
- Ranges from 4,500 ft to 10,516 ft and covers about 325 square miles, surrounded by desert.
- No lakes. Four permanent streams: Grant Creek, Ask (Ash) Creek, Big Creek and Frye Creek.
- Streams are very small, seldom averaging more than 4 cubic feet per second. In 1934 they were very low and dry for at least half their length in places.
- Gradient averages about 800 ft per mile, and no stream averages less than 500 ft per mile. Temperatures seldom rise above 65°F except where streams cross desert flats.
- Chemical conditions are uniform across streams, with water temperatures near 56–62°F and pH 6.4–6.8 at most stations.
- Plants were absent apart from scant algae. Food was very scarce; caddisflies were the most common, and dead caddisflies in their cases on dry rocks suggested that 1934 drought had sharply reduced the food supply.
- The streams contained no native fish. Fish agencies were establishing [[Rainbow Trout]], which were now found sparingly in all permanent streams. Natural spawning is very unlikely given steep gradients and little gravel.

**Clifton Division.**
- Lies south of the Apache Forest on the south slopes of the White Mountains, with elevations from 9,009 ft to 4,500 ft; mostly semi-desert.
- Main streams are the Blue River, San Francisco River and [[Eagle Creek]]. All drain to the [[Gila River]].
- Only the Eagle Creek drainage was surveyed. Its tributaries (Chitty, Salt House, Crabtree, Rattlesnake and East Eagle creeks) were dry in 1934. Eagle Creek runs 25 miles to the Gila; only the upper 8 miles offer fishery potential.
- Gradient averages about 100 ft per mile (maximum 200). Summer temperature in the suitable water averages about 74°F; at the upper station, water was 70°F and at a station 4 miles below the source, 77°F.
- Pools are large and fairly deep, and the Forest Service has added floating cover to several. Riffles produced about 100 organisms per square foot.
- Plants and algae are abundant. [[Mayflies]], [[Caddisflies]] and dobsonflies (hellgrammites) were dominant. Minnows and small suckers were common.
- Trout were reported but not confirmed. Seine hauls yielded many suckers and bonytails (up to 75 suckers and 45 bonytails in single hauls) but no trout.

## Key Findings

**Survey conclusions**
- Streams in both forests are generally rapid and cold, and most permanent streams are suited to trout.
- Drought conditions in 1934 depressed water levels, pools, riffles and food supplies, with recovery expected after normal rain and snow.
- Most natural lakes are unsuitable for fish. The [[Greer Lakes]] are the only lakes of easy access and fishery potential.
- Fish communities are poor in species. Rainbow trout dominate planted streams, while suckers and bonytails dominate warmer, lower stretches.

**Improvement recommendations**
- *Apache Forest:* The Forest Service had completed about 45 miles of improvements on 9 streams. Future work should focus on streams with the most anglers rather than remote headwaters, and all permanent streams should be systematically stocked with game fish.
- *Graham Mountain:* Build fewer, stronger dams able to withstand spring floods, make pools as large as channels allow, add cover soon after construction, and stock legal-size fish every two weeks or month during the fishing season to replace angler harvest.
- *Eagle Creek:* Avoid dams; use deflectors to form pools, add cover, narrow the channel where needed, develop shade near open pools, and keep structures permanent to prevent bank cutting and drift accumulation. Stock lightly with [[Brown Trout]], which tolerate warm water and spawn more successfully than other trout.

**Stocking plans**
- *Stream stocking* depends on temperature, substrate, food, width and depth, chemistry, fishing pressure, previous stocking, natural spawning, accessibility and resident species. The recommendations follow a planting table by Dr. G. C. Embody. See [[Stream Stocking]].
- *Lake stocking* uses area and food richness, with three-inch trout per acre by average fish weight (1/2 lb: 600, 374, 150; 3/4 lb: 400, 249, 100; 1 lb: 300, 187, 75 for excellent, average and below-average production; OCR-read values). See [[Lake Stocking]].
- *Apache Forest (Table 2):* About 24 streams were surveyed (Table 1). Recommended rainbow trout stocking ranged from about 810 to 10,800 fish per stream, planted annually. Greer Lakes #1 (35 acres), #2 (40 acres) and #3 (15 acres) were also recommended for stocking.
- *Crook Forest (Table 3):* Ash Creek 125, Frye Creek 150, Grant Creek 140 and Big Creek 80 rainbow trout, each week during the fishing season. Eagle Creek received 1,150 brown trout annually below the forks (4 miles) and 1,500 more in the next 4 miles.

**Survey limitations.** The author notes that stocking plans cannot yet be fully scientific, since data on annual harvest, yield and growth rates are not available. Recommendations are meant to be followed consistently and refined later.
