# Fishery Survey of the Gila River, Gila Wilderness Area, New Mexico (1994)

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Centrarchidae #GilaRiver #ConservationBiology

## Executive Summary
This report documents a fishery survey of the [[Gila River]] within the [[Gila Wilderness Area]], Gila National Forest, New Mexico, conducted 6–10 June and 18 August 1994 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, [[Western New Mexico University]], and the Gila National Forest. The survey aimed to characterize the fish community across four defined river reaches (A–D), plus an additional upstream reach (WNMU sites), using electrofishing, kick seining, snorkeling, and trammel netting. Fourteen fish species were documented, dominated by native [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and non-native [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), which together comprised 78% of the electrofishing catch. Species of special conservation concern — [[Loach Minnow]] (*Rhinichthys cobitis*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) — were detected but in low abundance, with roundtail chub particularly rare (only 3 individuals total across all gear types). August sampling by WNMU upstream of the June sites yielded no new species but recorded higher catch rates of native cyprinids (longfin dace, speckled dace, loach minnow).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat structure**: The river was stratified into four sampling reaches (A–D) plus an upstream WNMU reach, encompassing pools, riffles, and runs typical of a montane wilderness stream.
- **Sampling gear-habitat associations**:
  - **Electrofishing** (backpack, 24-volt Smith-Root unit): used in small pools and riffles; primary quantitative gear (23 sites, 18,174 seconds total effort).
  - **Kick seining**: paired with electrofishing specifically to target riffle-dwelling [[Loach Minnow]].
  - **Snorkeling**: qualitative sampling of large pools (day/night) to assess presence/absence of species of concern like [[Roundtail Chub]]; confirmed no additional species beyond those captured by other gears.
  - **Trammel nets**: set overnight across entire stream width in pools ≥1 meter deep; captured larger-bodied species less vulnerable to electrofishing (e.g., large [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Rainbow Trout]]).
- **Reach-level community composition**:
  - **Reach A** (upstream-most of June sites): highest CPUE (1.02 fish/min); dominated by *Catostomus insignis* (32.5%) and *C. clarki* (28.75%); notable presence of [[Loach Minnow]] (12.5%) and [[Yellow Bullhead]] (12.5%).
  - **Reach B**: lower CPUE (0.81 fish/min); dominated overwhelmingly by *C. clarki* (60%).
  - **Reach C**: lowest CPUE (0.276 fish/min); *Micropterus dolomieu* most frequent (34.62%), indicating possible non-native encroachment mid-reach.
  - **Reach D** (downstream-most): CPUE 0.801 fish/min; strongly dominated by non-native *M. dolomieu* (51.9%), alongside diverse native and non-native species mix including [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Green Sunfish]].
- **Upstream WNMU sites (August)**: Progressively higher CPUE moving upstream (1.18 → 1.36 → 3.18 → 3.46 fish/min), suggesting improved habitat quality or reduced non-native competition upstream; *Rhinichthys osculus* and *Rhinichthys cobitis* more frequently encountered here than in June reaches.
- **Species of special concern**: [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] are indicator taxa for native fish community health in the Gila River drainage; both were present but in low relative abundance, with roundtail chub notably scarce.

## Key Findings
- Total electrofishing effort: **18,174 seconds**; overall CPUE = **0.693 fish/minute**.
- **Desert Sucker** and **Smallmouth Bass** were co-dominant species (n=57 each), together with **Sonora Sucker** (n=47) comprising **78%** of total electrofishing catch.
- Trammel netting (97.3 hours, 5 nets, reaches A–D) yielded only **17 fish** across **6 species**; this gear uniquely captured the survey's only **Roundtail Chub** (n=2, set D5). One net (A1) caught zero fish.
- Snorkeling confirmed the rarity of species of special concern (e.g., roundtail chub) but detected no species not already captured by other methods.
- August WNMU sampling upstream of the June study area (extending to near the confluence of the [[East Fork Gila River]]) found **no new species**, but recorded **higher relative frequency** of [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Loach Minnow]] compared to June, suggesting a healthier native-dominated fish assemblage further upstream/within wilderness interior.
- A total of **14 fish species** were documented across the entire survey, reflecting a mixed native-nonnative assemblage typical of the lower Gila Wilderness reach, including significant non-native sport fish presence (*Micropterus dolomieu*, *Oncorhynchus mykiss*, *Lepomis* spp.).
- Non-native [[Smallmouth Bass]] dominance increased markedly in the downstream-most reach (D), raising potential concern for native fish conservation given known predatory/competitive impacts on Gila River native cyprinids and catostomids.
