# An Ecological Study of the Fishes of the Tampa Bay Area

#ichthyology #marine-biology #estuarine-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1960 Florida State Board of Conservation Marine Laboratory report (Professional Papers Series No. 1, by [[Victor G. Springer]] and [[Kenneth D. Woodburn]]) documents the fish fauna of the Tampa Bay area from roughly October 1957 to mid-1959. Monthly collections were made at shore and trawl stations in Tampa Bay, Boca Ciega Bay, Old Tampa Bay, the Gulf beach at Pass-a-Grille, and a mangrove-lined bayou (Cross Bayou Canal), with supplementary offshore rocky-reef diving. The study handled roughly 45,000 returned specimens from an estimated 500,000 collected, and presents a systematic account arranged by family, with notes on local names, salinity and temperature tolerances, spawning, growth, diet and cold-kill events. The authors frame the work as a contemporaneous comparison of several habitats and note that environmental degradation (dredging, filling, pollution) was already limiting future study.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The authors argue that an ecological study restricted to fishes can indicate conditions across aquatic niches, but that a full picture requires examining all major habitat types at the same time. Earlier Florida studies by [[Reid]] (1954) and [[Kilby]] (1955) covered single habitats (grass flats, coastal marshes), while [[Gunter]] (1945) on the Texas coast provided the foundational Gulf comparison.

**Study area.** The Tampa Bay area was defined as the west Florida coast between latitudes 27°32'N and 28°00'N, extending west to about longitude 84°W, plus connected freshwater of Pinellas County. Offshore rocky reefs lay about nine to twenty miles out in the Gulf.

**Stations and methods.**
- Tampa Bay shore station (St. Petersburg, sandy shore with *Diplanthera* and *Thalassia* beds).
- Maximo Point Park station (Boca Ciega Bay, vegetated, with tunicates).
- Pass-a-Grille Beach station (open Gulf sandy beach with concrete breakwaters).
- Cross Bayou Canal station (man-made mangrove-lined canal with low salinity, 0.5–20 ‰ range).
- Roller frame trawl stations in Old Tampa Bay (Lower Gandy and Big Island flats) and Boca Ciega Bay (Cabbage Key and Bird Key Middleground).
- Gear included 50 ft bag seines, 6–20 ft Commonsense minnow seines, roller frame trawl, and pushnets; rotenone was used at some stations and offshore.
- Salinity was measured with densimeters or salinometers, and temperature with mercury thermometers. Standard lengths were measured to the nearest 0.1 mm.

**Limitations.** Gear was biased toward small or young fish, collections could not be made both day and night at each station, and stations were sampled only about monthly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat gradients.** Salinities across stations ranged widely: the bayou ranged roughly from fresh (0.5 ‰ in mid-reaches) to about 20 ‰, whereas the bay and Gulf stations often exceeded 30 ‰.
- **Cold events.** The cold wave of 12–13 December 1957 killed many fishes in the bayou and elsewhere, including tarpon, ladyfish, needlefish, and snook, and destroyed the red mangroves at Cross Bayou. Water temperature that morning was recorded at about 13 °C.
- **Red tide.** A 1957 red tide outbreak caused mass mortalities washed ashore at Madeira Beach and the Gulf beaches (including Odontoscion dentex and Sphyraena-group specimens).
- **Anthropogenic pressure.** The preface notes rapid population growth, industrial pollutants, real estate dredging and filling, and increasing turbidity. Dredging obliterated the Big Island flat station after September 1958.
- **Fresh-water connections.** Lake Maggiore (St. Petersburg) and Salt Creek, which connects to Bayboro Harbor, produced several freshwater and brackish records, including a catfish die-off after heavy rain in March 1958.

## Key Findings

- **Scope of the survey.** The systematic account covers roughly 90 families and well over 100 species; the species list is organized by phylogeny following [[Briggs]] (1958). Common names are those used locally.
- **Sharks and rays.** Several large sharks, including [[Carcharias taurus]] (mako) and [[Carcharhinus leucas]] (bull shark), were reported. Bull sharks were caught commonly from September through December 1958, and the smallest recorded specimen was 920 mm. The authors consider *Carcharias taurus* a regular visitor, not merely a stray as earlier authors suggested.
- **Abundance.** Pinfish (*Lagodon rhomboides*) were among the most ubiquitous species (6,573 specimens returned, 1,225 from the beach). Spot (*Leiostomus xanthurus*) were also very common (7,067 specimens returned). Other common species included *Harengula pensacolae* (2,818 returned, but tens of thousands collected), *Cyprinodon variegatus* (502), and *Syngnathus scovelli* (905, the commonest pipefish).
- **Menhaden.** Bayou populations of *Brevoortia patronus* (1,112 specimens) showed a winter spawning pattern and an early recruitment size of about 20–31 mm compared with Louisiana data.
- **Pinfish diet.** Pinfish ingested plant material deliberately, not incidentally, according to the authors, contrary to [[Caldwell]] (1957B).
- **Spot migration.** Larger spot disappeared from Cross Bayou from August onward, and the authors infer offshore migration in late summer.
- **Croaker spawning.** Spawning of *Micropogon undulatus* was reported as October–January in Louisiana; the authors also discuss a fall migration recorded for Lydia Ann Channel, Texas.
- **Range extensions.** Several first or unusual records were noted, including *Anchoa cubana* from the Gulf coast between Marco and the Panhandle, *Microgobius thalassinus* south of the Panhandle, and *Callionymus calliurus* north of Key West.
- **Cold tolerance.** Fishes including *Orthopristis chrysopterus* and *Bairdiella chrysura* became common in Miami after the severe 1957–58 winter, suggesting cold-driven range shifts.
- **Mangrove snapper and grouper fishery.** Commercial and party-boat fishing relied on groupers (*Mycteroperca microlepis*, *Epinephelus morio*) from offshore reefs, with a reported shift in dominance from red to black grouper around 1958.
- **Conclusion.** The authors conclude that the study captured only part of the fauna due to gear and sampling limitations, and that rapid environmental change would soon make comparable studies impossible.
