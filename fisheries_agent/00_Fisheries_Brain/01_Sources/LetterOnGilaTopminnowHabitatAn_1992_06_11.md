## Executive Summary
This document is a 1992 interagency correspondence from the U.S. Forest Service (Tonto National Forest) to the Arizona Game and Fish Department regarding the status of a [[Gila Topminnow]] population at the Cottonwood Artesian Site. During a site visit on 22 May 1992, biologists were unable to locate any Gila topminnow in the concrete drinker that historically held the bulk of the local population. The apparent disappearance coincided with recent cleaning/removal of aquatic vegetation and sediment from the drinker. The letter documents baseline hydrological data (well discharge) collected to satisfy U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) requirements for a planned habitat improvement project, and proposes supplemental restocking of the species pending regulatory compliance (NEPA, Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type**: Small, spring/artesian-fed concrete livestock drinker with an associated shallow marshy overflow area — a marginal, human-modified freshwater microhabitat typical of remnant [[Gila Topminnow]] refugia in the arid Southwest.
- **Vegetation dependency**: Prior to disturbance, the drinker contained rooted aquatic vegetation; at time of visit, only a thin layer of floating algae remained, with no rooted vegetation present. The loss of rooted vegetation and sediment (via cleaning) is hypothesized as the probable cause for the fish's apparent disappearance, suggesting a strong ecological dependency on vegetative cover/structure for this population.
- **Co-occurring species**: [[Leopard Frog]] (unspecified species, likely *Lithobates chiricahuensis* or related) had previously been observed at the site but were also absent during this survey, indicating a possible broader disturbance to the site's aquatic community rather than a species-specific issue.
- **Refuge habitat**: The marshy overflow area below the drinker was noted as a potential remaining refuge for the species, though not thoroughly surveyed at the time.
- **Hydrology**: The site is fed by an artesian well; stopcock discharge at the wellhead measured 5 gallons per minute, with additional unmeasured flow piped to drinkers below the site and into the site drinker itself. This flow monitoring was conducted specifically to satisfy conditions set by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] for concurrence on the habitat improvement project.
- **Conservation context**: This site represents a small, isolated population management unit for the federally listed [[Gila Topminnow]], illustrating the species' broader ecological pattern of persisting in isolated spring/artesian systems across its historic range in the Gila River basin.

## Key Findings
- No [[Gila Topminnow]] individuals were located in the concrete drinker at the Cottonwood Artesian Site during the 22 May 1992 survey, despite this location historically holding the bulk of the population.
- The drinker had recently been cleared of rooted aquatic vegetation and sediment, leaving only floating algae; this disturbance is proposed as the likely cause of the fish's disappearance.
- [[Leopard Frog]] individuals, previously present at the site, were also not observed during this visit.
- The marshy overflow area below the drinker may still support Gila topminnow, but a thorough survey was not conducted to confirm this.
- Wellhead discharge was measured at 5 gallons per minute from the stopcock, with additional unmeasured flow distributed to downstream drinkers, fulfilling a monitoring requirement imposed by the USFWS as a condition of project concurrence.
- The author (J.E.A. Stefferud, Fishery Biologist) recommended supplemental stocking of Gila topminnow into the drinker following completion of the planned habitat improvement project.
- The proposed restocking action would require completion of [[NEPA]] (National Environmental Policy Act) and [[Section 7]] (Endangered Species Act) consultation requirements prior to implementation.
- This correspondence reflects routine interagency coordination between the [[U.S. Forest Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in managing recovery actions for this federally protected species.
