# Oil Field Brine as a Turbidity-Reducing Treatment for Farm Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #limnology

## Executive Summary

This Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory report by Dee Keeton tests whether [[Oil Field Brine]], a waste product of oil pumping, can clear turbid [[Farm Ponds]] by precipitating negatively charged [[Colloidal Clay]] particles. Two experimental ponds near [[Holdenville]], [[Hughes County]], were treated, and a [[Control Pond]] was monitored alongside them. Brine added intermittently to the First Experimental Pond cleared it and produced a long-term turbidity decrease. The single large application to the Second Experimental Pond, made during [[Thermal Stratification]], concentrated brine at the bottom, where it killed some organisms. The author concludes that brine treatment is recommended with reservations, and that intermittent introduction is safer than a single large application.

## Background & Description

Colloidal clay [[Turbidity]] is a widespread limit on productivity in southwestern waters. Turbidity in Oklahoma reached 500 ppm and was reported as high as 1,800 ppm. A 1959 survey of 70 lakes recorded values from 4 to 285 ppm. Existing remedies, including diversion ditches, soil conservation, wind-breaks, fencing and fertilizers such as cottonseed meal, superphosphate and powdered gypsum, are costly, hard to obtain or labor-intensive.

The underlying principle is that colloids are precipitated by ions of opposite charge. Brine is rich in positive ions (Na, Ca, K), so the author proposed it as a low-cost neutralizing agent. Brine was supplied free of charge by oil companies, since it has no use and is costly to dispose of.

Study ponds:
- **First Experimental Pond:** 0.8 acres, 118,933 cubic feet. Received intermittent brine introductions.
- **Control Pond:** 0.6 acres, 84,737 cubic feet. Untreated.
- **Second Experimental Pond:** about 0.9 acres, 108,938 cubic feet. Received a single large application.

Brine for the First Experimental Pond came from Calvin Sandstone. The Second Experimental Pond received a mix of Calvin and Calvin-Booch Sandstone brines. Table I reports brine composition at introduction, with Na near 18,500 to 23,000 ppm and Ca about 7,000 to 21,000 ppm. Table II lists Calvin Sandstone brines from eight Oklahoma localities, ranging from about 33,000 to 168,000 ppm total solids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Turbidity and light.** Light penetration is the main mechanism linking turbidity to production. Turbid water restricts photosynthesis and reduces the productive volume of water. In clear ponds, [[Aquatic Plant Growth]] and [[Phytoplankton]] increase, and the compensation point of higher plants moves deeper.

**Plankton and benthos.** Literature cited in the report shows that clear ponds produce more [[Net Plankton]] than turbid ones (Buck 1956). Turbidity also alters [[Benthos]] composition. Some benthic groups, such as [[Tendipedidae]], were reduced by brine, while [[Physa]] snails and [[Hyalella Azteca]] amphipods responded positively to vegetation.

**Fish reproduction and growth.** Turbid water often reduces spawning success and slows growth in centrarchids such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]. Catfish responses are mixed. [[Flathead Catfish]] tolerate turbidity well, while [[Channel Catfish]] grow faster in clear ponds. Turbidity thresholds for fish are given in the report: most adult fish survive up to 100,000 ppm but die at 175,000 to 225,000 ppm. Clay killed fish by clogging gills and opercular cavities.

**Brine toxicity.** [[Daphnia Magna]] was immobilized at 3,680 ppm NaCl. Field studies found few zooplankton species above 1,000 to 4,000 ppm Cl⁻. Fish median toxicity thresholds ranged from about 5,802 to 6,330 ppm Cl⁻.

**Dispersal.** Brine is denser than pond water (1.05 vs. 1.00 at 15.56° C). In a circulating pond it disperses uniformly. In a thermally stratified pond it sinks and concentrates at the deepest point.

## Key Findings

### Intermittent introduction (First Experimental Pond, Oct–Dec 1957)

- **Volume:** 9,360 gallons introduced at weekly or biweekly intervals, in lots of 1,120 to 1,400 gallons. Introduction stopped once the pond bottom became visible.
- **Dispersal:** Brine dispersed almost uniformly within about two weeks through autumnal circulation.
- **Ion behavior:** Chlorides rose from 22 to 447 ppm during introduction, then fell to 47 ppm by October 1958. Specific conductance peaked at 1,196 micromhos one month after the seventh introduction and fell to 154 micromhos by October 1958. Na, Ca and K followed the same pattern, which the author attributes to absorption by bottom sediments and flocculation of clay particles.
- **Turbidity:** Fell from 76 to 8 ppm one month after the last introduction, while the Control Pond stayed between 60 and 85 ppm. The pond stayed clear through a 5.7-inch rainfall. Turbidity stayed below 10 ppm through winter and spring 1958, rising to 18 ppm in August and falling to 6 ppm in autumn.
- **Light:** Light penetration increased. The First Experimental Pond had light reaching the bottom at 6 ft on May 19, 1958, while the Control Pond reached only 13 inches. By October 2, 1958, the 1% light level reached 49 inches in the First Experimental Pond.
- **Plankton:** Maximum net plankton occurred on January 17, 1958, when chlorides were about 400 ppm. [[Cyclopidae]] and [[Diaptomus]] were absent from the First Experimental Pond during the high-brine period, then returned after concentrations fell below 350 ppm Cl⁻. Net plankton volume was greater in the treated pond overall.
- **Phytoplankton:** A single bloom occurred in the First Experimental Pond in early May 1958, lasting about three weeks. On May 16, 1958, the bloom removed 473 micromoles of CO₂ per liter per hour, while the Control Pond showed a net respiration excess of 83 micromoles per liter per hour.
- **Benthos:** Production was greater in the Control Pond for most of the study, mainly because of [[Tendipedidae]]. Late in the study, [[Hyalella Azteca]] and [[Physa]] were more abundant in the treated pond, which the author links to expanded vegetation.
- **Aquatic plants:** Filamentous algae appeared in March 1958, and by late April almost the whole pond was covered by rooted and floating vegetation. In November 1958, 58% of the pond area was covered. The dominant species was [[Sagittaria Latifolia]]. No plants or algae were recorded in the Control Pond.
- **Fish growth (one year):**
  - [[Channel Catfish]] (stocked as fingerlings, October 1957): Experimental pond 12.7 in and 297 g average (n = 26) versus Control 10.2 in and 133 g (n = 21). Growth over the 1958 season was 7.0 in versus 5.5 in. Experimental fish were 51 g above the state average for yearlings, and Control fish were 3 g below.
  - [[Largemouth Bass]]: Experimental fish averaged 10.7 in and 262 g (n = 12) versus Control 9.1 in and 149 g (n = 7). Growth over the 1958 season was 6.2 in versus 4.7 in. Experimental fish were 3 g above the state average and Control fish were 9 g below. Young of the year in the treated pond averaged 6.0 in (range 5.5–6.4 in) and 39 g.
  - Average weight per fish, all species, was 3.4 times greater in the First Experimental Pond.
- **Stocking:** 50 fingerling largemouth bass and 50 fingerling channel catfish were stocked in both ponds in October 1957.

### Single large application (Second Experimental Pond, July 1958)

- **Volume:** 8,400 gallons introduced in seven 1,400-gallon lots at 30–45 minute intervals, starting July 14, 1958. The introduction was halted because one more lot would have exceeded the calculated requirement of 8,573 gallons.
- **Calculation:** Derived by ratio from the First Experimental Pond's 9,360 gallons, scaled by pond volume. The resulting 2,783 gallons per acre-foot was similar to the 2,769 gallons per acre-foot used in the First Experimental Pond.
- **Concentration:** Bottom specific conductance rose from 56 to 7,730 micromhos ten minutes after the first lot. Chloride at the bottom peaked near 8,244 ppm, and the highest bottom conductance was recorded three days after introduction. Concentrated brine stayed in a small zone at the deepest point, and dispersal to uniform levels took about three months.
- **Turbidity:** Surface turbidity fell from 60 to 42 ppm one day after introduction and reached 7 ppm by October 1958. Turbidity rose to 30 ppm after a 5.7-inch rainfall on August 21. The pond stayed clear at 12 ppm through winter 1958–59 and reached 80 ppm in late April 1959 after runoff.
- **Light:** The 1% light level was 18 inches before introduction, 24 inches one day after, and 61 inches on August 12, 1958.
- **Biota:** The bottom water became deleterious. Bottom samples spoiled, with yellow color and an offensive odor, and only four phytoplankton genera were found in the bottom precipitate. Surface phytoplankton bloomed strongly on July 23 and declined by August 12. Zooplankton survived at the surface, and fish showed no dead specimens. Benthos in the concentrated zone was probably killed, while shallow-water benthos was unaffected.

### Conclusions and recommendations

- Brine treatment is recommended "with certain reservations," and should follow careful study and planning.
- Ponds of half an acre to one acre, 7 to 10 ft deep (about 100,000 cubic feet or 2.5 acre-feet), should need 8,000 to 10,000 gallons of intermediate-strength brine.
- Oklahoma brines range from about 1,000 to 175,000 ppm Cl⁻, with most in the 40,000–60,000 ppm range.
- The author advises that intermittent introduction in any season is safer than large single applications, which can harm the biota.
- Clearing duration depends on soil type, degree of silting and brine quantity.
- Unsupervised treatment is inadvisable.
