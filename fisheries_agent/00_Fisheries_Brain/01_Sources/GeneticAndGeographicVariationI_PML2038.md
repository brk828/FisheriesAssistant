# Genetic and Geographic Variation in Rhinichthys (Apocope) osculus in Western North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #rhinichthys

## Executive Summary

This doctoral dissertation by David DeWitt Oakey, [[Arizona State University]] (March 2001), examines the historical [[Biogeography]] of the widespread western minnow, [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*). It uses [[Mitochondrial DNA]] [[Restriction Site]] maps and two analytical approaches: [[Phylogenetic Analysis]] of haplotype relationships across the species' range, and [[Mantel Test]] matrix correlations assessing how geographic distance and drainage-basin alignment shape genetic variation. The abstract concludes that drainage history, particularly hypothesized earlier drainage patterns, has a stronger influence on genetic structure than present-day basins or simple geographic distance.

The title page lists the dissertation committee: Dr. Michael E. Douglas and Dr. Thomas E. Dowling (Co-Chairs), Dr. W. L. Minckley, Dr. M. J. Fouquette, and Dr. Edmund Stump. The defense was scheduled for 10:00 a.m. in LSC 496.

## Background & Description

*Rhinichthys osculus* is described as a widespread western minnow. The study places it in a comparative framework with several congeners:

- *Rhinichthys falcatus* and *R. cataractae*, with which *R. osculus* appears paraphyletic.
- *R. atratulus*, from Michigan, used as the outgroup. It was consistently sister to all other *Rhinichthys* in the analyses.

The genus is written as *Rhinichthys* (subgenus or section *Apocope*, as given in the title).

The study used mitochondrial DNA restriction site maps, which allowed haplotype-level comparisons across drainages. Sample sizes and specimen counts are not reported in the document.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The research framework is historical biogeography in western North America. Key geographic and temporal elements include:

- **Drainage basins:** The [[Snake River]] and [[Colorado River Basin]], which correspond to the northern and southern clades of *R. osculus*, respectively.
- **Drainage history:** Hypothesized Miocene–Pliocene drainage configurations, including a large-scale "Fishhook pattern," which the author treats as more influential than present-day drainage alignments.
- **Glacial history:** Hypothesized post-glacial recolonization of higher elevations within the Colorado River Basin, linked to genetic variation in *R. osculus*.

## Key Findings

**Part I: Phylogenetic relationships**
- Using [[Parsimony]] and [[Neighbor-Joining]] tree-building algorithms, *R. osculus* haplotypes clustered with high [[Basin Fidelity]].
- Haplotypes consistently formed two clades: a northern clade (Snake River) and a southern clade (Colorado River).
- The species appeared paraphyletic with respect to *R. falcatus* and *R. cataractae*.
- The outgroup *R. atratulus* was consistently sister to all other *Rhinichthys*.

**Part II: Drivers of genetic variation**
- Modern drainage basins showed a greater influence on genetic structure than simple geographic distance, tested with [[Mantel Test]] two-way and three-way matrix correlations.
- Hypothesized earlier drainage patterns had a stronger effect on variation than current basins. Large-scale Miocene–Pliocene basin alignments, such as the "Fishhook pattern," were more influential than present-day arrangements.
- Within the [[Colorado River Basin]], genetic variation in *R. osculus* was strongly associated with the hypothesized [[Post-Glacial Recolonization]] of higher elevations.

**Conclusions**
- Historical drainage configuration and glacial-era dispersal, rather than contemporary geography alone, appear to shape the genetic structure of *R. osculus* in western North America.
