# Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #recovery-plan

## Executive Summary

This document is the revised recovery plan for the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), superseding the original 1978 plan. Prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] at the direction of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the plan incorporates research findings from 1978–1984 and outlines actions necessary to recover the species. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is the largest North American cyprinid (historically reaching 1.8 m and 36 kg) and the top carnivore of the [[Colorado River]] system. Once abundant throughout the mainstem Colorado River and major tributaries from Mexico to Wyoming, the species is now restricted to the upper basin (Colorado and Utah). The plan identifies population monitoring, habitat protection, life history research, hatchery propagation, reintroduction, and public education as critical recovery tasks. Primary habitat includes the [[Green River]] from its confluence with the Colorado to Echo Park, the lower 240 km of the [[Yampa River]], the lower 240 km of the [[White River]], and the [[Colorado River]] from Palisade to [[Lake Powell]].

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] was listed as endangered in 1967 (Federal Register Vol. 32(43):40001) and received full protection under the Endangered Species Act of 1973. The species is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], historically ranging from Mexico through Arizona, California, Nevada, Utah, Colorado, New Mexico, and Wyoming. It is elongate with a dorsoventrally compressed, pike-like body, large horizontal mouth, and pharyngeal teeth adapted for grasping prey (tooth formula 2,5-4,2). Adults are olivaceous dorsally, lightening to white ventrally; young exhibit a dark wedge-shaped spot at the caudal fin insertion. The species belongs to the minnow family [[Cyprinidae]] and is the largest cyprinid in North America. Maximum recorded weights exceed 36 kg, though specimens over 7 kg have been rare since the 1970s. The genus *Ptychocheilus* dates to Pliocene time and includes three other living species: *P. grandis* (Sacramento-San Joaquin), *P. oregonensis* (Columbia River), and *P. umpquae* (Umpqua and Siuslaw Rivers, Oregon).

Historical records indicate abundance throughout the mainstem and major tributaries, including the [[Gila River]] basin in Arizona. The species was used as fertilizer when stranded in ditches and enjoyed a favorable reputation as food ("white salmon of the Colorado"). Present distribution is restricted to the upper Colorado River system. Recent studies (1977–1983) documented adults, juveniles, and young in the [[Green River]] from the Yampa confluence downstream, spawning sites in [[Gray Canyon]] and the lower [[Yampa River]], and populations in the [[Colorado River]] from Grand Junction to [[Lake Powell]]. Adults were found in the lower 124 mi of the Yampa, [[Lodore Canyon]], and the [[White River]]. Single specimens were captured in the [[Duchesne River]] and [[San Juan River]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] evolved in a harsh, dynamic environment characterized by extreme flow variability (3,000–380,000 cfs at Yuma), high turbidity, high mineral content (0.62% silt by volume), and temperatures reaching 21°C during spawning. Adults occupy big water habitats over diverse substrates (silt, sand, gravel, boulder) at various depths and velocities. Young-of-the-year (up to 64 mm), juveniles (65–200 mm), and subadults (200–400 mm) prefer shallow backwaters with minimal current over silt/sand bottoms. At approximately 200 mm, habitat preference shifts to deeper, faster water, coinciding with the transition to obligate piscivory. Radiotelemetry studies (1981–1983) revealed long-distance migrations (up to 200 mi) to restricted spawning areas in the lower 32 km of the [[Yampa River]] and [[Gray Canyon]] of the [[Green River]] in July when temperatures reach 21°C.

Young feed on zooplankton and insect larvae; by 60 mm they begin consuming fish, completing the switch by 200 mm. Adults are piscivorous, historically feeding on native species but increasingly consuming nonnative fish as natives decline. Growth rates are variable: mean back-calculated lengths at annulus formation (Vanicek and Kramer 1969) were I=44 mm, II=95 mm, III=162 mm, IV=238 mm, V=320 mm, VI=391 mm, VIII=499 mm, X=600 mm. Laboratory-reared fish doubled length in 14 days and reached 48–50 mm by day 110. Sexual maturity occurs at 5 years (males) and 6 years (females) in captivity. Spawning was successfully induced at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in 1980 using pituitary extract and natural spawning over filter gravel.

Tributaries appear critically important to life history. Larvae collected below RK 32 on the Yampa (1980–1983) suggest net downstream drift from spawning grounds to the [[Green River]]. Adult upstream migration from the Green to the Yampa for spawning and juvenile movement from the Green to the [[White River]] indicate complex habitat requirements. During 1977 (low Yampa flow), young-of-the-year were absent from areas below Jensen, Utah, where they occurred in normal years, suggesting Yampa flows are important to downstream reproductive success.

## Key Findings

**Decline Factors**: The absolute cause of decline is unknown but likely involves habitat loss, altered flow regimes, blockage of migration routes, temperature changes, and competition with introduced species. Since [[Hoover Dam]] (1935) and 20+ subsequent dams, the river has transformed from a turbulent, muddy system to a series of reservoirs connected by clear, cold streams. [[Colorado Squawfish]] disappeared above some reservoirs (e.g., Salt River above Roosevelt Lake, Green River above [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], San Juan above Navajo Reservoir) due to pre-impoundment poisoning, habitat changes, and proliferation of 42 introduced fish species (of 55 total species in the upper basin), including predators like largemouth bass, smallmouth bass, striped bass, channel catfish, and walleye. Below dams, altered temperature regimes, reduced mean temperatures, altered flow patterns, lowered turbidity, and nonnative stocking effectively eliminated squawfish from 105 km of the Green River below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].

**Recovery Objective**: The species will be considered eligible for reclassification to threatened when naturally self-sustaining populations are maintained in the upper Colorado River basin and introduced/maintained in the lower basin, with no further fragmentation. Primary habitat includes: (a) Green River from Colorado confluence to Echo Park; (b) lower 240 km of Yampa River; (c) lower 240 km of White River; (d) Colorado River from Palisade to Lake Powell. Proposed reintroduction sites include the [[Salt River]] in Arizona and mainstem Colorado River in California and Arizona. Delisting criteria require downlisting criteria be met plus legal protection of primary essential habitats, migration routes, river flow, and water quality parameters.

**Recovery Tasks**: (1) Determine population status and life history requirements through monitoring all populations, compiling historical data, surveying potential habitat, defining self-sustaining populations, and expanding life history information (spawning requirements, feeding behavior, age/growth, migration patterns, fish passage requirements). (2) Implement management plan to protect populations and habitat by monitoring development projects, assessing nonnative species impacts, studying parasitism/disease, enforcing existing laws (ESA Section 7 compliance), identifying/monitoring essential habitat, and managing/restoring primary habitat. (3) Reintroduce squawfish into historic range by establishing hatchery facilities ([[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] as broodstock site, new FWS facility planned for FY-86, [[Imperial Valley Warmwater Hatchery]] ponds), maintaining genetic diversity, inventorying reintroduction sites, restoring stocking sites, and monitoring reintroduced populations. (4) Conduct information/education programs on aesthetic and recreational value.

**Research Findings (1978–1984)**: Studies by [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]], [[National Park Service]], and others documented spawning migrations, spawning sites, larval drift, habitat preferences by life stage, temperature preferences (laboratory: 21.5–25.7°C, optimal 25.4°C), and movement patterns. Radiotelemetry tracked 16 adults to Yampa spawning grounds in 1981–1983; 51 ripe fish were collected in 1981 and 1983, 18 in 1982. Recaptures documented movements over 100 km and fidelity to spawning grounds. Gray Canyon spawning was confirmed in 1983 with 19 ripe fish collected. Larvae (9–13 mm TL) were collected below RK 32–40 on the Yampa. Studies clarified that blockage of long-distance spawning migrations and interruption of larval drift could extirpate the species from remaining range.
