# Draft Razorback Sucker Augmentation Plan: Feasibility of Stocking to Augment or Reintroduce Razorback Sucker in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service draft plan (April 1991, prepared by [[Bob Burdick]] for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]]) evaluates whether experimental stocking of hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] can augment or reintroduce the species in the upper [[Colorado River]] and the lower [[Gunnison River]]. The total catch of adult razorback sucker has declined drastically since 1974, and no young fish have been documented in the upper Colorado River in 26 years, so the remaining population is composed almost entirely of old adults. The plan proposes a multi-phase program (pre-stocking, stocking, post-stocking) built on a [[Broodstock and Refugia]] population, controlled paired matings, [[PIT Tagging]] of progeny, grow-out ponds, and radiotelemetry monitoring. Comments were requested by July 1, 1991.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a native catostomid that was formerly common and widespread in warmwater reaches of the Colorado River Basin, from Wyoming to Mexico. Its range has been reduced by about 75% (Seethaler 1978; McAda and Wydoski 1980).
- **Status:** Proposed for listing as endangered under the Endangered Species Act of 1973; currently protected by Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, and Utah.
- **Causes of decline:** Recruitment failure is attributed to predation on eggs, larvae, and young, and to loss of high spring flows and inundated bottomlands that serve as spawning and nursery habitat.
- **Known adult concentration areas:** Lake Mohave (lower basin); the upper Green River from the Duchesne River mouth to the Yampa River; and the Colorado River in the [[Grand Valley]] near Grand Junction.
- **Diet:** Herbivorous; not piscivorous.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Monitoring data (ISMP):** Only one razorback sucker was collected between Lake Powell and Price-Stub Diversion Dam from 1986 to 1989, compared with 60 from the Green River in the same period.
- **Historical catch trends:** 52 fish collected from Lake Powell to Rifle (1979–1981); 35 collected (1982–1985); 7 collected in a 53-km Grand Valley reach (1986–1989).
- **2010 search effort:** In 1990, reaches from Parachute to Government High Line Diversion Dam and from Palisade to the Utah/Colorado state line were searched without capturing fish in the river, though four adults were captured in High Line Lake, an irrigation reservoir near Loma.
- **Barriers:** Government High Line Diversion Dam (completed 1916, about 20 ft high) blocks upstream fish movement at all flows.
- **Predation:** Predation by ictalurid catfishes and centrarchids (e.g., largemouth bass, green sunfish) hampered reintroductions in the lower basin.
- **Gravel pits and backwaters:** Off-channel gravel pits may strand fish and expose eggs and young to non-native predators, while graded pits and diked side channels (modeled on Yampa River examples) could provide managed backwater habitat.
- **Genetic and disease risks:** Straying, hybridization with other catostomids, loss of interpopulation genetic diversity, possible outbreeding depression, pathogen transmission, and competition with native fish.

## Key Findings

- **Recruitment failure:** No wild young have been found in the upper Colorado River in 26 years, and the population is largely restricted to large, old adults.
- **Study design:** Seven wild adults (4 males, 3 females) were collected (3 in 1989, 4 in 1990) and moved to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] to form the broodstock nucleus. Breeding strategy trials used four males and two females (one female did not produce eggs).
- **Proposed stocking areas:** (1) greater Grand Valley near Grand Junction, (2) Rifle to Debeque, and (3) the lower 55 miles of the Gunnison River above Redlands Diversion Dam.
- **Stocking size and number:** Large juveniles or adults (250–300 mm total length), about 10 fish per site initially (radio-tagged), with a minimum of 100 males and 100 females per location for a full program, rising to 250 of each sex if available. Initial phase fish would total up to 30 hatchery-produced fish, 5 males and 5 females per reach.
- **Timing:** Early spring (March) stocking is preferred to allow acclimation before runoff and potential first-year spawning (May–June).
- **Grow-out performance:** Razorback sucker stocked at about 55 mm in June reached over 300 mm in six months, with estimated survival of 99.3% over a 2-year period (Osmundson and Kaeding 1989b). Stocking density of at least 100 fish per surface acre is recommended.
- **Tagging:** Progeny PIT-tagged at 100–125 mm total length to allow family, wild-versus-hatchery, and movement tracking.
- **Monitoring:** Radio transmitters (18–24 months) with weekly monitoring during the spawning period and monthly otherwise; annual spring surveys; chi-square analyses of recapture frequencies among lots and sites.
- **Timeline:** Initial monitoring for 5–10 years, with benefits possibly requiring 7–10 years; the Recovery Program has a 15-year timeframe. Small-scale adult stocking is scheduled for 1994, with large-scale recommendations for 1995–2000.
- **Limitations:** Hatchery production is time consuming and costly; success or failure is hard to judge without defined recovery goals; genetic risks remain.
- **Recommended first step:** Obtain additional wild adults from the upper Colorado River to supplement the Dexter broodstock, and develop breeding strategies consistent with the Propagation Plan.
