# A Key to the British Fresh- and Brackish-Water Gastropods

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gastropoda

## Executive Summary

This document is the second edition (1960) of Freshwater Biological Association Scientific Publication No. 13, a dichotomous identification key by T. T. Macan to the British fresh- and brackish-water gastropods (snails), with notes on their ecology. The key is illustrated by R. Douglas Cooper and follows the nomenclature of Ellis (1926). It covers the freshwater snail fauna, a few brackish-water and estuarine species, and some dwellers in damp or marshy ground that lie near the aquatic/terrestrial boundary. Ecological notes, based largely on [[Boycott]] (1936), classify species into four groups according to distribution and calcium tolerance, with specific records for the [[Lake District]]. The item is a scientific reference work, not a proposal or budget.

## Background & Description

**Scope and purpose.** The author notes that freshwater biologists wanting to name snails usually had to buy a book that also covered all land snails. The key aims to fill that gap by being as comprehensive as possible for aquatic and near-aquatic species.

**Nomenclature.** No agreed checklist existed, so the author followed Ellis (1926). Synonyms are given in italics, and names marked with an asterisk are now widely regarded as valid. Subgenera of [[Limnaea]] and [[Planorbis]] are treated as genera by some authors and are included.

**Classification.** The key is artificial and built for identification rather than phylogeny. The major division used is between the Streptoneura (Prosobranchia), which have an operculum and generally gills, and the Euthyneura (Pulmonata), which lack an operculum. Pulmonata are split into Basommatophora ("pond snails", separate genital openings, one pair of tentacles with eyes at their bases) and Stylommatophora ("land snails", single genital opening, two pairs of tentacles).

**Morphology.** Key features used are:
- Shell coiling (dextral or sinistral), whorls, spire, body whorl, suture, umbilicus and aperture.
- Radula, used in classification (teeth arrangement).
- Reproductive anatomy, including the [[Spermatheca]], penis sheath and prostate, illustrated using *Limnaea pereger* as the model (Fig. 2).

**Preservation.** The author stresses that soft parts and shells should both be kept, because past over-reliance on shell characters caused much confusion. Soft parts are best kept in 70 per cent alcohol and shells dry. Animals may be killed by immersion in water de-oxygenated by boiling.

**Acknowledgments.** Named reviewers, testers and specimen providers are thanked, including G. I. Crawford, C. Diver, A. E. Ellis and H. Watson.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Ecological framework.** Unlike the insect groups treated in earlier volumes of the series, molluscs do not divide neatly into three categories (restricted, ubiquitous, and habitat-specialist). Boycott's analysis led to four groups:
- **Group 1:** Brackish-water species.
- **Group 2:** Species with limited distribution.
- **Group 3:** Hard-water species, never or practically never found in soft water.
- **Group 4:** Soft-water species, which can live in soft water.

**Optimum habitat.** Boycott concluded that the optimum is much the same for most species: a large volume of moderately warm, calcareous water, free of suspended inorganic matter and organic pollution, flowing not too rapidly over a shallow bottom, with moderate plant growth. Species number falls as any factor departs from the optimum, and a high level of one factor can partly compensate for another.

**Calcium threshold.** Water counts as soft by the snail criterion at about 20 parts of calcium per million. Boycott regarded a pond of a quarter of an acre or more as large from the snail standpoint.

**Lake District.** Lakes (in main valleys, draining large areas) are distinguished from tarns (small, in side valleys, often artificial fishponds) and pools (smaller, shallower, no perennial outflow). The area is underlain by older, harder rocks poor in lime, so all its waters are soft. The author lists 13 lakes sampled, including Windermere, Derwentwater, Ullswater and Esthwaite. Windermere has about 6 parts of calcium per million, yet *Planorbis carinatus* is one of its most abundant species, which the author attributes to large size compensating for low calcium.

## Key Findings

**Identification key.** The core product is a dichotomous key covering about 45 British species across roughly 25 genera, with keys to Operculate snails, Pulmonates and Basommatophora. The key is illustrated with 15 figure plates by Cooper. Dimensions are quoted where size is the critical distinction. Several taxa are flagged as introduced: *Physa acuta* (Mediterranean), *Physa heterostropha* (American), *Limnaea catascopium* (American, Leith engine pond, omitted from the key) and *Planorbis dilatatus* (American, first found near Manchester in 1869).

**Taxonomic notes.** Some points remain disputed:
- *Limnaea pereger* is highly variable, with 23 varieties listed by Ellis (1926). Forms *involuta* and *burnetti* may breed true in culture.
- Hubendick (1945) concluded there are only two species in the *Radix* group in north Europe, *L. (R.) limosa* and *L. (R.) auricularia*.
- Whether *Valvata macrostoma* and *V. piscinalis* are distinct is contested.
- *Planorbis complanatus* and *P. fontanus* are often confused in usage.

**Distribution highlights.**
- **Group 1 (brackish):** *Assiminea grayana* is common in the Thames Estuary; *Sabanaea ulvae* is common in estuaries and salt-marshes; *Hydrobia ventrosa* is fairly common in estuaries and lagoons.
- **Group 2 (limited):** *Planorbis acronicus* is confined to the Thames and tributaries; *Valvata macrostoma* occurs in Hampshire, Sussex, Norfolk, Suffolk and Cambridgeshire.
- **Group 3 (hard water):** *Theodoxus fluviatilis* and *Viviparus viviparus* are common in calcareous waters; *Viviparus viviparus* is not found in Ireland or north of Yorkshire.
- **Group 4 (soft water):** *Ancylastrum fluviatile* is very common; *Limnaea pereger* is the commonest species, recorded from every vice-county in the British Isles.

**Lake District records.** *Myxas glutinosa* was recorded from Windermere. *Hydrobia jenkinsi* was very abundant in Whitecross Bay, Windermere, in 1937, reduced by 1946 (possibly due to a concrete pier built during the war), and first recorded in an inflowing stream in 1948. *Viviparus fasciatus* was introduced to one tarn and had flourished there for 10 years.

**Reference value.** The bibliography cites roughly 15 works, including Boycott (1936) on the habitats of freshwater Mollusca in Britain, Ellis (1926) *British Snails*, and Kevan (1943) on *Stagnicola catascopium*. The index lists the synonyms and page references for each taxon.
