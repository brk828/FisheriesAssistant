# Private Conservation in Mexico: Land Tenure, Legal Tools and Challenges

#ichthyology #marine-biology #private-conservation

## Executive Summary

This 2000 report, prepared by Elisa I. Corcuera for the [[David and Lucile Packard Foundation]], reviews the emerging practice of private land conservation in Mexico. It explains how [[Land Tenure]] under the [[Ejido]] system shapes what conservation tools are feasible, catalogues seven legal tools, compares three options for who should implement them, and lists the main challenges and mitigation strategies. It also documents 29 initiatives (Annex 1), a contact list (Annex 2) and detailed case studies (Annex 3). The text is a policy and planning document rather than a biological study, so it contains no species-level or aquatic field data.

## Background & Description

**Definition.** Private conservation is voluntary land protection by owners or groups without government involvement. The institutionalized form, associated with [[Land Trust]] organizations in the United States, uses structured legal tools and incentives to secure conservation beyond the lifetime of individual owners.

**Origins in Mexico.** [[The Nature Conservancy]] (TNC) made private conservation in Latin America a core effort in 1996 through its Private Conservation Initiative. Two early partners were [[Amigos de Sian Ka'an]] and [[Pronatura A.C.]]. Pronatura closed Mexico's first conservation easement in the state of Veracruz at the end of 1998 ([[Rancho Las Cañadas]]). Other organizations followed, including the [[World Wildlife Fund]], [[Naturalia A.C.]] and [[Wildlands Project]], and [[Pronatura Noreste]].

**Motivations.** The report lists seven reasons for interest, largely borrowed from the US experience: rapid loss of open space and habitat, growing environmental consciousness, perception that government cannot do enough, scarce opposition to voluntary market-based mechanisms, greater NGO credibility, faster NGO action, and flexibility to negotiate tailored agreements.

**Land tenure.** Mexico has three ownership types:
- Small private property, which resembles US ownership but is subject to maximum landholding limits. About 32% of land is privately owned (INEGI 1994).
- Communities (2,571 in total), derived from prehispanic group land use and covering a small share of land, mostly in southern Mexico.
- Ejidos (27,412 in total), covering over 50% of national land and a larger share in rural areas. The report describes the ejido as found in no other country.

Ejido land has three parts: urban plots, individually held *parcelas* (which revert to the ejido if unused for two or more years), and communal ranges.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report links tenure to biodiversity. Ejidos hold some of Mexico's most pristine land, and their former status as "inefficient" land use means much of the country's biodiversity lies within them. Reforms, particularly the 1992 constitutional changes, now allow ejidatarios to rent, sell, form joint ventures and use parcels as loan collateral. The author warns that privatization and development could threaten this biodiversity quickly, and that government environmental action is slow.

Examples of targeted ecosystems include:
- Cloud forest at [[Huitepec]] and San Cristóbal de las Casas.
- Pine-oak and ancient forest in the Sierra de Bolaños, habitat of the [[Thick-Billed Parrot]] (habitat decline of 98% in Mexico this century).
- [[Cuatro Ciénegas]] gypsum dunes, described as of high endemism and compared to the Galápagos.
- Coastal wetlands and migratory bird habitat at [[Laguna Madre]] and the [[San Pedro River]] watershed.
- Marine-coastal ecosystems in Baja California, such as [[Bahía Concepción]], [[San Ignacio Lagoon]], and [[Isla Espíritu Santo]].
- Tropical forest and reserves in [[Sian Ka'an Biosphere Reserve]] and [[Calakmul]].

No fish, invertebrate, plankton or water-chemistry data are reported, though marine ecosystems and migratory birds are mentioned.

## Key Findings

**1. Seven legal tools are identified:**
- *Donations and fee-simple purchases*: most secure protection, but costly and rare in Mexico. Land is cheap, and many owners are willing to sell. No donation tradition or tax breaks exist.
- *Easements*: highly durable under Mexican civil law. The law requires dominant and servient plots, so workarounds include donating a 1-hectare parcel to the NGO or reciprocal easements.
- *Transfers to government*: no Mexican examples exist yet. The report advises lawyer-drafted clauses binding government protection, because future administrations can reverse protections.
- *Mercantile associations*: ejidos contribute land and an NGO contributes capital to a profit-based business. Conservation terms become enforceable, but duration is capped at 30 years.
- *Fideicomisos* (bank-administered trusts): the NGO is beneficiary, and a technical committee controls decisions. Maximum duration is 30 years, and the tool can also sidestep landholding limits.
- *Transferable Development Rights (TDRs)*: shift conservation costs to developers and are perpetual if zoning holds. In Mexico they require legal changes and prior zoning.
- *UMAs (Unidades de Manejo Ambiental)*: wildlife-use units created in 1997. They give landowners an incentive to protect species, but use plans are weakly monitored and any owner can withdraw.

**2. Three options for implementing agency:**
- A new local land trust is judged not the best option now, given low public confidence, few Mexican experts, and landholding limits of about 25 properties per trust.
- Direct action by international organizations is advised against except in exceptional cases, given historic and nationalist sensitivities and foreign land-ownership restrictions (50 km coastal and 100 km border strips).
- Partnering with a willing Mexican NGO is the preferred option, with US land trusts providing technical support.

**3. Major challenges** (Table 1) include political opposition, lack of a private conservation tradition, scarce NGO resources, lack of trained lawyers and stewards, NGO failure risk, ejido tenure complexity (many owners to negotiate with, requiring a 2/3 General Assembly quorum), irregular titles and land invasions, lack of enabling legislation, and inflated land prices once outside interest appears. Corruption and drug-related violence are also noted.

**4. Key mitigation priorities:** complete first case studies, publish lessons, build institutional capacity (lawyers, stewards, financial planning), set up endowment funds, prioritise local leadership, do rigorous due diligence, engage authorities early, and lobby for TDR and easement legislation.

**5. Annex 1 initiatives (29 listed).** Notable examples:
- [[Isla Espíritu Santo]]: an ejido-owned island (11,000 ha) in the Islas del Golfo protected area (over 450,000 ha, 900+ islands). The ISLA NGO is negotiating a deal involving purchase of 90 parceled hectares, a fideicomiso, and an eco-tourism venture. Reported estimates put the 90 ha at a couple of million US dollars, and expropriation rates run about USD 50/ha.
- [[Sian Ka'an TDR Scheme]]: the first proposed non-urban TDR system in Mexico. A cap of 0.5 rooms/ha (898 rooms reserve-wide) was proposed. The project was put on hold in 1999 because of political pressure.
- [[Carricito del Huichol]]: a conservation easement closed in 1999, with a 373 ha purchase by Bosque Antiguo and a proposed carbon-sequestration fideicomiso worth about USD 2 million.
- [[Tutuaca]]: 2,400 ha of pine forest protected for 15 years at a cost of USD 250,000, compensating the ejido 50% of wood value.
- [[Joya del Hielo]]: 200 ha purchased within the Sierra Gorda Biosphere Reserve, funded by local donations.
- [[Reserva Ecológica El Edén]]: a privately owned research reserve (1,492 ha) founded in 1990.
- [[Rancho Las Cañadas]]: Mexico's first conservation easement (180 ha, 1998), a reciprocal easement.
- [[Santa María Atarasquillo]]: a 5-year legal process taking an ejido from communal ownership to full ownership before a sale.

**6. Overall conclusion.** The author argues that US land trusts needed over 100 years to build enabling legislation and public support, so Mexico should expect slow progress. Still, the risk of not acting, given rapid land privatization and development, is judged greater than the risk of the challenges.
