# Native Fish Distribution in Grand Canyon Tributaries and Predation on Humpback Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This note covers two papers from the Desertfishes Council 1994 Symposium Volume (published 1995). The first, by Paul C. Mars, examines the biotic and abiotic factors that control native fish distribution in six small streams in or near [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. Seasonal surveys ran from January 1992 to August 1993. Physical habitat (especially water temperature), introduced trout, waterfalls, and spawning migrations were the most significant determinants of distribution.

The second paper examines the diet of non-native fishes in the [[Little Colorado River]]. It reports that native fishes, including the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], were minor dietary components, but that predation by abundant non-natives could still remove thousands of young chub each year. The text of this second paper is partly illegible in the source scan, so its author list and some details are not reliably recoverable.

## Background & Description

The first study concerns native catostomids and cyprinids in Grand Canyon tributaries to the [[Colorado River]]. The focal species are:

- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), a common native species.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), a common native species.
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), a common native species.

Introduced salmonids, [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), are the dominant fishes in some streams.

The second study addresses the predatory role of non-native fishes: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], and yellow and black bullheads. It focuses on [[Humpback Chub]], an endangered native species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study streams (Mars).** Six small streams were surveyed. Sampling covered the first 5 km upstream from each confluence with the Colorado River, and up to 14 km on some streams. The streams and their reported fish communities were:

- [[Shinumo Creek]] and [[Kanab Creek]]: perennial populations of bluehead sucker and speckled dace.
- [[Paria River]]: speckled dace always present; flannelmouth sucker only in spring.
- [[Bright Angel Creek]]: dominated by introduced trout (brown and rainbow), with flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and speckled dace present seasonally.
- [[Tapeats Creek]]: occupied only by rainbow trout.
- [[Deer Creek]]: no fish observed upstream from its mouth.

**Limiting factors.** The author identifies four main controls on native fish distribution: physical habitat conditions (especially water temperature), presence of introduced trout, waterfalls that block upstream fish movement, and spawning migrations.

**Non-native predation (Little Colorado River).** Samples were collected monthly from June 1991 to July 1994. Food items were low in diversity and dominated by detritus, algae, and aquatic insects. Native fishes were minor prey, occurring in 5% of the 315 stomachs examined.

## Key Findings

**Distribution (Mars, 1995)**

- Six streams were surveyed seasonally between January 1992 and August 1993.
- Physical habitat, particularly water temperature, was the strongest determinant of native fish distribution.
- Introduced trout dominated Bright Angel Creek and were the only fish in Tapeats Creek, excluding native species from those reaches.
- Waterfalls and spawning migrations shape upstream movement and seasonal presence.
- No fish were found in Deer Creek upstream from its mouth.

**Predation (Little Colorado River)**

- Native fishes occurred in 5% of 315 non-native fish stomachs, so they were minor dietary items.
- The author argues that low frequency of occurrence does not mean low impact, because predator abundance is high.
- Illustrative calculation: if 5% of predators each eat 2 fish per week (the average number per stomach), then 1,000 predators would consume 5,200 fish per year.
- Measured consumed chubs (n = 11) averaged 121 mm total length and were mostly yearlings.
- Recaptures of PIT-tagged chub over 150 mm total length suggest low mortality rates for larger individuals. Age- and size-specific mortality has not been quantified.
- The author concludes that annual losses of several thousand young chub to non-native predators could significantly affect the native population by reducing recruitment.

**Overall conclusion:** Non-native fishes affect native fish populations both by excluding them from habitat (trout in tributaries) and by consuming their young (predation on Little Colorado River chub). Management that addresses non-native fish is therefore central to native fish recovery in the Grand Canyon region.
