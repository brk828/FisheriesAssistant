# The Feeding Habits of Gambusia affinis affinis, with Special Reference to the Malaria Mosquito Anopheles quadrimaculatus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1942 study by [[A. D. Hess]] and [[Clarence M. Tarzwell]] examines how the viviparous top minnow [[Gambusia affinis affinis]] feeds on mosquito larvae and other invertebrates in the newly impounded [[Wheeler Reservoir]] of the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] region. Fieldwork ran from June 12 to September 20, 1940. Feeding preference was quantified with the [[Forage Ratio]], which compares the share of a prey type in fish stomachs with its share in the environment. The authors found that Gambusia's predation on [[Anopheles quadrimaculatus]] rises with mosquito density, that culicines are preferred at equal densities, and that pupae are strongly favored over larvae. The study concludes that availability matters more than choice in determining what Gambusia eats.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Gambusia affinis affinis]] (Baird and Girard), a viviparous top minnow long regarded as an important predator of mosquito immatures.
- **Prior work:** [[Hildebrand 1925]] studied the related subspecies [[Gambusia holbrooki]] near Augusta, Georgia (1921–24), using the common dipper method. Fish-containing waters had fewer mosquitoes than fishless waters, but complete control never occurred. Reported reductions were 80.8% for culicines and 57.8% for anophelines overall, and 85.5% (culicine) and 74.2% (anopheline) for pupae.
- **Howard (1920):** Observations in Hinds County, Mississippi, during 1918–19 found *Anopheles punctipennis* the most abundant anopheline. The author judged the experiments too limited for firm conclusions but saw "substantial hopes" for fish-based control.
- **Rationale for study:** Gambusia was the most conspicuous and abundant natural predator of Anopheles in Tennessee Valley reservoirs, and its value for control was disputed. It was selected for special study by the [[Technical Committee on the Relation Between Mosquito Control and Wildlife Conservation]] (Bishop, 1940).
- **Key variables** cited in earlier literature: aquatic vegetation and debris, Gambusia density, and the presence of natural enemies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites and ecological situations.** Three contrasting settings were sampled:

1. **Fluctuating-level bays** of the main reservoir (Spring Hill Church and Whiteside areas), where water level tracked the reservoir.
2. **Constantly high-level bay** (Sweetwater Bay), created by damming off a bay in spring.
3. **Newly impounded summer area** (Pryor Springs rearing pond), created by closing valves and raising the level about 2 feet when terrestrial vegetation was well advanced.

**Sampling methods.**
- Barrier seines enclosed plots for 1–3 days so that sampled fish had fed in the same plot as the prey samples.
- Food organisms were collected with a screen dipper and strainer pan (50-mesh copper screen, openings 0.34–0.38 mm). Each dip was 1 yard long, covered about 1 sq. ft., and represented about one-twelfth cu. ft. of water. At least 30 random samples were taken per plot, located using a card-based transect system.
- Fish were collected at early forenoon, when preliminary 2-hour sampling showed feeding peaked soon after daylight.
- Laboratory counts used a Sedgwick-Rafter cell, with 5 subsamples per sample. The coefficient of variation of counts was always below 10%.
- Only Entomostraca were countable among plankton in stomachs, so only they were counted in the plankton fraction.

**Dominant invertebrate taxa.**
- *Trichocorixa burmeisteri* (water boatman) was the most abundant littoral macroinvertebrate in Tennessee Valley reservoirs. Most consumed individuals were nymphs. Hemiptera peaked in fluctuating-level areas.
- Chironomidae included predaceous Tanypodinae, which at times made up almost 50% of the chironomid population.
- *Erythemis simplicicollis* (young dragonfly nymphs) was abundant in late summer surface vegetation and was heavily eaten.
- Naididae annelids live in tubes and had the lowest forage ratio of all groups studied.
- Chaoborus larvae appeared in stomachs but not in surface samples, suggesting night or early-morning migration into surface waters.

**Summary of sampling totals.** The summary reports 295 square-foot samples and stomach contents of 1,018 Gambusia. Site-level tables list 155 stomachs with 96 samples (Spring Hill Church and Whiteside), 120 fish with 36 samples (Sweetwater), and 93 fish with 64 samples (Pryor Springs). These site counts do not sum to the overall total as printed, so the overall figures are reported as given.

## Key Findings

### 1. Forage ratio and mosquito density
- The Anopheles forage ratio equaled 1 at a larval density of about 2 per sq. ft. Above this, the ratio rose; below it, the ratio fell.
- At Pryor Springs, where Anopheles averaged over 17 per sq. ft., Gambusia ate more than 14 times the number expected from relative abundance. A single female 8.2 mm long contained 19 Anopheles larvae.
- At Whiteside on September 20, nearly 1,000 larvae were in the enclosure (density 0.7 per sq. ft.), yet 75 stomachs contained no larvae. The authors interpret this as reduced preference at low density.
- Anopheles larvae were concentrated by wind at Pryor Springs. One 1 sq. ft. dip in heavy flotage yielded 195 larvae.

### 2. Proportion of fish feeding on mosquitoes
- At Type I areas (about 0.5 Anopheles per sq. ft.), fewer than 1% of fish fed on them.
- At Sweetwater (about 2 per sq. ft.), 12–14% of fish fed on them.
- At Pryor Springs, up to 86.5% of fish examined contained Anopheles. Over 75% ate more than one larva, and almost 30% ate more than six.

### 3. Culicines versus anophelines
- At equal densities, the culicine forage ratio was one-quarter greater than the anopheline ratio.
- Anophelines matched culicine forage ratios only when their density was one-third greater than culicine density.
- At Pryor Springs, Anopheles were almost twice as abundant as culicines, and their forage ratio exceeded the culicine ratio. The authors attribute the culicine preference to surface-hanging and active habits. Anophelines lie flat against the surface near flotage.

### 4. Life stage preferences
- No first-instar Anopheles larvae were found in stomachs. Preference increased with later instars, and pupae were preferred most.
- For all Diptera, Gambusia preferred pupae over larvae and later instars over early ones.
- At Whiteside (September 20), 75 Gambusia contained 145 Ceratopogonidae, all but 7 of which were pupae. The high Ceratopogonidae and Chironomidae forage ratios were driven by pupal feeding.

### 5. Feeding by sex and size
| Group | Entomostraca (avg. no.) | Macroscopic organisms (avg. no.) | Macroscopic share of total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Males, 15–30 mm | 6.7 | 2.4 | 26% |
| Females, 15–30 mm | 12.8 | 4.5 | 26% |
| Females, 31–45 mm | 22.6 | 5.5 | 20% |
| Females, 46–60 mm | 2.3* | 6.9 | 75% |

*The 2.3 value appears garbled in the source and should be checked against the original.

- Males ate about half as much food as same-size females, with similar composition.
- Anopheles made up 40% of the macroscopic food of males, 34% of females under 30 mm, and 33% of females over 30 mm.
- Large females ate proportionally more macroscopic prey and less plankton, making them the most effective predators of Anopheles.

### 6. Non-mosquito prey
- Entomostraca forage ratio was not significantly different from 1 in the first two situations, but at Pryor Springs it was 0.61 (significantly below 1), despite the highest Entomostraca concentration. The authors read this as feeding by necessity rather than preference.
- Seasonal change matters. Late-season *Erythemis simplicicollis* nymphs were eaten heavily, while mature nymphs earlier in the season were too large and had low forage ratios.

### 7. Conclusions
- The size of the forage ratio is driven by species composition, relative and absolute density, and developmental stage of prey. Gambusia density showed no apparent correlation with the forage ratio.
- Gambusia's feeding favors medium-sized, active, and less secretive prey.
- The authors conclude that **availability is more important than choice** in determining what Gambusia eats. Other factors, such as shelter for larvae, are also likely important.
- Gambusia is an effective predator of Anopheles mainly when mosquito densities are high. Its control value is therefore strongest in conditions that favor mosquito concentration, such as wind-driven accumulation or newly impounded, vegetated waters.
