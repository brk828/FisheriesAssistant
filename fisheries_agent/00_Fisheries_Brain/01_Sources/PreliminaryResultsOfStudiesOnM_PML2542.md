# Preliminary Results of Studies on Movement and Reproduction of the Razorback Sucker in Senator Wash Reservoir, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary report, prepared by Linda Ulmer (California State University, Long Beach) for the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] Inland Fisheries Endangered Species Program (August 1980), documents a four-month study (January to April 1980) of adult [[Razorback Sucker]] movement and spawning in [[Senator Wash Reservoir]]. Five adult fish were monitored with [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] (sonic tags), spawning sites were identified by tracking and confirmed with [[SCUBA Observation]], and eggs and larvae were sampled. The study found that the population is successfully completing spawning, but the author states that recruitment cannot yet be confirmed and that the results remain preliminary.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is one of four native freshwater fishes of the Colorado River drainage. It was once widespread and an important food source for Indian tribes, and supported a commercial fishery in southern Arizona reservoirs until the late 1940s.
- **Status:** Construction of dams from 1908 through the mid-1950s altered flow, temperature and backwaters. The species declined enough to be listed as endangered by California and Nevada. It persists at low but stable numbers in a few Lower Colorado River reservoirs.
- **Monitoring history:** For eight years before this study, all razorback suckers sampled in Senator Wash Reservoir were adults of similar size, with no evidence of recruitment.
- **Research questions:** Do razorback suckers spawn successfully in the reservoir? Where does spawning occur? What are the habitat characteristics? Are there peak activity cycles?
- **Study area:** Senator Wash Reservoir is an offstream storage reservoir on the California side of the Colorado River, about two miles upstream of Imperial Dam. It was built in 1956 by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] to re-regulate water released at [[Parker Dam]]. Its operating level fluctuates from 251 feet to 210 feet (13,831 to 11,580 acre-feet). It drains 21.1 square miles of the eastern Chocolate Mountains, with substrate of sand, gravel and cobbles. Lower turbidity than the Colorado River makes SCUBA observation feasible.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning habitat:** Spawning occurred on silt-free gravel and rock on littoral benches at 10–18 feet of water, at temperatures of roughly 14–16 °C (the exact range is partly illegible in the source).
- **Water-level effects:** A drop in reservoir level from 235 to 225 feet between February 26 and March 6, 1980 left Spawning Area I dry, and fish shifted to Area II.
- **Predators:** [[Channel Catfish]] fed on eggs and spawning sites immediately after spawning. [[Centrarchids]] (bluegill, redear sunfish and yearling [[Largemouth Bass]]) were abundant on the spawning area.
- **Threats to recruitment:** The author identifies predation (inter- and intraspecific), water-level fluctuation and its indirect effects (erosion, silt deposition, temperature swings from pumped water), inadequate larval diet, and larval drift out of the reservoir during drawdown.
- **Behavioral note:** Gustafson (1974) reports nuptial color in males, but no secondary sexual color change was seen in this population.

## Key Findings

- **Study population:** Five adult fish were tracked, and six were sonically tagged in January 1980 (two males, three gravid females, one spent female). One gravid female died early in monitoring, so movement data are limited.
- **Movement:** Three tagged females moved along depth contours at night. Tagged males showed less predictable activity and little movement away from their spawning site. No firm conclusions about activity cycles were drawn.
- **Spawning areas:** Only two areas were used. Area I (a cove at the northwest end, about 55 m long and 2–4 m wide) was used from January 27 to February 26, 1980. Area II (on the outer bank of a cove east of the launch ramp, 20 m by 3 m) was used from March 3 to the second week of March. Area II was not used for spawning during the SCUBA surveys; groups of two to four fish were only seen swimming over it.
- **Spawning aggregation:** Shore observations (January 27 and February 12, 1980) recorded 40–50 fish in water 2–10 feet deep, arranged in 7–10 spawning groups of one female and one to eight males.
- **Underwater observations:** Ten SCUBA observations were made between February 19 and February 22, 1980, and seven more were noted for the spawning period. Spawning groups averaged 7–15 fish (1–5 females). Visibility was 3–5 m. Males were identified by well-defined tubercles, and females by opaque white coloration on the caudal peduncle.
- **Behavior:** Spawning involved a female, one to three males, and substrate contact with vibration. Males competed in a "dominance hierarchy ritual" when more than three males attended a female; this occurred only when the male-to-female ratio was very high. Post-spawning, pairs picked up gravel in their mouths, a behavior seen only twice.
- **Eggs:** Nineteen eggs at various developmental stages were collected over three weeks. Eggs were nonadhesive and slightly negatively buoyant. Area I eggs were scattered and ranged from unfertilized to hatching, indicating extended use. On March 5, 1980, many one-to-three-day-old eggs were found clumped at Area II, but only one egg remained on March 6.
- **Larvae:** All larvae (2–9 mm) came from Area I at 10–15 feet. About one-third were free-floating, fungus-covered, and presumed dead.
- **Conclusion:** The population is successfully spawning and releasing viable gametes that reach the larval stage. Whether recruitment succeeds remains undetermined.
- **Planned follow-up (1981):** Tracking movements to spawning areas, quantifying spawning behavior, measuring environmental parameters (temperature, depth, light, particle size, pH, dissolved oxygen, conductivity, redox), protecting a spawning area with a raised screen, gut-content analysis of predators, and a reservoir survey for additional spawning sites.
