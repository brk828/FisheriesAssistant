# Species Composition by Age Groups and Stability of Fish Populations in Sections of Three Michigan Trout Streams During the Summer of 1937

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1938 study by David S. Shetter and Albert S. Hazzard, published in the [[Transactions of the American Fisheries Society]], used [[Block-and-Seine Census Method]] sampling to estimate fish populations in eight census sections across three Michigan trout streams from June to September 1937. Sections were sampled monthly, fish were counted, weighed, measured and fin-clipped for [[Mark-Recapture]] analysis, and trout scales were read for age. The method's efficiency was tested at 89.1 per cent in the most difficult section. Populations proved unstable from month to month, so the authors concluded that single or few counts are unreliable. Average depth of 14 inches or more was the only habitat characteristic linked to higher trout and fish density, and the age-group distribution in the North Branch of the Boardman was abnormal.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a basic data gap for [[Trout Stream Management]]. At the time, little was known about typical trout-stream population composition, variation among habitats, seasonal change, or the effects of angling and hatchery plantings. Earlier work included stream diversion surveys by Embody (1929) and the blocking-seine technique developed by [[Greeley]] and [[Bishop]] (1933), later refined by Moore et al. (1934) and [[Trippensee]] (1937) in New Hampshire.

The three study streams were all open to public fishing:
- South Branch of the Pine River (Alcona County): three sections
- Little Manistee River (Lake County): two sections
- North Branch of the Boardman River (Grand Traverse County): three sections

Sections were chosen at random within a variety of seinable habitats. Each was worked one day per month. Census results were expressed per acre and per mile, using plane-table maps at a scale of 1 inch = 10 feet and planimeter area measurements. Stream velocity was measured by timing a float over 30 feet. No artificial plantings occurred during the census period. Table 1 lists bottom composition (peat, muck, gravel, sand, clay), with depths of 9 to 17 inches.

Species recorded include brook, brown and rainbow trout, along with common sucker, muddler, blacknosed dace, creek chub, mud minnow, stickleback, Johnny darter, golden shiner, yellow perch, bluegill, bullhead, lamprey and two crayfish species (*Cambarus virilis*, *C. propinquus*). Fish were weighed live on a spring balance and fin-clipped with a different fin each month.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most productive sections had bottoms that were predominantly sand, which the authors described as relatively unproductive by former standards. Gravel-dominated sections were comparatively low in production. Shade and submerged cover were associated with the greatest numbers of young-of-year trout, though shade alone was not tested as a single factor.

Depth was the one habitat variable that correlated consistently with production. The three most productive sections (lower South Branch of the Pine, and upper and middle Little Manistee) averaged 14 to 18 inches deep, while the rest averaged 9 to 11 inches. Depth may therefore act as a limiting factor for [[Stream Carrying Capacity]].

Movement was substantial. Trout, suckers and muddlers showed high month-to-month losses, and minnows and coarse fish in some sections were not recaptured at all, suggesting replacement by new fish. The middle sections were at least partly repopulated within 30 days. Greeley (1933) reported that many young [[Rainbow Trout]] descend to [[Lake Michigan]] before reaching legal size, which would explain the migratory pattern seen in the Little Manistee.

## Key Findings

**Census efficiency**
- Block-and-seine efficiency was 89.1 per cent in the lower South Branch of the Pine section, the hardest to seine. Trout were considered fully captured (100 per cent). Trippensee (1937) estimated 90 to 100 per cent for relatively easy streams.
- A check seining after four hours recovered 16 fish (six blacknosed dace, six common suckers, four muddlers) totaling 9 grams. The regular seining took 131 fish.
- No correction was applied to density estimates, since trout error was judged negligible.

**Population density**
- Little Manistee was the most productive stream in pounds of fish and trout per acre and yielded the most legal trout per acre.
- South Branch of the Pine ranked second.
- North Branch of the Boardman was lowest in numbers, weight and trout per acre. One of its sections produced no legal trout at any time.
- Estimates are tentative because of variation between months, and no entire-stream totals were calculated.

**Stability of populations**
- Net monthly loss of marked trout ranged from 44 to 94 per cent (Table 11). Sucker and muddler losses ranged from 69 to 100 per cent (Tables 12 and 13).
- Authors concluded that one or two counts on short sections are not reliable for estimating stream populations.
- Repeated seining may have influenced fish movement, but the effect was not measured.

**Age structure**
- The North Branch of the Boardman sample was dominated almost entirely by age-group 0 brook and brown trout, which the authors termed abnormal.
- In the South Branch of the Pine and Little Manistee, older age groups were more numerous. The oldest trout were age-group III (fourth year of life).
- Rainbow trout dominated the Little Manistee despite nearly 50,000 brown trout having been planted there over the previous four years and no rainbows.
- Only 26.2 per cent of age-group I rainbows collected in August 1930 reached legal size (7 inches) in their second year, according to Greeley.

**Conclusions**
- Average depth of 14 inches or more was the only habitat characteristic definitely correlated with higher trout and fish populations.
- Further research is needed on other habitat factors, on what constitutes normal age-group distribution, and on the effects of angling and hatchery plantings.
