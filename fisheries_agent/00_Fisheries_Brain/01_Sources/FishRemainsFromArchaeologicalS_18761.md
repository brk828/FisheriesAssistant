# Fish Remains From Archaeological Sites at Irvine, Orange County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #zooarchaeology #archaeological-ichthyology #Triakididae #Serranidae #Bothidae

## Executive Summary

This zooarchaeological report documents 41 fish remains recovered from two [[Gabrielino]] archaeological sites (Ora-118A and Ora-119A, B) at [[Irvine]], [[Orange County]], [[California]], excavated in spring 1963. The collection represents seven edible fish species from six families, all consistent with exploitation of nearby [[Newport Bay]]. The species assemblage and absence of pelagic or deepwater taxa suggest shore-based hook-and-line fishing without watercraft. Sites are located on knolls bordering a former northeastern extension of [[Newport Bay]], approximately five miles from the [[Pacific Ocean]] at the 50-foot contour.

## Background & Description

The archaeological sites were excavated by Charles A. Schwartz and Carol Lytton of the [[University of California, Los Angeles]] during spring 1963. Sites Ora-118A and Ora-119A, B are situated within [[Gabrielino]] territory at approximately 33°39'N latitude, 117°51'W longitude, on or below the 50-foot contour on knolls bordering a former northeastern extension of [[Newport Bay]].

Of the 41 fish remains collected, 38 are identifiable to species level. The identified taxa include:

**[[Triakis semifasciata]]** ([[Leopard Shark]], Triakididae): 8 centra (1 complete, 7 incomplete) from sharks approximately 134 cm total length, perhaps 11 kg weight. Maximum recorded length 183 cm. One centrum has a central hole suggesting use as ornament. Good eating quality.

**[[Mustelus californicus]]** ([[Gray Smoothhound]], Triakididae): 1 complete centrum from shark approximately 65 cm total length. Maximum recorded length 107 cm. Edible species.

**[[Rhinobatos productus]]** ([[Shovelnose Guitarfish]], Rhinobatidae): 16 centra (6 complete, 10 incomplete) from rays up to at least 120 cm total length. Tail is edible. Maximum recorded length 156 cm, weight 18 kg.

**[[Myliobatis californica]]** ([[Bat Stingray]], Myliobatidae): 4 incomplete centra from rays of perhaps 10 kg weight. Widely utilized by California aborigines. Largest specimen on record was 95 kg female caught in [[Newport Bay]].

**[[Paralichthys californicus]]** ([[California Halibut]], Bothidae): 1 left premaxillary fragment, 1 incomplete haemal spine, 4 vertebrae (3 incomplete, 1 fragment) from fish 25-66 cm total length. Good food fish. Maximum recorded length 152 cm, weight 28 kg.

**[[Paralabrax maculatofasciatus]]** ([[Spotted Sand Bass]], Serranidae): 1 incomplete right posttemporal (18 mm length) from fish approximately 38 cm total length, 1 incomplete vertebra from fish approximately 28 cm total length. Good food fish. Maximum recorded length 46 cm.

**[[Porichthys myriaster]]** ([[Slim Midshipman]], Batrachoididae): 1 incomplete vertebra (5 mm length) from fish approximately 30 cm total length. Edible species. Maximum recorded length 38 cm. Characteristic form of muddy and sandy bays in southern California. Can be picked up by hand at extreme low tide.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

All seven species represented are known to occur in [[Newport Bay]] and are edible. The species assemblage reflects a bay/estuarine habitat rather than open ocean or deepwater environments. [[Newport Bay]] provided both the fish resources and abundant mollusk shells that could serve as bait.

The [[Bat Stingray]] ([[Myliobatis californica]]) is particularly associated with [[Newport Bay]], where the largest specimen on record (95 kg female) was caught. The [[Slim Midshipman]] ([[Porichthys myriaster]]) is characteristic of muddy and sandy bay habitats along the southern California coast.

At least five of the seven species have documented use by aboriginal peoples elsewhere in California: [[Leopard Shark]], [[Shovelnose Guitarfish]], [[Bat Stingray]], [[California Halibut]], and [[Slim Midshipman]].

The sites are located less than three miles from a moderately steep bank on the south shore of [[Newport Bay]] that would have provided favorable locations for shore-based fishing.

## Key Findings

**Species Distribution**: Seven species from six families were identified from 38 of 41 fish remains. Species representation varied slightly between sites: [[Porichthys myriaster]] only at Ora-118A; [[Mustelus californicus]] and [[Myliobatis californica]] only at Ora-119B. This difference is not considered significant as all species could have been caught throughout [[Newport Bay]].

**Fishing Technology**: The species assemblage strongly suggests hook-and-line fishing from shore. All species represented could readily be caught by simple hook and line cast from the shore of the bay. Abundant mollusk shells found among archaeological materials indicate available bait sources that would attract all represented fish species.

**Absence of Watercraft**: The complete absence of remains of pelagic or deepwater fishes, or any fishes not of common occurrence in [[Newport Bay]], suggests the [[Irvine]] people probably did not use watercraft for fishing. The assemblage is entirely consistent with shore-based exploitation.

**Fishing Locations**: A moderately steep bank on the south shore of [[Newport Bay]], less than three miles from the archaeological sites, would have been one of several favorable locations for hook-and-line fishing. All represented species could have been caught in most parts of [[Newport Bay]] or its former northeastern extension.

**Cultural Context**: The fish remains represent subsistence fishing by [[Gabrielino]] peoples exploiting nearby estuarine resources. One [[Leopard Shark]] centrum with a central hole suggests secondary use of fish remains as ornamental items (possible necklace component).

**Size Distribution**: Fish represented range from small individuals ([[Slim Midshipman]] ~30 cm, [[California Halibut]] 25-66 cm) to large specimens ([[Leopard Shark]] ~134 cm, [[Shovelnose Guitarfish]] up to 120 cm), indicating exploitation of diverse size classes within each species.
