# Sonoran Desert Native Fishes Status Report 1977

#ichthyology #desert-fishes #conservation #Cyprinidae #Poeciliidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 report to the [[Desert Fishes Council]] documents the status of native fish populations across the [[Sonoran Desert]] region during widespread drought conditions. While native fishes in natural streams are sustaining well despite lower-than-modal flows, populations are declining in areas affected by human activities. Critical concerns include threatened populations in the [[Rio Yaqui]] drainage, declining water levels from groundwater pumping, and the need for translocation efforts to establish refuge populations of endangered species like [[Xyrauchen texanus]] (razorback sucker).

## Background & Description

The report covers multiple desert fish species across Arizona, New Mexico, and northern Mexico. Key species of concern include [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis]] (Yaqui topminnow) and [[Gila purpurea]] (Yaqui chub) at [[San Bernardino Ranch]], [[Campostoma ornatum]] (Mexican stoneroller) in [[Rucker Canyon]], and [[Xyrauchen texanus]] (razorback sucker) in [[Lake Mohave]]. The [[San Bernardino Ranch]] (Texas John Slaughter Ranch) on [[San Bernardino Creek]] east of Douglas, Arizona, has been placed for sale, threatening critical habitat. Populations are maintained at [[Dexter Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico and have been introduced to [[Leslie Creek]] in the [[Swisshelm Mountains]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Widespread drought conditions have resulted in lower-than-modal flows in essentially all natural streams across the American Southwest. Groundwater pumping has increased rapidly in both the United States and Mexico, causing surface waters to decline rapidly. The [[Santa Clara Slough]] in Sonora, Mexico has increased dramatically in size due to increased canal water input and flooding by tidal bores from tropical storms extending upstream from the [[Sea of Cortez]]. Desalinization operations on the lower [[Colorado River]] mainstream are causing natural waters to dry. [[Aravaipa Creek]] native fishes remain in excellent condition despite low discharges, with [[Meda fulgida]] (spikedace) surprisingly being the most abundant species in many habitats.

## Key Findings

**Rio Yaqui Fauna Critical Status**: [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis]] and [[Gila purpurea]] persisted at [[San Bernardino Ranch]] in 1976-77, representing the only United States populations besides introduced populations in [[Leslie Creek]] and hatchery stocks at [[Dexter Fish Hatchery]]. Under hatchery conditions, the poeciliid has reproduced successfully; chub reproduction has not been verified. [[Catostomus bernardini]] and [[Notropis formosus mearnsi]] are almost certainly extinct in the United States.

**Razorback Sucker Age Structure**: Extensive age-and-growth analysis based on scales reveals that [[Xyrauchen texanus]] killed in 1974 aged between 17 and 23 years, with mean age of 21 years. The population of large individuals in [[Lake Mohave]] would have hatched about 1953, approximately two years after closure of [[Davis Dam]]. Little if any reproduction has occurred despite reproductively active adults being repeatedly taken and spawning activities observed. Adults comprise more than 50% of total fishes caught in nets and seen by divers.

**Translocation Recommendations**: Due to extended drought causing [[Roosevelt Lake]] to be nearly dry, adult razorback suckers should be transferred winter 1977-78 into the [[Salt River]] upstream from the reservoir to exploit the apparent tendency for fish to spawn in "newly created" waters when the reservoir fills. This pattern is obvious from [[Lake Mohave]] filling and strongly implied by data from [[Salt River]] Reservoirs.

**Gila Topminnow Conservation**: A Habitat Management Plan ([[Bureau of Land Management]]) for the [[Cocio Wash]] population of [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis]] has been accepted and will be expedited in 1977-78. [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] is maintaining stock and has introduced Gila topminnow in numerous places within its native range with variable success. Stock of [[Cyprinodon macularius]] (desert pupfish) from [[Santa Clara Slough]], Sonora was obtained and is being maintained jointly by Arizona Game and Fish and [[Arizona State University]] for anticipated stocking into parts of the [[Gila River]] drainage.

**Lower Colorado River Impacts**: Populations of [[Mugil cephalus]] (striped mullet) and [[Elops affinis]] (ladyfish) are doomed to extirpation with drying of natural waters resulting from desalinization operations.

**Ongoing Research**: Studies of [[Aravaipa Creek]] funded by [[Bureau of Land Management]] will be completed in 1978. Similar research on the [[Gila River]] in eastern Arizona has provided large amounts of comparative data on desert stream ecology and considerable insight on benthic invertebrates and other biotic components. Physical, chemical, and biological features of the mainstream [[Gila River]] near [[San Francisco River]] inflow are comparable to those of the [[Virgin River]] near Littlefield, Arizona, supporting proposed introduction of [[Plagopterus argentissimus]] (woundfin) in that area.

**Introduced Species Pressures**: [[Campostoma ornatum]] persists in [[Rucker Canyon]] under conditions of low discharge and pressures from repeatedly introduced [[Salmo gairdneri]] (rainbow trout) and [[Pimephales promelas]] (fathead minnow, established 1976).
