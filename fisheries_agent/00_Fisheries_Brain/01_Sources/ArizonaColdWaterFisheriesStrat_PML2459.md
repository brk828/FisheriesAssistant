# Arizona Cold Water Fisheries Strategic Plan 1985-1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department strategic plan for cold water (trout) fisheries covers 1985-1990. It was prepared by Richard L. Stephenson, Fisheries Planner, under Federal Aid Project FW-11-R. The plan compares the supply of trout habitat, stocked trout and angling opportunity against angler demand, and sets goals, objectives, management concepts, problems and strategies. Its base data are the 1981 inventories and angler surveys; the document describes itself as a dynamic plan subject to review and revision. Its central conclusion is that current supply roughly matches present demand, but projected 1990 demand is about 45% above the use supported by current programs, and stocking must increase to close that gap.

## Background & Description

The plan is organized around the Department's mission to develop, maintain and manage wildlife and habitat for present and future generations. Its legal authority rests on Arizona state statute, which vests responsibility for the state's wildlife resources in the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and its Commission. Federal excise taxes (Pittman-Robertson and Dingell-Johnson Acts) provide part of the funding.

The plan states five purposes: to critically evaluate management programs; to communicate the Department's position to the public, legislature and agencies; to ensure continuity across personnel changes; to inform the public of benefits, costs and problems; and to offer a formal channel for public comment. A comprehensive review is scheduled for the third year, with a new strategic plan every five years.

**History.** Only two trout species, the [[Apache Trout]] (Arizona trout) and [[Gila Trout]], were native to Arizona. Early settlers relied on them, but demand was believed to exceed natural supply by the early 1900s. The U.S. Bureau of Fisheries then introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (first, shortly before 1900, into Oak Creek), [[Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] by railroad car, with poor survival because deliveries arrived in August and fry were small. The first state hatchery opened on the South Fork of the Little Colorado River near Greer in 1922. Later additions included [[Brown Trout]] (1933), [[Arctic Grayling]] (1940), and sockeye and coho salmon (1950s and 1960s). Six hatchery stations now operate, down from fifteen earlier. The document notes that over 220,000 licensed trout anglers harvest over 3 million trout annually, make almost 1 million trips and contribute over $64 million annually to the state economy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat supply.** Arizona has 170 cold water streams (excluding the Colorado River) with a combined length of 1,552 miles (1,471 surface acres) managed primarily for trout, plus 599 miles (13,169 surface acres) managed primarily for warm water species. Primary trout streams are divided into 202 Stream Management Reaches. The Forest Service administers over half of these reaches. Trout lakes total 168 identified lakes, with 3,195 surface acres managed primarily for trout. About 35% of trout lakes lie on Indian Reservations.

**Natural reproduction.** In many streams natural reproduction is marginal and insufficient to sustain populations after floods, drought and other events. Consequently, trout fishing in Arizona remains dependent on hatcheries.

**Stocking.** Agency-stocked trout (AGFD plus USFWS) account for only about one-third of the combined total stocked, with private entities providing the rest, mostly for commercial fee fishing. Stocking did not keep pace with population growth: from 1955-81 the state population grew substantially while annual stocking increased by only a small proportion, so the relative supply per resident fell by about half from its 1960s level.

**Environmental threats.** The plan lists nutrient loading causing algal blooms, livestock grazing, timber harvest affecting shade and temperature, off-road vehicle damage, road construction, sand and gravel mining, mining pollution, dewatering by regulated flows, and deteriorating instream structures.

## Key Findings

**Supply and demand (1981 base year)**
- 1981 angler-day use on non-urban trout waters was about 1,326,000 (approximately 1.5 million, with 1981 use as the figure cited in Table 9), while the 1981 supply was estimated at about 2,081,500 angler-days in the comparison table.
- Projected 1990 demand: about 2.3 million angler-days, approximately 45% above the use then provided for. The plan's Table 8 gives a 1990 total of 2,028,500 angler-days excluding urban and private/reservation waters.
- Projected 1990 use, limited by supply, rises only about 17.9% over 1981 levels, to about 1.585 million angler-days.

**Management concept distribution**
- Intensive Use holds roughly 80% of combined lake and stream surface acreage managed primarily for trout; Basic Yield about 15%, with smaller shares for Blue Ribbon, Featured Species, Wildfish and Urban concepts.
- About 88% of projected 1990 demand is for Intensive Use and Basic Yield angling.
- Featured Species demand is estimated about 20,500 angler-days below potential supply, and Wildfish potential supply (about 98,000 angler-days) far exceeds estimated demand.

**Goals**
- Protect and enhance cold water habitats; conserve and provide public use of native trout; improve angling quality; diversify opportunities; increase supply by increasing hatchery-produced trout.

**Objectives (by 1990)**
- Increase the number of trout available for harvest from Department-stocked waters by 45% to 55%.
- Provide an additional 20,000 Blue Ribbon angler-days per year and 10,000 Featured Species angler-days per year.
- Add 300,000 Basic Yield angler-days per year.

**Key problems identified**
- Inadequate biological and creel survey information, and no centralized, computerized data repository.
- Hatchery facilities: several stations are inadequate, outdated or in disrepair; future trout egg sources are uncertain.
- Predation, poor water quality at stocking time, and poor distribution of stocked fish among anglers.
- Non-compliance with license and trout stamp rules, and sale of prohibited species by bait and pet dealers.

**Research needs identified**
- Survival and harvest rates for stocked trout in Intensive Use and Basic Yield waters; standardized creel methods; stocking equipment effectiveness; cost-effectiveness of aquatic weed control; user conflicts; nutrient budgets; and evaluation of law enforcement effectiveness.
