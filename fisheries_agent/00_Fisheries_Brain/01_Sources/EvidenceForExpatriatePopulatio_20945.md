# Evidence for Expatriate Populations of the Lanternfish *Myctophum punctatum* in the Northwest Atlantic

#ichthyology #marine-biology #myctophidae

## Executive Summary

Zurbrigg and Scott (1972) argue that [[Myctophum Punctatum]] populations in the northwest Atlantic are expatriate, meaning they are non-reproducing and maintained by individuals drifting in from elsewhere. The evidence is a catch dominated by very large adults, a near-absence of juveniles and postlarvae, ovaries containing only small oocytes, and irregular, sometimes regressive growth recorded in scales, otoliths and subopercular bones. The authors frame the species as physiologically stressed under marginal conditions and note that the apparent expatriation is consistent with findings for other mesopelagic fishes and zooplankton in the same region.

## Background & Description

*Myctophum punctatum* is a mesopelagic member of the [[Myctophidae]] (lanternfishes), a group that forms a significant link in North Atlantic food chains. The study cites predation by cod, pollock and swordfish as evidence of its ecological importance. Although the species is commonly caught off Canada's east coast, its ecology there was essentially unknown.

Sexual dimorphism is expressed through luminous caudal peduncle plates, which develop at 45–50 mm SL in northwest Atlantic fish (males 1–4 dorsal plates, mean 1.3; females 2–5 ventral plates, mean 2.9). Males average smaller than females. Revised meristic counts were reported for pectoral rays (13–15), upper gill rakers (6–8), anal rays (not less than 20), dorsal rays (usually 14) and branchiostegal rays (8–10, sometimes asymmetrical).

**Distribution:** Occurs over deep water near the continental shelf edge from Greenland and Labrador to Cape Hatteras, limited seaward by the Gulf Stream. Also recorded off Iceland, the Shetlands, Norway, Denmark, the Bay of Biscay, the Straits of Gibraltar, the Mediterranean, the Azores (resident), and south to Cape Blanc and the Canaries.

**Materials:** The study drew on roughly 2,000 specimens from multiple sources, including about 1,154 specimens from 100 otter-trawl collections (1946–1964), 381 specimens from Fisheries Research Board vessels (mostly 1966–1969), 773 specimens from the M/V Brandal Engel trawl (July 1968), 573 specimens from the Azores, Gibraltar and the Mediterranean (Atlantis II cruise 49), and smaller museum and loan collections. Over 2,500 specimens were examined for sexual plates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water masses:** A sharp transition between oceanic water masses east of Flemish Cap in July 1965 separated warm, saline eastern Atlantic water (carrying eastern faunal elements such as *Electrona rissoi*, *Gonostoma denudatum* and *Argyropelecus hemigymnus*) from cold Labrador water.
- **Faunal mixing:** Eastern-origin waters held 301 juveniles (30–47 mm SL) and 3 adults in eight tows, whereas Labrador waters held 429 adults over 64 mm SL and 1 juvenile in eight tows.
- **Circulation hypothesis:** The authors suggest a clockwise eastern Atlantic gyre (Worthington 1962) could carry mesopelagic fishes westward, though they note the two-gyre theory is not established as fact.
- **Parallel cases:** Expatriation has been reported for [[Lobianchia]] species (O'Day and Nafpaktitis 1967), possibly several [[Diaphus]] species, [[Benthosema Glaciale]] (Halliday 1970 provides only limited evidence of local spawning), the chaetognath described by Huntsman (1919), and the copepod [[Calanus Finmarchicus]] off Labrador (Grainger 1963).
- **Growth comparison:** The species grows considerably larger in the northwest Atlantic than in the Mediterranean, which the authors interpret as possibly vegetative growth.

## Key Findings

- **Non-reproduction:** Northwest Atlantic females have small, granular ovaries with oocytes of 0.05–0.10 mm and little or degenerated vitellogenesis. Ripe ovaries with 0.40–0.43 mm eggs were not found. Large, egg-filled ovaries (0.20–0.25 mm oocytes) occurred only in slope water east of the United States.
- **Size structure:** Specimens over 1,000 captured north of the Grand Banks were all over 60 mm SL (one exception at 46 mm). Juveniles (30–45 mm SL) occurred only sporadically, and no postlarvae were captured in any sample.
- **Reduced gonads:** Individuals over 50 mm SL lacking sexual plates, or with both dorsal and ventral plates, were rare (fewer than 10 in over 2,500 specimens) and had gonads reduced to minute threads of tissue.
- **Stress indicators:** Scales show irregular circuli, erosion and "checks," with occasional true annuli. All four Gulf of St. Lawrence specimens examined had partially decalcified subopercular bones.
- **Relative aging:** Otolith ring counts and scale checks agreed in 70 of 78 fish. Modes were 69 mm SL (range 64–74) for one ring and 78 mm (range 73–81) for two rings; five males (81–84 mm) and four females (88–94 mm) had three rings. The authors conclude relative aging is feasible, but absolute aging is not yet supported.
- **Supporting reasoning:** Myctophid spawning is usually protracted from winter into summer, and [[Isaacs-Kidd Midwater Trawl]] surveys with fine-mesh liners recorded no *M. punctatum* postlarvae, making sampling error unlikely.
- **Recruitment:** The scarcity of young fish implies at least some recruitment from the eastern Atlantic, where large reproducing populations occur.
- **Conclusion and call for further work:** The authors call for extensive mid-North Atlantic sampling and reliable aging techniques before the expatriation hypothesis can be firmly established. Sampling was incomplete from November to April.
