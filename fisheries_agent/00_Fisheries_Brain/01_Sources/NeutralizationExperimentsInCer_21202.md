# Neutralization Experiments in Certain Acid Ponds in New Jersey

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

Roland F. Smith, Fisheries Biologist with the [[New Jersey Division of Fish and Game]], reports on [[Liming]] (alkalization) experiments in acid lakes and farm ponds in New Jersey. Most waters on the [[Atlantic Coastal Plain]] are acidic because the underlying soils are low in calcium and poorly buffered. Acidity limits the fish that can survive, particularly [[Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]. Applications of [[Hydrated Lime]] and [[Limestone]], often with fertilizer, raised pH in several ponds. This allowed stocked fish to survive and, in some cases, spawn. The paper was presented at the Fiftieth Annual Convention of the [[National Lime Association]], Hot Springs, Virginia, May 8–10, 1952.

## Background & Description

- **Geological and soil context:** The northwest of New Jersey (Sussex and most of Warren and Morris Counties) is described as upwards of 500 million years old. The northeast and central regions are younger. The south Jersey area (Salem, Camden, Burlington, Monmouth, Ocean, Atlantic, Cumberland, and Cape May Counties) is said not to date back more than 40–50 million years. Soils are generally calcium-deficient and acidic, with the most acid region in the southern tier of the state.
- **Soil formation:** Older soils east of the [[Appalachian Mountains]] have lost carbonates, nitrates, and phosphates through leaching. Coastal soils are mainly sand (silica) and retain few nutrients.
- **Survey work:** Data came from the [[New Jersey Division of Fish and Game]] lake and pond survey over the past two summers, cooperative work with the [[Soil Conservation Service]] on farm fishponds, and correspondence with fisheries biologists from Delaware to Alabama.
- **Water classification:** Acid waters were grouped into three classes:
  - **Class I, [[Cedar Water Lakes]] and Ponds:** Drainage from cedar swamps or cranberry bogs. Acidity probably from tannic acids. pH rarely below 4.5. May produce good fishing in waters approaching neutrality.
  - **Class II, [[Clear-Water Impoundments]] in Sandy Basins:** Little swamp drainage, mostly spring-fed. Acidity probably from silicic and carbonic acids. Alkalinity extremely low. pH down to about 4.0. General productivity low.
  - **Class III, [[Mineral Acid Ponds]]:** Farm fishponds dug in soils high in iron, aluminum, and sulfates. pH down to 2.5. No fish present.
- **Historical and comparative practice:** Pond culturists in Germany have limed waters and bottom muds for probably several hundred years. In Wisconsin, [[Arthur Hasler]] added hydrated lime to small brown-colored kettle lakes. Lime precipitated colloidal matter (clearing the water and increasing light penetration) and oxidized bottom muds, releasing nutrients. Both changes increased productivity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Causes of acidity:** Waters reflect the chemistry of their soils and basins. Few dissolved solids are present, and carbonates are low, sometimes lower than laboratory distilled water. Poor buffering means a small acid input can lower pH substantially. Impoundments fed by heavily limed, cultivated land may have higher pH. Water pH is therefore an index of productivity and of likely fish species.
- **Acid sources:** Carbonic acid, silicic acid, tannic and humic acids (from [[Cranberry Bogs]] or cedar swamps), and mineral acids. Mineral acids are most prevalent below pH 4.0. Such waters are high in iron and aluminum, present as acid salts such as sulfates.
- **Biological tolerance:** Waters at pH 4.0–5.5 have their own fauna. Many south Jersey waters stocked for years with bass and bluegills had little or no success. The pH chart ("Relationship of pH of Lake and Pond Waters in New Jersey and Their Suitability for Game and Panfishes") indicates:
  - Below about pH 3.0–4.0: unfavorable to all fish species.
  - Around pH 4.0–5.0: pickerel and native species; bass and bluegill may reproduce, but growth is generally poor.
  - About pH 5.0–9.0: desirable range for most game and panfish.
  - Above about pH 8.0: highly alkaline, not common in New Jersey.

- Laurel and Union Lakes are extremes of cedar-water lakes in the same drainage basin. Union Lake is highly productive (bass, pickerel, bluegills, white and yellow perch). Laurel Lake has been stocked with all these species, but only pickerel reproduced successfully.
- Schauer Pond #2 had a heavy bloom of [[Euglena Mutabilis]], which accounts for its high volatile solids (202.9 in the table is listed for Schauer #2 as reported; see source for the full analysis).
- Schauer Pond had been fertilized at 100 lbs. per acre the year before analysis.

**Results of alkalization (as summarized by the author):**

1. In a limited number of ponds, a combination of hydrated lime and limestone gave the best results. Only high-calcium lime had been used to that point.
2. Mineral acid farm ponds (pH 3.0–8.5 as stated in the source), where no fish had survived, were alkalized and stocked with bass and bluegill fingerlings. These survived and were expected to produce fishing that summer.
3. Ponds at pH 4.0–4.5 with adult bluegills present and limited or failed spawning were alkalized. Spawning then succeeded.
4. Mineral acid farm ponds below pH 4.0 used for irrigation cause severe erosion of pipes and possibly pumps. Iron, aluminum, and sulfate accumulation in soil may harm crops. A nurseryman near Jamesburg nearly went out of business until he neutralized his pond.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**

1. Alkalization (with fertilization) of small acid ponds is practical and economical. Fish production appears good. One to two lime applications per year may be required.
2. Neutralizing extremely acid ponds used for irrigation appears necessary to protect crops and equipment.
3. Alkalization and fertilization of public waters should improve bass and bluegill fishing. The unique ecological niche of these waters should also restrain any "all out" neutralization program.
4. Further study is needed to determine the most suitable lime type for alkalizing acid waters and to refine current techniques.
