# Fishes of the Salton Sea and Atlantic–Pacific Hammer-head Comparison

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #sphyrnidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1916 issue of *Copeia* containing two short notes. The main note, by [[Barton Warren Evermann]], surveys the fishes of the [[Salton Sea]] in southern California, a lake formed by a flood breach that let [[Colorado River]] water enter the [[Salton Sink]]. It lists seven species known from the lake and nearby springs. The second note reports a morphological comparison of [[Hammer-head Shark]] specimens from the Atlantic and Pacific and concludes that they are essentially identical. Page 1 is a JSTOR notice about the Early Journal Content program and contains no scientific data.

## Background & Description

**Source context.** Page 1 is a JSTOR access notice describing the Early Journal Content collection, which includes journal material from the mid-seventeenth to early twentieth centuries. It has no scientific content. The remaining pages come from *Copeia*, No. 34 (New York, August 24, 1916), a journal "published to advance the Science of cold-blooded vertebrates."

**Salton Sea.** According to the author, the lake formed in 1905–1906 when a dike broke and Colorado River water flowed into the Salton Sink, an area below sea level. The break was later controlled, but the lake stayed large. Its depth varies within each year, greatest in winter and least in fall. The average depth in any year is slightly less than the year before. Seepage from [[Imperial Valley]] irrigation adds fresh water each year and keeps the lake fresher than it would otherwise be.

**Author's survey.** [[Barton Warren Evermann]] visited the lake from May 7 to 14, 1916. The fishes first arrived with the inflowing water, and early reports described them as very abundant. The author states that their former abundance and the full species list cannot now be determined.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water conditions:** The lake water is described as brackish, strongly alkaline, and very warm.
- **Desert springs:** [[Figtree John Spring]], a small oasis about 7 miles southwest of Mecca near the north end of the lake, was thickly grown with cattails (*Typha latifolia*) and covered with algae. The water was only a few inches deep and reached roughly 90–100°F on hot days.
- **Wider distribution:** The desert pupfish appears in many isolated springs and water holes across the deserts of southern California. [[Samuel Hubbard]] reported it as very abundant at Saratoga Springs, Death Valley.
- **Hammer-head specimens:** The comparison used specimens from Woods Hole, Massachusetts, and from Japan. Coloration differences were attributed to possible differences in preserving fluids.

## Key Findings

**Salton Sea fish fauna (seven species listed):**

1. **[[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*)**: The most abundant species. It probably escaped from private fish ponds in Arizona. Specimens at Cormorant Island weighed 3 to 10 pounds.
2. **[[Bony-tail]] (*Gila elegans*)**: Reported as uncommon. The author saw none.
3. **[[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen cypho*)**: Rather common. Several specimens had a starved appearance.
4. **[[Colorado River Trout]] (*Salmo pleuriticus*)**: Said to be fairly common and to thrive despite the brackish, alkaline, warm water. One specimen of about 16 inches was in excellent condition but somewhat bleached.
5. **[[Common Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*)**: Second in abundance after the carp. Numerous specimens were seen. Reported to reach 6 pounds and to be an excellent food fish.
6. **[[Desert Cyprinodont]] (*Cyprinodon maculatus*)**: Found at Figtree John Spring. The author caught several, brought two alive to San Francisco, and noted that one had died and one was still doing well. On June 1, Samuel Hubbard brought 14 specimens from Saratoga Springs, Death Valley.

**Hammer-head shark comparison:**

- Jordan and Fowler (1903) questioned whether the Pacific hammer-head usually called *Sphyrna zygaena* is identical to the Atlantic form.
- The author examined three specimens: one from Woods Hole, Massachusetts (60.5 cm), and two from Japan (51 and 56 cm).
- The specimens were "absolutely similar" in all diagnostic characters, including body proportions, fin shape and position, hammer form and size, and the position of the eye, mouth, and gill area.
- The only difference was coloration. The Woods Hole specimen was dark brown above, and the Japanese specimens were blackish gray. The author considered this unimportant because color varies across distant localities and may reflect preservation.
