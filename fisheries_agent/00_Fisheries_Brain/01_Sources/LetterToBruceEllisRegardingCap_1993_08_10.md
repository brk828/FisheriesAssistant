## Executive Summary
This 1993 letter from C.O. Minckley (Parker Fishery Resource Office, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) to Bruce Ellis (U.S. Bureau of Reclamation) outlines proposed Central Arizona Project (CAP) water enhancement actions to benefit native fish populations and riparian ecosystems along the Lower Colorado River and Gila River systems. The letter distinguishes between non-intervention management of introduced sport fisheries (allowed to persist via natural reproduction with biannual monitoring) and active, resource-intensive conservation efforts for federally listed native "big river" fishes—specifically the [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Key proposals include developing growout facilities on National Wildlife Refuges, redirecting CAP water allocations to support riparian restoration on the [[Bill Williams River]], and using Gila River Indian Tribe water allocations to restore instream flows below San Carlos Dam for native fish recovery (Gila topminnow, spikedace, loach minnow recovery plans). The correspondence emphasizes integrated, long-term, system-level conservation planning over piecemeal interventions.

## Background & Description
The letter does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of the fish species, but it does specify a target production size for growout stocking of native fishes:
- **Bonytail chub and razorback sucker growout targets**: individuals >12 inches reared in refuge facilities prior to release into the wild, intended to increase post-release survivorship by reducing vulnerability to predation compared to smaller stocked juveniles.

No further anatomical, meristic, or coloration data are given for any of the referenced species ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], [[Spikedace]], [[Woundfin]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Loach Minnow]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Big River Fishes**: The Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, bonytail, and humpback chub are identified as the primary large-bodied, listed native fishes of the mainstem Lower Colorado River, historically dependent on riverine and backwater habitats now heavily altered by damming (Davis Dam downstream) and impoundment (e.g., [[Lake Havasu]]).
- **Refuge Backwaters**: Isolated backwaters on [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] are proposed for development into growout ponds via dredging, serving as nursery/rearing habitat that mimics natural low-velocity backwater niches used historically by native big-river fishes.
- **Riparian Corridor Dependency**: The [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]] riparian corridor is highlighted as critical habitat requiring water for revegetation, supporting re-establishment efforts for native fish assemblages in that reach.
- **Smaller-bodied Native Fishes**: Little Colorado spinedace, spikedace, woundfin, topminnows, longfin dace, and Rio Yaqui fishes occupy smaller tributary and headwater stream niches distinct from the mainstem big-river guild, requiring separate management consideration by the Arizona Fishery Resource Office (AZFRO).
- **Gila River System**: Below San Carlos Dam, the river and its associated springs and cienega habitats support the Gila topminnow, spikedace, and loach minnow — species with completed but unimplemented federal recovery plans. Proposed flow augmentation via reallocated Gila River Indian Tribe CAP water would restore riparian corridor function and instream flow regimes.
- **Ecological Concern Areas**: The letter references the "Area of Ecological Concern" designation within the draft Lower Colorado River National Wildlife Refuge Comprehensive Management Plan, indicating a landscape-level conservation planning framework intended to integrate multiple taxa and habitat types (not fish-specific).

## Key Findings
- **No active sport fishery management** is planned on the refuges; introduced/naturalized sport fish populations will be left to natural reproduction with only biannual (or more frequent) monitoring of status and species composition — no supplemental stocking planned.
- **Active native fish management** is prioritized for bonytail chub and razorback sucker, including construction of growout facilities on all Lower Colorado River refuges (Lake Havasu, Cibola, Imperial), utilizing both existing isolated backwaters and newly dredged ponds.
- **CAP water enhancement** is proposed not for sport fish benefit, but specifically to:
  1. Expand/improve marginal growout facility sites for native fish.
  2. Support riparian revegetation on refuges and the Bill Williams River corridor (potentially via substituting CAP water delivery to Scottsdale in lieu of Planet Ranch aquifer pumping).
  3. Enable reallocation of Gila River Indian Tribe's CAP entitlement, freeing San Carlos Reservoir water for scheduled release to sustain Gila River riparian corridor and native fish populations downstream.
- **Recovery plans in limbo**: Gila topminnow, spikedace, and loach minnow recovery plans are noted as complete but not yet implemented — representing an identified opportunity for CAP-linked enhancement action.
- **Management philosophy**: Proposed actions are explicitly framed as needing integration across the broader biological community (not isolated single-species fixes), emphasizing whole-reach/habitat conservation (including springs and cienegas) over fragmented interventions, and commitment to long-term, biologically grounded monitoring programs to inform future adaptive management of native aquatic systems.
- **Cross-jurisdictional feasibility**: Native fish growout facility development is also considered feasible on Chemehuevi and Colorado River Indian Tribal lands, extending potential enhancement benefits beyond refuge boundaries.
