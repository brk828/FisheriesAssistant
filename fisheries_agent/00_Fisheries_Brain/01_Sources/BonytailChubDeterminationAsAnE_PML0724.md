# Endangered Listing and Experimental Reintroduction of Colorado Basin Cyprinids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document gathers US Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) regulatory material on native cyprinids of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It covers the 1980 final rule listing the [[Bonytail Chub]] as endangered, the 1985 rule designating reintroduced [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Woundfin]] as [[Nonessential Experimental Population]]s, a 1989 [[Biological Opinion]] on stocking Colorado squawfish below [[Headgate Rock Dam]], and several unrelated Pacific fishery closure notices. The central issue is decline of native fishes driven by impoundment, flow alteration, habitat loss, and competition and predation by introduced species. The bonytail was reduced to a single known adult population in [[Lake Mohave]].

## Background & Description

**Bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*).** Historically found throughout large, turbid mainstream rivers of the basin, in habitat alternating between swift, rapid-filled canyons and slow, meandering, sand-bottomed stretches. The 1980 rule lists it as endangered, with critical habitat not determined. Large adults have been recorded in [[Lake Mead]], Lake Mohave, and [[Lake Havasu]], and spawning was observed, but no young were found.

**Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*).** The largest cyprinid in North America and the top native carnivore of the Colorado River system. Maximum weights above 36 kg and lengths near 1.5 m have been recorded, though specimens above 7 kg have been rare recently. Juveniles become piscivorous at about 60 mm. Wild fish exceeding 30 years of age have been documented, and Dexter broodstock are up to 16 years old. The species tolerates a broad thermal range and wide chemical conditions.

**Woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*).** A native cyprinid of the Virgin River and historically the Gila, Verde, Salt, and San Francisco systems. It is held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico) and is naturally trapped below irrigation diversions in the Virgin River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat alteration:** Impoundments and diversions eliminated much of the bonytail's original habitat. Dams cut sediment and nutrient delivery, converted sand and silt channels to armored cobble, reduced backwaters, and replaced natural flood-drought cycles with steadier, regulated flows. Cold tailwaters below dams do not provide the roughly 65°F warmth bonytails need to spawn.
- **Thermal and flow effects:** Colder releases below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] were cited as unsuitable for bonytail. The most dramatic decline occurred in the Green River of Dinosaur National Monument during the 1960s. Upper-Ashley Forest Service comments noted similar temperature problems.
- **Introduced species:** Over 60 introduced fish species had established by the document's account, and exotic species outnumber natives in the basin. Predation on young chubs by [[Red Shiner]] and redside shiner was suggested, and competition with introduced species was called a probable cause of decline. Predation by bass, sunfish, and catfish on young fish was also considered probable.
- **Water quality:** A Tosco Corporation comparison of USGS data at Jensen, Utah (pre- and post-Flaming Gorge Dam) reported "no significant differences," but no analysis was provided. The Service judged that water quality had not changed enough to limit squawfish survival in the lower basin.
- **Surviving populations:** Colorado squawfish persist in the Green/Yampa, upper Colorado mainstream, and San Juan rivers. Woundfin are relatively stable in the Virgin River, although flows and irrigation vary.

## Key Findings

**1. Bonytail chub listing (Federal Register, April 23, 1980).** The FWS determined the bonytail is endangered, effective May 23, 1980, citing habitat loss, introduced predators and competitors, and reduced reproduction. No reproduction had been noted in ten years, and the most recent adult collection (1974) came from Pesolation Canyon, Green River, Utah. Lake Mohave was the only reservoir then supporting an adult population, so the Service expected that population to decline as its fish senesced.

**2. Public comment on the 1980 listing.** Commenters split along lines of water development. The Utah Environmental Coordinating Committee and others opposed the proposal. Arizona expressed strong reservations because of recreational fishery concerns. The Bureau of Reclamation argued that the Act should "allow some balance" against water-storage needs. The Colorado River Water Conservation District asked that the range table be corrected to show "Entire except Colorado" for the bonytail.

**3. Colorado squawfish and woundfin experimental rule (July 24, 1985).** The Service designated introduced populations in Arizona as nonessential experimental populations under Section 10(j) of the Endangered Species Act, treated as threatened. Sites include the Salt River (Roosevelt Dam to US Highway 60 bridge) and Verde River (Horseshoe Dam to Perkinsville) for squawfish. Woundfin sites include five reaches across the Verde, Gila, San Francisco, Tonto Creek, and Hassayampa rivers. The program is a 10-year reintroduction, with annual monitoring by seining.

**4. Stocking plan (1985 rule).** The first squawfish stocking could include as many as 100,000 individuals from Dexter NFH stock. The first woundfin stocking would include at least 5,000 individuals. Wild woundfin removal from the Virgin River is capped at 25,000 fish. Both species were treated as isolated from existing populations, with the nearest squawfish population more than 800 miles upstream.

**5. Opposition and responses (1985 rule).** The Arizona Department of Water Resources opposed reintroductions where water projects were planned. Phelps Dodge opposed the rule over possible future critical habitat designation and red shiner presence. The White Mountain Apache Tribe objected that state law did not apply on reservation lands; the rule was revised to cover tribal and state law. New Mexico opposed the proposal. The Salt River Project supported it. The FWS maintained that the nonessential designation minimized restrictions on agencies, industry, and landowners.

**6. Biological opinion on Headgate Rock to Imperial Dam (1989).** The Regional Director, Region 2, concluded that stocking juvenile Colorado squawfish into the mainstream between Headgate Rock and Imperial Dams (about 190 river miles, Arizona and California) is not likely to jeopardize the species and will promote its conservation. The Service expects about 70 percent of yearly reintroduced fingerlings to be taken within a year, mainly by predator fish. Reasonable and prudent measures include delaying authorized take until fish mature, angler education, and monitoring. Impacts on [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Bald Eagle]], and [[Sonoran Pronghorn]] were judged negligible.

**7. Broodstock and fish supply.** Dexter NFH maintains more than 400 Colorado squawfish, and the 22 fish used for one egg take produced more than 2 million eggs. No wild stock would be taken for the proposed reintroduction.

**8. Unrelated Federal Register notices (1985).** Two notices are in the compilation but do not concern freshwater fish. The first closed the non-Indian commercial coho fishery between Cape Alava and Leadbetter Point, Washington, after the 78,500-coho quota was reached by midnight on July 18, 1985. The second restricted widow rockfish fishing off Washington, Oregon, and California and revised the Sebastes complex management boundary.

**9. State clearinghouse review.** The FWS forwarded the 1985 rule to the Arizona State Clearinghouse for review. The review form asks agencies whether the project fits their goals, overlaps other responsibilities, or conflicts with their rules. No completed responses appear in the document.
