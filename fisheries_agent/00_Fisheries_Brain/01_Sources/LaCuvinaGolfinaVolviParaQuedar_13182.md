# Curvina Golfina and Gulf Fisheries: Voces del Mar, Vol. 2 No. 1 (Spring 1998)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This Spring 1998 issue of [[Voces del Mar]], the newsletter of the [[Biosphere Reserve Alto Golfo de California y Delta del Río Colorado]], focuses on the [[Gulf Corvina]] (*Curvina golfina*), a sciaenid whose fishery has recovered sharply after roughly three decades of scarcity. The lead article reviews its biology, migration, fishing history and the management risks of its boom-and-bust fishery. A second article announces a binational shark and ray research program, and a third introduces a participatory project with artisanal fishers. The editorial warns that the sudden return of the corvina is a repeat of the [[Totoaba]] collapse and calls for careful management.

## Background & Description

**Gulf Corvina (*Curvina golfina*)**
- Belongs to the family [[Sciaenidae]] together with the [[Chano]] and the totoaba, all abundant and valuable in the region.
- Described as likely endemic to the [[Gulf of California]], meaning it is not known from anywhere else.
- Known distribution runs from the La Paz region, Baja California Sur, and [[Bahía Kino]], Sonora, north to the mouth of the [[Colorado River]].
- Maximum recorded length is 80 cm.
- Biology and distribution resemble those of the totoaba, which is why the authors treat the two as a comparable case.
- Diet in the spawning area consists mainly of fish, shrimp and crabs.

**Migration and life cycle**
- Makes annual migrations to spawning and nursery grounds in the [[Upper Gulf of California]] and the [[Colorado River Delta]].
- Arrival in the Upper Gulf begins in October and continues until May.

**Sharks and rays (*Tiburones y mantas*)**
- Mexico's artisanal shark and ray fisheries supply more than 30,000 tonnes per year.
- More than 95% of the catch is consumed domestically as food.
- The fishery ranks seventh in Mexican production volume and Mexico is the sixth-largest shark producer worldwide, according to [[FAO]] statistics.
- Products include meat, fins, skins and livers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vulnerability of elasmobranchs.** The authors stress that sharks and rays have slow growth, late sexual maturity and low reproductive potential. These traits make populations fragile under intense, prolonged fishing. Similar fisheries in California and the North Sea did not withstand heavy exploitation. Mexican fisheries have not yet collapsed, although catches in Sonora, Sinaloa, Chiapas, Veracruz and Tamaulipas show slight declines.

**Corvina migration and reserve zoning.** Fishing concentrates on the spawning and nursery migration, so harvest falls heavily on the migrating population, especially inside the [[Core Zone]] of the reserve.

**Freshwater flow.** The article links the totoaba's historical decline to falling freshwater flow from the Colorado River, which altered spawning and nursery sites. Authors also cite the lack of fresh water as a possible near-term threat to the corvina fishery.

**Disappearance from the delta.** The corvina apparently stopped migrating to the delta from 1963, a gap of 20–30 years. The cause remains unknown. Fishers recalled that fish were gutted at sea, arriving on shore without guts or heads, a practice that may have affected stocks or records.

**Waste and social conflict.** Large volumes of fishery waste create health problems. Free access to fishing grounds has produced conflict among the three fishing communities (El Golfo de Santa Clara, San Felipe, and fishing camps on the Colorado River) over fishing rights and catch shares.

## Key Findings

**1. Historical fishery.** From 1917 to 1940 the corvina was a major fishery in the Upper Gulf alongside the totoaba. Production was exported mainly to California and also supported sport fishing.

**2. Recovery and boom.**
- Formal fishing resumed around 1992–1993, when about 30 tonnes were landed, mainly at [[Golfo de Santa Clara]].
- Catches rose steadily to nearly 3,000 tonnes in 1997.
- Landings came from [[Golfo de Santa Clara]], [[San Felipe]], the Colorado River, and a small amount at [[Puerto Peñasco]].
- Smaller catches also occur further south in Sonora, at Bahía Kino, at other times of year.
- The editorial notes that 1997 catches exceeded the highest recorded totoaba catches.

**3. Current gear and operations.**
- Fishers use monofilament gillnets with 5–6 mesh size (as given in the source) and an average length of 100–150 *brazas* (fathoms).
- Fish are gutted on shore and sold whole, gutted with head, or as fillets.
- Because it is a "zafra" (intensive seasonal) fishery, many fishers harvest large quantities in a short time.

**4. Seasonality and markets.**
- Fishing is concentrated in the spring tides from January to April.
- Early in the season (late January), fishing focuses on the southern Golfo de Santa Clara and shoals off Moreno and Chinero. In February–April it shifts to the Core Zone and the Colorado River channel.
- Demand peaks during Lent in cities including Mexicali, Ensenada, Hermosillo, Guadalajara and Mexico City, with a small share exported to the United States.

**5. Management concerns.**
- The authors judge the future of the fishery uncertain.
- Biological and fisheries information is insufficient, so no specific regulation exists beyond the reserve decree and management program protecting the Core Zone.
- The authors call on the totoaba's history as a guide for management, with the cooperation of all reserve fishers.

**6. Shark and ray research program.**
- The [[Instituto Nacional de la Pesca]] (INP) and the [[Universidad Autónoma de Baja California Sur]] (UABCS) are partnering with the [[Mote Marine Laboratory]] (MML) and [[Moss Landing Marine Laboratories]] (MLML) of the United States.
- Funded by the [[David and Lucille Packard Foundation]], the two-year project runs 1998–1999.
- Year-one activities: characterize major ports and fishing camps that land sharks and rays, interview fishers, cooperatives, permit holders and buyers, and conduct sampling to document species diversity.
- Year-two plan: establish continuous monitoring of catches at the main camps to measure the impact of artisanal fishing on populations.
- Results are intended to give [[SEMARNAP]] the scientific basis for sustainable use.

**7. Participatory fisher project.**
- The [[CEDO]] (Centro Intercultural de Estudios de Desiertos y Océanos) began the "Participación de Pescadores Artesanales" project a little over a year before this issue.
- Communities involved: Puerto Peñasco, El Golfo de Santa Clara and San Felipe.
- Goal: promote fisher participation in sustainable use so the Upper Gulf stays productive.
- First step: survey fishers' opinions, concerns and proposals, then organize responses by theme (for example, how many fishers say lack of Colorado River water affects fishing). The article is truncated at this point.

**8. Editorial message.** The director, Carlos Navarro S., urges researchers, fishers, buyers and distributors to treat the corvina as a fragile resource and avoid repeating the totoaba's "gold rush" collapse.
