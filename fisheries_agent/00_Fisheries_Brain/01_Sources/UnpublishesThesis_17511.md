# Systematic Revision of Notropis lutrensis and Notropis ornatus in Northern Mexico and the Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a doctoral-style systematic revision of the [[Notropis Lutrensis]] complex (a cyprinid minnow, commonly called the Red Shiner) and the related [[Notropis Ornatus]] (Ornate Shiner), based on material collected by the author in northeastern and northern Mexico and compared with museum collections from Texas, New Mexico and Arizona. The author recognizes **2 species and 10 subspecies (possibly 12) with 9 races**, compared with the 6 species and 2 subspecies in earlier classifications. Subspecies are arranged into two subspecies groups within *N. lutrensis* (the *lutrensis* and *formosus* groups), and *N. ornatus* is split into two subspecies. The author interprets the pattern of differentiation as the product of stream capture, drainage disruption and genetic drift in intermittent desert rivers, linked to Pleistocene drainage history of a former "Pluvial Rio Grande."

## Background & Description

The author's biographical sketch describes his training in Mexico (Escuela de Ciencias Biológicas, Monterrey; Universidad de Nuevo León) and his move to [[Tulane University]] in 1962 to pursue a PhD in ichthyology under [[Royal D. Suttkus]]. Scholarships and field grants from several Mexican and US institutions supported the work, and the acknowledgements credit specialists at Arizona State University, Cornell University and the Instituto Nacional de Investigaciones Biológico-Pesqueras for loaned material.

**Methods.** Counts and measurements followed [[Hubbs and Lagler Counting Standards]], with modifications for predorsal scales, lateral-line scales (paired small scales counted as one), pelvic and pectoral ray counts, and several body lengths. Usually 10 (occasionally 20) specimens per collection were measured; counts were taken on up to 100 specimens. Measurements are expressed in thousandths of standard length. Diagnostic characters were selected by statistical analysis, and results were displayed with the [[Hubbs and Hubbs Graphical Method]] and frequency distributions. Species and subspecies limits followed Gibbs (1957) criteria, with more weight on non-quantitative characters such as breeding colour, common origin and habits.

**Nomenclature.** The history of the group is complicated by the naming of local variants as full species, sometimes even from the same locality. The author reviews a long synonymy for each taxon and proposes new subspecies: *N. lutrensis meeki*, *N. lutrensis intermedius*, *N. lutrensis santaclarae*, *N. lutrensis tarahumarae*, and *N. ornatus septentrionalis*.

**Key morphological characters:**
- Pharyngeal teeth typically 4-4, well hooked.
- Anal rays modally 9 in the *lutrensis* group and modally 8 in the *formosus* group.
- Breast naked (incompletely scaled) in the *lutrensis* group; fully scaled in *formosus*.
- Head tubercles large in *lutrensis*, smaller in *formosus*.
- Breeding males blue with red or orange in *lutrensis*; blue with yellow in *formosus*, with *N. l. santamariae* all yellow.
- Dark chin stripe and scapular bar characteristic of *N. lutrensis*.
- In *N. ornatus*, body moderately compressed, dark bars along the sides (9 to 14), and head tubercles confined above the lips.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Both species occur in clear, flowing waters. [[Notropis Lutrensis Forlonensis]] was collected in swift water over riffles, which the author found cooler and better oxygenated than the deep pools described by Darnell (1962); Darnell's observation of selective feeding on insects was confirmed. *N. ornatus* inhabits pastwaters and riffles in clear streams, especially to the south, and is most often caught around rubble and small stones. It is mainly a headwater form.

**Hydrological setting.** Most of the study rivers are desert streams that are seasonal or dry almost completely in the dry season. The author argues that the few surviving populations are exposed to strong genetic drift, and that desert conditions impose hard selection, which may accelerate evolutionary change.

**Biogeography.** The author proposes a sequence of drainage events:
- During the late Pleistocene, a large "Pluvial Rio Grande" included the Nazas, Aguanaval, Carmen, Santa María, Casas Grandes and Mezquital headwaters, and tributaries of the Yaqui.
- Headwater capture by the Yaqui and Mezquital systems removed tributaries from the Rio Grande.
- Drying led to the formation of [[Pluvial Lake Samalayuca]] and later [[Pluvial Lake Mayrán]], which fragmented into the present seasonal lagoons.
- Stream capture by the [[Rio Conchos]] and [[Pecos River]] is considered likely, possibly during the Altithermal dry period.
- Southeastern *N. lutrensis* populations (Pánuco, Soto la Marina, Tamesí, San Fernando) probably reached their range by stream capture and gradual adaptation to tropical conditions.

**Environmental gradient.** Within the group, counts tend to be higher toward the northwest, in highland and arid areas, and lower toward the southeast, in lowland humid subtropical systems. Lateral-line and predorsal scale counts follow this pattern in the *N. ornatus* races as well.

## Key Findings

- **Revised taxonomy:** The author recognises 2 species and 10 subspecies (possibly 12) with 9 races, versus 6 species and 2 subspecies previously.
- **Two subspecies groups in *N. lutrensis*:** The *lutrensis* group (*lutrensis*, [[Notropis Lutrensis Forlonensis]], [[Notropis Lutrensis Meeki]], [[Notropis Lutrensis Garmani]]) is defined by modal 9 anal rays, large head tubercles, and breeding colours with blue and red or orange. The *formosus* group ([[Notropis Lutrensis Santaclarae]], [[Notropis Lutrensis Santamariae]], [[Notropis Lutrensis Formosus]], [[Notropis Lutrensis Mearnsi]], [[Notropis Lutrensis Tarahumarae]]) is defined by modal 8 anal rays, more even caudal peduncle tubercles, and blue and yellow breeding colours.
- **Intermediate forms:** *N. l. intermedius* from the Río Sáiz shares characters with both groups and is interpreted as of hybrid origin from *santaclarae* and *meeki* stocks, after stream capture by the Conchos and later isolation.
- ***N. l. forlonensis* is easily confused with juvenile *Notropis amabilis*:** The two are separated by the scapular bar, which is absent in *amabilis*, and by pharyngeal tooth counts. *Forlonensis* has a predorsal circumferential scale count commonly of 23 to 26.
- **Intergrades:** Intergrades between *N. l. lutrensis* and *N. l. forlonensis* are recognised in the Río Soto la Marina area, showing intermediate postorbital length, eye size and breast nakedness.
- ***N. ornatus* races:** Five races are recognised in the study (Yaqui, Upper Conchos, Lower Conchos, Nazas, Mezquital). Lateral-line counts range 30 to 38 among races, and predorsal circumferential scale counts 22 to 32. The Upper and Lower Conchos races are statistically more different than is normal for subspecies, and the author suggests further division may be appropriate.
- ***N. ornatus septentrionalis* (new):** Described from the Nazas and Mezquital drainages; differs from *ornatus* by a subhorizontal mouth, head pattern divided into three lobes, and fewer than 10 anal rays in most individuals.
- **Origin of *N. ornatus*:** Thought to have originated in the Lower Río Conchos and spread by stream capture, since it is a headwater form that cannot easily invade lowland drainages.
- **Caveats:** The author notes that his collections were not seasonally matched to those of Koehn, which may account for some differences in tubercle development. Some conclusions depend on small samples and remain provisional; the status of *N. l. blairii* and the Pecos-form relationships are unresolved.
- **Tabulated data:** Frequency tables for dorsal, anal, caudal peduncle and predorsal counts, plus morphometric ranges, are provided for each race (for example, mean anal ray counts of about 8.0 to 9.4 across *N. lutrensis* races). Some races have sample sizes ranging from very small to over 150 specimens.
- **Material base:** Specimens are deposited at Tulane University, the Escuela de Ciencias Biológicas (Monterrey), Arizona State University and the Instituto Nacional de Investigaciones Biológico-Pesqueras. The material examined comprises numerous collection lots from throughout the range.
