## Executive Summary

This 1976 study examines the diet of pelagic larval fish in [[Clear Lake (Iowa)]], a shallow eutrophic glacial lake, during May–June 1972. Researchers compared stomach contents of six co-occurring larval fish taxa — [[Walleye]] ([[Stizostedion vitreum vitreum]]), [[Yellow Perch]], [[Yellow Bass]] and [[White Bass]] (*Morone* spp.), [[Black Crappie]] and [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis* spp.), [[Spottail Shiner]], and [[Common Carp]] — against concurrent zooplankton samples from the lake. The study was motivated by a large 1972 walleye fry stocking event (~36,000 fry/hectare) that produced an unusually strong year class, prompting investigation into whether food availability and selective feeding contributed to this success. The central finding is that despite cohabiting the same pelagic environment, each larval fish species exhibited distinct food preferences, suggesting reduced interspecific competition through resource partitioning — a mechanism that may have supported the strong 1972 walleye year class.

## Background & Description

- **Sampling gear:** 0.5-m townet, 0.79-mm square mesh nylon, with 0.85-mm mesh collection cup; horizontal tows at surface and 1.5 m depth.
- **Walleye fry:** Stocked at ~7.5–8.0 mm total length; first began feeding at ~9 mm total length (some still retained yolk material at this size). By final sampling week (June 3–9), fry averaged 31.6 mm.
- **Yellow perch:** Sampled across a size range; nauplii-feeding dominant shortly after yolk sac absorption, shifting to adult copepods with growth.
- **Yellow/White Bass larvae:** Indistinguishable morphologically at 4–15 mm; later near-shore seining indicated yellow bass predominated (389:27 ratio). First appeared in townet samples at 4.0–7.2 mm.
- **Crappie larvae:** *Pomoxis annularis* and *P. nigromaculatus* indistinguishable at 5–11 mm; black crappie predominated in later samples (556:190). First collected May 19 at mean length 5.8 mm (range 5.3–6.0 mm); yolk sacs present in all specimens before May 28.
- **Spottail shiner:** Yolk-sac larvae with empty stomachs collected 4.5–7.0 mm (May 18–31).
- **Identification references:** May and Gasaway (1967), Norden (1961), Nelson (1968), Priegel (1967).
- Total length measured to nearest 0.5 mm via caliper ruler; digestive tracts washed in distilled water prior to dissection to prevent external contamination of gut content analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** [[Clear Lake (Iowa)]] is a shallow (max depth 5 m), eutrophic, glacially-formed lake spanning 1,474 hectares (7.7 km × 3.3 km) in north-central Iowa; supports 43 known fish species (Bailey and Harrison 1945).
- **Sampling zone:** Eastern portion of the lake, pelagic zone, both midday and night (2000–2200 hrs).
- **Trophic base:** Zooplankton community dominated by Cladocera (*Daphnia*, *Bosmina*, *Chydorus*, *Latona*, *Leptodora*, *Alona*) and Copepoda (*Cyclops*, *Diaptomus*, nauplii), with minor Rotifera (*Keratella*) and Chironomidae.
- **Niche partitioning:** Each species (except shiners) preferentially consumed a different zooplankton taxon or life stage:
  - [[Walleye]]: *Daphnia* (dominant), later shifting to *Latona*; adult *Cyclops* early on.
  - [[Yellow Perch]]: *Cyclops* and *Diaptomus*; heavy nauplii use post-yolk absorption.
  - [[Yellow Bass]] / [[White Bass]]: Copepod nauplii until ~7mm, then adult *Cyclops*.
  - [[Black Crappie]] / [[White Crappie]]: Copepod nauplii primary, then adult *Cyclops*.
  - [[Spottail Shiner]]: *Bosmina* (34% of diet by number), increasing use of nauplii and *Alona* with growth.
  - [[Common Carp]]: *Bosmina* and *Alona* heavily; *Cyclops* and *Daphnia* minor; nauplii rare.
- **Ontogenetic shift:** Walleye moved from pelagic to littoral feeding by May 31, evidenced by ingestion of *Latona* (a littoral-dwelling cladoceran, per Pennak 1953) absent from pelagic zooplankton samples — corroborated by near-shore seine catches.
- **Temporal resource succession:** Earlier-hatching species exploited an organism/life stage before shifting diets as later-hatching species began exploiting the same resource (e.g., walleye shifted from *Cyclops* to *Latona* as *Cyclops* became abundant in other species' diets).

## Key Findings

- Of 135 walleye stomachs examined in week one, 28 were empty; all walleye collected afterward contained food. Cladocera and Copepoda comprised 95% of food items across 424 walleye stomachs examined.
- **Species-specific first foods** contrasted with other studied lakes: Clear Lake walleye fry fed initially on larger zooplankton (*Daphnia*, adult copepods) rather than rotifers/nauplii as reported in Minnesota rearing ponds ([[Smith and Moyle 1943]]) or Little Cutfoot Sioux Lakes ([[Johnson 1969]]). No diatoms were found in Clear Lake walleye stomachs, unlike [[Lake Erie]] populations (Hohn 1966; Paulus 1969), indicating access to a different pelagic food complex.
- **Piscivory in walleye fry was limited**: even at 32 mm average length (week 4) with abundant small forage fish present, fish prey remained under 8% of diet items by number — consistent with findings from Red Lakes, Minnesota and Oneida Lake, NY, where walleye did not shift substantially to piscivory until ~50 mm.
- **Selective feeding hypothesis**: Even among same-sized larvae collected in week one, walleye selected adult *Cyclops* while yellow perch selected nauplii — direct evidence of resource partitioning at first feeding.
- **Management implication**: The authors propose that food selectivity among co-occurring larval fish minimized competitive overlap, potentially contributing to the unusually successful 1972 [[Walleye]] year class in Clear Lake, coinciding with an exceptionally large fry stocking event (~36,000 fry/hectare).
- Chironomid larvae/pupae, though numerically minor (<8% of diet items), were disproportionately significant in dietary contribution due to their larger body size relative to zooplankton.
- Coefficient of variation for *Daphnia* zooplankton sampling method ranged from 23–72%, indicating notable sampling variability in prey abundance estimates.
