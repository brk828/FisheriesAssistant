# Secondary Production and Decomposition in Lake Ecosystems

#limnology #aquatic-ecology #zooplankton #daphnia

## Executive Summary

This is a review by W. T. Edmondson (Washington, USA), prepared for the Fourteenth Limnological Congress (Verhandlungen Internationale Vereinigung für Theoretische und Angewandte Limnologie, Stuttgart, 1961), on secondary production and decomposition in lakes. Using the [[Daphnia Pulex]] energy budget worked out by [[Richman]] (1958) as a model, the review examines consumption, assimilation, growth and reproduction, egestion and excretion, and the return of nutrients from zooplankton and sediments to [[Phytoplankton]]. It also addresses [[Thermal Stratification]], [[Turnover]] concepts, the relation between productivity and standing crop, allochthonous organic inputs, and the benthos. The author concludes that many lake processes are controlled by several interacting factors and that field and laboratory work must proceed in parallel.

## Background & Description

The review covers consumers and their relations to primary producers, with emphasis on planktonic organisms (mainly Cladocera and copepods, with rotifers and benthos mentioned). The author sets out four stages of the consumer energy pathway: consumption of food, assimilation, growth and reproduction, and egestion and excretion. It is framed as an outline of recent advances and of unanswered problems rather than an exhaustive literature review. Russian literature and Congress papers are referenced only selectively.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Feeding and filtering:** [[Daphnia Pulex]] filtered 0.90 to 5.15 ml per day (0.7 to 1.8 mm animals) at 20 °C. Filtering rate was independent of food density between 25,000 and 100,000 *Chlamydomonas* cells/ml, so food intake tracked food standing crop. Some cladocerans (*Diaphanosoma*, *Simocephalus*, partly *Bosmina*) reduce filtering at high cell densities, so this is not universal.
- **Assimilation and egestion:** Only a fraction of food was assimilated, from 31% at the lowest food concentration to 14% at the highest. Proportionally more food is egested when food is abundant. Phosphorus assimilation by *Daphnia* ranged from 8% to 25%, compared with 75–98% reported for marine [[Calanus Finmarchicus]].
- **Growth and reproduction:** Adult size rose with food density (about 1.9 to 2.4 mm), and lifetime offspring increased roughly threefold across the food range. In the first six days, 55–59% of assimilated energy went to growth. Over 40 days, 60–73% of assimilated energy went to growth and reproduction, and 95–99% of that went to reproduction.
- **Population predation:** In [[Slobodkin]]'s cultures, yield to predators rose with predation rate up to 90%, the highest rate tested. Energy transfer efficiency ranged from about 1% to 9%. In laboratory [[Guppy]] populations (Silliman and Gurney 1958), maximum yield occurred at about 35% fishing rate; the population could not sustain a 75% rate.
- **Nutrient release:** [[Daphnia Pulex]] could release up to 0.014 mg CO₂ per day. Freshwater calanoids released about 0.002–0.006 mg per day. [[Daphnia Rosea]] in Hall Lake released 0.022 µg phosphate-P per individual per day at 15 °C. [[Chaoborus]] phosphorus release ranged from 30 µg P/mg dry weight at 10 °C to 125 at 20 °C.
- **Vertical migration:** In Hall Lake, 90% of Daphnia occupied the top 4 m by day but only the top 1 m at midnight, concentrating grazers and excretion in the trophogenic zone.
- **Sediments:** Tuomas (1955) found 11–14% of sedimented P and 44–69% of N were released from sediments over a year in three Swiss lakes. Radiophosphorus studies showed most exchange occurs within about 1 mm of the surface, with negligible release from below 12 mm. [[Lake Washington]] showed a steep phosphorus gradient in its cores that is hard to reconcile with routine mixing of 1–2 cm of sediment.
- **Stratification:** In [[Hall Lake]], a meromictic seepage lake near Seattle, a dense [[Oscillatoria Agardhii]] layer formed at 4 m. The monimolimnion held over 0.800 mg/l P, and fall mixing raised surface phosphate to 0.010 mg/l. Lake Washington stopped mixing in February 1958, allowing phosphate to accumulate in deep water before March mixing.
- **Allochthonous inputs:** Terrestrial leaves and imported organic matter may be important, and phytoplankton production accounts for only part of the CO₂ released in [[Windermere]]. Western red cedar (*Thuja plicata*) logs soaking in ponds were associated with a poor planktonic flora, suggesting toxic substances from wood.

## Key Findings

1. **Assimilation is not gross production.** Assimilation by consumers is the energy available for their work, and it differs fundamentally from primary gross production. Budgets must not confuse food consumed with food assimilated.
2. **Food supply sets consumer performance.** Daphnia's feeding is roughly proportional to food standing crop, with adult size and reproduction increasing with food density.
3. **Grazing reduces algae but can increase total production.** Net grazing lowers algal crop, yet CO₂ and nutrients released by grazers can permit new growth and greater long-run turnover.
4. **Vertical migration can move nutrients upward.** Concentration of zooplankton at the surface at night may intensify nutrient release in the trophogenic zone.
5. **Stratification and meromixis control nutrient availability.** Phosphate stored in the monimolimnion can fertilize the surface waters, which may partly explain high productivity in Hall Lake.
6. **Turnover is not one number.** Demographic turnover, biochemical turnover and isotope turnover differ. Isotope turnover can reflect exchange reactions rather than metabolic activity, and turnover times of minutes to weeks were reported for phosphorus.
7. **Standing crop and productivity are distinct.** Lakes of higher productivity tend to have larger standing crops, but the relationship is loose. Fertilization of Bare Lake raised photosynthesis more than algal standing crop.
8. **Research gaps remain.** Key needs include data on copepod and rotifer feeding, the mechanism of the Gause competition effect, the role of bacteria in phosphorus turnover, the fate of nutrients in feces, and the contribution of allochthonous material.
9. **Parallel field and laboratory work is essential.** Laboratory experiments must be designed for specific ecological questions, and field studies must be guided by known knowledge gaps.
