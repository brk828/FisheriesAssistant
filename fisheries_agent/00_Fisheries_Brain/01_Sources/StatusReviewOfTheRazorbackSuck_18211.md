# Status Review of the Razorback Sucker, *Xyrauchen texanus*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 status review, prepared by Kevin R. Bestgen of the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], compiles historic and present information on the [[Razorback Sucker]], an endemic catostomid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species was once widespread from Wyoming to Mexico but is now greatly reduced, with its largest remaining concentration in [[Lake Mohave]]. The review documents distribution and status, summarizes life history, assesses causes of decline, outlines current research and management under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]], and offers research and management recommendations. Its main conclusion is that stocking hatchery fish alone will not recover the species; controlled experiments are needed to identify the limiting factors, chiefly predation on early life stages and loss of seasonally flooded off-channel habitat.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was described in 1860 as *Catostomus texanus* and placed in the monotypic genus *Xyrauchen* by Eigenmann and Kirsch. It is recognized by an abruptly rising bony dorsal keel behind the head, which gives the species its common name. Key morphological data reported include:

- Primary dorsal fin rays usually 14-15 (range 12-17); primary anal rays 7.
- Total vertebrae 45-47; lateral scale series 68-87; first-arch gill rakers 44-50.
- Adults reach 1 m TL and 5-6 kg, but are more commonly 400-700 mm TL and under 3 kg.
- Breeding males are dark dorsally and bright yellow to orange ventrally and laterally, with more pronounced tubercles than females. Females are generally longer and heavier.

The species has been confused with hybrids. The "Uncompahgre humpback sucker" was shown by Hubbs and Miller (1953) to be a [[Hybridization]] product of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and razorback sucker. Eastman (1977, 1980) described a heavily ossified caudal skeleton, interpreted as an adaptation to strong river currents.

Historically the species was a food fish for aboriginal peoples and was commercially harvested into the 1940s and 1950s, with a fishery in Saguaro Lake as late as 1949.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and status.** The Colorado River Compact of 1922 divided the basin at Lee Ferry, Arizona, into the Lower (LCRB) and Upper (UCRB) Colorado River Basins.

- **LCRB:** Razorback suckers now persist mainly in man-made lentic habitats, including [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Havasu]] and Senator Wash Reservoir. Lake Mohave holds the largest population, estimated at about 60,000 fish (range 15,000-120,000) in spring 1989. Reservoir populations are small and declining, with little recruitment to older age classes. In the Gila River drainage, the last substantiated collection of young fish was in 1926.
- **UCRB:** The species is regularly found only in the upper [[Green River]] (Utah) and lower [[Yampa River]] (Colorado). Lanigan and Tyus (1989) estimated 948 fish (95% CI 758-1,138) in the upper Green River. Small concentrations also occur in the San Juan and Colorado arms of [[Lake Powell]]. The Colorado River sub-basin near [[Grand Junction]], Colorado, once held a large population that has declined sharply; only one adult was taken from the Colorado River in Colorado in 1986-1990 monitoring.

**Habitat.** Historically, the species used mainstream runs, backwaters, sloughs and off-channel habitats. Larvae in Lake Mohave concentrate in shallow water (0.3-1.0 m), and spawning occurs over wave-sorted gravel and cobble at 0.3-2 m depth. Optimal summer water temperature was 22-25 C (Bulkley and Pimentel 1983). Spawning in the Green River typically occurs at 14-16 C, and in Lake Mohave peak spawning occurs at 10-12 C.

**Threats.** The review identifies habitat loss from mainstream dams, flow regulation, channelization, loss of over-bank flooding, cold-water releases and non-native species as causes of decline. Over 50 non-native fish species have been introduced into the basin. Predation by [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] is considered a major factor limiting recruitment.

## Key Findings

- **Recruitment failure is the central problem.** Large numbers of larvae are produced in Lake Mohave, but juveniles have been extremely rare since the early 1950s. Predation on larvae was judged the factor most likely limiting recruitment, and predator-free cages and backwaters showed better larval growth and survival.
- **Longevity and age structure.** Otolith analysis of Lake Mohave fish (1981-1983, n=70) gave ages of 24 to 44 years, and 89% of these fish hatched before or during the construction of Lake Mohave. Green River fish were estimated at 12 to 26 years.
- **Slow adult growth.** Growth of wild adults is very slow in the UCRB (average 2.2 mm/year in Tyus 1987). Mean length has been rising since 1974 (2-3 mm/yr), suggesting an aging population.
- **Spawning and movement.** Spawning occurs in shallow gravel areas, with males outnumbering females (1.2-3.6:1 in Lake Mohave; about 2.5:1 in the Green River). Upper Green River fish showed a mostly sedentary population with migratory components. Of 88 fish, 66 remained local, and 19 migratory fish moved 53-190 km.
- **Fecundity.** Mean fecundity was 46,740 ova (range 27,614-76,576) for 10 Green River fish, and total fecundity was about 100,800 (74,600-144,000) for five Lake Mohave fish.
- **Hybridization.** Hybrids with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] are common across the sympatric range. Holden (1973) found 40 hybrids and 53 pure adults in the UCRB, and some low-keeled females may have been misidentified as hybrids.
- **Stocking results are poor.** Only 118 fish were recovered from more than 12,000,000 stocked in the Gila River, and most were recovered shortly after release. Predation on stocked fish was substantial.
- **Brood stock and propagation.** As of the report, 18,635 larvae had been produced in four years since 1987 at the [[Colorado River Fishes Propagation and Experiment Station]] in Ouray, Utah. About 2,000 juveniles were stocked in the Green River in 1988 and about 5,000 in 1990.
- **Recommendations.** The report calls for: (1) resolving genetic diversity; (2) resolving causes of recruitment failure through controlled experiments; (3) defining movements and habitat use; (4) collecting basic life history data; (5) developing brood stock and refugia populations; (6) refining reintroduction strategies; (7) identifying predator-free reintroduction sites; and (8) continuing monitoring and surveys for remnant populations, including the Gunnison River.
