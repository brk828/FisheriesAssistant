# Fish Community Structure and Stability in Warmwater Midwestern Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-community #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary
This synthesis by [[William J. Matthews]] draws on nearly two decades (1969–1986) of long-term fish assemblage data from three midwestern U.S. streams — [[Piney Creek]] (Izard County, Arkansas), [[Brier Creek]] (Marshall County, Oklahoma), and the [[Kiamichi River]] (southeastern Oklahoma) — to establish baseline expectations for natural variability in stream fish communities. The central goal is to provide impact-assessment practitioners with a reference for distinguishing anthropogenic disturbance from natural community dynamics. The work demonstrates that whole-stream fish faunas in these systems are remarkably persistent and stable over time, even through extreme disturbance events such as a 100-year flood, while local site-level assemblages show more variability tied to identifiable disturbance events (flood debris deposition, drought-induced intermittency). The paper also offers a key methodological recommendation regarding minimum sampling reach length for accurate community characterization.

## Background & Description
This paper is a community-ecology synthesis rather than a species-level morphological study; no individual taxon morphology is described. The relevant "structural" characteristics addressed are at the level of **community/assemblage structure** rather than organismal anatomy:
- **Species composition** (presence/absence) — used as a persistence metric.
- **Relative abundance structure** — used as a stability metric via [[Morisita's Index]] and percent similarity calculations.
- **Rank-order abundance** — assessed via statistical rank correlation across years.

No morphological, meristic, or physiological trait data for individual fish species are provided in this excerpt.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat types studied**: Warmwater streams of the Ozark uplands ([[Piney Creek]]) and Oklahoma prairie/plains streams ([[Brier Creek]], [[Kiamichi River]]), spanning headwater to mainstem reaches.
- **Environmental stability gradient**: [[Piney Creek]], located in the more environmentally stable [[Ozark Uplands]], exhibited the greatest degree of long-term fish population stability among the three systems — suggesting a link between physical habitat/environmental stability and biological community stability.
- **Headwater dynamics**: Intermittent headwater reaches present harsh, low-oxygen conditions during low-flow/drought periods. Recolonization of these reaches after die-offs is **not random**; species' ability to persist is tied to physiological tolerance of low dissolved oxygen (per [[Matthews 1987]] physicochemical tolerance study). This reveals both a **stochastic (chance) component** and a **deterministic (trait-based) component** governing headwater assemblage composition.
- **Disturbance regime**: Key natural disturbance agents affecting local assemblage composition include:
  - Flood-driven deposition of large woody debris (habitat alteration)
  - Drought-induced fish kills in intermittent headwaters
  - Catastrophic flooding (documented 100-year flood event in Piney Creek, with water level rises up to 12 m and extreme physical habitat destruction)
- **Post-disturbance recovery**: Despite the catastrophic Piney Creek flood, both whole-stream fauna and most local assemblages became statistically indistinguishable from pre-flood composition within **8 months**, indicating strong ecological resilience and rapid return to equilibrium — consistent with independent findings from other midwestern systems ([[Gelwick 1990]]; [[Harvey 1987]]).

## Key Findings
- **Whole-stream faunal stability**: Across all three streams (1969–86 for Brier Creek, 1972–86 for Piney Creek, 1981–86 for Kiamichi River), no common species was lost and no rare species became common in any stream over the study period — indicating high **persistence**.
- **Quantitative stability metrics**: [[Morisita's Index]], percent similarity indices, and rank correlation of species abundances all confirmed stability at the whole-stream level in all three systems.
- **Environmental stability ↔ biological stability link**: The most environmentally stable stream (Piney Creek) showed the highest degree of fish population stability, supporting a general relationship between habitat/environmental constancy and community stability.
- **Local site-level variability**: Individual collection sites showed more variation than whole-stream data; observed shifts were generally attributable to specific, identifiable disturbances (woody debris deposition, drought-related mortality) rather than random turnover.
- **Headwater recolonization is trait-mediated**: Persistence in harsh headwater conditions correlates with physiological tolerance to low oxygen, per [[Matthews 1987]] — combining chance and deterministic (niche-based) processes.
- **Flood resilience**: A 100-year flood event on Piney Creek caused immediate compositional shifts, but the fish community (both whole-stream and local assemblages) recovered to pre-flood statistical composition within 8 months, reinforcing that flood effects on midwestern fish communities are typically **transient**.
- **Methodological recommendation — sampling reach length**: A comparison of adjacent 100-m stream segments (5 sites, 10 segments total) in Brier Creek (1988) revealed only 60–70% percent similarity in fish assemblage composition between adjacent 100-m sections. This indicates that a standard 100-m sampling reach (common in many protocols) is likely **insufficient** to characterize the full fish community; the author recommends sampling **at least 200–400 m** of stream reach for both impact assessment and long-term ecological monitoring in midwestern warmwater streams.
- **Broader implication for impact assessment**: These long-term data sets provide a natural-variability baseline against which anthropogenic (cultural) impacts on stream fish communities can be more reliably distinguished from natural fluctuation.
