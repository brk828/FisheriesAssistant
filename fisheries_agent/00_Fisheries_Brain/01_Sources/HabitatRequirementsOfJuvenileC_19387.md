# Habitat Requirements of Juvenile Colorado River Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, prepared by Paul B. Holden of [[BIO/WEST Inc]] under the Federal Interagency Energy/Environmental Research and Development Program, examines the habitat use and movements of rare fishes in the [[Green River]] between Jensen and Green River, Utah, during August and September 1977. Its main focus is [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), with supporting data on [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The study concludes that young squawfish strongly prefer slow, silt-bottomed [[Backwaters]], that these habitats likely supply food for young fish, and that backwater availability may limit rare fish recruitment. Spawning was inferred to have occurred mainly in [[Gray Canyon]] in 1977. The authors caution that low 1977 flows and limited sampling reduced the reliability of several habitat estimates.

## Background & Description

The upper Colorado River basin contains two federally endangered fishes, the Colorado squawfish and the humpback chub. The bonytail chub and razorback sucker were rare and under consideration for endangered and threatened status, respectively. The report states that the central research need was determining these species' requirements for subsistence, because two factors largely control self-sustaining populations: adequate spawning and rearing habitat, and interaction with exotic fishes.

The study had five objectives:
1. Determine the physical and chemical parameters of habitats used by all sizes of rare fishes, with emphasis on young fish.
2. Determine whether spawning occurs in the study area and, if so, where.
3. Determine the movements of size (age) classes of rare fishes.
4. Determine the fish species associated with the study area.
5. Assess the relative importance of river habitats to rare fishes.

Sampling was done by boat from Jensen to Sand Wash (August 5–20, 1977) and by rubber raft from Sand Wash to Green River, including [[Desolation Canyon]] and Gray Canyon (September 7–14 and 22–26, 1977). Fish were collected mainly by [[Seine Sampling]], with [[Electrofishing]] and a fry shovel also used. Habitat variables (velocity, depth, temperature, substrate) were recorded at each site, and results were expressed as [[Electivity Curves]] following the [[Instream Flow Methodology]] of the Cooperative Instream Flow Service Group. Rare fish of over 100 mm total length were tagged, and smaller juveniles were marked by anal fin clipping.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish community.** Nineteen species were collected, totaling over 19,500 specimens. The introduced [[Red Shiner]] was by far the most abundant (13,711), followed by [[Fathead Minnow]] (1,951) and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (918, the most abundant native sucker). Other common species included [[Carp]] (388), [[Channel Catfish]] (947) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (678). Rare species counts were: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (202), [[Humpback Chub]] (91), [[Razorback Sucker]] (8) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (2). Introduced species such as [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Green Sunfish]] were also recorded. Over 330 samples were taken: 125 in runs, 121 in backwaters, 59 in eddies and 19 in riffles.

**Temperature.** Early morning temperatures were usually 3–5 °C cooler than late afternoon. Backwaters were the warmest habitats, often 3–8 degrees above the main river in late afternoon. Morning temperatures at Jensen in August were 15–17 °C, and downstream in August they were generally 19–21 °C. In September, Desolation Canyon reached a low of 10.5 °C.

**Flow regime.** Releases from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] cause daily level changes. Jensen showed a 16-inch daily rise and fall, while Desolation Canyon fluctuated only 3–4 inches. Flows in 1977 appeared lower than normal, and the Yampa River was described as exceptionally low.

**Water chemistry.** Chemistry changed little during the study and stayed within normal expected ranges. The authors note that fish cannot choose among chemical conditions because all habitats were chemically similar. Turbidity peaked after rainstorms, and native fishes appear adapted to sudden turbidity changes linked to flow increases.

**Threats.** The report identifies competition and predation from exotic species, and notes that [[Smallmouth Bass]] adds a potential predator, though turbidity and river conditions were expected to limit its reproduction.

## Key Findings

**Habitat preferences**
- Young-of-the-year squawfish (35–64 mm) were found only in backwaters with no current, a firm silt bottom and a depth of one to two feet.
- Juvenile squawfish (65–200 mm) also preferred backwaters with no current and silt bottoms, but were less selective about depth.
- Subadult and adult squawfish (200+ mm) used a range of velocities and depths, preferring sandy-bottomed eddies and runs over backwaters.
- Young-of-the-year razorbacks (six collected) preferred eddies with about 0.2 fps velocity, 1.0 foot depth and soft silt.
- Young-of-the-year humpback chubs preferred backwaters with a firm silt bottom and two feet depth, but also used eddies and runs at 0.4–0.8 fps.
- No size class of squawfish or humpback chub showed a clear temperature preference.

**Distribution and spawning**
- Young-of-the-year squawfish were found only in the lower study area, and no young-of-the-year were found between Jensen and Ouray.
- Spawning of squawfish was inferred to have occurred in [[Gray Canyon]] (below [[Range Creek]]) and nowhere else above it in 1977.
- Young razorbacks suggested spawning near and just below Ouray, and young humpback chubs indicated spawning in Desolation and Gray canyons.
- Spawning in the Jensen–Ouray reach may have been limited because early August temperatures (15–20 °C) were below the approximately 21 °C that squawfish required.

**Movement**
- A 2½-day study of one backwater (five seine samples; young-of-the-year captured 4, 31, 29, 11–1, and 12 across samples, per the text and Table 3 respectively) found three recaptures, with marked fish not found in adjacent habitats. The authors judged the movement data inconclusive but suggested fish moved within the backwater.
- Twenty juvenile squawfish were tagged and 13 more anal fin clipped; four subadults were tagged. One tagged juvenile was recaptured at its release site 4.5 hours later.
- No adult bonytail or chub recaptures were recorded from 1 adult and 1 juvenile bonytail and 14 adult and 9 juvenile chubs tagged.

**Associated fishes and competition**
- Young exotic species dominated young-of-the-year squawfish and humpback chub collections. Juvenile squawfish associated mainly with native species.
- Red shiners were the species most often found in samples with rare fishes.
- The authors state that competition and predation are possible but that there is insufficient data to identify the mechanisms.

**Backwater role**
- Backwaters are nutrient-rich, warm and rich in cladocerans, copepods and small insects. The authors suggest these provide food for young squawfish, and that preferred habitat abundance may limit rare fish survival.
- Backwaters were classified as short-lived (changing with about a 6-inch level change) or long-lived (protected from small level changes). Young squawfish and humpback chubs used both types, while young sunfish and bullheads used only long-lived ones.
- The report cautions against building long-lived backwaters to aid rare fish, because exotic populations would also increase.

**Management implications**
- Water pump intakes for Ouray National Wildlife Refuge ponds and irrigation should not be placed in slow water or backwaters; intakes in swift runs are recommended.
- Any change in flow or habitat must be carefully evaluated, since all four rare fishes were captured in the study area.
- The authors recommend several years of data to achieve excellent reliability for juvenile and adult curves. Only the young-of-the-year squawfish and humpback chub curves were rated good; the rest were fair.
