# Characterization of the Life History and Ecology of the Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in the Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report (1990–1991) from [[BIO/WEST Inc.]] to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] describes a multi-year study of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in the mainstem [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]]. The study tests how [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations affect the chub's life history, habitat use, movement, and population. Fifteen fish species were captured, and the chub was concentrated near the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) confluence (RM 57–65.4). Adults showed strong site fidelity, pre-spawning aggregation at the LCR mouth, and a preference for turbid, low-light conditions during daytime near-surface activity. Condition factor declined markedly during 1991, the cause of which was not determined. The report is labelled a preliminary report with provisional information.

## Background & Description

- **Species focus:** [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), a federally endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Adults were measured at 181–480 mm total length (TL); adult weights ranged from 46 to 999 g.
- **Sex ratio:** 45:55 male:female among 535 sexed adults.
- **Mean size:** Males averaged 346 mm TL and 392 g; females averaged 359 mm TL and 472 g.
- **Regulatory context:** The work addresses Conservation Measures 5 and 7 of the 1978 biological opinion on Glen Canyon Dam operations and feeds into the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program and the Glen Canyon Dam Environmental Impact Statement.
- **Study design:** Field trips alternated between 10-day and 20-day trips from October 1990 through November 1991 (seven of each). Sampling was split into three reaches:
  - [[Reach 1 (Upper Reach)]]: RM 56.0–77.4, the primary chub habitat.
  - [[Reach 2 (Middle Reach)]]: RM 77.4–160.0, granite gorges and eight perennial tributaries.
  - [[Reach 3 (Lower Reach)]]: RM 160.0–226.0, Lower Canyon and Lower Granite Gorge; no significant perennial tributaries.
- **Capture methods:** [[Electrofishing]], [[Gill Nets]], [[Trammel Nets]], [[Seines]], [[Minnow Traps]], and [[Hoop Nets]]. Radiotelemetry used [[Radio Transmitters]] implanted via [[Surgical Implantation]] protocols.
- **Historical context:** Humpback chub were recorded from Kolb brothers' 1908 account, and archaeological remains (~4,000 years old) were found at Stanton's Cave. Post-dam records (1963 onward) are far more numerous.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Reach 1 has steep, talus-dominated shorelines and boulder fans from tributaries. Runs dominated mapped macrohabitat (29–93% of surface area), followed by eddies (2–41%) and pools (3–43%). Adults used low-velocity eddies, return channels, and midwater zones, often near boulders and sand.
- **Microhabitat:** Adult radiotelemetry locations ranged 1.2–12.0 m deep (mean 7.4 m) with low velocities. Boulders were the dominant substrate at 63% of locations. Juvenile chub were found along talus slopes, earthen banks with root wads, and large boulders, not on vertical cliffs or sand beaches.
- **Turbidity and sediment:** Suspended sediment at [[Lees Ferry]] fell from 65.4 million tons (1948–1962) to 0.4 million tons (1982–1986), a 99.4% decrease after dam construction. The authors hypothesize that turbidity functions as cover for the species.
- **Water quality:** [[Hydrolab]] readings (365 mainstem readings in 1991, plus tributary and LCR readings) recorded temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity, and redox potential. Water temperatures in the canyon were approximately 8–10 °C.
- **Competing and predatory species:** Fifteen fish species were recorded, including ten non-natives. Non-native fishes (especially [[Rainbow Trout]]) were considered potential competitors and predators. Osprey predation on an adult chub was documented in May 1991.
- **Parasite:** The tapeworm *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi* was suggested as one possible cause of the condition decline.
- **Drift and diet:** 137 drift samples were dominated by Simuliidae, Chironomidae, and *Gammarus lacustris*, with *Cladophora glomerata* in most samples. Eight humpback chub stomachs (non-lethal pumping) contained mainly *Gammarus lacustris* and aquatic invertebrates.

## Key Findings

**Species composition and catch**
- 15 fish species were captured (5 native, 10 non-native). The most common non-natives were rainbow trout, common carp, brown trout, and channel catfish.
- 964 humpback chub were handled: 281 young-of-the-year (YOY), 77 juveniles, and 606 adults.
- 194 chub (20.1%) had been previously handled. BIO/WEST recaptured 61 of 619 of its own PIT-tagged chub (9.9%).
- 15 striped bass were captured (May–July), as far upstream as RM 159.5. Most stomachs were empty.

**Distribution**
- 569 of 606 adults (94%) and 76 of 77 juveniles (99%) were in a 13.5 km section between Malagosa Crest (RM 57.0) and Lava-Chuar Rapid (RM 65.4).
- 274 of 281 YOY (98%) were captured in Reach 1, mainly downstream of the LCR.
- Catch rates peaked near the LCR inflow in a bell-shaped distribution.

**Seasonal aggregation and spawning**
- Netting catch per effort was four to five times higher in March than in adjacent months, showing pre-spawning aggregation at the LCR mouth.
- Catch rates were lowest in April and May, when most adults were spawning in the LCR, and returned to pre-March levels in July.
- Young chub (50–125 mm TL) descended from the LCR from May through November, with peaks in August and September.

**Diel and turbidity effects**
- Nighttime netting rates exceeded daytime rates in every month except March and September 1991.
- Near-surface activity was 56% APFL at high turbidity versus 22% at low turbidity, supporting turbidity as cover.
- Remote telemetry showed daytime near-surface activity of 9.5% (low turbidity) versus 24.0% (high turbidity).

**Condition factor**
- Relative condition (Kn) was above 1.00 from October 1990 to March 1991, dropped below 1.00 from March to June 1991, and stayed low.
- November 1991 Kn (0.979) was significantly lower than October 1990 (1.070). Causes proposed include cyclic population change, interim flows (from August 1991), or tapeworm infection. Handling was ruled out.

**Population estimate (Schnabel method, fish >175 mm TL, RM 57.0–65.4)**
- March 1991: 1,395 fish (95% CI 916–2,254).
- September 1991: 2,407 fish (95% CI 1,102–6,564).
- Estimates were not used across April–June because of spawning migration violating model assumptions.

**Movement and fidelity**
- 48 radiotagged adults (mean 86 days at large) had mean net displacement of 1.34 km and gross displacement of 4.23 km; 83.3% moved less than 2 km net.
- 61 recaptured PIT-tagged fish moved a mean net displacement of 0.83 km (excluding outliers); 88.1% were recaptured within 2 km.
- Migratory fish (24 of 48) had significantly higher gross displacement (5.66 km vs 2.78 km), reflecting LCR spawning migrations.
- One PIT-tagged adult moved 98.95 km (the longest recorded movement for the species), excluded as an outlier.

**Surgical and tag performance**
- 53 adults were radiotagged. Transmitters were 9 g or 11 g, weight-limited to 2% of body weight.
- The 11 g transmitters lasted 56–147 days (mean 99 days) versus manufacturer estimates of 75–120 days.
- Of 12 recaptured radiotagged fish, two showed incision dehiscence. Later changes included Maxon absorbable suture, lateral incision, and the SNAG antenna guide.
- Recaptured PIT-tagged fish showed no significant condition difference from first captures, indicating no measurable tagging harm.

**Sampling efficiency**
- Electrofishing caught 340 chub (12.57 fish/10 h) and was the only gear that captured all three life stages.
- Trammel nets (1.5 in) had the highest adult catch rate (12.11 fish/100 ft/100 h).
- Electrofishing caused external effects in 90 of 5,643 fish (1.6%); 6 chub were affected, with no bruise marks.

**Habitat**
- Macrohabitat mapping (seven areas, 4,290–16,600 cfs) showed flow-habitat relationships were complex and could not be resolved from the flow range sampled.
- The LCR inflow area (LCRI) had the greatest habitat diversity and most riffle area.

**Recommendations and plans**
- Continue radiotelemetry, macrohabitat mapping, drift studies, and water quality monitoring through 1992–1993.
- Determine whether feeding increases before spawning and whether flow changes drive near-surface activity.
- Assess the role of turbidity in predator avoidance and food supply, given the 99.4% sediment reduction.
- Compare seasonal activity and condition data across more years before concluding a population-wide decline.
