# Fishes of Hunter's Hole, Yuma County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This survey documents the fish fauna of [[Hunter's Hole]], a set of interconnected backwater pools beside the [[Colorado River]] in Yuma County, Arizona. The data combine [[Electrofishing]] records from 1968 to 1974, [[Netting]] carried out in April 1974, fisherman interviews, and literature records from the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]. The author reports 17 species. Three salinity-tolerant species, [[Striped Mullet]], [[Threadfin Shad]], and [[Common Carp]], dominated netting samples. The fauna was otherwise sparse in native species, and the study found evidence of reproduction by striped mullet in the river system, which had not previously been recorded outside marine habitats.

## Background & Description

Hunter's Hole is a series of pools that appears to be an old meander of the Colorado River. It did not exist on 1938 aerial photographs, so it formed later through river meandering or another physical change. The system once received inflow from irrigation drains and the 21-mile wasteway. It is now fed mainly by seepage from the river or from nearby irrigation canals. The ponds cover about 12 surface acres and reach a maximum depth of about 19 feet in the larger pond.

Vegetation is zoned. Open, deeper water holds sparse [[Spiny Naiad]] and [[Stonewort]]. The banks are dominated by a dense stand of [[Giant Bulrush]] and [[Cattail]], with native [[Common Reed]], willows, [[Saltcedar]], and [[Cottonwood]] further back.

Historically, habitats like Hunter's Hole were likely frequented by seven of the eleven native fish species of the area. Only two of those seven had been taken commonly or frequently in recent years. Table 1 of the source lists the regional native species, including [[Machete]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado River Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Desert Pupfish]], and [[Gila Topminnow]]. Several of these are extinct or extremely rare in the lowermost river.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling bias.** The author stresses that each gear type is selective by species and size. Gill nets, trammel nets, trap nets, seines, and electrofishing therefore give different pictures. Absolute abundance or biomass per acre could not be estimated without an in-depth study. The data support only relative statements about composition and biomass.

**Introductions and invasives.** Most species in the current fauna were introduced. Threadfin shad were introduced to Lake Mead in 1953 and spread through the lower system within two years. [[Mosquitofish]] arrived in Arizona in the late 1920s. Carp escaped from domestic ponds before 1885. [[Flathead Catfish]] were placed in the lower river in the 1960s. The author suggests mosquitofish contributed to the disappearance of Gila topminnow and desert pupfish from habitats like Hunter's Hole. Redear sunfish and bluegill are widespread regionally.

**Salinity and food web.** Three salinity-tolerant fishes dominated the samples. Threadfin shad are a major forage base for most large fishes in the lower basin, but they were scarce in netting. The author notes a relative lack of food for juvenile game fish such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and black crappie.

**Associated fauna.** Macroinvertebrates included the crayfish *Procambarus clarkii*, the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula manilensis*), odonates, mayflies, chironomids, and oligochaetes. A pulse of rotifer plankton was present in April 1974. Vertebrates included bullfrogs, abundant softshell turtles (*Trionyx spiniferus*), a mud turtle, muskrats, and [[American Beaver]] (one juvenile was caught).

## Key Findings

**Species composition and abundance (Table 3, netting and electrofishing):**
- Striped mullet were the most numerous netted fish (about 1,000), and dominated biomass. Carp (about 400) and threadfin shad (about 350) followed.
- Yellow bullhead: 23 adults netted, all 10 to 13 inches. Many spawned, though no young were seen.
- Bluegill: 11 netted, the largest 6.5 inches. Larger fish were in breeding coloration but had not yet spawned.
- Tilapia: 5 adults netted (10 to 13 inches), one carrying eggs. The author expected tilapia to dominate and found this was not the case.
- Channel catfish: 5 netted, none over 14 inches. One female, 8.5 inches, extruded ripe eggs.
- Flathead catfish: 2 caught, a male about 33 inches (~20 lb) with mature sperm and a female about 24 inches (5.8 lb) with developed ovaries.
- Largemouth bass: 2 in netting (one young-of-the-year). Electrofishing suggested a larger population. Of 22 fishermen interviewed, 18 sought bass.
- Black crappie: 2 netted, including an 11-inch breeding male.
- Redear sunfish: 4 netted (3 to 6 inches). Two redear × bluegill hybrids were also taken.
- Mosquitofish, sailfin molly, and red shiner were uncommon, and some were only observed.
- Machete: one adult observed in 1974, and a fisherman reported a "small" machete in 1972.
- Goldfish: taken by electrofishing, not netted. Fishermen suggested bait release.

**Striped mullet:**
- Netted mullet ranged from 13 to 16 inches, indicating no more than two year classes. None had developed gonads, and many were very fat.
- A high proportion had parasitic cysts in the mesenteries and livers. Johnson (1969) also reported diseased mullet in this area.
- Mullet were found as far upstream as Imperial Dam, and small juveniles (under 1.5 inches) were taken near Morelos Dam in 1964. The author concludes that reproduction in the river or its backwaters is likely, which would be the first record of mullet reproducing outside marine environments.
- Aerial and field surveys by the Bureau of Reclamation (1973, 1974) found a land plug about 4 miles south of the Colorado River–Rio Hardy junction. This plug blocks water flow to the Gulf and supports the isolation hypothesis.

**Diversity and gear bias:**
- The fauna totals 17 species. Electrofishing and netting each captured species the other did not. Green sunfish were taken by electrofishing only, and tilapia were taken by electrofishing only in 1968 and 1969.
- The author judged the relative abundance estimates to be "valid, albeit somewhat qualitative." Mullet abundance was likely overstated by netting, and carp were likely overstated because they are susceptible to both gears.

**Historical context:**
- Hunter's Hole has been sampled since 1967. Historical records suggest seven native species could be found there. Only two were commonly taken in recent years.
- Two additional species, brown bullhead and warmouth, were expected but not collected. The author attributes their absence to sampling error.
