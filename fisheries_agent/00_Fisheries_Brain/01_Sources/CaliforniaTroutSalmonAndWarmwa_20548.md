# California Fish Hatchery Production (1968-69) and Salton Sea Salinity Tolerances

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This document bundles two [[California Department of Fish and Game]] Inland Fisheries Branch administrative reports. The first summarizes the production and operating costs of the Department's [[Fish Hatcheries]] for the 1968-69 fiscal year, when output first exceeded 3,000,000 pounds of fish. The second reports laboratory bioassays on the salinity tolerance of three fishes from the [[Salton Sea]], a hypersaline lake whose rising salinity threatened its sportfishery. Together they link fish production economics with the environmental limits of a key fishery.

## Background & Description

**Hatchery report (Administrative Report No. 70-1, submitted January 1970)**
- Covers all fish produced and planted by Department hatcheries in 1968-69, grouped into resident trout and salmon, anadromous fish, and [[Warmwater Fish Production]].
- Reports numbers, weight (pounds), size classes (fingerlings, subcatchables, catchables, broodstock) and costs per pound and per fish.

**Salinity report (Administrative Report No. 70-2, submitted March 1970)**
- Part of the [[Dingell-Johnson Project]] "Salton Sea Investigation", funded by Federal Aid to Fish Restoration.
- Author: Jack A. Bansow, Inland Fisheries Branch.
- Tests three species: [[Bairdiella]], [[Orangemouth Corvina]] and [[Sargo]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salton Sea setting**
- A large, shallow lake about 233 feet below sea level in southeastern California. Its elevation and salinity have fluctuated since formation.
- From 1950 to 1956 total dissolved solids approximated ocean levels. Many fishes were introduced from the Gulf of California; bairdiella, orangemouth corvina and sargo became established quickly.
- Salinity later rose to a high of 39 ppt, recorded in October 1968. It follows an annual cycle, peaking in fall and winter.
- [[Walker 1961 Estimate]]: the fishery could be destroyed between 1980 and 1990 if salinity kept rising.
- The three established species supported an estimated 359,000 angler trips per year, with harvests of 346,000 orangemouth corvina, 307,000 bairdiella and 195,000 sargo.
- Little prior data existed on salinity tolerances of the Sea's important fishes and invertebrates. The Department began a study in 1966 to set target levels for a [[Salinity Control Plan]].

**Hatchery context**
- Hatchery output supports stocking of lakes, reservoirs and streams. Some high mountain lakes were stocked by air, about 1,000,000 fingerlings per year.
- Water development agencies reimburse operation of several salmon and steelhead hatcheries.

## Key Findings

### Hatchery production, 1968-69
- **Total planted:** 39,410,505 fish weighing 3,068,482 lb, the first year above 3,000,000 lb. (The summary text gives 3,068,422 lb; the tables give 3,068,482 lb.)
- **Resident fish:** 21,192,726 fish, 2,585,786 lb.
  - [[Catchable Trout]]: 8,486,974 fish, 2,142,525 lb (about 4.0 fish per lb). This rose by 995,505 fish and 196,853 lb, mainly because [[Fillmore Trout Hatchery]] and [[Kern River Trout Hatchery]] returned to production.
  - Subcatchable trout: 2,284,210 fish, 243,603 lb.
  - Fingerlings: 10,361,415 fish, 97,327 lb. Numbers fell by 5,103,625 because of reduced kokanee production and the use of fewer, larger fingerlings.
  - Broodstock: 60,129 fish, 102,359 lb.
- **Anadromous fish:** 18,114,653 fish, 476,129 lb, up 4,357,783 fish and 177,633 lb from 1967-68.
  - [[King Salmon]]: 14,935,535 fish, 199,816 lb.
  - [[Silver Salmon]]: 1,411,921 fish, 116,925 lb.
  - [[Steelhead Trout]]: 1,767,197 fish, 159,388 lb.
- **Warmwater fish:** 103,126 fish, 6,567 lb, a drop of 157,003 fish and 11,180 lb. Causes were smaller allotments, fewer adults planted, and difficulty obtaining reservoir fish during heavy runoff.
- **Top producers:** [[Darrah Springs Trout Hatchery]] (427,652 lb, a record for one hatchery's output in this summary, 60,610 lb below the 1967-68 San Joaquin record) and [[San Joaquin Trout Hatchery]] (410,147 lb).

### Costs
- Total operating cost of all hatcheries: $3,109,870. After reimbursement, the Department's actual expenditure was $2,513,331, up $288,386 from 1967-68.
- Average cost per pound of all fish: $0.93.
- [[Trout Production Costs]]: $2,345,508, about $0.91 per pound. Catchable trout cost $0.81 per pound ($0.20 each), compared with $0.73 ($0.19 each) in 1967-68.
- Warmwater production cost: $63,160, down $19,334.
- Mitigation and reimbursable anadromous hatcheries: $637,114. Funders include the [[California Department of Water Resources]], [[East Bay Municipal Utility District]], the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and [[Pacific Power and Light Company]] (80% of [[Iron Gate Hatchery]] operating costs). [[Pacific Gas and Electric Company]] reimbursed 38,800 lb of trout planted in its project areas.
- Three-year trout cost comparison (Table 7): total trout production cost rose from $2,076,236 (1966-67) to $2,002,124 (1967-68) and $2,345,508 (1968-69), on pounds produced of 2,110,611, 2,390,666 and 2,585,786.
- Reconciliation: the Annual Cost Accumulation Report excludes a pro rata share of administrative costs, which this report adds.

### Salton Sea salinity tolerance bioassays
- **Method:** 96-hour shock bioassays, with an 8-day test on acclimated bairdiella. Fish were seined from five Salton Sea sites and held at 23 °C. Five to 10 fish were tested per 20-gallon aquarium, in duplicate, with controls. Acclimation lasted 6 to 15 days.
- **Bairdiella (*Bairdiella icistius*):**
  - Young: 100% survival at 52.5 ppt. 43.8% survived at 57.5 ppt.
  - Yearlings: 60% survived at 52.5 ppt and 40% at 55 ppt. Nearly all died at 62.5 ppt.
  - Over half died at 55 and 57.5 ppt. Only 2 of 30 survived at 62.5 ppt.
  - Acclimated yearlings survived 8 days at 58 ppt. Survival was 100% for the first 24 hours and 82.9% at the end of the test.
  - Mucus secretion began above 45 ppt, and loss of equilibrium occurred at 50 ppt.
- **[[Orangemouth Corvina]] (*Cynoscion xanthulus*):**
  - Young survived at 52.5 ppt (100%), 55 ppt (93.3%) and 57.5 ppt (91.4%). All died at 62.5 ppt.
- **[[Sargo]] (*Anisotremus davidsoni*):**
  - Control survival was 89.6%, so some deaths had other causes.
  - Survival was 78.6% at 47.5 ppt, 50% at 45 ppt and 15% at 50 ppt. Survival was 58.5%, 42.9% and 33.3% at 52.5, 55 and 57.5 ppt. None survived at 62.5 ppt.
- **Conclusions:**
  - Bairdiella and orangemouth corvina appear as tolerant of hypersalinity as their Gulf Coast relatives, and corvina tolerance might increase with acclimation.
  - Sargo tolerance is less certain, though it appears able to tolerate about 50 ppt.
  - Eggs and larvae are more sensitive than older stages, and hypersalinity is expected to slow growth. The author cautions that these results do not mean the fishery could survive at these salinities.
- **Comparative data cited:** [[Simmons 1957 Laguna Madre Survey]] reported spotted sea trout up to 75 ppt and silver perch at 45 ppt in Texas. [[Carpelan 1961 Lagoon Study]] recorded California corbina at 51 ppt. [[Kinne 1960]] showed hypersalinity reduced growth of the [[Desert Pupfish]].
- **Pending work:** the tolerance of the [[Pileworm]] (*Neanthes succinea*), egg and larval stages, and growth effects were to be reported separately.
