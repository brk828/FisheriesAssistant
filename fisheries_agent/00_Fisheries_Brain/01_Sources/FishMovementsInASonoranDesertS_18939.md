# Fish Movements in a Sonoran Desert Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study quantifies seasonal movements of native fishes in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a desert stream in southeastern Arizona. Fish biomass was higher inside the central [[Aravaipa Gorge]] in summer and outside it in winter. Tagged fishes moved toward the gorge in summer and away from it in winter, and this pattern did not respond to flooding. The authors propose an orthokinetic response to temperature, possibly combined with light, as the controlling mechanism.

## Background & Description

Larger native fishes were seasonally common, then nearly absent, in some reaches of Aravaipa Creek, suggesting regular annual movements. Before this work, no quantitative data existed on movements of native American desert fishes. Earlier accounts were anecdotal and concerned large species such as [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], which had formerly migrated in rivers that are now dry or dammed.

The study focused on three native species large enough to tag (>10 cm total length):
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), a minnow
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), a catostomid
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), a catostomid

Fish were captured by electrofishing and blocking nets from 1976 to 1978. Tagging with individually numbered anchor tags took place from January 1978 to November 1979. No tagging was done in June–September to avoid combined heat and electrofishing stress.

Study design:
- The stream was divided into six subreaches (Segments I–VI).
- Sampling was at 2- to 4-week intervals from September through May, and every 6 weeks from June through August.
- Minimum movement was calculated from the difference between original and later capture points.
- Analyses used [[Analysis of Variance]], [[Linear Regression]], and a [[Bernoulli Trial]] test of directional randomness against the binomial distribution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream setting.** Perennial flow surfaces about 6.5 km northwest of Klondyke and maintains surface discharge for 41 stream-km before water re-enters the streambed. The stream has three parts: the upper 10 km in an open valley, a 17-km central gorge with walls over 200 m high, and a 14-km lower steep-sided valley.

**Discharge and flooding.** Discharge is seasonal, variable, and generally low.
- Modal winter discharge is 0.32 m³/s.
- Maximum instantaneous discharge exceeded 560 m³/s.
- The recorded minimum was 0.008 m³/s.
- Flood probability peaks in February–April and July–September.

**Temperature.** Two thermal seasons are recognized.
- Winter (November–March): 4.6–20.5 °C.
- Summer (May–September): 17.0–33.2 °C.
- Midday summer temperatures in the gorge were 3.1–8.8 °C cooler than elsewhere. The gorge shades the water, and evaporation into dry air cools it.

**Habitat.** The channel is unstable, with braided runs over gravel and sand, short turbulent riffles, and pools 0.75–1.5 m deep with shifting sand bottoms.

**Broader context.** Desert streams alternate between flooding and drying. Canyons moderate temperatures and light, which may explain why fishes concentrate in shaded segments. Other fishes in the region, such as [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] in the [[Paria River]], also move according to temperature gradients between tributary and mainstem waters.

## Key Findings

**Biomass and distribution**
- The three largest species made up more than 75% of total fish biomass.
- Winter biomass was concentrated in Segment I, above the gorge.
- In summer, fishes concentrated within the gorge and almost vacated the upper reach.
- Spring biomass was highest above the gorge, and autumn biomass was highest outside the gorge near its lower entrance.
- Total numbers per m² were more uniform across the stream, because they reflect small species and young-of-the-year.

**Tagging and recapture**
- Almost 2,100 fish were tagged. Table 4 gives 2,097 total: 740 *G. robusta*, 948 *C. insignis*, and 409 *P. clarki*.
- The text reports 219 recaptures (10.4%) and 2 fish (1.1%) caught two or more times. Table 4 lists 245 recaptures in total.
- All three species moved. Among fish recaptured, 83.7% in lower segments and 80.5% in upper segments moved more than 100 m.
- Fish moving less than 100 m were classed as sedentary. None of these moved more than 60 m, which falls within home-range estimates for other species.
- Mean distance moved did not differ significantly among species, sizes, or sexes.

**Directional movement**
- Fish in upper reaches moved toward the gorge from late winter through summer, and upstream away from it in autumn.
- Fish in lower reaches moved upstream toward the gorge from late winter through summer, and downstream in autumn.
- Bernoulli trial results for all fishes were significant in summer 1978 (upper reach: 13 up, 39 down, p = 0.0001; lower reach: 45 up, 26 down, p = 0.007) and autumn 1978 in the upper reach (31 up, 14 down, p = 0.005).

**Distance and gorge proximity**
- Among fish from lower reaches, summer movement toward the gorge decreased as tagging sites were closer to it. The regression was y = −0.738x + 68.9, r = −0.490, p < 0.01.
- For upper reaches, the regression of distance toward the gorge on tagging location was y = 0.339x + 11.35, p < 0.01. The reported r = −0.383 has a sign inconsistent with the positive slope, and the source should be checked.

**Flooding**
- Movement patterns did not respond to flooding. Fish stayed in the gorge during summer spates and outside it during winter floods.
- Two major floods, one in December 1978 and one in March 1978, had no apparent effect on fish distribution or movement.

**Proposed mechanism**
- The authors argue that temperature gradients drive movement. Fish move more when outside their preferred range and slow or stop once they reach it.
- Light, especially intense sunlight avoided by [[Gila]] species, may reinforce or trigger this response.
- The authors rule out spawning migration, imprinting-based homing, and genetic control. Spawning did not match observed movements, imprinting could not operate on downstream trips, and two genetically distinct stocks would be needed for opposing movements.
- The simplest explanation is an orthokinetic response to temperature and/or light.
