## Executive Summary

This 1996 note from the *Great Basin Naturalist* documents the first confirmed record of [[Brook Stickleback]] (*Culaea inconstans*) in Utah and formally establishes the species as a new nonnative addition to the Upper Colorado River Basin fish fauna. Five specimens were collected between July and October 1995 from the middle [[Green River]] in Uintah County, Utah, at and near Old Charley Wash, a wetland on the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]]. The record extends a pattern of documented range expansions of brook stickleback well beyond its native Arctic and Atlantic drainage distribution, most likely via human-mediated introduction (bait bucket transfers or contaminated game fish stocking) rather than natural dispersal.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Culaea inconstans* (Kirtland 1841), family [[Gasterosteidae]] (sticklebacks).
- **Size range of collected specimens**:
  - One juvenile: 27 mm total length (TL), collected 18 July 1995.
  - Four adults: 41, 46, 48, and 54 mm TL, collected 1–12 October 1995.
- Small-bodied gasterosteid fish; specific meristic/morphological diagnostic characters not detailed in this note beyond size data.
- Voucher specimens deposited under catalog number **LFL 24871**, Larval Fish Laboratory, Colorado State University.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native range**: Arctic and Atlantic drainages of North America, extending west from Nova Scotia to British Columbia, and south from the Northwest Territories to southern Ohio drainages, including the Mississippi-Missouri River system above the confluence with the Illinois River.
- **Habitat preference in this study**: Collected in low- or no-velocity habitats — specifically a light trap at the outlet of Old Charley Wash (river kilometer 402 on the Green River) and within a wetland that connects to the Green River only during high spring flows.
- **Wetland dynamics**: Adult specimens were captured when the Old Charley Wash wetland was drained, suggesting the species can persist in isolated, seasonally connected wetland habitats within the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]].
- **Introduction pathway**: Likely anthropogenic — bait bucket transfer or contaminated game fish stocking, consistent with mechanisms documented for other nonnative introductions in the region (Zuckerman and Behnke 1986, Sublette et al. 1990).
- **Broader invasion context**: Prior to this record, [[Tyus et al. 1982]] documented 42 established nonnative fish species compared to only 13 native species in the Upper Colorado River Basin, underscoring a heavily invaded fish community. Brook stickleback adds to this list of established nonnatives.
- **Regional precedent**: Brook stickleback had already been reported in nearby drainages, including three tributaries of the [[Elk River]] (South, Coleman, and Deep creeks) in northwestern Colorado in 1993, as well as isolated/relict populations in the Canadian River drainage of New Mexico and documented occurrences in the Rio Grande drainage (New Mexico), Colorado's San Luis Valley, and the Klamath River, California.

## Key Findings

- **First Utah record**: This paper documents the first confirmed occurrence of brook stickleback in Utah, based on 5 specimens collected from the middle Green River in Uintah County between July and October 1995.
- **New basin record**: Brook stickleback is confirmed as a new nonnative addition to the fish fauna of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], joining a growing list of introduced species outside the species' native range (also reported in Alabama, Kentucky, Tennessee, New Mexico's Rio Grande drainage, Colorado, and California).
- **Collection localities**: Specimens were taken from Old Charley Wash (RK 402 on the Green River), a wetland habitat on the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] that connects to the Green River only during high spring flows.
- **Life stage timing**: A single juvenile (27 mm TL) was captured in mid-July, while four adults (41–54 mm TL) were captured in early-to-mid October, suggesting size/age progression through the sampling season within this wetland refugium.
- **Probable introduction mechanism**: The authors conclude the presence of brook stickleback in the basin is most likely due to human introduction (e.g., bait bucket release or contaminated stocking) rather than a natural range extension, consistent with established explanations for other extralimital occurrences of this species.
- **Regional invasion pressure**: The finding reinforces broader concerns regarding nonnative fish establishment in the Upper Colorado River Basin, where nonnative species already outnumber natives by more than 3:1 (42 vs. 13 species per Tyus et al. 1982).
