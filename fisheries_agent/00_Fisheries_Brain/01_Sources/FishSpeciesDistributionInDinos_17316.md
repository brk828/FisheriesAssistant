# Fish Species Distribution in Dinosaur National Monument During 1961 and 1962

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 Colorado State University Master of Science thesis by [[Joe L. Banks]] examines how stream-flow, water temperature, turbidity, [[Bottom Types]], food availability and an accidental rotenone pollution event shaped the distribution of the fish fauna of the [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]] within [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Thirteen species in five families were collected from 2,999 fish. Stream-flow was identified as the dominant factor controlling seasonal distribution, while the September 1962 [[Rotenone]] pollution produced a severe, localized fish kill, most pronounced at [[Gates of Lodore]].

## Background & Description

The study was conducted under the [[Colorado River Storage Project]], which authorized major reservoirs including [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (Ashley Dam) on the Green River, completed in November 1962. The dam was expected to alter downstream turbidity, year-round temperatures and the characteristic spring run-off, so a pre-impoundment baseline was sought. Very little was known about the ecology of the river's fishes, several of which were rare endemics.

The thesis area covered the Green River from Brown's Park Bridge to Split Mountain Campground, plus a 12-mile section of the Yampa River to Castle Park. Six study areas were established: Brown's Park, Gates of Lodore, Echo Park, Island Park, Split Mountain and Castle Park. Sampling occurred in October 1961, May 1962, June to November 1962 and October 1962. The study was explicitly qualitative because of gear selectivity.

Methods included a [[Foxboro Electric Thermometer]], a 86-inch [[Secchi Disc]] as a turbidity index, [[Alsterberg Modification of the Winkler Method]] for dissolved oxygen, a Helige comparator for pH, and titration for alkalinity. Fish were collected with a stream shocker, throw-lines, seines, experimental and standard gill nets, rotenone and hook-and-line. Bottom invertebrates were sampled with a Surber square-foot sampler and hand screen. Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin.

Taxonomic notes: bonytail chubs showed intermediate characters among Gila robusta robusta, G. r. elegans and G. cypha, and their status was unresolved. The hybrid sucker (Catostomus latipinnis × Xyrauchen texanus) was described by [[Hubbs and Miller]] (1953).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Physiography:** The Green River rises in the Wind River Range, Wyoming, and flows through Brown's Park, Lodore Canyon, Echo Park, Whirlpool Canyon, Island Park and Split Mountain Canyon into the Uinta Basin. The Yampa River rises in the Park Range, Colorado, and joins the Green at Echo Park.
- **Gradient:** The Green River drops 525 feet in 42 miles (about 12.4 ft/mile) from Lodore to Split Mountain. The Yampa falls about 590 feet in 47 miles.
- **Stream-flow:** Flow varied across space, season and year. Maybell (Yampa) peaked at 11,300 cfs on 5/14/62 (Table 3), while Jensen (Green) reached 25,200 cfs on the same date. The Little Snake River ceased flowing for many days in August and September.
- **Water temperature:** Yampa River temperatures ran higher than the Green's; August 1962 Green River temperatures averaged about two degrees F cooler at Echo Park.
- **Turbidity:** Secchi depths in the Green River never exceeded about 18.25 inches (Split Mountain, 4 November 1962). In August 1962, Green River visibility never exceeded one foot, while the Yampa frequently exceeded one foot and sometimes three feet.
- **Water chemistry:** pH was 7.0 to 8.6 range across stations; dissolved oxygen averaged 7.0 to 8.4 ppm; alkalinity fell within expected North American freshwater values. None appeared to limit distribution.
- **Bottom types:** Silt and sand dominated the lower, low-gradient reaches (Brown's Park, Gates of Lodore); rubble and rock occurred in steep canyons (Echo Park, Island Park); Split Mountain and Castle Park showed mixed combination substrates.

## Key Findings

**Species composition and abundance**
- 13 species from five families (6 Cyprinidae, 3 Catostomidae, 2 Ictaluridae, 1 Cottidae, 1 Centrarchidae) were collected.
- Total catch was 2,999 fish: 2,573 from the Green River and 426 from the Yampa.
- Most abundant: [[Redside Shiner]] (1,535), [[Speckled Dace]] (496), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (347), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (273).
- Humpback suckers (10), hybrid suckers (5), black bullheads (6) and green sunfish (1) were recorded from the Green River, and the humpback form of bonytail was not found in the Yampa.
- Mountain whitefish, rainbow trout, brown trout and yellow walleye were reported by other investigators but not collected in this study.

**Stream-flow and seasonal distribution**
- Stream-flow was the most influential factor. During spring high water, throw-lines caught fish only within about 10 to 15 feet of shore, and mid-channel efforts produced almost no fish.
- During low-flow late summer and fall, adults were collected in mid-channel pools with bag seines, gill nets and shockers, while young fish stayed in backwaters and side channels.

**Temperature and turbidity between rivers**
- Differences in run-off, temperature and turbidity were the likely cause of faunal differences. Humpback and hybrid suckers and black bullheads were confined to the Green; mottled sculpins and bluehead suckers were concentrated in the Yampa.

**Habitat preferences (Table 6)**
- Redside shiner and fathead minnow: shallow, quiet silt-and-sand areas and tributary mouths.
- Speckled dace, mottled sculpin, bluehead sucker: shallow riffles over rubble and rock.
- Adult bonytail: moderate to fast current over silt and sand; young in low-current areas.
- Colorado squawfish: small numbers, mostly above rubble, rock and combination bottoms; only 6 of 38 were larger than fingerlings.
- Carp, flannelmouth sucker and channel catfish showed no strong habitat specificity.

**Food habits (205 stomachs examined; 16 empty)**
- Equisetum (horsetail) stems dominated bonytail and channel catfish diets at high water, indicating foraging near shore.
- Carp and flannelmouth suckers at low water fed heavily on filamentous algae and Diptera larvae over rubble and rock.
- Food type appeared to have little effect on the distribution of bonytail and channel catfish; limited data prevented conclusions for squawfish, bluehead and black bullhead.
- Invertebrate densities differed sharply by substrate: an average of 2.6 insect larvae per square foot on rubble in June 1962 versus 16.9 per square foot in October and November 1962.

**Fish kill, September 1962**
- An upstream eradication project (about 450 stream miles, Wyoming and Utah) used rotenone at a target of 5 ppm. A neutralizing station with potassium permanganate at Brown's Park Bridge was not sufficient to prevent kills within the monument.
- Mortality at Gates of Lodore was severe, with about 80% mortality in live boxes of redside shiners and speckled dace; chloroform tests indicated about 0.25 ppm rotenone.
- Dead-fish counts ranged from 8 to 49 per station (Table 9), with flannelmouth sucker the most affected species.
- At Echo Park, only three of eleven 1961 species were recaptured in 1962; Gates of Lodore yielded no fish in 1962. Only 269 fish were collected in the entire October 1962 effort, versus hundreds from a single 1961 hole at Echo Park.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Flow was the dominant factor controlling fish distribution, followed by temperature and turbidity. Water chemistry and food were judged minor.
- Recommended: (1) continued fish monitoring in Dinosaur National Monument to detect post-[[Flaming Gorge Dam]] change; (2) life-history studies of rare endemic fishes of the Green and Yampa rivers; (3) surveys of remote canyon areas using boat shockers with rectified current.
- The author cautions that results may not represent the steep, nearly inaccessible canyons.
