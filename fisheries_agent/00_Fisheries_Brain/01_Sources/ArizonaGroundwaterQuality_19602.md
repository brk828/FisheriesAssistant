# Arizona Ground-Water Quality: National Water Summary 1986

#ichthyology #marine-biology #groundwater-quality

## Executive Summary

This State summary from the [[National Water Summary 1986]] assesses ground-water quality in Arizona. Ground water is the principal public water source: about two-thirds of the State's 1983 water use and 65 percent of its population depended on it, and roughly 73 percent of withdrawals went to agriculture. Quality problems are local rather than statewide. Median dissolved-solids and nitrate-plus-nitrite levels meet drinking-water standards in the nine major withdrawal areas, but contamination is linked to urbanization, irrigation, mine tailings, surface impoundments, hazardous-waste sites and landfills. The document also summarizes the [[Arizona Environmental Quality Act of 1986]], which created a new state environmental regulator.

Note: the source is a groundwater-resources report rather than a fish or aquatic-ecosystem study. It contains no fish or aquatic-species data, so this note records only its hydrogeological and water-quality content.

## Background & Description

- **Water use:** In 1983 about two-thirds of Arizona's water came from ground water, supplying 65 percent of the population. Agriculture took about 73 percent of ground-water withdrawals.
- **Withdrawal source:** About 95 percent of withdrawals came from alluvial aquifers in the [[Basin and Range Lowlands]] water province (Kister, 1973).
- **Monitoring:** Monitoring networks run by the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], the [[Arizona Department of Water Resources]] and the [[Arizona Department of Health Services]] are mostly tied to special studies or contaminated areas. Data are held in the [[WATSTORE Database]].
- **Drinking-water standards:** Primary standards are health-based and legally enforceable; secondary standards are aesthetic guidelines. Key limits cited include nitrate 10 mg/L, fluoride 4 mg/L (primary), dissolved solids 500 mg/L, sulfate 250 mg/L and fluoride 2 mg/L (secondary). These are set by the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]].
- **Principal aquifers:** Three types are recognized: unconsolidated alluvium (alluvial aquifers, basins 1–10), consolidated sandstones (basins 11–13) and crystalline bedrock. They fall into three water provinces: [[Basin and Range Lowlands]], [[Central Highlands]] and [[Plateau Uplands]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water provinces**

- **[[Basin and Range Lowlands]]:** Broad alluvial basins between north- to northwest-trending ranges. Alluvial aquifers are the most productive in the State. Dissolved solids are generally below 1,000 mg/L but range from under 100 to over 40,000 mg/L. Median dissolved solids exceed the secondary standard in basins 2, 3, 4 and 9, and median sulfate exceeds it in basins 2 and 9. Naturally occurring fluoride, barium, arsenic and chromium reach 5 to 10 times the maximum contaminant levels in places.
- **[[Central Highlands]]:** Mountainous, with bedrock aquifers that yield usable water only where fractured or faulted. Ground water generally holds less than 1,000 mg/L dissolved solids, though some springs are saline.
- **[[Plateau Uplands]]:** Sandstone aquifers (Coconino, Navajo and Dakota) supply about 3 percent of withdrawals. Medians meet standards, but dissolved solids range from 90 to over 12,000 mg/L. The Coconino Sandstone reaches over 25,000 mg/L in places.

**Land-use impacts**

- **Irrigation:** Salt recirculation raised dissolved solids by as much as 500 mg/L in the [[Willcox Basin]] since 1910. Agricultural irrigation increased nitrate in the upper [[Santa Cruz River Basin]] (Pima Association of Governments, 1983a).
- **Pesticides:** The soil fumigant [[DBCP]] (dibromochloropropane) was found in 59 of 170 wells sampled in 1979–1980 in Maricopa and Yuma counties. Four public wells were removed from service. Use peaked at 495,800 pounds in 1979. [[EDB]] (ethylene dibromide) was banned for most uses in 1983.
- **Mining:** Acidic seepage from mining and milling in the [[Pinal Creek Basin]] near Globe has produced a plume about 11 miles long and 2,000 feet wide, with more than 16,000 mg/L dissolved solids. A well sample had pH 3.6 and 10,800 mg/L sulfate. Sulfate of 650–850 mg/L downgradient of a Cochise County tailings pond poses an aesthetic and potential health concern.
- **Urban runoff:** In Phoenix, cadmium in storm-drain runoff exceeded the 0.010 mg/L maximum contaminant level. Dry-well runoff carried lead up to 230 µg/L, but monitor wells showed no contamination.
- **Contaminated wells:** Documented contamination covers 347 wells, including 115 with pesticides, 173 with volatile organic compounds and 59 exceeding maximum contaminant levels for compounds such as EDB, DBCP, PCE and TCE.

**Waste sites**

- Five [[RCRA]] hazardous-waste sites require ground-water monitoring. Five [[CERCLA]] (Superfund) sites are on the National Priorities List, with four more proposed. Contamination has been detected near all RCRA and CERCLA sites.
- Six military facilities have sites under the [[Installation Restoration Program]] (eight IRP sites in total had Phase I studies).
- Most waste sites lie near Phoenix and Tucson. Gasoline and solvents from leaking underground tanks have been documented at 11 locations.

## Key Findings

1. Ground water is the State's main supply, but quality problems are local, and median concentrations for dissolved solids and nitrate-plus-nitrite meet standards across the nine major withdrawal areas.
2. Naturally occurring fluoride, barium, arsenic and chromium exceed maximum contaminant levels in parts of the alluvial aquifers.
3. Contamination is driven by irrigation, pesticides ([[DBCP]], [[EDB]]), mining seepage, urban runoff and waste sites, with contamination detected near every RCRA and CERCLA site.
4. The [[Arizona Environmental Quality Act of 1986]] created the [[Arizona Department of Environmental Quality]] (from 1 July 1987). Its provisions include an aquifer classification and protection permit program, citizen suits, civil penalties of up to $25,000 per day per violation, and a Water Quality Assurance Revolving Fund.
5. The Act requires annual reports to the Governor and Legislature, a five-year report on aquifer contaminant levels, and an independent Auditor General performance review in fiscal year 1989.
6. A [[Pima Association of Governments]] model projected that agricultural nitrate would decline, but that copper-tailings contaminant plumes could eventually reach drinking-water supplies without mitigation.
