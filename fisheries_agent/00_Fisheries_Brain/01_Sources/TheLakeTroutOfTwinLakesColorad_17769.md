# The Lake Trout of Twin Lakes, Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation report (REC-ERC-77-4, March 1977) by John R. Griest documents the pre-operational ecology of [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*) in [[Twin Lakes]], Colorado. The study was started in 1969 and carried out mainly in 1974–1975 to establish baseline data before the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]] began operating, so that post-construction effects could be measured. Lake trout in Twin Lakes grow quickly early in life, feed mainly on the opossum shrimp [[Mysis Relicta]], and reach the 381 mm legal size limit at about four years of age. Annual mortality was near 50 percent. Natural reproduction was confirmed. The report identifies several plausible powerplant impacts, including entrainment, reduced Mysis availability, turbidity from resuspended glacial flour, and degraded spawning habitat.

## Background & Description

Lake trout were introduced to Twin Lakes in the late 1800s; no stocking record survives from that period. Juday examined lake trout from the lakes during 1902–1904 surveys, and the Colorado Division of Wildlife studied the population from 1958 to 1961. The study was initiated in 1969 because of concern about the pumped-storage facility, which was new and had little comparable data.

The stated objectives were to study:
- Age and growth, food habits, reproduction, mortality and survival, and recruitment to 381 mm.
- Population size.
- The use of Mysis relicta as food and its effect on growth.

The lakes are an impounded system of two former natural lakes at about 2802 m elevation on the eastern slope of the Sawatch Range. The upper lake covers 263 ha (maximum depth about 28 m), and the lower lake covers 736.5 ha (maximum depth about 27 m). Water levels are managed for irrigation and fluctuate by up to 7.6 m. The lake is dimictic, and it is ice-covered from late December to mid-May.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Chemistry.** Total dissolved solids were 32–88 mg/L and conductivity was 52–91 µS/cm at 25 °C. Bicarbonate was the main anion (20–30.5 mg/L) and calcium the main cation (8.8–12 mg/L). Dissolved oxygen was 7.3–8.9 mg/L in the upper lake and ranged from 1.3 to 7.9 mg/L in the bottom zones of the lower lake.

**Biota.** Rooted vegetation, including *Chara*, *Potamogeton*, and *Nitella*, is common at 3–9 m depth. Phytoplankton peaks within 4.5 m of the surface. Only two copepod genera are present (*Cyclops* and *Diaptomus*). Mysis relicta was introduced from Clearwater Lake, Minnesota, in 1957. Chironomid larvae and fingernail clams (*Pisidium*) are also present. Other fishes include brown, rainbow, cutthroat and brook trout, kokanee, longnose sucker, and white sucker. Fathead minnow and Bonneyville cisco were stocked, but none were recovered.

**Fishery.** Twin Lakes is mainly a rainbow trout fishery supported by summer stocking. Lake trout are the target mainly in winter, when most anglers fish for them.

**Sampling.** Most fish were taken with experimental nylon gill nets (19.1–44.5 mm bar mesh). Sampling took place in May, August and November 1974, with 17 sets per period. Scales from about 600 fish were used for ageing.

## Key Findings

**Growth**
- Growth is rapid until about year 4, levels off in years 5–8, and increases again after year 8. The authors attribute the slowdown to continued dependence on Mysis.
- The length-weight relationship is ln W = 3.17 ln L − 12.81, from 246 fish. The grand-mean condition factor is K = 0.760 (95% CI 0.756–0.764), which indicates a slim body form.
- Growth in years 1–5 is faster than in other lakes, and growth after year 5 is slower. Growth of fish aged 6 years and older is similar to the 1958–1961 study.

**Ageing**
- Scales can be read with high confidence up to age 6. Confidence declines with age because annuli crowd near the scale margin.
- Agreement between the two scale readings was 285 of 313 cases (91%) for the number of annuli.
- Otoliths and branchiostegal rays were not more readable than scales.

**Mortality and Population**
- The total annual mortality rate was 0.484 (instantaneous rate 0.661), which the author judges comparable to other lake trout populations.
- A Schnabel mark-recapture estimate gave 73,556 fish, with 95% limits of 33,690–200,608. Only six recaptures were made, so the authors place little confidence in this figure.

**Recruitment**
- Fish typically reach the legal size of 381 mm at about four years of age.
- About 20 percent of the population reaches legal size each year.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning occurs in late October to early November, mostly over boulder and rubble bottoms at 3–10 m depth. It lasted about 1–2 weeks in 1974 and about 9 days in 1975.
- Natural reproduction is supported by year-classes that were not stocked. Of 94 fish from the 1971 year-class sampled in 1974, 88 were from natural reproduction.
- Lake trout mature early (males from age 4, females from age 4). Historical data from Nolting (1958 and 1960) show about 782–1285 eggs per kg of body weight.

**Diet**
- Mysis relicta was the most important food. It occurred in 87.6 percent of stomachs and made up 22.7 percent of the volume. Chironomid larvae and pupae were also important.
- White suckers were the most frequently eaten fish, and rainbow trout were also consumed.
- Mysis use declined with age, while fish use increased. The shift appears to occur around year 9.

**Powerplant Impacts (Predicted)**
- Entrainment of fish, Mysis and fish eggs is a definite possibility. Liston reported a combined mortality of 74 percent for fish passing through a pumped-storage plant during both cycles.
- Loss of Mysis could reduce lake trout growth.
- Resuspended glacial flour could reduce foraging efficiency, reduce primary productivity, lower oxygen levels, and degrade spawning gravel.
- A tailrace channel with a berm was designed to deflect discharge away from bottom sediments. Model tests predicted little change in thermal stratification outside the local tailrace area.
- The report concludes that the plant "may have an adverse effect" on lake trout, and that the degree of entrainment mortality cannot be estimated.

**Applications**
- The data form the pre-operational baseline against which post-construction monitoring will be compared. The results are also relevant to cold-water fishery management and to assessing pumped-storage impacts elsewhere.
