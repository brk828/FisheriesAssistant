# Attempted Control of Bluegill Reproduction in Lakes by the Application of Copper Sulfate Crystals to Spawning Nests

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 field study, published in [[The Progressive Fish-Culturist]], tested whether periodic application of [[Copper Sulfate]] crystals to [[Bluegill]] spawning nests could suppress annual recruitment of young-of-the-year bluegills in Michigan lakes. Four lakes were treated during the 1962 spawning season and four served as controls, with fall [[Seining]] used to measure young-of-the-year abundance and length. The treatment did not significantly reduce bluegill numbers, although mean length of young-of-the-year bluegills increased significantly in treated lakes. The authors concluded that the method was of questionable value as a general lake management tool because of its labor demands and the need for repeated treatments.

## Background & Description

Bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*) reproduction is described as an unsolved management problem in many Michigan lakes. Bluegills are prolific, and earlier control attempts using [[Rotenone]], seining, and predator stocking did not provide long-term control. The authors argue that the most logical target is the egg and fry stage, since a size-selective [[Piscicide]] for bluegills was not yet available.

Earlier work had shown that centrarchid eggs and fry can be killed by chemicals applied directly to nests. Copper sulfate was used against bluegill nests by [[Allison]] (1964), and sodium hydroxide pellets were used against [[Pumpkinseed]] nests by [[Jackson]] (1956). Adult centrarchids returned to treated nests and were only irritated, with no treatment-related adult mortality reported. In a 2.75-acre pond (methyl orange alkalinity 176 ppm), Allison reported that adults abandoned treated areas within 4 hours and did not re-nest during the following month.

A preliminary Michigan trial in 1958–59 treated all visible nests weekly in a 66-acre lake. Autumn seining showed about half as many young-of-the-year bluegills in the treatment years compared with two years later, and anglers reported improved fishing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study waters:** Eight lakes, 80 to 200 acres, methyl orange alkalinity 13 to 140 ppm. Lakes were Big, Duck, and Selkirk (Allegan County) and Head, Middle, Mill, Pleasant, and Podunk (Barry County). All were bluegill-dominated.
- **Temperature:** Bluegill eggs hatch in 32 to 62 hours at 73°F. Mean water temperature across all lakes and years was 61°F (range 53–71°F).
- **Water level:** Lake levels dropped considerably in most lakes in 1961, forcing seining stations into different habitats (e.g., weedy to muddy at Pleasant Lake; sandy to dense weed at Head Lake).
- **Water chemistry:** Authors noted that copper sulfate toxicity depends on temperature, hardness, and organic matter, yet treatments appeared equally effective across the alkalinity range.
- **Non-target effects:** Fishes other than bluegill fry apparently were not killed. No adult mortality was attributed to treatment.
- **Nest depth:** Searching extended to 15 ft using [[Scuba Diving]], but no nests were found deeper than 6 ft.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Two years of pretreatment fall seining (40-ft bobbinet seine, 10 random stations per lake, about 1,600 sq ft per haul) preceded treatment.
- Four lakes were randomly assigned to treatment; four were controls.
- Treatment began May 18, 1962. Initial dosing of one or two crystals was insufficient; dosing was raised to about 25 g (half a handful) per nest and adjusted by nest size, turbulence, and egg or fry density. Broadcast spreading was used in dense colonies.
- Lakes were visited every third or fourth day. Treatment continued from May 18 to August 9, 1962. Spawn-taking tube samples (about 200) showed all bluegill eggs and fry dead.

**Results**
- Control lakes showed a marked drop in young-of-the-year catches in 1962.
- Treated lakes showed a pronounced drop between 1960 and 1961, and catches in 1962 (post-treatment) were higher than in 1961, so no effective reduction was demonstrated.
- Analysis of variance showed significant annual and lake-by-year variation before treatment, making it impossible to detect a treatment effect on numbers.
- Mean length of young-of-the-year bluegills rose significantly in treated lakes (from about 1.2 to 1.4 inches; contrast significant at the 95% level), but not in control lakes.

**Interpretation and Conclusions**
- Possible explanations for the length increase: early applications were less efficient, so earlier-spawned, larger fish survived; treatment mortality occurred earlier than natural mortality, reducing competition; or fall seining sites favored larger fish.
- The authors concluded that they did not kill substantially more bluegills than would have died naturally, did not reach the goal of eliminating young bluegills, and that collections after treatment exceeded the prior year.
- Practical limitations: about four workers for nearly 3 months, repeated (semiweekly) treatment needed, and risk that untreated nests could repopulate small waters. The method may be feasible only in ponds or small lakes with well-defined, easily located spawning areas.
