# Native Fishes and Natural Aquatic Habitats in U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Region II West of the Continental Divide

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 report by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University), prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region II, reviews the population and habitat status of native freshwater fishes in the arid American Southwest west of the [[Continental Divide]]. It concludes that natural aquatic habitats are disappearing under growing demands for water, that native fishes are losing that competition, and that habitat protection, reclamation and intensive species management can still reverse extinction trends if pursued as faunal complexes. Priority is given to already-protected and remote habitats; [[Big-River Fishes]] are the most endangered group, while intermediate-elevation streams hold the most persistent fauna. The author stresses that research on reproduction, species interactions and dispersal must proceed concurrently with recovery actions, and that the impact of [[Non-Native Fishes]] may be the single most important management problem.

## Background & Description

**Scope and structure.** The report covers the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] and the headwaters of three Mexican drainages that barely enter the United States, and includes the White River of eastern Nevada and the Salton Sea of California on the principle that geographic continuity outweighs political boundaries. It is organised in three sections: (I) a regional narrative on habitats and fishes; (II) detailed species accounts; (III) habitat accounts by watershed.

**Context.** The author notes that extinction is "forever," and cites at least 25 North American freshwater fish species or subspecies lost between 1900 and 1981 (Ono et al. 1983). Fifty-four U.S. fishes were listed as threatened or endangered by the Department of Interior (1985), with about 150 more believed in jeopardy. Almost four percent of the nation's 660 freshwater fish species had disappeared and more than 30% had declined to threatened levels. The [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 framed federal action; in Region II about 40 taxa were listed or given candidate status. Recovery work includes captive propagation at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and habitat acquisition such as [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]].

**Regional fauna.** A total of 37 species is known from the Region west of the Continental Divide. Five are restricted in the U.S. to the Rio Sonoyta, Concepcion and Yaqui basins. The lower Colorado River basin holds 31 species, of which 24 are endemic at the species level and six belong to endemic genera. Four subfaunas are recognised: marine species near the river mouth; big-river fishes; a Gila River component; and a "middle Colorado River" subfauna of the Little Colorado, Virgin and White rivers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic habitats.** Much of the region is desert, where evapotranspiration exceeds precipitation. Historically, springs were more numerous, water tables higher, and streams more reliable. Large marsh-lined lowland rivers were fed by canyon-bound intermediate-elevation streams, which were in turn fed by mountain creeks. Floods were moderated by dense [[Riparian Vegetation]] and broad floodplains. Native faunas comprised a few highly adapted species occupying discrete niches for millennia. Aboriginal canal irrigation in central Arizona supported agriculture for more than 100,000 people, but its effects were transient.

**Human modification.** Dams, diversions, groundwater pumping, channelization, overgrazing, floodplain agriculture and watershed uses such as lumbering have dewatered or altered most systems. Incision of channels in the late 1800s is attributed to human influence, possibly with climate shifts. Introduced fishes have been especially damaging; the author notes the number of fish kinds in many western U.S. waters has tripled through naturalisation of introduced forms.

**Figure 2 habitat estimates.** The report's summary estimates the proportion of habitat in four categories (springs/cienegas, high-elevation creeks, intermediate-elevation streams, big rivers) at three quality levels. Intermediate-elevation streams are judged the least damaged, because canyon confinement, shading, permanent bedrock-pool water and large watersheds limit modification and non-native success. [[Aravaipa Creek]] is cited as a relatively unmodified stream holding seven native species.

**Hydrologic change in key systems.**
- **Gila and Salt rivers:** Roosevelt Dam (1913) and later dams, plus [[Granite Reef Diversion]] and [[Coolidge Dam]], dewater the lower courses; Painted Rock Dam captures floodwater.
- **Colorado mainstream:** Storage reservoirs control discharge, cold hypolimnetic releases lower temperatures, and salinity increases downstream.
- **Santa Cruz, San Pedro and lower Little Colorado:** Largely dewatered, with historic fish populations requiring permanent reaches now lost.

**Biological interactions.** Non-native [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) directly preys on [[Sonoran Topminnow]] young and fins, and [[Red Shiner]] displaces [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]]. Stocked rainbow trout hybridise with [[Apache Trout]] and [[Gila Trout]]. Mechanisms of replacement are poorly known except for the mosquitofish-topminnow interaction.

## Key Findings

**Status of the fauna.**
- Among 57 taxa and four problematic populations or groups, at least 50 have lost 50% or more of their historic range since settlement.
- Of these, three are extinct, eight are federally listed as endangered, one as threatened, 11 are proposed for listing, and 12 are candidate species.
- Big-river fishes are the most damaged group; spring-fed and headwater species are next; generalist and intermediate-elevation species are most persistent.
- Big-river fishes include seven species. Five of them (bonytail, Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, woundfin and razorback sucker) are listed or under management; flannelmouth sucker is likely extirpated below Lake Mead.

**Management framework.**
- Fishes should be grouped into three kinds: (1) specialists of large habitats; (2) species isolated in small special habitats that must be maintained in place by purchase or setaside; (3) habitat generalists such as longfin and speckled dace, roundtail chub and some suckers.
- Habitats are to be ranked in six categories: I, undisturbed within natural range (highest "all cost" priority); II, natural range and reclaimable; III, natural range but highly modified; IV, outside natural range but isolated and undisturbed; V, outside native range and otherwise like III; VI, artificial refugia.
- Management plans should be drawn up by basin or subbasin teams combining agency, academic and recovery-team personnel, with monitoring as a central element.

**Priority sites and actions.**
- Protected or remote areas such as the [[Upper Gila River]], [[Aravaipa Creek]], the upper [[Bill Williams River]], and the southern Little Colorado River are singled out as persistent native-fish habitats.
- Recommended actions include acquiring or easing management of Leslie Creek, establishing refugia at San Bernardino NWR, eradicating mosquitofish there, banning live bait (especially red shiner) in Arizona and New Mexico, and opposing dams such as the proposed Conner and Hooker dams.
- Lower Colorado mainstream recovery is recommended via stocking of backwaters and refuge-based rearing, with [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] stocking in the Verde and Salt rivers begun experimentally in 1985.

**Information needs.**
- Basic data are needed on reproduction and recruitment, species interactions and dispersal/dispersion. Short-lived species need semi-annual monitoring; long-lived species such as razorback sucker (over 30 years) need long-term monitoring and brood stock maintenance.
- Predation by introduced species on young natives is a central research question, as are the mechanisms of replacement by non-native fishes.
- The author recommends that every major facility maintain libraries and make information dissemination a formal duty.

**Species-level highlights.**
- *Sonoran chub* (*Gila ditaenia*): restricted in the U.S. to Sycamore Canyon; proposed threatened.
- *Yaqui chub* (*Gila purpurea*): endangered; last natural U.S. locality (Astin Spring) dried in 1969.
- *Yaqui catfish* (*Ictalurus pricei*): threatened; likely no viable U.S. population.
- *Monkey Spring pupfish*: presumed extinct after introduced largemouth bass and a second unauthorised stocking.
- *Las Vegas dace* and *Pahranagat spinedace*: presumed extinct.
- *Bonytail*: most endangered in the Region; only 24 large adults taken in Lake Mohave trammel netting from 1975 onward.

**Overall conclusion.** Extinction of the regional fauna can be reversed if habitats are protected and restored quickly and non-native fishes are controlled. The author stresses that "such an end may only be attained if we move quickly to protect and improve habitats."
