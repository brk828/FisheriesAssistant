# Competition and Isolation Mechanisms in the Gambusia affinis x G. heterochir Hybrid Swarm

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This Texas Memorial Museum bulletin (No. 19, June 1971) by [[Clark Hubbs]] examines a long-running hybrid swarm between [[Gambusia Affinis]] and the endemic [[Gambusia Heterochir]] in the spring-fed headwaters of [[Clear Creek]], [[Menard County]], [[Texas]]. Hybrids made up more than 10% of adults, and the swarm persisted for at least 17 years (first sampled 22 February 1953). The study combines trap-based field sampling, thermal monitoring, diet analysis, fecundity measurements, and egg nutrient assays across 13 monthly visits between September 1967 and October 1968.

The author concludes that the two species occupy distinct thermal and trophic niches, that [[Thermal Stability]] and competition shape their distribution, and that a dam-created break allows *G. affinis* to invade the *G. heterochir* habitat. Hybridization persists because prefertilization isolation is weak, while postfertilization mechanisms, including reduced hybrid fecundity, limit gene flow.

## Background & Description

*Gambusia affinis* and *G. heterochir* are small live-bearing poeciliids that share similar arthropod-based diets and occupy overlapping habitats. Internal fertilization means that hybrids cannot arise by accidental gamete encounters, so their high frequency implies a breakdown of behavioral isolation.

The study site is the headwater system of Clear Creek, which begins with springs at the base of a limestone cliff. An earth-concrete dam built in the 1880s impounded most spring sources. Two further downstream dams were built about 40 years later and 30 years before the first sampling. The dam had a break and spillway through which fish could move in both directions. Nutria later decimated cattails and pecan trees in the lower pond, but these changes appeared to have minimal effect on relative fish densities.

**Methods (summary):**
- Fish were collected in plastic minnow traps (42 cm long, baited with dog food), set between about 1000 and 1200 hours and removed between 1700 and 1900 hours.
- Approximately 30 trap stations were distributed along shore series (A–R) above and below the dam.
- Size was recorded as standard length; the conversion from total length was approximately 4/5 (0.78 males, 0.79 females).
- Hybrid status was assigned using a male and female hybrid index code, with cutoffs such as females ≤80 *affinis*, ≥95 *heterochir*, and 86–90 as hybrids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regimes.** Stations were divided into thermally stable (spring-influenced; coefficients of variation of 1.83–18.80) and thermally variable (lower pool; coefficients above 30) groups. Stable spring areas have lower pH and host a different amphipod and *Ceratophyllum* morph, and they are where *G. heterochir* predominates.

**Community.** The fish fauna is depauperate relative to typical central Texas streams (14–18 species). Cyprinids are essentially absent. The only other fishes recorded were *Dionda episcopa* (the one cyprinid), *Lepomis megalotis* and other sunfishes, *Micropterus salmoides* (largemouth bass), and *Ictalurus natalis*. Predatory snakes (*Natrix*, *Thamnophis*) and amphibian larvae were also present. Hubbs argues that the lack of cyprinids and predators lets the two *Gambusia* occupy most available habitats.

**Sampling scale.** Preserved samples contained more than 95,323 *Gambusia*, and 1,028 live fish were used in tests. Fewer than 1,000 other vertebrates were recovered.

**Historical context.** Pre-impoundment conditions were inferred from a single 19th-century collection and landowner accounts. The author suggests that heterochir predominated along the slow spring-fed creek and that impoundment allowed *affinis* to expand.

## Key Findings

**1. Hybrid swarm persistence and genetic integrity**
- Hybrids exceeded 10% of adults in every study, persisting at least 17 years.
- Morphology changed little, but ecological distinction increased. Males were judged about 8.6% more similar and females about 8.7% more distinct in the 1967–68 samples than in 1956–57.
- Above the dam, *affinis* to *heterochir* ratios shifted from near 1:1 in 1956–57 to more than 5 *heterochir* per *affinis* ten years later.

**2. Distribution and migration**
- *G. heterochir* is essentially restricted to the upper pool above the dam; almost all *heterochir* below the dam are female.
- *G. affinis* is most abundant in fluctuating-temperature habitats in summer and in constant-temperature habitats in winter. Hubbs interprets this as a fall–winter migration toward the spring-fed areas.
- Sparse stations above the dam showed a strong sex-species reversal: about 70% of males were *heterochir* and about 70% of females were *affinis*.

**3. Sampling and capture behavior**
- Trap captures peaked in daylight and were lowest at night. *G. affinis* showed a morning peak near 1100 hours; *G. heterochir* was more active around 0700 and 1300 hours. Both species peaked at dusk (about 1900).
- Of 22,290 fish from 2-hour trap intervals, more than 10 fish per trap occurred between 0800 and 2000 hours, versus fewer than 2 between 2200 and 0600 hours.
- Traps left longer than 2 hours showed declining fish per trap-hour, suggesting escape. Fish were most abundant in shallow, densely vegetated water.

**4. Size, growth, and sex ratio**
- Females grow until maturity, with two generations per year. Males stop growing at maturity, and midsummer males are much smaller.
- Above the dam, stations averaged 140±59 females and 74±17 males; single-trap stations below the dam averaged 86±37 females and 19±5 males.
- Smaller males are thought to be excluded by dominance hierarchies, and the author links male size to access to females.

**5. Diet**
- Amphipods predominated in *heterochir* guts, insects in *affinis*, and both occurred in hybrids. The difference was greatest in summer and fall.
- The chi-square for the upper-pool diet comparison was 95.692 (1 df), far above significance.
- In winter, *affinis* switched to amphipods, which the author argues makes *affinis* swamp *heterochir* in the lower pool.

**6. Fecundity**
- July 1969 data from 13,955 reproductive *affinis* females show fecundity rising sharply with size, with the best-fit model starting reproduction at 11 mm (exponent 1.90).
- Relative fecundity: below the dam, fluctuating-temperature *affinis* outproduced consistent-temperature females by 1.49 young per brood. Upper-pool *affinis* had about 93% of the fecundity of lower-pool *affinis*.
- *G. heterochir* females had about 52% of *affinis* fecundity in optimal environments, but eggs were larger (1.75 mm vs 1.42 mm diameter) and more calorie-rich. Calorie values roughly doubled from *affinis* (index ~77) to *heterochir* (index ~100).
- Adjusting for egg volume and calories, *heterochir* fecundity above the dam was estimated at 1.43 and 1.23 times that of *affinis* there.

**7. Reproductive timing and isolation**
- Interbrood intervals at 25°C averaged 35.14 ± 1.978 days for *affinis* and 42.00 ± 2.142 days for *heterochir*. Intervals shorten at higher temperatures (about 3 weeks near 30°C).
- Females isolated in January still produced fertile broods, suggesting retained sperm.
- Peden's work showed that copulatory thrust directions differ between species, making heterospecific copulation less likely to succeed. Mate-choice tests showed weak homospecific preference.

**8. Isolation mechanisms**
- Following Liley (1966), the author concludes that prefertilization isolation (seasonal, habitat, ethological, mechanical) is inefficient here, while postfertilization mechanisms (reduced hybrid fecundity, backcross scarcity) limit introgression.
- The male hybrid index distribution is trimodal, with few backcrosses, indicating that F1 hybrids are more common than backcrosses.

**9. Experimental tests**
- In 3 m artificial pools, mixed populations were pure *affinis* after six months at ambient temperature. Pools with reduced thermal extremes retained a few *heterochir*, supporting competition as a major factor.

**10. Overall conclusion**
- Hybridization is maintained by dam-driven habitat disturbance, sex-biased migration and dominance, and inefficient prefertilization isolation. Each species nonetheless maintains a distinct, integrated gene pool.
