# Primary Production in Inland Waters

## Executive Summary
This 1958 review by J.W.G. Lund (Freshwater Biological Association) addresses the state of knowledge on primary production in British inland waters, emphasizing that most research has focused narrowly on **phytoplankton** while neglecting **phytobenthos** (attached algae, charophytes, and higher plants), which may contribute more biomass than plankton in many shallow waters, reed beds, and rivers. The paper argues that primary production does not straightforwardly translate into secondary (animal) production due to the complexity of food webs, and that quality — not just quantity — of plant material matters for downstream consumers, including [[Freshwater Fish]]. Lund reviews methodological challenges in measuring production (oxygen curves, CO₂/bicarbonate uptake, ¹⁴C uptake), and discusses the physical (light, temperature, water residence time) and chemical (nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium) controls on algal crops in British lakes and rivers. The paper closes with commentary on the economic dimensions of primary production, including its role in traditional [[Fish Farming]] practices in Asia and its status as a nuisance for British water utilities.

## Background & Description
*Not directly applicable — this source addresses ecosystem-level primary production processes rather than organismal morphology. Relevant structural/functional distinctions discussed include:*

- **Phytoplankton**: free-floating algae inhabiting open waters of lakes, ponds, rivers, and seas.
- **Phytobenthos**: algae, charophytes (e.g., *Chara*), and higher plants attached to substrates (rocks, mud, stones, other plants); includes rooted forms such as *Littorella*.
- **Nannoplankton**: small-celled planktonic forms, noted as having a distinct and less-understood ecological role compared to larger algae.
- Standing crop biomass of rooted plants (e.g., *Chara*, *Littorella*) can represent over a year's cumulative production, contrasted with planktonic algal crops that turn over on a 2–3 month cycle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Trophic pathways**: The classic view of phytoplankton being heavily grazed by zooplankton is challenged for larger freshwater algae. Instead, the primary pathway is proposed to be bacterial decomposition of algae in open water, or ingestion of live/dead algae by scavenging invertebrates once settled to the bottom — establishing more of a "food web" than a linear "food chain."
- **Link to fish production**: Phytobenthos is highlighted as potentially critical to food chains supporting [[Freshwater Fish]] populations, even in lakes where plankton is abundant. However, increased primary production does not guarantee proportional increases in secondary (fish) production due to trophic transfer inefficiencies and the number of intermediate links.
- **Habitat stratification effects**: In thermally stratified British lakes, only the illuminated upper (epilimnetic) zone supports net positive photosynthesis; the deeper, colder hypolimnion is too poorly lit for meaningful production, and algae sinking below the thermocline are effectively lost from the productive zone until stratification breaks down.
- **Lotic (river) systems**: Rivers, apart from a few extremely slow-flowing or canal-like ones, generally lack well-developed phytoplankton due to rapid water replacement (only the Thames above its estuary is noted as an exception). Benthic (attached) algae dominate primary production in most rivers, though they are vulnerable to physical disturbance (scouring, substrate overturning, abrasion).
- **Nutrient and geographic gradients**: Northern and western British waters (mountainous, non-calcareous, low agricultural input) are nutrient-poor (low calcium, nitrogen, phosphorus) and support lower algal standing crops than southern/lowland waters enriched by agriculture and sewage effluent. Downstream reaches and lower reservoirs in a chain tend to be more nutrient-rich than headwaters.
- **Anthropogenic inputs**: Sewage effluent (particularly phosphorus-rich) generally fertilizes downstream waters and boosts algal growth, except where pollution is severe/toxic, in which case algal growth is suppressed near the outfall before rebounding further downstream.
- **Aquaculture linkage**: Freshwater algal production underpins traditional [[Fish Farming]] systems, notably in China (practiced for over a millennium), where organic and inorganic fertilization maintains high algal biomass — though this is understood to support fish nutrition indirectly (e.g., via metabolites) rather than solely as direct algal food supply.

## Key Findings
- **Measurement methods**: Primary production is best measured via oxygen production, CO₂/bicarbonate uptake, or ¹⁴C uptake; the ¹⁴C method allows high-precision estimation on small natural algal populations. Standing crop remains a widely used but imperfect proxy for production rate.
- **Only British field data**: At the time of writing, the only primary production estimates for British waters were derived from diurnal oxygen curves in the Rivers Itchen and Lark (Butcher, Pentelow & Woodley 1930, as analyzed by Odum 1956).
- **Light and temperature co-control photosynthesis**: At low light intensities, photosynthesis is proportional to light; at high intensities it becomes light-saturated and can only be increased via temperature increase. This general limiting-factor relationship applies across algal taxa, though specific light/temperature optima vary by species.
- **Seasonal succession, not seasonal crop-size divergence**: Despite strong seasonal light/temperature variation, standing crop size often does not differ greatly between spring, summer, and autumn — instead, rapid nutrient depletion drives succession among algal species/groups, maintaining roughly similar total biomass even as community composition shifts.
- **Nutrient limitation**: Nitrogen and phosphorus are likely dominant limiting nutrients (as in marine systems), though quantitative relationships with calcium and other elements remain unclear for freshwater systems specifically.
- **Hydrology strongly regulates production**: High rainfall and runoff increase water replacement rates in lakes, which can both replenish nutrients and flush out algal populations — if replacement is fast enough, plankton cannot establish (explaining scarcity of riverine plankton in Britain).
- **Regional productivity gradient**: Northern/western Britain (mountainous, nutrient-poor, high rainfall) supports lower algal standing crops than southern/eastern lowland waters, due to combined physical (light, temperature, flushing) and chemical (nutrient poverty) constraints.
- **Economic/management perspective**: There is little economic incentive to boost freshwater primary production in Britain (unlike in fish-farming regions of Asia); rather, UK water authorities (e.g., the [[Metropolitan Water Board]]) are primarily concerned with suppressing algal production in reservoirs used for drinking water supply.
- **Fish production ceiling**: Even under optimal conditions, Lund concludes that British freshwater fish production could never approach the scale of marine fish harvests, reflecting fundamental limits on inland primary and secondary production capacity relative to the sea.
