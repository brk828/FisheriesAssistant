# Migrations of adult king salmon based on sonic telemetry

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report, [[Fish Bulletin 151]] from the California Department of Fish and Game, documents a four-year study (1964–1967) of adult [[King Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) migrating through the [[San Joaquin Delta]]. The authors used [[Sonic Tagging]] to follow individual fish. A total of 316 salmon were fitted with sonic tags and released, mainly at [[Prisoners Point]]. The report covers the capture and tagging method, the tag attachment trials, the sex and size of tagged fish, and observed behavior in tidal currents. The study was conducted by [[Richard J. Hallock]], [[Robert F. Elwell]] and [[Donald H. Fry Jr.]]

## Background & Description

The San Joaquin salmon run had been very low in 1963, which made it uncertain whether enough fish could be caught for tagging in 1964. The 1964 tagging policy was summarized as "get them while you can." Tagged fish were caught with [[Trammel Net]] and [[Gill Net]] gear. Former commercial fishermen were involved in releasing tangled fish unharmed.

Tagging procedure:

- **Handling:** Netted fish were lifted into the boat, untangled and tagged in a V-shaped cradle. Tagging took about 30 seconds, and fish were out of water for about two minutes in total.
- **Revival:** Fish that were slow to recover were given artificial respiration by moving them up and down in the water to pass water over the gills. Most began to struggle after about 10 seconds (6 or 7 strokes) and were released.
- **Anesthesia:** [[MS-222]] was used briefly at first but was discontinued because gill-net-caught fish were already placid. All three early tagging mortalities had been anesthetized.
- **Attachment:** Each [[Sonic Tag]] was fastened just forward of the dorsal fin, with its axis parallel to the fish. Two plastic straps and two pins secured the tag. Strap and pin materials changed over the years, from soft vinyl and surgical tubing (1964) to nylon (1965) and [[Mylar]] straps with nylon pins (1966–1967).

Tag attachment trials were run in 1965 with January and February spawning fish held at [[Coleman National Fish Hatchery]]. Twelve fish were tagged and held in a pond, and ten died. Recovery occurred 18 to 65 days after tagging. Tags placed forward of the dorsal fin stayed in place, while rear-mounted straps pulled out. Forward placement with 3/32-inch pins was judged best. Placing a tag inside the stomach was abandoned because the body wall muffled the sound and reduced reception range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study took place in the tidal channels of the [[San Joaquin Delta]], where tagged fish were tracked as they moved between the river and the [[Sacramento River]]. Tagging in 1964 was moved upstream from [[Schad Landing]] after most captured fish were found to come from the Sacramento River. [[Sevenmile Slough]], roughly halfway between Prisoners Point and Schad Landing, was used for a small number of releases.

Tagged fish showed no consistent pattern in relation to [[Tidal Currents]]. After release, fish initially swam away from the boat into the current. Afterward, some fish were stationary, some moved against the tide, and some moved with it, on both flood and ebb tides. During slack water, fish moved in any direction or stayed still.

The report also notes that the sex ratio of tagged fish and the mesh selectivity of trammel nets may bias the sample. The trammel nets were selective against jacks, but among larger salmon they were less size-selective than gill nets.

## Key Findings

**Tagging effort (316 fish, 1964–1967)**

| Year | Release site(s) | Tagged salmon |
|------|-----------------|---------------|
| 1964 | Schad Landing (49, Sept.), Prisoners Point (46, Oct.–Nov.), Sevenmile Slough (1) | 96 |
| 1965 | Prisoners Point (67), Sevenmile Slough (2) | 69 |
| 1966 | Prisoners Point | 63 |
| 1967 | Prisoners Point | 88 |

- Total tagged and released: **316 salmon**.
- Tagging targets were 10 fish per week (1965–1966, no more than 5 per day) and 20 per two-week period (1967, no more than 10 per day). Targets were often not met early or late in the season.
- Of the 220 salmon measured and sexed in 1965–1967, **158 (72%) were females**. The proportion of females was 71%, 72% and 73% in the three years.
- **About 80% of tagged fish were 30 to 38 inches (76 to 97 cm) long.** Measurements were taken as fork length to the nearest half inch.

**Method performance**

- Early in the program, three tagging mortalities occurred, all in anesthetized fish.
- Tags forward of the dorsal fin were retained. Rear-mounted tags pulled out.
- The softer nylon strap caused less abrasion than the mylar strap, but both were reasonably satisfactory. Soft vinyl straps were cut by the belt loops.
- The authors concluded that forward attachment with pins was the best option, and this method was used in 1966 and 1967.

**Behavior**

- No consistent movement with or against tidal currents was detected.
- Fish swam away from the boat into the current immediately after release, but this response was not consistent afterward.
