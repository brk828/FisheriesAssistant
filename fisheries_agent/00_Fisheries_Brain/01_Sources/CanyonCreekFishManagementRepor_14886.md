# Canyon Creek Fish Management Report (1990)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 report by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) evaluates a multi-year habitat restoration and fisheries management program on [[Canyon Creek]], a cold-water stream in the [[Tonto National Forest]]. The program, the [[Canyon Creek Aquatic Habitat Improvement Plan]] (CCAHIP, 1986), aimed to produce a quality "[[Blue Ribbon Fishery]]" for [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] by 1991. Fish surveys, creel censuses, habitat surveys, photo points and water temperature monitoring were run from 1987 to 1989.

Brown trout populations and catch rates rose over the period, and habitat measures (in-stream cover, pools, bank stability) improved in reaches 3 and 4. Stocked rainbow trout were largely caught within 11 days and showed little overwintering. The authors recommended a 12-inch minimum size limit, a 2-fish bag limit, a shift from catchable to sub-catchable stocking, and continued habitat work in reach 5.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Canyon Creek lies about 16 miles northeast of Young, Gila County, at roughly 6,200 feet elevation. The sport fishery extends about 5 miles from the springhead to the Fort Apache Indian Reservation boundary.

**Management history.** The stream was managed as an intensive put-and-take rainbow trout fishery with grown-out brown trout. Between 9,200 and 11,092 rainbow trout were stocked annually from mid-April through September on a bi-weekly schedule. A single brown trout fingerling stocking (6,000 fish) occurred in spring 1983. A 1986 postal angler survey estimated 20,334 angler-use days during the April–September high-use period.

**Problems identified.** The CCAHIP named three major limits on fish growth and reproduction:
- Thermal loading, caused by lack of shade
- High siltation from erosion
- Lack of pools, caused by stream scouring

**Planned solutions.** These included riparian tree planting, beaver control, bank re-vegetation, in-stream structure installation and riparian fencing. Responsibilities were set out in a Memorandum of Understanding among the U.S. Forest Service, AGFD and [[Trout Unlimited]], with AGFD tasked with beaver suppression, fisheries assessment (fish and creel surveys) and streamside habitat inventories.

**Methods (summary).**
- **Reaches:** The stream was divided into 7 reaches of about 1,000 m each, totalling 8,151 m (5.07 miles) per the text (Table 2 totals 8,154 m).
- **Fish surveys:** Late-summer sampling in 1987, 1988 and 1989 used a DC [[Backpack Electrofishing]] unit across riffle, run and pool habitats, with two concurrent passes per station. Densities were expressed per square meter.
- **Creel surveys:** Randomly timed surveys (minimum 8 days per season) recorded effort, success, bait type, angler origin and answers to eight subjective questions (see [[Creel Survey]]).
- **Habitat:** Stream photo points (established 1986, photographed annually through 1989), July 1989 habitat surveys (overstory, substrate, flow, pools) and 1989 thermograph monitoring at two sites.
- **Beaver trapping:** Live trapping with clamshell traps at active dens (1986–1988).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish community.** Four species were consistently recorded: [[Speckled Dace]] (most abundant overall), [[Gila Mountain Sucker]] (abundant in riffles and runs), Brown Trout and Rainbow Trout. Squawfish appeared in the April 1987 sample only. The 1984 pre-program survey showed suckers and dace providing a forage base for trout.

**Riparian and in-stream habitat.**
- Cottonwood and willow plantings from 1986–1988 had mixed success (20–40%).
- 65 in-stream structures were installed from 1986–1988, mainly in reaches 3–4.
- Riparian fencing was built from the OW Bridge downstream, completed in 1989, and was breached by cattle in several years. In 1988, when no livestock grazed the pasture, the fence was effective.
- Preliminary 1989 habitat results showed no detectable increase in overhead canopy in most reaches, but significant gains in in-stream cover (reaches 3, 4, 5), bank stability (reaches 2, 3, 4) and pool percentage (reaches 3 and 4).

**Water temperature.** Thermograph data from June–September 1989 showed:
- Site 1 (below the OW Bridge): maximum 20.79 °C (69 °F), minimum 7.4 °C (45 °F), mean 13.9 °C (57 °F), average daily fluctuation 7.5 °C.
- Site 2 (3,011 m downstream): maximum 26.7 °C (80 °F), minimum 9.29 °C (49 °F), mean 17.3 °C (63 °F), average daily fluctuation 10.9 °C.

The authors attributed the cooler, less variable conditions at Site 1 to vegetation and bank cover. A 1987 USFS reading at Site 1 had recorded a maximum of 23 °C, so summer maxima had dropped.

**Beaver.** Beaver were removed to protect new plantings: 6 animals in 1986 (4 died of hyperthermia linked to malnutrition on necropsy, 2 relocated), 2 in 1987 (both relocated) and none in 1988. Beaver activity appeared lower in 1988, although 193 trap-hours were spent that year.

**Stocking and survival.** Rainbow trout were caught out within about 11 days of stocking (see [[Stocking Strategy]]). Little winter holdover was evident, and no rainbow trout over 260 mm were found in the 1988 sample.

## Key Findings

**Fish populations**
- Brown trout CPUE rose from 4.47 (1987) to 14.54 (1989). Density rose from 0.03 fish/m² (1984) to 0.10 fish/m² (1989). The authors attributed this to better habitat quality and diversity.
- Rainbow trout CPUE rose from 1.69 (1987) to 5.08 (1989). Density stayed at 0.02–0.03 fish/m².
- Brown trout reproduction was evident in reaches 1 and 4. Rainbow trout reproduction was minimal and mainly in reach 1.
- 1989 population expansion estimates were 6,281 brown trout (61% in the smallest size class, reported as 200 mm, 37% at 200–350 mm, 2% above 351 mm) and 2,323 rainbow trout (11% under 180 mm, 89% at 181–270 mm).
- Mule Creek, surveyed in 1988 and 1989, had brown trout making up 70% of the 1988 sample. Juvenile trout indicated reproduction, and speckled dace were present in 1989.

**Angler use and success**
- Weekend catch rates were 0.47 FPH (1987) and 0.84 FPH (1989). Weekday rates were 0.34 and 0.46 FPH.
- Overall success declined from 37% (1987) to 29% (1989).
- Release rates rose from 22% (1984, pre-program) to 45% (1987), 75% (1988) and 62% (1989).
- Rainbow trout made up about 95% of the catch and 97% of the harvest. Brown trout were rarely caught.
- Most anglers came from Maricopa County (average 88%). Flies were used by 65% of anglers in 1987, falling to 61% in 1989.

**Angler opinion (subjective survey)**
- Goal shifted from limit (70% in 1987) to variety of sizes (65% in 1988, 73% in 1989).
- About 80% supported the artificial fly and lure restriction, and 60% supported a minimum size regulation.
- 20% reported seeing bait violations.

**Enforcement**
- Of 648 anglers checked in 1988–1989, 15 (2%) were illegally using bait, giving 98% adult compliance. Juveniles caught using bait were not cited.
- Including 30 other citations, overall compliance was about 95%.

**Habitat and restoration**
- Habitat gains in reaches 3–4 were judged to meet optimal conditions for both trout species.
- Reach 5 still lacked in-stream structures, and cattle trespass threatened new vegetation.

**Costs**
- Total monitoring cost was $30,927, including 155 mandays and 14,960 miles of driving, $2,125 in per diem and $3,000 in supplies and equipment.

**Recommendations**
1. From 1 January 1990, impose a 12-inch minimum size and a 2-fish bag limit on all trout (see [[Bag Limit]]).
2. Replace annual catchable rainbow stocking (about 11,000 fish) with a one-time spring plant of 4,000 six-inch fish, using domestic strains ("Bobby Williams" and/or "Lost River") until strain performance is assessed.
3. Continue in-stream habitat work through reach 5 using boulder and log structures.
4. Hire a seasonal stream manager (May–August) to run creel censuses, maintain structures and monitor the riparian fence.
5. Continue annual monitoring of fish, angler use and habitat, and file an annual summary.
6. Run an information and education program on the new regulations.

**Overall conclusion.** The report judged Canyon Creek to be "healing," with brown trout reproduction and population growth, but called for further work before the full 5.5 miles could be managed as a Blue Ribbon fishery.
