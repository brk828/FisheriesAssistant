# Functional Anatomy and Evolution of Male Genitalia in Poeciliid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 monograph by [[Donn Eric Rosen]] and [[Myron Gordon]] (New York Aquarium, New York Zoological Society) examines the functional anatomy of the male intromittent organ, the [[Gonopodium]], in [[Poeciliidae]] (live-bearing fishes). It combines histology, vascular and nerve mapping, muscle mechanics, behavioral observation and comparison with other cyprinodontiform families. The authors describe the sliding, folding and rotation mechanisms that form a transitory sperm trough, catalog terminal holdfast and sensory structures, and propose that the poeciliid gonopodium evolved from a simple, cyprinodontid-like anal fin. The paper also includes a systematic inventory of the family's genera and gonopodial structures, which the authors note may require partial taxonomic revision.

## Background & Description

- **Family scope:** Poeciliids are generally small fishes; the largest species does not exceed eight inches, and adult males of [[Heterandria Formosa]] rarely exceed three-quarters of an inch. Under the taxonomy used, the family comprises six subfamilies, eight tribes and forty-five genera.
- **Reproduction:** Poeciliids are viviparous, with internal fertilization made possible by the gonopodium, a modified anal fin. Broods reach as many as 315 young. Exceptionally, [[Tomeurus Gracilis]] from northern South America is not viviparous. Superfoetation occurs in some groups, where successive broods or embryos overlap in development.
- **Distribution:** The most widespread groups are the Gambusiini and Poeciliini. Cnesterodontini occur in east-central South America, Girardinini are restricted to Cuba, and Xiphophorini are found in freshwater streams of the Atlantic slope from Mexico to Honduras.
- **Gonopodium structure:** Rays 3, 4 and 5 are enlarged and strengthened, while the remaining rays are small and lie near the fin base. The organ is rigid and I-beam-like in cross-section. [[Xiphophorus Helleri]] is used as the reference species. Its ray 3 forms the main support, bears a hook, and is fused to a gristly blade that contains the only true bone cells in the gonopodium.
- **Hood terminology:** The integumentary thickening on ray 3 has been called léffel, hood, prepuce-like hood and gonopodial hood. The authors consider these structures probably homologous.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sexual isolation:** Behavioral differences between sympatric [[Xiphophorus Maculatus]] and X. helleri act as an effective sexual isolating mechanism (Clark, Aronson & Gordon, 1953).
- **Habitat and copulation:** Clark & Rosen (unpublished) suggested that fishes in turbulent waters may have stronger holdfast elements than those in slow waters. The authors also suggest that [[Lebistes Reticulatus]] living in rapid waters in Trinidad may need their claws for successful copulation.
- **Comparative cyprinodonts:** Oviparous [[Fundulus Heteroclitus]] and related killifishes show S-curve clasping during spawning, with anal and pelvic fins interlocked. Their anal fins may deflect sperm toward falling eggs. [[Anableps Anableps]] has an asymmetric, spiraled gonopodium that forms a tube continuous with the sperm duct. [[Horaichthys Setnai]] uses an elaborate anal-fin rod to attach barbed spermatophores externally.
- **Fossil evidence:** The Oligocene (possibly Pleistocene) fossil [[Proballostomus Longulus]] from South Dakota shows an anal-fin suspensorium with cephalad-arched hemal spines, closely resembling that of living cyprinodontids and suggesting a preadaptation for gonopodial evolution.

## Key Findings

1. **Gonopodial mechanics:** During erection, rays 4 and 5 slip backward and fold sideways against ray 3. Their collagenous binding sheets stretch, creating a transitory trough for spermatophore transfer. Ray 3 controls the abduction of the gonopodium and acts as the center of movement.
2. **Musculature:** Two erector muscles (erector analis major and minor) drive the forward swing. The erector analis major was estimated to rotate the fin through slightly more than 90°, and the two muscles together reach about 145°. Lateral rotator muscles, tensors, inclinators and depressors control orientation and release.
3. **Vascular system:** Three central arterioles supply blood to the gonopodium. The efferent (venous) system is larger and more numerous than the afferent system, with approximately three times as many venous routes as arterial supply lines.
4. **Innervation:** Three main nerve routes run in the cores of rays 3, 4 and 5. Spines on ray 3 are interpreted as sensory structures, with elaborate nerve supply and no muscle tissue.
5. **Holdfast devices:** In [[Xiphophorus]], holdfast relies on a dorsal claw or distal serrae plus a ventral hook. Clawless male guppies in laboratory experiments could still inseminate females, and amputated gonopodia regenerated. Copulations in Xiphophorus lasted about five or six seconds, compared with about two seconds in Lebistes.
6. **Symmetry:** Most poeciliids have bilaterally symmetrical gonopodia and can copulate from either side. Poeciliopsines (e.g., [[Poeciliopsis]], [[Phallichthys Amates]]) have permanently folded, asymmetric gonopodia (mostly sinistral) and copulate from one side only.
7. **Length and insertion:** Gonopodial length is about 25% of standard length in Xiphophorini and Poeciliini, and 50–70% in Gambusiinae, Poeciliopsinae, Xenodexiinae and Tomeurinae. The authors found no constant correlation between gonopodial length and female receptivity, contrary to Regan (1913).
8. **Evolutionary hypothesis:** The poeciliid gonopodium probably evolved from a cyprinodontid-like anal fin. Key changes include elongation of rays 3–5, their functional separation from posterior rays, enlargement and cephalad reorientation of hemal and interhemal supports, and development of powerful gonopodial muscles. Symmetrical gonopodia likely preceded asymmetrical forms in the poeciliopsines.
9. **Fluid physiology:** In viviparous species, spermatozoa are ejaculated in passive spermatophores within viscous fluid. The female ovarian secretion lowers this fluid's viscosity, which the authors propose lets sperm enter the oviduct, possibly by capillary action.
10. **Taxonomic notes:** Tomeurus may warrant separation as the family Tomeuridae. Pamphorichthys may be a synonym of Parapoecilia. The authors state that their findings may partially revise the classification but do not undertake that revision.
