# Agency Recalcitrance and Evasion Regarding Compliance with the National Environmental Policy Act Relating to Glen Canyon Dam Operations: A Documented Need for Congressional Intervention

#ichthyology #marine-biology #policy-analysis

## Executive Summary

This preliminary technical report by Bob Lippman (March 1990, OCR-recovered text) argues that the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (BOR) has, for roughly two decades, avoided the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) obligations triggered by operation of [[Glen Canyon Dam]], which was completed in 1963, six years before NEPA took effect. The author contends that lawsuits, operational changes, power plant upratings and the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program should have required an Environmental Impact Statement (EIS). The report traces a sequence of abandoned environmental assessments, court stipulations that were not honored, and short-term studies that deferred compliance. It concludes that these actions produced accelerated erosion of beaches and riparian habitat in [[Grand Canyon National Park]] and that Congressional investigation and legislation are required.

## Background & Description

- **Authorizing legislation:** The [[Colorado River Storage Project]] (CRSP), authorized in 1956 (PL 84-485), was designed to regulate river flow, store water, reclaim arid land, control floods and generate hydropower as an incidental purpose (43 USC 620). The [[Colorado River Basin Project Act]] (1968) reiterated these priorities.
- **Glen Canyon Dam:** Completed in 1963, originally designed to impound 27 million acre-feet in [[Lake Powell]] and generate 1,150 megawatts. The reported storage figure is 8.23 million acre-feet, and the dam's operations were intended for base-load generation.
- **Hydrologic change:** Closing the gates altered the river from seasonal, sediment-laden flows to clear, colder, controlled releases. Peak flows averaged roughly 90,000 cfs during pre-dam spring runoff, and post-dam releases could fluctuate from about 1,000 cfs to 28,000 cfs or greater, a pattern driven by peaking power demand.
- **Ecological consequences cited:** Loss of sand deposition, beach and riparian erosion, dense vegetation encroaching on channels, growing debris-flow hazards in side canyons, and extirpation of several formerly abundant fish species, some now listed as endangered or extinct.
- **Legal framework:** NEPA (42 USC 4321 et seq., effective January 1, 1970) requires a detailed statement on major federal actions significantly affecting the human environment, including actions begun before 1970 that continue to have effects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report emphasises that Glen Canyon Dam's flow regime is the principal driver of downstream change in Grand Canyon. Rapid ramping rates and fluctuating releases were said to affect recreation, aquatic resources and shorelines. The 1988 GCES Final Report, cited by the author, found that flood releases damage beaches and terrestrial resources, that such floods would occur in about one of every four years under current operations, that fluctuating releases primarily affect recreation and aquatic resources, and that modified operations could protect or enhance most resources. The same report acknowledged that understanding of dam-downstream relationships was incomplete. The National Academy of Sciences and the GCES Executive Review Committee reportedly judged the phase one information unreliable because of data limitations.

Record precipitation in 1983 and 1984 produced uncontrolled post-dam flows and bypass spills that caused severe impacts along the entire lower Colorado River.

## Key Findings

**Legal history**
- The Department of the Interior (DOI) and BOR formally began the NEPA process for Glen Canyon Dam in 1975 and published a draft environmental assessment (DEA) in January 1976, but never completed hearings, a final assessment or a Finding of No Significant Impact (FONSI).
- In *Badoni v. Higginson* (D. Utah 1977), the court held the NEPA issue unripe but noted the DOI was actively considering its applicability.
- In *EDF v. Higginson* (D.C. Cir. 1980-1981), BOR initially agreed to prepare a Comprehensive Environmental Impact Statement (CEIS), then shifted to site-specific statements addressing cumulative impacts. The appeals court affirmed the duty to prepare EIS documents but remanded the shift to site-specific statements.
- A 1982 stipulated settlement committed BOR and DOI to prepare EISs on Colorado River hydropower facilities with cumulative impacts analysed. The author argues this was not implemented.

**Operational and structural actions cited as major federal actions**
- Post-1970 operating criteria for Glen Canyon Dam, promulgated in 1980 and revisable every five years, are described as unlawful without NEPA review.
- The 1977 [[Western Energy Expansion Study]] (WEES) proposed a 250-megawatt addition to outlet works and a 104-megawatt uprating of existing turbines. The report notes the proposals were rated on twelve evaluation factors but lacked NEPA compliance.
- A 1979 BOR expansion study proposed raising high releases from 28,000–33,000 cfs to 40,000 cfs for short periods, with lower average low flows.
- A 1979 BOR-prepared Environmental Assessment for power plant upratings was reportedly criticised as understating impacts and was later withdrawn or disputed, with critiques prepared by outside consultants.

**Congressional and litigation history**
- Testimony before House Interior Committee oversight hearings (September 1983) reportedly showed operations were driven by hydroelectric revenue rather than CRSP's primary purposes.
- A 1989 notice of intent to prepare a draft EIS on Glen Canyon operations was published in the Federal Register on October 30, 1989. A separate suit in the U.S. District Court (Case No. W-C-1128-G) reportedly led to a court-ordered injunction against a fifteen-year power contract and related sales.

**Author's conclusions and recommendations**
- The record shows "apparent fraud, deceit, contempt of court, and bad faith" on the part of BOR, according to the author.
- The author argues that the NEPA threshold has been engaged and that the agency is estopped from denying its stipulated EIS commitments.
- Thirteen years of post-NEPA threshold delay are said to have imposed unnecessary, accelerated impacts within Grand Canyon National Park, which the author estimates covers roughly one-half of the dam's operating life to date.
- Recommended Congressional actions include:
  1. Immediate investigation and oversight of BOR's NEPA obligations and operations.
  2. Legislation directing a site-specific Glen Canyon EIS based on the preferred alternative of maximum attainable impact mitigation.
  3. Returning Glen Canyon Dam to a base-loaded flow regime.
  4. Studying restoration and stabilisation technologies for the Grand Canyon riparian environment.
  5. Interim flow management.
  6. A DOI comprehensive EIS on system-wide operations and cumulative impacts across all Colorado River dams.
  7. Congressional oversight of BOR's NEPA compliance.

The author argues these measures are necessary because continued agency evasion will otherwise allow irreversible damage to Grand Canyon's beaches and aquatic and riparian ecosystems to continue.
