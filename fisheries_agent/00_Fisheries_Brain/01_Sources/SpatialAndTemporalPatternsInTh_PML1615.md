# Spatial and Temporal Patterns in Fish Assemblages of Individual Stream Pools

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 study in *Environmental Biology of Fishes* (Matthews, Cashner & Powell) tests whether individual [[Stream Pools]] are biologically discrete units for stream fishes. Fish assemblages were counted by [[Snorkeling Surveys]] in 14 adjacent pools of a 1 km reach of [[Brier Creek]], Oklahoma, over eight census dates spanning roughly 19 months (November 1982 to May 1984), including two major floods. The reach-level fish fauna was highly consistent across time. Individual pools were generally consistent, but showed more variation than the whole reach. Most pools stayed within discrete subsets of multivariate space, and [[Largemouth Bass]] appeared to constrain numbers of small prey fishes in pools, though this was not absolute. The authors conclude that individual pools separated by shallow riffles are an appropriate spatial scale for biological research and monitoring.

## Background & Description

Stream fish community stability has previously been studied at the scale of entire streams, 100–300 m reaches, 10 m sections, 4–15 m mesohabitats, and individual pools and riffles. The authors argue that spatial scale should be chosen according to biologically meaningful units, such as pools separated by partial barriers, rather than by arbitrary linear distances.

Shallow [[Riffles]] (typically 5–10 cm deep at base flow) act as semi-permeable barriers. Small fishes such as cyprinids cross them readily, while large [[Largemouth Bass]] cross infrequently and with difficulty.

Brier Creek is a low-gradient, prairie-margin stream (24 km mainstream length, 30 known fish species) in Marshall County, Oklahoma. Headwaters drain pasture and cease to flow for much of the year, while the midreach has medium to large pools, clear water, and spring-fed base flow. The 1 km study reach contained 14 pools, up to about 120 m long and 45 cm to 1.5 m deep, with silt, sand, sandstone and fractured limestone substrates. Canopy cover ranged from nearly complete to about 75% open.

Fish were counted by snorkeling, a non-destructive method effective in clear water. The most abundant taxa were analysed after excluding [[Bullhead Catfish]], [[Ameiurus]] spp., and [[Orangethroat Darter]], which use cryptic microhabitats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dominant fauna:** Minnows (Cyprinidae), sunfishes and black bass (Centrarchidae), and topminnows (Fundulidae) dominated numerically.
- **Predator-prey structure:** Large centrarchids (bass >100 mm, sunfish >75 mm) were treated as predators. Small centrarchids, minnows and topminnows (except [[Bigeye Shiner]]) were treated as prey.
- **Grazing:** [[Central Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum*) grazing regulates algal distribution and standing crop in these pools.
- **Disturbance:** The 14 June 1983 flood crested 3.45 m above base flow, with a rapid stage rise that scoured or moved most of the bed. Another major flood occurred in April 1983.
- **Pool-to-pool movement:** Riffles reduce but do not stop movement by predators, prey, and competitors. Movement is size-dependent and varies with stage.
- **Reproduction:** Young-of-the-year of some species, such as stonerollers, dispersed widely among pools in summer and early autumn, including pools where they did not spawn.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and dominance**
- A total of 19,437 fish observations were recorded across 23 "ecotaxa" (including size classes) in eight snorkeling censuses.
- Central stoneroller (33.8%), bigeye shiner (14.5%), green sunfish <75 mm (about 12%), *Fundulus notatus* (8.2%), and longear sunfish >75 mm (8.1%) were numerically dominant.

**Reach-level consistency**
- The pooled fish assemblage for the entire 14-pool reach was highly consistent. Morisita's index for the seven intervals ranged from 0.83 to 0.98, despite the two floods.

**Within-pool consistency**
- Morisita's index for successive surveys of individual pools ranged from 0.07 to 1.00.
- The index exceeded 0.80 in 47.4% of cases, with a median of 0.78 across 95 comparisons. This suggests typically consistent assemblages.
- Pool-level assemblage change did not differ significantly between flood and non-flood intervals (one-way ANOVA, p = 0.24).
- Mean similarity did not differ significantly among pools (p = 0.77). The least similar pool, Pool 8 (mean index 0.42), was relatively large and heterogeneous, and contained 0 to 6 large bass at different times.
- Neither maximum pool depth nor pool volume significantly predicted pool stability, contrary to the prediction of Schlosser (1987).

**Species-level consistency**
- [[Sunfish]] (*Lepomis* spp.) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus* spp.) were more consistent in their distribution among pools than minnows or topminnows.
- Small fishes (cyprinids and blackstriped topminnow) varied more among pools across time, particularly after the June 1983 flood, which caused the greatest distributional change in stonerollers and topminnows.

**Species associations**
- Of 91 possible pairwise correlations among the 14 most abundant taxa (taxa observed more than 50 times, 109 samples), 25 were significant at p < 0.01 (18 positive, 7 negative). The authors note that about one of the 91 could be significant by chance.
- The UPGMA phenogram formed two main clusters. One contained large bass, large bluegill and large longear sunfish, along with blackstriped topminnow. The other contained small green sunfish, stonerollers, bullhead minnows, blacktail shiners and red shiners.

**Principal components analysis**
- The first three axes explained 22.2%, 14.2% and 10.8% of variance (47.2% combined).
- Axis I contrasted large bass and bluegill against small green sunfish and minnows. Bigeye shiner loaded strongly only on axis III, suggesting it varies independently at the pool scale.
- Most individual pools occupied a discrete subset of multivariate space across all dates. Pools were more discrete over time than the pools were from one another within any single survey, except in May 1984, when all pools were more similar.

**Predator-prey dynamics**
- Pools showed a continuum from many predators with few prey to few predators with many prey, with many intermediate pools. Segregation was therefore not absolute.
- When all large centrarchids were counted as predators, prey numbers varied widely across predator levels.
- When only bass >100 mm were counted, high bass numbers were associated with low prey numbers, with one partial exception (57 prey, mostly blackstriped topminnows, in a pool with 12 large bass). Bass therefore appeared to constrain prey more strongly than sunfish did.
- Before the floods, pools with many predators had few prey. Immediately after the floods, some pools with many predators contained many prey. The authors note this comparison was not tested statistically.

**Behavioral and mechanistic explanations**
- Blackstriped topminnows, which occupy surface water, occurred with large bass and sunfish, possibly reflecting microhabitat selection or behavior.
- Large sunfish may associate with large bass because bass suppress small fishes that prey on larvae (Harvey 1991), giving sunfish a reproductive advantage.
- Small fishes may move more readily across riffles, which may explain their lower consistency.

**Conclusions**
- Individual pools separated by shallow riffles are discrete, biologically meaningful units for stream fish assemblages. The authors recommend them as a useful spatial scale for experimental research and biological monitoring.
- The documented baseline of natural variability, including two major floods, provides a reference for judging future experimental or anthropogenic disturbances.
