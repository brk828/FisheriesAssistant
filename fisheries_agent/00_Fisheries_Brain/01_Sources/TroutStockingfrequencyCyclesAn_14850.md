# Trout Stocking-Frequency Cycles and a Device to Determine Age Classes of Trout Present

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 *[[Progressive Fish-Culturist]]* article by O. L. Wallis (National Park Service) presents a management framework for trout stocking in recreational fisheries. It describes how the interval between plantings, the stocking-frequency cycle, determines which age classes of trout are present and fishable in a lake over time. Wallis provides [[Age-O-Graph]] charts covering annual to 7-year cycles, a mechanical aid called the [[Age-O-Meter]] for reading age-class composition, and build instructions. A short companion piece by Donald E. Bevan describes a low-cost method for incubating small numbers of salmonid eggs in a domestic [[Refrigerator Incubation]] unit, based on work at Moscow University.

## Background & Description

**Stocking-frequency cycles.** The article frames the stocking cycle as a core tool for keeping a fishery at a fairly constant level without abrupt changes in fishing quality. Stocking frequency depends on:
- angler harvest rates
- existing fish populations
- presence or absence of natural reproduction
- the physical, chemical, and biological characteristics of the water

Knowing which age classes will be present lets managers evaluate stocking success against angler catches, plan future stocking schedules, and predict the sizes of trout anglers can expect, based on local growth rates.

**Age-o-graphs and Age-o-meter.** Age-o-graphs (Figure 1) give age-class data for rigid stocking cycles from annual to 7-year schedules. The Age-o-meter is a hand-built sliding device that reads the same information quickly. It has a masonite base, plexiglass overlay, and nine sliders. The article notes it can be expanded for multi-species stocking by adding a slot per species per stocking year, with colour-coded markers.

**Rainbow and cutthroat fingerlings.** The rotational-stocking section focuses on *[[Rainbow Trout]]* (*Salmo gairdneri*) and *[[Cutthroat Trout]]* (*S. clarki*) fingerlings, the species the author discusses for lake plantings.

**Egg incubation.** The refrigerator method concerns *[[Pacific Salmon]]* (*Oncorhynchus* or *Salmo*) and *[[Whitefish]]* eggs, though the author notes other species have also been reared for educational or experimental purposes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article ties stocking frequency to the ecology of each water body:

- **Heavily fished accessible lakes** have populations reduced by angling enough to warrant annual or 2-year stocking.
- **Isolated high-mountain lakes** can sustain fishable trout on less frequent cycles. Growth and survival are higher because predation and competition for food and space between age classes are reduced or eliminated.
- **Semi-isolated lakes with moderate fishing pressure** suit 2- or 3-year cycles.
- **Isolated lake groups without natural reproduction** can be managed by rotating plantings among lakes on a cycle of about 4 to 6 years, so older age classes are nearly exhausted or absent when new fingerlings go in.
- **Lakes near other lakes** are less likely to be left without fish and are stocked at shorter intervals to keep a constant fishable population.

For egg incubation, the shallow water layer in each dish is important for gas exchange. Water quality is managed through dechlorination, temperature control, and removal of slime, dead, and fungused eggs.

## Key Findings

**Stocking-cycle outcomes (from the text):**

- **2-year cycle:** age classes II and IV are caught in years 1 to 4 after initiation. Age class III predominates in year 5, and age class IV in year 6. After year 5 the cycle stabilises, alternating between age class III in one year and age classes II and IV in the next.
- **3-year cycle:** age class II appears in the second season, and the cycle repeats every 3 years with one age class dominant in each season. The article lists the sequence through year 10.
- **4-year cycle:** the initial plant dominates for 5 years, after which the second stocking (year 4) makes up the catch. Age class V predominates in year 5, and the cycle repeats in year 9.
- **5-year cycle:** returns from the initial plant last 5 to 6 years. From year 7, the fish from the year 5 plant appear, and age class II supplies most of the catch.
- **6-year cycle:** the fishery is thin for several years. Fish of age classes V, VI, and VII are few but large in years 5 to 7, and small age-class I trout appear in year 7.
- **7-year cycle:** only a few fishable trout are expected from year 5 through year 7. Age class I fish planted in year 7 form a fishable population in year 8.

**Summary conclusions:**

- The annual cycle suits heavily fished waters. Angler harvest limits competition between age groups, but growth is slower than under longer cycles.
- The greatest variety of age classes and sizes comes from 1-, 2-, and 3-year cycles.
- 4-, 5-, 6-, and 7-year cycles create periods of reduced fishable populations.
- Rotational stocking on a 4- to 6-year cycle keeps some lakes fishable each season, but sustained fishing cannot be expected in all lakes every year.

**Age-o-meter operation:** the device reads age classes by aligning sliders (one per stocking year) to the column for a target year. Worked examples cover:
- **1962 (from 1957, 1959, 1960, and 1962 plantings):** age classes V, III, II, and 0 are present.
- **1964 (from 1957, 1959, 1960, and 1962 plantings):** age classes VII, V, IV, and II are expected.
- **1960 (from 1957, 1959, and 1960 plantings):** age classes III, I, and 0 are present.

**Refrigerator incubation of salmonid eggs (from Bevan's note):**

- Developed by A. I. Smirnov and colleagues in the Department of Ichthyology, Moscow University, for use where hatchery facilities are not nearby.
- Eggs are kept in 10 cm petri dishes under a shallow water layer: 0.8 to 1.0 cm for salmon (about 25 eggs per dish) and 0.5 cm for whitefish (up to 150 eggs per dish).
- Temperatures are generally held between 2° and 7° C.
- Water is replaced every 2 to 3 days with dechlorinated water at the same temperature. Glassware is fully cleaned about every 5 days.
- Young fish are moved to aquaria once they swim up.
- No exact mortality data are given, but the author reports relatively high survival.
