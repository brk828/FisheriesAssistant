# Biological Opinion for Bridge Construction and Road Reconstruction on Aravaipa Creek Road

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #endangered-species-act #habitat-assessment

## Executive Summary

This biological opinion, issued November 7, 1996, evaluates the Federal Emergency Management Agency's (FEMA) proposed funding for bridge construction and road reconstruction on [[Aravaipa Creek]] road in [[Pinal County]], [[Arizona]]. The project addresses damage from 1993 flooding at two sites along approximately 1.1 miles of road. The consultation concerns two federally threatened species: [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), both endemic cyprinids of the [[Gila River]] basin. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service concluded that the proposed action is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of either species or adversely modify their critical habitat, provided that four reasonable and prudent measures with 14 terms and conditions are implemented. Incidental take is anticipated but cannot be directly quantified; it is indexed to fish community parameters and project activities.

## Background & Description

[[Spikedace]] was listed as threatened on July 1, 1986, and [[Loach Minnow]] on October 28, 1986. Critical habitat for both species was designated March 8, 1994, including approximately 15 miles of [[Aravaipa Creek]], though a 1996 10th Circuit Court decision set aside and enjoined enforcement pending NEPA compliance. Both species have experienced severe range contractions—approximately 85% for loach minnow—due to habitat destruction, competition, and predation from nonnative fish species.

**Spikedace** is a small silvery cyprinid with a well-developed dorsal spine. It historically occurred throughout mid-elevations of the [[Gila River]] drainage but currently persists only in [[Aravaipa Creek]], upper [[Gila River]] (New Mexico), middle Gila River, [[Eagle Creek]], and [[Verde River]]. The species inhabits flowing water with slow to moderate velocities over sand, gravel, and cobble substrates, particularly in shear zones, sheet flow areas, and eddies. Spawning occurs March through May over gravel-cobble substrate. Life span is approximately two years with reproduction primarily in one-year-old fish. Diet consists of aquatic and terrestrial insects.

**Loach Minnow** is a small, slender, elongate fish with upwardly-directed eyes. Historic range included the Verde, Salt, San Pedro, San Francisco, and Gila River basins. The species is a bottom-dwelling inhabitant of shallow, swift water over gravel, cobble, and rubble substrates, using interstitial spaces for resting and spawning. Life span is approximately two years. Spawning occurs primarily March through May, with some autumn spawning documented. Males may guard nests. Diet consists exclusively of aquatic insects. The species is rare or absent where fine sediments fill interstitial spaces.

Recent genetic and morphological studies indicate substantial differences between remnant populations of both species, which occupy isolated fragments of the Gila basin. These populations should be managed as separate units to preserve genetic variation.

The proposed project involves two sites:

**Site 1** (southeast corner Section 27): Installation of a 260-foot long, 21-foot wide, one-lane prefabricated panel bridge spanning [[Aravaipa Creek]] to replace approximately 250 feet of road completely removed by erosion. The bridge would rest on two abutments outside the normal high water mark but within the 100-year floodplain. No structures would be placed in the stream channel. Construction requires approximately 410 vehicle crossings of the existing low-water ford over an estimated 65-day period (average 5 crossings/day). Bridge assembly would occur on a point bar vegetated with cottonwood saplings, willows, and seepwillow.

**Site 2** (center of Section 26): Reconstruction of 1,000 feet of road, reducing width from 32 to 18 feet and raising elevation 3-5 feet. Approximately 3,000 cubic yards of fill would be placed, with wire gabion baskets filled with rock protecting the roadbed. All work would occur within the existing roadway; no vehicles or equipment would enter the watered stream channel.

Construction would occur November 1 to February 28 or June 16 to August 31 to avoid spawning periods. Water for dust control would be obtained off-site. A biologist would monitor activities and advise construction crews.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Aravaipa Creek]] is a perennial tributary of the [[San Pedro River]] with approximately 10 cfs base flow and median flow of 16 cfs. The watershed encompasses 537 square miles. Perennial flow is confined to 15-20 miles within Aravaipa Canyon. The creek supports a relatively intact native fish community—a rare situation in the Gila River basin—including seven native species: [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), spikedace, and loach minnow. All are federally listed or species of concern.

Nonnative species present include [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), and occasionally [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) invaded the entire perennial reach in 1990-91 but did not establish; this species is considered highly detrimental to small native cyprinids through competition and predation.

The watershed has been substantially altered by grazing, mining, timber harvest, water development, irrigated agriculture, roads, and recreation since European settlement. Historical accounts indicate the creek was more perennial, with extensive riparian forests and cienegas now largely eliminated. Perennial flow length has decreased; currently it typically begins about two miles downstream from its 1900 location. Channel modifications include diversion dams, channelization, riprap, and earthen dikes. Lead mill tailings from the 1920s located on the creek bank are eroding into the stream, with documented water quality violations.

The existing road alignment has caused recurring adverse impacts through repeated washouts and repairs at Site 1 (documented in 1967, 1978, 1983, and 1993), resulting in machinery use in the stream channel, sediment input, and likely direct mortality of fish. The low-water crossing adversely impacts both species through direct mortality, sediment generation, riparian vegetation loss, and channel changes.

Five previous Section 7 consultations have addressed [[Aravaipa Creek]]. A 1994 biological opinion on the Central Arizona Project found jeopardy and adverse modification of critical habitat from potential introduction of nonnative species; the reasonable and prudent alternative included construction of fish barriers on Aravaipa Creek to prevent upstream movement of nonnatives.

Cumulative effects include ongoing livestock grazing and watering, irrigated agriculture (declining), groundwater pumping, stream diversion, bank stabilization, channelization, and recreation (increasing). The Bureau of Land Management is proposing expanded recreation facilities. Potential exists for increased residential or commercial development on private lands along the creek, which would likely increase water use, pollution, and streambank alteration.

## Key Findings

**Non-Jeopardy Determination**: The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service determined that the proposed bridge construction and road reconstruction, as described, is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of spikedace or loach minnow and is **not likely to destroy or adversely modify** the designated critical habitat of either species.

**Anticipated Effects**: Short-term adverse effects would occur during construction, including:
- Direct mortality from crushing by vehicles in the stream (primarily at Site 1)
- Harassment causing increased predation risk and decreased fitness
- Potential poisoning from accidental introduction of toxic substances
- Habitat alteration from substrate and bank disturbance, sedimentation, erosion
- Loss of riparian vegetation, particularly on the point bar at Site 1
- Channel geometry changes

The low-water crossing area at Site 1 would likely become unsuitable for both species during and immediately after construction until sediment washes away and substrate becomes less compacted.

**Long-term Effects**: At Site 1, the bridge is expected to provide long-term benefits by eliminating the recurring cycle of washout, repair, and low-water crossing use. The clear-span design (no piers in baseflow channel) would allow unimpeded stream flow and minimize habitat alteration. At Site 2, raising the road would decrease inundation frequency and extend time between washouts, though the road's location within the riparian corridor would continue to restrict riparian width and buffering capacity.

**Incidental Take Statement**: Anticipated take cannot be directly quantified due to natural population fluctuations, habitat changes over time, and difficulty in observing small fish mortality. Take is indexed to:
1. More than 20 dead or dying fish of any species in project area or 500 yards downstream during any two-week period
2. Any toxic spill in stream or floodplain
3. Equipment use in stream more than 30 feet upstream or downstream from existing low-water crossing midlines at Site 1

Exceeding these thresholds requires immediate reinitiation of formal consultation.

**Reasonable and Prudent Measures** (4 objectives, mandatory):
1. Minimize direct mortality of spikedace and loach minnow
2. Minimize loss and alteration of habitat
3. Monitor fish community and habitat to document take levels
4. Maintain complete records of actions potentially resulting in take

**Terms and Conditions** (14 requirements, mandatory):
- Minimize activities in wetted areas and on point bar
- Prevent pollutant entry to surface waters
- No construction during spawning periods (September 1-October 31, March 1-June 15)
- No water withdrawal from Aravaipa Creek
- Limit channel alteration and equipment use to within 30 linear feet of low-water crossing centerlines at Site 1
- No vehicles/equipment in watered channel at Site 2
- Minimize riparian vegetation damage; no mature tree removal; prioritize preservation of cottonwoods and willows
- Use fabric silt fencing and chain link fencing as specified
- No construction debris in creek; no structures in stream
- Continuous monitoring for dead/dying fish during activities
- Biologist present during construction
- Written completion report within 60 days of each site completion

**Conservation Recommendation** (discretionary): Install interpretive signs during construction to inform the public of resource protection efforts, addressing the need for road repair and benefits to spikedace, loach minnow, and the broader riparian and native fish communities.

**Consultation History**: Extensive, beginning with 1993 emergency consultation for temporary ford crossing following flood damage. Previous formal consultations in 1994 (withdrawn March 1994) and October 1994 addressed a road rerouting proposal; a February 1995 biological opinion found no jeopardy, but technical and funding difficulties caused that plan to be abandoned. Current consultation initiated September 30, 1996.

**Traffic Estimates**: Current use averages 12-14 vehicles per day (corrected from draft EA estimate), with potential for increased recreational use following improved access.

**Species Status**: Both species warrant uplisting to endangered status due to increased threats to habitat, though reclassification proposals are precluded by higher-priority listing actions. Spikedace and loach minnow populations in Aravaipa Creek are found in suitable habitat throughout the perennial flow area.
