# Longitudinal Profiles of Ephemeral Streams in Southeastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ephemeral-streams

## Executive Summary

This geomorphological study examines the longitudinal profiles of ephemeral streams that drain mountains into alluvial valleys in southeastern Arizona. Profiles in these streams are segmented into a steeper, strongly concave upstream section and a nearly straight, low-concavity downstream section. Using dry-channel measurements of [[Drainage Area]], [[Bed-Material Size]] and [[Width-Depth Ratio]], the author derived regression equations for gradient in four groups (sedimentary or granitic lithology, concave or straight segment). The concavity change is attributed to hydrologic controls rather than tectonic or base-level changes. The resulting model predicted profiles in two test streams within the limits of field and map measurement error.

## Background & Description

The ephemeral streams studied drain the mountain ranges of southeastern Arizona and cross the alluvial valleys at their bases. Previous work (for example [[Leopold and Miller]] 1956, [[Hack]] 1957) showed that stream profiles adjust to discharge, velocity, sediment load, channel shape and roughness, as well as lithology, relief and climate. Because flow in these streams is infrequent, the study sought proxies that could be measured from dry channel morphology.

Nine main streams were studied: three drainage basins each in the Whetstone, Mustang and Sierrita Mountains, plus tributaries. Gradient was calculated over reaches 0.25 to 1 mi long, and concavity was defined as the exponent *n* in the power function S = kL^n, following Hack (1957). Basin relief, segment relief and precipitation were also considered. Sediment was sampled using a grid-based method (Wolman 1954) with Folk's (1965) graphical mean size. Mean daily July-August precipitation was estimated from elevation using gauge data from the [[Catalina Mountains]], [[Santa Rita Experimental Range]] and communities around the Whetstone Mountains.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Stream flow in the study area is produced almost entirely by summer rainstorms. Infiltration and evaporation across unsaturated alluvium are expected to reduce discharge downstream, and the author notes these losses are probably high in the low-concavity reaches. Lithology differs across the study area: the Mustang streams and Whetstone 1 cross sedimentary and volcanic bedrock (limestone, sandstone, rhyolite) and derived alluvium, while the other Whetstone streams and the Sierrita streams lie on granite and granodiorite. The primary border fault along each mountain front is buried, so tectonic controls were evaluated indirectly by comparing the concavity change with the topographic mountain front. Bedrock outcrops, active trenching and caliche-cemented channel walls were noted as local factors that produced deviations from predicted gradients.

## Key Findings

- **Two-segment profiles:** Each stream has an upstream high-concavity segment and a downstream low-concavity segment. The concavity change is governed by a change in the rate of increase of drainage area with channel length.
- **Cause of concavity change:** The concavity change does not coincide with the mountain front in most streams (six of nine showed little relation). Drainage area location matched the concavity change in eight of nine streams, and lowest bedrock matched in five of nine. The author concludes the segmentation has a hydrologic origin. A base-level explanation would require a relative drop of several hundred feet and would produce a regionally consistent downstream entrenchment increase, which was observed in only three streams (Mustang 1 and 3, Sierrita 2).
- **Controlling variables:** Drainage area (proxy for discharge and sediment load) was identified as the dominant control. Steeper concavity in the upper segment corresponds to faster downstream decrease in particle size and to an increasing width:depth ratio.
- **Regression models:** Gradient was related to area:segment relief² ratio, particle size and width:depth ratio. Four equations were fitted:
  - Sedimentary concave: S = (6.6 × 10⁻⁵) R^(−0.34) ... (r = 0.800, 99% significance; F = 18.7)
  - Sedimentary straight: r = 0.795 (97.5%); F = 9.3
  - Granitic concave: r = 0.940 (99%); F = 46.0
  - Granitic straight: r = 0.588 (99%); F = 5.76. Width:depth ratio was insignificant in the straight segments.
- **Precipitation:** Precipitation could not be included statistically because its relation to gradient was masked by the relation to elevation. Predicted profiles were not improved by precipitation, and the author suggests that storm intensity could be more important than mean daily precipitation, but the available data could not test this.
- **Model test:** Profile equations (H = integral of the gradient-length expression) were tested on two Tucson Mountain streams. Predicted profiles approximated real profiles within measurement error, with maximum vertical errors of 10% and 6.3% for the predicted profiles of the two streams (reported for Catalina 2 and Tucson 3 respectively in the figure).
- **Lithologic effects:** In sedimentary and volcanic rocks, the highest concavities occur where resistant limestone is present, and the lowest where rhyolite (nonresistant) is common.
- **Conclusions:** The profiles are adjusted to four independent variables: relief, local base level, lithology and climate. Relief and base level set the vertical drop and horizontal distance, while lithology and climate, expressed through discharge, sediment load and roughness, shape the profile between them. The author concludes ephemeral profiles adjust to the same hydraulic factors as perennial streams.
