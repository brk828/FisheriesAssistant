# Cave Adaptation in Amblyopsid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #amblyopsidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 reprint from *The American Midland Naturalist* (Vol. 70, No. 2, pp. 257–290) by Thomas L. Poulson, Yale University, examines how the five [[Amblyopsidae]] fishes change as they move from surface waters into caves. The family forms a morphological series: the epigean [[Chologaster Cornuta]], the troglophile [[Chologaster Agassizi]], and three troglobites, [[Typhlichthys Subterraneus]], [[Amblyopsis Spelaea]], and [[Amblyopsis Rosae]]. Poulson argues that food scarcity and the absence of predators are the main forces shaping this sequence. Eye and pigment degeneration serve as a time index of isolation in caves, and the author proposes a three-stage model of [[Cave Adaptation]] that can predict a species' adaptation stage from food supply, predator number, and the sensory endowment of its ancestor.

## Background & Description

The study compares five amblyopsids in order of increasing eye degeneration and pigment loss:

- **[[Chologaster Cornuta]] (swampfish):** epigean, with normal eyes and pigment; nocturnal and found mainly in cypress swamps and small shaded streams.
- **[[Chologaster Agassizi]] (spring cavefish):** a [[Troglophile]] with reduced eyes and pigment; active at night in spring systems and retreats underground by day.
- **[[Typhlichthys Subterraneus]] (southern cavefish):** a [[Troglobite]] that is effectively depigmented, with degenerate eyes.
- **[[Amblyopsis Spelaea]] (northern cavefish):** a troglobite with reduced eyes and pigment.
- **[[Amblyopsis Rosae]] (Ozark cavefish):** the most cave-adapted member, found in small cave streams, often on chert rubble.

Earlier work by Eigenmann (1909) described the eye and pigment morphology, and Woods and Inger (1957) revised the family's distribution and variation. This study adds brain and sense-organ morphology, growth, metabolism, behavior, and life history.

The author presents the amblyopsids as a preadapted group. The epigean ancestor already had small eyes, nocturnal habits, and sensory receptors that support feeding and orientation in darkness, but it could not survive in aphotic conditions. The troglophile can.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods and laboratory conditions.** Cave amblyopsids were collected at 68 localities and *C. cornuta* at 17. Fish were held at 13 ± 1.2 °C with dissolved oxygen of 7–10 mg/L. Cave species were kept in darkness at pH 7.5 ± 0.5 and total alkalinity of 100 ± 25 mg/L, maintained with limestone blocks. All species were fed live *Daphnia magna* and *Hyalella*.

**Habitat stability.** Aquatic cave habitats are better buffered than epigean habitats against abrupt changes in temperature, chemistry, and turbidity, though less so than terrestrial caves. Seasonal flooding and groundwater infiltration drive the variation that does occur, and floods bring in most of the organic matter that supports the simple cave food web.

**Food and associates.** Cave food is scarce. The only known primary producers are iron bacteria, which are autotrophic only under chemical conditions that are rare where cave life occurs. Prey are mostly copepods, ostracods, amphipods, and isopods. *C. cornuta* has 21 vertebrate associates, but only five occur in at least 50% of the same seine hauls. *C. agassizi* has 27 associates in spring-swamp habitats but only copepods as common associates in caves.

**Water chemistry ranges (Table I).** Periodic samples over two to three years in four to eight habitats per species show:
- *C. cornuta*: temperature 5–20 °C, pH 5.0–6.8, alkalinity 1–19 mg/L.
- *C. agassizi*: spring-habitat temperature 9.8–13.2 °C, pH 7.2–7.6, alkalinity 97–182 mg/L; cave-habitat temperature 5.1–15.3 °C, pH 6.9–8.3, alkalinity 75–200 mg/L.
- *Typhlichthys*: temperature 10.2–13.7 °C, alkalinity 174–235 mg/L.
- *A. spelaea* and *A. rosae*: lower alkalinity in some caves (80–190 mg/L for *A. rosae*).

**Annual cycles.** *C. cornuta* and *C. agassizi* show clear annual growth (annulus formation) and reproductive cycles tied to surface conditions. *A. spelaea* has a well-defined cycle with breeding from February through April during high water. The troglobite cycles are less pronounced, and the author links them to changing water levels and chemistry.

## Key Findings

**1. Sequence of cave adaptation.** Eye and pigment degeneration increase in the order *Chologaster cornuta* → *C. agassizi* → *Typhlichthys* → *A. spelaea* → *A. rosae*. The degree of degeneration tracks the length of isolation in caves. Within each phylogenetic line, the more cave-adapted species has the lower metabolic rate.

**2. Sensory compensation.** As cave adaptation increases, olfactory, neuromast, tactile, and equilibrium receptors and their brain centers become more hypertrophied. Head size, fin length, and neuromast exposure also increase. *Typhlichthys* has 45.6 neuromasts in rows 2+3+4 (at 45 mm standard length), against 21.6 in *C. cornuta* and 28.5 in *C. agassizi*. Tactile receptors number about 21/mm² in *Typhlichthys*, 14/mm² in *C. agassizi*, and 16–18/mm² in the *Amblyopsis* species.

**3. Growth and development.** Absolute growth rate falls with cave adaptation. Scales first form at roughly two-thirds of a year in *A. spelaea* and *A. rosae*, and the first annulus forms at 1⅔ years. Lower developmental rates produce larger, fewer eggs.

**4. Reproduction and parental care.** Mature ova per female average 98.0 (*C. cornuta*), 152.1 (*C. agassizi*), 49.8 (*Typhlichthys*), 69.5 (*A. spelaea*), and 23.0 (*A. rosae*). Egg size increases from 0.9–1.2 mm to 2.0–2.3 mm. Amblyopsids carry their young in the gill cavity (branchial incubation), which protects the early life stages. *A. spelaea* carries young for 4–5 months.

**5. Life history and population regulation.** Longevity and age at first reproduction increase with cave adaptation. Mean age at first reproduction is 11–12 months in the *Chologaster* species, 22–24 months in *Typhlichthys*, and 36–48 months in both *Amblyopsis* species. The oldest recorded individuals reached 14–15 months (*C. cornuta*), 38–50 months (*Typhlichthys*), 73–84 months (*A. spelaea*), and 45–57 months (*A. rosae*). Only a small proportion of potential breeders reproduce in a given year, which shifts populations toward adults. The author estimates that the intrinsic rate of population growth (r) falls roughly 500-fold with increasing cave adaptation.

**6. Population fluctuation.** In complete censuses, *Typhlichthys* populations vary 2.8-fold, *A. rosae* 2.4-fold, and *A. spelaea* 2.0-fold. Cannibalism of young appears to account for most pre-senescence mortality in *A. spelaea*.

**7. Metabolism.** Standard and active metabolic rates decline with cave adaptation. *C. cornuta* has the highest standard metabolic rate of any amblyopsid. The ratio of active to standard metabolic rate is 5.8–8.4 (mean 6.6) in amblyopsids, compared with 2.0–5.1 (mean 3.1) in epigean fishes. Estimated routine metabolic rate, as a share of active rate, is 25% (*C. agassizi*), 23% (*Typhlichthys*), 19% (*A. spelaea*), and 18% (*A. rosae*). Lower metabolic demand is therefore an energy-conserving adaptation rather than a loss of activity.

**8. Activity and behavior.** Average swimming velocity increases with cave adaptation, and the most cave-adapted species have better swimming efficiency. Troglobites are less excitable: *Amblyopsis* responds to touch with slow swimming or no response, while *C. agassizi* and *Typhlichthys* swim fast and undirected. Over repeated taps, *C. agassizi* habituates by the sixth to ninth tap, and *A. rosae* reacts similarly to every tap.

**9. Obstacle avoidance and spatial memory.** *C. agassizi* is the most thigmotactic and has the poorest obstacle avoidance and spatial memory. Both *Amblyopsis* species show better spatial memory than *Typhlichthys* or *C. agassizi*, which the author attributes to a larger cerebellum and telencephalon and to reduced dependence on touch.

**10. Eye variance.** Variance in eye and optic lobe size decreases in the later stages. It is high in *C. agassizi* and *Typhlichthys*, lower in *A. spelaea*, and lowest in *A. rosae*, which is consistent with the stabilization of allometry and mutation effects.

**11. Stage model.** Poulson divides the sequence into three stages: (1) preadaptation and endocrine adjustment with increased eye and pigment variance; (2) lowered growth and metabolic rates, with population shifts toward adults and parental care; and (3) population regulation, decreased eye and pigment variance, and better sensory integration. He concludes that, in most cases where food is limiting and the ancestor is only moderately endowed with sense organs, the stage of adaptation correlates closely with the extent of eye and pigment degeneration.

**12. Convergent cave fish.** The model is extended to other cave fishes, including *Anoptichthys*, *Caecobarbus*, *Stygicola* and *Lucifuga*, and *Typhlogarra*, with the caveat that factors such as food supply and sensory endowment modify the outcome.
