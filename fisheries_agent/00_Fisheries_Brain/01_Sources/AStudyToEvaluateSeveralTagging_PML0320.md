# Evaluation of Tagging and Marking Systems for Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker, and Bonytail

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final report (January 1993), funded by the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin, tests several identification systems on hatchery-reared juvenile and broodstock [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail]]. The authors compare survival, growth, tag/mark retention and readability, tissue response, cost, and minimum applicable fish size. The principal system tested is the [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag]] (PIT tag), implanted in the body cavity or dorsal musculature. The study concludes that body-cavity PIT tags performed best overall and recommends them as the primary long-term identification marker, while [[Carlin-Dangler Tag]]s should be discontinued for stocked fish.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Tagging and marking are fundamental to management of endangered fishes, particularly for broodstock identity, pedigree mating, and monitoring. No existing method is risk-free.
- **Prior tagging:** The Carlin-dangler tag was used extensively on wild and hatchery endangered fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin from 1979 to 1986. Critics cited poor retention on long-lived fish and injury from needle punctures (open wounds, secondary infections). Its use for endangered fish was suspended in 1990 (the source text reads "1999", which is an OCR error for the context of the study).
- **Prior PIT evaluation:** A 1987 study on adult roundtail chub, immature squawfish, and immature razorback sucker showed merit for PIT tags but yielded limited data, leading to the present study.
- **Objectives:** Compare performance characters (survival, growth, tag/mark verification, tissue response, convenience and cost, minimum fish size, anomalous behavior) and recommend long-term systems for hatchery and field use.

**Study design:**
- Five systems tested at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (Dexter NFHTC): PIT tag (body cavity), PIT tag (dorsal musculature), [[Visible Implant Tag]] (V.I.), Carlin-dangler tag (razorback and bonytail only), and acrylic dye; control groups received no tag.
- Sixty fish per species per group; 30 fish each held in an outdoor earthen pond (~0.04 ha) and an outdoor cement raceway (14 m x 1.8 m x 0.6 m), both on groundwater.
- Initial sizes: squawfish mean FL 72 mm (range 55–88 mm); razorback sucker mean FL 110 mm (range 84–217 mm); bonytail mean FL 76 mm (range 62–116 mm).
- Every fish received a secondary fin-removal mark to track mark loss.
- Companion PIT studies were run at [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] (juvenile squawfish, mean FL 125 mm) and [[Ouray Endangered Fish Hatchery]] (age-0/age-1 razorback sucker, mean FL 92 mm).
- Monitoring extended to 511 days at Dexter; statistical comparisons used Chi-square tests at alpha = 0.05.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Holding environments:** The pond received water at 94.6 L/min at 17.9 °C; the raceway received 56.8 L/min of ground water at 17.8 °C. The pond was netted October–April to limit avian predation.
- **Disease:** The only parasite recorded was *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* in raceway fish, treated with formalin and malachite green.
- **Survival contrast:** Survival was markedly higher in the raceway than the pond across all species (see Key Findings).
- **Growth contrast:** Fish grew faster in the pond, especially razorback sucker and bonytail.
- **Hatchery temperature constraint:** At Hotchkiss NFH, constant 15 °C water was colder than river temperatures and probably curtailed growth of these warmwater fish.

## Key Findings

**Overall performance ranking**
- [[PIT Tag]] implanted in the body cavity performed best overall. It did not appear to impair growth or survival, and verification over time was acceptable.
- Tag verification was 100% for 148 adult squawfish brood fish surviving 24 months (of 164 implanted; 16 died; 10 unaccounted for between 20 and 24 months).
- Tag verification was 100% for 245 surviving razorback sucker brood fish at 18 months (of 526 implanted; 280 transferred to another facility and not monitored).
- Juvenile squawfish, razorback sucker, and bonytail with body-cavity PIT tags showed verification above 92% at 511 days; experimental survival did not differ statistically from controls.
- Razorback sucker with dorsal-musculature PIT tags showed 100% verification through day 511.
- Only one in-situ tag failure was observed.

**Minimum size and placement**
- Body-cavity PIT tags were successfully used in squawfish and bonytail (mean FL 72–76 mm) and razorback sucker (mean FL 75 mm).
- Dorsal-musculature tagging was too risky for age-0 squawfish (mean FL 72 mm) and bonytail (mean FL 76 mm). Wound healing was slower and some fish showed erratic swimming, suggesting neurological damage.
- Juvenile squawfish (first PIT experiment at Hotchkiss NFH) retained tags with about 97% verification or better; the tag was fully verified on day 387.

**Visible implant (V.I.) tags**
- Retention was extremely poor for all three species at 75 mm FL; the subdermal tissue was too thin. The NMT representative noted a minimum of about 150 mm.
- Only one V.I. tag was detected and readable in razorback sucker (day 343).
- Cost is lower ($0.25–$0.70 per tag versus $3.75–$5.00 per PIT tag).

**Carlin-dangler tags**
- Retention in razorback sucker fell from 73% (pond) and 72% (raceway) on day 195 to 4% (raceway, day 343) and 0% by day 511. Pond retention remained at 67% at day 511.
- Bonytail retention was 0% at the end of the study.
- Wounds healed more slowly than PIT wounds, with persistent red or pink coloration. Healing was 59% at day 195 and 81–82% at days 343 and 511.

**Acrylic dye**
- Marks were poorly retained (red dye 24% on squawfish at day 195, 15% in pond). Dye was undetectable by day 343 for squawfish.
- The application was time-consuming, requiring more than twice the time of other systems.
- Dye may suit short-term batch marking but not long-term individual identification.

**Secondary fin mark**
- Fin regeneration was under 1% at day 195 but reached a mean of 23% across species by day 343, when the secondary mark became undetectable.
- The fin clip was adequate as a short-term (7-month) mark but unsatisfactory long-term.
- Fin removal had no significant effect on growth or survival at day 195 (Dexter and Ouray).

**Environmental effects**
- Survival ratio, raceway versus pond: squawfish 8.7×, bonytail 2.7×, razorback sucker 1.5×.
- Pond mortality for squawfish and bonytail peaked between days 0 and 195; raceway mortality for squawfish and razorback sucker peaked between days 343 and 511.

**Hotchkiss NFH and Ouray EFH**
- Hotchkiss squawfish: 37 tagged versus 38 controls at start; 33 of 33 verified at day 387. Wounds were undetectable at day 387. Growth was slow at constant 15 °C.
- Ouray razorback sucker: three groups of 40 fish; PIT-tag verification was high (100%). One PIT-tagged fish died within 48 hours, and no other mortalities occurred. Only one of 79 fish regenerated its pelvic fin over 8.7 months.

**Recommendations**
1. Implement PIT tagging, preferably in the body cavity, as the standard long-term marker for juvenile and adult endangered fish, especially below 175 mm.
2. Hold workshops and demonstrations to train workers in body-cavity implantation for fish under 175 mm.
3. Continue monitoring PIT-tagged adult squawfish and razorback sucker at Dexter NFHTC during annual spawning to gather long-term data.
4. Note PIT cost ($3.75–$5.00 per tag, plus about $1,200 for a field scanner) may limit use in batch marking; it suits individual wild and broodstock fish.
5. Discontinue Carlin-dangler tags for fish destined for stocking; use only in research with restricted budgets and with approval from the appropriate Technical Group of the Implementation Committee.
6. Investigate freeze-branding and fluorescent polymer batch marks for fish under 70 mm.
7. Record the 10-character alphanumeric PIT code carefully, since hand-recording increases error risk.
