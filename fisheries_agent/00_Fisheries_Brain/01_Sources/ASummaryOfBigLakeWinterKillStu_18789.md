## Executive Summary

This 1960 Arizona Game and Fish Commission special report, authored by Fisheries Supervisor D. I. Foster, synthesizes over 15 years (1944–1960) of investigations into recurrent winter fish-kill events at **Big Lake**, a shallow impoundment in Arizona's White Mountains. The report is not centered on fish morphology but rather on the limnological and management dynamics driving hypoxia-related winter mortality events in the lake's [[Trout]] fishery. Winter-kills are attributed to a self-reinforcing cycle of shallow lake morphometry, dense aquatic macrophyte growth, prolonged ice/snow cover, and increasing benthic organic matter decomposition with lake age. Multiple mitigation strategies were tested over the study period — including mechanical weed cutting, an air compressor/aeration system, and lake-level augmentation via canal diversion — with cost-benefit analysis ultimately favoring **permanent lake-level increase** over technological intervention. The report recommends discontinuing the costly air compressor program and pursuing additional water diversion into the Big Lake basin as the most durable solution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Type:** Big Lake is described as a shallow-basin impoundment (established 1936) prone to profuse rooted aquatic vegetation growth and seasonal ice/snow cover — conditions that simultaneously promote rapid [[Trout]] growth rates and predispose the system to winter dissolved-oxygen depletion.

**Key Abiotic Drivers of Winter-Kill (Causative Factors):**
1. **Ice cover** — blocks wind-driven surface aeration (the primary natural oxygen-replenishment mechanism) and reduces light penetration needed for photosynthetic oxygen production.
2. **Snow cover on ice** — further eliminates light penetration, shifting aquatic plants from net oxygen production to net oxygen-consuming respiration.
3. **Oxygen demand** — proportional to total ice/snow cover duration and the volume of organic matter undergoing decomposition on the lake bottom; this demand increases as the lake ages due to sediment/organic accumulation ([[Lake Eutrophication]] process).
4. **Low water levels** — favor excessive vegetative growth in shallow water and reduce total lake oxygen volume/buffering capacity.

**Correlational Findings (Ecological Thresholds):**
- Periods of ice cover **exceeding ~110 days** were consistently associated with subsequent fish-kill events (see Table I data, 1948–1960).
- **Low average maximum air temperatures** (rather than average minimums) correlated more strongly with winter-kill years, attributed to reduced solar radiation/increased cloud cover limiting photosynthetic oxygen production under ice.
- **Heavy, consistent snow pack** (Table III, Big Lake Fork Snow Pack data 1950–1960) correlated with known winter-kill years (e.g., 1952 and 1958, both winter-kill years, showed average snow packs of 40.8" and 31.8" respectively, versus much lower averages in non-kill years).
- The dam was raised 10 feet in 1953 to increase lake volume, following repeated recommendations dating back to 1944; a canal diversion from Crescent Lake was completed in 1955 to supplement runoff.

## Key Findings

- **Historical winter-kill/partial-kill years identified:** 1948-49, 1951-52, 1954-55 (partial), 1957-58, and probable kill in 1959-60, based on ice-cover duration and temperature records (Table I).
- **Consensus management recommendation (1944–1960, unanimous across multiple biologists/reports):** Raising lake surface level (increasing depth and volume) is the most permanent and effective solution, as it both inhibits vegetative overgrowth and increases winter dissolved-oxygen reserves.
- **Air compressor aeration system (installed 1953-54):** Designed to circulate warmer bottom water to melt surface ice; proved **inadequate under severe winter conditions** (documented failures in 1957-58 and 1959-60) despite continued operation through 1960.
- **Cost-benefit analysis:** Over 7 winters of air compressor operation (1953-54 to 1959-60), total operational cost was ~$29,400, compared to ~$19,500 in restocking costs for the two fish-kills that occurred during that same period despite the compressor's operation — indicating the intervention did not deliver cost-effective protection (Table II, detailed annual expenditures 1955-56 through 1959-60 totaling $20,144.34 for a partial 5-year subset).
- **Alternative interventions evaluated and rejected as impractical or temporary** (Table IV): mechanical weed cutting (discontinued after 1950-52 due to high cost despite cutting ~200 acres/year), chemical vegetation control, water fertilization (risk of triggering summer fish kills via over-enrichment), lamp black ice-melting, water pumping/agitation, ice breaking, and ice flooding — all deemed cost-prohibitive and/or only temporarily effective relative to permanent lake-volume increase.
- **Final recommendations (1960):**
  1. Discontinue the air compressor program.
  2. Continue researching lower-cost alternative temporary winter-kill prevention methods.
  3. Prioritize securing additional runoff water diversion (e.g., proposed Burro Creek–to–Crescent Lake canal, estimated $25,000–$30,000) into the Big Lake basin as the primary long-term solution, given the lake's inadequate natural runoff catchment area (~1,900 acres).
