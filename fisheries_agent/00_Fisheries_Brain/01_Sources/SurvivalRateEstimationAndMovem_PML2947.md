# Survival Rate Estimation and Movement of Hatchery-Reared Razorback Suckers in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program Project 128, Larval Fish Laboratory Contribution 159, April 2009) estimates post-stocking apparent survival and recapture probability of [[Hatchery-Reared Razorback Sucker]] stocked into the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], and describes their post-stocking movement. The work uses 119,129 individually [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT-tagged) stocked fish from 1995 to 2005 and analyzes them with [[Cormack-Jolly-Seber Model]] open-population models in Program MARK, with model selection by [[Akaike's Information Criterion]].

Recovery of the federally endangered species requires two self-sustaining adult populations, each exceeding 5,800 individuals, before downlisting or delisting. The integrated stocking plan assumed annual survival of 50% for age-2, 60% for age-3, and 70% for adult fish. The study found that first-interval survival of stocked fish was far lower than assumed, while post-first-interval survival was close to the adult assumption.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large, long-lived catostomid endemic to the Colorado River Basin, historically reaching about 1 m and 6 kg, with some individuals aged 24 to 44 years. Reproductive maturity occurs at about 4 years and more than 400 mm total length (TL). Spawning occurs in spring on cobble/gravel bars in riffles, with larvae drifting to low-velocity nursery habitat. Recruitment failure is considered the main cause of decline. Dam construction and [[Non-Native Fish]] introductions are identified as the most detrimental factors.

The species was listed as federally endangered in 1991, with a recovery plan in 1998 and recovery goals added in 2002. The [[Integrated Stocking Plan]] (Nesler et al. 2003) treats razorback sucker as the first priority among Upper Basin natives and recommends stocking 9,930 age-2 (>300 mm TL) fish per year in each of the middle Green and upper Colorado subbasins for six consecutive years. Earlier evaluations were limited by few stocked-fish recaptures, which this study's larger dataset (about 1995 to 2006) was designed to address.

The study area includes seven reaches: CO1, CO2, CO3, GU2, GR1, GR2, and GR3. Only CO2, CO3, GU2, GR1, and GR3 received stocked fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Upper Colorado River Basin is semi-arid, with streamflow driven by winter snowpack and regulated by diversions and reservoirs. Since [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] was completed in 1964, spring peak flows on the Green River are lower and summer base flows higher than historic levels. Recent low-flow years reduced spring peak magnitude and duration.

Hatchery fish are typically reared for 1.5 to 2.5 years in relatively benign conditions (stable flow, constant temperature, abundant food, and no predators), which the authors suggest leaves them unprepared for release. Predation by [[Non-Native Predatory Fish]] such as ictalurid catfish and centrarchids is a documented source of early mortality. Warm summer water temperatures (often above 25 °C in the middle Green River) may further stress fish and increase predator activity.

## Key Findings

**Survival (first interval, ry1):**
- Total length at stocking had a large positive effect on first-interval survival. Fish stocked below 200 mm TL approached zero survival, while fish above 500 mm TL reached 0.75 or higher.
- For the average stocked length of 252.5 mm TL, first-interval survival averaged over season was 0.05 (95% CI: 0.042–0.071).
- Stocking season had a large effect. For 252.5 mm TL fish, predicted first-interval survival was below 0.02 (95% CI: 0.012–0.022) for summer stocking, versus 0.07 (0.044–0.094) in spring, 0.09 (0.057–0.100) in autumn, and 0.08 (0.057–0.118) in winter. The pattern held at 300 mm and 400 mm TL.
- Survival confidence intervals for summer-stocked fish did not overlap those of other seasons until about 450 mm TL.

**Survival (post-first interval):**
- Overall post-ry1 survival was 0.75 (95% CI: 0.699–0.801), independent of length and season. This is similar to the 0.70 adult assumption in the integrated plan and to wild adult estimates (0.71 and 0.73 in earlier studies).

**Recapture probability:**
- Recapture probabilities were low, ranging from 0.002 to 0.128 for average-length fish.
- Fish stocked into GR1 had the highest recapture probabilities, followed by CO2, GR3, CO3, and GU2.
- First-occasion and subsequent-occasion probabilities did not differ within any group based on overlapping 95% CIs.
- Length at stocking had a small positive effect on recapture probability (0.0106; 95% CI: −0.0023 to 0.0237).

**Model selection:**
- The top model (24 parameters) had the lowest AICc and carried nearly 100% of AICc weight; the second-best model was about 10 AICc points behind.
- Survival was modeled with a first-interval effect, stocking season, and linear and quadratic TL. Recapture probability included group, time, first-occasion effect, and linear and quadratic TL.

**Movement:**
- The study included 150,121 stocked-fish records and 2,839 recapture events for movement analysis.
- Mean minimum distance per leg was 54.7 RK (range 0–514.9); mean time elapsed was 254 days (range 0–3,164); mean rate of travel was 0.87 RK/d.
- Distance, time, and rate were highest for initial legs (stocking to first capture).
- About 92.7% of legs were downstream movements.
- Winter-stocked fish moved the longest mean distance (86.7 RK), while summer-stocked fish moved fastest (1.34 RK/d).
- Fish fewer than 150 mm TL moved less than 20 RK over 13 or fewer days on average.
- Movement out of initial stocking reaches was more frequent in the Colorado and Gunnison subbasins (36.9%) than in the Green subbasin (7.7%).

**Management implications:**
- The authors recommend ceasing summer stocking and conducting a cost-benefit analysis of fish size, season, and first-interval survival.
- They suggest investigating causes of low first-interval survival (predation and lack of conditioning) and considering exercise conditioning, predator-avoidance training, and pond rearing.
- They recommend a balanced future dataset and a comprehensive monitoring program that includes early life stages.
