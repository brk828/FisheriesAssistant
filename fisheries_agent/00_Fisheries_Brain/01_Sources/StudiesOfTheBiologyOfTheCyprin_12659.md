# Life History and Biology of the Chub Genus Nocomis in Northeastern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 study by [[Ernest A. Lachner]] (Division of Fishes, U.S. National Museum) examines three North American chubs of the genus [[Nocomis]]: the [[Hornyhead Chub]], the [[River Chub]], and the [[Eastern Piedmont Chub]]. It covers distribution, abundance, habitat, nest-building and breeding behavior, nuptial male characteristics, scale-based age determination, growth, longevity, and sex ratios. The species are common, short-lived, and reach maturity at about three years (two years for faster-growing hornyhead males). Males are larger and grow faster than females, and the species differ clearly in nuptial coloration and head tubercle patterns.

## Background & Description

The paper is a reprint from *The American Midland Naturalist* (Vol. 48, No. 2, pp. 433-466, September 1952), produced from a doctoral thesis at [[Cornell University]]. The author notes that life histories are known for only a few North American Cyprinidae, and that such knowledge supports management of forage fish populations, which are valued as bait and prey rather than as food or game fish.

The three species differ in distribution and appearance:
- **Hornyhead Chub (*N. biguttatus*)**: Western and Great Lakes distribution, west of the Mississippi and in northern Ohio River drainages. Dorsal and caudal fins are orange-red in breeding males. Lacks occipital and interorbital swellings.
- **River Chub (*N. micropogon*)**: Atlantic slope and Ohio/Lake Ontario drainages. Has a vermilion spot absent in other species, and develops large occipital and interorbital swellings in breeding males.
- **Eastern Piedmont Chub (*N. leptocephalus*)**: Atlantic slope from the York River, Virginia, to the Savannah River drainage. Breeding males show orange or blue colors that vary among river systems.

Nuptial males are separated by tubercle number, size, and position, and by stripe and fin coloration (see [[Nuptial Male Characteristics]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and abundance.** All three species are common throughout their ranges and sometimes form most of the fish fauna in certain streams. *N. biguttatus* avoids sandstone-underlain streams and unglaciated uplands, and is abundant in more recently glaciated Illinois. *N. leptocephalus* is common in sandy Piedmont streams.

**Habitat.** *N. micropogon* favors larger, clear, clean streams and rivers with sand, gravel, or rubble bottoms, and young fish occur in faster riffles. *N. biguttatus* prefers smaller, clear, moderate-flow tributaries with some plant growth, and young occur in algal and vascular plant beds. Adults of both are associated with deep pools and large slab rocks.

**Nest associates.** Males build large dome-shaped stone nests, sometimes with about 10,000 pebbles, from late April to June. Nests attract spawning and breeding cyprinids, including shiners, dace, stonerollers, and darters, as well as egg predators such as the bluntnose minnow, hog sucker, and percids. A hornyhead male and common shiners showed close association, with both species found together in 99 percent of collections where either occurred (quoting Thompson and Hunt, 1930). Spawning over nests also favors hybridization, with hybrids reported among several associates.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction and fecundity**
- Total egg complements: *N. biguttatus* 460-725 (4 females, 80-89 mm); *N. micropogon* 400-625 (4 females, 92-100 mm); *N. leptocephalus* 710-800 (4 females, 82-92 mm).
- Several hundred nests can occur within a mile of clear-water stream with abundant *Nocomis*.

**Maturity**
- All three species mature and probably spawn at three years.
- Faster-growing *N. biguttatus* of both sexes mature at two years. A ripe two-year-old male was 119 mm.
- Males mature at a greater length than females.

**Nuptial males**
- Head tubercles: *N. biguttatus* more than 45 (range 40-60); *N. micropogon* under 40 (25-45); *N. leptocephalus* under 30.
- Breeding colors are most intense in males and absent in females, with species-specific stripes and fin colors (see [[Sexual Dimorphism]]).

**Age and growth**
- Scale analysis of 1,709 specimens from New York, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and North Carolina.
- Annulus formation occurs in late April or May, earlier in juveniles than adults.
- Males grow faster than females, with differences significant at the end of the first year.
- Mean lengths at end of first, second, and third summers:
  - *N. biguttatus* males 56, 90, 123 mm; females 49, 86, 106 mm.
  - *N. micropogon* males 63, 103, 138 mm; females 57, 94, 111 mm.
  - *N. leptocephalus* (James River) males 47, 77, 116 mm; females 40, 62, 86 mm.
- *N. micropogon* grows fastest and reaches the largest size. *N. leptocephalus* grows most slowly, with roughly equal annual increments over three years.

**Longevity and sex ratio**
- All three species are relatively short-lived.
- *N. micropogon* lived longest, with one male reaching its fifth summer (188 mm, the largest specimen recorded).
- *N. biguttatus* is shortest-lived; *N. leptocephalus* is intermediate.
- Overall sex ratios (males:females): *N. biguttatus* 1:1.3, *N. micropogon* 1:0.9, *N. leptocephalus* 1:2.3.
- More females than males survive to four summers.

**Largest recorded specimens**
- *N. micropogon*: male 188 mm, female 127 mm.
- *N. biguttatus*: male 124 mm, female 118 mm.
- *N. leptocephalus*: male 145 mm, female 102 mm.

**Conclusions**
- Sexual dimorphism in size and growth is pronounced, with males larger and growing faster. This is linked to nest-guarding behavior, paralleling percid patterns, though not to sexual maturity.
- The author notes the findings for *N. biguttatus* longevity differed from earlier reports, possibly due to environmental variation or racial differentiation.
