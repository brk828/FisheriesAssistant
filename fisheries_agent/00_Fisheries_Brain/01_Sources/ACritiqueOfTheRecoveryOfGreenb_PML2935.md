# Critique of the Recovery of Greenback Cutthroat Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This critique by Michael K. Young and Amy L. Harig, published in [[Conservation Biology]] (Volume 15, No. 6, December 2001), evaluates the recovery of the [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarkii stomias*) under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). Although the subspecies' population count is approaching the delisting goal, the authors argue that the recovery criteria are less rigorous than those used for other continental salmonids, that they underestimate extinction risk, and that delisting is unwarranted in the near future. They recommend replicating historical populations in additional waters, enlarging existing populations, improving access to refugia, and allowing natural recolonization, alongside a biological rationale and monitoring protocol for the recovery criteria.

## Background & Description

The ESA was intended not only to protect species at risk but to promote their recovery. Among fishes, few delisting successes exist: between 1979 and 1989, only one of 390 North American fishes listed as endangered, threatened, or of special concern was removed from the list due to recovery, and that one went extinct, while seven others improved in status (Williams et al. 1989).

Greenback cutthroat trout are a spring-spawning subspecies inhabiting relatively clear, cold waters, feeding largely on invertebrates. Like other cutthroat forms, they evolved unique adaptations to specific stream and lake environments, largely through isolation in large river basins during and after glaciation (Behnke 1992). The subspecies declined rapidly after large-scale settlement of Colorado's Front Range in the mid- to late 1800s, due to mining pollution, stream dewatering for agriculture, commercial harvest, and, most critically, the introduction of non-native trout. These hybridize with greenbacks and cannot coexist with [[Brook Trout]] or [[Brown Trout]]. By the 1930s the subspecies was considered extinct (Green 1937), though it was later rediscovered.

Historical range estimates are uncertain: the authors estimate the subspecies historically occupied up to 627 km of habitat in the Arkansas River basin and 695 km in the South Platte River basin (the source text's figures are OCR-affected; values are reported as given in the recovery analysis). Migration barriers and cold summer temperatures likely excluded them from most lakes and high-elevation streams.

The recovery program began after the 1973 ESA listing as endangered. The subspecies was downlisted to threatened in 1978 with a delisting target of 2000, after the discovery of a third genetically pure population and five translocations. A revised 1993 plan set a goal of 20 stable populations; the 1998 plan retained this goal with quantitative criteria.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population viability criteria under the 1998 plan** (four characteristics define a stable population):
1. A biomass of at least 22 kg/ha through natural reproduction.
2. At least 500 adults, defined as individuals ≥120 mm.
3. At least two naturally produced year classes in a 5-year period.
4. A physical barrier preventing upstream invasion by non-native salmonids.

A fifth criterion, a minimum of 2 ha of habitat, applies only to drainages fragmented by natural barriers.

**Status as of 1999:** 11 historical and 44 introduced populations existed. Of these, 18 stable populations were in the South Platte River basin and 3 in the Arkansas River basin. Fourteen of the 22 stable populations are in Rocky Mountain National Park. Eight of 14 stable introduced populations occur in high-elevation lakes outside the native range.

**Key vulnerabilities:** The authors highlight the risk from environmental stochasticity, citing the 1989 fire-induced flood that eradicated a Gila trout population, and note that large wildfires or severe winters could simultaneously extirpate many clustered populations. Isolation, though necessary to exclude non-natives, limits recolonization.

**Comparative context:** A comparison table of interior western salmonids (greenback cutthroat, [[Gila Trout]], [[Apache Trout]], [[Bull Trout]], [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], and [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]]) shows that greenback delisting goals are only somewhat more stringent than those for the endangered Gila trout and much less so than those for the threatened Apache and Lahontan cutthroat trout. Several reported figures in that table are illegible or incomplete in the source.

## Key Findings

- **Recovery is unprecedented but thin:** The Endangered Species Act has produced few fish recovery successes, and greenback cutthroat trout have seen one of the longest recovery efforts (over 40 years).
- **Biomass threshold is too low:** Median biomass for 62 Rocky Mountain populations was 54.5 kg/ha (Platts & McHenry 1988); the authors recommend 50 kg/ha rather than 22 kg/ha.
- **Adult abundance is insufficient:** 500 adults (effective population size of 240) may be inadequate. Effective population size may be much lower than census size, and the authors suggest approximately 1000 fish of ≥120 mm, with an effective size of 500 commonly recommended.
- **Demographic risk is high:** Estimated annual growth variance (0.24) implies under 50% probability of persistence for 100 years in populations under 2000 individuals, under model assumptions.
- **Year-class requirement is weak:** Reproductive failure in any year (probability 0.6) implies a 0.08 chance of unsuccessful reproduction across five consecutive years, so the two-year-class criterion may be too lenient. Cold summer temperatures appear to delay fry emergence and reduce survival in introductions.
- **Habitat criterion is inconsistent:** Only 6 of 26 streams with historical or introduced populations were long enough (7.5 km) to provide 2 ha of habitat, and Hilderbrand and Kershner (2000) suggest more than 10 km of stream may be needed.
- **Monitoring is inadequate:** Stream abundance estimates often extrapolate from one or two reaches, and the lack of a standardized protocol undermines evaluation.
- **Criteria lack biological justification:** The authors suspect criteria were based on what contemporary populations could achieve, not on long-term security, and that goals may be politically based, as argued for the [[Gila Topminnow]] (Simons et al. 1989).
- **Delisting is unwarranted:** Although extinction risk has declined since 1957, most remnant and introduced populations remain at moderate to extreme risk. Delisting is considered unwarranted in the near future.
- **Recommended actions:**
  - Replicate every historical population in at least one additional stream or lake, since only one founder population per basin has been used so far.
  - Enlarge existing populations and increase distribution to reduce catastrophe risk.
  - Select stream networks that can provide refugia and colonists.
  - Evaluate whether the two basins represent distinct population segments, which requires genetic and ecological analyses.
  - Provide explicit biological justification and statistically defensible monitoring protocols for all criteria.
- **Potential for recovery:** Recognition of recovery in part of the range (South Platte basin) may be appropriate if basins represent distinct population segments, which is likely given their geographic isolation for millennia.
