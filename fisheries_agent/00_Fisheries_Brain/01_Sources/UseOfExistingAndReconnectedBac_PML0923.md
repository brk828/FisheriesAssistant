# Habitat Use by Hatchery-Reared Adult Razorback Suckers and a Predictive Logistic Model for the Imperial Division, Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tracked hatchery-reared adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) fitted with ultrasonic transmitters in the Imperial Division of the [[Lower Colorado River]], Arizona and California, from December 1999 to July 2001. The fish strongly favored off-channel [[Backwaters]] over [[Main Channel]] habitat. A [[Logistic Regression]] model was built to predict backwater use from morphometric and water-quality variables. The model predicted nonuse well but predicted use poorly, so its main value is in ranking sites for [[Habitat Reconnection]] and identifying those least likely to be used. Razorback suckers used reconnected backwaters after restoration.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is native to the southwestern United States and once ranged throughout the [[Colorado River System]]. Its populations declined because of habitat alteration, water-quality decline, and competition and predation by [[Nonnative Fishes]]. Information on its life history and habitat requirements is limited, and recovery efforts, which have run for more than 20 years, have been hampered by this gap. Hatchery-reared fish are released as juveniles or adults to stock critical habitat.

The U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (USBR) planned reconnection projects within the Imperial Division after earlier habitat-selection work (Bradford et al. 1998; Gurtin and Bradford 2000). The expanded research goals were to (1) measure habitat characteristics alongside fish use before, during, and after reconnection, (2) develop predictive models of habitat use, and (3) determine whether razorback suckers would use newly reconnected habitats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The 9.5 km study reach has a mixed silt-sand substrate, numerous sand bars, and main-channel depths that rarely exceed 2.5 m, with very few riffles or pools. Floodplain habitats increase in number closer to Imperial Dam and include small oxbow side channels and backwaters. Riparian vegetation includes cattail (*Typha* spp.), bulrush (*Scirpus* spp.), reed grass (*Phragmites* spp.), and tamarisk (*Tamarix* spp.). Nonnative fishes, including [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Largemouth Bass]], were abundant throughout the lower river.

Habitat was classified into four types across 36 sites: 25 backwaters (0.6 to 28.1 ha), 9 side channels (0.53 to 4.17 ha), one main channel (about 133 ha, split into six 1.6 km segments), and one impoundment (3.2 ha) next to Imperial Dam. Measured variables included specific conductance, turbidity, temperature, pH, water velocity, mean and maximum depth, surface area, shoreline development index, distance to main channel, and sinuosity.

## Key Findings

- **Study fish:** 75 hatchery-reared adults (413–570 mm TL) were implanted with ultrasonic transmitters (about 5 g) and released in November 1999 (n = 38) and October 2000 (n = 37), using methods adapted from Clarkson et al. (1993).
- **Detection:** 25 roving surveys were completed. 410 contacts were recorded at 31 sites. 67 of 75 fish were contacted at least once, 8 were never contacted after release, and 6 were found outside the study area. The average was 5.6 contacts per fish, with a maximum of 71.
- **Habitat selection:** Habitat use was disproportionate to availability (χ² = 577.844, P < 0.01). Backwaters and the impoundment accounted for 80% of contacts while making up only 49% of the study area. Main-channel habitat, which covers about 45% of the area, held only 15% of contacts, and side channels held 5% (slightly less than availability).
- **Habitat exposure:** 57% of fish were exposed to more than one habitat type, 13 (17%) to at least three, and 5 fish to all types.
- **Diel use:** Within 16 individuals tracked over five diel surveys (January to June 2000), habitat use did not differ significantly among six daily periods (P = 0.879). Most movement stayed within a single habitat type.
- **Reconnection response:** After connectivity was restored (around April 2000), 23 of 42 fish released in the reconnection area stayed in the Imperial Restoration Project (IRP) area, and 69% of released fish remained there. 72 of 75 fish (96%) had moved from their release sites.
- **Correlates before restoration:** Use was significantly associated with backwater surface area, perimeter, shoreline development index, mean depth, entrance sinuosity, distance to main channel, and specific conductance. After restoration, only temperature and turbidity were significantly associated.
- **Logistic model:** The final model (model A) retained conductivity, pH, temperature, turbidity, mean depth, and distance to main channel, with surface area included as a static variable. Velocity and sinuosity did not contribute significantly. Physical features such as area and depth were judged more important than water quality for backwater selection. The model classified nonuse well (about 90%) but classified use poorly (about 17% correct for validation use cases). Performance dropped when applied to reconnected backwaters, which had changed in water quality and physical form.
- **Water quality change:** After dredging to reconnect habitats, velocity increased and temperature and conductivity decreased, probably because of greater flushing from the main stem. Turbidity did not change significantly.
- **Suitable habitat created:** Using model probabilities, three reconnected backwaters (ABl2-8, ABl2-4, and ABl2-3) were judged suitable, covering about 9.71 ha of the 18.94 ha reconnected area (about 56%, as reported by the authors).
- **Management implications:** Off-channel habitats are critical for adult razorback suckers in the Imperial Division. Natural flooding no longer maintains them, so [[Habitat Reconnection]] requires costly mechanical dredging. The model can rank sites by likelihood of nonuse, which can lower restoration costs and inform USBR water-management decisions.
- **Limitations:** Microhabitat structure and cover were not quantified because of homogeneous habitat and poor visibility. Results may not transfer to other regions or life stages, and the authors caution against applying the model to different habitats without testing.
