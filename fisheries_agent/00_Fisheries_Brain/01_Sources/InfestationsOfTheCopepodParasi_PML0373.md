# Lernaea cyprinacea Infestations of Native Fishes in Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report documents a recent outbreak of the copepod ectoparasite [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] on native fishes in the Colorado River system of [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. Infestations were found in two tributaries, [[Kanab Creek]] and the [[Little Colorado River]], and at the largest remaining breeding population of the endangered [[Gila Cypha]]. Parasitism was largely restricted to juvenile native fish, while exotic species carried no parasites. The authors attribute the pattern to the thermophilic biology of the parasite, which is favored by the warm, slow-flowing tributaries that are also used by juvenile natives. Because the parasite had not been documented in earlier Grand Canyon surveys, the authors consider the outbreak recent and a serious added threat to an already endangered species.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River and its tributaries in Grand Canyon historically supported eight native fish species. Construction of [[Hoover Dam]] (Lake Mead) and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (Lake Powell) altered flow, sediment transport, and water temperature. These changes, together with the invasion of exotic species, have caused declines and local extirpation of native fishes. [[Gila Robusta]], [[Gila Elegans]], and [[Ptychocheilus Lucius]] were once common in the canyon but are now apparently extinct there. [[Xyrauchen Texanus]] was rediscovered as a single specimen near the eastern end of the canyon. Three other native species, [[Rhinichthys Osculus]], [[Pantosteus Discobolus]], and [[Catostomus Latipinnis]], still occur in relatively high densities.

Earlier surveys (Miller 1944; Minckley 1973; Minckley and Blinn 1976; Suttkus et al. 1976) did not report any ectoparasite infestations in the mainstem or tributaries. Suttkus (cited in Johnson 1976) had found one *L. cyprinacea* on each of two specimens, one *G. cypha* and one *Cyprinus carpio*, but no outbreak was described.

The study area included the mainstem Colorado River near the mouth of the Little Colorado River, the Little Colorado River itself, and Kanab Creek. Fish were collected with seines and traps during the August and October 1978 sampling periods and preserved for examination. Each fish was measured for total length and, where possible, for shrinkage. Collection localities were recorded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Kanab Creek** originates on the Paunsaugunt Plateau in southern Utah and flows about 105 km into Arizona, entering the Colorado River at River Mile 143.5. Its mean discharge is reported as 0.14 m³/s, with extremes from flood flows to no flow. Water temperatures ranged from approximately 9 to 24 °C during the study (values partly garbled in the scanned text). Riparian vegetation includes *Tamarix chinensis*, *Juncus* spp., and *Typha latifolia*.

**Little Colorado River** is the largest tributary of the Grand Canyon. The mouth of the river is the only known successful breeding area for *G. cypha* in the system.

**Mainstem Colorado River.** Releases from Glen Canyon Dam, about 50 m below the surface of [[Lake Powell]], keep the hypolimnetic river cold year-round, roughly 7 to 10 °C (values partly garbled in the scan). Exotic fishes use both tributaries and the mainstem, but they show a preference for the mainstem.

**Thermal ecology of the parasite.** The optimum temperature range for *L. cyprinacea* development is 23 to 26 °C. Below 14 °C the parasite cannot complete its life cycle (Bauer 1959; Putz and Bowen 1964). The authors argue that warm August and October tributary conditions, together with sluggish currents and standing pools typical of late-summer and fall low flows, make these tributaries optimal for the parasite. Adult native fishes tolerate the mainstem but seek warmer tributary areas for spawning and rearing, so juveniles are the life stage most exposed.

## Key Findings

**Initial detection and spread**
- Infestation was first observed in August 1978. Juvenile *R. osculus* and *P. discobolus* from Kanab Creek were infested at that time (exact percentages are garbled in the scan).
- By October 1978, parasitism was more widespread. Juvenile *R. osculus* were infested at 10.0%, *P. discobolus* at about 55% (OCR-garbled), and *C. latipinnis* at 33.3% in Kanab Creek.
- For the first time, *G. cypha* (about 53%, OCR-garbled) and *C. latipinnis* (50.0%) in the Little Colorado River carried the parasite.
- Adults of all four native species were collected in both periods, but none had any parasites.

**Intensity of infestation**
- The largest load on a single juvenile was ten parasites on one *P. discobolus* from Kanab Creek in October.
- Most parasitized fish carried only a few parasites each.
- Attachment was most frequent near the base of the pectoral, pelvic, or dorsal fin, consistent with earlier studies (Bulow et al. 1979; Haley and Winn 1959; Janes 1968).

**Exotic species**
- No individuals of the exotic species examined (*C. carpio*, *I. punctatus*, *P. promelas*, *F. zebrinus*, and *S. gairdneri*) carried parasites, though all are known hosts elsewhere (Hoffman 1967; Janes 1968; Wilson et al. 1966). The authors suggest that the lack of infestation reflects habitat use and temperature preference rather than resistance.

**Comparison with earlier records**
- Suttkus et al. (1976) collected in the Little Colorado River nine times (1972–1976) and in Kanab Creek four times (1975–1976) without finding the parasite, which was virtually absent from the system until August 1978.
- The origin of the parasite is unknown. The authors consider it unlikely to have come from exotic fish stocks or hatcheries below Lake Powell, and it has no recorded source.

**Threat to Gila cypha**
- The Little Colorado River is the last known successful breeding refuge for [[Gila Cypha]], which is endangered. The authors warn that this population may be further threatened by the parasite.
- *Gila robusta* is known to be especially susceptible to *L. cyprinacea*, and the parasite can cause serious injury to its host (James 1968; McNeil 1981; Tidd 1934).

**Conclusion**
- The parasitism is an apparent recent outbreak linked to warm tributary habitats and the thermal sensitivity of the parasite. Native juveniles are most affected, and the threat to the endangered *G. cypha* is serious. The survival of parasitized juvenile natives is unknown, and the authors call for further monitoring.
