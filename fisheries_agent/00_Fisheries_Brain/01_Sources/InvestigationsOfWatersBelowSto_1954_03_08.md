## Executive Summary

This 1954 paper by Donald W. Pfitzer (Tennessee Game and Fish Commission, Dingell-Johnson Project) presents a three-year (1951–1953) ecological survey of ten tailwaters below major TVA storage reservoirs in the Tennessee Valley (South Holston, Watauga, Wilbur, Norris, Cherokee, Douglas, Calderwood, Apalachia, Dale Hollow, and Center Hill), spanning over 320 river-miles. The central finding is that the low-level, hypolimnetic power intakes of TVA dams (versus older, near-surface intakes) fundamentally altered downstream water chemistry — producing chronically cold, temperature-dampened, and seasonally oxygen-depleted discharge. This shift devastated native warm-water fish and invertebrate communities dominated by minnows and large insect taxa, replacing them with algal-dominated, cold-tolerant faunal assemblages. Paradoxically, these altered tailwaters proved highly suitable for stocked [[Rainbow Trout]], which exhibited excellent growth and survival, establishing artificial put-grow-and-take trout fisheries in waters that historically supported warm-water game and food fish.

## Background & Description

While the paper is primarily ecological rather than morphometric, it notes:
- **[[Rainbow Trout]] (Salmo gairdnerii irideus):** Stocked as fingerlings (2–5 inches); exhibited near-continuous growth (~1 inch/month) with no complete seasonal growth cessation except in fish migrating to tributaries to spawn, where a **spawning check** appears in the scales, accompanied by measurable weight loss over a 7-week migration period (n=30 tracked individuals).
- **Condition factor (K):** Values as high as **2.65** recorded for rainbow trout, indicating exceptional robustness/growth condition in tailwater habitats.
- **Gonadal abnormality:** Warm-water species in tailwaters exhibited an unusual reproductive pathology — ovaries retaining eggs well beyond the normal spawning period, with new egg masses forming around old, unspawned egg masses (paralleling findings of Eschmeyer and Smith 1942/1943 below [[Norris Dam]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physicochemical drivers:**
- Low-level (hypolimnetic) power intakes at TVA dams (contrasted with older, epilimnion/thermocline-level intakes) discharge cold-to-cool, oxygen-variable water year-round.
- **Density currents** (per Wiebe, 1940) in deep tributary reservoirs create erratic dissolved oxygen (DO) profiles in some tailwaters (e.g., [[Watauga Tailwater]]), while shallow, high-inflow reservoirs like [[Cherokee Reservoir]] and [[Douglas Reservoir]] lack density currents and instead warm progressively through summer as cold hypolimnetic water is replaced.
- Pre-dam temperature extremes of 80–85°F (max) and 32–34°F (min) were compressed post-dam to an average range of **39–65°F**, with annual averages of **50–55°F**.
- DO follows an inverted seasonal pattern relative to natural warm-water streams: near saturation in winter, crashing to <1 ppm in August–September in some systems (e.g., [[Cherokee Tailwater]]).
- **Reaeration rates** differ drastically by channel morphology: shoal-dominated rivers (e.g., [[Calderwood Tailwater]] on the Little Tennessee River) recover to near-saturation DO within ~3.9 miles, while pool-dominated rivers (e.g., [[Cherokee Tailwater]]) fail to reach saturation even after 50 miles. Reaeration coefficients were markedly higher in shoal reaches (k₂ ≈ 2.42 day / 1.88 night) than pool reaches (k₂ ≈ 0.208 day / 0.074 night).
- Tailwaters experience severe daily hydropeaking: high discharge during weekday peak power demand (~6 a.m.–9 p.m.), minimal/no discharge at night and weekends.

**Biotic community shifts:**
- Pre-impoundment surveys (Chance & Bailey, 1947) recorded 43 fish species in South Fork Holston and 32 in the Watauga River. Post-dam surveys found only **17 species** in [[South Holston Tailwater]] and **13 species** in [[Watauga Tailwater]]/[[Wilbur Tailwater]] — losses concentrated among minnow (Cyprinidae) species.
- Surviving species in South Holston tailwater include Shorthead redhorse, River redhorse, Northern hogsucker, River chub, Silver shiner, Warpaint shiner, Ohio stoneroller, Stone cat, Holston logperch, Bluebreast darter, Greenside darter, Northern smallmouth bass, Northern bluegill, Northern rock bass, Tennessee banded muddler, Longnose dace, and Blacknosed dace.
- Watauga/Wilbur tailwaters retained Rainbow trout, White sucker, Northern hogsucker, Mountain blacknose dace, River chub, Silver shiner, Warpaint shiner, Ohio stoneroller, Northern smallmouth bass, Northern rock bass, Tennessee banded muddler, Highfin sucker, and Blue catfish.
- Most remaining warm-water species show little to no successful natural reproduction.
- **Algal dominance:** Cladophora crispata dominates [[Norris Tailwater]] and [[Cherokee Tailwater]] (forming false bottoms requiring USGS gauge correction; also hosts Tendipedid larvae and Gammarus scuds); Ulothrix rivularis dominates South Holston tailwater; Nitella flexilis is important at Calderwood; Spirogyra sp. and Chara contraria co-dominate Watauga tailwater, supporting invertebrate mats considered key to that water's trout-fishery success. Stocked trout were observed ingesting and partially digesting algae, likely for nourishment.
- **Invertebrate community shift** (documented in detail for South Holston tailwater, Table 4): increases in Simuliidae, Tendipedidae (chironomids), Gammarus scuds, and Trichoptera (Hydropsyche), and snails; concurrent declines/disappearance of large Plecoptera (Acroneuria internata, Taeniopteryx nivalis, Neophasganophora sp.) and the hellgrammite Corydalus cornutus, especially at downstream stations. General tailwater trend: large warm-water benthic fauna replaced by small cold-water-tolerant taxa (Tendipedidae, Simuliidae, Hydropsychidae, Gammarus, snails). [[Cherokee Tailwater]] is a notable exception with extremely depauperate benthic fauna overall.

## Key Findings

- **Root cause identified:** Low-level (deep) power intakes on TVA dams — not dam presence per se — are the primary driver of tailwater ecological degradation, via chronic cold-water, oxygen-variable discharge.
- **Thermal regime homogenization:** Temperature extremes reduced from 80–85°F/32–34°F pre-dam to 39–65°F post-dam (annual avg. 50–55°F).
- **Reaeration is channel-morphology-dependent:** shoal-rich tailwaters (Calderwood, Wilbur, South Holston, Apalachia) reoxygenate quickly; pool-dominated tailwaters (Norris, Cherokee, Douglas, Watauga) recover DO very slowly (not reaching saturation even after 50 miles in Cherokee).
- **Massive fish diversity loss:** ~60% reduction in species richness in surveyed tailwaters, concentrated in minnows (Cyprinidae), with surviving warm-water species largely failing to reproduce successfully.
- **Reproductive pathology:** Warm-water fish in tailwaters show abnormal ovarian egg retention, paralleling prior findings below Norris Dam.
- **Ecosystem regime shift:** Benthic macroinvertebrate communities shifted from large warm-water forms (large Plecoptera, hellgrammites) to small cold-tolerant forms (chironomids, blackflies, caddisflies, scuds, snails); plant communities shifted to algal dominance (Cladophora, Ulothrix, Spirogyra, Chara, Nitella).
- **Trout fishery success:** Despite (or because of) these altered conditions, stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] fingerlings (2–5 in.) thrived with ~1 in./month growth, condition factors up to 2.65, and tolerance of DO as low as ~2 ppm; [[Brook Trout]] stocking uniformly failed to establish fisheries. Cherokee and Center Hill tailwaters failed to produce trout fisheries despite heavy experimental stocking of rainbow, brook, and brown trout.
- **Limited natural trout reproduction:** Fin-clipping of ~500,000 stocked fingerlings showed only 10–15% non-fin-clipped (wild) recruits in tailwater populations, with most natural reproduction inferred to occur in tributary streams rather than the main tailwater channel.
- **Fish localization behavior:** Rainbow trout populations show strong spatial localization patterns along tailwaters up to 50 miles long, persisting even through low-DO/high-temperature periods in fall.
- **Management implication:** Rough fish (redhorse, suckers) increases in tailwaters may partly reflect upstream/downstream migration from adjoining impoundments rather than true in-situ production, complicating assessment of net fishery value change from dam construction.
