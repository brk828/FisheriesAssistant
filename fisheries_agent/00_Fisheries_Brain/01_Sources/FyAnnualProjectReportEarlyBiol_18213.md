# Early Biology Studies of Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker in the Green River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #larval-fish

## Executive Summary

This FY 93 annual report documents comprehensive early life-history investigations on [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) conducted by the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at [[Colorado State University]] under the [[Colorado River Recovery Program]]. The multi-year study (FY 91-93) examined effects of flow, temperature, and environmental variables on early biology of endangered fishes in the [[Green River]] system, with direct application to [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations. Research focused on: (1) validating otolith-aging techniques for determining growth and survival factors; (2) describing ontogeny of sensory systems and behavioral responses to physical habitat conditions; (3) determining factors affecting growth, survival, and dispersal of early life stages; (4) evaluating marking techniques for razorback sucker; and (5) assessing diet overlap and potential competition between native and nonnative fishes in backwater nursery habitats.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Recovery Program]] addresses recovery of endangered fishes through integrated management of flows, habitat, and nonnative species. This project specifically supports RIPRAP elements related to instream flow recommendations, habitat management above and below the [[Duchesne River]] confluence, temperature enhancement options from [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], and research to fill critical life-history information gaps.

**Study Species and Rationale:**

[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is listed as endangered by the U.S. Department of Interior. The species has been eliminated from portions of its historic [[Colorado River Basin]] range due to cold summer water temperatures caused by mainstream dams. [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) faces similar threats, with recovery dependent on understanding reproductive and recruitment success in altered river systems.

**Methodological Approaches:**

The research employed laboratory-controlled experiments and field collections across 520 km of the middle-lower mainstem [[Green River]], from [[Echo Park]] in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], Colorado, downstream to [[Turks Head]], Utah. The study area was divided into upper reach (Echo Park to [[Sand Wash]]) and lower reach (Sand Wash to Turks Head). Laboratory facilities at [[Colorado State University]] enabled precise manipulation of temperature, flow, light, turbidity, and food availability to isolate effects of individual environmental variables.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and Hydrological Setting:**

The [[Green River]] system represents critical habitat for endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes. Operation of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] fundamentally altered natural flow and temperature regimes that historically characterized the system. The dam releases cold hypolimnetic water, depressing summer temperatures below levels that historically supported native fish reproduction and recruitment. Backwater habitats serve as critical nursery areas for larval and juvenile fishes, providing refuge from mainstem currents and access to zooplankton and insect prey.

**Community Composition:**

The fish assemblage includes four native species ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] *Catostomus discobolus*, [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] *Catostomus latipinnis*) and five nonnative species ([[Red Shiner]] *Cyprinella lutrensis*, [[Sand Shiner]] *Notropis stramineus*, [[Fathead Minnow]] *Pimephales promelas*, [[Speckled Dace]] *Rhinichthys osculus*, [[Channel Catfish]] *Ictalurus punctatus*, [[Green Sunfish]] *Lepomis cyanellus*). Nonnative species potentially compete with native fishes for food and space in nursery habitats, and some species (particularly [[Red Shiner]]) prey on native fish larvae.

**Temperature Regime Importance:**

Historical [[Colorado River]] environments were characterized by warm, variable summer temperatures. The species' tolerance of relatively wide temperature ranges reflects evolutionary adaptation to these variable conditions. Current cold-water releases from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] may limit survival and growth of temperature-dependent early life stages, making temperature management a critical recovery tool.

## Key Findings

**Otolith Microstructure and Age Determination:**

Otoliths of [[Colorado Squawfish]] form prior to hatching but do not deposit daily increments until after hatching. Daily increments were deposited in fish reared under all temperature conditions tested (18, 22, 26, and 30°C constant; 18, 22, and 26°C fluctuating with 5°C diel variation). Age determinations from otoliths were more accurate for fish reared in fluctuating temperatures than constant temperatures, and more accurate for fish <15 days old than >25 days old. However, wild-caught fish were generally much easier to age than laboratory-reared fish >25 days old. Otolith size appears consistent with somatic growth, making back-calculation of growth rates feasible. Otolith aging of nearly 2,000 drift-net-caught larvae from 1990-1992 collections is nearly complete, providing a tool to correlate growth and survival with environmental parameters such as streamflow and water temperature.

**Temperature Effects on Development and Survival:**

Laboratory experiments with [[Colorado Squawfish]] embryos from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] revealed:

- **Hatch success:** Average hatch was 72% at 18°C, 67% at 22°C, 62% at 26°C, and 38% at 30°C (constant temperature only). No significant difference existed between constant and fluctuating temperatures.

- **Larval survival to 7 days:** 68% at 18°C, 64% at 22°C, 83% at 26°C, and 13% at 30°C (constant only). Survival was 10-31% higher in fluctuating than constant temperatures. Poor survival at 30°C may reflect low hatch rates and poor larval condition.

- **Abnormalities:** Incidence was 2-22% at 18-26°C and 100% at 30°C. No detectable differences existed between constant and fluctuating temperature regimes.

- **Developmental timing:** Time to initiation of hatch, swim bladder inflation, and exogenous feeding decreased at higher temperatures. First feeding occurred approximately 31 hours earlier in fluctuating versus constant temperatures.

- **Growth:** Differences in larval lengths at hatching (day 1) and day 7 across temperature treatments were small and not considered ecologically significant.

**Growth and Survival Response to Food and Temperature:**

Experiments using response surface methodology with five food levels, four constant temperatures (18, 22, 26, 30°C), three fluctuating temperatures (18, 22, 26°C), and 750 degree-day accumulation as endpoint (N=120 experimental units across 35 treatment combinations with 3 replicates each) revealed:

- **Growth optimization:** Growth was maximized in the 28-32°C temperature range at the highest food level. Constant versus fluctuating regime had no effect on growth. A significant interaction term indicated that at 18°C, growth rates were much lower regardless of food level.

- **Survival optimization:** Survival was highest at intermediate to high food and temperature levels. Constant and fluctuating regimes had no effect on survival (analyses incomplete).

**Starvation Tolerance:**

Experiments on delayed food presentation determined the "point of no return" (time after which starved fish begin feeding but do not survive) was approximately 15 days. Fish that fed prior to 15 days grew and survived at rates comparable to control fish. Fish that did not feed after 15 days died. [[Colorado Squawfish]] larvae can withstand long periods without food under benign laboratory conditions; wild fish facing current, foraging demands, and predation pressure likely have shorter tolerance periods.

**Sensory System Ontogeny:**

Detailed microscopic examination of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae at key developmental stages (immediately after hatching, swim-up, first feeding, beginning of flexion, beginning of post-flexion) revealed:

- **Mechanoreception (neuromasts):** Neuromasts were present at hatching in both species. Number and distribution increased with age and size. Morphology differed with age, size, and location. Hair cell orientation suggests sensitivity to mechanical stimuli from both anteroposterior and dorsoventral directions. Neuromasts play important roles in feeding, predator avoidance, rheotaxis, schooling, and intraspecific communication.

- **Vision (retina) in Colorado Squawfish:** At hatching, retinal cellular layers were somewhat differentiated but cone outer segments (containing photon-excitable retinal) were absent, suggesting poor or absent visual function. Greatest differentiation occurred between hatching and swim-up; by swim-up, larvae had completely stratified retina with formed cone outer segments. By beginning of post-flexion, the retina was very complex with distinct cell layers, infiltrated pigment epithelial processes, and forming vitreal space. Only cone cells were found; no rod cells were detected through post-flexion stage.

- **Chemoreception (olfaction and gustation) in Colorado Squawfish:** At hatching, olfactory placodes were beginning to invaginate with few cilia and undifferentiated cells. Ciliated receptor, microvillar, ciliated nonsensory, and rod cells were all present. By swim-up, placodes had migrated dorsally and begun forming a lumen, with elongated receptor cells. By first feeding and beginning of flexion, primary lamella first appeared. By beginning of post-flexion, the olfactory organ was an open pit filled with cilia with protruding primary lamella. Taste buds developed by first feeding, with bulbous structures and ciliary projections found on lower-lip epidermis and roof of mouth.

**Functional Sensory Development and Feeding:**

Feeding experiments with naive larvae presented living or recently killed 24-hour-old brine shrimp nauplii under light or dark conditions (8 feeding trials at 3-day intervals beginning 4 days after first feeding; 8-10 replicates per treatment per trial) revealed species-specific sensory dominance:

- **Colorado Squawfish:** Mechanoreception was the dominant initial sense for feeding. As larvae aged, acuity and importance of chemoreception and vision increased. Mechanoreception was primary for larvae 1-10 days after first feeding, vision became more important at 13 days, and chemoreception became more important at 13-16 days after first feeding.

- **Razorback Sucker:** Vision was the dominant initial sense for feeding. As larvae aged, acuity and importance of mechanoreception and chemoreception increased. Vision was primary for larvae 1-4 days after first feeding, mechanoreception became more important at 7 days, and chemoreception became more important at 13 days after first feeding.

**Swimming Performance:**

Critical velocity experiments in a velocity tube with 7, 16, 34, and 57-day-old [[Colorado Squawfish]] showed maximum swimming speed typically increased with body size: 13.3 cm/s (7 days old), 15.2 cm/s (16 days), 16.7 cm/s (34 days), and 19.0 cm/s (57 days). Endurance times under sustained exercise also increased with growth. [[Razorback Sucker]] at 15, 24, and 31 days old showed maximum swimming speeds of 15.2, 15.2, and 16.5 cm/s respectively, very similar to [[Colorado Squawfish]].

**Recruitment Patterns and Drift Dynamics:**

Analysis of nearly 2,000 drift-net-caught [[Colorado Squawfish]] larvae captured downstream of [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]] spawning areas revealed unexpected patterns. Hatch dates of age-0 fish that survived to fall did not necessarily correspond to hatch dates of the majority of larval fish caught in drift. For example, at the [[Yampa River]] collection site in 1991, the largest drift peak occurred on 30 June, but analysis of age-0 fish caught in fall in the Jensen-Ouray reach suggested very few fish from that early drift peak survived in that reach. Reasons for disparities in recruitment patterns remain under investigation but may have implications for summer flow recommendations.

**Diet Composition and Overlap:**

Analysis of digestive-tract contents from 2,297 fish representing nine species (2,554 fish examined total; <3% empty) collected from backwaters throughout 520 km of middle-lower [[Green River]] during summer (30 June-27 August) and fall (22 September-10 December) 1987 revealed:

- **Colorado Squawfish (N=972; 7.5-73.0 mm TL):** Insects (mostly aquatic forms, particularly immature dipterans including chironomid larvae) were principal diet components. Zooplankton (cladocerans and cyclopoid copepods) was particularly important for fish <41 mm TL (especially <31 mm TL), with proportional contribution decreasing as TL increased. Relative importance of immature dipterans increased as TL increased. Ten individuals (21-73 mm TL) consumed fish prey; many prey were too digested for identification but identified specimens were primarily [[Red Shiner]] larvae <15 mm TL. The smallest piscivorous individual (21 mm TL) contained four prey fish (all [[Red Shiner]]), representing approximately 80% of gut contents.

- **Other Native Species:** [[Bluehead Sucker]] (N=42; 23.0-58.9 mm TL) consumed moderate amounts of algae and debris. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (N=21; 32.0-64.3 mm TL) consumed insects (especially immature dipterans), zooplankton, and debris. [[Speckled Dace]] (N=35; 23.1-39.8 mm TL) consumed insects with minimal debris.

- **Nonnative Species:** [[Red Shiner]] (N=729; 11.3-74.5 mm TL) and [[Sand Shiner]] (N=92; 22.2-53.2 mm TL) consumed more semi-aquatic or terrestrial insects than other fishes. [[Fathead Minnow]] (N=330; 11.0-65.9 mm TL) consumed predominantly debris. [[Channel Catfish]] (N=58; 22.5-70.0 mm TL) and [[Green Sunfish]] (N=18; 20.7-56.8 mm TL) consumed insects and zooplankton.

- **Diet Overlap:** Degree of overlap between young-of-year [[Colorado Squawfish]] and other fishes (measured by Schoener's resource-overlap index using mean volume percentages) was influenced mainly by proportions of zooplankton and immature dipterans consumed. Most comparisons yielded Schoener-index values <0.60. Biologically important overlap (values >0.60) occurred only for certain comparisons with [[Red Shiner]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Green Sunfish]], and especially [[Channel Catfish]]. Higher overlap values were primarily attributed to high relative importance of immature midges and secondarily to zooplankton consumption. Within each reach, overlap values for all TL subgroups of [[Colorado Squawfish]] generally decreased as TL of other species increased. Overlap values were generally higher in the lower reach than upper reach.

- **Parasites:** Digestive tracts of six [[Colorado Squawfish]] (all >27 mm TL, collected from both reaches in fall) contained cestode parasites (species undetermined); cestodes were not found in other fishes.

**Marking Techniques for Razorback Sucker:**

Laboratory experiments on mass-marking embryos and recently hatched [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae using fluorescent compounds (tetracycline hydrochloride [TC] fluorescing yellow; alizarin complexone [AL] fluorescing pink-red) revealed:

- **Single marking:** For embryos 3-4 days after fertilization, mark quality was best (lucid to bright) and survival highest (85-100%) at 150-350 mg AL/L for 12-30 hours and 150-350 mg TC/L for 18-30 hours. AL produced distinct pink-red fluorescent rings; TC produced clear yellow marks.

- **Double marking:** For recently hatched larvae, mark quality was best (lucid to bright) and survival highest (80-100%) at 12.5-25 mg AL/L for 6-18 hours followed by 24-hour recovery period, then 150-350 mg TC/L for 6-18 hours. Otoliths presented discernible double-circular marks with inner pink-red fluorescent ring and outer yellow fluorescent ring.

These methods can effectively mark late embryos and early larvae of [[Razorback Sucker]] for differentiation among fish groups, supporting hatchery propagation, reintroduction programs, and mark-recapture studies to assess abundance, distribution, and survival of naturally and artificially produced young.
