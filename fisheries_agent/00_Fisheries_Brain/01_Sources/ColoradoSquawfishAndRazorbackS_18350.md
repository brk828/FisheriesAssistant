# Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker Grow-out Pond Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 final report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Colorado River Fishery Project documents three years (1986–1989) of experiments on rearing endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in four former [[Gravel-Pit Ponds]] near Grand Junction, Colorado. The work was funded by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] as a conservation measure tied to the Green Mountain and Ruedi Reservoir water sales. The study aimed to refine pond-rearing techniques, to attempt spawning of wild squawfish, and to produce recommendations for future stocking.

Pond culture produced fast growth in some ponds but very low squawfish survival in most. Razorback sucker survival was very high in [[Humphrey Pond]]. The authors attribute squawfish losses to a mix of poor forage, [[Asian Tapeworm]] infection, low oxygen and [[Hydrogen Sulfide]], and possibly cannibalism and bird predation.

## Background & Description

**Study species.**
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is a large cyprinid of the upper Colorado River basin. Its wild populations are declining, possibly because of high early-life mortality.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a catostomid whose wild recruitment is also limited.

**Rationale.** Earlier work by [[Osmundson (1987)]] showed that squawfish grown in ponds with self-sustaining forage fish can grow more than twice as fast as fish in rivers or hatcheries. That study recommended rearing squawfish to 200–250 mm before stocking to reduce predation. [[Marsh and Brooks (1989)]] recommended rearing razorbacks to 300 mm or more.

**Study ponds.**

| Pond | Size | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| [[Fish Chalet Pond]] | 1.62 ha; max depth 3.2 m | Rotenoned to remove catfish before stocking |
| [[West Pond]] | 1.34 ha; max depth 3.2 m | Initially fish-free; dense muskgrass beds |
| [[13th-Hole Pond]] | Small golf-course pond | Good fathead minnow population |
| [[Humphrey Pond]] | 5.4 ha; 1.4–2.0 m deep | Rotenoned in May 1987 to remove resident fish |

**Methods (summary).**
- Forage base: [[Fathead Minnow]] was stocked as forage. Ponds were fertilized with triple superphosphate.
- Broodstock: Seven wild squawfish were held at a Grand Junction pond in 1986, and six were held at [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] in fall 1986.
- Stocking: About 7,000 squawfish (28–61 mm) were stocked into Fish Chalet and West ponds in late August 1986. In May 1986, 234 surviving squawfish (65–135 mm) were stocked into 13th-Hole Pond. About 50,000 swim-up fry (6–8 mm) went into Humphrey Pond on 11 June 1987, along with 430 razorback fingerlings (44–76 mm) on 17 June 1987.
- Monitoring: Length, weight and condition were measured monthly. Condition was calculated with [[Fulton's Condition Factor]]. Stomachs and parasites were examined. Dissolved oxygen, temperature, conductivity and Secchi clarity were measured biweekly.
- Survival: Final survival was estimated from fish recovered during [[Rotenone Treatment]] in 1989.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Forage and growth.** Squawfish growth depended on the availability of appropriately sized prey. In Fish Chalet Pond, carp were too large to be eaten, and fatheads were scarce. Growth stalled in mid-1987 and condition fell. Growth was faster and condition higher in 13th-Hole and Humphrey ponds, which had more forage relative to squawfish density.

**Temperature and oxygen.** Squawfish growth began above about 13 °C. Fish Chalet Pond reached 22–27 °C in summer. Dissolved oxygen fell to 1.0 mg/L on the bottom of West Pond in late August 1987. Hydrogen sulfide reached 0.101 mg/L at the bottom of Fish Chalet Pond in March 1988, which is above the 96-hour LC50 for juvenile bluegill (0.04778 mg/L).

**Parasites and disease.** Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) entered West Pond in 1986 via transferred fatheads, and later Humphrey Pond. Infection reached 100% in West Pond by August 1987 and in Humphrey Pond by July 1988, with a mean of 344 small worms per fish at the peak. Late-October 1988 adult worm density averaged 25 per fish in Humphrey Pond.

**Predation.** Predatory fish were absent from Fish Chalet and West ponds. Roundtail chub, which were removed from 13th-Hole Pond in early 1987, may have caused the heavy losses there. Osprey were observed taking squawfish at Humphrey Pond. Cannibalism was considered possible but was not confirmed.

## Key Findings

**Artificial spawning.** Attempts to spawn captive wild squawfish failed in 1986 and 1987. The authors attribute this to stress from handling and confinement.

**Survival by pond (Colorado squawfish):**
- **Fish Chalet Pond:** 107 fish remained in September 1987 (95% CI 80–135), about 1.5% of those stocked. No squawfish were found in September 1989, which is 100% loss over three years.
- **West Pond:** At least 34.9% survived 10 months, based on an estimated school of 2,440 fish. Of the 330 recovered in September 1989, the three-year survival rate was 4.7%.
- **13th-Hole Pond:** Only 2.1% of 234 survived the first seven months. Five fish persisted for at least another 18 months.
- **Humphrey Pond:** 131 fish were recovered in October 1989, about 0.3% of about 50,000 fry.

**Survival (razorback sucker).** Of the 430 razorbacks stocked into Humphrey Pond, 365 were recovered in 1989, and 62 had been removed earlier. The authors estimate overall survival at 99.3%.

**Growth.**
- Fish Chalet squawfish condition fell from 0.74 at stocking to 0.57 by November 1986. The mean for the period in the ponds was 0.62.
- 13th-Hole squawfish reached 318 mm by October 1987, with condition of 0.83–0.87. The last capture, on 29 June 1988, averaged 354 mm and 420 g with condition of 0.94.
- Humphrey squawfish averaged 317.2 mm in October 1989. The largest was 415 mm and 556 g.
- Razorbacks in Humphrey Pond grew from 54.8 mm and 1.48 g (June 1987) to 462 mm and 1088 g (October 1989). Fertilization raised plankton and algae, which supported fast growth.

**Diet.**
- Fish Chalet: The diet was mainly cladocerans, copepods and insects. Of 290 stomachs, 74% held food. Only eight of 215 fish with food (3.7%) had eaten fish, all fatheads.
- West Pond: Of 67 stomachs, 53.7% were empty. Diptera and mayflies dominated, and no fish were eaten.
- Humphrey Pond: Chironomids were the main prey until mid-1988. Fish entered the diet in July 1988, starting with fish of 155 mm and longer.

**Sexual maturity.** In West Pond, 2 of 15 squawfish sampled in May 1989 were tuberculated, at 260–263 mm. Published values put maturity at more than 400 mm for wild fish, and at 460–510 mm for Dexter NFH fish. The authors suggest maturity depends on size rather than age, and that it may be plastic under pond conditions. All 34 razorbacks dissected in fall 1989 had developing gonads, and 65% were male.

**Recommendations (summary).**
1. Conduct spawning at Dexter NFH, and leave wild adults undisturbed for about a year before attempting it.
2. Eliminate predatory fish before stocking squawfish.
3. Screen forage fish for parasites before introducing them to ponds.
4. Establish forage before stocking, and time stocking to match the size of available prey.
5. Match stocking densities to forage supply.
6. Remove squawfish at 200–250 mm TL, before survival declines.
7. Plan fish-recovery methods in advance. Draining ponds with pumps was suggested, at about $600 per week for two pumps.
8. Test stocking time and size. Razorbacks may be stocked after one growing season at about 300 mm.

**Conclusions.** Pond culture is useful for rapid growth of both species. Causes of squawfish mortality in ponds remain unresolved, and the best guesses are starvation, Asian tapeworm, low oxygen, and hydrogen sulfide. Future work should focus on those causes and on maximizing stocking density without compromising growth.
