# A Decade of Managed and Natural Population Change for Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This paper assesses the status of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the Arizona-Nevada border that holds the largest remaining population of this endangered species. Hatchery-reared juveniles have been repatriated to the lake and tracked as they join the wild adult population. Annual spring monitoring from 1991 to 1999 shows a decline in wild adults and a recent increase in the number and proportion of repatriated fish. The authors conclude that the management effort is on a trajectory toward recovery, but that the repatriation program needs enhancement, particularly larger release sizes, before the population can become self-sustaining.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was once widely distributed across the [[Colorado River]] basin, common in the main channel from Wyoming south through Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Arizona, and California to the Gulf of California. Abundance in Lake Mohave declined from an estimated 60,000 to 75,000 fish in the 1980s to about 25,000 by the mid-1990s (Marsh 1994). The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) and the Native Fish Work Group consider the species endangered, and the recovery plan (USFWS 1998) guides management.

Monitoring began in the 1960s under W. L. Minckley of [[Arizona State University]]. The [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), organized in 1990, includes the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the Nevada Division of Wildlife, the US Bureau of Reclamation, FWS, the US Geological Survey, the National Park Service, and ASU. Monitoring is conducted semi-annually, with a spring sampling event known as the [[Razorback Roundup]] and a fall collection. Fish are measured, examined for parasites, disease, and general health, and marked with a [[PIT Tagging]] tag if unmarked.

The proactive part of the program raises young fish in protected environments free of non-native fish. Juveniles larger than 30 cm total length are released into the lake. The stated goal is to establish a population of not fewer than 50,000 repatriated razorback suckers to replace the aging adult stock. Current practice harvests larvae from naturally produced fish, which preserves the species' genetic diversity (Dowling et al. 1996).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mohave is the largest remaining population stronghold for this species. Several threats constrain recovery:

- **Predation:** Predation by non-native fishes, including ictalurid catfishes, limits survival of small repatriated fish (Marsh and Brooks 1989). Survival of fish under 30 cm in central Arizona rivers was similarly limited by predation.
- **Genetic erosion:** As the wild population shrinks, its genetic variability declines. The authors warn that this loss is irreversible and would remove the raw material for adaptation and evolution.
- **Reproductive delay:** Repatriated fish take several years to reach reproductive maturity and join wild adults on spawning grounds, so the effects of stocking appear with a lag.
- **Hatchery genetics:** Mitochondrial DNA studies (Dowling et al. 1996a, b) inform how hatchery stocks should be managed to avoid losing diversity.

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- The NFWG database contains [[PIT Tagging]] records for all captured and recaptured wild adults and all released and recaptured repatriated juveniles.
- Data from 1991 to 1999 were analyzed. Older records from 1984 (Bazek et al.) were excluded because few recaptures remained and their inclusion would inflate results.
- Two mark-recapture estimators were applied: the [[Schumacher-Eschemeyer Estimate]] (multiple census) and the [[Schnabel Census Estimate]] (annual census).
- Because newly repatriated fish are small and do not yet mix with wild adults, a second analysis excluded fish stocked in 1997 and 1998. The authors consider this approach more realistic for estimating wild adult abundance.
- Percent repatriated juveniles (PRI) among total recaptures was used to estimate the repatriated population, and survival was calculated by dividing that estimate by the number released.

**Wild Adult Population**

- The Schumacher-Eschemeyer multiple census estimate for 1992 to 1999 was 18,248 fish. This estimate is similar to the Schnabel estimate for the same period (the paper's comparison was statistically tested; the reported result was not significantly different at P < 0.05).
- Schnabel annual estimates declined over the period. The 1999 estimate was 9,087, and the 8-year mean was 19,303.
- The authors state that fewer than 10,000 wild adults remain in Lake Mohave.
- The proportion of wild adults in the catch decreased steadily, while the proportion of repatriated fish increased.

**Sampling Totals**

- The NFWG recorded 2,326 untagged adult razorback suckers and 1,142 tagged (recaptured) wild adults during the Razorback Roundup sampling period.
- Recaptures of repatriated fish totaled 212 across 1992 to 1999.

**Repatriation Program**

- More than 23,000 juveniles were released into Lake Mohave between 1992 and 1998 (total 23,334).
- Releases rose from 131 fish in 1992 to 7,617 in 1998. Intermediate releases were 1,433 (1993), 2,201 (1994), 1,501 (1995), 3,085 (1996), and 7,366 (1997).
- Recaptures were highest in 1994 and 1995, when the largest average total length at release was recorded (35.2 cm for one of those years).
- Repatriated recaptures made up about 1% of the total number released.

**Survival and Size at Release**

- The estimated survival rate of repatriated juveniles was low. The authors note this rate may be underestimated, and they removed some data (1998 n=1; 1999 n=22) to produce a more realistic estimate.
- Of 192 recaptured repatriates from Lake Mohave (1992 to 1998, excluding multiple recaptures), most were greater than 30 cm at release, while only 8% were smaller than 25 cm.
- Recent data from the San Juan River show that 80% of repatriate recaptures were longer than 30 cm, which suggests that larger release size improves recapture success.
- Earlier estimates put repatriate survivorship near 50% (Marsh 1997), but the current analysis indicates that survival is now lower.

**Conclusions and Recommendations**

- The NFWG management results show significant progress and support continuing both monitoring and repatriation.
- Repatriation should be enhanced, especially by rearing fish to a larger size (above 30 cm) before stocking.
- Recovery is on a positive trajectory, but the authors stress that time is short because of the shrinking wild population and loss of genetic variability.
- The paper cites [[Mueller and Burke]] on stocking rates and predator presence as relevant to future survival work.
