# The Millerton Lake, Fresno/Madera, Striped Bass Fishery, 1960-1962

## Executive Summary
This California Department of Fish and Game administrative report (Wilson & Christenson, 1965) synthesizes creel census, gill-net, plankton-sampling, and angler-derby data collected between 1960 and 1962 on the introduced [[Striped Bass]] population of [[Millerton Lake]]. Striped bass were originally stocked in [[Millerton Lake]] in 1955, 1956, and 1957 on an experimental basis to test survival, catch contribution, and self-sustainability in a warmwater reservoir. The study found a small but persistent fishery characterized by low catch rates (~0.1 fish/angler-hour) but high angler satisfaction due to large trophy-sized fish. Evidence of natural reproduction was documented via age/scale analysis across multiple year classes (1957–1960). Growth rates improved markedly following the 1958 introduction of [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) as a forage base, with Millerton Lake striped bass growth rivaling the exceptional [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]] population in South Carolina.

## Background & Description
- **Species identification**: Striped bass, *Roccus saxatilis* (later reclassified as *Morone saxatilis*).
- **Size range sampled (1962)**: 17.1–27 inches fork length (gill net/population sampling); derby fish up to 30+ inches.
- **Length-weight relationship**: Established graphically (Figure 2) from 81 derby fish plus 4 D-J Project fish; total catch of 164 fish averaged 10.1 lbs each, with a reported maximum of 17 lbs in earlier years.
- **Scale aging methodology**: Non-torn, non-regenerated scales projected at 5:1 reduction ratio; annuli measured to nearest 0.1 inch; back-calculated growth assumed a "0" Y-intercept (a simplification versus Robinson's 1960 finding of 0.97 Y-intercept for Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta/San Pablo Bay fish), producing a moderate underestimation of early-age growth.
- **Total-to-fork length conversion**: Derby total length data converted using a graph adapted from [[Merriman 1941]].
- **Growth trajectory (average fork length at end of each year of growth, Table 3)**:
  - Age 0: 5.18 in
  - Age I: 11.83 in
  - Age II: 16.79 in
  - Age III: 21.89 in
  - Age IV: 26.59 in
  - Age V: 30.39 in
  - Age VI: 34.19 in
- **Annual growth increments**: Greatest increment occurs during the second year of life (~6.65 in), consistent with Figure 1 curve "B".

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Landlocked, introduced population in a warmwater reservoir (Millerton Lake, ~3,200 surface acres in 1962), part of California's Central Valley reservoir system connected to the Sacramento-San Joaquin drainage lineage of stocked striped bass.
- **Trophic dynamics**: Introduction of [[Threadfin Shad]] in 1958 provided an abundant forage base; Goodson (1964) identified threadfin shad as the dominant dietary item for Millerton Lake striped bass by 1963. This forage shift is directly correlated with accelerated growth rates beginning with the 1959–1960 year classes.
- **Reproduction**: Evidence of natural spawning first appeared in 1959; subsequent sampling (1960–1962) confirmed recruitment from year classes 1958, 1959, and 1960, indicating a self-sustaining reproductive component within the lake. Attempts to directly recover spawned eggs via plankton net hauls were consistently unsuccessful (1960 and 1961), despite confirmed larval/juvenile recruitment via scale-age analysis.
- **Population structure**: Mixed population of stocked (1955–1957 plants) and naturally produced fish; by 1962, natural production (1958+ year classes) comprised roughly 21.7–29.3% of the catch depending on sampling method.
- **Fishing pressure**: Relatively low and specialized; most anglers targeted other species, with striped bass fishing sustained by a niche of trophy-seeking anglers, notably stimulated by a local business-sponsored derby (Vic's Place, Madera side).

## Key Findings
- **Catch rates**: March 20, 1960 spot census: 46 anglers, 112.5 hours, 9 striped bass caught (0.08 fish/hour). Voluntary June–October 1960 census: 106 anglers, 493.5 hours, 49 striped bass, 66 zero-catches (0.10 fish/hour) — confirming a limited, low-success but high-value fishery.
- **1961 data gap**: Minimal Department investigation; of 1,815 angler-hours and 139 fish examined during creel census, only 1 was a striped bass, confirming that angler effort was overwhelmingly directed at other species despite anecdotal reports of "good" striper fishing.
- **1962 population sampling**: Of 4,725 fish taken via seine, fyke net, Oneida trap, rotenone, and gill net, only 31 were striped bass (nearly all via gill net), ranging 17.1–27 inches, from year classes 1957–1960; 18 of 19 scale-aged fish were naturally produced in the lake.
- **Derby dataset**: 102 striped bass registered at Vic's Place derby (Sept–Dec 1962); scale samples from 69 fish. Year class contribution: 1955 (3.0%), 1956 (26.1%), 1957 (49.2%), 1958 (20.3%), 1960 (1.4%) — indicating 21.7% natural-production contribution among scale-sampled fish.
- **Combined catch composition (Table 4, n=164 fish)**: 1955/1956 combined 28.0%, 1957 42.7%, 1958 23.8%, 1959 4.9%, 1960 0.6%; natural production (1958+) totaled 29.3% of overall catch. Yield estimated at 0.54 lbs/acre (likely 2–3x higher if unreported catch is included).
- **Growth rate acceleration**: First-year growth increased from ~4.7 in (1955–1958 year classes) to 6.8 in (1959–1960 year classes) after threadfin shad introduction; second-year growth increased from 5.1 in (1955–1957) to 8.2 in (1958–1960).
- **Comparative growth (Table 5)**: Millerton Lake striped bass growth exceeded [[Coos Bay Oregon]] and general [[Atlantic Coast]] populations from the third year onward, and surpassed even the exceptional [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]] (South Carolina) population by the fifth year of life (26.6 in vs. 25.8 in) — attributed to abundant threadfin shad forage.
- **Management implications**: Confirmed survival, reproduction, and strong growth support the viability of striped bass as a self-sustaining reservoir fishery; recommendations included continued derby-based data collection, marked-fish stocking to quantify natural reproduction rates, and potential supplemental stocking if natural recruitment proves insufficient long-term.
- **Historical correction**: Re-examination of an 8-inch striped bass scale sample from Cloyd & Ehlers (1960) revised its age classification from Age II to Age I, reassigning it to the 1958 year class based on back-calculated length comparisons with 1962 Age IV fish.
