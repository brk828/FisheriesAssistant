# Developmental Rates and Tolerances of the Plains Killifish, *Fundulus kansae*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Fundulidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the thermal tolerance and developmental rate of zygotes of the [[Plains Killifish]] (*Fundulus kansae*), a small prairie-stream fish from south-central Oklahoma. Eggs were experimentally incubated across a temperature gradient (12.6–35.8°C) to determine hatching success, developmental timing across four staged milestones (gastrulation, eye pigmentation, hatching, starvation death), and thermal limits. The study places *F. kansae* in comparative context with three other fishes — *[[Fundulus parvipinnis]]*, *[[Menidia audens]]*, *[[Cyprinodon rubrofluviatilis]]*, and *[[Etheostoma lepidum]]* — to test the hypothesis that summer-breeding fishes are more eurythermal in early ontogeny than winter-breeding fishes. The authors conclude that thermal effluent impacts from power plant development will disproportionately affect winter-breeding species, since summer breeders like *F. kansae* are adapted to rapid development under high, variable temperatures and can quickly escape thermally stressful conditions.

## Background & Description

- **Egg diameter**: 1.60 mm (larger than *M. audens* at 0.96 mm and *C. rubrofluviatilis* at 1.47 mm; comparable to *E. lepidum* at 1.38 mm).
- **Developmental staging** (per [[Oppenheimer 1937]] normal stages of *Fundulus heteroclitus*):
  - Gastrulation ≈ Oppenheimer Stage 19
  - Eye pigmentation ≈ Oppenheimer Stage 28
  - Hatching ≈ Oppenheimer Stage 34
- **Milt**: Male milt in *F. kansae* is not macroscopically visible, requiring pooling of multiple males to achieve adequate fertilization rates.
- **Larval abnormality**: At high (near-lethal) temperatures, hatched larvae are frequently deformed — characterized by ventrally curled tails and circular-only swimming behavior. Deformed larvae were often quieter and paradoxically outlived normal siblings at the same temperature, though their survival potential in nature would be low.
- **Terminal larval state**: Normal larvae after 3–25 days of free-swimming sink to the bottom, immobile but with visible heartbeat — scored as a half-day survival value, indicating imminent death.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Inhabitant of small prairie streams subject to extreme and variable thermal regimes ([[Cross 1967]]).
- **Breeding season variation**: Notably, *F. kansae* breeds in **summer** in northeastern New Mexico ([[Koster 1948]]) but in **winter** in south Texas ([[Hubbs and Wauer]], unpublished), suggesting strong seasonal/thermal plasticity in reproductive timing across its range.
- **Egg deposition site**: Quiet shallows — a microhabitat with more thermally variable conditions compared to midstream rapids (used by the winter-breeding *[[Etheostoma lepidum]]*).
- **Thermal ecology relevance**: Because summer breeders operate near the upper thermal limits at which proteins denature, embryos appear to accelerate development to hatch quickly and migrate to cooler stream areas — an adaptive strategy relevant to survival in thermally polluted or naturally extreme habitats.
- **Comparative breeding guilds**:
  - Summer breeders (central Oklahoma): *F. kansae*, *[[Menidia audens]]*, *[[Cyprinodon rubrofluviatilis]]*
  - Winter breeder (central Texas): *[[Etheostoma lepidum]]* — more cold tolerant, less warm tolerant, and shows reduced thermal sensitivity in developmental rate compared to summer breeders.

## Key Findings

- **Hatching temperature range**: Fertilized *F. kansae* eggs hatched successfully between **12.6°C and 35.8°C**.
- **Full successful development** (hatch, swim normally, deplete yolk) occurred between **15°C and 34.3°C**; more than half of eggs were successful between **16.8°C and 33.5°C**.
- **Comparison to *F. parvipinnis***: The upper thermal limit for *F. kansae* (34.3°C) is notably higher than the 28.5°C reported for *[[Fundulus parvipinnis]]* ([[Hubbs 1965]]). The lower limit is also colder in *F. kansae*, indicating *F. parvipinnis* is more stenothermal (narrower thermal tolerance).
- **Gastrulation rate**: 1 day (>30°C) to 8 days (<13°C); the greatest thermal effect on rate occurs below 18°C.
- **Eye pigmentation rate**: 2.5 days (~32°C) to 28 days (<13°C); delayed at both sublethal warm (>34°C) and increasing cold temperatures. Rate doubles per 9°C between 20–30°C, but doubles per ~5°C below 19°C.
- **Hatching rate**: 6 days (>30°C) to 40 days (<16°C); little thermal effect above 30°C, considerable delay between 22–30°C, and very strong thermal effect below 20°C.
- **Starvation death (post-hatch survival)**: 14 days (~32°C) to 63 days (15°C); larvae at the warmest temperatures survive about one day longer than at moderate temperatures; cold effects are more pronounced below 20°C.
- **Deformity threshold**: At the highest tolerable temperatures, all hatched larvae are deformed and die shortly after hatching.
- **Cephalized-egg transfer experiments**: Eggs incubated to the cephalized stage under optimal conditions and then transferred to extreme temperatures showed:
  - At 36.4°C: eye pigmentation occurs, then death.
  - At 35.8°C: many hatch, but all are deformed.
  - At 12.6°C: deformed larvae can still hatch.
  - At 10.2°C: eye pigmentation can still occur.
- **Post-hatch survival ratio**: Above 20°C, post-hatching survival time exceeds 50% of pre-hatching time in *F. kansae* — a marked contrast to *F. parvipinnis*, where post-hatching survival never exceeded 50% of pre-hatching time at any temperature.
- **Cross-species comparison** (eye pigmentation, hatching, starvation death):
  - The winter-breeding *[[Etheostoma lepidum]]* is more cold-tolerant and less warm-tolerant than the summer-breeding species, and shows the least thermally-driven variation in developmental rate.
  - The three summer breeders (*Fundulus*, *Cyprinodon*, *Menidia*) show similar prehatching developmental rates but markedly different starvation-death times.
  - Starvation death time correlates closely with **egg diameter** among fishes sharing breeding season and geographic area: larger eggs (more yolk) confer longer post-hatch survival, potentially offsetting reduced fecundity associated with larger egg size.
- **Taxonomic implication**: The strikingly different developmental rate profile of *F. kansae* versus *F. parvipinnis* supports prior hybridization-based evidence ([[Hubbs 1971]]) that *F. parvipinnis* is taxonomically distinct from other *Fundulus* species.
- **Applied/conservation implication**: Because summer-breeding fishes like *F. kansae* have rapid, heat-tolerant early development enabling quick hatching and escape from thermally stressful areas, they may be relatively resilient to thermal effluent/heat pollution from power generation facilities. Winter-breeding fishes, by contrast, are hypothesized to be more vulnerable to such thermal alterations.
