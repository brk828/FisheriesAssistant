# Hydrology of Black Mesa Watersheds, Western Colorado

#hydrology #watershed-science #rangeland-management

*Note: This document covers terrestrial watershed hydrology. It contains no fish, invertebrate, or aquatic-biota data, so the ichthyology-specific fields are set to N/A.*

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service General Technical Report (RM-13, June 1975) by [[Ernest C. Frank]], [[Harry E. Brown]], and [[J. R. Thompson]] reports 11 years (1957–67) of precipitation, [[Snowmelt Runoff]], [[Suspended Sediment]], and [[Trapped Sediment]] measurements from three small mountain-grassland watersheds on [[Black Mesa]]. The study tested whether [[Bare Intercept]], an index of exposed soil from [[Transect Sampling]], could predict runoff and sediment. It found no such relationship. Runoff was instead tied to precipitation, [[Basin Morphometry]], and channel incision. Sediment losses were small, and most of the annual runoff and sediment moved during spring snowmelt rather than [[Summer Storms]].

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** Runoff and sediment production are central concerns in multiple-use management of mountain rangelands. The authors sought ways to predict these from easily measured variables. Earlier [[Infiltrometer Studies]] had linked vegetative cover to runoff and erosion:

- [[Turner and Dortignac]] (1954) found that dense [[Thurber Fescue]] sites with about 6 percent bare soil gave maximum soil protection, while poor weed types with 58 percent bare soil did not.
- [[Packer]] (1951) proposed 70 percent ground cover (basal area plus litter) as a watershed protection criterion for bluebunch wheatgrass and cheatgrass ranges.
- A Utah study (USDA-FS 1948) placed the erosion threshold at more than one-third of the soil surface exposed.

The authors noted that high-elevation snowmelt erosion had received little attention compared with intense summer storms.

**Study area.** The three gaged watersheds (4, 5, and 6) lie on the [[Gunnison National Forest]], west of Gunnison, Colorado, at about 9,800 ft. Watersheds 4 and 5 drain to Mesa Creek and watershed 6 drains to Myers Gulch, both tributaries of the [[Gunnison River]]. Black Mesa is a segment of the Colorado Plateau capped by volcanic rock (Piedra rhyolite and Huerta andesite). Its loamy soils are fertile and have high infiltration capacity and erosion resistance.

**Physical characteristics (Table 1):**

| Watershed | Area (acres) | Elevation (ft) | Stream length (mi) | Drainage density (mi/mi²) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 91 | 9,730–9,840 | 1.3 | 9.0 |
| 5 | 169 | 9,770–9,860 | 1.3 | 4.9 |

**Vegetation.** Cover is grouped as aspen, [[Spruce-Fir]], and herbaceous types. The herbaceous (Thurber fescue grassland) type covers 34 percent of watershed 4, 57 percent of watershed 5, and 47 percent of watershed 6. Frequent associated species include [[Needlegrass]] (*Stipa lettermanii*, *S. columbiana*) and [[Idaho Fescue]] (*Festuca idahoensis*). Mima mounds, wet meadows, and hairy goldaster (*Chrysopsis villosa*) also occur. Spruce dominates more than half of watershed 4. Aspen is the main timber type on watershed 6. Timber survey averages were 104 years (aspen) and 95 years (spruce), with basal area of 224 ft² (aspen), 225 ft² (spruce), and 160 ft² (mixed timber).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and precipitation.**
- Knott Ranch records (1920–41) gave mean annual precipitation of 22.5 inches. The minimum was 13.5 inches (1931) and the maximum 29.0 inches (1929).
- Two 100-inch [[Storage Gages]] installed in 1958 averaged 28 inches for 1958–67, with 71 percent falling October–May. Growing-season precipitation ranged from 5 to 14 inches.
- Five [[Snow Courses]] were run annually from 1958. In 1957, the spruce course held 64 percent and the aspen course 90 percent of the herbaceous-type water equivalent. Wind scour on open grassland affected watershed 5, and sheltered watershed 6 probably accumulated the most snow.
- Maximum rainfall intensity for 1957–67 was 4.56 inches per hour over 5 minutes. The largest 24-hour storm was 1.10 inches (1959). A 120-year return period for a 24-hour rainfall of 2 inches was estimated by fitting the 1900–45 record to a [[Gumbel Extreme Value]] function.

**Hydrologic budget (water year 1958).** The water budget was expressed as P − RO − ET − DS = ΔS:

| Watershed | P | RO | ET | DS | ΔS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 34.5 | 4.0 | 13.9 | 17.8 | −1.2 |
| 5 | 34.5 | 5.9 | 12.9 | 16.3 | −0.6 |
| 6 | 34.8 | 13.0 | 15.4 | 8.1 | −1.7 |

Deep seepage differed by up to 9 inches between watersheds. Evapotranspiration varied by no more than 2.6 inches. Watershed 6 had the highest runoff and the lowest deep seepage.

**Grazing and cover.** Each watershed sat within a grazed pasture. Grazing was regulated from 1957 to 1967 to target 25, 40, and 60 percent utilization of Idaho fescue. Pasture 4 was light, 5 heavy, and 6 moderate. [[Gopher Activity]] (mounds and casts) strongly affected bare-soil hits. Casts peaked in spring and dropped sharply by fall, and cast-sourced soil was washed away in overland flow.

## Key Findings

1. **Runoff drivers.** Snowmelt runoff averaged 1.4, 3.1, and 6.8 inches on watersheds 4, 5, and 6, equal to 26, 14, and 28 percent of precipitation. A pooled multiple regression gave:
   - RO = −9.81 + 0.362 (P) + 0.137 (LR), R² = 0.854
   - where P = precipitation and LR = average lateral rise (ft).
   - Runoff appeared to depend on maximum basin length, degree of channel incision, and precipitation. Watershed 6 had the greatest longitudinal rise (260 ft), lateral rise (58 ft), and basin length (6,880 ft).
   - Total water yield (runoff plus deep seepage) was nearly equal: 21.8, 22.2, and 21.1 inches.

2. **Peak flows.** Instantaneous peak discharge averaged 4 csm (gage 4), 15 csm (gage 6), and 59 csm (gage 5). Watershed 5's higher peaks came from [[Slush Floods]] caused by snow-dam failure. Slush floods moved 6 to 24 percent of watershed 5's annual suspended sediment. Up to three slush floods occurred on successive days.

3. **Sediment yields.**
   - Suspended sediment averaged 53, 64, and 91 lb/acre in the herbaceous type (watersheds 4, 5, and 6). Based on total area it was 18, 37, and 43 lb/acre.
   - Trapped sediment averaged 103, 34, and 92 lb/acre (herbaceous type).
   - Total production was 146, 95, and 184 lb/acre/year.
   - Snowmelt sediment is likely biased low, because sampling was not around the clock and sediment discharge is zero when stage drops below the V-notch weir.

4. **Summer storms.** Of 167 storms of 0.1 inch or more in 11 years, 27 that produced runoff were sampled. Summer runoff averages less than 0.08 inch. Summer sediment peaked at 20 lb/acre and averaged 11 lb/acre. Concentrations after summer storms could reach six times snowmelt levels, but low runoff volume kept totals small.

5. **Seasonal dominance.** About 99 percent of yearly runoff and 89 percent of suspended sediment occurred during spring snowmelt.

6. **No bare-intercept relationship.** Regression and scatter analyses found no link between bare intercept and water yield or sediment production. The only correspondence was 1958 transect data with 1959 trapped sediment on watersheds 4 and 5, and later years did not confirm it. The authors attributed the null result partly to experimental error in measuring small sediment amounts.

7. **Ground cover trend.** Bare intercept fell on each watershed from 1957 to 1967 (linear on watershed 4, power function on 5 and 6), even under heavy grazing. Ground cover increased during the study.

8. **Sediment sources.** Sediment losses were very small. Suspended load equals removal of about 0.0001 inch of soil per year across the herbaceous area, or 0.02–0.06 inch per year of channel downcutting. Possible sources include channel erosion, gopher mounds and casts, and slush floods. The authors judged all sources probably contribute, with the load likely drawn from a few readily erodible areas.

9. **Classification.** Under current erosion classification schemes, these yields are "very minor amounts of geologic erosion."
