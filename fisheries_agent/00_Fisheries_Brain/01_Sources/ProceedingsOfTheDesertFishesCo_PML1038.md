# Proceedings of the Desert Fishes Council, Volume XXXII (2000 Symposium): Snail Photobehavior, Humpback Chub Growth, Pupfish Renovation and Native Fish Stocking

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of abstracts and a short program note from the *Proceedings of the Desert Fishes Council*, Volume XXXII (2000 Symposium, Death Valley, California, 16–19 November 2000; published 13 November 2001). It contains four items: a study of photophobic behavior in aquatic snails from the western United States, a laboratory experiment on temperature effects on juvenile [[Humpback Chub]] growth, a management report on genetic renovation of the [[Leon Springs Pupfish]] in Texas, and a description of an experimental stocking program for imperiled native fishes at the [[ASU Research Park]] in Tempe, Arizona. The common theme is the conservation of imperiled desert and western North American freshwater species.

## Background & Description

The proceedings are published by the [[Desert Fishes Council]]. The symposium volume includes abstracts in English and Spanish. The Spanish versions are summarized here only in content, not reproduced. The Spanish text contains several OCR errors, so figures were taken from the English abstracts where available.

The four items cover different taxa and approaches:

- **Gastropods (snails):** Photophobic responses of several freshwater snail species, including the invasive [[New Zealand Mudsnail]] (*Potamopyrgus antipodarum*) and several [[Pyrgulopsis]] species.
- **Humpback chub (*Gila cypha*):** Growth response of juveniles to water temperature, relevant to dam-release management in the [[Colorado River]].
- **Leon Springs pupfish (*Cyprinodon bovinus*):** Removal of genetically introgressed hybrids with the [[Sheepshead Minnow]] (*Cyprinodon variegatus*), using [[Antimycin A]] treatment and restocking.
- **Native fish stocking:** Establishment of a captive population of [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] at an urban research park.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Snail photobehavior (Owens River, California):** The study assessed the light sensitivity of *Pyrgulopsis owensensis*, *P. perturbata*, *P. wongi*, and *Potamopyrgus antipodarum* in the Owens River system. Responses were tested with a 10-minute exposure to natural light and darkness in a circular grid. The authors interpret the results as showing that predator avoidance alone does not explain cryptic behavior. Species and populations appear to seek conditions similar to their native microhabitats. One exception is the endemic *Taylorconcha serpenticola* of the Middle Snake River, which stays under rocks by day across all habitats and forages at night in adjacent open areas.

**Temperature and humpback chub (Colorado River):** Dam construction has made mainstem Colorado River temperatures too cold for successful recruitment of humpback chub and other endangered fishes. Warm-water releases are a management option, but their biological effects had not been well documented.

**Leon Springs pupfish (Diamond Y Draw, Texas):** The two closely associated but largely isolated watercourses of Diamond Y Draw are home to the endangered Leon Springs pupfish. Hybridization with introduced *C. variegatus* threatened the genetic integrity of the native species.

**Native fish stocking (Tempe, Arizona):** Urban research park ponds offer a controlled habitat for imperiled native fishes. The program also supports public education about these species.

## Key Findings

### 1. Photophobic responses of snails (Owens River, California)

- Sample sizes were 50 and 25 snails per species at each locality.
- Two species, *P. owensensis* and *P. wongi*, showed slight photophobic reactions.
- *P. perturbata*, from a locality with predators (Fish Slough), showed a rapid photophobic reaction.
- *Potamopyrgus antipodarum* from the Owens River above Benton Crossing did not react and was abundant on both rocky surfaces and aquatic vegetation.
- *Pyrgulopsis californiensis* from Bear Oasis (San Bernardino County, near Palm Springs) was not photophobic.
- Photosensitive species moved little in darkness. Nonreactive species moved very little in both light and dark.
- The report records a large population of *P. antipodarum* about one kilometer upstream of Benton Crossing, discovered in 1999. This is a new California record for this non-native species. It was also found in the Middle Snake River, Yellowstone National Park, the lower Columbia River, and Lake Ontario.
- Data are available on request.

### 2. Temperature effects on juvenile humpback chub

- The study tested three post-treatment temperatures (21, 24, and 27 °C) on juvenile humpback chub previously reared at 12, 18, and 24 °C.
- Growth rates, measured as total length and weight, were lower at 21 °C than at 24 and 27 °C.
- Fish previously reared at 12 °C grew faster at all experimental temperatures than fish previously reared at 24 °C. However, they did not catch up in length or weight.
- The authors conclude that warm-water releases can benefit humpback chub by increasing growth and shortening exposure to predators. They do not offset the effects of earlier cold-water exposure.

### 3. Genetic renovation of the Leon Springs pupfish (Diamond Y Draw, Texas)

- In August 1998, a 400 m segment of the upper watercourse was treated with antimycin A. This segment held most of the upper watercourse and contained a genetically introgressed *C. bovinus* × *C. variegatus* population. Other species of concern, including gastropods, amphipods, and an endangered *Gambusia*, were protected.
- About 500 genetically pure *C. bovinus* from a captive stock at the Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center were released after the treatment.
- The lower watercourse had a lower level of introgression than the upper watercourse. Its treatment was therefore different. (The introgression percentages are garbled in the OCR text and could not be reliably reported.)
- In March 1999, all pupfish were removed by seining and trapping. About 3,000 fish from the Dexter stock were then released in the lower watercourse to swamp the introduced genome.
- After 13 months, samples from the upper watercourse showed no introduced markers. These were assessed with five diagnostic allozyme loci and a diagnostic mitochondrial DNA RFLP marker.
- Results for the lower watercourse were still forthcoming at the time of publication.

### 4. Experimental stocking at the ASU Research Park (Tempe, Arizona)

- In late 1995, the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the Arizona Game and Fish Department stocked bonytail, Colorado squawfish, and razorback sucker into ponds at the ASU Research Park.
- The stated goals were to:
  1. Provide a controlled habitat for studying the basic biology of these fishes and their interactions with non-native fishes.
  2. Expose the public to native fishes and their imperiled status.
  3. Maintain a secure stock for future recovery needs.
- The authors hope the information gained will support recovery of these and other imperiled species in the wild.
