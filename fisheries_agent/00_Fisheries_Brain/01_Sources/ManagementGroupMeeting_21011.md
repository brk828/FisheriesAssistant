# Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program: Management Correspondence (1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #osmeridae #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This is a compilation of administrative material from 1989 concerning the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Implementation Program]], which aims to recover three endangered fishes and one threatened or rare fish while allowing water development to continue. It contains a Management Group meeting memo and agenda, a briefing on endangered fish and their effect on Mesa County water users, internal memos on overhead assessments and FY90 funding, a state water association funding appeal, a National Park Service objection to a proposed rainbow smelt introduction, mailing and contact lists, and a Flaming Gorge consultation roster. The document contains no new field data; its substance is program governance, funding, water-rights strategy and inter-agency disputes.

## Background & Description

The material is mostly internal correspondence and meeting papers from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS), Region 6, dated April to May 1989. Its main components are:

- **Management Group memo and agenda (May 22, 1989):** A meeting on June 14, 1989, in Lakewood, Colorado, covering recovery updates, the [[Razorback Sucker]] petition, the overhead assessment, technical group reports, FY89 work plans, [[Water Rights Acquisition]] and [[Section 7 Consultation]] activities.
- **Overhead assessment memo (May 8, 1989):** A request for an exemption from the overhead assessment on a [[Bureau of Reclamation]] water-rights funding agreement was denied. The agreement was assigned a 1% assessment, against a standard 8.5% pass-through rate.
- **Briefing for Mesa County water users (March 7, 1989):** A presentation by Bill McDonald of the [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]] on Endangered Species Act pressures.
- **Funding memo (April 14, 1989):** A reaffirmed $624,600 FWS commitment to the program.
- **Wyoming Water Development Association letter (May 5, 1989):** A request to Secretary [[Manuel Lujan]] for full federal funding.
- **Glen Canyon letter (April 20, 1989):** A National Park Service objection to introducing [[Rainbow Smelt]] into [[Lake Powell]].
- **Contact lists and mailing lists (April 4, 1989):** Roughly 300 names across agencies, states, water districts, industry, conservation groups and universities (pages 14–36), plus the Flaming Gorge Section 7 consultation team and Recovery Implementation Committee rosters. These are summarized here rather than reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document identifies four protected fishes of the Upper Colorado River system:

- [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (endangered)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (endangered)
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (endangered)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (listed as threatened in the briefing; described as "rare" in the Wyoming letter)

The briefing notes that the Colorado River system requires Endangered Species Act protection from the Colorado stateline upstream, on all western tributaries except the Dolores. The San Juan had previously been excluded from certain endangered species considerations. Under [[Section 7 Consultation]], federal projects need a [[Biological Opinion]] from FWS. A jeopardy opinion blocks a project unless mitigation is developed.

Key habitat concerns:

- **[[15-Mile Reach]]:** The stretch of the Colorado River from the Gunnison River confluence upstream to the diversion point for the Grand Valley Irrigation Company (GVIC) canal. The Redlands roller dam on the Gunnison is said to block upstream migration.
- **[[Instream Flow]] needs:** FWS wants additional minimum stream flows, with a minimum of roughly 128 cfs proposed for the 15-mile stretch.
- **[[Lake Powell]] and [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]]:** The National Park Service is concerned about the effect of non-native fish on endangered species, including potential nursery waters in upper Lake Powell.

Species-level research gaps are noted repeatedly. The briefing says many habits of the endangered fishes "are not understood", and the NPS letter calls for research on smelt distribution after introduction.

## Key Findings

**Program structure and funding**

- The 15-year recovery program began in FY89 under a cooperative agreement signed by the Secretary of the Interior, the governors of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, and the WAPA Administrator.
- The program's five elements are: water rights acquisition for fish benefit, habitat replacement, stocking of endangered fish, predator fish control, and research.
- The briefing describes an annual budget of "2.3 million" from Bureau of Reclamation sources, FWS and the states. OCR and rounding make the component figures unclear (e.g., "1.5 million" from basin electricity sales; "6.6 million" possibly for FWS).
- The Wyoming letter gives a total FY90 commitment of $2,394,000, with a FWS share of $624,000 ($424,000 base funding plus $200,000 in Section 6 funds). The FWS memo gives $624,600. The two figures differ slightly, possibly due to OCR or rounding.
- The FWS memo reports a possible $200,000 reduction because no Section 6 grants to States will fund that portion. Region 6 would cover the remaining $424,600: $300,000 from the 1990 base budget and $124,600 from regional funds.
- The Wyoming letter asks for $124,000 added to base funding and $200,000 in restored Section 6 funding. It also says the program is funded about 75% by upper-basin power revenues, state appropriations and water user fees.

**Cost recovery and assessments**

- Developers whose depletions have small effects can proceed with no further mitigation after paying a one-time charge of $18 per acre-foot and showing progress on the recovery program.
- The briefing says the $15 million capital costs are a federal obligation, and it cites an allocation of water rights and habitat funds that appears garbled in the scan.
- FWS set the assessment on the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] agreement at 1%, against an 8.5% pass-through rate. This matched the 1.00% rate for MBCA land acquisition and was lower than the 10.88% administrative rate and 0.90% rate for contributed funds.

**Water-rights strategy (Grand Valley)**

- [[Ruedi Reservoir]] releases: FWS's no-jeopardy opinion for Ruedi operations required 5,999 acre-feet for the 15-mile stretch, plus an additional 5,969 in four of five years (as scanned).
- Delivering Ruedi water depends on an agreement with Orchard Mesa Irrigation, but keeping water in the river below the GVIC diversion is described as a "crucial problem".
- The [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]] must decide whether to file only on Ruedi return flows or on all return flows. Filing on all return flows would make the board a junior appropriator in future water filings.
- The Bureau of Reclamation will run a public input process on future water sources, including purchase, lease, diversion changes, ground water and new upstream reservoirs. The [[Yampa River]] is a competing priority for cost-effectiveness.

**Stocking and non-native species**

- The NPS letter cites Executive Order 11987 (1977), which restricts introduction of exotic organisms, and Section 4.4.3 of the recovery program: "Stocking of non-native species will be confined to areas where absence of potential conflict with rare or endangered species can be demonstrated."
- NPS opposes the proposed smelt introduction (to feed [[Striped Bass]]) without substantial evidence of no adverse effect. It asks for a formal presentation and suggests a cooperative fisheries management agreement with the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources.

**Other items**

- A state hatchery for the endangered fishes is being considered, with a feasibility study requested from the legislature. Possible sites include the eastern slope, the San Luis Valley, or the western slope.
- Daily and seasonal operations of large federal reservoirs are an emerging issue.
- The Wyoming Water Development Association argues the program "provides a model to the nation" for resolving endangered species conflicts.
