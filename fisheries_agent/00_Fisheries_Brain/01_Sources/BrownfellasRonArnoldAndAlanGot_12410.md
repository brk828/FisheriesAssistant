# The Environmental Year in Preview: Ron Arnold, Alan Gottlieb and the Wise Use Movement

#environmental-policy #wise-use #rangeland-management #magazine-feature

## Executive Summary

This is a 1991 *Outside* magazine feature by Jon Krakauer profiling the [[Wise Use Movement]], a loose coalition of roughly 250 groups opposed to environmental regulation. The article centers on [[Ron Arnold]], its self-appointed strategist, and [[Alan Gottlieb]], a direct-mail fund-raiser who runs the [[Center for the Defense of Free Enterprise]] (CDFE). It also follows sheep and cattle ranchers in Lincoln County, New Mexico, who turned to the movement after proposed grazing-fee increases and public-land restrictions. The document does not address any aquatic species, fish, or water-quality data. Its ecological content is limited to land-use disputes that touch wildlife, forests, wetlands, and fisheries as political issues.

## Background & Description

The piece opens at a 1991 meeting in the Las Cruces Hilton, where about 150 sheep and cattle ranchers, members of the New Mexico Wool Growers Association, heard Arnold describe environmentalists as a menace to society. Arnold, 54, is a former [[Sierra Club]] conservation committee activist. He left the club in the late 1960s and early 1970s, saying it had shifted from advocating "wise use" of public lands to advocating "no use." He later wrote a biography of [[James Watt]], Reagan's Secretary of the Interior, and emerged as the movement's chief ideologue. The name "Wise Use" was borrowed from [[Gifford Pinchot]], who used the term "conservation" in 1907 to describe "the wise use of resources."

Gottlieb, 44, is president and founder of CDFE, based in Bellevue, Washington. His operation includes a publishing house for "battle books," a printing operation, an Oregon radio station, and a radio news network with 85 affiliates. His main engine is direct-response fund-raising. The article reports that he sends more than 20 million pieces of mail a year, collects an average of $5 million for CDFE and a pro-gun foundation, and raises another $12 million for other conservative causes. He holds one million names on a mainframe and 20 million more on magnetic tape, sorted by congressional district, donation history, and issue sensitivity.

The movement's founding document, *The Wise Use Agenda*, grew out of an August 1988 conference in Reno, Nevada. It lists 25 goals, including opening national parks, wildlife refuges, and wilderness areas to mining and oil exploration, systematic logging of ancient forests, and amending the [[Endangered Species Act]] to exclude "non-adaptive species such as the California Condor."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article's ecological content is political rather than scientific. Its main concerns are:

- **Public rangelands:** Environmental groups pushed for "Cattle Free by '93" on public lands from Montana to west Texas. A bill in Congress would have quadrupled grazing fees, which the article says could bankrupt some 30,000 ranchers.
- **Wilderness and forests:** Arnold's earlier work helped create the Alpine Lakes Wilderness Area in Washington in 1976, protecting nearly 400,000 acres. The article notes that the [[Spotted Owl]] controversy and old-growth logging were central to the debate, and that Arnold claimed 11,000 timber workers had lost jobs in the Pacific Northwest.
- **Water and fish:** The article mentions a wetland designation that blocked home construction and a dam that prevented fish from reaching spawning grounds, which Wise Use advocates cited as examples of environmental overreach. It presents these as anecdotes and provides no data.
- **Off-road vehicles:** The [[BlueRibbon Coalition]], an Idaho off-road vehicle group, rapidly became a political force by 1989, according to Sierra Club representative Rick Johnson.

## Key Findings

- **Coalition scope:** The Wise Use Movement includes about 250 groups, from the [[National Rifle Association]] and Exxon to small local clubs. Arnold claimed an active constituency of five million Americans and a potential one of 125 million. These are self-reported figures and the article does not verify them.
- **Strategy:** Arnold argued that opponents must take the offensive, litigate against environmental groups, and push for new laws that "put boxes around environmentalists." Gottlieb's direct-mail model was built to exploit "fear, hate, and revenge."
- **Ranch case study:** Ronnie and Beverly Merritt run 1,000 sheep and 40 Barzona cattle on 13,000 acres in Lincoln County. Their tax forms showed net earnings of $9,554. They already run 30 percent fewer head than their Bureau of Land Management permit allows, use propane-fueled trucks, and rotate pastures to prevent overgrazing.
- **Internal discord:** Some Wise Use groups, including the [[American Farm Bureau Federation]], publicly distanced themselves from Arnold and CDFE. Herb Manig said the Farm Bureau wanted "nothing to do with CDFE."
- **Gottlieb's legal history:** In 1984 Gottlieb was indicted on two counts of filing false income tax returns and failing to pay more than $40,000 in taxes for 1977 and 1978. He admitted underpaying by $17,000, was sentenced to 366 days, fined $5,000, and served time in a minimum-security facility in Spokane. Former employees then sued him for racketeering and conspiracy to defraud. The court found he had defrauded no one and ordered the ex-employees to pay $30,000 in damages for defamation.
- **Foundation property deal:** Gottlieb used $250,000 from two of his nonprofit foundations to buy Liberty Park, a building valued at $510,000. The foundations then paid him more than $8,000 a month in rent. The court did not find this unlawful.
- **Movement assessment:** The article ends with the Merritts' appeal that the movement is a matter of survival. Krakauer does not endorse either side and notes that critics question the motives and credibility of Arnold and Gottlieb.
