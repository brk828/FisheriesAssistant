# Fishery Management Program, San Carlos Indian Reservation: 1969 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #fishery-management

## Executive Summary

This 1969 annual report from the U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, written by fishery management biologist Gail C. Kobetich, reviews the [[Fishery Management Program]] on the [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]] in eastern Arizona. Fishing pressure rose approximately 75 percent over 1968. The program stocks about 100 warm- and cold-water tanks and lakes, mainly with [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]] and [[Channel Catfish]]. Water conditions were generally poor in late summer, causing localized [[Fish Kill]] events, while [[Point of Pines Lake]] stayed in excellent condition. The report recommends herbicide readiness for [[Aquatic Vegetation]] control, expanded stocking, and a formal [[Creel Census]], which the reservation lacked.

## Background & Description

The reservation covers rugged mountain terrain in eastern Arizona, with elevations reaching 8,000 feet. Most stock tanks can be reached only by four-wheel-drive vehicles. Many are remote, and anglers often find the reward worth the effort.

In 1969, the program stocked 56,000 trout raised at the [[Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]] and the [[Alchesay National Fish Hatchery]]. The hatchery supply was credited with a high-quality fish crop.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water conditions.** Conditions were generally poor but not critical until late summer, when many tanks became critically low, especially in the southern part of the reservation. Two complete kills occurred there: a count rendered as 113 fish at Madman Tank and 132 catfish at [[Ash Grove Tank]]. Both were attributed to low water levels combined with high water temperatures and low [[Dissolved Oxygen]]. Several tanks dried completely. Early fall rains raised levels, and the stocked fish were expected to recover quickly in spring.

In the northwestern section above Nantes Rim, conditions were bad but not critical. Partial kills happened from low oxygen and heavy fish infestations. At [[Dry Lake]], about 50 large trout died when the water dropped to a critical level in a small area. Most high-elevation tanks held water through summer, so many large fish were expected for the 1970 season.

**Point of Pines Lake.** This 30-acre lake was built specifically for angling and remained in excellent condition all year.

**Black River.** The river supported catfish, smallmouth bass and trout fisheries adequately.

**Seneca Lake.** A substantial kill occurred in late summer. Heavy growth of emergent and submergent aquatic plants, combined with efforts by the Tribe to remove emergent vegetation by hand, led to an accumulation of ash in shallow water edges normally occupied by non-game fish. The accidental kill, which mainly affected non-game species, was judged beneficial to lake management.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Vegetation is a recurring problem across many lakes and tanks. Treating small tanks would likely cost more than the added fishing use would return, so treatment was focused on larger impoundments (Seneca Lake, Point of Pines Lake and the lake above Point of Pines).

## Key Findings

**Fishing pressure and use**
- Fishing pressure in 1969 increased approximately 75 percent over 1968.
- Derived estimates indicate an increase in angler use from 1968 to 1969, rising to 28,991 angler-days. Income to the Tribe from fishing permits was reported as $12,295. These figures cover only January 1 to October 31, so full-year totals will be slightly higher.
- Growth in use was attributed mainly to the popularity of Point of Pines Lake, the use of catchable rainbow trout in more accessible areas, and the use of brown trout to extend the fishing season. Good economic conditions in Arizona and better access also contributed.

**Stocking results**
- Brown trout stocked in early spring 1968 contributed significantly to the 1969 fishery. In some tanks growth was disappointing, but most fish grew more than 12 inches in 12 months. Brown trout are harder to catch than brook trout or rainbow trout, so a larger share survived the early season, increasing angler interest all year. The report recommends continuing this program.
- Rainbow trout stocking was expanded. The report notes that Point of Pines Lake rainbow trout grew poorly, possibly from competition with larger rainbow trout planted during the fishing season. Fish stocked there recently will be planted exclusively with rainbow trout for comparative evaluation.
- Channel catfish fingerlings planted in fall 1968 now average about 14 inches. Fish from 1968 and 1965 plantings are providing excellent catches weighing 9 to 13 pounds. Catfish are compatible with bass and trout. Point of Pines Lake will be kept strictly for trout, and Dry Lake will be managed for catfish, with 6-inch catfish stocked for the first time.

**Seneca Lake development**
- Earlier introductions of many non-game fish species had complicated fishery management. The accidental, apparently complete eradication of undesirable fish partly solved this problem.
- The Tribe plans a development near Seneca Lake (service station, hotel, barn and cafe) during 1970. The cost was illegible in the scanned text. This will raise fishing pressure and require more stocked fish. Restrictive regulations, such as catch-and-release or one-fish limits, were proposed to limit undesirable non-game fish introductions.

**Black River**
- Fishing pressure on the Black River is rising because of new roads. The largest increase has been on the lower 100 miles of the river, which is inhabited by largemouth bass and channel catfish. Access road improvements and a planned camping area are needed to protect the primitive character of this resource.

**Creel census**
- No creel census existed because of limited funds and trained staff. Pressure estimates came from permit sales, which do not provide catch rates or individual area use. The report calls a formal creel census a near necessity for better management.

**Recommendations**
1. Keep a small supply of aquatic herbicides (copper sulfate and a second approved compound) on hand for immediate control of aquatic vegetation.
2. Hire a seasonal caretaker at Seneca Lake to assist with aquatic weed control, shade planting, and creel census work, and to keep the lake area free of litter.
3. If possible, hire a creel census clerk to collect data on the reservation fishery.
4. Establish an agreement between the San Carlos and White Mountain Apache Tribes for a carefully planned access and development program for the Black and Salt Rivers.
