# Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program and Recovery Goals

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species #recovery-planning

## Executive Summary

The [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] was established in 1988 as a partnership to recover four endangered fish species—[[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*)—while allowing continued water development in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. These species once thrived in the Colorado River system but were imperiled by dam installation, flow regulation, habitat modification, and nonnative fish introductions. Draft recovery goals developed in 2000-2001 supplement existing recovery plans by establishing objective, measurable criteria for downlisting and delisting under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The recovery goals address demographic viability (minimum viable populations), genetic viability, population redundancy, and site-specific management actions to minimize or remove threats associated with the five ESA listing factors.

## Background & Description

### Species Descriptions

**Colorado Pikeminnow** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):
- Olive-green and gold back, silvery-white belly
- Generally 18-22 inches long, weighing 2-4 pounds; historically reached up to 6 feet and 80+ pounds
- Torpedo-shaped body; upper jaw extends to or beyond middle of eye
- Largest minnow species native to North America
- Evolved 3-5 million years ago; can live 40 years
- Spawns at age 5-6 years in late spring and summer
- Known to migrate more than 200 miles to spawn
- Young feed on insects; adults primarily piscivorous
- Listed as endangered in 1967; full ESA protection in 1973
- Populations reproducing in wild in [[Green River]] and [[Colorado River]]; stable and increasing
- Small population in [[San Juan River Basin]]

**Bonytail** (*Gila elegans*):
- Dark gray or olive back, silver sides, white belly
- Small head, large fins, streamlined body becoming pencil-thin before tail
- Generally 16-18 inches long; can reach 22 inches
- Evolved 3-5 million years ago; can live nearly 50 years
- Spawns at age 5-7 years in spring and early summer
- Feeds on insects and plant matter
- Listed as endangered with full ESA protection in 1980
- Rarest of the four endangered fish
- No known reproducing populations in wild
- Populations being reintroduced through stocking in Colorado, Green, and [[Yampa River]]

**Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*):
- Maximum size approximately 480 mm total length, weighing up to 1,165 g
- Six wild populations known: [[Black Rocks]] (Colorado River, Colorado), [[Westwater Canyon]] (Colorado River, Utah), [[Yampa Canyon]] (Yampa River, Colorado), [[Desolation Gray Canyons]] (Green River, Utah), [[Cataract Canyon]] (Colorado River, Utah), and Colorado/[[Little Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona
- Restricted to deep, swift, canyon-bound regions
- Listed as endangered in 1967; full ESA protection in 1973

**Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*):
- Historically widely distributed in warm-water reaches of larger rivers from Mexico to Wyoming
- Remaining wild populations in serious jeopardy
- Largest riverine population in middle Green River
- Largest extant population in [[Lake Mohave]] (above Davis Dam)
- Small populations in [[Lake Mead]] (above Hoover Dam)
- Listed as endangered with full ESA protection in 1991

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Requirements

**Colorado Pikeminnow**: Long-distance migrator moving hundreds of miles to spawning areas in canyon regions. Adults utilize pools, deep runs, and eddy habitats maintained by high spring flows. Spawning occurs after spring runoff at water temperatures typically ≥18°C. Larvae drift to broad alluvial reaches, occupying sheltered nursery backwaters restructured by high spring flows and maintained by stable base flows.

**Bonytail**: Historically common to abundant in warm-water reaches of larger rivers. Considered adapted to mainstem rivers; observed in pools and eddies. Probably spawns in spring over rocky substrates in rivers; reservoir spawning observed over rocky shoals and shorelines. Flooded bottomland habitats hypothesized as important growth and conditioning areas, particularly as nursery habitats for young.

**Humpback Chub**: Restricted to deep, swift, canyon-bound regions. Adults utilize eddies and sheltered shoreline habitats maintained by high spring flows. Spawning occurs on descending limb of spring hydrograph at water temperatures >16°C. Young use low-velocity shoreline habitats including eddies and backwaters more prevalent under base-flow conditions.

**Razorback Sucker**: Adults in rivers use deeper runs, eddies, backwaters, and flooded off-channel environments in spring; runs and pools in shallow water with submerged sandbars in summer; low-velocity runs, pools, and eddies in winter. Spring migrations associated with spawning. River spawning occurs over bars of cobble, gravel, and sand substrates during spring-runoff flows at temperatures typically >14°C. Young require quiet, warm, shallow nursery environments such as tributary mouths, backwaters, or inundated floodplain habitats.

### Threats (Five ESA Listing Factors)

**Factor A**: Streamflow regulation, habitat modification, dam installation, curtailment of range

**Factor B**: Overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific, or educational purposes

**Factor C**: Disease, parasitism (e.g., [[Asian Tapeworm]] on humpback chub in Little Colorado River), predation by nonnative fish species

**Factor D**: Inadequacy of existing regulatory mechanisms

**Factor E**: Hybridization with other native *Gila* species (for bonytail and humpback chub) or [[White Sucker]] (for razorback sucker), pesticides and pollutants, hazardous-materials spills

### Nonnative Fish Impacts

Introduction of nonnative fish species changed river environment significantly. Problematic species include channel catfish, flathead catfish, and northern pike. Competition with and predation by nonnative fishes represent major threats to all four endangered species.

## Key Findings

### Recovery Definition

Recovery is achieved when management actions and associated tasks to minimize or remove threats associated with the five listing factors have been implemented and/or completed to allow genetically and demographically viable, self-sustaining populations to thrive under minimal ongoing management and investment of resources.

### Recovery Units

Recovery addressed for entire [[Colorado River Basin]] but criteria presented for two recovery units:
1. **Upper Colorado River Basin** (above [[Glen Canyon Dam]]), including San Juan River subbasin for Colorado pikeminnow and razorback sucker
2. **Lower Colorado River Basin** (below Glen Canyon Dam)

Separate units reflect different recovery programs and unique threats requiring site-specific management actions.

### Population Viability Criteria

**Minimum Viable Population (MVP)** defined as "a population that is sufficiently abundant and well adapted to its environment for long-term persistence without significant artificial demographic or genetic manipulations."

Species-specific MVPs:
- **Humpback Chub**: 2,100 adults (age 4+)
- **Colorado Pikeminnow**: 2,600 adults (age 7+)
- **Razorback Sucker**: 5,800 adults (age 4+)
- **Bonytail**: 4,400 adults (age 4+)

Generation times: 8 years for all species

**Genetic Effective Population Size** calculations considered:
- Sex ratios (1:1 for humpback chub and bonytail; 3:1 for Colorado pikeminnow and razorback sucker)
- Portion of breeding individuals (mean 0.30 from literature range 0.013-0.90)
- Compensation for annual adult mortality

### Downlisting Criteria (5-year monitoring period)

**Humpback Chub**:
- Each population maintained (no net loss)
- One core population in upper basin >2,100 adults (currently met)
- One core population in lower basin >2,100 adults
- Trend in adult population estimates does not decline significantly
- Mean estimated recruitment of age-3 fish equals or exceeds adult mortality
- Site-specific management tasks implemented

**Colorado Pikeminnow**:
- Green River core population >2,600 adults (currently met)
- Upper Colorado River population >700 adults
- San Juan River establish/maintain 800 adults
- Lower basin: two populations each >2,600 adults
- No significant decline; recruitment equals or exceeds mortality
- Site-specific management tasks implemented

**Razorback Sucker**:
- Self-sustaining populations in Green River and EITHER upper Colorado River OR San Juan River, each >5,800 adults
- Genetic refuge of 50,000 adults maintained in Lake Mohave
- Lower basin: two populations each >5,800 adults
- No significant decline; recruitment equals or exceeds mortality
- Site-specific management tasks implemented

**Bonytail**:
- Populations in Green River and upper Colorado River, each >4,400 adults
- Genetic refuge established/maintained in suitable Lower Basin locations (e.g., Lake Mohave, [[Lake Havasu]])
- Lower basin: two populations each >4,400 adults
- No significant decline; recruitment equals or exceeds mortality
- Site-specific management tasks implemented

### Delisting Criteria

**Humpback Chub** (3 years beyond downlisting):
- Two core populations in upper basin, each >2,100 adults
- One core population in lower basin >2,100 adults
- Populations maintained with no net loss
- Site-specific management tasks completed; necessary protection levels attained

**Colorado Pikeminnow** (7 years beyond downlisting):
- Green River core population >2,600 adults
- Upper Colorado River population >1,000 adults OR upper Colorado River >700 adults AND San Juan River >800 adults (self-sustaining)
- Lower basin: two populations each >2,600 adults maintained
- Site-specific management tasks completed

**Razorback Sucker** (3 years beyond downlisting):
- Populations maintained in Green River and EITHER upper Colorado River OR San Juan River, each >5,800 adults
- Primary genetic refuge maintained in Lake Mohave
- Lower basin: two populations each >5,800 adults maintained
- Site-specific management tasks completed

**Bonytail** (3 years beyond downlisting):
- Populations maintained in Green River and upper Colorado River, each >4,400 adults
- Genetic refuge maintained in Lower Basin
- Lower basin: two populations each >4,400 adults maintained
- Site-specific management tasks completed

### Management Actions Required

**All Species**:
1. Provide and legally protect flows and habitats necessary for recovery
2. Provide passage over barriers within occupied habitat
3. Investigate options for appropriate water temperatures in [[Gunnison River]]
4. Minimize entrainment in diversion canals
5. Ensure adequate protection from overutilization
6. Ensure adequate protection from diseases and parasites
7. Regulate nonnative fish releases and escapement
8. Control problematic nonnative fishes as needed
9. Minimize risk of hazardous-materials spills in critical habitat
10. Remediate water-quality problems
11. Reestablish populations with hatchery-produced fish
12. Develop conservation plans for long-term management beyond delisting

**Species-Specific**:
- **Bonytail and Razorback Sucker**: Identify and maintain genetic variability in Lake Mohave
- **Bonytail and Humpback Chub**: Minimize risk of increased hybridization among *Gila* species
- **Razorback Sucker**: Minimize adverse effects of selenium contamination; minimize threat of hybridization with white sucker
- **Humpback Chub**: Control Asian tapeworm parasitism in Little Colorado River

### Program Elements

1. **Habitat Management**: Identifying and acquiring instream flows, changing Federal dam operations, operating reservoirs to benefit endangered fish
2. **Habitat Development**: Restoring floodplain/wetland habitats, constructing fish passageways around dams and barriers
3. **Raising and Stocking**: Operating hatcheries to raise endangered fish and return them to native habitat
4. **Nonnative Fish Management**: Managing nonnative species to limit encounters with endangered fish
5. **Research, Monitoring and Data Management**: Collecting data to measure effectiveness of recovery efforts

### Nonnative Fish Control Measures

- Agreements signed between USFWS and states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming to review and regulate all fish stockings
- Prohibition of nonnative fish release within 50-year floodplain
- State regulation of privately-owned pond stocking
- Complete removal of nonnative fish where necessary
- Screening ponds to prevent nonnative fish from reaching river
- Reshaping ponds to prevent year-round habitation
- Removal of bag and possession limits on nonnative fish in critical habitat
- River reach closures to angling where mortality significant to native fish

### Timeline Estimates

**Humpback Chub**: If first reliable estimates available in 2001, downlisting could be proposed in 2006, delisting in 2009 (if all criteria met)

**Colorado Pikeminnow**: If first reliable estimates available in 2001, downlisting could be proposed in 2006, delisting in 2013 (if all criteria met)

**Razorback Sucker and Bonytail**: Habitat repatriation and augmentation programs underway; responses need evaluation to determine best strategies for establishing new populations. Once established, 5-year monitoring for downlisting, 3 additional years for delisting.

### Current Population Status

- **Colorado Pikeminnow**: Populations stable and increasing in Green and Colorado rivers; reproducing in wild; small population in San Juan River; extirpated from Lower Colorado River Basin in 1970s
- **Bonytail**: Rarest of four species; no known reproducing wild populations; being reintroduced through stocking
- **Humpback Chub**: Six wild populations known; latest estimates suggest currently meets population numbers for downlisting (requires verification and 5-year monitoring)
- **Razorback Sucker**: Remaining wild populations in serious jeopardy; most riverine individuals in Upper Basin; largest population in Lake Mohave

### Conservation Plans

Conservation plans will be developed before delisting to provide:
- Long-term management and protection of species
- Reasonable assurances that recovered populations will be maintained without need for relisting
- Legal protection of flows for habitat maintenance
- Regulation and/or control of nonnative fishes
- Minimization of hazardous-materials spill risks
- Ongoing population and habitat monitoring

### State Protections

All four species listed as endangered or threatened under state laws in Colorado, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada, and California. State protections vary but would remain in effect independent of federal delisting decisions unless states choose to remove species from their lists.
