# Preliminary Notes on the Biology of the St. Joseph River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1933 field survey of the [[St Joseph River]] in northern Indiana and southwestern Michigan documents severe organic pollution from the cities of [[South Bend]] and [[Mishawaka]]. Low dissolved oxygen, pollution-tolerant plankton, and a depauperate invertebrate fauna limit the river to a few tolerant fish, chiefly [[European Carp]]. The study also examines fish helminth infection and the potential role of the river in the life cycle of the [[Broad Fish Tapeworm]] (*Diphyllobothrium latum*), linking copepod first intermediate hosts to sewage-borne eggs.

## Background & Description

The river rises in the lake district of south-central Michigan, flows through Indiana, and enters [[Lake Michigan]] at St. Joseph and Benton Harbor. Its Indiana length is about 42 miles; the Indiana–Michigan line to the mouth is about 45 miles. The drainage basin of roughly 4,600 square miles (1,700 in Indiana, 2,900 in Michigan) lies entirely within glacial drift, with about four hundred small lakes providing storage. The river is very low-gradient in Indiana (about 2.2 ft per mile) but drops 17.8 ft per mile between Three Rivers and the Indiana–Michigan line. Multiple power dams, including those at Elkhart, Mishawaka, South Bend, Niles, and Berrien Springs (forming Lake Chapin), fragment the channel.

Field work concentrated on the reach from South Bend to Niles. Identification relied on Ward and Whipple's *Fresh Water Biology*, Kudo's *Handbook of Protozoology*, and Pratt's *Manual* for vertebrates, with mollusc checks by F. C. Baker.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pollution.** Finch (1928–30) estimated the pollutional load from South Bend and Mishawaka as equal to the sewage of 150,000 people. State surveys by the [[Indiana State Board of Health]] (1929) and the [[Michigan Stream Control Commission]] (1931) showed dissolved oxygen below 3 ppm at Angella Street and Healthwin Bridge, with biochemical oxygen demand exceeding dissolved oxygen between La Salle Street and two miles below the city. The highest *B. coli* count was 200,000 per cubic centimetre. Oxygen was over 5 ppm from the lake to Buchanan.

**Macroinvertebrates.** Mussels were abundant above Mishawaka (e.g., *Elliptio dilatatus delicatus*, *Actinonaias carinata*, *Lampsilis ventricosa*) but absent below the South Bend dam until Berrien Springs. Snails (*Pleurocera*, *Campeloma*) declined downstream. Crayfish in South Bend were few (three *Cambarus* species), while *Cambarus blandingi acutus* and *C. virilis* were abundant between Bertrand and Niles, where bass and sunfish fed heavily on them.

**Habitat.** Dense beds of *Potamogeton*, *Elodea*, and *Ceratophyllum* grew across the river, probably stimulated by sewage nutrients. Sloughs and bayous below Bertrand supported turtles (*Chrysemys*, *Emys blandingi*, *Chelydra serpentina*), frogs, and bryozoans (*Pectinatella magnifica*).

**Biological indicators.** Using Forbes and Richardson's (1913) scheme, the river was classed as *polluted* from South Bend to Bertrand and *contaminated* from Bertrand to Niles.

**Herpetofauna and avifauna.** A list of common birds (e.g., [[Belted Kingfisher]], [[Great Blue Heron]], [[Herring Gull]]) and reptiles was recorded along the river.

## Key Findings

- **Pollution gradient:** Dissolved oxygen, odour, *B. coli* counts, and invertebrate absence all indicate pollution, most severe between South Bend and Niles.
- **Plankton indicators:** Pollution-tolerant taxa included *Euglena oxyuris*, *E. acus*, *Epistylis plicatilis*, *Anuraea cochlearis*, and *Aeolosoma hemprichi*, alongside *Saprolegnia* and *Oscillatoria limosa*. Protozoa and pollution organisms were most concentrated near South Bend.
- **Plankton sampling:** Samples were taken at 19 stations (tow-net, Kofoid type, No. 20 bolting cloth). Nine taxonomic tables list Protozoa, Turbellaria, Annelida, Rotatoria, Gastrotricha, Cladocera, Copepoda, Ostracoda, and Hydracarina. Winter tables (November and December 1932, 1–3 °C and 0–2.5 °C) added station 1a.
- **Fish community:** Between South Bend and Niles, carp were the only fish regularly taken. Small mouth bass, bluegill, and suckers occurred above the South Bend city dam and near Brandywine Creek, and pickerel appeared near Niles.
- **Diphyllobothrium latum life cycle:** Copepods (*Diaptomus oregonensis*, *Cyclops* spp.) serve as first intermediate hosts. *D. oregonensis* was predominant in November and among the most abundant forms in December, so the host is present in the river. No St. Joseph fish were found infected with plerocercoids, though sampling was limited.
- **Hosts of plerocercoids in North America:** Pike, walleye, yellow perch, and burbot.
- **Native human cases:** Lyon reported three native cases from South Bend. One estimate suggested that a single tapeworm carrier might release about 1,000,000 eggs per day into the sewer system.
- **Parasite survey (1931–1932):** 336 fish representing 20 species were examined for parasites from the river and three small lakes, with 13 species infected (49%). The river sample alone comprised 315 fish, of which 35% were infected. Nine river species were infected.
- **Carp parasites:** 41 of 83 carp harboured a cestodarian, a group previously recorded only from Catostomidae. Carp were largely immune to other parasites in the river.
- **Parasite groups:** Acanthocephala were the most frequent helminths in the broader comparison, and trematodes were the most common internal parasites in river fish.
- **Sucker infection:** 82% of 23 suckers from near Niles carried *Pomphorhynchus bulbocoli*.
- **Fishing and fish quality:** Anglers avoided the river, and Michigan fishermen reported "gassy" fish, which the study associated with prolonged oxygen deficiency. Carp fishing increased in 1932, partly due to economic hardship.
- **Conclusions:** The river between Mishawaka and Niles is unsuitable for most fish and fresh-water life apart from pollution-tolerant forms. Recovery of fish life is expected only if raw sewage discharge is controlled. The Stream Control Commission and Indiana State Board of Health data form the basis for this assessment.
