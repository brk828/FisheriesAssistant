# Illinois Streams: A Classification Based on Their Fishes and an Analysis of Factors Responsible for Disappearance of Native Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

Philip W. Smith (Illinois Natural History Survey) classifies the streams of Illinois into 33 stream systems, each rated excellent, good, fair, poor, or variable based on the fish species present. The state's fauna is recorded as 193 species, of which 180 are native and 13 are naturalized. Comparing the [[Forbes and Richardson Survey (1876-1905)]] with the [[Illinois Natural History Survey Resurvey (1950-1971)]] showed that 8 native species have been extirpated and 60 others decimated. Five principal causes are identified: [[Silt]], [[Drainage]], [[Desiccation During Drought]], [[Species Interaction]], and [[Pollution]], with [[Dams and Impoundments]], [[Temperature]], and unknown causes as secondary factors.

## Background & Description

Illinois is bounded by great rivers on the west, south, and southeast and by [[Lake Michigan]] on the northeast. It contains many creeks, rivers, ponds, artificial lakes, and a few glacial lakes in the northeast. Almost 200 species have been recorded, and the stream diversity and variety of stream habitats are the main reasons for this richness.

Fishes are described as tolerant organisms, but their tolerances differ widely among species. The presence of fish alone says little about stream condition; the assemblage and numerical relationships of species give a reliable biological picture. Long-term survey data make fishes among the most sensitive indicators of aquatic environmental quality.

Two major collecting programs underpin the analysis:
- **1876–1905:** Fishes of Illinois, published as Forbes & Richardson (1908), with a separate atlas of 103 maps.
- **1950–1971:** A more thorough survey enabled by modern transportation, with Lopinot's Division of Fisheries contributing hundreds of later collections.

Ratings follow the American Fisheries Society common names (Bailey et al. 1970). The paper is a contribution from the Section of Faunistic Surveys and Insect Identification of the Illinois Natural History Survey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

A stream rated **excellent** retains its expected species in numerical relationships indicating little modification from original conditions. Ratings consider sampling adequacy, unusual species and habitats, and identifiable sources of problems. The Mississippi, Ohio, Wabash, and Illinois rivers are excluded from the annotated list, but their species are counted for adjacent stream systems where records exist.

Species richness tracks habitat availability. The [[Kaskaskia-Marys River System]] has the highest count (104 species) because it is the largest basin with the most habitat variety. High-diversity systems include the Fox (102), Mackinaw (100), Clear Creek (99), Rock-Green (98), Sangamon (94), and Embarras (92). Low-diversity systems include the Edwards (52), Little Vermilion of the north (52), Plum (53), Bear Creek (54), and Saline (57), most with small watersheds.

Representative system ratings:
- **Excellent:** [[Kankakee-Iroquois River System]] (72 species), [[Clear Creek-Horseshoe Lake System]] (99 species, including the Pine Hills swamp and the rare [[Spring Cavefish]])
- **Good to excellent:** [[Fox River System]] (102 species; unusual species include blackchin shiner, banded killifish, and mottled sculpin), [[Mackinaw River System]] (100 species), [[Big Bureau Creek]] (74 species)
- **Fair:** [[Vermilion (of the North) River System]] (80 species), [[Spoon River System]] (87 species), [[La Moine River System]] (78 species)
- **Poor:** [[Des Plaines River System]] (63 species, with some streams supporting only goldfish, goldfish-carp hybrids, or green sunfish), [[Wood River-Cahokia Creek]] (64 species), [[Saline River System]] (57 species; some reaches devoid of fish due to acid mine discharge)
- **Variable:** [[Sangamon River System]] (94 species) and [[Embarras River System]] (92 species, with a stretch from Charleston to Newton rated among the state's best)

The [[Rock River]] system (98 species) is rated good, and in places excellent, despite dams and urbanization. The Pecatonica and Green rivers are rated fair.

## Key Findings

**Long-term changes (Forbes & Richardson 1908 vs. 1950–1971 survey)**
- Total recorded species: 193 (13 naturalized, 180 native), excluding aquarium escapees.
- Of the 13 non-native species, 4 are exotic (brown trout, goldfish, carp, grass carp); 6 are native to North America but stocked in Illinois (rainbow trout, brook trout, coho salmon, chinook salmon, American smelt, white catfish); 3 have recently extended ranges into the state (sea lamprey, alewife, threadfin shad).
- Of the 180 native species, 8 expanded their ranges and abundance (silverjaw minnow, bigmouth shiner, red shiner, redfin shiner, fathead minnow, creek chub, mosquitofish, redear sunfish).
- 104 native species showed little change, possibly reflecting poorer early sampling.
- **8 native species extirpated** and **60 decimated** (range shrinkage or endangerment).

**Factors responsible (species affected)**

| Factor | Extirpated | Decimated |
|---|---|---|
| Silt | 2 | 14 |
| Drainage | 0 | 13 |
| Desiccation during drought | 0 | 12 |
| Species interaction | 2 | 7 |
| Pollution | 2 | 5 |
| Dams and impoundments | 0 | 4 |
| Temperature | 0 | 1 |
| Unknown causes | 2 | 4 |

- **Silt** is the leading cause. It removed aquatic vegetation, buried gravel and sand habitats, and reduced clear-water species such as the [[Bigeye Chub]] and [[Pugnose Minnow]]. The [[Crystal Darter]] has not been recorded in Illinois since 1900, and the [[Gilt Darter]] since 1932.
- **Drainage** of natural lakes, marginal sloughs, and prairie marshes eliminated lacustrine species such as the [[Pugnose Shiner]], [[Blackchin Shiner]], and [[Banded Killifish]], which are now confined to a few glacial lakes in northeastern Illinois.
- **Desiccation during drought** has been most damaging since 1930, when water-table variability increased. It reduced headwater species such as the [[Bluntnose Darter]] and large-river species such as the [[Emerald Shiner]].
- **Species interaction:** the [[Chestnut Lamprey]] replaced the [[Ohio Lamprey]], and the [[Redfin Shiner]] replaced the [[Rosefin Shiner]]. The [[Red Shiner]] hybridized with and displaced the [[Spotfin Shiner]] and [[Steelcolor Shiner]]. Sea lamprey predation in Lake Michigan in the 1940s was followed by a decline of cisco, whitefishes, lake trout, and burbot, and an alewife explosion.
- **Pollution** (industrial, domestic, agricultural, oil-field, and mine waste) ranks fifth. The [[Greater Redhorse]] disappeared from Salt Creek after 1901, and the [[Cypress Minnow]] was eliminated from the Little Muddy River. Fish kills from toxicants such as anhydrous ammonia and cyanide recur, but streams usually repopulate within 2–3 years; endemic and relict species do not.
- **Dams** eliminate riffles, convert the reservoir floor to a single silt habitat, and block migration. They decimated the [[Greenside Darter]], [[Eastern Sand Darter]], fantail darter, and [[Dusky Darter]] in the North Fork and Embarras rivers.
- **Temperature:** loss of shade and reduced spring flow likely caused the disappearance of the [[Northern Pike]] from western Union County.
- **Unknown causes:** the [[Muskellunge]] (northern Illinois) and [[Stargazing Darter]] (lower Wabash) were extirpated without an identifiable single cause. Decline of the lake sturgeon, blue sucker, and paddlefish is attributed to combined factors. Channel dredging and straightening destroy habitat but are not the principal cause of decimation for any native species.

**Conclusions**
- Stream ratings reflect habitat integrity more than fish presence. Long-term species-composition data make fishes sensitive indicators of watershed health.
- Silt, drainage, and drought now exert the strongest pressures, and the proposed reservoirs threaten some of the state's most valuable remaining waterways.
