# The Hermaphroditic Menace to Mosquitoes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #killifish

## Executive Summary

This document combines a mosquito-control public relations chapter, a biocontrol product advertisement, a field natural-history article on [[Rivulus Marmoratus]] by D. Scott Taylor (*Wing Beats*, Fall 1992), and two equipment advertisements. The central research content concerns a small killifish that lives almost exclusively in [[Land Crab Burrows]] in Florida salt marshes, is the only known vertebrate example of a synchronous, selfing functional hermaphrodite, and shows strong potential as a predator of mosquito larvae. The author concludes that the species is too rare in the wild to be a practical [[Biological Control]] agent, but recommends further study of its appetite and reproduction.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Rivulus Marmoratus]], a poorly known and presumably rare member of the killifish family. The genus name has been adopted as its common name ("rivulus").
- **Discovery:** The author first collected four fish from a dipper sample in a dried mangrove depression in east central Florida. A second visit to the same spot showed the "hole" was a burrow of the land crab [[Cardisoma Guanhumi]]. Thirteen more rivulus were then netted from the burrow.
- **Rarity:** Only about 50 rivulus had been collected on the entire east coast of Florida before this study. Florida's salt marsh fish fauna had been intensively sampled, yet the species rarely appeared. The state lists it as a Species of Special Concern.
- **Taxonomic significance:** Since its 1961 discovery, the species has been studied in the laboratory. It is the only vertebrate known to be a synchronous, selfing functional hermaphrodite, so it clones itself. Its ovotestes produce eggs that hatch into genetic copies of the parent, which makes it attractive to geneticists and immunochemists.
- **Appearance:** Hermaphrodites are drab and mottled (a 45 mm adult is pictured). Males are flashy, with bright orange/red flanks.
- **Non-research content:** The remaining material includes:
  - A mosquito-control public relations chapter. It compares controlled media (brochures, exhibits, presentations) with uncontrolled media (television, newspapers, radio). It also poses four planning questions (target audience, objectives, resources, strategies) and gives a fifth-grade example. The chapter closes by stressing that letters of support from citizens and civic groups carry more weight than special-interest committees.
  - A VorTex Biological Services video and training package on biological control and mosquito fish, priced at $29.95 plus $3.00 shipping and handling.
  - Advertisements for Dyna-Fog aerosol generators and Vectec mosquito control products.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Land crab habitat:** [[Cardisoma Guanhumi]] is a tropical crab found north to Brevard County (Kennedy Space Center) on the east coast, with scattered colonies nearly to St. Augustine. It digs burrows in muddy sediments in or beside salt marshes adjoining the [[Indian River Lagoon]] and inland along tributaries. Burrows sit high on the marsh profile, where [[Aedes Taeniorhynchus]] also breeds. Burrows are dug to the groundwater table and range from about one to a few meters deep. The water pool at the bottom is the habitat to which the fish appears to be restricted on this coast.
- **Microhabitat:** The burrow water volume is at most a few liters. Burrows also serve as reservoirs for [[Deinocerites Cancer]] larvae. The author never found this mosquito in a burrow containing rivulus.
- **Other habitats:** In south and southwest Florida, where Cardisoma is scarce, rivulus lives in isolated mangrove depressions. It retreats into leaf litter or the burrows of smaller crabs when pools dry. In Belize it occurs with the land crab *Ucides cordatus*.
- **Emersion and water quality:** Rivulus often leaves the water to cling to burrow walls for hours. This [[Emersion]] behavior was thought to relate to elevated [[Hydrogen Sulfide]] levels. The author observed it as an adaptation to poor water. Fish trapped by a receding tide survived when a few hundred others died and decomposed in the same burrow, and rivulus were the only fish left alive afterward.
- **Juvenile habitat:** Juveniles smaller than about 1/2 inch were not found in crab burrows. Juveniles were seen at openings of fiddler crab (*Uca*) burrows, which the author considered possible but unconfirmed nursery sites.

## Key Findings

**Movement and behavior**
- Captive fish switched burrows by crossing several inches of damp mud, and they took a longer route when a barrier was placed between burrows.
- Fish found a single surface access hole within 24 hours after the burrows were sealed.
- The author describes the fish as almost as much at home on land as in water.

**Feeding**
- Of sampled rivulus guts, 40% were empty and 60% contained both terrestrial and aquatic prey. The author suggests burrows act as pit traps for terrestrial invertebrates, and that fish also leave burrows during intermittent flooding to feed.
- The largest single-burrow catch was 26 fish.

**Reproduction**
- Eggs are about 1 mm in diameter. Captive fish laid eggs on damp mud away from the burrows, not in the water. The eggs hatch within minutes of flooding.
- Only a single wild egg has been found, on a burrow wall a few inches above the waterline.
- Males are rare in Florida, with about 10 recorded from southwest Florida and none from the east coast. In Belize, males make up to 25% of the population.
- Males court hermaphrodites with classic killifish displays. Histology showed viable sperm, but whether fertilization occurs with males present is unresolved. This would require the hermaphrodite to suppress self-fertilization.

**Mosquito predation (lab data, Ritchie, Johnson and Taylor)**

| Fish stage | Prey | Consumption in 24 h |
|---|---|---|
| 1-day-old | 1st-instar *Aedes* larvae | up to 21 |
| 10-day-old | larvae (same period) | up to 100 |
| Adult (55 mm) | 4th-instar *Aedes* larvae | over 100 |
| Adult | *Culex quinquefasciatus* egg rafts | nearly 100 |

- Two fish were also observed eating 150 4th-instar *Ae. taeniorhynchus* larvae in one aquarium session.
- Laboratory-reared adults released at isolated *Ae. taeniorhynchus* breeding sites were still surviving two and a half years later. Some of those sites dried for up to two months.

**Conclusions and cautions**
- The author concludes that rivulus is not naturally abundant enough to be a viable [[Biological Control]] alternative. Its appetite and hardiness are strong, but its wild fecundity is low compared with [[Gambusia]] and *Poecilia* (sailfin molly).
- The author suggests laboratory manipulation might raise reproduction and should be explored.
- Because the species is state-listed, collecting requires a permit from the [[Florida Game And Fresh Water Fish Commission]]. Any release should be coordinated with clone-distribution studies so natural distributions are not disturbed.
- Like most tropical fish, rivulus dies in water below 50°F.

**Public relations content**
- Controlled media give complete message control but less credibility. Uncontrolled media reach more people at lower cost but offer little control over the message or its timing.
- Planning should begin with the target audience, objectives, available resources and strategies. Agencies are encouraged to borrow and adapt existing [[Public Relations]] materials.
