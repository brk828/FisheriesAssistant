# Effects of Abiotic Disturbance on Coexistence of Predator-Prey Fish Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether large-scale abiotic disturbance (flash flooding) promotes coexistence between a native predator-prey pair of poeciliid fishes in Arizona. The native [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and the introduced [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) co-occur in a few flood-prone Southwestern systems. The author predicted that the introduced mosquitofish would be more affected by floods than the native topminnow, and that topminnows possess a stronger, largely innate behavioral resistance to flooding. Field surveys and [[Laboratory Flume Experiments]] supported these predictions. The paper proposes that periodic flash flooding removes disproportionate numbers of the predator before it eliminates the prey, enabling long-term coexistence.

## Background & Description

- **Theoretical framing:** Large-scale abiotic disturbances such as fire, flood, and drought may select for morphological, physiological, and behavioral traits that reduce their impact. Behavior is rarely documented because unpredictable, infrequent events are hard to observe in the field.
- **Methodological approach:** If morphologically similar organisms respond differently to a natural disturbance, the responsible behaviors can be reproduced and measured in the laboratory.
- **Flood regimes:** [[Flash Flooding]] in Southwestern arid drainages is rapid and brief, with discharge rising by as much as five orders of magnitude within seconds. Mesic (eastern and southeastern US) floods last hours to days. Canyon constraints in Southwestern streams amplify flood waves and reduce refuges.
- **Study species:**
  - *P. occidentalis* (Poeciliidae) is a small fish (<50 mm), native to springs, marshes, and streams of Arizona, and has declined sharply throughout its range.
  - *G. affinis* is a native of Gulf and Atlantic lowland drainages, introduced into Arizona in 1926, and has become prevalent since the 1950s. Both species have similar morphologies and life histories, but evolved under different flow regimes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mosquitofish as predators:** Mosquitofish have extirpated topminnows throughout most of Arizona, largely by predation on juveniles. Topminnows were usually eliminated within several months of contact with mosquitofish.
- **Persistence in flood-prone habitats:** Remaining large topminnow populations occur where introduced predators are absent or where habitats flood. Populations at [[Sharp Spring]] and the [[Santa Cruz River]] coexisted with mosquitofish in flood-prone settings.
- **Comparison of flood regimes:** At Sharp Spring (drainage 5.0 km²) and adjacent Sheehy Spring (drainage 0.5 km²), flooding is more frequent and intense at Sharp Spring. Mosquitofish colonized Sheehy Spring between March 1977 and December 1979. By June 1980 topminnows made up 6% of fish sampled, and by summer 1982 they were virtually eliminated.
- **Cross-system evidence:** Introduced predatory centrarchids and ictalurids were reduced or eliminated by flooding in at least six other unregulated Arizona streams, while native species were little affected. Introduced fishes dominate many regulated desert reservoirs that do not flood heavily.
- **Limits of flooding:** Severe floods that deeply incise beds or remove alluvium can decimate native populations, and some erosive streams are fishless.
- **Community regulation:** Fish communities in Southwestern streams appear regulated by both biotic and abiotic processes. Without flooding, mosquitofish predictably eliminate topminnows by predation.

## Key Findings

1. **Field evidence:** Post-flood surveys of the native and introduced species showed that mosquitofish were disproportionately removed, while topminnow abundance was maintained, consistent with the prediction that topminnows resist flood displacement better.
2. **Laboratory displacement:** In flume experiments, mosquitofish were displaced more readily than topminnows at all age classes tested. Topminnows showed quick, innate responses to a pulse of water. Mosquitofish were more affected by flooding cues, and their resistance was partly learned.
3. **Innate versus learned resistance:** Newborn (1-day-old) topminnows already resisted displacement, indicating an innate response. Resistance improved between 1-day and 6-week-old topminnows. Experienced fish (after a 3-week interval) showed reduced displacement, indicating retained learning in both species.
4. **Size and ontogeny:** Size differences did not explain the pattern. Older mosquitofish still performed poorly in the flume, so the difference is attributed to species, not development.
5. **Coexistence mechanism:** Periodic flash flooding removes proportionally more of the predator, *G. affinis*, before it eliminates the prey, *P. occidentalis*. Long-term coexistence is therefore a direct result of differing species responses to disturbance.
6. **Predictions for future evolution:** Mosquitofish from fluctuating Arizona habitats are expected to become more flood-adapted over time, potentially eventually extirpating topminnows even in regularly flooding habitats.
7. **Falsification criterion:** Long-term coexistence in a lentic (non-flowing) habitat, with little or no topminnow decline for 1 to 4 years after mosquitofish colonization, would falsify the disturbance hypothesis.
