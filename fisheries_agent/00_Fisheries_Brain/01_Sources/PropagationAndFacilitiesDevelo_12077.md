# FY93 Annual Project Report: Propagation and Facilities Development (Colorado River Recovery Program)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This FY93 annual report from the [[Colorado River Recovery Program]] (Project No. 24) covers the propagation, captive breeding, and pond-rearing work for four endangered fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. Work was conducted at the [[Ouray Fish Hatchery]] and the [[Horsethief Ponds]] at Horsethief State Wildlife Area, with a budget of $219,000 provided and fully expended. The year yielded large numbers of juveniles for refugia, research, and stocking, but losses to disease, [[Gas Bubble Disease]], and bird predation were significant, particularly for bonytail and surplus squawfish.

## Background & Description

The project is run by Fish and Wildlife Service staff (Principal Investigators Holt Williamson, Timothy Modde, and Frank Pfeifer) under the Fisheries and Federal Aid office in Denver, Colorado. Initial year was 1987, with the study ongoing. It supports two activities in the [[Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin]]: operation and maintenance of the Ouray and Horsethief facilities.

Stated goals were to develop and implement an integrated propagation and genetics management work plan. Objectives included:

- Captive breeding as called for in the draft outline (Williamson 1990).
- Maintaining secure facilities with adequate equipment and personnel.
- Holding fish securely in captivity and propagating them successfully.
- Preliminary tests of fish and facilities for production compatibility.

End products were secure refuges, back-up refuges, and broodstock for the four target species.

The report frames [[Artificial Propagation]] as an essential recovery element, one of five listed in the Recovery Implementation Program. Propagation is presented as a short-term buffer against extinction and a source of fish for broodstock, experimental stocking, population establishment, augmentation, research, and education.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Wild-adult and refuge populations were held at multiple locations, and several fish came from the Lake Powell arm, San Juan River, Green River (Razorback Bar and Escalante Bar), and Grand Valley. Water supply at Horsethief carried disease organisms, and Colorado River water pumped into ice-covered ponds caused nitrogen gas supersaturation. Great blue herons were a major predator of juvenile fish in open ponds.

The report also notes that disease risk, not just treatability, shapes management: [[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]] ("Ich") surged in warm water, and [[Lernaea]] (anchor worm) was anticipated, though it had affected only a few fish.

## Key Findings

**Broodstock and refugia (end of FY93)**
- Wild adult razorback suckers held at Ouray: 1 male (Yampa, Echo Park); 12 unsexed fish from Razorback Bar (originally 14 adults, 7 males and 7 females, including 2 from the Duchesne River mouth); 9 (5 males, 4 females) from San Juan transferred to UDWR on April 29, 1993; and 5 (3 males, 2 females) from Lake Powell, Colorado.
- Back-up refuge: 398 Green River sub-adults (1989–1991 year classes from stream-side spawning at Escalante Bar); 7,802 juveniles from the 1993 year class in 13 paired-mating lots, of which 6 had at least 500 young-of-year (YOY); 1,664 San Juan razorback sucker sub-adults (1992 year class) with 75% pond survival, with an estimated 6 of 10 lots meeting the 250-fish criterion.

**Research and development propagation**
- Chemoreception (imprinting) program: 509 Green River razorback sucker sub-adults, 446 and 1,230 imprinted Dexter squawfish, 926 imprinted Dexter razorback suckers, and 32 imprinted Green River razorback suckers.
- Eggs from 4 males and 2 females of the Colorado River Arm were imprinted with Gunnison and Uncompahgre river water and furnished to CDOW.
- About 2,700 Green River fry went to the Old Charley Wash bottomland habitat study; about 3,600 eggs went to contaminant studies; 620 fry were sent to the Columbia Research Lab to test irrigation drain water toxicity.
- Technology development: 14,352 Green River fry were used to test cage culture of 10–12 day post-hatch fry.

**Horsethief pond stocking**
- About 1,100 PIT-tagged F1 Colorado squawfish (Grand Valley stock) for refugia, plus about 4,400 surplus squawfish for rearing-limit tests.
- 108 razorback suckers (1992 year class, Colorado River Arm progeny), 50 F1 bonytail, 3 wild Grand Valley razorback suckers, and 10 adults from Etter's Pond.

**Losses and problems**
- Bird predation: initial pond netting was penetrated by herons, and perimeter netting failed; about 2,000 surplus squawfish (6–8 inches) were lost. Complete pond netting was installed over all juvenile ponds.
- Deformities: the 108 Colorado River Arm razorback suckers showed misshaped mouths, crooked spines, and shortened gill covers. The team suspects formalin treatment for Costia; 25 fish remain and cannot be used for experimental stocking in the Gunnison and Colorado rivers.
- Disease: bacterial septicemia in adults was controlled with oxytetracycline. An Ich outbreak in August killed all 50 bonytail adults within 5 days, shifting the team toward prophylactic treatment.
- Gas bubble disease: 13 bonytail adults died, while trout, fathead minnows, green sunfish, roundtail chub, and bluehead suckers survived in the same pond. The report warns that tolerant species can give a false sense of security for endangered fish.
- Corrective actions: pond aerators and packed column degassers were to be tested, and a saturometer was purchased.

**Regulatory and budget status**
- An Investigational New Animal Drug (INAD) application for standard fish culture chemicals was still pending, with availability expected for the 1994 season; a local veterinarian was prescribing off-label drugs in the interim.
- Budget: $219,000 provided and $219,000 expended, with no difference.

**Recommendations**
1. Seek additional paired matings in 1994 to complete the 5x5 matrix for Green River adults; matrix completion may take 5 years or more, and cage culture and pond management should improve so YOY can be PIT-tagged.
2. Back up every Horsethief population with other populations, since disease is the greatest threat.
3. Let surplus squawfish grow to test pond capacity limits and prophylactic treatment intervals.
4. Avoid moving fish into ponds during cool months, when immunity drops; consider treated feed or injections beforehand. Repeat bonytail rearing trials, and delay wild humpback chub transfers until bonytail rearing succeeds.
5. Inventory Horsethief ponds at least three times a year to cull feral river fish; keep filter socks on inlets.
6. The Biology Committee should ask the Propagation Subcommittee to prepare a fish disposition document.
