# Desert Pupfish Population Status and Listing Recommendation (1978)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #endangered-species #conservation

## Executive Summary

This September 1978 correspondence from Boyd Kynard (Massachusetts Cooperative Fishery Research Unit) to Dr. James E. Johnson (U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service) documents the critical status of [[Desert Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon macularius) populations across Arizona, Mexico, and California. The letter provides detailed population assessments and formally recommends federal listing of remaining U.S. populations as protected species due to rapid declines, particularly in California following [[Tilapia]] introduction, and ongoing threats from pesticides, water extraction, and habitat modification.

## Background & Description

[[Cyprinodon macularius]] (Desert Pupfish, family [[Cyprinodontidae]]) historically occupied desert aquatic habitats across the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. The species inhabits springs, pools, streams, and irrigation infrastructure in arid environments. This assessment covers populations in [[Quitobaquito]] (Arizona), multiple sites in [[Sonora]] (Mexico), and remnant California populations.

The document references previous ecological research by Kynard and Garrett (Arizona Coop. Park Service Report #1, 1976) on habitat requirements at Quitobaquito.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Arizona Populations:**
The [[Quitobaquito]] population in [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]] experienced significant pesticide-related mortality in summer 1976. Wind-borne pesticides (m-parathion) from Mexican agriculture <1 km south caused fish tissue to reach lethal contamination levels. Normal population fluctuations ranged from 6,000 (minimum) to 10,000 fish (maximum) based on Peterson population estimates (1975-1977).

**Mexican Populations:**
[[Rio Sonoyta]] populations coexisted with [[Longfin Dace]] but faced increasing agricultural development with doubled cropland area by 1978. Lower [[Colorado River]] populations showed variable status: [[Santa Clara]] maintained >20,000 fish despite desalinization plant discharge and introduced species; [[El Doctor]] lost one pool entirely to irrigation pumping (2,000 fish lost) with two pools (~2,000 fish each) remaining; [[Laguna Prietta]] was extirpated by April 1976 due to agricultural water pumping; [[Rio Hardy]] and [[Pozo Caliente]] maintained large populations (~30,000+ individuals) despite channelization and [[Gambusia affinis]] (mosquitofish) introduction, though threatened by commercial salt drying pool development.

**California Populations:**
Rapid, general decline occurred since 1975-76 following introduction of [[Tilapia zillii]]. By 1978, only 1-2 populations remained free of exotic species.

## Key Findings

1. **Population Crash in Arizona**: Quitobaquito population declined from normal levels (6,000-10,000) to <1,800 individuals (spring 1977) following pesticide kill; partial recovery occurred during 1978 breeding season.

2. **Refugia Establishment**: Conservation efforts established backup populations at [[Bates Well]] tank (Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument), [[Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum]], Arizona Historical Museum (Tucson), and Research Ranch south of Tucson (renovated June 1978, stocked September 1978 by Arizona Game and Fish).

3. **Mexican Population Threats**: Intensive agricultural development in Rio Sonoyta region (spring 1975-summer 1978) with doubled cropland and new irrigation infrastructure; direct habitat loss through water pumping at multiple sites; complete extirpation at Laguna Prietta.

4. **California Crisis**: Rapid decline of Desert Pupfish populations following Tilapia introduction, reducing distribution to 1-2 populations free of exotics by 1978.

5. **Federal Listing Recommendation**: Author recommends U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service list remaining U.S. Desert Pupfish populations as protected species based on: (a) small number of remaining populations (1 in Arizona, 1-2 in California), (b) small population sizes, (c) high sensitivity to anthropogenic biological and physical disturbances, (d) precedent of federal protection for other western pupfishes.

6. **Threat Summary**: Primary threats identified include pesticide contamination, water extraction for agriculture, habitat modification (channelization, canal construction), and introduced species (Tilapia zillii, Gambusia affinis).
