# Aging in Fish: A Review of Senescence, Growth, Mortality and Reproduction

#ichthyology #marine-biology #senescence

## Executive Summary

This review by J. F. Craig (Department of Fisheries and Oceans, Freshwater Institute, Winnipeg; published in the *Canadian Journal of Zoology*, 1985) synthesizes theories and empirical evidence on [[Senescence]] in fish. Aging is defined as the increase in mortality rate and decrease in fecundity after maturity and at advanced age. Two main theories are contrasted: aging by wear and tear (random error catastrophe) and genetically determined aging controlled by a biological clock. The review concludes that theory is well advanced but empirical data lag, mainly because few fish populations are unexploited and survive to old age. Growth, natural mortality, maturity and longevity are closely linked, and the author proposes that the future of aging research lies in [[Bioenergetics]] and the environmental factors that control energy use.

## Background & Description

Fish show extensive plasticity of growth and body functions in temperate waters, which makes them useful subjects for [[Gerontology]] and [[Life History Tactics]]. The brown trout illustrates this: stream-resident fish in the headwaters of Windermere rarely exceed about 50 g, whereas lake fish reach 5 kg (roughly 100 times larger).

Fish senescence is supported by cohort survival curves and histological and physiological changes. The review notes that fish age changes are similar to those in mammals, including the appearance of malignant tumors, declining soluble-to-insoluble collagen ratios, and degeneration of the pituitary, thyroid, thymus, liver, kidney and cardiovascular system.

Key theoretical frameworks discussed include:
- [[Pearl Rate of Living Hypothesis]]: aging rate is inversely related to the rate of body activities such as growth, metabolism and reproduction.
- [[Gompertz Constant]]: Rosen et al. (1981) found the product of the Gompertz constant and maximum life-span to be approximately constant across taxa.
- [[Von Bertalanffy Growth Model]]: used by Beverton and Holt (1959) to fit adult growth data, although its physiological basis is questioned.
- [[R and K Selection]]: deterministic life history models predicting how mortality patterns shape maturity, brood size and longevity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and food.** Higher temperatures and rich food speed growth and physiological aging, while cooler temperatures and poor food extend life expectancy (Craig 1978, on perch). Temperature optima and deviations from them are considered more important than simple increases or decreases. Stunted brook trout in an alpine lake, shaped by low temperature, food scarcity and little angling, showed increased longevity (Reimers 1979). Zebrafish on a high-energy diet at 28°C had higher mortality and lower growth than fish at 24°C (Craig and Fletcher 1984).

**Crowding.** Crowding reduces growth, delays maturity, lowers fecundity and can increase longevity, possibly via food shortage or pheromones.

**Exploitation.** Heavily exploited populations show increased growth rates, increased fecundity, earlier maturity and shorter generation times, and remain at lower numbers even after exploitation ceases (Botsford 1981). Age determination of old, slow-growing fish tends to underestimate age and thus overestimate growth and mortality rates.

**Life history strategies.** In [[Bullhead]] populations, upland stream fish (variable spring spates) grow slowly, live long, mature late and spawn once yearly. Lowland chalk stream fish (stable conditions) grow fast, live short lives, mature early and spawn several batches annually. Transplant experiments showed these traits are environmentally controlled, with both populations retaining the genetic capacity for both strategies.

**Mosquitofish evolution.** Populations of [[Western Mosquitofish]] introduced about 75 years earlier into reservoirs with fluctuating versus stable water levels differed significantly in age and length at maturity and offspring weight (Stearns 1983). Females from stable reservoirs matured younger and larger, with smaller offspring.

## Key Findings

**1. Growth and mortality are linked.** Growth and natural mortality are closely correlated within and between species. Beverton and Holt (1959) found the rate of completing the growth pattern relates more closely to longevity than to ultimate size. Beverton (1963) estimated the M/K ratio at 1–2 for clupeid and engraulid species.

**2. Pauly's equations.** Pauly (1980) analyzed 84 freshwater and marine fish species, deriving equations to estimate natural mortality (M) from asymptotic length or weight, K and mean annual temperature. M had its highest partial correlation with K. The author found 1/M (approximately equal to Tmax) was highly correlated with oxygen consumption (p < 0.001). For perch in Windermere, Craig (1980) reported M/K = 2 across cohorts.

**3. Genetic influence on growth.** Clear inheritance effects are mainly shown in salmonids. Precocious male [[Coho Salmon]] had a maximum age of 24 months versus 36 months for normal adults (Childs and Law 1972). Progeny of precocious males grew 24% greater in length and 50% greater in weight in 65 days than progeny of normal crosses.

**4. Environmental masking.** Many genetic effects are obscured by environment. Stunted perch improved growth when transplanted (Alm 1946). Underfed female guppies resumed growth when fed well (Comfort 1960, 1963). In [[Desert Pupfish]], growth potential was greater among small fish, which helps explain growth compensation.

**5. Reproduction and longevity trade-off.** Reproductive effort and longevity trade off to maximize lifetime progeny. Maturity drives senescence in semelparous species: castrated kokanee salmon lived longer (Robertson 1961), and early maturation in mosquitofish led to early senility (Krumholz 1948). Reproductive uncertainty favours iteroparity and longevity, and variable juvenile mortality favours iteroparity while variable adult mortality favours semelparity ("bet-hedging").

**6. Energy and reproduction.** Stored fat in perch declined with age, and in some old females there was insufficient surplus energy to develop gonads (Craig 1977). Newsome and Leduc (1975) linked pre-spawning mortality in yellow perch to insufficient stored energy.

**7. Temperature and immunity.** Lower temperatures extended the life of the annual fish *Cynolebias adloffi* (Liu and Walford 1966), and goldfish accepted scale transplants more readily at 10°C than at higher temperatures (Hildemann 1957). The author notes that temperature dependence of immune responses appears widespread among poikilotherms.

**8. Cellular evidence for a biological clock.** Brain DNA in tilapia decreased with age while free amino acids rose until near maximum age (Panigrahi et al. 1979). RNA/DNA ratios declined with age in carp and smallmouth bass (Haines 1973), and correlated with growth in brook trout and Atlantic salmon (Wilder and Stanley 1983). No build-up toward an error catastrophe was found.

**9. Population regulation.** Lake whitefish response to exploitation was proportional to exploitation rate (Healey 1980). Perch in Windermere maintained stable biomass across varying conditions through altered growth, mortality and fecundity (Craig 1982).

**10. Modelling gaps.** The Ricker stock-recruitment equation and Beverton and Holt yield equation underpin fishery management. Dorazio's (1984) method separating death rates into longevity and other components has been tested only on short-lived invertebrates, and the author knows of no fish application.

**11. Research priorities.** The author calls for long-term quantitative data on natural mortality causes and their partitioning, comparative population studies across habitats and cohorts, and greater attention from physiologists, endocrinologists and biochemists to gerontology in fish, an area of "almost total lack of knowledge."
