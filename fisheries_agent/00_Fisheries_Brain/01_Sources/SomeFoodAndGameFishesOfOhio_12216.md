# Some Food and Game Fishes of Ohio

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #percidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1931 Ohio Division of Wildlife bulletin, authored by [[Edward L. Wickliff]] and [[Milton B. Trautman]] (reprinted through 1962), is a field identification guide for anglers covering fourteen common food and game fishes of Ohio. It includes a descriptive family key, species accounts with distribution, habitat, food, stocking and propagation notes, and state policy on fish propagation and sale. Its central message is that fish stocking succeeds only when matched to each species' habitat requirements; planting fish indiscriminately does not create good fishing.

## Background & Description

The bulletin's stated purpose is to help fishermen identify their catch and to present the basics of fish distribution, habits and habitats. The fourteen species covered are:

- Black basses: [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]]
- Sunfishes and relatives: [[Rock Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Long-ear Sunfish]], [[Green Sunfish]]
- Crappies: [[White Crappie]], [[Black Crappie]]
- Temperate basses and perches: [[White Bass]], [[Yellow Perch]]
- Catfishes: [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Bullhead]]

The key separates six families by dorsal fin structure, anal spine count and mouth position. Centrarchidae (black basses, sunfishes, crappies) is the largest group in the key and the most important to Ohio anglers. Ictaluridae is distinguished by a single dorsal spine, absence of scales and barbels. Cyprinidae (minnows, 48 Ohio species recorded) are described as the principal food of most adult game fishes. The Catostomidae (suckers) include 3 buffaloes, 5 carp-suckers, 1 blue sucker and 14 true suckers. The bulletin also notes 40 spiny-rayed species in the Ohio fauna and 22 darter species in the Percidae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food cycles.** Young fishes first feed on microscopic crustaceans such as water fleas and copepods. Largemouth and Smallmouth Bass shift to midge larvae and pupae at about one inch, then to fish, crayfish and adult insects at several inches. The authors stress that these food cycles differ among species and are essential to understanding natural propagation.

**Habitat partitioning.** The bulletin emphasizes that closely related species often occupy distinct habitats:
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] favors gravel or rock bottoms, boulders and good current, typical of upland streams and limestone bottoms.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] favors weedy, muddy lakes, ponds, bayous and overflow ponds.
- [[Spotted Bass]] occupies quiet, silty lowland pools of southern Ohio streams.
- Although all three may occur in one stream, they "remain separate to a remarkable degree."

**Water quality and lakes.** Reservoir dredging that removed aquatic vegetation and muck produced hard bottoms favored by Smallmouth Bass, while the authors warn that continued dredging could let Smallmouth supplant Largemouth in such areas. [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]] and [[White Crappie]] are associated with lakes, ponds and vegetation, while [[Black Crappie]] prefers cold, clear, weedy water.

**Distribution patterns.** Several species are concentrated in particular regions of Ohio: [[Pumpkinseed]] and [[Yellow Perch]] are largely confined to northern Ohio, [[Long-ear Sunfish]] is most common in the south, and [[Bluegill]] is much less common in southern Ohio, which has almost no lakes.

**Introduced species.** The authors note that, apart from trout, carp and goldfish, there is little evidence that stocking has materially changed the distribution of Ohio's native fishes.

## Key Findings

- **Identification guidance.** The bulletin gives a stepwise descriptive key to six families and detailed diagnostic characters for each species, such as the maxillary extending beyond the eye in Largemouth Bass versus not beyond it in Smallmouth Bass, and the forked tail of Channel Catfish versus the square tail of bullheads.
- **Species size records.** Reported maxima include Smallmouth Bass around five pounds, Largemouth Bass close to nine pounds, White Crappie 2.5 pounds and 17 inches, Black Crappie about one pound and a quarter, Channel Catfish about thirty pounds, and White Bass about three pounds and 18 inches.
- **Spotted Bass recognition.** The Spotted Bass was not formally recognized until 1927. Ohio propagated it for the first time in 1932, and it is described as the dominant bass in extreme southern Ohio.
- **Minnow harvest.** Over 634 million minnows were reported taken by Ohio licensed minnow dealers in 1930, excluding minnows taken by anglers for personal use.
- **Stocking policy.** The authors argue that stocking must reflect each species' habits, habitat and water character. Hatchery-raised larger fish are favored for lower mortality, and the authors state that the number of legal-sized or adult fish caught is the final test of a successful stocking program, not the number of "paper" fish in reports.
- **Propagation rules.** Fish legally taken may be used for propagation, but a $1.00 permit is required to propagate, sell and use live fish for propagation and stocking. The State does not stock private waters, since its funds come from angler licenses. In the Inland Fishing District, only certain species (including gar, carp, catfish, buffalo and suckers) may be sold in unlimited numbers, and catfish sales are limited to a two-day possession limit of 40.
- **Recommended propagation species.** Bluegill and Largemouth Bass are judged better suited to average fish ponds than other species. Most listed species except White Bass are considered suitable for pond culture.
- **Recommended references.** The bulletin recommends two U.S. Bureau of Fisheries documents on pond fish propagation, by M. C. James (Document No. 1056) and by H. S. Davis and A. H. Wiebe (Document No. 1085).
- **Black Bullhead management.** Black Bullheads should be stocked only in sluggish streams and small ponds, since larger Brown and Yellow Bullheads are preferred for big lakes and reservoirs, and Black Bullheads may compete with and displace them.
