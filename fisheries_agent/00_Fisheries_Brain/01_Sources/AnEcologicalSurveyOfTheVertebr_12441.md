# The Fish Population of White Oak Lake, Roane County, Tennessee, 1950–1953

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This report, part of the ORO-587 Ecological Survey of the Vertebrate Fauna of [[White Oak Lake]] and Environs, documents six semi-annual netting studies (August 1950 to April 1953) and a final [[Rotenone]] kill in April 1953. The study estimated fish numbers by species with mark-recapture methods, described age, growth and condition, and examined diet and radionuclide accumulation. Numbers were highly variable between seasons. Bluegills and carp dominated by weight. Several species, notably [[White Crappie]] and [[Northern Redhorse]], declined to near disappearance. The authors attributed much of the instability to sampling selectivity, natural winter losses, and the lake's fertilization and radioactive effluent from [[Oak Ridge National Laboratory]].

## Background & Description

The survey aimed to determine species composition, abundance, growth, condition and reproductive success of the lake's fish. Population size was estimated by [[Petersen Method]] principles, specifically the [[Schnabel Estimate]] used throughout. The authors noted that Schnabel estimates tend to run high, but are adequate when marked fish are a small share of the population. The six assumptions required by [[Ricker]] (1948) were reviewed. Because the lake had no fishery exploitation, marked-fish losses to harvest were absent.

Fish were captured with [[Hoopnet]]s set at up to 14 stations, with five to eight nets fished at a time. Fish above minimum size were marked by fin excision (left pectoral, right pectoral, left pelvic, right pelvic, or dorsal fin, varying by estimate). Size thresholds were: bluegill, black crappie and white crappie 100 mm; largemouth bass 200 mm; carp, goldfish, bullheads and redhorse 150 mm. Gizzard shad were not estimated because they were rarely caught and too fragile to handle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

White Oak Lake receives all treated domestic sewage effluent from Oak Ridge National Laboratory. The authors judged its fertility and productivity higher than most regional lakes, comparable to a 35-acre water body serving about 3,000 people. Heavy siltation occurred after impoundment in 1943. Water was turbid in 1950, clearer during later studies. Rooted aquatic plants were virtually absent, which the authors partly attributed to carp. Carp roiling was also suggested to impair sight-feeding fish such as [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Black Crappie]] and [[Bluegill]]. Fish in the lake were continuously exposed to low-level external and internal radiation.

Waterfowl stopover and wintering were recorded, and an ice cover of about one inch persisted for roughly five weeks in winter 1950–51.

## Key Findings

**Population estimates (sum of species estimates, fish large enough to be netted):**

| Study | Total estimate | Notable species |
|---|---|---|
| Fall 1950 | 17,360 | Bluegill 6,100; Carp 6,450; Black Crappie 2,850 |
| Spring 1951 | 13,505 | Bluegill 5,600; Carp 4,830; Black Crappie 2,200 |
| Fall 1951 | 31,090 | Bluegill 22,000; Black Crappie 5,100 |
| Spring 1952 | 12,275 | Bluegill 6,500; Black Crappie 2,700 |
| Fall 1952 | Not estimated | Catch too low; bluegill and carp estimates impossible |
| Spring 1953 | Not estimated | Carp estimate judged unreliable (epidemic) |

- The pooled-data total for fall 1950 was 13,050; treating species separately gave about 31.9 percent more individuals.
- Bluegills fell about 8 percent over winter 1950–51, and black crappies about 22.8 percent.
- Carp fell about 25 percent over winter 1950–51.

**Species-level results:**

- **Bluegill:** Abundant. Estimates ranged from 1,750 (spring 1953) to 22,000 (fall 1951). Year classes 1947 to 1951 contributed.
- **Black Crappie:** About twice as vulnerable to hoopnetting as bluegill. Only nine fish were four years old, about 0.06 percent of 1,128 individuals scaled.
- **White Crappie:** Declined from about 1,200 (fall 1950) to 6 marked fish (spring 1953), and none were recovered in the rotenone study.
- **Largemouth Bass:** Estimated 130–140 in fall 1950 (about 4 per acre). Of 200 adults jaw-tagged in March 1950, only two were recaptured, so the stocking was judged unsuccessful.
- **Carp:** Largest by total weight. In spring 1953, more than one-fourth of netted carp were dead from an ulcerative infection.
- **Yellow Bullhead:** Estimates rose from 250 (fall 1950) to 875 (fall 1951), then fell to 160 (spring 1953).
- **Northern Redhorse:** Declined steadily from 375 (fall 1950) to 23 new marks (spring 1953). No recruitment occurred after the 1949 year class.
- **Gizzard Shad:** A heavy winter die-off in 1950–51 removed a large year class. Young shad were prominent after 1951, and 231 were netted in spring 1953.

**Rotenone study (April 1953):**

- 255,261 fish were handled; 210,938 were gizzard shad.
- Recovery was about 60 percent of marked fish overall (bluegill 66.2 percent, black crappie 63.9 percent).
- Corrected estimate: about 391,519 fish weighing 35,420 lb, or about 10,915 fish per acre weighing 987.5 lb over 35.87 acres.
- Fish small enough to escape the nets made up 88.73 percent of the number recovered but 58.69 percent of the weight.

**Age and growth:**

- Scale analysis (1,896 samples) showed [[Lee's Phenomenon]]: calculated early-year lengths decreased in older year classes.
- Black crappies grew more slowly than those in [[Norris Reservoir]] and the Hiwassee Reservoir. Maximum age was about three to four years, roughly 25–30 percent shorter life span than nearby impoundments.
- Bluegills reached age five; largemouth bass and redhorse grew more slowly than in nearby reservoirs.
- Gizzard shad grew fastest, reaching about 9.5 inches by the end of the first year.

**Condition and diet:**

- Bluegills were heavier per length than either crappie. Black crappies were heavier than white crappies.
- Black crappie diet was dominated by [[Chaoborus]] larvae, with copepods, cladocerans and Tendipedidae midge larvae also important. Bluegills were omnivorous, consuming filamentous algae, vascular plants, terrestrial insects and midge larvae.

**Radionuclide accumulation:**

- Concentrations in fish tissues varied seasonally, rising with water temperature above about 55 °F and peaking near 80 °F in August 1952.
- Bone-concentration factors were nearly 20,000:1 for black crappie and more than 30,000:1 for bluegill (relative to lake water).
- Hard tissues mainly held strontium and phosphorus; soft tissues mainly held cesium-137.
- Waterfowl that fed at the lake carried higher radionuclide loads than birds that used it as a sanctuary. The authors estimated a minimum body burden of at least 5 microcuries for the average feeding bird.
- A muskrat from the White Oak Creek area developed an osteogenic sarcoma after accumulating very high radiostrontium levels in its skeleton.

**Waterfowl banding (1952–1953):**

- 449 birds were banded, including 390 mallards, 137 wood ducks and 96 black ducks.
- The authors estimated more than 6,500 waterfowl visited the lake. Banded mallards were recovered in Kentucky, Alabama and Tennessee, suggesting wintering within about 100 miles of the lake.

**Conclusions:**

- The netting data were highly selective and sometimes failed to produce usable estimates, especially in fall 1952 and spring 1953.
- Seasonal decline over winter appeared to be a normal feature of fish population dynamics, consistent with Indiana pond experiments showing roughly 20 percent average winter loss.
- Several species disappeared or declined sharply, and the authors concluded that the lake's fish community was becoming dominated by bluegill and gizzard shad under sewage-enriched, silty, and radioactive conditions.
