# Significance and Origin of Big Rivers

#sedimentology #geology #big-rivers #paleodrainage

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Paul Edwin Potter]] (H. N. Fisk Laboratory of Sedimentology, University of Cincinnati) argues that ancient [[Big Rivers]] are an overlooked theme in Earth history. The author proposes that large river systems can be identified in the geologic record using a combination of [[Facies Mapping]], [[Paleocurrent Analysis]], [[Unconformity Mapping]], and regional [[Tectonic History]] analysis. Big river systems tend to follow structural lows such as [[Rift Valley|Rifts]], [[Aulacogen|Aulacogens]], and [[Geofracture Systems]], and their longevity depends largely on the tectonic and marine history of their drainage basins. The paper is a review and argument rather than a field dataset, and it builds on the author's study of modern big-river sands as a petrologic and chemical baseline for interpreting ancient sandstones.

## Background & Description

Sandstone is genetically tied to continental erosion and is found in nearly all depositional environments. The author notes that sandstones are among the oldest preserved rocks, with examples in Greenland dated at 3.7 billion years. Despite this, the petrology and chemistry of modern big-river sands have been much less studied than ancient sandstones or sedimentary structures.

The study has four stated goals for modern big-river sands:

1. Provide a standard of comparison for ancient sandstones.
2. Improve theories of the mineralogical and chemical composition of ancient sandstones, which have largely been built on ancient rather than modern samples.
3. Help geochemists estimate the chemical mass balance between continental erosion and oceanic sedimentation.
4. Give metamorphic petrologists a benchmark for the pre-metamorphic composition of high-rank quartzofeldspathic rocks.

**Defining a big river.** A big river may have a large drainage basin, a long course, a large sediment load, or a large fluid discharge. Because paleodischarge is hard to estimate for ancient rivers, the author emphasizes drainage area, sand composition, and facies patterns. Drainage area and length are used as proxies. For the world's 50 largest rivers, all but one exceed 1,000 km in length. A 1,000 km stream would, on average, drain about 100,000 km². These 50 rivers drain about 47% of the continents (excluding Greenland and Antarctica). The five largest basins (Amazon, Congo, Mississippi, Nile, Yenisei) together account for about 10% of continental area, and the Amazon alone for about 5%.

**Delta and fan associations.** Big rivers are commonly associated with a delta, a submarine canyon, and an abyssal fan. The Ganges-Brahmaputra system, with its combined deltas and a Bay of Bengal submarine fan over 2,000 km long, is a modern example. The author cites [[Moore and Asquith]] (1971) for the definition of a delta as a subaerial and submerged sediment mass deposited primarily by river action.

**Mineral and petroleum significance.** Deltas, submarine fans, and canyon fills contain a large share of the world's petroleum reserves. Many major coal basins are associated with rivers. The author also notes a shift in mineral-deposit research toward stratiform copper and sulfide deposits in sedimentary basins, which invites analysis of their paleodrainage systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Controls on basin fill.** The presence or absence of a big river strongly changes how a basin fills:

- A rift far from a big river may fill with marginal alluvial fans and lake deposits, or, if marine, with evaporites.
- A big river draining a well-watered, high-relief hinterland into a rift produces a large terrigenous volume, typically a major delta.
- Large mud volumes can inhibit carbonate-producing organisms and reduce coastal wave power by building gently sloping shorelines.

**Global sediment contribution.** The ten largest rivers (Yellow, Ganges, Brahmaputra, Yangtze, Indus, Amazon, Mississippi, Irrawaddy, Mekong, Colorado, and Red) supply about 35% of the suspended load carried to the ocean (Drake 1976). The author assumes a comparable role for big rivers in the past, provided continents were as well watered and as high as today.

**Coastline type and river density.** Inman and Nordstrom (1971) found that the 28 largest rivers discharge across trailing-edge or marginal-sea coasts, where 25 of the world's largest deltas also occur. The exception is the Columbia, which is 27th in size and drains a collision coast. The author attributes this to the asymmetry of continental watersheds, with high orogenic belts on collision sides, and to low wave power in marginal seas.

**Tectonic setting and aulacogens.** Rifting is thought to cause most aulacogens, which are long, narrow, deeply filled troughs extending into cratons. A rift or fault system at an angle to the coast tends to focus paleodrainage, as with the Niger, Amazon, Paraná, and Mississippi. Plume-generated triple junctions, following [[Burke and Dewey]] (1973), are proposed as another control: the failed arm of a triple junction can become a rift that localizes a major river and delta, as with the Niger.

**Variable river longevity.** The author defines the age of a river system as the earliest date at which a continuing, persistent river occupied the region. A system can begin after a marine regression, a tectonic uplift, the waning of an ice sheet, or the end of lava outpourings. It can end through marine inundation, new tectonic deformation, glaciation, lava flows, or a shift in rainfall. Systems that persist can still have major interruptions.

## Key Findings

**1. Identification methods.** Ancient big-river systems can be reconstructed using four combined tools: facies mapping (including turbidites of submarine fans), paleocurrent analysis (crossbedding orientation), mapping of unconformities (structure, isopach, and paleogeologic maps), and regional tectonic analysis. The author argues these methods are available to anyone and apply wherever sedimentary basins remain substantially intact, which is common on cratons. Such river systems may be traced back at least into the middle Precambrian and perhaps earlier.

**2. Tectonic control.** Big rivers tend to follow rifts, abortive aulacogens, geofracture systems, broad lows between arches, intermontane longitudinal faults, structural reentrants and salients, and major fold trends. Structural reentrants and longitudinal intramontane faults channel debris away from uplifted orogenic zones. Post-orogenic molasse rivers are commonly short-lived, perhaps several million years or less, but can run 1,000–2,000 km.

**3. Longevity case: the Michigan-Mississippi River.** The Michigan-Mississippi river system, reconstructed by Swann (1963) through facies and crossbedding studies, has flowed south and southwestward across the North American craton since the early Carboniferous. It persisted for about 250,000,000 years, roughly one-sixteenth of Earth history. Deltas of this system range from thin and widespread on the craton to thick and stable where the Mississippi enters the Gulf of Mexico on oceanic crust. Delta migration during a single Chesterian cycle reached about 1,000 km, with lateral shifts up to 350 km. Its paleochannels follow the pre-Pennsylvanian unconformity, and the present river follows the Mesozoic–Cenozoic fill of the Mississippi Embayment, which is linked to a rift that began in the late Precambrian.

**4. Tertiary delta alignments.** Fisher and McGowen (1967) mapped seven small Tertiary deltas along the Texas Gulf Coast hinge line. These deltas lie almost exactly beneath present south-draining rivers. Where ancient rivers prograded into areas of either rapid subsidence or deep water, the deposits show vertical persistence of facies through time. A long-lived river on a stable craton, by contrast, can migrate across distances and contribute to the fill of several basins.

**5. Remote sources and the Canadian example.** McMillian (1973) reconstructed a Tertiary paleodrainage system across central and eastern Canada. Its source may have been several thousand kilometers from the basin margin, so sand composition in a basin may have little relation to neighboring rocks. Lakes between source and basin, such as Lake Winnipeg, can act as sediment traps.

**6. Gondwana-era drainage in southwestern Africa.** Martin (1975) mapped a paleodrainage system from outcrops in southwestern Africa, with Paleozoic valleys up to 1,000 m deep later filled by glacial deposits and Lower Cretaceous basalts. The system flowed toward the present African coast, suggesting a proto-South Atlantic in the later Paleozoic.

**7. Rio Grande rift control.** The Rio Grande in New Mexico closely follows a Miocene rift, which the author considers most likely related to a preexisting crustal flaw. The river's course is thus controlled by tectonics even though the rift is younger than the river's large-scale drainage pattern.

**8. Four morphologic types.** Modern big rivers fall into four patterns based on their relation to tectonics: marginal to a large craton (Amazon, Mississippi), marginal to a fold belt and parallel to it (Ganges, Paraná), along the strike of a mountain chain (Mekong, Magdalena), or superimposed across several mountain chains (Columbia, Danube). A fifth type, a river on a large craton without bordering mountains, was probably more common when relief was low.

**9. Basin area versus drainage area.** Large cratonic rivers that discharge onto oceanic crust at continental margins probably fill thick, elongate basins whose area is perhaps only 5–10% of the drainage basin. Intracratonic basins supplied by big rivers are likely a fraction of their watershed size.

**10. Flysch and molasse contrast.** Kuenen (1958) noted that flysch basins are usually filled with fine to medium sands and shales, which suggests distant, moderate-relief sources. Molasse basins contain coarser gravels and conglomerates, which suggests proximal, high-relief sources. Subaerial deltas and estuaries can trap coarse debris before it reaches basins.

**11. Pre-Devonian vegetation and sand maturity.** Schumm (1968) argued that before land plants, runoff was more episodic and floods carried sand from unvegetated slopes, producing compositionally immature sands. The author notes that the pre-Devonian record instead contains mineralogically mature and supermature sandstones, such as the St. Peter and Simpson sandstones (Ordovician) of North America. This points to either accelerated weathering under higher CO₂, or other sources of maturity, and remains an open question.

**12. Speculations on super-rivers and early Earth.** The author considers whether Pangaea could have supported rivers larger than the Amazon, because a large, well-watered land mass with active fold belts might suit "super-Amazons." Meyerhoff (1970) argued that such a continent would be largely arid. The author suggests that relief and mountain position relative to marine air masses matter more than size alone. On early Earth, Hadding (1929) envisioned the first rains falling on an ash-covered, hot surface, producing big flash-flood rivers. The author notes that Mars shows channels of fluvial origin, interpreted by Sharp and Malin (1975) as about 3–3.5 billion years old, which may resemble early terrestrial landscapes better than the rock record does.
