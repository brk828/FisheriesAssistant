# Field Tests of Stainless Steel and Tantalum Wire with Disk Tags on Striped Bass

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #fish-tagging #fisheries-research

## Executive Summary

This 1954 study by the California Department of Fish and Game evaluated tantalum and stainless steel as alternative materials for fish tagging wires on [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*). Previous tagging programs using monel, nickel, and silver had proven unsatisfactory for marine and anadromous fishes. A total of 1,211 striped bass were tagged in 1951 with [[Petersen Disk Tags]] using three wire types: tantalum, type 302 medium soft temper stainless steel, and type 302 passivated dead soft stainless steel. The study demonstrated that both tantalum and stainless steel provided significantly improved tag retention compared to earlier materials, with tags returned after up to 1,005 days at large. Stainless steel was recommended as the preferred material due to lower cost and comparable performance to tantalum.

## Background & Description

[[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*) is an anadromous species that was the subject of extensive tagging studies in California during the early 1950s. The fish in this study ranged from 10 to 39 inches in fork length, representing a broad size distribution of the population.

The tagging methodology employed [[Petersen Disk Tags]] consisting of cellulose nitrate disks one-half inch in diameter and 0.045 inch thick. Three types of wire were tested, all 0.032 inch in diameter:
- Tantalum wire (used with disk serial numbers ending in 1, 4, and 7)
- Type 302 medium soft temper stainless steel (numbers ending in 2, 5, and 8)
- Type 302 passivated dead soft stainless steel (numbers ending in 0, 3, 6, and 9)

All fish were tagged on the [[Caudal Peduncle]] rather than beneath the dorsal fin, which had been the standard location in earlier studies. Fish were captured using [[Gill Nets]] during April and May 1951 in California waters.

Previous tagging materials including monel, nickel, and silver had proven inadequate for marine and anadromous fishes, with few or no tags returned after 18 months. Earlier studies by Merriman (1941) using nickel pins achieved 21% return with maximum 608 days at large; Clark (1936) reported 10% return with maximum 477 days; and Morgan and Gerlach (1950) achieved 13% return with maximum 128 days elapsed time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Striped Bass]] are anadromous fish that migrate between freshwater and marine environments, subjecting tagging materials to varying salinity conditions and potential corrosion. The species' behavior and habitat use patterns make tag retention a critical consideration for population studies and fisheries management.

The study area was California waters where striped bass populations support both commercial and recreational fisheries. The tagging program was designed to evaluate fishing mortality, requiring tags that would remain attached for at least one year to provide meaningful data on population dynamics.

Environmental factors affecting tag retention include:
- Salinity variations between freshwater spawning areas and marine feeding grounds
- Physical abrasion from the fish's movement through various habitats
- Biological factors such as tissue growth and healing around the tag attachment point
- Corrosion and degradation of materials in the aquatic environment

The [[San Francisco Striped Bass Club]] and [[Bethel Island Rod and Gun Club]] conducted publicity programs offering prizes ($100 yearly prize and other awards) to encourage tag returns, demonstrating the importance of angler cooperation in tagging studies.

## Key Findings

**Tag Return Rates and Retention:**
- Total of 1,211 striped bass tagged during April-May 1951
- 204 tags returned (16.85% overall return rate) by February 1, 1954
- Elapsed time between tagging and recovery: 0 to 1,005 days
- Mean elapsed time: 225 days for combined returns
- Maximum retention period of 1,005 days represents significant improvement over earlier studies

**Comparative Performance by Wire Type:**
- Tantalum: 363 fish tagged, 63 returns (17.36%)
- Medium soft stainless steel: 483 fish tagged, 84 returns (17.39%)
- Dead soft stainless steel: 365 fish tagged, 57 returns (15.62%)
- Analysis of variance showed no statistically significant differences between wire types in total returns or mean days at large

**Returns by Time Period:**
- Year 1: 164 returns (13.5% of total tagged)
- Year 2 (366-730 days): 35 returns (2.9%)
- Year 3 (over 730 days): 5 returns (0.4%)
- Pattern of returns essentially the same for all three metals across time periods

**Mean Days at Large by Wire Type:**
- Tantalum: 246 days (SD ± 189.5)
- Dead soft stainless steel: 201 days (SD ± 171.1)
- Medium soft stainless steel: 236 days (SD ± 194.4)

**Size-Related Differences:**
- Statistically significant differences (5% level) in returns for shorter length classes
- Medium soft stainless steel showed fewer returns of smaller fish compared to tantalum and dead soft stainless steel
- Larger fish showed opposite trend (more returns with medium soft steel) but did not reach statistical significance
- Explanation: Medium soft stainless steel less flexible, more likely to cut through soft flesh of smaller fish; more difficult to tie with appropriate slack for small fish
- Tantalum and dead soft stainless steel break more readily with repeated bending, potentially explaining fewer returns of larger fish

**Material Performance Characteristics:**
- Medium soft stainless steel: Less flexible, more difficult to tie, more resistant to breaking with repeated bending
- Tantalum and dead soft stainless steel: More flexible, easier to tie with appropriate slack, break more readily with repeated bending
- Both tantalum and stainless steel showed considerable improvement over monel and nickel used in pre-1951 California experiments

**Recommendation:**
Stainless steel preferred over tantalum due to lower cost and similar performance, meeting the objective of developing tags remaining attached for at least one year to permit evaluation of fishing mortality.

**Comparison with Historical Studies:**
- Current study: 16.85% return, maximum 1,005 days
- Merriman (1941): 21% return, maximum 608 days (nickel pins, dorsal fin location)
- Clark (1936): 10% return, maximum 477 days (nickel pins, dorsal fin location)
- Morgan & Gerlach (1950): 13% return, maximum 128 days (nickel pins)
- Pre-1951 California studies: Few or no returns after 18 months (monel/nickel wire, dorsal fin location)

The study successfully demonstrated that [[Caudal Peduncle]] tagging with stainless steel or tantalum wire provides superior long-term tag retention compared to dorsal fin tagging with nickel-based materials, achieving the research objective of one-year minimum retention for fisheries mortality studies.
