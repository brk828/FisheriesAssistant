# Physical Changes in the Gunnison and Colorado Rivers from Construction of the Aspinall Unit, with Hypotheses to Assess Effects on the Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1991 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final report, prepared by [[Charles W. McAda]] and [[Lynn R. Kaeding]] of the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]], quantifies how the [[Aspinall Unit]] and related reservoirs changed the physical environment of the [[Gunnison River]] and upper [[Colorado River]], and proposes hypotheses and a five-year study plan to evaluate the effects on four federally endangered fishes. The dominant change is a sharp reduction of spring runoff, with peak discharge and May–June flows falling substantially. Water temperature is essentially unchanged in the Colorado River but reduced in the Gunnison River, and sediment loads have declined. The authors propose test flows from Blue Mesa Reservoir that mimic the natural hydrograph, with higher spring peaks and lower fall and winter flows.

## Background & Description

The report covers four endangered natives of the upper basin: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]]. The authors attribute their decline mainly to human-induced alteration of flow regimes, along with introduced fishes and watershed management problems.

**Water development history**
- Private irrigation in the Gunnison basin began in the 1880s, with about 180,000 acres irrigated by 1906 and consumptive use estimated at 229,000 AF per year.
- The [[Redlands Diversion]] (1918, 750 cfs water right) blocks upstream fish passage on the lower Gunnison River.
- The [[Gunnison Tunnel]] (1909) diverts water to the Uncompahgre Valley; annual diversions average about 559,000 AF.
- The Aspinall Unit ([[Blue Mesa Reservoir]] 1966, [[Morrow Point Reservoir]] 1970, [[Crystal Reservoir]] 1976) was built for storage for Colorado River Compact deliveries, hydropower, flood control and recreation. It was completed before the [[Endangered Species Act]], so its fish effects were not considered at construction.
- Related projects include [[Dallas Creek Project]] (Ridgeway Reservoir, 1987) and [[Dolores Project]] (McPhee Reservoir, 1984), which were built with a stipulation that Aspinall water partly offset their effects.

**Species accounts**
- Colorado Squawfish: Historically common in the upper Colorado River (Jordan 1891). Numbers in the 1970s were probably a product of increased sampling effort, and populations had likely declined before quantitative collection began. A remnant population may persist in the Gunnison above Redlands Dam.
- Razorback Sucker: Once abundant. A combined 284 fish were collected near Grand Junction in 1974–1976, but very few were collected in the late 1980s. Only 3 were taken in intensive 1989–1990 sampling in the Colorado River.
- Humpback Chub: Described in 1946; populations at Black Rocks, Westwater Canyon and Cataract Canyon are small, disjunct but relatively stable.
- Bonytail: Extremely rare; historical records are confused with roundtail chub before 1970. Nineteen tentatively identified specimens were collected in Cataract Canyon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Streamflow was compared at two long-term USGS gages: Gunnison River at Whitewater (from 1917) and Colorado River at Cisco (from 1914). Periods were defined as early development, middle development, and post-Aspinall (1966–1989). Water temperature was modeled with the [[SNTEMP Model]], using a "worst case" pre-dam adjustment of −6 °C (March, April, May, September) and −11 °C (June–August) at the Gunnison Tunnel, based on 1964–1965 thermograph data and 1979–1980 comparisons. Sediment analysis drew on Elliott and DeFeyter (1986) and Thompson (1984).

**Water temperature.** The Aspinall Unit lowered Gunnison temperatures in summer and raised them in winter. Modeled differences reached a maximum of about 2 °C at river mile 20 and were smaller at the mouth. Largest modeled summer differences were about −1.9 °C (June 16–30 at RM 2 and −2.13 °C at RM 20). Colorado River temperatures at RM 49 and RM 136 differed from estimated pre-Aspinall values by at most 0.5 °C.

**Sediment.** The Gunnison suspended-sediment load declined after Blue Mesa closure (mean 0.928 T/AF before, 0.688 T/AF after), and year-to-year variability was reduced. Thompson's double-mass analysis attributed a slope break in the Colorado River at Cisco to Aspinall. Reduced sediment has contributed to armored substrates in the Gunnison.

**Streamflow.** Spring runoff is the main change. Maximum-annual discharge fell about 47% in the Gunnison and 37% in the Colorado between early and post-Aspinall periods. Aspinall is responsible for an estimated 75–85% of the decrease in spring runoff in the Colorado at Cisco. Flows in winter more than doubled in the Gunnison in some months.

**Ecological consequences.** Reduced spring flows simplify channels: side channels and backwaters silt in, sandbars are colonized by tamarisk, and gravel-cobble spawning substrate is armored or embedded. High flows also create flooded bottomlands used by spawning and rearing fishes, and they appear to suppress non-native species.

## Key Findings

- **Spring runoff reduction:** Mean-monthly flows in the Gunnison fell 25.1% in May (1,864 cfs) and 32.2% in June (2,326 cfs) between the middle-development and post-Aspinall periods. Corresponding Colorado River reductions were 2,477 and 2,778 cfs.
- **Temperature:** Gunnison temperatures were reduced, which may have hurt Colorado Squawfish spawning success. Colorado River temperatures were essentially unchanged.
- **Sediment:** Loads declined in both rivers, but the effect on endangered fishes is unclear.
- **Squawfish reproduction:** Age-0 abundance in the Colorado River was highest after peak flows near 33,000–38,000 cfs at the stateline gage (1985–1986). Hypothesis 1 predicts maximum reproductive success at 30,000–40,000 cfs.
- **Non-native fishes:** Fathead minnow, red shiner and sand shiner increased after low runoff years. High runoff may limit them, though it cannot eliminate them.
- **Razorback reproduction:** Razorbacks spawn during high spring flows, but the main channel is often below 15 °C while hatching success peaks at 20 °C. The authors hypothesize that flooded off-channel areas, which reached 22 °C when the main channel was 13 °C, are the historical spawning sites. Dike building and channelization have reduced these areas.
- **Redlands Diversion:** It blocks access to about 55 miles of apparently usable habitat. A remnant Colorado Squawfish population may exist above it. Removal or fish passage is recommended.
- **Test flow regime:** The proposed five-year test flows peak in the Gunnison at 2,000–5,000 cfs (one year), 5,000–10,000 cfs (one year), above 12,000 cfs (two years) and above 15,000 cfs (one year), with peaks between May 15 and June 15. Fall, winter and summer flows are drawn from the low end of the recent observed range.
- **Study design:** Ten hypotheses (reproduction, over-winter survival, non-native control, Gunnison fish survey, radiotagging, habitat, razorback augmentation, flooded habitat mapping, sediment and channel morphology, humpback chub reproduction) are scheduled over five years. Use of unallocated Blue Mesa storage is proposed.
