## Executive Summary

This 1973 study by Stalnaker and Holden (Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit, Utah State University) documents the historical and post-impoundment distribution of seven indigenous large-river fish species endemic to the upper Colorado River Basin, with a focus on the [[Green River System]]. The research was commissioned following a review of the Green River Fish Manipulation Program (a rotenone treatment) and was designed to assess environmental and faunal changes resulting from the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962. Field studies spanned 1963–1966 and resumed in 1968 through the time of publication. The paper concludes that the construction of large dams and reservoirs (Flaming Gorge Dam, Glen Canyon Dam/Lake Powell) has caused severe range contraction and population decline among native large-river endemics, with introduced trout replacing native fishes in newly cold-water reaches. Only three refugia—the [[Yampa River]], the Canyonlands section of the Green/Colorado rivers, and [[Desolation Canyon]]—were identified as still capable of supporting reproducing populations of these endemic species.

## Background & Description

The seven indigenous large-river species discussed exhibit distinct morphological adaptations to historically swift, turbid, warm-water riverine environments:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): North America's largest native minnow (Cyprinidae); historically attained weights exceeding 50 lbs.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) and **[[Humpback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Both possess an abruptly humped back, hypothesized as an adaptation for swift-water hydrodynamics.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): Streamlined body morphology adapted for swift-water conditions; was formerly considered a subspecies of *Gila robusta* until systematic revision in the late 1960s (Holden and Stalnaker, 1970).
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*): A more robust minnow body form, characteristic of upper-basin populations.
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** (*Catostomus latipinnis*): Most common endemic; comprises the greatest percentage of total native fish biomass in the system.
- **[[Bluehead Sucker]]** (*Catostomus discobolus*): Occurs as two distinct polymorphs in the Colorado Basin — a narrow-peduncled form and a wide-peduncled form.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Colorado River System historically consisted of large, warm-water, turbid rivers running through steep desert canyons with torrential rapids and highly variable flow/turbidity — a hydrological regime that shaped a fish fauna with one of the highest degrees of endemism of any North American river system. Endemics fell into two ecological categories:

1. **Small-bodied endemics** restricted to limited ranges (often single springs) in the lower Colorado Basin.
2. **Large-river endemics** occupying mainstem habitats throughout the basin, particularly the [[Green River System]] — the seven species profiled in this study.

Post-dam ecological shifts:
- Below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] to the mouth of the [[Yampa River]], seasonal flow fluctuation decreased, daily fluctuation increased, summer water temperatures dropped, winter temperatures rose, and turbidity was greatly reduced.
- These altered conditions favored introduced **rainbow trout** and **brown trout**, which displaced native large-river fishes in the newly cold, clear tailwater reach.
- The Yampa River, an unregulated tributary, retained natural flow/temperature/turbidity characteristics and acted as a buffering refuge, moderating impacts on the Green River downstream of its confluence.
- Only the flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and roundtail chub were recorded above the Yampa confluence pre-impoundment, and none were known to reproduce there even before dam closure.
- Three areas were identified as the last remaining ecologically suitable refugia for reproducing populations of large-river endemics: the **[[Yampa River]]**, the **Green and Colorado Rivers in the Canyonlands region**, and the **[[Desolation Canyon]]** reach of the middle Green River.

## Key Findings

- **Trout replacement**: Rainbow and brown trout have functionally replaced native large-river fishes in the cold tailwater reach between Flaming Gorge Dam and the Yampa River confluence (Vanicek, Kramer & Franklin, 1970).
- **Growth rate decline**: Growth rates of Colorado squawfish, roundtail chub, and bonytail chub declined markedly following dam closure (Vanicek and Kramer, 1969).
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]**: Most widespread and abundant native species; found at all collection sites, reproducing successfully basin-wide except in cold water below Flaming Gorge Dam.
- **[[Bluehead Sucker]]**: Found at all sampling sites and reproducing throughout the upper basin; comparatively rare in the lower Green River near Canyonlands National Park.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: Found in reduced numbers at all Yampa River stations and in the Green/Colorado rivers down to Lake Powell; young-of-year recorded only from the Yampa River, Dinosaur National Monument reach of the Green, Canyonlands, and Desolation Canyon; abundance markedly lower than pre-1960s levels; listed as "rare and endangered" (USDI, 1968).
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]**: Present and reproducing in the upper Green and Yampa rivers plus tributaries (White River, Little Snake River).
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Declining toward rarity; restricted to mainstem rivers; reproducing successfully only within [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (Yampa and Green rivers); a few adults observed in Desolation Canyon and Canyonlands.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]**: One of the rarest Colorado Basin endemics; known only from canyon reaches of the Yampa and Green rivers in Dinosaur National Monument and Desolation Canyon; listed as "rare and endangered" (USDI, 1968).
- **[[Humpback Sucker]]**: Found in limited numbers in the Yampa and Green rivers, but almost exclusively as large adults, indicating minimal reproductive success basin-wide; abundance has declined considerably over the 20th century.
- **Overall conservation status**: Four of the seven species — humpback chub, Colorado squawfish, bonytail chub, and humpback sucker — are classified as rare and potentially endangered, with only three refugial areas remaining suitable for sustaining reproducing populations in the upper Colorado River Basin.
