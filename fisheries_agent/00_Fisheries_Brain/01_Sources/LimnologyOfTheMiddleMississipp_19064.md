# Life Histories of Aquatic Diptera in Floodplain Lakes of the Middle Mississippi River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #diptera

## Executive Summary

This 1956 study from the [[Illinois Academy of Science]] reports observations on the life histories of several aquatic dipteran (midge and phantom midge) larvae collected from five shallow lakes on the leveed floodplain of the Mississippi River near Quincy, Illinois. Dredge samples were taken at roughly monthly intervals from August 1949 to July 1951, and larval length-frequency distributions were used to infer life cycles and brood structure. The paper is a component of a broader limnological study of these lakes. Its scope is invertebrate benthos; no fish are discussed.

## Background & Description

The five study lakes occupied depressions formed by stream action on the floodplain. They were very shallow, with seasonal depths of roughly one to four feet, muddy bottoms, and no submerged or floating aquatic plants. Morphometric data (see Table 1 in the source) include:

- **Big Lake:** 56 acres, maximum length 6770 ft, depth 14-48 in.
- **Goose Lake:** 63 acres, maximum length 5030 ft, depth 24-78 in.
- **Hagerbamer Lake:** 24 acres, maximum length 1770 ft, depth 11-36 in.
- **Mud Lake:** 39 acres, maximum length 4660 ft, depth 12-65 in.
- **Sand Lake:** 42 acres, maximum length 4400 ft, depth 16-60 in.

The work was drawn from a [[Doctoral Thesis]] at the [[University of Illinois]] under Dr. Max R. Matteson, and is companion to a general limnology report on the same lakes. The author thanks Dr. Selwyn S. Roback for verifying larval identities. Larvae passing a 40-mesh-per-inch screen were not retained, which biases small-size records.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sampling method:** [[Dredge Sampling]] at stations near the middle of each lake, with body length measured and length-frequency analysis as the main tool.
- **Benthic role:** Chironomid and chaoborid larvae formed an important part of the bottom fauna; their seasonal cycles drive fluctuations in benthic abundance.
- **Comparative literature:** The paper draws on prior work from [[Lake Minnetonka]] (Minnesota), [[Douglas Lake]] (Michigan), [[Lake Mendota]] (Wisconsin), [[Linsley Pond]] (Connecticut), [[Lake Providence]] (Louisiana), and Esrom Lake (Denmark).
- **Plankton:** No [[Chaoborus]] larvae were found in plankton collections, although the larvae are occasionally limnetic.
- **Fish:** Fish are not addressed in the document.

## Key Findings

**1. *Coelotanypus concinnus* (Chironomidae: Tanypodinae)**
- Occurred occasionally in small numbers in all lakes; a Goose Lake series indicated a two-year life cycle.
- Size range 3 to 11 mm; larvae pupated at 10 to 11 mm.
- Two size classes coexisted at any time (Class I to V were recognised across cohorts), with progressive movement into larger size intervals over two years, supporting a two-year cycle.

**2. *Pelopia* (= *Tanypus*) sp. (Chironomidae)**
- Unnamed species, referred to as Tanypus species B by Malloch (1915).
- Present in open-water bottom fauna from late May to September; absent from open-water bottom during the rest of the year, though found near shore in winter.
- Bimodal distribution in June; pupal respiratory horns appeared only in late summer. Likely a two-year cycle, with small larvae carrying over.

**3. *Procladius* spp. (Chironomidae: Chironominae)**
- Populations typically peaked in March or April and declined to a low in early summer.
- A second summer peak was followed by an October decrease from adult emergence, indicating two broods or populations per year.
- Provisionally identified species: *P. culiciformis* (probably more common) and *P. nr. adumbratus*, based on a few pupae. Species separation among larvae is unreliable.
- Earlier work reported two generations per year (Wood 1938 at Lake Minnetonka; Moffett 1943 at Douglas Lake).

**4. *Chaoborus punctipennis* (Chaoboridae; formerly *Corethra*)**
- Important bottom-fauna component with pronounced seasonal fluctuations.
- Recurrent midwinter declines followed by sharp spring increases in February to April across several lakes (e.g., Big Lake April 1950 and 1952; Goose Lake January 1950 and February 1951).
- The author argues the winter decline is too widespread to be a sampling artefact and that the spring increase is not explained by migration (Deevey 1941) or by late-winter hatching alone.
- Hagerbamer Lake data: small (about 4 mm) larvae were 4.8% of the sample on April 20, 1952, and were also present on March 14 (2.2%) and November 24, 1951 (7%), suggesting late-summer hatching rather than new winter hatching.
- Pupae were last found on September 26, 1949, at Goose Lake; most emergence ended by mid-September, when water temperature was still about 15°C or higher (probably near 20°C).

**Conclusions:** Each taxon displays a distinct seasonal and generational pattern in these shallow lakes. The study notes that the interrelations among *Procladius* species and the origin of the spring *Chaoborus* increase require further research.
