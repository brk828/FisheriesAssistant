# Distribution, Density, and Biomass of the Asiatic Clam (*Corbicula*) in Roosevelt and Apache Lakes, Arizona

#malacology #freshwater-biology #corbicula

## Executive Summary

This 1974 *The Nautilus* paper reports horizontal and vertical distribution, density, size, and biomass of the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula*) in two central Arizona reservoirs, [[Roosevelt Lake]] and [[Apache Lake]], and in the [[Phoenix Canal]]. Quantitative quadrat and dredge sampling, supplemented by fish-predation and water-quality observations, indicate that clam numbers and biomass vary strongly with substrate, depth, distance from inflows, and harvesting pressure. The author concludes that food supply, rather than turbidity alone, is likely the more limiting factor for these populations.

## Background & Description

*Corbicula* is an exotic bivalve in North American freshwater systems. The study area is the lower Salt River reservoir chain in central Arizona. Roosevelt Lake is more turbid and carries more phytoplankton, and Apache Lake is the clearer, lower reservoir. Both reservoirs have rock-rubble and sand-gravel shorelines, which the author describes as the primary microhabitats for clams.

The author is listed as John N. Rinne, a research associate in the Department of Zoology at the Arizona State University. The paper draws on his 1973 dissertation on the limnology and horizontal fish distribution of central Arizona reservoirs.

Sampling methods included:
- **Shallow-to-deep transects** with 0.25 m² quadrats, from the waterline to depths near 3–5 m.
- **Ekman dredge samples** of soft sediment, used to supplement quadrat data.
- **Size and weight measurements** of individual clams, with a length-weight equation used to derive biomass per hectare.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Substrate:** Clam density was consistently higher on rock-rubble and boulder slopes than on sand-gravel or compacted gravel. At Apache Lake site A-2, which had compacted gravel, clams were scarce. Upslope sites with rock-boulder substrate held about eight times as many clams per unit area.
- **Depth:** Densities generally increased with depth at Roosevelt Lake. Apache Lake analysis of variance showed no significant differences in numbers, sizes, or biomass with depth at the transects.
- **Turbidity and food:** Roosevelt Lake is more turbid and has more suspended organic solids near the Salt River inflow. The author suggests these conditions may suppress clam populations despite adequate food. Densities were higher downlake, away from the inflow.
- **Harvesting:** Clam densities at Frazier's Landing, a major boat-launch area, were drastically lower than at other sites, reflecting human collection.
- **Fish predation:** Carp and buffalofishes (genus *Ictiobus*) consume large numbers of clams. The author suggests that greater clam densities in shallow water may partly reflect lower fish predation in those areas.
- **Filter feeding:** Clams remove suspended particles and plankton from the water column. Laboratory work at Arizona State University suggested that clam ingestion rates are directly related to algal concentration.

## Key Findings

**Density and biomass ranges**
- **Roosevelt Lake:** Clam numbers ranged from 2 to 243/m², with biomass from about 1.4 to 137.8 kg/ha (OCR-uncertain values).
- **Apache Lake:** Densities ranged up to roughly 1,000/m², and biomass ranged from 0.65 to 1,969 kg/ha (values as recovered by OCR; the figure is uncertain).
- **Canal below Granite Reef Dam:** The greatest concentration recorded was about 1,600 clams/m², where water was clear and food was abundant.

**Dredge samples**
- Ekman dredge samples at Apache Lake gave estimated densities of 43 to 86 clams/m², comparable to the quadrat estimates.

**Statistical results**
- Differences in clam numbers and biomass between the two lakes were significant at the 0.05 level.
- Within lakes, differences with depth were not significant at most transects.

**Interpretation**
- Clam populations were densest in clear, food-rich water and on rock-rubble substrate.
- The author concludes that food supply appears more limiting than turbidity for *Corbicula* in these reservoirs, though turbidity may still suppress populations near inflows.
- Downlake declines in clam size and density at Apache Lake coincided with increased fish predation and reduced food.

**Cited literature**
- The author cites the following comparative studies: Prokopovich (1969) on declining densities downstream of the Gila River canal in California and Bickel (1966) on increased spring abundance in the Ohio River under high turbidity. Heinsohn (1958) and Greer (1971) address clam filtration rates and plankton removal.
