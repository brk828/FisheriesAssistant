# Razorback Sucker Movement Strategies across a River–Reservoir Habitat Complex

#ichthyology #catostomidae #endangered_species #telemetry #movement_ecology

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a federally endangered catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species is a large-bodied, periodic strategist that historically occupied diverse and connected river systems including access to a productive delta. Adults in this study averaged 499–527 mm total length and 1,326–1,668 g weight. The species experiences a recruitment bottleneck throughout its range, with successful wild recruitment occurring consistently only in [[Lake Mead]]. Competing hypotheses for recruitment failure include altered flow and temperature regimes, reduced habitat availability, habitat quality degradation, nonnative species introductions, and food limitation. Razorback Sucker spawn over sand, gravel, and cobble substrates during spring runoff in rivers (water temperatures 8–17°C) and on shallow, wave-washed shorelines in reservoirs. Optimal adult temperatures range from 22.9–24.8°C. Nearly 624,000 individuals have been stocked across the upper Colorado River basin from 2002–2019, as all populations except Lake Mead are maintained by intensive stocking.

**Habitat Associations**: Razorback Sucker utilize a wide variety of habitat types including main-stem rivers, tributaries, off-channel wetlands, and reservoirs. Fish aggregate at river–reservoir inflow areas, possibly to access turbid, productive water and submerged vegetation. The study area encompassed a continuum from riverine zones (lotic habitat with slowing velocities, well-mixed water, limited light penetration) through transition zones (most spatially dynamic) to lacustrine zones (increased light penetration, lower nutrient concentrations, longer water residence time). The [[Great Bend]] consisted of canyon-bound habitat with homogeneous shorelines and sand/silt substrates. [[Neskahi Bay]] exhibited more complex shorelines with coarser substrates and steeper slopes on the north side, and finer gravel substrates with shallower slopes and numerous coves on the south side. Fish preferentially used shallow water (<2 m depth, mean 1.4 ± 1.3 m) with maximum recorded depth of 5.7 m. Hypothesized important habitats for recruitment include backwater and floodplain habitats, now limited throughout the basin.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Of 69 tracked fish, 58% moved from the reservoir and were detected upstream at the [[Piute Farms Waterfall]] (a 6-m barrier ~30 km upstream), while 42% remained entirely within the reservoir. Fish were detected at the waterfall in all seasons except winter, demonstrating year-round (not just spawning-season) movements between reservoir and riverine habitats. Detection efficiency was high across receiver gates (0.78–0.99), higher in Neskahi Bay (0.93 ± 0.06) than Great Bend (0.82 ± 0.05). Reservoir-resident fish moved long distances (up to 326 km total) but maintained relatively small linear home ranges (<18 km). Total distance moved was highest in spring (median 94 km, IQR 41.5–150 km), intermediate in summer (50 km, 23–81 km), and lowest in autumn (2 km, 0–38 km) and winter (4 km, 0–12 km). Home range size was six times larger in spring and summer compared to autumn and winter. Fish spent most time in upper Neskahi Bay based on detection frequencies. Results suggest potential adfluvial life history strategy in a significant proportion of the population, doubling previous estimates of annual upstream movement. Intrinsic variation in movement strategies may reflect natural individual variation or responses to habitat alteration and fragmentation.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Not reported in detail. The document notes that inflow areas provide access to turbid and productive water and submerged vegetation, and that Lake Mead inflows support successful recruitment possibly due to these productive conditions. Food limitation has been hypothesized as one mechanism contributing to recruitment bottlenecks (Papoulias and Minckley 1990, 1992; Horn 1996).

**Environmental Drivers**: Monthly fish activity increased significantly with mean monthly river temperature (P < 0.001; marginal R² = 0.59, conditional R² = 0.58) but showed no relationship with river discharge (P = 0.276). River discharge in 2018 was below the long-term 95% confidence interval with little snowmelt runoff, remaining relatively low and constant throughout the study. River temperature followed longer-term seasonal patterns. Results suggest temperature alone can drive Razorback Sucker movements, which were presumed to coincide with spawning. Fish used deeper water during summer months (July–September) when mean monthly river temperatures peaked at 26.2°C, possibly tracking optimal temperatures within the reservoir. Depth use decreased significantly toward the inflow area across all seasons (P < 0.01), with significant seasonal differences in mean depth (P < 0.001). Reservoir water elevation declined over the study, leading to increased riverine habitat and downstream movement of the transition zone; proportional detections increased in Neskahi Bay as the inflow area moved farther downstream. The lack of clear flow cues (due to atypically low flows) but strong temperature correlation suggests temperature may be a more influential movement and spawning cue than flow for adult Razorback Sucker in inflow areas.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Razorback Sucker is federally listed as endangered (USFWS 1991). With the exception of Lake Mead, all populations are maintained by intense stocking efforts. The species experiences a recruitment bottleneck throughout the Colorado River basin. Previous mark-recapture assessments suggested most individuals occupy relatively small home ranges (<10 km) even across several years in the San Juan River upstream of the study area. The proportion of fish detected that were tagged in 2017 was lower (43% from waterfall, 19% from Neskahi Bay) than those tagged in 2018 (96% from waterfall, 87% from Neskahi Bay), though causes (tag loss, mortality, or lack of movement) could not be determined. Tag malfunction may have contributed to failed detections. Detection probability at the waterfall PIT antenna was previously estimated at 0.6–0.9.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Major threats include habitat fragmentation by dams and water diversion structures, altered flow and temperature regimes, reduced availability and degradation of habitat quality, introduction of nonnative species, and potential food limitation. The Colorado River basin is one of the most altered and managed systems in the world. Rivers have been fragmented by large hydropower dams (including Glen Canyon Dam forming Lake Powell), smaller diversion structures, and unique geomorphic barriers like the Piute Farms Waterfall. Dams have altered flow, temperature, and turbidity regimes throughout the basin. The two largest U.S. reservoirs by volume (Lake Mead 35.7 km³, Lake Powell 32.3 km³) both occur on the main-stem Colorado River. Backwater and floodplain habitats hypothesized as important for recruitment are now limited throughout the basin. The Piute Farms Waterfall (formed through sediment deposition and channel migration) has remained a barrier to fish movement since 2001, blocking access to upstream riverine habitat. Multiple waterfalls have emerged at different locations in this 4-km reach since the late 1980s with reservoir water level fluctuations.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Conservation efforts would benefit from a more complete understanding of how fish use river–reservoir habitats and their potential trade-offs in altered riverscapes. Comprehensive management of habitat resources (food, refuge, thermal, spawning) in connected river–reservoir systems might benefit endangered Razorback Sucker populations. Facilitating fish passage at barriers such as the Piute Farms Waterfall might allow fish access to important habitats, potentially increasing long-term population stability if benefits of reservoir versus riverine habitats vary under different environmental conditions (e.g., high-flow versus low-flow years). Understanding the importance of movement among river and reservoir habitats should encourage inclusion of reservoir habitats in management plans. Recovery of fish in highly managed ecosystems like the Colorado River basin requires creative solutions balancing water and fish management. Further effort should assess potential benefits of inflow areas to younger life stages, especially since large adult aggregations preferentially use these areas. The apparent dichotomy of resident and transient fish could reflect selection against mobile contingents due to fragmentation, leading to increased residency; variation in movement strategies might be important for population persistence. Reservoirs may provide warmer temperatures for longer periods relative to regulated rivers, potentially benefiting warmwater fishes.
