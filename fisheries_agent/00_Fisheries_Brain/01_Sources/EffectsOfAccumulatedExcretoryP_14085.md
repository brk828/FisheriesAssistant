# Effects of Accumulated Excretory Products on Hatchery-Reared Salmonids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 report by [[Roger E. Burrows]], Fishery Research Biologist at the [[Salmon-Cultural Laboratory]] (Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife), examines which excretory products accumulate in salmon and trout rearing ponds and how they affect hatchery fish. It is the second in a series on factors limiting production in rearing ponds, following a study of pond types by Burrows and Chenoweth (1955).

The work identifies [[Ammonia]] and [[Urea]] as the principal excretory products. Their relative proportions shift with fish density and metabolic activity: urea dominates at low loading, while ammonia dominates at high loading. Controlled exposures showed that [[Un-ionized Ammonia]] causes [[Gill Hyperplasia]] and that prolonged exposure reduces growth and physical stamina. The author proposes that this gill damage precedes [[Bacterial Gill Disease]].

## Background & Description

The study addresses a gap in hatchery science. Earlier investigations, which the author describes as relatively unexplored, often held fish in aquariums where wastes accumulated. Those conditions can produce misleading conclusions, because rearing ponds receive constant water exchange. This study instead sampled effluent hourly over 24-hour periods in operating [[Raceway Ponds]].

Terminology was a central issue. The author defines ammonia as the gas NH₃, ammonia nitrogen as the combined form, and un-ionized ammonia as the toxic gas fraction. Many earlier papers used these terms almost interchangeably, which the author considers a source of confusion.

The study species were fingerling [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*). Comparative literature covered [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), and [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*). Earlier work by Smith (1929) and Wood (1958) reported ammonia and urea as the main end products of catabolism.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report treats excretion as a water-quality problem driven by stocking density, water inflow, temperature, and pH.

- **Loading and inflow:** Fish weight per gallon per minute (gpm) of inflow determined which product dominated. Ammonia took over at higher loads.
- **Temperature:** Higher temperatures raised both metabolic rate and the share of un-ionized ammonia.
- **pH:** This was the key control on toxicity. Insignificant un-ionized ammonia occurred at pH 7, and the proportion rose markedly as pH increased. The author states that this correlation explains large differences in carrying capacity among water supplies.
- **Hydraulics:** Poor flow patterns and inadequate inflow lengthened detention time and exposure. Stratified, laminar-flow areas could accumulate ammonia.
- **Diurnal cycle:** Ammonia appeared mainly in the afternoon (p.m.) and urea in the morning (a.m.). The low point of the cycle occurred in early morning.

## Key Findings

**1. Principal excretory products**
- Chinook fingerlings excreted mainly ammonia and urea. Only these two appeared in measurable amounts among the four analytes tested (ammonia, urea, creatinine, uric acid).
- Wood (1958) reported 75 to 98 percent of total nitrogen excreted in three marine species as ammonia and urea. Smith (1929) found ammonia excretion much greater than urea. This study found that either product could dominate, depending on fish size and time of day.

**2. Field experiments in raceways**
- Two 4- by 40-foot raceways were stocked with equal fish poundage. Inflow was 125 gpm in one and 250 gpm in the other.
- In the high-inflow raceway, urea was the principal product at the low loading rate (1.64 lb/gpm, 46°F) and throughout the season.
- Ammonia became dominant at higher loads: 5.6 lb/gpm at 52°F in the low-inflow raceway, and 8.16 lb/gpm at 55°F.
- Ammonia dominance was approached at 4.37 lb/gpm at 55°F.
- The detection limit of the direct nesslerization method is 0.1 ppm, so small ammonia excretion at low loading may have gone undetected.

**3. Controlled ammonia toxicity (gill damage)**
- Two experiments ran 6 weeks at 43°F and 57°F. Four lots of chinook fingerlings were exposed to ammonium hydroxide at 0.0, 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7 ppm (as NH₃).
- Gill epithelial proliferation increased with concentration. Extensive clubbing and hyperplasia appeared at the highest level, and fusion of lamellae was seen at 0.7 ppm.
- Proliferation was progressive for the first 4 weeks, after which no further measurable increase occurred.
- At 57°F, fish with larger size recovered markedly after 3 weeks in fresh water. At 43°F, the damage had not consolidated or recovered after 3 weeks.
- Urea at 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4 ppm for 3 weeks caused no gill proliferation. The author concluded that ammonia, not urea, was the toxicant.

**4. Un-ionized ammonia calculations**
- At pH 7.8, calculated un-ionized ammonia was 0.006 and 0.008 ppm at the 0.3 ppm ammonium hydroxide level, 0.010 and 0.012 ppm at 0.5 ppm, and 0.014 and 0.018 ppm at 0.7 ppm (43°F and 57°F, respectively).
- Table 1 (after Wuhrmann and Woker, 1949) gives the percentage of un-ionized ammonia across pH values at 10°C, 15°C, and 20°C. Percentages rise sharply with pH. For example, the table lists 57.6 to 62.1 percent at the highest pH shown.
- Literature cited in the report gives a lethal figure of 0.84 ppm un-ionized ammonia at pH 8.2 (Lloyd and Herbert, 1960). It also gives 18 ppm ammonia nitrogen as lethal to rainbow trout at 50°F. The author considers that concentration highly unlikely in rearing ponds unless water is extensively reused.

**5. Growth and stamina**
- Over a 5-month period, the low-inflow raceway, where ammonia was dominant for the last 3 months, showed a growth rate 9.1 percent lower than the high-inflow raceway. No mortality or disease outbreak was reported. The author describes this as a single use of the water.
- Stamina trials used groups of 100 fish in a tunnel at fixed water velocities (Thomas et al., 1964). The performance index was 16 in the continuously ammonia-exposed fish and 23 in controls. The author reports the 3-point difference as significant.

**6. Exposure duration as the key factor**
- Salmon fingerlings tolerated 0.7 ppm ammonium hydroxide for 1 hour per day with apparent impunity.
- Exposure beyond 12 hours per day at 0.1 ppm or above reduced growth. Continuous exposure reduced stamina and disease resistance.
- In raceways, this condition occurred at 8.5 lb/gpm, at 60°F and pH 7.8.
- At 65°F, the safe carrying capacity dropped to 8.0 lb/gpm or less. Above pH 8, toxicity increased greatly.

**7. Conclusions**
1. Chinook fingerlings excrete mainly ammonia and urea.
2. Below 5 lb/gpm of inflow, urea is the principal product. Above that level, ammonia dominates.
3. Un-ionized ammonia at 0.006 ppm, held continuously for 6 weeks, can cause extensive gill hyperplasia.
4. Continuous urea exposure for 3 weeks at normal pond levels caused no detectable harm.
5. Prolonged intermittent exposure to un-ionized ammonia reduces growth and stamina. Continuous exposure is postulated to precede bacterial gill disease.
6. pH and temperature control the un-ionized fraction, while pond type and loading determine exposure duration.
7. Measurable ammonia in effluent during the low point of the diurnal cycle signals dangerous levels.

**8. Management implications**
- Continuous exposure to un-ionized ammonia should be avoided. The author argues that resulting debility may reduce survival of salmon and trout both before and after release.
- Monitoring effluent for ammonia at the early-morning low point is recommended as an early warning.
