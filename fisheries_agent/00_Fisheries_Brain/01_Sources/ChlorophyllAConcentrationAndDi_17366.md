# Chlorophyll a Concentration and Distribution in Twin Lakes, Colorado, Prior to Operation of Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant, 1977–81

#limnology #phytoplankton #chrysophyceae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation report (REC-ERC-85-6, January 1985, by S. G. Campbell and J. F. LaBounty) presents preoperation chlorophyll *a* data from [[Twin Lakes]], Colorado, for 1977–81. Data were collected before the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]] began operating in September 1981, so they serve as a baseline for judging later effects on the lake ecosystem. The report describes chlorophyll distributions, the algal taxa behind the peaks, and the limnological drivers (runoff volume, winter severity and snow cover). Appendix A documents a methodological investigation of errors in chlorophyll pigment measurement that were corrupting chlorophyll *c* and chlorophyll *a*/*c* ratios.

## Background & Description

Twin Lakes lie about 24 km southwest of Leadville, Colorado, on [[Lake Creek]], at the eastern foot of the Sawatch Range at 2802 m. The vegetation is characteristic of the Montane or Canadian Life Zone. The lakes are glacially formed, with origins in morainic damming of Lake Creek.

- **Lower lake:** about 736.5 ha maximum surface area, depth about 27 m, maximum capacity 112 653 088 m³. It is described as the largest natural mountain lake in Colorado.
- **Upper lake:** about 263.4 ha, depth about 28 m, maximum capacity 41 078 107 m³. It receives Lake Creek inflow and acts as a settling basin for the lower lake.

The present ecosystem reflects outlet-control works, channel dredging, human activity, and the introduction of [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Lake Trout]] and [[Mysis Relicta]]. Chlorophyll *a* is the pigment present in all plants, so it is used as the standing-crop index and a quick trophic-status measure. Accessory pigments (chlorophylls *b* and *c*, carotenoids) identify the algal groups present. Chlorophyll *b* occurs in terrestrial plants and green algae, while diatoms use chlorophyll *c* as their accessory pigment.

Limnological monitoring of Twin Lakes began in 1971 under project DR-331, *Ecology and Limnology of Pumped-Storage Reservoirs*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical-chemical conditions (mean and range, 1977–81):**

- Combined mean annual surface water temperature 8.4 °C; range 0 to 18 °C.
- Combined mean annual bottom dissolved oxygen 5.7 mg/L. Low to anoxic bottom DO occurs in late winter to early spring and at the height of summer stratification.
- Combined mean annual pH 7.6; range 6.4 (bottom) to 8.3 (surface).
- Relatively soft, dilute calcium bicarbonate lakes. Principal anions are HCO₃⁻ and SO₄²⁻, and the principal cation is Ca²⁺.

**Nutrients and productivity:** Productivity is [[Phosphorus Limitation|phosphorus-limited]]. Total phosphorus is very low, and orthophosphate is rarely detected. Total phosphorus in comparison lakes is about an order of magnitude higher than in Twin Lakes.

**Winter stress and winter kill:** Anoxic bottom conditions in the early 1970s and 1979 released heavy metals (notably manganese) from sediments. This produced a [[Winter Kill]] in 1974–75 from which plankton and benthos took several years to recover. In 1979, a rapid thaw of Lake Creek and snowmelt limited metal release to about two weeks in early April.

**Hydrology:** Runoff volume is the critical parameter controlling limnology. The 10-year mean inflow (1972–81) is 9,448 acre-ft (11,659 × 10² m³).

**Other high-mountain lakes:** Twin Lakes resemble oligotrophic, hydrographically open, cool, generally dimictic high-mountain lakes, but have much lower total phosphorus. Comparison lakes in Utah and Colorado (e.g., Dillon Reservoir, Grand Lake, Lake Granby) were used as reference points.

## Key Findings

### Chlorophyll levels and trophic status

- Mean chlorophyll *a* for the study period was 3.2 mg/m³, with average values of 1.5 to 4.1 mg/m³ for both lakes.
- Individual readings ranged from less than 0.1 to 52.1 mg/m³.
- Twin Lakes are classified as [[Oligotrophic Lakes]] using the Likens (1975) criteria, and fall at the low end of the range for comparable oligotrophic lakes in Canada and Europe.

### Seasonal and depth patterns

- Chlorophyll *a* generally increases in fall after turnover. Winter peaks can occur when clear ice admits enough light.
- Winter peaks are concentrated near the surface. Summer peaks lie lower in the water column, near the [[Thermocline]] (about 9 m).
- Minimum concentrations occur just before spring and fall turnover.
- Summer peaks have never occurred in both lakes simultaneously.

### Influence of runoff and winter severity

| Year | Runoff (% of 10-yr mean) | Ice cover (days) | Snow cover (cm) | Bottom anoxia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1977 | 49 | 146 | 3 | No |
| 1978 | 133 | 147 | 14 | No |
| 1979 | 127 | 163 | 22 | Yes |
| 1980 | 91 | 170 | 23 | No |
| 1981 | 57 | 141 | 4 | No |

- Above-average runoff limits light penetration and flushes plankton from the lakes. Below-average runoff years (1977, 1981) had the upper lake's summer peaks.
- The lower lake was more productive in the second of two consecutive above-average runoff years (1978–79). The 1979 lower-lake bloom reflected the upper lake acting as a settling basin.
- Winter 1979 (snow cover 38 cm, anoxia, metal release) reduced upper-lake algae.

### Methodological findings (Appendix A)

An internal investigation found three sources of error in chlorophyll *a*/*c* ratios that shifted from about 1:1 to 1:2 or 1:3 between June and November 1978:

1. **Spectrophotometer zeroing:** Failure to zero at 700 nm overestimated absorbance at 663 nm by about 0.010. Combined with instrument tolerance, chlorophyll *c* was overestimated by a factor of two to three. Proper zeroing reduces but does not fully eliminate the anomaly. [[Strickland and Parsons Method]] procedures were used.
2. **Magnesium carbonate preservative:** Treated samples processed within 2–3 weeks showed chlorophyll *a* about 14 percent lower than untreated samples. Preservation is advised only for storage beyond 3 weeks, by pretreating filters with 1 mL of magnesium carbonate solution.
3. **Fluorescent light exposure:** Extracted pigments lost chlorophyll *a* within 15 minutes, with significant losses (about 25 percent at station 2 and 29 percent at station 4) within 30 minutes at about 1.0 lux. Analysis should be done under reduced lighting.

### Study context and implications

- Preoperation data (1974–81) form the baseline for comparison with postoperation conditions after the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]] began operating in September 1981.
- The Bureau of Reclamation uses these results in designing other pumped-storage facilities.
- Postoperation chlorophyll results, and the follow-up reports listed in the bibliography, were to be published separately.
