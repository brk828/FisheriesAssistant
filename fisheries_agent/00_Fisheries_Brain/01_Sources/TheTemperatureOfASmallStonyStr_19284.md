# The Temperature of a Small Stony Stream

#freshwater-ecology #limnology #stream-temperature

## Executive Summary

T. T. Macan (1958, *Hydrobiologia*) reports several years of continuous thermograph records from Outgate Beck, a small stony stream in the English Lake District. The study examines seasonal trends, the effect of sunshine and rain on summer highs, daily cycles, comparison with other streams, a two-year comparison, and stream-versus-air temperature. It also asks how much an observer with only a simple maximum-minimum thermometer could have learned. The paper is ecological rather than ichthyological: no fish are studied. The stream-dwelling organisms mentioned are a mayfly ([[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]]) and a diving beetle ([[Dytiscus Marginalis]]), both cited for temperature-dependent development. Key conclusions are that stream temperature is strongly shaped by shading, underground channels, and evaporation, and that a single daily maximum-minimum reading gives a usable average.

## Background & Description

The records were made with a mercury-in-steel [[Thermograph]] supplied by the Cambridge Instrument Company. The instrument was installed and calibrated in autumn 1950, but few satisfactory records were obtained before January 1951. The original clock was faulty and caused many gaps before failing in early 1953. A new clock was fitted in April 1954, after which the instrument ran continuously for three years. The author's wording is "five years" in the Summary, but the continuous period described is three years.

The instrument sat in a hut in the author's garden near the downstream end of an underground channel, which the author acknowledges was not ideal. The stream drains a flat, saucer-shaped catchment of less than 1 km². A peat bed (a "moss") lies in the middle, and an artificial cut runs through it and an outcrop of rock. Beyond this, the bed steepens, the current keeps a stony bottom, and the stream passes through a series of tunnels and falls before joining [[Ford Wood Beck]].

The paper's framing is that temperature acts mainly through growth, reproduction, and breeding or hatching timing, and also indirectly through oxygen and salinity. Freshwater organisms therefore depend as much on how long a temperature persists as on its peak. Water in these latitudes does not freeze except at the surface, so extreme and sudden cold seen on land is largely absent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal regime.** Four periods were recognised. January and February are the coldest, with fine weather producing low temperatures through night-time radiation. Late winter to midsummer shows rising temperature and large fine-weather fluctuations. July and August are sustained high temperatures, with small daily ranges. From autumn to year-end temperature falls steadily, with small daily differences (rarely more than 3°C).

**Shading and evaporation.** Stream water cooled markedly as it passed through shade or underground channels. The author attributes most of this cooling to evaporation, which depends on humidity and wind. Heat exchange with soil and air, and inflow of cold groundwater, also contribute. Eckel and Reuter (1950) provide the theoretical basis cited.

**Air versus water.** Air temperature at [[Ambleside]], about 4 km away, was used for comparison. In winter air can fall well below the water. In spring and early summer air is typically a degree or two warmer, and in late autumn it is the same amount cooler. In July and August 1955, air was 2–3°C warmer than the beck, yet the tunnel cooling effect was strongest then.

**Diurnal lag.** Maxima and minima came late in the day. Even in March the maximum was rarely reached before 4 p.m. The author links this to the cut through the moss, which the sun does not reach until nearly 4 p.m. In [[Schmitz 1954]]'s stream, the maximum moved from about 2 p.m. at the source to about 6 p.m. at the mouth, 4.47 km downstream.

**Cold-water sources.** [[Whelpside Ghyll]], which rises at 800 m, warmed quickly after emerging from its spring. Spring temperature ranged from 4.4°C to 4.9°C, but reached 12.5°C at 600 ft on 25 August 1952. This was a steep stream with an average gradient of about 1 in 24.

**Biological time scales.** The author notes that the mayfly *Rhithrogena semicolorata* completes its development between October and May, and that *Dytiscus* species begin growth at different threshold temperatures (5°C and 10°C, after Blunck, 1924). Degree-hour totals above several baselines are therefore relevant to aquatic insect life history.

## Key Findings

**1. Extreme temperatures and seasonal range**
- January 1955 was the coldest winter on record. Snow dropped the water to 2°C, and the next day's rain pushed it to a maximum of 6.9°C, a level not exceeded until late March.
- In 1957, the warmest winter, temperatures rarely fell far from 5°C in January–February, reaching a maximum of 8°C early in the year.
- The stream never froze, though surface ice has occurred locally.
- The greatest single-day range in the April 1951 week shown in Fig. 2 was 6.0°C. The greatest recorded range was on 16 May 1952: 10°C at 6 a.m. (GMT) and 16.8°C at 5 p.m.

**3. Summer highs depend on sun after rain, not on sun alone**
- During a fine, rainless spell in May 1951, 14.1 hours of sunshine on 10 and 11 May did not push the stream past 10°C. The stream was low and its feed water was cold.
- The highest summer temperatures came when sun followed rain. On 30 June 1952, after drizzle and five days with some rain, the stream reached 19°C under 8.2 hours of sunshine at [[Ambleside]], with an air maximum of 23°C.
- The author proposes two mechanisms: humidity after rain suppresses evaporative cooling, and warm surface soil drains into the stream.

**4. Shading and tunnels cool the stream**
- In the upper tunnel (B–C), water cooled from 19.6°C to 16.0°C on 24 July 1955.
- On 25 July 1955, water at 19.5°C at the top of the falls fell to 13.1°C after the falls and the lower tunnel (H–J).
- On a sunny day with an easterly wind, a stream on open moorland was 21.6°C above a wooded slope and fell to 14.0°C in the shade, a drop of 7.6°C.
- On [[Belle Grange Beck]], Dr B. Douglas recorded differences of up to 5.3°C between open and shaded reaches on sunny days with slight flow. On wet days with good flow, temperature varied little.

**5. Comparison with other stations**
- A maximum-minimum thermometer near the mouth of Ford Wood Beck read monthly for a year always exceeded the Outgate maximum. The smallest mean difference was 0.1°C in October and the largest 2.9°C in April. Differences exceeded 2.0°C on four of twelve readings.
- Minimum differences were smaller, exceeding 2.0°C only once (2.4°C).
- [[Sykeside Beck]] was very similar to Outgate Beck.
- In [[Whelpside Ghyll]], the highest reading over three summers was 15.0°C (1952), against 19°C at Outgate over the same period. The maximum in both 1951 and 1953 was 13.9°C.

**6. Two-year comparison (1954 vs. 1955)**
- 1954 was wet and sunless in summer; 1955 was fine in July–August but cold until mid-June. From late June, 1955 stayed warmer for seventeen weeks.
- Over the comparable period, total degree hours were 35,674 (1954) and 40,072 (1955). Using a 0°C baseline over twelve weeks from 21 June, the figures were 29,859 (1955) and 26,311 (1954). With a 10°C baseline, they were 9,689 (1955) and 6,143 (1954).
- Winter comparison (October–May, 1954/55 vs. 1956/57) showed the colder winter had many more hours below 4°C (Table III). In Table IV, the two winters reached similar total degree hours at week 14. After that, the warm winter led, reaching 20 weeks' worth of totals when the cold winter needed 25 weeks. By the end, the warmer year was 3–4 weeks ahead. Above 5°C, the warmer year led by up to 10 weeks.

**7. Diurnal patterns**
- Fig. 6 shows the typical fine-weather daily cycle, with late maximum and minimum.
- Irregular cycles occurred under other conditions. Floating snow islands during a thaw caused temporary drops of more than 1°C. Summer thunderstorms caused sudden rises of up to 1.5°C, with slower and usually incomplete recovery.

**8. What a simple thermometer could reveal**
- Spot readings at different becks can mislead on sunny days, when temperature changes quickly and differently across the stream.
- A maximum-minimum thermometer buried in the bed is inexpensive and gives a reasonable mean. Reading once a day at about 1 p.m. (roughly the daily mean) gave a 1955 total of 53,078 degree hours by thermometer average against 52,336 by thermograph, a difference of 742 (1.4%). The thermometer average was the higher in 13 of the 27 remaining weeks.
- Reading once a week gave 53,104 degree hours for the 25-week period, against 53,078 for daily readings, a difference of 26. Individual weeks could differ markedly (Table V), but long-term means were close.

**9. Stream-air equilibrium**
- A pond-fed stream studied by Schmitz reached 13.25°C 3.16 km from its source and did not warm further over the next 1.3 km. This agreed closely with Eckel and Reuter's theoretical calculation.
- The author concludes that small streams reach thermal equilibrium within short distances of their source, and their mean temperature is not far from mean air temperature.

**10. Concluding recommendations**
- Stream temperature records are most useful for seasonal and degree-hour analyses, and a single daily maximum-minimum reading, or even weekly readings, is adequate for long-term averages.
- The study is limited by a single instrument site located in a cooling tunnel, which the author regards as unfortunate.
