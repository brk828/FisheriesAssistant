# Osteology and Relationships of the Lanternfishes (Family Myctophidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #myctophidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 monograph by John R. Paxton, published as Bulletin 13 of the [[Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County]], analyses the osteology of all then-recognized genera of the lanternfish family [[Myctophidae]], excluding the neurocranium and most of the axial skeleton, and compares the results with luminescent organ patterns. The study uses cleared-and-stained specimens (341 in total, with one specimen of each of 98 lanternfish species and 12 non-myctophid species dissected in detail). The author establishes two subfamilies, [[Myctophinae]] and [[Lampanyctinae]], with five new or re-ranked tribes, and proposes a phylogeny largely congruent with earlier photophore-based classifications, while arguing that photophore patterns mirror evolutionary history. Myctophids are placed closest to the family [[Neoscopelidae]].

## Background & Description

Lanternfishes are the most speciose midwater fish family, with more than 300 nominal species and roughly 200 valid species. In a Bermuda trawling project they ranked second only to the bristlemouth *Cyclothone* in specimens captured, and in many areas they form the largest fish biomass of midwater trawl catches. Adult size ranges from under 20 mm to over 200 mm standard length. Most feed on crustaceans and are important prey for larger fishes.

The family is defined by [[Photophores]], light organs usually restricted to the ventral body half, whose patterns are often species-specific. Photophore function is argued to be primarily species recognition, though this remains largely untested because midwater fishes could not be maintained alive.

Historically, the family was placed in the order [[Myctophiformes]] (Iniomi, Scopeliformes) following [[Regan]] (1911); [[Greenwood et al.]] (1966) subordinated the group within Salmoniformes, whereas Gosline et al. (1966) and Rosen and Patterson (1969) supported ordinal status. Fraser-Brunner (1949) recognized 24 genera and 11 subgenera; subsequent work raised the count to 30 genera.

**Methods.** Specimens were cleared with trypsin ([[Taylor]] 1967) rather than KOH, which had disarticulated lanternfish specimens, and stained with alizarin. Bone nomenclature follows [[Jollie]] (1954). Variation was scored with categorical characters in tables, many tied to correlated jaw-length changes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Vertical migration by myctophids corresponds to the migration of the [[Deep Scattering Layer]], as confirmed by bathyscaphe observations. Some species undergo no vertical migration, and deep-dwelling taxa such as *Taaningichthys* are among the deepest-living myctophids. Reproduction is poorly known; some species reportedly spawn over six to nine months of the year, and myctophid eggs are rarely captured in plankton nets.

Caudal luminous glands show sexual dimorphism in most Myctophinae, with supracaudal glands often in males and infracaudal glands in females. The author suggests caudal glands function to startle or divert predators, and that orbital organs in [[Diaphus]] and [[Gymnoscopelus]] may have taken over sexual and specific recognition functions from caudal glands. Gonichthyini are linked to nocturnal visits to surface waters and rapid swimming.

Specimens were collected with the [[Isaacs-Kidd Midwater Trawl]] off southern California and northern Mexico, and in the southern oceans aboard the R/V Velero IV and USNS Eltanin.

## Key Findings

- **Subfamily Myctophinae** (two tribes, 11 genera, ~70 species): short jaws, no secondary photophores, caudal glands usually sexually dimorphic, larval eyes elliptical. Tribes: [[Myctophini]] (seven genera, including *Protomyctophum*, *Electrona*, *Benthosema*, *Diogenichthys*, *Hygophum*, *Myctophum*, *Symbolophorus*) and [[Gonichthyini]] (four genera: *Loweina*, *Tarletonbeania*, *Gonichthys*, *Centrobranchus*; "slendertailed" lanternfishes).
- **Subfamily Lampanyctinae** (new subfamily; four tribes, 17 genera, ~130 species): long jaws, secondary photophores, larval eyes round. Tribes: [[Notolychnini]] (monotypic, *Notolychnus valdiviae*, a maximum of 25 mm), [[Lampanyctini]] (eight genera), [[Diaphini]] (*Diaphus* and *Lobianchia*), and [[Gymnoscopelini]] (six genera).
- **Osteological trends:** streamlining in Gonichthyini, lengthening of jaws, sculpturing of circumorbitals, fusion of extrascapulars in Lampanyctinae, increase in procurrent caudal rays and a supramaxillary in Gymnoscopelini, and fusion of procurrent rays in several lines.
- **Key characters:** jaw length relative to the orbit is treated as the major evolutionary advance; more than sixty osteological characters were scored, but many are too variable to define lineages on their own.
- **Relationships:** Myctophidae derive most closely from [[Neoscopelidae]] within an aulopid–chlorophthalmid–neoscopelid line. The author regards similarities between gonostomatids and myctophids in photophore patterns as convergent.
- **Subfamilial origin:** the subfamilies probably diverged early, and jaw lengthening and elevated photophores arose independently in each, following larval evidence from [[Moser and Ahlstrom]] (1970).
- **Photophores:** photophore patterns closely parallel osteological trends, so the author concludes they mirror lanternfish evolutionary history.
- **Taxonomic changes:** *Metelectrona* and *Elampa* are sunk into *Electrona*; *Hierops* is treated as a subgenus of *Protomyctophum*; *Parvilux* is sunk into *Lampanyctus*; *Bolinichthys* is erected as a new genus; *Lepidophanes* is restricted to *L. guentheri* and *L. gaussi*; *Nasolychnus* is recognized at subgeneric level within *Gymnoscopelus*; *Cyrtorhynchus* is excluded from Myctophidae as probably an engraulid.
- **Unusual findings:** a perforate ceratohyal is reported in *Notolychnus valdiviae* and *Triphoturus mexicanus*, and a supramaxillary is present in some species, neither previously reported for the family.
- **Specimen basis:** 98 lanternfish species were dissected, with 341 cleared-and-stained specimens in total; the largest cleared specimen was a 203 mm *Gymnoscopelus*.
- **Open questions:** the author notes that *Diaphus* (~50 valid species) requires much more material before subdivision, and that the ecology of most species is poorly known.
