# Emergent Biological Patterns and Surface-Subsurface Interactions at Landscape Scales

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[C. M. Pringle]] (University of Georgia) and [[Frank J. Triska]] (U.S. Geological Survey) argues that the exchange of water between streams and their surrounding groundwater shapes biological patterns in rivers at landscape, regional and global scales, yet remains poorly understood. The authors examine how geology controls nutrient supply, how natural [[Hydrothermal Systems]] serve as tracers and models, how human activities decouple streams from their catchments, and what research is needed. Fish are discussed only incidentally (for example [[Striped Bass]] and salmonids, and fish assemblages used in bioindicator indices), so the content is primarily a stream-ecosystem and hydrogeology review rather than an ichthyological study.

## Background & Description

The chapter is Chapter 7 (pp. 167–193) of *Streams and Ground Waters* (eds. J. B. Jones and P. J. Mulholland, Academic Press, 2000). It is organized into five parts:

- **I. Introduction**: defines "landscape" as scales larger than a river reach or low-order catchment, including regional and global patterns.
- **II. Balance of Physical and Chemical Factors on the Geologic Template**: covers nutrient limitation and upwelling effects.
- **III. Hydrothermal Systems as Models**: uses geothermal waters in Costa Rica as natural tracers.
- **IV. Human Impacts**: subdivided into nutrient and toxicant loading, land use, hydrological modification, and atmospheric deposition and global warming.
- **V. Synthesis and Recommendations**: three research priorities.

The reference list contains roughly 150 citations spanning hydrology, limnology, geothermal geochemistry and fisheries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geologic template and nutrient limitation.** Low phosphorus in parent material produces low surface-water phosphorus, so phosphorus limitation of primary production is predicted in such areas. Where geologic phosphorus is abundant, nitrogen usually limits production. Hyporheic delivery of nutrients can increase algal standing crop and shorten recovery after spates.

**Soils and nitrogen retention.** Well-drained soils transport nitrate readily, whereas poorly drained riparian zones favour denitrification. On coastal plain landscapes, riparian zones may retain 70–90% of nitrogen inputs, while piedmont landscapes with deeper groundwater and narrower riparian zones allow direct nitrate emergence into streams.

**Thermal refugia.** Upwelling cold groundwater provides refugia for [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) in a Gulf of Mexico coastal river. Warm winter upwellings keep juvenile [[Steelhead]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) and [[Coho Salmon]] (*O. kisutch*) from freezing in northern California, and allow [[Chum Salmon]] (*O. keta*) to spawn in winter in Alaska's Chilkat River. The chapter notes that this links salmon spawning with the migration of the largest bald eagle population in the world.

**Costa Rican hydrothermal model (Barva Volcano).** On the Caribbean slope, high-elevation acid-sulfate streams support acidophilic algae and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria. Low-elevation Na–Cl–HCO₃ springs carry up to 400 µg P L⁻¹ and emerge in lowland tropical wet forest (24–27 °C). Of approximately 45 fish taxa in these streams, 12 consume significant algae and many are omnivorous (Figure 2 summary).

**Land use and biotic integrity.** In 23 sites on the Raisin River basin (2800 km², southeastern Michigan), [[Allan et al. 1997]] found agricultural land-use proportion to be the best single predictor of biotic integrity, measured with Karr's (1991) Index of Biotic Integrity, a summation of ten metrics based largely on fish assemblage composition. Local riparian vegetation was a better predictor in a second six-site study.

## Key Findings

- **Landscape controls dominate.** Catchment geology, soil type and land use set the magnitude of surface-subsurface exchange and therefore emergent biological patterns, often overriding site-level factors.
- **Hydrothermal systems are effective natural tracers.** Distinctive solute signatures allow flowpaths to be traced over long distances; in Costa Rica they reveal how geothermal waters are distributed across the landscape.
- **Agricultural nutrient loading** now rivals natural global nitrogen fixation. In the [[Mississippi River]], nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations at the mouth rose in response to 20- and 4-fold increases in fertilizer application in the catchment.
- **Contamination transfer:** in the channelized [[Rhine River]], decoupling from the floodplain raised phosphate, chloride and mercury in riverside groundwater over a 1–2 km fringe, while an intact floodplain improved water quality.
- **Groundwater depletion:** Ogallala Aquifer extraction runs about 10,000 times faster than recharge, lowering groundwater up to 1 m per year. [[Bangkok]]'s water table fell 25 m since the late 1950s, with saltwater intrusion.
- **Pulp plantations in Brazil:** eucalyptus plantations are associated with the disappearance of 156 streams in Espírito Santo state, according to Carrere (1996) as cited.
- **Irrigation effects in Central Asia:** a rise of 1.0–1.5 m in groundwater level converted desert Artemisia shrublands to halophytic vegetation within 5–6 years; about 45–55% of water is lost through seepage from unlined canals.
- **Selenium toxicity:** at Kesterson Reservoir, California, roughly 15,000 adult birds were dying each year in the mid-1980s, and elevated selenium was found on irrigation projects in 17 western states.
- **Reservoirs:** impoundments shift exchange from advective to diffusional flux, promote anoxia and methylmercury production, and about 15% of land precipitation is held in large-dam reservoirs.
- **Atmospheric change:** in boreal Canada, drought and lowered water tables oxidize stored sulfur, releasing acid pulses; groundwater inflow buffers lakes against acidification.
- **Global water distribution:** streams and lakes make up only 0.3% of freshwater, and groundwater holds 66 times more freshwater than lakes, streams, soil moisture and the atmosphere combined.

**Research priorities (Section V):**
1. Determine the balance between landscape and waterscape control on biotic pattern, extending the River Continuum Concept (Vannote et al., 1980) to varied geologies.
2. Identify the capacity of landscape types to process nutrients before riparian and hyporheic zones are overwhelmed or decoupled, including the role of irrigation tile drainage.
3. Separate anthropogenic from natural controls in large rivers, testing the Serial Discontinuity Concept (Ward and Stanford, 1983) for impoundment effects on biogeochemistry.
