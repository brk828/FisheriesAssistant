# Review of Glen Canyon Environmental Studies: Humpback Chub Research Programs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-cypha

## Executive Summary

This peer review, dated 24 November 1991 and written by Dr. William J. Matthews of the [[University of Oklahoma Biological Station]], evaluates the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) endangered species projects on the federally endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). The review assesses four research programs ([[Biowest]], [[Arizona State University-Navajo Natural Heritage Program]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]) against three questions: scientific design, progress, and direct benefit to conservation and to [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations.

The reviewer concludes that the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) is probably the only extant spawning and larval-production site between Glen Canyon Dam and Lake Mead, making the species highly vulnerable. He argues that the most urgent gaps are in larval and juvenile biology, bioenergetics, and temperature effects, which are best addressed through a dedicated laboratory facility. He also recommends establishing alternative spawning populations and placing the project under an independent coordinating scientist.

## Background & Description

The review is based on written proposals, progress reports, and meeting summaries through autumn 1991, plus attendance at review-panel and PI meetings. Each program was judged on three criteria: whether its research is well designed, whether it has made substantial progress, and whether its results can directly inform conservation or dam management.

The reviewer disclosed prior professional contacts with several PIs, including a co-authored manuscript and chapters in a 1987 stream fish ecology book he edited. He stated that these contacts did not bias the evaluation.

The humpback chub is described as a species with one remaining stronghold in the middle Colorado River system, with its greatest numbers centered near the mouth of the LCR. The LCR is the only confirmed reproduction site in this reach, and its population is estimated at 5,000 to 20,000 adults.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning and natal habitat.** The reviewer considers it unlikely that humpback chub spawn in the mainstem, because mainstem water temperatures of 9–10 °C are probably too cold for successful larval feeding, conversion, and growth. Flow fluctuations below Glen Canyon Dam are also thought to flush any potential backwaters on a schedule unsuitable for larvae. Exact spawning sites and spawning behavior have not been observed directly in the wild, although good larval production appears to occur in the LCR each year.

**Movement and dispersal.** Juveniles occur in both the mainstem and the LCR. How they move from the LCR to the mainstem is unknown. Two hypotheses are considered: passive drift of larvae, which the reviewer finds tenuous given the temperature shock, and dispersal of more developed juveniles. Radiotelemetry suggests that adults may wander relatively little. If the LCR is the only natal site, dilution alone would keep standing crops low at distant downstream reaches.

**Temperature and exotic species.** Cold releases from the dam may partly protect mainstem humpback chub by excluding warmwater exotics from [[Lake Mead]]. The reviewer notes a possible balance between temperatures cold enough to exclude exotics and warm enough to support humpback chub growth. He disputes the assumption that cold water alone excludes exotics, citing the cold-tolerant [[Striped Bass]] in Lake Texoma. [[Rainbow Trout]] made up 57% of exotic fish reported in one study.

## Key Findings

**Overall assessment**
- The LCR is probably the only extant natal site from Glen Canyon Dam to Lake Mead. Its population is considered vulnerable to unpredictable events.
- Reproduction is probably restricted to the lower 14 km of the LCR.
- Research on early life stages, bioenergetics, and temperature effects is the most urgent need, because management decisions depend on these data.

**Priority research questions (seven in total)**
1. **Spatial structure:** Is there one panmictic population or resident subgroups? Long-term [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging]] (PIT tagging) and [[Radiotelemetry]] are recommended, with a null model of random movement to test for directional dispersal. PIT tags last 10 years or more.
2. **Reproduction:** What cues trigger gamete ripening and spawning, and where and how do spawning events occur? Controlled laboratory streams with temperature control are recommended, because field observation is expected to yield mostly anecdotal information.
3. **Early life stages:** What are the dietary, thermal, and habitat needs of larvae, and what drives mortality? The reviewer recommends [[Larval Fish Feeding]] studies, [[Entrainment]] flume experiments, and [[Drift Net Sampling]] that distinguishes living from dead larvae.
4. **Juvenile performance:** How do temperature, food, and microhabitat affect growth, survival, and swimming? [[Bioenergetics]] studies in respirometers and flumes are recommended.
5. **Adult microhabitat:** Does adult microhabitat matter energetically? The reviewer notes no evidence yet that adults are stressed by habitat changes and recommends respirometry before major management decisions.
6. **Exotic predators:** Do exotic fish prey on humpback chub? Stomach analyses of captured exotics and comparative predation trials at several temperatures are recommended.
7. **Alternative spawning sites:** Can new breeding populations be established? The reviewer recommends relocating adults and juveniles to other tributaries, with the caveat that new sites must link back to the mainstem population.

**Program-by-program evaluation**
- **Biowest** (mainstem radiotracking under contract to GCES): Monthly trips since October 1990 captured 848 humpback chub, radiotagged about 50 adults, and PIT tagged more than 500 fish. The reviewer rated progress as solid. He recommended more emphasis on movement tracking than on microhabitat quantification, expanded juvenile habitat characterization, and full exchange of PIT tag records with ASU/NNHP.
- **ASU/NNHP** (LCR movements and reproductive biology): The team established LCR basecamps, ran five data-producing trips in 1991, and PIT tagged about 5,000 individuals with minimal mortality. Hoop nets gave the largest catches. Funding delays caused a missed spawning season, so larval and spawning work must wait until 1992. The reviewer recommended comparative, [[LC50]]-style predation trials with realistic escape opportunities.
- **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service** (habitat availability and occupancy in the LCR and eight other tributaries): The team mapped the LCR downstream of Blue Spring (21 km) and collected 391 humpback chub. Other catches included about 1,400 fish from minnow traps and about 800 from seining. It had collected no data from other tributaries as of 22 October 1991. The reviewer favored experimental work, such as streamside flow chambers, over field microhabitat studies, and proposed that USFWS choose sites for one-third of ASU/NNHP's [[Hoop Net]] sets to avoid conflicts over sampling design.
- **Arizona Game and Fish Department** (long-term monitoring and early life stages): The department's monitoring of the lower 1220 m of the LCR is the only long-term index of population trends and should continue with identical methods. The reviewer found the broader plan unfocused, criticized the lack of clear hypotheses, and questioned the justification for some water chemistry measures, such as REDOX. Only part of the 1991 work was started.

**Recommendations**
- Establish a substantial laboratory research facility with replicated, temperature-controlled experimental streams for all life stages.
- Accept a reasonable level of sacrifice of individual fish for physiological studies, and sacrifice exotic fish to examine their stomach contents.
- Transplant adults to new tributaries and airlift adults into the LCR reach between Atomizer Falls and Blue Spring.
- Sustain PIT tagging and tracking for many years, until tagged juveniles reach spawning age.
- Assign project oversight to an outside senior scientist with decision-making authority and a centralized data base. Written agreements should set authorship priority for peer-reviewed publications.
- Redirect some funding from whole-community studies toward laboratory approaches to humpback chub biology.
