# Big Bend Gambusia Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

The [[Recovery Plan]] for the [[Big Bend Gambusia]] (*Gambusia gaigei* Hubbs, 1929) was prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) with the [[Rio Grande Fishes Recovery Team]] and approved in 1984. The species is a small livebearing poeciliid known only from warm spring habitats in [[Big Bend National Park]], Texas. Its extremely limited range and fragile habitat mean the plan states the species may never be downlisted or delisted. Downlisting to threatened could be considered if all proposed actions are implemented.

The plan's core strategy is to maintain and enhance existing habitat, establish new populations in suitable warm-spring locations, keep a captive reserve at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], monitor populations, and control competition from [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish).

## Background & Description

- **Size and form:** Up to 30 mm long. It is a relatively plain, yellowish poeciliid with a faint lateral stripe, a bar beneath the eye, and a faint dark chin bar.
- **Male anatomy:** The anal fin is modified into a [[Gonopodium]] with a pronounced elbow and only one or two segments.
- **Taxonomy:** Described by Hubbs (1929). [[Rosen and Bailey]] (1966) proposed synonymy with *G. hurtadoi* and *G. alvarezi*, but this was rejected by several later authors, so *G. gaigei* is retained as a distinct species. Most authors place it in the [[Gambusia Nobilis Species Group]], although Rivas (1963) placed it in the *G. senilis* group.
- **Reproduction:** The species is [[Viviparity|viviparous]]. Hubbs and Broderick (1963) recorded two broods totalling 40 young from two small females. Females can probably store sperm for several months, with interbrood intervals of roughly 1–2 months. Reproductive data are otherwise scarce.
- **Historic distribution:** Two populations originally existed: at [[Boquillas Spring]] (about 200 m north of Boquillas Crossing) and at [[Spring 4]] near the Rio Grande Village Campground. The Boquillas population is extinct and the Spring 4 population was extirpated. All current stock derives from three individuals taken from a declining Spring 4 population in 1956.
- **Current populations:** Descendants are maintained in a [[Refugium]] near [[Spring 1]] and at Dexter National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and temperature**

- All documented natural occurrences are in spring outflows.
- Spring 4 outlet temperature was 32–35.5°C (Moore 1980). Boquillas Spring was about 23°C on March 6, 1982.
- Fish lived downstream of the springhead, where temperatures were influenced by air temperature. Summer 1954–56 readings in the densely inhabited Spring 4 outflow ditch were 28–32.5°C, with air temperatures in the mid-20s.
- Historic habitat was clear, shallow, spring-fed marsh with dense submerged and emergent vegetation. In the refugium, the species is most abundant among vegetation (mainly *Typha* and *Chara*) near shore.

**Associated species**

- At Spring 4, the species coexisted with two minnows (*Notropis braytoni*, *N. lutrensis*), mosquitofish, a presumed channel catfish, and green sunfish.
- Predatory [[Green Sunfish]] were introduced twice (1960s and 1968) and removed. No fish species are known to coexist with the Big Bend gambusia today.
- At least 18 other fish species inhabit the adjacent Rio Grande but are not syntopic with the gambusia.

**Geology and recharge**

- Springs 1 and 4 are about 270 m apart and are intermediate thermal springs, part of a cluster of six in the area with temperatures of 30–50°C.
- Chemical analyses over 24 years suggest a common source. Water issues along the contact of the upthrown [[Santa Elena Limestone]] (Cretaceous), and the principal recharge zone is in the higher elevations of the Sierra del Carmen.

**Threats**

- **Competition and replacement by mosquitofish:** The main threat. Gambusia gaigei has been replaced by G. affinis in Spring 4 (1954–56), in the first refugium (1960–61), in Brownsville pools, and at Boquillas Spring. Replacement is fastest in eurythermal (fluctuating-temperature) habitats and slowest in constant-temperature warm springruns.
- **Declining spring flow and water demand:** Diminished outflows have reduced habitat. Campground water is pumped from Spring 4, which uses most of its natural flow.
- **Flooding and runoff:** Rio Grande flooding, regulated by releases from Presa Luis L. León into the Rio Conchos in Mexico, is the greater flood threat. Runoff can cause silt deposition and introduce mosquitofish.
- **Cold shock:** A massive mortality in the refugium in December 1975 matched cold-shock lethality.
- **Visitor pressure:** Visitation is rising, and the Rio Grande Village area may be near its maximum compatible with species survival.

## Key Findings

**Status and outlook**

- Because of the very limited distribution and fragile habitat, the plan states the species may never be downlisted or delisted. Downlisting could be considered only if all recovery actions are implemented.
- The only known natural population lives in a refugium in Big Bend National Park, so a small environmental change could eliminate the species from the wild.

**Management history**

- Spring 4 mosquitofish eradication in 1956 failed, and all fish removed for holding died.
- A refugium built in 1956–57 became dominated by mosquitofish. The refugium near Spring 1 was built in 1960 and stocked with twenty fish on August 7, 1960.
- A constant flow-through system fed by Spring 1 was established in late 1976 to buffer thermal extremes.
- Fish were first taken to Dexter National Fish Hatchery in 1974. Maintaining a few fish in warm hatch-house water has allowed the population to survive since 1981.

**Recovery priorities (Step-Down Outline and Implementation Schedule)**

1. **Maintain and enhance the existing population and habitat (Priority 1):**
   - Identify population needs, including competition, predation, diet, survivorship, and disease.
   - Supplement spring flow with wells upstream of Springs 1 and 4, prioritizing stable stenothermal conditions over flow rate.
   - Redirect Spring 4 flow into its original channel to approximate predevelopment stream conditions.
   - Extend the Spring 1 outflow as a slow, shaded creek, with vegetation monitored and cattails and bulrushes controlled.
   - Irrigate the campground from a well at least 50 m from the river, rather than from pumped river water.
   - Eradicate mosquitofish from campground ponds by drying, while recognizing that eradication from the Rio Grande is not feasible.
   - Close or relocate campsites near spring outflows.
2. **Establish populations in suitable locations:**
   - Only warm-spring habitats in the Boquillas Spring–Rio Grande Village area are suitable.
   - Transplant at least 100 females and 100 males from the Spring 1 refugium into the upper Spring 4 run, repeating efforts if initial introductions fail.
   - Stock the park's large overflow pool, which currently holds a large mosquitofish reservoir, after campground eradication.
   - Explore use of the species for mosquito control in the Big Bend area.
3. **Maintain a captive population:** Keep a reserve gene pool at Dexter National Fish Hatchery, checked annually for genetic integrity.
4. **Public information:** Produce an information pamphlet, interpretive programs, and news releases.
5. **Law enforcement:** Enforce Federal and State protection and maintain habitat integrity. Section 7 consultation requires that federal projects avoid adverse effects on listed species.

**Monitoring**

- Sample all known populations twice a year, in February and August, with 3 mm mesh seines.
- The refugium should yield 15 preserved specimens unless that exceeds 1% of the standing population.
- Monitoring should be paired with habitat-quality assessment, and an emergency response procedure should be ready if status declines.

**Review and implementation**

- Agency reviews (Texas Parks and Wildlife, National Park Service, and USFWS headquarters) led to revisions. These included prioritizing the species' water needs over campground domestic use, adding quantifiable goals, expanding subtasks, and rewriting the Implementation Schedule.
- The Implementation Schedule is a key funding and permit reference. Annual USFWS costs include about $5,000 per year for the captive population, $5,000 per year for law enforcement, and $3,000 for the information pamphlet.
