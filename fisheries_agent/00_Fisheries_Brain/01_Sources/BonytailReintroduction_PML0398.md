# Bonytail Reintroduction in the Upper Colorado River Basin (FY2000 Annual Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila

## Executive Summary

This FY2000 annual project report from the [[Colorado River Recovery Program]] covers the reintroduction of [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) to the Upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species is thought to be functionally extirpated there. The project stocks juvenile bonytail and tracks radio-tagged adults to define habitat use and test whether it overlaps with that of [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*). The project ran from 1996 to 2000, was led by the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] (UDWR), and spent its full FY2000 budget of $76,000. Status is reported as "on-track and ongoing." The report concludes that more fish must be stocked each year and that the current coded-wire tagging method limits the biological information available for managing stocking.

## Background & Description

- **Target species:** Bonytail (*Gila elegans*), a native Colorado River fish now largely absent from the Upper Basin. Roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*) is the comparison species.
- **Project goal:** Re-establish bonytail in the Upper Colorado River Basin.
- **Methods:** Stocking of juveniles, radio-telemetry of adult bonytail, and monitoring by [[Electrofishing]], [[Seining]], and [[Trammel Netting]].
- **Fish source:** Fish were hatched at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and reared at the UDWR [[Wahweap Hatchery]] (Big Water, Utah). All stocked fish carried [[Coded Wire Tag]]s.
- **Program link:** The work supports the Recovery Program's genetic-integrity and population-restoration goals, specifically the action to implement a basin-wide bonytail restoration plan.
- **Study schedule:** Initial year 1996; final year 2000.
- **Project leads:** Pete Cavalli (UDWR, Price) and J. Michael Hudson (UDWR, Moab Field Station). Flow-related work (Objective 4) was conducted by Dr. Todd A. Crowl of [[Utah State University]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking sites and habitat**
- The [[Green River]] stocking site (near river mile RM 120–122) has little [[Swift Water]] habitat and little woody debris.
- The [[Colorado River]] site at Dewey Bridge (RM 94.7) has more of the habitat types bonytail appear to prefer.
- Bonytail captured by electrofishing were mostly in swift-water habitats (riffles, cobble bars, rapid bases) or near woody debris. Swift water and woody debris are both uncommon at the Green River site, so the authors suggest a habitat preference, though they caution the pattern is not yet confirmed.

**Movement patterns**
- Most captured bonytail were found within a few river miles of the stocking site soon after release. A few were found many miles downstream, and some were found upstream of the Colorado River site a few days after fall stocking.
- Radio-tagged adults showed no single movement pattern. Some moved far downstream, some moved downstream then back upstream, and some stayed relatively sedentary. Several fish appeared to share a habitat patch, especially in the first month after stocking.
- Movement of fish appeared to begin about one month after stocking, when discharge began to rise.

**Overlap with roundtail chub**
- No wild roundtail chub of suitable size were captured in the study area, so the planned radio-telemetry comparison with bonytail could not be carried out.

## Key Findings

### 1. Stocking Effort (Objective 1)
- **Cumulative totals to date:** Over 56,000 bonytail stocked in the Colorado River, over 81,000 in the Green River, and 10,000 in Dinosaur National Monument (about 5,000 each in the Yampa and Lodore reaches).
- **FY2000 stocking:**
  - Green River: about 20,000 fish in April and about 48,000 in October.
  - Colorado River at Dewey Bridge (RM 94.7): about 15,000 in April and about 21,000 in November.
  - Dinosaur National Monument: 10,000 in July.
- Most fish were stocked after holding in ponds for only one year; some were held for two or more years.
- Stocking was higher in FY2000 than in previous years.

### 2. Recapture and Monitoring (Objective 2)
- Electrofishing and seining were the main capture methods. Limited [[Trammel Netting]] caught larger bonytail than the other gear.
- Total stocked bonytail captured in FY2000 was low but higher than in previous years. The field-season table gives 217 fish for the Green River and 186 for the Colorado River, with mean lengths and size ranges reported by gear type.
- Nearly all recaptured fish appeared to come from the most recent stockings. However, stocking date, location, and size at stocking cannot be determined without killing fish to read the coded-wire tag. Growth rates and movement cannot be measured either.
- **Task 2:** A draft final stocking evaluation report is due in May 2001.

### 3. Radio-Telemetry of Adults (Objective 3)
- **Sample:** 14 adult bonytail were radio-tagged and released in the Green River at RM 120.0 on March 9, 2000. Tags weighed 6 g with a battery life of about 90 days.
- **Tracking:** Fish were located by moving downstream with a scanning receiver and triangulating positions. Monitoring covered the release area (most intensive), Tusher Wash Diversion Dam (RM 128.5) to Mineral Bottom (RM 52.2), and Mineral Bottom to the Colorado River confluence (RM 0.0). One fish was tracked intensively for 24 hours.
- **Duration:** Most fish were followed for at least one month; some for more than four months.
- **Extreme movements (Table 3):** Greatest distances from the release site ranged from about 0 to more than 76 river miles, with downstream movements recorded as negative values. Examples include 76.5 river miles downstream for one fish and 13.0 river miles upstream for another. Several fish were recontacted through June and July 2000.
- **Note:** The report gives the release site as RM 122.0 in one section and RM 120.0 in another. The Table 3 caption uses RM 120.0.
- **Task 2:** Telemetry results will be included in the May 2001 draft report.

### 4. Management Recommendations
1. **Stocking volume:** Continue stocking according to the approved annual plan. The authors conclude that more than a few thousand fish per year are needed to recapture fish in later years. The best size and timing of stocking are not yet known.
2. **Tagging method:** Coded-wire tags do not yet provide enough information for sound decisions. Either approve lethal sampling to read the tags or switch to [[PIT Tag]]s, so that stocking methods can be evaluated.
3. **Future release sites:**
   - Consider stocking further upstream on the Green River, for example at [[Nefertiti Rapid]] in [[Gray Canyon]], to reach swift-water and woody-debris habitat.
   - On the Colorado River, release bonytail at RM 110.5 so that more preferred habitat lies downstream of the release point.
