# Fish Bulletin 136: Ecological Studies of the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta, Part II: Fishes of the Delta

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae

## Executive Summary

Fish Bulletin 136 (California Department of Fish and Game, 1966), compiled by [[Jerry L. Turner]] and D. W. Kelley, is the second volume of the [[Delta Fish and Wildlife Protection Study]]. It reports twelve papers on the distribution, relative abundance, food and spawning habits of fishes in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]], sampled monthly from September 1963 to August 1964 at 16 stations. The work was funded through the California Water Bond Act to answer questions raised by proposed water development. The bulletin's central theme is [[Striped Bass]], which receives five of the twelve papers, and the study concludes that the [[Peripheral Canal]] plan should be operated to protect and enhance these resources. The authors' explicit aims were to evaluate the effects of water development on fish, and the data are presented as a baseline for that purpose.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Delta receives flows from roughly 26,000 square miles of the Central Valley. It is a reclaimed tidal marsh of about 30 large farmed islands, bordered by some 700 miles of sloughs and channels, with tidal fluctuations of 2 to 3 feet.
- **Purpose:** Water development under the [[California Water Plan]] was expected to change flow patterns, water quality and salinity intrusion. The study sought to understand the factors governing fish distribution and abundance.
- **Sampling design:** Monthly collections at 16 stations, with three gear types: set [[Gill Net]]s for large fish, an [[Otter Trawl]] for small bottom-dwelling fish, and a [[Midwater Trawl]] for small mid-depth fish. Trawl tows lasted 10 minutes; gill nets were set overnight.
- **Measurements:** Fork length was recorded, stomach contents examined, maturity staged on a four-stage female scale (immature, developing, ripe, spent), and temperature, turbidity, salinity and conductivity measured alongside catches.
- **Seasons:** Fall (September–November), winter (December–February), spring (March–May) and summer (June–August).

The bulletin's contents comprise papers on striped bass (adult, spawning, young, juvenile and food habits), American shad, king salmon and steelhead, smelt, sturgeon and starry flounder, ictalurid fishes (catfish), centrarchid fishes (sunfish), cyprinid fishes (carp and relatives), and threadfin shad, tule perch, sculpins and crayfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Delta supports many species through part or all of their life cycles. Key environmental drivers identified in the bulletin include:

- **Salinity and total dissolved solids (TDS):** Spring runoff controls salinity. In 1964, a dry year, the San Joaquin River above Stockton held 650 to 1,000 ppm TDS, which blocked upstream striped bass migration. Irrigation return flows give the San Joaquin high sodium chloride concentrations in dry years.
- **Temperature:** Striped bass spawning began at 14.4 to 15.6°C, peaked near 18.3°C, and stopped after sudden cold snaps.
- **Turbidity:** Secchi readings ranged from 5.1 to 35.4 inches. The authors suggest turbidity may limit the growth of centrarchids and the feeding of catfish.
- **Food web:** The mysid shrimp [[Neomysis awatschensis]] and the amphipod [[Corophium]] were the dominant invertebrate prey for most species, while [[Threadfin Shad]] and small striped bass formed the main forage fish.
- **Habitat use:** Dead-end sloughs (Hog, Sycamore, Indian) held most centrarchids and bullheads. Flooded islands and the lower San Joaquin River were key nursery and overwintering areas.
- **Introduced species:** Most catfish, centrarchids, carp, goldfish and threadfin shad are introduced. Only the Sacramento perch is native among the centrarchids.

## Key Findings

**Striped bass (Papers 2–6)**
- Roughly 6,000 adult and subadult bass were caught by gill nets. Few were present in fall and winter, with a large spring spawning run led by males.
- Bass entered the Sacramento River freely, but few migrated up the upper San Joaquin River or into the extreme south Delta. Elevated TDS was the best explanation. Radtke concludes that current velocity alone did not block migration.
- Farley's spawning study, based on egg and larva sampling at 26 stations in 1963 and 33 in 1964, found that spawning began at 14.4 to 15.0°C and that no significant spawning occurred where TDS exceeded 180 ppm. Spawning in the Sacramento River was farther upstream in the cool, wet year of 1963 (river miles 50–170) than in the warm, dry year of 1964 (below river mile 60).
- Estimated egg production from 1964 was about 66 percent from the Sacramento River and 34 percent from the San Joaquin Delta.
- Young bass (1963 year-class) were most abundant in fall. They emigrated to San Pablo Bay, and the lower San Joaquin River was the most important nursery. Otter trawls were estimated to be about 4.7 times more efficient than midwater trawls.
- Juvenile males matured in spring while juvenile females did not. Juvenile striped bass also emigrated in fall; the lower San Joaquin River was again the most important nursery.
- Food habits (8,628 stomachs analyzed): Young bass ate mainly Neomysis and Corophium. Juveniles, subadults and adults shifted to threadfin shad and small striped bass. Adult feeding was sharply reduced during spawning.
- Growth and condition of young bass were best in the lower San Joaquin River, associated with greater food intake.

**American shad (Paper 7)**
- 269 adult stomachs were examined. Adults were abundant only during the spring spawning migration, and the Sacramento and Mokelumne systems supported larger runs than the San Joaquin.
- Adult shad fed mainly on Neomysis, copepods and cladocerans, with food occurrence tied to zooplankton concentrations.
- Young shad were abundant from July through November and migrated seaward through the fall.

**King salmon and steelhead (Paper 8)**
- Only 50 adult king salmon were taken. Of 47 adult stomachs examined, 46 were empty.
- Of 469 young king salmon stomachs, 322 contained food, and insects were the primary food. The downstream peak occurred in May and June 1964, later than past reports from the 1940s and 1950s.
- Only 30 adult and 15 young steelhead were caught. Adult steelhead did not feed in the Delta.

**Smelt, sturgeon and starry flounder (Paper 9)**
- Pond smelt (1,960 midwater and 461 otter trawl) and Sacramento smelt (45 and 51) entered the Delta in winter to spawn. Single size groups dominated, suggesting heavy adult mortality after spawning.
- Juvenile white sturgeon (75 gill net, 35 otter trawl) were present all year. Over 60 percent were caught in the Sacramento River.
- Green sturgeon (138 gill net, 28 otter trawl) were concentrated at Santa Clara Shoal in summer.
- Sturgeon fed mainly on Neomysis and Corophium.
- 273 starry flounder were caught in the otter trawl, mostly young-of-the-year.

**Ictalurid fishes (Paper 10)**
- The white catfish made up over 97 percent of the total catfish catch, with more than 25,000 caught. Growth was slow compared with Santee-Cooper Reservoir, possibly due to limited food.
- Brown and black bullheads (189 total) were concentrated in dead-end sloughs.
- Channel catfish (571) were concentrated in swift river channels. None were taken in the western Delta.
- Young white catfish (967 stomachs) fed almost entirely on Corophium, Neomysis and tendipedids.

**Centrarchid fishes (Paper 11)**
- Black crappie, bluegill and warmouth were the most abundant centrarchids. Of 11,699 caught, 10,670 came from dead-end sloughs.
- Fish were unusually small, with only a third of black crappie over 20 cm.
- Largemouth bass were rare (34 caught); Sacramento perch and white crappie were extremely rare (one each).
- Black crappie diet shifted from invertebrates (small fish) to forage fish with increasing size.

**Cyprinid fishes (Paper 12)**
- Carp (10,452 caught) were most abundant. Catches peaked at Mossdale in May–June. Carp did not appear to spawn in the Delta, and the authors suggest a post-spawning migration from the upper San Joaquin.
- Sacramento blackfish (394; 73 percent at Mossdale) and Sacramento hitch (251) were concentrated in high-TDS water at Mossdale.
- Sacramento squawfish (never taken at Mossdale) were concentrated in the Sacramento River drainage.

**Threadfin shad, tule perch, sculpins and crayfish (Paper 13)**
- More than 64,000 threadfin shad were captured, mostly with the midwater trawl, and abundance peaked in September.
- Winter water temperatures appeared to cause heavy mortality.
- Diet was mainly crustacean plankton and plant material.
- The authors judge competition between young striped bass and threadfin shad unlikely, because few young bass inhabit the threadfin-rich areas.
