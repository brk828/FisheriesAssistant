# Literature Review of the Striped Bass Roccus saxatilis (Walbaum)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Serranidae #anadromous-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1969 literature review by Gary Edwards synthesizes existing knowledge on the [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*) in preparation for studying a population in the [[Colorado River]] between Davis and Parker Dams. The review covers taxonomy, distribution, feeding ecology, growth, reproduction, early life history, migrations, population dynamics, and hatchery production. Native to the [[Atlantic Coast]] from Canada to Florida and the Gulf of Mexico, striped bass were successfully introduced to the [[Pacific Coast]] in 1879-1882 and have established landlocked populations in reservoirs including [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]]. The species supports major commercial and recreational fisheries on both coasts but has experienced population declines attributed to pollution, dam construction, and possibly overfishing. Recent advances in hatchery production show promise for establishing additional inland populations.

## Background & Description

The [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis* Walbaum) is a bony fish of order [[Perciformes]], family [[Serranidae]] (sea bass family). It has three North American relatives: [[White Bass]] (*Roccus chrysops*), [[White Perch]] (*Roccus americanus*), and [[Yellow Bass]] (*Roccus interruptus*).

The species is highly euryhaline, frequenting brackish or fresh water with wide temperature tolerance, though becoming inactive below 40°F. Striped bass are coastal in habit, seldom found more than a few miles from shore. Spawning occurs in fresh water requiring extensive spawning grounds where ova drift in current. Young remain in fresh water initially before typically moving to sea.

Native range extends along the Atlantic coast from the [[St. Lawrence River]], Canada, to the [[St. Johns River]], northeastern Florida, and Gulf of Mexico tributaries from western Florida to [[Lake Pontchartrain]], Louisiana. The center of abundance is from [[Cape Cod]] to northern North Carolina, including [[Chesapeake Bay]]. Following introductions from New Jersey stock in 1879 and 1882, Pacific populations now range from San Diego, California, to the [[Columbia River]], Oregon. Landlocked populations were discovered in 1957 in [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]] (South Carolina) and [[Kerr Reservoir]] (North Carolina/Virginia).

Sexual dimorphism is not externally evident except when fish are running ripe. Gonadal biopsy proved 94.7% accurate for sex determination. Scales are ctenoid with radii only on the anterior field, forming 100-150 circuli during one year's growth. Scale analysis is validated as the best age determination method, though scales from bass over eight years become difficult to read due to thickening of the central portion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Striped Bass]] are voracious carnivores whose diet reflects prey availability more than selectivity, with one exception: in [[Chesapeake Bay]], anchovies ([[Engraulidae]]) occurred at higher frequency in stomachs than the more numerous silversides ([[Atherinidae]]). No evidence exists of striped bass feeding on their own young. Feeding intensity varies seasonally (heaviest in summer) and diurnally (peaks in early evening and morning).

Ontogenetic dietary shifts are pronounced. Young feed largely near bottom on crustaceans and small invertebrates. During their second summer they begin schooling and feeding heavily on other schooling fish species.

**Atlantic Coast Diet**: In [[Chesapeake Bay]], analysis of 1,736 stomachs (June 1936-April 1938) showed 969 (55%) contained food; fishes comprised 95.5% by weight; 26 different fish species were identified; crustaceans varied 0-46.3% in occurrence but comprised less than 2% of total weight.

**Pacific Coast Diet**: In [[San Francisco Bay]], 387 stomachs showed seasonal variation. Summer: 72% contained food; shrimp (*Crago*) present in 35% and comprised 53% of volume; anchovies (*Engraulis mordax*) ranked second. Winter: 42% empty; small fish remains comprised 64% of volume; mysid shrimp (*Neomysis*) most frequent, comprising 20% of volume. Young-of-the-year in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] fed primarily on shrimp (*Neomysis*), copepods, *Corophium*, and chironomid larvae; fishes were unimportant.

**Landlocked Population Diet**: In [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]], 1,500 adult stomachs showed clupeid fish (principally *Dorosoma*) support the population except in spring when mayfly nymphs become dominant (April-June).

**Habitat Requirements**: Spawning occurs in fresh water with preference for rapids or strong current. Water temperature is the principal factor controlling spawning timing, with minimal spawning at 58°F, peak activity at 60-65°F, and rapid decline thereafter. Current velocity of approximately one foot per second is required to maintain egg suspension in fresh water. Considering the two-day hatching period, approximately 50 miles of channel flowing at one foot per second would be required to maintain suspension throughout development.

**Population Structure**: Based on meristic studies, the Atlantic Coast population has differentiated into several "races." Three sub-populations exist within [[Chesapeake Bay]] tributaries. In North Carolina, an endemic race is differentiated into an upstream form (may not go to sea) and a downstream form (does go to sea). Gulf of Mexico populations form a distinctive race separated from the southeastern Atlantic by the Florida peninsula.

## Key Findings

**Growth Patterns**: Young striped bass grow most rapidly in late spring and early summer; older bass increase rapidly in length during summer and early autumn, with growth greatly slowed in late autumn and winter. Males and females grow at similar rates for the first three years. After age four, females grow significantly faster, maintaining consistent size differences. Greatest annual increment for both sexes (160 mm males, 165 mm females) occurs in the second year in [[Chesapeake Bay]]. In [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]], greatest growth increment occurred in the first year, attributed to the long spawning season.

California growth rates are similar to other populations, but Robinson (1960) found significant increases in growth rate over the previous 30 years, occurring after the second year and greater in females (approximately 10% increase in length, 25% in weight).

Average lengths by age class show consistency across regions: Age I ranges 6.4-8.5 inches; Age V ranges 24.0-25.8 inches; Age IX reaches 31.0-31.1 inches where data exist.

**Size Records**: Fish commonly taken by sport and commercial fishermen weigh 5-10 pounds; 25-30 pound fish are not rare. Fish over 50 pounds were once common but are now rare; those exceeding 60 pounds are exceedingly rare. The two largest striped bass on record each weighed 125 pounds, taken at Edenton, North Carolina in commercial nets. On the Pacific Coast, no fish over 100 pounds is known; records include a 78-pound fish in 1910 and a 62-pound specimen caught near Napa, California in 1911.

**Reproductive Biology**: The species is anadromous (excluding landlocked populations), spawning April-July. Most males mature at two years (all by three years). Only 25% of females spawn at four years, 75% are mature at five years, and 95% at six years in Connecticut and [[Chesapeake Bay]] populations. In [[Coos Bay]], Oregon, all second-year females were immature, 18.2% of third-year and 67.9% of fourth-year females were mature.

Males are disproportionately abundant on spawning grounds on both coasts; 50-60 small males may accompany a single larger female (weighing 5-10 pounds). Fecundity averages near 100,000 eggs per pound of body weight. In fifteen [[Coos Bay]] females, egg numbers ranged from 900,000 in an 8.8-pound fish to 4,775,000 in a 50-pound fish. Egg number per female increases with size up to at least 14 years of age.

Spawning dates vary with latitude: [[Coos Bay]], Oregon (mid-May to end of June, peak June 10); California (late March to early July, peak May); [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]] (April 8 to June 2, peak April 21-May 5).

**Early Life History**: Eggs are pelagic, buoyant but slightly heavier than fresh water, spherical, transparent, non-adhesive, and relatively large (1.3 mm unfertilized, 3.4 mm when fertilized and water-hardened). The granular yolk sac is green in living eggs, whitish-yellow when preserved (1.2 mm diameter). Single amber-colored oil globule measures 0.6 mm diameter.

Prolarvae hatch in 36-73 hours at average water temperature of 63°F, ranging 2.9-3.7 mm total length. They are relatively undeveloped with no mouth opening, unpigmented eyes, and greatly enlarged yolk sac and oil globule. At 5-6 mm length, yolk sac and oil globule are partially or wholly assimilated and they become postlarvae. Between 10-20 mm all postlarvae complete metamorphosis with segmented fin-rays; they are obviously distinguishable as serranids with most meristic characters well developed. Between 20-30 mm, scales appear on all young and full complement of meristic structures is attained except pectoral fin-rays.

Immediately after hatching, movements are directed largely by the anterior yolk sac. After 1-2 days, swimming ability develops sufficiently to remain near surface. Larvae are positively phototactic in aquaria. Egg suspension is one of the most important factors for egg survival. Temperature fluctuations and constant sunlight exposure do not appear to limit egg and larval survival. Starvation is believed the principal cause of larval mortality.

**Migration Patterns**: Striped bass are gregarious, traveling in large schools in later life. During first and second years they remain in small feeding groups, but by end of second summer form large schools. Some populations undertake non-spawning migrations; spawning migrations involve upstream runs.

On the [[Atlantic Coast]], tagging operations in [[Chesapeake Bay]] and North Carolina showed each area generally maintains its own populations. However, during certain years some bass vacate native waters and undertake northward coastal migrations. Rapid increases in northern catches coincide with previous strong year-class production in [[Chesapeake Bay]]. In New Jersey waters, winter populations begin moving as early as March, migrating to New England and remaining through summer.

On the [[Pacific Coast]], mass movements occur up into fresh water of the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] in spring, where bass disperse and spawn before returning to salt water for summer. In [[Coos Bay]], Oregon, two migrations occur: spawning migration upstream in spring and migration into sloughs in autumn. No evidence exists of alongshore migrations on the Pacific Coast.

**Population Status and Threats**: Abundance is relative and varies geographically. On the Atlantic Coast, bass were once common between North Carolina and Canada, but recent history shows decline broken occasionally by short periods of abundance from large year-classes. In [[San Francisco Bay]], seasonal patterns of fishing quality and average weight repeat year after year. Until at least 1950, populations appeared constant with slight downward trend.

Pollution, dam building, and possibly overfishing are important factors contributing to Atlantic Coast population decreases. Striped bass historically used lower reaches of practically all large Atlantic rivers as spawning areas. Pollution and silting from increased human population, industrial growth, and watershed destruction have greatly reduced spawning areas and forage grounds for young bass.

Overfishing vulnerability stems from the species being easily caught, always close inshore in relatively shallow water, with no offshore reservoir. Of the dominant 1934 year class, approximately 40% were taken during first year in fishery (1936), and at least 25-30% of remainder caught in 1937.

In California, large water diversions from nursery grounds threaten the fishery. Studies concluded diversions might ultimately destroy as many as 25% of fish-of-the-year at approximately 25 mm length. In recent years, young fry have been collected from diversion developments and relocated in California and adjoining states.

**Fishery Value**: The striped bass is considered the outstanding anadromous sport fish along the [[Atlantic Coast]]. All along this coast, the species supports extensive commercial and recreational fisheries, yielding more than 5,000,000 pounds annually to market fishermen and approximately the same to sport fishermen.

Commercial fishing was abolished in California in 1935. Since then, sport fishermen catches have exceeded highest market catches. In 1948, an estimated 160,000 anglers (16% of all state fishermen) fished successfully for striped bass, capturing over 1,650,000. In [[San Francisco Bay]], trolling and surf-casting are successful, but greatest catch is made still-fishing with sardines. In 1953, California biologists valued the state's striped bass sport fishery at $18,000,000 based on average expenditure of $9.00 per angler-day.

**Hatchery Production**: Widespread interest has developed in establishing bass in reservoirs throughout the United States following establishment of landlocked populations in [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]]. Although problems in hatchery production of striped bass fry have apparently been overcome, attempts to establish populations from hatchery fry have met with general failure, probably due to predation and lack of essential requirements for early fry development. Introduction of fingerlings instead of fry may be a solution.

The most extensive program is carried on by the [[Edenton National Fish Hatchery]], South Carolina, including experimental production of hybrid *Roccus*. Percentage hatch on eggs fertilized with striped bass spawn was 26.3%, compared to 12% hatch from eggs fertilized with [[White Bass]] sperm. Hybrids had average survival rate of 72.2%; striped bass averaged 72.5%. Striped bass showed better percent gain in weight than hybrids. Best gains in hatchery ponds (1,438.2 pounds per acre) were obtained from ponds stocked at 25,000 fish per acre and fed ground herring, much more acceptable than pellets.

Stomach analyses showed striped bass preferred *Cyclops* over *Bosmina*; *Daphnia* was not used as frequently as it occurred. No correlation could be established between production and water quality. Only four genera of ectoparasites were found: *Trichodina*, *Scyphidia*, *Chilodonella*, and *Trichophrya*. High mortalities (up to 100%) occurred when fry were reared in troughs and concrete holding tanks. Of therapeutic and herbicidal compounds tested, copper sulfate was most toxic, polyotic least toxic.
