# Fishery Investigations at Fort Randall Reservoir, South Dakota, 1956 (Third Year of Study)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percidae #Centrarchidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary
This job completion report (Federal Aid project F-1-R-6) covers the third consecutive year of fishery study on [[Fort Randall Reservoir]] and its [[Tailwaters]] on the [[Missouri River]]. Net sampling showed a continuing shift from rough fishes toward pan and game fishes. [[Black Crappie]] dominated frame-net catches (71.5%), [[Sauger]] increased sharply, and [[Largemouth Bass]] reproduction was excellent. Growth was declining slightly from earlier years but remained average or better than in other waters. The reservoir fishery was severely under-harvested (about 1.28 fish and 0.57 lb per surface acre), while the tailwaters supported very heavy angling pressure. Recommendations include continued [[Carp Control]] by drawdowns, better access, a possible commercial fishery, no reservoir stocking, and relaxed harvest regulations.

## Background & Description
- Study carried out under the Federal Aid in Fish and Wildlife Restoration Act by the [[South Dakota Department of Game, Fish and Parks]]; author James T. Shields. Earlier reports covered 1954 and 1955.
- Pool elevation fluctuated 1311–1342 ft (31 ft), giving 13,500–72,000 surface acres; about 70,000 acres in the April–August field season. Average width about 1-1/4 miles, length 65–85 miles, maximum depth just over 100 ft at the dam.
- Tailwaters: about 200 surface acres below the dam, maximum depth about 25 ft.
- Methods: three study areas (I White Swan, III Platte Creek, V Kramer) netted twice each with three frame (fyke) nets, three 250-ft experimental nylon [[Gill Nets]] and a 40-ft small-mesh [[Seine]]; tailwater gill netting for sauger tagging. [[Scale Analysis]] covered 1,729 reservoir fish of 21 species plus 96 tailwater sauger. Condition factor "C" = W×10^5/L³ (weight in lb, length in inches).
- Seining covered 255,500 sq ft (5.87 acres): 3,200 fish, of which 2,126 were young-of-the-year and 1,074 minnows.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- No permanent stratification, owing to the large water throughput. Temporary stratification in late June: 17 °F difference over 90 ft. Usually less than 5 °F difference in July–August. Maximum surface temperature 79 °F (July 26); tailwaters maximum 75 °F (July 20).
- Oxygen was adequate for warm-water fishes at all depths. pH 7.7–8.3; alkalinity 138–185 ppm. Turbidity slight (up to 25 ppm only in the upstream end or along shores in wind); 10–15 ft of silt in the old channel upstream.
- Most species began growth when water reached about 60 °F (late May to early June); growth began about two weeks later than in 1955. About 85% of growth was complete by September 1.
- Spawning temperatures: [[Yellow Perch]] and sauger spawned before 55 °F; carp, buffalofishes, carpsucker and gar between 55 and 70 °F; bass, shad, bullheads, sunfishes and crappies only above 70 °F (mostly 75 °F or more).
- Depth distribution: most species were well distributed to 60–70 ft. Black crappie occurred mainly at under 20 ft; white crappie and perch at 30–50 ft. [[Goldeye]] and perch were found off the bottom in vertical nets. Crappies preferred the upstream areas, catfish the central and upper reaches, and perch the central and lower reaches.
- Winter mortality of young [[Gizzard Shad]] occurred in 1956–57; many dead shad were passed through the dam tunnels.
- No stocking in the reservoir. Experimental [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking began in the tailwaters (10,000 six-inch fingerlings, right pelvic fin clipped, August 1956).

## Key Findings
**Population composition**
- Average catch per frame-net set was 100 (1954), 108 (1955) and 114 (1956). Gill-net sets averaged 82 fish in 1955 and 65 in 1956, because adult gizzard shad were almost absent.
- Reproduction in 1956: excellent for bass; good for sauger, perch, bluegill and carpsucker; fair to poor for crappie, bullhead, drum, carp and buffalofish.

**Species notes**
- [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]]: 3 caught in 1956 versus 88 in 1955. Paddlefish were taken only in the tailwaters. A 1957 law legalized snagging, with a daily limit of two.
- [[Shortnose Gar]]: declining (gill-net catch 0.6; frame nets 0.1 versus 5.0 in 1954). A single age class has dominated for three years. Spawning just before carp drawdowns is a suspected cause.
- Gizzard shad: gill-net catch 3.6 (14.9 in 1955); 928 young seined versus 3,122 in 1955. Severe winter mortality is suspected.
- Goldeye: gill-net catch 7.8 versus 2.9 in 1955. Age I and II growth was 4.8 and 9.6 in. Condition 32.8. Spawned between May 25 and June 26.
- Northern pike: 3 netted and about 160 angled; growth excellent but reproduction poor.
- Bigmouth and smallmouth buffalo: steady decline, with reproductive failure in 1954–56 and drawdowns suspected.
- River carpsucker: stable; 60% of the catch was Age IV or older.
- Carp: 5.5% of frame-net catches (50.3% in 1954). Growth and condition declining. The 1953 year class made up 85% of the catch. Only 14 young seined; spawning June 6–14 was followed by a 1.5-ft drawdown.
- Channel catfish: 6.8% of gill-net catches; important in the tailwaters.
- Black bullhead: 1.0% of frame-net catches. Condition about 54 in Area III versus about 46 in Areas I and V.
- Largemouth bass: about 5,100 taken by anglers; spawned June 12–30; young were 8.4% of seined fish; reproduced successfully for four consecutive years.
- Hybrid green sunfish × bluegill appeared for the first time (0.6% of frame-net catches).
- Bluegill: 9.1% of frame-net catches; condition 75.3; spawned early July to about August 10.
- White crappie 5.4% and black crappie 71.5% of frame-net catches. Black crappie were dominated by the 1954 year class (74% Age II), grew fast and had condition 50.8.
- Yellow perch: 5.2% of frame-net and 11.2% of gill-net catches; growth rapid, condition below average; spawned May 10–20.
- Walleye: 20 netted (five times the 1955 number); growth above average.
- Sauger: 10.7% of gill-net catches (4.7% in 1955). Growth faster than elsewhere; fish over 25 in. and 7 lb taken in the tailwaters. As many as 103 were netted in one set of under an hour. Spawned early May.
- Freshwater drum: 6.9% of gill-net catches; growth rather slow; condition 39.3.

**Growth and maturity**
- Growth rates declined slightly since 1954 because of a shorter growing season, yet remained average or better than in other regions ([[Carlander]] handbook comparisons). Reservoir fishes matured at younger ages than elsewhere. Panfish and gamefish spawn by Age III; most rough fish mature at Age IV–V (goldeye and shad earlier).

**Creel census – reservoir**
- 47,485 fisherman-days (April–December), 16% more than in 1955; 2,244 anglers (4.7%) contacted.
- Catch 89,421 fish (39,876 lb) at 0.56 fish/hr. Composition by number: 81% panfish, 9% game fish, 10% rough fish. Bullhead 38.2% and perch 23.5% of the catch.
- Yield 1.28 fish and 0.57 lb per acre, only 1–2% of the harvestable surplus. Average trip 32.9 miles one way; non-residents 14.1%; 19% of anglers traveled over 50 miles.

**Creel census – tailwaters (April 1956–March 1957)**
- About 27,000 fishermen (down 14,800 from 1955 owing to poor winter fishing); 73,803 fish (58,625 lb); 369 fish and 293 lb per acre; 0.68 fish/hr.
- Sauger (41.1% of weight) and catfish (22.6%) dominated the catch by weight. Non-residents 31.3%.
- Combined use of 217,000 fisherman-days over 1954–56 implies a fishery value above $1 million (at $5 per fisherman-day).

**Sauger tagging**
- 919 sauger and 27 walleye tagged September–November 1956. Annual harvest from tag returns was about 25% (1954 tagging) and about 5% (1955), with an adjusted estimate of 38.1% versus 33.7% for 1955.
- Total annual mortality is estimated at about 60%. No over-harvest was indicated. Small fish (11–16 in.) apparently shed size #3 strap tags, so size #1 tags will be used in 1957.

**Recommendations**
1. Continue carp control through spawning-time drawdowns.
2. Improve access and recreational facilities.
3. Study a commercial fishery for surplus fish.
4. Continue the no-stocking policy for the reservoir.
5. Further relax harvest regulations.
6. Study methods to raise catches in diverse reservoir habitat.
7. Federal government to fund extensive reservoir fishery studies.
