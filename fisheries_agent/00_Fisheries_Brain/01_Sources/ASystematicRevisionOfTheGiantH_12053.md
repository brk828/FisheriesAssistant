# A Systematic Revision of the Giant Hairy-Scorpion Genus Hadrurus (Scorpionida: Vejovidae)

#arachnology #scorpion-taxonomy #vejovidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 monograph by [[Stanley C. Williams]], published in the *Occasional Papers of the California Academy of Sciences* (No. 87), is a systematic revision of the North American scorpion genus [[Hadrurus]]. The genus is treated as comprising seven species and three subspecies. The revision describes one new species, [[Hadrurus obscurus]], and two new subspecies, [[Hadrurus arizonensis pallidus]] and [[Hadrurus arizonensis austrinus]]. It also synonymizes [[Hadrurus thayeri]] under [[Hadrurus hirsutus]]. Species accounts include keys, redescriptions, measurement tables, distribution records and maps, and the author draws biogeographic and evolutionary conclusions about the genus.

## Background & Description

[[Hadrurus]] is among the largest, most heavy-bodied and most conspicuous scorpions in North America. The genus was first documented from specimens collected by John Xantus de Vesey near Cabo San Lucas, Baja California Sur, between 1859 and 1861. [[Horatio Wood]] described [[Hadrurus hirsutus]] (as *Buthus hirsutus*) in 1863, and [[Thorell]] erected the genus in 1876. Mature individuals are usually 100 to 127 mm in total length; second-instar nymphs are about 28 mm.

Diagnostic features include densely hirsute walking legs, dorsal metasomal keels and telson; pedipalps with granular keels and 8 or 9 oblique denticle rows per finger; and, in males, larger pectines with more teeth than in females. Species are separated mainly by:

- carapace and mesosomal color pattern
- metasomal coloration and inferior keel outlining
- hirsuteness of the metasomal intercarinal spaces and dorsal keels
- pectine tooth counts
- presence or absence of paired dorsal telson glands in adult males

The author notes that the genus had been confused for about a century because many early descriptions were based on juveniles or unique specimens with poor locality data. Preservation of large, impermeable specimens required injecting fixative into the mesosoma or slitting the pleural membranes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Species of [[Hadrurus]] are most abundant in desert habitats of western North America and on the desert islands of the [[Gulf of California]]. The genus is restricted to North America and occurs roughly between 18° and 45° N latitude.

Habitat associations described in the text include:

- *H. hirsutus*: coarse gravelly soils of the Cape region, Baja California Sur.
- *H. concolorous*: sandy soils, the Viscaino Desert and Magdalena Plain, and volcanic river valleys.
- *H. pinteri*: volcanic regions of the peninsula, never in predominantly sandy habitats.
- *H. arizonensis*: open Sonoran Desert basins dominated by Larrea–Franseria associations; *H. arizonensis pallidus* on sand dunes of the [[Colorado Desert]].
- *H. spadix*: arid regions from Oregon to Colorado, including the [[Death Valley]] area.

Ecological observations reported by the author:

- Species spend most of their life underground, in their own burrows or those of other animals.
- Adults emerge shortly after sunset and typically return underground before midnight.
- Juveniles are uncommon on the surface relative to adults.
- Seasonal surface activity leads to mass road crossings by mature males, interpreted as courtship migration.
- All modern species are obligate carnivores, taking lizards, small snakes, mice, insects and other arachnids, including scorpions.
- Individuals in laboratory conditions went up to six months between meals without apparent harm if water was supplied.
- Population density reached up to 1 individual per 2 m² in some Colorado and Viscaino desert habitats.

Venom notes from Guerrero, Mexico: the local name for *H. aztecus* is "alacran de caballo." Studies by Dr. Luis Gutierrez reported low venom virulence to humans and horses, with burning as the main initial effect and no proteolytic or systemic effects at 24 hours.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic results**
- Seven species and three subspecies are recognized in the genus.
- [[Hadrurus thayeri]] is placed in synonymy under [[Hadrurus hirsutus]], which is endemic to the southern Baja California peninsula. Its holotype is probably a topotype of *H. hirsutus*.
- New taxa: *Hadrurus obscurus* (new species; holotype from near Panoche Pass, San Benito County, California), *H. arizonensis pallidus* (holotype from 26 miles east of San Luis, Sonora), and *H. arizonensis austrinus* (holotype from 8 miles north of Bahia San Luis Gonzaga, Baja California Norte).
- Several earlier species were removed from *Hadrurus* and placed in *Hadruroides*. Records from Guatemala were judged erroneous.
- *H. aztecus* is restricted to central and southern Mexico; the author found no evidence it occurs in northern Mexico or the United States.

**Distribution and records**
- *H. hirsutus*: 29 localities in Baja California Sur.
- *H. aztecus*: 7 localities in Mexico (Veracruz, Puebla, Guerrero, Oaxaca); only two specimens were found in US research collections.
- *H. spadix*: 46 localities across Oregon, Idaho, California, Nevada, Utah, Colorado and Arizona.
- *H. concolorous*: 58 localities in Baja California Norte and Sur and Gulf islands.
- *H. pinteri*: 16 localities in Baja California, including two Gulf islands.
- *H. arizonensis arizonensis*: 81 localities in California, Nevada, Arizona and Sonora.
- *H. arizonensis pallidus*: 56 localities in California, Arizona, Sonora and Baja California Norte.
- *H. arizonensis austrinus*: 6 records, all from the Gulf coast of Baja California Norte.
- *H. obscurus*: 8 localities in California.
- Hybrids of *arizonensis* × *pallidus* were recorded from 25 localities along the Colorado Desert and Mojave/Sonoran desert boundary.

**Biogeographic and evolutionary conclusions**
- The genus is a North American group and is most diverse in recently formed arid habitats. The author concludes it is a relatively recent invader of temperate and subtropical areas, probably of tropical origin.
- Three subgroups are proposed: (1) *H. aztecus*, distantly related and possibly close to the ancestral stock; (2) *H. arizonensis*, *H. spadix* and *H. obscurus*; (3) *H. concolorous*, *H. pinteri* and *H. hirsutus*. Only the third group develops paired dorsal telson glands in adult males (in *pinteri* and *concolorous*).
- Much speciation is attributed to the Pleistocene and Recent. The habitats of *pallidus* and *austrinus* had not yet formed in the Pliocene, when the northern Colorado and Mojave deserts were under extensions of the Gulf of California.

**Methods and data**
- Studies relied on specimens from numerous institutions and on field expeditions in 1968 and 1969 to peninsular and Gulf island sites.
- Standard measurements and pectine counts are tabulated for each species (Tables 1–9). Example pectine counts: *H. hirsutus* 28–35 in males and 22–27 in females, based on 20 males and 20 females.
- Sampling methods included pit traps and ultraviolet detection. *H. pinteri* was rarely abundant in samples.

**Notable conclusions**
- Color and morphological variation within *H. concolorous* and *H. arizonensis* forms local color races rather than distinct subspecies in some areas.
- Intergradation between *arizonensis* and *pallidus* is clinal, and the author retained both as subspecies.
