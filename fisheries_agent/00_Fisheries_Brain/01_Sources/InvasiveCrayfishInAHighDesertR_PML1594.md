# Invasive Crayfish in a High Desert River: Implications of Concurrent Invaders and Climate Change

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This research article (Martinez, *Aquatic Invasions* 2012, vol. 7, issue 2, pp. 219–234) documents the explosive increase of invasive [[Virile Crayfish]] (*Orconectes virilis*) in the middle [[Yampa River]], a high desert tributary of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] (UCRB) in northwestern Colorado. Crayfish density and biomass were quantified in 2005 and 2006 using quadrat sampling. The study links the crayfish surge to a concurrent invasion by [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), a severe decline in small-bodied native fishes, and drought conditions in the early 2000s. The author argues that the two invaders interact through [[Hyperpredation]] (apparent competition), and that climate change will likely intensify these impacts. The paper concludes that all UCRB states should prohibit importation, movement, sale, possession and stocking of live crayfish.

## Background & Description

- **Study organism:** Virile crayfish is not native to the Colorado River Basin (CRB). It is a generalist, omnivorous, and highly invasive species. It has a wide native range in North America, and it is the only crayfish species in the Yampa River that is classed as invasive among the four highly invasive species documented there.
- **Native fish community:** The Yampa River contains 12 native fish species. Its middle reach (RK 75–224) is critical habitat for four endangered endemic fishes: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The reach also supports bluehead sucker, flannelmouth sucker, roundtail chub, speckled dace and mottled sculpin.
- **Nonnative fish:** At least 20 nonnative fish species occupy the Yampa River. Key predators include [[Channel Catfish]] (introduced 1944), [[Northern Pike]] and Smallmouth Bass. Northern pike and smallmouth bass were introduced via stocking into Elkhead Reservoir in the late 1970s. Smallmouth bass were flushed into the river during the 1992 reservoir draining.
- **Historical crayfish records:** Virile crayfish established in Crosho Lake near the river headwaters before or during the early 1950s. No crayfish were recorded in river surveys in 1951, the late 1970s, or 1978–1984. They appeared in fish diets from 1988–1991 and were found upstream of Craig in 1991.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area and sampling:** The study used three stations on the middle Yampa River: Juniper Springs (RK 145), Morgan Gulch (RK 165) and Milk Creek (RK 191). Sampling used 1 m² quadrats with hand-picking, in August 2005 and August 2006. Fifty-eight quadrats were sampled per year.
- **Physical conditions:** Mean discharge was 4.4 m³/s (2005) and 4.7 m³/s (2006). Water depth averaged 42 cm, and mean water velocity was about 0.3 m/s. Gravel was the dominant substrate (45% cover).
- **Drought and temperature:** Mean summertime stream temperature rose from 19.0 °C (pre-drought) to 20.6 °C (2000–2006). Water temperature stayed above 16 °C from late May to mid-June during 2000–2006, compared with late June during 1979–1999.
- **Climate projections:** Future droughts are predicted to recur with greater frequency and duration. Reduced flows and warmer water are expected to favor both invaders. Water development may also be accelerated.
- **Key habitat-temperature relationships:** Virile crayfish become more active above 15 °C, and their food consumption increases most steeply between 18 °C and 22 °C.

## Key Findings

- **Crayfish density:** Station densities were 3.6/m² (Juniper Springs), 10.4/m² (Morgan Gulch) and 22.1/m² (Milk Creek) in 2005. Weighted annual estimates were 6.4/m² in 2005 and 9.3/m² in 2006. The riverwide estimate was 7.9/m² for the mid-2000s.
- **Sample totals:** 923 crayfish were captured (483 in 2005, 440 in 2006). Including missed animals, 1,197 were counted (631 in 2005, 566 in 2006). Missed crayfish made up 23% (2005) and 22% (2006) of counts.
- **Biomass:** Riverwide crayfish biomass was 122 kg/ha. Non-crayfish macroinvertebrates were estimated at 28.5 kg/ha and fish at 92.2 kg/ha, so crayfish biomass roughly equaled the combined biomass of other macroinvertebrates and fish (120.7 kg/ha).
- **Body size:** Mean carapace length was 16.1 mm in 2005 and 19.2 mm in 2006. The length-mass relationship was M = 0.00014 CL^3.23 (R² = 0.99). Sex ratios were 0.97 (2005) and 1.17 (2006), with no significant departure from parity.
- **Habitat associations:** Crayfish density had a weak negative relationship with fine substrates (r = −0.13) and a slightly positive relationship with coarse substrates (r = 0.23).
- **Sampling method:** Missed-crayfish rates were similar between years and were not explained by depth, velocity or substrate (R² < 0.03). The author concludes the quadrat method is suited to wadeable, clear-water streams.
- **Hyperpredation:** Virile crayfish dominated smallmouth bass diets (52–78% of biomass) in 2003–2005, when small-bodied fish were scarce. Smallmouth bass relative weights stayed high (Wr 102 in 2003–2004; 104 in 2007), indicating no decline in condition.
- **Predator diet shifts:** Crayfish consumption by channel catfish rose from about 18% (1980s–1990s) to 54–82% (2003–2005). Consumption by northern pike rose from about 5% to 19–25%.
- **Drought effects:** Community shifts during the 2000s coincided with the drought. Crayfish populations appear resistant to drought, so severe drought may sustain crayfish dominance rather than eliminate it.
- **Invasion risks:** Four invasive crayfish species (papershell, ringed, rusty and virile) occur in the upper Yampa basin. Rusty crayfish now occur in the upper Yampa River and in reservoirs there.
- **Management recommendation:** Regulations vary across UCRB states, from restrictive to permissive. Because species identification is difficult in the field, species-specific bans are ineffective. The author recommends that all UCRB states prohibit the importation, movement, sale, possession and stocking of any live crayfish.
