## Executive Summary

This study by Deacon and Hardy (1984) examines the relationship between streamflow and recruitment success of the endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) in the Virgin River, a tributary of the Colorado River system. Using seining surveys from 1977–1982 across five stations (both above and below major irrigation diversions), the authors correlated mean size of fish in fall populations and percentage of juveniles with spring (April–June) streamflow and pre-collection streamflow. The central finding is that woundfin recruitment is strongly flow-dependent: streamflows below ~130 cfs during spring result in poor recruitment, streamflows between ~200-800 cfs (optimally 290-820 cfs) yield the best recruitment, and streamflows exceeding ~1000 cfs are again associated with poor recruitment. Populations below irrigation diversions (disturbed segments) are more vulnerable to low-flow-driven recruitment failure than those in undisturbed canyon reaches. The paper underscores that irrigation diversions compound natural low-flow stress, threatening the persistence of this endangered minnow.

## Background & Description

- **Adult size:** Woundfin grow to approximately 3.5 inches (90 mm) total length (T.L.).
- **Larval/juvenile size progression** (from [[Greger and Deacon 1982]]):
  - Newly hatched fry: ~7–8 mm T.L.
  - ~4 weeks post-hatch: ~20 mm T.L.
  - ~8 weeks post-hatch: ~40 mm T.L.
  - ~30-60 days post-hatch: 20-40 mm T.L. (defined "juvenile" size class for this study)
  - ~5 months (typical summer temperatures): ~60 mm T.L.
- **Population size structure as a recruitment proxy:** Mean size of individuals in fall populations is inversely related to recruitment success — smaller mean sizes indicate strong juvenile recruitment dominating the population; larger mean sizes indicate poor recruitment (older/larger adults dominate).
- No detailed external morphological description (fin rays, scale counts, coloration) is provided in this streamflow-focused paper; it references [[Cross 1978a]] for baseline biological description.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preference:** Woundfin are most frequently found in **runs over sandy bottoms** ([[Cross 1978a]]), within a river system characterized by high flow variability, unstable substrate, and high turbidity.
- **Spawning habitat:** Selects substrate of **rocks (5-10 cm diameter)** in current, at water temperatures of approximately **25°C** ([[Greger and Deacon 1982]]).
- **Thermal tolerance for development:** Young reach 20-40 mm T.L. in 30-60 days post-hatching at water temperatures of **19-26°C**.
- **Co-occurring native species:** [[Virgin Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*, also endangered), [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus (Pantosteus) clarki*).
- **Exotic/introduced species pressure:** 13 introduced fish species reported in the river ([[Cross 1975]]); exotics occur more frequently in disturbed/diverted river segments. A large permanent population of [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) is established below the Mesquite Diversion.
- **Range limits:** Upstream distribution limit is marked by La Verkin Spring (a warm, saline spring), likely due to salinity/temperature constraints.
- **Habitat disturbance:** Major anthropogenic stressors include the Hurricane Diversion (~50 cfs removed), a hydroelectric diversion (~35 cfs), the Washington Fields Diversion (~90 cfs), and the Mesquite Diversion (~60 cfs). These diversions physically alter banks/substrate (via heavy equipment for dam maintenance) in addition to reducing flow.
- **Flow regime as a niche-structuring force:** Extreme streamflow variability prevents establishment of aquatic macrophyte communities, contributing to a naturally low-diversity, flow-adapted fish fauna ([[Cross 1975]]).

## Key Findings

- **Critical low-flow threshold:** Mean spring (April-June) flows below **~100-130 cfs** are associated with the poorest woundfin recruitment; 1977 (lowest flow on record) showed the worst recruitment of the study period.
- **Optimal flow window:** Best recruitment occurs at mean spring flows of **~200-800 cfs**, with the narrower optimal juvenile-development flow range identified as **290-820 cfs** in undisturbed reaches.
- **High-flow threshold:** Mean spring flows exceeding **~1000 cfs** are also associated with relatively poor recruitment, though less severely than low flows; flash floods (e.g., 10 Sept 1980, 11 Aug 1981) may disproportionately harm juveniles.
- **Disturbed vs. undisturbed segments:** Below-diversion ("disturbed") reaches require **higher absolute flows** than undisturbed canyon reaches to achieve equivalent recruitment. Specifically, mean spring flow must exceed **183 cfs (Littlefield gage)** or **237 cfs (Hurricane gage)** for any reasonable recruitment below diversions, and flows >400 cfs are typically needed for good recruitment there.
- **1981 flow event:** Despite not being the lowest flow year, 1981 produced recruitment failure in disturbed segments equivalent to the historic low-flow year 1977, indicating diversions push effective flow near a minimum threshold incompatible with sustainable recruitment.
- **Juvenile percentage relationship:** In undisturbed reaches, of 11 collections at flows <130 cfs, only 2 had good juvenile populations; of 8 collections at 290-820 cfs, 7 had good juvenile populations — supporting the identified optimal flow band.
- **Spawning cue:** Spawning appears to coincide with **declining flows in spring**, consistent with [[Cross 1978a]]'s observation that low, clear water may initiate spawning.
- **Management implication:** Irrigation diversions adversely affect woundfin primarily by reducing streamflow during the critical spawning-to-recruitment window (spring through early summer), independent of physical habitat disturbance. This directly informs the species' status as [[Endangered Species]] and underlies subsequent [[Instream Flow Requirements]] policy for the Virgin River.
- **Species conservation context:** Woundfin and Virgin Roundtail Chub are both listed as endangered ([[Deacon et al. 1979]]); findings support the need for minimum instream flow protections to maintain viable native fish populations in the Virgin River.
