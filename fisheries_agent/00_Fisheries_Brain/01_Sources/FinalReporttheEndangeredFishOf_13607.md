# The Endangered Fish of Cataract Canyon: Final Report (BIO/WEST Report No. 134-3)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared by [[BIO/WEST Inc]] for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Contract No. 6-CS-40-03980), synthesizes four years (1985–1988) of the "Cataract Canyon Studies." The study sampled the lower 50 miles of the [[Green River]] and [[Colorado River]], the 16-mile whitewater reach of [[Cataract Canyon]], and the [[Lake Powell Inflow]] to address three objectives: locating endangered fish spawning sites, determining whether a [[Humpback Chub]] population exists in the canyon, and assessing how [[Lake Powell]] operations influence [[Colorado Squawfish]] nursery habitat.

Non-native species dominated the catch, and the four rare fishes ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]) made up only about 3% of fish captured. The study confirmed a previously unknown humpback chub population in Cataract Canyon, a possible bonytail enclave, and important nursery habitat for young Colorado squawfish in the lower Green River. It also recommended changes to the [[Sensitive Areas Document]] and a set of follow-up studies.

## Background & Description

The Cataract Canyon Studies were a federal response to the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. Interest in the area began in April 1980, when 45 adult Colorado squawfish were captured in upper Lake Powell during a striped bass study. Radiotelemetry work from 1982 to 1984 found larval and young-of-the-year (YOY) squawfish, suggesting local reproduction. A 1985 pilot study by [[Ecosystems Research Institute]] was followed by a three-year BIO/WEST contract (1986–1988).

Cataract Canyon is described as among the most difficult fisheries settings in the Upper Colorado Basin: a roadless area inside [[Canyonlands National Park]], flows that ranged from about 3,000 to 120,000 cfs between 1984 and 1988, and hazardous rapids. Sampling used four basic gear types (electrofishing, gill and trammel nets, seines, and drift nets) across eight habitat types. Twenty-nine trips were made (25 canyon trips, 4 confluence trips). Larval fish were identified by the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] in Fort Collins.

The report includes a [[Chub Biography]] supplement and a [[Cataract Canyon Database]] (dBASE III+ files). The front matter includes a quotation from [[John Wesley Powell]] (1869) describing the rapids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study regions.** The study area was divided into five regions:
1. Green River above the confluence (RM 50–0): low gradient (~3 ft/mile), silt substrate, the most backwaters.
2. Colorado River above the confluence (RM 50–0): sand substrate, second-highest backwater density.
3. Confluence to Brown Betty (RM 216.5–212.5): steeper (~5 ft/mile), with Lower Red Lake Canyon as a tributary.
4. Cataract Canyon (RM 212.4–201.5): about 11 miles of rapids averaging 8 ft/mile, with two spring-fed pools at Rapids #3 and #11.
5. Lake Powell Inflow (RM 201.4–195.0): a transition zone whose habitat changed sharply as sediment and [[Tamarisk]] stabilized sand bars.

**Hydrology and temperature.** Record wet years of 1983 and 1984 were followed by high flows in 1985 and 1986 (peaks near 62,000 and 70,000 cfs). The drier years of 1987 and 1988 peaked at about 40,000 and 28,000 cfs, with base flows of about 5,000 and 4,000 cfs. Water reached 25°C by mid-July in 1985 and early August in 1986, versus late June in 1987 and 1988.

**Habitat.** Shoreline habitats included tamarisk/willow, talus slopes, rock ledges, and vertical walls. The report notes that [[Tamarisk]] (an exotic phreatophyte) has been narrowing channels and reducing backwaters. The bankfull width of the Green River reportedly declined by 10–27% over two to three decades.

**Introduced species.** Twenty-three of the 31 species recorded were non-native. Two unexpected records were an adult [[Northern Pike]] in Cataract Canyon and an adult [[Kokanee Salmon]] at the Lake Powell inflow. Striped bass were recorded above Lake Powell for the first time, and small numbers of crayfish (*Orconectes virilis*) were noted as a possible added predation threat.

## Key Findings

**Species composition**
- 31 species from 11 families were captured (1985–1988): 23 non-native and 8 native. Of the natives, six were endemic to the Colorado Basin (Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail, razorback sucker, roundtail chub, flannelmouth sucker) and two were not (bluehead sucker, speckled dace).
- Non-natives made up over 95% of the catch; natives made up about 5%. Endangered species made up about 3%.
- Five species accounted for 90% of the catch: red shiner (~50%), sand shiner (~21%), channel catfish (~9%), carp (~6%), and fathead minnow (~5%).

**Rare fishes**
- Colorado squawfish: 4,348 captured, including 4,161 larvae/YOY, 175 juveniles, and 12 adults. Two adults had been tagged upstream by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, 120 and 172 miles away.
- Humpback chub: 108 captured (11 larvae, 19 YOY, 56 juveniles, 22 adults). Most were in Cataract Canyon, concentrated in a roughly 4-mile reach.
- Bonytail (suspected): 14 fish (1 YOY, 7 juveniles, 6 adults), taxonomically uncertain and requiring peer review.
- Razorback sucker: one adult, captured at the mouth of Salt Creek (RM 3.6) on September 11, 1987.

**Humpback chub population**
- The report confirms a self-sustaining population in Cataract Canyon, which may be a remnant of a larger population that existed before [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closed in 1963. Its morphology differs from other upper-basin populations.
- Spawning was suspected but not confirmed (no strippable eggs). A reach between RM 207.4 and 207.6 was identified as a likely site. The report recommends a "suspected spawning area" designation for RM 201.5–212.4.

**Non-native year class dynamics**
- Red shiners, sand shiners, and fathead minnows had their weakest year class in 1985 after high flows and rebounded to their strongest in 1988 (low water). Their densities increased three- to four-fold over two to three years.
- The authors conclude that periodic flooding is an ineffective control, and that only long-lasting measures would work.

**Spawning**
- Colorado squawfish spawning was suspected, not confirmed, at cobble bars. Nine were identified in the lower 50 miles of the Green River and four in the lower 50 miles of the Colorado River. Larval captures suggested local hatching around early June to late June in the study years.
- Spawning times varied by up to one month. In 1986, the late spawn (peak early August) produced YOY averaging 30.6 mm TL in early October, versus 34.0–38.7 mm for other cohorts.

**Nursery habitat and stranding**
- The lower 50 miles of the Green River is a high-density nursery area, with a seine catch rate of 72 fish/100 m² (maximum in the canyon was 32 fish/100 m²).
- About 20% of young squawfish (849 of 4,348) were found stranded in isolated pools, in over 60% of the pools sampled. The authors could not determine whether stranding is natural or flow-driven.
- The Lake Powell Inflow (RM 200–201) provided backwaters only seasonally. Backwater numbers peaked at eight when the lake was between 3692 and 3698 feet. The area yielded just 3% of squawfish larvae/YOY, and the authors did not recommend specific lake levels.

**Recommendations**
- Revise the [[Sensitive Areas Document]] classifications. Key proposals include extending the YOY nursery area from the confluence through Cataract Canyon, designating the lower 50 miles of the Green River as a suspected spawning area, and designating Cataract Canyon as an adult humpback chub distribution and concentration area.
- Future studies: (1) confirm humpback chub spawning with spring trips before runoff; (2) assess YOY overwinter survival with fall and March/April sampling; (3) sample the Lake Powell Inflow during spring runoff to test transport of endangered fish; (4) incorporate Cataract Canyon chubs into the ISMP; (5) describe the *Gila* complex through peer review of morphologic, meristic, genetic, and cytogenetic data.
