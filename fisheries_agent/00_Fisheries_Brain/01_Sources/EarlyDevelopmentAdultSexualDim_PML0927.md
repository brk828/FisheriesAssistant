# Early Development, Sexual Dimorphism, and Fecundity of the Razorback Sucker *Xyrauchen texanus*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1975 contract report, prepared by Eric S. Gustafson and W. L. Minckley of the [[Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory]] and Arizona State University, documents the early life-history development, adult sexual dimorphism, and fecundity of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) from Lake Mohave reservoir. The work was part of a five-year program, funded by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], intended to provide criteria for a management program to prevent extinction of this native genus. Embryos and larvae were reared from fish collected in 1975 and the descriptions were compiled from 1974 and 1975 specimens. The report also documents population threats, including disease, hybridization, and altered river flow.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and range:** The razorback sucker is a large catostomid fish endemic to the Colorado River basin, formerly abundant throughout the basin in western North America.
- **Decline:** The species has declined steadily over the twentieth century. It is protected by law only in Nevada and California, and its status in the upper basin was assessed by [[Holden and Stalnaker]] (1970) and [[Vanicek and Franklin]] (1970).
- **Study context:** Field work took place in March and April 1975 at two Lake Mohave localities: approximately 65 river miles north of [[Davis Dam]] (immediately below [[Hoover Dam]]) and a cove in Cottonwood Basin about 20 river miles north of Davis Dam.
- **Broodstock:** A total of 101 razorback suckers were netted; 69 males and 29 females were transported to the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Thirty-two additional fish were returned to the reservoir after treatment with potassium permanganate for net marks and external parasites.
- **Hormonal induction:** Females were injected with human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG); only one produced eggs upon stripping. About 5,000 ova were fertilized with sperm from two males.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors attribute the decline to several interacting factors:

- **Habitat modification:** Lowering of subsurface water, impoundment, channelization, and pollution of the Colorado River.
- **Competition and predation:** Introduction of exotic predaceous and competitive fishes, which appear related to a general decline of native fishes since the 1930s.
- **Flow fluctuations:** Lowered mean water temperatures and fluctuating downstream water levels below [[Hoover Dam]] destroy embryos along shorelines; stranded eggs were observed in 1975 when water levels dropped more than one meter.
- **Dam effects:** Davis Dam impeded upstream spawning migrations, which had been documented by earlier authors, and isolated the Lake Mohave population.
- **Pathogens:** More than 30 broodstock fish died at Willow Beach Hatchery from bacterial infections (*Pseudomonas* sp. and *Aeromonas* spp.), which were already present when the fish were removed from the reservoir.
- **Hybridization:** Field data indicated a minimum of 10 percent hybridization between *X. texanus* and an undetermined *Catostomus* species in Lake Mohave.
- **Reproduction:** Natural recruitment in the lower basin appears to be very low; the species is nearly extinct there, and the largest reproducing population is thought to occur in Lake Mohave.

## Key Findings

**Early development**
- Early development is divided into four phases after Hubbs (1943): embryonic, prolarval, postlarval, and juvenile. Staging was based on embryos from 1974 and 1975 specimens.
- Eggs from 1974 were about 2.9 mm in diameter and those from 1975 about 1.9 mm, but both sets attained equivalent lengths by hatching.
- Hatching occurred in about six days, with larvae feeding actively by about day 10. The mouth stays terminal until a ventral shift occurs by termination of the notochord, around day 75.
- Juveniles, defined by complete ossification of pelvic fin rays, grow relatively slowly; two-year-old fish averaged about 150 mm total length under laboratory conditions.

**Sexual dimorphism**
- Two characters are sexually dimorphic only during the reproductive season (November through June): nuptial tubercles and coloration.
- Four characters are dimorphic regardless of season: size (standard length and weight), relative pelvic and anal fin length, and the length and morphology of the urogenital papilla.
- Females are larger than males in the sampled populations. Males have relatively longer pelvic and anal fins, while the female urogenital papilla is substantially longer and broader, with a fleshy, rounded form. In males, the papilla is thinner and more acuminate.
- Male body weight ranged 1.38–2.90 kg (mean 2.07 kg); female weight ranged 2.10–6.67 kg (mean 3.04 kg).

**Fecundity**
- Fecundity appears high and increases with length and/or weight, consistent with the general pattern in fishes.
- Relative fecundity was expressed as eggs per cm of standard length. Estimates for four of five females were similar, but one fish had a proportionally larger ovary and considerably more eggs per cm; the reason is unknown.
- Samples of the largest fish (over 7.0 kg, the reported maximum size) were not obtained, so a fuller fecundity picture requires fish from other size classes.

**Management recommendations**
- Maintain selected young stocks in captivity to determine age at maturity and growth variation.
- Stock only fish of at least 20.0 cm to maximize survivorship.
- Monitor hybridization and track any changes in the population.
