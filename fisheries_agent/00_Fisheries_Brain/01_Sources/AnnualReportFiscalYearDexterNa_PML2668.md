# Dexter National Fish Hatchery Annual Report FY 1989

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Fiscal Year 1989 annual report from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in Dexter, New Mexico, documents the station's role in propagating, holding, and reintroducing imperiled freshwater fishes of the American Southwest. The hatchery, established under the White Act of 1931, shifted into the [[Endangered Species Act]] program in 1978. In FY 89 it held 13 imperiled species, reared razorback sucker, Colorado squawfish, and woundfin, and supported field surveys on the [[Pecos River]], [[San Juan River]], [[Lake Mohave]], and in Gila trout and Virgin River habitats. The report also covers disease outbreaks, water rights risks, and staffing needs.

## Background & Description

The station occupies 540 acres in the northern fringes of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] at an elevation of 3,500 feet, with about 12 inches of annual rainfall and a 180 to 200 day growing season. Water comes from three shallow aquifer wells (about 150 feet deep, capable of 1,800 gpm). The well water is a constant 64°F, alkaline (pH 7.5), with total hardness near 2,000 mg/L and total dissolved solids near 3,500 mg/L.

Culture facilities include a holding house with sixteen 360-gallon concrete tanks, a hatching battery, two 5' x 40' raceways, ten 3' x 10' fiberglass circular tanks, and 48 earthen ponds totaling about 22 surface acres. Station objectives include maintaining a viable gene pool of imperiled southwestern fishes, developing culture techniques, studying biological requirements, supplying live and preserved specimens, and running a public information program.

FY 89 holdings included, among others, [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Chihuahua Chub]], [[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]], [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]], [[Woundfin]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], [[Yaqui Catfish]], [[Leon Springs Pupfish]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Pecos Pupfish]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Big Bend Gambusia]]. Table 1 of the source lists each species with its scientific name and federal status (endangered, threatened, proposed, or candidate).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several threats appear throughout the report:

- **Hybridization and invasive species:** Massive hybridization between [[Pecos Pupfish]] and introduced [[Sheepshead Minnow]] is the primary threat to Pecos pupfish. Red shiners were removed from the Virgin River by rotenone treatment in 1988 and 1989.
- **Predation:** Overwinter survival of subadult razorbacks was poor, with predation by catfish suspected.
- **Water quality:** Groundwater pumping in Chaves County has increased salinity in refuge surface waters at [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]], ending fish stocking there.
- **Parasites and disease:** Ich (*Ichthyophthirius*) killed more than 70% of overwintered woundfin, and an Asian tapeworm persisted in beautiful shiners.
- **Habitat barriers:** A natural barrier on White Creek protects a newly found Gila trout population. Other surveyed streams lacked suitable barriers or habitat.

Cited regional threats include the [[Brantley Reservoir]] impoundment on the Pecos River, which eliminated native stream cyprinids from its collections by September 1989.

## Key Findings

**Razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**
- 14 females were spawned on 1 March 1989 using [[Chorionic Gonadotropin]].
- Egg take totaled 1,395,809 (mean 99,700 per female). Fry totaled 641,628, a 45% egg-to-swimup survival, up from 26% in 1988.
- Subadult distribution was 18,515 fish weighing 2,552 pounds. Shortfalls against commitments were due to poor overwinter survival.
- Broodstock of 526 fish was maintained, with about 19,000 young-of-year held for fall 1990 stocking. All broodfish were [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging]] (PIT) tagged in September.

**Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)**
- 22 females were spawned using carp pituitary. All spawned successfully.
- Egg take totaled 2,087,422 (mean 94,885 per female, range 57,980 to 165,100). Fry totaled 463,080.
- About 658 juveniles returned from fry ponds. Most fingerlings were distributed after 30 September and fell into FY 90 reporting.

**Bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*)**
- No fry were spawned because California cancelled its spring request. Natural spawning in holding ponds yielded 12,540 harvested fingerlings.
- 69 adults went to the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources for radio tracking in the Green River.

**Woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*)**
- Ich killed over 70% of overwintered fish. Treatment shifted to 10 days of daily formalin (25 ppm) and malachite green (0.1 ppm) with improved results.
- Overwinter losses were 21% (adults), 64% (subadults), 72% (Virgin River age group I), and 57 to 91% (age group 0).
- Spawning produced only 426 fry, far below the 484 produced by a single tank in 1989's comparison group.
- About 1,060 young-of-year were collected from the Virgin River on 29 August, with few losses during transport or treatment.

**Virgin River roundtail chub (*Gila robusta seminuda*)**
- 162 subadult and adult chubs were collected from a pond near Quail Creek Reservoir in March 1989 and transported to Dexter without mortality.
- Six females produced 59,584 eggs (mean 9,930 per female). Swimup fry totaled 20,540, a 34.4% survival rate.

**Other species**
- Chihuahua chub: 466 adult and juvenile fish held, with reintroduction production tentatively planned for 1990.
- Pahranagat roundtail chub: 100 adults and subadults held for a possible 1990 or 1991 introduction.
- Beautiful shiner: Tapeworm infection persisted. Fish were treated with praziquantel and 648 negative fish were moved to a pond.
- Yaqui catfish: Genetic testing of five frozen specimens confirmed possible introgression with channel catfish from the Rio Aros, Sonora. The stock of 59 fish now requires separating pure from hybrid individuals.
- Desert and Leon Springs pupfish: Healthy populations allowed 735 desert pupfish to be shipped to Arizona agencies and other institutions.
- Gila topminnow: 249 fish went to Red Rock Wildlife Area for refugium purposes.
- Big Bend gambusia: More than 8,000 fish were released in the fall, but only three were recovered in spring. The hatchery proposed transferring this species to [[Uvalde National Fish Hatchery]], where winters are milder.

**Field and management results**
- [[San Juan River]]: One adult Colorado squawfish was collected, and roundtail chub were found to be declining across their range.
- [[Lake Mohave]]: 389 razorback suckers were collected for a mark-recapture study, with 33 recaptures.
- [[Gila Trout]]: A new population in White Creek, a tributary of the West Fork of the Gila River, appears to be pure, based on preliminary mitochondrial DNA analysis.
- [[Pecos River]]: [[Pecos Pupfish]] were found at about 80 locations, with about 20 additional suitable sites. Bluntnose shiner occurred throughout the river between Roswell and the Texas state line. No blue suckers were collected.
- [[Mescalero Lake]]: A carp removal effort was initiated but was unsuccessful in FY 89. A trap-based project is planned for FY 90.

**Future outlook and needs**
- Water rights (1931 to 1933 priority dates) appear secure, but existing allotments are fully used. Expansion would require purchasing water or sealing ponds. Shortfalls are likely in the early 1990s.
- Storage is inadequate for vehicles and equipment, which are exposed to deterioration and liability concerns.
- A "Genetic Plan" for hatchery operations is needed, based on a review by Dr. Tony Echelle, along with a staff position with genetics expertise.
- An additional FTE is needed for fishery management, since one biologist is spread too thin.
