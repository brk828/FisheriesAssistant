# Solicitor's Opinion Request on Colorado River Endangered Fishes Section 7 Consultations (1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This April 1989 memorandum from the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Region 6) to the Regional Solicitor, USDI, asks for a legal opinion on the Service's approach to [[Section 7 Consultation]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] for water depletion projects affecting the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The Service has issued over 100 [[Biological Opinion]]s since 1978 and maintains that depletions threaten these fishes with jeopardy. Its response is the [[Colorado River Recovery Program]], which lets project proponents offset depletion impacts through a $10 per [[Acre-Foot]] contribution and legal protection of [[Instream Flow]]s under state law. The Service states that uncertainty over the timing of instream flow protection leads it to consider additional conservation measures for new large depletion projects. Two biological opinions are on hold pending the Solicitor's response, requested by May 10, 1989.

## Background & Description

- **Species covered:** The memorandum names three endangered Colorado River fishes: Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, and bonytail chub. It does not describe their morphology, life history, or taxonomy. The scientific names above are supplied for reference and are not from the source.
- **Regulatory history:** Since 1978 the Service has issued over 100 biological opinions on water development effects on these fishes. It has held that a jeopardy situation exists because of habitat loss, other factors, and declining fish numbers.
- **1984 draft conservation plan:** The Service proposed minimum flows for listed fishes throughout the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. Several states and water development organizations objected that this conflicted with state water rights systems and interstate compacts.
- **Coordinating Committee:** In August 1984 the Service formed the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Coordinating Committee]]. Participants included the Service, the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming, private water development interests, and environmental groups.
- **Recovery Program:** After nearly 4 years of discussion, data analysis, and negotiation, the committee produced "The Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin." It was implemented in January 1988.
- **Key sections:** Section 4.1.5 sets out the assumptions behind the Section 7 process (Attachment 1). Section 5.3.4 covers project proponent contributions.
- **Definitions:** A large depletion is any project depleting more than 3,000 af per year. The Service decides case by case whether smaller depletions are large or small, based on location, timing, and impact on the fishes.
- **Consultation status:** The Service is currently involved in several Section 7 consultations on large depletion projects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat conditions:** The fishes occupy large, turbid, and often inaccessible rivers, which makes flow-needs research difficult. Specific flow needs have been identified only for a few river reaches during a few months of the year.
- **Depletion impacts:** Depletions include flow reductions and corresponding changes in temperature, salinity, and turbidity.
- **Flow determination:** Preliminary flow determination for a 15-mile segment of the Colorado River (July through September) took nearly 3 years. The memo states that many more years of research will probably be needed across the Upper Basin.
- **Priority reaches:** The 15-mile reach of the Colorado River immediately upstream from the Gunnison River confluence is a high-priority area for water rights acquisition.
- **Other reaches and habitats:** Ruedi Reservoir releases must reach occupied habitat below the Grand Valley irrigation diversion dam. The memo also mentions Yampa River, Little Snake River, and Green River work, including nursery habitat monitoring and a sediment study.
- **Interstate issues:** Wyoming cannot protect instream flows within 1 mile of the state border, which may block water delivery to occupied habitat in Colorado and Utah.

## Key Findings

**Uncertainty about instream flow protection**
- Water rights acquisition depends on the Service quantifying the fishes' flow needs. Flow needs have been difficult to define because of scarce data and difficult field conditions.
- The Colorado Water Conservation Board is the only entity that can hold an instream flow right under Colorado law. Its progress has been slow, and its director has voiced concern about appropriating rights before flow needs are defined for all Upper Basin rivers.
- In Colorado, the federal government's enforcement of a state instream right is limited if the state does not defend or administer it.
- A filing for an original instream flow right in the 15-mile reach is expected to be opposed. The State Supreme Court may need to decide the issue, a process that could take 6 years.

**Cost and availability of water rights**
- Rights will be purchased only from willing sellers, with focus on a few "controlling" rights. On the Yampa River, the Juniper and Cross Mountain rights are key, and their holders have not been receptive to selling unless a smaller reservoir is built in lieu of the Juniper and Cross Mountain reservoirs.
- The value of the Juniper-Cross Mountain water rights has been estimated at $0 to $100 million.
- Ruedi Reservoir water is expected to sell for $50-60/af/year. Buying 50,000 af for a 2-month period would cost $2.5 million annually, which would deplete the anticipated $10 million acquisition fund in 5 years.
- Agricultural water rights sell for $200-$1000/af of transferable consumptive use.

**Recovery Program offsets and funding**
- Depletion impacts can be offset by a one-time contribution of $10 per af of average annual depletion, adjusted annually for inflation, and by legal protection of instream flows under state law.
- For a large depletion project to receive a favorable biological opinion, progress toward instream flow protection must be sufficient to offset its impacts. The Service decides this case by case, 30 days before the last federal permit is issued.
- Congress appropriated $1M to acquire water rights. A $2.3M FY-89 work plan funds 39 high-priority recovery projects/activities.

**Progress reported (April 1989, Attachment 2)**
- Signed cooperative agreement to implement the Recovery Program. Resolutions of support came from water user groups in Utah, Colorado, and Wyoming and several major environmental groups.
- 15-mile reach: Service flow recommendations for July-September are near completion. Work plan for October-June flow recommendations is complete. A Bureau of Reclamation agreement to deliver Ruedi releases to the head of the reach is near completion. Preliminary Colorado Water Conservation Board approval to file for a water right is in progress.
- Green River Basin: Juniper-Cross Mountain rights negotiations are in progress. Little Snake River rights negotiations and evaluation are in progress. Reoperation of Flaming Gorge is pending consultation. The Yampa River Phase I biological needs report is near completion.
- Monitoring and studies: Long-term monitoring of Green River nursery habitats is under discussion. A sediment study on the Little Snake and Yampa rivers is in progress.

**Requested action and interim measures**
- The Service is considering additional conservation measures or reasonable and prudent alternatives. The option under discussion would require project proponents to withhold a portion of annual project yield for instream flows until there is reasonable assurance that the Recovery Program will achieve adequate instream flow protection.
- The Service asks the Solicitor for the legal basis for its position and whether it has correctly interpreted its responsibilities under the Endangered Species Act. A response is requested by May 10, 1989, because two biological opinions are on hold.
