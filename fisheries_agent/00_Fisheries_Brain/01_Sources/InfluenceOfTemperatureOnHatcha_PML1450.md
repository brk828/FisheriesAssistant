# Influence of Temperature on Ova Survival and Hatchability of Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1982 contract report by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University Center for Environmental Studies), submitted to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, tested how incubation temperature affects survival and hatch of ova from four endangered "big river" fishes of the Colorado River basin: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Ova were incubated in a controlled aquarium system at nominal temperatures of 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 °C at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico. Hatch succeeded only at 15–25 °C, with all ova dying at 5, 10 and 30 °C. The author concludes that thermal conditions in many river reaches may preclude successful reproduction, but that other factors, notably predation by introduced fishes, must also explain population declines.

## Background & Description

The four species are described as "big river" fishes of the southwest, all in danger of extinction, with ranges and abundance sharply reduced since Anglo-American settlement. Recognized causes of decline include:

- dewatering of the Gila River and tributaries in the late 1800s and early 1900s;
- mainstream dams and reservoirs such as [[Hoover Dam]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]];
- altered flow, channel morphology and water quality caused by dams;
- introductions of exotic species.

Earlier spawning temperature estimates were largely observational: about 18–23 °C for Colorado Squawfish, 18–21 °C for Bonytail Chub, and 6–20 °C for Razorback Sucker. Prior incubation work by [[Bulkley et al. 1981]] and [[Hammon]] (1981, 1983a,b) provided quantitative data, but no defined thermal limits or optimum incubation range existed before this study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental setup.** Six 22-L aquaria held nominal temperatures of 5–30 °C. Five aquaria (5–25 °C) received continuous flow of well water (about 1.0 L/hr), and the 30 °C aquarium was static. Incubation chambers were 76 mm PVC pipe with 0.5 mm mesh bottoms. Well water at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] was high in sulfate and nitrate and conductive, but comparable to summer-flow native waters of the Colorado and Gila rivers.

**Broodstock.** Wild adults came from Lake Mohave (AZ–NV) and the Little Colorado River. Hatchery-bred fish came from Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery and Dexter. Adults were held at 17.5–18 °C for 3–6 days before induced spawning with HCG or carp pituitary. Each trial used 100 fertilized ova per chamber, with 3–6 replicate chambers per temperature.

**Measured conditions.** Actual mean temperatures were within ±0.7 °C of nominal, except that the 5 °C means were 7.3 °C (Bonytail, Humpback) and 6.9 °C (Squawfish). Dissolved oxygen stayed within 3% of saturation.

**Thermal history of habitats.** The author compared experimental results with historical and present-day river temperatures across basins:

- **Salt River:** 1959–1965 monthly means of 6–26 °C; temperatures above 15 °C from April to mid-October.
- **Gila River:** upper reaches 3–30 °C; tributaries 3–34 °C; the lower river had been dewatered by the late 1800s.
- **Lower Colorado River:** tailwater temperatures below Hoover Dam of 9.0–15.0 °C (1941–1980), which the author considers thermally constraining.
- **Green River (Flaming Gorge tailwaters):** post-closure temperatures of 0.0–22.0 °C at Jensen, with a sharp change in the seasonal regime.
- **Grand Canyon:** post-Lake Powell temperatures of about 9–15 °C year-round.

## Key Findings

**Temperature limits on hatch**

- [[Razorback Sucker]]: hatch was highest at 20 °C (35%), with 19% at 15 °C and 29% at 25 °C. Development rate equals zero at 9.6 °C (v = 0.81T − 7.71, r² = 0.99, N = 18).
- [[Bonytail Chub]]: greatest success at 15 and 20 °C (statistically equivalent); 21% at 25 °C; no hatch at 5, 10 or 30 °C. Biological zero is 8.6 °C (v = 0.83T − 7.09, r² = 0.77, N = 11).
- [[Humpback Chub]]: much lower hatch at 15 °C (0.8%) and 25 °C (22%); 6% at 20 °C per the trial summary. Biological zero is 11.0 °C (v = 1.17T − 12.94, r² = 0.90, N = 8).
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: 27% hatch at 20 °C and 9% at 25 °C in trial II (trial I values also reported). Biological zero is 12.2 °C (v = 1.41T − 17.26, r² = 0.91, N = 9), based on only two incubation temperatures.

**Developmental rate.** Analysis of covariance showed significantly different slopes between Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker and Humpback Chub, and Bonytail Chub and Colorado Squawfish. Biological zeros also differed significantly for the same pairs.

**Abnormal fry.** Abnormal fry were significantly lower at 20 °C (9%) than at 15 °C (14%) or 25 °C (32%) for Razorback Sucker. Humpback Chub had 33% abnormal at 15 °C versus 13% at 20 °C and 17% at 25 °C. Squawfish abnormal fry reached 25% at 25 °C.

**Predicted thermal window.** The author concludes that reproduction is likely to fail when spawning and incubation occur below 10–15 °C or above 25 °C. Optimum incubation probably occurs near 20 °C for Razorback Sucker, Humpback Chub and Colorado Squawfish, and between 15 and 20 °C for Bonytail Chub.

**Hatchery limitations.** Fungal infection, handling stress, wild versus hatchery broodstock differences and poor "bad egg" quality all confounded absolute values, though relative comparisons were judged valid. Fungus mortality was under 1% of total ova. Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave showed lower adult survival after trammel-net capture than after seine capture.

**Habitat implications.** Temperatures in the lower Colorado River below reservoirs, and in Lake Mohave, appear to be thermally adequate, yet there has been no apparent successful reproduction by Razorback Sucker or Bonytail Chub since about the mid-1950s. The author argues that [[Predation By Introduced Species]] on larvae is a likely additional cause and proposes a bay exclosure experiment in Lake Mohave to test it.

**Recommendations.** Research priorities include defining thermal preferenda and lethal limits for adults and larvae, obtaining field spawning and hatch records with temperature data, quantifying population abundance and recruitment, and enforcing stricter disease, predator and habitat controls at fish hatcheries.
