# Endemic Fishes of the Cuatro Ciénegas Basin, Northern Coahuila, Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by W. L. Minckley of Arizona State University, is a chapter from the *Transactions of the Symposium on the Biological Resources of the Chihuahuan Desert Region, United States and Mexico* (Sul Ross State University, Alpine, Texas, 17-18 October 1974; National Park Service Transactions and Proceedings Series No. 3, 1977). It reviews the freshwater fishes of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], a desert basin the author describes as one of the most important areas of reliction and endemism in the Western Hemisphere. The paper covers intrabasin distributions, hybridization among [[Pupfishes]] and cichlids, ecological allopatry among [[Poeciliidae]], and the zoogeographic origins of the fauna.

## Background & Description

The Cuatro Ciénegas basin has been studied extensively, with an earlier review of its aquatic and semi-aquatic biota by Minckley (1969). The author states that although its fishes are notable, they are now overshadowed in importance by several invertebrate groups and by the far greater diversity and probable endemism of higher vascular plants.

Eight fish families are represented in the basin:
- **Northern and eastern affinities:** Ictaluridae, Cyprinidae, Centrarchidae, Percidae. These mainly occupy larger warm or cool springs and their outflows.
- **Southern and coastal affinities:** Cyprinodontidae (closest to coastal eastern North America), and Characidae, Poeciliidae and Cichlidae (Middle American affinities). Genera with the most southern affinities, *Cichlasoma* and *Astyanax*, favor larger warm springs.

Figures 1-6 map habitat preferences and distributions of individual species across the basin, including towns, ranches, lakes, springs, sinkholes, rivers, intermittent channels and canals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat mosaic:** The basin contains all known types of desert aquatic habitat except a large, erosive river. Surface drainage patterns were apparently distinct until recently interconnected by man through canals, and fish dispersion appears governed mainly by ecological preferences and tolerances.
- **Thermal endemism:** [[Cyprinodon Bifasciatus]] is a thermal endemic tied to thermal springs at the foot of the Sierra de San Marcos.
- **Hydrologic change:** Many hybrid populations studied between 1960 and 1973 disappeared, mainly because of changes in human water-use patterns. A population of *C. bifasciatus* in a series of deep pits is reported to be failing as water levels drop rapidly, and its fishes are considered doomed in the near future.
- **Canal-mediated invasion:** Canal connections with the Río Salado system have allowed invasion of the basin by [[Rio Grande Cichlid]] forms, with *Cichlasoma (Herichthys) cyanoguttatum* recently invading.
- **Invertebrate context:** Crustaceans and mollusks show comparable endemism, e.g. the stenasellid isopod *Mexistenasellus coahuila* (first Western Hemisphere record of Stenasellidae at discovery) and the amphipod *Hyalella azteca*, which is widespread across North and Middle America.

## Key Findings

**Species inventory and endemism**
- Of 20 fish taxa known from the basin, 15 are differentiated at least to subspecies, and 11 are endemic.
- Of the 11 endemics, 2 (*Notropis xanthicara* and an undescribed *Etheostoma*) have northern and eastern affinities to the middle Rio Grande fauna. Nine have southern and eastern affinities, including *Xiphophorus gordoni*, the four-species *Cichlasoma (Parapetenia)* flock, and *Gambusia marshi* (endemic to the Río Salado system).
- The summary states that the endemic fauna is strongly weighted toward the south coastal fauna of Mexico, and that multiple invasions of the basin have occurred.

**Pupfish hybridization**
- *Cyprinodon bifasciatus* and *C. atrorus* hybridize where artificially brought into contact by canals, and in two natural situations.
- In the Río Churince sink, a hybrid swarm developed as the pool grew to about one hectare by 1973. Arnold (1970) documented physico-chemical intermediacy of its waters.
- The author suggests such hybridization and loss of intermediate populations has probably occurred for millennia in transitory habitats, supporting Hubbs (1955).

**Poeciliid ecology**
- *Gambusia marshi* has two color phases: a dark-striped form in thermal springs, and a spotted form on the basin floor and in downflow habitats. These phases show strong homotypic mating tendencies in the laboratory.
- *G. marshi* and *G. longispinis* are ecologically allopatric. No forced mating between them succeeded despite repeated attempts.
- *G. longispinis* and *Xiphophorus gordoni* are geographically allopatric. Males of *Xiphophorus* did not discriminate between homospecific and heterospecific females, whereas male *G. longispinis* totally discriminated against platyfish females.

**Cichlid complexity**
- The *Parapetenia* species-flock was first reported by Taylor and Minckley (1966) as three species, and LaBounty (1974) found a fourth form. LaBounty related the flock more closely to southern forms (subgenus *Parapetenia*) than to coastal and Río Grande cichlids (subgenus *Herichthys*).
- Two *Herichthys* kinds occur in the Río Grande drainage, and both are present in the Cuatro Ciénegas basin.
- LaBounty proposed three alternative explanations for the *Herichthys* situation. The author finds his third, a combination of primary hybridization and introgression, most acceptable based on data to 1973.

**Sunfish and catfish**
- *Lepomis megalotis* and *Micropterus salmoides* are distinctive relative to Río Salado counterparts but are not differentiated to species level. Recent (post-1968) importation of largemouth bass may have occurred, and in less isolated habitats the isolated elongate form is being replaced by a heavier-bodied form.
- The flathead catfish (*Pilodictis olivaris*) is known from only two young specimens and visual observation of three subadults.

**Biogeographic conclusions**
- Some taxa (*C. bifasciatus*, *G. marshi*, the cichlid flock, possibly *X. gordoni*) may represent an ancient component of the fauna.
- Secondary invasions probably include middle Rio Grande related forms.
- Several taxa share affinities with the north-central Mexican Plateau, suggesting another westward-to-east movement of fishes.
- *Micropterus* and *Lepomis* arrived late, and *C. cyanoguttatum* is a recent invader.

**Invertebrate parallels**
- Reported endemism: crustaceans 7 families, 8 genera (50%), 10 species (60%); mollusks 7 families, 22 genera (27%), 34 species (50%).
- Molluscan and crustacean assemblages show ancient lineages, and several molluscan species appear restricted to the basin.

**Research gaps**
- The author notes the *Dionda episcopa* subspecies need definition, and that Hubbs and Miller were working on the genus.
- The origins of *Dionda*, *C. atrorus*, and some other taxa remain difficult to determine.
