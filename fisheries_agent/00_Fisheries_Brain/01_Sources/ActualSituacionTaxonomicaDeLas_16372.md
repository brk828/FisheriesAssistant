# Taxonomic Status of Tilapiine Species Introduced into Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This Spanish-language taxonomic review by [[José Luis Arredondo-Figueroa]] and [[Manuel Guzmán-Arroyo]] (UNAM) examines the confused systematics of tilapia species introduced into Mexico, where confusion hampers their management, culture and promotion. The authors recognize five distinct species that are probably present in the country, plus an albino line of [[Mossambique Tilapia]] (*Oreochromis mossambicus*) and hybrids of *O. urolepis hornorum* and albino *O. mossambicus*. The paper reviews infrageneric classification schemes proposed by [[Thys (1968)]] and [[Trewavas (1982, 1983)]], and provides diagnostic characters for the tribe Tilapiini and a dichotomous identification key.

## Background & Description

The Tilapiini are cichlids native to Africa, with fossil remains dating back more than 18 million years. Boulenger recognized 96 African species in works published between 1909 and 1916, and more than 50 names he treated are now considered synonyms. Thys (1968) recognized about 80 species, grouped into three sections and 15 subgenera, while Trewavas later split the tribe into genera on the basis of reproductive and feeding biology, particularly mouthbrooding. Trewavas (1983) placed the tribe in five genera: *Tilapia*, *Tristramella*, *Danakilia*, *Sarotherodon* and *Oreochromis*.

Diagnostic features of the tribe (after Smith 1840 and Trewavas) include a tall or moderately elongate body, a large spiny dorsal fin, nostrils on each side above the lips, an interrupted lateral line, cycloid scales, and a dark spot near the end of the dorsal fin (the "tilapia mark"). Most species have three anal spines. Pharyngeal teeth and jaw dentition are used as key diagnostic characters.

Species introduced into Mexico, following Thys and Trewavas classifications, are:
- *Tilapia rendalli* (subgenus *Coptodon*), introduced 1964 from Alabama, USA, at Temascal, Oaxaca.
- *Oreochromis urolepis hornorum*, introduced 1981 from Florida, USA, at El Rodeo, Morelos.
- *Oreochromis mossambicus*, introduced 1964 (Alabama) and 1981 (albino line, Florida).
- *Oreochromis aureus*, introduced 1964 from Alabama, USA, at Temascal, Oaxaca.
- *Oreochromis niloticus*, introduced 1978 from Panama, at Tezontepec de Aldama, Hidalgo, later moved to Temascal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Tilapias were first brought to Mexico on 10 July 1964 from Auburn University, Alabama, and were placed at the Temascal Tropical Aquaculture Station in Oaxaca, at the base of the Miguel Alemán (Temascal) dam. Their adaptability led to widespread stocking of dams, lakes, coastal lagoons and farm ponds across the tropical zone and parts of the subtropical and temperate regions. The authors note that after about twenty years tilapia occupied major water bodies including [[Lake Chapala]], [[Lake Pátzcuaro]], and reservoirs such as Infiernillo, La Angostura, Nezahualcóyotl, Chicoasén and Vicente Guerrero. Tilapia reportedly ranked first in continental fishery production in Mexico, with an estimated annual catch of 100,000 tonnes.

The authors stress that the ecological effects on native fish communities and on the trophic structure of reservoirs remain poorly understood. Introductions were often deliberate or accidental, and some failed to establish. Tilapias are herbivorous or planktivorous depending on species and have been used to control aquatic macrophytes. The authors also note that the hybrid's sterility is questionable, since hybrids reproduce among themselves under culture conditions.

## Key Findings

- **Five species** are probably present in Mexico, together with one albino line and one hybrid whose sterility is disputed.
- **Taxonomic confusion** arose partly because early Mexican studies (Lee et al. 1976) reported *Tilapia nilotica* and *Sarotherodon niloticus* rather than the species actually introduced, which weakened the scientific value of those publications.
- **Separating *S. niloticus* from *S. aureus***: Payne and Collinson (1983) reported that the dorsal spine height is greater in *niloticus* and that its edges are dark, not red or pink. In Egypt, 83.3% of sampled *niloticus* had 17 dorsal spines, versus 10.3% of *aureus*, which mostly had 16 (81%). Salvadores (1980) identified the Vicente Guerrero specimens as *O. aureus*.
- **Genus-level differences** between *Tilapia* and *Oreochromis* (after Trewavas 1973, Balarin 1979) include diet (herbivory vs. planktivory), gill-raker counts (8–12 vs. 14–28), egg number (over 7,000 vs. under 700), egg size and colour, parental care (biparental vs. maternal mouthbrooding for 20–30 days), and nest type.
- **Identification key**: The key separates *T. rendalli* from *Oreochromis* spp. by gill rakers and pharyngeal teeth. Within *Oreochromis*, the four species are separated by gill-raker count, dorsal and anal fin formulae, scale count, body colour, and genital papilla and fin colour. Colour patterns (Rothbard 1979) are supplied as supporting characters.
- **Conclusion**: Taxonomic status is still unresolved. The authors call for confirmation of the taxonomic position of *O. niloticus* in Mexico, and note that classifications may change as biological knowledge grows.
