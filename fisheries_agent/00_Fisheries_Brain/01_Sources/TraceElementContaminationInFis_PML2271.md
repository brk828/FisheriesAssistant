# Trace Element Contamination in Fish, Biota and Sediment of the Bill Williams River NWR

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This graduate thesis documents a 1992–1993 study of trace element and organochlorine contamination in the [[Bill Williams River]] and its floodplain, within the [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]] (NWR) in western Arizona. The work was funded by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Division of Refuges) and the University of Arizona through the Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit. Sediment, water, algae, invertebrates, and fish were sampled to establish baseline contaminant levels and to determine whether the 1993 flood altered them. The main conclusions are that most elements were not present at levels of environmental concern, that [[Selenium]] is the principal concern, and that the flood generally lowered contaminant concentrations in the sediment and water, with selenium the main exception.

## Background & Description

The thesis was prepared under the direction of Dr. O. Eugene Maughan, with committee members Drs. William J. Matter and Carole Mellor. Field assistance and logistical support came from the refuge and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Arizona Fishery Resources Office in Pinetop, Arizona.

The study addresses the [[Bill Williams River]] watershed, where historic mining is a potential source of metals. The [[Planet Mine]] area, a historic copper, gold and silver operation in the upper watershed, is cited as a possible contributor of heavy metals through runoff. Runoff from ephemeral washes is a further pathway. The refuge is a major Arizona wetland and habitat for migratory birds and listed species, including the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] and [[Yellow-billed Cuckoo]], which makes contamination of its food web a management concern.

Fish sampled included:
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*)
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*)
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*)
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*)

Invertebrates (including snails, crayfish, odonates, and tadpoles), algae, and turtles were also collected. Fish were collected by seine, dip net, gill net, electrofishing (boat and backpack), and hoop or trap methods. Composite whole-body fish samples were made from 3 to 5 individuals of similar size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area is a regulated, seasonally flooded river system in the lower [[Colorado River]] Valley, with marsh, backwater, and open lake habitats along the river. Sampling was done at sites upstream and downstream of key inputs, including the confluence with the Colorado River, the [[Alamo Dam]] area, and wash inflows. Only sediment, algae, mosquitofish, and snails were sampled at every site and in both years. Historic flow records show the 1993 flood exceeded the 100-year flood level and inundated refuge lands for an extended period.

Analytical methods included:
- Total organic carbon (TOC) and moisture content by percent weight
- Metal analysis by a commercial laboratory using standard methods
- Organochlorine analysis, with all sediment results below detection limits
- Statistical analysis using ANOVA and General Linear Models (GLM), with Tukey's test for significant main effects

Results are reported as dry weight (µg/g or mg/kg) and converted to wet weight for comparison with other studies. Comparisons relied on the [[National Contaminants Biomonitoring Program]] (NCBP), Long and Morgan (1990) sediment screening values (ER-L and ER-M), and [[Predator Protection Limit]] values for fish and wildlife (for example, [[Eisler]] and [[Lemly]] thresholds).

## Key Findings

**Scope of sampling and quality control**
- 62 composite samples were analyzed for organochlorine residues and 49 trace elements and heavy metals.
- Organochlorine compounds were below detection limits in all sediment samples.
- Precision and accuracy were judged acceptable, using duplicate analysis, spike recovery, and standard reference materials (NIST 1573a for tissue; EPA reference sediments for 1992 and 1993).

**Flood effects**
- The 1993 flood generally decreased concentrations of arsenic, cadmium, copper, lead, and zinc in sediment, possibly through dilution and flushing.
- Selenium was the only element that increased in sediment after the flood, consistent with deposition of material from upstream sources.
- Contaminant levels in water and biota were altered by the flood, with no evidence that flushing of washes raised contaminant levels in the main channel.

**Arsenic**
- Arsenic levels in sediment were generally higher than in other lower Colorado River studies but within published baseline ranges.
- Mean fish arsenic was 0.14 µg/g (wet weight), with an 85th percentile of 0.27 µg/g and a maximum of 1.08 µg/g in carp at site 3 (1992).
- Only carp exceeded the predator protection threshold of 0.50 µg/g, and only four of nine carp samples were above it.

**Cadmium**
- Sediment cadmium (mean 0.25 µg/g; geometric mean 0.30 µg/g) was below levels of environmental concern.
- Fish cadmium was generally below detection, with a high of 0.13 µg/g at sites 4 and 5. Fish levels did not exceed the predator protection limit of 0.50 µg/g.

**Copper**
- Sediment copper (mean 21.2 µg/g; geometric mean 15.5 µg/g) was higher than sediment at other lower Colorado River sites but within published baseline ranges.
- Copper in fish showed no biomagnification in whole-body samples.
- Copper in water was elevated during flooding, and the study notes that hardness strongly affects copper toxicity.

**Lead**
- Sediment lead was generally below Long and Morgan ER-L values (about 35 µg/g) and within baseline ranges.
- Lead in fish was generally low, and fish values did not exceed NCBP comparison values or predator protection limits.

**Mercury**
- Sediment mercury was generally low.
- NCBP whole-body fish mercury had a geometric mean of 0.10 µg/g and an 85th percentile of 0.11 µg/g.
- Most fish contained less than 0.1 µg/g mercury. Red shiners at site 1 had elevated levels, likely reflecting inputs from [[Alamo Lake]].

**Selenium (main concern)**
- Sediment selenium was below 0.20 µg/g in all samples (N = 21), so sediment was not contaminated.
- Fish and invertebrate selenium was elevated relative to sediment. The NCBP fish geometric mean was 0.2 µg/g, with a maximum of 2.30 µg/g in carp from [[Martinez Lake]].
- Red shiners consistently contained selenium above 3.00 µg/g (dry weight): 93% of samples exceeded this, and most exceeded 4.00 µg/g, the [[Lemly]] threshold for chronic toxicosis.
- Two carp collected in 1993 near the confluence contained 3.62 and 4.67 µg/g.
- Fish from the confluence area had elevated selenium, which the authors attribute to water from the Colorado River rather than the Bill Williams River.
- Selenium levels were significantly higher in mosquitofish and bass in certain years and sites, but the flood-related differences were not uniformly significant.
- Lemly's (1993) predator protection limit of 3.0 µg/g (dry weight) was exceeded in fish, and the study concludes that selenium poses health risks to fish, to aquatic birds, and to predators that feed on them.

**Zinc**
- Sediment zinc (mean 79 µg/g; geometric mean 70 µg/g) exceeded levels in other lower Colorado sites but was within the 17–102 µg/g baseline range.
- Fish zinc was generally higher in carp than in other species. The NCBP geometric mean whole-body zinc in fish was 34.2 µg/g, and carp zinc exceeded NCBP values but did not exceed the predator protection limit.

**Conclusions**
- Most sediment elements, including arsenic, cadmium, copper, lead, mercury, and zinc, were not present at levels of environmental concern.
- Selenium is the primary contaminant of concern, reflecting elevated levels in fish and in water from the Colorado River, with the greatest risk at the Bill Williams confluence.
- Historic mining in the watershed is a plausible source for metals, but the present sediment data do not show metal contamination large enough to cause ecological harm.
- The authors recommend continued monitoring of selenium and the establishment of baseline conditions for water, sediment, and biota before and after flood events.
