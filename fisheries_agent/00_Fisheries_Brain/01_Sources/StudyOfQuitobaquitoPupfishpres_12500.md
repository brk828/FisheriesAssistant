# Quitobaquito Pupfish Preservation, Habitat, and Population Monitoring (1979 Final Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, submitted by [[Boyd E. Kynard]] to the [[National Park Service]] in August 1979, closes a three-phase study (1975–78) of the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] at [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], Arizona. It covers water quality, population size and spawning success, pesticide analysis, and the establishment of pupfish refugia for the period September 1977 to September 1978. The population had crashed in 1976, and this report documents its recovery to pre-crash levels by fall 1978. Analysis of fish tissue detected [[Methyl-Parathion]], which the author links to the 1976 decline. The report concludes with management recommendations to protect the species from agricultural pesticide drift from Mexico.

## Background & Description

The [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*, a member of the [[Cyprinodontidae]]) is a small desert fish that lives in the spring-fed [[Quitobaquito Pool]] and its outflow at [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. The three-phase project covered:

- **Phase 1 (1975–76):** Population biology (size, age, growth, survival, breeding behaviour, and activity relative to environmental factors) and the physico-chemical environment (Kynard 1976; Kynard and Garrett 1979).
- **Phase 2 (1976–77):** Continued environmental monitoring, baseline pesticide data, establishment of a refugium at [[Gachado Tank]] (later aborted), and a systematic comparison of morphology and behaviour (Kynard, in prep.).
- **Phase 3 (Sept 1977 to Sept 1978; this report):** Four areas: water quality, population and spawning success, pesticide levels, and refugium establishment.

Monitoring used a [[Yellow Springs Instrument]] Model 57 temperature–oxygen meter in the field. Water samples were analysed at the [[University Of Arizona]] Soils, Water, and Plant Testing Laboratory. Population estimates used [[Minnow Traps]] and a [[Peterson Mark-Recapture Estimate]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Water quality at [[Quitobaquito Pool]] and [[Williams Spring]] was essentially unchanged from earlier years and remained excellent for pupfish. Spring flow at Quitobaquito was 31.5 gal/min (17 Dec 1977) and 30.0 gal/min (21 Jun 1978). Water at [[Bates Well]] and [[Finley Tank]] differed markedly from Quitobaquito but stayed within pupfish tolerance limits. Other ponds surveyed were judged unsuitable as refugia.

**Temperature and elevation.** The [[Finley Tank]] site lies at about 1,600 m elevation, much cooler than Quitobaquito. Winter 1977–78 was unusually mild, so the planned thermograph record did not capture a typical winter. The author accepted the lower temperatures because pupfish live above 1,600 m in New Mexico and Mexico.

**Pesticide exposure.** Agricultural development in the [[Sonoyta River]] valley, about 1 km south of Quitobaquito, had produced large cotton crops in 1976 and 1977. The author considers that prevailing winds may carry pesticides north across the border. [[Sonoyta River]] fish showed large peaks of [[P,P'-DDE]], [[P,P'-DDD]], and [[P,P'-DDT]].

**Habitat.** [[Potamogeton]] abundance in [[Finley Tank]] was similar to that at Quitobaquito. The spring-head at Quitobaquito, once used by pupfish (up to 1975), had become choked with reeds and was no longer occupied.

## Key Findings

### 1. Water quality
- Quitobaquito and Williams Spring remained excellent for pupfish, similar to 1971–77 conditions (Tables 1–2).
- Bates Well refugium water (Dec 1976 vs Feb 1978) had pH 7.8–8.5, bicarbonate 268–225 mg/l, and nitrate 3.55–2.25 mg/l.
- Finley Tank water (Jan 1977 vs Mar 1978) had total alkalinity of 176.2 mg/l as CaCO₃ and a lower-temperature winter regime.

### 2. Population size and recovery
- The 1976 population crash was followed by a partial recovery. Markable-size fish (≥24 mm total length) made up 47% of the population in 1976 but 83.6% in fall 1977.
- **Peterson estimate, 11–12 Oct 1977:** 311 fish were marked and 429 markable fish were examined (35 recaptured). The estimate was 3,812 markable fish (95% CI 2,391–6,205), with a total population of about **4,560 fish**.
- The population rose by only about 500 fish between 1976 and 1977. The author interprets the age-structure shift as higher survival to larger sizes, not a large increase in numbers.
- **Fall 1978:** population was about **8,000 fish** (14% sub-markable), a 100% increase over 1977 and close to the 7,986 fish counted in October 1975, before the crash.
- Breeding males counted along the shoreline in May 1978 were similar to 1975 levels, indicating a successful 1978 spawning season.
- Surveys in fall 1978 were conducted by [[Terry Peters]] of the National Park Service with [[Robert Garrett]], and the spring 1978 survey was cancelled because of large visitor numbers.

### 3. Pesticide analysis
- Baseline samples collected 15 Dec 1976 detected [[Methyl-Parathion]] in adult fish, but the sample was too small to quantify. Laboratory contamination was also considered.
- Samples analysed in Nov 1977 at the [[Pesticide Residue Laboratory]], Entomology Department, University of Arizona, found methyl-parathion at approximately **0.03 ppm** in Quitobaquito fish and **0.04 ppm** in Sonoyta River fish.
- The author concludes that pesticide exposure is the most likely cause of the 1976 decline. No other natural or human factor appeared correlated with it.

### 4. Refugium establishment
- **[[Bates Well Refugium]]** (Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument): established Feb 1978 with 350 pupfish. Fish bred in spring 1978 and appeared established. The site is considered the monument location least likely to receive Mexican pesticide drift, but it is small (a livestock tank, probably supporting fewer than 500 fish) and vulnerable to loss.
- **[[Finley Tank Refugium]]** (Research Ranch, Elgin, Arizona): the pond was about two-thirds drained in June 1978 and treated with powdered rotenone (supplied by the Arizona Game and Fish Department) to remove resident bass and sunfish. Capacity was about 200,000 gallons, with dimensions of roughly 40 m × 20 m and an average depth of 2.3 m. Stocking was delayed until November 1978, so fish missed the spring breeding season. The refugium entered winter at roughly **300–400 fish**, and only about half or fewer were expected to survive to spawn.
- **[[Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum]]:** a Quitobaquito population was established in May 1978 and bred during observation visits through July. In June 1978 several hundred fish were given to the museum to replace an inbred population (said to be showing inbreeding depression) and to add wild-type genes. Experimental populations from [[Rio Sonoyta]] and three lower [[Colorado River]] populations were also provided.
- The refugia drew on a captive population established in 1976 in the author's observation pool at the University of Arizona. This protected the natural Quitobaquito population from disturbance.

### 5. Management recommendations
- **Refugia:** establish additional refugia on the monument and off it, so that a local catastrophe cannot eliminate all populations. Build a larger refugium at Bates Well, comparable in area to Quitobaquito, since the existing tank is too small and fragile (compare the loss of [[Gachado Tank]] refugium when water deliveries stopped).
- **Re-establish fish in the spring-head** at Quitobaquito. Clearing reeds from the spring-head would allow a refuge within the pool's own drainage, and fish there could repopulate the pool after an aerial pesticide kill. Kynard (1976) also recommended clearing the lower drainage and creating slow-moving warm-water stream habitat.
- **Long-term monitoring:** continue fall population estimates and age-structure surveys. National Park Service staff have been trained to carry these out. Check all refugia periodically, with priority on [[Finley Tank Refugium]] in 1979.
- **Pesticide surveillance:** the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] pesticide laboratory at Columbia, Missouri, should monitor Quitobaquito and its habitat over the long term.

The author concludes that Quitobaquito pupfish are too valuable to risk total loss and that the species requires close supervision by the National Park Service.
