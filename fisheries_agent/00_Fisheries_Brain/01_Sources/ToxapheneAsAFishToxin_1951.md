## Executive Summary

This 1951 report by Jack E. Hemphill of the Arizona Game and Fish Commission documents the discovery and experimental application of **[[Toxaphene]]**, a chlorinated camphene insecticide, as a piscicide for controlling undesirable "rough fish" populations (primarily [[Carp]] and [[Yellow Perch]]) in Arizona lakes and reservoirs. Initial observations came from unintentional fish kills caused by agricultural aerial dusting drift into farm ponds. Controlled field trials at **Lyman Reservoir**, **Becker's Lake**, and **San Carlos Reservoir** demonstrated that toxaphene at 0.1 p.p.m. could achieve near-total eradication of undesirable fish species while allowing subsequent restocking with game fish such as [[Rainbow Trout]]. The paper also addresses toxicological safety for livestock and humans, environmental breakdown conditions, and cost-effectiveness of the treatment.

## Background & Description

*Note: This paper is toxicological/ecological in nature rather than morphological. Species-specific size and growth data are reported as follows:*

- **[[Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*, implied): Individuals recovered post-drainage at Lyman Reservoir ranged up to large adults; two- to four-pound individuals dominant at Becker's Lake.
- **[[Boneytail]]** (*Gila robusta elegans*): Recovered in large numbers (~3,000 lbs and later 3,000 individuals) at Lyman Reservoir.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]**: 112 individuals recovered post-drainage averaging **6 lbs each**.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]**: 192 individuals recovered ranging **3–12 lbs**; notably absent during the active toxicity period, suggesting avoidance behavior or differential sensitivity.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]**: Experimental stockings showed strong growth post-treatment:
  - Lyman Reservoir: 3,600 six-inch fingerlings grew to an average of 16 inches within 11 months.
  - Becker's Lake: 25,000 fry grew at an average rate of **1.2 inches/month** over the first 12 months (based on a 121-fish sample).
- **[[Yellow Perch]]** (*Perca flavescens*): First species to exhibit toxic distress symptoms at Becker's Lake.
- **[[Yellow Bass]]** (*Morone interrupta*): Large unestimated population destroyed at San Carlos Reservoir.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat types studied**: Shallow eutrophic farm ponds, mid-sized alkaline reservoirs (Lyman Reservoir, Becker's Lake), and a large volume river reservoir (San Carlos Reservoir on the [[Gila River]]).
- **Water chemistry conditions**:
  - Lyman Reservoir: pH 8.6, 370 p.p.m. total dissolved salts, Secchi disc 6 inches, surface temp 70°F.
  - Becker's Lake: pH 8.2, volcanic ash-driven turbidity (12-inch Secchi disc), surface temp 52°F, max depth 29 feet.
- **Trophic/community effects**: Toxaphene treatment eliminated dominant rough fish (carp, perch), triggering a **zooplankton bloom** post-treatment due to nutrient release from decomposing fish carcasses — used by researchers as a bio-indicator for toxin dissipation and optimal restocking timing.
- **Turbidity changes**: Dramatic reductions in turbidity followed carp removal (Lyman Reservoir: 6 inches → 4 feet Secchi reading), consistent with the classic "carp-turbidity" ecological feedback loop via benthic disturbance and vegetation loss.
- **Non-target fauna**: [[Frogs]] and [[Water Snakes]] were the only non-fish vertebrates showing toxic symptoms; insect life was affected but not eliminated.
- **Livestock exposure**: Cattle and horses drinking from treated waters (0.1 p.p.m.) for up to 60 days showed no toxic symptoms; lethal threshold estimated at >7 grams toxaphene/cow/day (~18,000 gallons of treated water needed).
- **Environmental degradation pathway**: Toxaphene breaks down more rapidly under alkaline conditions (pH ≥ 8.0 lakes safe to restock within 4 weeks); acidic waters presumed to retain toxicity longer. Thermal breakdown occurs at 190°F, rendering toxin-killed fish apparently safe for human consumption.
- **Limitations noted**: Ineffective in flowing streams due to slow action; large-volume/high-inflow systems dilute effectiveness; untested in deep lake stratification scenarios since toxaphene dust settles rapidly to the bottom.

## Key Findings

- **Lethal concentration established**: 1 part in 10 million (0.1 p.p.m.) toxaphene produced total carp kill at 45°F within 72 hours in laboratory conditions.
- **Field application rate**: 0.7 lbs of 40% toxaphene dust per acre-foot achieves 0.1 p.p.m. concentration.
- **Lyman Reservoir trial (Aug 1951)**: Killed ~220 tons of carp, 3,000 lbs boneytail, plus incidental losses of bass, bluegill, brown trout, and bullhead catfish; heavy rainfall diluted but did not stop the kill.
- **Species-selective resistance**: Channel catfish showed no signs of distress during the active toxic period at Lyman Reservoir, suggesting differential toxaphene sensitivity among species — later confirmed effective against catfish only at higher concentrations.
- **Becker's Lake trial (Oct 1951)**: Achieved apparent 100% rough-fish eradication (verified via 2 years of gillnetting, seining, and dynamite sampling), enabling long-term rainbow trout stocking success.
- **San Carlos Reservoir trial**: Demonstrated feasibility of large-scale reservoir treatment (Dingell-Johnson project) — destroyed ~800 of an estimated 1,100 tons of carp and ~2 million stunted crappie at a cost as low as **$0.05 per acre-foot**; confirmed findings of Lawrence (P.F.C. 12(13)) regarding large reservoir applications; effectiveness limited if fish can migrate to untreated fresh water inflow.
- **Cost efficiency**: At time of publication, 40% toxaphene dust cost $0.165/lb (Phoenix, AZ), translating to $0.115 per acre-foot treated at 0.1 p.p.m. — a substantial improvement from previous market costs.
- **Safety profile**: Comparable in handling risk to other agricultural chemicals like sodium arsenite and copper sulfate; primary risks were dermal absorption and inhalation, mitigated by gloves and respirators.
- **Restocking synergy**: Post-kill zooplankton blooms driven by decomposing fish biomass created favorable conditions for rapid growth of newly stocked game fish (e.g., rainbow trout growth rates of 1.2 in/month).
- **Practical constraints identified**: Not suitable for flowing streams, high-inflow water bodies, or domestic water sources; unverified efficacy in deep-water stratified systems.
