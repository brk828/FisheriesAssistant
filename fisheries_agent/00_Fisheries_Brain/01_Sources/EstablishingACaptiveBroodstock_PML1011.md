# Establishing a Captive Broodstock for the Endangered Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This genetic management study evaluates how to build a new [[Captive Broodstock]] for the critically imperiled [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The wild population is reduced to an unknown number of large, old adults in [[Lake Mohave]] and scattered individuals in the upper [[Colorado River]] basin. The authors estimate the effective number of founders behind the 1981 F1 progeny at roughly 3.5 (allozyme and mtDNA based) to 8.5 (production records). They conclude that re-using F1 progeny alone further erodes genetic variation, so wild fish must be incorporated into the future broodstock. The approach is proposed as a general model for other endangered species held in captivity.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*), one of the most critically imperiled North American freshwater fishes (Minckley et al. 1989; Williams et al. 1985).
- **Population status:** The only known wild fish are an unknown number of large, old adults in Lake Mohave, plus scattered individuals in the upper Colorado River basin (Marsh 1999). Concern centred on apparent lack of wild recruitment.
- **Origin of captive stock:** In 1981, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service produced F1 progeny at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] from wild adults captured in Lake Mohave (the "original captive broodstock").
- **Retained progeny:** About 2,300 F1 progeny were sent to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center; approximately 350 remained there in late 1997.
- **Context:** Long-term persistence of threatened species depends on avoiding inbreeding depression and loss of genetic variation, which is linked to extinction probability (Hedrick et al. 1996; Saccheri et al. 1998; Westemeier et al. 1998).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses a small, relict population whose remaining wild individuals are old and few. Captive management is the main tool for retaining variation until natural populations reach adequate size. The authors note that a small effective population size, not just the number of individuals, drives loss of heterozygosity, and that predators and environmental factors affect survival in the wild. Fish held at Dexter were protected from such pressures, a point relevant to how captive stock is managed.

## Key Findings

**Materials and methods**
- Genetic analyses covered 30 F1 progeny from Dexter and a Lake Mohave sample of wild-type fish (possibly including some F1 and F2 individuals).
- Three polymorphic allozyme loci (*Sod-A*, *Gpi-A*, *Pgm-A*) were scored in the F1 sample. mtDNA was analysed in 20 of the 30 progeny and 16 Lake Mohave fish using single-strand conformational polymorphism (SSCP) of one cytochrome b fragment and two NADH dehydrogenase fragments.

**Reproduction in wild fish**
- Five wild females were pair-mated with five males captured in 1981. Female 1 produced no eggs.
- Eggs from the other five females ranged from 5,850 to 37,700 (mean 25,090). Fertilization proportions ranged from 0.049 to 0.290 (mean 0.200).

**Genetic data**
- Allozyme frequencies did not differ significantly from earlier F1 samples (Minckley et al. 1989). The sample was consistent with Hardy-Weinberg expectations except at *Pgm-A*, which showed a large, statistically significant excess of heterozygotes.
- Six three-locus genotypes were observed. Genotype I was found in 22 of 30 individuals. Binomial analysis gave a probability ratio of 3.02 in favour of a single mating producing most genotype I fish over multiple matings.
- Eight progeny carried mtDNA haplotypes a, b, and c. Haplotypes a and b differed at several nucleotides, which suggests a strong difference between them. Simulations showed that a sample of 20 F1 fish would likely be monomorphic for mtDNA, with a probability of about 0.95 under the high estimate.

**Effective number of founders**
- Allozyme and mtDNA data suggest an effective founder number of about 3.5.
- Production records suggest about 8.5, which the authors treat as a reasonable upper limit.
- Re-using F1 progeny to start a new broodstock reduces the effective number of founders further.

**Recommendations**
- Wild bonytail chub must be included in the future broodstock to increase genetic variation, even under the most optimistic estimates.
- Using a mix of F1 progeny and newly captured wild fish, and avoiding reliance on a few mating pairs, is preferred. Equalising contributions from mating pairs is also advised.
- The method is suggested as a general model for retaining genetic diversity in other captive endangered species until they have stable, adequately sized wild populations.
