# Filter Sand Layers in Groundwater Recharge Facilities as Artificial Subterranean Biotopes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #harpacticoida #stygobionts

*Note on scope:* This document is a hydrobiological study of invertebrate fauna, not of fish. The tags above follow the required format; the taxa concerned are stygobiontic crustaceans, nematodes, oligochaetes and water mites.

## Executive Summary

Husmann (1961) tested whether the sand layers of [[Groundwater Recharge Basin]] filters in the Ruhr valley, where river water is infiltrated into the aquifer, are colonised by subterranean organisms in the same way as natural [[Mesopsammal]] (interstitial sand fauna). The study found that these filter sands function as artificial biotopes for characteristic stygobiontic and stygophilic interstitial fauna. Nematodes, oligochaetes, copepods and water mites were recorded across several facilities. Water chemistry changed markedly along the infiltration path, and some taxa appear to have entered from the groundwater, while others were probably transported in with raw sand.

## Background & Description

The study is set against a long history of research on interstitial groundwater fauna. In 1927, [[Sassuchin et al.]] showed that the intergranular pore systems of groundwater-bearing bank sands are inhabited, overturning the earlier view that fluvial sands were nearly sterile. Later work described a rich [[Hydropsammon]] or mesopsammon, dominated by rotifers, turbellarians, nematodes, oligochaetes, copepods and syncarid crustaceans.

[[Ruhr Valley Groundwater Recharge]] is needed because intensive groundwater abstraction in the heavily industrialised Ruhr area threatened to lower the water table. Artificial recharge infiltrates river water through slow sand filters and holding basins. Basins are typically up to 150 m long and 50 m wide, set in loamy floodplain deposits. The filter sand layer is 40–80 cm thick and sits above gravel and sand deposits. Inflow passes first through a bed of loosely packed rubble, then through the sand, so the sand layer forms an artificial overburden above the natural aquifer. Infiltration can occasionally reverse, with groundwater rising into the filters during high-water periods, which may allow stygobiontic animals to migrate in.

The sampled facilities belong to the municipal waterworks and utilities of [[Dortmund Utilities]], Gelsenkirchen, Hamm and Mülheim. Sampling took place in 1955, 1956, 1960 and 1961.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry along the flow path.** Table 1 (11 August 1961, Wandhofen) shows clear treatment gains between river inflow and the end of recharge basin 5. Ammonia fell from 0.72 mg/l to 0.10 mg/l and nitrite from 0.29 mg/l to 0.13 mg/l. Nitrate rose in the sand passage, from 9.6 mg/l to 16.8 mg/l at the deepest sampling point, a change the author attributes partly to biogenic mineralisation. Nitrate was also depleted near the basin end, where dense algae were growing, possibly through algal uptake. Surface pH reached 8.7 near the basin end, compared with 7.3 in the subterranean mixed water, which suggests the peak was a short-lived daytime effect. Raw Ruhr water analysed in 1960–1961 (Table 2, Styrum and Kettwig) showed permanganate consumption of 8.5–12.6 mg/l, total hardness of 9.6–13 °dH, and nitrate of 20–24 mg/l.

**Vertical distribution of fauna.** Unlike Bremen slow sand filters, where the highest densities occur at 20–30 cm depth, the Ruhr facilities showed no uniform pattern. At Sty/16 nematodes, oligochaetes and copepods peaked in the upper layers. Ge/7 peaked at the base of the sand. Sty/6 was densest at 10–14 cm. Ha/5 showed two density peaks, one at the surface and one just above the base. Copepods made up a large part of the biomass at Ge/7, Ha/5 and Sty/16, whereas they were rare in the Bremen filters.

**Grain size.** The author argues that grain size cannot explain the vertical differences, because filter sand is sieved to a fairly uniform grain-size distribution from surface to base.

**Dispersal and origin.** Invasion from groundwater is considered possible for *Parastenocaris*, which is absent from the Bremen filters that discharge to drinking water supply rather than to the subsurface. *Epactophanes richardi* tolerates complete desiccation of raw sand, which makes transport with sand plausible. *Lobohalacarus weberi quadriporus* is probably carried in with sand, possibly from the Lower Rhine, where the raw sand is thought to have been dredged.

## Key Findings

- The filter sand layers of the examined Ruhr valley recharge facilities are colonised across all depths, mainly by nematodes, oligochaetes, harpacticoid and cyclopoid copepods, and water mites.
- All identified arthropods are stygobiontic elements of the groundwater fauna. Their natural habitat is groundwater-bearing sandy-gravel deposits.
- Five arthropod taxa were identified from the Ruhr filters: *Phyllognathopus viguieri*, *Parastenocaris fontinalis borea*, *Parastenocaris glacialis*, *Epactophanes richardi*, *Graeteriella unisetigera*, and *Lobohalacarus weberi quadriporus*. Nematode and oligochaete determinations were still pending at the time of writing.
- In Do/Wa/1, *Parastenocaris glacialis* numbered 148 specimens, about twice as many as *Epactophanes richardi*. This points to strong colonisation-regulating factors in artificial mesopsammal communities.
- *Parastenocaris* is a probable indicator of groundwater invasion into recharge filters. Its absence from the Bremen filters is consistent with this.
- Vertical density patterns differ between Ruhr and Bremen filters. Nitrate increased through the sand passage, consistent with earlier Bremen and Rotterdam findings.
- The author plans to extend the work with paired water and sand sampling at defined depths, and to link water-quality change to the physiology of individual sand-interstitial organisms.
