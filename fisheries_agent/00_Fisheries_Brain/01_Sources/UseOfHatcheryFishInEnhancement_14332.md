# Use of Hatchery Fish in Enhancement Programs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Robert R. Stickney]] (University of Washington School of Fisheries, published in *Fisheries*, Vol. 19, No. 5, May 1994) examines the use of hatchery-raised fish for [[Fisheries Enhancement]]. It traces the history of [[Enhancement Hatcheries]] in the United States, uses the [[Columbia River Basin]] as a central case study, reviews genetic and ecological criticisms, and argues that future hatchery use must be reshaped around [[Genetic Stock Integrity]], habitat restoration, and [[Interdisciplinary Collaboration]] rather than simply maximizing output.

## Background & Description

Stocking hatchery fish has been a core tool of U.S. fisheries management for more than a century. Hatcheries were first established by state and federal agencies to boost recreational and commercial catches. Early practice was extensive: in 1881 more than 100 railroads cooperated in delivering fish, and by 1906 six specially designed fish-hauling cars travelled more than 93,000 miles. Early releases often involved newly hatched fish, and recruitment from them was probably minimal, especially for marine species.

Hatchery production later focused on species reared to fingerling, smolt, or catchable size. Most enhancement species are freshwater or [[Anadromous Fish]]. The [[Red Drum]] is a notable marine exception, with Texas enhancement hatcheries believed to contribute significantly to Gulf of Mexico recruitment (personal communication cited in the text). Interest is growing in marine species such as cod, sablefish, and halibut, but survival to and beyond first-feeding and the supply of nutritious feeds remain bottlenecks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Columbia River basin illustrates the interplay of hatcheries and habitat loss. Dams built from 1910 (Swan Falls and Lower Salmon Falls on the Snake River) and 1933 (Rock Island Dam on the Columbia) blocked or reduced access to spawning habitat. Bonneville Dam, operating from 1938, had fishways, yet spawning habitat losses still reduced production potential. Outmigrating [[Salmon Smolts]] suffered high losses from turbine passage and [[Gas Bubble Disease]] caused by nitrogen supersaturation. Trucking and barging were used to improve survival.

The [[Mitchell Act]] of 1938 authorized federal funds to mitigate salmon and steelhead losses, and hatcheries became the preferred remedy. Despite growing hatchery output, salmon runs in the Columbia and elsewhere continued to decline (Hilborn 1992a). In one pattern described by Nelson and Bodle (1990), declining native [[Chinook Salmon]] returns led to introduction of an exotic stock, a practice geneticists criticize for eroding genetic integrity.

Broader threats were acknowledged even by critics, including dam construction, habitat destruction, agriculture, forestry, overfishing, and urbanization. Other ecological concerns include predation, competition, habitat modification, and disease introduction associated with introduced salmonids (Krueger and May 1991).

## Key Findings

**Criticism and the shift in attitude**
- Criticism of enhancement hatcheries moved from being ignored, to being dismissed as ill-informed, to prompting introspection and reevaluation among hatchery workers.
- Critics have described hatcheries as "techno-arrogance" (Meffe 1992) and as "ill advised and highly dangerous" to wild stocks (Hilborn 1992b).

**Genetic concerns**
- Three major concerns were identified by Waples et al. (1990b): (1) differing genetic variability between hatchery and wild populations; (2) increasing homozygosity in hatchery fish; and (3) negative consequences of mixing stocks on locally adapted gene pools.
- Electrophoresis study by Waples et al. (1990b) found that hatchery and wild Pacific Northwest chinook salmon have very similar levels of heterozygosity, suggesting the situation may be less dire than some claim.
- Stocking outside native watersheds remains contentious; many U.S. watersheds have had intermittent stocking from various sources for decades.

**Endangered Species and Conservation**
- The [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) defines any distinct population segment that interbreeds when mature as a "species," and the National Marine Fisheries Service has listed four Pacific Northwest units as threatened or endangered: Redfish Lake sockeye, Snake River spring/summer chinook, Snake River fall chinook, and Sacramento River winter chinook.
- Nehlsen et al. (1991) indicated at least 214 native salmonid stocks exist in California, Oregon, Idaho, and Washington, with more than 200 at risk of extinction.
- For Redfish Lake sockeye, hatcheries are described as the only realistic option: in 1991 three females and one male returned, and in 1992 a single male returned whose milt was cryopreserved.
- Hatcheries will not succeed unless constraints such as temperature regimes, river blockage, food supply, and overfishing are also addressed.

**Management alternatives**
- Fishing bans can restore some depleted stocks, lasting a few years or decades; combined with enhancement stocking they were used for striped bass on the East Coast and red drum in the Gulf of Mexico.
- Policies should require stocking with progeny from the augmented water system to protect genetic integrity.
- Future hatchery management should prioritize quality and genetic integrity over numbers. Returns to coho salmon hatcheries ranged from virtually zero to 30% (Hopley 1991).
- Suggested reforms include lower fish densities, provision of natural foods, feed that behaves more like natural prey, and training fish to avoid shadows that signal feeding by predators such as eagles, herons, and kingfishers.

**Ecosystem questions**
- The text raises unresolved questions about effects of stocking on non-target species, such as walleye displacing a genetically distinct northern pike strain, or hybrid sunfish affecting native green sunfish.

**Collaborative approach**
- The author calls for interdisciplinary teams, including fisheries managers, geneticists, fish culturists, bioengineers, water quality specialists, nutritionists, and ecologists, working toward agreed goals with objective, unbiased reporting.
- Policy decisions remain with policy makers, while scientists should supply high-quality data and alternative options with estimated probabilities of success.
