# Glen Canyon Dam and the Colorado River: Rebuilding a River Through Environmental Flows

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riverine-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This narrative essay examines the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in Arizona, where a regulated flow of water functions as a "waterworks" rather than a self-constructing river. It traces the history of the dam, the creation of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program, the environmental impact statement (EIS) that followed, and the 1996 experimental flood. The document frames the river as a system of sediment transport, flow, and landforms that the dam has disrupted, and it presents the 1996 flood and the adaptive management plan as partial steps toward restoring some of that function. The document contains no formal species data; the only aquatic animals mentioned are unnamed fish and an unnamed endangered native species.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The river runs through red sandstone canyons from the [[Colorado Plateau]] to [[Lees Ferry]] and the [[Grand Canyon]]. Below the dam, a flow of water is released from the spent-water pipes beneath the generator deck.
- **Dam:** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] is a concrete structure of about ten million tons, roughly 500 feet high. It impounds [[Lake Powell]], which the author describes as an "inland sea."
- **Hydrologic history:** Before the dam, the river ran three to six times the June flow observed on the day of the trip (about 20,000 cubic feet per second). In 1884 it reached close to 300,000 cfs.
- **Geologic history:** Research from a 1964 meeting at the [[Museum of Northern Arizona]] proposed that the "ancestral Colorado" once drained the southern Rocky Mountains. The modern river formed after a stream cut back into the plateau and captured this flow within the last five million years. The river now drops more than 3,000 vertical feet over 400 miles, from the junction of the [[Green River]] and Colorado in Utah to the end of the canyons below [[Hoover Dam]].
- **Key people:**
  - [[Dave Wegner]], program manager of GCES within the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], trained as an ecologist at [[Colorado State University]].
  - [[Jack Schmidt]], a hydrologist at [[Utah State University]] who has studied the canyons since the mid-1980s.
  - [[Dan Beard]], Commissioner of Reclamation from 1993 to 1995.
  - [[George Miller]], California Representative and chair of the House Committee on Natural Resources.
  - [[Delbert Havatone]], Hualapai Chairman.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment and flow:** Before the dam, tributaries above Glen Canyon carried more than 66 million tons of sand, silt, and debris per year into the canyons. Another 20 million tons entered from plateau tributaries such as the [[Paria River]] and [[Little Colorado River]]. Canyon walls limited the deposition of this material into meanders, marshes, and wetlands, and each year's flood removed about as much as it delivered.

**Seasonal regime:** The basin drains almost a fifth of the United States but is very arid. For the past century the river has carried about 13 to 14 million acre-feet per year, compared with about 450 million acre-feet for the [[Mississippi-Missouri Basin]]. Before the dams, most flow arrived as a spring snowmelt flood, with occasional flash floods from plateau storms.

**Post-dam degradation:** The overall trend since the dam has been erosion of sandbar beaches in the upper canyon, with conditions staying about the same in the lower reaches. Reduced sediment input means the river is not replacing material it loses. Schmidt reported that reducing and regularizing flow does not stop erosion, since wave action from rafts also erodes beaches. The document states that the system would need about 700 years to overtop [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and upstream dams naturally and resume carrying large sediment loads.

**Biota:** The text mentions:
- Ten fish and ten rocks fitted with radio transmitters to track how far high water moved them. The fish are not named.
- A colony of an endangered native species moved from riverside habitat, where vegetation had been swept away, to protect it.
- Seeds and plant fragments that help stabilize beaches and sandbars.
- Sparse, scrubby vegetation on the dry lands above the canyon.

**Cultural sites:** The 1996 flood helped stabilize cultural sites along the canyon, and the [[Hualapai Tribe]] and other [[Native American Tribes]] expressed concerns about how the flood was managed.

## Key Findings

**Early history and policy conflict**
- Interior Secretary [[James Watt]] initiated GCES in 1982. The program was created after the [[Environmental Defense Fund]] sued over the dam's expanded generating capacity and a consent decree required an EIS. Congress passed a rider blocking funding for the study, so the office opened without an explicit EIS mandate.
- Wegner was reportedly hired as "window-dressing," but he pursued the research anyway.
- In 1989 Miller threatened to legislate the EIS if the Bureau did not produce it. Interior Secretary [[Manuel Lujan]] ordered the study.

**Environmental impact statement (completed spring 1995)**
- The EIS included a "non-use value" study. About a third of respondents said they were probably willing to pay for alternatives that could raise their electric bills by as much as $200. Almost half said they were probably willing to pay increases under $30.
- It recommended a "modified low fluctuating flow" regime, with daily fluctuations of no more than 8,000 cfs, compared with 20,000 to 25,000 cfs previously.
- It also recommended an [[Adaptive Management Program]] involving power suppliers, environmental and recreation groups, federal and state agencies, and tribes.
- The Bureau estimated that the new flow regime would cost about one-third of the maximum peaking power output from the dam's eight generators.

**1996 experimental flood**
- In spring 1996, the Bureau released a one-week flow of 45,000 cfs, the most the dam can pass without filling the reservoir to the spillways.
- More than 150 scientists worked along the canyon, with helicopter and satellite support.
- Lake Powell dropped 3.5 feet as 360,000 acre-feet were released through the dam's [[Hollow Jet Tubes]].
- Costs were $1.5 million in scientific costs and $1.5 to 4 million in lost power revenues.
- Outcomes reported by GCES in three areas (biological, physical, cultural):
  - More beaches in Marble Canyon and more camping beaches.
  - Over 80 percent of erosion and deposition occurred in the first 20 to 48 hours.
  - Organic debris enriched deposits, creating potential for "nutrient surges," and marsh habitats developed.
  - Cultural sites were stabilized, and erosion rates in side channels and tributaries declined.
- Wegner's assessment: "Technically we appear to have the ability to restore and rejuvenate critical ecosystem processes and elements through the operation of Glen Canyon Dam."

**Agency change**
- Under Beard, the Bureau decentralized management to field offices, downsized by about 25 percent, and received a $100,000 [[Ford Foundation]] award for a restructuring program. New hires were increasingly biologists and ecologists.

**Outlook**
- Schmidt predicted that the Bureau will eventually need to bring sediment from [[Lake Powell]] below the dam, which is accumulating more than 60 million tons per year. He estimated it "may be twenty years" before that happens.
- The essay concludes that the goal is not to restore a pristine river but to help the river finish building itself, in the words of Wegner: "We're all here for the river."
