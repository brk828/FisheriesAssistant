# Effectiveness of Rotenone in Pond Reclamation

## Executive Summary

This 1951-1952 field study, conducted across 30 farm ponds in Oklahoma, evaluated the effectiveness of [[Rotenone]] as a piscicide for total fish eradication in pond reclamation efforts. The research addressed a critical gap in fisheries management: rotenone had been used extensively in Oklahoma from 1948-1950 without adequate controlled study of its efficacy, particularly regarding vertical penetration in thermally stratified ponds. Key findings revealed that over 90% of studied ponds were thermally stratified, and rotenone applied at the surface frequently failed to penetrate to bottom waters in lethal concentrations — even when concentration was quadrupled. Mechanical agitation via outboard motor significantly improved vertical mixing and rotenone penetration. Detoxification rates varied substantially with alkalinity, turbidity, and temperature, ranging from 1 day (high alkalinity, clear, summer) to 9-10 days (high alkalinity, clear, winter). Post-treatment surveys 2-3 months later found that 16 of 18 re-evaluated ponds still contained fish, predominantly young-of-year, indicating that "complete kill" assessments made shortly after treatment were frequently inaccurate. The study concludes with ten practical recommendations for improving rotenone application protocols in farm pond reclamation.

## Background & Description

This paper is a field ecology/fisheries management study rather than a morphological analysis; however, relevant biological/taxonomic observations include:

- **Bioassay indicator species**: [[Notropis lutrensis]] (red shiner) was the primary test organism used to detect lethal rotenone concentrations in water samples via loss of equilibrium and cessation of opercular movement.
- **Differential species susceptibility**: [[Green Sunfish]] were observed to be less susceptible to rotenone poisoning than [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] (per Moorman and Ruhr, 1951), and were capable of recovering after showing poisoning symptoms in at least one documented case.
- **Bullhead resilience**: [[Bullhead]] species (black and yellow) demonstrated a capacity to recover after showing definite physiological response to rotenone exposure, complicating "complete kill" assessments for bottom-dwelling fish.
- **Egg resistance**: Per Leonard (1939), trout eggs were found to be largely unaffected by rotenone exposure directly, though resulting fry died quickly post-hatching — a finding with implications for the "duplicate application" recommendation in this study.
- **Species identified in survey ponds** (18 total, see Table 5): [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Longear Sunfish]], [[Orangespotted Sunfish]], [[White Crappie]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Plains Killifish]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Bluntnose Minnow]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Warmouth]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Small farm ponds (1.0–8.6 acres), typically 6-13 feet in depth, distributed throughout Oklahoma.
- **Thermal stratification**: The overwhelming majority (>90%) of studied ponds exhibited thermal stratification during summer months, creating a physical barrier (thermocline) that impeded rotenone diffusion to bottom waters. This is a crucial ecological factor governing piscicide efficacy in small standing water bodies.
- **Chemical stratification**: Density gradients associated with depth likely reduced mixing of bottom waters, contributing to lower rotenone concentrations and possibly greater rotenone dissipation rates near the pond floor.
- **Oxygen-limited refugia**: In stratified ponds, [[Dissolved Oxygen]] concentrations often declined sharply with depth, meaning fish were physiologically excluded from deep, non-poisoned strata and forced back into the lethal surface zone — a key mechanism enabling effective kills despite incomplete vertical rotenone penetration.
- **Alkalinity gradient across sites**: Ponds ranged from low alkalinity (~16 p.p.m., southeastern Oklahoma) to high alkalinity (60–284 p.p.m.), directly influencing [[Rotenone]] persistence and detoxification time.
- **Turbidity effects**: Turbid, high-alkalinity ponds showed slower detoxification (1-2 days longer) than clear ponds of comparable alkalinity, attributed to reduced light penetration (light is known to accelerate rotenone photodecomposition per Ginsburg 1933; Gunther 1943).
- **Recolonization pathways**: Post-treatment fish presence (particularly [[Green Sunfish]], a common stream-dwelling species) suggested pond recolonization via runoff connectivity to upstream pools rather than incomplete kills alone, highlighting the ecological connectivity between farm ponds and surrounding drainage networks.
- **Seasonal ecology**: A winter trial (Smith's Pond, Kiowa County, homothermous at ~44-51°F) showed dramatically prolonged rotenone persistence (9-10 days) compared to summer conditions, underscoring temperature-dependent detoxification kinetics.

## Key Findings

1. **Stratification barrier**: Rotenone applied to the surface of thermally stratified ponds did not penetrate to bottom waters even at 4x the standard concentration (0.5 vs 2.0 p.p.m. emulsifiable rotenone tested at [[Rattlesnake Pond]] and [[Ranch House Pond]]).
2. **Agitation improves mixing**: Running an outboard motor at full speed over treated pond surfaces successfully broke down thermal stratification and increased lethal rotenone penetration from ~4-5 feet to at least 6 feet (with distress effects detectable to 9 feet). Recommended treatment: ~30 minutes per half-acre.
3. **Alkalinity strongly affects detoxification time**:
   - Low alkalinity (16 p.p.m.): 0.5 p.p.m. rotenone persisted 72-94 hours; 1 p.p.m. persisted 26-84 hours; 2 p.p.m. persisted 98-146 hours.
   - High alkalinity, clear (60-284 p.p.m.): 1 p.p.m. rotenone dissipated in as little as 8-30 hours.
   - High alkalinity, turbid: dissipation took 1-2 days longer than comparable clear ponds.
4. **Concentration vs. dissipation time is non-linear**: Quadrupling concentration (0.5→2 p.p.m.) never more than doubled the persistence time of rotenone in the water column.
5. **Temperature drives winter persistence**: At ~44-51°F, a 2 p.p.m. rotenone dose persisted roughly 6-7 days longer (at least twice as long) as the same concentration during summer conditions in comparable ponds.
6. **Dissipation direction**: Rotenone consistently dissipated first near the bottom of ponds and later at the surface, though for practical purposes disappearance from all levels tended to occur within the same day during summer.
7. **"Complete kill" is frequently illusory**: Of 18 ponds re-evaluated 2-3 months post-treatment, 16 still contained fish (mostly young-of-year, <1 inch), despite 5 of these having been scored as "complete kill" shortly after treatment. This strongly suggests reliance on short-term (2-3 day) post-treatment observation is insufficient to confirm eradication.
8. **Species-specific vulnerability**: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[White Crappie]], [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Orangespotted Sunfish]], and [[Longear Sunfish]] were absent in post-treatment re-surveys, while [[Green Sunfish]] and bullheads were disproportionately likely to survive or recolonize.
9. **Recolonization mechanisms**: The prevalence of stream-associated [[Green Sunfish]] in post-treatment surveys, rather than pond-typical bass or crappie, suggests washing-in from upstream pools during runoff rather than incomplete kill or deliberate restocking as the dominant repopulation pathway.
10. **Practical protocol recommendations**: The authors propose a 10-step application protocol, including: assessing thermocline/oxygen profiles pre-treatment, motor-agitation for mixing, thorough shoreline/vegetation treatment, minimum concentrations of 1 p.p.m. (low alkalinity) to 2 p.p.m. (high alkalinity, clear), duplicate treatments spaced days apart to counter egg survival, and treatment of upstream pools to prevent post-treatment recolonization.
