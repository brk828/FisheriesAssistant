# Climate Impacts on Urban Water Resources in the Southwest: The Importance of Context

#hydrology #water-resources #urban-water #water-management

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Barbara J. Morehouse]] examines how climate variability and potential long-term climate change may affect urban water systems in the Arizona portion of the U.S. Southwest. It argues that sensitivity to climate is shaped strongly by local context, including [[Institutional Arrangements]], population growth, legal frameworks, and the physical hydrology of each area. The analysis draws on the [[CLIMAS Urban Water Study]] of the Phoenix, Tucson, and Santa Cruz [[Active Management Areas]] and Sierra Vista/Fort Huachuca, with the Middle San Pedro Basin treated as a broader community-level case. The author concludes that existing management tools are unlikely to be sufficient under severe, prolonged drought, and that integrated, locally oriented sensitivity analyses are essential for planning under longer-term climate change.

## Background & Description

The Southwest is one of the fastest-growing regions of the United States, with rapid urbanization beginning after World War II and continuing. Water management in Arizona is structured around the [[Arizona Groundwater Management Code]] (1980), which requires the [[Arizona Department of Water Resources]] (ADWR) to balance supply and demand in designated AMAs. The four urban AMAs aim to reach safe yield, where renewable supply equals demand, by 2025. Each AMA produces management plans with enforceable conservation requirements and water budgets projecting supply and demand by category. The Third Management Plans cover 2000 to 2010.

Key water sources and infrastructure include groundwater, surface water from the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) carrying [[Colorado River]] water, effluent, and imported supplies. Sierra Vista lies outside AMA designation and relies on groundwater that may reduce subflows to the [[San Pedro River]] and the [[San Pedro National Riparian Conservation Area]], a habitat for many bird species and one of few remaining riparian areas in the state.

The study uses the water budget categories and format of ADWR but adds changes in precipitation. Sensitivity analysis is defined as an assessment of how an urban water system, made up of supply and demand components, responds to prescribed changes in climatic conditions, following [[Lins and Stakhiv]] (1998). The design draws on prior Colorado River and San Pedro work, including the [[Powell Consortium]] severe sustained drought study. The project is supported by [[NOAA]] through CLIMAS.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Riparian areas:** The San Pedro and Santa Cruz rivers support valued riparian zones and bird habitat. Pumping for urban supply can intercept subflows feeding these rivers. Effluent from Nogales, Sonora, is a major contributor to the Santa Cruz riparian zone.
- **Groundwater recharge:** Most aquifer recharge occurs at mountain fronts through winter precipitation and snowmelt. Winter runoff from high elevations is the main predictor of surface water availability, except in the Santa Cruz AMA, where annual precipitation was used because local aquifers respond to rain throughout the year.
- **Climate patterns:** Summer [[Monsoon]] rains are often localized but predictable. Winter frontal precipitation covers broader areas but varies greatly over time. [[El Niño-Southern Oscillation]] (ENSO) events and their increasing frequency are noted as intensifying existing variability.
- **Evapotranspiration:** Higher temperatures combined with reduced precipitation increase landscape water demand. Outdoor water use in Tucson correlates more directly with evapotranspiration than with precipitation.
- **Historical drought:** Tree-ring records indicate the 1950s drought was the most extreme in the Southwest in the past 400 years, with earlier sustained droughts in the 1500s.

## Key Findings

**Sensitivity scenarios and drought impacts**

- Driest one-year winter period: 1904 (all areas except the Santa Cruz AMA, where it was 1948).
- Driest five-year period: 1900 to 1904 for all areas.
- Driest ten-year period: 1946–1955 (Phoenix AMA), 1948–1957 (Santa Cruz AMA), and 1947–1956 (other areas).
- For Sierra Vista in 2025, the share of demand met by groundwater mining rises to 76% under the 1904 one-year drought, approximately 56% under the 1900–1904 five-year drought, and 47% under the 1947–1956 ten-year drought. Under normal conditions, mining accounts for 32% of demand.

**Growth and demand projections**

- Arizona's population is projected to double over 40 years; statewide water demand is expected to rise 15%.
- Phoenix and Tucson populations are projected to grow by factors of 2.19 and 1.82, respectively.
- Tucson AMA municipal water use is projected to grow about 72% by 2025 under the base scenario; total use rises about 31%. Under a 155 gallons-per-capita-per-day scenario, municipal demand still rises 59%.
- Phoenix total demand is projected to rise 26% under baseline conditions; under the Third Management Plan scenario, total demand rises 12%.

**Institutional and legal constraints**

- Surface water, groundwater, and effluent are managed under different rules; ADWR lacks authority to manage them conjunctively.
- Grandfathered agricultural rights and banking provisions created large credits; in 1997, agricultural credits in the Prescott, Phoenix, and Tucson AMAs exceeded six times total 1995 groundwater consumption of all users in those AMAs.
- Outside AMAs, consumer protection is minimal: no prohibition exists on subdivision sales without sufficient water, and no subsequent buyer notification is required.
- Private providers regulated by the Arizona Corporation Commission cannot pass conservation costs to customers, creating potential inequities.
- Tucson voters and the City Council ban direct potable delivery of CAP water, relying on groundwater despite holding the state's largest CAP allotment.

**Regional differences**

- Phoenix has diverse surface and groundwater supplies, offering a buffer against short-term drought.
- Tucson depends almost entirely on groundwater.
- The Santa Cruz AMA depends on a narrow, shallow aquifer and faces binational water management with Mexico.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- Shifting agricultural water to urban uses and storing water are key policies, but preliminary CLIMAS analyses for 2025 indicate they are insufficient alone for severe, prolonged drought. Stringent conservation enforcement and pricing changes are also needed.
- Sensitivity analyses should be run every year or two using updated GCM results and water budgets.
- Current GCMs poorly represent Southwest topography and summer monsoons. Table 2 scenarios (Hadley CM2, Canadian CGCM1, and High-Resolution model) show divergent temperature and precipitation projections; higher temperatures could increase evapotranspiration and reduce supplies even where precipitation rises.
- Complacency may develop because the last major drought occurred in the 1950s, before current population levels. The author notes that the past 20 years have been relatively wet, and asks how residents would respond to 10 or 20 years of drier conditions.
- Integrated research spanning natural and social sciences, water managers, and decision makers is presented as the most promising approach.
