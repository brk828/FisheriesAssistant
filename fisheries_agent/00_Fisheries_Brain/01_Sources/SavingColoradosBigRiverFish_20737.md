# Saving Colorado's Big River Fish

#ichthyology #conservation #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This article describes conservation efforts in Colorado to save four unique big-river fish species found nowhere else on Earth: the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). These species evolved over approximately 10 million years in the warm, turbulent waters of the [[Colorado River]] system but have experienced drastic population declines due to river regulation, habitat loss, and competition from introduced species. The [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] is conducting research to determine population sizes, distributions, and optimal habitat requirements necessary for species recovery.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] system began forming approximately 10 million years ago during the Tertiary Period when the [[Colorado Plateau]] was gradually uplifted. This geological process created the immense canyons and deep gorges characteristic of the region and led to the development of a unique assemblage of big-river fishes adapted to warm, turbulent waters originating in the Rockies.

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] was historically the most prominent of these species, once called the "white salmon of the Colorado." Early accounts from Colonel William H. Ashley in 1825 and Major John Wesley Powell's mid-nineteenth century expeditions documented the species' importance as food for Native Americans and its commercial harvest by white settlers. Ashley described them as "an excellent kind of species...similar in appearance to our pike." Historical records document squawfish reaching five feet in length and 50-80 pounds.

The [[Humpback Chub]] is described as "bizarre" in appearance, while the [[Bonytail Chub]] is characterized as "ultra-streamlined." The [[Humpback Sucker]] is noted as "odd" in appearance. All four species were once widespread and abundant throughout the Colorado River system, with ranges extending from Wyoming through Colorado's West Slope rivers to the Gulf of California.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The decline of these big-river fishes began in the late nineteenth century as river modification advanced from the lower basin toward the headwaters. The [[Colorado River]] is now one of the most highly regulated river systems in the world. River regulation has had disastrous impacts on native fishes through:

- Streamflow reductions
- Barriers to movement created by dams
- Reduction of total overall habitat
- Severe curtailment of reproductive success
- Complete drying of some formerly important habitat during parts of the year

The introduction of highly competitive non-native fishes (both accidentally and purposefully) has accompanied river regulation and possibly contributed to native fish decline.

Current distribution is restricted to relatively small sections of the upper drainage, including the [[Yampa River]], [[Green River]], [[Gunnison River]], [[White River]], and [[Colorado River]]. The [[Bonytail Chub]] may be extinct in Colorado.

Both the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] are classified as state and federally endangered species. The [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Sucker]] are classified as endangered by the State of Colorado.

## Key Findings

**Conservation Status and Classification:**
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]]: federally and state endangered
- [[Bonytail Chub]]: state endangered, possibly extinct in Colorado
- [[Humpback Sucker]]: state endangered

**Recent Research Discoveries (1979-1980):**
- Young and larval [[Colorado Squawfish]] discovered in the [[Colorado River]] west of [[Grand Junction]]
- Largest squawfish captured in August 1979: approximately 14 pounds, just under three feet in length (still far smaller than historical five-foot specimens)
- Slightly smaller squawfish captured in July in the Colorado and Yampa rivers
- [[Humpback Chub]] population appears stable in [[Black Rocks]] area of Colorado River

**Research Objectives:**
The [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] nongame wildlife program is conducting research to:
- Determine size and distribution of existing populations
- Identify factors characterizing existing and potential optimal habitat
- Overcome difficulties of capturing rare fish in varied river conditions

**Critical Conservation Needs:**
- Preservation of sufficient natural habitat providing necessary conditions for growth and reproduction
- Habitat enhancement
- Maintenance of habitat as demand for Western Slope water increases
- Prevention of future habitat alteration, particularly in unique canyon areas like [[Black Rocks]]

**Future Management Potential:**
If species can be sufficiently recovered and removed from endangered lists, proper management may provide unique freshwater fishing experiences. While extensive spawning migrations of the "white salmon of the Colorado" will likely never return, recovery could present the possibility of challenging 5-foot (50-80 pound) fish on hook and line.

**Public Participation Request:**
Anglers who catch these rare fishes are requested to:
- Return fish to water with minimal handling
- Record tag numbers (bright orange or red tags behind dorsal fin) and exact catch location
- Report information to Division biologists
- Photograph fish without jeopardizing the specimen
- Not disturb tags (which may be covered with brown-green algae coating after a month or two)
