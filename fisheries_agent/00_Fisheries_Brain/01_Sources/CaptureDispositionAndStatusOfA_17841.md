# Razorback Sucker Capture, Disposition and Health Status in Lake Mohave, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1975 progress report from the [[Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory]] at [[Arizona State University]], submitted to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Regional Office, documents efforts in March and April 1975 to recover live [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) from Lake Mohave. The goals were to establish a brood stock at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and to gather information on population size, distribution, behavior and health. Netting yielded 101 adult razorbacks, and about 5,000 fertilized eggs were obtained on 29 April 1975. Disease and hybridization were identified as serious threats. Most hatchery fish later died from systemic bacterial infection, and a non-native [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*) was reported, raising the risk of hybridization. The authors call for research and disease-management systems.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a catostomid fish native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Spawning aggregations were observed in the last week of March 1975 along the lake shore about one mile south of [[Hoover Dam]], where hot springs line both banks of an essentially lotic habitat. Divers counted about 200 razorbacks in shallow water (1.0–2.5 m) and deep water (3.0–7.5 m). Spawning was consistent with the account of Douglas (1952): one female is flanked by two males. Spawning occurred among boulders near a hot spring effusion in about 1 m of water.

The report also mentions [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), with four gravid females captured in the Cottonwood Cove net. Hybrids between *Xyrauchen* and *Catostomus* (sucker genera) were also taken.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Field sampling used trammel nets (1 in mesh within 1 ft mesh) at several sites:

- **Arizona bank, late March 1975:** one shallow net over gravel-boulder bottom and one deep net on the edge of large submerged boulders. Seven males and eight females were taken from the deep set, along with one hybrid.
- **Cottonwood Cove, April 1975:** a small cove about 20 miles north of Davis Dam, with one net set off a wave-washed reef and one parallel to shore in shallow water. Ninety-one suckers were captured.

Pathological surveys from October 1973 to April 1975 (by [[Bruce D. Rosenlund]], U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) examined fish from Cottonwood Cove and the hatchery. Water temperature in March 1974 was 60°F, and the authors suggest that winter temperatures may have reduced parasite incidence.

Pathogens and parasites identified include [[Lernaea]] (anchor worm), [[Myxosoma]] sp., [[Saprolegnia]], [[Pseudomonas]] sp., [[Aeromonas hydrophila]] and *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*.

## Key Findings

- **Recovery and brood stock:** Of 101 adult razorbacks netted in four weeks, 40 males and 29 females were placed in a raceway at Willow Beach. About 90% of females and 44.5% of males had already spawned. Human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) was injected to induce ovulation. Eggs were stripped from one female and fertilized with sperm from two males, yielding approximately 5,000 fertilized eggs on 29 April 1975.
- **Disease:** Autopsies of nine dead suckers found lethal concentrations of *Pseudomonas* sp. and *Aeromonas* sp. in internal organs. By the report's date, all but 18 of the hatchery razorbacks had died. Antibiotic injections were started, and potassium permanganate was applied topically to fungal lesions with limited success.
- **Eye condition:** In March 1974, of 38 fish kept for spawning, 40% had normal eyes, 34% showed some abnormality, 5% were probably nonfunctional, and 21% were nonfunctional or fully degenerated. About 50% of degenerated eyes contained *Myxosoma* spores. Causes were unresolved (*Myxosoma*, *Lernaea*, bacteria, or a combination).
- **Gonad condition:** Rudimentary or absent gonads were noted in several autopsied fish. The authors suggest pathogens or backcrosses of hybrids with *X. texanus* could be responsible; this was under investigation.
- **Hybridization:** Data indicated at least a 6% hybridization incidence between *Xyrauchen* and *Catostomus*. An unidentified *Catostomus* species was preliminarily identified as the White Sucker, which is native to eastern North America and not previously recorded in the lower basin. Its long-term effects on hybridization and competition are unknown.
- **Spawning damage:** In 1974, blind males showed no spawning damage, sighted males showed marked damage, and females (blind or sighted) showed damage.
- **Conclusions and recommendations:** Disease and hybridization pose a "very real threat" to a viable Lake Mohave razorback population. The authors recommend research on population size, hybridization frequency, ecology and reproduction, plus disease detection and treatment systems and monitoring of introduced species.
