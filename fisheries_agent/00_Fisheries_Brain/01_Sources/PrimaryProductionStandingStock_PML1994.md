# Primary Production, Standing Stock, and Export of Organic Matter in a Mohave Desert Thermal Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Robert J. Naiman (Arizona State University) quantifies the energy budget of [[Tecopa Bore]], a small thermal artesian stream in the [[Mohave Desert]] near [[Death Valley]], California. The stream emerges at 47.5°C and cools 8–12°C across a 300 m study area, supporting year-round growth of [[Algal Mats]]. The only significant herbivore is the [[Amargosa Pupfish]], and aquatic invertebrates are rare. Annual energy input is accounted for entirely by [[Autochthonous Production]]: an annual [[Primary Production]] of 11,065 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹. Most of this energy is respired or passed to decomposers, while a substantial share leaves the system as [[Dissolved Organic Carbon]] and [[Particulate Drift]]. Pupfish convert a small fraction of primary production into biomass.

## Background & Description

**Study site.** Tecopa Bore lies about 1.7 km north of Tecopa Hot Springs, Inyo County, California, along the ancient shoreline of [[Pleistocene Lake Tecopa]]. The hot artesian water was discovered during exploratory drilling for mineral deposits in 1967. After unsuccessful attempts to plug the drill hole, the water was channeled into an existing marsh containing Amargosa pupfish, which rapidly colonized the 300 m study site. The system consists of a thermal artesian well with an outflow stream 3–6 m wide and less than 10 cm deep.

**Rationale.** The stream was chosen for a [[Bioenergetics]] study because it combines features rarely found together:
- Almost no terrestrial vegetation shades the water, so the aquatic flora receives nearly all available solar radiation.
- Allochthonous detritus contributes insignificantly, unlike many streams.
- Only one significant herbivore is present, the Amargosa pupfish (*Cyprinodon nevadensis amargosae* Miller).

**Methods overview.** Monthly sampling was conducted at 10 permanent stations, each 30 m apart, from preliminary observations in August 1971 through September 1973. Methods included:
- Temperature and light measurements (YSI thermistor; cadmium-sulfide light meter calibrated against an Epply pyrheliometer)
- Water chemistry (atomic absorption spectrophotometry, colorimetry, standard methods, and total organic carbon analysis)
- Primary productivity by ¹⁴C-bicarbonate incubation of algal cores
- Particulate drift sampled with two Wildco drift nets
- Caloric determinations by bomb calorimetry, with a conversion factor of 9.33 kcal g C⁻¹

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and vegetation.** The region is hot and dry, with low relative humidity, strong winds, summer temperatures approaching 53°C, and annual evaporation 30–40 times greater than annual precipitation. Semiaquatic vegetation is dominated by smooth cyperus (*Cyperus laevigatus*) and salt grass (*Distichlis spicata*), with ditch grass (*Ruppia maritima*) rare. Terrestrial vegetation is sparse, mainly a few creosote bushes (*Larrea*).

**Thermal regime.** Mainstream temperatures fall 8–12°C on calm days before the water leaves the study area, and cool faster on cold or windy days. Margins and side channels are 1–4°C cooler than the mainstream, and ice can form in side channels on winter nights. Station 4, nearest the source, is the warmest; station 98, at the lower end, is the coolest.

**Light.** No trees or objects shade the algal mats. Corrected for altitude (415 m) and photosynthetic spectrum (50%) and reflection (7%), the annual energy input is 757,488 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹. Monthly values range from a high of 95,555 kcal m⁻² in May to 35,805 kcal m⁻² in December.

**Hydrology.** Discharge follows a semiannual cycle: high in late spring and summer, low in autumn and winter. The greatest discharge was in September 1972 (6.7 m³ min⁻¹) and the smallest in March 1973 (2.3 m³ min⁻¹). About 1.87 × 10⁶ liters of water are discharged each year. Mainstream velocity is slow and steady, averaging 0.311 m s⁻¹.

**Water chemistry.** Tecopa Bore resembles typical sulfate-chloride desert waters. Total alkalinity is nearly constant, from 601 to 645 mg L⁻¹ (mean 618 mg L⁻¹, as CaCO₃). Bicarbonate and carbonate are stable, and pH stays near 8.5. Inorganic carbon averaged 135.3 mg L⁻¹ (SD = 17.0). Sodium, sulfate, silicate, and bicarbonate are abundant, and the cation-anion balance is equal.

**Biota.** Algal diversity is low, as is typical of thermal systems: four species of Cyanophyta and three common diatoms. *Oscillatoria cf. terebriformis* forms most of the blue-green biomass and is the principal primary producer at most stations. Diatoms (including *Nitzschia*, *Navicula*, and *Rhopalodia gibberula*) dominate only station 98, the cool grassy reach.

## Key Findings

**Energy input and fixation**
- Primary production fixes an average of 1,186 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹, totaling an estimated 1,409,920 g C fixed within the ecosystem annually.
- At 9.33 kcal g C⁻¹, this equals 11,065 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹, or 1.315 × 10⁷ kcal yr⁻¹.
- The entire energy input to the biological system is autochthonous, so the stream depends on its own primary production.
- Mean monthly productivity varies between 2.5 and 4.0 g C m⁻² day⁻¹, with an overall yearly average of 3.25 g C m⁻² day⁻¹. This sustained productivity provides pupfish with a reliable food supply year-round.
- Station 4 near the source averages 4.78 g C m⁻² day⁻¹, the highest rate. Stations 24–86 average about 3.5 g C m⁻² day⁻¹. Station 98 averages 2.03 g C m⁻² day⁻¹. Nutrient depletion does not explain the low rate at station 98, since essential ions stayed high.
- Photosynthetic efficiency is about 1.46% of the 48.5% of the light spectrum available for photosynthesis, which is high relative to most natural systems.

**Standing stock**
- The mean annual standing crop of organic matter is 718 kcal m⁻². Monthly means ranged from 522 kcal m⁻² (October 1972) to 1,302 kcal m⁻² (August 1973).
- The standing crop turns over about 15.4 times per year, with a turnover time of about 23 days (the study text also cites 93 days in one place).
- Standing crop falls in autumn and rises in spring.

**Fate of primary production**
- Respiration and decomposers consume 81% of annual primary production.
- Dissolved organic carbon accounts for 9%, and particulate drift for 4%.
- Combined downstream export (DOC plus particulate drift) is about 13% of primary production.

**Dissolved organic carbon export**
- The DOC export is estimated at 279,000 g yr⁻¹, or 1,395,000 kcal yr⁻¹ at 5.00 kcal g DOC⁻¹. The body text gives this as 10.6% of annual net production.
- DOC export is about 2.2 times greater than particulate drift export.
- Export is highest in the summer peak (around 2,000 g DOC day⁻¹, June to August) and lower in the winter peak (up to about 1,000 g day⁻¹, September to April).

**Particulate drift**
- The estimated export is 14,400 g AFDW yr⁻¹, equal to 633,600 kcal yr⁻¹, or about 4% of primary production.
- Drift peaks from late spring through summer and is near zero in winter. Sunlight drives drift by trapping oxygen bubbles on algal mats, causing mats to float and break loose.
- One comparison showed a 2.1-fold increase in solar radiation producing a 20-fold increase in drift.

**Pupfish energetics**
- Pupfish ingest 17% (1,878 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹) of annual primary production, of which 119 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹ is deposited as growth. This gives a gross growth efficiency of 6.3%.
- Assuming pupfish respire about five times their growth, roughly 10.5% of primary production passes through their metabolism. Their feces are of poor food quality, and much of the ingested energy ends up as detritus.
- Mean annual standing crop of pupfish is 23.6 kcal m⁻². The population turns over about five times per year, with a mean generation time of 72 days (about 2.4 months), and appears to be in a steady state.
- Pupfish densities reached 190 individuals per m², with a mean of 89 per m² in the inhabited channel.

**Comparisons**
- Tecopa Bore's primary productivity is comparable to other thermal streams. Only the values reported by Lehn (1966) and Duke (1967) differ significantly.
- Unlike Bear Brook, New Hampshire, which is more than 99% allochthonous and exports 48% of its energy as DOM, Tecopa Bore is almost entirely autochthonous and exports about a fifth of Bear Brook's share.
- Less than 1% of organic matter is consumed by macroconsumers in Bear Brook, while at Tecopa Bore pupfish cycle about 6% of annual input.

**Conclusion.** Tecopa Bore is a highly productive, structurally simple thermal ecosystem. Most fixed energy is lost through respiration or channeled to decomposers, and the pupfish function as a minor but persistent energy pathway in an otherwise microbially dominated system.
