# Biotic Effects of Grazing by Tilapia (Oreochromis aureus) on Phytoplankton Populations in Solar Silos

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #oreochromis-aureus

## Executive Summary

This experimental study tests how grazing by the omnivorous fish [[Oreochromis Aureus]] (blue tilapia) at two stocking densities affects phytoplankton abundance, biomass and community composition. The experiment ran for 15 weeks in five outdoor [[Solar Silos]] (free-standing, mixed microcosms). The fish reduced phytoplankton cell numbers by one to two orders of magnitude in the high-density silos, while cell numbers rose in the low-density silos. Grazing also cut [[Chlorophyll A]] and total protein, and it shifted the algal community toward gelatinous forms. The author argues that fish grazing, not only zooplankton grazing, can structure phytoplankton communities in eutrophic systems.

## Background & Description

- **Study organism:** *Oreochromis aureus* (blue tilapia). Gut-content evidence describes it as an omnivore with a distinct preference for phytoplankton, but no published work had quantified its effects on dense natural phytoplankton at different densities.
- **Context:** Phytoplankton abundance and succession are usually attributed to physical and chemical factors (light, temperature, nutrient loading). Grazing by herbivores has long been inferred but seldom tested. Classic limnology texts devote little attention to grazing, and [[Zooplankton]] are generally treated as the main link between phytoplankton and higher trophic levels.
- **Fisheries relevance:** Tilapia, milkfish (*Chanos chanos*) and carp support some of the most important world fisheries. The introduction cites a study of Mojave Desert flora (Grinnell? the scanned citation is partly illegible) suggesting that the presence or absence of tilapia was the most important ecological factor structuring the biota in that system.
- **Author affiliation:** Barry A. Pierce, Department of Zoology, University of Vermont, Burlington, VT (present address: Department of Oceanography, University of Hawaii, Honolulu). The paper was submitted 29 March 1983 and accepted 8 July 1983.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Solar silos and system design**
- Five cylindrical silos, each 1.52 m in diameter and height, were set on 7.6 cm of styrofoam insulation.
- Each silo was scrubbed with warm water, left for a week to remove possible toxic contaminants, rinsed and refilled with well water (about 2,500 L after rinsing).
- Diffused aeration (about 100 cm³/min per diffuser) kept the water mixed, which prevented succession driven by differential sinking rates. Pumps ran each night, and dissolved oxygen stayed at or above 5 mg/L throughout.

**Nutrients and water chemistry**
- Silos were fertilized with compost (25 L) when nutrients dropped below set thresholds (about 0.1 mg/L soluble phosphorus or nitrogen, per the cited method). Nutrient checks were made at sunset with Hach kits for nitrate and phosphate.
- Approximately 20% of the water was siphoned from the bottom every two weeks and replaced with fresh well water.
- pH was measured weekly; the document notes pH fluctuations but treats them as a secondary factor.

**Inoculum and fish stocking**
- On 2 June 1979, each silo received 50 L of water from a local eutrophic pond (Curtis Pond, Calais, VT) and 50 L from the Winooski River.
- Fish were stocked on 24 July 1979, near the midpoint of the 15-week experiment. Fish were about 5.0 ± 0.4 cm total length.

**Experimental treatments**

| Treatment | Silos | Fish | Biomass per silo |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (Control) | 1 | None | None |
| 2 (Low density) | 2 and 3 | 20 individuals | 0.23 kg |
| 3 (High density) | 4 and 5 | 44 individuals | 0.44 kg |

The abstract reports densities of roughly 7 and 17 fish per m³ (OCR-garbled in the source; these are consistent with the stocking numbers and silo volume).

**Sampling**
- Water was sampled weekly in the morning, shortly after aeration was stopped, using a subsurface glass bottle.
- Chlorophyll a was measured from duplicate filtered aliquots, and total protein and phytoplankton cell counts were also recorded.
- The experiment ran 24 May to 12 September 1979.

## Key Findings

**1. Phytoplankton abundance responded strongly to fish density**
- Phytoplankton reached about 5.1 × 10⁸ cells (units garbled in the scan) in the silos.
- In high-density silos, fish grazing reduced cell numbers by one to two orders of magnitude.
- In low-density silos, cell numbers increased over the experiment.

**2. Biomass indicators declined under grazing**
- Chlorophyll a was reduced by roughly 45–76% in the grazed silos (figure partly garbled in the OCR).
- Total protein fell by 61–72% during the last four weeks of the experiment.

**3. Community composition shifted**
- Under low-density grazing, the community moved toward chlorophytes such as *Chlorella* spp. and *Golenkinia*, along with *Dictyosphaerium pulchellum*.
- In high-density silos, a gelatinous alga became dominant.
- The author notes that similar shifts toward gelatinous algae have been reported under intense grazing by herbivorous zooplankton in eutrophic lakes.

**4. Zooplankton were largely absent**
- The discussion states that zooplankton were virtually absent throughout the experiment. This means the observed effects are attributable to fish grazing alone, which supports the paper's central claim.

**5. Mechanisms and implications**
- The author suggests that fish-driven grazing pressure may be needed to produce gelatinous algal communities, and that mucous secretions by *O. aureus* may influence the outcome. The author notes that selective feeding effects could not be ruled out.
- Grazing by fish may act in a [[Trophic Cascade]] that maintains phytoplankton diversity at intermediate levels, a pattern previously proposed by Hutchinson (1961) for zooplankton.
- The author also cites Porter (1973), who considered gelatinous algae of low nutrient value to grazers, and notes that digestibility data for these forms were not measured in this study.
- The paper calls for further work on how fish feeding affects [[Eutrophication]] and on the nutritional value of gelatinous algae to grazers.

**6. Methodological notes**
- The study used replicated silos (two per treatment), a single control, and a 15-week time series. The small replication and single season limit how far results can be generalized.
- The solar silo approach is presented as a way to run controlled, outdoor, mixed-community experiments with natural phytoplankton inocula.
