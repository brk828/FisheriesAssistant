# Factors Affecting Fish Forage Production in Four Arizona Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fishery-biology

## Executive Summary

This 1969 Master of Science thesis by Eric P. Bergersen, submitted to the [[University of Arizona]] in fishery biology, investigates why [[Parker Canyon Lake]] has low fish production. Between September 1967 and September 1968, the author compared forage-supporting factors across Parker Canyon, [[Woods Canyon Lake]], [[Pena Blanca Lake]], and [[Becker Lake]]. The main finding is that inflow [[Nitrate]] concentrations correlate strongly with the standing crops of planktonic, benthic, and littoral forage organisms. The author concludes that the low productivity of Parker Canyon Lake is attributed to low nitrate import.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Thesis (Master of Science, Fishery Biology), Department of Biological Sciences, University of Arizona, 1969.
- **Author:** Eric P. Bergersen.
- **Stated purpose:** To better understand the low rate of fish production in Parker Canyon Lake.
- **Study lakes:** Four reservoirs in Arizona, each described in its own subsection of the study area:
  - [[Parker Canyon Lake]] (focal lake, low fish production)
  - [[Pena Blanca Lake]]
  - [[Becker Lake]]
  - [[Woods Canyon Lake]]
- **Study period:** September 1967 to September 1968.
- **Structure:** Ten tables and several figures. The figures cover temperature gradients, relative food consumption by major fish groups, and [[Largemouth Bass]] growth in Parker Canyon and Becker lakes (December 1966 to August 1968). The thesis also includes an appendix of raw tabular data and a literature cited list.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study examined the chain from water chemistry to fish, with these measured components:

- **Plankton and benthos:** Standing crops of [[Zooplankton]], [[Benthic Invertebrates]] (fish food organisms), and [[Phytoplankton]] measured as [[Chlorophyll]].
- **Littoral zone:** Invertebrate standing crops associated with [[Littoral Zone]] weeds, notably [[Myriophyllum Exalbescens]] and [[Ceratophyllum Demersum]].
- **Water chemistry:** Alkalinity, nitrate, and phosphate in inflowing waters (inflows of 2.0 cfs or less), plus [[Trace Metals]] in lake water, analysed by [[Atomic Absorption Spectrometry]].
- **Plant tissue:** Kjeldahl nitrogen and metal content of aquatic plant petioles.
- **Physical structure:** [[Temperature Stratification]] and oxygen stratification.
- **Fish biology:** [[Fish Food Habits]] and [[Fish Growth]].

## Key Findings

1. **Nitrate drives forage production.** Inflow nitrate concentrations were highly correlated with standing crops of:
   - planktonic forage organisms (r = .993)
   - benthic forage organisms (r = .929)
   - littoral forage organisms (r = .950)

2. **Phytoplankton supports the food web, but nitrate sets its value.** Fish forage organisms appeared to be supported primarily by phytoplankton. The author suggests that the forage value of phytoplankton is influenced by nitrate import.

3. **Metals are not limiting.** Heavy and trace metal concentrations were not believed to limit invertebrate production. Atomic absorption analyses covered three inflows to Parker Canyon Lake (5 January 1968) and one unnamed inflow to Woods Canyon Lake (30 March 1968).

4. **Stratification constrains benthos and fish access.** Temperature and oxygen stratification limited benthic invertebrate production and restricted fish access to deep benthic communities.

5. **Slow plant turnover limits the plant food chain.** The low turnover rate of aquatic plant biomass in Parker Canyon Lake may limit how much these plants contribute to invertebrate food chains.

6. **Central conclusion.** The low productivity of Parker Canyon Lake is attributed to low nitrate import.
