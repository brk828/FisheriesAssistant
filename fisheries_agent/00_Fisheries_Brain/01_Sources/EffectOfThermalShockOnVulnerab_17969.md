# Effect of Thermal Shock on Vulnerability of Juvenile Salmonids to Predation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonids

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study tested whether sublethal exposure of juvenile [[Chinook Salmon]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] to high temperatures makes them more vulnerable to predation by larger fish. The work was motivated by juvenile salmonids passing through the [[Thermal Discharge]] mixing zones of the [[Hanford Site]] reactors on the upper [[Columbia River]]. Shocked fish were selectively eaten by larger rainbow trout once exposure exceeded a minimum duration, about 10% of the median death time at the test temperature (range 26 to 30 C). Longer exposures increased vulnerability almost exponentially, and partial recovery occurred after holding in cool water. The author argues that predation-based criteria can detect harmful thermal effects at much lower stress levels than death or visible equilibrium loss.

## Background & Description

The study was published by [[Charles Coutant]] (Ecosystems Department, Battelle Memorial Institute, Pacific Northwest Laboratories) in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada* (1973, vol. 30, pp. 965-973). It was funded under U.S. Atomic Energy Commission Contract AT(45-1)-1830.

Three observations motivated the work:

1. In [[Thermal Resistance Time]] experiments, fish lost equilibrium before opercular beating stopped, the accepted endpoint for death. Equilibrium loss occurred at exposures averaging as low as 40% of the lethal exposure duration for some temperatures.
2. Predatory fishes and gulls gathered near a [[Hanford Site]] thermal mixing zone ([[Prentice 1969]]).
3. Larger fish are known to be an important source of juvenile salmonid mortality in nature ([[Ricker 1941]]; [[Thompson 1959]]; [[Roos 1960]]).

Two experimental series were conducted:

- **Series 1 (1968-1969):** Several exposure durations at one lethal temperature for each prey species. Goals were to quantify differential predation and to compare predator perception of stress with investigator-detected [[Loss Of Equilibrium]].
- **Series 2 (fall 1969):** Rainbow trout at 26, 28, and 30 C, to test whether susceptibility follows a classical [[Dose Response]] pattern in which temperature and duration both matter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Prey and predator biology**

- Juvenile [[Chinook Salmon]] were reared from eggs of adults captured at Priest Rapids Dam, 43 km upstream. They averaged 64.3 mm (SD 9.9) and 3.8 g (SD 1.9) in 1968-1969 tests.
- Juvenile [[Rainbow Trout]] from laboratory brood stock averaged 75.4 mm (SD 9.6) and 8.0 g (SD 2.7). Fall 1969 fish averaged 95.03 mm (SD 15.74) and 16.26 g (SD 6.97), and only the smallest fish were used.
- Predators were large rainbow trout, mostly 2-year-olds, with 3 to 5 per tank. They were the native form for the central Columbia River, including [[Steelhead]] migrants.

**Thermal context in the river**

Juvenile salmonids migrating seaward passed through zones at the [[Hanford Site]] where temperatures exceeded 48 C. Predators waited outside these zones at 2-3 C above ambient. The laboratory simplified this by using a constant high temperature, whereas the river produced an abrupt rise followed by fluctuating decline. Test shock temperatures were 28.0 ± 0.05 C for chinook (median death time about 22.5 min) and 30.5 ± 0.05 C for rainbow (median death time about 15.5 min). Holding temperature was 15 C for chinook, while rainbow were held in pumped [[Columbia River]] water at about 17-19 C.

**Experimental setup**

- Fish were marked with a [[Liquid Nitrogen Cold Brand]] and recovered for at least 72 hr.
- Shocked and control groups (25 fish each) were exposed in separate tanks, then recombined and offered to predators within 1 min, or after a recovery period.
- Predation tanks were 1.2 or 1.8 m diameter, with water depths of 65 or 80 cm, and about 15 L/min flow. They had no cover other than inlet, standpipe, and walls.
- Predation was stopped after about 50% of prey (about 25 fish) were eaten, within a 4-hr maximum. Tests were valid only if 15 to 35 of the initial 50 fish remained.

**Analysis**

Predation was expressed as the ratio d_p = i₁/i₂ of instantaneous mortality rates for shocked versus control fish, following [[Bams 1967]]. Heterogeneity and significance were assessed with [[Chi-Square Test]]s, and survivor lengths with Student's t-test.

## Key Findings

**Series 1: Chinook and rainbow, single shock temperature**

- **Rainbow, no recovery:** Predators took significantly more shocked fish after exposures of 30, 40, and 50% of the median time to loss of equilibrium. Exposures of 15, 20, and 25% showed no significant selection. At 15%, shocked fish actually survived better than controls. The estimated onset was about 18% (about 90 s), but replicate variability was high.
- **Rainbow, 1/2-hr recovery:** Significant selection occurred at 80 and 100% of the equilibrium-loss time, but not at 50 or 60%. Recovery was therefore substantial.
- **Chinook, no recovery:** Exposures of 10, 20, 30, and 50% of the equilibrium-loss time caused significantly greater predation (P < 0.01). Exposure at 5% (33 s) was not significant. The estimated onset was about 5%.
- **Chinook, recovery:** After 1/2 hr, the 80% exposure remained significant. After a 1-hr holding period, selective predation still occurred at 80%.
- **Length:** Shocked and control survivors never differed significantly in length, so size differences did not explain the results.

**Series 2: Rainbow, three shock temperatures**

| Shock temperature | Estimated onset of selective predation |
|---|---|
| 26 C | About 32 min (significant at 64, 90, 120 min; 16 min showed less susceptibility) |
| 28 C | About 2 min (extrapolated; significant at 4, 8, 16 min) |
| 30 C | About 0.55 min (significant at 1.5 min and above) |

- Across temperatures, selective predation began at an average exposure of **10.9% of the median death time**.
- Vulnerability onset occurred at temperatures about **2.5 C lower** than for death at the same exposure time.

**Conclusions and implications**

- Brief thermal shocks that do not kill or visibly disorient fish can still raise predation risk. Once above the threshold, vulnerability rises almost exponentially with exposure time.
- Behavior of shocked fish (disorientation, erratic bursts, abnormal posture) likely speeded discovery and capture. Reduced responsiveness to a forceps stimulus suggested weakened escape ability.
- Predation, death, and equilibrium loss curves appear roughly parallel when plotted per [[Fry 1946]] and [[Brett 1952]], supporting **correction factors** for other species: reduce exposure time to about 10% of the death-time exposure, or reduce temperature by about 2 C.
- Recovery appears partial and possibly two-stage. Short exposures recovered within 1/2 hr, while longer ones left lasting susceptibility that persisted after 1 hr, suggesting more permanent damage. The author calls for longer recovery studies.
- The author argues that predation-based [[Thermal Discharge]] criteria would be more protective than criteria based on death or visible equilibrium loss, and that the data could guide design standards for thermal releases that do not stress aquatic organisms.
- Limitations noted: constant rather than fluctuating shock temperatures, the second (cold) shock on return to holding temperature was not separated from the first, and tank cover was not fully absent. Tests were also affected by a bias from the 50% consumption rule.
