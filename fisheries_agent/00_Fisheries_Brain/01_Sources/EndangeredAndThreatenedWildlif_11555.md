# Endangered Species Determination: Desert Pupfish (Cyprinodon macularius)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae #endangered-species #federal-listing

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register document (Vol. 51, No. 61, March 31, 1986) announces the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's final determination to list the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) as an endangered species under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, as amended. The rule also designates critical habitat in Imperial County, California (San Felipe Creek system, San Sebastian Marsh, Salt Creek) and Pima County, Arizona (Quitobaquito Spring, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument). Viable, self-sustaining populations are believed to exist in only two historic U.S. habitats; Mexican populations are also declining or vulnerable. The effective date is April 30, 1986.

## Background & Description

The [[Desert Pupfish]] is a small, laterally-compressed fish with a smoothly rounded body shape. Adults rarely exceed 75 millimeters (3 inches) total length. Males are larger than females and during reproductive season become brightly colored with blue on the dorsal head and sides and yellow on the caudal fin and posterior caudal peduncle. Females and juveniles typically have tan to olive backs and silvery sides. Most adults display narrow, vertical, dark bars on their sides, often interrupted to create a disjunct lateral band appearance. The species was described in 1853 by Baird and Girard from specimens collected in the [[San Pedro River]] of Arizona.

Historically, the desert pupfish was common in desert springs, marshes, and tributary streams of the lower [[Gila River]] and [[Colorado River]] drainages in Arizona, California, and Mexico. It formerly occurred in slow-moving reaches of large rivers including the Colorado, Gila, San Pedro, and Santa Cruz. Currently known from only two historic U.S. locations: (1) California - two [[Salton Sea]] tributaries (San Felipe Creek system with San Sebastian Marsh in Imperial County; Salt Creek in Riverside County) plus shoreline pools and irrigation drains around the Salton Sea; (2) Arizona - [[Quitobaquito Spring]] within [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], Pima County. The species is believed to inhabit the [[Rio Sonoyta]] drainage and [[Santa Clara Slough]] in Sonora, Mexico. Recent surveys (Moore 1983; McMahon and Miller 1985) indicate populations in Salt Creek, Salton Sea irrigation drains, and Rio Sonoyta may be reduced to non-viable levels. The status of Santa Clara Slough population is unknown, though 1983-1984 Colorado River delta floods may have given exotic fishes ([[Tilapia]], [[Largemouth Bass]]) access to this habitat.

Refugia populations have been established in Arizona at Bog Hole (Santa Cruz County), Research Ranch (Santa Cruz County), Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum (Pima County), Boyce Thompson Arboretum (Pinal County), and Arizona State University (Maricopa County). California refugia exist at Salton Sea State Park (Riverside County), Living Desert Reserve (Riverside County), and three locations in Anza-Borrego State Park (San Diego County). Most refugia populations are maintained in highly artificial environments with relatively small numbers. Desert pupfish are also held at Dexter National Fish Hatchery, Dexter, New Mexico, for research and future reintroduction. Recent introductions into natural and manmade spring habitats on Bureau of Land Management (BLM) land in Arizona include Peoples Canyon (Bill Williams River drainage, Yavapai County), Howard Well (Gila River drainage, Graham County), and Mesquite Spring (Gila River drainage, Pinal County); viability of these populations remains uncertain.

Desert pupfish are adapted to harsh desert environments and survive extreme conditions: water temperatures exceeding 43.3°C (110°F) (Moyle 1976), oxygen levels as low as 0.1 to 0.4 parts per million (Lowe et al. 1967), salinities nearly twice that of seawater (Barlow 1958), extreme temperature fluctuations (Lowe and Heath 1969), and daily salinity changes of 10 to 15 parts per thousand (Kinne 1960). Despite this hardiness, they cannot tolerate competition or predation and are readily displaced by exotic fishes.

Desert pupfish mature rapidly and may produce up to three generations per year. Spawning males defend small territories in shallow water. Eggs are laid and fertilized on flocculent substrate and hatch within days. Young begin feeding on small plants and animals after a few hours. Spawning occurs throughout spring and summer months. Individuals typically survive about one year. These characteristics, plus aquarium adaptability, make the species valuable for ichthyological and biological research on ecology, genetics, behavior, and physiology. The rapid differentiation of desert pupfish and other *Cyprinodon* species may provide insights into speciation processes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Land ownership of remnant natural U.S. habitats is divided between private and Federal interests. [[Quitobaquito Spring]] is entirely on National Park Service lands within [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. Lands along [[San Felipe Creek]] are arranged in checkerboard pattern, approximately evenly divided between Federal and private holdings.

The precarious status is recognized by California (classified as "endangered") and Arizona (listed as native species in danger of state extirpation). The desert pupfish was included in the Service's December 30, 1982 Review of Vertebrate Wildlife (47 FR 58454) as Category 1, indicating substantial information supported listing. On April 12, 1983, the Desert Fishes Council petitioned for listing. The Service published notice of finding on June 14, 1983 (48 FR 27273) that the petition presented substantial information. Proposed rule published May 16, 1984 (49 FR 20739).

Critical habitat designation includes approximately one-half acre of aquatic habitat at Quitobaquito Spring with 100-foot riparian buffer, and approximately 11 miles of stream channel along San Felipe Creek and two tributaries with 100-foot riparian buffer zones on both sides. Constituent elements include clean unpolluted water relatively free of exotic organisms (especially exotic fishes) in small slow-moving desert streams and spring pools with marshy backwater areas.

## Key Findings

**Listing Determination**: The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service determines [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) to be an endangered species. Effective date: April 30, 1986.

**Critical Habitat Designation**: 
- **Arizona, Pima County**: Quitobaquito Spring (approximately 25 miles WNW Lukeville) in Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument (T17S R8N) with 100-foot riparian buffer zone
- **California, Imperial County**: 
  - San Felipe Creek: approximately 8.5 stream miles with 100 feet on either side, from State Highway 86 bridge upstream to eastern boundary of Section 31, T12S, R10E
  - Carrizo Wash: approximately 1.5 stream miles with 100 feet on either side, from confluence with San Felipe Creek upstream to southern boundary of N½ Section 33, T12S, R10E
  - Fish Creek Wash: approximately 0.75 stream miles with 100 feet on either side, from confluence with San Felipe Creek upstream to southern boundary of N½ Section 32, T12S, R10E

**Threats to Species (Section 4(a)(1) Factors)**:

**(A) Habitat Destruction/Modification**: Starting in 1880s, desert rivers experienced major erosional cycles causing loss of permanent waters and drying of shallow littoral areas (Miller 1961 related to overgrazing). Construction of mainstream dams on Gila, Colorado, and Salt Rivers for irrigation and flood control dewatered lower reaches and eliminated marshy sidepools, forcing pupfish into mainstream channels with increased predation and competition. Salton Sea area populations severely reduced by exotic fish introductions, modifications to water conveyance facilities, agricultural pesticide application, ground-water pumping dewatering spring habitats, and inundation of spring habitats by rising Salton Sea waters. San Felipe Creek (only California habitat where desert pupfish dominate fish fauna per Black 1980) threatened by proposals to convert privately owned lands to irrigated agriculture; ground-water removal could desiccate San Sebastian Marsh. Geothermal development is potential threat (lease applications filed with BLM near San Sebastian Marsh). Federal lands around Salt Creek leased for geothermal development and oil/gas exploration. Quitobaquito Spring population located downwind from Mexican farms sprayed with organophosphates and chlorinated hydrocarbons; studies (Kynard 1981) detected parathion and DDT derivatives in fish in late 1970s. Major accidental spills or increased pesticide drift could devastate entire Quitobaquito population.

**(B) Overutilization**: Few individuals may be taken incidentally from Salton Sea by anglers collecting sailfin mollies for bait. No evidence of current overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific, or educational purposes.

**(C) Disease or Predation**: Several predators and competitors established in Salton Sea tributaries: [[Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica*, *T. zillii*), [[Sailfin Molly|Sailfin Mollies]] (*Poecilia latipinna*), shortfin mollies (*Poecilia mexicana*), [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), pothole livebearers (*Poeciliopsis gracilis*), and members of families Centrarchidae, Ictaluridae, and Cyprinidae. Populations infected by parasitic copepod (anchor worm, family Lernaidae). In Arizona, displaced from historic spring habitats by [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). Studies show juvenile tilapia compete for same food items; adult tilapia prey on fish and eggs. Field and laboratory observations reveal tilapia interfere with reproductive behavior (Schoenherr 1980); extent of reproductive suppression unknown. Largemouth bass are voracious predators capable of complete elimination from small spring habitats (Miller and Pister 1971).

**(D) Inadequacy of Regulatory Mechanisms**: California State law (Endangered Species Act of 1970, Chapter 1510, Stats. 1970) prohibits taking without permit. Recently amended (Chapter 1240, Stats. 1984) to require State agency consultation with California Department of Fish and Game (CDFG) on projects affecting listed species. However, few threatening activities require State agency approval; California law does not provide adequate protection. No known regulatory mechanisms protect surviving Mexican populations or alleviate threats to Quitobaquito Spring from aerial pesticide spraying and ground-water pumping in Mexico.

**(E) Other Factors**: Exotic aquatic weed [[Hydrilla]] (*Hydrilla verticillata*) recently introduced into All American Canal. Plant spreads rapidly and is difficult to control; may invade desert pupfish habitats. Direct effects unknown, but extreme chemical, mechanical, and biological control methods used elsewhere would likely be detrimental to pupfish habitat.

**Public Comments**: Received written comments from 28 interested parties. Four commenters supported proposed rule; one submitted recommendations without expressing support/opposition. Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), Bureau of Land Management (BLM), and Arizona-New Mexico Chapter of American Fisheries Society (AFS) supported listing but recommended excluding introduced populations in Arizona (Service responded that reintroductions are essential for recovery; experimental population designation under Section 10(j) is appropriate mechanism). Western Regional Office of National Park Service (NPS) supported California listing but withheld support for Quitobaquito Spring pending ongoing studies, expressing concern about single-species management precluding other native species research/management (Service responded that listing is appropriate based on documented threats; NPS provided no data showing exclusion benefits outweigh inclusion benefits). Four water user groups (Coachella Valley Water District, Imperial Irrigation District, Imperial Dam Advisory Board, Yuma County Water User's Association) expressed concern or opposition, questioning listing justification, sampling methodology, and potential impacts on irrigation operations (Service responded that decline is well-documented; sampling techniques are scientifically valid; incidental take may be authorized under Section 7 or 10(a)). Three California State agencies supported listing. Multiple biologists and conservation organizations provided supporting data and observations.

**Conservation Measures**: California Department of Fish and Game requested emergency Federal listing on three occasions. State of California classified species as endangered. BLM designated San Sebastian Marsh area as Area of Critical Environmental Concern (ACEC). Interagency land exchange efforts in progress since 1980. Section 7(a) of Act requires Federal agencies to ensure actions do not jeopardize listed species or destroy/adversely modify critical habitat. Section 9 prohibitions make it illegal to take, import, export, ship in interstate commerce, or sell listed species. Permits may be issued under 50 CFR 17.22 and 17.23 for scientific purposes, propagation/survival enhancement, or incidental take with otherwise lawful activities.

**Economic Impact**: Department of Interior determined designation does not constitute major action under Executive Order 12291 and will not have significant economic effect on substantial number of small entities under Regulatory Flexibility Act. Land use in critical habitat currently limited to recreation, scientific research, and oil/gas leasing. Public lands adjacent to critical habitat recently leased for geothermal exploration. Potential for geothermal or oil/gas development considered low based on negative results from nearby test wells. Management objectives of NPS and BLM for critical habitat portions are compatible with designation. No known involvement of Federal funds or permits for private land included as critical habitat. No significant economic or other impacts expected; no direct costs, enforcement costs, or information collection/recordkeeping requirements imposed on small entities.
