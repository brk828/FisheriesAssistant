# Total Mercury in Fishes and Selected Biota in Lahontan Reservoir, Nevada: 1981

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by James J. Cooper (Desert Research Institute) measured total mercury in muscle, heart, liver and whole-body tissues of fish and other biota from [[Lahontan Reservoir]], Nevada, collected in summer 1981. The reservoir sits on the [[Carson River]], which received mercury lost during gold and silver milling on the [[Comstock Lode]]. Of 53 muscle samples, 36 (68%) exceeded the 1 mg/kg [[Food and Drug Administration]] action level. The study reports that mercury increases with fish size and that concentrations are higher in higher-trophic-level fishes, which the author interprets as [[Biomagnification]] within the reservoir food web.

## Background & Description

Mercury has been used for industrial, agricultural and medicinal purposes for centuries. Following the discovery in Japan and Sweden in the 1950s and 1960s that fish accumulate mercury to levels far above the surrounding water, fisheries in the United States were found to be contaminated.

**Historical source.** Mercury entered the Carson River during [[Comstock Lode]] silver and gold milling, which used the [[Washoe Process]] at a ratio of 1:10 (quicksilver:ore). Key figures:
- Twelve millsites operated along the river from Empire to Dayton after 1869.
- Average quicksilver loss was 0.68 kg per ton of ore milled.
- About 200,000 flasks (approximately 7,500 tons, or 6.75 x 10^6 kg) were lost during the 30-year peak (1865-1895), with about 0.5% later recovered.

**Study site.** [[Lahontan Reservoir]] lies 72 km southeast of Reno and was created in 1915 for the [[Newlands Irrigation Project]]. It receives the entire Carson River flow (average 3.26 x 10^8 m3 per year, 1913-1970) plus water diverted through the [[Truckee Canal]] (average 2.18 x 10^8 m3 per year since 1964, about 40% of inflow). Its morphometry is summarized below:

| Characteristic | Value |
|---|---|
| Maximum length | 27 km |
| Maximum width | 4 km |
| Maximum depth | 26 m |
| Mean depth | 8.1 m |
| Surface area | 4,410 ha |
| Capacity | 3.57 x 10^8 m3 |
| Hydraulic residence time | 0.77 yr |

The reservoir is used for irrigation storage and is one of the most heavily used recreation areas in northern Nevada, within the Nevada State Park System.

**Objectives.** The study aimed to (1) present mercury concentrations in fish and other biota from the reservoir, (2) test whether species higher on the food chain accumulate more mercury, and (3) compare results with earlier Carson River studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment and prior contamination.** A 1973 U.S. Geological Survey study reported background sediment mercury (upper 7 cm, upstream of pre-1900 mills) below 0.1 mg/kg. Downstream levels reached up to 200 times background (20 mg/kg), with the highest concentrations just upstream of Lahontan Reservoir.

**Prior biota studies.** [[Richins]] (1973) was the first to document that aquatic organisms in the Carson River, including the reservoir, had muscle mercury above FDA safe levels. [[Ekechukwu]] (1976) reported carp below the reservoir at 5.0 to 11.5 mg/kg. FDA data from 1970 downstream in the Newlands Project included white bass (3.2 mg/kg), white catfish (3.0 mg/kg), largemouth bass (1.6 mg/kg), rainbow trout (1.3 mg/kg), yellow perch (0.97 mg/kg) and black bullhead (1.1 mg/kg). Earlier Carson River studies recorded 0.02 to 2.72 mg/kg across 200 fish of seven species.

**Methylation and trophic status.** Mercury in water occurs mainly as inorganic mercury, but aquatic organisms carry mainly [[Methylmercury]]. Methylation is biologically controlled and enhanced by bacteria associated with organic enrichment. The reservoir is classified as [[Eutrophication|eutrophic]] based on primary production. Water quality models predict that upstream nutrient control will reduce algal growth, which should lower the potential for in-reservoir methylation.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods.**
- Fish were collected in summer 1981 by gill netting, beach seining and dip netting.
- Total mercury was measured by [[Cold Vapor Atomic Absorption]] after acid digestion in a Parr bomb.
- Recovery from spiked samples averaged 92%. Results are reported on a wet-weight basis.
- Group differences were tested with one-way ANOVA with unequal sample sizes.

**Concentrations.**
- Muscle mercury in 11 species ranged from 0.11 mg/kg (young-of-the-year [[White Bass]]) to 9.52 mg/kg ([[Striped Bass]], 16 years old, 15,165 g).
- Of 53 muscle samples, 36 (68%) exceeded the FDA 1 mg/kg action level.
- Heart tissue ranged from 0.17 mg/kg ([[Carp]]) to 5.58 mg/kg (striped bass).
- Liver tissue ranged from 0.21 mg/kg ([[Brown Bullhead]]) to 23.65 mg/kg (striped bass), and was consistently higher than heart tissue.
- Levels were well above the 0.20 mg/kg background for fish cited by D'Itri (1972).
- [[Crayfish]] (Pacifastacus sp., abdomen 0.57 to 5.72 mg/kg) and California gull (Larus californicus; muscle 0.36 mg/kg, liver 0.97 mg/kg, eggs 0.13 and 0.19 mg/kg) also had elevated mercury.
- Mosquitofish ([[Gambusia Affinis]], whole fish) measured 0.53 mg/kg.

**Size relationships.** Mercury generally increased with fish weight. Significant positive correlations were found for:
- [[Yellow Perch]]: r = 0.82, P < 0.05
- [[White Crappie]]: r = 0.83, P < 0.01
- [[White Catfish]]: r = 0.66, P < 0.05
- White bass: r = 0.85, P < 0.01

Other species showed no significant correlation or had too few samples for analysis. The size relationship was strongest in piscivores.

**Trophic groups.** Species were grouped by feeding strategy (Table 3):

| Trophic group | Species | Muscle Hg range (mg/kg) | Mean (mg/kg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Omnivores | Sacramento blackfish, carp, Tahoe sucker | 0.71-2.34 | 1.16 |
| Second-order piscivores | Brown bullhead, yellow perch, white catfish, channel catfish | 0.95-2.83 | 1.64 |
| First-order piscivores | White crappie, white bass, striped bass | 1.66-9.52 | 3.39 |

- A significant difference was found among the three groups (P < 0.05).
- Omnivores (1.16 mg/kg) differed significantly from second-order piscivores (1.64 mg/kg; P < 0.05).
- Second-order piscivores were significantly lower than first-order piscivores (3.39 mg/kg; P < 0.001).
- Heart and liver concentrations also rose from lower to higher trophic levels. The author concludes that biomagnification is occurring.

**Conclusions and recommendations.**
- Mercury levels in the reservoir's fishes are generally within the range of earlier Carson River studies.
- Future work should focus on controlling mercury release from [[Sediment Mercury]] in the Carson River and reservoir, following the sediment decontamination approaches outlined by Feick et al. (1972).
- A larger sample of sport fishes is needed to develop regression equations relating fish size to mercury content. Such equations would let anglers weigh a catch in the field to estimate its mercury content and choose fish selectively.
- Anticipated improvements in water quality may reduce methylation within the reservoir.
