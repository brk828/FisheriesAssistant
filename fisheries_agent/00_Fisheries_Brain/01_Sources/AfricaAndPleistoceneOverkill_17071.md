# Africa and Pleistocene Overkill

#ichthyology #marine-biology #paleontology

*Note: This document is a palaeontological review rather than an ichthyological study. It addresses large terrestrial mammals, reptiles, primates, and lemurs; no fish taxa are discussed.*

## Executive Summary

This 1966 review by Paul S. Martin, reprinted from *Nature*, re-examines the [[Pleistocene Extinction]] of large mammals using radiocarbon chronology and pollen evidence. It compares the timing and intensity of generic extinction across [[Africa]], [[North America]], and [[Madagascar]]. Martin argues that the pattern of loss matches the arrival of human hunters better than any known climatic upset, and he concludes that the [[Overkill Hypothesis]] "stands unrefuted on chronological grounds."

## Background & Description

- **North America:** Thirty-five extinct genera of large mammals and one reptile genus characterize Late Pleistocene deposits of the [[Rancholabrean]] fauna. Most disappeared within about the last 12,000 years. Only 13 genera were lost in the preceding one to two million years.
- **Africa:** The [[Acheulean Culture]] hand-axe fauna contained a large megafauna. Table 2 lists 19 extinct genera from the Villafranchian and Early Middle Pleistocene and 26 genera lost in the Late Middle Pleistocene (last ~100,000 years). Large mammals were defined as those exceeding about 50 kg adult body weight. Sources are Cooke's review and Table 2 sampling.
- **Madagascar:** About 16 extinct vertebrate genera are known from late post-glacial deposits, including [[Aepyornis]], [[Megaladapis]], and [[Palaeopropithecus]]. The island covers 588,000 km² and lies about 410 km from Africa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Vegetation and climate:** Pollen records show a major upward shift in montane forest and alpine zones in Africa about 12,000 years ago. A similar shift occurred in the Andes about 20,000 years ago. Martin argues that no obvious loss of range carrying capacity occurred in arid America at the time of extinction.
- **Drought hypothesis:** Martin questions drought as a cause on two grounds. It is ad hoc, since migration routes such as the wildebeest migration of about 800 miles between the Serengeti and Lake Victoria would have allowed escape. Also, the extinctions were not synchronous across continents.
- **Human technology:** Acheulean hand-axe and cleaver assemblages appear with extinct genera at sites including Olduvai Gorge (Bed IV), Olorgesailie, Kariandusi, Hopefield, Ismilia, and the Vaal River gravels. Later [[Middle Stone Age]] cultures are associated with few extinctions.
- **Selection for small size:** Martin proposes that the small size of many Pleistocene survivors reflects "nanism" imposed by human hunting. Extinct Madagascan lemurs were larger, diurnal, and sluggish, whereas surviving species are smaller, nocturnal, and arboreal.

## Key Findings

- **Extinction intensity:** Table 3 gives a later Pleistocene extinction intensity of 39 percent for Africa and 71 percent for the U.S.A. and Canada, with 26 African and 35 North American genera lost.
- **Timing:** No clear extinction of North American megafauna is dated before 15,000 B.P. or after 6,000 B.P. Africa's final Acheulean is placed at about 50,000 B.P. by [[Clarke]]'s carbon-14 dates at Kalambo Falls. No generic extinction is known in Africa within the last 1,000 years.
- **Madagascar:** Extinctions there occurred within the last 1,000 years, after the Melanesian invasion. No Acheulean or Middle Stone Age artefacts were found on the island.
- **Intensity gradient:** Martin ranks extinction as moderate in Africa, heavier in America, and extremely heavy in Madagascar. Madagascar's losses affected smaller species than on the continents.
- **Agriculture:** Martin speculates that the survival of plains game in sub-Saharan Africa delayed Neolithic agriculture. In contrast, the loss of New World megafauna around 11,000 years ago may have pushed early humans toward plant foods.
- **Conclusion:** Martin argues that unbalanced megafaunal extinction appears only where human hunters are known. The hypothesis holds until a continent or island is found where major extinction predates human arrival.
