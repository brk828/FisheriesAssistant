# Life History and Early Development of New York Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae #life-history

## Executive Summary

This 1959 popular-science article, written by Edward C. Raney (Professor of Zoology and Fishery Biology, Cornell University) and reprinted by the [[New York State Conservation Department]] from *The N. Y. State Conservationist*, describes the spawning behavior, egg and larval development, and early life stages of about two dozen fish species found in New York State. Its central argument is that the embryonic, larval and young-of-the-year stages are the most critical to the survival of each year class, and that habitat destruction (dams, filling, dredging, road building and pollution) most strongly affects these stages. The species accounts cover the [[Pike Family]], [[Eel]], [[Bullhead]], [[Sucker]], [[White Perch]], [[Carp]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Pike Perch]], [[Smelt]], [[Shad]], [[Sheepshead]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Trout and Salmon]] and the [[Bass Family]], plus [[Sunfish]].

## Background & Description

The article opens with terminology for fish development. Stages within the egg are *embryonic*. After hatching, young are *larvae* or *fry*. In the *pro-larval* stage the yolk sac supplies nourishment; *post-larvae* have lost the yolk sac but still differ in body shape from the juvenile. Salmon and trout pass directly from pro-larva to juvenile and are called *alevins* at that stage. During the first year after the larval stage, fish are *young-of-the-year*. Trout about two to three inches long are *fingerlings*. *Juveniles* resemble adults but are not sexually mature.

The article describes the Pike Family (Esocidae) as well-known game fishes. Three species reach 5 pounds or more: [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), [[Muskalonge]] (*Esox masquinongy*), and [[Chain Pickerel]] (*Esox niger*). The fourth, [[Grass Pickerel]] (*Esox americanus*), rarely exceeds 14 inches. All prefer quiet, weedy areas and ambush prey, mostly fishes.

Several species are discussed at length:
- **[[American Eel]]** (*Anguilla rostrata*): a catadromous species with the most unusual life history of North American fishes that spend time in fresh water.
- **[[Brown Bullhead]]** (*Ictalurus nebulosus*): widely distributed in New York; mostly nocturnal.
- **[[Common White Sucker]]** (*Catostomus commersoni*): found in most habitats, from large lakes to small streams.
- **[[White Perch]]** (*Roccus americanus*): brackish and fresh-water species, now in Lake Erie via the New York Barge Canal.
- **[[Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*): an Asian species introduced via Europe about 1880 and considered a nuisance.
- **[[Yellow Perch]]** (*Perca flavescens*) and **[[Yellow Pike Perch]]** (*Stizostedion vitreum*).
- **[[American Smelt]]** (*Osmerus mordax*) and **[[American Shad]]** (*Alosa sapidissima*).
- **[[Sheepshead]]** or freshwater drum (*Aplodinotus grunniens*).
- **[[Striped Bass]]** (*Roccus saxatilis*), a marine and brackish species that spawns in fresh water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article repeatedly links reproduction to water temperature. Pike, pickerel and muskalonge spawn in spring shortly after ice-out, in marshy margins. Chain pickerel spawn at about 48° F, and northern pike at about 48° F; spawning periods overlap in central New York. Hatching is temperature-dependent: longer incubation occurs in colder water. Smallmouth bass begin nests at 59 to 65° F and spawn successfully only above 65° F. Sudden drops in temperature can halt spawning or kill larvae.

Habitat alteration is a major theme. The article attributes losses to:
- Dam construction
- Filling lakes and ponds for cottage sites
- Bulldozing and dredging of streams for "channel improvement"
- Road building
- Domestic and industrial pollution

Siltation is singled out as a threat that can bury and suffocate eggs and young. Filling and dredging along lake-shore marshes have reduced populations of northern pike, chain pickerel and muskalonge in some areas.

Several species make spawning migrations, including [[Chautauqua Lake]] muskalonge, white suckers ("runs" up tributaries at night in April and May), smelt (after ice-out, up tributary streams), and American shad and striped bass (up rivers such as the [[Hudson River]]). Many young fish depend on specific food webs: pike larvae take crustaceans and insect larvae, and young pike pickerel eat fishes once they grow larger. The Lake Erie sheepshead feeds heavily on mayflies and crustaceans.

## Key Findings

**Pike family reproduction**
- Pike, pickerel and muskalonge broadcast eggs without nests or parental care. Eggs are non-adhesive and sink to the bottom.
- Hybrids occur between northern pike and chain pickerel, muskalonge and northern pike, and chain pickerel and grass pickerel.
- Chain pickerel: females 12 to 13 inches long laid 6,000 to 7,100 eggs; eggs about 2 mm; hatching in 6 to 12 days.
- Northern pike: eggs 2.3 mm; hatching in 7 to 10 days at about 52° F (14 to 30 days overall, depending on temperature). Egg output ranged from 7,700 (15.7-inch fish) to 97,300 (35-inch female).
- Muskalonge (Chautauqua Lake): spawning begins in early April at 47° F; eggs hatch in about 20 days in cold water and 12 to 14 days in warmer water. A 32- to 40-pound female may deposit a quarter million eggs.

**Eel life history**
- The American eel is the only North American freshwater-associated species known to spawn in the ocean. The question was solved in 1922 by Danish ichthyologist Johannes Schmidt.
- Spawning occurs in the [[Sargasso Sea]] between 20° and 30° N and 60° and 78° W. The largest females (10 to 15 pounds) may carry as many as 20 million minute eggs.
- Larvae (leptocephali) drift with ocean currents. They change into glass eels in winter, then elvers in spring, which migrate upstream in large numbers.
- Females live upstream in fresh water; males stay in coastal areas and estuaries. Descent to sea occurs in fall. Captive eels have lived over 25 years, and one reputedly lived 88 years.

**Other species**
- Brown bullhead: nests are cleaned by the mouth in late May and June at 65° F or more. Eggs number 500 to 2,000 or more, and 11- to 13-inch females may produce 6,000 to 13,000 eggs per season. Both parents guard and fan the nest.
- White sucker: large females 16 to 21 inches long deposit 22,000 to 48,000 eggs; no nest is built; eggs hatch in seven days at 60° F.
- White perch: first spawning at age three; eggs about 0.73 mm with an oil globule; hatching in about six days at 52° F.
- Carp: a three-year-old female produces about half a million eggs; ten-year-old carp weighing 21 pounds may lay 1.5 million. Spawning peaks at 65 to 68° F in New York. Studies by the Conservation Department were underway to find better control methods.
- Yellow perch: females weighing 12 to 14 ounces lay 44,000 to 48,000 eggs in ribbon-like strings 2 to 7 feet long; hatching takes 27 days at 47° F.
- Yellow pike perch: females of 4.5 to 5 pounds lay 118,000 to 128,000 eggs; hatching takes seven days at 57° F.
- American smelt: adults usually 5 to 10 inches; eggs are adhesive and attach to gravel by a pedicle; incubation lasts two to four weeks.
- American shad: spawning run from late March to late June; females produce 224,000 to 307,000 eggs; young reach 3 to 5 inches by fall and then migrate to sea.
- Sheepshead: males mature at four years (12 inches), females at five (15 inches); average Lake Erie size is about 15 inches; 10-pound fish are not rare.
- Striped bass: spawning begins at 58° F in the Hudson near West Point, mid-May to mid-June. A four-year-old female weighing about three pounds produces about 65,000 eggs. The article states that a single large female may produce "414 million" eggs (as printed; this figure may be a scan or typographical error and should be checked against the original).
- Salmonids: most species build redds (nests) in gravel. Rainbow trout spawn in spring; brook, brown and lake trout spawn in fall. Lake trout spawn over unprepared gravel or rubble with 2 to 10 participants and no nest.
- Red salmon (introduced): dies after spawning in October and November.
- Smallmouth bass: males clear nests of 18 inches or more in diameter, guard them and fan eggs. Incubation takes 14 days at 64 to 70° F. Fry are 0.2 inches long and young bass may be guarded for up to nine days.
- Largemouth bass: builds nests at about 63° F and spawns at or above 65° F.
- Sunfish and bluegill: males build and guard nests; hybridization between bluegill and sunfish occurs and is common in some ponds.
