# Does Morphology Predict Ecology? Hypothesis Testing Within a Freshwater Stream Fish Assemblage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ecomorphology

## Executive Summary

Douglas and Matthews (1992, *Oikos* 65:213–224) test whether body morphology can serve as an index of stream fish assemblage structure, using [[Ecomorphology]] data from 17 fish species in five families collected in the upper [[Roanoke River]] drainage, Virginia. Morphology was compared with microhabitat use, diet, and [[Taxonomic Distance]] using [[Mantel Test]] and [[Three-Way Mantel Test]] analyses. Across families, body shape was strongly congruent with taxonomy and, after controlling for taxonomy, not with diet. Within the family [[Cyprinidae]] (8 species), morphology was congruent with microhabitat but not with diet. The authors conclude that ecomorphology is valid only when restricted to within-family comparisons, and that assemblage structure reflects phylogenetic history more than current environmental convergence.

## Background & Description

Ecomorphology assumes that the morphological phenotype reflects ecological function, and it has been widely applied to fishes, birds, and other vertebrates. Its appeal rests partly on phenotypic stability: morphology responds slowly to environmental fluctuation, is easily quantified, and can be supplemented with museum collections (see Rutherford et al. 1987). Earlier work on [[Trophic Ecology]] and [[Microhabitat Use]] in fishes reported morphology–diet and morphology–habitat agreement, but many of those studies were univariate or descriptive, and some (e.g., Wiens and Rotenberry 1980) found little correlation.

The authors frame the central question as whether morphological "space" maps onto ecological "space." A negative result would imply that assemblage structure is shaped by factors other than physical adaptation to niches. The study uses two nested tests: a 17-species assemblage spanning five families, and an 8-species single-family subset (Cyprinidae) to reduce the confounding effect of taxonomy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Fish were sampled from April to November 1978 at 11 fixed locations in the upper Roanoke River drainage, Roanoke and Montgomery counties, Virginia. Sites ranged from headwater brooks to a fifth-order mainstem. Headwaters had steep gradients, riffle-pool zonation, and gravel, rubble, shale, or bedrock substrates. Mainstem reaches had lower gradients, large swift riffles, backwater pools, and uniform channels. Collections were dominated numerically by minnows (Cyprinidae), darters (Percidae), suckers (Catostomidae), madtom catfishes (Ictaluridae), and sculpins (Cottidae). Centrarchids were rare and were excluded from analyses.

**Microhabitat categories.** Habitat was characterized by current speed (cm/s), substrate size (cm), depth (cm), and water column position (benthic or non-benthic). Current speed ranged from under 25 to over 76 cm/s. Substrate was classed from sand and fine material to solid bedrock, and depth from under 35 cm to over 76 cm.

**Biological sampling.** Approximately one hour of seining was spent at each site in each discrete microhabitat (slow and fast riffles, deep and shallow pools, main channel, and others). Microhabitat use was expressed as percent occurrence across categories.

## Key Findings

### Sample and Methods

- **Collections:** 65 fish collections at 11 sites.
- **Species:** 17 species in five families for the full assemblage; 8 *Cyprinidae* species for the within-family analysis.
- **Morphology:** 34 morphological characters measured on 10 adult individuals per species. Characters were chosen for ecological relevance (body form, fin placement, eye position, trophic apparatus size). Diagnostic characters, such as pharyngeal tooth counts, were excluded. Only adults were used to limit allometric effects.
- **Diet:** Stomach contents from 73–120 individuals for six species and 20 individuals each for the other 11 species, scored across 13 food categories (e.g., aquatic larvae, chironomid larvae, plant material, terrestrial insects, fish eggs).
- **Transformations:** Morphological data were log-transformed and converted to taxonomic distances. Size-free shape was derived from sheared principal component analysis, and canonical variate analysis (CVA) was run on the same data. Diet and microhabitat percentages were arcsine-transformed. All matrices were clustered by UPGMA into phenograms.
- **Taxonomic model:** Distances were scored 1 (same genus), 2 (same family, different genus), 3 (same order, different family), and 4 (different order), following Nelson (1976).
- **Statistics:** Pairwise and three-way Mantel tests were run with 10,000 permutations. The Bonferroni correction was applied, with a one-tailed test because positive covariation was predicted.

### Results: Full 17-Species Assemblage

- **Microhabitat phenogram** (r = 0.85) produced three clusters. Cluster 3 was the most distinct, composed of four cyprinids preferring slower, deeper water over sand to small pebble. Cluster 1 included a sucker and a darter; Cluster 2 included a sculpin, a madtom, and a sucker.
- **Trophic phenogram** (r = 0.89) produced three clusters. Cluster 1 was a tight group of three cyprinids feeding mainly on plant material and detritus. Cluster 3 comprised three darters feeding over 60% on chironomid larvae.
- **Morphological shape phenogram** (r = 0.90) showed a strong taxonomic signal. Cluster 2 was almost entirely the family Cyprinidae, and the most divergent cluster contained three darters and a sculpin.
- **Two-way Mantel tests:** Microhabitat was not significantly related to morphology or trophic matrices. The only congruent comparisons were trophic–taxonomic and trophic–shape.
- **Three-way Mantel tests (Table 4):**
  - Shape–taxonomy: r = 0.79, p < 0.0001
  - Shape–trophic: r = 0.42, p < 0.0004
  - Taxonomy–trophic: r = 0.39, p < 0.0005
  - Partial shape–taxonomy (controlling for trophic): r = 0.76, p < 0.0001
  - Partial shape–trophic (controlling for taxonomy): r = 0.19, p > 0.07 (not significant)
  - Coefficient of multiple determination: taxonomy alone R² = 0.628; taxonomy plus trophic R² = 0.644. Trophic alone R² = 0.176, rising substantially when taxonomy was added.
- **Community-wide matrix correlation** (Fig. 4): r = 0.95.

### Results: Within Cyprinidae (8 Species)

- Canonical variate and shape matrices were each significantly related to the microhabitat matrix (p < 0.005).
- Comparisons between morphology and diet were not significant.
- Matrix correlations were r = 0.90 (microhabitat), r = 0.94 (food), r = 0.86 (shape), and r = 0.98 for the combined cyprinid matrices.

### Interpretation and Conclusions

- Taxonomy and body shape together strongly predicted diet across the full assemblage, but after controlling for taxonomy, body shape did not predict diet. The authors interpret this as [[Phylogenetic Constraint]]: body form restricts the range of prey and feeding modes, and those forms are themselves taxonomically structured.
- Within the Cyprinidae, morphology tracked microhabitat use, the niche axis the authors identify as most important for this family. This matches earlier findings that cyprinids partition habitat more than food.
- The authors argue that morphologically dissimilar species can occupy similar microhabitats through different adaptations, which they see as evidence for "alternate solutions to the same ecological problems."
- They also cite Gatz (1979a), whose first factor axis, explaining 31% of variance, grouped cyprinids apart from percids. Gatz noted this axis's strong taxonomic bias at the family level.
- **Conclusion:** Stream fish assemblages appear structured more by [[Evolutionary History]] and phylogeny than by convergent environmental adaptation. Ecomorphology is valid for assemblage analysis only within families. Across families, phylogenetic history confounds ecological signal. Competition may structure assemblages mainly within genera and between recently evolved forms, and may become important when exotic species are introduced.
