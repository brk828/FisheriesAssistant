# Wildlife 2000: Arizona Game and Fish Department Wildlife Program Strategic Plan (1996–2000)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #wildlife-management

## Executive Summary

Wildlife 2000 is the five-year strategic plan (1996–2000) of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and its Commission, approved on December 15, 1995. It sets goals, issues, strategies and objectives for three subprograms: Game Management, Sportfish Management, and Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Management. The plan is built on public input (a scoping survey with more than 500 responses, a March 1994 telephone survey, and hunter and angler surveys), and it states that it is "largely species driven" and that its goals will be revised by amendment if needed. It also acknowledges that habitat, sportfish, native fish and recreation interests will conflict and that the plan provides a framework for resolving them case by case.

## Background & Description

The Department's mission is to conserve, enhance and restore Arizona's diverse wildlife and habitats, and to provide wildlife resources and safe watercraft and off-highway vehicle recreation for present and future generations. Its authority rests on [[Arizona Revised Statutes Title 17]] (A.R.S. 17-102, 17-201, 17-231), which makes wildlife state property and vests management in the [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]].

The plan uses a defined planning vocabulary (goal, issue, mission, objective, program, project, strategy, subprogram) and a comprehensive management system that includes five-year project narratives, annual work plans, cost accounting, evaluation and [[Responsive Management]].

Funding context: the [[Arizona Heritage Fund]] (established 1990, $20 million per year from the state lottery, split equally with Arizona State Parks) and the Off-Highway Vehicle Recreation Fund (about $1.8 million per year) supplement license revenue. Total 1994–95 funding sources were listed at $42,336,000.

The state is described as having two physiographic regions: the [[Colorado Plateau]] (north) and the [[Basin and Range Province]] (south). Arizona lists more than 800 species of fish, amphibians, birds, reptiles and mammals (788 native and 74 non-native in Table 1). Of 32 native fish species, only the longfin dace is described as still abundant and widespread.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Climate and precipitation:** Winter precipitation comes from Pacific frontal systems; summer monsoon storms come from Gulf of Mexico and Gulf of California moisture. Annual precipitation ranges from under five inches along the lower Colorado and Gila rivers to more than 25 inches in high mountains. Prolonged drought cycles strongly affect wildlife populations and harvest.
- **Land ownership:** Of Arizona's 113,909 square miles, about 70 percent is under federal jurisdiction. The [[Bureau of Land Management]] controls 12 million acres (17%), the [[U.S. Forest Service]] 11 million acres (15%), and private owners about 12 million acres (17%). Tribes manage 20 million acres (28%).
- **Water:** Almost every aquatic environment has been modified by dams, diversions, well fields and canals. Groundwater tables have dropped drastically since the 1800s, and land has subsided as much as 50 feet in some basins. About 60 percent of endemic vertebrates are obligate riparian dwellers, and another 10 to 20 percent are facultative riparian users.
- **Human and social trends:** Arizona's population was about 4.04 million, and it was projected to grow by more than half a million by 2000. More than 76 percent of residents live in urban Maricopa and Pima counties. Hunting license sales fell from 203,384 (1986) to 163,270 (1991), then rose to 187,266 by 1994.
- **Economics:** Hunting, fishing and other wildlife-associated recreation had a total economic impact of more than $1.65 billion in 1991, supporting nearly 25,000 jobs with earnings of $479 million.

## Key Findings

**Game Management (hunted species)**
- The plan sets statewide population and harvest objectives for [[Mule Deer]], [[White-tailed Deer]], [[Pronghorn]], [[Elk]], [[Wild Turkey]], [[Javelina]], [[Bighorn Sheep]], [[American Bison]], [[Black Bear]] and [[Mountain Lion]].
- 1994 estimates include about 120,000 post-hunt adult mule deer (12,000 harvested) and 82,000 post-hunt white-tailed deer (5,600 harvested); about 29,000 elk (11,000 harvested); about 38,000 javelina (6,700 harvested in 1995); about 6,500 bighorn sheep (100 harvested); and about 250 buffalo (47 harvested).
- Black bear population was estimated at 2,500 (239 harvested), and mountain lion at 2,500 (247 harvested). The Yuma puma's taxonomic status is under study.
- Small game (cottontails, [[Gambel's Quail]], [[Scaled Quail]], [[Mearns' Quail]], doves, [[Tree Squirrels]], waterfowl) is managed through monitoring and habitat rather than strict quota. In 1994 about 989,000 Gambel's and scaled quail, 1.1 million mourning doves and 136,000 white-winged doves were harvested. Small game supply continues to exceed hunter demand.
- Small game hunting declined after below-normal rainfall in 1986–91, and concerns about zoonotic diseases (hantavirus, plague, tularemia) may have reduced hunter numbers.
- Survey methods include aerial line transect and block surveys. Elk, deer and pronghorn populations are estimated with a life-table model calibrated to male:female survey ratios.

**Sportfish Management**
- In 1993, more than 319,000 Arizona residents and 57,000 non-residents bought fishing licenses. Estimated angler days were about 7 million annually.
- Arizona has 159 managed stream reaches (1,470 miles, mainly trout) and 64 lakes (about 3,000 acres) managed primarily for trout. The Department manages about 354,800 acres of impounded water for warmwater species.
- Objectives include providing fishing for 2.6 million coldwater and 5.4 million warmwater angler days by 2000, and 70 percent angler satisfaction. Anglers rated satisfaction at a mean of 7.0 out of 10 in 1994.
- Anglers spend about $55 million per year in Arizona.
- Sportfish and native fish management conflict. In the 1992 Angler Survey, 37 percent of anglers said sportfish should not be disrupted to protect native fish, while 26 percent said Arizona should do everything possible to preserve native fish.

**Nongame and Endangered Wildlife**
- Arizona has 32 native fishes (30 freshwater, 2 saltwater). Several, including the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], need stocking to avoid extirpation. Aravaipa Creek is the richest remaining native-only fish site, with seven native species.
- The Department lists 502 native bird species (about 291 breeding in the state). Of 42 raptor species, 4 are federally listed and 13 are on the state Wildlife of Special Concern list.
- 25 native amphibian and 95 native reptile species occur. Bullfrogs and introduced fish and crayfish have devastated many leopard frog populations. The [[Mohave Desert Tortoise]] was federally listed as threatened in April 1991.
- 134 native mammal species occur, and 18 are on the state special concern list, with 9 federally listed as threatened or endangered.
- The Kanab ambersnail is federally listed as endangered. More than 40 other Arizona snails and mollusks are under review for federal listing.
- The outlook is mixed: many common species are secure, but riparian and aquatic specialists face serious decline.

**Plan Structure and Strategic Issues**
- Nine general issues are addressed: planning and philosophy; information; management actions; habitat management and protection; partnerships; laws and legal conflicts; law enforcement; wildlife recreation; and public attitudes, information and education.
- Strategies include Total Quality Management, ecosystem-based planning, watershed-based management plans, standardized survey protocols, habitat acquisition from willing sellers, and expanded wildlife-watching opportunities.
- The plan commits to evaluating the 1996–1999 accomplishments, a performance report in FY 1999–2000, and drafting a Wildlife 2005 plan.
