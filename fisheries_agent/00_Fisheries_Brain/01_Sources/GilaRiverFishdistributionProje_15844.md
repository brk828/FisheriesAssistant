# Gila River Fish-Distribution Project

#ichthyology #conservation #freshwater-ecology #native-fish #species-decline #database #museum-records #Gila-River #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

The [[Gila River]] Fish-Distribution Project represents a comprehensive database effort documenting the dramatic decline of native freshwater fish fauna in the [[Gila River Basin]] through analysis of museum specimens and peer-reviewed literature. Led by W.L. Minckley at Arizona State University and funded through [[American Rivers]], the project compiled fish distribution records at two levels: preserved museum specimens and published primary literature. The database reveals precipitous declines in native fish distributions between pre-1980 and post-1981 periods, with particular emphasis on federally listed species including [[Apache Trout]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and "big-river" species such as [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Woundfin]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. The project addresses fundamental questions about using historical data for conservation planning, defining "past conditions," and identifying key areas for maximizing conservation benefits in the face of extensive habitat destruction and introduced species impacts.

## Background & Description

The natural aquatic and semi-aquatic habitats of western North America have been severely decimated by human water-resource development, resulting in precipitous declines of aquatic-obligate, riparian-obligate, and riparian-associated organisms and communities. Freshwater fishes serve as sensitive indices of environmental conditions since they are absolutely tied to the aquatic environment and unable to move overland in absence of water. The [[Gila River]] Fish-Distribution Project developed a multi-purpose database consisting of individual fish records compiled from preserved specimens permanently housed in museums (ASU, UMMZ, ANSP, TNHM, MCZ, CNHM) and records published in primary, peer-reviewed literature.

The project emphasizes two major aspects: documentation and ecological interpretation of environmental change, and identification of key areas of maximal remnant diversity to target conservation efforts. The database includes both native and introduced species records, with temporal distribution analysis of native fish records spanning from pre-1950 through 1995. Species diversity was measured by numbers of species per 10 × 10-km grid sections, comparing pre-1980 and 1981-1996 periods.

The project addresses critical methodological questions about historical data interpretation, including biases in early collections toward access points (trails, roads, railways, campsites), whether samples were qualitative or quantitative, and whether collectors "high-graded" specimens due to inadequate preservation/transportation facilities or scientific/commercial interests. Early samples are acknowledged as biased and relatively few in number, requiring considerable knowledge of historical conditions for estimating past distributions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Stream habitats in the [[American Southwest]] represent "average" geomorphic conditions based on previous environmental experiences. Climate changes alter streams; erosion modifies channels; flooding patterns affect riparian vegetation and beaver dams; fish populations fluctuate naturally. Recently, human alterations have changed streams in ways resembling natural forces but at unprecedented scales. The project emphasizes that natural systems are ever-changing, yet agency planning often adopts a "preservation" philosophy that attempts to fix defined entities in time and space.

The impact of introduced organisms represents a critical ecological concern. Co-occurrence of native and introduced fishes is exceptional; even where introduced species seem innocuous, native fishes eventually suffer. Introduced species should be treated as pollutants (point- or non-point-source depending on circumstances). Introduced crayfish to the [[Colorado River System]] (none native) appear to impact native fishes, with unknown effects on other ecosystem components including invertebrates and algal/detrital food bases. The upper [[Colorado Basin]] rivers are referenced as suffering loading of introduced fishes and organisms far exceeding other locations, yet are still termed "natural" riverine environments.

Habitat persistence varies temporally: seasons, years, decades, or centuries. Habitats may be fixed in space or migrate with changes in channel morphology and climatic conditions. The project questions whether simple extrapolation among sites, watersheds, or drainages based on assumptions of more connected and stable habitats in the past can be justifiably used for present-day conservation efforts. Time perspective for interpreting datasets remains problematic: 100 years ago, pre-settlement, Middle Holocene, or other periods.

The concept of "viable, self-sustaining populations" underlying most native western fish recovery is characterized as unrealistic, conjuring fantasies that recovered species could continue without human intervention. In arid zones subject to human pressures in the American West, some level of intervention is and will be necessary into the foreseeable future. No habitat, species, or community of concern will persist unless directly managed, requiring replication and redundancy to guard against natural or human-made catastrophe.

## Key Findings

**Database Development and Scope:**
The project compiled comprehensive fish distribution records from multiple museum collections (ASU, UMMZ, ANSP, TNHM, MCZ, CNHM) and primary peer-reviewed literature, creating taxon-by-taxon maps documenting geographic range changes between pre-1980 and post-1981 (to 1995) periods. Species diversity maps summarized overall changes measured by numbers of species per locality across the same time period. The database includes temporal distribution of native fish records spanning pre-1950 through 1995.

**Species Documented:**
Listed species analyzed include [[Oncorhynchus apache]] ([[Apache Trout]]), [[Oncorhynchus gilae]] ([[Gila Trout]]), "big-river" fishes combined ([[Gila elegans]] (bonytail), [[Ptychocheilus lucius]] ([[Colorado Squawfish]]), [[Plagopterus argentissimus]] (woundfin), [[Xyrauchen texanus]] ([[Razorback Sucker]]), [[Catostomus latipinnis]] ([[Flannelmouth Sucker]])), [[Meda fulgida]] (spikedace), [[Tiaroga cobitis]] ([[Loach Minnow]]), [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis]] ([[Gila Topminnow]]), [[Cyprinodon macularius]] and Cyprinodon sp. (desert and Monkey Spring pupfishes), [[Agosia chrysogaster]] ([[Longfin Dace]]), [[Rhinichthys osculus]] ([[Speckled Dace]]), [[Gila robusta robusta]] ([[Roundtail Chub]] nominal subspecies), Gila robusta subsp. (roundtail chub "grahami"), [[Gila intermedia]] ([[Gila Chub]]), [[Pantosteus clarki]] ([[Desert Sucker]]), and [[Catostomus insignis]] ([[Sonora Sucker]]).

**Documented Declines:**
The database reveals dramatic range contractions and local extinctions across the [[Gila River Basin]]. Maps demonstrate overall changes in species diversity showing both positive (additive) and negative (subtractive) changes in numbers of species from pre-1980 to 1981-1996. The "wave of extinction" has already dissipated its energy on beaches of the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] in cases of squawfish and flannelmouth sucker, representing complete loss of critical ecosystem components.

**Data Limitations and Interpretive Challenges:**
Early collections are clearly biased toward access points and relatively few in number. Collectors obviously "high-graded" specimens due to inadequate preservation/transportation facilities and scientific/commercial interests. Samples are not quantitative except in "presence-absence" sense. Many comments on rarity and abundance were second-hand from locals, and "fish stories" are notoriously poor in fact. Little understanding of species biology existed; apparent great abundance of squawfish below obstructions during spawning migration was not understood, while seasonal rarity when scattered in home ranges (supporting single adults per kilometer or more) paints different pictures. Interpretations of old records, other than presence-absence, should only be made by experienced people.

**Conservation Strategy Recommendations:**
Despite problems and admonitions, tentative recommendations identify sites and habitat types for conservation in the [[Gila River Basin]], varying from single localities to entire watersheds, single-species populations to intact fish communities, and from natural systems to artificial, designed, and constructed habitats for special uses. Goals should include maintenance of as much biological diversity as possible, in as many habitats as feasible, for the longest term available, under conditions allowing natural climatic, geomorphic, biotic, and other phenomena to operate unimpeded by human interference.

**Management Requirements:**
At minimum, replication and redundancy must be assured to thwart losses to natural or human-made catastrophe. Unnatural components such as introduced species must be guarded against and managed when they appear, directly by removal or indirectly through exclusion. Direct intervention must be practiced in lieu of natural processes of emigration and immigration, which native fishes can no longer accomplish without assistance due to human-constructed barriers. Multi-species, ecosystem-level management scenarios represent the wave of the future, but continued need for single-species management is inevitable to avoid loss of critical components.

**Conceptual Issues for Conservation Planning:**
The project raises fundamental questions about defining "past" and "past conditions" (10, 100, 1000, or 10,000 years ago and duration of persistence). Agencies want set time, place, and condition for planning purposes, but achieving the "plan" rather than the functioning ecosystem often becomes the goal. Most plans adopt "preservation" philosophy, fixing defined entities in time and space, despite knowledge that natural systems are ever-changing. Prioritization is necessary but "high priority" items must be acted upon, and "low-priority" items should not automatically drop below any interest level. When "critical habitat" is designated, everywhere else suddenly becomes "non-critical," yet in linear systems like rivers, water flows through it all.

**Biodiversity Conservation Questions:**
The project questions what kind of biodiversity is desired: genetic variation, species diversity, or both. How many kinds (or variable alleles) must be present at a site for it to be "valuable enough" to conserve? Is a natural creek at low elevation with single species like [[Longfin Dace]] less valuable than the same stream at intermediate elevation with four or five taxa? Should "intact communities" be thought of as continuum from headwaters to lowlands, or can remnants be conserved within the continuum as short segments besieged from both up- and downstream by non-indigenous invaders?

**Negative Interactions and Management Conflicts:**
Negative interactions between sport fish and native fish management must be pursued further, removed from adversarial arena into venue allowing reasonable compromise. The two "camps" work for same things at all levels except which species or group to emphasize, losing sight of "ecosystem management" principles. Denial practiced by some workers relative to biota being part of habitat is remarkable, with continuing reference to systems as "natural" when suffering loading of introduced organisms.

**Global Context and Comparative Advantage:**
Habitat destruction and species extinction exists worldwide in arid lands, where human pressures are stronger than in the United States. In arid lands of much of the world, little hope exists that natural aquatic systems will be conserved. This is not the case in the United States, with strong public support for conservation, abundant and effective environmental legislation, comparatively high potentials for funding, and wealth of expertise. Proceeding on all available fronts is necessary if biotic diversity of [[Gila River]] fish fauna is to be conserved. Intact systems and parts of systems, replicates and reasonable facsimiles of systems must be sought and secured.
