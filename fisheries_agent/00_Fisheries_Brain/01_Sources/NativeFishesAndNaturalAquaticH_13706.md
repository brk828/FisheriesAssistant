# Native Fishes and Natural Aquatic Habitats in USFWS Region II West of the Continental Divide

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by W. L. Minckley of the [[Arizona State University]] Department of Zoology (1985) for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS), reviews the population and habitat status of native freshwater fishes in USFWS Region II west of the Continental Divide. Natural aquatic habitats in the arid American Southwest are scarce, and native fishes are losing direct competition with expanding human water demand. The author argues that judicious husbandry of remaining habitats, reclamation of semi-natural waters, and intensive management of native fish faunas can halt and reverse extinction trends.

The core recommendations are to manage fishes as faunal complexes, to define and acquire habitats able to support groups of species in perpetuity, and to prioritize already-protected, remote habitats first. Big rivers and their fishes are the most endangered; intermediate- and high-elevation streams are least so. Basic biological research (reproduction, species interactions, dispersal) must run concurrently with recovery operations because there is insufficient time for research to precede action.

## Background & Description

The report is a three-part review:
1. A narrative summary of regional habitats and fishes, with figures and tables.
2. Detailed species accounts organized by geographic and ecological subfauna, including historic and present distribution, abundance and listing status.
3. Watershed-by-watershed reviews of habitat condition and management potential.

The region includes the vast [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] and headwaters of three Mexican drainages (Rio Yaqui, Rio Concepcion, Rio Sonoyta) that contribute much of the Southwestern fish fauna. Some areas outside the region, such as the White River of eastern Nevada and the Salton Sea, are included because geographic continuity matters more than political boundaries. A total of 29 species (per the text's count) is known from the region's portion west of the divide, most unique to the region. Of 31 species known from the lower Colorado River basin, 24 are endemic at the species level and six are in endemic genera.

Four geographic subgroups structure the lower Colorado fauna: marine species near the river mouth; [[Big-River Fishes]] ranging the mainstream; a [[Gila River]] component (including the Bill Williams); and a "middle Colorado River subfauna" localized in the Little Colorado, Virgin and White River drainages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic habitats.** Past aquatic systems were less diverse than today, consisting of large, meandering, braided rivers in broad alluvial valleys fed by canyon-bound streams. Historic floods were ameliorated by dense riparian vegetation, and cienegas and springs were the main permanent habitats in an otherwise arid landscape.

**Principal threats.** Dams, diversions, groundwater pumping, arroyo cutting, cienega destruction and wastewater inputs have dewatered or altered most systems. Regulated streams are unsuitable for natives because of altered temperature and turbidity, flow fluctuations that disrupt reproduction, and enhanced populations of non-native species. Introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia affinis) has reduced [[Sonoran Topminnow]] populations through direct predation, and stocked sport fishes hybridize with native trouts.

**Habitat classes.** Figure 2 of the source gives relative estimates of four habitat categories (springs/cienegas, high-elevation creeks, intermediate-elevation streams, big rivers) at three quality levels (unmodified, modified, destroyed). The authors note that intermediate-elevation canyon streams have suffered the least damage because they are isolated by bedrock, shaded, cooler, less saline, and too small or too warm for sport fishery development.

**Habitat categories for management** (applied throughout):
- I: within natural range and relatively undisturbed (highest priority)
- II: within natural range and reasonable to reclaim
- III: within natural range but highly modified or costly to reclaim
- IV: out of natural range but isolated and relatively undisturbed
- V: out of native range and otherwise as in III
- VI: artificial refugia

## Key Findings

**Status of fishes**
- Among 57 taxa (species and subspecies) plus four problematic populations or groups in the region, at least 50 have experienced range reductions of 50% or more since settlement.
- Of these taxa, three are extinct, eight are federally listed as endangered, one is threatened, 11 are proposed for listing, and 12 are candidates.
- Big-river fishes are the most damaged faunal group; spring-fed and headwater species are second; intermediate-elevation and ubiquitous species are most persistent.
- Big-river fishes include seven species; five are endangered or under management. Flannelmouth sucker is likely extirpated downstream from Lake Mead.
- Mexican forms: all those of the Rios Sonoyta and Concepcion persist, though Longfin Dace has disappeared from the US portion of Rio Concepcion. The Rio Yaqui fauna includes two endangered forms and several threatened or candidate species; Roundtail Chub is extirpated from the US portion.

**Management framework**
- Three kinds of imperiled species were identified: (1) specialists of large habitats (e.g., [[Loach Minnow]] in riffles) that are hard to manage because water-development needs overlap their habitats; (2) species isolated in small, special habitats (trouts, pupfishes, springfishes) that must be maintained in place by purchase or protection; (3) habitat generalists (longfin and speckled dace, roundtail chub, some suckers) threatened mainly by non-native competitors and predators.
- Habitats should be prioritized by the six-category scheme above, with Category I and II emphasized.
- Existing protected places (e.g., [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]], Aravaipa Creek, upper Bill Williams River, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Dexter National Fish Hatchery) are the first priority for management.
- Monitoring should be intensive for short-lived species (semi-annual) and long-term for long-lived species such as razorback sucker, which can persist more than three decades.

**Research and recovery priorities**
- Basic information on reproduction and recruitment, species interactions, and dispersal is urgently needed.
- Aravaipa Creek fish populations remained stable over more than 40 years of record, with no native species lost and none invading, illustrating that stable native assemblages are possible.
- Non-native species (e.g., [[Red Shiner]] displacing Loach Minnow and Spikedace) may be more important to control than other forms of perpetuation in some habitats.
- Experimental work at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] has already contributed to knowledge of species such as Razorback Sucker and Bonytail.

**Species-specific priorities**
- [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] are long-lived with little evidence of recruitment in the lower basin since the 1950s; hatchery brood stocks are maintained at Dexter NFH.
- [[Yaqui Chub]] is restricted in the US to Leslie Creek and San Bernardino NWR; reintroduction into Astin Spring-derived habitats has been partly successful, with a Dexter stock failing to reproduce after about five years.
- [[Mosquitofish]] control on San Bernardino NWR was identified as a top priority to protect Sonoran Topminnow.
- Reintroduction of Colorado Squawfish began experimentally in summer 1985 in the Verde and Salt rivers.

**Recommendations**
- Protect or acquire remaining habitats (through purchase, easements or refuges) and exclude non-natives from them.
- Form basin-scale management teams combining Service, agency and academic personnel to coordinate habitat identification, restoration and recovery planning.
- Continue hatchery propagation and genetic stock maintenance as insurance against catastrophe.
- Produce regular, high-quality reports to disseminate results.
