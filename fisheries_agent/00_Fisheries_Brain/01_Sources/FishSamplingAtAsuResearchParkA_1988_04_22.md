# Fish Sampling at ASU Research Park, April 1988

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary

This memo from Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, Center for Environmental Studies) reports results of trammel net, seine, and Oneida trap net sampling conducted at the [[ASU Research Park]] ponds in April–June 1988, as part of an ongoing program to assess reintroduced native [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes. Trammel netting on 19–20 April captured 14 taxa, including two new park records — [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Walleye]] — alongside strong representation of all three stocked natives: [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Razorback suckers dominated catches (>40% of trammel net samples) and showed evidence of reproductive readiness (ripe males), though no larvae were detected despite repeated larval sampling through early June. A borrowed Oneida trap net (deployed in Middle and North Lakes) substantially improved capture rates of bonytail and razorback sucker but failed to detect Colorado squawfish. The cumulative species list for the Research Park now totals 23 taxa, and native stocked fishes continue to perform well, supporting the project's goals of studying decline factors in native big-river fishes.

## Background & Description

### Bonytail (*Gila elegans*)
- Single trammel-net specimen (April 1988): 296 mm TL, 250 g — consistent with range of 5 fish taken 15 March 1988.
- Likely derived from the November 1984 stocking cohort (75 mm juveniles at release).
- April 1987 sample of same cohort averaged 154 mm (N = 9, largest 230 mm) — suggesting the April 1988 fish was **not** a survivor of the 4,656 juveniles stocked November 1987.
- Oneida trap net sample (Middle/North Lakes): 13 fish averaged 265 mm TL (range 211–308 mm) and 152 g (range 110–200 g); included 3 females, 7 males (sex undetermined for remainder).
- Males were tuberculate, running ripe, with orange breeding coloration on belly and paired-fin bases; females did not express eggs.
- Mild-to-moderate *Lernaea* (anchor worm) infestation noted; otherwise excellent condition.
- Winter growth (over preceding winter) was negligible.

### Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- First recaptured since April 1987 (when 2 fish, 85 and 102 mm, taken by seine).
- April 1988 trammel net sample: 6 specimens, mean 310 mm TL (range 272–395 mm), mean 275 g (range 180–500 g).
- Growth since October 1986 stocking described as "excellent."
- Notably absent from Oneida trap net catches (both deployments), despite targeted effort.

### Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- Dominant native taxon; comprised >40% of total trammel net catch.
- 41 fish averaged 410 mm TL (range 376–477 mm) and ~738 g (range 500–1200 g).
- Sex determined for 22 fish: 16 males, 6 females (ratio 2.7:1).
- 10 males were ripe; no ripe females collected.
- Slightly smaller than the 15 March 1988 sample (24 fish, mean 459 mm TL; 15 males, 6 females), suggesting reproductively active fish may represent larger individuals in the population.
- Oneida trap net sample (9 fish): 4 females, 5 males, none ripe or showing secondary sexual characters; mean 429 ± 23 mm TL (range 395–460 mm), mean weight 829 ± 127 g (range 470–1000 g). Little/no winter growth observed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Artificial pond system (North, Middle, South Ponds/Lakes) at the ASU Research Park, hydrologically linked to the Salt River Project (SRP) canal system, which serves as a colonization source for several non-native taxa (e.g., threadfin shad, red shiner, channel catfish).
- **Stocking history**: Native fishes ([[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]]) were stocked by AZGFD (Arizona Game and Fish Department) in cooperation with USFWS, ASU, and the Park in November 1984, October 1986, and April 1987.
- **Community structure**: Trammel net catch varied by pond — North Pond had the highest species richness (9 taxa) and included [[Gila Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Channel Catfish]]; South Pond was dominated by razorback sucker (43.2%) and tilapia.
- **Seine catch patterns**: No native fishes captured by seine — attributed to natives now being too large for effective seine capture. Catch per unit effort varied greatly among ponds (2.0 fish/haul in Middle Pond to 141+ fish/haul in North Pond). North Pond catch was dominated (>93%) by [[Red Shiner]] and was also the only pond lacking largemouth bass, suggesting predation may structure community composition (possible [[Predator-Prey Dynamics]] influencing red shiner recruitment).
- **Larval sampling**: Conducted at 7–10 day intervals from mid-March through early June using bright-light attraction at night. Larvae collected were dominated by [[Threadfin Shad]], with centrarchids second. No cyprinid or cichlid larvae were found despite adult abundance, and **no native larvae (bonytail or razorback sucker) were detected** — interpreted as possible non-spawning, low egg viability, or predation/removal of young before capture.
- **Non-native introductions**: Includes accidental/unauthorized stockings — e.g., [[Largemouth Bass]] introduced by an unidentified local resident (later regularized by Park stocking of ~100 mm fingerlings in May 1987); [[Tilapia Zillii]] intentionally stocked for algae control, with [[Tilapia Aurea]] and [[Tilapia Mossambica]] suspected as unintentional contaminants of that stocking, since both are rare in the SRP canal source population.
- **Non-lethal sampling gear**: An Oneida trap net (on loan from California Department of Fish and Game), a large multi-chambered device (4.7 × 2.1 × 1.8 m throated box with wing traps and lead net, 6 mm mesh), was used specifically to improve capture rates of native fish for research while allowing live release.

## Key Findings

1. **New park records**: [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Walleye]] recorded in Research Park ponds for the first time; walleye likely originated from an AZGFD stocking of 3 million fry in Canyon Lake (1985, 1987) and moved downstream, or were transported/released directly.
2. **Species list expanded to 23 taxa** for the ASU Research Park system.
3. **All three stocked natives** ([[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]]) are surviving, growing, and (in the case of razorback sucker and bonytail) showing reproductive readiness (ripe males) — considered a strong success for the reintroduction project.
4. **No larval native fish detected** despite repeated targeted sampling (mid-March–June), despite presence of ripe adults — raising open questions about spawning success, egg/larval survival, or predation pressure from non-natives.
5. **Oneida trap net proved far more effective** than trammel nets/seines for capturing bonytail and razorback sucker in high numbers non-lethally, but did not capture any Colorado squawfish, indicating gear- or behavior-specific catchability differences among native species.
6. **Possible predation effects on community structure**: absence of largemouth bass in North Pond correlated with a red-shiner-dominated seine catch (>93%), suggesting [[Predation]] may regulate prey fish abundance/composition in these constructed pond systems.
7. Project is positioned to examine impacts of non-native predation on native fish reproduction once spawning is confirmed, with next major sampling scheduled for September 1988.
