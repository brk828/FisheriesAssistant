# Evaluation of the Status, Life History, and Habitat Requirements of Endangered and Threatened Fishes of the Upper Colorado River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 report (FWS/OBS-77/62; Part 2 of a three-part study) evaluates the status, life history, and habitat requirements of the endangered and threatened fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. It was prepared by Ecology Consultants, Inc. (Timothy W. Joseph and James A. Sinning) with co-authors [[Robert J. Behnke]] and [[Paul B. Holden]], under an EPA interagency agreement for the Fish and Wildlife Service's Western Energy and Land Use Team. The authors note that more than two-thirds of the native fishes are endemic, and that the basin's flow is already over-allocated while energy development (coal, oil, oil shale, uranium) increases pressure on water. The report concludes that large-river endemics have declined sharply, that [[Water Depletion]] and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]-type regulation are the principal causes, and that introduced species and hybridization compound the threat.

## Background & Description

The upper basin covers roughly 109,580 square miles above Lee's Ferry, Arizona. Headwaters are cold, clear, first- to second-order mountain streams, which join into large third- to fourth-order rivers that cross desert canyons. The native fauna evolved in isolation for a very long time, producing a high degree of endemism.

The authors recognize 13 native, 7 endemic, at least 27 exotic, and 6 threatened or endangered species (the federally listed ones are [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] (state-listed) and [[Kendall Warm Springs Dace]]). Their species accounts cover the following:

- **Large-river endemics:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (largest North American cyprinid, reported to 6 ft and 80 lb or more), [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- **Cold-water species:** [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Mottled Sculpin]], [[Piute Sculpin]], and [[Speckled Dace]].
- **Warm-water and intermediate-zone natives:** [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Mountain Sucker]].

A substantial taxonomic section addresses the "[[Gila Complex]]" (humpback, bonytail, and roundtail chubs), where hybridization and intergrades complicate species versus subspecies status. The report adopts the view that *G. elegans* and *G. cypha* are valid species, pending studies of current hybridization.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Abiotic conditions.** Flows are highly variable, with snowmelt driving most runoff. The basin's mean annual discharge at Lee's Ferry is 12,500,000 acre-feet for 1896–1975 (virgin flow estimated at 14,900,000 acre-feet). Annual discharge in 1975 was 11,288,300 acre-feet, and depletions rose from 250,000 acre-feet (1896) to 3,823,900 acre-feet (1975). Irrigation accounts for about 56% of consumptive use, transmountain exports about 19%, reservoir evaporation about 19%, and other uses about 6%. Reservoirs trap roughly 75–80% of the sediment that formerly reached [[Lake Mead]], making the river below dams clear rather than turbid. Temperature is strongly altered below dams: at Flaming Gorge, preimpoundment monthly means ranged from 0.0°C to 19.5°C, while postimpoundment means ranged from 4.0°C to 9.5°C. Salinity rises downstream through evaporation, leaching of marine shales, and natural sources such as the [[Paradox Valley]] salt anticline. Waters are generally pH 7–8, with acid mine drainage and trona (alkaline) spills as local threats.

**Biological structure.** The system is divided into three zones:

- **Upper zone:** "trout water," with clear, cold, high-gradient streams; primary production in periphytic algae; salmonids dominant.
- **Intermediate zone:** warmer, more turbid water with cyprinids and catostomids increasing.
- **Lower zone:** warm, very turbid canyon and flat-river reaches, largely sand substrate, where energy depends on allochthonous detritus and terrestrial insects.

Benthic invertebrate communities shift from Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, Trichoptera, and Diptera in the upper zone to burrowing, turbidity-tolerant forms in the lower zone. Planktonic crustaceans (copepods, cladocerans, ostracods) are an important but poorly studied food source for young fish.

**Major human-induced threats** (Section V summary): livestock grazing since the mid-1800s, mining, unsound forestry, channelization, water diversion, dam construction, introduced fishes, [[Hybridization]] with introduced trout and suckers, and industrial and municipal pollution.

## Key Findings

- **Endemism and status:** Of the native fishes, more than two-thirds are endemic; the large-river endemics have been sharply reduced and are gone from most impounded reaches.
- **Flow alteration:** The June-season (May) depletions of the Colorado, Gunnison, and San Juan rivers rose sharply after 1960, with May flows reaching record lows, so the high flows needed for channel maintenance and scour no longer occur. San Juan depletions increased four- to five-fold after [[Navajo Dam]] was completed.
- **Water budget:** Of about 95 million acre-feet of precipitation, nearly all is lost to evapotranspiration; man uses about 3.82 million acre-feet of surface flow.
- **Salinity:** The authors estimate 2.0 tons of dissolved solids are picked up from each newly irrigated acre, with 1.5 acre-feet of additional depletion per acre. Each 1 mg/l salinity increase at Imperial Valley was estimated to cost users $230,000 annually.
- **Squawfish decline:** Colorado Squawfish declined precipitously after large dams and competition with introduced species; Vanicek et al. found it common in the Green River in 1969, but only two juveniles have been collected above Jensen, Utah, since 1970. Actual spawning in nature has never been observed.
- **Spawning temperature:** Squawfish spawning is associated with temperatures reaching about 70°F; roundtail and bonytail chubs spawn near 18°C.
- **Razorback Sucker:** Abundant in the late 1800s and early 1900s and commercially harvested, it is now rare, with reproduction lacking in some formerly productive areas. Hybridization with Flannelmouth Sucker is increasing.
- **Humpback Chub:** Probably never abundant; collections are sporadic, and reproduction is documented only from Desolation Canyon on the Green River.
- **Bonytail Chub:** Once abundant, it is now known from the Green River in Utah only; no young have been found in recent years.
- **Colorado Cutthroat:** Pure populations are rare, persisting mainly in isolated headwater streams in Wyoming and Colorado; hybridization with stocked rainbow and non-native cutthroat is the main cause of decline.
- **Kendall Warm Springs Dace:** Restricted to Kendall Warm Springs (about 29°C), protected by federal ownership; the authors consider major research unjustified given the stable population.
- **Development threats:** The proposed Juniper Springs project on the Yampa River would likely eliminate major Green River spawning habitat for Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker, and flood control could eliminate the backwater nursery habitat that young large-river fish require.
- **Research priorities:** Key gaps include inventory of undiscovered isolated cutthroat populations, humpback and bonytail chub life history (age, growth, food, spawning), the minimum river size and flow needed by large-river endemics, and taxonomic resolution of the Gila complex.
