# The Effect of Channelization on the Fishery of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This report, published in January 1953 by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] Bureau of Fish Conservation, assesses how [[Channelization]] of the lower [[Colorado River]] reduced its value as a game fish habitat. Dam construction in 1936 to 1938 ([[Hoover Dam]], [[Parker Dam]], and [[Imperial Dam]]) was followed by river straightening and dredging by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], including a 1951 project that confined the river to a new channel between Needles and Topock. The author concludes that channelization and marsh drainage reduced the annual sport fishery value from an estimated $37,800 to $5,600, a loss of $32,200 per year. The report recommends compensatory fish habitat improvements elsewhere on the river and modified dredging that keeps more lake and slough habitat connected to the river.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River below Needles was historically a meandering channel bordered by a large marsh in [[Mohave Valley]], extending to Topock. The marsh and its associated lakes supported a fish population estimated at 4,300 (unit partly illegible in the scan). Before channelization, the main channel contained eddies and holes that provided fish cover and food.

The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] Office of River Fishery Investigations conducted surveys in 1948 to 1949. Although no complete census data were obtained, the fishing quality was considered high. The fishery was dominated by:

- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), predominant in the lake habitats
- [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*)
- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), with largemouth bass supporting the river fishery

Earlier estimates indicated that the California river bank in the project area received about 1,200 fisherman-days per mile per year. The author notes this is not a formal evaluation standard but indicates the fishery's value.

Channelization began in 1951 with the goal of flood protection for the Town of Needles and reducing water losses through [[Evaporation]]. The resulting channel is 17 feet deep and 259 feet wide, and it is designed to carry approximately 15,000 cubic feet per second (value partly garbled in scan) at maximum capacity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Channelization changed the river from a sinuous, structurally complex system to a straight, dredged sluice. The main effects described are:

1. Drainage of adjoining [[Backwater Habitat]]
2. Elimination of [[Riparian Vegetation]] cover
3. Elimination of eddies and holes along the littoral zone
4. Increased [[Turbidity]] due to higher flow velocities
5. Increased bank erosion
6. Reduced spawning area

The author states that the channel was the primary source of habitat loss, and that where backwater was drained, the fishery value loss was 100 percent (OCR reading). Damage to river habitat is described as "quite severe," although the changes to the river itself were harder to measure.

At the time of writing, few fishable waters remained on the California side. Deal Slough and the old river channel, totaling 76 surface acres, lie west of and join the dredged channel. On the Arizona side, still water remains at Powell Lake and Topock Bay. A planned [[Marsh Rehabilitation]] project by the Fish and Wildlife Service on the Arizona side would reflood part of the lost marsh, primarily for waterfowl, with game fish waters maintained where applicable.

## Key Findings

- The fishery value of the channelized reach fell from $37,800 to $5,600, a loss of $32,200 per year, based on [[Fishery Valuation]] estimates.
- Dredging and marsh drainage removed most lake and slough habitat that supported the fishery.
- The channel shift from a meandering stream to a straight sluice reduced cover and food availability and increased turbidity.
- The 1948 to 1949 Fish and Wildlife Service surveys indicated high fishing quality and a valuable [[Sport Fishery]], although formal census data were lacking.
- The author states that the damage to the river habitat is severe, but few measurements were available.

**Recommendations:**

1. Because losses in the channelized section are difficult to reverse, the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] should compensate by improving fishing conditions in other parts of the river as needs arise.
2. Where feasible, dredging operations should be modified to keep the maximum area of lake and slough habitat open to the river.

**Reference note:** The report draws on Dill (1944) on the lower Colorado River fishery and Stephens (1938) on the effects of silt and flow regulation on the Rio Grande and Colorado Rivers. Full citations are omitted per the note format.
