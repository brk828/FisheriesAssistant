# Detection of Larval Remains After Consumption by Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (2008), tests whether gut content analysis can reliably detect predation on native fish larvae by small nonnative fishes. Five nonnative predator species were each fed a single larval [[Razorback Sucker]], a federally significant native catostomid whose recruitment has largely failed in the lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. Predators were euthanized at 15, 30, 45 or 60 minutes after consumption and their guts were examined. Identifiable larval remains declined rapidly with time, and the authors conclude that routine gut analysis substantially underestimates larval predation. Predation by nonnative fishes should therefore not be dismissed as a cause of recruitment failure on the basis of negative gut samples.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a native fish that spawns successfully every year but shows near-total recruitment failure. Field evidence points to consumption of larvae by nonnative fishes, particularly [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), as the likely cause.

**Predators tested.** The five nonnative predators were:
- [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*)
- [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*)
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*)
- [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*)
- [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*)

Red shiners, green sunfish and yellow bullheads were the target predators. Sampling yielded too few of these, so bluegills and fathead minnows were collected as a contingency.

**Knowledge gap.** Earlier studies have attributed the scarcity of direct evidence to rapid [[Digestion Rate]] of small larvae, specimen collection and preservation methods, timing of collections, and fragile larval tissues. The present study examines these issues experimentally, using [[Gut Content Analysis]] within short intervals after consumption.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Collection sites.** Predators were collected by [[Electrofishing]] from the [[Salt River]] and [[Sycamore Creek]] in Maricopa County, Arizona. Razorback sucker larvae were collected from [[Lake Mohave]] on the Arizona-Nevada border using dip nets and submersible lights.

**Laboratory conditions.**
- Predators were held in 37.9-L aquaria and fasted for 72 hours before trials.
- Larvae were fed hard-boiled egg yolk to prevent starvation.
- Water was aerated, dechlorinated and treated with Stress Coat and buffered formalin to prevent infection by *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*.
- Water temperature was 20°C, within the spawning range of razorback suckers in the lower Colorado River (10.5–21°C; hatching in 7–21 days).
- Each predator was placed alone in a 1-L, 1.5-L or 2.0-L jar, with isolation chambers reducing visual stress.

**Temperature and digestion.** The authors note that digestion speeds up with temperature. The 20°C laboratory temperature was near the warm end of the observed range, so digestion in the field is probably slower. Field conditions are also less favorable for detection, which means these results may represent a best-case scenario for identifying larval remains.

## Key Findings

**Methods and categories**
- Digestion was scored in four categories: (1) fully intact; (2) partially digested with identifying characters intact; (3) extensively digested with no identifiable characters; (4) no prey material present.
- For analysis, states 1 and 2 were pooled as "identifiable" and states 3 and 4 as "unidentifiable."
- [[Logistic Regression]] (SAS version 9.1) modeled the probability of identification against time since consumption.
- Stepwise contingency-table analysis (CATMOD) showed that pooling the two sunfishes and the two minnows did not significantly affect results (sunfishes χ² = 4.22, P = 0.3764; minnows χ² = 1.25, P = 0.5358).

**Consumption and retrieval**
- 65% of experimental predators consumed a razorback sucker larva.
- Of the larvae consumed, 54 of 72 (75%) were retrieved from the predator or preservation jar.
- 18 of 72 (25%) fish regurgitated the larva into the jar during the brief period before euthanization.
- Most larvae not regurgitated were found in the gill basket or foregut. Feeding mechanics differed across taxa and probably influenced results.

**Green sunfish and bluegill (pooled, n = 37 in figure caption)**
- Predators swallowed larvae whole and mastication was not observed.
- The relationship between identification and time was significant (G = 20.96, P < 0.0001).
- All eight larvae in the 15-minute group were identifiable, and none was identifiable at 60 minutes.
- The model predicts a 50% or greater probability of identification only within about 35 minutes.

**Yellow bullhead (n = 24)**
- Yellow bullheads damaged larvae during consumption, and some fish regurgitated and reconstructed their prey.
- Digestion was rapid (under 1 hour), and prey quickly became unrecognizable.
- Identification probability declined with time (G = 3.31; the P-value is obscured in the scan).
- A 50% probability of identification required analysis within about 38 minutes of consumption.

**Red shiner and fathead minnow (pooled, n = 11)**
- Both species masticated prey thoroughly in the pharyngeal mill, which rendered most remains unrecognizable.
- Only 1 of 11 larvae was recognizable at any interval, and none was identifiable at 15 minutes.
- The relationship with time was not significant (G = 0.2105).
- Digestion was rapid (under 30 minutes). Identification may succeed only when remains are in the foregut or gill basket.

**Preservation effects**
- Direct transfer to formalin caused regurgitation in a number of fish, and gastric regurgitation also occurs in fish held in collection buckets.
- In an earlier study, anesthetizing red shiners with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222) before fixation found larvae in 15% of foreguts. Other studies that preserved fish directly in formalin found few or no larvae.
- Fragile stomach contents can be lost among other material at the bottom of a collecting jar.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Gut content analysis is problematic and unreliable as evidence of predation on native fish larvae by small nonnative fishes.
- Detection of larval predation requires: (1) consumption shortly before capture; (2) an active capture method such as electrofishing, seining or angling, rather than passive gear with time lags such as gill nets, trammel nets or traps; and (3) preservation techniques that limit regurgitation.
- Even under these conditions, gut content analyses are probably biased.
- Investigators should not dismiss larval predation as a major factor in population decline merely because routine gut analysis fails to detect larval remains.
- Alternative methods are recommended to verify results, including [[Larval Tagging]] (calcein marks), [[Bioenergetics Modeling]] and [[Immunoassay]] (ELISPOT).

**Context**
Field-based detection of [[Nonnative Fish Predation]] on native larvae was rare in the literature. Several previous studies, including [[Native Fish Decline]] work in the southwestern United States, documented extirpations linked to nonnative fishes. The authors argue that these studies probably underestimated predation because of the limits of gut analysis.
