# Hudson River Fisheries Investigations 1965-1968: Evaluating a Proposed Pumped Storage Plant at Cornwall, New York

#ichthyology #marine-biology #serranidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared for [[Consolidated Edison Company]] and the [[Hudson River Policy Committee]], evaluates whether a proposed [[Pumped Storage Hydroelectric Plant]] at Cornwall, New York would harm fish in the [[Hudson River Estuary]]. The [[Hudson River Technical Committee]] compiled three years of field work (October 1965 to September 1968) carried out by [[Northeastern Biologists, Inc.]]. The study focused on [[Striped Bass]], the only species with a major fishery that also spawns and nurses in the estuary near Cornwall. The data cover 10,551 collections of fish eggs and larvae and 2,161 collections of young fish.

The Policy Committee concluded that the plant would not significantly harm the striped bass or [[American Shad]] fisheries. It recommended post-construction monitoring, operational adjustments if adverse effects appear, coordinated studies of cumulative impacts from multiple water-intake plants, and development of electrical fish-screening devices.

## Background & Description

**Regulatory context.** The [[Federal Power Commission]] granted Con Edison a license on March 9, 1965. The [[United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit]] set that license aside on December 20, 1965, after a court challenge brought by the Scenic Hudson Preservation Conference and local towns. The Commission then required a fuller record, including fish-damage evidence. Con Edison agreed to fund the studies. Oversight came from parallel Policy and Technical committees drawn from New York, New Jersey, the [[U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries]], and the [[U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]]. Connecticut joined as an advisor in June 1967.

**Proposed plant.** Water would be pumped from the estuary through a tunnel to a storage reservoir in the Hudson Highlands, mainly at night, and released through turbines during peak demand. The intake was to be about 225 ft long, 560 ft wide, and 50 ft deep, faced with a [[Stainless Steel Intake Screen]] of 0.375 × 0.375 inch clear opening. Approach velocity at the screens would not exceed 1.0 ft/sec during pumping.

**Study objectives.** The studies sought to determine (1) the spatial and temporal distribution of life stages vulnerable to intake withdrawal, and (2) the impact of possible losses on striped bass fisheries. Egg, larval, and young-of-the-year stages were studied because older striped bass could avoid the screens.

**Estuary description.** The Hudson River is a tidal estuary between the Battery and Troy (m.p. 0.0 to 154.0). Depths exceed 150 ft between Peekskill and Cornwall. Net freshwater discharge at Poughkeepsie averages 43,000 cu ft/sec in April and 6,870 cu ft/sec in August (1947–1965 averages). Water temperature at Poughkeepsie ranges seasonally from 33° to 78°F. Calculated velocities near Cornwall average 1.1 ft/sec on flood tide and 1.3 ft/sec on ebb.

**Species of focus.** Of 56 species reported in the 1936 Greeley survey, 33 were collected with haul seine in 1965. Commercial fisheries target American shad and striped bass. The sport fishery north of the George Washington Bridge is valued at an estimated $20 million annually. Species selected for study were those of economic importance or seasonally abundant at Cornwall that could be drawn into or impinged on the intake: [[Striped Bass]], [[American Shad]], [[Blueback Herring]], [[Alewife]], [[Bay Anchovy]], [[Atlantic Tomcod]], and [[White Perch]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Striped bass life history (as reported).** Adults spawn in virtually fresh water when temperatures reach 58°F, with peak spawning at 60° to 67°F. Fertilized eggs average 0.134 inches in diameter, are semi-buoyant, and hatch in 1.5 to 2.0 days at 62° to 63°F. Newly hatched larvae average 0.13 inches. Fecundity ranged from 68,000 to 4,536,000 eggs in females of 4.4 to 35 lb (Jackson and Tiller, 1952).

**Shad and other species.** American shad eggs average 0.143 inches and lack an oil globule. Young shad remain in the estuary through summer and emigrate by autumn. Blueback herring and alewife spawn in open water but have adhesive eggs. White perch spawn in spring in areas used by striped bass, with smaller, adhesive eggs. The bay anchovy spawns in late spring and summer in brackish water.

**Salinity and oxygen.** The salt front (50 ppm chlorides) moves downstream during high spring runoff and upstream during low flow. Dissolved oxygen drops in summer in the upper estuary: at Coeymans it fell below 1.0 ppm in 1966 and 1967, while at Poughkeepsie it stayed above 6.0 ppm in 1966.

**Plant hydraulics.** Total flow near Cornwall varies between 0 and over 200,000 cu ft/sec, averaging 100,000 cu ft/sec over a tidal cycle. Pumping withdrawal would average 18,000 cu ft/sec, or about 18% of passing water. The withdrawal zone was estimated to lie within the 40-ft contour, or 1000 ft out from the intake.

## Key Findings

**Egg and larval distribution (1966–1968)**
- Striped bass eggs were most abundant from Saugerties to Peekskill, mainly between Hyde Park and Peekskill in 1966. Eggs increased in abundance with depth and were rarely taken at the surface.
- Eggs were rarely collected at salinities above 0.3% and mostly where salinity was below 0.1%.
- Larvae concentrated farther downstream, between Marlboro and Croton. Larvae were more bottom-oriented by day and more dispersed through the water column at night.
- At Cornwall in 1968, egg abundance peaked in early to late May at 58° to 63°F. Larval abundance was bimodal, with peaks in late May and early June.

**Entrainment estimates**
- Eggs: less than 0.7% of the estuary's total egg production would be vulnerable to withdrawal. About 15.4% of eggs were estimated in the Cornwall area in 1966 and 13.9% in 1967. In 1968, 4.0% of available eggs at Cornwall were in the pumping zone, about 462,900 eggs per day.
- Larvae: about 2.8% of larvae in the estuary would be subject to removal (2.9% in the Abstract). In 1968, about 12.2% of larvae at Cornwall would have been vulnerable.
- Young-of-the-year: up to 6.2% of young striped bass at Cornwall in late summer would be vulnerable. This fraction fluctuated and generally fell as fish grew, ranging from 6.2% to 0.4% between mid-July and early September 1968.

**Screenability**
- Kerr (1953) found that 0.375-inch mesh screens effectively screen striped bass of 2.0 inches or more at 1.5 ft/sec. The report concluded that striped bass larger than about 2.0 inches would be screened out at the proposed intake.
- Striped bass reached at least 2.8 inches by early autumn. Atlantic tomcod would be largely screenable by early August, American shad and alewives by mid-August, white perch by mid-September, and blueback herring by early October. Bay anchovies would not be screenable as young-of-the-year or yearlings.

**Gear and methods**
- Eggs and larvae were sampled with 1.5-ft plankton nets. Mesh sizes changed across seasons, and a 0.020 × 0.031-inch mesh was used for routine sampling in 1968.
- Sampling covered 10,551 egg and larval collections and 2,161 young-fish collections. Over 136,000 fish larvae from 1968 were sorted.

**Policy Committee conclusions**
- Withdrawal of eggs, larvae, and young-of-the-year would have minimal effects on populations because of the widespread distribution of species in the estuary. The bay anchovy was an exception, with losses possible for at least two years of life, but the Committee expected little effect on the total population.
- The evidence indicated no significant adverse effects on the striped bass and American shad fisheries.

**Recommendations**
- Conduct further studies after construction to establish actual plant effects.
- Modify operations if post-construction studies reveal adverse effects not seen in pre-construction surveys.
- Conduct coordinated studies of fish distribution at existing and future plant sites to assess cumulative mortality.
- Install [[Electrical Fish Screening Device]] if one is successfully developed for projects drawing large water volumes from the river.

**Cumulative risk.** The report warned that the combined operation of several large-volume water-intake plants could significantly reduce fish production, with particularly severe effects if plants are built in areas of heavy fish concentration.
