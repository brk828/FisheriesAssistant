# Notropis edwardraneyi: A New Cyprinid Fish from the Alabama and Tombigbee River Systems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document formally describes [[Notropis Edwardraneyi]] (Fluvial Shiner), a moderate-sized minnow endemic to the [[Alabama River]] and [[Tombigbee River]] systems. The description, by Suttkus and Clemmer of [[Tulane University]], is based on 32,493 specimens. The species is restricted to main channels and larger tributaries below the [[Fall Line]], where it is the dominant minnow. It is compared with its closest relatives, [[Notropis Blennius]] and [[Notropis Potteri]], and is separated mainly by its very large eye and its pigmentation. The authors also discuss how the three species are related and how the range of the group may have been shaped by river connections and possible bait-bucket introductions.

## Background & Description

**Discovery and history.** The senior author first collected the species on 1 August 1957 below [[Lock No. 2]] on the Tombigbee River. In 1962 [[Meredith May Blackwell]] requested identification of Cahaba River specimens, which proved to be the same form. An intensive survey of the Alabama River main channel began in 1964 and found the species common, if not the most abundant cyprinid. The study was supported by National Institutes of Health grants and by many collectors, field assistants, and landowners.

**Type material.** The [[Holotype]] is Tulane University (TU) 49485, an adult 57.6 mm standard length (SL). It was collected on 8 March 1967 from the Alabama River at [[Yellow Jacket Bar]] (River Mile 129.8) in Wilcox County, Alabama. Paratypes were distributed among more than ten institutions, including the United States National Museum, Cornell, Michigan, Stanford, and the Field Museum. The paratype series (8,224 specimens, 22.1–56.0 mm SL) was supplemented by 11,773 additional specimens from the Alabama River main channel, between Watts Bar (River Mile 204.5) and Choctaw Bluff (River Mile 45). A further 1,297 specimens of other material were examined from the Tombigbee, Alabama, Black Warrior, Cahaba, and Tallapoosa systems and their tributaries.

**Diagnosis.** The species is a moderately stocky shiner of medium length. The largest of the 32,493 specimens measured 65 mm SL. Key characters are:

- Usually 34 lateral line scales (range 32–35).
- Usually 35 vertebrae (range 34–36).
- A very large, supralateral eye. Orbit diameter is greater than upper jaw length, about one-third of head length, and averages more than three-fourths of the postorbital distance.
- Typical pharyngeal tooth formula of 2, 4-4, 2 (47 of 50 examined specimens).

**Comparison with N. blennius.** The two species have nearly identical head-to-standard-length proportions, but orbit size separates them. In *N. edwardraneyi* the orbit is nearly always larger than the snout and always larger than the upper jaw. In *N. blennius* the orbit averages only slightly more than half the postorbital distance, is never as large as the upper jaw, and the ratio of orbit to standard length shows no overlap with *N. edwardraneyi*. The snout of *N. edwardraneyi* is blunter and its eyes are more dorsal.

**Morphology and coloration.** Scales are large over most of the body, but the head and breast are naked. The lateral line is complete and slightly decurved anteriorly. Nuptial tubercles on the pectoral rays occur only in adult males. The species is silvery, without striking colors. Its dorsal surface is pigmented, with a pronounced light margin on many dorsolateral scales. A faint wedge-shaped basicaudal spot appears in some specimens, especially juveniles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** All specimens came from the main channels of the Alabama and Tombigbee rivers and the lower reaches of their major tributaries. The authors note that the irregular pattern on the distribution map reflects uneven collecting rather than gaps in the species' range. The species is absent from the Pascagoula and Pearl rivers and other small systems between the Alabama–Tombigbee basin and the Mississippi River.

**Reproduction.** Ripe females were collected in May and June at water temperatures of 21–28 °C. Spawning fish were taken in moderate current over sand, gravel, or a mixture of gravel, sand, and silt, in water one to two and a half feet deep. Distended females collected in the Tombigbee River in April 1966, at 15 °C, were "squeeze ripe".

**Geographic variation.** No clines were detected in meristic counts, proportions, or pigmentation. The only notable deviation was lateral line scale count in the Choctaw Bluff sample.

**Relationships.** *N. edwardraneyi* is considered a close relative of *N. blennius*. The authors suggest it may be an offshoot of *blennius* stock that reached the Alabama and Tombigbee systems through a former Tennessee River connection. Movement from the Mississippi basin is considered unlikely because the species is absent from intervening rivers. No *blennius* population examined showed close affinity to *edwardraneyi* in orbit size. This suggests that differentiation occurred mainly after the stock was isolated, though convergence in large-river habitat cannot be ruled out.

**Related species.** The authors treat *N. potteri* as more distinct from *edwardraneyi* than *blennius* is. They regard *potteri* as possibly a descendant of a different, unknown stock that converged in several traits. *N. potteri* lives close to the bottom, while *N. blennius* is often found at mid-depths. In the upper Red River, [[Notropis Bairdi]] is the dominant ecological representative. Its absence from the lower Red River is discussed as a factor in the distribution of *potteri*.

## Key Findings

- **Identity and abundance:** *N. edwardraneyi* is endemic to the Alabama–Tombigbee system and is the dominant minnow of its main channels and larger lower tributaries. It is named in honor of [[Edward C. Raney]].
- **Diagnostic trait:** The very large eye (orbit averaging over three-quarters of the postorbital distance) separates it from *N. blennius* with no overlap. Its other notable traits are blunter snout, more dorsal eyes, and characteristic pigmentation.
- **Counts:** Lateral line scales average 33.8 (n = 181). Caudal peduncle scale rows were 5-2-5 in 177 of 181 specimens. Vertebrae are usually 35 (Table 3 lists 105 of 131 specimens with 35).
- **Reproductive timing:** Spawning occurs in May and June at 21–28 °C, over gravel or sand in moderate current.
- **Relationships:** It is closest to *N. blennius* and probably arrived in its present range through a former Tennessee River connection. Its relationship to *N. potteri* is less clear, and the authors suggest possible convergence.
- **Red River controversy:** The authors argue that *N. potteri* reached the lower Red River naturally, through tributary captures or stream connections. They do not fully exclude bait introductions, especially for recent collections. They also note that *potteri* may have displaced *blennius* in shallow marginal areas of the lower Red River.
- **Taxonomic conclusion:** The species is described as new, with the authors noting that earlier reports of *N. blennius* in the Tombigbee likely referred to this form.
