# Owens Pupfish (Cyprinodon radiosus) Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan addresses the [[Owens Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon radiosus*), a small endemic cyprinodontid of the [[Owens Valley]], California, listed as endangered federally (1967), by the State of California and by the American Fisheries Society. Habitat loss from water diversion and impoundment, plus introduced predators and competitors, reduced the species to three small, man-modified refugia by July 1984. The plan's primary objective is to downlist and ultimately delist the species. Recovery requires five populations of at least 500 overwintering fish each, established in five separate areas and free of threats for five consecutive years. Downlisting to threatened status requires at least three new refugia to be established alongside the existing ones.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Originally referred to as *Cyprinodon macularius* (Kennedy 1916). [[Miller]] (1948) described it as a new species endemic to the Owens Valley, with its type locality at the northwest spring of [[Fish Slough]]. Its closest relative is considered to be the ancestral form of *C. macularius*, which entered the Owens and Death Valley areas during the Pliocene.
- **Morphology:** A small, deep-bodied, laterally compressed fish. Maximum total length is 60 mm, but most adults are 30 to 45 mm. It differs from *C. nevadensis* and *C. diabolis* by a more anteriorly placed dorsal fin, higher fin-ray counts, a slenderer head and a larger caudal peduncle.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Breeding males are blue with an olive dorsal surface and a slate-colored venter, and are territorial. Females are olive with brown blotches and show an ocellus on the posterior dorsal fin.
- **Reproduction and life history:** The spawning season runs from April to October and begins once water exceeds 20 °C. Females lay 50 to 200 transparent, demersal eggs per season (about 2 mm diameter), which hatch in 7 to 10 days. Fish mature in 3 to 4 months, allowing one spawn before their first overwintering.
- **Diet:** Omnivorous. The primary food item in a 14-fish sample was chironomid larvae (90%). Pupfish are not piscivorous but eat their own eggs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat requirements:** Good-quality water, sparse vegetation (cattails and bulrush), and a silt- or sand-covered bottom. Typical habitat is shallow water (8–15 cm) adjacent to deeper areas used for territories and spawning. Dense tule stands appear to exclude the species.
- **Hydrology:** Winter and spring runoff creates shallow nursery areas. Historically, populations peaked in mid- to late summer before seasonal marsh desiccation.
- **Historic distribution:** From Fish Slough (Mono County) south to, but not including, Owens Lake (Inyo County). Numbers declined progressively from 1915 through 1941. Kennedy (1916) observed large schools in 1915, while a 1934 survey by Hubbs found only a few fish.
- **Causes of decline:** Upstream impoundments and diversions, including the Los Angeles Aqueduct, removed flooding cycles and water from the valley. Introduced [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Crayfish]] and bullfrogs prey on or compete with pupfish. Groundwater pumping, vandalism and unauthorized stocking further threaten refugia.
- **Co-occurring species:** The [[Owens Sucker]], [[Owens Tui Chub]] (proposed endangered in 1984), and an Owens speckled dace form are endemic to the Owens Basin.
- **Fish Slough uniqueness:** The area hosts the Fish Slough milk vetch (*Astragalus lentiginosus* var. *piscinensis*), found only there, several other rare plants, and an undescribed Fish Slough snail (*Fontelicella* sp.). The Bureau of Land Management proposed Area of Critical Environmental Concern status for 35,926 acres.
- **Water quality:** Natural fluoride at BLM Springs (1.5 mg/l) may approach the reported maximum acceptable level for aquatic life.

## Key Findings

- **Current status (July 1984):** Three populations in two man-modified refugia: the [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]] (about 8.5 ha fenced, with a 2.3 ha pond), [[BLM Spring]] in Fish Slough, and [[Warm Springs]] (near Big Pine). None is secure.
- **Rediscovery:** Believed extinct for 14 years. A population was found in Fish Slough in 1956 and rediscovered in 1964, with about 200 individuals at the time. All subsequent Owens pupfish populations descend from it.
- **Recovery criteria:** Five populations, each with at least 500 overwintering fish, in five separate areas, free of threats for five consecutive years. Delisting is contingent on this.
- **Interim objective:** Downlisting to threatened status through expanding existing populations, establishing three additional populations elsewhere and minimizing threats.
- **Proposed new refugia:** [[River Spring Lakes]] (Adobe Valley, also holding an introduced [[Amargosa Pupfish]] that must be removed to prevent hybridization), Little Lake (owned by California Department of Fish and Game, within historic range), Black Lake and Antelope Spring (Adobe Valley, outside historic range). Adobe Valley introduction would require approval from the Director of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
- **Management actions:** Exotic fish eradication (drawdown and selective removal), bullfrog and crayfish control, emergent and submergent vegetation control, dam maintenance, fence and outflow redesign at Warm Springs, and a multiagency management plan for Fish Slough.
- **Monitoring:** Surveys twice annually, with census in early spring (overwintering) and late summer (peak), plus reproductive success and habitat condition.
- **Research priorities:** Habitat requirements, and interactions between [[Owens Pupfish]] and mosquitofish. Use as a mosquito control agent is limited to the Owens Valley.
- **Enforcement and outreach:** Enforcement of the Endangered Species Act of 1973 and state and local regulations, review of regulations every two years, public education, press and brochure materials, and signage at refugia.
- **Implementation schedule:** Part III tabulates actions by category, priority (1 to 3), responsible agency, timetable and estimated cost. The cost figures in the scanned table are not reliably legible and are not reproduced here.
- **Review:** Reviewing agencies were the California Department of Fish and Game, California Department of Parks and Recreation, Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, U.S. Bureau of Land Management, National Fishery Research Center (Seattle) and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Washington, D.C.).
