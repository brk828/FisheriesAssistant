# Fish Movement Through Navigation Locks and Commercial Netting Evaluation in Kentucky Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two studies by Ellis R. Carter of the Kentucky Division of Fisheries. The first examines whether fishes use [[Navigation Locks]] on the [[Kentucky River]] to move between pools. Ten of fourteen locks were sampled by poisoning, yielding 2,821 fish weighing 864.27 pounds that had moved into lock chambers from the pools below the dams. The second, the main study, evaluates nine types of commercial fishing gear in [[Kentucky Lake]] over 26 months. It reports 11,212 fish weighing 11,575.2 pounds, from 36 species captured in nets, and compares netting results with [[Rotenone Sampling]] of cove areas. Game and pan fishes made up 58.75 percent of the net harvest by weight, commercial species 35.56 percent, and forage and trash species 5.69 percent.

## Background & Description

### Lock Movement Study (Kentucky River)

- **Objective:** Determine whether and to what extent fishes use navigation locks to travel between pools.
- **Method:** Ten of the fourteen locks were used as test areas. Fish in lock chambers were recovered by poisoning, initially and again at a second poisoning. Figure 2 plots pounds recovered per lock for each event.
- **Population composition (second poisoning, all chambers):** Table III lists 449 fish weighing 84.56 pounds across 29 species. The largest share by weight came from a few game and commercial species, with Flathead Catfish, Emerald Shiner, Storer's Chub, Bluntnose Minnow and Log Perch each contributing very small numbers.
- **Key figures:** 2,821 fish (864.27 lb) moved into chambers from below the dams. All but 449 fish (84.56 lb) were assumed to have moved upstream into the pools above.
- **Acknowledgments:** The U.S. Corps of Engineers and their lockmasters supported the work.

### Netting Study (Kentucky Lake)

- **Lake:** A man-made impoundment formed in 1945 by a multipurpose dam on the [[Tennessee River]], 22 miles above its confluence with the Ohio River. It covers about 260,000 acres and extends 184 miles south from the dam at Gilbertsville, Kentucky. Maximum width is two miles and average depth at conservation pool is 23 feet.
- **Objectives:** (1) compare gear efficiency and selectivity for rough fishes by depth and bottom type; (2) assess the present status of the fish population; (3) determine the value of rotenone sampling as an indicator of harvestable fish.
- **Funding:** Federal Aid Project F-2-R, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
- **Gear types (nine):** [[Hoop Nets]], [[Fiddler Nets]], [[Wing Nets]], [[Gill Nets]], [[Trammel Nets]], [[Wire Basket Nets]], [[Wood Basket Nets]], [[Lead Nets]] and [[Heart-Lead Nets]]. Hoop and fiddler nets were the only types legal for licensed commercial fishermen. The other seven were experimental.
- **Study areas:** Five of seventeen candidate areas were netted, chosen after 37 Ekman dredge bottom samples. Areas varied in depth, shoreline, bottom type, and whether they were coves, channels or main-lake shelves.
- **Effort:** Nets were raised daily, seven days a week for 116 days, except in hazardous weather. Each net was treated as a separate set for each calendar month.
- **Data recorded:** Date, total length, weight, sex, maturity, and physical condition. Scale samples were taken from crappies and white bass.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Physical conditions:** Water is seldom clear, with a typical Secchi disc reading of about two feet. The lake does not stratify, with less than 3°F variation between surface and bottom. Dissolved oxygen varies less than 1 ppm from surface to 70 feet or more. Apart from this, the lake is primarily eutrophic.
- **Water level and weather:** Annual fluctuation was eight feet or less. Wind action is considerable, and the lake is sometimes unsafe for small boats.
- **Seasonal behavior:** Harvest peaked in April, probably tied to spawning migrations. Summer catches were low, possibly because of high water temperatures reducing fish movement. Cooler water in September coincided with a rise in catch.
- **Introductions:** Northern Pike were introduced into Kentucky Lake in March 1953 by the Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources. All other species taken are common in the region.
- **Bait:** Oil cake, a cottonseed meal by-product, was used in hoop nets to increase carp catches.

## Key Findings

### Lock Movement (Kentucky River)

- Movement through locks is substantial: 2,821 fish moved into chambers from below the dams, and most were assumed to have passed upstream.
- The effects of this movement on individual pool populations and on the wider river fishery are unknown. The author discusses possible effects but does not quantify them.

### Gear Performance (Kentucky Lake)

- **Overall:** 11,212 fish (11,575.2 lb) taken in 5,347 net days. The study reports 36 species from nets (15 game and pan, 16 commercial, five forage and trash), plus two forage species from rotenone samples.
- **Hoop nets:** 1,761.9 lb. Easiest to use and most successful with commercial fishes (79.18 percent by weight).
- **Fiddler nets:** 769.0 lb over 601 net days (1.28 lb/day). Commercial species made up 86.09 percent. Two-throat designs outperformed single-throat designs, as with all barrel-type nets.
- **Wing nets:** Caught the most fish by number, but 2,491.5 lb (1.74 lb/day over 1,429 net days) were 82.07 percent game and pan fishes.
- **Gill nets:** 1,159.0 lb over 727 days (1.59 lb/day). Commercial fishes made up 50.9 percent. Gill nets were inefficient with mesh over three inches and selective for game fish with mesh under three inches.
- **Trammel nets:** 534.0 lb over 421 days (1.27 lb/day), with slightly more commercial than game fish.
- **Wire baskets:** 202.1 lb; 75.30 percent game fish. These caught the smallest fishes, averaging 0.30 lb each.
- **Wood baskets:** Lowest return per unit effort (0.23 lb/day). Commercial types made up 79.5 percent of the 69.4 lb catch.
- **Lead nets:** 378.3 lb over 107 days, 68.8 percent game and pan fishes.
- **Heart-lead nets:** The highest total (4,210.0 lb) and the highest per-day yield (12.42 lb/day, per Table III). Game fishes made up 84.94 percent.

### Area Comparisons

- **Area II** (deeper cove with channels): 5,899.3 lb over 1,447 net days, 4.08 lb/day, more than twice any other area. It was the only area where fish averaged over one pound each.
- **Area I** (shallow cove): 1,942.1 lb, 1.90 lb/day, with 78.06 percent game fish.
- **Area III** (shallow, with carp baiting): 1.24 lb/day, with 56.58 percent commercial fish and carp making up 71.4 percent of the commercial catch.
- **Area IV** (main lake shelf and channel): 1.54 lb/day, with commercial fish at 54.99 percent.
- **Area V** (deep channel): The best commercial catch, with 61.83 percent commercial fish.

### Legal vs. Experimental Gear

- Legal nets (hoop and fiddler) caught 81.2 percent commercial fishes by weight.
- Experimental nets caught 70.0 percent game fishes by weight.
- Legal gear produced higher pounds per net day than experimental gear in Area IV only.

### Species Composition and Economic Value

- Game and pan fishes: 58.75 percent of the net harvest by weight. White crappie alone provided 48.70 percent of the total and 82.89 percent of game fish.
- Commercial species: 35.56 percent. Carp made up 22.67 percent of this group, and the authors state carp are not considered overabundant.
- In 1950, Kentucky licensed 1,460 commercial fishermen, who took an average of 1,085 lb each and marketed their catch for $329,680.00.

### Seasonal Patterns

- April 1952 was the peak month: 2,037.0 lb of game fish and 855.5 lb of commercial fish. A study of spring 1952 white crappie found 826 of 925 fish sexually developed, supporting a spawning-movement explanation.
- Game fish were better caught in shallow areas (I–III) during cooler months and in deep areas (IV–V) from July through October.

### Rotenone Sampling

- Six population samplings in 1952–1953 covered 7.4 acres of cove area, treated with 3 lb of powdered derris (5 percent rotenone) per acre foot.
- Game and pan fishes: 9.33 percent of weight (5,884 fish, 12 species, 151.21 lb).
- Commercial species: 15.89 percent of weight (2,117 fish, 257.69 lb).
- Forage and trash fishes: 74.78 percent of the 1,621.40 lb total. Shad dominated this group.
- The authors conclude rotenone samples are probably a reliable index of relative abundance and spawning success in typical coves, but not a reliable indicator of harvestable population.
- Discrepancies with netting were attributed to differing habitats sampled, rotenone's kill of small forage fish missed by nets, and gear selectivity (gizzard shad made up nearly three-quarters of rotenone poundage but about 1 percent of net poundage).

### Injury and Mortality

- In 1952, 6,538 fish were handled. 356 were killed and 220 injured (576 total, 8.81 percent).
- Gill nets injured or killed 61.4 percent of their catch, and trammel nets 50.0 percent.
- Mortality peaked in January, mainly due to easily killed blue herring. Injuries peaked in May (106 of 1,070), possibly from spawning-related weakness.
- Overall loss represented 12.95 percent of pounds caught, mostly shad and blue herring.

### Limitations and Discrepancies Noted

- Species counts vary between sections (36 vs. 38 species; 15/16/5 vs. 15/16/7 functional groups).
- Net-day totals differ between Table III (15,347) and the text summary (5,347). The summary figure is used here.
- The lock study does not state how the "assumed" upstream movement was verified.
