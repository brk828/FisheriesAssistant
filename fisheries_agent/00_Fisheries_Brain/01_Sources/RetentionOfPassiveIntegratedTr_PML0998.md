# PIT Tag Retention in Largemouth Bass Brood Fish and a Low-Cost PVC Cage for Aquatic Animals

#ichthyology #marine-biology #micropterus-salmoides

## Executive Summary

This document contains two technical notes from *The Progressive Fish-Culturist* (1989). The first evaluates [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT tags) for individually identifying [[Largemouth Bass]] brood fish at the [[Texas Parks and Wildlife Department]] (TPWD) Fish Hatcheries Branch. Over a 24-month trial, all tagged fish retained their tags and no tag-related infection was observed, and the authors judged the tags satisfactory for brood stock management. The second note describes a cheap, easily built circular cage made from polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipe for holding aquatic animals in research and production, developed at the [[Texas A&M Shrimp Mariculture Facility]].

## Background & Description

### Note 1: PIT Tagging of Brood Fish

- **Need:** Maintaining distinct genetic strains and year classes of [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) requires individual identification, sexing and pairing of brood fish. Existing methods (separate ponds, fin clips and similar) had only partial success and were labor-intensive.
- **Tag technology:** A PIT tag is a 2 × 10 mm glass-encased microchip with an electromagnetic coil carrying a unique 10-digit code (about 34 × 10⁹ codes possible). The code is read by a hand-held wand that activates the tag and displays the number on a digital readout. Tags are injected with a needle on a spring-loaded syringe.
- **Prior use:** Similar tags had been used in salmonids with retention rates above 90% ([[Prentice Et Al 1985]]).
- **Objectives:** (1) Determine whether brood fish retain PIT tags over at least one production season, and (2) determine whether tag implantation affects spawning and survival.

### Note 2: Circular PVC Cage

- Cages for aquatic animals are needed for brood stock and fry holding, low-cost replicate treatments in research, and the live-bait trade (fish and shrimp).
- Before 1975, cages made of wood, metal or fiberglass with wire mesh cost US$38–50 each, needed frequent maintenance, and deteriorated quickly in marine settings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hatchery setting:** Brood fish were held in earthen ponds and a concrete raceway at a water depth of 40 cm. Spawning was carried out in ponds and the raceway, and fry were harvested from them.
- **Marine setting:** The PVC cage was designed for saltwater ponds, where conventional cages often needed replacement due to corrosion and fouling. The cages lasted up to five years in saltwater ponds.
- **Spawning biology:** Brood fish lost on average about 19% of body weight during spawning. The authors described this as consistent with the typical 15–21% weight loss of spawning fish (OCR-ambiguous range; unpublished data cited).

## Key Findings

### PIT Tag Trial (Note 1)

- **Sample and injection:** 22 brood fish were injected with PIT tags on April 28, 1986, using a 12-gauge needle inserted between the pelvic fins. Fish were given a 15-second bath in 2.2% sodium chloride solution before release.
- **Stocking:** Pond 1 received 3 males and 8 females; pond 2 received 2 males and 7 females; a raceway received an additional pair.
- **Readability:** All tags were readable immediately after injection.
- **Retention:** Fish were examined on September 10, 1987 (17 months after injection) and May 10, 1988 (24 months after injection). All 22 retained functioning tags; no tag loss, active infection or tissue necrosis at implant sites was observed.
- **Spawning output:** Pond 1 yielded 45,100 fry and pond 2 yielded 12,100 fry. The raceway also produced an additional, OCR-illegible number of fry. The authors noted that spawning of the two fish in the raceway was successful, so any effect of tagging on spawning appeared minimal, though a direct test of physiology or fecundity was not performed.
- **Mortality:** Two brood fish died after return to the holding pond. One of these had its tag reimplanted into another fish, where it functioned perfectly.
- **Juvenile tagging:** A subsequent trial tagged juvenile bass (OCR-ambiguous average total length, approximately 251 mm). Twenty fish deaths were recorded, which the authors attributed mainly to fish size and the team's initial inexperience with tag injection rather than the tag itself.
- **Costs:** Each tag was reported at approximately US$5.25 and the reader/display unit at approximately US$2,500 (both OCR-ambiguous). Tagging over 2,000 brood fish of various species was expected to justify the reader cost through amortization. Tag reuse was expected to lower long-term costs.

**Management benefits:**
- Only one initial sexing is needed before tagging, reducing handling time and stress.
- Several year classes and genetic strains can be combined in one pond.
- Holding ponds fell from 13 ponds (2.6 ha total surface area) to 4 ponds (1.8 ha total surface area), with six genetic strains placed in one pond and seven year classes spread across three others.
- The hatchery typically spawns largemouth bass brood fish for five production seasons; the 24-month trial was considered short-term but encouraging.
- The authors concluded that PIT tags are "an indispensable part" of their brood-fish management program.

### PVC Cage (Note 2)

- **Design:** A circular cage with a bottom area of 1 yd² and anchoring legs, built from ½-inch PVC pipe and fittings and covered with extruded plastic mesh.
- **Construction:** Four 4-ft side supports (fitted with reducers, tees and crosses), four 1-ft anchoring legs, and 10-ft sections forming the top and bottom hoops. A single cage can be assembled in about 30 minutes using glue joints and a cost breakdown in the source's Table I.
- **Cost and durability:** Cheaper than conventional cages (US$38–50 each) and durable, lasting up to five years in saltwater ponds.
- **Applications:** Holding brood stock and fry, low-cost replicate treatments where pond or raceway numbers are limited, and holding bait animals for sale.
