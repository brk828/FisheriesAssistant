# Radio-Telemetry Study of Juvenile Razorback Sucker Movements and Habitat Use Prior to Reintroduction in the Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1996 proposal, prepared by the [[Hualapai Department of Natural Resources]] for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], describes a radio-telemetry study of juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Grand Canyon. The study is intended to gather baseline data on survival, movements, activity patterns and habitat use before a [[Reintroduction Program]] begins. The project would stock 20 juvenile fish raised at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] into the [[Colorado River]] near [[Spencer Creek]], then track them for several months. The proposal also justifies a new [[Hualapai Endangered Fish Rearing Facility]] as a way to strengthen regional capacity for endangered fish recovery.

## Background & Description

The document is a proposal, not a research report, and it contains no measured results. It is dated September 8, 1996 and was accompanied by a December 17, 1996 cover memo from Ben Zimmerman to Marsh and Glen Knowles of the Center of Environmental Studies. The memo says Dr. Chuck Minckley had informed the center of its interest in the implants.

**Stated purpose:** Implement a successful [[Reintroduction Program]] for razorback suckers in the Grand Canyon. The authors argue that studies are needed to understand fish behavior, responses and habitat use before stocking.

**Objectives:**
1. Gather information on movements, survival and activity patterns.
2. Evaluate habitat use versus availability.
3. Provide recommendations for future stocking and reintroduction efforts.

**Project team:**
- **Ben Zimmerman** (Hualapai Department of Natural Resources): project manager, with about four years of Grand Canyon monitoring experience.
- **Bill Leibfried** (SWCA, Inc.): telemetry technical lead and surgeon, with prior [[Humpback Chub]] telemetry experience.
- **Helen Yard**: surgical assistant.
- **Hualapai technicians** (Mike Vaughn, Scott Crozier, Dawn Hugruley, Richard Beecher): logistics and telemetry monitoring.
- **Chuck Minckley** (USFWS): technical assistance.
- **Glen Gould and other BoR staff**: technical and logistical support.
- **Jim Hansen and staff**: rearing razorback suckers at Willow Beach.

The report is to be sent to the Arizona Game and Fish Department, USFWS, Bureau of Reclamation, [[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]] and [[Grand Canyon National Park]] for review.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Decline of native fishes:** The document states that the construction and operation of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] contributed to the decline of native fishes in the Colorado River. Only four of the eight species that occurred before the dam remain as viable populations. Razorback sucker is still abundant in [[Lake Mead]], but its population is declining. The source cites an estimate of 50,000 fish for Lake Mead, now reduced to roughly 2,500–5,000 (attributed to Burke in the source; the exact date is unclear in the scan). The text names the altered water temperature regime, discharge fluctuations and introduced fishes as the main causes.

**Regulatory context:** The Final [[Glen Canyon Dam]] Environmental Impact Statement proposed reducing daily discharge fluctuations and studying how modified dam operations could enhance native fish populations. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion and recovery documents call for a second population of [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and address the need for captive-reared razorback suckers.

**Rearing capacity:** [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico was described as the only facility in the Southwest fully dedicated to rearing endangered fishes. Johnson (1991) is cited as calling for additional rearing capacity. The proposed Hualapai facility would offer a state-of-the-art rearing system, support genetic diversity in captive stocks, and reduce the risk of catastrophic losses from accidents.

**Study area:** The study covers 51 miles of the Colorado River and the upper end of Lake Mead, from Bridge Canyon to Pearce Ferry.
- [[Lower Granite Gorge]] (river miles roughly 225–258) has an average channel width of about 240 ft (73 m), a moderate slope and a substrate dominated by bedrock and boulders. Its geology resembles the lower portion of Upper Granite Gorge.
- Perennial tributaries in this reach include [[Diamond Creek]], [[Spencer Creek]], [[Spencer Canyon]], [[Surprise Creek]], [[Lost Creek]] and [[Quartermaster Canyon]].
- The upper reach near Lake Mead is lake-like, with a shoreline of cliffs and talus. Alluvial deposits there were inundated by the lake.

## Key Findings

The proposal is a plan rather than a findings report. Its main elements are:

**1. Fish acquisition and handling**
- Obtain the necessary permits from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the state agency before research begins (scheduled for December 1996 in the work plan).
- Acquire 20 juvenile razorback suckers from Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery.
- Transport the fish by helicopter from Willow Beach to Spencer Creek, holding them in tanks and anesthetizing them as needed.

**2. Tagging**
- Surgically implant radio transmitters and passive integrated transponder (PIT) tags for individual identification.
- A buffer group of 5 fish is held back to replace any mortality during surgery or transport.
- Release 5 fish in each of three locations: [[Separation Canyon]], the mouth of Spencer Creek, and the braided area near the [[Bat Caves]].

**3. Post-release tracking**
- Two tracking teams will be set up. One will follow the 10 fish released at Spencer Creek, and the other will follow those released near the Bat Caves.
- Monitoring trips are planned at two per month for four months. Each trip includes a survey or location trip and a 24–48 hour tracking trip.

**4. Habitat measurements**
- At each location, record depth, velocity, substrate, temperature, overhead cover and lateral structure.
- Record water-quality variables, including dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity and turbidity.
- Map movements using aerial photographs.

**5. Equipment**
- Order radio transmitters from [[Advanced Telemetry Systems]]. The order lists a quantity of 15 units with a pulse rate of 55–56 ppm, a battery life of 130 days and standard circuitry, shipped under job number 11421 with a ship date given as 6/12/96.

**6. Schedule (as stated in different parts of the document)**
- Fall 1996: permits, equipment purchase and fish acquisition.
- Late 1996 or early 1997: surgery and stocking.
- April–July 1997: monitoring trips.
- Summer 1997: preliminary draft for agency review, with comments requested within 4 weeks.
- Late summer to December 1997: final report. Dates differ between the work plan and the schedule pages, so the exact timing should be confirmed.

**7. Expected outcomes**
- Survival, movement and activity-pattern data for juvenile razorback suckers after stocking.
- Recommendations for future stocking sites, to be incorporated into the stocking plan for the planned Endangered Fish Rearing Facility on the Hualapai Indian Reservation.

**Reference to Source Documents**

(1992) on humpback chub life history, and Williamson and Wydoski (1994) on genetics management guidelines.
