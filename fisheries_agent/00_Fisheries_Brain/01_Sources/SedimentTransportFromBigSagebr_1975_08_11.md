# Sediment Transport from Big Sagebrush Watersheds

## Executive Summary
This paper is a **hydrology and watershed management study**, not a fish or aquatic-organism-focused paper. It reports suspended sediment and bedload transport data from two perennial, spring-fed streams (**[[Loco Creek]]** and **[[Sane Creek]]**) draining big sagebrush (*[[Artemisia Tridentata]]*) rangeland in south-central Wyoming. No fish species, fish populations, or aquatic biota are directly discussed in the source text. However, the data are highly relevant to **stream habitat quality and aquatic ecosystem context**, since suspended sediment concentration, bedload deposition, and streamflow regime are key abiotic drivers of salmonid and other coldwater stream fish habitat (e.g., spawning gravel embeddedness, turbidity, water clarity). This note extracts the physical/hydrological findings with an eye toward their ecological relevance to stream-dwelling aquatic fauna.

## Background & Description
*Not applicable — this source contains no morphological data on fish or other aquatic organisms.* The paper instead characterizes the physical stream/watershed environment:

- **Loco Creek**: 664 ha (1,639 acres) drainage; gauge located 1.6 km downstream of a perennial spring; well-vegetated, stable banks; 1–2% slope on lower reaches.
- **Sane Creek**: 238 ha (587 acres) drainage; spring only 45 m above the gauge; southwest-to-southeast exposure increasing solar radiation input; well-vegetated, stable banks; 1–2% slope on lower reaches.
- Both streams are instrumented with stilling ponds, 120° V-notch weirs, and PS-69 pumping samplers (400 mL samples filtered through 0.45 µm membrane filters).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
While no organisms are named, the study describes the **abiotic template of a coldwater montane stream ecosystem** that would define habitat suitability for aquatic life such as trout or macroinvertebrates:

- **Hydrologic regime**: Baseflow sustained by groundwater discharge (~0.05 × 10⁻² m³/sec/km²) during winter under ice/snow cover; snowmelt-driven peak flows in spring (April–June); low, stable summer baseflow.
- **Water clarity/turbidity regime**:
  - Winter (Oct 1–Mar 31): average suspended sediment 6 ppm (range 1–28 ppm); <11 ppm 89% of the time — indicating very clear water and low turbidity stress during winter incubation periods.
  - Snowmelt runoff (Loco Creek): sediment spiked to a maximum of 296 ppm (May 7, 1974), one day before peak hydrograph; strong diurnal fluctuation (e.g., 65 ppm AM vs. 296 ppm PM on the same day) — a pulse-disturbance regime relevant to spawning/incubation timing.
  - Summer: sediment declined to <10 ppm by late September; typically <40 ppm even during rain events (peak 72 ppm during a 23 mm rain event).
- **Extreme event habitat disturbance**: "Over-snow flow" events (1970, 1973) at Sane Creek produced extreme turbidity spikes (max 862 ppm) and bank erosion via channel-cutting into snowpack — a rare, high-magnitude sediment pulse disturbance distinct from normal snowmelt.
- **Substrate/bedload dynamics**: Annual bedload deposition (proxy for channel substrate turnover, relevant to spawning gravel maintenance) ranged:
  - Loco Creek: 132–388 × 10⁻² m³/km²
  - Sane Creek: 9–196 × 10⁻² m³/km² (12× and 6× higher in over-snow-flow years 1970 and 1973, respectively)
- **Comparative context**: Sediment yields from these sagebrush-steppe streams were comparable to or lower than forested subalpine watersheds in Colorado ([[Fraser Experimental Forest]]) and Arizona mixed-conifer sites, suggesting these streams offer relatively low ambient sediment stress typical of well-vegetated, geologically stable rangeland catchments — a favorable baseline for coldwater aquatic habitat.

## Key Findings
- **No fish species, fish community, or fish population data are reported in this source.**
- Suspended sediment concentrations remained low (<20 ppm) most of the year, with the exception of brief, intense snowmelt pulses — a pattern that would minimize chronic turbidity stress on gill function and visual foraging in stream fish, while creating short-duration high-turbidity windows during peak runoff.
- "Over-snow runoff" events represent a unique, geographically specific disturbance mechanism causing extreme (up to 862 ppm) sediment pulses and elevated bank erosion, which could have outsized short-term impacts on stream habitat structure (e.g., redd scouring, gravel embeddedness) if fish were present.
- Bedload sediment yields from these sagebrush watersheds were within the range reported for undisturbed, forested subalpine watersheds, indicating that well-managed, vegetated sagebrush rangeland streams can maintain "near-natural" sediment regimes supportive of stable aquatic habitat.
- The study underscores that **land management and vegetative cover** (rather than sagebrush cover type per se) are the primary controls on sediment delivery to streams, with direct implications for downstream aquatic habitat quality in rangeland-dominated watersheds.
