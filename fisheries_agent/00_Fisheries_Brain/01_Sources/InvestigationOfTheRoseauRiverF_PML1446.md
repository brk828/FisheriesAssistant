# Investigation of the Roseau River Fish Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae #esocidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by Paul Inlarsh and Ira R. Adelman of the [[University Of Minnesota]] Department of Entomology, Fisheries and Wildlife, documents a 1978 fisheries survey of the [[Roseau River]] in northwestern Minnesota. The work was commissioned by the [[U.S. Army Corps Of Engineers]] Environmental Resources Branch to supply data for a supplement to the Environmental Impact Statement for a modified [[Roseau River Flood Control Project]]. Data covered length, weight, species composition, population size, and habitat for fish across nine river reaches sampled between 7 and 18 October 1978.

## Background & Description

The survey was required because design modifications to the proposed flood control project changed the scope of the impact assessment. Fish were collected mainly by [[Electrofishing]] (Smith-Root Type VIA unit, pulsed DC, 4.0–5.0 amps at 400 volts) and supplemented by [[Seine Netting]] with a 3.0 x 0.9 m minnow seine. Measured game and forage fish were returned alive except lamprey and unidentified specimens. Population size was estimated by [[Mark-Recapture Estimation]] using fin clipping, and an attempt at [[Floy Tagging]] of large pike and walleye was halted because of high post-tagging mortality. Ages were determined from scales impressed on cellulose acetate slides and counted under magnification.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The river was divided into nine reaches spanning roughly river mile M138.0 to M93.0. Upstream (Reaches 1–2) and downstream (Reaches 8–9) sections were relatively high-gradient, with solid substrates, rocks and sparse vegetation. Intermediate reaches (3–7) were generally slow, with soft substrates and considerable aquatic vegetation, including [[Potamogeton]] and [[Elodea]] beds. Cover was greatest upstream in Reach 1. Water clarity ranged from about 8 to 18 inches, and water temperatures in the survey window ranged from 44°F to 52°F. Reaches 1–2 included deep, turbid water that was sampled at night to capture fish moving into shallow areas. Oxbow habitat (Reach 6) was largely unnavigable, with dense [[Typha]] marsh and pools of soft muck.

## Key Findings

**Overall community**
- Sixteen fish species were collected by electrofishing and seining (Table 2).
- Electrofishing catch was dominated by white sucker (47%), walleye (28%) and northern pike (22%). Northern redhorse, trout-perch, black bullhead, carp and sauger made up the rest.
- By weight, white sucker (42%) and northern pike (26%) were the leading contributors in the electrofishing catch.
- Total catch per unit effort (CPUE) was highest in downstream reaches (7–9) and lowest in the middle reaches; the summary states this as a reach-level ranking.
- Very few small fish were collected by either seining or electrofishing.

**Game fish and age structure**
- Walleye and northern pike were the most abundant game species.
- The walleye catch showed a distinct paucity of fish between about 150 and 249 mm. Scale analysis indicated that these "missing" fish were age group I. State data (Peterson and Enblom 1978) also showed a scarcity in that size class.
- Age 0 walleye were very poorly represented, while all age classes of northern pike were present.
- Northern pike ranged from age 0 to age group VII. Mean length by age: 189 mm (0), 343 (I), 414 (II), 466 (III), 543 (IV), 610 (V), 820 (VII). No age VI fish were collected.
- Walleye ranged from age 0 to age XII. Most were age II, and only 12 were age I. Mean length by age: 129 mm (0), 270 (I), 286 (II), 309 (III), 347 (IV), 374 (V), 411 (VI).

**Population estimates and tagging**
- Population estimates for predominant species were generally unreliable because very few marked fish were recaptured. Some estimates had extremely high negative bias. Estimates underscored in Table 7 were considered statistically valid. The authors reported that fish abundance was somewhat greater in downstream areas.
- Of 59 northern pike and 88 walleye tagged, only 44 pike and 8 walleye survived handling and tagging. High mortality occurred in both species even though only larger individuals were tagged.
- No tagged fish were recaptured during the investigation.

**Habitat associations**
- Larger walleye and white sucker were associated with pool habitat. Larger northern pike were associated with vegetation in relatively shallow water.
- Smaller fish were generally found in riffles (white sucker) or stream-margin vegetation (northern pike and walleye).
- Fish concentrations were rarely found near brush piles or log jams. The authors noted this may reflect low electrofishing efficiency in those situations.

**Seine collections**
- Seine catches were extremely low. Seven species were unique to seine collections: common shiner, johnny darter, blackside darter, fathead minnow, longnose dace, rock bass and central mudminnow.
- Seven species reported by the DNR (sand shiner, creek chub, silver redhorse, stonecat, tadpole madtom, burbot and freshwater drum) were not captured in this survey.
- Black bullhead was captured in this survey but not by the DNR.

**Conclusions relevant to the project**
- The river contains a substantially higher proportion of game fish than most comparable waters.
- The authors emphasized the scarcity of walleye in the 150–249 mm range, which they considered potentially significant for the species' distribution within the Roseau River and adjacent waters.
