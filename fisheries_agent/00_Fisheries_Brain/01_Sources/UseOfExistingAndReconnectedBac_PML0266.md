# Razorback Sucker Habitat Use and Predictive Modeling in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Gurtin, Bradford and Brouder (Arizona Game and Fish Department, published in the Transactions of the American Fisheries Society, 2003) examines how hatchery-reared adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) use existing and reconnected off-channel habitats in the Imperial Division of the lower [[Colorado River]]. Using ultrasonic telemetry tracking of 75 fish, the authors found strong selection for backwaters over main-channel habitat, built a [[Logistic Regression]] model to predict backwater use, and concluded that the model can help prioritize sites for [[Habitat Reconnection]] to support species recovery.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is native to the southwestern United States and once ranged throughout the Colorado River system. Its populations declined because of habitat alteration and destruction, degraded water quality, and competition and predation by nonnative fishes. Recovery efforts have run for more than 20 years but have been limited by poor information on the species' life history and biological requirements.

Recovery programs rear fish to juvenile or adult stages in hatcheries before release into critical habitats. The authors stated two aims: to develop a model of habitat use by hatchery-reared fish, and to test whether fish would use reconnected side-channel habitats. The research was linked to reconnection projects planned by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (USBR) within the Imperial Division.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** The lower 9.6 km of the Imperial Division, upstream of [[Imperial Dam]]. The main channel was homogeneous, with a silt-sand substrate, sand bars, and depths rarely above 2.5 m. Riffles and pools were very scarce.
- **Riparian and invasive species:** Cattails (*Typha* spp.), bulrushes (*Scirpus* spp.), reeds (*Phragmites* spp.) and tamarisk (*Tamarix* spp.) dominated the banks. Nonnative fishes, including flathead catfish, channel catfish, common carp, largemouth bass and various centrarchids, were abundant throughout.
- **Habitat classification:** Four habitat types were identified and split into 36 sites: 25 backwaters (0.68–28.10 ha), 9 side channels (0.53–4.17 ha), six 1.6-km segments of [[Main Channel]] (about 133 ha in total), and one [[Impoundment]] (13.2 ha) next to Imperial Dam.
- **Measured variables:** Dynamic traits included specific conductance, turbidity, temperature, pH and water velocity. Static traits included surface area, perimeter, shoreline development index, mean and maximum depth, distance to the main channel, and backwater entrance sinuosity.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- 75 hatchery-reared adult razorback suckers (413–570 mm total length) from [[Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery]] were implanted with ultrasonic transmitters (Sonotronics, 69–83 kHz, about 5 g in water) and released on 24 November 1999 (n = 38) and 3 October 2000 (n = 37).
- Fish were located in daylight surveys every 2–3 weeks from December 1999 to July 2001 (25 reconnaissance surveys), plus five diel surveys.
- Habitat selection was analysed with chi-square goodness-of-fit tests, Spearman rank correlations, and logistic regression. The model was validated by splitting the data roughly in half.

**Habitat use**
- Backwaters and the impoundment accounted for 80% of fish contacts while making up 49% of the study area. Only 15% of contacts were in main-channel habitat, which covered 45% of the area. Side channels accounted for 5% of contacts, slightly less than their availability.
- Of the fish, 57% (mentioned as 57%) were exposed to more than one habitat type, 13 (17.9%) to at least three, and five fish to all available types.
- Diel habitat use did not differ significantly among six periods of the day (P = 0.879).
- The average number of contacts per fish was 5.6 (maximum 21).

**Reconnection response**
- After reconnection (about April 2000), slightly more than half of contacts occurred in backwaters and side channels in the Imperial Restoration Project (IRP) area.
- Of 42 fish released in reconnection areas, 23 remained in the IRP area and 6 left and later returned. Overall, 69% of released fish in the IRP remained there.
- 77 of 75 released fish (96%) moved from their original release site, indicating active habitat selection.

**Predictive model**
- Logistic regression was developed for backwaters. Surface area, mean depth and distance to main channel were retained, with surface area and perimeter/SDI excluded for collinearity (r > 0.6).
- The model predicted nonuse of backwaters well but was indeterminate for predicting use. Its performance dropped on post-reconnection data, because physical habitat and water quality had changed significantly.
- Models for main-channel and side-channel habitats performed poorly, given few fish observations.

**Water quality changes after reconnection**
- Velocity increased and temperature and conductivity decreased significantly in reconnected habitats, probably due to greater flushing from the main stem. Turbidity did not change significantly.

**Management implications**
- Three backwaters (AB12.8, AB12.4, AB12.3) were judged suitable, covering about 10.71 ha of the 18.94 ha reconnected area (about 57%; the source text gives "5!a/o," probably 57%).
- Off-channel habitats are no longer maintained by natural floods and must be kept open by dredging and excavation. The model can help prioritize low-cost reconnection sites and avoid unsuitable ones.

**Limitations**
- Hatchery-reared fish may not behave like wild fish.
- Available structure and cover were not measured because of homogeneous habitat and poor visibility.
