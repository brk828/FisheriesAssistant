# Factors Affecting Accuracy of Ichthyoplankton Samples Used in Power Plant Entrainment Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ichthyoplankton

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service Topical Brief (FWS/OBS-76/20.7, June 1978) reviews how sampling error affects [[Ichthyoplankton]] data (fish eggs and larvae) used in [[Power Plant Entrainment]] impact assessments. It catalogs the seven general categories of sampling gear in use, identifies abiotic and biotic factors that prevent gear from collecting a "representative" sample, and provides a checklist for evaluating or designing sampling programs. The brief was prepared by R. R. Bowles, John V. Merriner (Virginia Institute of Marine Science) and John Boreman (National Power Plant Team) under the [[Biological Services Program]] of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].

## Background & Description

The [[Biological Services Program]] was established within the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to supply scientific information and methods on environmental issues affecting fish and wildlife, particularly for environmental impact assessment and Department of the Interior energy development. Its project areas include coal, power plants, geothermal/mineral/oil shale development, water resource analysis, coastal ecosystems and [[Outer Continental Shelf]] development, and systems and inventory (including the National Wetlands Inventory).

The brief belongs to the Topical Briefs series, written for fish and wildlife biologists who review electric power projects. It addresses only technical issues of power generation and transmission.

Impact assessment relies on three sampling program types, each with different requirements:
1. Taxonomic surveys (species composition).
2. Differential intake/discharge mortality studies (effects of transport through the plant).
3. Absolute or relative abundance surveys (population or community effects).

Errors can arise in sample design, collection, processing (mislabeling, misidentification) and data processing (miscoding, inappropriate statistics).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Ichthyoplankton sampling dates to the early 1900s, when marine commercial fisheries used egg and larval abundance indices to forecast year-class strength. Power-plant-related sampling expanded rapidly from the early 1970s.

**Seven gear categories** (Table 1 gives advantages and disadvantages):
- **Low-speed nets** (towed below ~3 knots; e.g., [[Bongo Net]], [[Meter Net]], benthic sleds): [[0.5 m Conical Plankton Net]] is the most used gear in the US.
- **High-speed nets** (e.g., Jet, Gulf, Miller samplers; towed at 5-10 knots).
- **Plankton recorders** (e.g., [[Hardy Continuous Plankton Recorder]]).
- **Pump samplers** (centrifugal, trash and sewage pumps).
- **Mid-water trawls** (e.g., [[Issacs-Kidd Mid-Water Trawl]]).
- **Diver-operated gear** (hand nets, slurp guns, "cookie cutter" samplers).
- **In-line samplers** (filters and pipe taps in cooling water mains), attractive for low operating cost, but accuracy not yet demonstrated.

**Abiotic factors** (Table 2): habitat type, water body morphometry, thermal stratification, water velocity, turbidity, time of day, weather, salinity, tidal motion; plant intake type, withdrawal volume, intake velocity and location; gear filtration efficiency, mouth and mesh size, flow meter placement, tow speed, pumping rate, clogging, extrusion, and turbulence.

**Biotic factors**: vertical preference, diel and seasonal migrations, mobility, swimming speed, and response to physical variables.

## Key Findings

- **Hydraulics dominate accuracy.** Turbulence around a towed net reduces filtering efficiency; steady, laminar-like flow is ideal. Vibrations or turbulence can trigger gear avoidance.
- **Net clogging and flow measurement.** Clogging is monitored with flow meters outside and inside the net mouth. In bridled nets, center-mounted flow meters under-register filtered volume (Mahnken and Jossi 1967; Tranter and Smith 1968). Placing meters two-thirds of the distance from center measures flow more accurately. Unbridled nets showed relatively uniform flow across the mouth.
- **Mesh selection.** Commercially available meshes from 0.100 mm to 0.790 mm are used. Common choices include 0.202, 0.333 and 0.505 mm (Bongo nets); 0.500 mm (freshwater) and 0.200 mm (marine/estuarine) for meter nets; and 0.500 mm for pump samplers.
- **Tow speed trade-off.** Faster tows reduce larval avoidance but excessive speed extrudes small organisms through meshes. Pump samplers need longer deployments but rarely clog.
- **Environmental effects.** Cold water or night sampling may increase vulnerability to gear by reducing activity or perception. Salinity changes can alter buoyancy and depth distribution.
- **Patchy distributions.** Variance in catch often equals or exceeds the mean, so counts may range from zero to many times the average. Demersal and pelagic eggs and larvae require gear deployed to match the three-dimensional distribution (Figure 3: stratified, oblique, vertical and fixed deployments).
- **Avoidance.** Larvae may detect towing bridles or clogged nets via pressure waves; passive avoidance from hydraulic conditions also occurs.
- **Comparative gear performance (limited evidence):**
  - Circular meter nets outperformed 0.5 m nets in size range and larvae per volume filtered (Quirk, Lawler, and Matusky Engineers 1974; Texas Instruments 1977).
  - Bridleless Bongo nets were more efficient than 0.5 m or 1 m nets (Marine Research Inc. 1975).
  - Sherman and Honey (1971) found no difference between Bongo nets and high-speed samplers for fish eggs.
  - Aron (1958) found close agreement between a 0.5 m net and a centrifugal pump.
  - Beers et al. (1967) collected more zooplankton diversity with a submersible pump.
  - Portner and Rohde (1977) found significantly more striped bass eggs and 3-6 mm larvae in a stationary net than in a pump sample, but no significant difference for 76 mm striped bass.
- **Continuity matters.** Long-term studies benefit from identical gear; inadequate but consistently biased gear may be preferable to preserve historical data. If gear must change, run simultaneous comparative samples to make datasets compatible.
- **Gear selection checklist:** evaluate the sampling environment (including inaccessible areas such as coves and marshes); identify target species life stages, egg type (floating, semi-buoyant, demersal) and life-stage durations; compare several gear types before sampling; calibrate flow meters frequently and do not rely on theoretical flows; balance tow speed and duration against avoidance, extrusion and clogging; and select oblique, stratified, vertical or fixed tow patterns by life stage and distribution.
