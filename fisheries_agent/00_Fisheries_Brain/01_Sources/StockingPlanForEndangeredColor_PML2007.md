# Stocking Plan for Endangered Colorado River Fishes in Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This revised 2001 plan from the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] sets out restoration stocking for three endangered Colorado River fishes: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]. [[Humpback Chub]] is discussed but stocking is not anticipated at this time. The plan links stocking numbers, sizes, seasons and river reaches to recovery goals of the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]]. Stocking would run seven to nine years, with fish tagged by [[PIT Tagging]] and monitored after release. Recovery depends on hatchery broodstock, which is limited for all three species, and on other actions such as fish passage and nonnative fish control.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The plan follows earlier stocking plans for the Upper Colorado River basin, including [[Burdick and Bonar]] (1997) for Razorback Sucker, [[Nesler]] (1998) for Colorado Pikeminnow, and [[Hudson et al.]] (1999) for Utah stocking.
- **Priority species:** Three species are listed in priority order: Razorback Sucker, Bonytail and Colorado Pikeminnow.
- **Razorback Sucker:** Wild numbers in the Colorado and Gunnison rivers were too low to supply breeding stock. The [[Genetics Management Plan]] created a "blended stock" from fish taken from the Colorado River, Lake Powell, the San Juan arm of Lake Powell, riverine sources and [[Etter Pond]]. Founders from [[Etter Pond]] and other sources are closely related, so mating between any two of them was judged genetically unacceptable because of inbreeding.
- **Bonytail:** Functionally extirpated in the wild. The only captive broodstock descends from 10 artificially spawned fish taken from [[Lake Mohave]] and is held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Wahweap State Fish Hatchery]].
- **Colorado Pikeminnow:** Broodstock development began in 1991 (as printed). Broodstock is held at [[Horsethief State Wildlife Area]] and Dexter National Fish Hatchery. Future progeny are to be obtained through capture of wild adults from the Colorado and Gunnison rivers.
- **Humpback Chub:** In September 1995, sixteen fish were collected at [[Black Rocks]] for captive propagation and as a safeguard against extinction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking objectives**
1. Establish new populations of Razorback Sucker, Bonytail and Colorado Pikeminnow in unoccupied reaches.
2. Augment and stabilize existing populations of these species in occupied reaches.
3. Establish multi-age-group populations of at least three adult age groups.

**Expected outcomes**
- Bonytail: 1–2 new populations.
- Razorback Sucker: 1–3 populations in the Colorado River, complementing the existing two in the middle and lower Green River reaches.
- Colorado Pikeminnow: expansion of the Colorado River population, with possible new spawning aggregations in [[Debeque Canyon]] and [[Dominguez Canyon]].

**Habitat and reach selection**
- [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] on the Gunnison River has a working fish passage facility. Similar passage on the Colorado River is considered essential.
- Razorback Sucker reaches: Colorado River from Rifle to Debeque and from Palisade to Stateline; Gunnison River from Hartland Dam to Redlands Dam.
- Bonytail reaches: Yampa River in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] and the Colorado River in the [[Grand Valley]] reach.
- Colorado Pikeminnow reaches: Colorado River from Rifle to Debeque and Gunnison River from Hartland Dam to Redlands Dam.
- Stocked Razorback Sucker may compete with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] for low-velocity habitat. Control of [[Common Carp]] and [[White Sucker]] may reduce competition for native suckers.
- Selenium concentrations appear higher in the Gunnison River reach, which may reduce larval survival there. This makes the Upper Colorado River reach potentially more favorable for Razorback Sucker.

**Hybridization and niche**
- Bonytail stocking raises concern about hybridization with [[Humpback Chub]]. The plan notes that hybridization within the Gila complex is partly natural (McElroy and Douglas 1995). Niche separation may reduce the risk.
- Roundtail chub is mainly a tributary species, and humpback chub is mainly a canyon species. This leaves mainstem and floodplain habitat as an available niche for Bonytail.

**Survival and buffer**
- Survival rates come from [[Ryden]] (1997) and [[Wydoski]] (1990) for hatchery conditions, later adjusted by the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Biology Committee]]. The committee acknowledges these rates may underestimate wild survival.
- A buffer equal to presumed annual adult mortality in the wild was added to each species' recovery abundance criterion.

## Key Findings

**Stocking schedule and fish size**

| Species | Stocking duration | Fish size at stocking |
|---|---|---|
| Bonytail | 7 years | After second growing season |
| Razorback Sucker | 8 years | After second growing season |
| Colorado Pikeminnow | 9 years | After third growing season (Age 3+, 150 mm) |

- Target adult age groups: Razorback Sucker ages 5–7; Bonytail ages 4–6; Colorado Pikeminnow ages 7–9.
- Colorado Pikeminnow target: 1,150 adult fish at ages 7–9, based on the draft recovery goal of 1,000 fish for the Upper Colorado River plus natural mortality of 15% for adults.
- Colorado Pikeminnow stocking: 2,680 fish of age 3+ and 150 mm per year, split equally between the Upper Colorado and Gunnison rivers.
- Bonytail target: adult populations of at least 4,400 fish up to six years old in two geographically distinct locations in the Upper Colorado River subbasin.

**Cost and monitoring**
- PIT tags are recommended for every stocked fish at $3.75 per tag.
- Estimated tag cost is $161,700 per year if all three species are stocked annually.
- Per-population costs are estimated at $5,000 to $45,000 per year, based on 1,340–12,000 fish.
- Population estimates follow [[Osmundson and Burnham]] (1996).

**Colorado Pikeminnow population status**
- Adult density was about 4 fish per mile in the Grand Valley reach and 3–3.4 fish per mile below [[Westwater Canyon]].
- The population was judged unlikely to recover without stronger year classes. Survival of 0.96 was used for the existing population.
- Osmundson and Burnham suggested that a 4% survival increase would double population size in 18 years.

**Translocation**
- Translocating wild adults from the Grand Valley to new reaches is not ruled out. Concerns include reproductive success of moved fish, incomplete knowledge of spawning behavior, and differences in habitat between sites.
- Natural recolonization of the Upper Colorado reach is also possible if passage is provided at [[Redlands Diversion Dam]].

**Recommendations**
1. Stock Razorback Sucker, Bonytail and Colorado Pikeminnow into designated reaches at the sizes and seasons in Table 1.
2. Begin monitoring after release, covering survival, distribution and causes of mortality.
3. Adjust hatchery production and stocking numbers based on monitoring results.
4. Discontinue stocking in a reach if monitoring shows significant downstream movement away from the target reach.
5. Use translocation of wild adult Colorado Pikeminnow as a supplement to stocking.
6. Install artificial passage at [[Price-Stubb Diversion Dam]] and [[Highline Diversion Dam]] to allow wild pikeminnow movement.
7. Estimate propagation facility capacity against stocking needs to find any shortfalls.

**Key uncertainties**
- Survival of stocked fish in the wild.
- Habitat needs and life history of Bonytail.
- Female reproductive success in the wild.
- Natural migratory and spawning behavior, especially for Colorado Pikeminnow.
- Effects of downstream movement and swamping by hatchery-raised fish.
