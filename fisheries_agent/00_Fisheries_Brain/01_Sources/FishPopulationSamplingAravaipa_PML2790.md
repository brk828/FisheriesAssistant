# Fish Population Sampling: Aravaipa Creek, Arizona, 1991–1992

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report by Anthony L. Velasco (Arizona State University, submitted to [[The Nature Conservancy]] under contract AZFO-053091-1, final version dated 31 December 1993) documents monthly fish sampling in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a perennial desert stream in southeastern Arizona. The study began as a monitoring program to track the expected establishment of the non-native [[Red Shiner]], but when shiners failed to establish by February 1991 the aim shifted to building a baseline dataset on population trends, native–non-native interactions, and responses to discharge. The creek supports the most diverse native fish assemblage persisting in a single desert stream in the American Southwest: seven native and eight non-native species. Native populations were broadly stable and comparable to the roughly 30-year [[Minckley Long-Term Dataset]], with [[Gila Spikedace]] the notable exception, ranking higher than before in the eastern and canyon reaches. Non-native species were rare and episodic in 1991–92, though their overall relative abundance had risen over the long-term record.

## Background & Description

Aravaipa Creek heads in a broad valley between the Pinaleño/Santa Teresa and Galiuro Mountains in Graham County, flows through an 18-km canyon with vertical walls exceeding 200 m, and then runs about 10 km across a wider floodplain in the western reach to its confluence with the San Pedro River at roughly 650 m elevation. Riparian vegetation includes [[Fremont Cottonwood]], [[Sycamore]] and willow in the eastern and canyon reaches, while the western reach is dominated by mesquite and is more exposed to sun.

The climate is bimodal: winter frontal rains, a spring drought, short violent summer monsoon storms, and an autumn dry period. Mean annual precipitation at Ft. Grant is 32 cm. A single major spate exceeding 85 m³/s occurred in March 1991; other high flows in 1991–92 did not exceed 28 m³/s.

Three sampling reaches were defined: eastern, canyon, and western. Each was assigned 200-m sites, sampled monthly, quarterly, or as available, using seines and backpack electrofishers. Habitat notes (riffle/run/pool proportions, substrate, depth, width, canopy and temperature) were recorded for each site.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fish habitat in Aravaipa Creek is governed mainly by sediment supply and transport, which depend on the volume and timing of precipitation and runoff. Winter floods scour and redistribute channel and floodplain alluvium, creating new habitat. Pools persist only where anchored by canyon walls, bedrock, large roots or other obstructions. Long periods of normal flow lead to armoring of riffles and filling of pools with sand and silt, whereas major floods reset the channel.

Non-native species were concentrated in the western and canyon reaches. The high-gradient canyon appeared to limit their upstream dispersal, and floods removed them from the most constrained segments. Native species were scarcely affected by flooding. Invasion pathways included downstream movement from the [[San Pedro River]] during periods of connectivity, livestock watering tanks in the upper watershed, and riparian ponds linked to the creek through diversions. Spring-fed or tributary connections also appear to introduce non-natives episodically.

Per the report's species table, native taxa are spawning-timed to flood and sediment regimes: suckers typically spawn after peak winter floods, short-lived species such as spikedace and loach minnow spawn later and respond to summer or early-autumn spates, and longfin dace spawn in loose sands whenever they become available.

## Key Findings

**Native fish community**
- Seven native species and eight non-native species are recorded from the creek (Tables 1–2).
- Longfin dace ([[Agosia chrysogaster]]) remained relatively constant in the eastern and western reaches. Seine relative abundance was about 35% in the eastern reach and 80–85% in the western reach.
- Speckled dace ([[Rhinichthys osculus]]) were rare, comprising about 1% or less of the total community, mostly in the eastern reach.
- Spikedace ([[Meda fulgida]]) dominated the eastern and canyon reaches, with seine relative abundance ranging from about 25% to 60%. It was absent from the western reach in 14 of 16 months in 1991–92, but absent in only 4 of 25 years between 1965 and 1990. Its rank was generally higher than in the annual record.
- Loach minnow ([[Tiaroga cobitis]]) averaged about 5% of seine catches in the eastern and canyon reaches.
- Roundtail chub ([[Gila robusta]]) averaged roughly 1–5% of seine catches, with a preference for the canyon reach and evidence of reproduction in spring and early summer.
- Desert sucker ([[Catostomus insignis]]) averaged about 5% in the eastern reach and up to about 10% in the canyon. Electrofishing over-represented larger individuals.
- Sonora sucker ([[Pantosteus clarkii]]) was rare in seine samples, with electrofishing again yielding higher values.

**Non-native species**
- Non-native fishes made up a small, variable proportion of catches. Most were captured in the western reach.
- Yellow bullhead ([[Ameiurus natalis]]) was uncommon, with a single late-summer peak attributed to a riparian pond. Juveniles indicated local reproduction.
- Western mosquitofish ([[Gambusia affinis]]) occurred only in the western reach, with spring reproduction and disappearance of most adults by autumn. Relative abundance peaked near 8% in summer.
- Green sunfish ([[Lepomis cyanellus]]) first appeared in the eastern reach after a 1983 flood, probably from livestock tanks. A 1991 flood removed most of the population from the eastern and canyon reaches.
- Largemouth bass ([[Micropterus salmoides]]) invaded in the first summer of the study via a diversion into a western-reach pond, then disappeared.
- Red shiner ([[Cyprinella lutrensis]]) was first found in 1990 (27 adults in October 1990). Only three individuals were recorded in 1991–92, all in February 1991, so no population established.
- Fathead minnow ([[Pimephales promelas]]) was found once in May 1991 in the canyon reach.
- Diet analyses showed predatory non-natives (largemouth bass, bullheads, sunfish) fed extensively on native fishes, without selective predation apparent in the limited sample.

**Discharge and population dynamics**
- Native recruitment was most closely associated with winter flooding or monsoonal spates that redistribute sediment.
- Short-lived species (spikedace, loach minnow, speckled dace) showed strong seasonal juvenile pulses, and their adult populations increased with juvenile recruitment.
- The long-term record indicates an increase in non-native relative abundance through 1990, followed by a lower level in 1991–92. Non-native populations tend to peak in summer and decline with winter floods.

**Management conclusion**
- The author recommends that non-natives be excluded from the watershed through upstream eradication, that downstream entry be prevented, and that enforcement and public education accompany these measures to deter illegal stocking.
