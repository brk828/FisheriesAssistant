# Flexible Food Habits of Threadfin Shad

#ichthyology #marine-biology #threadfin-shad

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Cooperative Fishery Research Unit report (Research Paper Series, Number 77-2, University of Arizona, Tucson) describes the food habits of [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) in [[Deer Island Lake]], a small, shallow backwater of the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Monthly plankton sampling and night electrofishing from October 1973 to August 1974 showed that shad fed mainly on [[Zooplankton]] when net plankton were abundant, then switched to a [[Benthos]]-based diet during a four-month plankton decline. The authors suggest this [[Flexible Feeding]] behavior may help explain the species' wide geographic distribution and should guide more effective [[Stocking Management]] of shad as forage fish.

## Background & Description

Before this study, published information on the diets of threadfin shad from riverine and backwater environments was lacking. Earlier diet studies came from [[Reservoirs]] (for example, Baker and Schmitz 1971; Haskell 1959; Kimsey et al. 1957; Miller 1967) and from estuaries and saline waters (for example, Darnell 1958; Finucane 1965; Hendricks 1961). These studies generally described shad as primarily plankton feeders that occasionally consumed small amounts of benthos.

The study aimed to describe the diet of shad inhabiting a shallow backwater and to test whether shad forage extensively on benthos when plankton becomes scarce.

**Study area:** Deer Island Lake is a small (40.7 ha), shallow backwater of the Lower Colorado River in Yuma County, Arizona. It lies on the [[Colorado River Indian Reservation]] about 8 km southwest of Parker, Arizona, and nearly 60 km north-northeast of Blythe, California.

**Methods:**
- Plankton was collected monthly by towing a No. 20 mesh [[Clarke-Bumpus Net]] at 1 m depth in the [[Limnetic Zone]]. Net plankton abundance was estimated by volume after settling in graduated centrifuge tubes for at least 15 minutes.
- Threadfin shad were collected by [[Electrofishing]] at night in October 1973 and January, May, and August 1974.
- Foregut contents (esophagus and gizzard) were identified under a dissecting scope and analyzed by frequency of occurrence and damp-dry biomass (after Windell 1971).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study found that the limnetic zone of Deer Island Lake is relatively small and shallow, which limits the potential for developing and maintaining a large plankton crop. In contrast, most reservoirs have a larger and deeper limnetic zone. The authors conclude that shad in reservoirs are unlikely to be forced to feed mainly on benthos because of plankton shortage, while Deer Island Lake's conditions make such shifts more likely.

Earlier work reported that shad in reservoirs feed heavily on both zooplankton and phytoplankton. Applegate and Mullan (1969) found that shad in a reservoir fed on zooplankton until those organisms declined, after which they ate phytoplankton. The authors note it is unclear whether this sequence is the normal order of switching.

Threadfin shad have an important biological and management context. Their ability to feed on benthos may have helped them achieve a wide geographic distribution by allowing them to pass through estuaries and rivers while colonizing freshwater lakes in their native [[Gulf of Mexico Drainage]]. Shad are now established in parts of the Atlantic and Pacific drainages, where they were usually introduced as [[Forage Fish]] often without assessing whether their food supply was adequate.

## Key Findings

**Plankton dynamics (60 plankton samples):**
- The standing crop of net plankton was highest in December and lowest from May through August.
- [[Zooplankton]] (primarily rotifers, cladocerans, and copepods) dominated all samples. [[Phytoplankton]] was present but only in trace amounts.

**Diet by size class and season (100 threadfin shad analyzed, per OCR reading "loo"):**
- **Fry and fingerlings:** Absent from Deer Island Lake in October and January.
  - May fry (2.1 to 4.6 cm total length) mainly consumed phytoplankton and unidentifiable materials, indicating feeding mostly in the limnetic zone.
  - August fingerlings (5.1 to 9.2 cm) frequently ate zooplankton and unidentifiable materials, but sand and dipteran larvae contributed most of the biomass.
- **Large shad (14.6 to 19.5 cm):**
  - Planktonic feeders in October and January, with zooplankton and unidentifiable materials as the main foods.
  - Benthic feeders in May and August, with sand, ostracods, and unidentifiable materials predominating.

**Main conclusions:**
- Shad can subsist almost entirely on benthos for several months. This is the first documented case of this species doing so.
- When both zooplankton and phytoplankton were limited, shad switched to a benthic diet. This demonstrates [[Flexible Feeding]] beyond the zooplankton-to-phytoplankton shift reported earlier.
- The authors suggest that feeding flexibility may partly explain shad's broad distribution, since it allows the fish to move through low-plankton aquatic habitats.

**Management recommendations:**
- Before stocking shad into exotic waters as forage fish, managers should evaluate the types and quantities of food available.
- Assessment is needed to determine whether new areas can support the large populations required to sustain piscivores.
