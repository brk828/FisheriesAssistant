# Key to the Adults and Nymphs of the British Stoneflies (Plecoptera)

#entomology #freshwater-biology #plecoptera

## Executive Summary

This document is a taxonomic key to the adults and nymphs of the British [[Stoneflies]] (order [[Plecoptera]]), written by H. B. N. Hynes and published by the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] as Scientific Publication No. 17 (1958). It is a revised and enlarged successor to the 1940 key. It covers 34 species in seven families, uses morphological characters of the wings, [[Genitalia]] and nymphal mouthparts, bristles and colour, and adds ecological notes and vice-county distribution maps for each species.

## Background & Description

Stoneflies are among the most primitive groups of winged insects. The order was separated from the Neuroptera by later workers, and the adults are closely related to Lower Permian fossils. The keys separate adults of both sexes, and nymphs longer than about 5 mm, to species. Because many small species can be identified only by their genitalia, these structures are illustrated for every species.

Key morphological features include:

- **Wing venation**: Based on the [[Comstock Wing Venation System]], which the author follows for compatibility with major European works.
- **Genitalia**: Eight male genitalia types are recognised in Britain. The female [[Sub-Genital Plate]] has three non-homologous forms.
- **Nymphal characters**: Mouthparts (labrum, lacinia, galea, paraglossa), tarsal segment proportions, the M-line, tentorial callosities, prosternal and pleural gills, and bristle arrangement on femora, terga and cerci.

Nymph identification is less reliable than adult identification. Aberrant specimens occur among small species, so the author recommends basing nymph identification on several specimens from the same locality. Colour patterns can be misleading in young or newly moulted nymphs.

Nomenclature has changed substantially since 1940, mainly through revisions by [[Aubert]], [[Brinck]] and [[Kimmins]]. The document lists the name changes and the species that have been confused in British literature, such as [[Perla Bipunctata]] (formerly called *P. carlukiana*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat classes**: The author recognises five habitat types: (1) still waters with emergent vegetation; (2) stony lake shores; (3) emergent vegetation on river and stream banks; (4) small stony streams; (5) rivers with stony substrata and moss on larger stones.
- **Life cycle**: Most British species take one year from egg to adult. [[Perla Bipunctata]] and [[Dinocras Cephalotes]] take three years. Eggs of several species take many weeks to hatch, leaving periods of three or four months when many species are hard to find.
- **Feeding**: Nymphs of Perlidae, Perlodidae and Chloroperlidae are carnivorous but also eat some plant material. Nymphs of the other families are herbivorous. Adults of Perlodidae, Perlidae and Chloroperlidae do not feed, although they drink. In [[Nemoura Cinerea]], adults scraping lichen and algae from trees and fences were shown to be necessary for survival until egg production.
- **Adult behaviour**: Adults live only a few days and rarely stray far from water. Mating occurs on the ground soon after emergence. Eggs are carried in a sticky mass and deposited on the water surface, and attachment mechanisms anchor them to the bottom.
- **Emergence**: Ripe nymphs (recognisable by black wing pads) crawl out of the water, usually at night, to emerge under stones on the bank.
- **Altitude**: Some normally fully winged species become short-winged at high altitude. For example, [[Leuctra Inermis]], [[Leuctra Nigra]] and [[Nemoura Erratica]] are noted as tending to be short-winged there.
- **Pollution**: The author notes that stoneflies are very sensitive to pollution, and suggests this may explain the apparent extinction of some large-river species.

## Key Findings

- **Scope**: 34 species are keyed. Most are described from British specimens, and the keys cover both sexes of adults and nymphs of at least 5 mm.
- **Revised nomenclature**: Many British names changed after 1940. The author treats several taxa as uncertain: *Perla bipunctata* and *P. carlukiana* may not warrant separate status, and the British *Isoperla grammatica* subspecies claim (*swbarmata*) was judged invalid by Illies (1952).
- **Subspecies and variation**: *Teniopteryx nebulosa* subsp. *britannica* is described as a British subspecies, and *Capnia vidua* subsp. *anglica* differs from the typical form. *Perlodes microcephala* in Britain is wholly short-winged.
- **Restricted species**: *Rhabdiopteryx anglica* appears confined to England, and *Brachyptera putata* appears confined to Britain.
- **Doubtful or possibly extinct species**: Three species are marked with asterisks because their British status is uncertain: *Isogenus nubecula* (possibly extinct after pollution), *Isoperla obscura* (last recorded from the River Trent near Nottingham early in the century, and not found there in 1940), and *Chloroperla apicalis* (known only from three unlabelled female specimens). All three are large-river species on the continent.
- **Distribution**: Distribution maps are provided, based on the [[Vice-County System]]. Only the Lake District, Yorkshire, the Isle of Man and parts of North Wales had been thoroughly studied. The author asks collectors for specimens from unmapped vice-counties.
- **Identification of nymphs**: Several genera can be split by simple nymphal characters, such as tarsal segment proportions and the number of pronotal gills. These include *Protonemura*, *Amphinemura* and *Nemoura*.
- **Preservation method**: Specimens should be killed in 70% alcohol and transferred to 2% formalin. This preserves colour better than alcohol alone and avoids drying and distortion. Specimens should be wetted in alcohol before formalin to prevent floating.
- **Common names**: Anglers' names include "Creeper" (nymphs of Perlidae or large Perlodidae), "Yellow Sally" (*Isoperla grammatica*), "February Red" (female *Teniopteryx nebulosa*), "Early Brown" (early-emerging Nemouridae, including *Protonemura meyeri*), "Willow Fly" (*Leuctra geniculata*), and "Needle Flies" (other *Leuctra*).
