# Spawning and Culture of Colorado Squawfish (Ptychocheilus lucius) in a Raceway Environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980-era U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, authored by Roger L. Hamman at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in Arizona, describes an attempt to spawn and culture the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] ([[Ptychocheilus Lucius]]) in modified trout-rearing raceways. Wild and hatchery-reared ("domestic") broodstock were induced with [[Carp Pituitary Injection]] or allowed to spawn naturally on [[Cobble Substrate Spawning]] beds. Eggs were incubated in trays, and fry were reared in [[Recirculating Raceway]] systems. Juveniles reached 42–50 mm by day 107, and survival to harvest ranged from 76% to 80% across three systems. The author concludes that pituitary injection combined with natural spawning on cobble substrate worked best, and that hatchery propagation could benefit the species' recovery.

*Note: several numbers in the source are garbled by OCR and are flagged below where relevant.*

## Background & Description

**Species profile.** The Colorado Squawfish was historically widespread in the upper and lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. It is the largest native cyprinid in North America, once reaching lengths of over 1 m and weights of about 16 kg. Specimens over roughly 2 kg (OCR-uncertain) had become rare in the decade before the report. By the report's account, the species had virtually disappeared from the lower basin and was probably extinct there.

**Habitat importance.** The report identifies several upper-basin reaches as important habitat: the Green River from the mouth of the Yampa River to the Colorado River; the Yampa River from Craig, Colorado, to its confluence with the Green River; the White River; the Colorado River from Rifle, Colorado, to Palisade; the Gunnison River from Delta, Colorado, to its confluence with the Colorado River; and the Duchesne River from Utah to its confluence with the Green River.

**Conservation status.** The species is listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and by several states, including Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming. The [[Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan]] (approved 1978) was prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] and has three goals:
1. Preserve, protect, and enhance existing populations and habitats.
2. Restore populations to their former range.
3. Acquire propagation and maintenance capability for captive populations.

**Project purpose.** The study aimed to improve old techniques and develop new ones for spawning and culturing this endangered fish. The author notes that only one prior paper (Toney 1974) addressed propagation and rearing of the species in the preceding decade.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Broodstock origin.** Wild adults were collected from the Green River (near Ouray National Wildlife Refuge and Jensen, Utah) and the Yampa River (near Maab, Utah) during fiscal years 1975, 1976, 1978, and 1980. Domestic broodstock had been reared at Willow Beach since July 1974.

**Holding facilities.** Broodstock were held in five concrete raceways (approx. 6.1 × 2.4 × 1.2 m) designated A1–A4 and A9–A10. Raceways A1–A4 and A9–A10 were linked for recirculation, with a 1 hp aerator pumping about 46.8 cfm. Fresh river water was added to maintain temperature and offset evaporation.

**Spawning substrate.** Prior work showed that Colorado Squawfish (Toney 1974) and [[Northern Squawfish]] (Jeppson 1961; Patten and Rodnan 1969) spawn over gravel, rubble, and rock. Raceways A1 and A9 were fitted with a two-layer substrate: boulders (10–30 cm) on the bottom and cobbles (4–10 cm) on top, with water depth of 10–26 cm. Water flowed up through the substrate to create an artificial spawning environment.

**Seasonal and sexual cues.** In late April, when water reached about 15 °C, males developed nuptial tubercles on the head, opercula, and pectoral and pelvic fins. By June, at about 20 °C, tubercles covered most of the male body except the abdomen and caudal fin. Females showed few or no tubercles; they were generally larger than males, and their abdomens softened and distended when gravid.

**Water quality tolerance.** Juveniles withstood conditions that would be stressful for many fishes, including dissolved oxygen below 1.5 mg/L, ammonia above 2.0 mg/L, carbon dioxide above 15.0 mg/L, pH above the reported threshold, overcrowding, and abrupt temperature changes of up to 10 °C within 10 minutes.

## Key Findings

### 1. Broodstock Induction and Spawning
- **Injected domestic females:** Acetone-dried [[Carp Pituitary Injection]] was given at 4.0 mg/kg (some fish at 8.0 mg/kg) with a 0.1 mL-per-injection dose protocol. Anesthesia used [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222). Injections were intraperitoneal.
- **Wild females:** Three wild females received no hormone injections; the source's abstract gives wild-female numbers that are partly garbled by OCR.
- **Domestic spawning:** Ten domestic females produced eggs, with approximately 78,000 eggs (OCR-uncertain) averaging about 2.0–2.5 mm in diameter. Domestic fish spawned at water temperatures of 18–20 °C.
- **Wild spawning:** Wild squawfish spawned at 20–24 °C. The author observed the first complete spawning act at 18:00 on June 24, with a mating behavior described as a [[Spawning Behavior]] involving two to three males pursuing each female to the spawning site. Spawning activity was observed over a 48-hour period.
- **Hormone response:** Some females ovulated after only one carp pituitary injection, and most others needed a second dose.

### 2. Egg Collection and Fertilization
- **Hand stripping:** Eggs were hand-stripped from anesthetized females and mixed with milt for about 30 seconds. Adding water activated sperm, and the eggs were then stirred and washed for 45 minutes to prevent clumping.
- **Fertility:** The first domestic spawn showed low [[Fertilization Rate]] (less than 0.03%), which the author attributed to first-time spawners or egg-collection technique. A separate observation reported about 80% fertility (OCR reads "o8O%") for hand-stripped, artificially fertilized eggs, and about 99% for naturally spawned eggs (both values should be verified against the original).
- **Incubation trays:** Water-hardened eggs were placed on screen trays set at a 10° slant in aluminum troughs with a constant flow of about 8 L/min.

### 3. Incubation and Hatching
- **Warm water (20–21 °C):** Hatching began at about 95 hours, peaked near 100–120 hours, and was complete by about 110–120 hours.
- **Cold water (about 12–14 °C, OCR-uncertain):** Hatching took 145–180 hours.
- **Hatchling size:** Newly hatched fry measured about 5.0–7.0 mm in length.
- **Natural vs. artificial:** Naturally spawned eggs yielded a higher hatch rate than hand-stripped eggs.
- **Fry totals:** Approximately 2,000 fry hatched from the first domestic batch. Larger natural-spawn lots yielded roughly 22,000 to 27,000 fry each (figures OCR-uncertain).

### 4. Larval and Juvenile Rearing
- **Rearing system:** Three recirculating systems (six raceways) were used, each aerated by a 0.5 hp pump. Fry were stocked at about 19,000 (system 1), 14,000 (system 2), and 10,000 (system 3).
- **Feeding:** Raceways were pre-fertilized to build a zooplankton bloom, with cladocerans appearing about 10 days after fertilization. Commercial fry starter (50% protein) was added on day 28, and fry were fed three to four times daily.
- **Growth:** Fry doubled in length during the first 21 days and reached 42–50 mm by day 107 (see Growth Rate Table below).
- **Cannibalism:** Cannibalism occurred around day 61 due to overcrowding, but caused no unusual mortality.
- **Oxygen crash:** On day 91, an oxygen depletion in one system caused about 50 deaths, which was corrected by immediate flushing.

- Juveniles were transferred to other facilities for culture and experimental [[Bioassay]] studies.

### 6. Sexual Maturity Observations
- Previous studies reported that Colorado Squawfish matured at age VII or older (Vanicek and Kramer 1969), and that maturity required a total length over 400 mm and an age of VI years (Seethaler 1978).
- Hatchery-reared domestic fish reached maturity at **age V (males)** and **age VI (females)** (OCR-uncertain for the females), about one year earlier than previously reported. The author calls this significant because propagation could begin earlier.

### 7. Comparison of Spawning Techniques
The author compared four methods:
1. Carp pituitary injection with hand stripping and artificial fertilization.
2. Carp pituitary injection with natural spawning on gravel.
3. Natural spawning on cobble substrate without injection.
4. Carp pituitary injection with natural spawning on cobble substrate.

**Method 4 proved superior.** Progeny survival was excellent in the modified raceways.

### 8. Conclusions and Recommendations
- Results were "very encouraging," but more time and effort are needed.
- Raceway culture may not be ideal for rearing fry, but it is considered feasible until better culture techniques are developed.
- Hatchery propagation and culture would benefit the species' survival if the Colorado Squawfish is to recover.
- Notable early-maturity and hatchery-survival findings support its use in restoration.
