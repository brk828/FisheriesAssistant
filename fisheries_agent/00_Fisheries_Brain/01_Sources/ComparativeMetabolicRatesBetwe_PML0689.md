## Executive Summary

This is a scanned front matter and abstract collection from *[[American Zoologist]]* (the journal's volume 33, number 5 issue, labelled "A Journal of Integrative and Comparative Biology"). It presents short abstracts from comparative physiology, biochemistry and ecology. Most studies concern invertebrates (insects, crustaceans-free arthropods, echinoderms), with one neurotoxicology study in mice and one fish study comparing two [[Cyprinidae]] species. ## Background & Description

The collection contains seven abstracts:

1. **Hydroperoxide model for brain oxidative damage** (Department of Pharmacy, University of Southern California, Los Angeles; authors partly illegible). Uses *tert*-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BuOOH), a lipophilic hydroperoxide that penetrates brain membranes, administered intracerebroventricularly in mice.
2. **Acid-base balance in [[Alkali Fly]] larvae** (*Ephydra hians*; T.J. Bradley, University of California, Irvine). Examines pH and ion regulation in larvae of a [[Mono Lake]] species.
3. **Short-term hemolymph changes in larval [[Yellow Fever Mosquito]]** (*Aedes aegypti*; T.M. Clark and T.J. Bradley, UC Irvine). Tests how feeding and salinity alter hemolymph effects on [[Malpighian Tubules]] fluid secretion.
4. **Hemoglobins of the brittle star** (*Hemipholis elongata*; A.M. Beardsley, J.M. Cohen, R. Cashon and C.J. Bonaventura, Clemson University and Duke University Marine Lab, Beaufort, NC). Characterizes blood-cell hemoglobin and ligand binding.
5. **Hemolymph acid-base status and ventilation in grasshoppers** (*Romalea guttata* and *Melanoplus differentialis*; K.A. Krolikowski, S.L. Gulinson and J.F. Harrison, Arizona State University, Tempe).
6. **Comparative metabolic rates of two cyprinid fishes** (*Gila elegans* and *Pimephales promelas*; University of California, Los Angeles; author partly illegible).
7. **Hemoglobin of the brittle star** (continuation of item 4, partly illegible).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mono Lake** is extremely alkaline (pH about 10) and saline (about 2250 mOsm/l, as printed). The Alkali Fly larvae there must drink concentrated water, absorb electrolytes and excrete dilute urine. The external-to-internal pH gap exceeds 3 units, yet the larvae regulate hemolymph pH.
- **Brittle stars** normally sit buried in anoxic sediment with two arms extended into the water column. Their blood-cell hemoglobin probably circulates through the water vascular system to the extended arms, providing an oxygen-transport role for the buried body.
- **Fishes:** the bonytail chub is endangered and extinct in California, whereas the introduced fathead minnow may be displacing native California fishes.
- **Grasshoppers** were studied under differing ambient CO₂ (hypercapnia), linking blood chemistry to breathing rate.

## Key Findings

**Brain oxidative damage model (mice)**
- t-BuOOH penetrates brain membranes and produces massive oxidative damage when given intracerebroventricularly.
- Histochemical staining showed specific damage to cholinergic, GABAergic and catecholaminergic neurons in the striatum and pons.
- Neurotransmitter assays showed damage to serotonergic and adrenergic neurons in the hypothalamus and thalamus.
- The authors propose t-BuOOH as a model for central nervous system diseases such as Parkinson's disease and stroke.

**Acid-base regulation in Alkali Fly larvae (*Ephydra hians*)**
- Larvae maintain hemolymph pH near 7 despite a highly alkaline external environment (pH about 10).
- The midgut has a lower pH than the external water. The hindgut, particularly the colon, holds a pH similar to external water.
- The colon is the main site of excretion and an important organ for acid-base regulation.
- The larvae are euryhaline osmoregulators, drinking hypersaline water and excreting concentrated urine.

**Hemolymph effects on Malpighian tubule secretion (*Aedes aegypti* larvae)**
- Hemolymph from feeding larvae stimulated faster fluid secretion than hemolymph from food-deprived larvae.
- Hemolymph from larvae reared at higher salinities also stimulated faster secretion.
- Feeding effects began within about 15 to 30 minutes (OCR partly unclear) and peaked within one hour after salinity change.
- Salinity effects were rapidly reversible after return to fresh water.
- The authors suggest an endocrine (hormonal) mechanism, and tested a peptide and 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) as candidate stimulatory factors.

**Brittle star hemoglobins (*Hemipholis elongata*)**
- Blood-cell hemoglobin P50 (oxygen pressure at half saturation) was reported as about 11 mmHg, with SD given in the source (n = 10; OCR partly unclear).
- Hemolysate P50 was about 11 mmHg (n = 2).
- At lower pH the hemolysate P50 rose to 8.8 mmHg (n = 2), indicating a possible Bohr shift.
- Five distinct bands appeared on native gel electrophoresis, with three major components resolved by DEAE Sepharose FPLC.
- Stopped-flow kinetics showed biphasic O₂ and CO binding. O₂-off rate constants were about 134.6 s⁻¹ and about 22 s⁻¹ without ATP, and about 144.3 s⁻¹ and 24.2 s⁻¹ with ATP. CO-on constants were about 5.2 × 10⁸ M⁻¹s⁻¹ and 2.9 × 10⁸ M⁻¹s⁻¹ (OCR-affected values; verify against the original).

**Hemolymph acid-base status and ventilation (grasshoppers)**
- In *Romalea guttata*, NaHCO₃ injection increased ventilation rate (VR). HCl, NaOH and NaCl injections had no effect.
- No consistent relationship was found between hemolymph pH, PCO₂ or HCO₃⁻ and VR.
- In *Melanoplus differentialis*, the post-hopping increase in VR was unaffected by hemolymph acid-base manipulation.
- The authors conclude that hemolymph acid-base status does not directly affect ventilation. Correlations during hypercapnia may reflect effects of tracheal PCO₂.

**Comparative metabolic rates of cyprinid fishes**
- Oxygen consumption was measured at 15, 20 and 25 °C in Blazka hyperespirometers. Experiments ran from August 1990 to November 1991.
- Bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*) mass-specific metabolic rates (MR) were 0.15 (15 °C), 0.35 (20 °C) and 0.26 (25 °C), in mL O₂/kg/h. These differed significantly across temperatures.
- Fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*) MR was 0.19 (15 °C), 0.08 (20 °C) and 0.06 (25 °C). MR at 20 and 25 °C did not differ.
- Standard metabolic rates (SMR) of bonytail chub were 1.5 at 20 and 25 °C, versus 2.3 for fathead minnow. At 15 °C, SMRs were similar (1.7 and 1.6).
- Bonytail chub were obtained from Dexter National Fish Hatchery, NM. Fathead minnows were collected in Ventura County, CA.
