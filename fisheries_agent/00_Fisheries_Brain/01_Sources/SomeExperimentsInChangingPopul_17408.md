# Changing Population Balance in Iowa Farm Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper by [[Kenneth D Carlander]] and [[Robert B Moorman]] (Iowa State College) evaluates management attempts to correct unbalanced fish populations in farm ponds of Marion County, Iowa. The work was part of Project 37 of the [[Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]]. The authors compare a rapid [[Minnow Seine]] method with a more detailed [[Population Analysis]] method, and assess three interventions: [[Supplemental Stocking]], [[Seine Thinning]] of an overabundant crappie population, and [[Rotenone Treatment]] followed by restocking. Most supplemental stockings failed, the crappie thinning showed measurable effects on year-class growth, and rotenone with restocking succeeded in five of eight ponds.

## Background & Description

The study focuses on [[Farm Ponds]] in Iowa, where livestock have priority for water use. Because of this, draining and restocking ponds with poor fish populations is generally not feasible, which makes in-pond management the main option. The target "balanced" condition is one in which [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] populations are adequate and able to produce an annual harvestable crop of fish.

Other fish mentioned include [[White Crappie]] and [[Bullhead Catfish]]. Bullheads were noted to escape small seines easily and to survive rotenone treatment in some ponds.

The paper was published in April 1957 in *The Progressive Fish-Culturist*. The text also cites a doctoral thesis by Moorman (1953) on fish populations in Iowa farm ponds in relation to past history and management.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several environmental and biological factors influenced pond outcomes:

- **Winter conditions:** Ponds that were low during winter or had a history of [[Winterkill]] were poor candidates for restocking. Winterkill also caused failures after restocking and invalidated some comparisons between survey years.
- **Water clarity and growth:** In some ponds, suspended clay or other factors slowed bass growth. In these ponds, bass did not reproduce in the second spring after stocking but did so the following year.
- **Reproduction timing:** Bluegill sometimes hatched late in the season and were detected only in August inspections, after earlier surveys had missed them.
- **Species introductions:** Farmers sometimes stocked fish from creeks or other sources, which could confuse the species picture a minnow seine gave.

## Key Findings

### 1. Comparison of Assessment Methods

The [[Minnow Seine]] technique described by [[Swingle]] (1945) was used for preliminary inspections of 60 ponds. It classified 22 ponds as "in balance." Later assessment using a large seine and [[Age Analysis]] of scales identified 27 ponds with adequate bass and bluegill populations.

Table 1 shows the two methods agreed on 47 ponds: 18 balanced by both and 29 unbalanced by both. The 13 disagreements break down as:

- **4 ponds** (Nos. 9, 27, 30, 50) balanced by the minnow seine but unbalanced on later study. Winterkill intervened in each case.
- **9 ponds** (Nos. 12, 14, 18, 25, 33, 42, 43, 51, 60) unbalanced by the minnow seine but balanced on later study.

The detailed method revealed problems the minnow seine missed:

- In 4 ponds (Nos. 12, 14, 25, 51), young-of-year bass were not caught by minnow seine but were found later that year.
- In 4 ponds (Nos. 18, 33, 42, 60), bass did not reproduce in the inspection year but did in later years.
- In Pond No. 43, a heavy population of bullheads and slow-growing bass was missed but later reached balance.

The authors conclude that the minnow seine gave a "fairly reliable representation" of population balance in most ponds, but it should be interpreted with caution.

### 2. Supplemental Stocking Largely Failed

Technicians of the Marion County Soil Conservation District had earlier restocked some ponds. The present study made further attempts from 1946 to 1951, using fingerling bass and bluegill from nearby high-reproduction ponds, often at higher rates than original stockings. In some cases yearling bass or adult bluegill were used.

Table 2 lists 9 restocking events. Most failed. Successes were limited to Nos. 14, 33, 42, 59, 30, 49, and 51 year-restocks. Pond No. 16 was restocked repeatedly (1946, 1948, 1949, 1950, and 1951) and only became balanced after the 1951 stocking, as assessed in 1953. The authors attribute most failures to the same causes that affected original stockings, and advise against restocking ponds with low winter water levels or winterkill histories.

### 3. Seine Thinning Changed Year-Class Growth

Pond No. 6 was inspected in early summer 1950. It had large numbers of adult white crappies, very large numbers of young-of-year crappies, good bass reproduction, and some bullhead reproduction. Concerned that crappies would dominate, the authors removed as many as possible with a 60-foot seine.

- An estimated 124 large crappies were present; 105 were removed.
- Large numbers of young-of-year crappies were also removed.
- Scales from these fish and from a sample of 45 crappies taken the following year showed the prominent 1950 year class grew from a mean length of 7.5 inches to 10.8 inches during the year.
- The 1950 class had reached 6.7 inches in the same period.

The paper reports these growth figures but does not give a full evaluation of the thinning's long-term effect on the population.

### 4. Rotenone and Restocking Had the Highest Success Rate

Eight ponds with undesirable populations were treated with [[Rotenone]]:

- **5 ponds** were successfully restocked and developed balanced bass-bluegill populations. In two of these, a few bullheads survived treatment but did not interfere with restocking.
- **2 ponds** failed because of winterkill in the first winter after restocking.
- **1 pond** failed for an undetermined cause; bullheads had survived the treatment there.

### 5. Future Monitoring Technology

The document closes with a note on a planned [[Electronic Fish Counter]] for fishways. Fish pass through two plastic tunnels in the uppermost pool of a fishway, where low-voltage electrical fields are created. Because fish flesh conducts electricity better than water, a fish's passage increases current flow. This trips electronic relays, and a logic device records each passage as upstream or downstream based on the relay sequence. This is a proposed method and is not tested in the study.
