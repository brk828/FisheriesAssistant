# Dexter National Fish Hatchery Annual Report, Fiscal Year 1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Cyprinodontidae #Poeciliidae #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This annual report from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (Dexter NFH), a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service facility in southeastern New Mexico, covers fiscal year 1990. The station's mission centers on refuge, study and propagation of imperiled warmwater fishes of the Southwest, particularly those listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. In FY 1990 the hatchery held 14 species, produced and distributed thousands of eggs, fry and fingerlings of several listed fishes, and carried out a [[Yaqui Fish Collection Expedition]] into Mexico. The report also notes persistent problems with [[Chara]] in ponds, shortfalls in overwinter survival, a 275-acre wildfire, and serious water-rights concerns for future expansion.

## Background & Description

Dexter NFH lies in the Pecos River valley, about 20 miles southeast of Roswell and 180 miles southeast of Albuquerque. The station was authorized under the [[White Act of 1924]] to supply warmwater game fish across the Southwest. It covers 640 acres, was acquired by the federal government in 1936 (purchase price garbled in the scan), and began operations in 1938 (OCR-uncertain). The hatchery's mission expanded after the Endangered Species Act of 1973 and successful trials with endangered fishes in 1974 and 1976, and it became fully engaged in endangered-species work in 1978.

Seven station objectives were defined: (1) maintain a viable, protected gene pool of imperiled southwestern fishes; (2) develop culture techniques for them; (3) study their biological requirements; (4) provide live and preserved specimens to agencies and institutions; (5) exchange expertise and data; (6) publish findings; and (7) run a public information program.

Facilities include a holding house with sixteen 360-gallon concrete tanks, a hatching battery, a laboratory, four 6-foot raceways, ten 3-foot and 4 to 10-foot fiberglass circular tanks, and earthen ponds totaling roughly 11 surface acres (individual ponds 0.9 to 1.8 acres). Wastewater collects in two sumps isolated from other surface waters. Fish culture operations share the property with a wildlife management area co-managed with the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]].

The station is in the northern fringes of the Chihuahuan Desert at about 3,350 feet elevation, with average rainfall near 13 inches and a 180 to 200-day growing season. Water comes from three shallow aquifer wells (about 150 feet deep) pumping roughly 1,800 gpm, at a constant 64°F, pH 7.5, total hardness near 200 mg/l, and dissolved solids near 1,000 mg/l.

Staffing in FY 1990 comprised eight full-time employees, a Co-op student and a volunteer. Key personnel included Project Leader Buddy L. Jensen (GS-13/14 level noted in the table), Assistant Manager Roger L. Hamman, Fishery Biologist Donald E. Hales, and Fishery Biologist Troy D. Winham.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The station holds fishes from several families, most of them federally listed. [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Gila Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Woundfin]], [[Yaqui Beautiful Shiner]], [[Yaqui Catfish]], [[Leon Springs Pupfish]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Big Bend Gambusia]] are among the species mentioned. Table 1 lists the full set of species held, with Federal status given as Endangered, Threatened or Proposed.

Recurring biological threats include [[Chara]] (a macroalga) that smothers pond productivity and complicates harvest, [[Asian Tapeworm]] infection in [[Gila Robusta]] and [[Woundfin]] broodstock, encysted yellow grub parasites in Yaqui catfish, gull predation on overwintering razorback suckers, and a [[Wound-Fin Parasitic Disease]] outbreak ("ich") that killed broodstock. The report also notes that the intermediate snail host for the tapeworm is absent on station, limiting that parasite's life cycle there.

Water rights are a central environmental concern. The station holds shallow and artesian groundwater rights, with a shallow right of 1,723 acre-feet per year and an artesian right of 318 acre-feet per year. Lawsuits by the State of Texas and the Mescalero Apache Tribe may threaten rights with priority dates after the mid-1940s.

## Key Findings

**Razorback sucker**
- 17 female razorbacks (average 5.8 lb) were spawned on February 18, 1990, yielding 1,601,617 eggs, a mean of 94,214 eggs per female.
- Swimup fry totaled 187,177, a 11.7% survival from egg to swimup, down from 46.7% in FY 1989.
- 3,492 fingerling and subadult razorbacks weighing 333 pounds were distributed.
- 536 domestic and 7 wild brood fish are maintained and PIT-tagged.

**Colorado squawfish**
- 35 females (average 5.8 lb) were spawned on May 30, 1990, using carp pituitary; all spawned successfully.
- Total eggs were 3,589,000 (about 117,564 per female), and swimup fry totaled 419,000, a 11.7% egg-to-swimup survival, down 6.7% from FY 1989.
- Survival in rearing ponds was extremely poor, with 2,383 fingerlings harvested from three ponds.
- 1,156 fingerlings (386 lb) were distributed; 155 '74-year-class and 275 '81-year-class brood fish are maintained.

**Bonytail chub**
- Spawned only to supply eggs for research; 1,543 domestic and 8 wild brood fish are held.
- Stocking totaled 14,894 fish across several sites.
- Wound-fin culture plans were scrapped when the fish were lost to Ich.

**Other species**
- Chihuahua, Fahrenagat roundtail and Virgin River roundtail chubs were held or shipped to partner institutions; Virgin River progeny were destroyed after genetic analysis showed impure stock.
- Yaqui beautiful shiners and Yaqui catfish were collected in Mexico in October 1989 and April 1990; 191 catfish from the Rio Sirupa collection were added to the 56 surviving fish from the 1987 collection, and 331 Yaqui catfish were held at year end.
- Desert and Leon Springs pupfish populations were healthy, with 400 desert pupfish shipped to Arizona.
- Gila topminnow overwintered in ponds and aquaria, and Big Bend gambusia survival was poor outdoors.

**Operations and partnerships**
- Collaboration included the [[Klamath Tribe]], Spectrum Sciences, Genetic Analysis (Smithville, TX), Arizona State University, Eastern New Mexico University and the Upper Colorado River Basin working group.
- A Region 6 funded tagging study on PIT, Carlin, VI tags and acrylic dyes began in October 1989.
- Sucker work with the Klamath Tribe and Lost River/shortnose suckers produced successful spawns, but most fry from all combinations died from unknown causes.
- A January 15 escaped controlled burn consumed about 275 acres in the wildlife management area.
- About 1,500 visitors were recorded, including 15 guided tours.

**Future outlook and recommendations**
- Water rights may be insufficient for expansion; lining ponds or purchasing more water is needed.
- A "Genetic Plan" for all hatchery operations was proposed, drawing on Dr. Tony Echelle's review of genetic diversity in Region 2 fishes.
- Establishing a genetics technology center was recommended, possibly filling the position of Dr. Hales upon his fall retirement.
