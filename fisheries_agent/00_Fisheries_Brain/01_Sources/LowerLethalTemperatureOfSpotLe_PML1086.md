# Lower Lethal Temperature of Spot Larvae (Leiostomus xanthurus): Manuscript Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a peer-review packet for a short manuscript by John H. Hook (Pan American University, Biology Department, Edinburg, Texas) on the [[Lower Lethal Temperature]] of larval [[Spot]] (*[[Leiostomus Xanthurus]]*, Sciaenidae) from the [[Laguna Madre]], Texas. The study exposed captive larvae to rapid cooling and found 100% mortality at 6 °C, with the first death at 8 °C. The reviewer, Paul C. Marsh (18 November 1985), recommended against publication. The main criticism was that the research had little substance and that the lethal-temperature determination needed more work and better data acquisition. The journal was the *Texas Journal of Science*, with manuscript number TJS 85-10-16 and editor J. Knox Jones, Jr.

## Background & Description

*Leiostomus xanthurus* is a common sciaenid of Texas bays. It is not a commercial or sport target species, but it is an important prey species for fisheries. Its larvae enter Texas bays during a protracted recruitment period from December through April (Pearson 1929). Larvae use near-shore, shallow waters, where temperatures drop faster and to lower levels than in deeper bay waters, which raises their exposure to lethal cold.

The manuscript notes that no direct experimental value for the lower lethal temperature of spot had been published. The earlier estimate came from Dawson (1958), who inferred it from fish kills in North Carolina ponds. That value is partly illegible in the scanned copy. The author's stated purpose was to supply an experimentally derived value so that field temperature ranges could be compared with it and damage to larval stocks could be estimated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Cold fronts:** Air temperature in southern Texas can fall from 30 °C to −2 °C within four or five hours when a cold front or "norther" passes. Such events are not annual but are not rare.
- **Historical freezes:** Major cold events occurred in 1940, 1951 and 1983. Gunter (1941) and Gunter and Hildebrand (1951) documented large fish kills after the 1940 and 1951 freezes. Many affected fish were residents of higher latitudes, which normally experience colder temperatures.
- **Management response:** After the 1983 freeze, the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department reduced bag limits by 50% in 1984 for *[[Cynoscion Nebulosus]]* (Spotted Seatrout) and *[[Sciaenops Ocellatus]]* (Red Drum).
- **Recruitment timing:** Field data from January 1985 showed a water temperature drop from 23 °C to 4 °C over three days, but the author suspects most of the decline occurred within a day or less. Spot larval abundance peaks late in the recruitment period, when cold fronts are less frequent and weaker.
- **Latitudinal gradient:** The author suggests spot may face stressful lows in the southern part of their range but not the northern part, because of large winter air-temperature fluctuations in southern Texas.

## Key Findings

**Study design (as reported)**
- **Collection:** Larvae were collected on 9 March 1985 from the lower [[Laguna Madre]], north of the Queen Isabella Causeway landing on South Padre Island, Cameron County, Texas. Water temperature was 22 °C and salinity was 33 ppt.
- **Transport and acclimation:** Larvae were moved to the Pan American University Coastal Studies Laboratory, about 2 km away, and acclimated at 20 °C for two weeks in a 200 L aquarium. Salinity stayed close to capture conditions, and the larvae were fed commercially prepared dry food.
- **Experiment:** Ten larvae were placed in each of three cold-exposed replicates (5 L aquaria). A fourth aquarium held at 20 °C served as the control. Temperatures were monitored with a thermometer.
- **Endpoint:** A larva was considered dead when no body movement occurred. The "lower lethal temperature" was taken as the temperature at which 50% (or 100%) mortality occurred.

**Results (Table 1)**
- The control had 0% mortality.
- Temperature fell 12 °C with no mortality for the first hours of the trial.
- In the tenth hour, one fish in Replicate 1 died at 8 °C.
- All remaining cold-exposed fish died within the next hour at 6 °C. Replicates 1, 2 and 3 each reached 100% mortality at 6 °C.
- A one-way ANOVA found no significant difference among cold-exposed replicates (reported as F = 3.35, P > 0.05; the degrees of freedom are partly illegible in the scan).

**Author's conclusions**
- Larval spot can be killed when water cools quickly, and the lower lethal temperature found here is about 6 °C.
- Late-season spawning may match the reduced frequency and severity of cold fronts, which could explain the late peak in larval abundance.
- Because March-collected larvae face little thermal stress, the lethal temperature found in March may not apply to the whole recruitment period, unless no acclimatization or selection occurs during recruitment.
- The author suggests that spot may experience lethal cold in the southern part of their range but not the northern part.

**Reviewer's assessment (Paul C. Marsh, 18 November 1985)**
- Recommendation: publication **not recommended**.
- Main criticisms: the paper has "little substance," and determining lethal temperatures "requires a bit more work" than presented. The reviewer encouraged the author to refine data acquisition and continue the inquiry.
- The reviewer also noted that acceptance decisions can depend on factors other than review comments, such as financial considerations.
