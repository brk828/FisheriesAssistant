# A Legacy of Change: The Lower Colorado River, Arizona-California-Nevada, USA, and Sonora-Baja California Norte, Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This American Fisheries Society symposium paper (Mueller, Marsh and Minckley, 2005) reviews how the [[Lower Colorado River]] went from a river with a unique native fish fauna to one with an almost entirely introduced fish community. The basin's nine native freshwater fishes were all endemic. Five are now extirpated from the main stem, seven are federally endangered, and three persist mainly through stocking. The authors attribute the decline to predation by nonnative fishes and physical habitat degradation driven by [[Dam Construction and Water Regulation]], diversion, and channelization.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The Colorado River is the fifth-largest river by volume in the conterminous United States. Its flow is fully allocated and no longer reaches the sea. Lower basin reservoirs flood nearly one-third of the channel and store two years of annual flow.
- **Human use:** Diverted water irrigates 1.5 million ha of cropland and supplies more than 22 million people in the southwestern United States and northern Mexico.
- **Historical habitat:** The delta once held nearly 1 million ha of riparian marshes, sloughs, old channels, oxbows and shallow playas, with a delta corridor about 50 km wide. Temporary flood lakes supported freshwater and marine fishes.
- **Natural regime:** Flows were dominated by spring snowmelt floods peaking in late May to early July, with summer monsoon flash floods and low flows in midwinter. Discharge at Yuma averaged more than 2,000 cubic metres per second (CMS) over 1904–1935 and dropped to 15 CMS in August 1934. A flood in the early 1870s reached nearly 11,300 CMS.
- **Native fish assemblage:** The main-stem fauna comprised nine freshwater species representing four families (four cyprinids, two catostomids, one cyprinodontid, two poeciliids). Notable taxa include [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (reaching about 2 m), [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Woundfin]], [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Sonoran Topminnow]]. Striped mullet and a marine elopid also entered the lower river.
- **Estuary:** The estuary held more than 75 marine species. The authors list 50 families and 125 total species (87 native and 38 nonnative) for the lower river, delta and Sea of Cortez (Minckley 2002).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Life history:** The long-lived main-stem natives (longevity exceeding 30 years) could tolerate high temperatures and spawn in flowing or standing water. Recruitment was infrequent, and annual recruitment could be below 10% of that of short-lived species.
- **Flood ecology:** Flooding appears necessary for successful spawning and nursery development, and the river's floodplain and delta provided refuges. Drought reduced predator numbers, which helped natives recover.
- **Physical barriers:** Laguna Dam (1909) first blocked upstream migration. Hoover Dam (1935) and later structures, including Imperial, Parker, Headgate Rock, Davis and Palo Verde dams, further fragmented the river. The longest unobstructed reach was reduced to 140 km.
- **Alien-dominated systems:** Reservoir storage reduced flood frequency and created lentic and cold tailwater habitats, which favored [[Nonnative Fishes]] over natives. The authors note that the Colorado basin provides the most complete record of alien fish problems of any large river.
- **Channelization:** Dredging and riprap levees from 1947 separated the river from its floodplain and produced deep, permanent habitat favoring aliens. Mohave Valley had 1,740 ha of off-channel wetlands before dredging.
- **Recreational fisheries:** Largemouth bass, bluegill, crappie, rainbow trout and brown trout were stocked from the 1930s onward. Recreational species are mostly piscivorous.

## Key Findings

- **Native decline:** Native assemblages declined sharply after about 1900, were rare in the lower reaches by 1930, and last recruitment for main-stem species was reported in 1950.
- **Current status:** Seven of nine natives are federally endangered, and the other two are under reconsideration. Six species (desert pupfish, Sonoran topminnow, woundfin, Colorado pikeminnow, roundtail chub and humpback chub) are gone from the lower river. Humpback chub persists precariously in Grand Canyon.
- **Introduced fauna:** Nearly 80 alien species from 17 families have been introduced, more than 22 are relatively common, and aliens make up 99.9% of the lower main-stem fish assemblage (Minckley 1979).
- **Mortality factors:** Documented causes include pollution (a 1894 fish kill and a 1939 kill during Lake Havasu filling), commercial harvest (until 1911 for some species), stranding from agricultural diversions, and migration barriers.
- **Economic contrast:** Angler use at Lakes Mead and Mohave exceeds half a million angler-days per year with a harvest above 1.5 million fish. Anglers contribute US$15 to $56 million to the local economy. Reported 2000 habitat work on Lake Havasu is credited with an annual increase of $18 million in angling revenue. The authors state that recreational fishing generates more interest and revenue than native species.
- **Recovery programs:** The Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Implementation Program has run more than 15 years and cost millions of dollars, with mixed biological results. The Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program (formed 1995) had no finalized plan after seven years. Refuge ponds (2–50 ha) are being developed for bonytail and razorback sucker.
- **Reintroduction:** A flannelmouth sucker reintroduction from 1976 onward produced a population of 3,500–6,800 fish, described as the only successful reintroduction of a fish into the Colorado River main stem. Larger bonytail and razorback suckers are being stocked in Lake Havasu and Lake Mohave, though preliminary razorback survival may be below 10%.
- **Central conclusion:** The authors argue that recovery is unlikely while habitats are dominated by alien fishes, that predation has not been resolved, and that refuge populations may be the only viable option for larger species.
