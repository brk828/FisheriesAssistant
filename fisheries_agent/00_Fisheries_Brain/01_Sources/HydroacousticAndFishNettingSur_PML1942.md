# Hydroacoustic and Netting Surveys of Buffalo Bill Reservoir, Wyoming (1992)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation applied sciences memorandum (Referral Memorandum No. 93-2-6) reports a 1992 comparison of [[Hydroacoustics]] and active and passive netting for monitoring fish in [[Buffalo Bill Reservoir]], impounded by [[Buffalo Bill Dam]] on the [[Shoshone River]]. Surveys were run in May and August 1992. The authors conclude that netting gives species composition and condition data but covers small areas, while [[Echosounding]] adds reservoir-wide distribution and density estimates with little extra effort. The study also links the late-summer concentration of [[Lake Trout]] in cold deep water to their vulnerability to entrainment in dam releases. The memo was prepared for the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] Denver Office, which collaborated with the [[Wyoming Game And Fish Department]] (WGFD) and with Israeli researchers on monitoring techniques.

## Background & Description

- **Dam and reservoir:** Buffalo Bill Dam, about 9 miles upstream of Cody, was completed in 1910 and was described as the world's tallest concrete dam at the time. The reservoir's storage capacity is about 5.24 × 10^8 m³ (425,000 acre-feet). The irrigation acreage figure is illegible in the scan.
- **Recreation and fishery:** The area supports camping, picnicking and boating. Rainbow trout, cutthroat trout and lake trout are the predominant game species.
- **Purpose of study:** The surveys were conducted as part of a technical exchange with Israeli researchers on monitoring methods, using a [[HADAS System]] (HydroAcoustical Data Analyses System) developed by Dr. Torfin Ludemann.
- **Equipment:** A single-beam Simrad EY-2000 scientific echosounder at 200 kHz, with a 7° transducer, recorded echograms and audio data. Time-variable gain corrected for sound absorption.
- **Sampling methods:**
  - Limnology: water quality profiles at Station D, upstream of the dam, and a station C.
  - Zooplankton: 5-m vertical tows, sorted into [[Cladocerans]], [[Copepods]] and [[Rotifers]].
  - Hydroacoustic transects: four transects (intake, midchannel or thalweg of the North Fork Arm, and two diagonal transects, B and C), surveyed by night in May and August 1992.
  - Vertical gill nets: six panels, each 2.4 m wide, set about 300 m upstream of the dam.
  - Sinking gill nets: set on steep canyon walls and on gradual banks.
  - Trap nets: set along the shore.
  - Purse seines: 9.1-m (30 ft) deep, 1/4-inch mesh.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water quality:** Most parameters were within general norms. Station D, near the dam, showed poor conditions. Dissolved oxygen dropped to 1.5 mg/L near the bottom, and pH fell to 5.9, the lowest reading recorded. These conditions were attributed to [[Geothermal Springs]] entering the reservoir near its bottom just upstream of Station D. Thermal springs are also active in the Shoshone River below the dam.
- **Temperature and stratification:** Bottom-to-surface temperatures ranged from 7.9 to 11.2 °C in May (average 9.4 °C) and from 13.7 to 17.4 °C in August (average 15.6 °C). The reservoir was slightly stratified, with a [[Thermocline]] at about 20 m in both months. Dissolved oxygen was depleted in the [[Hypolimnion]] during summer.
- **Zooplankton:** Cladocerans dominated in May and copepods in August, with rotifers a minor component. The memo reports that abundance in August was slightly higher at Station A.
- **Fish community:** The reservoir supports rainbow, brown, cutthroat and lake trout, along with native suckers, white suckers, longnose suckers, yellow perch, common carp and mountain whitefish.

## Key Findings

**Netting results**

- **Vertical gill netting, May:** 11 fish captured, 9 (82 percent) in the top 3 m. Three trout species were represented: rainbow, brown and cutthroat.
- **Vertical gill netting, August:** 30 fish from four trout species. Rainbow trout dominated near the surface, but were found at all depths. Lake trout, absent in May, made up 33 percent of the catch and occurred below the thermocline at depths greater than 20 m.
- **Sinking gill nets, May 11–15 (4 nets, 71.0 hrs):** 88 fish from 8 species. [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] dominated (41 percent), followed by [[White Sucker]] (19 percent). The top 7 m held 56 percent of fish, and fish were caught down to 30 m.
- **Trap nets, May:** 457 fish, 82 percent white suckers.
- **Sinking gill nets, August 24–29 (4 nets, 69.5 hrs):** 87 fish from 8 species. [[Longnose Sucker]] (45 percent), rainbow trout (16 percent) and white sucker (13 percent) were most common. Only 38 percent were in the top 7 m, so fish were more widely dispersed in depth.
- **Trap nets, August:** 692 fish from 11 species. Longnose suckers (54 percent) and white suckers (28 percent) were most common.
- **Purse seining, May:** Five hauls captured rainbow trout, cutthroat trout, lake trout and mountain whitefish.

**Hydroacoustic results**

- **Transect surveys:** Surveys covered four transects, one of them the midchannel or thalweg of the North Fork Arm, by night in May and August 1992.
- **Density, May:** Average estimates were 99 to 123 fish/hectare. The highest densities, 134 to 140 fish/hectare, occurred near the dam and at transects B and C. Transect A, which contained no shallow shoreline habitat, had the lowest density.
- **Depth distribution, May:** More than 90 percent of fish were in the top 4 m. Near the dam, 94 percent (126 fish/hectare) were in the top 4 m.
- **Density, August:** Averages were 41 to 44 fish/hectare, substantially lower than in May. The highest densities (104 to 66 fish/hectare, as reported) were in the channel transect (A).
- **Diel pattern:** Fish densities were generally higher at night.

**Purse seine and fish size**

- Purse seine catch near the dam declined in August to 2.75–9.9 fish/haul, compared with May.
- Purse seining in the geothermal bubble plume caught 8.7 and 30.0 fish/haul in August. Echosounding could not distinguish fish from bubble interference in that area.
- Target-strength data showed that fish near the dam were mostly medium to large, consistent with the purse seine catch.

**Lake trout and dam releases**

- Lake trout were rare in May collections but became more numerous in the hypolimnion by August.
- They are reported to tolerate high temperatures least among salmonids, preferring water below 13 °C. As the reservoir warmed, they were thermally restricted to deeper water.
- Large targets, suspected to be lake trout, were concentrated below 25 m, near the approximate depth of the [[Outlet Works]].
- The sieve-netting program by WGFD, the Shoshone Project Office and the Denver Office (1991–1992) found low fish passage through reservoir releases from May through July. Passage rose in mid-August and September, mainly with lake trout. The authors note that lake trout are likely to become entrained as they concentrate near the outlet works in late summer, and that entrainment drops once the reservoir mixes in fall.
- The surveys detected no concentration of fish in front of the outlet works in May or August.

**Methodological limitations**

- Echosounding rarely determines species composition, and the single-beam system ignores weak targets.
- The HADAS system requires at least 200 targets, and ideally 1,000 or more, for accurate target strength distributions. Transects recorded fewer than 100 single returns, so results should be interpreted cautiously.
- Canyon walls and [[Geothermal Springs]] bubble plumes produced background noise that made distinguishing fish difficult.

**Conclusions**

- Netting and echosounding are complementary. Netting gives species, condition, length and relative abundance data over small areas. Echosounding gives reservoir-wide distribution and density with relatively little extra effort.
- The hydroacoustic data support WGFD's purse seine site selection. The area near the dam and the eastern reservoir support the highest densities of pelagic salmonids.
- Fish were more abundant, and nearer shore and the surface, in May. In August, densities were higher mid-reservoir and fish were more widely distributed in depth.
- Warming water appears to restrict lake trout to deeper, cooler water, where they are more vulnerable to transport through reservoir releases.
