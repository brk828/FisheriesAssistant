# Effects of Predation by Nonnative Smallmouth Bass on Native Salmonid Prey: the Role of Predator and Prey Size

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how the body sizes of [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), a nonnative piscivore, relate to predation risk for native salmonids in the lower [[Yakima River]], Washington. Data were collected from 1998 to 2002 using electrofishing, [[Mark-Recapture Abundance Estimation]], [[Gastric Lavage]] diet sampling of 4,135 predators, and a [[Meal Turnover Method]] to estimate consumption. Predation risk to salmonids generally **decreased** as predator size increased, the reverse of the pattern documented for the native [[Northern Pikeminnow]]. The authors conclude that the introduction of smallmouth bass has altered the size-based predation dynamics that were historically dominated by pikeminnow.

## Background & Description

**Study rationale.** Predation risk assessments depend on both the abundance and the consumption rate of predators at each size. Because small predators may be far more numerous than large ones, reducing predation by small fish may be the most effective way to lower impacts on salmonids. The authors note that relatively few studies have combined abundance and size-specific consumption data, mainly because abundance is difficult to estimate for nonnative predators.

**Predator background.** Northern pikeminnow were historically the main fish piscivores on salmonids in the Columbia River and its tributaries, including the John Day and lower Yakima rivers. They are still dominant in the Columbia River, but are now rarely captured in the lower Yakima. Smallmouth bass are now the dominant piscine predators of salmonids there, and they become piscivorous roughly 2–3 years earlier than pikeminnow (at about 100–150 mm versus 200–250 mm).

**Study system.** The [[Yakima River]] is a Columbia River tributary in south-central Washington. Its lower 68 km were represented by two sections: the "Vangie" section (river km 13–21, within a 28.1 km reach) and the "Benton" section (river km 49–57, within a 39.9 km reach), separated by a low-head diversion dam at river km 28.1. Salmonids present were [[Chinook Salmon]] (stream-type and ocean-type), [[Coho Salmon]], and [[Steelhead]]. Hatcheries release approximately 2 million fall Chinook, 800,000 spring Chinook, and 1 million coho salmon annually. Mean prey lengths were 128 mm for spring Chinook, 77 mm for fall Chinook, 150 mm for coho, and 174 mm for steelhead.

**Methods summary.**
- **Sampling:** Drift-boat [[Electrofishing]] along the left bank (three times in spring 1998, then weekly or biweekly in spring 1999–2002). Smallmouth bass of 150 mm and larger were targeted.
- **Abundance:** Mark-recapture estimates were made once or twice per year. A CPUE-based relationship (Abundance = 4,785 × CPUE) was applied to weekly data. Abundance was partitioned by size-class using Seber's method. No valid estimates were obtained in 2001.
- **Age and growth:** Scales were used for [[Scale-Based Back-Calculation]] of length at age, using the Fraser-Lee model with a standard intercept of 35 mm.
- **Diet:** Pulsed gastric lavage on a systematic subsample, with a minimum of 20 fish sampled per day. Prey were identified with a [[Diagnostic Bone Analysis]] key. Prey lengths were back-calculated from bone measurements where fish were digested.
- **Consumption:** Daily consumption was estimated from gastric evacuation time (after Rogers and Burley 1991) and weekly abundance by size-class, then extrapolated to each reach.
- **Statistics:** Linear regression with predator size as the independent variable. Significance was set at p < 0.05. Consumption data were log-transformed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Size-based predation theory.** Predator size is positively related to the maximum prey size consumed, and mouth gape limits prey size. Small prey may not be energetically profitable to pursue, which is consistent with [[Optimal Foraging Theory]].
- **Historical versus current dynamics.** Under pikeminnow dominance, the greatest threat to juvenile salmonids came from pikeminnow over 400 mm, and larger salmonids were at greater risk. Under current smallmouth bass dominance, bass under 250 mm pose the greatest threat, and ocean-type Chinook salmon appear to be at the greatest current risk.
- **Adaptation.** The authors note it is unknown whether salmonids have adapted to the new size-based predation pattern, and suggest a lack of adaptation could affect survival.
- **Comparison with other systems.** Smallmouth bass in the adjacent [[Hanford Reach]] can reach 300 mm in their third year. Pikeminnow scales from the Columbia River indicate they reach 250 mm at age 4 or 5.

## Key Findings

**Predator size and salmonid consumption**
- Consumption of salmonids was inversely related to smallmouth bass length (F = 33.82, P < 0.001).
- The 150–199 mm size-group consumed the largest share of salmonids (42.9% of the total from 1998 to 2002). The 200–249 mm group consumed 26.7%.
- Cumulative consumption: 69.6% by bass under 250 mm and 83.6% by bass under 300 mm.
- Bass of 450–549 mm consumed no salmonids in the sampled years (per Table 3).

**Abundance and diet**
- Mean abundance declined with increasing bass size (F = 15.66, P = 0.01). Small bass were far more numerous.
- The proportion of bass containing salmonids decreased with size (F = 18.99, P = 0.007).
- The 150–199 mm group had the highest mean estimated abundance (1,586 fish), and larger groups were much less abundant.

**Relative prey size**
- The maximum relative prey length (prey length/predator length × 100) was 56.6%.
- The mean relative prey length was 25.0%, and bass generally ate prey at under 50% of their length.
- Relative prey length decreased with predator size (F = 97.57, P < 0.001, R² = 0.17).
- Only 3.6% of bass that ate fish consumed a salmonid of 100 mm or larger.
- Consumed salmonids ranged from 22 to 153 mm (mean 59 mm).

**Growth and piscivory timing**
- Back-calculated mean lengths were 90 mm at age 1, 149 mm at age 2, 254 mm at age 4, and 301 mm at age 5. The Discussion gives 311 mm at age 5, a discrepancy in the source.
- A 100-mm bass could theoretically consume a 30–35 mm fall Chinook fry, based on the mean 25% relative prey length.

**Overall conclusions**
- Introduction of smallmouth bass has reversed the size-consumption relationship found for native pikeminnow.
- Reducing predation by small bass may be the most effective management approach for protecting salmonids in the lower Yakima River.
- Because few small bass (under 150 mm) were examined, the minimum size at which bass become predaceous on salmonids remains unconfirmed.
