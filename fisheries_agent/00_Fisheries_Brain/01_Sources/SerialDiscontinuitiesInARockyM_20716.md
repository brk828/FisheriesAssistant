# Serial Discontinuities in a Rocky Mountain River: Distribution and Abundance of Trichoptera

#entomology #freshwater-ecology #trichoptera #hydropsychidae

## Executive Summary

This paper (Part II of a series, companion to the [[Plecoptera]] study) examines how river regulation on the [[Gunnison River]] in Colorado alters the distribution and abundance of caddisfly larvae (Order [[Trichoptera]]). Twenty-five species were collected from mainstream samples. Species richness was highest at an unregulated rhithron segment above the central reach dams. At sites immediately below the three hypolimnial-release dams and a reregulation dam, species richness fell by 35–90 per cent and abundance by more than 95 per cent. Downstream, at sites 60–80 km below the central reach dams, [[Net-Spinning Caddisflies]] of the family [[Hydropsychidae]] reached high densities. The authors interpret this pattern as a classic expression of continuum resets predicted by the [[Serial Discontinuity Concept]].

## Background & Description

Caddisfly larvae are an important part of most running-water ecosystems and often contribute substantially to zoobenthic biomass and energy processing (Benke and Wallace, 1980; Cushing, 1963; Ward, 1986). Their longitudinal distribution along rivers can be strongly shaped by regulation (Armitage, 1978; Boon, 1979; Hauer and Stanford, 1982; Ward, 1987).

The Gunnison River system has a wide altitudinal and longitudinal gradient, impounded by a series of high, deep-release dams. The serial arrangement of dams along the gradient allowed the authors to examine longitudinal patterns of zoobenthos and their spatial recovery after regulation. Effects on other taxa are reported elsewhere (Stanford and Ward, 1989; Ward and Stanford, 1989).

**Methods (summary):**
- Sampling and study-area details follow the companion paper (Stanford and Ward, 1989).
- Trichoptera biomass was quantified with a [[Centrifuge Technique]] (Stanford, 1973) to obtain wet mass. Wet mass was converted to dry mass using a constant derived from 20 randomly selected samples dried at 60 °C for 24 h.
- The authors state the conversion was valid because samples at all sites were dominated by Hydropsychidae, and case-dwelling species were rare or had been removed from their cases.
- Larvae were sorted to taxa (Wiggins, 1977) and verified against adult collections and a reference collection of Rocky Mountain caddisflies held at the [[Flathead Lake Biological Station]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling layout.** Sites spanned roughly 2900–1400 m a.s.l. and about 300 km from source (Figure 1). Unregulated sites (open symbols), regulated sites (closed symbols) and one reregulation site (hybrid) were positioned relative to dams and four reservoirs: [[Taylor Park Reservoir]], [[Blue Mesa Reservoir]], [[Morrow Point Reservoir]] and [[Crystal Reservoir]].

**Physical and chemical drivers.** Below the upper dam, the authors attribute the moderate loss of diversity and the significant reduction in density and biomass largely to altered temperature, substrata and trophic conditions. Other factors included:
- constant flow and substratum stability
- elevated nutrients
- absence of transported fine sediments
- high water clarity
- warm winter conditions

These conditions produced dense, continuous growth of filamentous algae. The authors note that this favoured grazers (e.g., *Oligophlebodes* sp., *Glossosoma* sp.) and case-attaching taxa (e.g., *Brachycentrus* sp.) (Hauer and Stanford, 1986; Ward, 1976).

**Continuum and discontinuity.** Species richness at unregulated sites follows the longitudinal pattern predicted by the [[River Continuum Concept]] (Vannote et al., 1980). Deviations from this pattern correspond with predictions of the Serial Discontinuity Concept (Ward and Stanford, 1983). The [[Rhithron]] conditions were displaced downstream, producing a discontinuity distance of approximately 68 km and shifting the [[Rhithron-Potamon Boundary]] from Station 6 to Station 8.

**Regulation as permanent disturbance.** The authors describe regulation as a form of permanent disturbance (Resh et al., 1988) that moderates the recovery mechanisms zoobenthic species depend on (Fisher, 1983). Net-spinning caddisflies appear especially responsive, particularly where the regulated river below a dam is long enough for a downstream discontinuity to develop.

## Key Findings

**1. Richness and abundance collapse below dams.**
- Twenty-five Trichoptera species were collected from mainstream samples.
- Greatest richness occurred at the unregulated rhithron segment above the central reach dams.
- Immediately below the three hypolimnial-release dams and the reregulation dam, species richness fell 35–90 per cent and abundance fell more than 95 per cent.
- At the first regulated site, diversity dropped from 15 species (Site 1) to 10 species (Site 2). Most species recovered by Site 3.
- Density and biomass were also markedly reduced at Site 2. The authors contrast this with stoneflies (Plecoptera), which showed a small increase in density and biomass at Site 2.

**2. Net-spinners dominate unregulated sites.**
- Net-spinning larvae made up 50 per cent or more of Trichoptera abundance and biomass at all unregulated sites.
- Upper-basin unregulated sites were dominated by *Arctopsyche grandis*, *Hydropsyche cockerelli*, *Brachycentrus occidentalis* and *Glossosoma* sp. *Arctopsyche grandis* reached 218 organisms and 586 mg dry mass m⁻² at the upper reaches.
- At the lower four sites, nearly all caddisfly production was from three hydropsychids: *H. cockerelli*, *H. occidentalis* and *Cheumatopsyche pettiti*.
- In the lower river, where seston values were very high, these net-spinners reached densities above 6000 m⁻² and biomass as high as 6621 mg m⁻² dry weight. The lower basin total was 9041 organisms.

**3. Downstream hydropsychid succession.**
- *Hydropsyche cockerelli* reached maximum densities at the uppermost unregulated lower-basin site (Station 8).
- *Hydropsyche occidentalis* and *Cheumatopsyche pettiti* dominated downstream at Sites 9, 10 and 11.
- This hydropsychid sequence closely matches that reported for the [[Flathead River]], Montana (Hauer and Stanford, 1982; Stanford et al., 1988). The exception is the absence of the large arctopsychine *Parapsyche elsis*, which apparently does not occur in Colorado (Ward, 1986).

**4. Serial discontinuity explains the pattern.**
- Low caddisfly densities in dam tailwaters contrast with high hydropsychid densities 60–80 km downstream.
- The authors conclude the Gunnison River is "a classic expression of continuum resets and adjustments" to regulation, consistent with the Serial Discontinuity Concept.

**Conclusions.** Stream regulation significantly reduces caddisfly diversity and abundance directly below dams. Recovery is gradual and depends on distance downstream. Net-spinning hydropsychids, which are highly responsive to regulation, are the key indicators of this recovery.
