# Effect of Suspended Mineral Solids on the Survival of Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study by D. W. M. Herbert and J. C. Merkens, of the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] in Stevenage, tested whether inert mineral particles held in suspension at relatively low concentrations (30 to 810 p.p.m.) harm [[Rainbow Trout]] directly. Two commercial solids were used, [[Kaolin]] and [[Diatomaceous Earth]]. Concentrations of 30 p.p.m. caused no detectable harm. Concentrations of 90 p.p.m. showed a slight adverse effect. Concentrations of 270 p.p.m. and above were harmful, with mortality usually above 50 per cent. Surviving fish in the higher concentrations developed thickened and fused [[Gill Lamellae]] and more severe [[Fin-Rot]]. The authors conclude that the solids probably acted by abrading the gills and by increasing susceptibility to infection and other stresses, rather than through a simple dose-response toxicity.

## Background & Description

The paper deliberately excludes indirect effects of suspended solids, such as silted spawning beds, reduced light penetration, and reduced food production. It focuses on direct effects on fish. Prior work showed that many freshwater fish tolerate very high concentrations briefly:

- [[Wallen 1951]] (cited as Wallen, I. E.) found that most species survived a week at about 100,000 p.p.m. of erosion silt (mostly montmorillonite clay), but died within 2 hours at 175,000 to 225,000 p.p.m. (see [[Lethal Turbidity]]).
- Griffin (1938) reported that salmon and trout fingerlings withstood 300 to 750 p.p.m. over 3 to 4 weeks, with concentrations raised to 2,300 to 6,500 p.p.m. during stirring.
- Cole (1935) found that a 2 per cent (20,000 p.p.m.) wood-fibre suspension was not harmful to healthy fish, though it hastened the death of unhealthy individuals.

The authors note that long exposure to lower concentrations was less studied. Average surface-water values of about 100 p.p.m. have been reported for waters holding trout (Warp 1938). Schneeberger and Jewel (1928) found that reducing turbidity to an average of 100 p.p.m. in ponds coincided with higher fish production. The range below 1,000 p.p.m. had received little attention, and this was the gap the study addressed.

### Methods and Materials

- **Fish:** Rainbow trout aged about 9 to 18 months, taken from one trout-farm pond per experiment and randomly allocated, with ten fish per test.
- **Dilution water:** Hard borehole water, 320 p.p.m. as CaCO3, aerated and brought near room temperature.
- **Apparatus:** Circular conical-base tanks were tried first (Experiment A) but were unsuitable. Fish abraded their fins on the walls and developed sores. The final design was a stirred glass aquarium (2 ft × 1 ft × 1 ft, 35 litres) with a central perforated plastic pipe and a stainless steel propeller at 900 to 1,000 r.p.m., circulating about 35 litres/min, plus aeration. See [[Stirred Aquaria]].
- **Husbandry:** Fish were fed daily to near repletion on minced bullock's heart and liver. Fish were acclimatized for one to two weeks before solids were added. Terramycin was added daily as a precaution against fin-rot, but it did not fully prevent the disease.
- **Solids:** Particle sizes were characterized by two methods. The [[Sedimentation Method]] gave median sizes of 0.46 µm for kaolin and 2.2 µm for diatomaceous earth. The [[Optical Method]] gave larger medians of 3 µm and 17.5 µm respectively. The authors attribute the difference to particle shape (flat plates settle with their largest face down) and to the optical method not detecting particles under about 1 to 2 µm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Conditions were kept consistent across experiments:

- Dissolved oxygen was near air saturation.
- pH ranged from 8.0 to 8.4.
- Temperature followed room temperature, 13 to 20 °C.
- Suspended solids were checked at intervals, usually weekly. Measured means were close to nominal values, for example 240 p.p.m. against a nominal 270 p.p.m. kaolin in Experiment B and 740 p.p.m. against 810 p.p.m. diatomaceous earth in Experiment D.

The authors stress that aquarium stresses, such as overcrowding, fighting, and favourable conditions for disease spread, differ from river conditions. Results therefore cannot be transferred directly to polluted rivers, although a stress that lowers survival in one setting probably does so in another, to a different degree.

## Key Findings

**Experimental series**

- Seven experiments were run: A (circular tanks, kaolin), B to C (stirred aquaria, kaolin), and D to F (stirred aquaria, diatomaceous earth). Experiment G examined gill histology over time.
- Nominal concentrations were 0 (control), 30, 90, 270, and 810 p.p.m.
- Controls had only two deaths among 60 fish.

**Survival**

- 30 p.p.m.: mortality was at control level, so no harm was detected.
- 90 p.p.m.: mortality was slightly higher than at 30 p.p.m. or in controls, suggesting some adverse effect (Experiment B was an exception).
- 270 p.p.m. and above: mortality varied between tests but was usually above 50 per cent and always greater than in the 0, 30, or 90 p.p.m. tanks.
- No clear difference in lethal effect was found between kaolin and diatomaceous earth.
- Deaths in the higher concentrations were a mix of fish with fin-rot or infected wounds and fish with no external signs. Deaths with disease signs were a majority only in Experiment F.
- Fish held for weeks at high concentrations became extremely pale, almost white.
- No clear dose-response relationship was found, unlike the typical pattern for soluble poisons.

**Growth**

- In Experiment F, survivors were weighed and measured at the start and again after about 4.5 months.
- Analysis of variance showed no significant difference in length or weight among diatomaceous earth concentrations, including 810 p.p.m. Survivors appeared to grow as well as controls.

**Gill pathology**

- Gills of fish from higher concentrations showed thickened respiratory epithelium and fusion of adjacent lamellae, often at the tips.
- Affected fish included three fish at 270 p.p.m. kaolin (Experiment C), two surviving fish at 270 and 810 p.p.m. kaolin, and one fish at 810 p.p.m. diatomaceous earth (Experiment E; the survivors' gills were apparently normal).
- Fish at 30 and 90 p.p.m. (Experiment B, more than 5 months) and controls plus 30 p.p.m. fish (Experiment E, more than 2 months) had normal gills.
- In Experiment G, fish held at 270 p.p.m. diatomaceous earth for 148 days, then 1,000 p.p.m., showed gill changes. Some were affected after about 11 weeks. Others remained normal after eight months or more, so the time to onset was variable (see [[Gill Pathology]]).

**Fin-rot**

- Experiment G graded caudal fin condition on a 1 to 6 scale, where 1 is normal and 6 is body-wall infection near the fin base.
- At 57 days, controls were all Grade 1, while trout at 270 p.p.m. averaged Grade 2.
- At 121 days, controls were still all Grade 1, while suspension fish averaged 2.9.
- The authors conclude that suspended trout clearly suffered more fin infection than controls.

**Interpretation**

- The thickened gills were probably caused by continual abrasion by the particles. Affected fish would be less efficient at respiration and less viable.
- Similar gill lesions have been attributed to bacterial irritation (Davis 1926, 1927) and to other causes (Fish 1935), suggesting thickening may be a general response to several irritants, including inorganic solids.
- Solids may also have made healing of small wounds slower, raising the risk of fin-rot and other infection.
- The solids probably reduced survival mainly by potentiating other environmental stresses that were too mild to kill controls, a process described as [[Stress Potentiation]]. This explains the variable mortality at higher concentrations.
- The authors note that the study did not establish a safe threshold. Their data show no harm at 30 p.p.m. and a probable adverse effect at 90 p.p.m. Harm was clear at 270 p.p.m. and above.
