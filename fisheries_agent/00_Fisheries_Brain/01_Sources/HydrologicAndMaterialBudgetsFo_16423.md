# Hydrologic and Material Budgets for a Small Sonoran Desert Watershed During Three Consecutive Cloudburst Floods

#hydrology #biogeochemistry #arid-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Fisher]] and [[Grimm]] quantifies hydrologic and material budgets for [[KR Wash]], a 0.65 ha [[Sonoran Desert]] watershed in central Arizona, during three cloudburst storms on 22, 23 and 24 August 1982. Nitrogen and phosphorus were abundant in precipitation, but 75–90 per cent of rainfall did not run off, so the storms were net inputs of dissolved N and P to the catchment. Runoff carried far more particulate and organic matter than rain delivered. The authors caution that single-storm data should not be used to build "typical year" budgets, because desert precipitation is highly variable in time and space.

Background and description are not applicable to fish or other aquatic fauna: the paper states that aquatic biota are absent from these small watersheds.

## Background & Description

- **Climate and rainfall:** Summer precipitation comes from a [[Monsoon Precipitation]] pattern. Daytime heating produces [[Cloudburst Storm]]s, typically in late afternoon or early evening, that are intense but cover small areas. Annual precipitation in low deserts ranges from 18 cm (Phoenix) to 31 cm (Tucson), while potential evapotranspiration is about ten times higher. About half of annual rain falls May–September.
- **Flow regime:** Depending on rainfall, a small wash may flow for only minutes to hours a year, or not at all for several years. Isolated [[Flash Flood]] events are rarely studied, so few nutrient budgets exist for desert watersheds of the southwest.
- **Study site:** KR Wash drains a 0.65 ha watershed at 616 m elevation near Kings Ranch, about 10 km east of Apache Junction (33°23′N, 111°26′W). Channel slope is 4.2 per cent. Vegetation is lower Sonoran desertscrub, dominated by saguaro (*Carnegiea gigantea*), jojoba, bursage, creosote bush, palo verde and ironwood. The watershed lies on a [[Bajada]] of the Superstition Mountains, with coarse, deep soils overlain by [[Desert Pavement]].
- **Methods:** Precipitation was collected in a polyethylene funnel-bottle collector, and discharge was estimated from cross-sectional area and timed-flotation velocity. Samples were taken every 2–5 min during runoff, stored at 4 °C and filtered within 18 h. Analyses covered [[Suspended Solids]], [[Fine Particulate Organic Matter]], [[Dissolved Organic Matter]], [[Nitrate]], ammonium, total dissolved N and P (after UV oxidation), soluble reactive P, chloride, sulfate, alkalinity and conductivity. Nutrient budgets were calculated as inputs (precipitation) versus outputs (integrated concentration times discharge).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Nutrient pathways:** Inputs to small watersheds come from precipitation, rock weathering and biological vectors such as [[Nitrogen Fixation]]. Outputs leave as dissolved and particulate load. In mesic regions, precipitation inputs are frequent and stream output is continuous, whereas in deserts both fluxes are rare and restricted in time.
- **Infiltration and runoff thresholds:** Compacted desert soils absorb little, so overland flow develops quickly. Other studies cited report that 1–2 mm of rain on rock and 3–5 mm on stony soil can generate flow in small Negev watersheds. Infiltration at KR Wash was estimated at 0.17–0.22 mm/min during the three runoff events, based on water retained and elapsed time (see [[Infiltration]]).
- **Downstream links:** The authors note that runoff from these catchments feeds larger, more permanent streams. Nitrate is a key constituent in the metabolism of Sonoran Desert permanent streams, where nitrogen often limits algal primary production at the base of the aquatic food web. Nitrate from storm runoff is routed into sandy channel sediments and emerges at spring heads, supplying permanent reaches with nitrogen and sustaining flow year-round. The authors conclude that permanent desert streams owe much of their chemical and biological character to storms that may have occurred miles and months away.
- **Soil processes:** [[Denitrification]] following soil wetting can be rapid (maximum rates in about 22 h at a Sonoran mesquite site), and nitrification of ammonium can augment soil nitrate, possibly more quickly than in Great Basin soils.

## Key Findings

**Hydrology**
- Rainfall on the three days was 14, 11 and 5 mm, with similar intensities.
- Runoff was 25, 24 and 10.3 per cent of precipitation, and increased with rainfall amount. Maximum discharge was 27.2, 20 and 10 l/s.
- 99 per cent of water yield occurred in 18, 20 and 12 min respectively. Runoff was intense and short-lived, yet 75–90 per cent of precipitation was retained by the watershed.
- Three early-September storms of 5–7 mm produced no flow because intensities were below 0.2 mm/min. The authors conclude that precipitation intensity, not just amount, governs flow.

**Precipitation chemistry**
- Nitrogen and phosphorus in rain were high (total dissolved N 1.07–1.76 mg/l; total dissolved P 0.02–0.27 mg/l, with the highest values in storms 1 and 2). Total dissolved N was three to four times higher than comparable western US desert data.
- Rain was acidic (pH 4.5–5.1), so bicarbonate was rare or absent.
- Sampling included leached dry fallout and entrained dust, which likely contributed to chemical variation between storms.

**Runoff chemistry**
- Most constituents were higher in runoff than in rain, especially particulates, dissolved organic matter, bicarbonate and soluble reactive phosphorus.
- Highest concentrations of conductivity, nitrogen and phosphorus species generally occurred early in each flood, consistent with rapid solubilization of surface salts and low adsorption capacity of these soils.
- Clockwise hysteresis in concentration–discharge plots indicated depletion of transportable materials during the event. The authors stress the need for serial sampling.
- Nitrate in flood 3 rose nearly threefold over flood 1, though flood 3 runoff was only 15–20 per cent of the earlier floods.

**Budgets (per storm)**
- The watershed was a net source of suspended solids, fine particulate organic matter, dissolved organic matter, bicarbonate and soluble reactive P in all storms, and of total dissolved P and dissolved organic P in storm 3.
- Chloride, hydrogen ion, sulfate, ammonium, total dissolved N and dissolved organic P were retained (output/input ratios often well below 1).
- Particulate losses dominated. Suspended solids output in flood 1 reached 52 kg/ha, of which 4.6 kg/ha was organic. Dissolved organic matter output in storm 1 was 1.2 kg/ha.
- Sulfate input in storm 1 was 909 g/ha. Total nitrogen input ranged from 54 to 192 g/ha, more than half retained. Total phosphorus input ranged from 0.8 to 29 g/ha.
- Dissolved losses were 2.9 per cent (flood 1) to 10 per cent (floods 2 and 3) of total inorganic output; the rest was particulate.

**Annual extrapolation (approximate)**
- The three storms represented about 10 per cent of annual rainfall at the site (2.9 of 30.5 cm). Fluxes were roughly scaled by ten, assuming 23 per cent of precipitation runs off annually, which the authors consider possibly an overestimate.
- Estimated annual solution output was 15.9 kg/ha and particulate output 611 kg/ha/yr. Particulate output is high relative to New England forested and clear-cut watersheds but well below Colorado River basin estimates.
- Total dissolved N output was 1.75 kg/ha/yr, at the low end of reported values. Total dissolved P output was 0.3 kg/ha/yr.
- Total N input was estimated at 4.3 kg/ha/yr and total P input at 0.46 kg/ha/yr, within ranges reported elsewhere.

**Conclusions and implications**
- Dissolved N and P were retained during summer storms, but soluble reactive P was exported at nearly twice its input rate. Total P loss exceeded input nearly fourfold when particulates were included.
- Budgets depend strongly on chemical form, so the same element can move through a catchment in different ways.
- Single-storm data are unreliable for annual budgets. Desert budgets require sampling during events, periodic precipitation chemistry, and multi-year records, because year-to-year variation is substantial.
- Dynamics within a storm are largely physical-chemical, more so than in vegetated mesic watersheds.
