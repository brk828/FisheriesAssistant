# Aphanius iberus and Aphanius dispar: Aquarium Husbandry and Field Ecology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This note compiles three short pieces on two killifish of the genus [[Aphanius]]. The first is a hobbyist article on [[Aphanius Iberus]], one of the few killifish native to Europe, based on observations of four pairs in a community tank. The second is a follow-up on [[Aphanius Dispar]] by a keeper in Cornwall, covering spawning, water chemistry and hatching. The third is a technical summary of the ecology of *A. dispar* in [[Oman]], drawn from a 1982 study by R. Hass. Together they cover captive husbandry, diet, breeding, colour variation, and the environmental range of wild populations.

## Background & Description

**Aphanius iberus (Spanish Minnow)**
- Described by P. Barrow as one of the few European killifish, with little published information available at the time.
- Body shape resembles [[Nothobranchius]] species. Males are a mid-grey base with silver vertical flank stripes, a silver mottled area above the stripes, and a black bar running from eye to eye through the lower lip, giving a "masked" appearance.
- Males show between two and five curved vertical bands on the caudal fin. Two colour changes occur: a slight mauve cast over the body, and a more dramatic sulphur-yellow cast covering the body and fins, with bright yellow pelvic fins. Neither change is linked to breeding.
- Females are mid-grey with brown blotches, metallic green or mauve flank sheen, and an unmarked silver belly. Individual females showed distinct patterns, "like fingerprints."
- Young fish resemble females. Males develop adult colouration at about 3 to 4 months, with some batch-mates taking up to a month longer.

**Aphanius dispar**
- Males are described as about 3 inches (roughly 7.6 cm) in length in the hobbyist report, and up to about 4 cm total length in the Omani field study.
- Hobbyist-reported fish from Oman have dark vertical bars from the tail up to the end of the dorsal fin. Fish from Bahrain, featured in a 1978 *T.F.H.* article, had larger filamentous dorsal and anal fins and were spotted along the body.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Captive conditions (A. iberus)**
- Community tank held at about 72°F with one teaspoon of cooking salt per gallon. Tankmates included [[Rasbora]], [[Loach]], livebearers and baby [[Cichlid]]s. The only aggression observed was toward baby cichlids.

**Captive water chemistry (A. dispar)**
- The keeper's tap water had a pH of 7.2 and hardness of 140 ppm. Water from two Omani sites closely matched this. Water from a third site exceeded the kit's range (above 360 ppm hardness).

**Natural habitat (A. dispar, Oman)**
- Occurs in nearly all fresh bodies of water with a periodic or permanent sea connection, and in man-made channels and cisterns.
- The coastal plain of northern Oman has no natural surface water. Irrigation water is pumped from underground, and fish were introduced into wells and cisterns as [[Mosquito Control]] agents, where they are now breeding.
- In non-coastal areas, fish occur in man-made cisterns, [[Wadi]]s, and the [[Falaj System]], which are narrow channels carrying mountain water for irrigation.
- Fish are strong swimmers, found in the fastest-flowing falajs, and also thrive in still cisterns. Substrates range from soft detritus to sand and gravel.
- Water temperature ranged from 28.4 to 38.4°C and pH from 7.4 to 8.
- Fish tolerated salinities up to 100% sea water if acclimatised slowly.
- The Omani stream described by the keeper was crystal clear, heavily planted, and held only *A. dispar*, in numbers large enough to surround wading people.

## Key Findings

**Husbandry and diet (A. iberus)**
- Four pairs were kept together. The fish coexisted with community species, with aggression directed only at baby cichlids.
- Accepts most foods, including flake, pellets, frozen beefheart, peas, prawns, algae and live food, and is particularly fond of [[Bloodworms]] and [[Daphnia]].
- A "hollow belly" condition resembling tuberculosis was linked to diet. Large feeds of daphnia and [[Cyclops]] restored condition within days. Brine shrimp tablets had a similar effect, suggesting the fish lacks something found in the crustacean shell.

**Breeding (A. iberus)**
- First viable eggs appeared when the fish were 8 months old.
- An 18"×10"×10" tank with two pairs was too large; males were not territorial enough and few eggs were found. A 12"×8"×10" tank with a trio, two spawning mops and a clump of [[Java Moss]] was more successful.
- Egg output was about 6 per day. Females ate many eggs, so a single pair per tank was recommended.
- Most eggs were found in nylon spawning mops, probably because the dense structure hid them from females. Very few were found in Java moss.
- Courtship is subdued. The male clamps his fins toward the female and circles in front of her to entice her into the spawning area. Eggs are laid in twos or threes, are clear, about 1.5 mm in diameter, and sticky. One female laid amber-coloured eggs.
- Embryos develop gold-rimmed eyes after about a week. Hatching takes about 10 days at 75°F.
- Hatchlings are about 3 mm long. First food should be [[Microworms]] or [[Brine Shrimp]]. Stopping brine shrimp too early caused stunted growth in one keeper's batch when a hatch failed.

**Breeding and rearing (A. dispar, Cornwall)**
- Two pairs remained healthy in tap water over marine aggregate. Spawning was low, about ten eggs per week.
- Eggs were incubated in strong [[Methylene Blue]]. Six eggs laid ten days before the report all hatched (100% hatching). Two fry from an earlier batch survived three weeks.
- Keeping the tank full of daphnia supplied newborn food and appeared to reduce water fouling. The keeper had previously lost fry to the bottom debris and syphoning.

**Ecology (A. dispar, Oman; Hass 1982)**
- Gut contents of 15 specimens were 90–95% unicellular algae and 5–10% filamentous algae, with one exception. The author concluded the species is herbivorous but readily eats animal matter.
- In an aquarium with abundant algae, two females and one male ate 1,142 [[Mosquito Larvae]] in four days, reported as an average of 96 per fish per day.
- Spawning occurs on vertical surfaces in flowing water, such as plant material or algae-covered rock. No breeding males or spawning were observed in the fast-flowing falajs or in still cisterns.
- Males in breeding condition form daily-changing territories. In the November–December survey, only 5–10% of males were in breeding condition at any time. The number of territorial males depends on suitable combinations of flow and vertical surfaces.
- Females do not hold territory. They gather in groups and approach territorial males when ready to spawn, laying two or three eggs in quick succession.
- Reproduction peaks in spring and early summer and is lowest in winter.

**Source notes**
- The Oman ecology study is cited as R. Hass (1982), "Notes on the Ecology of *Aphanius dispar* (Pisces, Cyprinodontidae) in the Sultanate of Oman," *Freshwater Biology* 12, 89–95.
- The Cornwall report notes that the keeper was the only [[British Killifish Association]] member in Cornwall at the time.
