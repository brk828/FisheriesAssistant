# Temperature and Oxygen Tolerance of the Unarmored Threespine Stickleback; Fish Remains from an Archaeological Site at Rancho Carrillo

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gasterosteidae #triakidae #sciaenidae #scombridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1976 issue section of the *Bulletin of the Southern California Academy of Sciences* (Vol. 75) containing two papers. The first examines the [[Oxygen Consumption]] and [[Gill Ventilation]] responses of the [[Unarmored Threespine Stickleback]] ([[Gasterosteus Aculeatus]]) to declining [[Dissolved Oxygen]], with a critical threshold near 2.0 ppm. The second, by W. I. Follett, reports 170 fish remains from an archaeological midden at the [[Rancho Carrillo Site]] on the [[Silver Strand]], San Diego County. The remains represent nine species, and the author infers both persistence of a former [[Warmwater Fauna]] and [[Tule Balsa]] fishing by the [[Diegueño]] people.

## Background & Description

**Paper 1: Stickleback respiration.** The unarmored stickleback is studied as a physiological model. The paper summarizes earlier work on how fish maintain steady oxygen consumption, and it reports experiments on gill ventilation rate across oxygen concentrations. Figure 3 presents means for five fish, with five ventilation measurements per minute averaged for each fish.

**Paper 2: Archaeological fish remains.** The collection was recovered by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and Laurence G. Jones in July 1962 from a former Spanish ranch on the Silver Strand (Coronado Strand), a sandspit separating the surf-beaten ocean beach from the mud flats of [[San Diego Bay]]. The specimens are deposited at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography under Accession No. 1962-VII:21. Most specimens are incomplete or fragmentary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Oxygen physiology.** Unarmored sticklebacks hold oxygen consumption at a fairly constant rate above about 2.0 ppm. Below that, consumption falls rapidly. The "no excess activity" point is strongly tied to the oxygen concentration of a species' native habitat (Macan, 1963). [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) have a lower incipient limiting point than [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*). Acclimation and temperature both shift these thresholds. Brook trout survival in a lethal 1.06 ppm O₂ rose after 150 hours at 3.8 ppm. The paper places the stickleback physiologically between stagnant-water carps and cold-stream salmonids.

**Field setting of the stickleback.** The daytime mean oxygen in the upper [[Santa Clara River]] was 7.18 ppm, well above the limit. Lower levels could occur in isolated stagnant pools or on warm summer nights, when photosynthetic oxygen production is absent. The author notes that sticklebacks strongly prefer flowing water and are absent from ponds lacking flow.

**Archaeological setting.** The Rancho Carrillo site lies on the Silver Strand between Coronado and Coronado Heights, about 10 km apart. Two occupation stations were productive:
- **Station B:** a [[Diegueño]] camp with abundant pottery, at 6.4 m elevation. A surface-midden radiocarbon date of 270 ± 150 years B.P. (A.D. 1680) was obtained.
- **Station C:** probably a late [[La Jollan Culture]] layer, at low elevation. A radiocarbon date of 3470 ± 175 years B.P. (1520 B.C.) was obtained from 5–15 cm depth. The author notes the material may be a redeposit washed down from Station B.

Fish remains came only from Stations B and C.

## Key Findings

**Stickleback oxygen tolerance**
- Oxygen consumption of the unarmored stickleback stays nearly constant above about 2.0 ppm and drops rapidly below it.
- Ventilation rate rises steeply as oxygen falls. At a field level of 3.0 ppm, which is not lethal, substantial energy would still be needed for ventilation. The author argues this would reduce growth, reproduction, and general activity.
- Jones (1952) observed sticklebacks avoiding water below 2.0 ppm O₂ by turning back immediately. The author finds this matches the oxygen-consumption data, and that death would soon follow below 2.0 ppm.
- The relatively high critical oxygen level suggests a preference for moving, well-oxygenated water.

- The collection includes at least nine species in nine genera and seven families.
- Remains of *Cynoscion parvipinnis* came from three distinct levels. The author concludes the species persisted in the San Diego area for probably more than two centuries. This supports [[Hubbs]]' (1948) view that northward subtropical occurrences were long-lasting warmwater faunas rather than single-season events.
- Shortfin corvinas netted in salt ponds of southern San Diego Bay between 1927 and 1929 may be holdovers from a possible 1926 northward occurrence.
- Large barracuda and mackerel are inferred to have been caught offshore, not from shore, pointing to aboriginal use of the tule balsa. The author cites Eigenmann (1892) that adults rarely entered San Diego Bay.
- A local eddy created by the southeastward current deflected by [[Point Loma]] may have made the area a productive fishing ground.
