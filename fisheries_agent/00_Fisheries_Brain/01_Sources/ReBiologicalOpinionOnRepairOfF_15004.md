# Biological Opinion on Forest Road 449A Repair Effects on Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #endangered-species #poeciliidae #conservation-management

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biological opinion, issued August 18, 1994, evaluates the effects of repairing Forest Road 449A in [[Gila County]], [[Arizona]] on the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (Poeciliopsis occidentalis). The consultation addresses a 1.5-mile road repair project crossing [[Campaign Creek]] six times in an area containing a reintroduced topminnow population established in 1983. The Service concludes that the proposed road repair is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of [[Gila Topminnow]], though incidental take is anticipated. The population has not been observed since 1989 but is still considered extant due to sampling difficulties in complex habitats. The opinion authorizes road maintenance activities through August 1999 under specified terms and conditions.

## Background & Description

[[Gila Topminnow]] was listed as endangered on March 11, 1967. This small (1-2 inch), livebearing fish of the family [[Poeciliidae]] historically occurred in the Gila, Sonora, and de la Concepcion River drainages in [[Arizona]], [[New Mexico]], and Sonora, Mexico. Once among the most common fishes in the [[Gila River]] and tributaries, the species has been extirpated throughout most of its range due to habitat destruction (water diversion, stream downcutting, channelization, impoundment) and competition/predation from nonnative species, particularly [[Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia affinis). At the time of this opinion, only 10 naturally occurring populations remained in the United States, plus approximately 20 reintroduced populations.

[[Campaign Creek]] and [[Upper Horrell Springs]] lie within the historic range and were stocked with [[Gila Topminnow]] in 1983 as part of species recovery efforts. The stock originated from Boyce-Thompson Arboretum with mixed genetic lineage (Monkey Spring, Cocio Wash, Bylas Springs). [[Upper Horrell Springs]] consists of several small hillside seeps and two moderate-sized springs (modified with springboxes for domestic water supply) on the east side of [[Campaign Creek]] in T.2N., R.12E. The springs provide the primary water source for the 1-2 mile perennial section of [[Campaign Creek]].

Riparian vegetation includes [[Fremont Cottonwood]] (Populus fremontii), [[Arizona Sycamore]] (Platanus wrightii), willow (Salix sp.), walnut (Juglans major), hackberry (Celtis sp.), seep willow (Baccharis sp.), cattails (Typha sp.), grasses, sedges, and rushes. The stream is rocky with a high proportion of pools in relatively rugged, steep topography.

Fish recorded from [[Campaign Creek]] include [[Longfin Dace]] (Agosia chrysogaster), [[Green Sunfish]] (Lepomis cyanellus), and [[Gila Topminnow]]. Initial surveys for stocking found no fish; 1987 surveys found only [[Gila Topminnow]]. However, 1989 surveys found [[Longfin Dace]] to be common. Crayfish (Orconectes sp.) were not recorded before 1989 but were reported as abundant in 1991 and 1994 following unauthorized stocking by the proprietor of Reevis Mountain School.

[[Gila Topminnow]] were abundant in surveys from 1985, 1987, and 1989, but none have been observed or captured since September 1989. The population crash likely resulted from multiple factors: reduced surface flow in summer 1990, unusually cold winter temperatures in 1990-91, moderate flooding in spring 1991, major late winter flooding in 1993, and predation from the burgeoning crayfish population. Despite five years of negative sampling, the Service considers the population still extant due to the high probability of not capturing the species at low population levels in complex habitats like [[Campaign Creek]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The environmental baseline reveals significant degradation of [[Campaign Creek]] habitat. Livestock damage to streambanks and riparian vegetation has caused simplification of aquatic habitat, readily observable by contrasting grazed Forest Service lands with ungrazed Reevis School property. Recovery from 1993 scouring floods occurred much more rapidly on ungrazed lands. A new grazing system instituted in 1991 following formal section 7 consultation may improve conditions, but recovery is expected to be slow due to continued livestock use of stream banks and channel. Cattle were present in the perennial stream channel during the July 18, 1994 site visit.

Forest Road 449A contributes to stream channel degradation through loss of riparian vegetation in the roadbed, increased sediment from soil disturbance, altered runoff characteristics from vegetation loss and soil compaction, and direct streambed alteration at six ford crossings. Impacts are most pronounced in the intermittent lower portion where the road occupies the stream bottom for several miles. Road maintenance over the past 50-100 years has likely resulted in temporary increases in these impacts.

Unauthorized private road repair work was discovered during the July 18, 1994 site visit, including a concrete and rock dam approximately 4 feet high and 30 feet long constructed at the first stream crossing above the box canyon. The dam, inscribed "1993" with names of builders including "Peter Bigfoot," was built on Forest Service land without authorization and is not in compliance with sections 7 or 9 of the Endangered Species Act, the National Environmental Policy Act, or section 404 of the Clean Water Act.

The natural life history of [[Gila Topminnow]] as a short-lived, colonizing species involves large population fluctuations and local extirpations followed by reinvasion. This natural pattern is no longer possible due to species extirpation throughout almost all of the [[Gila River]] basin and widespread habitat alteration. Survival and recovery may require replacing the natural loss/reinvasion pattern with artificial human-mediated stocking and augmentation programs, particularly in isolated habitat fragments like [[Campaign Creek]].

## Key Findings

**Project Description**: The proposed project repairs the final 1.5 miles of FR 449A using a D-6 bulldozer and small front-end loader. Work includes blading the road surface, installing water bars, sloping back channel banks at ford crossings, removing large rocks from channel and roadway, and hauling away excess sand and gravel. Heavy equipment would cross the stream only at ford crossings and work outside the wetted channel. No fuels or lubricants would be stored on site. The consultation covers repairs for five years (August 1994 to August 1999).

**Road Use**: Existing use is light at approximately 354 vehicles per year (250 for trail access, 104 for Reevis School), averaging 1 vehicle per day. The need for 4-wheel drive deters casual use. This limited use and rocky valley bottom have prevented serious erosion at ford crossings.

**Direct and Indirect Effects**: The proposed repair is expected to have relatively small adverse effects on [[Gila Topminnow]] and habitat. The road eliminates a 5-10 foot wide strip of riparian vegetation and directly alters streambank and substrate at six ford crossings. Ford crossings adversely affect topminnow through reduced bank stability, increased erosion, streambed compaction, leveling, filling of interstices, loss of cover, and destruction of aquatic invertebrates. The presence of six ford crossings within 1 mile represents significant stream channel alteration. Operation of heavy machinery carries small risk of accidental introduction of toxic substances (petroleum products) into the water.

**Cumulative Effects**: Future non-Federal cumulative impacts include additional unauthorized introductions of fish and aquatic species into [[Campaign Creek]] and future private road repair actions. These activities are reasonably certain to occur based on past events and difficulty of prevention or law enforcement. Such actions will continue to adversely affect [[Gila Topminnow]], exacerbating Federal action effects.

**Incidental Take**: The Service anticipates incidental take through direct mortality (crushing, stranding during equipment operation, or poisoning from accidental toxic substance introduction) and habitat loss/alteration. Because population estimates are not obtainable and would have little value for this short-lived, highly fecund species, incidental take due to direct mortality cannot be quantified. The threshold for exceeded take is set at more than 20 dead fish of any species found in the project area or within 500 yards downstream. Habitat take is anticipated as up to all suitable habitat for 10 feet on either side of centerline at ford crossings, totaling 120 linear feet of stream for the 6 crossings.

**Reasonable and Prudent Measures**: Five measures are required: (1) minimize direct mortality; (2) minimize habitat loss and alteration; (3) monitor topminnow and habitat to document incidental take; (4) maintain complete records of actions resulting in take; (5) take actions to offset incidental loss.

**Terms and Conditions**: Specific requirements include minimizing activities within wetted stream channel, preventing pollutant entry to surface waters, limiting channel and riparian alteration to existing roadway, restricting heavy equipment to 6 existing ford crossings within 20-foot wide roadbed, monitoring for dead/dying fish during activities, submitting written reports within 60 days of work completion, and providing authorizations for augmentation stocking of [[Gila Topminnow]].

**Conservation Recommendations**: The Service recommends the Forest Service discuss unauthorized road repair with Peter Busnack (Peter Bigfoot) of Reevis Mountain School, as his construction of a concrete barrier may violate section 9 of the Endangered Species Act and other Federal statutes.

**Conclusion**: The biological opinion concludes that repair of Forest Road 449A as proposed is **not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of [[Gila Topminnow]]**. Reinitiation of formal consultation is required if incidental take is exceeded, new information reveals unconsidered effects, the action is modified causing unconsidered effects, or a new species is listed or critical habitat designated that may be affected.
