# Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in the Yampa and Green Rivers, Dinosaur National Monument

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Karp and Tyus (1990, *Great Basin Naturalist* 50) assesses the distribution, habitat use, spawning and species associations of the federally endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]] within [[Dinosaur National Monument]], Colorado and Utah, sampled from 1986 to 1989. Humpback chub were rare, found mainly in whitewater reaches of [[Yampa Canyon]] and [[Whirlpool Canyon]], and were captured chiefly in seasonally flooded [[Shoreline Eddies]] alongside 7 native and 12 nonnative fish species. Ripe humpback and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) overlapped in eddies during the spawning period, which followed peak spring runoff. The presence of abundant nonnative [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]] in these habitats suggests possible negative interactions, and the authors conclude that protecting Yampa River flows is essential to the persistence of chubs in the area.

## Background & Description

- **Humpback chub** is a large-river cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River basin and protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. Remaining populations are isolated, including in the Little Colorado and mainstream Colorado rivers in Arizona, the upper Colorado River, the Green and Yampa rivers, and the mainstream Colorado River in Utah. All stocks are presumed native except in [[Cataract Canyon]], where some fish may derive from a 1981 stocking.
- Humpback chub were first reported in Dinosaur National Monument in the 1960s, mostly near the Yampa–Green confluence. Distribution and status in the area remained poorly documented because canyon whitewater is hard to access and sample, the fish is rare, and diagnostic features were not well established.
- **Roundtail chub** occurs with humpback chub in the Monument but is more widespread and abundant. It is not listed under the Endangered Species Act. Gila remains in Indian sites over 1000 years old suggest long-standing presence in the area.
- The study was part of a broader program to assess habitat needs of endangered fishes in the Yampa River (Tyus and Karp 1989). Objectives were to locate humpback chub, evaluate habitat use (including flow and temperature needs), identify spawning areas, and determine species associations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and sampling**
- The lower 73.6 km of the Yampa River (Yampa Canyon, Deerlodge Park to Echo Park) was sampled weekly from mid-May through early July 1987–1989 using [[Electrofishing]] and angling with natural baits such as [[Mormon Crickets]] and megalopteran larvae.
- Echo, Island, Whirlpool and Split Mountain canyons on the Green River, and Lodore Canyon, were sampled less intensively, usually at least twice each spring in 1987 and 1988.
- Trammel netting in July 1986 was discontinued because it traumatized fish.
- Sampling was suspended during peak runoff and ended at base flows (late June to early July). In September 1989, low-flow surveys by helicopter and foot assessed habitat in Big Joe and Warm Springs rapids.

**Habitat**
- Yampa Canyon's upper 44.8 km is a moderately steep reach (3.2 m/km gradient) of rocky runs, riffles and rapids. Lower Yampa Canyon (km 0–28.8) has a gentler gradient (1.0 m/km) with long, deep runs and incised meanders.
- Water turbidity prevented visual observation, so fish were identified by capture location. Velocities were not quantified because currents swirled in both directions.
- Mean water depth at humpback chub capture points was 1.3 m. Substrate was mostly sand and boulders.
- Flows in Yampa Canyon fell below 2.83 m³/s in September 1989, limiting fish habitat to shallow riffles and deeper pools.

**Temperature**
- Ripe humpback chub were captured at water temperatures averaging about 19.5 °C (range 14.5–23 °C), close to the optimum egg incubation temperature of 20 °C (Marsh 1985).

**Diet**
- Gut and fecal evidence showed heavy use of hymenopterans and other terrestrial insects, including Mormon crickets, which chubs and carp were observed eating at the water surface in eddies.

## Key Findings

### Distribution and Abundance
- **Humpback chub:** 133 captured in total (130 in Yampa Canyon, 3 in Whirlpool Canyon). They made up about 11% of total catch and 7.3% (n = 51) of standardized angling and 11% (n = 58) of standardized electrofishing catch. Only one ripe fish, a male, was found in the Green River, at Whirlpool Canyon.
- **Spatial pattern:** 85% of humpback chub captures (n = 113) occurred in the upper 44.8 km of Yampa Canyon. Lower Yampa Canyon yielded only 17.
- **Roundtail chub:** 1482 captured across all reaches except Split Mountain Canyon and the upper 29 km of Lodore Canyon. They were at least three times more abundant in Yampa Canyon than in the Green River section, and 73% of all roundtail captures (n = 1085) came from the upper 44.8 km of Yampa Canyon.
- Juvenile humpback chub (88–228 mm TL) made up about 22% (n = 29) of captures. They were mostly taken by electrofishing in rocky shoreline runs and small eddies.

### Habitat Use
- During spring runoff, humpback chub were mainly caught in larger shoreline eddies (20–100 m²) downstream of boulders or upstream of rapids, and in smaller eddies (<20 m²) within shoreline runs.
- Adults (>230 mm TL) consistently selected seasonally flooded eddies dominated by low or negative velocities and influenced by river surges.
- Humpback chub were not collected in riffles or rapids.
- Roundtail chub occupied eddies, pools, shoreline runs, riffles and the lower parts of rapids.

### Movement and Site Fidelity
- Of 76 Carlin-tagged humpback chub (mean 312 mm TL, SD = 18), 11 were recaptured one week to two years later.
- Ten of those 11 were recaptured in the immediate vicinity of their original capture, and one about 8 km downstream.
- Eight of the 11 (73%) were recaptured in breeding condition at least once. No growth was detected in recaptured fish.
- The authors interpret this as fidelity to specific eddies across spawning seasons, although they did not confirm egg deposition in these eddies.

### Species Associations
- Humpback chub were captured with 7 native and 12 nonnative species. Dominant species in the standardized catch included [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], roundtail chub, common carp and channel catfish.
- In eddies occupied by humpback chub, angling caught 350 fish: roundtail chub about 45%, channel catfish 35%, and humpback chub 15%.
- Channel catfish were the most abundant nonnative fish in these eddies (n = 328, 47% of the angling catch).
- Introduced common carp and channel catfish were most abundant in Split Mountain Canyon.
- The most common native fish in electrofishing catch were flannelmouth sucker (n = 2049, 29%) and bluehead sucker (n = 1801, 26%).
- Other fishes in the Monument included [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], which were recorded in the Yampa River system.

### Spawning
- **Sample:** 39 humpback chub in reproductive condition were captured: 16 ripe males, 5 ripe females, and 18 tuberculate but nonripe fish. Of these, 37 came from Yampa Canyon (km 20.8–68.8) and 2 from Whirlpool Canyon.
- **Timing:** Ripe humpback chub were collected from mid-May to late June 1987–1989, following peak spring discharge. The authors presume spawning occurred only in the 5–6 week period after highest flows. Four humpback chub in breeding condition were also collected in July 1986.
- **Size:** Ripe males averaged 311 mm TL (n = 16, SD = 35, range 232–370 mm) and 229 g. Ripe females averaged 300 mm TL (n = 5, SD = 20, range 280–333 mm) and 230 g.
- **Roundtail chub:** Ripe roundtail chub (117 males, 6 females, 119 tuberculate nonripe fish) were captured during declining spring runoff. Ripe males averaged 344 mm TL (n = 117, SD = 24) and 329 g. Humpback and roundtail chub in breeding condition were collected together on 13 occasions, but ripe females of the two species were never collected together.

### Threats and Management
- **Nonnative fishes:** Channel catfish were abundant in eddies used by both chubs, and the authors observed bite-like abrasions on chubs that suggest predation by catfish. Humpback chub remains were found in channel catfish stomachs from the Little Colorado River. Common carp may affect native fish through predation on eggs.
- **Flow regime:** Declining Gila populations relative to earlier decades in Split Mountain and Lodore canyons are attributed partly to loss of historic temperature and flow regimes from regulated releases at [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], and to proliferation of nonnative fishes.
- **Recommendations:** The authors state that existing Yampa River flows may be singularly responsible for allowing chub persistence in the Yampa and Green rivers. They recommend that Dinosaur National Monument be treated as a refugium for native fishes and that Yampa River flows be protected, because altering them could reduce spawning habitat and increase competition and predation from introduced species.
