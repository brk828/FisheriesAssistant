# Life-History and Ecology of Amphigonopterus aurora and Other Viviparous Perches of California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #embiotocidae

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1921 paper by [[Carl L Hubbs]] presents the first detailed life-history study of *Amphigonopterus aurora*, the sole species of its genus, a viviparous perch of the [[Embiotocidae]] found in the rocky tide pools of the central California coast. The study covers habitat preference, breeding season, sex ratios, sexual differentiation, copulation and sperm storage, embryonic development, natal metamorphosis, fecundity, scale-based aging, and growth rate. Much of the analysis is compared with [[Micrometrus Minimus]] and [[Cymatogaster Aggregatus]]. The author proposes a new "metamorphic annulus" for aging and argues that delayed fertilization, natal male maturity, and growth compensation are key features of the life cycle.

## Background & Description

**Viviparity in fishes.** Fishes protect eggs in many ways (burial, nests, mouth brooding, capsules). Viviparity, development inside the mother, represents the most advanced form. In the [[Embiotocidae]] (suborder Holconoti, Acanthopterygii), yolk is greatly reduced, cleavage approaches the holoblastic type, and embryos are structurally modified. This contrasts with [[Poeciliidae]], where embryos rely on yolk and meroblastic cleavage persists.

**Taxonomy.** The family is considered relatively old, since its viviparity-related traits are shared by all species and generic differentiation came later. The origin of viviparity cannot be determined because the family's immediate relatives are unclear. Within the family, *Amphigonopterus aurora* and *Micrometrus minimus* form the subfamily Micrometrinae. The author recently reviewed the family taxonomically (Hubbs, 1918).

**Study species.** *Amphigonopterus aurora* is characterized by a longitudinal golden-orange band and tricuspid teeth with a relatively elongate intestine. Its diet is mainly algae, though young individuals feed on [[Copepods]]. Adults and young of *Micrometrus minimus* feed similarly, with young taking small crustaceans. History: the viviparity of embiotocids was first described by Dr. A. C. Jackson in 1853, and the group was later studied by Agassiz, Eigenmann, Jordan, and others.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat partitioning:** *A. aurora* lives in tidal pools and channels of medium tidal height, especially open pools with sand floors and little [[Eelgrass]] or algae. *M. minimus* lives at or below the lowest tide levels in deep, plant-filled pools. Its range extends south into warmer waters, including [[Lower California]].
- **Range:** Both species range from the San Francisco region to the reefs near Point Conception, where *A. aurora*'s habitat ends abruptly. *M. minimus* continues southward.
- **Schooling:** *A. aurora* forms schools in low-tide pools. During high tide, individuals take refuge in crevices to avoid surf-driven rock abrasion. Occasionally they leap from the water (observed at Pt. Purisima, August 13).
- **Coloration:** *A. aurora* is more silvery and lacks the dark pattern of *M. minimus*, consistent with its freer-swimming habits.
- **Parasites:** Like most reef fishes examined, *A. aurora* has low parasite loads, probably because of strong wave and tidal currents. Occasionally a slender [[Lernaeid Copepods]] was found on the pectoral or anal fins.
- **Associated fishes:** *Embiotoca jacksoni*, *E. lateralis*, *Hypsurus caryi*, and *Cymatogaster aggregatus*.
- **Breeding habitat of young:** After birth, young move into high-tide pools, which offer seclusion from predators and warm water for rapid early growth.

## Key Findings

**Breeding and sex ratio**
- The breeding season is summer. It begins shortly before June 1, peaks around mid-July, and has ended well before October 26. It is approximately synchronous with *Cymatogaster aggregatus*.
- *M. minimus* breeds somewhat later in central California, but earlier in warmer southern waters.
- In poisoned pools near Piedras Blancas, 264 females and 139 males were sexed in adults, giving a female-biased ratio of nearly 2:1. The author suggests this may reflect female longevity.
- Among young fish in autumn pools, the sexes were nearly equal (83 males, 82 females).
- Among 630 *A. aurora* embryos (mothers 1 to 4 years old), 337 were male and 293 female (100 males to 87 females). In embryos from yearling females only, the ratio was 100 to 86.5. For 150 *M. minimus* embryos, there were 76 males and 74 females.
- Sibling embryos in the same ovarian sheet showed no tendency toward uniform sex, so polyembryony does not occur.

**Sexual differentiation and natal male maturity**
- The number of anal rays, which differs by sex, is visible in embryos of about 12 mm, when total length reaches roughly 10 mm.
- At about 20 mm, testes enlarge and a secondary gland on the male anal fin begins to form. Both structures are fully developed at birth.
- Newborn males are sexually mature. Males of the two-year-old and four-year-old classes showed similarly developed testes.
- Two young males only slightly longer than birth size were already "spent" when collected in June.
- Natal maturity was not found in *Embiotoca lateralis*, where one-year-old males (111 and 116 mm, caudal length) were immature. The author suggests natal male maturity may be restricted to smaller embiotocids.
- Females first bear young at one year of age. The author notes that natal male maturity is unique among fishes, with partial parallels in protandric hermaphroditism and the early-maturing "grilse" male of Salmonidae.

**Copulation and sperm storage**
- The author records the first observed copulation of an embiotocid. The female contained one young partially protruding from the oviduct, of the same size as other newborns nearby.
- The author proposes that copulation occurs in summer, sperm is stored, fertilization occurs in winter, and about one year elapses between copulation and birth. Six lines of evidence support this, including:
  1. Parallels with *Cymatogaster aggregatus*.
  2. Autumn females carrying no young, and autumn males with small, non-functional testes.
  3. Smallest June embryos (12 mm) would need to grow to 30–35 mm, implying gestation of several months.
  4. The largest females are spent by July–August.
  5. Smaller yearling females bear fewer young, which implies delayed fertilization that allows size variation to develop.
  6. Newborn males are mature while associated only with newborn females, which do not bear young until the next season.

**Embryonic development and natal metamorphosis**
- Embryos are nourished mainly by nutritive ovarian fluid, circulated by cilia, and absorb nutrients through a hypertrophied hindgut lined with vascular villi.
- Respiration occurs mostly across the body surface and fin flaps, which have capillary networks.
- At birth, the body thickens, fins shorten, the hindgut normalizes, and color pattern develops. The author terms this change the "natal metamorphosis."
- Newborn *A. aurora* measure about 30–35 mm (caudal fin excluded), roughly one-third to one-fourth their mother's length. The smallest free-swimming young measured 29.0 mm, and the longest unborn embryo 35.5 mm. Variation within a brood is usually one to two mm, so the sexes are of nearly equal size at birth.
- *E. lateralis*: a 257 mm female carried 26 young (46–49 mm), and a 265 mm female carried 26 young (50–54 mm). A 200 mm female carried 10 young (55–58 mm). Newborns in June ranged from 43 to 58 mm.

**Fecundity by size and age**
- Table III (48 breeding *A. aurora* females, collected June 1916):
  - One-year-old females with 5–9 young: 76–94 mm (average 83 mm).
  - One-year-old females with 10–15 young: 85–103 mm (average 96 mm).
  - Three- or four-year-old females with 16–30 young: 121–129 mm (average 125 mm).
- The same pattern holds for *M. minimus* (Table II) and other embiotocids, as Eigenmann (1894) also found.
- Smaller, younger females breed later in the season. The author suggests this lets yearling females keep growing during the older females' breeding period, which increases their brood size.

**Aging by scales and the metamorphic annulus**
- Scales show seasonal rings (annuli) formed during winter, when growth slows. In embiotocids the annuli are marked by changes in circuli direction rather than only by crowding.
- Young collected October 26 and November 25 showed no winter annulus, and no annulus appears on the margin of gravid or spent females, so it is not a breeding mark.
- The author proposes the metamorphic annulus, a zone formed soon after birth in summer. It reflects a temporary growth check caused by the shift from absorbing nutrients through the body surface to feeding and breathing through the mouth and gills. The mark is usually more distinct in males, which have natal maturity.
- Some species show doubled annuli, which may cause occasional errors in aging three- and four-year-old fish.

**Size of the sexes and growth rate**
- Males are decidedly smaller than females at all ages, and the difference is largely postnatal. The author notes that male testes are small relative to body size, consistent with the conservation of stored sperm.
- Growth is fastest in the first half-year (birth to first winter). Estimated growth to the natal annulus is 20–32 mm. The author reports 69 mm growth from natal annulus to end of first winter (70 specimens), and growth is progressively smaller in later years. Growth never fully stops but declines with age.
- The four-year-old male's growth pattern matched the females'.
- Table VIII (20 females aged 3 to 6 years) indicates growth compensation, a pattern described by [[Gilbert]] for [[Sockeye Salmon]]. Fish that grow most in their first year tend to grow least later, and vice versa. The variation in computed length was 52–93 mm at the first winter and 97–124 mm at the third winter, so variation narrows with age.
- Growth-rate estimates from scales (via a direct-ratio formula) have known error sources: late scale formation, uneven growth across scale fields, and the proportionally larger head in young fish. These likely cause the computed natal-annulus lengths (20–32 mm) to underestimate observed lengths (29.0–35.5 mm).

**Summary of broader significance**
- The author argues that the combination of sperm storage, delayed fertilization, natal male maturity, and a metamorphic annulus makes the embiotocid life cycle unusual among fishes and worth further study. The author also recommends that Eigenmann's and Jordan's bibliographies be consulted for earlier work.
