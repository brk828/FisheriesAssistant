# Introduced Fishes of the Colorado River and Grand Canyon: A Literature Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #salmonidae #introduced-species

## Executive Summary

This draft literature review, prepared for the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program in 1991, examines the introduction of nonnative fishes into the Colorado River aquatic system through the Grand Canyon, and assesses their effects on native fish. The review was motivated by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations and the [[Glen Canyon Dam Environmental Impact Statement]], which could allow nonnative species to gain an ecological advantage over native populations. Of 19 introduced species collected between Glen Canyon Dam and Lake Mead, the author judges seven to be established in numbers sufficient to affect the native fauna. The author concludes that native fish populations have been greatly diminished by a combination of environmental change caused by the dam and competition and predation by introduced species, and that the post-dam fish community is still stabilizing.

## Background & Description

The review synthesizes historical and current records for the Colorado River through the Grand Canyon. Native fish fauna consisted of only eight species, highly adapted to a variable, turbid environment with high endemism (Minckley, 1982). Four native species are federally listed as endangered: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Bonytail and Squawfish are believed extirpated from the Grand Canyon reach, and the Razorback Sucker is rare.

The review addresses each introduced species in turn, covering its introduction history, spawning requirements, food habits and documented impacts. Survey data are heterogeneous: nine comprehensive ichthyological studies since 1958 used different gear and goals, so catch rates cannot be directly compared, and the author relied on researcher interpretations of abundance (see Table 1 of the source).

Introduced species discussed in detail include:

- [[Common Carp]] (first introduced by the U.S. Fish Commission in the 1890s)
- [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Red Shiner]] (the latter spread from bait bucket introductions)
- [[Killifish]] (likely a bait bucket introduction before 1938)
- [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Black Bullhead]]
- [[Striped Bass]] and [[Walleye]]
- [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]]

Incidental species (e.g., Coho Salmon, Golden Shiner, Largemouth Bass, Green Sunfish, Utah Chub, Threadfin Shad, Yellow Bullhead) are summarized briefly. [[Rainbow Smelt]] is a proposed introduction into Lake Powell.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pre-dam conditions:** Spring runoff peaks averaged 2400 m³/s, with secondary peaks from summer thunderstorms. The river carried heavy sediment loads and had a constantly changing substrate. Temperatures ranged from near-freezing winter lows to summer highs of about 25°C.

**Post-dam conditions:** Since 1963, [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Lake Powell]] have controlled flows. Sediment load at Lees Ferry dropped from an average of 1500 ppm pre-dam to 7 ppm. Releases are much cooler and less variable, rarely exceeding about 16°C in the mainstem after leaving the dam, which restricts reproduction of warmwater species and limits native [[Humpback Chub]] spawning to a few tributaries. Clear releases allow more light penetration, increasing primary production. The base of the food web is the green alga [[Cladophora glomerata]] and diatoms, with the introduced amphipod [[Gammarus lacustris]] as a key food item.

**Tributaries:** The [[Little Colorado River]] is a critical spawning and nursery habitat for native fishes, but it also supports catfish, carp and fathead minnows that compete with and prey on young natives.

**Lake Mead lower reach:** This section is noted as nutrient-poor and fish-depauperate, possibly limiting the forage base that would allow predators to colonize.

## Key Findings

- **Native fish status:** Squawfish last reported 1975; Bonytail extirpated; Razorback rare (one 1984 capture near Shinumo Creek); Humpback Chub locally common around the Little Colorado River. Speckled Dace, Bluehead Sucker and Flannelmouth Sucker are stable and common.
- **Carp:** Among the most abundant fishes in the river. Young-of-year captures verified successful spawning in the Little Colorado River. Carp feed on eggs and larvae of natives, though predation on native eggs is only speculative.
- **Red Shiner:** Highly competitive; displaced Spikedace and Loach Minnow in other systems. Now one of the most abundant fishes in Lake Mead.
- **Fathead Minnow:** Unlikely to be piscivorous; competes with young natives in backwaters. Holden and Stalnaker (1975) judged it not a serious threat.
- **Channel Catfish:** Widespread; catch rates appear to have declined since dam closure. Stomach contents included native fishes, and predation on Humpback Chub and juvenile Razorback Suckers has been documented.
- **Striped Bass:** Introduced to Lake Mead in 1969 to prey on Threadfin Shad, and reproducing in Lake Powell since 1979. Considered rare in the Grand Canyon, with most captures near Havasu Creek and below. Reports suggest possible seasonal migration from Lake Powell during high-flow spillway releases. Striped bass are a proven piscivore in reservoirs; the author notes that water temperatures in the river are at the lower survival limit for eggs, which may limit establishment.
- **Walleye:** Currently common in Lake Powell; rare in the Grand Canyon. Highly piscivorous and effective in clear water.
- **Trout (Brook, Brown, Rainbow):** Stocked widely and now numerous, especially in the Glen Canyon reach. Rainbow Trout are reproducing and have been documented eating native fishes (Speckled Dace, Bluehead Sucker larvae, one Humpback Chub). Brown Trout feed heavily on suckers and chubs. Brook Trout are dependent on stocking and contribute little to the fishery.
- **Mechanisms of impact:** Six mechanisms (competition, predation, inhibition of reproduction, environmental modification, transfer of parasites and disease, hybridization) are present in the Grand Canyon except hybridization. Anchor worm (*Lernaea cyprinacea*) has been found on juvenile natives in the Little Colorado River and Kanab Creek.
- **Declines:** Evidence of complete elimination of native species by exotics is limited; the replacement of Speckled Dace by rainbow trout in Tapeats Creek is the notable case.
- **Community stability:** The author suggests the fish community is stabilizing, but the stability is "very tenuous," given fluctuating hydropower discharges and continued trout stocking.
- **Management implications:** Cold dam releases currently restrict nonnative warmwater species, but warming releases to aid Humpback Chub could allow warmwater predators from Lake Mead to invade. Managers are advised to exploit the stable community to increase native recruitment while maintaining physical buffers against nonnative encroachment.
- **Proposed introduction:** Rainbow Smelt into Lake Powell, to supplement declining Striped Bass forage. Stated to be likely to establish throughout the reservoir, with entry into the river limited by barriers over 2 feet high. Effects on native fishes are considered probable if young forms enter the main river, but unstudied.
- **Research gaps:** The author identifies unanswered questions about why Humpback Chub spawn mainly in the Little Colorado River, and why adults are not more abundant in the lower, turbid river reach.
