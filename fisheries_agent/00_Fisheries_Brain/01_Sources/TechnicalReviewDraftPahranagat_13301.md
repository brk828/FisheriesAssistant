# Pahranagat Roundtail Chub Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #recovery-planning

## Executive Summary

This technical review draft recovery plan addresses the critically endangered [[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta jordani*), a thermal spring-endemic cyprinid restricted to [[Pahranagat Valley]], [[Lincoln County]], Nevada. Listed as endangered in 1970, this subspecies represents a relict population from the Pleistocene when the pluvial [[White River]] connected to the [[Colorado River]] drainage. The species has declined from its historic range in Crystal, Hiko, and Ash Springs to a single population of 37-45 adults confined to approximately 2,300 meters of the [[Pahranagat River]] on the Burns Ranch. Primary threats include habitat alteration through concrete channelization for irrigation and introduction of exotic species including [[Convict Cichlid]] (*Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Shortfin Molly]] (*Poecilia mexicana*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), and [[Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*). The recovery strategy emphasizes artificial propagation, ecological research, habitat restoration, and population reestablishment across the historic range.

## Background & Description

The [[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]] is taxonomically aligned with the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) complex of the [[Colorado River]] drainage. First described by Tanner (1950) from three individuals from Hiko Spring and three from Crystal Springs, it is most similar to *G. r. robusta* but differs in having more scales in, above, and below the lateral line, being less elongate, and displaying greenish coloration with black blotches. Adults reach approximately 25 cm in length.

The species is one of six native fishes in [[Pahranagat Valley]], coexisting historically with [[White River Springfish]] (*Crenichthys baileyi baileyi*), [[Hiko White River Springfish]] (*C. b. grandis*), [[Pahranagat Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda altivelis*) (now extinct), [[White River Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus velifer*), and [[White River Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus intermedius*). All native species have experienced dramatic declines, with the spinedace extinct by the 1950s and the desert sucker by the 1960s.

The entrance of the chub's progenitor into [[Pahranagat Valley]] occurred during the Pleistocene Epoch when the pluvial [[White River]] was tributary to the [[Colorado River]]. The species is endemic to thermal waters and was never collected before habitats were dramatically altered beginning in the late 1800s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Characteristics**: Adult chubs occupy protected pools in spring outflows with highly specific conditions: water temperatures 27-30°C, current velocities averaging 0.03 m/s (maximum 0.18 m/s), dissolved oxygen 5.9-6.4 mg/L, pH 8.1, water hardness 184-234 mg/L, and alkalinity 160-170 mg/L. Dominant vegetation includes *Spirogyra* sp. (spring/summer), *Chara zeylanica*, *Compsopogon coeruleus*, *Najas marina*, and various diatoms.

Juvenile chubs occupy less specific habitats, typically runs with average current 0.23 m/s and depth 0.54 m, though they tolerate velocities to 0.35 m/s and depths from 0.73-2.3 m. Substrate varies from mud to firm sand depending on current velocity.

**Life History**: Spawning is presumed to occur in spring when adults migrate from their holding pool; young-of-year first observed in May. Nothing is known of spawning habitat requirements, fecundity, or egg incubation period. An estimated 200 juveniles observed in summer 1980 indicates successful spawning, but dramatic autumn decline and stable small adult population indicate extremely limited recruitment. Hardy (1981) speculates low recruitment results from difficulty during dietary transition as juveniles approach maturity.

**Feeding Ecology**: Adult chubs are primarily herbivorous based on visual observations in the Burns Ranch holding pool. Juveniles observed in Ash Springs outflows are primarily insectivorous. This ontogenetic dietary shift is known in other *Gila robusta* complex members.

**Population Dynamics**: From late April through January, the entire adult population (37-45 individuals) remains localized in a single pool approximately 10 meters long. Seasonal migrations occur during spawning period. Growth rates, age and size at maturity, and longevity remain unknown.

**Threats**: Habitat alteration through diversion and concrete lining of spring outflows has eliminated most historic habitat. Only 2,300 meters of unlined channel on Burns Ranch remains occupied. Introduction of non-native species has caused direct predation and competition. The 1971 transplant of 20 adults to the [[Bureau of Land Management]] endangered fish facility at [[Shoshone Ponds]] near Ely, Nevada survived several years but failed to reproduce; that population no longer exists.

## Key Findings

**Recovery Goal**: Prevent extinction and restore to non-endangered status by: (1) developing artificial propagation techniques; (2) conducting life history and ecological studies; and (3) securing and reconstructing habitats within historic range.

**Critical Research Needs** (Priority 1, 2-year duration each):
- Artificial propagation development using both artificial streams and traditional hatchery approaches
- Reproductive studies: fecundity, spawning season, spawning habitat, migration characteristics, egg incubation time, nursery areas
- Food and feeding habitat studies for fry and adults
- Age and growth studies: size/growth rates at all life stages, longevity determination
- Preferred habitat determination for juveniles and adults (physical and biological preferences)
- Population dynamics: annual/seasonal fluctuations, recruitment rates, mortality rates and causes
- Interspecific interactions with native fishes (*Crenichthys* spp., *Rhinichthys osculus velifer*)
- Interactions with exotic species: ecology comparison, competitive/predatory effects (biological, physical, physiological)
- Effects of habitat alteration: physical disturbance and exotic species presence

**Habitat Restoration Strategy**:
- Identify suitable habitats on and off [[Pahranagat National Wildlife Refuge]]
- Prepare Ascertainment Reports for: Pahranagat River (Burns Ranch), Ash Springs, Crystal Springs, Hiko Spring
- Develop aquatic habitat management plans for each site
- Remove competing and predatory exotics
- Prevent reintroduction of exotics
- Reestablish native fish habitat conditions

**Reintroduction Plan**: Following habitat restoration, reintroduce chubs to Ash Springs, Crystal Springs, Hiko Spring, and waters on Pahranagat NWR. Monitor all populations (including existing Burns Ranch population) to assess size, structure, and viability.

**Protection Measures**:
- Enforce 1973 Endangered Species Act provisions
- Protect groundwater aquifer discharging in Pahranagat Valley to ensure spring discharge integrity
- Coordinate with Lincoln County Board of Commissioners
- Establish public information program: brochures, interpretive signs

**Ecosystem Management Approach**: The 1973 Endangered Species Act requires conservation of ecosystems, not just individual species. Recovery must address the entire [[Pahranagat Valley]] aquatic ecosystem, including candidate species [[White River Springfish]] and [[Hiko White River Springfish]] (under consideration for endangered listing) and [[White River Tryonia Snail]] (*Tryonia clathrata*, formerly proposed as threatened). Successful ecosystem restoration may secure these species without requiring listing.

**Historic vs. Present Range**: Historically occupied approximately 30 miles of stream habitat across three springs (Crystal, Hiko, Ash) and small lake at valley's south end. Currently restricted to 2,300 meters of Pahranagat River on Burns Ranch. Last observed in Crystal and Hiko Springs in early 1950s.

**Implementation Timeline**: Recovery plan outlines 3-year propagation program and multiple 2-year research initiatives, with 1-year timelines for acquisition reports and management plan development. Delisting criteria: implementation of all recovery actions with viable populations established across multiple sites within historic range.
