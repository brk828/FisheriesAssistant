# Ethological Isolating Mechanisms in Goodeid Fishes of the Genus Xenotoca

#ichthyology #marine-biology #goodeidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by John Michael Fitzsimons of the [[Louisiana State University]] Museum of Zoology, tests whether premating behavioral barriers keep three freshwater goodeid species of the genus [[Xenotoca]] apart where they live together. Laboratory crosses produced fertile hybrids from all combinations of stocks, yet sympatric fish never mismated in choice trials, while allopatric fish sometimes did. The author attributes this to refined [[Ethological Isolation]] in sympatry, reinforced by reduced hybrid fitness. The excerpt also begins with a reference list (Krumholz et al. 1962; Lindsey 1954; Mayr 1963; Snedecor and Cochran 1967, among others) that belongs to preceding material, not to this study.

## Background & Description

The [[Goodeidae]] is a family of about 35 species of cyprinodontiform freshwater fishes restricted to the [[Mesa Central]] of Mexico. Unlike their livebearing relatives (anablepids, jenynsiids and poeciliids), goodeids show true [[Viviparity]]. In all but one species, yolk is exhausted early and embryos are nourished by the mother through [[Trophotaeniae]], structures analogous to mammalian placentae. Taxonomy of the family was built largely on these structures and ovarian anatomy in the [[Hubbs and Turner Classification]] (1939), and later revisions were relatively few. The author's earlier revision of [[Characodon]] and [[Xenotoca]] (Fitzsimons 1972) showed that courtship displays were often better taxonomic indicators than morphology, because they reflect features the fish themselves use to recognize species.

The genus *Xenotoca* includes three species:

- **[[Xenotoca Variata]]** (Bean): the type species and perhaps the most widely distributed goodeid. It occurs in lentic and lotic habitats across several basins, including the Rio Santa María (San Luis Potosí), Presa El Gigante (Aguascalientes), the Río Grande de Santiago, Río Lerma and Río de la Laja.
- **[[Xenotoca Eiseni]]** (Rutter): a disjunct distribution. It occurs in the Río Compostela and above 600 m in the Río Grande de Santiago basin of Nayarit and northwestern Jalisco, and at several localities in the Ríos Tamazula and Tuxpan of southern Jalisco.
- **[[Xenotoca Melanosoma]]** Fitzsimons: known only from Jalisco, with its type locality in the Río Tamazula. It ranges into streams and ponds south of Guadalajara, east to Lago de Chapala, and into the Ríos Ameca and Grande de Santiago basins.

*Xenotoca variata* has not been collected sympatrically with the other two species. *X. eiseni* and *X. melanosoma* co-occur at several localities in the Ríos Grande de Santiago and Tamazula.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mode of isolation.** Because no natural hybrids are known, the author examines premating barriers. Mayr's (1965) framework names two possibilities for sympatric species: potential mates do not meet (seasonal or habitat isolation), or they meet but do not mate (ethological isolation). The paper finds no evidence for the first:

- Females of both species bear young year-round in aquaria.
- Pregnant females and neonates of both species are collected at the same times of year, from both sympatric and allopatric populations.
- Ecological requirements are similar, and both species can be taken in the same seine haul.

Behavioral differences in mating are therefore considered the main barrier maintaining the two potentially hybridizable species where they are sympatric.

**Courtship as a barrier.** In goodeids the male initiates pair formation with displays ranging from broadside presentations to elaborate dances that expose color and elongate fins. Females respond with [[Head-Wagging]] if receptive, or with rejection behaviors (evading, hiding, attacking, pseudo-feeding) if not. Female behavior was very similar across 21 goodeid species observed, whereas male displays varied strongly between species and were therefore the more useful taxonomic and isolating signal. The displays appear inherited rather than learned, since they were identical in fish reared in isolation, with only conspecific experience, or with only heterospecific experience.

## Key Findings

**1. Hybridization outcomes**
- Fertile laboratory hybrids were readily produced from *X. eiseni* and *X. melanosoma* in forced crosses lacking conspecific mates.
- In choice crosses, sympatric stocks never hybridized, while allopatric fish frequently mismated.
- *X. variata* crosses with its congeners and artificial inseminations were unsuccessful.
- The abstract reports that hybrid inferiority, shown by reduced survival and inability to compete for mates, is essential to divergence in sympatry.

**2. Courtship repertoires (male displays)**
- ***X. variata*** (Rio Santa María and Presa El Gigante): seven displays, including Head-Flicking (the most frequent), Lateral T-formation, Round Dance, Half-Dance, Lateral Tilting, Lateral Wheeling and Oblique. The author detected no individual or populational variation in display form between these two populations. Population differences in display frequency and effectiveness were small (P = 0.01).
- ***X. eiseni*** (Rio Tamazula and Manantial El Sacristán): five displays at Rio Tamazula (Loop Dance, Half-Dance, Lateral T-formation, Lateral Wheeling, Oblique) and four at Manantial El Sacristán (no Oblique). The Loop Dance is the most elaborate, with one to six figure-eight movements. Rio Tamazula males show exaggerated sigmoid flexure and strong tilting, while Manantial males hold their bodies straight with little or no tilting.
- ***X. melanosoma*** (Presa de la Vega and Rio Tamazula): six displays at Presa de la Vega, including the Zig-zag Dance, Half-Dance, Lateral T-formation, Dart and Lateral Sidling. Rio Tamazula males lack sigmoid posturing and tilt rarely, never beyond 10°. The Dart display, unique to Rio Tamazula fish, consists of rapid forward-backward movements with the dorsal fin raised and lowered.

**3. Reinforcement in sympatry**
- The author interprets the marked differences between sympatric and allopatric populations of *X. eiseni* and *X. melanosoma* as reinforcement of premating isolation where the species co-occur.
- In *X. eiseni*, the Loop Dance was the only display leading directly to copulation in the sympatric Rio Tamazula population. In allopatric Manantial El Sacristán fish, other displays led to copulation in nearly one-third of observations.
- Displays and their effectiveness differed significantly between populations of both *X. eiseni* and *X. melanosoma*.

**4. Methods and sample sizes**
- About 240 hours of courtship observations were tape-recorded.
- A minimum of 20 males and 20 females from each population were studied, with half the pairs raised in complete isolation.
- Fourteen pairs of laboratory hybrids were available for mate selection experiments.
- Discrimination tests used a three-compartment aquarium. Test males and females were scored for conspecific or heterospecific choice, and each test male was run consecutively against the same choice females to control for reproductive condition.
- A closed sequential test grid (Cole 1962) set the number of animals and tests required for predetermined significance levels (P = 0.9 for discrimination; P = 0.5 for nondiscrimination).

**5. Conclusions**
- Premating ethological isolation is considered the main barrier keeping *X. eiseni* and *X. melanosoma* distinct in sympatry.
- Sympatric populations show the most refined courtship differences and the strictest mate discrimination, consistent with selection against costly hybridization.
