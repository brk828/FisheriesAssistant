# Life History of Red Shiners (*Notropis lutrensis*) in the Skunk River, Central Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #leuciscidae

## Executive Summary

This 1971 study by K.D. Laser and K.D. Carlander, published in the *Iowa State Journal of Science* (Vol. 45, No. 4, pp. 557-562), describes the life history of [[Red Shiner]] populations in the [[Skunk River]], a sand-bottomed, moderately flowing stream in central Iowa. Field sampling ran from June 19 to August 14, 1968, across ten stations between Ames and Colfax. The red shiner was the most abundant fish taken. The authors report seasonal diet shifts toward plant material during high water, territorial behavior in breeding males, intermittent spawning with 485-684 eggs per female, and a population dominated by Age 0 and Age I fish, with very few reaching Age II.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), a cyprinid minnow described as one of the most abundant fishes in western and southern Iowa streams, and common to occasionally common in natural and artificial lakes (Harlan and Speaker 1956).
- **Ecological profile:** Cross (1967) noted the species tolerates [[Intermittent Stream Flow]], high turbidity, and environmental variability typical of Plains streams, and is most numerous where few other fish occur.
- **Prior knowledge:** Little had been published on its life history at the time of the study.
- **Study area:** About 50 km of the Skunk River, from 100 m above old U.S. 30 at Ames to U.S. Interstate 80 near Colfax. The river is a typical midwestern, moderately flowing stream with a sand bottom, occasional silt and gravel, and scoured pools around woody debris and structures.
- **Conditions:** Water levels in June and July 1968 were higher than normal and the water was quite turbid.
- **Funding and affiliation:** Iowa Agriculture and Home Economics Experiment Station (Project No. 1373), the [[Iowa Cooperative Fishery Unit]], and a National Science Foundation research participation grant for high school teachers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods**
- 3,709 red shiners were collected at ten stations using a 5 × 1 m, 4 mm bar mesh [[Minnow Seine]]. Three hauls were made per station weekly for six weeks.
- Fish were preserved in the field in 10% formalin.
- Preservation effects were measured on 20 live fish: after 24 hours, preserved standard length was 0.96 of live length and preserved weight was 1.03 of live weight. All analyses used preserved total length (mm) and weight (g).
- Standard length averaged 0.78 of total length across 150 fish.

**Food habits**
- Whole digestive tracts were examined under a 30X dissecting scope. Most food was hard to identify, so only plant and animal material were recorded.
- Plant material was mostly algal, with little higher-plant tissue.
- Insect material fell from 52% (July 1) to 17% (July 30), while plant material rose from 48% to 83% over the same period. The share returned to 25% insect and 75% plant by August 5.
- Starrett (1950) attributed this shift to increased turbidity and water volume limiting insect larvae availability. Paloumpis (1956) reported an entirely insect-based diet for red shiners in Squaw Creek, Iowa.

**Behavior (laboratory and field)**
- In 5-gallon aquaria, breeding males were aggressive and chased females. A single male often defended half of a tank, though he roamed the whole tank.
- Territoriality was suppressed in a 20-fish tank, where no aggression was seen, while aggression appeared in tanks of only 3-5 fish.
- No substrate preference (sand versus rock) was noted in aquaria.
- On July 12, 1968, about 20 red shiners were observed hovering near a submerged bare tree, feeding by picking at its surface.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction**
- Sex ratio: 582 males to 904 females overall. Among fish under 40 mm, 240 males to 257 females. More females than males reached 40 mm.
- Breeding colors were observed from June 19 to August 14. Paloumpis (1956) recorded breeding males in Squaw Creek from April 27 to mid-August.
- Fecundity: 50 gravid females held 485-684 eggs each, with no correlation to length or weight.
- At least three egg size classes were present, with two predominant in larger females. The authors interpret this as **intermittent spawning** through summer.
- Young-of-the-year (30-35 mm) peaked on July 1-3, suggesting the main spawning peak was late May or early June.

- Most of the population by August was Age 0. Few fish reached Age II, and most spawned and died at Age I.

**Length-weight relationship**
- Based on 1,486 red shiners over 26 mm: Log W = -0.700 + 2.56 Log L (W in g, L in mm total length).
- The observed slope of 2.56 falls below the isometric expectation of 3.0. Possible causes include slimmer large (spent) fish, or upward bias in weights of small fish from residual water.
- Deleting fish under 34 mm raised the slope to 2.75; deleting those under 38 mm raised it to 2.80.
- Preservation (3% heavier and 4% shorter) implies the live slope would be slightly lower than 2.56.
- Live K(TL) equals 0.86 times preserved K(TL). Table 3 gives mean weights and K(TL) across 12 length classes (17-20 mm to 75 mm; n from 1 to 403 per class).

**Conclusions**
- Red shiners in the Skunk River follow a short life span dominated by Age 0 and I fish, with intermittent summer spawning.
- Diet shifts from insects to plant matter during high-water periods.
- Small-fish length-weight analysis is sensitive to preservation and water-retention effects.
