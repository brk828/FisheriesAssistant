# Montana Grayling in Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #experimental-stocking #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1939 report by J. W. Leonard of the Institute for Fisheries Research documents an experimental stocking of [[Montana Grayling]] (*Thymallus montanus*) in a small northern Michigan lake, following the extinction of the native [[Michigan Grayling]]. The study aimed to determine whether Montana grayling could survive and thrive in Michigan waters and to investigate why the native species disappeared. Five thousand fingerling grayling were planted in October 1936 in an 11-acre lake after complete removal of existing fish populations, primarily [[Yellow Perch]]. Regular monitoring revealed successful survival and growth, though slower than in native Montana waters. The fish demonstrated voracious feeding habits, consuming primarily predacious aquatic insects and showing seasonal dietary shifts based on water temperature.

## Background & Description

The [[Michigan Grayling]] had become extinct by 1939, with the last known population in the [[Otter River]] yielding no specimens despite intensive sampling efforts by Fish Division personnel. The species joined the [[Passenger Pigeon]] and [[Heath Hen]] as extinct fauna. Taxonomic differences between Michigan and Montana grayling species remained unclear, with only slight morphological differences noted and possible physiological differences unexplored.

Historical stocking efforts between 1926 and 1936 included nearly 2 million fry and approximately 500,000 yearling Montana grayling planted in various Michigan streams and lakes, with minimal documented success. Earlier attempts dating to 1900 had similarly failed when Michigan grayling could not be successfully propagated.

The experimental lake measured less than 11 acres in surface area, with no inlet or outlet. Prior to 1936, it supported an overabundant population of undersized, scrawny yellow perch that had outstripped their food supply. The lake's thermal stratification provided cold deep water during summer when surface temperatures exceeded grayling tolerance limits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several hypotheses were proposed for the Michigan grayling's extinction: disruption of spring spawning by annual log drives during the lumber era; competition from introduced [[Brook Trout]]; unfavorable ecological changes from deforestation and increased cultivation; or overfishing due to the species' unwary nature and eagerness to take any bait.

The experimental lake was located on state-owned land, facilitating patrol and closure to angling. In September 1936, all existing fish were removed to eliminate competition. The grayling were hatched June 24, 1936, and 5,000 fingerlings were planted in October 1936.

Stomach content analysis revealed seasonal feeding patterns:
- Spring and fall: Large quantities of terrestrial insects, indicating surface feeding
- July and August: Feeding in cooler, deeper regions on tiny crustaceans ([[Water Fleas]]) and aquatic insects
- Year-round: High proportions of predacious forms including [[Dragonfly Nymphs]] and [[Diving Beetles]], despite abundant herbivorous organisms like [[Mayfly Nymphs]]

Water temperature strongly influenced feeding behavior, with surface feeding occurring only when surface waters remained cool. During July and August warming, fish retreated to deeper, cooler strata.

## Key Findings

**Growth and Survival:**
- Montana grayling successfully survived and grew in Michigan lake conditions
- Growth rates were slower than in native Montana waters
- At various ages, fish ranged from 4.625 inches (one year) to 11 inches (three years) total length
- Fish maintained good body condition (described as "plump" and "waxing fat")

**Feeding Ecology:**
- Not one stomach examined was completely empty, indicating voracious feeding behavior (contrasting with frequently empty stomachs in [[Trout]] and pan fish)
- Preference for predacious invertebrates over herbivorous forms
- Seasonal dietary shifts correlated with thermal stratification
- Surface feeding limited to periods of cool water temperatures

**Management Implications:**
- Stream stocking proved uniformly unsuccessful based on historical plantings
- Lake stocking in competition-free environments showed promise
- Future stocking would require: complete eradication of existing fish fauna in designated lakes, annual stocking (grayling reproduce only in streams), special enforcement, restricted seasons (spring emergence to early summer, possibly fall), low catch limits
- Potential for special grayling license to offset management costs
- Species characterized as "intolerant" requiring special conditions

**Research Questions Identified:**
- Role of interspecific competition in stocking success
- Compatible species for cohabitation
- Potential for growth acceleration through water fertilization or environmental manipulation
- Physiological differences between Montana and Michigan grayling species

The experiment demonstrated that Montana grayling could survive in Michigan but required specialized management approaches fundamentally different from typical fisheries practices.
