# Basin-Range Structure in Western North America: A Review

#geology #tectonics #basin-range-structure #earth-science

*Note: This document is a geological review, not an ichthyological or aquatic-ecology study. It contains no fish, invertebrate, plankton, or aquatic habitat data; the fields above are therefore "N/A" except for location.*

## Executive Summary

J. H. Stewart's Geological Society of America Memoir 152 review synthesizes the origin and development of [[Basin-Range Structure]] across more than 1,500 km of the western Cordillera of North America. The structure consists of alternating elongate mountain ranges and alluviated basins produced by late Cenozoic extensional faulting that follows older Mesozoic, early-Tertiary, and early-to-middle Tertiary igneous and deformational patterns. Extension is estimated at 10% to 35% of the province's original width, and locally as much as 100%. The province is marked by anomalous upper mantle, thin crust, high heat flow, and regional uplift. Stewart reviews four competing origin theories and concludes that the combined characteristics best fit a [[Back-Arc Spreading]] model, possibly accelerated by the cessation of subduction and the development of a [[Transform Boundary]] along the western margin of North America.

## Background & Description

Basin-range structure is one of the most distinctive geologic features of western North America and is more extensively developed there than anywhere else in the world. The [[Basin and Range Province]] extends from southern Oregon and Idaho through most of Nevada and parts of California, Utah, Arizona, and New Mexico into northern Mexico. In the United States the province is about 500 to 800 km across in the [[Great Basin]] of Nevada and western Utah.

Two end-member structural models are debated:

- **Tilted-block (listric fault) model:** Large blocks rotate along downward-flattening faults, so the uplifted part of each block forms a mountain and the downtilted part a valley.
- **Horst-and-graben model:** Downdropped grabens form valleys and upthrown horsts form mountains. Stewart's earlier work (Stewart, 1971) favored this model, which is supported by high-angle earthquake first motions and thick valley alluvium.

Stewart concludes that the province probably involves elements of both models. Surrounding provinces include the [[Colorado Plateau]], a tectonically stable region, and the [[Rio Grande Rift]], which forms the east margin of that plateau.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although the document is geological, it describes the physical setting that shapes the region's basins and drainage:

- **Elevation:** Great Basin valley floors are typically 1,300 to 1,600 m; mountain crests 2,000 to 3,000 m, locally about 3,600 m. Southeastern California, southern Arizona, and southwestern New Mexico sit 500 to 1,000 m lower.
- **Drainage:** Drainage in the Great Basin is predominantly internal, so eroded sediment is trapped in closed basins. Late Cenozoic lakes such as the Lahontan and Bonneville basins (Pleistocene lakes) occupied some of these depressions.
- **Crust and mantle:** The crust is 20 to 35 km thick in the province (versus 35 to 50 km on the Colorado Plateau and Rocky Mountains). Upper-mantle velocities below 7.9 km/s may indicate partial melting.
- **Heat flow:** Values above 2 µcal·cm⁻²·s⁻¹ characterize the province, compared with an average of about 1.5.
- **Seismicity:** Most earthquake focal depths are less than 15 km, coincident with a crustal low-velocity zone. Earthquakes concentrate in the [[Intermountain Seismic Belt]] and the Walker lane.

No aquatic species, fish communities, or water-chemistry data are reported.

## Key Findings

**1. Structural character and extent**
- Block-faulted basin-range structure extends over more than 1,500 km; in the U.S. it is about 500 to 800 km wide in the Great Basin.
- Mountain ranges are typically 15 to 20 km across, separated by alluviated valleys of comparable width.
- Valley fill ranges from a few hundred metres to more than 3,000 m. Structural relief between valley floors and adjacent peaks is generally 2,000 to 5,000 m, locally about 6,000 m.
- About two-thirds of Great Basin range tilts are to the east and one-third to the west. Tilts are generally a few degrees to about 30°.

**2. Amount of extension (estimates vary widely)**
- Hamilton and Myers (1966): 50 to 100 km of east-west extension (8% to 18%) in late Cenozoic time.
- Stewart (1971): about 72 km (13%) using the graben rule.
- Proffett (1977): more than 100% locally at Yerington, Nevada; about 50% in the western and eastern Great Basin.
- Wright and Troxel (1973): 30% to 50% in the Death Valley area.
- Thompson and Burke (1974): about 100 km total (18%) across the Great Basin, based on 5 km of extension in Dixie Valley.

**3. Uplift-thinning relationship (Table 1-1)**
- Extension of 5% to 40% corresponds to crustal thinning from 30 m to 183 m... specifically, calculated thinning ranges from 639 m (5% extension) to 5,206 m (40% extension) in the model slab.
- Stewart estimates late Cenozoic uplift within the Great Basin of 2,000 to 3,000 m.

**4. Timing**
- Calc-alkalic to basaltic volcanic transition in most of the Great Basin occurred about 17 m.y. ago, marking the onset of extension.
- Large sedimentary basins were well defined about 11 to 13 m.y. ago; basins resembling today's formed about 7.5 m.y. ago.
- The grain of present topography probably did not develop before 10 m.y. ago.
- Extension may have started earlier in southern Arizona and New Mexico (transition 21 to 37 m.y. ago).

**5. Plate-tectonic framework**
- About 29 m.y. ago the East Pacific Rise intersected the subduction zone, producing a transform system along the western margin of North America.
- The [[San Andreas Fault]] forms the major right-lateral transform component.

**6. Competing origin theories**
- **Wrench faulting:** Oblique tensional fragmentation within a right-lateral shear belt (Atwater, 1970). Explains strike-slip zones and the transform link, but not regional uplift or the broad extent of the shear.
- **Back-arc spreading:** Mantle upwelling behind a subduction zone (Scholz et al., 1971). Best explains high heat flow, thin crust, anomalous mantle, uplift, and mid-Cenozoic silicic volcanism.
- **East Pacific Rise subduction:** Convection and lateral spreading on the flanks of the rise (Menard, 1960). Largely discounted, since basin-range structure extends far east of the ridge remnant in the Gulf of California.
- **Mantle plumes:** Narrow rising plumes (Morgan, 1971; Wilson, 1973; Suppe et al., 1975). The [[Yellowstone Hotspot]] is the only area credibly underlain by a plume, and even that is controversial.

**7. Stewart's conclusion**
- The six key characteristics are: (1) low upper-mantle seismic velocity indicating partial melting; (2) thin crust; (3) high heat flow; (4) regional uplift and extension; (5) prior Mesozoic/early-Cenozoic deformation and middle-Cenozoic silicic volcanism; and (6) position inland of a transform boundary.
- Together they best fit back-arc spreading. Spreading may have been accelerated by slackening confining pressure after subduction ceased. Right-lateral shear may explain motion along the [[Walker Lane]], which accommodates an estimated 130 to 190 km of right-lateral displacement.

**8. Open questions**
- The three-dimensional character of basin-range structure at depth remains unresolved.
- Better understanding will require integrated surface geology, deep seismic profiles, earthquake first-motion studies, and aftershock distributions.
