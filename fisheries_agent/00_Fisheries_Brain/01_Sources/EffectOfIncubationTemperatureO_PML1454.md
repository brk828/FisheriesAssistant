# Effect of Incubation Temperature on Survival of Embryos of Native Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This experimental study by Peter C. Marsh (1985) quantifies how [[Incubation Temperature]] affects [[Embryo Survival]] and hatching in four endemic "big river" fishes of the [[Colorado River]]: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. All four are near extirpation in the wild. Eggs were fertilized at about 19°C and incubated at 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30°C. Survival and hatch peaked at about 20°C. Embryos died before hatching at 5, 10 and 30°C, and anomaly rates rose at 15 and 25°C. The author suggests that cold [[Hypolimnetic Reservoir Release]] waters may limit reproduction in the wild, but that other factors, such as [[Predation]] on young, also need investigation.

## Background & Description

The four species form the endemic big-river fish fauna of the Colorado River. All are listed as threatened or endangered by one or more of six western states, and all except the razorback sucker are classified as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

- **Historic range:** Razorback sucker, bonytail chub and Colorado squawfish occurred throughout the large rivers of the basin. Humpback chub occurred in the middle and upper Colorado and Green rivers.
- **Present range:**
  - Razorback suckers occur only above [[Grand Canyon]] and in [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]] and Lake Havasu.
  - Bonytail chubs persist in the [[Green River]] and Lake Mohave.
  - Humpback chub populations persist at the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]] and in Desolation Canyon of the Green River.
  - Colorado squawfish are restricted to the upper river in Colorado, Utah and Wyoming.
- **Proposed causes of decline:** Dewatering, mainstream dams and impoundments, altered flow, channel morphology and water quality, and introduced exotic fishes. Lowered water temperatures from hypolimnetic reservoir releases have also been proposed as curtailing reproduction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Published spawning and hatching temperatures**

| Species | Inferred spawning temperature | Hatchery hatching range |
|---|---|---|
| Razorback sucker | 6–20°C (field observations) | Within about 12–26°C (pooled hatchery studies) |
| Bonytail and humpback chubs | 18–21°C | Within about 12–26°C (pooled hatchery studies) |
| Colorado squawfish | 18–23°C | Within about 12–26°C (pooled hatchery studies) |

Development time is inversely related to temperature, and survival declines at temperatures far from 20°C.

**Water quality of the test water:** Water came from two wells at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico. It was high in salts, especially sulfate and nitrate, and had high conductivity. The author notes that the ionic composition is not markedly different from historic and present summer flows in the Colorado and Gila rivers. The study does not quantify the microhabitat of natural spawning sites.

**Thermal regimes in the field**
- The Salt River above reservoirs exceeded 15°C from April to mid-October and 20°C from early May to early October, with reported annual extremes of 3–34°C in tributaries.
- Mainstream Colorado temperatures before the closure of Hoover Dam (1935) ranged from 10 to 30°C, similar to today's unaffected reaches.
- Downstream of Glen Canyon Dam, temperatures were about 9–15°C year-round, with excursions rarely above 15°C.
- Tailwater temperatures below Hoover Dam ranged from 9.0 to 15.0°C.
- Upstream of Jensen, Utah, on the Green River, annual ranges (0.0–22.2°C) were similar to the unaffected upper basin.

Dam releases can create cool summers and warm winters downstream, and Lake Mohave has had no documented successful recruitment of bonytail or razorback since about 1950. The author attributes reproductive failure partly to cool water temperatures, and notes that the decline of these fishes persists in thermally suitable reaches, pointing to other factors.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Apparatus: six 22 L glass aquaria held at 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30°C, using cold-water inflow (about 1.0 L/h) and thermostatic heaters. The 30°C aquarium was static, and all tests at that temperature were concluded within 24 hours.
- Incubation chambers: 6–8 per aquarium, 3–6 replicate chambers per aquarium, each stocked with 100 fertilized ova (18–36 chambers per species).
- Broodstock: wild fish from Lake Mohave and the Little Colorado River, plus hatchery-bred fish from Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery. Spawning was induced with human chorionic gonadotropin or carp pituitary extract. Adults were not acclimated to test temperatures.
- Measurements: fertilization at 40×, staging, hatch timing, swimup (attainment of neutral buoyancy), percent survival and hatch, and temperature (6–10 times daily). Dissolved oxygen remained within 3% of saturation.
- Development rate: V = 10⁶ / P, where P is hours to 50% hatch.

**Razorback sucker**
- Hatch was highest at 20°C (35%) and 25°C (29%), and significantly lower at 15°C (19%). All eggs died at 5, 10 and 30°C.
- Peak hatch fell from 216 h (15°C) to 84 h (25°C).
- Normal prolarvae were 6.5–8.6 mm TL. Swimup occurred at 103–312 h.
- Stunted and deformed fry were 8.1% of hatch at 20°C, versus 14.5% at 15°C and 32.2% at 25°C.

**Bonytail chub**
- Hatch was statistically equivalent at 15°C (3.5%) and 20°C (32%). Hatch at 25°C was 0.5%, and no hatch occurred at 5, 10 or 30°C.
- Peak hatch fell from 204 h (15°C) to 103 h (20°C).
- Prolarvae were 6.0–6.3 mm. Swimup took 148–396 h and lengthened with decreasing temperature. Abnormality rates (0–47% of hatch) were not related to temperature.

**Humpback chub**
- Hatch was highest at 20°C (60%) and significantly lower at 15°C (0.8%) and 25°C (about 2%). All embryos died at 5, 10 and 30°C.
- Peak hatch fell from 156 h (15°C) to 72 h (25°C).
- Abnormal fry were significantly more frequent at 15°C than at 20 or 25°C.

**Colorado squawfish**
- Hatch was 27% at 20°C in trial I. In trial II it was 27% at 20°C and about 9% at 25°C. All ova died at 5, 10 and 30°C (trial I) or 5, 10 and 15°C (trial II).
- Peak hatch was at 78–108 h at 20°C.
- Abnormal fry were about 25% of hatch at 25°C, more than twice the 11% at 20°C.
- Prolarvae reacted to bright light with frantic swimming, which lasted 15–30 seconds.

**Development rates**
- Development rates were similar across species: about 4.4–5.1 at 15°C, 8.4–9.9 at 20°C and 12.4–18.8 at 25°C (values partly garbled in the source scan).
- Linear regressions differed significantly between some species pairs, which the author attributes to physiological differences.

**Conclusions**
- Survival and hatch peaked near 20°C. Mortality increased sharply at 5, 10 and 30°C, and anomalies rose at 15 and 25°C.
- Low water temperatures may limit reproduction in the wild, although successful hatching is not precluded.
- Other explanations for the decline in thermally suitable reaches, especially predation on young, should be examined.
- Uncontrolled factors (broodfish condition, wild versus hatchery origin, induced versus natural spawning and differences from natural incubation conditions) may affect absolute values, but are less likely to alter relative differences among temperatures.
