# Minnesota Fishing Guide: Game Fish, Angling Practice, and Water Safety

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-game-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1950s publication from the [[Minnesota Department of Conservation]] is a popular angling and fish-care guide rather than a research report. It promotes the state's lakes and streams, gives boating and shore-fishing safety rules, explains how to clean and preserve fish, and offers species-by-species fishing advice. Its core message is that Minnesota offers diverse fishing, that sound safety and handling practices protect anglers and fish, and that knowledge of each species' habits determines success. A large appendix lists the best-known fishing waters by regional area, with the species each usually holds and recommended baits.

## Background & Description

- **Publisher and authors:** Issued by the Bureau of Information, Minnesota Department of Conservation, Saint Paul. Contributors include Commissioner Dr. George A. Selke and several outdoor writers and fisheries staff (including Alfred Nelson, Hjalmar O. Swenson, Dr. John B. Moyle, and [[Robert Page Lincoln]] on muskellunge).
- **Game fish featured:** The front matter profiles [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*), [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*), [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy masquinongy*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and [[Pumpkinseed]] (*Lepomis gibbosus*). The text also discusses [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Catfish]], [[Bullhead]] and other panfish.
- **Structure:** Introduction by the Commissioner; water-safety rules; fish care for food and mounting; hook safety; species-specific "How to Catch" articles; a regional index of roughly 800 named lakes and streams (the directory spans dozens of regional sections, from [[Grand Marais Area]] to [[Worthington Slayton Marshall Area]]), each with species lists and baits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The guide describes habitat preferences that shape where anglers should fish:

- **Largemouth bass** tolerate warmer water and are abundant in shallow, warm southern lakes, especially bays choked with lily pads and bottom vegetation.
- **Smallmouth bass** favor colder lakes north of the Twin Cities, deeper gravel bottoms, and running, cool, spring-fed rivers.
- **Walleyes** occur in water with rocky, gravelly, or sandy bottoms, though mud flats can hold fish at certain seasons. The author states they are found in the majority of Minnesota lakes regardless of size. They move into shallow shorelines to feed at night and school together.
- **Northern pike** are described as solitary, rarely found in deep water, and concentrated in weed beds, rushes, and stream mouths.
- **Muskellunge** are associated with the Mississippi River (Bemidji to Anoka), Leech and Winnibigoshish lakes, and shore areas with pads and grasses at dusk.
- **Trout** need cool, clean, spring-fed water and are found in North Shore streams, the Whitewater area near Winona, and the Straight River near Park Rapids.
- **Lake trout** feed along shores and reefs in spring, then move to depth after about June 1 as water warms.
- **Panfish** gather at weed-bed edges, and early-spring black-mud bays warm first to spawning temperatures.
- **Catfish** favor rivers, notably the Mississippi (including [[Lake Pepin]]), the St. Croix, and the Minnesota.

## Key Findings

**Fishing resource and promotion**
- Minnesota is described as having "10,000 lakes—11,007 lakes to be exact" and "25,000 miles of streams and waterways."
- The Commissioner cites Fish and Wildlife Service data that Minnesota has more licensed fishermen than any other state.
- An angling report describes 51 large muskies brought into the landing at the Federal Dam on [[Leech Lake]] in two days, with 200 silver muskies reported taken within two weeks, weights given from 18 to 40 pounds.
- Panfish angling provides sport for "perhaps a million persons in Minnesota each year."

**Record catches (as printed)**
- The record table lists [[Walleye]] at 16 lb 8 oz and [[Largemouth Bass]] at 8 lb 15 oz. Column alignment in the scanned table is partly garbled, so other entries (such as northern pike and rainbow trout values) cannot be reliably assigned.

**Water safety**
- Several dozen fishermen are described as dying in water accidents each year; the author states 90 to 99 percent of accidents could be avoided by following safety rules.
- Key rules: check that rented boats are sound and not overloaded; carry a safety belt or cushion for each person; keep low and avoid shifting positions on the water; never stand to cast, land fish, or lift a minnow bucket; head for shore at the first sign of an approaching storm.
- If a boat swamps or capsizes, stay with it. The guide notes people have been rescued one to three hours after clinging to an overturned boat, while many drowned attempting to swim ashore.
- Fouled anchor ropes and fishlines on the outboard motor have caused fatalities; shore anglers should beware drop-offs, slippery banks, and stumbling objects.

**Fish care and preservation**
- Terminology: whole ("in the round"), drawn (gutted), and dressed (scaled, gutted, finned, headed, tailed).
- Fish should be packed in ice promptly for transport, and washed in cold water after dressing.
- Fish for mounting should be wrapped in cloth, kept on ice, and taken to a taxidermist promptly, protecting scales and skin.
- Bass should be skinned before eating because the lateral "mud-streak" under the skin imparts a muddy taste.

**Hook and handling safety**
- Carry wire-cutting pliers; push a barbed hook through and cut the barb if it catches skin or clothing.
- Never cast when someone is close behind you.
- Stun large fish on the head before removing a plug; never grab a hooked fish in the water (use a gaff or landing net).
- Bullheads and many fishes have dorsal and lateral spines; northern pike and muskies have needle-sharp teeth.
- For catch-and-release, wet hands before handling, minimize damage, and use barbless hooks.

**Angling methods by species**
- **Walleye:** Fish near the bottom and work upward, troll or cast at night in 3 to 4 feet of water, scout shorelines by day, and fish drop-off edges, bars, and reefs. The windward shore often produces better than the lee shore.
- **Northern pike:** Cast to the edge of weed beds from 35 to 50 feet away; use a light wire leader because the teeth cut line.
- **Muskellunge:** Combine trolling and casting with large jointed plugs or spoons; use a solid steel casting rod; fish topwater lures at dusk; approach quietly, ideally by canoe.
- **Lake trout:** Troll spoons on copper wire in spring; in summer use deep trolling or metal line. Use sharp, large triple hooks and avoid giving slack.
- **Bass:** Fish live frogs or minnows for largemouth; smallmouth prefer minnows and crustaceans. Best hours are dawn to mid-morning and late afternoon to dark.
- **Panfish:** Dry or wet flies, worms, or grubs; ice fishing has shifted toward sunfish in recent years.
- **Stream trout:** Dry flies in clear water, wet flies and nymphs in discolored water; the guide advises against salmon eggs.
- **Catfish:** Use worms or minnows in current, cork and sinker in calm water; the author describes the species as unpredictable and feeding by its own schedule.
- **Bullheads:** Use angleworms, liver, chicken entrails, or cheese on a simple pole.

**Regional directory**
- The appendix lists lakes and streams in the Grand Marais, Gunflint Trail, North Shore, Ely-Tower, Cloquet, Hibbing, Bemidji, Brainerd, Detroit Lakes, Alexandria, and Twin Cities areas, among others. Each entry names the species most commonly found there, and each section closes with recommended baits.
