# Physical Habitat Use of Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) in Southwestern Desert Streams, with Reference to Competition by Red Shiner (*Notropis lutrensis*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1989 research manuscript, submitted for peer review (the reviewer form is attached), by John N. Rinne of the Forest Service Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, Arizona State University. It quantifies the [[Physical Habitat Use]] of the threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), an endemic cyprinid of the [[Gila River Basin]], across several southwestern desert streams. It also compares that habitat use with published accounts of the introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) to assess possible competition for physical space.

## Background & Description

- **Spikedace** is a small cyprinid endemic to the Gila River basin in Arizona and New Mexico. It is listed as threatened by Arizona and federal agencies and as endangered by New Mexico.
- Prior literature was largely qualitative, covering distribution and taxonomy, with limited quantitative habitat data.
- The study was initiated to quantify the physical habitat requirements of spikedace in several desert streams, building on earlier work in [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Rinne and Kroeger 1988, 1989).
- **Study areas:**
  - *Aravaipa Creek*: small (3–5 m wide), often braided, hydrologically stable gravel-pebble stream with discharge of 0.5–1.0 m³/s, sampled seven times across seasons.
  - *Upper Gila River streams and Verde River*: larger (5–15 m wide), steeper and at higher elevation than Aravaipa; sampled in spring (upper Gila) and in April 1985 and December 1982–May 1985 (Verde).
- **Methods:** Fish were captured by electrofishing with a one-meter block net. Depth, velocity and substrate were measured on 21-point transects with a habitat sampler, with substrate scored in seven classes. Statistics included unpaired t-tests, ANOVA (Welch's test when variances were heterogeneous) and Tukey's multiple range tests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Spikedace occupied deeper, slower water than was available in larger streams, but velocity did not follow the stream-size pattern.
- Spikedace preferred eddies and shear zones, often in pools associated with the convergence of braids, along canyon walls, and below large boulders. These features provide reduced current and adequate depth for a water-column lifestyle.
- Gravel-pebble substrate with little sand was most frequent in these areas.
- Red shiner is a habitat generalist that tolerates turbid, warm, low-oxygen water, and it is often more abundant in western streams with low fish diversity. Its habitat preferences overlap with spikedace's, suggesting possible competition for physical space.
- The authors note that abiotic disturbance (flooding, land use) and biotic interactions both need evaluation before conclusions on competition can be drawn.

## Key Findings

- **Depth and velocity:** Spikedace occupied significantly deeper water than available in Aravaipa Creek (P < 0.05) and slower velocities (P < 0.05). Depth did not differ significantly among streams where spikedace was present (P = 0.60). Velocities used differed significantly among streams (P = 0.004).
- **Schools:** Transects with schools of 10 or more fish averaged 22.8 cm depth, versus 27.3 cm for transects with 2 or more fish. Transects with a single fish averaged 13.7 cm. Velocities at these transects averaged 28.7 cm/s and 17.7 cm/s compared with 55.6 cm/s at transects with a single fish.
- **Life stages (Aravaipa Creek):** Larvae, juveniles and adults occupied depths of 14.4 cm, 17.1 cm and 20.4 cm, respectively, but these differences were not significant (P = 0.11). Velocity and substrate use also did not differ significantly among life stages.
- **Substrate:** Spikedace used substrate significantly smaller than available in most sampling periods (P < 0.05), with gravel-pebble and gravel-rubble (15–110 mm) mixes frequent in the Gila streams.
- **Comparison with red shiner:** Mean depth used by spikedace (~20 cm) closely matched that reported for red shiner (~20 cm), while velocities reported for red shiner (0–7 cm/s) were much lower than those used by spikedace (~20–35 cm/s). The authors conclude that the two species may partition physical habitat and that co-occurrence studies are needed to determine actual competition.
- **Overall conclusion:** Quantitative data confirmed earlier, mostly subjective descriptions of spikedace habitat. The species favors deeper, slower water and eddies, shear zones and pools, with gravel-pebble substrate.
