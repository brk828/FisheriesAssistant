# Ecology of Mysis relicta in Twin Lakes, Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mysidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation report (REC-ERC-76-14, December 1976), based on a Colorado State University master's thesis by Ronald E. Gregg, studies the freshwater opossum shrimp [[Mysis Relicta]] in [[Twin Lakes]], Colorado. Mysis is the main food base supporting the lake trout fishery, so the study assesses how the planned [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]] could affect the population. Mysis were studied for vertical migration, life history, spatial distribution and response to turbulence and turbidity, using field sampling and laboratory experiments. The central conclusion is that substantial numbers of Mysis will be entrained by the plant and a significant share will not survive, although the long-term population effect is unknown.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** Mysis relicta Loven belongs to class Crustacea, order Mysidacea, family Mysidae. It is a fresh- and brackish-water glacial relict, closely related to the marine *Mysis oculata*. It is a large zooplankter with a circumpolar arctic distribution along the southern edge of Pleistocene glaciation.

**Introduction to Twin Lakes.** Twin Lakes was stocked on October 3, 1957 from a single thermos of shrimp obtained from Clearwater Lake, Minnesota (600 to 1,000 mysids). The population grew extensively, and Twin Lakes became the source for transplants to 57 lakes in Colorado, Wyoming, Utah, and the Dakotas. The lake trout fishery was estimated to be worth $500,000 per year, and 87 percent of lake trout examined in 1974-75 contained Mysis.

**Lake setting.** The lakes lie in a glacially scoured basin on the eastern side of the Sawatch Range, below Mt. Elbert (4395.5 m). Maximum depths are about 28 m (upper lake, 263 ha) and 27 m (lower lake, 737 ha). Bottoms are mainly glacial flour (rock flour), and the lakes are dimictic.

**Project context.** Twin Lakes is part of the Fryingpan-Arkansas Project (Public Law 87-590, 1962). The pumped-storage plant generates and pumps water between the Mt. Elbert Forebay and Lower Twin Lake, with a planned two 100-MW units.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vertical migration.** Mysis are mainly benthic by day and migrate into the water column at night. Migration intensity varied by month: it was greatest in June, July and August, dropped sharply in September, rose slightly in October, and was high again in November. Juveniles migrated earliest and furthest, while adults mostly reached upper strata only after dark. Light and temperature were identified as the main controls, consistent with [[Beeton]] and [[Teraguchi]].

**Distribution.** The benthic population is clumped (contagious), fitting a [[Negative Binomial Distribution]] (chi-square 16.19 against a table value of 31.41 at 20 df, so a significant fit). Mysis preferred deeper, colder water in summer, and moved into shallower water in fall. Lower Twin Lake held significantly higher densities than the upper lake.

**Habitat and water quality.** Water quality is very good: conductivity 52–91 µS/cm at 25 °C, dissolved solids 32–88 mg/L, and calcium 8.8–12 mg/L. Summer bottom temperatures reach about 7 °C and surface about 14 °C. Dissolved oxygen falls to 1.5–2.5 mg/L below 21 m by late summer. Benthic habitat is dominated by glacial flour, with pondweed (*Potamogeton amplifolius*) and stonewort (*Chara globularis*) in the vegetated zone.

**Pumped-storage operation.** Plant operation could alter the lakes through turbulence in the powerplant area and forebay, turbidity from resuspended glacial flour, and changes in thermal stratification. Daily water fluctuation is predicted at 0.23 m (one unit) and 0.47 m (two units) on average. The tailrace bed will sit at 7.6 m depth. Pumping at 172.7 m³/s with a 9-hour cycle would move about 6.2 percent of Twin Lakes' volume (3.1 percent with the enlarged reservoir).

**Other pumped-storage studies cited.** Entrainment mortality at other plants has been documented, including copepods at Millstone Point (70 percent mortality) and 40 percent mortality of entrained phytoplankton and zooplankton from pump damage. Neomysis americana showed no significant viability loss under simulated Cornwall pressures.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and migration**
- Simultaneous sampling at surface, two intermediate depths and bottom (three nets plus a benthic sled on one cable) was adopted to capture the whole population.
- Migration is greatest in summer and during darkness; mature mysids rarely reached the surface in July and August.
- Juveniles reached the surface first, remained longest and descended last. During June, July and August, 3–8 mm mysids dominated surface samples.
- An unusual October surface aggregation dominated by large mature females may indicate breeding-related social behaviour; this could not be confirmed.

**Life history (two-year life cycle in Twin Lakes)**
- Young are released in spring (about April to June) at 3–4 mm; growth averages about 1 mm per month in the first summer and about 0.33 mm per month in the first winter.
- Males breed in fall (October–November), die after breeding, and reach a maximum of 17 mm. Lifespan is 18–20 months.
- Females mature by September at about 1.5 years, brood over winter, and some survive into a third year. The largest recorded size is 22 mm.
- Mysis in Twin Lakes take about 16 months to mature, longer than temperate populations (about 1 year), consistent with slow growth in cold oligotrophic lakes.

**Distribution and density**
- Dive observations recorded densities of 10–50 per m², up to 500–1000 per m² in pockets, including 500–1000 per m² among pondweed. Mysis were never seen in the water column during the day.
- Across 190 trawl samples over two years, the variance exceeded the mean, indicating clumping.
- Summer shallow (under 15 m) samples had significantly lower mean density than deep samples; fall distribution was equal between depths.
- Mean density declined in 1975 relative to 1974. The report attributes the upper-lake decline to oxygen depletion and heavy-metal release from sediments, linked to mine tailings in the Upper Lake Creek watershed.

**Turbulence and turbidity experiments**
- Turbidity (up to 1470–1558 APHA units) had no significant effect on mortality.
- Turbulence (K = 0, 1.9, 3.0, 5.2) significantly increased mortality, with a linear regression coefficient of 0.998 between K and mean mortality.
- Mortality increased linearly with time over an 8-day trial; a temperature rise to about 9 °C in two replicates appeared to increase mortality.
- In the trough test, mysids avoided the turbulent quarter (chi-square 410.64 versus control 4.62), suggesting an avoidance reaction.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Substantial numbers of Mysis will be entrained, mainly from the 2200–0600 hour window, when about 17.6 percent of the population in the area was present (range 1–56 percent).
- Entrained mysids will suffer pressure, mechanical abrasion and turbulence, and turbulence could last up to 23 hours per day.
- Avoidance may reduce entrainment in later cycles, but the population in pump plant bay will probably stay reduced.
- Mysis regeneration takes about 2 years, so losses will be replaced more slowly than for most zooplankton. Whether regeneration will exceed plant-caused attrition is unknown.
- Operating schedules could be adjusted using the migration data to avoid the most vulnerable hours.
