# Use of Glass Minnow Traps in Trout Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #minnows

## Executive Summary

This 1948 reprint from *Michigan Conservation* (by C. Troy Yoder, [[Institute for Fisheries Research]], [[Michigan Department Of Conservation]]) describes a legalized method for harvesting bait minnows from designated trout streams using [[Glass Minnow Traps]]. A shortage of bait minnows for anglers, caused by heavy harvest of open waters and closure of trout streams to commercial dealers, prompted 1944 experiments by [[George Washburn]]. The trapping study found that glass traps caught very few game fish (22 of 5,442 animals) and that captured trout were released alive. The article also sets out the 1948 regulations and practical trapping methods.

## Background & Description

**Problem.** Michigan had an acute shortage of good bait minnows, especially in July and August, when demand for larger minnows rises. More than 1,300 retail minnow dealers in the state drew on open waters, which were largely stripped of minnows before July 1. Trout streams, by contrast, held unharvested minnow populations because all trout streams were closed to commercial dealers out of concern that seining and trapping would harm trout.

**Study design.** In summer 1944 (June, July, and August), twelve commercial-type glass traps were used in 24 streams classified as trout water, located in 13 counties in the Lake Michigan and Lake Huron drainage systems. Sites included well-known trout streams such as the [[Au Sable River]] (North and East Branches), Betsie, Clam, and Rifle Rivers, as well as marginal and less-known streams. The published details of the work appear in Washburn (1945), Institute for Fisheries Report No. 984.

**Trap description.** The trap is a glass cylinder with a funnel opening at one end and a screw cap at the other for removing minnows. The article describes two common sizes:
- The "two-gallon" trap: about 13 inches long, 23 inches in circumference, with a 1-1/8 inch funnel opening. More durable and efficient.
- The "one-gallon" trap: smaller, less durable.
- A flat-base variant with a seven-inch square base, which is more stable in faster currents.

Both have metal carrying handles. The author recommends a compartmented wooden carrying case because glass traps break easily.

**Measurement.** Relative minnow abundance was expressed as minnows caught per trap-hour.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Minnow abundance.** Catch rates varied widely. The highest was 109 minnows per trap-hour in the Upper [[Thunder Bay River]] (Montmorency County), and the average across streams where minnows were taken was approximately 30 per trap-hour. Catches also varied within a single stream, depending on habitat.

**Habitat and placement.** During the day minnows usually occur in pools or near obstructions. The article's sketch identifies productive trap sites as:
- The head or side of deep pools
- Undercut banks, which give shelter and concentrate minnows
- Areas in weeds, where minnows watch for food drifting from upstream
- Above logs or other obstructions

Traps set in the midst of dense weed beds consistently caught fewer fish. Swift water or vegetation can also reduce catches.

**Trout-minnow interactions.** The author argues that minnows and trout share similar natural food, mainly aquatic insects, and that minnows may be carnivorous toward small trout fry. He concludes that competition from minnows for insects outweighs their limited food value to trout, and that an excess of minnows may be detrimental to trout populations. He states that more research is needed.

**Selectivity.** Trapped minnows were mostly over two inches long, and none over six inches were captured, although larger minnows were seen. The author views this selectivity as protecting a brood stock of larger minnows for spawning.

## Key Findings

**Catch composition (1944 study)**
- Total catch: 5,442 fish, of which 5,420 were minnows and 22 were game fish.
- Minnows represented 12 species, seven of which occurred frequently. In descending order of numbers: [[Common Shiner]], [[Creek Chub]], [[Hornyhead Chub]], [[Longnose Dace]], redbelly dace, bluntnose minnow, and northern finescale dace.
- The three most common species (common shiner, creek chub, hornyhead chub) totaled 4,429, or 81 percent of the catch.
- Game fish ratio: one game fish for every 247 minnows.
- Game fish taken: six fingerling trout, seven [[Yellow Perch]], one [[Largemouth Bass]], two pumpkinseed sunfish, three longear sunfish, and three [[Rock Bass]]. All were alive and could be released unharmed.

**Trout impact**
- The Au Sable system yielded 1,810 minnows and five trout, a ratio of 362 minnows to one trout.
- The author extrapolates that harvesting 200,000 minnows from a stream like the Au Sable would take about 550 trout fingerlings. He considers this an estimate from one sample, and says the total trout catch over the whole experiment was too small to reduce angler success.
- Trout are not destroyed by glass trapping. The traps have smooth interiors and sufficient water flow for oxygen, so trout can be released in good condition.

**Trapping methods**
- Set traps in a moderate current, with the perforated cap upstream and the funnel downstream. Rapid current can wash traps away or break them against rocks. Still water does not carry bait out of the trap.
- Bait with finely ground soda crackers or bread, or rolled oats. A small handful per trap lasts about 30 minutes. A trap that is not well baited rarely takes minnows.
- Some trappers leave a live minnow in the trap when emptying and rebaiting.
- Empty every trap at least once every 30 minutes. Trapped minnows escape once food is gone.
- A common commercial method is a trap-line of 10 to 12 traps set along the stream. The trapper empties and rebaits each trap in turn, about 30 minutes per round. Poor-catch traps are moved a few feet. Concentrating traps works better than scattering them over one to two miles.
- Holding in 20-gallon punctured cans in the stream keeps minnows in better condition than holding them on a truck for hours in warm weather.

**1948 regulations (issued November 1, 1947)**
- The Legislature authorized the Director of Conservation to designate trout streams, or portions of them, open to glass minnow trapping.
- Open season: June 15 to September 5, 1948, inclusive. The late opening lets the more important bait minnows spawn, and it reduces conflict with trout fishermen.
- Rules: traps no longer than 24 inches with openings no larger than 1-1/8 inches in diameter; hook and line only otherwise; each trap marked with a white float marker at least 2 x 6 inches bearing the owner's name and address; owner or representative in immediate attendance; cereal bait only.
- Open waters are listed by the Conservation Department and vary by local conditions. Anglers should check with the local conservation officer.

**Conclusions**
- Glass trapping is considered effective for obtaining desirable-sized minnows, and less damaging to streams and minnows than seining.
- Reported trout losses were low, and the author concludes that glass trapping cannot remove enough trout to decrease angler success.
- The author recommends harvesting minnows from trout streams with glass traps as a means to relieve the bait shortage, while noting that research on minnow effects on trout is still needed.
