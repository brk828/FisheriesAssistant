## Executive Summary

This 1982 taxonomic and distributional study by Burr and Mayden documents the historical occurrence of the [[Cypress Minnow]] (*Hybognathus hayi*), one of the rarest members of the Illinois fish fauna, presumed extirpated from the state since its last collection in 1940. The authors report six previously unpublished distributional records from museum specimens (collected primarily in 1936–1940 by Aden C. Bauman and Milton B. Trautman), substantially expanding the known historical range of the species in Illinois beyond the single previously published record from the Little Muddy River. The paper also provides morphometric and meristic characters — most notably mouth-to-eye distance and anal-fin-origin-to-caudal-base distance — that yield 100% accurate discrimination between *H. hayi* and its sympatric congener, the [[Silvery Minnow]] (*Hybognathus nuchalis*). The authors argue that suitable habitat persists in the Cache and Big Muddy River systems, suggesting *H. hayi* may still occur, albeit rarely, in southern Illinois, and they recommend further targeted surveys.

## Background & Description

### Diagnostic Meristic Features (H. hayi, per Fingerman & Suttkus 1961)
- Pharyngeal tooth count: 0,4–4,0
- Anal fin rays: 8
- Body-circumferential scale rows above lateral line: 9–13
- Body-circumferential scale rows below lateral line: 10–15
- Lateral-line scales: 34–41

### Body Shape, Proportion, and Pigmentation (H. hayi vs. H. nuchalis)
| Character | *H. hayi* | *H. nuchalis* |
|---|---|---|
| Body shape | Compressed | Subterete |
| Profile | Angular | Rounded |
| Scale pigment pattern | Distinctly diamond-shaped | Less distinct |
| Anterior lateral-band melanophores | Small, only slightly larger than dorsal melanophores | Large, noticeably larger |
| Median dorsal stripe (pre-dorsal) | Narrower, lighter | Broader, darker |
| Intestine length (relative to SL) | Relatively short | Relatively long |
| Snout | Broadly rounded, does not project beyond upper lip | More projecting |
| Mouth | More upturned | Less upturned |
| Eye | Larger | Smaller |
| Dorsal/anal fin position | More anteriorly placed | More posteriorly placed |

### Quantitative Diagnostic Regressions (100% Accurate Discriminators)
- **Mouth-to-eye distance vs. standard length**: *H. nuchalis* consistently greater than *H. hayi* at all sizes.
  - *H. nuchalis*: y = 0.0543x + 0.5119
  - *H. hayi*: y = 0.0455x + 0.3573
- **Anal fin origin-to-caudal base distance vs. standard length**: greater in *H. hayi* (reflecting more anteriorly placed anal fin).
  - *H. hayi*: y = 0.3330x + 0.0507
  - *H. nuchalis*: y = 0.2934x + 0.4243
- **Snout length vs. anal-fin-origin-to-caudal-base distance**: *H. nuchalis* has longer snout in adults, though overlapping at smaller sizes (allometric).
  - *H. nuchalis*: y = 0.2438x + 0.2406
  - *H. hayi*: y = 0.1601x + 1.0239

- *H. hayi* averages **fewer trunk vertebrae** and **more caudal vertebrae** than *H. nuchalis*, though ranges overlap.
- Genus-level traits: [[Hybognathus]] species are mostly silvery/brassy, possess long coiled intestines, and reach a maximum ~150 mm total length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Sluggish backwaters of streams, cypress lakes/ponds, and drainage ditches; soft bottoms of sand overlain with silt/mud and detritus; accumulations of logs and brush/debris common; submerged aquatic vegetation typically absent; shallow water (~0.5 m depth typical at collection sites).
- **Substrate behavior**: Collectors noted sinking ~20 cm into mud while seining, indicating a very soft, unconsolidated bottom typical of occupied sites.
- **Geographic range**: Western Florida panhandle to eastern Texas, north to Illinois and Indiana; largely restricted to the Gulf Coastal Plain except in Illinois and Indiana, where the species transcends that boundary — representing the **northernmost periphery of its range**.
- **Sympatry**: *H. hayi* is sympatric only with *H. nuchalis* in Illinois, and the two species were collected together at only one locality (Horseshoe Lake Dam).
- **Illinois drainages documented**: [[Cache River]], [[Big Muddy River]], [[Clear Creek]], and [[Horseshoe Lake]] drainages (Pulaski, Union, Perry-Washington, Washington, Jefferson, and Alexander Counties).
- **Co-occurring species of note**: *Notropis emiliae* (co-collected historically with *H. hayi* but absent in 1980s resurveys); *Notropis lutrensis* (an aggressive invasive-like species newly present at historical sites, likely linked to increased turbidity).

## Key Findings

- **Range extension**: Six previously unpublished Illinois localities for *H. hayi* were documented (1936–1940 collections), showing the species was historically more widespread in the state than the single previously published record (Little Muddy River, misattributed in Smith 1979 to Trautman rather than the actual collector, Aden C. Bauman).
- **Extirpation status**: No specimens collected in Illinois since 1940; the species was not included on the state's endangered/threatened list (Kenney 1978) because it was already presumed extirpated rather than merely rare.
- **Resurvey results**: The authors resurveyed all seven historical Illinois localities but failed to collect *H. hayi*, despite finding generally similar overall fish species composition to Bauman's era and high abundance of other species.
- **Co-occurring declines**: *Notropis emiliae* was also notably absent from resurvey collections, paralleling a documented range contraction attributed to siltation and loss of aquatic vegetation — likely analogous stressors affecting *H. hayi*.
- **Habitat degradation drivers**: Siltation, stream channelization, pollution (e.g., oil field pollution noted in the Little Muddy River drainage), impoundments, and fluctuating water levels are implicated as primary causes of decline for *H. hayi* and other native Illinois cyprinids, consistent with broader patterns described by Smith (1971, 1979).
- **Regional status comparison**:
  - Missouri: Presumed rare/extirpated since early 1940s, but rediscovered in 1979 in southeastern Missouri.
  - Indiana: Not collected since early 1940s; no dedicated search conducted.
  - Arkansas: Indeterminate status (data deficient).
  - Kentucky: Initially indeterminate, later shown to be more common than previously thought (Burr et al. 1980).
  - Oklahoma: Known from a single locality (Mountain Fork River drainage); presumed rare.
  - Florida: Listed as threatened due to restricted range in the western panhandle.
  - Central range (Mississippi, Tennessee, Alabama, Texas, Louisiana): Sporadic but more evenly distributed.
- **Conservation recommendation**: Because suitable habitat persists in the Cache and Big Muddy River systems and around Horseshoe Lake, and because historical collections sometimes yielded large numbers of specimens, the authors conclude *H. hayi* may still persist in Illinois in low numbers, and recommend targeted follow-up surveys of suitable habitat.
- **Taxonomic context**: The genus [[Hybognathus]] was historically considered to comprise four species but is now recognized as containing six (with *H. argyritis* and *H. regius* elevated from synonymy/subspecies status), five of which occur in Illinois.
