# Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge: A Plan for the Future

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This planning document from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] sets out the long-range objectives, management direction and public-use program for [[Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge]], established in 1984 primarily to conserve threatened and endangered species. The plan is explicitly described as a flexible guideline rather than a fixed prescription. Its central management goal is to restore the refuge to a natural ecosystem, reversing agricultural alterations made in the 1960s and 1970s, while protecting the [[Endemic Species]] assemblage of spring-fed desert habitats, and offering wildlife-oriented recreation and education compatible with that purpose.

## Background & Description

The refuge covers 23,407 acres when complete and lies in Nye County, Nevada. It supports 25 plant and animal species found nowhere else in the world, including four fish, nine plants, one aquatic insect, one mammal and ten snails. Twelve of these 25 species are listed as threatened or endangered. Water emerging from approximately 30 seeps and springs creates the habitat that sustains this concentration, which the plan describes as the greatest concentration of endemic species of any local area in the United States.

Land acquisition history is central to the plan. [[The Nature Conservancy]] purchased 12,613 acres in 1984 and transferred them to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, which received authority to establish the refuge. The document's current land-status legend lists USFWS lands of 12,614 acres, 2,681 acres of public domain to be withdrawn, 1,550 acres of private land, 6,562 acres to be managed cooperatively with the [[Bureau of Land Management]], and 40 acres managed by the [[National Park Service]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The plan attributes the isolation and speciation of the refuge's fauna to the end of the [[Pleistocene Ice Age]], about 12,000 years ago, when receding interconnected lakes and rivers separated fish populations across the Southwest. [[Paiute Peoples]] were the earliest known inhabitants, drawn by fresh water and wildlife. Carson Slough was once the largest marsh in southern Nevada, with high concentrations of waterfowl, before it was drained for agriculture.

Agricultural development in the 1960s and 1970s diverted most spring outflow streams to irrigation, and wells pumped groundwater for crops. A proposed community for 50,000 people also threatened the area. The plan notes that management has since stopped groundwater pumping and banned off-road vehicles to protect endangered species, and that restoration will remove concrete irrigation ditches, redirect spring outflows to former streams, allow farm fields to revert to native vegetation, and restore historic marshes.

## Key Findings

**Management Objectives**
- Preserve, restore and enhance endangered and threatened species in their natural ecosystems, aiming for populations large enough to be considered [[Species Recovery]].
- Perpetuate the migratory bird resource and maintain natural diversity and abundance of fauna and flora.
- Provide education on fish and wildlife ecology and humans' role in the environment.
- Offer safe, wildlife-oriented recreation compatible with refuge purposes.

**Protected Species**
- Four endangered fish: Devil's Hole pupfish, Warm Springs pupfish, Ash Meadows Amargosa pupfish and Ash Meadows speckled dace.
- One endangered aquatic insect (Ash Meadows naucorid) and seven endangered plants, including Ash Meadows blazing star, Ash Meadows milk-vetch and Ash Meadows ivesia.
- The [[Sensitive Species Program]] covers about eleven taxa, including the Ash Meadows vole, Devil's Hole riffle beetle, ten snail species, and birds such as the American white pelican, white-faced ibis and yellow-billed cuckoo.

**Waterfowl and Waterbird Targets**
- Habitat to support peak populations of 15,000 ducks and 750 geese during migration and wintering.
- Marsh-associated waterbirds, including great blue heron, common loon, western grebe, snowy egret, sandpipers, gulls and terns, to be maintained at observable levels.

**Research and Cooperation**
- At least one research study per year to inform management decisions.
- Participation in Youth Conservation Corps or similar programs when available.

**Education and Recreation**
- Environmental education study site with lesson plans, and interpretive trails covering springs, endangered plants and fish, and geology.
- Wildlife observation and photography year-round, with photography blinds.
- Quality waterfowl hunting with minimal regulation, plus quail and cottontail hunting.
- Frog harvesting, which will expand as marsh habitat grows. Sport fishing is excluded because sport fish compete with endangered fish.
- Canoes, rowboats and small motorboats permitted, but not power boats for water skiing.
- Swimming allowed in Crystal Reservoir but prohibited in springs occupied by endangered fish.

**Economic and Administrative Notes**
- Nye County receives an annual in-lieu-of-tax payment equal to 0.75 percent of the value of purchased lands.
- Local businesses are expected to benefit from increased visitation.
- The plan is framed under the [[Department of the Interior]]'s broader mandate for water, fish, wildlife and land resources.
