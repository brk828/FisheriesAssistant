# Totoaba (Cynoscion macdonaldi) Endangered Species Listing and Environmental Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1979 U.S. National Marine Fisheries Service package under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] concerning a final rule to list the [[Totoaba]] (*Cynoscion macdonaldi*) as an endangered species under Section 4 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. It contains a Finding of No Significant Impact, an Environmental Assessment, and the proceedings of a September 18–19, 1978 workshop at La Jolla, California. The workshop concluded that the totoaba is very likely endangered, with incidental take in the [[Shrimp Fishery]] and [[Gillnet Fisheries]] identified as the principal current threat. The document also addresses the absence of critical habitat designation, trade restrictions, and expected socio-economic effects.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Totoaba, a large sciaenid endemic to the upper [[Gulf of California]] in Mexican waters. Reported maximum length is about 2 m and weight over 90 kg.
- **Age and growth:** Four-year-old fish weigh approximately 7 to 8 kg. Fish over 23 kg are probably at least 6 years old. Otolith studies indicate many fish taken were around 20 years old. The average age of recruitment into the spawning stock is about 6 years.
- **Diet:** A carnivore feeding on a wide variety of fishes, crabs, shrimp and other crustaceans.
- **Regulatory context:** The species is listed in Appendix I of [[CITES]]. Mexico banned directed fishing in 1975 and designated it a protected species on June 19, 1975.
- **Legal framework:** Under the Act, it is unlawful to import, export, take, possess, sell or transport listed species, with exemptions for scientific or enhancement purposes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning and migration:** Adults spawn in April and May in the upper Gulf, having migrated north along the eastern shore from deeper water. Post-spawning adults move south along the western side and return to the upper Gulf in warmer months. Spawning runs were recorded as early as January in the 1940s.
- **Colorado River diversion:** Reduction and cessation of [[Colorado River]] flow altered the delta nursery grounds, raised salinity near the river mouth, and changed currents. The workshop noted that extended historical absences of flow have occurred.
- **Salinity:** Upper Gulf salinity rose from typical levels of about 35 ppt toward approximately 40 ppt or more in 1978. Field observations suggested totoaba tolerate a wide range of environmental fluctuations.
- **Fossil evidence:** Otoliths indicate a past range extending from the San Joaquin delta in central California to around Baja California. Totoaba is the only surviving member of a group of 12 or 13 sciaenids found in Miocene deposits about 20 million years old.
- **Associated fauna:** Ten fish species were found at the mouth of the Colorado River, four of which are endemic to the upper Gulf. Of these, the totoaba and *Micropogonias* species were noted as affected, with one species described as extremely numerous and increasing.
- **Pesticides:** Insecticide use in the U.S. and Mexico before the early 1960s may have harmed the population, but effects could not be demonstrated.

## Key Findings

1. **Historical catch:** Commercial catch rose from the early 1920s to over 2,000 metric tons in 1942, fell to about 300 metric tons, peaked at about 1,100 metric tons in 1966, and reached an all-time low of about 58 metric tons in 1975. No recent estimates exist for the sport fishery.
2. **Incidental take:** Juvenile totoaba are caught by shrimp trawls, and adults by gillnets for sharks. The number of shrimp boats continued to rise (about 40 new boats were preparing to enter in 1978), implying increasing effort.
3. **Trade:** Between 15,000 and 20,000 totoaba entered the United States annually in recent years. A U.S. import ban was enforced from September 1978.
4. **Workshop conclusions:** The population is greatly reduced from its pre-exploitation level and very likely endangered. Overfishing for directed fisheries was a main cause of decline, together with non-fishery factors such as reduced Colorado River flow. Incidental take in shrimp and shark fisheries is currently the principal threat. Data are insufficient, and a research program was recommended.
5. **Proposed action:** Listing is expected to provide deterrence to sport and commercial take by U.S. jurisdiction persons and serve as notice to foreign governments. It is unlikely to have adverse environmental effects.
6. **Critical habitat:** The Act does not require critical habitat for species occurring wholly in foreign waters. The species occurs only in Mexican waters, so designation was judged not appropriate.
7. **Finding:** The determination was made that there will be no significant environmental impact. The Deputy Assistant Secretary for Environmental Affairs signed the finding, and only one public comment was received, from a commercial fishing interest.
8. **Irreversible commitments:** None were foreseen.
9. **Recommendations:** Prevent incidental take in commercial fisheries, pursue cooperation and joint research with Mexico, and develop artificial propagation of eggs and larvae. The Salton Sea was considered an unlikely transplant site, and rearing in Galveston was considered undesirable.
