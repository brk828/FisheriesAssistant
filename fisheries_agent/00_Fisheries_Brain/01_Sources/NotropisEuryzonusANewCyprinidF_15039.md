# *Notropis euryzonus*: A New Cyprinid Fish from the Chattahoochee River System of Georgia and Alabama

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 description by [[Royal D. Suttkus]] of [[Tulane Studies in Zoology]] (Vol. 3, No. 5) formally names *Notropis euryzonus*, a small, colorful minnow that appears restricted to tributaries of the lower [[Chattahoochee River]]. The name refers to the broad dark lateral band. The species is distinguished from its nearest relative, [[Notropis Hypselopterus]], mainly by male dorsal fin shape and pigmentation. The paper also recognizes two geographic races based on meristic counts, and it proposes that the species is an endemic of the [[Apalachicola River System]] that likely arose from a *hypselopterus*-like stock during the [[Pleistocene Epoch]].

## Background & Description

**Materials.** The type series comprises 163 specimens (21–53 mm standard length) from eight localities in [[Uchee Creek]]. An additional 301 specimens (21–55 mm) came from 11 localities in other Chattahoochee tributaries. Most lots are held in the Cornell University (CU), Tulane University (TU), and University of Michigan Museum of Zoology (UMMZ) collections.

**Type specimens.**
- **Holotype:** CU 28346, adult male, 49 mm SL, Uchee Creek, 0.7 mi E. Marvyn, Lee Co., Alabama, collected June 12, 1949, by Suttkus, Robert H. Gibbs, and Charles F. Cole.
- **Paratypes:** 36 paratypes (CU 15990, 28–47 mm) share the holotype data, and further paratype lots are listed from Uchee Creek and Brush Creek.

**Diagnosis (summary).** The species has 2, 4–4, 2 teeth and modally 10 anal rays. Other counts are:
- Dorsal rays: 8
- Pectoral rays: 13–16
- Pelvic rays: 8
- Caudal rays: 19
- Lateral line scales: 35–40
- Scale rows from dorsal fin to opercle: 17–22
- Scales around the caudal peduncle: 12 or 13

The body is deep and compressed. The lateral line is strongly decurved. The male's dorsal and anal fins are greatly elevated. The maximum size is 55 mm SL.

**Morphometrics.** Table 1 gives proportional measurements (per thousand SL) for the holotype and paratypes, including five male and five female paratypes. Body depth is 3.1–3.8 times into SL in males and 3.6–3.7 in females. The head runs 3.8–4.2 times into SL.

**Color and sexual dimorphism.**
- A broad dark-brown (in alcohol) or bluish-gray (in life) lateral band runs from the snout tip to the caudal base.
- The most striking feature in life is a bright orange caudal fin.
- Males have a heavily pigmented dorsal fin, with melanophores spread across most interradial membranes. Females have less pigment, concentrated centrally.
- Males bear breeding tubercles on the lower jaw and head. Females have fewer tubercles, limited to the dentaries and a few other areas.
- Some larger males and specimens from [[Hodchodkee Creek]] show a clear "window" in the caudal fin. This character was absent in Uchee Creek specimens collected in September 1955.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The type locality, Uchee Creek, is a shallow stream about 10 feet wide with a sand bottom. Its water was brown and clear on June 12, 1949, and the estimated flow was 8 cubic feet per second. Most streams where the species was seined had colorless water. The Uchee Creek tributaries and a Chattahoochee tributary near Eufaula had brown water.

**Substrate.** Collection sites varied:
- Hodchodkee Creek (Stewart Co., Georgia): mud and clay
- Abbie Creek, Omussee Creek, Hichitee Creek: shifting sand and silt
- Hatchechubbee Creek and one Uchee tributary: exposed bedrock and drifting sand

**Cover and vegetation.** Specimens were usually taken near logs or aquatic vegetation. The most common recorded plants were *Orontium aquaticum* (golden club) and *Sparganium* sp. The association with golden club was less consistent than that reported for [[Notropis Signipinnis]].

**Distribution.** [[Notropis Euryzonus]] appears to be endemic to the Apalachicola River system, as does [[Notropis Hypsilepis]]. The two species overlap in range from Hodchodkee Creek to Uchee and Upatoi creeks. Map 1 shows the distribution, with the type locality marked.

**Co-occurrence.** Suttkus and Charles D. Hancock collected *N. hypselopterus* in a tributary only two miles from a *N. euryzonus* population. No apparent habitat differences were found between the two streams, so the author suggests that additional collecting may show the two forms can share a stream.

## Key Findings

- **New species.** *Notropis euryzonus* is described as new. Its diagnostic traits are the broad lateral band, the male dorsal fin pattern, and the orange caudal fin.
- **Two races.** Specimens from Uchee Creek represent one race, and those from other tributaries represent a second, the "Lower Chattahoochee River race." The races differ mainly in anal ray count, which averages 9.6 in Uchee Creek and 10.2 in the Lower Chattahoochee race (Table 7). Uchee Creek fish also show:
  - Lower pectoral ray counts (mean 13.96 vs. 14.36; Table 6)
  - Higher circumferential body scale counts (mean 29.08 vs. 28.46; Table 2)
  - More scale rows from dorsal origin to opercle (mean 19.66 vs. 19.14; Table 3)
  - Higher peduncle scale counts (mean 13.10 vs. 12.24; Table 4)
- **Clinal pattern.** The anal ray count does not appear clinal (Table 8). Vertebral counts show a possible cline, with means ranging from 36.0 to 36.7 among streams (Table 9).
- **Relationships.** The species is most closely related to [[Notropis Hypselopterus]] and [[Notropis Stonei]], and less closely to [[Notropis Signipinnis]]. Its dorsal fin is rectangular, whereas *N. hypselopterus* has a triangular dorsal fin with greatly extended posterior elements. The caudal "window" is absent in *N. hypselopterus*. Melanophores are evenly distributed over the male dorsal fin rather than forming a crescent.
- **Origin.** The author suggests *N. euryzonus* most likely evolved from a *hypselopterus* stock that moved up the Apalachicola River system during the Pleistocene. Isolation was probably caused by one of the Pleistocene coastal inundations. The author also suggests that the Flint and Choctawhatchee systems were colonized later, or that speciation there was slower.
- **Etymology.** The name derives from Greek *eury* ("broad") and *zona* ("zone"), referring to the broad lateral band.
