# First California Record of the Spotted Sleeper, *Eleotris picta* (Eleotridae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #eleotridae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 reprint from *California Fish and Game* (Vol. 39, No. 1) by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] of the [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]] reports the first capture of the [[Spotted Sleeper]] (*Eleotris picta*, family [[Eleotridae]]) in California. A non-breeding adult male was caught on April 16, 1952, at a canal spillway between Winterhaven and the [[Colorado River]] in [[Imperial County]]. The record extends the known northern range of the species by roughly 750 miles from the nearest published record in Baja California. The paper combines the specimen description with a full synonymy, a map of 16 known records, a review of museum holdings, and comments on the genus's distribution, nomenclature, and potential impact on California fauna.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Eleotris picta* Kner and Steindachner. Described as a large gobioid fish. Its name is spelled *pictus* in the 1865 text and *picta* on the plate.
- **Taxonomic context:** The paper includes an extensive synonymy. Several earlier records were misidentifications, and some authors treated *E. picta* as a synonym of [[Eleotris Pisonis]]. Hildebrand (1938) noted that some specimens, probably hybrids, combined characters of *E. picta* and *E. pisonis*, possibly from intermixing in the [[Panama Canal]]. Hildebrand (1939) reported hybridization in the locks on the Pacific side of the canal.
- **Nomenclature:** The genus name *Eleotris* was added to the Official List of generic names with fixed application to the *E. pisonis* group. Hubbs concluded that the feminine gender is correct and adopted it.
- **The California specimen:**
  - Caught by [[Clifford Fox]] of Inglewood, California, on April 16, 1952, at a canal spillway between Winterhaven and the Colorado River, Imperial County.
  - Fishing for channel catfish with shrimp bait, midday, on the bottom at a depth of about four feet. The fish "struck the bait and pulled like a catfish."
  - Identified by Hubbs after being sent by [[Arthur F. Halloran]], Manager of the Imperial National Wildlife Refuge.
  - A non-breeding adult male, recorded as "114 inches" total length and 228 mm standard length. The total length as printed appears inconsistent with the standard length and is probably a transcription error in the source; it is reported here as written.
  - Deposited in the fish collection of the [[California Academy of Sciences]], Catalog No. 20582.
- **Color (fresh specimen, notes by Halloran and Gerald E. Duncan):** Dark brow on the dorsal surface; yellowish-brown sides with small yellow spots; light gray to white, mixed with yellow spots, on the ventral surface forward of the anal fin; dorsal fins dark with yellow spots; caudal fin reddish-brown with light streaks at the end; other fins reddish with dark spots.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Known range (Figure 1 and text):** Records run from Cape San Lucas and Mazatlan, Mexico, through Central America to Ecuador. The 16 records listed north to south are:
  1. Canal spillway, Imperial County, California
  2. Rio San José del Cabo, near Cape San Lucas, Baja California
  3. Rio Presidio, near Mazatlan, Mexico
  4. Cleopha Island, Tres Marias group
  5. Colima, Mexico
  6. Rio Tehuantepec, Mexico
  7. Jesús Maria, Costa Rica
  8. Rio Grande at Orotina, Costa Rica
  9. Panama Canal, including Miraflores
  10. Rio Bayano, Panama
  11. Rio Chucunaque, Panama
  12. Rio San Juan, Colombia
  13. Puerto Utria, Colombia
  14. Gorgona Island, Colombia
  15. Chone, Ecuador
  16. Guayaquil, Ecuador
- **Northern limit before this record:** Rio Presidio near Mazatlan, Sinaloa, and Rio San José near Cape San Lucas. Both lie just within the Tropic of Cancer. The California specimen was about 750 miles north of the Rio San José record and about 860 miles north of the Rio Presidio mouth.
- **Likely origin:** Hubbs considered it "highly probable" that the fish was a stray from the Gulf of California or the lightly collected tidal reaches of the Colorado River. It may have moved about one-half mile directly from the river to the spillway, or through the overflowing canal.
- **Museum and survey checks:**
  - United States National Museum (USNM) series from north of Panama: Nos. 2492 (Cape San Lucas), 30948 (near San José), 37142 (Rio Presidio, Sinaloa), and 102265 (Rio Tehuantepec).
  - Stanford University Natural History Museum series: Nos. 2907 (Mazatlan), 6869 (Panama), 8130 (San José del Cabo), 37538 (Puerto Utria), and 37539 (Gorgona Island).
  - No cataloged specimens at the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, and none from recent collections between Mazatlan and the Colorado River by [[Ralph G. Miller]] or from [[University of California, Los Angeles]] collecting.
  - Not recorded from the lower Colorado River surveys by Gilbert and Scofield (1898), Snyder (1915), and Dill (1944), or from Hubbs, Miller, and party's March 1950 trip.
- **Rio San José:** A perennial stream flowing through San José del Cabo, with many gobies and other fishes of marine affinities. The published "Cape San Lucas" records probably came from this stream near its mouth.
- **Related eleotrids:** [[Gobiomorus Maculatus]] and [[Dormitator Latifrons]] are commonly associated with *E. picta* and might also stray up the Colorado River, but neither had been found as far north as the Colorado Delta.
- **Other gobioids of the region:** [[Gillichthys Detrusus]] was previously the only gobioid reported from the lower Colorado River. Hubbs regarded all Salton Sea *Gillichthys* specimens as the California species *G. mirabilis* Cooper. Evans and Douglas showed that *G. mirabilis* is well established in the [[Salton Sea]].
- **Pacific vs. Atlantic patterns:** The genus is relatively uniform along the Pacific coast and does not break into species as it does on the Atlantic coast. The Atlantic analog *E. pisonis* ranges north to Texas, Florida, and Georgia. Another species, reported as *E. abacurus* or *E. amblyopsis*, has been recorded from South Carolina, at about the latitude of Imperial County. Fowler (1944b, 1945a) described a distinct species from the Upper Rio Jurubida at Nuquí, Colombia, at about 3,000 feet, characterized by a small number of scales (51 rows along the axis).
- **Ecological role and management concerns:**
  - Hildebrand (1938) gave the species a low rating as a food or game fish.
  - Its large, well-toothed mouth with a projecting lower jaw suggests predatory habits, and several authors found that it eats fish.
  - Hubbs hoped it would not follow the [[Striped Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*) and the [[Pacific Ten-Pounder]] (*Elops affinis*) in dispersing from the Colorado River into the Salton Sea.

## Key Findings

- **New state record:** *Eleotris picta* (spotted sleeper) was documented for the first time in California, from a single non-breeding adult male caught in Imperial County on April 16, 1952 (CAS Catalog No. 20582).
- **Range extension:** The record lies about 750 miles north of the nearest published record (Rio San José) and about 860 miles north of the Rio Presidio mouth. It adds a genus and family (Eleotridae) to the California list and a species to the U.S. fauna.
- **Probable stray origin:** Hubbs judged it highly probable that the specimen came from the Gulf of California or the tidal Colorado River rather than from an established California population. No other specimens were found in the regional museum collections or recent surveys.
- **Misreported northern range:** Earlier reports of the range extending to Sonora were judged unsupported and likely written in error for Sinaloa. Reports of a range extending to California were likely errors for Baja California or the Gulf of California.
- **Taxonomy and nomenclature:** The paper gives a detailed synonymy, identifies historical misidentifications with [[Eleotris Pisonis]], notes possible hybridization in the Panama Canal, and adopts the feminine gender for *Eleotris*.
- **Management outlook:** The species has low value as a food or game fish, and Hubbs hoped it would not establish itself in the Salton Sea through the Colorado River, as had occurred with striped mullet and Pacific ten-pounder.
