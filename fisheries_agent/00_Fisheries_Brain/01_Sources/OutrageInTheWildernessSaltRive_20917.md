# Salt River Rapids Blasted in "Vigilante Action": Quartzite Falls Destroyed

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fish-conservation

## Executive Summary

This 1994 news article from *The Arizona Republic* (by Steve Yozwiak) reports that [[Quartzite Falls]], a notorious rapid in the [[Salt River Canyon]], was deliberately destroyed with high explosives. The blast reduced a 6-foot waterfall and a Class 6 rapid to a series of ripples. The falls acted as a natural barrier that protected rare native fish upstream from predatory, introduced sport fish in the lower Salt River reservoirs. Officials from the [[Tonto National Forest]] and [[United States Forest Service]] described the act as a vigilante, or "eco-terrorism," action. The article also covers the site's remoteness, the hazards it posed to rafters, and a $1,000 reward for information leading to an arrest.

## Background & Description

- **Site:** Quartzite Falls lies between the US 60 bridge over the Salt River and [[Roosevelt Lake]], inside the Salt River Canyon Wilderness Area, where motorized vehicles are prohibited.
- **Access:** No foot trails reach the rapids. The nearest four-wheel-drive road is about two miles away, so the easiest access is by river.
- **Designation:** A 60-mile stretch of the Salt River that includes the rapids was part of 1,700 miles of the [[Colorado River]] and its tributaries designated in March as habitat critical to the survival of endangered fish species.
- **Hazard:** The rapids were described as nearly as deadly as any on the Colorado River in the [[Grand Canyon]]. Rafters sometimes waited up to five hours to portage equipment and guide loaded boats through the falls with ropes.
- **Prior incidents:** The article reports that experienced rafters died while attempting to run the rapids. The OCR text is garbled at this point, so the exact dates and number of victims are unclear. One survivor was reportedly rescued by [[Helicopter Rescue]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The main ecological concern is the loss of a natural barrier. [[W.L. Minckley]], a professor of zoology at [[Arizona State University]] and a recognized authority on native Southwestern fish, warned that "any native fish above that falls are now at risk." Before the blast, the falls kept predatory sport fish, introduced to lower Salt River reservoirs over the previous century, from moving upstream into habitat occupied by rare native species. Removing the barrier allows these non-native predators to colonize that upstream habitat.

The article does not name the native or sport fish species, give population counts, or report water chemistry or other habitat measurements.

## Key Findings

1. **Deliberate destruction:** Quartzite Falls was blown up with high explosives. Forest Service officials first noticed something was wrong during the winter, but high water levels delayed confirmation for several weeks. Investigators concluded in March that the act was deliberate, not natural.
2. **Ecological impact:** The 6-foot falls, a natural barrier, were eliminated. This puts upstream native fish at risk from introduced, aggressive sport fish.
3. **Suspected motive:** Officials speculated that disgruntled rafters, frustrated by long portages, may have caused the explosion. No arrest had been made when the article was written.
4. **Management response:**
   - A $1,000 reward was offered for information leading to an arrest.
   - Forest Service personnel planned to travel the river during the coming months to monitor rafting activity.
   - Officials said that if more rafters use the river, the Forest Service would need to begin issuing permits to track them.
5. **Safety warning:** Weinel warned that with the falls gone, rafters may assume the river is now easy to run, and the Forest Service advised against running the Salt River Canyon in its current state.
6. **Policy context:** The article ties the incident to the critical-habitat designation for endangered fish in the Colorado River system, which made the reach ecologically significant.
