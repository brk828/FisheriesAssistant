# Maximum Temperature Limits of Heptagenia Lateralis in English Lake District Streams (1959)

#ephemeroptera #freshwater-biology #aquatic-ecology #heptagenia-lateralis

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Thomas T. Macan]] tests whether the distribution of the mayfly [[Heptagenia Lateralis]] in small [[English Lake District]] streams is governed by summer maximum water temperature. Maximum-minimum thermometers were read at 18 stations in 8 streams during summer 1959. The author concludes that the species is limited by a maximum temperature of about 18°C, that the egg tolerates higher temperatures than the nymph, and that the nymphs fail to complete development because warming in spring is rapid relative to their slow winter growth. The paper is a reprint from *Wetter und Leben* (vol. 12, nos. 9–10, 1960), a memorial issue for Dr. Franz Sauberer, with English and German summaries.

## Background & Description

*Heptagenia lateralis* (Curtis) is an Ephemeroptera species that is among the commonest in some small stony streams but scarce or absent in others. An earlier analysis of stream temperature ([[Macan 1958]]) suggested a possible explanation, and the 1959 measurements were designed to test it.

The study was conducted at the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] laboratory in Ambleside. Standard maximum and minimum wall thermometers, protected by metal tubes, were placed at 18 stations in 8 streams. They were read and reset at intervals through the summer and calibrated against a laboratory instrument. Three instruments were lost during the summer: one was probably washed away, one was probably destroyed, and one was certainly stolen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Weather and water conditions.** Summer 1959 was fine from May to mid-October, with no unusually high temperatures recorded. Rainy periods occurred in June, July and early August. Visible water disappeared from much of several stream beds in September.

**Temperature range.** Recorded maxima ranged from 16 to 28°C, a difference of 12°C. Readings within a stream were generally similar, and May maxima were rarely far below the summer peak. [[Windermere]] (1 m depth) warmed more slowly and reached maxima several degrees below those of other years.

**Stream settings.** The streams differed in origin and shading:
- [[Nor Moss Beck]] rises in a former tarn now a swamp, with shallow pools and islands.
- [[Holbeck]] rises on a hillside and flows sluggishly across a flat area just over 300 m above sea level.
- [[Smooth Beck]] drains a fishpond; [[Stock Ghyll]] rises on open moorland at about 400 m.
- Nor Moss Beck, Holbeck, Smooth Beck and Stock Ghyll all flow through woods or tree-lined gorges, where temperature drops below the upper, unshaded stations.
- Outgate (a tributary of Ford Wood Beck), Dan Beck and Pull Beck flow across open country where water warms.
- [[High Wray Beck]] emerges from peat on a north-facing slope and runs through a wooded gorge.
- [[Whelpside Ghyll]], the coldest stream studied, rises at about 800 m and has few trees beside most of its length.

**Comparison species.** [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]] was used as a comparison. Its emergence ends when average temperatures reach or exceed 16–17°C (Macan, 1960, in press).

## Key Findings

1. **Temperature limit.** The occurrence of *H. lateralis* suggests a maximum temperature of about 18°C as the limiting threshold. The author notes that an exact thermal deathpoint cannot be established from field observations, because lethality depends on exposure duration and prior acclimation temperature.

2. **Anomalies examined.**
   - The species was abundant at Holbeck upper, one of the warmest stations. This is attributed to its position just above a long stretch where the species was abundant, with spread expected during the cold season.
   - Stock Ghyll showed similar conditions.
   - It was scarce in Whelpside Ghyll, the coldest stream. The author previously attributed this to steepness, but the paper also notes the low temperature reflects the high source (about 800 m).
   - In most streams, the low temperatures associated with the species result from tree shading, so the correlation could be with vegetation rather than temperature. The species' occurrence in [[Windermere]] and other lakes (Harris, 1952) indicates temperature, not shading, is the controlling factor.

3. **Life stage sensitivity.** The author assumes the nymph is the critical stage, since the species occurs in Windermere, where maxima exceed the 18°C threshold late in the season. The egg is considered more tolerant of heat. Absence from warm becks is therefore attributed to failure to complete nymphal development before lethal temperatures arrive.

4. **Mechanism: rapid spring warming.** *H. lateralis* grows little in winter and completes most development in spring and early summer. Winter temperatures are assumed to be similar across streams, and all streams warm at about the same time. Warmer streams may therefore reach the lethal threshold before nymphs mature. Studies by [[Pleskot]] (1951) and [[Harker]] (1952) indicate emergence is temperature-linked, supporting this explanation over a non-thermal emergence cue. Winter temperature data existed only for [[Ford Wood Beck]].

5. **Comparison with *Rhithrogena semicolorata*.** *H. lateralis* grows less in winter than *R. semicolorata* and has a narrower temperature range at both ends, which explains its more restricted distribution. However, *H. lateralis* can live in still water, which *R. semicolorata* cannot.

6. **Conclusion.** The findings suggest that distribution is governed primarily by temperature, with maxima above 18°C too warm for the species. The author states the conclusions are provisional and that the winter-temperature assumption remains untested.
