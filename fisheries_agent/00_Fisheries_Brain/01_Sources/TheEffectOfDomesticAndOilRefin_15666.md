# The Effect of Domestic and Oil Refinery Effluents on Meristic and Morphometric Characteristics of Three Cyprinid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #pollution-ecology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This doctoral dissertation (Oklahoma State University, 1966) investigated how domestic sewage and oil refinery effluents affected the morphological characteristics and population structure of three cyprinid fish species in [[Skeleton Creek]], Oklahoma. Between June 1963 and May 1964, the author collected fish from eight stations: four below combined effluent outfalls, one above outfalls, one on [[Boggy Creek]] (receiving only domestic sewage), and two unpolluted control streams ([[Turkey Creek]] and [[Otter Creek]]). The study examined three meristic characters (lateral line scales, pectoral fin rays, predorsal scale rows) and seven morphometric characters (standard length, body depth, pectoral fin length, head length, head depth, nape length, body width) in [[Notropis lutrensis]] (Red Shiner), [[Notropis stramineus]] (Sand Shiner), and [[Pimephales promelas]] (Fathead Minnow). Statistical analyses revealed seven distinct subpopulations of N. lutrensis, four of N. stramineus, and three of P. promelas, with effluent outfalls acting as effective barriers isolating upstream from downstream populations. Populations above outfalls were more stable, while those below showed seasonal fluctuations linked to immigration/emigration and variable effluent concentrations. Domestic effluents alone (Station 2) appeared beneficial, producing larger fish, whereas combined industrial and domestic effluents created morphological variations and reduced fish abundance at certain times of year.

## Background & Description

### Study Area and Design

[[Skeleton Creek]] is a sixth-order permanent stream originating near Enid, Oklahoma, flowing southeasterly approximately 75 miles through Garfield, Kingfisher, and Logan Counties before emptying into the [[Cimarron River]]. Stream elevation drops from 1,244 feet at Enid to 910 feet at the mouth (average gradient 6 ft/mi). The drainage basin consists of Permian Red Beds (sandstone, shales, limestones) with Quaternary sand deposits along narrow areas. Climate is continental with mean annual rainfall 29-30.6 inches and mean annual temperature 59.3-61.8°F. The frost-free season extends approximately 215 days (March-October).

Eight collecting stations were established:
- **Station 60**: 60 miles below effluent outfalls (near Cimarron River confluence)
- **Station 46**: 46 miles below outfalls
- **Station 33**: 33 miles below outfalls
- **Station 15**: 15 miles below outfalls
- **Station 5**: 5 miles above outfalls on Skeleton Creek
- **Station 2**: On Boggy Creek, 2 miles above Enid effluent outfalls (domestic sewage only)
- **Turkey Creek**: Unpolluted control
- **Otter Creek**: Unpolluted control

### Pollution Sources

Multiple waste streams entered [[Skeleton Creek]]:
- North Enid: ~90,000 gal/day domestic effluent
- Enid State Hospital: domestic effluent
- Air base (via Boggy Creek): ~185,000 gal/day
- Enid municipal sewage: >4 million gal/day treated (1.5 million pumped to refinery)
- Oil refinery: ~720,000 gal/day after 27-day retention

The combined oil refinery and municipal sewage outfalls entered [[Boggy Creek]] approximately 300 feet apart, just above its confluence with Skeleton Creek.

### Species Studied

**[[Notropis lutrensis]]** (Red Shiner): Deep-bodied cyprinid ranging west of Mississippi River from South Dakota/Wyoming south to Mexico. Body depth ~3× in standard length; D.8, A.9, P.11-16 fin rays; 29-37 lateral line scales. Breeding males display brilliant red coloration with orange-red fins and white nuptial tubercles. Stream minnow abundant in swift riffles; spawns late May to mid-August at night. Natural foods include algae, insects, crustaceans.

**[[Notropis stramineus]]** (Sand Shiner): Small minnow ranging from Rocky Mountains to Appalachians, Great Lakes to Mexico. Seven anal rays (rarely 8); 29-38 lateral line scales; prominent mid-dorsal stripe; large bulging eyes; short pectoral fins. Straw-colored with silvery lateral band. Inhabits sandy streams and gravel-bottom riffles with currents; spawns May-August. More restricted in habitat preferences than N. lutrensis; rarely found among rooted vegetation. Surprisingly tolerant to some inorganic pollutants (mine wastes) but less tolerant to oil refinery effluents than N. lutrensis or P. promelas in some studies.

**[[Pimephales promelas]]** (Fathead Minnow): Robust-bodied species ~2 inches long ranging throughout Great Plains and much of eastern North America. Body depth 3.5-4× in standard length; 41-56 lateral line scales; small subterminal mouth. Oklahoma form (P. p. confertus) has nearly complete lateral line, less oblique mouth, and lacks chin tubercles compared to northern races. Males develop secondary sex characters ~30 days before spawning; guard nests on undersides of objects. Eggs hatch in ~5 days; young grow rapidly and may spawn same summer. Spawning season May-August. Prefers quiet waters and small pools; commonly raised commercially.

### Methods

Fish were collected bimonthly (June 1963-May 1964, except one December collection) using two Common-Sense minnow seines (10'×3.5' with 1/8" mesh; 6'×4' with 1/4" mesh). Specimens were fixed in 10% formalin for four days, washed, and stored in 50% isopropyl alcohol.

**Meristic characters** (10 studied, 3 reported): lateral line scales, pectoral fin rays, predorsal scale rows counted following Hubbs and Lagler (1957). Samples were pooled into three four-month periods to increase sample size. An IBM 1410 computer compared stations using Student's t-test at 95% confidence level.

**Morphometric characters**: Seven characteristics measured with calipers plus weight (nearest 0.1 g): standard length (independent variable), body depth, pectoral fin length, head length, head depth, nape length, body width. Analysis of covariance tested homogeneity of regressions and intercepts at 99% confidence level.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Environmental Conditions

Stream flow and turbidity exhibited seasonal and longitudinal variations. Spring and summer showed high flow and turbidity; fall and winter showed reverse conditions. Longitudinal variation in dissolved oxygen concentrations exceeded seasonal fluctuations:
- Spring: mean 3.1 ppm
- Summer: mean 3.4 ppm  
- Fall: mean 4.5 ppm
- Winter: mean 7.1 ppm
- Range: 0.2 ppm (25 miles below outfalls, May) to 21.5 ppm (4.4 miles below outfalls, March)

Water temperature varied from 0°C (January) to 35°C (August). At Station 60: 1.5°C (February) to 30°C (August); at Station 15: 2.3°C (February) to 34°C (August).

Diurnal dissolved oxygen fluctuations were extreme:
- **Station 15** (June 28-29): 1.20-5.8 ppm (15-79% saturation)
- **Station 15** (February 28-29): 9.6-15.70 ppm (72-130% saturation)
- **Station 46** (August 12-13): 6.05-19.55 ppm (79-257% saturation)

A large tributary at Station 46 contributed oil field brines (chlorides 349 ppm).

### Species Distribution Patterns

Species composition and abundance varied markedly with season, especially at upstream stations below effluent outfalls. [[Phillips]] (1965) documented these patterns, suggesting effluents were limiting factors during certain or all seasons.

**N. lutrensis**: Most abundant and widespread; present at all stations but with varying densities. Showed highest tolerance to combined effluents.

**N. stramineus**: Restricted habitat preferences; absent from Stations 15, 5, and Otter Creek (only 5 total specimens). Required sand/gravel bottoms and moving water. Absence at Station 5 indicated limiting factors in intermittent stream sections. Absence at Station 15 possibly due to combined effluent concentrations or critical dissolved oxygen levels during summer.

**P. promelas**: Insufficient numbers at Stations 60, 46, 15, Turkey Creek, and Otter Creek for analysis. Preferred quiet waters and small pools in tributaries rather than main stream currents. Most specimens collected at Station 33 where dissolved oxygen fluctuations were most extreme below outfalls. Presence during spawning season and capture of juveniles indicated successful reproduction in stream despite effluents.

### Previous Research Context

The author reviewed extensive literature on environmental effects on fish morphology:
- Temperature: Northern populations typically larger with smaller heads, eyes, maxillaries, and fins than southern counterparts (Hubbs 1926). Lower temperatures generally produce higher meristic counts (vertebrae, scales, fin rays) in experimental studies.
- Salinity: Interacts with temperature effects; different genetic stocks show opposite responses to salinity changes (Heuts 1949).
- Oxygen: Low oxygen tension produces effects parallel to low temperature (Hubbs 1926, Taning 1952, Seymour 1956).
- Developmental timing: Characters appearing last in development are more labile (Barlow 1961). Body size at relative growth stanzas influences body proportions (Martin 1949).

Domestic effluents: Effects vary with concentration and decomposition level. Fish are resistant unless dissolved oxygen is exhausted. Effects vary seasonally (fish more resistant in cold water). Sewage may kill fry, prevent egg hatching, cause abnormal development, or cover spawning beds with septic sludge. However, some studies (Moore and Mizelle 1939, Cross 1950) found domestic sewage beneficial to fish fauna in [[Stillwater Creek]], Oklahoma.

Industrial effluents: Multiple studies demonstrated effects on fish distribution, but neither sewage nor industrial effluents had previously been implicated in influencing meristic or morphometric variations in fishes prior to this study.

## Key Findings

### Population Structure and Distinctiveness

**Seven distinct subpopulations of [[Notropis lutrensis]]** were identified (Fig. 2):
1. Station 60 (near Cimarron River)
2. Station 46 (influenced by oil field brines)
3. Stations 33-15 (combined, relatively homogeneous)
4. Station 2 (Boggy Creek, domestic sewage only)
5. Station 5 (above outfalls)
6. Turkey Creek (control)
7. Otter Creek (control)

Differences maintained throughout year despite seasonal variations in mean counts/measurements at individual stations. Seasonal variations attributed to immigration/emigration of distinct fish schools.

**Four distinct subpopulations of [[Notropis stramineus]]**:
1. Station 60
2. Stations 46-33 (homogeneous)
3. Station 2
4. Turkey Creek

**Three distinct subpopulations of [[Pimephales promelas]]**:
1. Stations 60-15 (homogeneous)
2. Station 2
3. Station 5

### Statistical Differentiation

**N. lutrensis comparisons**:
- Skeleton Creek and Boggy Creek fish differed from Turkey Creek and Otter Creek controls in 66 of 108 possible meristic comparisons and 46 of 86 possible morphometric comparisons
- Otter Creek differed from Turkey Creek in 2 of 9 meristic and 4 of 7 morphometric comparisons
- Fish below outfalls differed from those above in 36 of 72 meristic and 29 of 56 morphometric comparisons
- Stations 2 and 5 differed in 6 of 9 meristic and 5 of 7 morphometric comparisons
- Station 46 differed from other downstream stations in 22 of 48 possible comparisons
- Station 60 differed from other downstream stations in 20 of 48 possible comparisons

**N. stramineus comparisons**:
- Skeleton Creek and Boggy Creek fish differed from Turkey Creek in 22 of 36 meristic and 11 of 28 morphometric comparisons
- Fish below outfalls differed from Boggy Creek (Station 2) in 18 of 27 meristic and 12 of 21 morphometric comparisons
- Station 60 differed from Stations 46-33 in 15 of 32 possible comparisons

### Population Stability and Movement Patterns

**Stable populations** (present throughout year with minimal fluctuation):
- Turkey Creek (all species)
- Otter Creek (N. lutrensis only)
- Station 2 on Boggy Creek (all species present)
- Station 5 above outfalls (N. lutrensis, P. promelas)

These populations appeared permanently or nearly permanently isolated from one another, representing resident populations minimally influenced by migrating fish.

**Variable populations** (marked seasonal fluctuations):
- Stations below effluent outfalls (60, 46, 33, 15) showed sharp seasonal variations in abundance and composition
- Influenced by immigrating and emigrating schools from Cimarron River (Station 60) and tributaries
- Station 60 showed obvious influx from Cimarron River, especially during spring spawning runs
- Considerable variability among seasons indicated subgroups moved in and out freely

### Effluent Barrier Effects

Effluent outfalls formed an **impassible barrier** preventing downstream fish from reaching Skeleton Creek headwaters. During floods, some upstream fish may have been washed downstream, contributing to downstream variability. Example: [[Notemigonus crysoleucas]] (Golden Shiner) appeared at Station 15 only after heavy rainfall.

Bioassay data (Phillips 1965) confirmed fish could not move through effluent outfalls, maintaining permanent isolation between upstream and downstream populations.

### Seasonal Migration Patterns

Fish at Stations 33-15 and 46 appeared to be **resident populations that moved into adjacent tributaries** when main stream environment became intolerable:
- Young fish captured at all stations during summer and fall, indicating early development occurred in modified environments
- Winter absence not explained by downstream movement (Station 60 means did not shift toward upstream values)
- Some fish captured in tributaries during winter
- Suggests upstream populations moved into tributaries rather than downstream during fall/winter migrations

### Species-Specific Responses

**N. lutrensis**: Most tolerant and adaptable; present at all stations with varying densities. Habitat generalist able to utilize swift riffles and various stream environments.

**N. stramineus**: Habitat specialist requiring sand/gravel bottoms and moving water. More sensitive to effluent effects than N. lutrensis. Absence at Station 15 (closest to outfalls) indicated combined effluent concentrations or low dissolved oxygen were limiting. Irwin (1965) bioassay tests found N. stramineus more resistant to oil refinery effluents than N. lutrensis and P. promelas, contradicting Clemens and Finnell (1956) findings.

**P. promelas**: Preferred quiet waters and small tributary pools rather than main stream currents. Homogeneity of population below outfalls suggested unrestricted movement in Skeleton Creek or greater tolerance to different effluent concentrations than other species. Most abundant at Station 33 where dissolved oxygen fluctuations were most extreme (Wilhm 1965).

### Environmental Factors and Morphological Variation

**Dissolved oxygen** appeared to be a limiting factor:
- Station 15 summer conditions (1.20-5.8 ppm) potentially limiting for developing embryos
- Station 15 winter conditions (9.6-15.70 ppm) high, suggesting other limiting factors present
- Station 46 dissolved oxygen high (6.05-19.55 ppm), probably not limiting for embryos

**Oil field brines** at Station 46 (chlorides 349 ppm) may have been responsible for extreme morphological values at this station, distinguishing it from other downstream populations.

**Temperature and seasonal factors**: Fish appeared less resistant to effluent effects during certain seasons, or effluents varied in concentration/toxicity seasonally. Heavy rainfall caused temporary changes in dissolved oxygen, chloride content, and conductivity (Baumgardner 1966).

### Beneficial Effects of Domestic Sewage Alone

Station 2 (receiving only domestic effluent, no refinery wastes) produced **larger fish** than other stations. This finding supported earlier observations by Moore and Mizelle (1939) and Cross (1950) in [[Stillwater Creek]], Oklahoma, where domestic sewage appeared beneficial to fish fauna. The author concluded: "Domestic effluents, in the absence of refinery effluents, appeared to be beneficial in increasing the size of fishes."

### Mechanisms of Morphological Variation

The study could not definitively separate genetic from somatic (environmentally induced) variations, but the author concluded most observed variations were likely **somatic** based on:
- Low level of differentiation among subpopulations
- High variability within samples and among seasons
- Known environmental influences on fish morphology (temperature, salinity, oxygen, light)

**Possible mechanisms**:
1. **Indirect effects**: Effluents modify environmental factors (temperature, dissolved oxygen, salinity, light) which then influence developmental processes
2. **Direct effects**: Effluents themselves directly influence developmental processes
3. **Isolation effects**: Effluents isolate subgroups long enough to permit population divergence
