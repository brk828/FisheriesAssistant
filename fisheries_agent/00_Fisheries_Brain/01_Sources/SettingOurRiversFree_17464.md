# Setting Our Rivers Free

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This 1999 *New York Times* opinion column by [[James Conaway]] argues for removing obsolete dams to restore fish migration, using the [[Embrey Dam]] on the [[Rappahannock River]] in [[Fredericksburg]], Virginia, as its central case. The author holds that the dam blocks [[Anadromous Fish]] runs such as [[American Shad]] and [[River Herring]] and that its hydropower function is defunct. The piece frames dam removal as both an ecological and an economic decision, and as a moral question about allowing migratory fish to reach their spawning grounds. It is an advocacy text rather than a research report, and it contains no quantitative field data.

## Background & Description

The column is built around [[Kenny Mills]], a 58-year-old retired farmer who lifts yellow and white perch over the Embrey Dam in buckets and carries shad and herring upriver by net each spawning season. He reports that he has spent his life on this stretch of the Rappahannock and that he recalls a sturgeon "as big as himself" in 1954. He has not seen one since.

The Rappahannock rises in the [[Blue Ridge]] and flows through the Virginia [[Piedmont]] toward [[Chesapeake Bay]]. The author describes the river as once full of migrating fish during spring runs, which supported Fredericksburg as a sporting destination.

The [[Embrey Dam]] was built in 1910 to generate electricity and divert water into a canal. According to the article:

- Its generator is no longer operating.
- Water for the canal and for drinking could be piped in from upstream instead.
- It has blocked the spawning migrations of an uncountable number of anadromous fish.
- It has altered the river "beyond recognition."

The column also cites a national figure of roughly 75,000 dams in the United States. It states that the Embrey Dam is typical of many small, low-value structures, in contrast to large western dams such as those on the [[Columbia River]].

Anadromous fish are defined in the text as species that live in salt water but spawn in fresh water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article presents the following as the main ecological impacts and pressures:

- **Migration blockage:** Dams prevent anadromous fish from reaching upstream spawning habitat. The author describes these fish as following an innate route "like celestial coordinates."
- **Declining runs:** Perch, shad and herring runs are described as "a mere shadow of what was," and sturgeon have effectively disappeared from the local reach.
- **Fisheries:** The text says that dwindling fish stocks are affecting the nation's fishing fleet.
- **Recreation:** Tourists are described as chasing a fast-disappearing resource with rods and coolers.
- **Regulation:** The [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] is requiring environmental and safety adaptations at some dams during relicensing. In some cases, the article says, the commission is questioning whether the dams should continue to operate at all.

The text also notes that breaching small dams is less expensive than repairing them. This is presented as the main driver of removal decisions, rather than aesthetic or ecological motives.

## Key Findings

The column's main points and proposals are:

1. **Obsolete dams should be removed.** The author argues that the Embrey Dam serves "no purpose" and imposes costs that outweigh its benefits. He concludes that it should be taken down.
2. **Small dams matter as much as large ones.** Attention often focuses on big western dams, but the author argues that the many small, obsolete dams across the country have comparable ecological effects.
3. **Removal is already under way.** The article reports that more than a dozen small dams were breached in the past year. Others are scheduled for removal, including dams on the [[Elwha River]] in Washington State and the Edwards Dam on the [[Kennebec River]] in Maine.
4. **Removal is driven by economics.** The author states that removal is generally cheaper than repair, and that declining fish stocks affect the fishing industry. He also states that the tourism economy depends on the fish that remain.
5. **The Embrey Dam removal is costly and slow.** The scanned text is partly garbled, but it refers to a total cost of up to $10 million, to a cost-sharing arrangement involving the county, the state and the [[U.S. Army Corps Of Engineers]], and to a timeline of several years.
6. **Fish migration has intrinsic value.** The author argues that the migration of fish from the sea to their natal shallows is a natural mystery worth preserving "for its own sake," and that blocking it is wrong for the sake of future generations.
7. **Stated conclusion:** The article closes by arguing that free-flowing rivers are as necessary to human "psychic" well-being as they are to the survival of salmon and shad.

**Source note:** This is a single journalistic opinion piece. It provides no sample sizes, population counts, or measured statistics on fish stocks. Species identifications are common names only, and the scan contains OCR errors that were corrected where the intended word was clear.
