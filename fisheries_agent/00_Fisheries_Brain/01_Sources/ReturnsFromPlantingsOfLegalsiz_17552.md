# Returns from Plantings of Legal-Sized Brook, Brown and Rainbow Trout in the Pigeon River, Otsego County, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 study, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, evaluates the yield and survival of legal-sized hatchery trout planted during the open season in a 4.8-mile section of the [[Pigeon River]] in [[Otsego County]], Michigan. Using a compulsory permit-based [[Creel Census]] and [[Electrofishing]] population estimates, the author examined fishing effort, catch quality, planting methods, creel limits, species performance, movement after release, tag versus fin-clip recovery, and effects on wild trout. Hatchery fish made up about 70 percent of the total catch, yet roughly half of fishing trips were unsuccessful. Rainbow and brook trout outperformed brown trout, and stocking had no detectable effect on the catch of wild trout.

## Background & Description

The [[Pigeon River Trout Research Area]] was established in April 1949 at the State Forest Headquarters, 13 miles east of Vanderbilt, Otsego County. The 4.8-mile reach was divided into four fishing sections (A to D) of roughly equal length, with no weirs or barriers. Section A supported a poor native trout population, while the other sections were physically similar. Native [[Brook Trout]] outnumbered [[Brown Trout]] about 3:1, and wild [[Rainbow Trout]] were rarely encountered. Morphometric data (Table 1) gave a total area of 24.10 acres, an average width of 41 feet, and an average gradient of 9.74 feet per mile.

The study continued earlier [[Michigan Trout Stocking Experiments]] (Hazzard and Shetter, 1939; Shetter and Hazzard, 1941; Shetter, 1947), with improved creel data from the permit system and better residual-population estimates. Anglers registered at a checking station, paid no permit fee, and reported their success on leaving each section.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fishing intensity:** The three-year average was 2,414 daily trips per year, equivalent to 278 hours of effort per acre per year. Planted Sections B and C attracted about three times as much effort (155 trips per acre per year) as unplanted sections (52 per acre per year). Weekends and holidays accounted for about half of all fishing.
- **Angler residence:** Anglers came from 58 of 68 Lower Peninsula counties and from 23 states and Manitoba. Out-of-state anglers made up about 10 percent, led by Ohio, Indiana, and Illinois. Almost none came from the Upper Peninsula.
- **Water temperature:** Planting success depended strongly on temperature. Plantings made in cold water (42 to 45°F) moved downstream rapidly, while plantings above 50°F stayed near the release site. Summer plantings in June and July 1949 gave poor returns, likely due to high mortality or predation.
- **Wild fishery:** The native fishery was low-yield and stable. Fishing for wild trout averaged less than 1 fish per 5 hours of effort, attributed to heavy annual pressure and a depleted native population.

## Key Findings

- **Planting method:** Scatter-planting from a live crate offered no advantage over [[Spot Planting]] (large numbers released at one locality). Spot-plants gave slightly more recoveries (1949: 931 vs. 706; 1950: 1,049 vs. 933) and successful trips. However, making many small plantings on different dates spread catches among more anglers and successful trips. The 1949, 1950, and 1951 comparisons used 5, 3, and 7 plantings, respectively.
- **Creel limits:** Reducing the daily limit from 15 to 5 trout did little to lower unsuccessful trips. Reducing it to 2 trout, with stocking at about 400 legal fish per acre per year, cut unsuccessful trips to 36 percent. About half of all trips were unsuccessful even with 4,500 trout planted annually.
- **Species performance:** Rainbow and brook trout gave much higher returns than equal numbers of brown trout. Rainbow trout planted in 1951 (3,856 fish, excluding one cold-weather planting) exceeded 67 percent recovery. Returns from individual plantings ranged from 9.7 to 82.0 percent.
- **Overwinter survival:** Rainbow and brown trout survived over winter at similar, low rates (under 10 percent). Brook trout overwinter survival was below 3 percent. Legal-sized hatchery trout grew less than one inch per year in the stream.
- **Exploitation timing:** Brook trout were exploited most quickly, with 96 percent of recoveries in the first 40 days (only 4 percent after 40 days). Brown and rainbow trout had 74 and 79 percent of recoveries within that window.
- **Movement:** Cold-water plantings (below 50°F) moved downstream strongly, with some trout covering 1,200 to 1,600 yards in three hours. Warm-water plantings (55 to 75°F) mostly stayed in the central sections. Brook trout moved downstream most (23.8 per 1,000 hours of angling), whereas upstream movement was minimal.
- **Marking method:** Fin-clipped fish were recovered more readily than jaw-tagged fish, especially in the first week after planting. In the June 1, 1950 rainbow plantings, fin-clipped fish returned 82 percent versus 60 percent for tagged fish. The author warned that this bias could distort return estimates if confirmed.
- **Effect on wild fish:** Planting up to 431 trout per mile had no detectable effect on the catch of wild trout. The correlation between hatchery and wild catch per hour was 0.12 (Table 12). The author rejected Chamberlain's (1943) claim that stocking protects wild fish. Increased fishing pressure lowered the catch rate for wild fish, but total exploitation stayed the same.
- **Catch quality:** Catch per hour per fishing trip was generally poor for native fish, but hatchery fish made up about 70 percent of the total catch over the three years.
- **Management implication:** Stocking many small, frequent plantings with fin-clip marking and a lower daily limit was favored over single large releases. Brook and rainbow trout were preferred over brown trout, and cold-water timing improved returns.
