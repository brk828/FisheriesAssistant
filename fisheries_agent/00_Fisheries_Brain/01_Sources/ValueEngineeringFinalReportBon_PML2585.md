# Bonita Creek Fish Barrier Value Engineering Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Value Engineering (VE) report, prepared by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Region, Phoenix Area Office) in June 2006, reviews the design of a proposed [[Fish Barrier]] on [[Bonita Creek]], a perennial tributary of the [[Gila River]] in Graham County, Arizona. The barrier is required under a [[Biological Opinion]] issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to prevent the upstream spread of non-native fishes introduced through the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP), thereby protecting existing native fish.

A five-person [[Value Study Team]] met on May 15, 2006, for a one-day study of the baseline concept, estimated at $1,399,195 (excluding unlisted items and contingencies). The team developed four proposals, of which Proposals 1, 2 and 3 are mutually exclusive. Maximum potential savings if all proposals were accepted total $500,477, after deducting the $34,000 study cost once. Two additional ideas were referred to the design team.

## Background & Description

**Regulatory origin.** Concerns in the late 1980s about non-native fish spreading into the Gila River Basin via CAP water deliveries led the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to issue a Biological Opinion. Fish barriers are specifically required by that opinion.

**Site characteristics.**
- Perennial creek with a flow of 24 cfs at the barrier site.
- Canyon width abutment to abutment: approximately 163 feet.
- Abutment rock: [[Volcanics and Conglomerates]], believed to continue below alluvium; rock dives steeply and was not encountered at reasonable depths (<60 ft) 15 ft or more from the abutments.
- Groundwater about 4 ft deep across the channel width, requiring extensive [[Dewatering]].
- Nearest city: Safford, Arizona, about 18.5 miles away, with adequate local supplies for construction.
- Design flood: 100-year instantaneous peak flow estimated at 32,700 cfs (USGS gauging).

**Baseline design.** The structure is entirely reinforced concrete with four components:
1. Upstream scour wall (extends to natural channel scour depth; arched to transfer load to abutments, eliminating piles).
2. Crest, providing the minimum four-foot vertical drop needed to block fish movement.
3. Apron, a sloping grade that dissipates high-velocity flow and further impedes fish.
4. Downstream scour wall, with riprap to prevent a scour hole, since scour holes serve as staging and refuge areas for non-native fish.

**Access constraints.** A [[City of Safford]] infiltration gallery about three miles upstream feeds a conveyance pipeline attached to the canyon walls near the barrier. Roadway access along the canyon must be maintained, and a vehicle ramp at one abutment must still preserve the four-foot drop. The [[Bureau of Land Management]], which owns the land, opposed rerouting the road out of the canyon.

**Code and permit requirements.** These include the Biological Opinion, Reclamation Safety and Health Standards, Arizona Department of Environmental Quality turbidity limits, Clean Water Act 404 and 401 permits, NEPA compliance, Storm Water Pollution Prevention, and Arizona Department of Water Resources approval for dewatering wells. Site clearing must occur outside the [[Willow Flycatcher]] breeding season, and construction must occur in low flood-probability periods.

**Value methodology.** The team used the [[Value Method]] (Job Plan approach originally developed by Lawrence Miles in 1943) and a [[Function Analysis System Technique]] (FAST) diagram to identify critical functions such as preventing passage, withstanding flood, maintaining access, and protecting species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Target concern:** preventing passage of non-native fishes upstream into native fish habitat in the Gila River Basin.
- **Stakeholders** listed in the function analysis include the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the Central Arizona Water Conservation District, the Arizona Game and Fish Department (terrestrial species impacts), the City of Safford (water quality and supply), the cattle rancher (access), and the general public.
- **Habitat issues:** scour holes below structures create non-native fish refuge; construction turbidity and pond formation are functions to control; the design must avoid destroying habitat and must mitigate for CAP canal impacts and [[Willow Flycatcher]] habitat.
- **Fish passage physics:** the air cavity immediately below the barrier wall and the "tree-nappe" zone downstream could allow fish to leap over the wall if not vented or capped, particularly at flows above roughly 800 cfs.

## Key Findings

**Baseline cost:** $1,399,195 (before unlisted items and contingencies).

**Proposal summary** (Proposals 1–3 are interdependent; Proposal 3 is independent of the others):

| No. | Proposal | Value Concept Cost | Net Savings (after implementation cost) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Construct barrier as a straight crest (reinforced earth gravity structure) | $1,294,794 | $29,402 |
| 2 | Use roller compacted concrete (RCC) for crest, upstream scour wall and apron; eliminate downstream scour wall and riprap | $821,305 | $502,890 |
| 3 | Place grouted riprap for portions of the downstream apron (concrete kept in low-flow channel) | $1,342,608 | $31,587 |
| 4 | Reduce apron and cutoff wall thickness by 3 inches (from 24 in.) | $1,379,133 | $10,062 |

Note: the RCC proposal (No. 2) accounts for nearly all of the maximum potential savings. Proposal 1 is credited with $104,402 in gross savings less $75,000 implementation costs.

**Key risks and design considerations:**
- RCC placement may be affected by high ambient temperature and requires foundation treatment; the unconsolidated substrate at excavation depth may need stabilization to prevent sliding.
- A straight gravity structure depends on suitable compacted backfill; reinforced earth construction may shorten the structure's life.
- Grouted riprap may create lower velocity near the boundary; borrow material may be hard to source if excavation material is insufficient.
- Proposal 4 must confirm resistance to overturning and shear at the crest wall, based on estimated water impact about 7.5 ft downstream (drop time 0.498 sec, velocity 15 ft/s at discharge above 25,000 cfs).

**Design concerns raised by the team (seven items):**
1. Vent the air cavity below the barrier wall to prevent fish being drawn over it.
2. Re-evaluate dewatering cost, which may be low.
3. Verify riprap placement cost, including whether blasting is required.
4. A cantilevered cap may be needed to prevent fish passage above about 800 cfs.
5. Numerous warped (two-direction sloped) sections could complicate construction.
6. Mass and structural concrete unit costs may be low; local suppliers should be consulted.
7. Additional item noted in the source.

**Alternatives screened:** about 40 ideas were considered and documented, including electrical barriers, sheet piles, inflatable dams, microwave barriers, fish screens, ice dams, Obermeyer gates, Denil fish ladders, and dewatering wells. Most were rejected for feasibility or cost. Two were referred to the design team for further consideration: a single trench with sheet pile wall, and a slurry backfill beside a sheet pile wall.

Kramer (Value Study Team Leader), Charlie Kriewald, Will Doyle, Brad Prudhom (geologist), Paul Marsh (biologist, Arizona State University), and Brent Mefford (hydraulic engineer).
