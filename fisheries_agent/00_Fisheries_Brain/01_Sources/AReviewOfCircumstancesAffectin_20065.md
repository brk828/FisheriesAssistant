# Circumstances Affecting the Abundance of Gambusia Gaigei, an Endangered Fish Endemic to Big Bend National Park

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This review traces the decline, rescue, and recovery of [[Gambusia Gaigei]], an endangered poeciliid fish known only from springs near the [[Rio Grande]] in [[Big Bend National Park]]. The species' status has been shaped less by its biology than by human actions: a 1950s children's fishing pool built at [[Rio Grande Village]] that introduced competing fishes, later invasion by [[Gambusia Affinis]] and [[Green Sunfish]] through irrigation water, and cold-related die-offs in isolated refugia. Remedial efforts, including [[Rotenone Treatment]], translocations, and captive culture at several institutions, kept the species alive. The entire surviving population descends from a few individuals, and the document proposes a natural spring-fed stream as the long-term solution.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and relationships.** [[Carl L. Hubbs]] described *G. gaigei* in 1929 and placed it in the [[Gambusia Nobilis Species Group]]. Later authors placed it in either the *G. nobilis* or *G. senilis* group, and all considered it closely related to *G. senilis*. The status of two Chihuahuan species, *G. hurtadoi* and *G. alvarezi*, remains disputed. [[Rosen and Bailey]] (1963) treated them as junior synonyms of *G. gaigei*, while Rivas (1963) and Peden (1973, 1975) regarded all three as distinct. [[Miller]] (1977) listed all three in the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] fish fauna. The authors note that, whatever the species-level outcome, distinct races are involved and would fall under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973.

**Type locality.** The species was described from specimens collected by Frederick M. Gaige on 3 August 1928 from a marshy cattail slough fed by springs near the Rio Grande at Boquillas. No further specimens were collected until 11 June 1954, when fish were obtained from [[Spring 4]] (also called Graham Ranch Warm Spring). Gaige later insisted his collection came from a different spring, one with a built bathhouse and concrete walls, now identified as [[Boquillas Spring]]. Field reports from 1937–1938 by Van Tyne and Murray better fit Spring 4, suggesting that observers at the time may have considered it the type locality. The authors conclude that the type locality is probably [[Boquillas Spring]], while acknowledging that the evidence is uncertain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The original populations lived in small spring-fed sloughs on the north shore of the Rio Grande, 2–4 km west of Boquillas Canyon. Spring 4 is distinctly above the flood plain and was enclosed in a circular concrete and rock wall about 2 m high. Its overflow fed a dredged ditch. Boquillas Spring went dry during drought years (reported as "a few years ago" by Ranger John Palmer) and was later colonized by [[Gambusia Affinis]] from the adjacent river. The cattail slough near the original type site was reported to be eroding rapidly by river side-cutting.

**Human modifications.** Around 1955, a children's fishing pool was built at Rio Grande Village with a dredged ditch of "1 to 10 foot wide and 2 feet deep" and a pool about 20 × 40 feet and 4 feet deep. Catchable fish seined from the Rio Grande were added, and this also released [[Gambusia Affinis]], [[Notropis Lutrensis]], and other minnows into the system. Emergent vegetation grew rapidly, and in drought years little or no water left the pool.

**Refugium management.** Irrigation of campground shade trees with Rio Grande water fed a pond that overflowed into the refugium. This allowed mosquitofish and green sunfish to enter, and by 1960 the old refugium had been invaded. The new refugium pool, a dug pond filled with water from [[Spring 1]], is located in a brushy grove and is protected by barbed-wire fencing and a locked gate. Spring 1 has no surface flow, which limits the chance of fish introduction with incoming water.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic status.** The validity of *G. hurtadoi* and *G. alvarezi* as separate species is disputed, but all authors agree that *G. gaigei* is closely related to *G. senilis* and that the populations are distinct enough to be protected.

**Historical distribution.** Evidence supports the hypothesis that in 1930 *G. gaigei* lived in at least two springs (possibly three) 2–4 km west of Boquillas Canyon. Later surveys of Hot Springs, Bathtub springs, river-bank outflows, and Glenn Springs found only [[Gambusia Affinis]]. Today the species "abounds" in the outflows of Boquillas Spring and Spring 4, but the authors state that the entire known population descends from Spring 4 stock.

**Decline and invasion.** In 1954 most fish sampled were *G. gaigei*. By 27–28 April 1956, *G. affinis* was far more abundant. The 1956 rotenone treatment, applied in an unsuccessful attempt to remove *G. affinis*, left fewer than 25 additional *G. gaigei*. This was followed by the reintroduction of *G. affinis* from the adjacent river.

**Population numbers and rescue.**
- 9 October 1956: 25 *G. gaigei* seined and held in live tanks. Fewer than 25 more were found after rotenone treatment.
- 15 fish were placed in Boquillas Spring, Glenn Springs, and a stock tank. None was seen again.
- 10 fish were divided between Panther Junction (6, with 2 surviving to reproduce but all dying over the winter) and Austin (4). Of the Austin fish, 1 female and 2 males survived the winter in a laboratory aquarium room.
- The three survivors were returned to the park in spring 1957, and the descendants "commonly exceeded 1000 individuals."

**1960 second decline.** On 16 April 1960, the refugium was found to be experiencing the same invasion as before. Fifteen *G. gaigei* were taken to Austin (2 died), where 40 young were born. Sixteen of 50–60 fish were sent to the University of Michigan as an insurance stock. On 7 August 1960, 20 of the 38 surviving Austin fish were returned to the park's new pool, and 8 fish were recovered from the old refugium. The remaining 18 were cultured at the Brackenridge Field Laboratory.

**Green sunfish incursion (1968).** [[Green Sunfish]] were found in the new refugium, likely introduced by anglers who had caught them in the old pond. The pool was drained, the non-target fish were eliminated, and the *G. gaigei* were returned and the population recovered.

**Contamination of backup stocks.** In 1972, a hose to the concrete-lined pond in Austin had been moved, so dead fish were found after a cold night. The hoses for the *G. gaigei* and [[Gambusia Amistadensis]] backup pools were connected by an unvalved T and placed in the same water, so cross-contamination between the two species was possible. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] tested the survival of these stocks at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in 1973, and the Dexter stocks were replaced with pure stocks on 9 August 1976.

**Cold shock (December 1975).** Dead and erratically swimming fish were observed after a cold night, with low water and accumulated organic debris on the bottom. The authors attribute this to [[Cold Shock]]. Sediment accumulation had reduced the pool's thermal buffer.

**Recovery and current status.** The refugium was found empty on 16 March 1976 and again on 6 May 1976. On 7 August 1976, live *G. gaigei* were found, presumably from surviving females or fish kept indoors, and the stock was supplemented with fish from Austin.

**Management plan.** The [[National Park Service]] installed a flow-through system fed by Spring 1 to ensure survival until a more permanent solution is available. The preferred long-term plan is to create a natural spring run using water from Spring 1 or Spring 4. Spring 4 is a known habitat but lies close to the campground, which may cause problems. Spring 1 is regulated, probably once held *G. gaigei*, shares an aquifer with Spring 4, and lies near historical sites that could support an educational guided tour. The authors argue that either option could restore a natural stream system that would favor the natural *G. gaigei* genome.
