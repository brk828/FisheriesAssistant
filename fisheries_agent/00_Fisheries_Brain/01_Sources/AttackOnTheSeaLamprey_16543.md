# Sea Lamprey Attack on the Great Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Petromyzontidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 special report by [[James W. Moffett]], of the [[Bureau of Commercial Fisheries]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service), reprinted from the *Michigan Conservation Magazine* (May-June 1958), argues that the [[Sea Lamprey]] caused the collapse of the [[Lake Trout]] fishery across the upper [[Great Lakes]]. Overfishing, disease, other predators and food shortage are each ruled out on the evidence. The report then describes a two-part control program: [[Electrical Weir Barriers]] with mechanical traps to block spawning runs, and the selective larvicide [[Dowlap]] (3,4,6-trichloro-2-nitrophenol), tested in [[Elliott Creek]] in 1957. It closes by arguing that lake trout must be restocked artificially, a process expected to take about ten years.

## Background & Description

**Invasion history.** Sea lampreys entered the upper Great Lakes above [[Niagara Falls]] through the [[Welland Canal]] around 1921. The canal bypassed the falls, and conditions in the lakes were ideal for an explosive increase. Lake trout were abundant, and their habits suited a parasite that feeds exclusively on fish blood. Lampreys reached [[Lake Superior]] only after getting through the locks in the [[St. Mary's River]]; by 1947 they had established a rapidly growing population there.

**Parasitic biology.** The adult lamprey attaches to fish with rasping teeth and sucks blood from the wound. Aquarium-reared lampreys each killed an average of 18.6 pounds of fish during their parasitic phase, and the author notes that wild lampreys probably eat more.

**Life cycle (as described):**
- Adults enter streams in spring to spawn. Each female deposits an average of 61,500 eggs in a crescent-shaped gravel nest that the lamprey builds by moving stones with its mouth. Adults die after spawning.
- Eggs hatch in 2-3 weeks, depending on water temperature. Larvae drift downstream to silt beds and burrow into soft mud.
- Larvae remain as harmless burrowers for 5-7 years, then transform into adults about five inches long.
- Adults move to the lakes in winter and early spring and parasitize fish for 2-20 months.
- They stop feeding the following spring, return to stream mouths, mature, and migrate upstream to spawn. They have never been known to spawn successfully in the lakes.

**Lake trout.** [[Lake Trout]] are deep, cold-water fish. Burbot, which shares their habits and size, also disappeared. Their main prey was deep-water chubs, herring, cottids and smelt.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Scale of decline.**
- Lake Huron: trout production fell from 5 million pounds (1938) to under 400,000 pounds (1947). It is now nil in the main lake.
- Lake Michigan: production fell from 6.5 million pounds (1944) to 400 pounds (1953). Fishermen lost more than 5.5 million dollars in annual income, and losses to related trade and sport fisheries are described as inestimable.
- Lake Superior: average production of 4.4 million pounds per year dropped to less than half that by 1956, and the fishery was expected to collapse soon.
- The collapse spread from lake to lake in order of proximity to Niagara Falls.

**Effects on other species.** Species not originally major targets are now in serious peril. Two species of large chubs are almost extinct, and other large chubs have declined markedly. Whitefish stocks are very low, and there is evidence of serious predation on [[Walleye]] and suckers in several localities.

**Spawning-stream impact.** At the Ocqueoc River, 24,643 adult lampreys were captured in one season. That single run destroyed at least 458,000 pounds of fish. The author cites at least 50 such spawning streams tributary to Lakes Huron and Michigan.

**Alternative causes ruled out.**
- *Overfishing:* fishing intensity in Lake Michigan was about 10% below normal for the three years before 1945. The trout fishery had been absent from Lake Huron for ten years and from Lake Michigan for seven, with no sign of recovery.
- *Disease:* no evidence was observed.
- *Other predators:* the burbot was the only other deep-water predator, and it disappeared along with the trout.
- *Food shortage:* chubs, herring, cottids and smelt were abundant and, in Lake Michigan, small chubs were about 350% more numerous than in 1930-1932.
- *Size-selective evidence:* small trout and burbot, not especially attacked, declined less than fish weighing one pound or more. Thousands of dead trout and burbot bearing fresh lamprey scars were also found after storms.

**Lake Ontario.** The lamprey came to the upper lakes from [[Lake Ontario]], where lake trout production has been poor despite good environmental conditions.

## Key Findings

**1. Lamprey predation is the main cause of the trout collapse.** The report rules out overfishing, disease, other predators and food shortage, and it documents the lamprey-scarred mortality pattern.

**2. Control at spawning runs is partly effective.** Early [[Weir]] designs were expensive and unreliable. A combination of mechanical traps and an electrical field blocks upstream migration without clogging from debris. Fish are released upstream, while some lampreys are turned back and others pass through the field and are killed. The method was then being applied in streams of Lakes Superior and Michigan.

**3. Downstream adults and larvae are hard to control.**
- Newly transformed adults resisted electric shock. Lethal voltage was uneconomical, and sound and light experiments failed. Inclined-plane screens were nearly impossible to maintain during peak migration.
- Larval control options ranged from bulldozing stream banks to chemicals.

**4. Dowlap is a selective larvicide.** After three years of testing some 6,000 chemicals, [[Nitrophenol]] compounds containing halogens were found specific to lamprey larvae and harmless to most fishes (trout especially), most fish foods, wildlife and domestic stock. The selected compound, 3,4,6-trichloro-2-nitrophenol, was developed with the [[Dow Chemical Company]] of Midland, Michigan.

**5. Elliott Creek trial (October 1957).**
- Application: lower two miles of Elliott Creek, Cheboygan County, Michigan, at a uniform 30 ppm.
- Test larvae: of more than 1,000 caged larvae, 96.6% were killed.
- Fish mortality: of more than 1,000 brook trout, 19 were found dead. Of 400 rainbow trout, 11 died. None of 100 brown trout died. Some mudminnows, northern redbelly dace and small common white suckers were killed among 14 other species present.
- Invertebrates: the small shrimp [[Gammarus]] was the only susceptible invertebrate, and not all were killed.

**6. Expected results of treatment.** Parasitism by lampreys from a treated stream should stop in the year after treatment and should not return for three or four years. Further trials were scheduled, and chemical control combined with electrical barriers was judged economical if results held.

**7. Restocking is required.** Control of lampreys is only half the job. The lake trout niche is currently unoccupied, and there are not enough surviving trout in Lakes Michigan and Huron to rebuild the population naturally. Stocking yearling trout would take about ten years to establish a self-sustaining breeding population. Open questions include the supply of eggs (especially if Lake Superior trout disappear), hatchery capacity, fishing regulations, exploitation levels, and which trout races to use.

**8. Conclusion.** The author judges that the restoration can succeed and that the fishery will be needed in the future for sport and food.
