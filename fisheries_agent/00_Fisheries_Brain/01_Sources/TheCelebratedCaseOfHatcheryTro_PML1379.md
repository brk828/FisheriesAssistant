# Hatchery Trout Versus Wild Trout: A Mock Trial and Fish Culture Compendium from The Progressive Fish Culturist

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a multi-part issue of *The Progressive Fish Culturist* (mid-1930s, U.S. Bureau of Fisheries) built around a satirical mock trial, *The Celebrated Case of Hatchery Trout vs. Wild Trout*. The trial "verdict" favours hatchery-reared trout against the defendant Mr. Average Angler, whose claim is that hatchery fish are inferior. The surrounding articles supply technical evidence: selective breeding results, liberation and stomach-content studies, digestion physiology, the economics of stocking fish of different sizes, fish food formulations, potassium permanganate parasite control, and abstracts from the 29th annual American Fisheries Society meeting (Tulsa, Oklahoma, 1935).

## Background & Description

The mock trial is a rhetorical piece: a plaintiff's attorney argues that hatchery trout are fit for angling, and a defence counsel argues the opposite. Testimony is attributed to Bureau of Fisheries staff and academics, including [[Dr. H. S. Davis]] (fish pathology), [[Dr. C. M. McCay]] (animal nutrition, Cornell), [[Dr. G. C. Embody]] (Cornell, stocking and selective breeding), [[R. F. Lord]] (Pittsford, Vermont hatchery), and [[Dr. Paul Needham]] (Bureau of Fisheries).

The remaining sections are technical articles:
- **Digestion in the Stomachs of Trout** by Professor C. M. McCay, a historical review from Hunter and Spallanzani through pepsin and hydrochloric acid discovery to modern work on temperature effects on fish pepsin.
- **Comparative Costs of Rearing Fish of Different Sizes** by A. C. Taft, costing stocking sizes from 1 to 6 inches.
- **Breed More Bass**, a note on small-mouthed black bass introduced to Britain.
- **Oklahoma Meeting of the American Fisheries Society**, meeting summary and paper abstracts on planned water utilization, fish food supply, trout nutrition, salmon by-products as food, dry diets, feeding in California hatcheries, potassium permanganate, an adjustable fish grader, minnow (bait) rearing, and chemical treatment of lakes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The issue frames hatcheries as tools for restoring depleted wild stocks. It notes that wild trout had declined in numbers and that hatchery brood stock was developed from eggs of wild-run fish. The Ohio paper on planned water use argues that water areas should be inventoried and managed for their full range of functions, with fish production as one service among several. Its proposals include protecting forage fish, building riffles and pools, reservoirs for overwintering, pollution control, and managed harvest with closed seasons and bag limits.

Lake-management abstracts (Lake Monona, Wisconsin) report that copper sulfate spraying controlled algae, while rooted water weeds persisted and required mechanical or arsenical removal. Nutrient supply (nitrogen, phosphorus, bicarbonates) was identified as the main driver of algae and reed growth.

## Key Findings

**Selective breeding and hatchery performance (Vermont and New Jersey data):**
- Twenty-two wild brook trout females spawned 7,412 eggs in total, averaging 109.65 eggs per fish (fish 5.75 to 6.25 inches).
- Twenty-two hatchery-reared yearling females produced 7,738 eggs in total, averaging 351.7 per fish (6.75 to 9 inches).
- Best two-year brook trout at Pittsford: 958 eggs (1928), 1,115 (1930), 1,606 (1931), with individual fish reaching 2,800 eggs at 84% fertility (1952).
- At Hackettstown Hatchery (1931), yearling trout averaged 1,623.98 eggs; a second strain averaged 1,120.83. Dr. Embody's records give about 270 eggs for an average hatchery fish at first spawning versus under 200 for wild fish of the same age.
- Fingerlings from Pittsford eggs grew about three times as fast as those from a New Hampshire brood stock at Leetown, West Virginia (March to 16 August).

**Liberation experiments (Pittsford, Vermont):**
- 100 marked brook trout yearlings were released and held 34 hours. Stomachs of 78 recaptured fish contained 949 insects (79% aquatic, 21% terrestrial), averaging 12.16 per fish. Mayfly nymphs (49%) and caddis larvae (54%) were dominant among aquatic forms.
- Dr. Paul Needham's survey of 251 wild brook trout from New York State averaged 16.24 insects per fish.
- A rainbow trout duplicate experiment: 100 released, 49 recaptured. Stomachs of these fish held 573 insects (average 11.69 per fish; 95% aquatic, 21% terrestrial, with 65% mayfly nymphs), and they ate more algae than brook trout.

**Digestion (McCay):**
- Trout stomachs when empty were found to be closed and neutral in reaction; when full of food they were strongly acidic.
- Fish pepsin activity was reported to be optimal at lower temperatures, around 10 °C, in contrast to warm-blooded animals (around 40 °C). Later work (Pyatnitzki, 1925) claimed pepsin in lower and higher animals was the same.
- Food residence times in trout stomachs were recorded for various diets (liver, spleen, fish flesh, larvae, water fleas).

**Stocking economics (Taft; Table I, with 100,000 six-inch trout as the target):**
- Using Embody's mortality table and Hazzard's tables, about 500,000 trout of 1.5-inch length (or 285,700 at 1.5 inches equivalent to fewer) would be most economical in food and egg terms, with 2 and 2.5-inch fish the most favourable on mortality. Above 3 inches costs rose rapidly.
- Brown's Cache National Forest report (Table II) compared large and small stocking sizes for brown, rainbow, and cutthroat trout.

**Fish food and nutrition:**
- Hatcheries used about 1,455,000 pounds of fish food in 1934, valued at about $608,000; roughly three-quarters was meat products.
- Meat protein costs were about three times that of fishery products on a per-unit-protein basis.
- Dry-meal diets for trout were tested at 47°F (about 8.3°C) and 50–69°F. The best diet (15% beef liver, 29% skim milk, 28% cottonseed meal, 28% salmon egg meal) gave 55% monthly gain at about 25 cents per pound of fish.
- Salmon by-product trials found that 30% liver plus 70% air-dried salmon meal gave the most rapid growth at about $0.20 per pound of fish.

**Disease and parasite control:**
- A one-hour bath in potassium permanganate at 1:75,000 killed most *Gyrodactylus* on trout (5–6 inches) at 34–38°F; weaker solutions were less effective. The effect was often not visible until the next day. A weak 1:232,000 bath for 45 minutes killed all fish treated for fin rot.

**Other practical results:**
- An adjustable fish grader (brass combs fitted with rods at 1/8 or 1/4 inch spacing) was developed to reduce cannibalism.
- Minnow (bait) rearing in ponds gave better growth than in troughs; herring was superior to earthworms as bass food.

**Mock trial verdict:** The jury found the defendant guilty of libel against the Clan of Trout, and awarded the plaintiff a penalty requiring Mr. Average Angler to fish "rodless" in a hatchery-stocked stream each Sunday from May to September.
