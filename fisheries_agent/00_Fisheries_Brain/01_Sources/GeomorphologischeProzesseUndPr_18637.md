# Sources of Runoff and Sediment Supplied by the Slopes of a First Order Drainage Basin in an Arid Environment

#bioturbation #geomorphology #soil-erosion

## Executive Summary

This study by Aaron Yair examines where runoff and sediment originate on the slopes of a small first-order drainage basin in the [[Northern Negev]], near [[Sde Boker]], Israel. The half-basin was divided into ten contiguous plots draining from the upper divide to the channel mouth, and monitored during the 1972–73 rainy season. Runoff and sediment supply were strongly non-uniform across the slopes. One plot covering 13.4% of the area supplied 21% of the runoff and 42% of the sediment. The author attributes this mainly to the digging activity of burrowing animals ([[Porcupine]] and [[Isopod]]), which loosens the soil crust and produces fine, easily removable sediment, rather than to slope length or gradient. The paper is a single-season report and the author explicitly notes that the conclusions require further confirmation.

## Background & Description

Desert floods are thought to begin mainly with [[Overland Flow]], since interflow is assumed to be minor in arid terrain and channel flow generally follows intense overland flow. Earlier work on runoff and erosion relied on small plots, whose results were often extrapolated to whole watersheds. The author argues this is unreliable, because runoff does not occur uniformly across the slopes of a small watershed even under uniform rainfall. Slope length and angle vary systematically along a watershed: slopes are gentle and short near the upper divide, long and steep in the middle, and short and gentle near the mouth. These differences influence soil depth, grain size and other surface properties.

To address this, the author drained the whole slope area of one side of the channel using contiguous plots. The basin was chosen because it is small enough to be fully instrumented and, according to the author, quite representative, since first-order basins generally cover about 50% of the surface of higher-order basins.

**Study area.** The site lies in the Northern Negev near Sde Boker. Mean annual precipitation is estimated at 90 mm, mostly from low-intensity showers, with only exceptional rains exceeding 20 mm per day. The site covers 11,335 m², divided into ten plots ranging from 510 m² to 1,830 m². Relative relief is about 50 m, and slope lengths range from 55 to 76 m. Mean slope gradients are 24–29.5% for plots I–VIII and lower for plots IX and X (17.5% and 11.5%), which include the flat upper part of the watershed.

**Instrumentation.** Rainfall was recorded by one rainfall recorder and seven small rain gauges. Low walls at the slope bases directed runoff into collectors emptied through volumetric flow meters. Coarse sediment (>0.5 mm) was trapped in two metal sieves. Finer sediment was measured by concentration analysis of a 30-litre sample taken from the flow after it passed the sieves.

**Geology and soils.** The strata are Turonian limestones of three formations, each forming a distinct ecological environment:

- [[Drorim Formation]] (lower slope): strata 10–30 cm thick and densely jointed, weathering to cobbles and boulders. Bare rock covers 20–30% of the surface. Soil is 20–60 cm deep and stony, a desert brown lithosol grading to loessial serozem at the slope base. A soil crust up to 1 cm thick limits erosion.
- [[Shivta Formation]] (central site): massive limestone forming rock terraces, 20–80 cm thick, with spaced jointing. Bedrock is exposed over 40–50% of the surface. Shallow loessial serozem fills joints as strips at the terrace bases, and lichens cover 30–50% of these strips.
- [[Netser Formation]] (upper site): limestone with flint nodules, 10–30 cm thick and very densely jointed. Bedrock covers 20–30% of the surface, and the very shallow, stony soil forms small patches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rainfall and runoff in 1972–73.** The season was a drought year, with 42 mm of rain on 14 rain days. Most of the rain fell in three storms, which produced all the measured runoff. Rainfall was fairly evenly distributed during the first two storms. During the third, base-of-slope gauges recorded 13–16 mm, while divide gauges and the recorder recorded 9–10 mm. Runoff occurred in storms whose peak intensities exceeded about 0.2–0.4 mm per 3 minutes. The author suggests peaks occurred near the end of storms, when soils were probably already saturated. Runoff coefficients were positively related to rainfall amount and intensity, and highest on the first flow event.

**Animal-driven sediment supply.** Detailed mapping showed that the highest sediment supply followed the animals rather than the topography:

- [[Porcupine]] digging on the [[Shivta Formation]] targets the root tubers of [[Erodium Hirtum]] (hairy storksbill). This breaks the soil crust and makes fine, loose aggregates available to overland flow.
- [[Isopod]] activity on the [[Drorim Formation]] is less intense. Isopods break the crust, dig to about 40 cm, and excrete soil as small rectangular cubes, about 2 × 1 × 1 mm, composed of silty clay. These cubes disintegrate under raindrop impact and are easily removed even by shallow flows.

**Interpretation by formation.** On the Shivta formation, extensive rock outcrops generate relatively high runoff per unit area. Runoff moving orthogonally to the rock terraces gains energy at terrace bases, where it meets loose, disaggregated sediment. This combination is interpreted as the cause of accelerated erosion on plot VII (entirely on Shivta) and on plots VIII and IX (lower parts on Shivta). On the Drorim and Netser formations, erosion is less intense. The author cites small burrowing-sediment amounts, densely jointed and discontinuous outcrops, low terrace height (≤20 cm), and high surface roughness from stoniness as limiting factors on runoff volume and flow velocity.

## Key Findings

**Runoff distribution (1972–73, Table 3):**

- No clear difference in runoff contribution could be established among plots, but plot VII produced more runoff per unit area than any other plot.
- Plots II and III produced the lowest runoff per unit area.
- The remaining plots contributed runoff roughly in proportion to their area.
- Plot location, slope length and slope angle did not explain the runoff pattern, although the author notes more data are needed.

**Sediment distribution:**

- Total sediment removed from the drained area is estimated at 800 kg/ha. This gives an average denudation rate of 0.005 mm/year for the short measurement period, which the author describes as very low.
- Sediment removal increased in all plots with increasing runoff volume.
- At the three recorded flows, three plots (VII, VIII and IX) supplied 75–88% of the total sediment. Their areas were 15.2%, 10.5% and 14.4% of the drained area, supplying 42%, 22% and 17% of the sediment respectively.
- The remaining plots, covering about 60% of the surface, supplied only 19% of the sediment, ranging from 1.5% to 5.4% per plot.
- These sediment differences greatly exceed differences in runoff coefficients and in plot area, slope length or gradient.

**Burrowing sediment (mapped September 1973):**

- The site was divided into a grid of 5 × 10 m squares. Burrows of porcupines and isopods were counted in each square and multiplied by an average sediment weight per burrow.
- Estimated available sediment was 121 kg on the Drorim formation and 392 kg on the Shivta formation. The Netser value in Table 4 is illegible in the scanned source and is not reported here.
- The author concludes that loose sediment from burrowing animals could account for most of the sediment removed from the site, since direct shear erosion by overland flow is inhibited by low flow velocity, the soil crust, and stone and lichen cover (up to 50% of the surface).

**Implications stated by the author:**

- Common soil-erosion prediction equations (e.g., Musgrave 1954; Meyer & Monke 1965; Smith & Wischmeier 1962; Zingg 1950) ignore the role of animal digging, which the author argues exceeds the influence of slope length and gradient in this area.
- Most digging occurs in hot, dry seasons, so winter sediment yield depends partly on the previous summer's burrowing. Following a rainy year, a drought year can reduce active isopod burrows by 50–90% (Shahak, personal information), which would limit sediment supply. Rainfall properties of a season may therefore affect the next season's erosion more than static surface properties.
- Sediment movement appears discontinuous. Upper plots III–VI lie on the Shivta formation, where sediment production is high, yet little sediment reached their bases. The author suggests much of it was deposited within the underlying Drorim outcrops. If so, runoff from upper slopes may not have reached the base, and runoff coefficients and denudation rates may not apply to the whole area.

**Abstract summary:** Data from one rainy season show non-uniform runoff and, mainly, sediment contributions. The author highlights the decisive influence of the burrowing activity of desert animals on the production of loose sediment easily removed by shallow flows, with spatial patterns closely linked to the three lithological units.
