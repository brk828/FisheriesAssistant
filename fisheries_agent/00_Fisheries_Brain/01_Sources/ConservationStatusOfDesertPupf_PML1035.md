# Conservation Status of Desert Pupfish, *Cyprinodon macularius*, in Mexico and Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 field survey, published in *Copeia*, assesses the conservation status of the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] ([[Cyprinodon Macularius]]) at 23 historic and potential habitats in northwestern Mexico and Arizona. The authors found the species persisting at several localities, but usually at low abundance and in habitats dominated by exotic fishes. They also report a large, apparently exclusive, reproducing population at [[Cerro Prieto]], Baja California, and conclude that the long-term prospects for the species in the wild are poor without continued human intervention.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Desert Pupfish]] ([[Cyprinodon Macularius]]) was listed as endangered by the [[International Union for Conservation of Nature]] (IUCN, 1979) and by the [[United States Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1986).
- **Taxonomic context:** The study notes that a new subspecies, *C. m. eremus*, had been described from Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona, by [[Miller and Fuiman]] (1987). Uncertainty about the species' status had prompted the survey.
- **Study objectives:** The authors aimed to:
  - Resample historic localities.
  - Report recent populations in Sonora and Baja California.
  - Document the success of [[Reintroduction]] efforts in Arizona.
- **Sampling methods:** Fish were collected during 1–8 September (year partly garbled in the scan; consistent with the 1988 acceptance date) using seines, with some specimens preserved as vouchers and others counted and released. Vouchers were deposited in the collection of the Centro Ecológico de Sonora in Hermosillo. Figure 1 maps the surveyed localities, with solid circles marking sites where pupfish were collected and open circles marking sites where they were not.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Rio Sonoyta
- Pupfish were continuously distributed along a reach of at least 3 km above the point where surface flow sank into the streambed, extending to below the highway bridge in the town of Sonoyta.
- A dry reach separated this section from a second reach of surface flow, about 17–30 km downstream. Pupfish abundance dropped sharply there, with 0.7% of 1,124 fish taken at one locality.
- Across the basin, exotic fishes made up 51.8% and native species 48.2% of all specimens.
- [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish) was present at every basin locality and was the only species at localities 1–3 and 23.
- [[Agosia Chrysogaster]] (longfin dace) was the other native fish in the basin. Mosquitofish and longfin dace made up 46.4% and 46.6% of fish at localities 6 and 21, respectively, where pupfish were also collected.
- [[Ameiurus Melas]] (black bullhead) was first recorded in the basin in 1983 and is now abundant at locality 4, where pupfish have not been taken.

### Lower Colorado River and El Doctor
- Along the lower Colorado River, pupfish were found at localities 6–10 near El Doctor, a small town with spring-fed marshes.
- These marshes were dominated by exotics, especially [[Poecilia Latipinna]] (sailfin molly) and mosquitofish, along with [[Tilapia]] sp. Pupfish were rare, and their share of the catch was small.
- Localities 14–16 and 18 consisted of 90–100% exotic fishes.

### Cerro Prieto and Associated Habitats
- At [[Cerro Prieto]], a geothermal power plant area with saline marshland, pupfish were abundant and reproducing. This population appears to be exclusive, with no other fishes present in the marsh.
- The authors estimate about 6 km² of saline marsh at the site, based on a 1:50,000 topographic map.
- Pupfish were also found at locality 18, a lower-salinity ditch (9 ppt) downstream of Cerro Prieto, suggesting dispersal via canals. At the Cerro Prieto marsh, seven exotic species made up 97.7% of 2,583 specimens found at the site.

### Laguna Salada and Other Sites
- At the north end of [[Laguna Salada]], pupfish had been extirpated since 1970. The authors found a healthy, reproducing population along a large, homogeneous shoreline, where the species was easily seined.
- Thousands of dead fish, including tilapia, carp, and catfish, were found along the shore. Pupfish were far less common among the dead fish than in seine samples. The authors suggest the die-off may have been linked to increasing salinity, though salinity was well below levels known to be lethal to pupfish (Barlow, 1958).
- Santa Clara Slough, at the terminus of the Sánchez Taboada canal, held pupfish that were generally rare at two localities.
- At [[Laguna Prieta]], a record from [[May]] (1976) was judged probably erroneous. Salt deposits indicated very high salinities, making recent occupancy highly improbable.

## Key Findings

1. **Habitat loss:** Irrigated agriculture, reservoir construction, and grazing-related erosion caused much pupfish habitat to be lost. The authors estimate that approximately 95% of the species' habitat was lost in the past century (the scanned value is partly garbled and interpreted here as 95%). Declines likely began before 1900.
2. **Salinity and flow changes:** Salinity in Mexican waters has risen greatly since historic times, and surface flow stopped reaching the Gulf of California by 1961.
3. **Exotic fishes:** The first exotic fish introduction to the lower Colorado River was in 1878 (catfish near Yuma, Arizona). By 1982, 44 exotic species had been recorded from the lower river in the United States. Interactions with exotics are considered a major cause of decline, and [[Tilapia]] in particular is thought to impact pupfish.
4. **Recent increase:** Pupfish were more widespread in 1988–89 than recent surveys had indicated, but they were generally rare. The authors attribute this to higher discharge after 1983, which created new habitat and aided dispersal, and they consider it a transient response.
5. **Cerro Prieto refugium:** The Cerro Prieto population may be protected from exotics by high salinity or high selenium content (per the power plant's water quality analyst), making it a potential refugium.
6. **Survival outlook:** The authors agree with [[Miller and Fuiman]] (1987) that the prospect for continued survival of *C. macularius* in any part of its original range, other than at Quitobaquito, is bleak.
7. **Refugia and introductions:** Multiple introductions to refugia in Arizona, California, and Mexico help ensure the species is not extinct in the near future. However, these semi-natural, relatively stable habitats appear unsuited to long-lived wild populations without constant human intervention.
8. **Recovery recommendations:** Recovery planners may benefit from considering riverine habitats and manipulation of flows or other disturbances, rather than relying only on marginal refugia.
9. **Other threats:** Periodic flooding and possible pesticide effects were also discussed as possible threats, though the authors did not find direct evidence of pesticide impacts in this survey.
