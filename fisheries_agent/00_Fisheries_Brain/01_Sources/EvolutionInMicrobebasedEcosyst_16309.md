# Evolution in Microbe-Based Ecosystems: Desert Springs as Analogues for Early Ecological Development

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona State University (ASU) proposal, submitted to the NASA Space Life Sciences program under an Additional Research Letter dated 2-11-00, requests roughly $749,685 in total cost over 07/01/00 to 06/30/03 to expand biologist participation in the [[NASA Astrobiology Institute]] (NAI). The project uses living [[Stromatolites]] in thermal springs of the [[Cuatro Cienegas Basin]] (CCB), Chihuahuan Desert, Mexico, as analogues for early Earth ecosystems and possible Martian habitats. It combines theoretical eco-evolutionary modelling with field sampling, experiments and paleobiological comparison, focusing on how chemical constraints (especially [[Ecological Stoichiometry]]) and genetic exchange shape the stability and tempo of evolution. The team is led by [[James J. Elser]] (Lead Investigator) under Institutional PI [[Jack D. Farmer]], with co-investigators at ASU and collaborators at the Universidad Nacional Autonoma de Mexico (UNAM) and the University of Washington.

## Background & Description

The proposal is a formal grant application package, not a research report. It contains the cover and certification forms, title and proposal summary, integrated research plan, training, management, outreach and institutional commitment sections, literature cited, curricula vitae of key personnel, facilities descriptions, budget forms, current and pending support, and appendices (letters of support and a Mexican federal collecting permit).

Key personnel and roles:
- **Jack D. Farmer** (Geology, ASU): Principal Investigator; micromorphometry and taphonomy of stromatolites.
- **James J. Elser** (Biology, ASU): Lead Investigator; limnologist, environmental monitoring, stoichiometry.
- **Co-investigators at ASU:** [[William F. Fagan]] (theoretical ecology), [[Thomas E. Dowling]] (evolutionary genetics), [[W. L. Minckley]] (conservation biology, emeritus at retirement), [[Carol M. Tang]] (paleoecology), [[Ferran Garcia-Pichel]] (geomicrobiology).
- **External collaborators:** [[Garrett M. Odell]] (University of Washington, mathematical modelling), [[Valeria Souza]] and [[Luis Enrique Eguiarte]] (UNAM, microbial population genetics).

Total requested direct costs are $518,284 and total costs $749,685 for the entire project period, with ASU providing cost share (total budget $886,285 including cost share). Indirect costs are charged at 52.5%.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study system.** The CCB is a protected area (Area de Proteccion de Flora y Fauna, administered by the Instituto Nacional de Ecologia) of about 500 km² at the eastern edge of the Chihuahuan Desert. The document reports more than 500 springs and spring-fed habitats. Springhead areas range from under 1.0 m² to over 1.0 ha; outflow ranges from seepage to about 1.0 m³/s; temperatures are 24 to about 40 °C; total dissolved solids (TDS) are 0.3 to 2.0 g/L; and water chemistry shifts from MgSO4- to Ca(HCO3)2-dominated. Downstream playa lakes (about 2.0 km²) reach 0 to about 50 °C seasonally, shift toward NaCl, and may exceed 300 g/L TDS.

**Biota of interest.** Primary producers are stromatogenic microalgae and cyanobacteria. Heterotrophic microbes include Pseudomonas, Enterobacteriaceae, Cytophaga/Flexibacter group members, fermentative anaerobes, sulfate-reducers, and halophilic Archaea and Eubacteria. Metazoan grazers include snails (such as [[Mexipyrgus]] species) and fishes, including [[Cyprinodon atrorus]] and its hybrids with C. bifasciatus, which are named in the collecting permit. The permit also lists [[Nymphophilus minckleyi]], [[Mexithauma mutilineatum]] and [[Paleomonetes]] sp.

**Analogue rationale.** The authors argue that spring waters rising through fracture zones resemble early-Earth habitats and that desert springs may resemble freshwater refuges on a desiccating planet such as Mars.

## Key Findings

The proposal sets out its central ideas and planned work rather than reporting new data. Main points:

1. **Stoichiometric constraint hypothesis.** Autotroph C:nutrient ratios rise when N, P or light are limiting, while heterotrophic consumers are homeostatically regulated. The authors hypothesise that a Precambrian-Cambrian shift (declining CO2 and rising P availability) made herbivory feasible, possibly explaining the late appearance of metazoans and the decline of stromatolites near the end of the Proterozoic (about 800 Ma).

2. **Eco-evolutionary model.** Fagan and Odell, with student Eli Meir, have built a mass-balanced differential-equation model in which consumer-resource stoichiometry constrains adaptive landscapes and food-web complexity, with nutrient content itself evolving.

3. **Gene flow and horizontal gene transfer.** The team will quantify the balance of genetic drift, gene flow and lateral gene transfer in microbes and consumers across contrasting springs, using DGGE, MLEE, SSCP, and sequencing. Target: 20 to 25 individuals or clones per locality per taxon.

4. **Field design.** Three drainages will be sampled twice yearly at three fixed sites each, spanning headspring-to-playa gradients. Year 3 will feature field and laboratory experiments, including displacement of stromatolites up- or downstream and flow-chamber manipulations of phosphorus, light and temperature.

5. **Biosignature and fossil interpretation.** Microsensor profiles (O2, pH, H2S, Ca2+), SEM, confocal and isotope analyses will link mat processes to fabric and preservation, aiming to inform interpretation of Precambrian and Martian records.

6. **Expected outcomes.** Improved understanding of conditions favouring stable ecological systems, the search for life beyond Earth, and training of students in a cross-disciplinary astrobiology programme.

7. **Budget and staffing.** ASU commits 100% funding for a full-time technician (cost savings of nearly $137,000), and provides seed money of $40K. Requested support covers 4 to 5 graduate students and 3 undergraduates per year, with two field trips of about 3 weeks each year.

8. **Permitting.** A Mexican federal permit (valid one year) authorises limited collection of Agave and Opuntia tissue, up to 30 specimens per species of several snail, shrimp and fish taxa per pool, and bacteria needed for the project. It requires written notice before and after fieldwork and a detailed report within 30 days of completion.

9. **Outreach and education.** Planned cross-listed courses in Geomicrobiology, Molecular Phylogeny and Evolution, and Analytical Biosedimentology, plus K-12 outreach via existing ASU programmes.

10. **Overlap with other funding.** Several co-investigators hold related grants (e.g. NSF IRCEB stoichiometry grant held by Elser, Fagan and Odell). The proposal states that the NASA work is distinct but shares methods and concepts, and that NASA funding would accelerate both.
