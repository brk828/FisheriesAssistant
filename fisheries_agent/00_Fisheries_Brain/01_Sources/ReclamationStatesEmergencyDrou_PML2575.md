# Drought Relief and Management: Bureau of Reclamation Response in New Mexico (1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-policy

## Executive Summary

This document combines two sources. The first is the federal statute at 43 U.S.C. Chapter 40, the Reclamation States Emergency Drought Relief Act of 1991 (Public Law 102-250). The second is a set of 1996 materials on the New Mexico drought: a May 1996 briefing exchange, a May 28, 1996 memorandum from [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Commissioner Martinez to the New Mexico Congressional Delegation, and a drought status report. Together they describe the legal tools available to respond to drought, the funding limits that constrained the response, and the hydrological conditions in New Mexico in spring 1996. The fish content is limited. The main aquatic concern is the [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] and, secondarily, the [[Pecos River Bluntnose Shiner]], both listed as endangered.

## Background & Description

**Statutory framework (43 U.S.C. Chapter 40).** The chapter defines the [[Secretary of the Interior]] as the responsible official and covers "Federal Reclamation laws," including the Act of June 17, 1902. It authorizes the following:

- **Emergency construction and conservation (§ 2211):** Temporary, non-permanent measures to reduce drought losses. Wells may be permanent facilities. The Secretary may also facilitate willing-buyer/willing-seller water purchases and participate in state water banks.
- **Temporary water availability (§ 2212):** [[Temporary Water Contracts]] for project and nonproject water, with use of Federal facilities. Contract costs must be repaid within the contract term. Temporary water does not make non-Reclamation lands subject to Federal acreage limits, and it is not treated as a new or supplemental benefit under the Reclamation Reform Act of 1982.
- **Loans (§ 2213):** Loans to water users for emergency construction, management, conservation, and water acquisition. Applicants must show repayment ability. Interest is set by the Treasury Department. Congressional committees must be notified at least 30 days before final approval.
- **Water supply opportunity studies (§ 2221):** Studies to identify conservation, augmentation, and use opportunities.
- **Drought contingency plans (§ 2222):** Cooperative plans with federal, state, tribal, and local entities. Plan elements may include water banks, conservation, reservoir storage changes, and minor structural actions. Plans must comply with NEPA and the Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act.
- **Program period (§ 2214):** Authorities were set to expire 10 years after March 5, 1992, which the 1996 memo summarizes as "through 2002."

**Program funding.** The 1991 Act authorized up to $90,000,000 in total for fiscal years 1992 through 1996. The 1996 memo states that Congress appropriated funds through the end of Fiscal Year 1996.

**Contingency planning growth.** The memo states that the number of drought contingency plans rose from 3 in 1982 to 22 in 1990. The source text is OCR-degraded, so these figures should be checked against the original.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Endangered aquatic species.** The memo identifies the [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] as the most problematic species in the 1996 drought. Maintaining flows for it was costly. Acute concerns centered on the critical stretch from San Acacia Diversion Dam to [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]], where Reclamation concentrated efforts on the first 20 miles. The [[Pecos River Bluntnose Shiner]] was also endangered, and depletion of Carlsbad Irrigation District storage would have adverse implications for it.

**Regulatory gap.** New Mexico does not recognize instream flows for fishery or environmental purposes as beneficial uses of water under state law. The memo calls this a major complicating factor.

**Hydrology (as of May 1996).**

- Stream inflows were very low, but storage was sufficient to meet obligations for the year.
- Snowpack, as reported by the Natural Resources Conservation Service on May 8, 1996: San Juan at 44% of normal and Pecos at 0%. The Upper Rio Grande, Rio Chama, and Sangre de Cristo values are partly illegible in the scan.
- The [[Otowi Gaging Station]] May 1 forecast of 200,000 acre-feet was the seventh driest in 101 years of record. The 1904 low was 118,241 acre-feet. The San Marcial forecast of 1,290 acre-feet was described as more severe.
- The [[San Juan-Chama Project]] basin expected 39% of average April–July runoff. [[Navajo Reservoir]] held 1,441,000 acre-feet of a 1,708,600 acre-foot capacity, adequate for downstream users but leaving upstream users in drought.
- Reservoir contents on May 1996 included Heron (344,752 af), Cochiti Lake (56,448 af), and Elephant Butte (1,933,269 af).

**Water use impacts.**

- The [[Pojoaque Valley Irrigation District]] was expected to run out of project storage in early June.
- The [[Fort Sumner Irrigation District]] on the Pecos River was experiencing reduced deliveries.
- Reduced supplies were hindering New Mexico's ability to deliver water to neighboring states.
- Recreational reservoir levels were expected to decline.
- Poor carry-over storage was expected to create long-term problems if drought continued.

## Key Findings

**Funding limits.** The May 28, 1996 memo reports the following:

- Reclamation obtained $237,000 from drought response funds, up from $211,000.
- It could transfer $300,000 from another account to a Construction program line item under existing reprogramming rules.
- Any additional funding would require Congressional appropriation.
- No appropriated funds were specifically available for the 1996 drought. Preliminary New Mexico estimates alone indicated that more funds would be needed.

**Reclamation's role.** The memo states that Reclamation's capacity is that of a "participant" rather than the "sole-source" provider of relief. It relies on the Federal interagency task force approach to fill gaps. The Albuquerque Area Office and field divisions at Alamosa, Carlsbad, Socorro, Truth or Consequences, and El Paso would assist New Mexico and partner agencies.

**Drought response activities.** Under Title I, no drought response activities were underway at the time of the memo. The memo lists possible actions, all subject to applicable state and federal law including NEPA:

- Construction and management: well drilling, dike and dredge work, temporary canal lining, temporary fish screens, temporary pumps, improved measurement, participation in state water banks, and diversion schedule changes.
- Short-term loans of up to 15 years to non-profit water users.
- Water purchases from willing sellers and storage or transport of water for fish and wildlife.

**Priority activities (Public Law 102-250).** The memo divides candidate actions into two categories, with rough cost estimates:

- *Category 1 (immediate benefit):* water right leasing or forbearance payments (about $1,000,000 or more); technical assistance to irrigation districts ($100,000–$500,000); and Middle Rio Grande Project infrastructure investments ($200,000).
- *Category 2 (future preparedness):* hydrologic data collection ($100,000) and accelerated system modeling for the Upper Rio Grande Water Operations Model ($100,000). The model is covered by a February 1996 Memorandum of Understanding signed by five federal agencies.

**Interim Reclamation actions.** These included the following:

- Facilitating [[San Juan-Chama Project]] contractors' release of water that would otherwise have been administratively lost, to augment the [[Middle Rio Grande Valley]] supply in water year 1996. This was expected to benefit endangered species.
- Investigating augmentation options for the silvery minnow.
- Developing an emergency onsite backup system to supply water to critical Rio Grande locations.
- Working with the [[Middle Rio Grande Conservancy District]] on a water banking proposal.
- Completing the Middle Rio Grande Water Assessment with the City of Albuquerque.
- Running a canal-lining demonstration with the Fort Sumner Irrigation District and issuing conservation grants with the New Mexico Interstate Stream Commission.

**Conservation recommendations.** The report recommends that Reclamation help water users develop Water Management and Conservation Plans, provide technical staff for short-term emergency measures, fund demonstration grants, expand public education on river operations, and remove institutional barriers to conservation.

**Statutory obligations.** The Secretary must give specific consideration to fish and wildlife needs. Contingency plans must be submitted to Congress, and a report on drought expenditures must be included in the President's annual budget.
