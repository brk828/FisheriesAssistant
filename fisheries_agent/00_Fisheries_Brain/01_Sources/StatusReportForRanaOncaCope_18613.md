# Status Report: Rana onca Cope (Utah Frog)

#herpetology #amphibian-ecology #ranidae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 status report, prepared by [[James E Platz]] for the [[U S Fish and Wildlife Service]] Office of Endangered Species, evaluates the taxonomic standing and current survival of the [[Utah Frog]] ([[Rana Onca]]), a leopard frog known from a narrow desert corridor in southwestern Utah and southern Nevada. The study combined museum specimen examination, multivariate discriminant analysis, and field surveys in summers 1983 and 1984. The author concludes that *R. onca* is a valid species, that [[Rana Fisheri]] is a synonym, and that the species is most likely extinct. No living individuals were found despite searches of numerous aquatic sites.

## Background & Description

**Nomenclature and type material.** *Rana onca* was described by [[Edward Drinker Cope]] in 1875 from a single adult female ([[Type Specimen]] USNM #25331) collected by [[H C Yarrow]] in 1872, presumably near [[St. George]], Washington County, Utah. Synonyms include *R. fisheri* (described by [[Stejneger]] in 1893 from [[Las Vegas Meadows]], Clark County, Nevada) and several other names. Most workers place *R. onca* nearest the [[Rana Pipiens Complex]], although superficial resemblance to [[Rana Pretiosa]] has been noted.

**Legal status.** The species has no international or federal listing. The current federal status is "None," and the recommended status is "Extinct." The state of Utah regards *onca* as rare and routinely denies collecting permits.

**Morphology.** Diagnostic traits, drawn from [[Wright and Wright]] (1949) and the present study, include:
- Short, indistinct dorsolateral folds
- No supralabial lip stripe anterior to the eye
- Males lacking vestigial oviducts
- Tympana slightly larger than the eye in males and smaller in females
- Yellow to orange groin coloration
- Thighs usually spotted rather than barred dorsally

No calls have been recorded. Wright and Wright described the call as shorter and less loud than that of *R. pipiens*. The author notes that *R. onca* is the most geographically restricted leopard frog in North America, which makes its systematic position of interest.

**Life history.** Virtually nothing is known. Specimens were collected repeatedly from the early 1900s to about 1940 without natural history study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate.** The region has a southern desert climate with mild winters and hot summers. Daytime temperatures often exceed 44°C, nights are cool, and total annual precipitation is often less than 15 cm. Hot, dry winds persist from late morning to near midnight. Under these conditions, leopard frogs are restricted largely to springs and some stream pools with adequate vegetation to buffer sun and dry heat.

**Habitat.** All known collection sites lie in a narrow, low-elevation corridor of the [[Mojave Desert]] along the [[Virgin River]], extending about 85 miles from Las Vegas to just north of St. George. Suitable permanent sites were mainly cold springs and stream pools at least 12 to 16 inches deep, which allow escape from predators. Backwaters were probably needed for breeding, although egg masses have never been documented.

**Threats.** The report identifies several pressures:
- **Habitat loss:** Las Vegas springs were capped for municipal water, and the original meadows no longer exist.
- **Groundwater alteration:** Human alteration of groundwater flow is implicated at Las Vegas and [[Berry Springs]].
- **Bullfrogs:** [[American Bullfrog]] introductions are a predation and competition hazard across the region.
- **Flooding:** Scouring floods remove frogs from streams. Following a 1971 flash flood on East Clear Creek, Arizona, no frogs were seen for 18 months.
- **Collecting:** Heavy, repeated collecting over 35 to 40 years at known sites.
- **Marginal range:** The St. George area sits at the periphery of several leopard frog species' ranges, making local populations vulnerable to extinction.

## Key Findings

**1. Taxonomic status.** The author concludes that *R. onca* is a valid species. Type specimens of *onca* and *fisheri* show agreement in gross morphology, especially short dorsolateral folds. Because *onca* was described first, it takes name priority. [[Mark Jennings]] independently reached the same conclusion.

**2. Multivariate analysis.** A [[Stepwise Discriminant Analysis]] (BMDP7M) used eight measured features on 101 individuals, including type specimens, Arizona and Utah *R. pipiens* populations, and taxa suggested as synonyms. Key results (mean separations from *R. onca*):
- *R. chiricahuensis*: about 5 standard deviations
- Arizona lowland form: beyond 2 standard deviations
- *R. pretiosa* (three adults): about 3 standard deviations
- Duckwater frog population: about 4 standard deviations
- *R. pipiens* populations: 2.5 to 5 standard deviations

The Duckwater population was later confirmed by [[Fenton Kay]] as a peripheral population of *R. aurora draytoni*, probably recently introduced.

**3. Misidentified illustrations.** Published photographs of *onca* in [[Wright and Wright]] (1949) and [[Dickerson]] (1906) actually show the Arizona lowland form, which likely explains recent confusion. The Wrights' own text hints at two taxa in their Nevada material.

**4. Berry Springs (St. George area).** Among 14 adult leopard frogs in the [[Brigham Young University]] collection from Berry Springs (1950, collected by [[Vasco Tanner]]), six were *R. onca*, one was *R. pipiens*, and seven were intermediate hybrids. This indicates sympatry with *R. pipiens*, which rules out the idea that *onca* represents aberrant *pipiens* populations. One St. George specimen also plotted with *onca*.

**5. Field surveys (1983 and 1984).** No living *R. onca* were found.
- **Las Vegas:** Springs capped; the old stream is now a sewage-effluent wash. The University of Nevada at Las Vegas collection held no matching specimens.
- **[[Rogers Springs]] and [[Blue Point Springs]]:** Moderate-sized springs draining to [[Lake Mead]]. Frogs were absent, and the Rogers site has been developed for recreation.
- **[[Ivins Reservoir]]:** Heavily used and polluted; judged unsuitable.
- **[[Quail Creek]]:** About 5 miles surveyed by day and night; no leopard frogs found.
- **[[Ash Creek]]:** About 1.7 miles surveyed; permanent water but narrow vegetation zone; no leopard frogs found.
- **Berry Springs:** Spring diverted in 1973 to a ranch pond stocked with catfish and goldfish. Only *Bufo woodhousii* were found.

**6. Comparison site.** At [[Littlefield Arizona]], 80 yards of Dry Beaver Wash held six adult lowland leopard frogs and over 30 juveniles. Vegetation bands of 25 to 35 yards on each side of the stream were a key contrast with the St. George and Nevada sites.

**7. Habitat hypothesis.** Plant zone width appears central to long-term persistence. Dense vegetation lowers temperature maxima, raises humidity, breaks hot winds, and supports insect prey. Springs above stream channels escape flood risk, which makes Berry Springs unusually suitable.

**8. Conclusions.** The author concludes that *R. onca* existed in a narrow corridor along the Virgin River below 3000 ft, mostly in springs, and is now most likely extinct. Local extinction at Las Vegas is attributed to habitat loss and repeated collecting. The author also reports that no suitable habitat was identified for transplanting had live animals been found.

**9. Recommendations.** The author states that the extinction question is "problematic," but the weight of evidence supports extinction. The report also notes that the search effort focused on sites that, in retrospect, could not meet the needs of frog populations.

**10. Project outcome.** The author plans to submit a manuscript version to a herpetology journal and to keep in touch with local contacts.
