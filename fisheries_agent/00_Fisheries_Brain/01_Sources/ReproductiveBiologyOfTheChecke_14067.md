# Reproductive Biology of the Checkered Madtom (*Noturus flavater*) with Observations on Nesting in the Ozark and Slender Madtoms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the reproductive biology of the [[Checkered Madtom]] (*[[Noturus Flavater]]*), one of the largest and least known catfishes of the genus *Noturus*. Data came from nest observations in the [[North Fork White River]] (Missouri) during July 1981 and 1982, plus preserved museum specimens. The authors report size and age at maturity, fecundity, nest sites, nest guarding, embryonic development and larval growth. They also summarize nesting of the syntopic [[Ozark Madtom]] (*[[Noturus Albater]]*) and [[Slender Madtom]] (*[[Noturus Exilis]]*).

## Background & Description

*Noturus flavater* was described in 1969 from fewer than 70 specimens, and most collections contain five or fewer individuals. It is endemic to the [[White River System]] in the southern Ozarks of Arkansas and Missouri. Adults occur mainly in clear, deep pools, alongside *N. exilis* and *N. albater*.

**Size.** The species reaches at least 142 mm standard length (SL) and is the second largest *Noturus* known. Taylor (1969) reported a largest specimen of 114 mm SL, and Pflieger (1975) gave a maximum of 180 mm total length (TL), or about 150 mm SL. Among the 25 described *Noturus* species, maximum adult size ranges from 36 mm SL (*N. stanauli*) to at least 240 mm SL (*N. flavus*).

**Sexual dimorphism.** Body colour, pattern and fin lengths show no discernible dimorphism. During breeding, males have an elongate, swollen genital papilla and enlarged cephalic epaxial muscles with swollen lips, giving a flattened head. Females have a shorter papilla, a heavier body from eggs, and more pigment around the papilla. Males are apparently longer than females; among 3-year classes, females are 6–25 mm smaller.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The North Fork White River at the study site is a typical high-gradient Ozark stream with fast riffles, moderately fast raceways and sluggish pools up to 2 m deep (non-flood stage). The channel is about 15 m wide, with gravel beds (chert, dolomite and limestone) and large boulders and flat rocks in pools.

**Thermal conditions.** Nests were found at 27 °C on 11 July 1981. A *N. albater* collection from the same river was taken at 19.5 °C, and a *N. exilis* nest in the [[Meramec River]] was found at 29 °C.

**Reproductive guild.** Following [[Balon (1975)]], embryos of *N. flavater* belong to the speleophil reproductive guild, which typically has good oxygen conditions during incubation. Embryonic respiratory organs are moderately developed, and the yolk supply is well developed.

**Regional comparison.** Clutch sizes of *N. exilis* in Missouri are larger than in southern Illinois, which the authors suggest may reflect racial differences linked to vertebral counts and hatchling length.

## Key Findings

**Maturity and age**
- Some females matured at 1 year and 57 mm SL. Most mature females were 2–3 years old and 71–112 mm SL (x̄ = 92.8 mm, N = 10).
- Nest-guarding males were 3–5 years old and 115–142 mm SL (x̄ = 130 mm, four males).

**Fecundity**
- Mature ova per female ranged from 93 to 340 (x̄ = 240, N = 7).
- Mature ova measured 1.3–3.2 mm (x̄ = 2.0 mm, N = 90), orange or yellow. Maturing ova measured 0.5–1.0 mm (x̄ = 0.7 mm, N = 15), white.
- Ova number correlated with female length (r = 0.826) and adjusted body weight (r = 0.891), but not with age.
- Gonadosomatic index (GSI) ranged from 49 to 174 (x̄ = 106.9) in seven mature females. The largest ovaries equalled 17.4% of adjusted body weight, in an 85 mm, 2-year-old female collected 14 April 1972.
- In three of four large females, the left ovary held significantly more ova (P < .025). The authors conclude that doubling counts from one ovary is not an acceptable fecundity estimate for *Noturus*.
- Three large females had not spawned by early or mid-July.

**Nesting and parental care**
- Four nests were found on 11 July 1981, and two large females were found under potential nest rocks on 1–2 July 1982.
- Three nests were in pools at 99.1–119.0 cm depth (x̄ = 104.9 cm), and one was in a raceway.
- Covering rocks measured 35.6 × 30.5 to 61.0 × 53.3 cm, were thick and flat beneath, and sat flush with the substratum over silt-free gravel and sand.
- Males guarded nests, facing the opening to block intruders. Females were not found with males, and males guarded embryos or larvae for at least 10–12 days after hatching.

**Embryos and larvae**
- The one complete clutch contained 304 embryos (per the abstract). Three incomplete broods each held an estimated 200–300 larvae.
- Embryos (about 105–115 h old) had chorion diameters of 4.6–5.0 mm (x̄ = 4.8 mm) and yolk diameters of 3.4–3.7 mm (x̄ = 3.6 mm, N = 10).
- Mesolarvae (2–3 days post-hatching) measured 9.3–10.3 mm TL (x̄ = 9.7 mm, N = 10).
- Metalarvae (10–12 days post-hatching) measured 12.7–14.0 mm TL (x̄ = 13.3 mm). The adult pigment pattern did not appear until at least 33 mm SL.
- In contrast, the close relative [[Noturus Miurus]] developed a near-adult pattern by 12 days (14.0 mm TL).

**Syntopic species**
- *N. albater*: seven nests under small flat rocks (31–76 cm deep), split evenly between pools and raceways. Clutches of 60–85 embryos (x̄ = 70.6, N = 3). Nest-guarding males were 75–88 mm SL (x̄ = 80.3 mm) and 2 years old. Clutches were 20–55 embryos larger than those reported earlier by Mayden et al. (1980).
- *N. exilis*: nest-guarding males were about 100 mm SL, in water 91–104 cm deep. Five clutches held 26, 52, 64, 95 and 124 embryos. Yolk diameter averaged 3.5 mm. Southern Illinois clutches averaged 51 embryos (largest 74) across 16 nests.

**Interpretation**
- The authors propose that large males are selected because their size and experience improve nest protection and reduce nest abandonment, though nest quality by male size is untested.
- Comparative female GSI (May–July) was 79 in [[Noturus Nocturnus]], 145 in *N. miurus* and 211 in *N. exilis*.
