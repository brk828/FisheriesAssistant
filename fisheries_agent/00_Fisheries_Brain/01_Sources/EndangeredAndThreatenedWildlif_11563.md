# Endangered and threatened wildlife and plants; Proposed reclassification of the Gila trout (Salmo gilae) from endangered to threatened

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary
This 1987 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Federal Register notice proposes reclassifying the [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*) from endangered to threatened status under the Endangered Species Act. Originally listed as endangered in 1967 due to severe range contraction (reduced to five streams) and threats from non-native salmonids, the species has since been the subject of extensive recovery efforts including chemical removal of exotic fish, construction of stream barriers, and population replication via translocation. By 1987, twelve secure populations existed (five indigenous, seven reintroduced), fulfilling recovery-plan criteria for downlisting. The proposal also included a special rule (50 CFR 17.44(u)) permitting regulated sport fishing for the species under New Mexico Department of Game and Fish oversight.

## Background & Description
- Species-level taxonomic identity: *Salmo gilae* (Miller), a distinct salmonid species endemic to the Gila River drainage system.
- The source document does not provide detailed morphometric or meristic data (e.g., fin ray counts, coloration, scale counts); it instead emphasizes **population-level "morphotypes"**, i.e., distinguishable genetic/phenotypic lineages associated with specific ancestral streams:
  - **Main Diamond Creek morphotype** — replicated into Sheep Corral, McKnight, and Gap Creeks.
  - **South Diamond Creek morphotype** — replicated into Trail Canyon Creek.
  - **Spruce Creek morphotype** — replicated into Big Dry Creek.
  - **McKenna Creek morphotype** — replicated into Little Creek.
  - **Iron Creek morphotype** — expanded in place via downstream renovation and stocking within Iron Creek itself.
- These morphotypes suggest population-specific genetic differentiation among the five indigenous ancestral stocks, likely resulting from long-term geographic and reproductive isolation in separate headwater streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Relatively undisturbed, high-altitude mountain headwater streams within the [[Gila National Forest]] (New Mexico) and [[Prescott National Forest]] (Arizona), largely within federally protected Wilderness Areas (Gila Wilderness Area, Aldo Leopold Wilderness Area, Cedar Bench Wilderness Area).
- **Historic range**: Verde and Agua Fria drainages (Arizona), upper Gila drainage (New Mexico), with possible historic presence in Eagle Creek, Arizona, and tributaries of the San Francisco River, New Mexico.
- **Community interactions**:
  - Competes with and is subject to predation and hybridization from introduced non-native salmonids: [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*), and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*).
  - Brown trout are identified as a particularly serious predator of juvenile Gila trout below stream barriers.
  - Notably lacks disease and parasite burdens common to other trout species, attributed to extreme habitat isolation and pristine water conditions.
- **Physical barriers**: Constructed by the U.S. Forest Service to prevent upstream reinvasion by non-native trout, effectively isolating restored populations.
- **Vulnerability to natural disturbance**: Populations are susceptible to flash floods (which can displace >50% of a population downstream into non-native-trout-dominated waters), drought (reducing habitat and increasing predation risk from birds and raccoons), and forest fires (causing siltation and vegetative cover loss).

## Key Findings
- **Population recovery trajectory**: Increased from 5 indigenous populations at time of 1967 listing to 12 total secure populations by 1987 (5 indigenous + 7 reintroduced/replicated).
- **Reclassification rationale**: Recovery Plan (USFWS 1984) criteria for downlisting were met — survival of all five ancestral populations secured, and all morphotypes successfully replicated.
- **Replication/translocation methodology**: Adults from reproducing indigenous populations were transplanted into chemically renovated (exotic-species-free) streams within historic range, protected by physical barriers.
- **Specific replication pairings**:
  - Main Diamond Creek → Sheep Corral, McKnight, Gap Creeks
  - South Diamond Creek → Trail Canyon Creek
  - Spruce Creek → Big Dry Creek
  - McKenna Creek → Little Creek
  - Iron Creek → expanded in situ
- **Primary historic threats**: Hybridization, competition, and predation from non-native salmonids (rainbow, cutthroat, brown trout), compounded by habitat degradation from timber harvesting, mining, and watershed disturbance.
- **Current protections**: 10 of 11 occupied streams lie within federally designated Wilderness Areas; all Gila trout streams closed to sport fishing (with minor illegal take by uninformed backpackers); New Mexico and Arizona both provide state-level protective designations.
- **No critical habitat designated**: Because habitat lies within already-protected Wilderness Areas, the Service determined no additional conservation benefit would result from formal critical habitat designation.
- **Proposed special rule (§17.44(u))**: Would allow the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish to authorize regulated sport fishing for Gila trout — intended to build public support for conservation and reduce population pressure in streams nearing carrying capacity, while maintaining take prohibitions outside authorized instances (educational, scientific, propagation, permitted seasons).
- **Remaining threats justifying continued (threatened, not delisted) protection**: Non-native salmonids still dominate the majority of historic Gila trout habitat below stream barriers; natural catastrophic events (fire, flood, drought) remain unavoidable risks compounded by the species' historically reduced genetic diversity.
- **Key references**: Turner, P.R. (1986) *Restoration of the Endangered Gila Trout*; USFWS (1984) *Gila Trout Recovery Plan*.
