# Flathead Catfish Age Determination and Growth in Lake Carl Blackwell, Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This report, a fisheries study of the [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) in [[Lake Carl Blackwell]], Oklahoma, evaluates methods for estimating age and back-calculating growth from spine cross-sections. The authors found that the conventional [[Pectoral Spine Basal Recess Sections]] method underestimates age in larger fish because lumen enlargement destroys the innermost annuli. The authors recommend [[Pectoral Spine Articulating Process Sections]] instead. The study also describes the species' ecological role, harvest value and the reservoir's environmental conditions, and it summarizes growth data for fish in the reservoir and in [[Boomer Lake]].

## Background & Description

The flathead catfish is native to large rivers and reservoirs of the Mississippi River and Gulf coastal drainages. It attains a large size and has piscivorous habits, so it has been widely introduced outside its native range. In Oklahoma reservoirs, adults consumed mainly non-game prey fishes larger than those eaten by other predators. Commercially, flathead catfish ranked first in total value among commercial species harvested in Oklahoma from 1961 to 1969, although it was second to buffalo fishes in total weight. It is also a valued game species, with trophy appeal to anglers; fish up to 48 lb have been caught in Oklahoma.

Earlier work on the species at [[Lake Carl Blackwell]], conducted by the [[Oklahoma Cooperative Fishery Unit]] from 1967 to 1972, examined food habits, condition, reproduction, horizontal distribution, population size, mortality and movement using mark-and-recapture and [[Telemetry]] methods. The report's stated objectives were to (1) describe methods for age determination and back-calculation of growth, (2) relate growth patterns to food habits and reproduction, and (3) determine the effects of lake-level fluctuation and population reduction on growth and fecundity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Carl Blackwell is an impoundment created in 1936-1938 by the Works Progress Administration on Stillwater Creek, in Payne County, Oklahoma. It has a surface area of 14.0 km², a volume of 67.84 million m³, a shoreline length of 90.4 km and a shoreline development index of 6.8 at spillway elevation. During the study, surface area and volume averaged only 48.0% and 36.1% of these spillway values. Its watershed lies in the Redbeds Plains physiographic region, with soils derived mainly from Permian clays and shales. The reservoir is a municipal water source for Oklahoma State University and the City of Stillwater.

The lake was highly turbid. Secchi transparencies after heavy runoff were 5-10 cm, and typical values were 25-50 cm. Jackson Turbidity Units ranged from 20 to 180. The water column was generally well mixed because of wave action, which prevented thermal stratification and oxygen depletion except during brief summer periods. Oxygen depletion was mainly restricted to deeper water (>5 m). Lake-level declines from January to March 1968 exposed mud flats and reduced surface area and shoreline length by 16% and 64%. Aquatic plant growth was precluded by turbidity, wave action and fluctuating levels, so littoral cover for flathead catfish was limited to a few submerged trees and boulders. The littoral substrate is mainly sand and coarse silt.

Other concurrent studies on the lake covered [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], phytoplankton, sediment cycling and primary productivity.

## Key Findings

**Age determination method**
- Pectoral spine [[Basal Recess Sections]] (BR sections) suffer from lumen enlargement, which resorbs the earliest annuli. In flathead catfish, the first annulus is typically missing by age 3-5 (61-98% of fish in age-groups 3-6 in the Lake Carl Blackwell sample), and two or more annuli are missing in most older fish.
- Pectoral [[Articulating Process Sections]] (AP sections) gave reliable ages. Fish of known age from Boomer Lake (stocked as fry in 1967) had the expected number of annuli. All four age-5 fish from 1972 showed five annuli. Only 1 of 10 age-4 fish from 1971 had an annulus missing (the first, due to sectioning error).
- Dorsal AP sections were less useful. The first annulus was obscured in 41% of fish and the second in 9%, and the outermost annuli were often too closely spaced in large, slow-growing fish.
- Annuli formed once per year, confirmed by tagged fish. Six fish tagged in 1970 and recaptured in 1971 had formed one new annulus each.

**Growth**
- Calculated growth matched empirical lengths of age-1 fish. Mean calculated length at age 1 was 88 mm in 1967 and 116 mm in 1968, against empirical means of 81 mm and 116 mm (as reported).
- Growth rates of tagged fish in 1968 were 3.6 mm/month (males) and 2.6 mm/month (females). Growth from fish tagged in 1968 and recaptured in 1969 was 3.6 mm/month (males) and 2.4 mm/month (females), equal to 41 and 29 mm/year.
- Growth in the 1962 and 1968 year classes differed. The 1962 year class appeared faster-growing, probably because smaller fish were not caught by the 25-mm gill-net mesh.
- Boomer Lake fish grew substantially faster than fish from Lake Carl Blackwell at ages 1-4. The authors attribute this to abundant forage (white crappie and sunfishes) and less intraspecific competition in the newer population.
- Growth at Lake Carl Blackwell was slower at ages 1-4 than at other waters where age data appeared accurate, but similar to that in the Missouri River at age 5 and older.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The authors concluded that pectoral AP sections should be used for age determination and back-calculation. Using only BR sections, as in prior studies, probably underestimated age and overestimated growth rates in many reports.
- A series of carefully cut AP sections from both pectoral spines should provide at least one section with the correct number of annuli.
- Dorsal AP and BR sections remain useful for distinguishing false annuli, but they may underestimate the age of older fish.
