# Biology and Immature Stages of Malacophagous Diptera of the Genus Sepedon (Sciomyzidae)

#entomology #marine-biology #sciomyzidae #sepedon #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1966 Virginia Agricultural Experiment Station bulletin by S. E. Neff (Virginia Polytechnic Institute) and C. O. Berg (Cornell University) presents biological and morphological data on 16 species of [[Sepedon]] flies (Diptera: [[Sciomyzidae]]): 10 Nearctic, 4 Neotropical, and 2 Palearctic. The larvae of these flies kill and consume freshwater pulmonate snails. Data derive from laboratory rearings through complete life cycles, supplemented by field observations. The work describes eggs, three larval instars, and puparia, provides keys to identify them, and summarizes the potential of these predators for [[Biological Control]] of snail hosts of schistosomiasis and fascioliasis.

The document is a scientific bulletin rather than an ichthyological study. It contains no fish data; the taxon is an aquatic-margin insect group.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Sepedon Latreille (1804) is the largest genus in the subfamily Tetanocerinae, with about 90 nominal species, and is the best-known sciomyzid genus worldwide. It has a wider distribution than any other tetanocerine genus.
- **Adults:** Large, conspicuous "marsh flies" with long, porrect antennae and grasshopper-like hind legs. Adults remain near larval breeding sites and rest with the head lifted and abdomen sloping toward the leaf surface.
- **Larvae:** Aquatic, predatory, with a tapered retractable anterior end, warty tubercles, and a posterior spiracular disc surrounded by fleshy lobes. Float hairs (palmately branched interspiracular processes) and a swallowed air bubble in the midgut provide buoyancy. The first instar is metapneustic; second and third instars are amphipneustic.
- **Diagnostic characters:** The cephalopharyngeal skeleton, notably a ventral arch with a serrate anterior margin (family) and a broad median notch in its posterior border (Sepedon), is the most reliable diagnostic feature for larvae and puparia.
- **Puparia:** Formed in water and adapted for floating in most aquatic species; in *S. tenuicornis* and *S. spinipes* they are attached above water on emergent vegetation, and in *S. borealis* they are often buried.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Adults occur in open, unshaded wet areas such as cattail (Typha) marshes, grass-sedge meadows, and pond and lake margins. *S. tenuicornis* is largely restricted to shaded swampy sections, avoiding competition with *S. fuscipennis* in open marshes.
- **Food:** Larvae attacked and consumed living or freshly killed pulmonate snails from all common families (e.g., [[Helisoma Trivolvis]], [[Physa]], [[Australorbis Glabratus]], [[Lymnaea]]) with little selectivity. Larvae did not feed on duckweed, naiad, moss, ostracods, rotifers, oligochaetes, or artificial media. A larva commonly destroyed more than a dozen snails. Killing was quick: a *Physa* sp. 4 mm long usually died in under 20 minutes.
- **Adult diet:** Nectar sources (e.g., apple blossom, marsh marigold), honey and yeast mixtures, and scavenging. Egg production increased markedly when crushed snails were added, suggesting that adults depend partly on protein from snails killed by larvae.
- **Seasonality:** Adults are active in all but near-freezing weather. In cold regions, adults overwinter and reproduction resumes in spring; in tropical areas breeding is continuous. In the Ithaca, New York region, *S. armipes* and *S. fuscipennis* were recorded from March to November, with *S. armipes* records as early as March 10.
- **Natural enemies:** Pathogenic bacteria and a virus that causes histolysis of larvae; parasitoid Hymenoptera (Trichogrammatidae on eggs; Ichneumonidae, Diapriidae, Braconidae, and Pteromalidae on larvae and puparia); predators including blackbirds, nighthawks, dragonfly and damselfly naiads, *Mesovelia*, and the ant *Pheidole megacephala*.
- **Geography:** [[Sepedon Macropus]] was introduced to Hawaii and Guam for liver fluke snail control and became established there. [[Sepedon Praemiosa]] was sent to Australia, with no proof of establishment.

## Key Findings

1. **Snail-killing behaviour was confirmed.** The 1953 work by Berg provided the first conclusive evidence that sciomyzid larvae kill and consume gastropods. Earlier literature had described these larvae as saprophagous, phytophagous, or catholic in diet. Gercke's 1876 conclusion that *S. sphegea* larvae feed on aquatic plants was shown to be erroneous; larvae in a Lemna-only jar died within five days.
2. **Snail-killing is widespread.** All 143 species investigated (26 genera) in the family kill and feed on gastropod mollusks. The authors suggest that this shared larval food habit supports a monophyletic origin of the family.
3. **Tetanocerine larvae are not a family-wide type.** The aquatic larval form described in older literature applies only to the aquatic Tetanocerinae, not to all sciomyzids.
4. **Diagnostic keys were produced.** Keys for eggs, mature larvae, and puparia distinguish the 16 species. Egg size ranges from 0.8–0.9 mm (*S. pusilla* group) to 1.35–1.45 mm (*S. caerulea*). Third-instar larval length ranges from about 5.5 to 13 mm across species.
5. **Biological-control potential.** In preliminary laboratory exposures, planorbid hosts of *Schistosoma mansoni* were vulnerable, *Bulinus* species hosting *S. haematobium* were intermediate, and operculate hosts of *S. japonicum* were invulnerable. Introductions to Hawaii established *S. macropus* on the four major islands and Guam, with subjective evidence of reduced snail populations.
6. **Mass rearing was improved.** Large redwood tanks raised average monthly output from about 400 to about 4,500 adult flies. Mass-rearing innovations included adding crushed snails to adult diets, using aureomycin to reduce larval mortality, and using McPhail traps baited with crushed snails. Trapping rates were 100–400 flies per hour in Hawaii but disappointing in the Americas and Europe.
7. **Fecundity and development.** Females lay eggs in groups of about 10–30 (sometimes up to 45 in the lab). Egg hatching took about 3–5 days. Larval development typically took about 11–12 days in Hawaii and 14–22 days depending on the species; *S. macropus* completed its life cycle in 22–24 days in Hawaii. Preoviposition periods ranged from 4 to 24 days for *S. caerulea* (mean 9.6 days).
8. **Diapause and generations.** Most species breed continuously with overlapping generations. The *S. pusilla* group (*S. borealis*, *S. neili*, *S. pusilla*) probably has two generations per year, with the late-summer generation overwintering as adults.
9. **Taxonomic relationships.** The authors argue that larval characters do not support separating *Sepedon* into a distinct subfamily (Sepedoninae). They also suggest that Sepedon larvae are typical of aquatic Tetanocerinae.
10. **Methodological recommendation.** The authors advocate a group-of-organisms approach. Studying one group intensively across its life cycle, rather than surveying a single habitat, is argued to yield better insight into the conditions of life that affect population growth.
