# Management Plan for the Big-River Fishes of the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the [[Lower Colorado River Management Plan]] (LCRMP) for the "big-river fishes" of the lower Colorado River basin, prepared by the Recovery Implementation Planning/Scientific Workgroup for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 2). It supplements existing recovery plans for the federally endangered [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]], and addresses [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] through provisional recovery goals. The plan covers the mainstem from Lees Ferry to the International Boundary with Mexico, divided into six subunits: Grand Canyon, Lake Mead, Lake Mohave, Lake Havasu, Parker-Imperial, and Gila River basin.

The plan aims to maintain genetic refugia for Razorback Sucker and Bonytail, establish self-sustaining populations of these species, maintain the current Humpback Chub population size without decline, and maintain an experimental, nonessential population of Colorado Pikeminnow. It relies on three strategies: augmentation through hatchery and wild-collected stock, isolated habitats, and exploitation of habitats created by reservoir drawdown or drying.

## Background & Description

The "big-river fishes" are characterized by endemism and specialized adaptations to a harsh, variable environment. Their populations are now small and isolated. The lower basin drains a watershed of about 650,000 km² and is among the most heavily used river drainages in the USA and perhaps the world. Historically, the river had canyon-bound reaches with rapids and alluvial reaches with braided channels, backwaters, and marshes, and flows were highly variable, with spring snowmelt peaks.

Beginning with Laguna Dam in 1905 and later [[Hoover Dam]], [[Davis Dam]], and [[Parker Dam]] in the 1930s-1960s, impoundments and diversions converted the river to managed flows, altered thermal regimes, blocked migration routes, halted sediment transport, and isolated floodplain habitats. Two inter-related factors are primarily responsible for native fish loss: habitat alteration and the introduction of nonnative fishes, which are mostly ecological generalists and competitors or predators of native eggs and young.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Current status by species:**
- **Bonytail:** Wild populations are virtually extinct in the basin. Small populations persist in Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu, and the species has recruited at High Levee Ponds on Cibola NWR. Stocking is ongoing.
- **Colorado Pikeminnow:** Wild populations are extirpated from the lower basin. Experimental nonessential populations have been reintroduced in the Salt and Verde rivers.
- **Humpback Chub:** Occurs in Grand Canyon, with the largest concentration in the lower 14.2 km of the [[Little Colorado River]]. Spring 2002 estimates were 2,666 fish >15 cm (2,002 >20 cm), a marked decline from 4,500-5,500 fish >15 cm estimated in 1992.
- **Razorback Sucker:** Small populations persist in Grand Canyon and Lake Mead. Lake Mohave holds the largest population, with about 4,000 wild individuals in 2003 according to the text, and about 70,000 subadults stocked since 1992.

**Management context:** Multiple agencies share responsibility, including the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[National Park Service]], the [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], and the [[Adaptive Management Work Group]], which oversees Grand Canyon research under the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] of 1992. Critical habitat for several species is designated in multiple reaches (USFWS 1994a).

**Key concepts:** [[Genetic Refugia]], [[Minimum Viable Population]], [[Self-Sustaining Population]], [[Augmentation Strategy]], [[Isolated Habitats]], [[Hatchery Stocking Survival]], [[Soft Release]].

## Key Findings

- **Strategy framework:** Three strategies are proposed: (1) augmentation through hatchery or wild-collected stock, (2) isolated habitats (long-term and production), and (3) unique opportunities from reservoir refilling after drawdown.
- **Stocking survival:** Past lower-basin stockings indicate less than 10% survival and recruitment into adult stocks under most circumstances (Marsh 1999). Total stocking needed is unknown but expected to be considerably higher than final population targets.
- **Genetic management:** Four options are identified to maximize contributing individuals. Collection of wild early life stages is preferred because it preserves the most genetic variability. Hatchery broodstock should include the maximum number of wild fish, with pedigree management to limit inbreeding.
- **Conditioning before release:** Fish raised in lentic habitat and exposed to current before release are better conditioned to flowing water. Razorback suckers under 30 cm show little return to the stocking site, and fish held several days before release show less erratic behavior than hard-released fish.
- **Recovery criteria:** Downlisting and delisting criteria specify minimum viable populations (MVP) for each species, such as 2,100 adults for the Grand Canyon Humpback Chub core, 4,400 adults for Bonytail, 5,800 adults for Razorback Sucker, and 2,600 adults for Colorado Pikeminnow.
- **Lake Mohave bonytail:** About 200,205 bonytail have been stocked since 1981, with 85% under 25 cm. Only 113 fish were collected between 1980 and 2003, 14 of them considered wild.
- **Lake Havasu bonytail:** About 15,000 bonytail over 25 cm have been stocked under a 1993 biological opinion, with only 9 individuals recovered to date.
- **Verde River reintroductions:** Since 1995, 11,216 Colorado Pikeminnow over 30 cm have been stocked, with 84 recaptured. Since 1994, 21,414 Razorback Suckers have been released, with 137 recaptured.
- **Future needs:** Priorities include securing long-term funding, expanding hatchery rearing space for fish over 30 cm (an estimated $1.5 million for facilities and $200,000 annually for operations), creating a Lower Colorado Basin Management Group with a full-time coordinator, outreach to stakeholders, annual working sessions, and research on wetlands as nursery habitat.
