# Experimental Largemouth Bass Management in Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[George W. Bennett]] of the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] examines [[Largemouth Bass]] management at Ridge Lake, an 18-acre artificial impoundment in [[Fox Ridge State Park]], over the 1941–1949 period. Using biennial draining censuses, creel records and annual spawning counts, the author tests whether population manipulation can sustain bass fisheries in reservoirs that typically decline after their first years. The central conclusions are that bass reproduction depends strongly on the abundance of small competing and predatory fishes, that the lake's bass population stayed high despite removals, and that conventional regulations (length limits, closed seasons, bag limits) showed little measurable benefit. The author argues that a drain outlet is the most valuable management tool for artificial lakes.

## Background & Description

Bennett describes a predictable four-stage sequence in new Illinois water-supply reservoirs (Bennett, 1946). Bass, crappies, [[Bluegill]] and [[Bullhead]] catfish are first stocked as adults. Bass spawn greatly exceeds that of other species in the first year, producing excellent fishing in years two to four. From about year five, bass fishing declines, and by years seven or eight the lake is dominated by small, stunted panfish and muddy water caused by rough fish.

Ridge Lake was constructed in 1941 for recreation and fisheries research in a steep-sided valley containing a tributary of the [[Embarras River]]. The dam has a 4-foot gate valve for draining. The watershed is dominated by Ward silt loam soils (95.8 percent of the area). Local agricultural data indicated corn yields of no more than 25 bushels per acre, compared with about 75 bushels on black soils farther north, so the lake was expected to be less productive than northern Illinois impoundments.

Stocking history:
- May 1941: 100 sexually mature bass; 335 yearling bass added after spawning in early June. No other bass were stocked.
- Late June 1944: 129 adult bluegills.

Public fishing began in 1942 from boats, with anglers checked in and out at the laboratory pier. This allowed complete creel information. Draining censuses were conducted in 1943 and then biennially (1945, 1947, 1949). Marked bass of 9 inches or more and marked bluegills of 6 inches or more were returned. Other species that entered via the feeder stream or over the spillway were eliminated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Ridge Lake study frames bass population dynamics as a function of competition and predation among fish cohorts. Bennett attributes the first-year bass spawn in new reservoirs and the later decline to intra- and inter-specific competition among offspring of stocked and immigrating fishes. Immigrating species included [[Black Bullhead]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Common Carp]] and [[Black Buffalo]]. The bullheads and green sunfish multiplied in the lake between censuses. Carp and buffalo were believed to have entered over the spillway during high water.

Bass fry and eggs were judged highly vulnerable to predation by small fishes in the egg, yolk-sac and free-swimming stages. Under abundant small fish, a whole year's reproduction could be nearly eliminated. Physical conditions at spawning were considered less important.

Bass diet is described as omnivorous. Bass preferred [[Crayfish]] and their own young over companion stocked species. The author therefore rejects the idea of a fixed predator-prey weight relationship between bass and other fishes, citing [[Johnson]] (1949) and disputing [[Swingle]] (1950).

Fishing effort was recorded as 105 man-hours per acre in 1945 and 155 man-hours per acre in 1947.

## Key Findings

**Harvest and population totals (1941–1949)**
- Anglers took 3,228 bass weighing 2,384.7 lb and 8,169 bluegills weighing 1,168.4 lb.
- Draining censuses removed 6,406 small bass (862 lb) and 94,582 small bluegills (4,859.3 lb).
- Totals across all sources: 9,634 bass weighing 3,246.6 lb, and 102,751 bluegills weighing 6,027.7 lb.
- The angler yield of bass ranged from 10.9 to 30 lb per acre per year, except in 1941 and 1943, when the lake was closed to public fishing.
- The bluegill angler yield ranged from 6.2 to 45.9 lb per acre during 1946–1948.

**Carrying capacity**
- Four censuses suggested a maximum bass carrying capacity of about 50 lb per acre.
- When bluegills expanded to 193 lb per acre, bass were depressed to 31.5 lb per acre.
- The 1947 census suggested bluegill carrying capacity was "something over 190 lb per acre."
- Bass rose above 50 lb per acre by 1949, and the author notes that the 1948 and 1949 angler yields were the highest recorded.

**Spawning and reproduction (Table 3)**
- In years when small fish were culled before spawning, the estimated number of young bass reaching the schooling stage averaged 54,200 (total 271,000 across five years).
- In years when small fish were present, the average was 5,900 (total 29,500).
- No schools of young bass were found in 1948 or 1950. A fine-mesh minnow seine in 1948 caught no small bass, and one small bass was taken in five hauls in 1950.
- Bennett acknowledges the counts are approximate but considers them valid for year-to-year comparison.

**Regulation assessment**
- Bass were protected during spawning, yet the number of spawning-size bass showed no correlation with the number of young produced.
- The author concludes that legal length limits, closed seasons and bag limits have done little or nothing to increase bass populations. He bases this on the maintenance of bass numbers despite removal of small fish and the spawning differential.
- Illegal bass were confiscated at the pier from 1945 onward. Bluegills had no length limit and a bag limit of 50.

**Management recommendations**
- A drain outlet is, in the author's view, the most valuable tool for managing fish in artificial lakes. Impoundments should be designed so that all water and fish can pass under the dam to a screened trap.
- Drains also reduce the importance of correct stocking, make it easier to exclude undesirable species, and prevent overpopulation and stunting without fertilization or poisoning.

**Comparative pond survey**
- Among 37 Illinois ponds and lakes, the five with no bass were at the top of the ascending list. The next five had bass below 5 lb per acre, and those showed no reproduction.
- The five highest-bass waters (50 lb per acre or more) were Fork Lake (122 lb per acre), Duck Valley Lake (76.5), Upper Twin Lakes (57.9), Onized Lake (50.8) and Elam Pond (49.9). Each was heavily cropped, artificially stocked and fed, or stocked with bass alone.

**Caveats**
- This is a single 18-acre, low-productivity lake with a limited sampling design. The author's broad conclusions about regulations and bass-population independence rest on his interpretation of these data and a comparative survey.
- Counting methods for spawn were admittedly approximate.
