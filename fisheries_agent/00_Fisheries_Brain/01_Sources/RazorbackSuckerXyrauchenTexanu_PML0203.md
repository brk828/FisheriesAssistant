# Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) Status Review and Management Recommendations for the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document comprises a cover-letter correspondence set, a reviewer-comment compendium, and a draft status review (dated 30 September 1989) prepared by [[Kevin R. Bestgen]] of the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]], Colorado State University, for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]]. The review summarizes the taxonomy, description, historic and present distribution, life history, reasons for decline, and current management of the [[Razorback Sucker]], an endemic species of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Present populations are much reduced and are largely composed of large, old adults. Non-native predation on larvae, with little recruitment to adult size, is identified as the most detrimental impact, compounded by habitat alteration and flow modification. Reviewers criticized the draft for length, excessive literature, and recommendations that appeared idealistic; the authors stated the document was intended as a biological summary rather than a complete recovery plan.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *Xyrauchen* is one of three monotypic genera in the family Catostomidae. The species was first described as *Catostomus texanus* in 1860 from a specimen collected in Arizona, and was later placed in *Xyrauchen*. The "Uncompahgre humpback sucker" was later considered a hybrid of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and razorback sucker.
- **Morphology:** Distinguished from other catostomids by an abruptly rising, bony dorsal keel behind the occiput (hence "humpback" or "razorback"). Body is elongate, robust and laterally compressed, with a short, deep caudal peduncle and a ventral mouth with a cleft lower lip. Counts reported: dorsal fin rays usually 14–15, anal rays 5 (as printed in source), vertebrae 45–47, lateral-line scales 69–82 (as printed), gill rakers 44–50.
- **Size:** Adults may reach 1 m TL and 5–6 kg, but are more commonly 400–700 mm TL and under 3 kg.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Breeding males are dark dorsally with bright yellow-orange lateral and ventral coloration, and show more pronounced breeding tubercles. Females are generally longer and heavier.
- **Feeding anatomy:** Pharyngeal teeth in a comb-like arrangement are adapted to feeding on benthic macroinvertebrates. The heavily ossified caudal skeleton is thought to be an adaptation to strong currents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and status.** Razorback sucker were historically widespread and common in warmwater reaches of the Colorado River Basin from Wyoming to Mexico, and were harvested as food by Aboriginal cultures and commercially in Arizona as late as 1949. Present populations are much reduced:

- **Lower Colorado River Basin (LCRB):** Regularly occurs in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona–Nevada), probably the largest remaining population; small numbers in [[Lake Havasu]], Senator Wash Reservoir, and below Hoover Dam.
- **Upper Colorado River Basin (UCRB):** Regularly found in the upper [[Green River]] (Utah), lower [[Yampa River]] (Colorado), and the Colorado River near Grand Junction; small populations in the Dirty Devil, San Juan and Colorado arms of [[Lake Powell]].

**Threats and drivers of decline:**
- Predation by non-native species on larvae, with negligible recruitment to adult size, is considered the most detrimental impact.
- Habitat alteration and flow modification (dams, diversions, channelization) compound predation effects by eliminating off-channel habitats and overbank flooding.
- More than 50 non-native fish species have been introduced into the Colorado River Basin, many of them piscivorous.
- Declines were noted as early as the 1930s in the LCRB and by the early 1940s in the Colorado River sub-basin.

**Habitat and life history:**
- Warm-water reaches; winter water temperatures in the lower Colorado River seldom fell below 10 °C, while summer temperatures often approached 32 °C. Optimal summer temperature was reported as 22–25 °C.
- Adults use slow backwaters and off-channel habitats, and lentic environments are important for pre-reproductive staging and spawning. Poor swimming performance in currents above 0.3 m/s was observed in laboratory trials.
- Spawning in reservoirs occurs over gravel/cobble shallows (~0.3–2 m); in the UCRB it occurs from mid-April to mid-June as water temperatures are 12–16 °C.
- Growth of wild adults in the UCRB is very slow (about 2.2 mm/year in one study).
- Hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] is widespread; in the San Juan River hybrids with bluehead sucker are also recorded.

## Key Findings

**Population status (from the draft review):**
- Lake Mohave population size is unknown; Bozek et al. (1984) reported a population at least as large as in 1994 (as printed).
- Upper Green River population estimate: 948 fish (95% confidence interval 758–1,138), based on 1980–1988 capture–recapture data (Lanigan and Tyus 1989).
- Only seven adult razorback suckers were collected in a 53-km reach of the Colorado River in Grand Valley from 1986 to 1988.
- Only eight juveniles (<100 mm TL) were recorded from the entire UCRB in 1952–1964 collections of 2,785 fish; these were the last verified juveniles documented from that basin.
- Only two specimens are known from the Gunnison River since 1970.

**Reproduction and recruitment:**
- Abundant larvae in Lake Mohave indicate successful reproduction, but intermediate-sized juveniles have been extremely rare since the early 1950s, suggesting negligible recruitment to older age classes.
- Hatching success was highest at 20 °C (35%) and 25 °C (29%) in one study; no hatching occurred at 30 °C.
- Eggs subjected to temperature drops of more than 3–5 °C below fertilization temperature hatched at much lower rates or not at all.
- Predation by common carp and channel catfish on eggs and larvae has been documented, though predation on eggs was not found to be substantial in one study.

**Management and recovery:**
- The reviewers urged immediate action to forestall extinction, and questioned whether recommendations were feasible.
- The authors identified successful research outcomes, including identification of early life stages, spawning-habitat description, efficient propagation techniques, and identification of non-native predation as the major factor suppressing recruitment.
- Key research priorities include: assessing genetic variation among wild populations; studying life history attributes; investigating habitat use and movement with telemetry; and addressing stocking issues with a conservative approach.
- The document recommends forming a razorback sucker advisory group with direct responsibility for populations and dedicated funding.
- Regarding stocking, the authors note that the inability to distinguish hatchery-produced from naturally produced individuals makes stocking assessment impossible.
- Discussion of recovery objectives such as minimum population sizes was considered premature given limited basic biological data for the UCRB.

**Administrative context:**
- The draft was circulated for review with comments requested by 6 November 1989.
- A step-down outline for recovery was planned for discussion at a Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team meeting on 28–29 March 1990 in Denver. The Colorado River Recovery Implementation Committee had requested the review, and the reviewer correspondence requested additional unpublished information from researchers.
