# Brook Trout Mortality, Idaho Fossil Cyprinids, and Predorsal Bones: Michigan Academy Papers, Vol. XLVI (1961)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt compiles three contributions from the *Papers of the Michigan Academy of Science, Arts, and Letters*, Vol. XLVI (1961). The first is a short note by G. R. Alexander and D. S. Shetter on heavy, unexplained winter mortality of stocked [[Brook Trout]] between November 9 and January 2. The second, the main body of the excerpt, is a systematic paper by Teruya Uyeno on Late Cenozoic [[Cyprinidae|Cyprinid]] fishes from Idaho, based mainly on [[Pharyngeal Bone|pharyngeal bones]]. It describes two new species and one new genus, and revises previously named North American fossil minnows. The third is the opening of a paper by C. Lavett Smith and Reeve M. Bailey on the evolution of [[Predorsal Bones]] in [[Percoidea|percoid]] fishes, which is only partly reproduced.

## Background & Description

**Brook trout mortality note (Alexander & Shetter).** The authors report a poor return of brook trout, attributed mainly to low survival between November 9 and January 2. They note that brook trout stayed in shallow water while [[Rainbow Trout]] remained in deeper water. Brook trout could be approached easily by rowboat from late October into early November, the period that coincides with their spawning season and with the heavy mortality. Most female brook trout captured around November 9, 1958, were ripe but had not yet spawned.

**Fossil cyprinid paper (Uyeno).** Uyeno introduces the poor paleontological record of North American cyprinids. The author attributes this partly to the lack of comparative osteology of Recent forms. Fossils were collected by surface picking by Dwight W. Taylor (1955–1957) and C. W. Hibbard (1956). Specimens are deposited in the United States National Museum (USNM) and the University of Michigan Museum of Paleontology (UMMP).

**Predorsal bone paper (Smith & Bailey).** The authors discuss awl-shaped bony elements anterior to the dorsal fin in teleosts. They are generally regarded as rayless pterygiophores. The excerpt ends in the introduction, before any results.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Trout mortality setting:** The authors considered and largely could not confirm several explanations for the die-off. Predation by [[Belted Kingfisher|kingfishers]], [[Great Blue Heron|herons]], [[American Mink|mink]], and [[River Otter|otter]] was plausible, since these predators frequent the inlet delta, where trout were concentrated in shallow water. [[Furunculosis]] was proposed because spawning may lower physical resistance. A spawning-related physiological change was also suggested. The authors note that hatchery brook trout of similar age and size die at the same time of year in hatchery ponds. Planting mortality was eliminated on timing grounds.
- **Fossil localities:** The 20 Idaho localities span the Snake River Plain. They include the [[Brown Formation]] (Middle Pliocene), the [[Sand Point Local Fauna]] (Late Pliocene), the [[Hagerman Formation]] (Early Pleistocene, probably Aftonian), and the [[Grand View Local Fauna]] (Middle Pleistocene). Lithologies include tuffaceous silts, sandy coquinas, fine cross-bedded sands, carbonaceous clay shales, and oolite. Several localities are associated with ash and pumice beds and basalt.
- **Paleoclimate and dispersal:** Uyeno suggests that cyprinids reached North America from Asia by way of the [[Bering Land Bridge]] during the Miocene. Temperate conditions in the northern continents are thought to have allowed this. The oldest North American fossil cyprinid is from the Miocene of British Columbia.
- **Molluscan prey and diet:** Based on tooth morphology, [[Mylocyprinus Robustus]] is thought to have crushed mollusk shells. Taylor's account of a drastic extinction of the molluscan fauna in the Late Pliocene or Early Pleistocene is offered as an explanation for the loss of this species.

## Key Findings

### Brook trout mortality (Alexander & Shetter)

- Survival was poor between November 9 and January 2. The cause was not identified, and the authors list the possibilities for future research: predation, furunculosis, and a spawning-related physiological change.
- Brook trout were concentrated in shallow water near the inlet delta during the period of heavy mortality. This made them vulnerable and easy to observe.

### Fossil cyprinids (Uyeno)

- **Pharyngeal bone terminology** follows Chu (1935), with modifications. Key measures are pharyngeal length (L), width (W), relative width (W/L), and tooth row counts. The tooth formula is written as major row and minor row counts, for example 0,5-5,0.
- **Mylocyprinus robustus Leidy** is the largest fossil species in the study. The pharyngeals are very large and robust, with a relative width of about 2.5. USNM 22305 (complete pharyngeal, right side) measures 50.0 mm long and 19.4 mm wide. No Recent North American cyprinid has pharyngeals as large. The molariform teeth are interpreted as adapted for crushing mollusks.
- **Ptychocheilus cf. oregonensis (Richardson)** is identified tentatively, because no pharyngeal is complete. Its teeth are cylindrical and strongly hooked on tooth I. This indicates a carnivorous habit. The largest tooth is reported as 113 mm long and 38 mm in diameter. The genus includes the largest Recent North American cyprinids, reaching 150 cm.
- **Diastichus parvidens Cope** is known only from pharyngeals. The shape resembles the subgenus Siphateles of [[Gila]]. The largest pharyngeal is reported as 323 mm long (likely a scan error for 32.3 mm), with a width of 10.8 mm.
- **Sigmopharyngodon, gen. nov., and S. idahoensis, sp. nov.** are described from the Hagerman Formation (holotype USNM 21900, a left pharyngeal with four teeth). The genus is defined by a strongly recurved posterior tip of the pharyngeal and very sharp grinding surfaces on all teeth. The holotype measures 7.5 mm in length, and the relative width is 4.0. The sharp grinding surface suggests a herbivorous diet. The species occurs at 11 additional localities.
- **Mylopharodon hagermanensis, sp. nov.** is based on holotype UMMP 40582 (a left pharyngeal from locality 20765) and paratypes UMMP 40583 (46 pharyngeal parts and 10 isolated teeth). It is collected at 12 additional localities, including USNM 22288 (79 pharyngeal parts and 74 isolated teeth) at locality 19128. The tooth formula is 2,5-4,2. The holotype measures 16.1 mm in length.
- **Mylopharodon cf. condonianus (Cope)** is a single tentative right pharyngeal (USNM 22304) from locality 20095.

### Revision of North American fossil minnows

- Uyeno proposes that about one third of fossil minnows described before 1950 are referable to living genera. Many names are treated as synonyms or reassigned:
  - [[Gila]] (subgenus Siphateles) receives *Alburnus angustarcus*, *Anchybopsis altarcus*, and *A. breviarcus*.
  - *Mylocyprinus kingii* and *M. longidens* are synonymized with *M. robustus*.
  - *Oligobelus arciferus* is considered identical with *M. robustus*.
- Several Cope type specimens lack the Smithsonian numbers he recorded, and none is recognized as a type. Uyeno declines to designate lectotypes without further study.
- The paper calls for more research on this neglected field, because the zoogeography, systematics, and evolution of the family remain poorly known.

### Predorsal bones (Smith & Bailey)

- Predorsal bones vary in number and form. They are usually slender rods, sometimes T-shaped, and sometimes L-shaped with a forward-directed short arm.
- A procumbent spine, formed by an enlarged first pterygiophore in genera such as *Scatophagus* and *Hapalogenys*, has been counted as part of the dorsal-fin formula by some authors.
- In lower fishes, such as *Leptolepis dubius* and *Palaeoniscus macropomus*, both supraneurals and pterygiophores are present in the median series before the dorsal fin. In *Lepisosteus*, supraneurals are associated with predorsal vertebrae.
