# Hydrobiid Snails of the Cuatro Ciénegas Basin: Systematic Relationships and Ecology of a Unique Fauna

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Robert Hershler]] revises the systematics and documents the ecology of the hydrobiid snail fauna of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], Coahuila, Mexico. Earlier work by [[Taylor 1966]] had treated the fauna as highly endemic, with three endemic subfamilies, five endemic genera and 12 species. Anatomical reanalysis shows that every basin taxon belongs to either the [[Nymphophilinae]] or [[Littoridininae]], both widespread across North America, so no subfamilies are endemic. The fauna is partitioned among large springs, small springs and groundwater outlets, and the authors infer a local and recent origin for the endemic taxa.

## Background & Description

The basin hosts nine genera (five endemic) and 13 species (nine endemic) recorded from intensive sampling. Of these, one genus and three species are new and will be described elsewhere, and two species (*Cochliopina riograndensis* and an *Orygoceras*(?) species) are new records for the basin. The six nominal species of *Mexipyrgus* are reduced to one, *M. churinceanus*, because shell differences intergrade across 33 populations.

Shells of many basin species are unusually large, sculptured or colour-banded, unlike most hydrobiids. The basin's local diversity of nine genera and 12 species in a 30 x 40 km valley is compared with ancient-lake faunas such as [[Lake Baikal]], [[Lake Ohrid]] and [[Lake Titicaca]] (Table 1 of the source).

Earlier classifications relied heavily on shell and external features, which converge among rissoacean taxa. The study therefore uses soft-part anatomy, specifically the female reproductive system and penis morphology, to define subfamilies. This classification is described as tentative, since at least 90% of North and Central American hydrobiid species lack anatomical study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat partitioning.** Three groups occupy distinct habitats:
- Species with large, thickened shells (*M. churinceanus*, *M. quadripaludium*, *N. minckleyi*) dominate large springs (spring pools over 900 m²).
- Minute, blind, unpigmented species (*Paludiscala caramba*, *Coahuilix hubbsi*, *Coahuilix* n. sp., *Orygoceras*(?) sp.) are restricted largely to small springheads and possibly groundwater.
- Pigmented species (*Durangonella coahuilae*, "*Stiobia*" n. sp.) occupy small-spring outflows; *D. coahuilae* also reaches marshes, playa lakes and pools.

**Micro-habitat partitioning.** In large springs, *M. churinceanus* burrows in soft sediment (densities up to 40,000/m²), *N. minckleyi* clings to aquatic vegetation, and *M. quadripaludium* occupies travertine undersides. Where micro-habitats are reduced, species switch substrates, which suggests partly competitive structuring.

**Environmental gradients.** Abundances of most large-spring species decline downstream as vegetation thins, algal cover increases, temperature fluctuates and salinity rises. Water temperatures at one locality ranged 31.0–34.5°C at the springhead versus 20.6–37.8°C 77 m downstream over four days.

**Predation and reproduction.** Molluscivorous [[Cichlid Fishes]] in large springs are proposed to explain the scarcity of intermediate-sized snails: thick shells may resist crushing, while tiny juveniles may be overlooked. At least one species, *N. minckleyi* in [[Laguna Mojarral East]], reproduces year-round at 26–32°C, with juveniles at least 48% of each monthly sample. Embryos occurred in brood sacs of all viviparous species throughout the year.

## Key Findings

- **Endemism is smaller in scale than previously thought.** No hydrobiid subfamily is endemic; all basin taxa fall in Nymphophilinae or Littoridininae.
- **Taxonomic revision:** the six *Mexipyrgus* nominal species are reduced to one. Two species are new to the basin and four taxa are new (to be described elsewhere).
- **Sampling effort:** snails were collected from 103 localities (100 in an arc around the northern Sierra de San Marcos). Mop samples from 38 small springheads yielded 8,071 individuals.
- **Dominance in small springs:** *Paludiscala caramba* was by far the most common springhead species, present in 21 of 23 analysed springs and comprising more than 0.75 of the sample in 12.
- **Substrate-based partitioning in Laguna Mojarral East:** *M. churinceanus* comprised 0.99 of soft-sediment samples; *N. minckleyi* predominated on *Nymphaea* and *Chara*; *M. quadripaludium* was three times as common as *N. minckleyi* on travertine undersides.
- **Phylogeny:** a phenogram and cladogram from 36 characters group the six littoridinine genera into three clades: *Paludiscala–Coahuilix* (egg-layers), *Mexithauma–Cochliopina* and *Mexipyrgus–Durangonella*. Three of the five endemic genera closely resemble non-endemics.
- **Origin:** the authors propose a recent (possibly late Cenozoic) and local origin, citing reduced endemism, similarity to non-endemics, proximity to the [[Rio Grande]] drainage and low intra-basin differentiation. Rapid in situ evolution under cichlid predation is suggested for the three strictly endemic genera, all large-spring inhabitants.
- **Methods note:** the study proposes a fine mesh net over groundwater outlets to test whether blind species truly inhabit subterranean waters, since mop colonisation cannot distinguish washed-in from surface snails.
