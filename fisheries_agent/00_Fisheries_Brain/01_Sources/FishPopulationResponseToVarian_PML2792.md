# Fish Population Response to Variance in Stream Discharge, Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Master of Science thesis by Anthony Lawrence Velasco (Arizona State University, December 1997) examines how the spatial variance of native fish populations in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, responds to variance in stream discharge. Using both a long-term data set (decades, from [[Minckley Long-Term Data Set]]) and a short-term monthly data set (March 1991 to February 1993), the author tests whether fish spatial patterns follow discharge variance. The alternative hypothesis was supported by the long-term data, and the short-term data suggest that recruitment of some species tracks discharge-related disturbance. The thesis concludes that natural flood disturbance maintains habitat heterogeneity, and that fish populations track it through spatial responses, demonstrating resilience. It closes with management implications for flow regimes, channelization, and water use in the American Southwest.

## Background & Description

**Conceptual framework.** Recent ecological theory treats dynamical processes such as [[Natural Disturbance]] as essential to ecosystem integrity. Under the maintenance paradigm, communities that evolved with disturbance regimes should be resilient to disturbance. Temporal variance is the difference in abundance over time; spatial variance is differential distribution among habitats. The two often interact.

**Study system.** Aravaipa Creek is described as one of the last perennial tributaries of the [[San Pedro River]] supporting a diverse native fish assemblage, making it a refugium and reference point for regional change. The creek has three segments: an eastern reach, an 18 km [[Aravaipa Canyon]] reach with vertical walls over 250 m, and a western reach that becomes ephemeral before joining the San Pedro River. The [[Bureau of Land Management]] manages a Wilderness Area in the canyon, and [[The Nature Conservancy]] owns much of the surrounding land and holds instream-flow water rights.

**Fish community.** Seven native fishes were recorded: five cyprinids (minnows) and two catostomids (suckers). Four minnows are small-bodied and short-lived; the roundtail chub and both suckers are large-bodied and long-lived. Eight non-native species have also been recorded.

**Habitat associations (Table 1 summary).**
- *Agosia chrysogaster* (longfin dace): low-elevation, hot, shallow, sand-bottomed reaches.
- *Meda fulgida* (spikedace): pools and eddies.
- *Rhinichthys osculus* (speckled dace) and *Tiaroga cobitis* (loach minnow): coarse substrates, freshly scoured riffles and turbulent margins.
- *Gila robusta* (roundtail chub) and *Catostomus insignis* (Sonora sucker): deep undercuts and debris piles.
- *Pantosteus clarki* (desert sucker): open pools and riffles with abundant periphyton.

**Methods.** Sampling used two seine sizes (1.8 × 1.2 m with 3.2 mm mesh; 4.6 × 1.2 m with 6.4 mm mesh) at two sites per reach, covering at least 200 linear meters per site. Long-term sampling dated to 1964 (sites visited twice annually), and short-term sampling was monthly. Juveniles were distinguished from adults using estimated total lengths at sexual maturity (e.g., 38 mm for *T. cobitis*, 150 mm for *C. insignis* and *G. robusta*). Coefficient of variation (CV) measured spatial variance for fishes and temporal variance for discharge, daily mean discharge came from USGS Gaging Station 09473000, and relationships were smoothed with Distance Weighted Least Squares and analyzed with second-order polynomial regression.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Precipitation is strongly bimodal: cyclonic winter rains lasting days to weeks, a springtime drought, convective summer monsoons with short, intense downpours, and an autumn drought. Annual precipitation at Klondyke averages 39 cm (per the source text), half in summer. Modal discharge for the long-term record is approximately 0.34 m³/s. Discharge exceeded 60 m³/s five times at 5–8-year intervals between 1964 and 1993. One major flood (>85 m³/s) occurred in March 1991, and 1992 high discharges approached 28 m³/s.

**Habitat formation.** Habitat is controlled by sediment transport and deposition, which depend on bedload and precipitation. Steep canyon reaches carry coarse, bedrock-dominated substrates, while wider, lower-gradient floodplains accumulate fine sediment. Floods scour pools, armor riffles, and build sediment dams that form shallow braided channels. These braids serve as nurseries for larval and juvenile fishes and are protected from aquatic predators.

**Biotic and human influences.** Native fishes are largely unaffected by flooding as adults, while non-natives are concentrated in the western reach and are limited from the canyon by winter floods and monsoon spates. Historic water diversion, groundwater pumping, [[Channelization]], and bank stabilization have homogenized habitat, and the thesis argues these changes reduce heterogeneity and promote exotic species establishment.

## Key Findings

- **Long-term result:** Spatial variance of several species increased with discharge CV, up to a threshold after which spatial variance declined. Regression models were highly significant (p < 0.001). For example, one model reported R² = 0.57 for a species and R² = 0.906 and 0.985 for others (as printed in the source).
- **Species-specific responses:** *Rhinichthys osculus* and *Tiaroga cobitis* responded similarly, with nearly identical threshold values. The two suckers and *Meda fulgida* showed strong positive slopes with little threshold response. *Agosia chrysogaster* showed an early threshold, and *Gila robusta* responded in a way the author attributes to unmeasured variables.
- **Short-term result:** Regressions were less statistically robust than the long-term analyses. Discharge values within the long-term range gave similar results; outside that range, short-term responses extended in the same directions.
- **Recruitment and lag:** Recruitment of adults and juveniles appeared to track the hydrologic cycle after a short lag. Temporal-shift analysis showed juveniles of all species had greater spatial variance after a three-month lag, and a two-month lag approximates the time before newly hatched fish become catchable by seine.
- **Spawning cues:** Spawning appears linked to flooding and flood-scoured substrates more than to photoperiod alone, as shown for *Rhinichthys osculus* and *Meda fulgida*.
- **Canyon migration:** Long-lived species (*Catostomus insignis*, *Pantosteus clarki*, *Gila robusta*) may move into the canyon during conditions that limit habitat elsewhere, but results were inconsistent across studies.
- **Carrying capacity:** The author suggests heterogeneity supports high fish standing crops (reported by Minckley as among the largest known), reducing density-dependent effects.
- **Management implications:** Spatial variance should not be confused with temporal variance in management. Small, boom-and-bust populations face extinction risk. Disturbance regimes must be maintained for native fish survival, and the thesis recommends natural channel dynamics over artificial structures.
