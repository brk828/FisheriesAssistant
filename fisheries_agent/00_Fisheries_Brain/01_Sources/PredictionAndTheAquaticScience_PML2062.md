# Prediction and the Aquatic Sciences

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gadidae #limnology #aquatic-science

## Executive Summary

This invited perspective argues that [[Prediction]] is a central, under-examined objective of aquatic science and should be treated as a standard for judging research progress. It traces the influence of the [[Empirical Limnology]] school (F.H. Rigler and R.H. Peters), which promoted predictive relationships but presented prediction too narrowly. The author argues that prediction is best seen as an aid to building scientific understanding, and that the most effective predictive models are condensed representations of key system features. The essay illustrates a range of approaches, including [[Regression Models]], [[Time Series Analysis]], [[Bayesian Models]], [[Decision Tables]] and [[Data Assimilation]]. It concludes that technological advances in studying aquatic systems will fail to deliver on their promise unless predictive capability improves.

## Background & Description

The essay begins with the late work of [[F.H. Rigler]], who argued that many ecological phenomena were unpredictable under current theory and advocated empirical relationships such as regression models as a path to more explanatory theory. Rigler also called for closer integration of [[Fisheries Science]] and [[Limnology]]. These ideas formed the basis of [[Empirical Limnology]], which was controversial (Lehman 1986; Carpenter and Kitchell 1988; Shrader-Frechette and McCoy 1993) but focused attention on prediction.

The author defines prediction as stating an outcome in advance, based on a conceptual framework that is organized into theories and models. Predictions can be derived from theory or from empirical patterns, and many are hybrids. Because aquatic predictions are usually probabilistic, models that estimate the most likely states of a system with associated uncertainties are particularly relevant to management (Walters 1986).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Large research programs.** Mission statements of major marine and freshwater programs make prediction a core objective:

- [[GLOBEC]] (Global Ocean Ecosystem Dynamics), with its [[Cod and Climate Change]] component, aims to predict fish recruitment for [[Atlantic Cod]] across short and long time scales. Cod is pan-Atlantic and its abundance is sensitive to past environmental variability.
- [[JGOFS]] (Joint Global Ocean Flux Study) aims to understand carbon fluxes and to predict the response of ocean biogeochemistry to anthropogenic perturbations, particularly climate change.
- The [[Freshwater Imperative]] (Naiman et al. 1994) seeks "a predictive understanding of freshwater ecosystems and resources" to improve detection, assessment and forecasting of environmental effects.

The author notes that these statements may partly serve to attract funding, but also reflect scientists' aspirations to address environmental problems.

**Practical pressures.** Human water use is estimated at 54% of accessible runoff (Postel et al. 1996). Even water-rich boreal Canada faces problems from climate change, acid deposition and ultraviolet exposure (Schindler 1998). Global capture fisheries appear to be near a maximum of about 100 Mt per year (Botsford et al. 1997; FAO 1998). Fishery management failures, such as the collapse of Atlantic cod (Walters and Maguire 1996; Myers et al. 1997), are cited as evidence of the need for predictive science, although political and management factors also contribute (Hutchings et al. 1997).

## Key Findings

**1. Prediction is a standard for understanding, not an end in itself.** Testing predictions is the traditional means of judging whether understanding is adequate. Given the complexity of aquatic systems, the author argues, it is easy to overestimate understanding. Predictions allow general models to be confronted with data.

**2. Three reasons to value prediction are given:**
- Human-driven change will increase demand for predictive analysis and management.
- Predictive goals keep research focused on ultimate objectives rather than small problems.
- Taking prediction seriously should generate dissatisfaction that spurs creative approaches.

**3. Regression predictions from comparative studies have clear limits.** In a study of zooplankton biomass (Pace 1986), a regression of mean summer [[Zooplankton]] biomass on [[Total Phosphorus]] explained 86% of variation. However, the 95% confidence interval for a typical lake with TP of 12 mg m⁻³ spanned roughly threefold, so the model was a poor basis for predicting an individual lake. Regressions predict only annual or seasonal means and are often statistically problematic (McArdle 1987; Prairie et al. 1995).

**4. Comparative analyses remain valuable for evaluating patterns.** A companion study (Pace 1984) found that zooplankton biomass did not explain residual variation in phosphorus–chlorophyll relationships, but dominance by larger zooplankton was associated with lower chlorophyll per unit TP. Comparative papers are also heavily cited; in aquatic microbial ecology, comparative papers were among the 10 most cited (Duarte et al. 1997).

**5. Models should be tested with independent data.** Model–data comparison, including forecasts tested against data not used in model construction, should become a standard for presenting models. An example is a [[Time Series Model]] of rotifer abundance in the [[Hudson River]] (Pace et al. 1998). [[Zebra Mussel]] invasion caused a significant decline in rotifers beginning in 1992. The model, with data for 1987–1995, forecast 1996 conditions and was compared with previously unpublished 1996 data.

**6. Probabilistic decision tools make uncertainty explicit.** A sample fishery decision table (after Hilborn et al. 1993) gives expected yields over 5 years for three stock biomass states (0.9 Mt, probability 0.57; 1.5 Mt, probability 0.40; 2.1 Mt, probability 0.03) under constant 150-kt and 200-kt quota policies. Such tables summarize predictions and expose risk to decision-makers.

**7. Cyanobacterial bloom risk can be forecast.** A logistic regression of spring total phosphorus on the probability of [[Cyanobacterial Blooms]] exceeding a bloom threshold of 5 µg L⁻¹ was developed for [[Lake Mendota]] (Stow et al. 1995). The model suggests that lowering spring TP below a threshold would substantially reduce bloom risk. The author notes that such models are useful because they quantify uncertainty.

**8. Mechanistic understanding and prediction should be integrated.** Predictions can be tested and refined by mechanistic research, and understanding can in turn improve models. The author gives an example from [[Chaoborus]] larvae (Hare and Tessier 1996, 1998). Predictions of larval cadmium concentration from water concentrations improved when competition with H⁺ for uptake sites and complexation with dissolved organic matter were included. The resulting [[Cadmium]] model is condensed, derives from first principles, and works across a wide range of environmental conditions. The author treats it as an example of prediction and understanding reinforcing each other.

**9. Inductive and deductive models both have a role.** Following Livingstone and Imboden (1996), the author distinguishes [[Inductive Models]] (empirical, built from correlations among variables) from [[Deductive Models]] (built from general principles). The classic [[Nutrient Loading Models]] (Dillon and Rigler 1974; Vollenweider 1975) combine elemental mass balance with a continuously stirred reactor concept. Models formulated in analytical frameworks are more robust and general, and uncertainty should be stated explicitly rather than presenting single answers as graphs.

**10. Prediction should be pursued despite limited current ability.** The author acknowledges that the ability to make general or specific predictions about aquatic systems is currently "very limited and imprecise." This should create productive dissatisfaction. The author warns that technological improvements in data acquisition alone will not guarantee progress, and that if predictive ability does not improve over the next couple of decades, this will represent a significant failure. Prediction should be deliberately made a standard for aquatic science, not expected as an accidental byproduct of advances.
