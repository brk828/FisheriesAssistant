# Cuatro Ciénegas Food Web: Cichlasoma minckleyi and Invasive Species Impacts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This research proposal outlines a multi-year study of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas]] desert spring ecosystem in the Chihuahuan Desert (Northern Mexico), a globally significant site of freshwater fish and mollusc endemism. The central research subject is **[[Cichlasoma minckleyi]]**, a highly polymorphic cichlid that functions as a keystone species across multiple trophic levels. The study integrates historical museum records, modern field surveys, stable isotope analysis, and manipulative field experiments to test three core hypotheses regarding **native biota decline**, **invasive species impacts** (specifically [[Hemichromis sp.]] and [[Melanoides tuberculata]]), and **water extraction effects**. Results are intended to feed into an adaptive management simulation model (using Stella software) for conservation decision-making by The Nature Conservancy (TNC) and Profauna.

## Background & Description

### Cichlasoma minckleyi (Trophic Polymorph)
This species exhibits a rare case of **discrete trophic polymorphism**, with intermediate forms also occurring in the wild. Documented morphs include:

| Morph | Diet | Feeding Structure Adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| **Papilliform** | Detritus (Detritivore) | Adapted for scraping/processing organic sediment |
| **Molluscivore** | Native snails (Hydrobiidae) | Robust pharyngeal jaws for crushing shells |
| **Piscivore** | Small fish, algae | Predatory dentition; restricted to larger pools |

*Note: [[C. minckleyi]] is unique in that a single species may constitute the majority of biomass across the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th trophic levels simultaneously, effectively acting as "up to four ecological species" within the food web.*

### Comparative Morphology: Native Snails vs. Invasive Snail
- **Native Hydrobiid Snails**: Smaller size, thinner shells (per Hershler 1984 classification: 1. Large-spring thick-shelled species; 2. Small unpigmented groundwater species; 3. Small-stream species).
- **[[Melanoides tuberculata]]** (Invasive African Snail): Larger body size and significantly thicker shell, hypothesized to confer a "size refuge" from predation (measured via shell "crushability" per Osenberg & Mittelbach 1989).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Context
The Cuatro Ciénegas basin (<1500 km²) contains **>200 distinct pools** varying in salinity, size, and water chemistry, creating a mosaic of habitat types: spring heads, spring runs, rivers, ditches, playas, and marshes.

### Trophic Web Structure (See [[Figure 1 - Partial Food Web]])
The base of the food web relies on **algae and detritus**, consumed by:
- Native herbivores: *[[Dionda episcopa]]* and *[[Cyprinodon bifasciatus]]*
- Native hydrobiid snails
- Aquatic insects (restricted to lower-salinity pools)

**Apex Predators**: *[[Micropterus salmoides]]* (Largemouth Bass, non-native but established) and the piscivore morph of *C. minckleyi*, limited to pools with sufficient habitat volume.

### Invasive Species Interactions (Table 2 Summary)

| Invasive Species | Trophic Role | Known Distribution | Hypothesized Ecological Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[[Hemichromis sp.]]** (Jewel Cichlid) | Detritivore | 1 large pool (Laguna Churince) + canal | Competitive exclusion of papilliform *C. minckleyi* morph |
| **[[Melanoides tuberculata]]** | Detritivore/Grazer | 2 large pools + 1 canal | Competes with native snails; resistant to fish predation |
| *Sarotherodon sp.* (Tilapia) | Herbivore/Omnivore | Widely distributed, low density | Unknown (lower priority) |
| *Cyprinus carpio* (Carp) | Omnivore | 1 lagoon | Unknown (lower priority) |
| *Orconectes sp.* (Crayfish) | Omnivore | Rio Mesquites | Unknown (lower priority) |

### Documented Population Shift (Case Study: Laguna Churince)
A critical empirical observation driving Hypothesis 2B:
- **Pre-invasion (1975)**: Papilliform morph = ~75% of *C. minckleyi* population (Sage & Selander 1975; Smith 1982).
- **Post-invasion (1998)**: Papilliform morph declined to ~10%, coinciding with *Hemichromis sp.* becoming a dominant component of total fish biomass.

## Key Findings

1.  **Keystone Species Identification**: *C. minckleyi*'s broad trophic plasticity makes it the central node for cascading effects; changes in its morph ratios serve as a bio-indicator for ecosystem health.

2.  **Hypothesis 2A (Snail Competition)**: *M. tuberculata* is predicted to outcompete native hydrobiid snails while being resistant to predation by the molluscivore morph of *C. minckleyi*, reducing the food base for that morph. Tested via:
    - Food choice enclosure experiments (predation preference).
    - Growth rate experiments (comparing *C. minckleyi* growth on native vs. invasive snail diets).
    - Snail-on-snail competition trials in Becerra canal.

3.  **Hypothesis 2B (Morph Shift)**: *Hemichromis sp.* is predicted to competitively displace the papilliform morph via detritus competition, causing an adaptive/demographic shift in *C. minckleyi* populations toward the molluscivore morph.

4.  **Hypothesis 3 (Hydrologic Alteration)**: Water extraction is predicted to shrink marginal habitats and increase temperature/salinity beyond species tolerance thresholds, independently threatening populations (particularly larger-bodied apex predators requiring deep-water habitat).

5.  **Isotopic Signatures as Diagnostic Tools**: 
    - **δ¹⁵N** (Nitrogen): Expected to be enriched in molluscivore morphs relative to papilliform morphs, serving as a quantitative marker of trophic level shift.
    - **δ¹³C and δ³⁴S** (Carbon/Sulfur): Expected to reveal shifts in basal energy source (detritus → snails/insects) in invaded pools, providing a historical/modern comparative tool using both museum specimens and fresh fin-clip samples.

6.  **Management Framework**: The study proposes a tiered management-options matrix (Tables 3 & 4) ranging from "Do Nothing" to "Eradicate," explicitly weighing feasibility against cost — e.g., eradicating *M. tuberculata* is rated "Very High" cost with "Low" feasibility, whereas reducing *Hemichromis sp.* abundance is rated more moderately feasible.
