# Distribution of green river fishes in utah and colorado following closure of flaming gorge dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports a 1963–1966 survey of fish populations in the Green River below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], Utah, which closed in November 1962. The study sampled 24,040 fish in 667 collections from the dam to Ouray, Utah, and compared the results with [[Pre-Impoundment Surveys]]. The authors found that dam-altered flow and water temperature have largely replaced native fishes with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] in the first 26 miles below the dam. Reproduction of native fishes was absent above the [[Yampa River]] mouth in the cold-water years of 1964 and 1966. Below the Yampa, fish populations resembled pre-impoundment conditions.

The document opens with the closing fragment of an unrelated squirrel population study, which reports that 89% of the 1967 squirrels descended from only 20% of the 1963 females. That material is not analyzed here.

## Background & Description

Flaming Gorge Dam is a 455-foot-high, arch-type concrete dam completed in November 1962 by the Bureau of Reclamation as part of the [[Colorado River Storage Project]]. It lies on the Green River in lower Red Canyon, Ashley National Forest, northeastern Utah. Its purposes are hydroelectric power generation and river flow regulation. High dams of this type draw water from deep reservoir layers, which typically lowers average water temperature, reduces turbidity, damps seasonal flow extremes, and lowers dissolved oxygen immediately downstream.

Before the dam closed, the Green River and its tributaries from Pinedale, Wyoming, to 7 miles above the damsite were treated with [[Rotenone Treatment]] in September 1962 to remove non-game fish. The aim was to establish a sport fishery in the new reservoir and its tailwaters. Closure inundated about 90 miles of river.

The study was part of a follow-up assessing effects of the fish-control program on fishes of [[Dinosaur National Monument]], 46 miles downstream of the dam. Its objectives were to:
1. describe changes in the river environment after closure;
2. determine species composition, distribution, and abundance of fishes from the dam to Ouray; and
3. compare 1963–1966 fish distribution with pre-impoundment collections.

The Green River, the largest tributary of the [[Colorado River]], originates in western Wyoming and drains nearly 45,000 square miles of mostly arid or semi-arid land. The 160-mile study area includes intermittent tributaries that flow mainly during spring runoff. Browns Park and reaches below Jensen are slow-flowing with silt and sand bottoms. Red Canyon and the Monument canyons are steep, with rapids, riffles, eddies, pools, and runs over boulder, rubble, sand, and silt.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Continuous temperature recorders were run at Echo Park and Island Park. Pocket thermometers were used at all stations during sampling. Water-quality variables (dissolved oxygen, alkalinity, conductivity, pH, turbidity) were measured at each visit. Flow, temperature, and quality data came from USGS gauges at Greendale and Jensen. Sampling gear comprised:
- gill nets (223 collections)
- seines of various mesh sizes (264 collections)
- boat-mounted 230-volt a-c [[Electrofishing]] (121 collections)
- tow nets, food strainers, and a modified scoop shovel for larval and small fish (59 collections)

Intensive sites were at Lodore, Echo Park, Island Park, and Split Mountain. Supplemental sites were at Little Hole, Bridgeport, Willow Creek, and Ouray. Access by vehicle and boat limited site choice.

**Flow.** Before impoundment, flow was lowest in winter and peaked in May or June. After closure, the high spring and low winter flows were replaced by a more stable seasonal pattern. Daily flows became less stable because releases follow power demand. Below the Yampa mouth, the seasonal pattern at Jensen resembles pre-impoundment conditions, though peak runoff now occurs in May rather than June. Late-fall and winter flows are higher than before impoundment.

**Water temperature.** Before closure, water at Greendale rose from winter lows of 33 °F to about 72 °F in July. After closure it stayed in the 35–55 °F range, because water comes from the reservoir hypolimnion. Maximum summer temperature was inversely related to discharge:
- Summers 1963 and 1965 (low releases of 102–1,439 cfs): temperatures from Bridgeport to Jensen approximated pre-impoundment values.
- Summers 1964 and 1966 (higher releases of 1,466–2,441 cfs): cooling extended further downstream, and the Yampa inflow was much warmer than the Green River at Echo Park.

Maximum summer temperatures at [[Little Hole]] (7 miles below the dam) were 63 °F (1963), 59 °F (1965), 53 °F (1964), and 55 °F (1966). At [[Bridgeport]] (17 miles below) they were 76 °F (1963), 68 °F (1965), 58 °F (1964), and 60 °F (1966).

**Water chemistry.** Mean annual bicarbonates, total dissolved solids, specific conductance, and pH showed no permanent change after closure. In the first post-closure water year (1962–63, mean flow 231 cfs), bicarbonates, dissolved solids, and conductance were much higher, which the authors attribute to low flow during reservoir filling.

**Sediment and turbidity.** The Green River at Greendale was nearly sediment-free, and silt load increased downstream. Mean annual sediment at Jensen was 1,529 ppm before closure (range 753–2,430 ppm) and 870 ppm after (range 703–1,190 ppm). Turbidity was lowest at Little Hole (under 25 Jackson Turbidity Units) and highest at Island Park (up to 5,000 JTU).

## Key Findings

**Overall sampling and diversity**
- A total of 24,040 fish in 667 collections was taken from July 1963 to October 1966.
- The catch comprised 9 indigenous and 12 exotic species (21 total).
- Native species included [[Colorado Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Humpback Sucker]], mottled sculpin, and mountain whitefish. A hybrid sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis* × *Xyrauchen texanus*) was also collected.
- Exotic species included [[Rainbow Trout]], brown trout, [[Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Redside Shiner]], channel catfish, black bullhead, and walleye, among others.

**Zone-by-zone distribution**
- **Little Hole (7 miles):** Flannelmouth sucker was the only native species (one adult captured, two observed). Rainbow trout were abundant. No reproduction was seen.
- **Bridgeport–Willow Creek (17–23 miles):** Rainbow trout remained dominant. Native fishes included flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and speckled dace. Native reproduction was recorded only in 1965.
- **Lodore (46 miles):** Colorado chub, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and speckled dace increased in abundance. Young-of-the-year of seven species were collected in 1965.
- **Echo Park (65 miles):** Species number rose. Colorado squawfish, humpback sucker, and mottled sculpin were added. Native species were more abundant than upstream. Successful reproduction of all native fishes except humpback sucker was apparent.
- **Island Park (77 miles) and Split Mountain (87 miles):** Composition was very similar to Echo Park. Reproduction of all natives except humpback sucker was observed in 1964–1966.
- **Ouray (160 miles):** Carp were very abundant. Colorado chub, speckled dace, and both sucker species were relatively uncommon, probably because of the lower gradient. Young-of-the-year of all native species except humpback sucker were collected.

**Reproductive failure above the Yampa**
- In 1964 and 1966 (high summer releases, cooler water), no reproduction of any species was found in the 65-mile Green River reach above the Yampa mouth.
- The authors attribute this to water temperatures that stayed below the mid-60 °F range thought necessary for spawning of native cyprinids and catostomids.

**Pre- versus post-impoundment comparison**
- Five pre-impoundment surveys (1959–1962) recorded 8 indigenous (including one hybrid) and 6 exotic species. The authors caution that these collections were far fewer than post-impoundment sampling.
- Above Browns Park, the disappearance of Colorado squawfish, Colorado chub, humpback chub, and humpback sucker, plus the appearance of rainbow trout, marked the largest change. Composition from Browns Park to Echo Park changed less but still differs from Echo Park.
- Below the Green–Yampa confluence, species composition was unchanged. All native species known from pre-impoundment Monument collections, except humpback chub, were collected.

**Humpback chub status**
- Pre-impoundment reports of humpback chub in the study area were not substantiated. Specimens were reidentified as bonytail-form [[Colorado Chub]] or left unconfirmed.
- Only three humpback-like specimens were collected in 1963 (one at Echo Park, one at Island Park, one at Swallow Canyon). Only the Echo Park fish was considered a likely bona fide humpback chub. The taxonomic status remained unresolved.

**Dominant species**
- Rainbow trout were most abundant from the dam to at least 26 miles downstream.
- Redside shiner and speckled dace were most abundant in Dinosaur National Monument. By biomass, flannelmouth sucker dominated there.
- Carp were the most numerous species at Ouray.
- Flannelmouth sucker was the most widely distributed species in the study area.

**Principal conclusions**
- Closure caused a major ecological change by altering seasonal flow and water temperature. Native fishes have nearly disappeared from at least the first 7 miles below the dam.
- Native species number has declined as far as the Yampa mouth (65 miles). Below the Yampa, indigenous and introduced abundances appear unaffected.
- Cold discharge from the dam curtails reproduction in the 65-mile reach.
- Temperature change is the most likely primary driver of the fish-fauna shift. The effects of altered flow and reduced turbidity are unknown.
- Long-term ecological changes appear to have outweighed any short-term effects of the pre-impoundment rotenone fish-control operation.
