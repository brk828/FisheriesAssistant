# Effects of Reservoir Releases on Tailwater Ecology: A Literature Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #tailwater-ecology #fish-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1981 literature review, prepared by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's [[National Reservoir Research Program]] (East Central Reservoir Investigations, Bowling Green, Kentucky) with the U.S. Army Engineer Waterways Experiment Station, synthesizes published evidence on how dam releases alter downstream ("tailwater") environments and their biota. It was written to support development of reservoir discharge guidelines that enhance tailwater habitat and project benefits. The review covers reservoir limnology, outlet-structure design and operation, the physical and chemical character of tailwaters, invertebrate and fish communities, and research needs. The authors conclude that tailwater biota reflect the depth, volume, and schedule of releases, and that present understanding is incomplete, with most studies narrow in scope and few linking specific physical or chemical factors to observed biological change.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** The Corps of Engineers operates reservoirs to meet downstream environmental objectives, but no quantitative guidelines exist for determining minimum releases needed to maintain a desired downstream aquatic environment. Many requirements of downstream habitat and biota are poorly understood.
- **Scope:** The review covers literature on tailwaters supporting warmwater fish or trout, excluding the extensive anadromous fish literature. It cites 105 U.S. tailwaters (listed in [[Appendix B]]) and a list of 113 tailwaters in Appendix A.
- **Structure:** Parts I–VIII address introduction, [[Reservoir Limnology]], outlet design and operation, physical and chemical tailwater conditions, metabolism and trophic structure, [[Aquatic Invertebrates]], [[Tailwater Fishes]], and conclusions. Appendices give species checklists (fishes from North American and European tailwaters) and life-history summaries.
- **Authorship:** Charles H. Walburg, Jerry F. Novotny, Kenneth E. Jacobs, William D. Swink, Terra M. Campbell, John M. Nestler, and Gary E. Saul. Report No. Technical Report E-81-12.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reservoir limnology controls tailwater quality.** Hydraulic residence time, thermal stratification (monomictic, dimictic, or unstratified reservoirs), dissolved oxygen, nutrients, and reduced compounds in the hypolimnion determine what is released. Short-residence-time run-of-river reservoirs produce releases similar to inflows; long-residence reservoirs behave more like lakes.

**Outlet structures and operations.** Selective withdrawal structures allow release from different depths. Bottom-release gates, common in older flood-control projects, typically discharge cold, low-oxygen water. Hydropower peaking produces daily flow fluctuations and reduced flows during low demand.

**Physical and chemical alterations.** Impoundments delay seasonal temperature changes, reduce temperature fluctuations, dampen high flows, and reduce sediment transport, leading to channel degradation and bank instability. Releases may be colder, lower in dissolved oxygen, enriched with nutrients, and contain reduced compounds (iron, manganese, hydrogen sulfide, ammonia).

**Biotic communities.** Tailwater invertebrate communities tend to show lower diversity and higher densities of tolerant taxa, with [[Chironomidae]] and [[Simuliidae]] often dominant. Fish communities shift toward species tolerant of altered conditions, and trout may replace native warmwater species below cold-release dams.

## Key Findings

**1. Thermal effects are central.**
- Below deep-release reservoirs on warmwater streams, tailwater temperatures can be as much as 20°C lower than unregulated regional streams during summer (Ward and Stanford 1979).
- A Montana tailwater: spring water temperatures are delayed 5–8 weeks; a Colorado example recorded 3.2–5.0°C summer reductions.
- Daily temperature fluctuation below one Utah dam fell from about 20°C in the unregulated river to a range of 2–10°C after impoundment.
- Epilimnetic releases from a Montana reservoir raised summer temperatures 2.7°C, retarding growth of trout over 27 cm long.

**2. Dissolved oxygen and gas.**
- Most tailwater oxygen problems occur below hypolimnetic releases; reaeration can restore concentrations to about 7 mg/l, but some discharges are severely deoxygenated.
- Gas supersaturation (most common below high dams in the Pacific Northwest) causes gas-bubble disease and mortality in fish and invertebrates.

**3. Flow regime changes.**
- Flood-control dams reduce peak flows and extend high flows, reducing bank erosion but altering channel size and vegetation.
- Hydropower peaking causes daily flow fluctuations, stranding, desiccation, and reduced productivity; these conditions often eliminate most stream invertebrates.
- Stable releases favour benthic colonisation and algal mats but can eliminate species that require clean rock surfaces.

**4. Invertebrates.**
- Dipterans (chironomids, simuliids) are best adapted to altered tailwater conditions.
- Ephemeroptera and Plecoptera are generally absent from tailwaters, particularly those with fluctuating flows or stable thermal regimes.
- Tailwater drift is dominated by reservoir-produced organisms such as *Chaoborus* and microcrustaceans.
- Zooplankton discharged from reservoirs declined rapidly downstream; one study found a 99 percent loss within the first 16 km.

**5. Fish.**
- Padd­lefish: populations declined with dam construction; spawning is blocked upstream, and tailwater aggregations increase vulnerability to fishing.
- Trout: cold tailwaters below hypolimnetic releases support trout fisheries (rainbow trout most common), but reproduction is often limited, and populations depend on stocking. Recommended minimum flows for trout in one Wyoming tailwater were 14.2 m³/s (winter), 22.7 m³/s (spring and summer), and 8.5 m³/s for drought.
- Carp, suckers, and gizzard shad are common in warmwater tailwaters; carp are often dominant.
- Walleye and sauger recruitment depends on stable water levels; reduced fluctuations greatly increased sauger year-class strength in one South Dakota tailwater (about 15-fold).
- Warm epilimnetic releases may favour warmwater species such as carp and sunfish over trout.

**6. Water quality thresholds cited.**
- Iron: 1.00 mg/l acceptable for freshwater aquatic life (US EPA 1976).
- Hydrogen sulfide above 0.002 mg/l has objectionable odour; a 96-hour LC₅₀ of 0.045 mg/l was reported for adult bluegill.
- Ammonia concentrations of 0.5–2.0 mg/l are lethal to fish after short-term exposure.
- Fish generally prefer oxygen above 5 mg/l.

**7. Research gaps and recommendations.**
- Most tailwater research has been narrow, typically compiling species lists or creel census data; causal links between specific physical or chemical changes and biotic responses are rarely established.
- The report recommends longitudinal studies combining continuous monitoring of temperature, flow, oxygen, and nutrients with periodic sampling of periphyton, plankton, benthos, and fish, analysed statistically.
- It advocates developing conceptual models for each major tailwater type to predict effects of management changes without large-scale intensive studies.
