# Distribution, Status, Biology, and Conservation of the Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) in New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Endangered Species Report (No. 15, 1986), prepared by David L. Propst, Kevin R. Bestgen and Charles W. Painter for the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish, documents the historic and current distribution, status, life history and microhabitat use of the [[Spikedace]], a small cyprinid minnow endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of New Mexico and Arizona. Surveys from 1982 to 1985 found the species restricted to a few reaches in New Mexico, with the [[Cliff-Gila Valley]] population the largest and most important for its survival. Habitat modification, water diversion and the spread of [[Non-Native Fishes]] are identified as the principal causes of decline.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and range:** The spikedace belongs to the tribe Meda-related minnows, an assemblage restricted to the lower Colorado River basin. Historically it occurred in the Gila, Salt, Verde, San Pedro and Agua Fria systems in Arizona and in the upper Gila and San Francisco drainages of New Mexico.
- **Preferred habitat:** Low-to-moderate gradient, intermittent to perennial streams with sand, gravel and cobble substrates and slow to moderate velocities. In autumn, spikedace shift into slow-velocity water and remain there through winter.
- **Study background:** The work was initiated in 1982 by the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish under the Nongame Wildlife program and later under federal Endangered Species Act funding (Project E-3, "Survey and Management of the Spikedace and Loach Minnow"). The species was first documented in New Mexico in 1972 and had been the subject of a single M.S. thesis (Anderson 1978).
- **Specimen basis:** Historic records were drawn from the Fish Data Base compiled by the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish; specimens are curated at the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish Laboratory in Santa Fe and at numerous museums, including [[University of New Mexico]] and [[Arizona State University]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Natural flow regime.** Flows in the Gila system fluctuate seasonally with snowmelt pulses in spring and summer floods from monsoon rains. Spikedace evolved strategies to persist through these cycles.

**Principal threats.**
- *Land and water use:* Agricultural settlement, irrigation diversions, channelization, groundwater pumping, overgrazing and timber cutting lowered water tables, desiccated reaches and increased sediment loads.
- *Dams:* Altered flow timing and magnitude, disrupted nutrient transport and eliminated the cleansing action of floods.
- *Water quality:* Toxic mine tailings, industrial discharges and municipal effluents.
- *Non-native fishes:* Predation (notably by [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]]) and competition (notably from the [[Red Shiner]]), often acting together with habitat degradation.

**Associated fauna.** Native companions included the [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Sonoran Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]] and, historically, [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Table 1 lists roughly 60 fish taxa referred to in the text, of which seven native and 15 non-native species were collected in the wider survey.

**Study sites.** Four main areas were monitored: the East Fork and West Fork headwaters (including the Forks area), the Cliff-Gila Valley, the Redrock and Virden valleys, and the Middle Box/Conner area. Permanent sites included the Cliff Dwellings, Middle Fork Trailhead, Riverside and Conner sites.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status**
- The spikedace was presumably distributed along the San Francisco River from the Arizona border upstream to Reserve, and in the Gila River from the Middle Box to the East and West Forks.
- The San Francisco River population appears extirpated in New Mexico; the last New Mexico records date to 1949–1950, and recent surveys in Arizona also failed to locate the species.
- In the East Fork, the species declined from 14.8% relative abundance pre-1960 to a much smaller share by 1961–1980. Floods in 1983 and 1984 were followed by a resurgence of native numbers, with spikedace reappearing in 1985.
- The [[Cliff-Gila Valley]] population was the largest in New Mexico and the key to species survival. The Redrock/Virden valley population was apparently not self-sustaining, and the Conner population was small and declining by late 1984.

**Microhabitat use**
- Larvae (<25 mm TL) were associated with slow-velocity water near stream margins, mostly over sand (60%) and in depths under 32 cm.
- Juveniles (26–35 mm TL) used a wider velocity range; adults (>36 mm TL) occupied velocities of 0.0–74.7 cm/s (mean 29.6 cm/s) and depths averaging 19.5 cm.
- Forks-area adults used velocities below 29 cm/s; Cliff-Gila Valley adults preferred 29–59.5 cm/s. The authors attribute the difference largely to habitat availability rather than intrinsic preference.
- Seasonal shifts in the Cliff-Gila Valley involved movement to slower velocities in the cold season, while Forks-area shifts involved depth changes.

**Reproduction and life history**
- Spawning occurred from about April into May or early June in 1984, in shallow riffles over gravel and cobble; eggs were probably demersal.
- Age I females carried roughly 100–200 mature ova per mode, and Age II females up to about 300–350 in total counts.
- The species rarely lived beyond 24 months; most individuals lived no more than 18 months. Age 0 fish averaged 38 mm SL at the end of their first summer and Age I fish 50 mm SL at the end of their second. Maximum recorded size was about 58 mm SL.
- Diet consisted mainly of mayfly (Ephemeroptera) larvae and other aquatic invertebrates; the authors state drift-fed invertebrates were important.

**Flood and drought responses**
- The October 1983 flood increased spikedace relative abundance at the Cottonwood site (from 11.0% to a dominant share after the flood) and at other sites, partly through displacement from upstream habitat.
- Non-native fishes, which tended to occupy isolated pools after floods, were more susceptible to displacement than natives.
- Levee construction at the Indian Ruins site confined flows, deepened the channel and eliminated habitat for native fishes, showing human-induced modification can outweigh flood effects.

**Conservation conclusions**
- The species is most secure in the Cliff-Gila Valley; its maintenance there is considered critical to the survival of the species in New Mexico.
- Non-native predators and competitors, especially the red shiner, have displaced or prevented recovery of native populations in the Redrock and Virden valleys.
- Recommended priorities included controlling non-native fishes, protecting natural flow regimes and securing habitat in the Cliff-Gila Valley.
