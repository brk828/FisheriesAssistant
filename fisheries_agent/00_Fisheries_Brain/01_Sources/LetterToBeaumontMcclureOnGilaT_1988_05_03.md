## Executive Summary

This document is a 1988 administrative letter from the Arizona Game & Fish Department (Deputy Director Duane L. Shroufe) to Beaumont McClure of the Bureau of Land Management, updating him on the regulatory status of reintroduction efforts for two federally listed imperiled fish species: the [[Gila Topminnow]] and the [[Desert Pupfish]]. The letter is not a primary research paper but a policy/status update summarizing communications with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) Office of Endangered Species regarding an upcoming Federal Register notice. The notice would propose designating reintroduced populations of both species as "experimental nonessential populations" under Section 10(j) of the Endangered Species Act (1973 et seq.), a mechanism intended to facilitate reintroductions onto public and private lands within the species' historic range with reduced regulatory constraints.

## Background & Description

No morphological, meristic, or anatomical data are presented in this source. The document is purely administrative/regulatory in nature and does not describe body form, coloration, fin counts, or other taxonomic characteristics of either the [[Gila Topminnow]] or [[Desert Pupfish]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat context**: Both species are native to isolated desert aquatic habitats (springs, small streams, and riparian systems) within the Gila River drainage and broader Sonoran Desert region of the southwestern United States.
- **Historic range**: The letter explicitly references reintroduction efforts targeting the species' "historic range," implying substantial range contraction from historic distribution — a hallmark of both species' listing under the Endangered Species Act.
- **Land management context**: Reintroduction sites span both public lands (U.S. Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management) and private lands, reflecting the fragmented, multi-jurisdictional nature of remaining/potential habitat for these species.
- **Conservation mechanism**: Discussion centers on [[Section 10j Experimental Nonessential Populations]], a regulatory ecological tool allowing reintroduced populations to be managed with more flexibility (e.g., reduced take prohibitions) to encourage landowner and agency cooperation in recovery efforts.

## Key Findings

- As of May 1988, the USFWS Albuquerque office (via Dr. James E. Johnson, Office of Endangered Species, and assistant Jerry Burton) was preparing to forward a Federal Register entry to Washington D.C. addressing reintroduction of both the [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]].
- The proposed action would designate reintroduced populations as "experimental nonessential" under **Section 10(j)** of the Endangered Species Act, seeking public and agency comment on reintroductions to both public and private lands across the species' historic range.
- Agency policy prevented USFWS staff from releasing draft materials prior to formal Federal Register publication, limiting the Arizona Game & Fish Department's ability to plan ahead.
- Estimated timeline (as of 1988): 6–9 months until Federal Register publication, followed by a mandatory 90-day public comment period, and additional time for final determination — totaling an estimated **1 to 2 years** before concrete reintroduction decisions could proceed.
- This regulatory step was described as the "next step in recovery efforts," following an **aggressive reintroduction program on U.S. Forest Service lands** initiated approximately 7 years prior (circa 1981), indicating a long-term, multi-decade recovery effort for these two species.
- Key personnel involved: Duane L. Shroufe (AZGFD Deputy Director), Terry B. Johnson (AZGFD Endangered Species Coordinator), Dr. James E. Johnson (USFWS, Office of Endangered Species), Jerry Burton (USFWS), and Sotero Muniz (USFS) — indicating an interagency coordination effort spanning state, federal, and land management agencies.
- The letter underscores the protracted regulatory timeline characteristic of Endangered Species Act reintroduction processes, even for species with established, multi-year recovery programs already underway.
