# Primary Production in Inland Waters

#limnology #marine-biology #primary-production #phytobenthos #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This 1958 review by J. W. G. [[Lund]] of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] examines [[Primary Production]] in British inland waters. It argues that attached plants ([[Phytobenthos]]) may outproduce [[Phytoplankton]] in many shallow waters, reed beds and rivers, and that standing-crop measurements are an unreliable index of production. The review identifies light, temperature, nutrients, depth and flow as the main controls, and concludes that British freshwater production is too limited for commercial exploitation, while the water industry should monitor biology to manage fertile supplies.

## Background & Description

The text reprints a chapter from *The Biological Productivity of Britain* (Institute of Biology, 1958). Primary production is defined broadly as the amount of plant material produced in a given volume of water per unit time. Common measurement approaches include:

- Oxygen production during [[Photosynthesis]]
- Uptake of carbon dioxide or bicarbonate
- Uptake of carbon-14 (C¹⁴), which allows measurement on small natural populations with high accuracy

Historically, [[Standing Crop]] served as an index of production, but estimating true production requires knowledge of multiplication rates, which were often guessed. The author notes that the only estimates for British waters at the time came from [[Odum]] (1956), based on diurnal oxygen curves at single stations on the rivers [[River Itchen]] and [[River Lark]] (Butcher, Pentelow and Woodley, 1930).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food webs.** Benthic plants are the only primary producers in most British rivers, apart from the larger or more sluggish ones. Even in plankton-rich lakes, phytobenthos may be important for fish food chains. Large freshwater algae are not extensively grazed by zooplankton. The first link is more a web than a chain: bacteria use decomposing algae in open water, or scavenging invertebrates ingest live or dead algae on the bottom. Primary production being large does not guarantee large secondary production.

**Seasonal dynamics.** Algal crops are generally smaller in winter than summer, mainly because low light makes respiration exceed photosynthesis. Losses to sediments, outflow, parasitism and grazing (for non-planktonic algae) also reduce numbers. Diatoms can increase in February and early March even when water is coldest, and can grow under ice if it is free of snow. Light and temperature jointly control photosynthesis: at low light intensity the rate is directly proportional to light, while at high intensity it depends on temperature.

**Stratification and depth.** Deep British lakes are thermally stratified in summer. The lower zone is too dimly lit for photosynthesis, so only the upper zone yields net production. The upper layer of Scottish lakes in summer is three or four Celsius degrees cooler than that of English lakes.

**Hydrology.** Higher rainfall and run-off in northern and western Britain replace lake water more quickly, which can replenish nutrients but also flush out algae. Rapid replacement can prevent plankton development, as in most British rivers. Only the [[River Thames]] is known to have a well-developed phytoplankton above its estuarine portion. Rain-driven flow also tears attached algae from substrata, and stones and boulders can scour or bury them.

**Nutrients and geology.** Northern and western British waters are poor in algal numbers (though not in species), owing to mountainous terrain, non-calcareous rocks, thin soils, little agriculture and small settlements. Their waters carry less calcium, nitrogen and phosphorus. In a chain of lakes or reservoirs, the lowest is likely to be richest, and this effect is almost universal in rivers. Human activity enhances natural fertility through intensive farming, leaching and effluent from modern sewage treatment, which is rich in phosphorus. Poorly treated sewage, however, can reduce algal growth through organic pollution or toxic inorganic substances.

**Nutrient limitation.** Nitrogen and phosphorus are probably the chief limiting substances, as is established for marine plankton, but freshwater algal chemistry in nature is poorly understood. A direct quantitative link between calcium, phosphorus or nitrogen concentrations and crop size is doubtful.

**Applied context.** Fish farming in China has been practised for well over a thousand years, using organic and inorganic fertilizers. Algal production there is often so high that light penetration limits further production. The author judges that British freshwater fish production could amount to only a minute fraction of the sea harvest, even if climatic and consumer barriers were overcome. Water undertakings and some industries generally seek to suppress production. The [[Metropolitan Water Board]] is cited as an example of an authority making regular biological observations.

## Key Findings

- **Attached plants may dominate production.** In shallow waters, reed beds and most rivers, phytobenthos can produce far more than plankton and is important for fish food chains.
- **Standing crop is a misleading index.** A bed of [[Chara]] or [[Littorella]] may hold more than a year's production, compared with two to three months for a maximal plankton crop.
- **Seasonal timing matters.** Rodhe (1958) found that as much as one-third of the total annual carbon assimilation occurred in April and May.
- **Light, temperature and flow set the limits.** Winter light deficits, summer stratification and high-flow flushing constrain production, and nutrients are rarely fully used.
- **Geology and land use drive fertility.** Calcareous, agricultural and sewage-enriched catchments produce richer waters than upland non-calcareous ones.
- **Limited economic case in Britain.** Commercial freshwater algal or fish production is judged unlikely to be economic in Britain. The author sees more value in monitoring reservoirs, with biological observation recommended for the water industry, since "forewarned is forearmed."
- **Knowledge gaps remain.** Few British production estimates exist, freshwater algal chemistry is poorly known, and the role of small nannoplankton in food chains needs more study.
