# Razorback Sucker Larval Collection Protocol for Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This technical protocol, prepared for the Native Fish Work Group (dated 01 February 1994), sets out procedures for capturing wild razorback sucker larvae from [[Lake Mohave]] on the Arizona–Nevada border and rearing them for stocking. The program addresses a population decline in an endangered species. Earlier sourcing approaches (hatchery spawning, on-site backwater rearing, and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] production) had produced limited and unpredictable results. The document argues that direct harvest of wild larvae best preserves the genetic diversity of the broodstock, and it retains other methods as backups.

## Background & Description

The document describes the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) as a species in decline in Lake Mohave, with the adult population described as small and non-native fish representing a substantial share of the reservoir community. A multi-agency Native Fish Work Group oversees recovery efforts. Stocking sizes are noted as roughly 23–300 mm total length in some sourcing contexts, though the text is partly garbled by OCR.

Larval sources include:
- Natural spawning in isolated backwaters
- Manual spawning and artificial propagation at DNFH
- Direct collection from the lake

Numbers from each source were described as unpredictably variable. Grow-out has been tried in isolated backwaters and in self-contained fish farms, but unknown and unpredictable mortality and differing sampling efficiency among grow-out habitats limited recovery of fish for release.

Research by [[Arizona State University]] researchers found that previous acquisition approaches may have compromised genetic diversity and variability of the broodstock. The remaining population is genetically diverse, and that diversity cannot be replaced from a small hatchery or on-site gene pool.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Adult razorback suckers aggregate and spawn at or near several known locations on the reservoir, including [[Arizona Bay]], Nine-Mile Area, Cottonwood Cove, Willow Point, and [[Eldorado Canyon]] (see Table 1 in the source). They also use other areas. Larvae are most abundant in Arizona Bay and Hammerhead Cove, while collections at other sites have been unproductive recently.

Key ecological behaviour:
- Larvae are positively phototactic and are readily attracted to white light at night.
- Collection is most productive between approximately dusk and dawn (about 1600–2400 hrs as given in the source; the text reads "2400" and the dawn-end hour is partly garbled).
- Reconnaissance sampling should begin in mid-January and be repeated at frequent intervals until larval presence and concentration are confirmed.

Candidate grow-out sites (Table 3 and Table 4) include [[Davis Cove]], [[Sidewinder Cove]], [[Nine Mile Cove]], [[Willow Cove]], [[Chemehuevi Cove]], and [[Yuma Cove]]. Table 4 lists bottom and berm elevations, surface area, and estimated carrying capacity for seven candidate sites. Carrying capacity estimates were based on a stocking rate for razorback sucker larvae.

## Key Findings

**Collection protocol**
- Collections require a minimum of two persons.
- Equipment includes 12 VDC lamps (12 to 55 W) on a cord, a 12 VDC battery power source, fine-mesh dip nets, white buckets or dish pans, data sheets, and personal and support gear (see Table 2).
- Larvae are captured with dip nets while the lamp is used to attract them. Care is taken to avoid injuring larvae, and captured larvae are transferred to holding containers.

**Holding and transport**
- Lake-collected larvae are placed in labeled aquaria at a temporary holding facility (a shed behind the maintenance shop at the Cottonwood Landing station, Nevada).
- Larvae are then moved as quickly as possible to a U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (USBR) or Recreation facility in Boulder City, Nevada, where they are held for approximately 2–3 weeks at constant temperature and fed live brine shrimp (*Artemia*) or a suitable prepared mixture.

**Records**
- Participants must keep an accurate running count of all larvae captured.
- Each collection record should include date, collector, location, collection effort (start and stop times and duration), surface area, depth, number of larvae captured, equipment description, weather, water conditions, and miscellaneous observations.
- A separate log is required whenever larvae are transferred to holding facilities or grow-out sites.

**Program conclusions**
- Direct harvest of wild larvae is the preferred method for maintaining genetic diversity.
- Other sourcing methods (natural spawning, manual spawning, DNFH production) should be retained as backups, not abandoned.
- Grow-out capacity estimates and proportional larval distribution among designated sites are planned for the 1994 season, with supplemental facilities (local fish farms, DNFH, and other hatcheries) used as needed.
