# The Curious Behavior of the Stickleback

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gasterosteus-aculeatus

## Executive Summary

This reprinted essay by [[Niko Tinbergen]] (December 1952) summarizes two decades of experimental work on the three-spined stickleback (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) as a model for [[Innate Behavior]]. The author chose the fish for a University of Leiden animal behavior course because it was abundant, tame, hardy and easy to keep, and it went on to become the focus of a sustained research program with [[Jan Van Iersel]]. The essay describes the stickleback's stereotyped [[Courtship Behavior]], [[Nest Building]] and [[Parental Care]], and shows that its responses depend on [[Sign Stimuli]] rather than whole objects. It also introduces [[Displacement Activity]] as an explanation for seemingly irrelevant actions such as digging during fights and fanning during courtship. The author argues that studying a simple animal in depth reveals general principles that comparative work on mammals and humans can build on.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The three-spined stickleback (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) is described as a small, tame fish that depends on its spines for protection and tolerates handling well.
- **Why it was chosen:** The author, then a young lecturer in zoology at the University of Leiden, recalled catching sticklebacks as a boy in ditches near home and keeping them in a backyard aquarium. Sticklebacks could be collected in numbers from almost any ditch and thrived in a tank "no larger than a hatbox." In a comparison with other experimental animals (newts, bees, water insects and birds), the author judged it the most reliable subject.
- **Research scale:** The laboratory used hundreds of sticklebacks each year. The author notes that the species was also a popular subject at the universities of Groningen and Oxford. The author compares its role in European ethology to that of the rat for American psychologists.
- **Breeding biology:** Sticklebacks mate in early spring in shallow fresh water. Males establish territories, build nests, change color, court females and care for the brood. Females carry 50 to 100 large eggs when ready to spawn.
- **Male coloration:** The normally gray fish shows a faint pink chin and greenish gloss before nest completion. During breeding, the chin turns bright red and the back a bluish white. Photo captions describe the underside as bright vermilion and the eyes as blue in full sexual markings.

### Mating sequence

1. **Territory:** A male leaves the school and defends a territory against any intruder, male or female.
2. **Nest:** He digs a pit about two inches square, piles on thread algae coated with a sticky kidney secretion, and shapes the mass into a mound. He then bores a tunnel through it. The tunnel is slightly shorter than an adult fish.
3. **Courtship:** The male performs a zigzag dance to lead a female to the nest. She follows with her head up.
4. **Spawning:** At the entrance he thrusts with his snout and raises his dorsal spines. The female enters, and he prods her tail base until she lays eggs. The whole courtship and egg-laying ritual takes only about one minute.
5. **Fertilization:** He glides in to fertilize the clutch, then drives the female away and seeks another partner. One male may escort three, four or even five females in turn.
6. **Parental care:** He guards the nest, fans water over the eggs with his breast fins, and keeps the young together for a day or so after hatching by retrieving stragglers in his mouth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document places the stickleback in shallow fresh waters, ditches and aquaria. It describes the species' breeding season (early spring), territorial and nesting behavior, and reliance on sand bottoms and weed for nest materials. The essay does not present quantitative data on water chemistry, habitat extent, population status or other ecosystem components. Its ecological framing is mainly behavioral: the fish's responses are shaped by seasonal breeding condition, and its egg-fanning rate tracks the developmental needs of eggs.

The author stresses comparative context. He argues that mammals are an unusual group specializing in plastic behavior, while the simpler, more rigid behavior of fish like the stickleback is closer to the rule across the animal kingdom. He also suggests that mammals retain an innate base beneath their plastic behavior, which is why a low vertebrate may illuminate broader patterns.

## Key Findings

### 1. Red color and sign stimuli drive aggression
- Males attacked red-colored intruders more aggressively than fish of other colors. A red mail van at about 100 yards caused males in the tank to charge the glass.
- Painted models (red, pale silver, green) on wires showed that red models were always more provoking, though silver and green intruders also caused some hostility.
- Responses depended on specific "sign stimuli" rather than the whole object. A female followed a red model and tried to enter a non-existent nest, and she could be induced to spawn by prodding her tail base with a glass rod after the red fish was removed.
- The author concludes that the signal, not the object, determines the response, a pattern he sees as typical of innate behavior and of social systems based on signs.

### 2. Breeding condition controls responsiveness
- Sticklebacks responded to experimental stimuli only during breeding condition. At other seasons they ignored the signs.
- Parental drive was measured as the number of seconds spent fanning per time unit, and the drives follow cycles (fight, nest, court, brood).
- A male will not build until it has defended its territory for a while, and will not court until the nest is finished. Females approaching before completion are driven off or greeted with a few zigzags.
- Within cycles, a fixed sequence persists: a filled-in pit is re-dug before nest material is collected. After several refills, the male builds a nest without completing the pit, showing that inner drive can override outside interference.

### 3. Hormones likely regulate drive cycles (preliminary)
- The author suspects that rising and falling drives reflect hormonal changes and reports ongoing studies of castration and hormone treatment.
- One finding so far: castration abolishes the first phases of mating but has no effect on parental drive. A castrated male given a nest of eggs ventilates it "with abandon."

### 4. Displacement activity explains "irrelevant" behavior
- Two males at a territorial border perform a seesaw of attacks and retreats without touching, showing that attack and retreat tendencies are aroused simultaneously.
- In crowded conditions with small territories, fights shift into a head-down posture and both fish dig into the sand as if building a nest. The author interprets this as a [[Displacement Activity]]: unable to attack or escape, the fish releases tension in an unrelated action.
- The author cites parallels in other animals: fighting starlings preen, roosters peck at the ground, wading birds assume a sleeping posture, and humans scratch behind the ear under stress.

### 5. Experimental test of displacement
- A red model placed in a male's territory was struck by the researchers, causing the fish to flee and hide in weeds. Its attack drive then rose, and after a few minutes it approached cautiously. When attack and retreat were balanced, it adopted the head-down posture.

### 6. Dual drives in courtship
- The zigzag dance combines a sexual leading movement (away from the female) and an incipient attack (toward her).
- When sex drive is strong (willingness to lead a standard female model), the "zig" component is pronounced and may become complete leading. When fighting drive is strong (bites aimed at a standard male model), the "zag" is emphatic and may become a straightforward attack.
- Because every entering female provides sign stimuli for both aggression and sexuality, even a large female evokes hostility as well as courtship.

### 7. Fanning as displacement during spawning
- The snout thrusts at the nest entrance resemble fanning, which is irrelevant at that moment. The author interprets this as a displacement activity because the male cannot release his sex drive until the female lays eggs.
- The quivering motion used to prod the female is similar to fanning and stops when eggs are laid. Hostility toward a strange fish near the nest also frustrates the sex drive, as seen when the male raises his dorsal spines while showing the nest.

### 8. Fanning time is shaped by internal and external factors
- Fanning time per 30 minutes rises from the first day after laying through about the eighth day, then stops by the tenth day.
- When eggs were removed on the sixth day and replaced with a fresh batch, fanning began anew, but fanning time on day six remained longer than on day one. The author concludes that fanning is controlled by both internal and external factors.

### 9. Value of long-term work on one species
- The author argues that studying a single species for a long time is necessary to understand its full behavior pattern. The aggressive component of courtship, for example, could be detected only by studying fighting and courtship together.
- Stickleback behavior is "much more purely innate and much more rigid" than that of mammals such as the rat, which makes some phenomena clearer, including sign stimuli, specific motivation, and displacement activities.
- The author suggests the study may bear on human conflicts and neuroses, and notes the role of hostility in courtship in birds and other species.

### 10. Author background
- [[Niko Tinbergen]] was lecturer in animal behavior at the University of Oxford at the time of writing. He had worked with [[Konrad Lorenz]] since 1937 and had earlier lectured at Leiden. He cites his book *The Herring Gull's World* to defend the value of studying wild animals even without immediate practical use.
