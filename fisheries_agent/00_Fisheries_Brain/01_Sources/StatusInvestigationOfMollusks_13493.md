# Status Investigation of New Mexico Freshwater Mollusks

#malacology #freshwater-biology #Hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This Department of Game and Fish final report, prepared under the [[Endangered Species Project]] (Project No. E-1-3, Job No. 1, contract period January 15, 1981 to June 30, 1983, amended), assesses the conservation status of New Mexico's living freshwater mollusks. The work was carried out largely under a professional service contract with [[Dwight W. Taylor]] of San Francisco State University, using literature, museum specimens, and fieldwork in April–May and September–October 1981.

Key results:
- **51 species** in **19 families** and **two classes** (Gastropoda and Pelecypoda) were recorded in New Mexico. **48 are native** and **3 are introduced**.
- **4 species** appear extirpated and **1** may be extinct.
- **29 of the 48 native species** are considered rare, local, or otherwise vulnerable and are potentially endangered or threatened.
- After Department review (February 15 and June 6, 1983), **6** species were recommended for state listing as Endangered (Group I) and **14** as Endangered (Group II), for a total of **20**.
- Of these 20, **6** are suggested as candidates for federal listing.
- At least **10 undescribed species and one undescribed genus** were identified.

The report concludes that the findings are an excellent baseline, that more survey work is needed, that undescribed taxa should be published promptly, and that state and federal listing should proceed. It states that three-quarters of New Mexico's native freshwater mollusks are endangered, extirpated, or extinct.

## Background & Description

### Objective and Rationale
The objective was to determine and report the status of freshwater mollusks that may be endangered or threatened in New Mexico and to recommend conservation measures. Mollusks fall under the [[Wildlife Conservation Act]] (Chapter 17-2-32 through 17-2-46, NMSA 1973), which directs the [[New Mexico Department Of Game And Fish]] to conserve them. The contract also drew on the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973 and amendments). Growing threats to aquatic habitat in New Mexico added urgency.

### Procedures
The combined contracts (No. 519-83-01, January 15, 1981 to June 30, 1982, and No. 519-83-01-A, June 1, 1981 to June 30, 1982; the final report deadline was verbally extended to early 1983) required:
1. A list of New Mexico freshwater mollusks, including potential federal and/or state listing candidates.
2. Descriptions of undescribed candidate species for publication.
3. Information relevant to conservation.
4. A final report by candidate species covering names, range, relative abundance, habitat requirements, threats, management recommendations, description and taxonomy, illustrations, and other relevant information.

Fieldwork covered 14 counties, including [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]], the [[Pecos River]], the [[Gila River]], the [[Rio Grande]], the [[San Juan River]], the [[Conchas River]], Nambe Lake, Santa Fe Lake, and Socorro springs. Specimens were consulted from the Academy of Natural Science (Philadelphia), California Academy of Science, University of Colorado (Boulder), University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, University of Texas (El Paso), and the United States National Museum of Natural History. Department personnel assisted to a limited extent.

### Taxonomic Background
The freshwater fauna has been studied since the 19th century. The appendix describes the species accounts, the hot-spring and spring-snail genera (Fontelicella and Tryonia), the pulmonate snails, and the freshwater clams (Unionidae and Sphaeriidae). Taxonomic uncertainty is noted for several genera, including Musculium, Planorbella, and Gyraulus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Endemism and isolation:** New Mexico has 48 native species, and 11 are endemic (23%). Colorado, for comparison, has 45 species and no endemics. The report links the endemism to long isolation and continuity of localized waters. Most species began differentiating one to five or more million years ago. Some are Pleistocene relicts from cooler, wetter times.
- **Biogeography:** Of the native fauna, 14 are more northern and 4 are more southern in distribution. Many threatened species are northern or eastern forms at their southern or western limits. The Great Plains and the [[Pecos River]] form biogeographic barriers. The Pecos is the eastern range limit for Fontelicella and Tryonia.
- **Habitat specialization:**
  - *Fontelicella* is a rheocrene spring snail found in watercress-covered, non-thermal springs with low dissolved solids. It is usually found in springs too small to support fish.
  - *Tryonia* is a limnocrene spring snail found in mildly thermal springs with alkaline efflorescence. It is often associated with localized pupfish (*Cyprinodon*).
  - *Assiminea* is semi-aquatic and lives among sedges along spring margins, with a vestigial gill and possible lung-like mantle cavity.
  - Seasonal-water species are generally widespread opportunistic colonizers. Those at range margins are the most vulnerable.
- **Clams:** Unionid mussels depend on fish hosts for their glochidial larval stage. The fish hosts of *Cyrtonaias berlandieri* and *Popenaias popei* are unknown. Sphaeriid clams (*Pisidium*, *Musculium*, *Sphaerium*) are filter-feeders with sexes united in one individual.
- **Threats:** Groundwater pumping and drawdown, impoundment, spring capping, pesticides, overgrazing, sewage and mining wastes, channel entrenchment, drainage, real estate development, flood scour, and burning of vegetation.

## Key Findings

### Species Inventory
- **Total:** 51 species, 48 native, 3 introduced (including *Radix auricularia*, *Planorbella duryi*, and *Corbicula fluminea*).
- **Extirpated (X):** 4 species (*Promenetus exacuous*, *Cyrtonaias berlandieri*, *Uniomerus tetralasmus*, *Pisidium pauperculum*). *Popenaias popei* and *Cyrtonaias berlandieri* were also noted as likely extinct in the Pecos River. Note that the species list marks *Cyrtonaias berlandieri* with X, while the text places it among those "largely a Texas species." Both statements are reported here as given.
- **Possibly extinct (Z):** 1 (*Physa* sp. a).
- **Potentially endangered or threatened:** 29 of 48 native species (16 peripheral, 13 narrowly distributed). Among the 13 narrow-range species, 11 are endemic.

### Undescribed Taxa
- **Ten** undescribed species and **one** new genus were identified: one *Assiminea*, five *Fontelicella*, two *Tryonia*, one new Planorbidae genus and species, one *Physa*, and one *Pisidium*.
- Dr. Taylor was preparing descriptions for publication.

### Recommendations for Listing
- **Group I (state Endangered, 6 species):** *Assiminea* sp., *Fontelicella neomexicana*, *Fontelicella* sp. (undescribed), *Anodonta imbecillis*, *Popenaias popei*, *Musculium transversum*. (Some numbering in the source is garbled. The list is reproduced as recovered.)
- **Group II (state Endangered, 14 species):** *Fontelicella* sp. (a, b, c, e), *Lymnaea caperata*, *Tryonia* sp. (a, b), *Gyraulus crista*, Planorbidae sp., *Musculium raymondi*, *Pisidium lilljeborgi*, *Pisidium partumeium*, *Pisidium ventricosum*, *Pisidium* sp. (a).
- **Federal candidates (6):**
  1. *Assiminea* sp. (possibly endangered)
  2. *Fontelicella neomexicana* (possibly endangered)
  3. *Fontelicella* sp. (a, undescribed; possibly threatened)
  4. *Fontelicella* sp. (d, undescribed; possibly threatened)
  5. *Tryonia* sp. (a, undescribed; possibly threatened)
  6. Planorbidae sp. (genus and species undescribed)

### Key Species Accounts (Status Data as Reported)
- **Pecos Assiminea (*Assiminea* sp.):** One remaining population, probably no more than 5000 individuals, in an area under 200 square feet at Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge. The main threat is burning of vegetation. It lives further from the sea than any other *Assiminea*.
- **Socorro Spring Snail (*Fontelicella neomexicana*):** Known from Socorro (now extinct) and Torreon Springs. One free-running spring remains, with an estimated fewer than 5000 individuals. Impoundment eliminated the principal flowing-water habitat. The report calls the situation "extremely precarious."
- **Chupadera Spring Snail (*Fontelicella* a):** One population at Willow Spring, Socorro County, on private land. The species is stable but potentially endangered, and the snails are abundant at the spring sources.
- **Gila Spring Snail (*Fontelicella* b):** Two springs in the Gila River drainage, Grant County. The cool-spring population numbers several tens of thousands, and the warm-spring population may number only a few hundred.
- **Pecos Spring Snail (*Fontelicella* c):** Blue Spring has hundreds of thousands of individuals and is stable. Castle Spring is much smaller.
- **New Mexico Hot Spring Snail (*Fontelicella* e):** Two hot springs in the Gila Wilderness, with populations reaching several hundred thousand. The species lives on steep rock faces in water up to 38 °C. It is protected within the wilderness area.
- **Roswell Spring Snail (*Fontelicella* d):** Populations at Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge are stable. The Roswell Country Club population is marginal and possibly extinct.
- **Alamosa Spring Snail (*Tryonia* a):** Two abundant populations near former Fort Harmony (Ojo Caliente and an improved source). Females are about twice the size of males. Threats include capping, impoundment, and pumping.
- **Koster's Spring Snail (*Tryonia* b):** Four large populations on Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge and one marginal population at Roswell Country Club.
- **Say's Pond Snail (*Lymnaea caperata*):** Two populations, one in Valle Grande (Jemez Mountains, Sandoval County) and one at Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge. Both are small (fewer than a few thousand each). The Bitter Lake population is a Pleistocene relict and vulnerable to burning.
- **Paper-Shell Mussel (*Anodonta imbecillis*):** One marginal population, represented by rare fresh shells in Conchas Reservoir, San Miguel County. The Conchas River population is considered extinct.
- **Berlandier's Mussel (*Cyrtonaias berlandieri*):** Known from a fragmentary valve below Carlsbad. A living population is unlikely.
- **Pope's Mussel (*Popenaias popei*):** The largest mollusk in the state (up to 100 mm). Life span is estimated at about 10 years. Recent living individuals have not been collected. The report describes its prognosis as poor.
- **Pond-Horn Mussel (*Uniomerus tetralasmus*):** Extinct in New Mexico (listed as extirpated). Known from archaeological sites. It is the most drought-resistant mussel in North America and can survive months buried in dry mud.
- **Striate Pea-Clam (*Sphaerium striatinum*):** Four narrowly localized populations (Rio San Jose, upper Conchas River, upper Dry Cimarron River, and North Canadian River).
- **Raymond's Pea-Clam (*Musculium raymondi*):** A single population in the Sangre de Cristo Mountains, Colfax County, at the southern limit of its range.
- **Circular Pea-Clam (*Musculium partumeium*):** One population in a tributary of the Dry Cimarron River, Union County.
- **Truncate Pea-Clam (*Musculium truncatum*):** One population at Clayton Lake, Union County.
- **Wide Pea-Clam (*Musculium transversum*):** Formerly abundant in the lower Pecos River; now extinct there. Two surviving populations (Conchas River, marginal, and a Dry Cimarron tributary, small).
- **Lillejeborg's Pea-Clam (*Pisidium lilljeborgi*):** One population at Nambe Lake, Santa Fe County, at 11,365 ft (3465 m). This is reported as the southernmost and highest known locality in North America or Eurasia. The species is stable.
- **Held's Pea-Clam (*Pisidium milium*):** Four populations in New Mexico, three in the San Juan Mountains and one in the southern Sangre de Cristo Mountains.
- **Wide-Hinged Pea-Clam (*Pisidium pauperculum*):** Extinct in New Mexico after groundwater development at Ojo del Gallo, San Rafael.
- **Singley's Pea-Clam (*Pisidium singleyi*):** Three populations (Blue Spring and Geyser Spring, Eddy County, and a spring near former Fort Harmony, Socorro County). A southern species at its northern limit.
- **Globular Pea-Clam (*Pisidium ventricosum*):** Two populations, Nambe Lake and Santa Fe Lake. Rare at both: only four specimens out of thousands in Nambe Lake, and two out of hundreds in Santa Fe Lake.
- **Sangre de Cristo Pea-Clam (*Pisidium* a, undescribed):** Known only from Middle Fork Lake, 10,845 ft, Taos County. It is common there. The report calls it the most narrowly localized *Pisidium* in North America.
- **Ferrissia rivularis (Say's River Limpet):** One population in the Rio Grande canyon above Velarde (Rio Arriba and Taos Counties), probably extending the whole length of the river in the canyon.
- **Physa* a (Ashmun's Physa):** Two populations were known. Ojo del Gallo is extinct and the Block Ranch (near Bland) population has not been relocated. The species may be extinct.

### Report Discrepancies Noted
- The abstract reports 29 native species and then "Of these 20 species." The Findings count 29 vulnerable native species. The Appendix introduction states that 30 species are considered endangered, potentially endangered, or of special concern.
- The Analysis text lists 20 species total (six Group I and 14 Group II), while the Group I list in the source is partly garbled in scanning.
- The Appendix states eleven endemic species, and the Findings also refer to 11 endemics among 13 narrowly distributed species.

These discrepancies are reported as they appear in the source document.
