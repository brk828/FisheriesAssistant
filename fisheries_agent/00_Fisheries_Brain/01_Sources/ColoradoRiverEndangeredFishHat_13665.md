# Colorado River Endangered Fish Hatchery Feasibility Study

#ichthyology #endangered-species #hatchery #conservation #cyprinidae #colorado-river #feasibility-study

## Executive Summary

This September 1983 feasibility study by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] evaluates the need for and design of a dedicated hatchery to produce endangered [[Colorado River]] fishes. The study addresses production requirements through 1996 for three species: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). Cultural methods have been developed at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona) and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico) since 1973. The study estimates annual production needs of 500,000 fingerlings (2-3 inches) by 1990-1992 to support the draft Conservation Plan for the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] and recovery efforts in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]. Site evaluations focus on [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] (Utah) and [[Fish Springs National Wildlife Refuge]] (Utah) as primary candidates, with detailed engineering and biological assessments recommended for FY 1984.

## Background & Description

### Species Biology and Culture History

[[Colorado Squawfish]] were first artificially propagated in 1973, with several thousand offspring produced at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in 1974. Warm-water culture methods adapted from other species have proven successful. Spawning is induced via hormonal injection (carp pituitary). Eggs are either stripped and fertilized artificially or fish are returned to spawning ponds with gravel/rock substrate for natural spawning. Stripped eggs are incubated on screens in troughs with flowing water; hatching occurs in 4-6 days at 68-72°F. Fry begin feeding approximately 3 days post-hatch and are transferred to rearing ponds where they consume natural plankton, later supplemented with commercial trout feed.

Cultural methods for [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] closely parallel those for squawfish, though chubs spawn earlier at lower temperatures and exhibit faster growth. Survival rates have been variable: [[Colorado Squawfish]] hatching success ranged from 3-90% at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (1981) and 29.4% at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (1982), improving to 60-70% by 1983. Fry survival averaged 80% at Willow Beach (1980) but only 8.4% at Dexter (1982). Bonytail and humpback chub showed higher survival: 88-91% and 72% egg hatch respectively, with fry survival of 71% (bonytail) and 75-85% (humpback).

### Fecundity and Broodstock

Female [[Colorado Squawfish]] produced an average of 62,500 eggs per female at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (1982). [[Bonytail Chub]] females yielded approximately 25,000 eggs, while [[Humpback Chub]] produced only 2,500 eggs per female. Wild broodstock numbers have declined critically since 1981 due to theft (Colorado squawfish at Willow Beach) and mortality. Geneticists recommend maintaining at least 50 breeding adults with equal sex ratios to preserve genetic variability and avoid inbreeding. Pure [[Bonytail Chub]] are extremely rare, occurring only in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]].

### Growth and Stocking Success

Optimum growing temperature for [[Colorado Squawfish]] is approximately 77°F. At [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], fingerlings reached 1.5-3.5 inches in one 120-day growing season (1982), compared to only 2 inches for wild fish in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] grow faster, reaching 4-5 inches in 150 days at Dexter versus 2-3 inches for wild humpback chub in the UCRB.

Hatchery-raised [[Colorado Squawfish]] demonstrate survival capability in the wild. Approximately 1,500 fish (8-15 inches) from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] were tagged and stocked in the [[Upper Colorado River]] (1980); fifteen were recaptured up to 3 years post-stocking, showing good growth and association with wild conspecifics. About 30,000 fingerlings (2-3 inches) from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] were coded-wire tagged and released near [[Grand Junction, Colorado]] (October 1982); recaptures in July 1983 showed growth from 38-88 mm TL to 110-120 mm TL despite high runoff and cold temperatures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] system historically supported populations of these three endemic cyprinids throughout the Upper and Lower Basins. The [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] completed a three-year study (1979-1981) recommending procedures to protect and preserve these fishes and their habitats. A draft Conservation Plan for the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] calls for hatchery production to support: (1) research/management studies (1983-1996), (2) stocking to augment wild populations (post-1987), and (3) development of a sport fishery for [[Colorado Squawfish]] in a small reservoir (1990).

The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] was updating Recovery Plans for [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] and preparing a plan for [[Bonytail Chub]] (to be released 1983). Recommendations included hatchery propagation and reintroduction to reestablish multiple self-sustaining populations of each species, with the ultimate goal of delisting or downlisting.

### Habitat Requirements

Natural spawning occurs over gravel/rock substrate. [[Colorado Squawfish]] convert from planktivorous to piscivorous feeding at approximately 50 mm (2 inches). Cannibalism has been observed at 30 mm (1.2 inches) in raceways and becomes problematic in pond culture when fish exceed 2 inches if stocking densities are too high or natural food becomes scarce.

Water quality requirements include: temperature range 20-30°C, minimum 7 ppm dissolved oxygen, pH 7-9, and relative freedom from contaminants (H₂S, Mn, heavy metals). The [[Green River]] and [[Colorado River]] exhibit seasonal temperature ranges from 0-81°F, with growing season temperatures of 68-81°F.

## Key Findings

### Production Requirements

Based on the Conservation Plan, estimated annual production needs are:
- **1983**: 182,000 fingerlings
- **1985-1988**: 1,160,000-1,515,000 (includes 1-3 million sac-fry for imprinting studies)
- **1990-1992**: 500,000 fingerlings (peak production)
- **1993-1996**: 250,000-375,000 fingerlings

Species breakdown at peak production (500,000 total):
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: 250,000 (50%)
- [[Humpback Chub]]: 125,000 (25%)
- [[Bonytail Chub]]: 125,000 (25%)

[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] projects increasing production from 200,000 (1983) to 800,000 (1986) for [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] needs, though water rights are already overextended and pond leakage over limestone substrate limits expansion.

### Facility Design Options

**Pond Culture System** (estimated cost $3,907,721):
- 25 one-acre production ponds
- 10 small ponds (0.1-0.25 acres) for broodstock and experiments
- 6 raceways (6'x60') for holding and quarantine
- Holding house with 16 tanks, egg incubation room, laboratory
- Production capacity: 30,000 fingerlings/acre for [[Colorado Squawfish]], 50,000/acre for chubs
- Growing season: 120 days (squawfish), 150 days (chubs)

**Raceway Culture System** (estimated cost $2,932,103):
- 15 raceways (ten 6'x60', five 8'x80')
- 10 ponds (0.2-0.5 acres) for broodstock
- Hatchery building with egg incubation room, 16 circular tanks, laboratory
- Requires constant 77°F water temperature and 7 ppm oxygen
- Allows year-round production and better control of fish size/timing
- Higher survival rates than pond culture (80% vs. variable)

Both systems would require a four-person staff: Manager (GS-11), Assistant Manager (GS-7/9), Bio/tech (GS-5/7), and Clerk (GS-4/5).

### Site Evaluations

**Primary Recommendation - [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]]** (Utah):
- Located on [[Green River]] within present range of all three target species
- Water rights: 23,000 ac-ft/year from Green River, 1,890 ac-ft from [[Pelican Lake]]
- Pelican Lake water: 90-foot head available, 68-81°F during growing season, gravity flow
- Soil conditions and topography favorable for pond/raceway construction
- Strategic location: 4-8 hours to Upper Basin stocking sites (<300 miles)
- Enables in situ studies using natural river water (turbidity, salinity, contaminants)
- Potential for establishing Colorado squawfish spawning run and studying homing behavior
- Elevation ~4,700 ft, 110-day growing season

**Secondary Recommendation - [[Fish Springs National Wildlife Refuge]]** (Utah):
- Nine springs with total flow ~40 cfs (28,908 ac-ft/year)
- Water temperature: 77-83°F year-round (ideal for accelerated growth)
- Water quality: chloride 640-1,280 ppm, TDS 1,878-5,640 ppm (acceptable for target species)
- Soil reportedly water-tight (clay, sand, peat)
- **Drawbacks**: Remote location (6-7 hours to stocking sites), single-phase electrical service, limited hydraulic head, high salinity may affect eggs/fry, year-round warm water may prevent broodstock maturation

**Other Sites Evaluated**:
- [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona): Limited expansion potential, 500+ miles from Upper Basin, currently produces <100,000 fingerlings
- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico): Water rights overextended, pond leakage issues, 500+ miles from Upper Basin, holds multiple species from different watersheds (disease risk)
- [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] (Colorado): Chipeta Unit destroyed by 1981 landslide, headquarters unit too small for required production
- [[Logan Fisheries Experiment Station]] (Utah): Outside Colorado River system, water temperature limiting (50-63°F), requires recirculation system

### Recommendations for FY 1984

1. **Engineering evaluations** of [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Fish Springs National Wildlife Refuge]]:
   - Topographical surveys, core drilling, soils analysis (Oct-Nov)
   - Development of alternative conceptual designs with cost analyses (Dec-Mar)
   - Report and decision by Regional Director (Apr-Jul)
   - Well exploration at selected site (Jul-Sep)
   - Estimated cost: $66,000 per site

2. **Fish culture study** at [[Fish Springs National Wildlife Refuge]]:
   - Install experimental flow-through rearing system (Apr-May)
   - Test survival and growth of 10,000 [[Colorado Squawfish]] sac-fry/advanced fry from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (Jun-Sep)
   - Estimated cost: $28,750

3. **Renegotiate MOU** with Region 2 to obtain priority delivery (1985-1988):
   - 150,000 (2-3 inch) [[Colorado Squawfish]]
   - 50,000 (2-3 inch) [[Humpback Chub]]
   - 50,000 (2-3 inch) [[Bonytail Chub]]
   - Additional 1-3 million sac-fry for larval drift and imprinting studies

### Conservation Strategy Rationale

The study emphasizes the need for an [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] hatchery to complement [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Multiple facilities are essential to protect against catastrophic loss from floods, disease, or vandalism. A riverside facility at [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] would enable:
- Direct stocking without disease clearance complications
- In situ research using natural river conditions
- Establishment of a spawning run for long-term population monitoring
- Consolidation of fragmented recovery efforts across four to seven states
- Public education and demonstration of conservation work

The facility could serve as both a production hatchery and field laboratory, conducting controlled studies on temperature/salinity/turbidity effects, contaminant toxicity, genetics/taxonomy/hybridization, fish passage/migration/spawning, and competition/predation with exotic species. This integrated approach would be more cost-effective than the current fragmented system with university overhead rates of 35-60% and extensive transportation costs.
