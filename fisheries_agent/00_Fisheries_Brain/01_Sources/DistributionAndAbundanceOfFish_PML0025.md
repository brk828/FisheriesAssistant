# Distribution and Abundance of Fishes in Shinumo Creek in the Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This M.S. thesis from the University of Arizona (1993) by Nathan Allan examines fish distribution, abundance and habitat in [[Shinumo Creek]], a tributary of the [[Colorado River]] in the [[Grand Canyon]]. A waterfall (over 2 m high, about 120 m upstream of the confluence) blocks fish access from the mainstem. Above it, only [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]] (native) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (introduced) were found. The author attributes their coexistence to resource partitioning and the similarly small body size of sucker and trout. The waterfall's permanence is linked to regulation of the Colorado River by [[Glen Canyon Dam]], which prevents the flood flows that once inundated it.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Shinumo Creek enters the Colorado River at River Mile 108 at an elevation of 670 m. It drains about 232 km², mostly within Grand Canyon National Park, and is about 17 km long with an average gradient of about 92 m/km.
- **Study period:** Field sampling ran from July 1992 to June 1993 on several trips, with systematic transects extending up to 10 km.
- **Objectives:** Evaluate seasonal habitat availability (depth, velocity, substrate) and the distribution and relative abundance of fishes above and below the waterfall.
- **Funding and support:** The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] funded the work through the Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit, with logistics support from Glen Canyon Environmental Studies, the Arizona Game and Fish Department, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
- **Native fish context:** Of eight historic fish species in Marble and Grand Canyons, only three remain common. Four are virtually extirpated and four of the five listed species are federally endangered. Six of the eight species are endemic to the Colorado River Basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical habitat**
- Small, clear, cold, high-gradient stream with pools up to about 1.5 m deep and long riffles less than 0.5 m deep.
- Dense overhanging vegetation shades the channel. Substrates are mainly sand and rock-cobble.
- Mean water temperatures range from summer highs of 27 °C to winter lows of 3 °C.
- Summer flows range from 1 to 14 cfs. Spring snowmelt runoff in May 1990 was estimated at 57 cfs.

**Water chemistry**
- Low salinity, high carbonate levels with roughly equal calcium and magnesium, and an extremely low N/P ratio.
- pH ranged from 8.2 to 8.8 (mean 8.5). Conductivity ranged from 183 to 361 µS (mean 309).
- Dissolved oxygen was near 100% saturation (7.4 to 9.1 mg/L).

**Regulated Colorado River**
- [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (completed 1963) was operated for peak power generation, causing daily discharge fluctuations and eliminating seasonal variation.
- Altered temperatures from hypolimnetic releases and exotic species are suggested as primary causes of native fish declines.

**Fish community**
- Native and introduced fish below the waterfall represent the common species of the mainstem, including [[Humpback Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], brown trout, [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Fathead Minnow]].

## Key Findings

**Fish distribution**
- Only bluehead sucker, speckled dace and rainbow trout were found above the lower waterfall.
- Speckled dace were found as far as 9.2 km upstream. No bluehead sucker were observed above a second waterfall at about 7 km.
- Rainbow trout were present at least to 9.2 km, the furthest point of any fish sampling.
- Below the waterfall, 4 native and 5 introduced species were captured in July 1992.

**Fish size**
- Bluehead sucker: 55 to 230 mm TL, mean 160 mm (n = 77). The largest fish was 230 mm.
- Rainbow trout: 62 to 300 mm TL, mean 149 mm (n = 46).
- Shinumo bluehead suckers are smaller than mainstem individuals (up to 397 mm TL in Usher et al. 1979 and over 450 mm TL in Maddux et al. 1987).
- No fish of any species exceeded 300 mm TL.

**Abundance**
- Speckled dace were the most abundant species in every sample period in which fish were captured.
- Relative abundance varied by method. In July 1992, speckled dace made up 81%, bluehead sucker 5% and rainbow trout 13% of 683 fish. Snorkeling gave the highest catch for all three species.
- No native fish were captured in January or March 1993, although the same sites had yielded them in October 1992.

**Habitat availability**
- Most K-S comparisons showed no significant difference between fish sample sites and systematic 200-m transects, so sites were considered representative.
- Exceptions: in June 1993, fish sites were deeper with smaller substrates. In March 1993, substrates were smaller at fish sites.
- Across seasons, 14 of 30 K-S tests were significant. Substrate varied most (7 of 10 tests significant in one comparison).
- Flood events are likely to drive seasonal and yearly changes in depth, velocity and substrate.

**Coexistence and interactions**
- Competition for space may be reduced because bluehead suckers and rainbow trout are similar in size.
- Predation by trout on native fish is considered less likely here because few trout exceed the approximately 300 mm TL size at which piscivory is reported.
- Other Grand Canyon tributaries (Tapeats, Bright Angel, Kanab, Paria) are generally dominated by either native or introduced fish alone.

**Isolation and the waterfall**
- The waterfall at the mouth is likely a barrier only at normal flows. It may have been less than 0.5 m high during the 90,000 cfs discharge of 1983.
- Mainstem flows above about 120,000 cfs were estimated as sufficient to breach it. Pre-dam records show one flood above 200,000 cfs and several above 120,000 cfs.
- Isolation of fish in Shinumo Creek is attributed to regulated flows that have reduced the frequency and magnitude of floods.

**Reproduction and life history**
- No young-of-year bluehead suckers were observed, suggesting intermittent successful spawning.
- One ripe female was captured at the mouth on June 23, 1993.
- Historic peak flows often fell in the spawning window (mid-March to June). Between 1922 and 1962, mean daily flows exceeded 100,000 cfs on 68 days, most between May 8 and June 23.

**Humpback chub**
- One adult (435 mm TL) and one juvenile (100 mm TL) humpback chub were captured in the mixing zone at the mouth, suggesting this area may matter to mainstem populations. No evidence of reproduction was found.

**Management implications**
- Shinumo Creek may be the only Grand Canyon tributary where natural conditions allow controlled experiments on microhabitat use and native-nonnative interactions.
- Research is constrained by access and the difficulty of staying in the area for extended periods.
- The author notes that the bluehead sucker population above the waterfall may be genetically unique and warrants further study.
