# Genetic Structure of Helicopsyche borealis Across Streams in Eastern and Western North America

#entomology #trichoptera #helicopsychidae #population-genetics #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study uses [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] at 17 loci to describe the genetic structure of the stream caddisfly [[Helicopsyche borealis]] in three northern California drainages and three eastern North American sites. Its central results are that populations separated by drainage basins are genetically distinct, that populations within a basin differ only slightly, and that the taxon as currently defined contains several genetically distinct groups. The authors argue that these groups are probably separate species, that gene flow is limited by dispersal and physical barriers, and that stream invertebrate populations should be treated as spatially subdivided units in research design and impact assessment.

## Background & Description

*Helicopsyche borealis* is a case-bearing caddisfly (Helicopsychidae) whose larvae build mineral cases. Its identity was complicated by the species' broad geographic range. The original description (Hagen, 1861) was based on specimens from Washington DC and the St Lawrence River, and these localities were not available for analysis. Morphological work by Holzenthal and Hamilton suggested that only one species occurred across most of eastern North America, but the authors could not confirm this genetically.

The authors framed the study around the concept of a population: a group of conspecific individuals that interbreed and share a common gene pool. They noted that stream invertebrate studies have often sampled at scales that do not match population boundaries, ranging from a single riffle to an entire drainage basin.

Study sites:
- **[[Rice Fork of the Eel River]]** (Lake County, CA): three sites (ER1–ER3), elevation roughly 233–555 m a.s.l.
- **[[Big Sulphur Creek]]** (Sonoma County, CA): three sites (BSC1–BSC3)
- **[[Alameda Creek]]** (Alameda County, CA): three sites (AC1–AC3), with some sites subject to intermittent flow
- **Christiana Creek** (Elkhart County, Indiana, CC)
- **Byrd's Mill Creek** (Pontotoc County, Oklahoma, BMC)
- **Susquehanna River** (Bradford County, Pennsylvania, SR)

Sampling collected pupae that were reared to adults. Adults were stored at −70 °C before analysis. California samples used males only, because band resolution was poor for females, likely due to abdominal fat. Eastern samples included females, with abdomens removed. Thirty to thirty-four males per California site and 13–14 individuals per eastern site were analysed. Data for all 17 loci were obtained for 296 of 335 individuals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Genetic structure and dispersal.** Larval case-bearing caddisflies usually disperse short distances. In a mark–recapture study at BSC1, 75% of recovered *Gumaga nigricula* larvae remained in their release pool, and the rest moved a median of 18 m (maximum 436 m). *Helicopsyche* larvae at Alameda Creek were observed moving at least 100 m over four months, probably by drift during winter spates. Adult *Helicopsyche* abundance at 40 m and 150 m from Big Sulphur Creek was 5% and 0.5% of streamside abundance, respectively. These observations indicate limited dispersal, although long-distance adult flight has been recorded in some Trichoptera.

**Environmental disturbance at Alameda Creek.** Larvae were abundant at AC2 in the early 1980s. During drought years that severely modified stream habitat, they were relatively rare in spring 1986 and 1987 and virtually absent in spring 1989. The authors propose that these environmentally induced [[Population Bottleneck]] events reduced genetic variability and influenced the genetic structure of the Alameda population.

**Environmental differences.** The eastern sites are large rivers or streams in the deciduous forest, while the California sites are coastal mountain streams. Differences in habitat also occur within drainages. For example, the Rice Fork site ER3 lies near Lake Pillsbury, a reservoir about 200 m downstream, which may contribute dispersing individuals.

**Implications for biomonitoring.** Toxicity bioassays usually use organisms from a single site. If these organisms differ genetically from those at other sites, results may not apply to other locations, and biotic indices may be inaccurate.

## Key Findings

**1. Genetic variability within California sites was low.**
- Polymorphic loci ranged from 12% to 29% (2–5 loci per site).
- Mean heterozygosity across all loci and sites was 9.0%.
- Alameda Creek had the lowest heterozygosity (mean 6.4%, range 5.9–7.1%). The Rice Fork averaged 9.9% (9.3–10.4%), and Big Sulphur Creek averaged 11.3% (10.8–11.8%).

**2. Eastern North American sites were more variable.** Polymorphic loci ranged from 29% to 41%, and heterozygosity ranged from 13% to 17%.

**3. Hidden genetic groups in California.** Heterozygote deficiencies at AC1 and AC2 were explained by two groups with significantly different allele frequencies (a [[Wahlund Effect]]). The authors divided these into:
- Individuals similar to the main California form (seven of 33 from AC1, 31 of 33 from AC2).
- Individuals genetically distinct from all other California *H. borealis* (26 from AC1, two from AC2), which carried seven private alleles and fixed differences at five loci.

BSC1 also differed genetically, with five private alleles at four loci and a fixed difference at 6Pgd.

**4. Groups in Indiana.** Of the 14 individuals from Christiana Creek, 11 were genetically similar to the Oklahoma and Pennsylvania samples. The other three were very distinct, with a mean [[Nei's Genetic Distance]] of 0.508 from eastern and California *H. borealis*.

**5. Proposed taxonomic units.** Because the groups were sympatric with no evidence of interbreeding and were genetically divergent, the authors classified them as four types:
- **Type A:** the common form, found at all eight California sites except BSC1, and at all three eastern sites.
- **Type B:** BSC1 individuals.
- **Type C:** AC1 and AC2 individuals.
- **Type D:** three individuals from Christiana Creek.

Mean Nei's distance between type A and type B was 0.396. Distances between other type pairs exceeded 0.6. The authors state that these values are much greater than the typical intraspecific variation among local insect populations (rarely above 0.150). Several morphological characters of the male genitalia were also consistently distinct among types. The authors conclude that the four types are probably distinct species. They also identified diagnostic alleles that could aid identification.

**6. Population structure within drainages.** Genetic differences among sites within the same drainage were small. Mean [[Fixation Index]] (FST) values were 0.034 (Rice Fork), 0.008 (Big Sulphur Creek), and 0.015 (Alameda Creek). Significant differences occurred at some loci in the Rice Fork and Big Sulphur Creek, and no significant differences were found among Alameda Creek sites. The authors interpret this as isolation by distance within these streams, and treat the Alameda result as evidence that gene flow among groups there is not strongly limited.

**7. Population structure among drainages.** Type A populations from different basins differed much more.
- Across all 11 sites, mean Nei's distance was 0.153 and mean FST was 0.524.
- Among the three eastern sites, mean Nei's distance was 0.115 and FST was 0.308.
- Among the eight California sites, mean Nei's distance was 0.085 and FST was 0.425. Excluding Alameda Creek, these fell to 0.005 and 0.044.
- Fixed differences at G6pd separated Alameda Creek from the Rice Fork and Big Sulphur Creek.
- Mean Nei's distance between the Rice Fork and Big Sulphur Creek was 0.156, with significant allele-frequency differences at five loci.

**8. California and eastern North America.** Type A from California and eastern North America had a mean Nei's distance of 0.236. The authors note this is similar to differences among sibling insect species, but the geographic separation (over 2000 km with mountain ranges and arid regions between) means the result could reflect either extreme isolation within one species or two closely related species. Morphological differences between these regions were not distinct enough to confirm two taxa.

**9. Why Alameda Creek is distinct.** Alameda Creek type A differed strongly from the other California streams despite being only 160–200 km away. The authors attribute this mainly to drought-induced bottlenecks. Its lower variability relative to the Rice Fork and Big Sulphur Creek supports this interpretation.

**10. Broader conclusions.** The authors argue that many aquatic invertebrate species are probably not single panmictic populations, but are subdivided regionally and sometimes locally. They recommend that studies choose sites and generalise results with population structure in mind, and that species such as *H. borealis* require information on [[Population Structure]] before data from different sites are combined. The authors state that more extensive morphological and genetic work is needed before the taxonomy of *Helicopsyche* can be resolved.
