# Habitat Use by Hatchery-Reared Adult Razorback Suckers in the Imperial Division, Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how hatchery-reared adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) use existing and reconnected off-channel habitats in the [[Imperial Division]] of the [[Lower Colorado River]] in Arizona and California. Fish were tracked with [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] transmitters from late 1999 to mid-2001. The fish strongly preferred [[Backwater Habitat]] over [[Side Channel]] and [[Main Channel]] habitats. A [[Logistic Regression]] model built from backwater morphology and water quality predicted nonuse of sites well but predicted use poorly. The authors recommend using the model to prioritize sites for [[Habitat Reconnection]], because the fish did use the newly reconnected backwaters.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is native to the southwestern United States and once ranged throughout the Colorado River system. Its populations have declined severely because of habitat alteration and destruction, declining water quality, and competition and predation by [[Nonnative Fishes]]. Recovery efforts have run for more than 20 years, but they have been limited by poor information on the species' life history and biological requirements. Habitat alteration has also continued across the basin, even though its effects on native fishes were largely unknown.

The study had three objectives:
1. Measure habitat characteristics alongside fish habitat use before, during and after reconnection activities.
2. Develop predictive models of habitat use.
3. Determine whether razorback suckers would use newly reconnected habitats.

Earlier work by the same group (Bradford et al. 1998; Bradford and Gurtin 2000; Gurtin and Bradford 2000) had already shown that the fish preferred off-channel habitats and that habitat use was associated with physical and water quality characteristics. The [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (USBR) then planned reconnection projects in the Imperial Division, which prompted the expanded design.

**Study area.** The study covered the lower 9.6 km of the Imperial Division, upstream of [[Imperial Dam]]. The main channel was fairly homogeneous, with a mixed silt-sand substrate, sand bars and depths rarely above 2.5 m. Riffles and pools were scarce. Riparian vegetation included cattails, bulrushes, reed grass and tamarisk. Several nonnative fishes were abundant, including [[Flathead Catfish]], channel catfish, [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and other centrarchids.

**Study area classification.** Aerial photographs, on-site surveys and USBR surface-area data defined 36 sites:
- **25 backwaters** (0.68–28.10 ha): permanent water bodies connected to, but physically distinct from, the main or side channels.
- **9 side channels** (0.53–4.17 ha): flowing, narrow corridors linking main-channel and off-channel habitats.
- **Main channel**: about 133 ha, divided into six 1.6-km segments.
- **Impoundment** (13.2 ha): the area adjacent to Imperial Dam, which shares features with backwaters and the main channel.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study fish.** Seventy-five adult hatchery-reared razorback suckers (413–570 mm total length) were reared at [[Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery]] in Page Springs, Arizona, and implanted with sonic transmitters following [[Clarkson et al. 1993]] techniques. Fish were released in two cohorts: 38 on November 24, 1999, and 37 on October 3, 2000. The release used the [[Hatchery-Reared Fish]] model that current recovery programs depend on.

**Habitat characteristics measured.** Dynamic variables included specific conductance, turbidity, temperature, pH and water velocity. Static variables included backwater surface area, perimeter, mean and maximum depth, [[Shoreline Development Index]], distance to the main channel and entrance sinuosity.

**Tracking.** Fish were located from a boat with a directional hydrophone and receiver, roughly every 2–3 weeks from December 1999 to July 2001. Five diel surveys were also run. Transmitters had a nominal range of about 1,000 m, but vegetation, noise and currents often limited detection, so the team added listening sites. A fish that stayed in the same spot for three consecutive surveys was assumed dead or to have shed its tag.

**Analysis.** Movement was classified by displacement between survey locations, and exposure to habitat types was inferred from movement sequences. Observed use was compared with habitat availability using a [[Chi-Square Goodness-Of-Fit Test]]. Diel use was tested with chi-square on cross-tabulated data. Water-quality changes were compared with independent-samples t-tests. [[Spearman Rank Correlation]] analyses linked habitat characteristics to use. The logistic regression used the form P = 1/(1 + e^−Z), with a 0.5 cutoff for predicting use. Validation used a jackknife technique and a split-sample approach, with model A (all static and dynamic variables) as the main model.

## Key Findings

**1. Strong selection of off-channel habitat.** Use of habitat types differed significantly from availability (P < 0.01). Backwaters and the impoundment accounted for about 80% of fish contacts while making up only about 49% of the study area. Main-channel habitat made up about 45% of the area but held only about 15% of contacts. Side channels held about 5% of contacts, slightly less than their availability.

**2. Broad habitat exposure.** Of the released fish, 43 (57%) were exposed to more than one habitat type, 13 (17%) to at least three, and 5 to all available types. Individual behavior varied: some fish stayed sedentary and others moved among many sites.

**3. Tracking summary.** The study completed 25 reconnaissance surveys over December 1999–July 2001. Fish were contacted at 31 sampling sites, with 410 total contacts. Of the 75 tagged fish, 67 were contacted at least once, 8 were never contacted after release, and 6 were found outside the study area. The average was 5.6 contacts per fish (maximum 21).

**4. No diel preference.** Diel data came from 16 fish observed across six periods in five surveys (January–June 2000). Movement occurred in 57 of 95 observations, mostly within the same habitat type. Overall habitat use did not differ significantly among periods (P = 0.879).

**5. Use of reconnected backwaters.** Reconnection was completed around April 2000. Forty-two fish were released into areas where reconnection work was underway. Twenty-three remained in the Imperial Restoration Project (IRP) area and 6 left and later returned, so 69% of released fish in the IRP stayed in the area. Four fish released outside the IRP later moved into it. Most fish moved away from their release sites over time, which the authors read as active habitat selection.

**6. Habitat characteristics predicting use.**
- *Before restoration*, use was significantly associated with backwater surface area (r = 0.591), perimeter (r = 0.570), Shoreline Development Index (r = 0.370) and mean depth (r = 0.379). Use was also associated with sinuosity, distance to the main channel and specific conductance.
- *After restoration*, only temperature and turbidity were significantly associated with use.
- Physical characteristics (area and depth) mattered more than water quality (turbidity, pH, temperature).

**7. Logistic regression model.** Model A retained surface area, mean depth and distance to the main channel (P ≤ 0.001 to 0.001), along with specific conductance, pH and temperature (P ≈ 0.04–0.05). Velocity, turbidity and entrance sinuosity did not contribute significantly.
- *Phase III (training data)*: nonuse was classified correctly 94.3% of the time (93.5% in validation) and use 58.4% (59.5% in validation). Overall accuracy was 84.0% (83.0% in validation).
- *Phase IV (reconnected backwaters)*: accuracy fell to 72.0% overall, with nonuse at 82.9% and use at 17.1%.
- The authors concluded that the model is reliable for identifying sites that are *unsuitable* for razorback suckers, but not for confirming suitable sites. Use observations (N = 396) were far fewer than nonuse observations (N = 1,204), which skewed classification.

**8. Water quality changed after reconnection.** Dredging raised backwater velocity, and temperature and specific conductance decreased, likely due to greater flushing with main-stem water. Turbidity did not change significantly. Physical changes included two access points (inlet and outlet) cut into reconnected backwaters, where existing ones had only one.

**9. Suitable reconnected habitat.** Using the model, three reconnected backwaters (AB12.8, AB12.4 and AB12.3) were judged suitable, covering about 10.74 ha. The reconnected backwaters totaled about 18.94 ha, so roughly 56% of the reconnected area appears suitable. The authors suggest razorback suckers prefer large, irregularly shaped backwaters with mean depth above 1.5 m.

**10. Limits and cautions.** The model may not transfer to environments that differ substantially from the Imperial Division. Available structure and cover were not quantified, because habitats were largely homogeneous and deep water had poor visibility. The researchers' presence sometimes made fish move away from the boat. Hatchery-reared fish may not behave like wild fish.

**11. Management implications.**
- Hatchery-reared adults actively select backwaters, so losing those habitats could harm recovery.
- Natural floods that once cleared silt, sand and vegetation from backwaters no longer occur under current flow regimes. Access must now be maintained by dredging and excavation, which is costly and labor-intensive.
- The model can help prioritize reconnection sites by identifying those unlikely to be used even after restoration, reducing costs. It can also help the USBR avoid jeopardizing razorback suckers or damaging critical habitat.
