# Research in the Radioactive Contamination of the Environment

#environmental-monitoring #radiological-health #radioactivity

## Executive Summary

This 1960 paper by [[Conrad P. Straub]] reports the research program of the [[Robert A. Taft Sanitary Engineering Center]], a [[Public Health Service]] unit in Cincinnati, Ohio, to measure radioactive contamination of air, rainfall, cistern water, [[Ohio River]] water, milk and food. It is not a fish or aquatic-biology study. Water and biota appear only as sampling media. The paper summarises Cincinnati monitoring data from 1953 to 1957 and 1958, estimates [[Strontium-90]] intake from environmental sources, and calls for further surveys and controls.

## Background & Description

Before 1945, environmental radioactivity came mainly from [[Cosmic Radiation]] (producing H-3 and C-14) and naturally occurring radionuclides such as uranium, thorium, K-40 and Rb-87. Nuclear energy added new sources: fuel fabrication plants, fixed and mobile reactors, reprocessing plants, medical, research and industrial radioisotopes, and [[Fallout]] from nuclear tests.

The paper frames environmental exposure alongside [[Natural Radiation]], [[Medical Irradiation]] and [[Occupational Exposure]]. It cites [[Lauriston S. Taylor]] and others, who suggested that genetically based exposure to age 30 should not exceed 14 million rem per million population. The tabulated budget gives:

| Source | Man-rems |
|---|---|
| Natural radiation | 4,000,000 |
| Medical irradiation | 5,000,000 |
| Occupational exposure | 150,000 |
| Plant environs (one-tenth of new MPD) | 150,000 |
| Plant environs (one-tenth of old MPD) | 450,000 |
| Fallout | 200,000 |
| **Total** | **9,800,000** |
| Balance | 4,200,000 |

The paper uses two approaches. The first measures gross alpha, beta and gamma activity to show trends, provide routine control at nuclear facilities, detect accidental releases and track radioactive clouds. The second identifies specific radionuclides in surface, ground and marine waters and their biota, bottom muds, air, milk, bone, vegetation and soil. Methods were adapted from high-concentration procedures to the low levels typical of environmental samples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Surveillance networks.** The [[Public Health Service]] Center ran three national networks with state and municipal partners:

- [[National Air Sampling Network]]: 161 stations, 24-hour particulate samples at least bi-weekly, gross beta counts.
- [[Water Quality Basic Data Network]]: weekly samples from 50 stations on principal U.S. streams; gross beta on all, gross alpha on one-quarter.
- [[National Milk Surveillance Network]]: monthly samples from 10 milk sheds, with specific radionuclide analysis.

At Cincinnati, rainfall, cistern water, vegetation, food and [[Radon]] and [[Thoron]] daughter products were also measured.

**Air.** Samples were collected on 8 × 10 inch glass fibre filters and counted in a large-area gas flow counter. Counts were repeated after two days and again after four to seven days to separate decay of natural alpha and beta-gamma activity from the gross beta activity at collection.

**Water and rainfall.** Rainfall was collected in a two-square-metre container and filtered to separate suspended and dissolved activity. Rainfall activity tracks nuclear test operations. The suspended fraction is highest soon after tests and then declines.

**Milk and food.** Milk sampling began in May 1957. Sheds were chosen to represent at least 1,000 cows, and the same farms were sampled each month. Fluid milk was counted for [[Iodine-131]], then ashed for radiochemical analysis of strontium, barium-140 and [[Cesium-137]], with stable calcium and potassium measured by flame spectrophotometry. Market foods were analysed for gross beta-gamma activity, Sr-90 and stable calcium.

## Key Findings

**Air (Cincinnati, 1953–1957, Table 1).** Average particulate activity rose from about 0.2 to 4.3 (units as printed, pc/m³). Sample counts were 250 in 1954, 55 in 1955, 40 in 1956 and 18 in 1957. The 1957 maximum was 219.

**Radon and thoron.** Morning radon ranged from 70 to 2,320 pc/m³ and afternoon values from 8 to 400 pc/m³. Thoron ranged from 4 to 10 pc/m³ in October and from 1 to 5 pc/m³ in November. Average radon was 1,850 under calm, low-wind conditions and 495 during rain with wind above 2 mph. Pipe-tunnel radon ranged from 732 to 4,230.

**Rainout.** Rainout rose from under 0.1 to about 0.54 curies per square mile over 1953–1957. The paper reports that natural removal increases with rain activity.

**Cistern water.** Gross activity averaged 119 pc/L in January 1958, peaked in August at 459 pc/L (six determinations), and averaged 346 pc/L in December.

**Surface water (Ohio River).** Gross activity ranged from 1 to 340 pc/L. About 44% was associated with suspended matter, which conventional water treatment (coagulation, sedimentation and filtration) should largely remove.

**Rainfall strontium.** Total Sr ranged from 0.6 to 12.3 pc/L and Sr-90 from 0.3 to 7.5 pc/L. Gamma scans identified Ce-141/144, Ru-103/106, Cs-137, Zr-95/Nb-95, Zn-65, Co-60 and K-40.

**Cistern sludge.** Total Sr ranged from 1.0 to 4.4 µc/g ash across March 1955 and March–May 1956. Sr-90 ranged from 0.3 to 3.1 µc/g ash in 1956.

**Milk (Cincinnati, 1958).** Stable calcium averaged 1.145 g/L. Gross activity peaked in July. Sr-90 averaged 8.5 pc/L in the summary estimates.

**Market foods.** Sampled foods ranged from about 40 to 45,300 pc/kg gross activity (beverages, instant) and included eggs, fruit, cereals, rice, dairy, seafood, meat and vegetables.

**Strontium-90 intake estimate (Table 10).**
- Air: 0.002–0.24 pc/day (smallest contributor).
- Water: 0.7–16.5 pc/day (cistern); 2.2–11 pc/day (surface).
- Milk: 8.5 pc/day.
- Food: 7 pc/day.
- Total: 16.2–17.7 (minimum) to 26.2–32.2 (maximum) pc/day.
- Against the permissible water-based intake of 176 pc/day (80 pc/L × 2.2 L), this is 9–10% to 15–19%.

Radon intake was estimated at 166–42,000 pc/day and thoron at 80–200 pc/day. The authors note that strontium-90 dominates the hazard, since most other radionuclides are less biologically hazardous.

**Conclusions.**
- The work is incomplete; more surveys are needed across many areas and sample types.
- High-level areas need detailed study to find sources and trace radionuclides through the environment and food chain to humans.
- Remedial measures should be planned to limit transfer to people.
- Environmental data should inform permissible occupational exposure limits. Occupational exposure is easier to control than environmental exposure.
