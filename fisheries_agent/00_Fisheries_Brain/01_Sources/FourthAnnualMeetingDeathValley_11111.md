# Fourth Annual Meeting — Death Valley System Committee (Desert Fishes Council), 1977

## Executive Summary

This document is the report of the Fourth Annual Meeting of the [[Death Valley System Committee]] (part of the [[Desert Fishes Council]]), held February 25, 1977 at Furnace Creek, [[Death Valley National Monument]]. The meeting reviewed conservation status, threats, and recovery efforts for a suite of highly endemic desert fishes restricted to isolated springs, pools, and marshes across the Death Valley hydrographic system (California/Nevada). Key topics include the Devils Hole Supreme Court decision, mining moratoria, invasive species threats (particularly [[Tilapia zillii]] and [[Gambusia]]), permitting issues for endangered species research, and detailed population status reports for numerous pupfish (*Cyprinodon* spp.), the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], [[Pahrump Killifish]], several chub and dace subspecies, and the [[Owens Sucker]]. The report exemplifies mid-1970s U.S. endangered species conservation practice for isolated desert aquatic endemics.

## Background & Description

The [[Desert Fishes Council]] convened its Death Valley System Committee to coordinate management across a network of geographically isolated, often single-spring endemic fish populations in the Mojave Desert and Great Basin. The Death Valley System comprises remnant pluvial-lake-era aquatic habitats — isolated springs, seeps, and short stream reaches — that support numerous endemic species and subspecies with extremely restricted ranges, many limited to a single spring or artificial refugium.

Species and subspecies discussed include:

- **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) — restricted to [[Devils Hole]], with a small transplanted refuge population near [[Hoover Dam]].
- **[[Pahrump Killifish]]** (*Empetrichthys latos*) — maintained via refugium ponds at [[Corn Creek]], an aquarium population at UNLV, and historically at [[Manse Spring]] (now extirpated) and [[Latos Pools]] along the Colorado River.
- **[[Ash Meadows Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon nevadensis mionectes*) and **[[Ash Meadows Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus nevadensis*) — multiple spring populations in [[Ash Meadows]], including [[Big Spring]] and [[Jackrabbit Spring]].
- **[[Warm Springs Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon nevadensis pectoralis*) — [[School Spring Refugium]] population, managed by a BLM-led recovery team.
- **[[Owens Chub]]** (*Gila bicolor snyderi*) — sole known population in [[Owens River Gorge]] below [[Crowley Lake Dam]]; hybridization concerns in Hot Creek–Mammoth Creek area.
- **[[Owens Dace]]** (undescribed *Rhinichthys osculus* subspecies) — widespread in northern Inyo County tributaries.
- **Amargosa speckled dace subspecies** (*Rhinichthys osculus* spp.) near Beatty, NV and Amargosa Gorge below Tecopa.
- **[[Mohave Chub]]** (*Gila bicolor mohavensis*) — primary refuge at [[Fort Soda (Zzyzx)]], secondary population at Lark Seep Lagoon, [[China Lake Naval Weapons Center]].
- **[[Owens Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon radiosus*) — populations at [[Fish Slough]] (Mono County) and [[Warm Springs Refugium]] near Big Pine.
- **[[Tecopa Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon nevadensis calidae*) — possibly extinct; unconfirmed sightings prompted a planned field survey.
- **[[Cottonball Marsh Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon milleri*) — stable, healthy population, no known threats.
- **[[Saratoga Springs Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon nevadensis nevadensis*) — stable, healthy.
- **[[Amargosa Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon nevadensis amargosae*) — abundant along the lower Amargosa River.
- **[[Salt Creek Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon salinus*) — stable; benefiting from new boardwalk infrastructure limiting habitat disturbance.
- **[[Owens Sucker]]** (*Catostomus fumeiventris*) — widespread and abundant in the upper Owens Valley/Crowley Lake, threatened by geothermal development.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*) — declining in the wild, believed linked to invasive Tilapia; active refugium establishment in [[Anza-Borrego Desert State Park]] (Fish Creek/Split Mountain area).
- **[[Railroad Valley Springfish]]** (*Crenichthys nevadae*) — habitat monitored due to petroleum exploration in Railroad Valley, Nevada.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) and **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — Council supported endangered/threatened status designations to Colorado state wildlife authorities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Endorheic desert spring ecosystems**: Nearly all species discussed are relictual endemics confined to single or few isolated spring/pool systems, remnants of Pleistocene pluvial lakes, making them acutely vulnerable to habitat loss, groundwater withdrawal, and stochastic events (e.g., flash flooding, spring desiccation).
- **Invasive species pressure**: *[[Tilapia zillii]]* was formally condemned by Council resolution (76-2) due to its correlation with declines in *Cyprinodon macularius*. *[[Gambusia]] affinis* introductions threaten multiple native pupfish populations (e.g., Scotty's Castle area, Santa Catarina Springs) and were targeted for chemical eradication using **Antimycin**, though supply shortages delayed treatment.
- **Groundwater and mining/energy threats**: Mining moratorium in Death Valley National Monument reduced habitat degradation risk. However, groundwater pumping (Nevada), geothermal exploration (Tecopa area, Owens Valley), and petroleum extraction (Railroad Valley, Nevada — Northwest Exploration Company, 3 active wells with 20 more planned) posed ongoing risks to spring-fed habitats.
- **Legal/regulatory framework**: The **Devils Hole Supreme Court decision** underscored federal groundwater protections for endangered species habitat. Species status changes were pursued through petitions to state (Colorado Division of Wildlife) and federal (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) agencies.
- **Habitat engineering for conservation**: Refugia and artificial ponds (e.g., Anza-Borrego's 60'x10' pond, Corn Creek runways, School Spring Refugium, Fish Slough acquisition) represent early applied conservation aquaculture/restoration efforts for endangered desert fishes.
- **Flash flood vulnerability**: Anza-Borrego introduced *C. macularius* populations were repeatedly eliminated by flash flooding, highlighting the fragility of small artificial refugia in arid, flood-prone terrain.

## Key Findings

1. **Devils Hole Pupfish (*Cyprinodon diabolis*)**: Population count exceeded 200 on Feb 24, 1977 but dropped below 200 by March 24, consistent with seasonal fluctuation; high reproductive activity observed. Auxiliary Hoover Dam refuge population held steady at ~60 individuals. Species remained in jeopardy.

2. **Ash Meadows transplant efforts**: 88 *Cyprinodon nevadensis mionectes* and 4 *Rhinichthys osculus nevadensis* were transplanted from Big Spring to the rewetted Jackrabbit Spring on March 12, 1977 (water temps 26°C at Big Spring, 25°C at Jackrabbit Spring; flow ~1 CFS at Jackrabbit).

3. **Pahrump Killifish**: Upper Corn Creek pond population healthy; lower ponds rehabilitated with reintroduction planned for spring 1977. Manse Spring population confirmed extinct; Latos Pools status unconfirmed since August 1975.

4. **Tilapia threat quantified**: Increasing Tilapia abundance correlated with sharply decreasing *Cyprinodon macularius* numbers, prompting a threatened-status proposal for the species.

5. **Anza-Borrego refugium success**: An artificial pond established in May 1970 with 48 founder *C. macularius* from the Salton Sea grew to over 1,000 individuals by 1977, demonstrating refugium viability despite repeated failures of other introduced populations due to flash flooding.

6. **Owens Chub hybridization concern**: Chubs from the Hot Creek–Mammoth Creek area were identified by Robert R. Miller as hybridized forms, complicating conservation of genetically pure *Gila bicolor snyderi*.

7. **Bibliographic resource produced**: A comprehensive bibliography, *"Fishes and Aquatic Resources of the Death Valley System, California-Nevada, 1889–1976,"* compiled by Robert R. Miller, David L. Soltz, and Peter G. Sanchez (240 titles), was finalized for publication by the National Park Service, to be distributed to Desert Fishes Council membership and relevant agencies.

8. **Council resolutions enacted**:
   - **76-1**: Commendation of U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service for developing [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] as an endangered fishes refugium.
   - **76-2**: Condemnation of *Tilapia zillii* in U.S. natural waters, urging caution with other *Tilapia* species.

9. **Species status petitions**: Council formally supported endangered status for *Gila elegans* and threatened status for *Xyrauchen texanus* to the Colorado Division of Wildlife.

10. **Recovery team consolidation**: Consensus reached to establish a single **system-wide recovery team** for Death Valley System fishes rather than species-specific teams, streamlining coordination with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

11. **Permitting challenges identified**: Agencies noted that most collecting permit applications lacked sufficient justification; California's streamlined permitting process (issuance within hours given adequate data) was highlighted as a model.

12. **Petroleum development monitoring**: Railroad Valley, Nevada had 3 active oil wells (20 more planned in 1977–78) under a well-spacing plan of one per 80 acres; 14,000 acres withdrawn from mineral entry, with no drilling within ¼ mile of water sources, to protect *Crenichthys nevadae* habitat.

13. **Institutional reorganization**: [[Fort Soda (Zzyzx)]] transitioned to a desert research center under a State College/University System consortium, improving management control over Mohave chub habitat and enabling coordinated recovery efforts with desert tortoise (*Gopherus agassizii*) conservation.

14. **Stable/secure populations reported**: [[Cottonball Marsh Pupfish]], [[Saratoga Springs Pupfish]], [[Amargosa Pupfish]], and [[Salt Creek Pupfish]] were all reported in good condition with no immediate threats, though groundwater and geothermal development were flagged as long-term concerns.

15. **Possibly extinct taxon**: The [[Tecopa Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis calidae*) had not been located in recent surveys; a field trip was scheduled to investigate unconfirmed local sighting reports amid looming geothermal exploration threats.
