# Serological Differentiation of Yellowstone Lake Cutthroat Trout Spawning Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 study by James F. Liebelt, published in the *Proceedings of the Montana Academy of Sciences*, used serological methods to test whether cutthroat trout spawning in seven tributaries and the outlet of [[Yellowstone Lake]] belong to distinct populations. Blood serum pooled by sampling site was used to produce rabbit antisera, and antigenic differences were measured with [[Immunoelectrophoresis]] using absorbed antisera. The analysis indicated at least five discrete populations, consistent with the long-standing hypothesis that trout return to their natal streams to spawn.

## Background & Description

The study concerns [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki lewisi*, reported in the document as *Salmo clarki lewisi*; the taxon is now usually placed in *Oncorhynchus*). Earlier work on [[Yellowstone Lake]] trout showed that fish from a given spawning run generally return to their home stream ([[Homing Behavior]]). [[Cope]] (1957), using morphological characters, proposed that distinct races occupy the lake, each tied to a particular stream.

Serology was already widely used to separate fish populations. The paper cites work on erythrocyte antigens and blood groups in trout, [[Sockeye Salmon]], and several marine species, including [[Pacific Sardine]], [[Atlantic Herring]], [[Atlantic Redfish]], [[Spiny Dogfish]] and [[Winter Skate]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area:** Seven tributaries and the outlet of [[Yellowstone Lake]], sampled in Yellowstone National Park. Sites were spaced from about 1.6 km to about 29 km apart. Tributaries sampled were [[Pelican Creek]], [[Cub Creek]], [[Clear Creek]], [[Columbine Creek]], [[Chipmunk Creek]], [[Grouse Creek]] and the [[Yellowstone River]] inlet, plus the Yellowstone River outlet.

**Spawning condition:** Most fish were in spawning condition during the sampling window. Fish from Chipmunk Creek, and a few from Grouse Creek and the outlet, lacked mature gametes.

**Lake geography:** The paper notes no apparent geographic barriers within the lake that would prevent mixing of spawning runs. Isolation by the lake's arms (north, east, southeast and south) was proposed as a factor in population separation.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**
- Blood was collected June 29 to July 13, 1966, from at least 30 cutthroat trout per site (usually half female and half male) at eight sampling locations.
- Fish were anesthetized with MS-222, and blood was drawn from the heart. Serum was pooled by site.
- Rabbits were immunized with pooled serum through six intravenous injections. Antisera titers ranged from 1:6400 to 1:38,400.
- The Ouchterlony immunodiffusion method gave no clear distinctions. [[Immunoelectrophoresis]] (7 V/cm, 90 minutes) was more successful and required absorbed antisera.

**Results**
- Each antigen pool was tested against the other seven antisera (Table 1 summarizes all tests). Differences were usually single lines.
- Pelican Creek antiserum differed from Cub Creek, Columbine Creek, Yellowstone River outlet and Chipmunk Creek pools, and showed no difference with Clear Creek, Yellowstone River inlet or Grouse Creek pools.
- Chipmunk Creek antiserum differed from all other pools, making it the most distinct population.

**Population structure (five populations proposed)**
- **Population I:** Cub Creek, Clear Creek, Columbine Creek and Yellowstone River outlet. Their antisera gave nearly identical reactions with unrelated pools, indicating a homogeneous group. Tagging data from [[McCleave]] (1967) showed 2.6% (29 of 1137) of Clear Creek fish strayed into Cub Creek and 6.2% (119 of 1908) of Cub Creek fish strayed into Clear Creek.
- **Population II:** [[Pelican Creek]], considered discrete. The author suggests its earlier spawning run may explain the difference.
- **Population III:** [[Yellowstone River Inlet]], a distinct population in the southeast arm. Its lack of difference with Columbine Creek may reflect mixing, since the two mouths are about 9 km apart.
- **Population IV:** [[Chipmunk Creek]], the most antigenically diverse population.
- **Population V:** [[Grouse Creek]], discrete from Chipmunk Creek despite mouths only about 3 km apart and near-identical spawning times. Chipmunk and Grouse creeks are the sites farthest from others, which may explain their distinctiveness.

**Interpretation**
- Time of spawning may be more important than distance in isolating populations.
- Lack of differences between Cub and Clear creeks likely results from mouths only 1.6 km apart and nearly coincident spawning runs.
- Isolation by the lake's arms may have been significant in establishing the three southern and southeastern populations.

**Limitations**
- Results were affected by poor rabbit immune response, repeated freezing and thawing, antigen–antibody excess, and possible age or contamination effects.
- Each test was run at least twice, and some up to eight times.
