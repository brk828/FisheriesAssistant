# Recovery of Gila Trout Descended from Recently Hybridized Populations in the Mogollon Creek Drainage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Wild Trout and Salmon Genetics Laboratory report (University of Montana, Report 99/1) by [[Robb F. Leary]], [[Fred W. Allendorf]] and [[Naohisa Kanda]] evaluates an attempt to recover pure [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*) from populations in the Mogollon Creek drainage. [[South Diamond Creek]] was the source of those populations, but in 1995 a fire and rain event extirpated Gila trout there, and the replicate Mogollon Creek populations were found to contain hybrids with [[Rainbow Trout]] (*O. mykiss*). Because South Diamond Creek is one of only four remaining native Gila trout populations, the authors tried to salvage genetically pure fish from Mogollon Creek and Trail Canyon through pair matings in 1997 and 1998, screening parents with [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] at seven diagnostic loci.

The 1997 progeny were mostly Gila trout and first-generation backcrosses, and were judged valuable. The 1998 progeny showed roughly double the rainbow trout allele frequency, with evidence of second-generation backcrosses, so some apparent Gila trout may be hybrids. The authors recommend either excluding 1998 progeny or genotyping individuals at 40 or more nuclear DNA markers.

## Background & Description

Gila trout are native to the Gila River drainage of New Mexico and Arizona and the Verde River drainage of Arizona. The species is listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It is believed extinct in the Verde River drainage. Sampling from 1995 to 1997 found only four native populations in the Gila drainage: Main Diamond Creek and its replicate McKnight Creek (Mimbres drainage), a replicate of South Diamond Creek (Mogollon Creek drainage), Whiskey Creek, and Spruce Creek with its replicate in Big Dry Creek.

Two broad threats drove the decline. Human alteration of habitat reduced vegetation and increased sedimentation, water temperature and drought stress. Non-native fishes added further pressure: [[Brown Trout]] are thought to eliminate populations through predation and competition, and rainbow trout have eliminated many populations through [[Hybridization and Introgression]], producing random-mating hybrid swarms.

The surviving populations are isolated in small headwater streams, so drought, fire or flood can extirpate them. Allozyme data show substantial genetic divergence among native populations, with some alleles confined to single populations and others varying widely in frequency. The authors conclude that preserving this variation requires keeping all native populations extant.

The study used the [[Mescalero National Fish Hatchery]] as the rearing and spawning facility.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Mogollon Creek drainage populations were a replicate of South Diamond Creek stock. Once the source population was lost, hybrid descendants in the replicate populations made them a probable path to permanent loss of this genetic resource "within a few more generations" without intervention. The study's framing is therefore a conservation-triage problem: retain genetically pure individuals while hybridization is still limited.

Hybridization is the central ecological concern. Hybrid index distributions and gametic phase disequilibrium were used to infer how recently hybridization began. Frequent backcrossing into Gila trout, or new rainbow trout introductions, could make these populations unrecoverable.

The Discussion attributes the increase in rainbow trout genes between 1997 and 1998 to an additional infusion of rainbow trout genes into both streams. Possible causes include repeated rainbow trout introductions, or longer lifespans and multiple spawning in rainbow trout. The data cannot separate these explanations.

Field methods were tied to the habitat. Fish were collected by [[Electrofishing]] from the lower reaches of Mogollon Creek and Trail Canyon, transported by helicopter, and the streams were then treated with [[Antimycin A]] to remove remaining fish before reintroduction.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and breeding design**
- In 1996, a combined total of 600 trout were collected by electrofishing from Mogollon Creek and Trail Canyon and raised separately at the hatchery.
- Pair matings (one male, one female) were performed in 1997 and 1998. Each family egg lot was incubated separately, and parents were sacrificed for tissue sampling. Only egg lots appearing to derive from Gila-by-Gila matings were retained. Because parents were sacrificed, the 1997 and 1998 parent groups were entirely different individuals.

**Genetic markers and analytical methods**
- Horizontal starch gel electrophoresis assessed seven diagnostic loci: ADH*, FH-1*, GAPDH-4*, LDH-C*, PEPB*, PGK-2* and PGM-1*.
- Three tools were used: average allele frequency across loci, gametic phase disequilibrium (tested with [[Cockerham and Weir]] 1977 methods), and the [[Hybrid Index]] calculated per individual (Gila = 0, rainbow = 14 across the seven loci).
- Expected disequilibrium halves with each generation of hybridization (1.0 for F1, 0.5 for F2 and first backcrosses, 0.25 for second-generation derivatives, and so on).

**Trail Canyon results**
- Rainbow trout allele frequency roughly doubled from 1997 to 1998 (chi-square = 17.742, P < 0.001). Average Gila allele frequency (Table 1) was 0.879 in 1997 and 0.708 in 1998.
- Samples were small: N = 13 (1997) and N = 16 (1998).
- Mean gametic phase disequilibrium was 0.416 (1997) and 0.210 (1998) across 21 locus pairs. Sign test P = 0.040, so 1998 disequilibrium was lower, indicating a longer history of hybridization.
- Mean hybrid index was 1.69 in 1997 and 4.00 in 1998 (Wilcoxon P < 0.001).
- The 1997 group was roughly half pure Gila trout and half first-generation backcrosses. The 1998 group contained first-generation backcrosses, a second-generation hybrid group and, in the smaller group, fish of likely hybrid origin.

**Mogollon Creek results**
- Rainbow trout allele frequency also roughly doubled (chi-square = 48.901, P < 0.001). Average Gila allele frequency (Table 3) was 0.952 in 1997 and 0.881 in 1998.
- Samples were N = 80 (1997) and N = 209 (1998).
- Mean gametic phase disequilibrium was 0.676 (1997) and 0.395 (1998), with 19 of 21 pairs positive (P = 0.0001). All comparisons were significant in 1997 and 19 of 21 in 1998.
- Mean hybrid index was 0.66 in 1997 and 1.69 in 1998 (P < 0.001).
- The 1997 parents were mainly Gila trout and first-generation backcrosses. The 1998 parents were a mixture of Gila trout, first- and second-generation backcrosses, and a few second-generation hybrids.

**Reliability of zero hybrid index**
- 1997 progeny with a zero hybrid index had high probability of being pure Gila trout (Mogollon ≈ 0.91; Trail Canyon ≈ 0.95).
- 1998 Trail Canyon fish with a zero index had about a 0.25 chance of hybrid origin.
- 1998 Mogollon Creek fish with a zero index had a very high chance (≈ 0.9995) of including hybrids. The authors state this is "very likely."

**Management recommendations**
- The 1997 progeny have high conservation and restoration value. Crosses between zero-index fish from 1997 should be used for recovery.
- Excluding all 1998 apparent Gila-by-Gila progeny would destroy many Gila trout, though the authors describe this as the simplest option.
- Fin-clip genotyping at nuclear DNA regions could identify pure individuals. Heterozygosity of third-generation backcrosses is about one-eighth of loci, so about 40 loci per individual are needed for 97 percent certainty of excluding hybrids (probability a backcross is homozygous Gila at all loci = 0.031).
- Genotyping is expensive. For example, with 5 percent hybrids among 1,000 fish, 40 loci would detect all 50 hybrids with only about 0.218 probability, whereas 72 loci would give about 0.907.
- Spawning more fish from the 1999 hatchery stock is unlikely to be fruitful, since hybridization in potential 1999 parents is expected to be equal to or greater than in 1998. Fin-clip screening before spawning is suggested if the option is pursued.
