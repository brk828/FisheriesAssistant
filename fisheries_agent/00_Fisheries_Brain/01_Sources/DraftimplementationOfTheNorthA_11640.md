# U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Implementation of NAFTA with Mexico (Draft)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fishery-resources

## Executive Summary

This draft plan from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) assesses the fish and wildlife consequences of the [[North American Free Trade Agreement]] (NAFTA) along the U.S.-Mexico border and in Mexico. It argues that trade-driven growth will raise pressure on habitats, endangered species, wetlands, and wildlife trade, and that the agency currently lacks the staff and funds to respond. The plan requests roughly **$35.3 million and 184 FTEs**, split between U.S. border operations (about $10.5 million) and cooperative programs in Mexico (about $24.8 million). It treats fish and wildlife as "trust resources" and places its emphasis on training, baseline data, and bi-national coordination rather than on structural fixes alone.

## Background & Description

**Geography and population.** The border runs nearly 2,000 miles from the Gulf of Mexico to the Pacific Ocean. The [[Rio Grande]] forms about 1,200 miles of the boundary, and the [[Colorado River]] meets the border near Yuma, Arizona. The border population exceeds 6 million, roughly double the figure of a decade earlier (3 million in 1980). Close to 200 million crossings occur each year, making it the busiest international boundary crossing in the world.

**Institutional structure.** Two FWS regions cover the border: Region 1 (Portland, Oregon) handles California, and Region 2 (Albuquerque, New Mexico) handles the rest. Seven national wildlife refuges lie on or adjacent to the border. Cooperation dates to the 1936 Migratory Bird Convention, the 1974 agreement that created the [[U.S.-Mexico Joint Committee on Wildlife Conservation]], and the 1988 [[Tripartite Wetlands Agreement]]. Mexico joined [[Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species]] (CITES) in 1991 (the draft also gives 1992 for its party status; the two dates differ within the text).

**Economic context.** Maquiladora assembly plants, begun in 1965, are concentrated in border cities (73 percent). They earned US$3.6 billion in 1990, making them Mexico's second-largest source of foreign earnings. The document treats these plants as a central driver of both growth and environmental strain.

**Biological significance.** Mexico is described as having the greatest reptile diversity in the world, the greatest mammal diversity in the Western Hemisphere, and more endemic Neotropical species than any other country. It is also the main wintering ground for waterfowl and Neotropical birds that breed in Canada and the U.S.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat pressures.** Industrialization, urban growth, road and bridge construction, and agricultural expansion are expected to fragment habitat and degrade water. The document identifies more than 20 proposed Rio Grande bridges between the Gulf and Del Rio, Texas, each with potential to destroy brush and riparian habitat.

**Groundwater and springs.** Groundwater pumping in Sonora threatens springs that originate on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] in Arizona. Three federally listed fishes (Yaqui chub, Yaqui topminnow, and beautiful shiner) depend on these springs. The FWS has asked the [[International Boundary and Water Commission]] to help determine long-term plans for the agricultural lands and water depletion.

**Wetlands.** The document notes that 56 percent of U.S. wetlands have already been lost and that Mexican wetland losses cannot be monitored because inventory data and regulations are lacking. The Laguna Madre of Tamaulipas supports about 550,000 wintering redhead ducks, important pintail habitat, and five endangered sea turtle species. Coastal wetlands also serve as nursery and spawning grounds for commercial fish and shellfish.

**Contaminants.** Nuevo Laredo has no municipal wastewater treatment for about 218,000 people and discharges roughly 27 million gallons per day of untreated sewage into the Rio Grande from 30 point sources. Elevated organochlorine levels have been measured in the Rio Conchos. At the [[Tijuana Slough National Wildlife Refuge]], the Tijuana River carries up to 12 million gallons per day of untreated wastewater to the Pacific. The draft notes that its long-term chemical effects on refuge resources have not been assessed.

**Fishery and aquatic biodiversity.** The border's fish fauna ranges from abundant and diverse at the humid eastern and western ends to less diverse in the arid interior. Four basins are covered: the Rio Grande and Colorado (mostly U.S.), and the Rio Yaqui and Lago de Guzman (mostly Mexico). Interior mountain streams hold cold-water species, while lowland desert streams hold warm-water, often intermittent-stream fauna. Nonnative fish are identified as a serious threat. Baseline life-history data for many species are lacking, and some species remain undescribed.

## Key Findings

**1. Trade-driven pressures on wildlife and habitat.** NAFTA is expected to increase habitat conversion, water withdrawal, pesticide use, and wildlife trade. The document calls the agency's current capacity inadequate for these pressures.

**2. Wildlife trade and enforcement gaps.** The report cites a World Wildlife Fund analysis concluding that NAFTA will likely increase wildlife trade, with exotic leathers the most affected category. Key facts from the document:
- El Paso: about 60 wildlife import violations per month in 1990; 815 legal commercial shipments valued at US$25 million; seizures rising about 33 percent per year.
- San Diego: over 900 wildlife import incidents detected annually; about 44 percent of physically inspected shipments were found to contain illegal wildlife.
- Houston: about 25 percent of wildlife shipments seized; the document seeks port-of-entry designation.

**3. Endangered species concentration.** The document reports at least 460 threatened, endangered, proposed, and candidate species within 25 miles of the border. Examples include 37 federally listed and 158 candidate species in the first tier of Texas counties, 12 listed species in New Mexico, and 8 fish species in Arizona (including the Yaqui chub, Yaqui topminnow, beautiful shiner, desert pupfish, Gila topminnow, Sonoran chub, Yaqui catfish, and razorback sucker).

**4. Refuge management needs.** The refuge sections call for more staff, fencing, law enforcement, and public education. A proposed Operation Alliance/Joint Task Force 6 drug interdiction road is identified as a concern for the Buenos Aires, San Bernardino, and Cabeza Prieta refuges. Cabeza Prieta (nearly 900,000 acres) is proposed for an International Biosphere Reserve to protect the endangered Sonoran pronghorn.

**5. Fishery management recommendations.** Four components are proposed: biodiversity protection (fish surveys, monitoring, and restrictions on nonnative introductions), baseline data (life histories, nutrient capacity, and the potential use of native species such as Yaqui catfish for food and fishing), water management, and a fish management plan for recreational fishing. The fishery budget is about $1.35 million and 11 FTEs.

**6. Capacity building in Mexico.** Most Mexican states have no environmental agencies or programs, and the protected-area system is poorly managed. The plan proposes:
- Reserve manager training (RESERVA) and a national biodiversity training institute.
- Field stations and monitoring for migratory birds.
- Environmental impact assessment training under Mexico's 1988 General Ecology Law.
- Contaminant baseline data through the FWS Biomonitoring of Environmental Status and Trends (BEST) program.

**7. Budget summary.** The itemized budget lists the following U.S. border amounts: Law Enforcement $1.87 million (27 FTEs), Ecological Services $3.985 million (21 FTEs), Wildlife Resources $3.3 million (31 FTEs), and Fishery Resources $1.35 million (11 FTEs). Mexico items total about $24.8 million, with Environmental Assessment and Planning the largest ($10 million, 50 FTEs). The overall stated total is $35.3 million and 184 FTEs. The itemized FTE lines actually sum to 181 (90 U.S. and 91 Mexico), a discrepancy the reader should verify against the source budget.

**8. Conditions and phasing.** The initiative would start in stages, proceed only with Mexican government consent, and place FWS staff in Mexico only as a last resort and in advisory roles. The document expects it to be scaled down after about a decade as trained Mexican conservationists take over.
