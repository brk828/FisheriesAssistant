# Paleohydrology of the Southern Great Basin: Water Table Fluctuations Beneath the Nevada Test Site During the Late(?) Pleistocene

#ichthyology #hydrogeology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Open-file Report (80-569) by Isaac J. Winograd and Gene C. Doty, prepared with the U.S. Department of Energy, reconstructs past groundwater levels in the southern [[Great Basin]] to judge whether the [[Nevada Test Site]] (NTS) remains suitable as a repository for high-level and transuranic radioactive waste. The authors combine a field reconnaissance of spring deposits with Darcy's Law calculations. They conclude that pluvial water tables during the [[Pleistocene Epoch]] rose only modestly, and that deep water tables, thick unsaturated zones and long flow paths characterized the region then and are likely to again during future pluvials. The report is primarily a hydrogeologic and geomorphic study. The fish content is limited to the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], used as evidence for past spring-fed habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Objective:** Determine how high past water tables rose during pluvial (wetter) climates, and how far ground-water flow paths shortened, because both affect the isolation of buried waste.
- **Study area:** The eastern half of the NTS drains to the [[Ash Meadows]] ground-water basin. Discharge occurs along a spring lineament at Ash Meadows, about 40 km southwest of Mercury, Nevada.
- **Key features of the regional system:**
  - Very deep water tables, as deep as 600 m.
  - Low hydraulic gradients in the principal aquifers.
  - Ground-water flow paths tens of kilometers long.
  - Tuffaceous rocks with high sorptive capacity for radionuclides.
- **Aquifers:** A thick sequence of fractured Paleozoic carbonate rock forms the [[Lower Carbonate Aquifer]], which carries regional flow beneath topographic divides. Cenozoic valley-fill and tuff aquifers lie above it, separated by a Tertiary tuffaceous aquitard.
- **Terminology:** The authors separate pedogenic [[Caliche]] and arroyo-bottom [[Calcrete]] from spring-derived [[Tufa]], using field morphology alone. Tufa is recognized by fossilized vegetative mats, draped or mound-like forms, and restriction to the spring vicinity.
- **Dating context:** Pluvial periods between roughly 40,000 and 10,000 years ago (Wisconsin time) produced lakes and woodland at lower altitudes, with juniper growing as much as 600 m lower than today.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish evidence:** [[Miller (1948)]] and [[Hubbs and Miller (1948)]] reported the pupfish *Cyprinodon diabolis* in Devils Hole. The authors cite this as implying that the fish survived in a spring-fed habitat after a middle-to-early Pleistocene lake receded, then retreated into the deeper cavern as the water table dropped. The report does not study the fish directly.
- **Devils Hole:** A near-vertical cavern in the lower carbonate aquifer. The 1966 water level was about 15 m below the land surface, at an altitude of 719 m. Notches on its walls show former levels up to 6 m higher. Calcitic veins along a fault at its southwest end may record periods when water flowed from its mouth.
- **Desert setting:** Denudation rates of 9 to 18 cm per 1000 years mean that only about 9 to 18 m of erosion occurred per 100,000 years. Vegetation during pluvials was likely denser, which could have offset increased percolation.
- **Anthropogenic impacts:** Pumping for irrigation lowered the Ash Meadows water level. Dudley and Larson (1976) documented effects on desert pupfish habitat.

## Key Findings

1. **Past discharge was higher and farther northeast.** Calcitic veins in alluvium and lakebeds lie at roughly 720 to 770 m altitude north and northeast of Ash Meadows. The highest vein in alluvium (site 2, about 770 m) is about 14 km northeast of the modern spring line and about 50 m above the modern 719 m level.
2. **No tufa occurs above about 760 m.** Tufa was found at 720 to 760 m, but none between 760 and 940 m along the carbonate ridges. This absence suggests pluvial discharge did not exceed roughly 760 to 770 m.
3. **Modeled water-level rise beneath Frenchman Flat is modest.** Using the underflow form of Darcy's Law, with recharge assumptions of 2X to 5X modern and transmissivity of 0.5X to 1.5X modern, the authors estimate rises of 6 to 90 m beneath central [[Frenchman Flat]], 58 km northeast of Ash Meadows. During Wisconsin time the rise probably did not exceed 30 m.
4. **Future pluvials are unlikely to raise levels beyond 30 m.** Future levels might even fall about 10 m below modern levels, with the water table about 210 m beneath the playa center.
5. **Flow-path shortening does not preclude NTS use.** A 40 to 50 m rise could move discharge about 14 km closer; a 150 m rise could move it about 21 km closer. The authors argue this alone does not rule out the NTS, because suitability depends also on sorption, velocity and waste-form solubility.
6. **Recharge increase is uncertain.** Estimates range from a fivefold increase (Mifflin and Wheat, Brakenridge) to a much smaller one. The authors assume recharge mainly occurred on the uplands, and that a doubling of recharge with halved transmissivity (equivalent to fourfold recharge) gives a more realistic rise of about 20 m.
7. **Devils Hole's present level likely reflects tectonism and base-level lowering, not climate alone.** Possible causes include uplift of the ridge it sits on, warping, and the rise of [[Crystal Pool]] and [[Big Spring]] as new discharge outlets. Their altitudes (about 670 to 683 m) are 35 to 50 m lower than Devils Hole.
8. **Calcitic veins need caution as paleo-water-table indicators.** Veins high above the water table in carbonate rock likely formed below an earlier water table before late Cenozoic faulting. Dating is unresolved: site 4 veins gave 72,000 and 100,000 year uranium-thorium ages but sit about 200 m above the Frenchman Flat playa. Vein site 7 was dated at 400,000 to 750,000 years.
9. **Discharge may have migrated southwestward.** The authors tentatively propose a Pleistocene shift from northeast of Ash Meadows toward the modern spring lineament, with base level lowered by as much as 50 m. This is attributed to integration of the Amargosa and Death Valley drainages and periodic faulting.
10. **Conclusions for waste isolation.** The authors state that deep water tables, thick unsaturated zones (up to several hundred meters) and ground-water flow paths tens of kilometers long characterized the NTS region during the late Pleistocene, and will likely recur during future pluvials. These conditions support further technical consideration of the NTS as a repository.
