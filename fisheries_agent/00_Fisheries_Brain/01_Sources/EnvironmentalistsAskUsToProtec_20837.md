# Razorback Sucker Endangered Species Petition (1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

A coalition of six environmental groups petitioned the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] on March 15, 1989, to list the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The article, published in *The Salt Lake Tribune* on March 16, 1989, reports that the species is among the rarest fishes in the [[Colorado River Basin]], that recruitment has not been documented, and that only about 1,000 adults remain above [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Earlier listing attempts had failed because of a [[Memorandum of Understanding]] that the agency had signed with state wildlife agencies in 1981.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*), among the largest suckers in the United States, often reaching 12 pounds.
- **Appearance:** Olive-brown to black dorsally, yellowish-white ventrally. The common name derives from a sharp dorsal hump behind the head.
- **Native range:** Colorado River and major tributaries such as the [[Green River]]. Historically abundant from Mexico to Wyoming.
- **Companion native species:** [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]], all already federally listed as endangered.
- **Research context:** [[Colorado River Fish Project]], led by [[Harold Tyus]], has studied the species since 1980.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary threats:** Construction of [[Dams]] in the Colorado River system altered natural flow, and introduced non-native species disrupted the ecosystem.
- **Flood-dependent life cycle:** Razorbacks spawn during the peak of the spring flood cycle, and young fish use flooded bottomlands as nurseries. Dams capture floodwater, greatly reducing flooded bottomland habitat.
- **Predation:** Tyus suggested young razorbacks may be eaten by non-native fishes such as [[Common Carp]] and [[Catfishes]].
- **Reservoir populations:** Razorbacks in reservoirs along the lower Colorado River show the same reproductive failure. [[W. L. Minckley]] of [[Arizona State University]] has studied the lower-basin population for about 20 years and documented larvae and eggs but no young fish.

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** Approximately 1,000 razorbacks live in the Colorado River basin above Glen Canyon Dam, and the population is aging with no recorded recruitment.
- **Reproduction failure:** Adults are spawning and producing eggs and larvae, but young fish are not surviving to recruit into the population.
- **Conservation assessment:** Minckley described the species as "biologically endangered"; Tyus described it as in "bad shape" and one of the most endangered fishes in the Upper Colorado River basin.
- **Petitioners:** The [[Sierra Club]], [[The Wilderness Society]], [[National Audubon Society]], [[Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance]], [[Colorado Environmental Coalition]], and [[Northwest Rivers Alliance]].
- **Legal obstacle:** A 1981 memorandum of understanding between the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the Arizona Game and Fish Commission, signed May 27, 1981, committed the Service not to re-propose the species for listing as long as a 10-year reintroduction program in the Colorado, Verde, and Salt rivers of Arizona continued successfully. The agreement was described as legally dubious.
- **Pending action:** Bob Ruesink, state supervisor for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, said the agency would evaluate the petition to determine whether the razorback requires protection.
- **Stated rationale:** Petitioners argued that listing would bar federal agencies from funding or permitting projects that jeopardize the species and could generate additional research funds.
