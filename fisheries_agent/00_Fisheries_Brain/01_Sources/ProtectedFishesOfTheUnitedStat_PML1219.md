# Protected Fishes of the United States and Canada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document, a checklist-style conservation review by James E. Johnson, catalogs native freshwater fishes of the United States and Canada that receive legal protection or are of special concern to state, provincial, or federal agencies. It traces how the number of protected taxa has grown since the early 1970s, compares the status categories used by the [[American Fisheries Society]] Endangered Species Committee and the [[Deacon Classification]] (1979), and reports that by the mid-1980s roughly 90% of taxa in need of assistance received some form of protection. The text is largely a set of state-by-state and taxon-by-taxon lists, summarized here.

## Background & Description

Native North American freshwater fishes have been studied because of their vulnerability to habitat alteration, pollution, and introduced species, driven by extensive development in both nations. Early efforts were minimal: prior to the early 1970s, [[Miller 1972]] found that most states required size and bag limits on game fishes, and only five states (California, Florida, Kentucky, Tennessee, Idaho, based on the source text) documented protection for non-game native fishes.

The AFS Endangered Species Committee's first formal list (Miller 1972) used categories of "Endangered," "Threatened," "Rare," and "Vulnerable," later refined by [[Deacon et al. 1979]] into a two-category system of "Protected" (legal protection) and "Special Concern" (species that could become threatened, with information lacking). The author notes these categories were used by agencies to make listing decisions, and that "Special Concern" status does not confer legal protection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Drivers of decline:** Habitat alteration, pollution, and introductions are cited as principal causes of reduced native fish populations.
- **Regional variation:** Protection levels differ markedly. Only four states (Alabama, Alaska, Delaware, Hawaii) and six provinces reported no protected or special-concern fishes at the time of the earlier survey, while Western states hold many endemic, geographically restricted taxa (e.g., Nevada, Arizona, Texas, Utah).
- **Local extirpation consequences:** The text notes that losing the Johnny darter from eastern Colorado would not threaten the species overall, but Colorado's fauna would be poorer for it.
- **Endemism and small ranges:** Many listed taxa are spring-dwelling or restricted to single drainages, including pupfishes (*Cyprinodon*), springfishes (*Crenichthys*), and desert chubs (*Gila*).

## Key Findings

- **Growth in protection:** In 1972, 55% of fishes needing protection were already receiving some degree of it. By 1986, more than 90% received at least special-concern protection, and 126 taxa (62%) were fully protected by state or federal law.
- **Total scope:** The 1986 report lists about 517 taxa (described and undescribed species and subspecies) of native fishes deserving some degree of protection, drawn from the United States and Canada. Of these, about 50% (?) are fully protected by one or more government agencies. (Source-stated: 82% of the native fish fauna in the cited Western list; 37% in the Lee et al. 1980 summary.)
- **Protection gaps:** Only four states and six provinces had no protected or special-concern fishes in the earlier survey.
- **Western states' status:** The Western AFS Endangered Species Committee compiled 172 taxa of listed fishes in 13 Western states (Rinne 1984). Johnson and Rinne (1982) reported that 91% of native Arizona fishes were protected by state or federal law, and 42% of those in New Mexico were under protection.
- **Tennessee example:** Starnes and Etnier (1980) reported 59% of Tennessee's 290 fishes were protected or in need of management.
- **Table summaries:** Table 1 lists about 400+ taxa by family, including lampreys (8 taxa), sturgeons (8), salmonids (43), cyprinids (167), suckers (40), percids (106), and sculpins (16). Table 2 summarizes protection and special-concern status by state and province, with [[Arizona]] (8 protected, 17 special concern), [[California]] (15 protected, 3 special concern), and [[Kentucky]] (79 special concern) among the largest counts. Table 3 compares family-level totals across the 1972 [[Miller 1972]] list, the [[Deacon et al. 1979]] recommendations, and the 1986 [[Johnson Protection Survey]] results.
- **Example of key taxa:** Notable listed species include the [[Shortnose Sturgeon]] (protected in many Atlantic and Southeastern states), the [[Lake Sturgeon]], the [[Cui-ui]] (Nevada and Utah), the [[Razorback Sucker]], and the [[Pahrump Killifish]].
- **Table 3 totals:** The text reports 202 taxa recommended for protection by Deacon et al. (1979) versus 305 in 1986, with 182 taxa (126 legal protection) in the 1986 total of the table; the document notes these figures reflect differing scope and inclusions.
- **Purpose:** The document is intended to assist regulatory agencies in preparing endangered species lists and regulations, and it acknowledges review by AFS committee members and state and federal agencies.
