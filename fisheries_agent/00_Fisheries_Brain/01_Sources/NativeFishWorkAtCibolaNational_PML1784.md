# Native Fish Work at Cibola National Wildlife Refuge: January 2004 Maintenance and High Levee Pond Survey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum, dated 2 February 2004 and issued by the [[Arizona Fishery Resources Office]] in Parker, Arizona, reports two activities at [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] (CNWR). The first is a January 2004 maintenance check of a managed pond, which found little floating or bottom algae after chemical treatments. The second is a night survey of [[High Levee Pond]] using [[Trammel Netting]] and [[Electrofishing]], conducted with an [[Arizona State University]] fishery management class. The survey captured [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] and was interpreted as evidence that the pond functions as native fish habitat and that juvenile recruitment is occurring.

## Background & Description

The memorandum is an internal communication addressed to fisheries staff, refuge managers and academic partners, including the Lower Colorado River list-serve. Its subject is native fish work at CNWR. The author is Chuck Minckley, Project Coordinator, MFRO-Parker. The document is a short field report rather than a research paper, and it includes a cover-letter style header, a memo body, and a document control number (DCN NO. USFWS-AZFRO.PA-04-012).

Two species are reported as the focus of the native fish work:

- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

A third non-fish animal, the [[Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*), was also captured. The memo mentions a large [[Flathead Catfish]] that staff hoped to catch with baited lines.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond maintenance and algae control.** The January 2004 maintenance check on 30 January 2004 was a visual inspection for floating and bottom algae. Only a small amount of floating algae (<10 cm²) was seen, and the water was clear enough to show that little algae remained on the bottom. Previously, dead or senescent algae on the bottom had built up to several inches thick. Summer algae production produces a thick surface mat. Decaying material raises [[Biological Oxygen Demand]] in the system.

The author attributes the massive algal blooms to [[Phosphorus Loading]] from the pond's concrete walls. Two remedies were used:

- An [[Alum Bentonite Treatment]] to bind and remove excess phosphorus.
- A commercial bacterial product ("Bacteria in a Bag") to break down bottom organic debris.

The memo states that these treatments "seem to have worked," and that bi-annual monitoring will continue. No fish were seen during the January visit. The author assumes they were buried in the substrate because water temperatures were cool. Refuge staff reported seeing fish on warmer days during the month.

**Native fish habitat at High Levee Pond.** The author concludes that High Levee Pond functions as native fish habitat. This conclusion rests on the number of juvenile razorback suckers and small bonytail captured. The memo is careful to say that the catch cannot directly measure recruitment, but it notes that recruitment appears to have occurred over the last two years.

## Key Findings

**Survey logistics (30–31 January 2004)**

- Survey began at 6 PM on 30 January with an orientation for the Arizona State University Wildlife Management Class.
- Two 300' [[Trammel Netting]] nets were set and the pond was electrofished. Nets were left overnight, and activities ended around 11 PM.
- On 31 January, six baited lines attached to small buoys were set to target the large flathead catfish. These lines will remain in the pond and be checked periodically.
- Fish were processed for species, sex (when possible), length, weight, and presence or absence of [[PIT Tagging]] tags. Untagged fish over 15 cm were tagged with 125 MHz PIT tags.

**Bonytail catch**

- 81 bonytail were captured, including recaptures.
- 24 were new fish over 15 cm and were PIT tagged.
- 55 fish were under 15 cm.

**Razorback sucker catch**

- 42 razorback suckers were captured.
- 6 were tagged recaptures.
- 7 untagged large individuals were over 650 g. Two of these were ripe, tuberculate males. No running-ripe females were captured.
- 29 small juveniles measured 13–26 cm and weighed 30–90 g (the scan reads "g0"; 90 g is the likely value).
- Totals: 13 adults and 29 juveniles.

**Other captures**

- Four bullfrogs were also taken.

**Recruitment and habitat conclusion**

- The memo notes that only a small number of juvenile razorback suckers had been taken in the previous three years, which raised concern about recruitment.
- The 29 juveniles, together with the many small bonytail, are taken as evidence that High Levee Pond is supporting native fish habitat and that recruitment has occurred in the last two years.
