# Respiratory Quotients in Goldfish and Rainbow Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 study by [[Meruit Narayan Kutty]] (University of Toronto, published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, 25(8)) measured the [[Respiratory Quotient]] (R.Q., the ratio of CO2 produced to O2 consumed) of [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*, tested at 20 °C) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*, tested at 15 °C). The goal was to test the common assumption that fish metabolism is entirely aerobic, and to determine whether anaerobic energy contributes to total expenditure across levels of spontaneous and forced activity and ambient oxygen concentrations.

Key result: fish in well-oxygenated water were essentially aerobic at rest (R.Q. ≈ 1), but goldfish sustained R.Q. values well above unity under low oxygen, while rainbow trout showed a much smaller anaerobic capacity.

## Background & Description

The study builds on earlier [[Respiratory Quotient]] work, beginning with [[Provencal and Humboldt Studies]] (1809) on tench and carp, followed by [[Gréhant Measurements]] (1870), and the more accurate [[Jolyet and Regnard Studies]] (1877), whose R.Q. values ranged from 0.56 to 0.96. [[Bosworth Et Al 1936 Methodology]] introduced decarbonated, pH-controlled water with refined carbon dioxide analysis, which this study adopted.

Methodological challenges are central: measuring CO2 in natural waters is difficult, and CO2 may not be released immediately from tissues. The author addressed these by using a [[Phosphate Buffer Solution]] circulation system (285 mg/L Na2HPO4, pH about 8.0), which keeps dissolved CO2 analyzable, and by holding fish in closed respirometers for extended periods.

Two respirometers were used:
- [[Fry Respirometer]]: an annular, rotatable chamber used for spontaneous ("routine") activity and low swimming speeds.
- [[Blazka Respirometer]]: a tubular, propeller-driven chamber used for higher steady swimming speeds.

Fish were yearling trout and goldfish of unknown age, of similar size, held in outdoor stock tanks and acclimated for at least 12 days in six 70-L tanks (air saturation, 90–95%) or a 15-L tank (about 15% air saturation, 1.4 ± 0.5 ppm O2). Trout were fed a liver, yeast and terramycin supplement to maintain health.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fish are often exposed to hypoxia, and the species differ in tolerance. Goldfish are known to withstand low oxygen well, whereas rainbow trout are relatively intolerant. The study examines how each species balances aerobic and anaerobic energy supply during:
- **Routine (spontaneous) activity**, measured as body lengths travelled per 30 min.
- **Forced steady swimming**, measured as body lengths per second, with fish exercised for 5–12 hr in most runs.

Related concepts include [[Anaerobic Metabolism]], the [[Lactic Acid Accumulation]] pathway, [[Swimbladder Gas Exchange]], and [[Standard Metabolic Rate]]. The author notes that results apply only to the test temperatures (20 °C for goldfish, 15 °C for trout), citing [[Crucian Carp]] studies in which anoxic survival differed greatly between 5 °C and 20 °C.

## Key Findings

**Routine R.Q. in fish acclimated to air saturation**
- Goldfish R.Q. was 1.02 ± 0.043 (one SE) at 50–95% air saturation. Below 50%, R.Q. rose progressively. At about 15% air saturation, 27 determinations gave a mean of 1.94 ± 0.244.
- Rainbow trout R.Q. was 0.96 ± 0.0037 at 50–95% air saturation. Nine determinations below 50% gave a mean of 1.42 ± 0.15 for the five runs at about 25% air saturation (2.3–2.7 ppm).
- Metabolic rate dropped below 50% air saturation, while spontaneous activity stayed high.
- Slopes of O2 and CO2 against activity did not differ significantly in goldfish (t = 0.45, d.f. 54), so R.Q. did not change with activity at high oxygen.

**Routine R.Q. after acclimation to low oxygen**
- Goldfish acclimated to 15% air saturation (three fish, 23 determinations at 50–95%) gave R.Q. 0.90 ± 0.084. Below 25% air saturation (0.7–1.9 ppm), the mean R.Q. was 2.12 ± 0.206 (46 determinations below 50%).
- Acclimation did not change R.Q. at a given oxygen level, so it did not increase anaerobic capacity. It did reduce oxygen consumption, with activity rates about 50% higher.

**Forced (steady) swimming**
- Initial (first-hour) R.Q.s were higher than steady values: goldfish initial mean 1.32 vs. steady mean 1.03; rainbow trout initial 1.16 vs. steady 0.91. Both differences were significant (P < 0.01).
- Goldfish steady R.Q. fell with increasing swimming effort, suggesting protein and fat use at higher effort. At the lowest effort it stayed above unity, implying continuous anaerobic contribution.
- Rainbow trout steady R.Q. was below unity at all effort levels, suggesting aerobic steady-state swimming.
- Peak O2 consumption was about 400–418 ml/kg/hr in goldfish (about 5 body lengths/s) and about 350–440 ml/kg/hr in trout. Peak CO2 output was about 600 ml/kg/hr in goldfish (estimated) and about 375 ml/kg/hr in trout.
- Goldfish acclimated to low oxygen (one fish, 2.4 lengths/s) showed O2 consumption about 100 ml/kg/hr lower than air-saturated fish, with an R.Q. trend similar to the controls.

**Interpretation and conclusions**
- At adequate oxygen, both species are aerobic at rest and during steady swimming, but both derive some energy anaerobically during the initial exercise phase.
- Goldfish can sustain R.Q. near 2 under near-anoxic conditions for long periods; rainbow trout asphyxiated when R.Q. reached about 1.4, so its anaerobic capacity is much lower.
- The lower metabolic rate at a given activity in low oxygen is not compensated by anaerobic energy, a finding consistent with [[Beamish 1964 Oxygen Studies]].
- Results apply only to the tested temperatures. An analytical error of about 20% in individual R.Q. determinations limits fine-scale interpretation.
- The author cites [[Hochachka 1961 Goldfish Study]], who showed goldfish excrete organic CO2 under anoxia, and [[Privolnev 1954 Crucian Carp]], who reported an R.Q. of 4.3 in hypoxic crucian carp at 20 °C.
