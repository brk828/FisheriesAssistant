# An Early-Season Mayfly

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Leptophlebiidae #aquatic-entomology #mayfly

## Executive Summary

This article describes [[Paraleptophlebia adoptiva]], commonly known as the [[Iron Blue Dun]], an early-season mayfly that emerges during Michigan's trout season opening, often in harsh weather conditions. The species is notable for hatching from mid-April through early June, with peak emergence in early May, providing critical food for trout when few other aquatic insects are active. The document details the complete life cycle from egg through nymph to winged adult stages (dun and spinner), including morphological descriptions, habitat preferences, and emergence behavior. Three additional congeners ([[Paraleptophlebia mollis]], [[Paraleptophlebia praepedita]], and [[Paraleptophlebia debilis]]) are briefly noted as later-season species.

## Background & Description

[[Paraleptophlebia adoptiva]] nymphs are approximately 3/8-inch long at maturity, with one-third of the length comprising three slender, thread-like tails. The nymphs are dark brown to almost black with smooth, polished body surfaces. Distinctive features include narrow, deeply-cleft gills on the first seven abdominal segments that are gray with black centers, contrasting with the dark body. Wing pads extend well back over the third abdominal segment near maturity. A narrow pale line may appear down the middle of the thorax shortly before transformation.

The dun stage (first winged form) has a dull grayish-mahogany colored body about 3/8-inch long with three slender, smoky-amber tails of equal length. Wings are cloudy slate blue (source of common name), and legs are drab. The insect is appropriately imitated with a No. 16 hook.

The spinner stage (final adult form) exhibits clear, transparent wings with faint yellowish-brown veins, longer and more slender legs and tails than the dun, and a trimmer, darker, polished body. Males have disproportionately elongated front legs and mahogany abdomens with indistinct dark gray bands on middle segments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Paraleptophlebia adoptiva]] occupies [[Michigan]] trout streams north of Town Line 16. Eggs are laid by females in the stream, settling to the bottom and adhering to stones and other objects. Several weeks elapse before hatching into tiny nymphs near the limit of naked-eye visibility. Nymphs feed and grow during late summer, fall, and winter.

Nymphs feed primarily on algae, sessile diatoms, and tissues of aquatic plants. They spend most time under stones in the stream bottom but also clamber over other bottom materials and through submerged plants and mosses. Despite secretive habits, nymphs are frequently eaten by fish.

The species demonstrates remarkable cold-hardiness, emerging even during snowstorms at trout season opening. Hatching occurs from mid-April until the first week of June, with peak emergence during the fore part of May. Weather conditions may influence hatching period by a few days. Hatching may occur at almost any time of day—morning, afternoon, or evening.

Emergence takes one or two seconds, but duns float on the water for considerable time (perhaps a minute or longer) before flying to protective cover of streamside trees or shrubs. In swift-flowing sections, duns may float 60 yards or more before taking flight, making them vulnerable to trout predation.

## Key Findings

**Life Cycle Characteristics:**
- Eggs require several weeks to hatch after being laid on stream bottom
- Nymphs develop through late summer, fall, and winter
- Emergence period: mid-April to first week of June, peak in early May
- Dun stage duration: 24 hours in warm, dry weather; several days in cool, rainy weather
- Spinner stage is of short duration; adults cannot feed as jaws and alimentary tracts are atrophied and functionless

**Unique Mayfly Biology:**
- Mayflies are the only insects that molt after developing functional wings
- First winged stage (dun) transforms into final stage (spinner) after molting
- Mating and egg-laying occur only in spinner stage
- Adults survive only on nutrients stored during nymphal stage

**Related Species in Michigan:**
- [[Paraleptophlebia mollis]]: emerges June to early July, smaller than adoptiva, gray wings in dun stage, males with semi-transparent white middle abdominal segments in spinner stage
- [[Paraleptophlebia praepedita]]: larger species, hatches July-August
- [[Paraleptophlebia debilis]]: emerges August-September on smaller creeks and brooks, closely resembles adoptiva in size and color

**Ecological Significance:**
- All [[Paraleptophlebia]] species are valuable as fish food
- [[Paraleptophlebia adoptiva]] is particularly important to fly-fishermen due to early-season emergence when few other stream insects are active to stimulate trout feeding
- Hardy habit of appearing during harsh weather conditions at season opening makes it a reliable food source

**Fly-Tying Recommendations:**
- No. 16 hook for dun stage
- Plain slate blue feather for wings
- Mixture of olive and gray hackle, tied sparse
- Small wisp of olive hackle for tails
