# Age and Growth of Pike (*Esox lucius*) in Windermere: Opercular Bone Method

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 paper by W. E. Frost and Ch. Kipling, presented at the Verhandlungen Internationale Vereinigung für Theoretische und Angewandte Limnologie (Stuttgart), tests the [[Opercular Bone]] as an alternative to the [[Scale Method]] for estimating age and growth of [[Northern Pike]] from [[Windermere]]. The authors report that the opercular bone has well-defined annuli, allowing direct [[Back-Calculation]] of growth. Its main limitation is discoloured spongy tissue, which can obscure the first or second annulus in pike over 50 cm. To detect missing annuli, the authors use the [[Walford Plot]]. They conclude that opercular bones are acceptable for age and growth determination but cannot be used for age alone, and that discarding "bad" bones biases results.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), referred to as pike throughout.
- **Previous methods:** European and American workers had used scales to determine pike age and growth.
- **Problem with scales:** Critical examination revealed difficulties and inaccuracies, particularly for back-calculated growth.
- **Alternative structure:** The [[Opercular Bone]], previously used by [[Le Cren]] (1947) for [[Perch]] (*Perca fluviatilis*), was applied to pike for the first time in this study.
- **Measurement technique:** Figure 1 of the paper shows the cardboard-strip method for back-calculation on both scales and opercula. The growth origin on the opercular bone is located indirectly from indentations on the bone outline.
- **Study material:** Pike caught in winter-set [[Gill Net]]s in Windermere during 1946–47.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spongy bone artefact.** A discoloured spongy tissue at the base of the opercular bone is the main obstacle. It is found particularly in pike over 50 cm and can obscure the first and sometimes second annuli. The authors therefore needed a way to determine whether an annulus was missing on a given bone.

**Length overlap between age classes.** Pike of age 1 and age 2 overlap in length, particularly between 25 and 35 cm. A back-calculated length of 30 cm to the first visible annulus could therefore represent either age 1 (no missing annulus) or age 2 (first annulus missing).

**Growth model.** The authors assume a female Windermere pike has an ultimate length of 100 cm, to which growth is directed. The [[Walford Plot]] is valid for this portion of pike life, where successive annual increments decline proportionally. Prior knowledge of the fish's age is not required, which made the method suitable.

**Comparison with other English data.** [[Hartley]] (1947) determined pike ages from scales in 93 fish from small rivers and dykes in East Anglia. These fish appeared to grow more slowly than the Windermere fish. Hartley later indicated that the East Anglian ages were probably overestimated, meaning those fish grew faster than published data suggest. The authors note this qualifies the value of these frequently cited data for comparisons.

## Key Findings

### 1. Opercular bone is suitable for back-calculation
The opercular bone has well-defined annuli, and direct back-calculation to these annuli was judged justified for estimating growth. The authors accept it for age and growth determination, but not for age alone.

### 2. Walford plot detects missing annuli
- The x-axis represents length at age *n* and the y-axis length at age *n* + 1, both drawn to the same scale.
- A straight "growth line" results if successive annual increments decline proportionally.
- The line is drawn through the back-calculated pairs and through a point on the diagonal at 100 cm (ultimate length).
- Where the line cuts the y-axis gives length at age 1. In the worked example, this intercept is 24 cm.

### 3. Worked example: a missing first annulus
- Pike length: 88 cm.
- Back-calculated lengths (cm) across six annuli: 42.4, 57.6, 67.5, 74.8, 80.2, 85.4, with the last annulus at the edge (88 cm).
- The length at the first visible annulus (42.4 cm) does not match the length at age 1 (24 cm).
- The growth line gives a length at age 2 of 42.5 cm, which closely matches the first visible annulus (42.4 cm). This indicates the first annulus is missing.
- Result: the fish is aged **8 years**, with the first (missing) annulus at 24 cm.

### 4. Theoretical growth table
- A theoretical growth table (Table 1) was constructed so individual back-calculated growth data can be checked without drawing a plot for each fish. The table is described in Frost and Kipling (1959).
- Table 1 gives theoretical lengths at ages 1–7 for growth categories (fast, medium, slow, very slow) for females with ultimate length 100 cm, and for a second series with ultimate length 75 cm.
- Figure 3 shows the theoretical Walford plot, based on a length at age 1 of 25 cm and an ultimate length of 100 cm.

### 5. Example of applying the table to a "bad" bone
- A female of 68.5 cm gives back-calculated lengths of 27.0, 49.0, 62.0, and 68.5 cm.
- The first visible ring is at 27 cm, which could be a slow grower with a missing first annulus at 15 cm.
- Because the subsequent growth is not that of a slow grower, the fish is considered a fast grower with 27 cm as age 1. The authors conclude there is no missing annulus.

### 6. Bias and the role of scales
- Omitting "bad" bones gives biased results, so they must be retained and interpreted.
- Where growth is difficult to interpret from the opercular bone, the scales may provide further information.

### 7. Growth data
- Figure 4 presents growth results from opercular readings of Windermere pike caught in 1946–47.
- These data are the authors' main contribution to the length-for-age record of English pike.

### Summary of Methodological Conclusions
- The opercular bone is an accepted structure for age and growth estimation of pike.
- The [[Walford Plot]] and theoretical growth table are used to identify missing annuli.
- Operculars should not be used for age alone, and scales can supplement difficult cases.
