# Fishery Investigation of Lake Mead, Arizona-Nevada, Separation Rapids to Boulder Canyon, 1978-79

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) for the [[Water and Power Resources Service]] provides a baseline inventory of the fishes, invertebrates, plankton and aquatic vegetation of the upper [[Lake Mead]] reservoir, from Separation Rapids on the [[Colorado River]] to Boulder Canyon. Fieldwork ran from June 1978 to April 1979. The study recorded 10,129 fish of 19 species at 57 sampling sites, and concluded that the reservoir fishery has shifted from [[Largemouth Bass]] toward [[Striped Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]], while native Colorado River fishes are nearly extirpated from the reservoir. The benthic invertebrate community is extremely sparse, and submersed aquatic vegetation is largely absent because of annual water-level fluctuation.

## Background & Description

**Reservoir history.** [[Hoover Dam]] was built across Black Canyon between 1931 and 1935, and the reservoir reached capacity in 1941. The lake extends 185 km east-west across three basins (Gregg, Virgin and Boulder), with a maximum storage area of 63,902 ha, a volume of 32,937 x 10^6 m^3 and 1,363.8 km of shoreline. Management responsibilities are split: the [[National Park Service]] handles recreation, the AGFD and Nevada Division of Wildlife (NDW) manage fish and wildlife, and the Bureau of Reclamation controls water releases.

**Fishery phases.** From 1935 to 1969 the reservoir was managed as a warm-water fishery built on centrarchids. Threadfin shad were released in 1954 as forage. Glen Canyon Dam (1964) and the filling of Lake Powell in the late 1960s reduced inflows, and the bass fishery declined. In 1969 the NDW began diversifying the fishery with exotic salmonids and, most importantly, 20,000 fingerling striped bass, which reproduced successfully in 1973.

**Study design.** Zooplankton were sampled at 29 limnetic stations (September-October 1978); benthic invertebrates at 26 littoral transects (1977-1979); and fish at 57 locations using trammel and gill nets, seines and electrofishing, with 76 collections over ten months. Ten fish per species per location were examined for endoparasites, and scales or spines were used for age and growth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical limnology.** Surface temperatures range from about 2 to 29 °C, with a recorded high of 34.1 °C in August 1952. The lake stratifies from late May or June, with the thermocline at 10-15 m by July, turns over in October, and is fully destratified by January or early February. Dissolved oxygen is at least 4 ppm in the hypolimnion in winter. Secchi readings exceed 13 m in clear areas, but turbidity is high near the Virgin and Moapa river mouths (10-13 cm) and below the silt-laden Colorado River.

**Trophic status.** The lake is classed as mesotrophic to oligotrophic overall. [[Las Vegas Bay]] and [[Las Vegas Wash]] are meso-eutrophic and eutrophic, respectively, because of wastewater nutrient loading.

**Water-level fluctuation.** A strong seasonal drawdown (March-November, for downstream irrigation) and daily hydropower fluctuations keep the littoral zone regularly exposed. The author argues this is the root cause of the sparse invertebrate and plant communities, and of poor spawning success for black bass and other species. Evaporation removes an estimated 7,402 x 10^6 m^3 annually.

**Vegetation.** Ten higher aquatic plant species were identified (Table 2). [[Sago Pondweed]] is the dominant submersed macrophyte, but growth is limited to about 10-13 m depth and is largely absent where shorelines are steep. Salt cedar (Tamarix) is the most common riparian shrub, and [[Common Reed]] and cattails occur along the river. The author describes this as a "bath-tub ring" reservoir typical of hydropower impoundments.

**Invertebrates.** Zooplankton averaged 37.4 organisms/L across stations (cladocerans 18.4, copepods 12.6, rotifers 6.4), with the highest values in lower Overton Arm (89.4 organisms/L maximum). Benthic invertebrates were extremely depauperate: abundance ranged from zero to 0.763 organisms/m^2, with [[Midge Larvae]] (Chironomidae, 14.3% frequency of occurrence) and tubificid oligochaetes (7.1%) most common. [[Scuds]] (Gammarus lacustris) were absent from the reservoir but important in the Grand Canyon. [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] (Procambarus clarkii) is now established and an important prey item.

**Parasites.** The main findings were: leeches on 79% of channel catfish; [[Anchor Worm]] (Lernaea cyprinacea) on 2% of largemouth bass and 0.1% of red shiners; a [[Bass Tapeworm]] (Proteocephalus sp.) in 6% of largemouth bass; the roundworm Contracaecum sp. in 84% of largemouth bass, 24% of channel catfish and 14% of bluegill; and larval flukes (strigeid metacercariae) in 46% of bluegill. The author judged that none reached levels that seriously impact the fishery.

## Key Findings

**Fish inventory and relative abundance**

- 10,129 fish of 19 species were collected at 57 sites.
- Seines (16 locations, 9,404 fish, 13 species; 6,423 m² sampled) were dominated by [[Red Shiner]] (0.63 fish/m²), [[Threadfin Shad]] (0.50) and [[Mosquitofish]] (0.21).
- Gill and trammel nets (600 net-hours, 173 fish, 9 species) caught mainly [[Carp]] (0.10 fish/hr), threadfin shad (0.05) and channel catfish (0.03).
- Electrofishing (500 min, 551 fish, 13 species) produced largemouth bass (0.332 fish/min), carp (0.290) and bluegill (0.216), together over 76% of catch.

**Native species**

- Native fishes are largely absent from the reservoir. [[Speckled Dace]] (56 fish, Spencer Creek only) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (3 adults in 1978, including two gravid females) are the notable records.
- The razorback sucker is rare, with no evidence of successful reproduction in Lake Mead. The author recommends it be treated as endangered; Arizona added it to its protected fish list effective 1 April 1980.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Mountain-Sucker]] persist in the Colorado River, not the reservoir.
- Historical and hypothetical records include [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Several are federally listed as endangered. Humpback chub broodstock were collected from the Little Colorado River in 1978, and 12 adults remained as of April 1980.

**Introduced species and their effects**

- [[Threadfin Shad]] (3,285 collected) became the main forage base but caused suspected competition with bass fry and crappie.
- [[Striped Bass]]: angling effort rose from 2.3% (1975) to 11.3% (1977); the estimated harvest grew from 185 (1971) to 13,250 (1977). Moribund spring fish were observed. The author warns of a possible population boom and crash.
- [[Channel Catfish]] replaced largemouth bass as the leading game species in 1977 (about 170,000 harvested, 30% of catch). Fecundity of 13 fish ranged 2,200 to 33,000 eggs.
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: harvest fell from over 400,000 per year (1958-1960s) to 136,000 in 1977 (24.5% of catch). The author attributes the decline mainly to water-level fluctuation destroying nests and littoral habitat.
- [[Carp]] (242 collected) were the second most abundant large fish. The author documents uprooting of aquatic vegetation, silt-driven turbidity, and egg predation on centrarchid nests.
- [[Black Crappie]] (7 collected in the survey, but an estimated 148,000 harvested in 1977, second only to catfish by number).
- [[Rainbow Trout]] harvest peaked at about 127,553 (1975) and fell to 4,463 (1976) and 13,826 (1977), with poor survival attributed to limnological change, striped bass predation and migration losses.
- [[Coho Salmon]] were stocked from 1969 (36,710 fish); the fishery declined from 1975 and was eliminated by 1977.
- [[Cutthroat Trout]] stocking rose to 148,426 in 1977, but harvest fell to 171 that year (0.02% of effort).
- [[Mosquitofish]] (1,329 seined) reached 0.21 fish/m² but are seldom used as prey, and the author judges them unlikely to become abundant beyond shallow warm thermals.

**Management conclusions and recommendations**

- The author concludes that water-level manipulation is the most important limiting factor for the fishery; he recommends that reservoir fisheries be given greater weight in release decisions through Hoover Dam.
- Management objectives proposed: increase the average size and numbers of gamefish, and restore the missing invertebrate trophic level.
- Recommended research: a joint AGFD-NDW study on drawdown impacts on bass nesting (initial results suggested a 25% loss of nests, Jacobson 1977) and a striped bass and shad population study, which the author notes was only proposed at the time.
- Recommended restrictions on live bait species (for example [[Golden Shiner]], [[Utah Chub]], [[Red Shiner]]) to prevent further introductions, with the warning that carp control techniques remain undeveloped.
