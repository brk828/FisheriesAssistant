# Resurrecting the Dammed: A Look at Colorado River Restoration

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This BioScience feature (December 2001) by Jeffrey P. Cohn examines efforts to restore [[Riparian Habitat]] and native fish along the [[Colorado River]] after more than a century of dam building, diversion, and flow regulation. The river is now among the most controlled in the world, and its historic flood pulses, backwaters, and cottonwood-willow forests have largely disappeared. Restoration is under way at several sites, including the [[Ahakhav Preserve]] and four national wildlife refuges, and a multispecies conservation plan covering 57 listed or candidate species is in development. The article stresses that water availability, the choice of restoration methods, and whether river flows can be reshaped will largely determine success.

## Background & Description

The Colorado flows about 1,450 miles from the Rocky Mountains to the Gulf of California. It drains 242,000 square miles in seven US states, plus about 2,000 square miles in Mexico, and drops more than 10,000 feet in elevation.

Before regulation, the river flooded each spring and early summer, scouring sandbars, creating backwater marshes, oxbows, and sloughs, and recharging groundwater. Flows varied widely from year to year. The river is now managed, and its water is allocated among users:

- Upper and lower US basins are each entitled to 7.5 million acre-feet per year.
- Under a 1944 treaty, the US delivers 1.5 million acre-feet to Mexico.
- The 15.5 million acre-feet total exceeds the river's average annual flow of about 15 million acre-feet.
- The river is interrupted by 10 major dams, 4 reservoirs, and more than 80 diversions, beginning with Laguna Dam (completed 1909). [[Hoover Dam]] and Glen Canyon Dam together hold more than 55 million acre-feet.
- Dams irrigate more than 3.7 million acres in the US and Mexico, supply drinking water to nearly 30 million people, and generate more than 4,400 megawatts.

Regulation changed the river's thermal regime (warm to cold water), trapped sediment that would have formed sandbars, and stopped nearly all freshwater from reaching the Mexican delta. Philip Fradkin's 1981 description of the Colorado as "a river no more" is quoted.

**Riparian loss:** Robert Ohmart (Arizona State University) estimates that as much as 400,000 acres of riparian habitat existed before dam construction, though he notes this is uncertain. About 90,000 acres remained in 1938, and the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] estimates about 5,000 acres exist today. Most remaining riparian areas are dominated by [[Saltcedar]], an introduced Eurasian tree that tolerates salty, dry soils where native cottonwoods and willows fail.

**Species context:** The [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], a subspecies of Traill's flycatcher, was once locally common in the Southwest. Recent surveys found about 900 territories, and the bird was listed as endangered in 1995. It was the first riparian bird in the Southwest to be listed. Native Colorado River fishes, adapted to flood pulses and warm, uninterrupted flows, have been hurt by altered flows and by introduced sunfish, bass, carp, and catfish, which prey on native eggs and young.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regulatory drivers:** The [[Endangered Species Act]] shaped restoration priorities. The [[US Fish And Wildlife Service]] (FWS) designated critical habitat for four native Colorado River fishes in 1994 and issued a 1997 biological opinion requiring the Bureau of Reclamation to consider how routine dam operations affect listed species. According to Robert Marshall (The Nature Conservancy, Arizona), these actions forced agencies to take issues "previously given little more than lip service" seriously.

**Water as the limiting factor:** Restoration depends on securing water from agricultural, municipal, and industrial users. Bertin Anderson (Revegetation and Wildlife Management Center, Blythe, California) reports that many early efforts failed because they were too small and did not test soil, water table depth, or salinity. He advises periodic flushing with water to leach salts and mimic natural flooding.

**Natural processes:** Without floods, salts and litter accumulate and seedlings fail to establish. Ohmart argues that patches smaller than about 50 acres cannot sustain fully functioning riparian systems and that a large flood every 25 to 50 years is needed to rearrange sediments and seed beds. Julie Stromberg (Arizona State University) argues that restoration focuses too narrowly on cottonwoods, willows, and mesquites, rather than the 300 to 500 riparian plant species she says historically occurred in the system.

**Refuges and sites:** Restoration is under way at several locations:

- [[Ahakhav Preserve]] (Colorado River Indian Tribes, about 1,000 acres): a 2-mile side channel was dredged in 1998 and 1999 and supports bulrushes, cattails, and beaver lodges.
- [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] (25,125 acres, created 1947): a 3.5-acre cottonwood and willow nursery was started in 1993, and more than 50 acres are now wet enough to support riparian plants.
- [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] (more than 17,000 acres, created 1964): a new river channel was dug to replace the straightened old one. It hosts thousands of wintering ducks, Canada geese, and sandhill cranes, the largest flocks in Arizona.
- [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]]: floods in 1993 and 1995 naturally restored a 2,200-acre section, and cottonwoods, willows, and mesquites recolonized the flood plains.
- [[Havasu National Wildlife Refuge]] (33,500 acres): restoration covers 1,500 acres around [[Topock Marsh]], which had been clogged with cattails. Plans include burning cattails to benefit the endangered [[Yuma Clapper Rail]].
- [[Lake Havasu]]: a 220-acre refuge at Lake Beal is being restored, and dredging of more than 150,000 cubic yards of sediment began in May.

## Key Findings

**Restoration results**

- The [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]] used part of their water allotment to restore 250 acres of aquatic habitat and another 250 acres of riparian area since 1996, with 200 more acres planned. Vermilion flycatcher and Yuma clapper rail numbers have increased, and Bell's vireos and southwestern willow flycatchers have reappeared.
- Pilot projects on agricultural land show mixed results. A 40-acre site near Tacna (mesquite and palo verde planted in the mid-1980s) and a 12-acre site at Pratt Farms (cottonwoods and willows restored in 1999) are on Bureau of Land Management land. Glen Gould (Bureau of Reclamation) reports that mesquites and palo verdes are "doing fine on their own," while cottonwoods and willows "are a struggle."
- At Bill Williams, FWS biologists released about 50 razorback suckers behind beaver dams since 1998, and bonytails were planned for release.

**Fish status**

- Virtually no young native Colorado River fishes survive to adulthood. All native species are listed as endangered or threatened, and some have disappeared from large river sections.
- Bonytail chubs are no longer known to reproduce successfully in the wild after 30 to 40 years. More than 1,500 hatchery-raised bonytails have been stocked into [[Lake Mojave]] since 1996, with largely unsuccessful results.
- The razorback sucker population in Lake Mojave has fallen from an estimated 65,000 two decades ago to about 8,000 today, most from hatchery releases.

**Conservation planning**

- The [[Multispecies Conservation Plan]] was launched in 1994 with a $6.7 million budget. Five federal agencies, six state agencies, three Indian tribes, and local water and power companies in California, Arizona, and Nevada are involved.
- The plan covers 57 mammals, birds, and fish that are listed or proposed for listing. Its aim is to aid recovery and prevent future listings.
- Planners have identified more than 60 "conservation opportunity areas" from Lake Mead to Yuma for riparian restoration, slough and oxbow recreation, and new ponds to offset wetland loss.
- Paying farmers and landowners to grow native plants rather than crops is central to the plan, since many of the best riparian sites are on agricultural lands with irrigation infrastructure and water rights. A draft plan was due to FWS by February 2002, with a final plan planned for the end of 2002.

**Open questions**

The article ends with unresolved issues: whether enough water can be found without harming agricultural, municipal, and industrial users; which restoration methods work best; whether river flow patterns must change; and whether the United States bears responsibility for restoring habitat in Mexico's delta, which has been dry for decades.
