# America's Most Endangered and Threatened Rivers of 1993

#river-conservation #environmental-policy #habitat-protection #water-quality #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1993 report by [[American Rivers]] identifies the ten most endangered and fifteen most threatened rivers in North America based on importance as natural resources, degree of threat, and imminence of those threats. The document serves as an advocacy tool to raise public awareness about deteriorating river conditions across the continent. Rivers are selected to represent critical cases illustrating the most dangerous and overriding threats to all river systems, including pollution, dam construction, water diversion, habitat destruction, and inadequate regulatory protection. The report covers diverse ecosystems from the [[Rio Grande]] system facing severe human health threats from industrial pollution, to the [[Columbia River]]/[[Snake River]] system where salmon populations continue alarming declines, to urban waterways like the [[Anacostia River]] suffering from combined sewage, channelization and toxic runoff.

## Background & Description

[[American Rivers]], founded in 1973, prepared this annual listing to document threats to North American river systems and advocate for protective measures. The organization has helped preserve over 17,000 river miles through federal and state protection programs and over four million acres of adjacent lands. Their program addresses hydropower policy reform, protection of endangered aquatic and riparian species, western water allocation and instream flows, clean water protection, and urban river enhancement.

The report provides detailed fact sheets for each listed river, documenting specific threats, ecological values, affected species, and ongoing conservation efforts. Rivers span diverse geographic regions and face varied threats: the [[Rio Grande]]/[[Rio Conchos]] system (Colorado, New Mexico, Texas, Mexico) faces headwaters-to-mouth degradation and border industrial pollution; the [[Columbia River]]/[[Snake River]] system (Idaho, Washington, Oregon) continues to lose native salmon despite decades of "restoration" efforts focused on barging fish rather than restoring natural flows; the [[Everglades]] (Florida) suffers from massive water diversion and agricultural pollution; urban rivers like the [[Anacostia River]] (Washington D.C.) and [[Los Angeles River]] (California) exemplify problems of channelization, sewage leaks, and toxic runoff.

Western rivers face dam threats: the [[Virgin River]] (Utah, Arizona, Nevada), one of few remaining undammed western rivers, faces proposals for twelve dams despite hosting two endangered fish species. The [[Rogue River]] and [[Illinois River]] (Oregon) face habitat destruction from old-growth forest cutting. The [[Penobscot River]] (Maine), a model [[Atlantic Salmon]] restoration river, faces a proposed major hydropower dam. The [[Clavey River]] (California) faces two proposed hydropower dams threatening pristine Sierra Nevada habitat.

International threats include the [[Alsek River]] and [[Tatshenshini River]] system (Alaska, British Columbia), threatened by a massive acid-producing copper mine, and the [[Great Whale River]] (Quebec), threatened by the [[James Bay Hydroelectric Project]]. The [[Platte River]] (Nebraska), critical migratory bird habitat, faces threats from the [[Kingsley Dam]] relicensing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report documents extensive ecological degradation across North American river systems. On the [[Rio Grande]], mining pollution from cyanide leaching operations (including the [[Summitville Mine]] disaster costing taxpayers up to $60 million), plutonium contamination from [[Los Alamos National Laboratory]], and severe water quality problems along the U.S.-Mexico border create what the American Medical Association labeled "a virtual cesspool and breeding ground for infectious disease." Near Brownsville, Texas, 50 anencephalic infants (born without developed brains) occurred in one 18-month period, linked to industrial solvents from maquiladoras.

The [[Columbia River]]/[[Snake River]] system historically supported 16 million wild salmon annually across 15,000 miles of spawning habitat. Today only 300,000 wild salmon and 2 million hatchery fish remain. The American Fisheries Society concluded 76 runs are at risk of extinction, with over 200 runs already extinct. [[Snake River]] sockeye were listed as endangered in 1991; fall and spring/summer chinook as threatened in 1992. Despite this crisis, the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] continues "transportation programs" barging juvenile fish downstream, which American Rivers characterizes as failed experiments while salmon slide toward extinction.

The [[Everglades]], once supporting over 2 million wading birds in the 1870s, now hosts only 20,000-30,000 pairs. Fewer than 50 [[Florida Panthers]] and 750 [[Snail Kites]] remain. The [[Central and Southern Florida Project for Flood Control]] constructed 1,400 miles of dikes and canals, fragmenting the ecosystem and cutting off [[Lake Okeechobee]] from the system. Agricultural lands increased fivefold to over 1,000 square miles, with 222 tons of fertilizer leaving agricultural areas annually. Phosphorus concentrations in water from the [[Everglades Agricultural Area]] reach 10-20 times normal during drought.

The [[Anacostia River]] exemplifies urban waterway degradation. By 1830, river depth dropped from 40 feet to 8 feet due to sedimentation. In 1890, ten million gallons of raw sewage were dumped daily. The 1,000 acres of tidal wetlands existing in 1912 in the District were reduced to about 50 acres by channelization efforts in the 1930s-1940s. Despite degradation, the river supports over 200 bird species and shows resilience with increasing fish abundance and diversity.

The [[Virgin River]], one of few remaining free-flowing western rivers, supports the endangered [[Virgin Roundtail Chub]] and [[Woundfin Minnow]] found nowhere else in the world. Habitat is provided for [[Peregrine Falcon]], [[Spotted Bat]], and [[Common Black Hawk]]. The river faces at least twelve proposed dams despite domestic water consumption in the region reaching 350-400 gallons per capita per day compared to 150 in Tucson, Arizona, indicating conservation alternatives exist.

The [[Rogue River]]/[[Illinois River]] system historically supported 40-pound salmon and 15-pound steelhead. Since 1940, anadromous stocks declined severely. Chum salmon runs are extinct; wild coho of the upper Rogue severely endangered. Summer steelhead, sea-run cutthroat trout, and fall chinook salmon are threatened. Nine major tributaries were identified as critical salmon habitat in the "Gang of Four" report on old-growth forest management.

The [[Penobscot River]] restoration program, designated a model by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in the late 1960s, increased Atlantic salmon returns from a few hundred to thousands annually. The proposed [[Basin Mills Dam]] would reduce probability of successful restoration from 72% to 39% according to USFWS studies.

The [[White River]] (Arkansas) faces severe oxygen deprivation from excess nutrients. Animal waste production in a two-county area of the upper basin exceeds 30 million pounds per day, comparable to human waste from Los Angeles. 95 streams along the upper basin are unfit for primary body contact due to elevated fecal coliform bacteria. Almost 400 miles of White River streams are "fisheries threatened" due to animal wastes.

## Key Findings

**Top Ten Most Endangered Rivers for 1993:**

1. **[[Rio Grande]]/[[Rio Conchos]] System** (Colorado, New Mexico, Texas, Mexico): Presents greatest human health threat of any North American river due to headwaters-to-mouth degradation and pollution from newly developed industrial plants along the Mexican border. Regulation depends on environmental agreements under [[NAFTA]]. Near Brownsville, 50 anencephalic infants born in one 18-month period.

2. **[[Columbia River]]/[[Snake River]] System** (Idaho, Washington, Oregon): Topped 1992 listing; minimal improvement in past year. Proposal to barge juvenile fish rather than restore natural flows through improved dam operations. Up to 95% of juveniles killed before reaching sea during summer low flows.

3. **[[Everglades]]** (Florida): One of largest surface freshwater systems threatened by one of largest plumbing systems ever built. Diverting water and polluting with agricultural/animal waste caused dramatic wading bird population declines. Only 5% of original delta habitat intact.

4. **[[Anacostia River]]** (Washington, D.C.): Typifies urban waterway degradation from leaking sewage, channelization, poison runoff, sedimentation, and dumping within 1.5 miles of U.S. Capitol.

5. **[[Virgin River]]** (Utah, Arizona, Nevada): One of few remaining undammed western rivers now slated for twelve dams despite two endangered fish species and flowing through majestic canyon lands.

6. **[[Rogue River]] and [[Illinois River]] System** (Oregon): Ancient forest cutting threatens salmon survival vital to Pacific Northwest economy and heritage. Nine tributaries identified as critical salmon habitat.

7. **[[Penobscot River]]** (Maine): Major hydropower dam proposed for model Atlantic salmon restoration river. Basin Mills project would reduce restoration success probability from 72% to 39%.

8. **[[Clavey River]]** (California): Two hydropower dams proposed for pristine Sierra Nevada wild river. One of longest remaining free-flowing Sierra streams; first California Wild Trout Stream potentially destroyed by development.

9. **[[Alsek River]] and [[Tatshenshini River]] System** (Alaska, British Columbia): North America's most wild river system threatened by massive acid-producing copper mine. Would store 200 million tons of acid-generating waste behind 360-foot earthen dam in seismically active region.

10. **[[Platte River]]** (Nebraska): Major migratory bird stopover (7-9 million ducks/geese, 500,000 [[Sandhill Cranes]] annually) and endangered species habitat. [[Kingsley Dam]] relicensing holds key to river's future.

**Fifteen Most Threatened Rivers** (alphabetical): [[Animas River]] (Colorado) - $640 million Bureau of Reclamation project for marginal farmland; [[Beaverkill River]]/[[Willowemoc River]] (New York) - lower sections degraded by land use despite upper protection; [[Blackfoot River]] (Montana) - timber harvest, grazing, mining threats; [[Eleven Point River]] (Missouri) - exploratory lead drilling near National Wild and Scenic River; [[Great Whale River]] (Quebec) - [[James Bay Hydroelectric Project]] threatens wilderness; [[Little Bighorn River]] (Wyoming) - 1,000-megawatt pumped storage project; [[Los Angeles River]] (California) - Army Corps proposes concrete walls along 21 miles; [[Moose Creek]] (Alaska) - state-approved riverbed mining in [[Denali National Park]]; [[Skokomish River]] (Washington) - [[Cushman Project]] dams caused virtual extinction of salmon stocks; [[St. Marys River]] (Virginia) - acid rain from 17 proposed fossil fuel plants; [[Susquehanna River]] (Pennsylvania) - nonpoint source pollution, 58.2 million pounds nitrogen annually to [[Chesapeake Bay]]; [[Tennessee River]] (Kentucky) - toxic/hazardous pollution, one of top ten counties for permitted hazardous waste discharges; [[Thorne River]] (Alaska) - old-growth logging in [[Tongass National Forest]]; [[White River]] (Arkansas) - animal waste (30 million pounds/day in two counties), low dissolved oxygen; [[South Yuba River]] (California) - flood control dam proposals despite cheaper levee alternatives.

**Key Threats Identified:** Hydropower development without adequate environmental protection; water diversion and over-appropriation; mining pollution (cyanide leaching, acid drainage, heavy metals); agricultural runoff and animal waste; inadequate sewage treatment; channelization and flood control projects; old-growth forest logging; industrial pollution along international borders; climate change impacts on flows and temperatures.

**Conservation Strategies:** Wild and Scenic River designation; Endangered Species Act listings; FERC relicensing conditions; interstate compacts; international agreements; water conservation and marketing; dam removal; habitat restoration; improved land use planning; nonpoint source pollution controls; instream flow protection.
