# Effects of Black and Yellow Grubs on Lepomis cyanellus and L. humilis in a Natural Environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 study by [[Thomas Ralph Rabideau]] and [[J. Teague Self]] (University of Oklahoma) examined whether parasitism by trematode "grubs" affects the condition of two [[Sunfish]] species, [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) and [[Dwarf Sunfish]] (*L. humilis*), in a wild population. The population, sampled in fall 1949 from the Little River in Norman, Oklahoma, was overcrowded, stunted and heavily parasitized. The authors tested whether the [[Fulton's Condition Factor]] (K factor) correlated with infection intensity and concluded it did not, so the technique cannot reliably indicate parasite damage in natural populations.

## Background & Description

- **Study taxa:** *Lepomis cyanellus* (203 fish) and *L. humilis* (138 fish), 341 fish in total, all from the same mixed population.
- **Parasites studied:**
  - Strigeid metacercariae, the "[[Black Grub]]", found heavily in the muscles and other tissues.
  - Yellow grubs of [[Clinostomum Marginatum]], found less heavily.
- **Collection:** Fish were taken with [[Rotenone]] poisoning. Each fish was classified, weighed and measured for standard length, and scale samples were taken for age determination (ages 1 to 4 years). Specimens were preserved in alcohol.
- **Methods:** Grubs in muscle were counted by examining longitudinal muscle slices over a light. Other organs were dissected for cysts. Growth was expressed as the percentage of total growth in the last year, and fish were grouped into 5 mm length classes within each age group.
- **Background:** The authors reviewed literature showing that parasites can be minor, or can damage populations by stunting, reducing reproduction or causing death under heavy infection. Key references include [[Elliott and Russert 1949]] and [[Krull 1934]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The population was chosen because it showed overpopulation, stunting and heavy parasitism at once. Fish were concentrated in small holes along the stream, which suggests crowding.
- Crowding and other ecological conditions could affect both host condition and parasite load, which would confound any direct link between the two.
- The authors argue that the relationship between K factor and parasitism can only be established under carefully controlled conditions, not in natural settings.

## Key Findings

- **Infection rates by age:** Infection increased with host age, which the authors interpret as cumulative infection.
  - Black grubs: *L. cyanellus* averaged 7.05 (age 1), 104.7 (age 2), 183.5 (age 3) and 565.5 (age 4) grubs per host. *L. humilis* averaged 0.078, 12.59 and 30.1 for ages 1 to 3.
  - Yellow grubs: infection was much lower, averaging 0.34 to 0.69 per host in *L. cyanellus* and 0.95 to 1.55 in *L. humilis* at ages 2 to 3.
- **Totals:** Across all 341 fish, 37,795 black grubs (average 192.17 per host) and 236 yellow grubs (average 0.51 per host) were counted. Black grub infection rose with host length within age groups, whereas yellow grub infection showed no correlation with length.
- **Condition factor:** Plotting parasite number against K factor for all 1 to 4 year old fish showed no apparent correlation. The most heavily infected fish fell within the K factor range shared by most lightly infected fish.
- **Comparison:** Results matched [[Elliott and Russert 1949]], who found no link between K factor and heavy *Clinostomum marginatum* infection in [[Yellow Perch]].
- **Conclusion:** The authors concluded that K factor is not an effective indicator of parasite damage in natural populations, because ecological factors such as overcrowding can affect both variables independently.
- **Reference:** Published in *The American Midland Naturalist* 50(2): 402–406 (October 1953), University of Notre Dame Press.
