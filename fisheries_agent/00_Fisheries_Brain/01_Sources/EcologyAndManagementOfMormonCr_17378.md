# Ecology and Management of the Mormon Cricket (*Anabrus simplex*)

#entomology #orthoptera #tettigoniidae #pest-management #range-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1987 final report to the [[National Park Service]], prepared by John Capinera and Charles MacVean of [[Colorado State University]], reviews the biology, ecology, and economic history of the [[Mormon Cricket]] (*Anabrus simplex*) and presents results of a two-year study (1984–1986) at [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. The study tested three control agents in bait formulations: the entomopathogenic nematode [[Steinernema Feltiae]], the insecticide [[Carbaryl]] (Sevin), and the microsporidian [[Nosema Locustae]]. Carbaryl bran bait was effective; nematodes and N. locustae showed no field effect under the conditions tested. Rangeland damage was found to be slight under favorable precipitation and moderate grazing. The report closes with management recommendations emphasizing education, weather-aware decision-making, reactive crop protection, and further study of the microsporidian [[Vairimorpha]] sp.

## Background & Description

- **Identity:** A flightless, shield-backed grasshopper-like insect (Tettigoniidae: Decticinae). Of 22 North American genera in the subfamily, *Anabrus* includes four recognized species: *simplex*, *cerciata*, *longipes*, and *spokan*. *A. simplex* is the most widespread and most damaging. Its closest relative, the [[Coulee Cricket]] (*Peranabrus scabricollis*), is similar in habits and has periodically become a pest in Washington.
- **Name origin:** The name derives from a 1848 encounter with Mormon settlers in the Salt Lake Valley, where crickets damaged wheat.
- **Life cycle:** Univoltine. Eggs laid singly in soil hatch March–May; nymphs pass through 7 instars; total lifespan about 100 days.
- **Coloration:** Gregarious adults are black; solitary individuals are smaller and lighter, ranging from emerald green to tan.
- **Gregarious behavior:** After the first 2–3 molts, crickets form migratory bands of several square kilometers. Reported bands measure 2–16 km long and 1–2 km wide, moving an estimated 1–2 km per day.
- **Mating and role reversal:** Males select mates and reject smaller females; females compete for males. Males produce a spermatophore up to 27% of their body weight, which females partly consume to fuel egg production. Role reversal occurs at high density (5–10 adults/m² or higher) but not at low density (<1/m²).
- **Diet:** Omnivorous and opportunistic. Swain (1944) recorded over 400 food plant species. Crickets prefer succulent forbs and crops (wheat, alfalfa) over range grasses. Ueckert and Hansen (1970) found the diet was ~50% forbs, ~21% arthropods, ~16% fungi, and ~8% grasses and related plants.
- **Historical pest status:** Severe outbreaks in the 1920s–1930s, peaking 1938–39, forced farm abandonment in northwestern Colorado (420 farms in 1920 reduced to 258 in 1927). About 19 million acres were estimated infested in 1939.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Natural enemies**
- *Palmodes laeviventris* (solitary digger wasp) provisions nests with paralyzed crickets; prey was female-biased in Gwynne and Dodson (1983).
- About 50 bird, rodent, and reptile species feed on crickets. Penned-cricket declines in the 1985–86 study were linked to bird predation: bird-netted pens had significantly higher survival (F = 11.32, P < .01).
- Parasites are few. *Sparaison pilosum* parasitized under 3% of eggs in a 1939 survey, though Cowan (1929) reported up to 50% in western Montana. A horsehair worm (*Gordius robustus*) is limited by water scarcity.
- Cannibalism is common, especially of injured or molting individuals.

**Weather and population regulation**
- Prolonged cold, wet conditions reduced early nymphal numbers in older reports, but the role of weather remains unclear.
- Drought in the 1930s and overgrazing worsened herbivory impacts.

**Range damage debate**
- Cowan and Shipman (1947) projected that crickets at 12/m² would consume 4.4 times the forage cattle use over 4 months.
- Swain (1944) found total losses averaged 15% of current growth; losses of livestock forage averaged 35% for cattle and horses and 40% for sheep and goats. Only 4 of 36 transects (11%) lost all available forage, excluding broadleaf-tree sites.
- In this study, crickets consumed about 11% of arrowleaf balsamroot, 18% of grasses, and 59% of western groundsel, but these values were preliminary and possibly inflated by an "exclosure effect."

## Key Findings

**Literature and history**
- Most of the literature (pre-1960) concerns life cycle and control. Wakeland (1959) provided the comprehensive review; the appended bibliography lists about 200 citations from Wakeland plus 14 papers published since 1959.

**Two-year study (Dinosaur National Monument, 1984–1986)**
- Enclosures: 16 large pens (10 × 10 m) and 21 small pens (2 m²) near Echo Park Overlook.
- *Steinernema feltiae*: In the first field trial (15 kg/ha, 800 crickets), no significant survival reduction and no infection (n = 195). Causes: desiccation at ground temperatures of 40–50 °C and capsule unpalatability. In the second trial (765 kg/ha), a significant lab reduction in survival occurred (t = 5.0, P < .01), with 4% field infection (n = 138) but no significant field survival effect.
- Lethal limit: Infective nematodes die at 34 °C body temperature, which adult crickets often exceed.
- *Carbaryl (2% AI bran bait)*: Highly effective at 15 kg/ha (F = 27.82, P < .01) and 5 kg/ha (t = 12.32, P < .01). At 20 adults/m², 5 kg/ha yielded ca. 90% control after 48 h.
- *Nosema locustae*: No effect on survival or egg production in late instars (6–7; eggs 26 vs. 31, P = .39). Instars 4–5 showed no detectable effect in lab or field. Instars 1–3 at the highest dose (10⁸–10¹⁰ spores/mg, per the source) showed ~50% survival reduction (F = 8.46, P < .05) and retarded molting. Spores were not detected in midgut or feces of late-instar treated insects.
- *Vairimorpha* sp. (new microsporidian, identified 1985): Infection rates of 50–100% at Blue Mountain, Diamond Mountain, Pot Creek, West Cactus Flat, and Elk Springs. High spore production suggests strong horizontal transmission potential.
- *Vegetation damage (1986)*: Paired exclosure plots showed significant consumption, but no significant difference between N. locustae-treated and control pens. Cricket-days in pens (~100/m²) were estimated at 2–3 times natural levels.

**Management recommendations**
1. Run a prolonged education program for ranchers and farmers, with materials aimed at school children, possibly delivered through extension services.
2. Account for weather: crickets had minimal impact because forage growth was excellent. Consider computer simulation of forage production under varied precipitation.
3. Recognize that the monument is a source of infestation for surrounding lands. Protect cropland with border treatments and consider subsidizing crop protection over large rangeland treatments.
4. Use N. locustae only at high rates applied early in development; use insecticide bait for small infestations. Investigate *Vairimorpha* as a potential key limiting factor. Area-wide suppression on rangeland is justified only during prolonged drought. Routine monitoring is recommended.

**Sampling gaps:** Three sampling levels are needed (within-band density, band size, band abundance), and economic injury levels are not established.
