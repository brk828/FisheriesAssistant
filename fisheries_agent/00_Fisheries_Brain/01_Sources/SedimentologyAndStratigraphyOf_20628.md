# Sedimentology and Stratigraphy of the Basin-Fill Sediments in the Safford Valley

#geology #sedimentology #stratigraphy

## Executive Summary

This short paper, by Jerry Harbour of the Department of Geology, University of Arizona, examines the late Tertiary sediments of the [[Safford Basin]] in the Safford Valley, Arizona. The author argues that the informal term "lake beds" should be dropped in favor of "[[Basin Fill]]", because most of the fine-grained sediment is floodplain rather than lacustrine in origin. The paper distinguishes an older basin fill from a much younger inter-valley fill tied to recent cutting and filling by the [[Gila River]] and [[San Simon River]]. It also classifies the basin's conglomerates by setting and argues that the high-elevation channel conglomerates near [[Sanchez]] are reworked, not interbedded with the basin fill. The text ends mid-argument on sediment provenance. The document is geological and contains no biological or fish data.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The late Tertiary fill of the Safford basin is mostly fine-grained floodplain deposits, with minor river gravels and lacustrine beds.
- **Terminology proposal:** The "[[Lake Beds]]" label, used in the literature and in the field, is misleading because only a very small share of the sediment volume is lacustrine. The author proposes "basin fill" instead.
- **Two fill units:**
  - *Older basin fill*: the bulk of the basin sediment.
  - *Younger inter-valley fill*: occupies the axial valley and relates to recent cutting and filling by the Gila and San Simon rivers.
- **Typical lithology:** Fine-grained sand and silt, with gentle cross-bedding, ripple marks, and small-pebble conglomerates. These indicate a [[Fluvial Floodplain Deposits|fluvial floodplain]] depositional environment.
- **Lacustrine facies:** Brightly colored laminated clay and silt, volcanic ash, diatomite, and limestone. These grade laterally into floodplain sediment in some areas and have sharp contacts in others.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses physical landscape and depositional history rather than living communities. The relevant context is:

- **Elevation and preservation:** Basin fill is preserved along the margins up to about 3,800 ft. Where it meets the crystalline basement of the mountains, the fill is mostly fine-grained, and lacustrine clay and silt often contact crystalline rock.
- **Erosion:** The Gila River enters the basin at about 3,050 ft and falls to 2,700 ft at [[Fort Thomas]], about 33 miles northwest. The author infers that roughly 500 to 800 ft of basin-fill sediment has been removed from the valley center.
- **Terraces:** At least three step-like terrace surfaces run parallel to the valley axis. They are often capped by cobble-to-boulder conglomerate about 20 ft thick.
- **Conglomerate settings:** The paper recognizes four positions for pebble-to-boulder conglomerates:
  1. Channel sheets interbedded with sand and silt, mostly pebble-textured, mainly in the lower section near Sanchez.
  2. Cobble-textured channel fill tied to modern inter-valley fill or to preserved older, higher inter-valley fill remnants.
  3. [[Terrace-Cap Conglomerates]] (also called pediment-cap), a lime-cemented cobbly mantle about 20 ft thick over most of the north side of the valley.
  4. Scattered [[Fanglomerates]] of relatively recent origin at the mouths of large mountain canyons.
- **Key observation:** No fanglomerates were observed interbedded within the basin fill anywhere in the valley.

## Key Findings

1. **Terminology:** The author recommends replacing "lake beds" with "basin fill," since most of the sediment is fluvial floodplain material rather than lacustrine.
2. **Age and origin of high conglomerates near Sanchez:** Cobble-to-boulder conglomerates above about 3,250 ft occur in old channels with thin intercalated silt beds. They sit at or near the present land surface and do not contact the thick upper silts of the basin fill in any observed exposure. The author judges that most, if not all, are much younger reworked deposits, not interbedded with the basin fill.
3. **Downstream channel fills:** Near Solomon and Safford, similar channel fills lie near the crests of lower terrace scarps. Although they could be read as interbedded with the basin fill, the author argues they are unconformable with underlying lacustrine and floodplain deposits. They extend laterally no more than about half a mile from the modern Gila valley floor, usually much less.
4. **Conglomerate correlation:** The petrologic and textural similarity of the channel conglomerates to the higher terrace-cap conglomerates suggests they formed during comparatively recent downcutting of the Gila River.
5. **Provenance:** Almost all coarse material is of local origin, as is a small amount of fine material. However, the volume of sediment filling the basin from below 1,400 ft to over 3,600 ft is so large that much of the fine fill probably came from outside the basin. The author suggests either past climatic differences or a much larger watershed to supply both the water and the sediment. The text ends here, and the paper's conclusions are incomplete in the source.
6. **Unresolved question:** The author notes that determining the time relationships of the several recognized sub-basins is one of the study's problems. The source does not resolve it.
