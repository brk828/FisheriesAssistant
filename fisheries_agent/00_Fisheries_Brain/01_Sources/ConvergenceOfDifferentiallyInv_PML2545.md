# Convergence of Differentially Invaded Systems toward Invader-Dominance: Time-Lagged Invasions in Desert Fish Communities

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Actinopterygii

## Executive Summary

This study compares the historical development of nonnative fish assemblages in two similar North American desert drainages: the heavily invaded [[Gila Basin]] (principally southwestern USA) and the lightly invaded [[Yaqui Basin]] (principally northwestern Mexico). Using the comprehensive [[SONFISHES Database]], the authors test whether invasion in the Yaqui is time-lagged relative to the Gila. They find that the Yaqui is following the Gila's trajectory roughly 40 to 50 years behind, and they warn that its native fish fauna may decline similarly unless strong action is taken.

## Background & Description

- **Research question:** Do invasion patterns in one region generalize to another? Answering this would let managers apply lessons from one system to another, which is a central goal of [[Invasion Biology]].
- **Study systems:** The Gila and Yaqui basins are similar in size (the Gila has about 12,000 km of stream reach versus about 10,000 km for the Yaqui), physiography, and ecology. Both flow southwestward from mountains above 3,000 m through [[Sonoran Desert]] lowlands.
- **Contrast:** The Lower Colorado Basin, which includes the Gila, has more than 90 introduced species, about half established. The Yaqui has far fewer nonnatives, most with restricted distributions, and most native fishes there remain abundant and widespread.
- **Background on native declines:** Of 31 native fish species in the Lower Colorado Basin, 25 are extinct, extirpated, listed under the US Endangered Species Act, or have suffered major range declines. Early declines were linked to hydrological alteration and dams, but nonnative fishes are increasingly implicated.
- **Stocking history:** Resource managers stocked more than 70 million fishes representing 17 species into the Little Colorado and Black rivers (Arizona) from the 1930s onward. At least seven nonnative fishes were present in the Lower Colorado Basin by 1904.
- **Impact categories:** Nonnative fishes affect natives through competition, predation, and hybridization. Competition has rarely been clearly demonstrated, and most impact evidence is correlative. Hybridization is well documented in chubs, salmonids, and pupfishes.
- **Data source:** SONFISHES contains more than 38,000 georeferenced locality records (within 1 km) for 52 native and 90 nonnative freshwater fishes, drawn from about 150 years of research, museum collections, agency records, and literature. The authors analyzed only the 45 nonnative species with extant populations in the two basins, assuming a species once recorded in a reach persisted there.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Life history of invaders:** The majority of extant nonnative fishes are known piscivores, and many show high levels of parental care, a trait the authors say gives considerable advantages over native fishes.
- **Habitat diversity:** Both basins contain high-elevation meadow streams, canyon-bound rivers, and ephemeral lowland creeks. Headwater and smaller tributary streams in the Gila include some of the most pristine and least invaded habitats.
- **Sampling constraints:** Interior Yaqui streams are harder to reach (few roads, steep terrain), so they have been more poorly sampled than peripheral areas. Sampling in the Yaqui also became much more restricted after 1978, and no region-wide survey has been undertaken since then.
- **Shared phylogeny:** Each basin historically held 20 native fish species, and nine Yaqui fishes have biogeographic links to the Gila or to other northern basins.

## Key Findings

**1. Richness of nonnative fishes grew roughly linearly in both basins.**
- As of 2000, 45 extant nonnative species were known from the two basins: 42 from the Gila and 22 from the Yaqui. Nineteen species were shared.
- On average, one new species was recorded in the Gila every 2.2 years, versus one every 3.3 years in the Yaqui.
- The Yaqui's rate of increase averaged 69% of the Gila's (71% after correcting for stream length). The rates did not differ significantly (F-test on slopes).
- Of the shared species, only [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) was recorded in the Yaqui before the Gila.

**2. Spatial spread grew exponentially in both basins.**
- Cumulative reach records for nonnatives rose roughly exponentially in both systems as established species kept spreading.
- By 2000, the Gila averaged 3.29 nonnative reach records per 25 km reach, versus 0.25 in the Yaqui. At the 500 km scale, the figures were 16.7 for the Gila and 3.5 for the Yaqui.
- Nonnative reach records equaled about 80% of native records in the Gila at the 25 km scale and 96% at the 500 km scale. In the Yaqui they were only 25% and 34%.

**3. The Yaqui lags the Gila by roughly 40 to 50 years.**
- The Yaqui's 2000 nonnative richness matched the Gila's count from 48 years earlier.
- Standardized by stream length, the time lag was about 41 years.
- Using reach records, the lag was 49 years, and using reach record density, 47 years. The authors summarize the overall lag as 40 to 50 years.
- At comparable sampling effort ([[Reach Records]] standardized against database size), the Yaqui's nonnative totals were nearly identical to the Gila's.

**4. Key assumption and its effects.**
- Assuming continuous occupancy inflates species-by-period records by 16.6% for the Gila and 77.5% for the Yaqui at the 25 km scale. The authors argue that this does not change the overall picture, because no nonnative species in the study has been permanently extirpated from a 25 km reach.

**5. Predicted Yaqui trajectory.**
- The authors note that the Yaqui's post-1978 leveling off probably reflects reduced sampling rather than real stability.
- If trends through 1979 continued, a thorough survey would find more than 0.4 nonnative records per 25 km reach and more than 5 per 500 km reach, compared with current estimates of 0.25 and 3.5.
- Localized surveys (Abarca et al. 1995 on the upper Río Bavispe; Campoy et al. 1989 on the southern portion of the basin) report continuing nonnative spread in the Yaqui since 1980.

**6. Threat-relevant species already present in the Yaqui.**
- Most nonnatives that proved problematic in the Gila, including most Ictaluridae and Centrarchidae (major predators of natives), are already established or recently introduced in the Yaqui.
- Of the most damaging species, only the [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) is absent from the Yaqui. The authors say it would likely spread quickly there if introduced, as it did in the Gila, where it is linked to declines of [[Spikedace]].
- Salmonids have been major predators of native trouts and have caused introgressive hybridization.
- [[Hybridization]] is already documented: [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) with the native [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*), and likely [[Rainbow Trout]] with the undescribed native [[Yaqui Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus* sp.).

**7. Conclusions and recommendations.**
- The Yaqui's native fishes are currently widespread and abundant, but the authors predict significant decline if nonnative richness and spread continue.
- They recommend resampling the Yaqui to test whether pre-1979 trends have continued. If confirmed, they urge immediate protection of high-priority watershed portions.
- Surveys should focus on upstream reaches and smaller tributaries, which tend to be colonized last and are easiest to protect.
- Active removal of nonnatives is likely to fail long-term because it does not address regional spread. The authors see the best opportunities in [[Fish Barriers]] to block upstream spread, together with local education about the consequences of introductions.
- The authors caution that the Yaqui is in a window that may soon close, since preventive action could be too late in many areas if the predictions hold.
- The approach may generalize to other systems where multispecies invasion dynamics are a concern, especially where invasions follow political boundaries.
