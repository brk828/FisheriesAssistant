# Maryland's Trout and Salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 popular article by [[Frank J Schwartz]] of the [[University of Maryland]] [[Chesapeake Biological Laboratory]], published in the [[Maryland Conservationist]], surveys the trout and salmon of Maryland. It reports one native trout, three introduced trout, and one native salmon. Historical habitat loss from logging, mining, agriculture, pollution and dams reduced wild populations, so stocking became necessary by the late 19th century. Today, native and introduced trout persist largely through a "put and take" stocking program, and the Atlantic Salmon run in the Chesapeake Bay has nearly disappeared. The article closes with a call for pollution abatement and better land-use practices, and it suggests options such as searun brown trout and Kokanee Salmon stocking.

## Background & Description

**Species present in Maryland**
- **Native trout:** [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), the only trout native to the state.
- **Introduced trout:** [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*, as written in the source).
- **Native salmon:** [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*), the only naturally occurring salmon.
- **Failed introductions:** [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) and [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*).

**Family traits ([[Salmonidae]])**
- Elongated body, smooth cycloid scales, naked head, large mouth.
- A fleshy [[Adipose Fin]] behind the dorsal fin.
- Caudal fin strongly or slightly forked.
- Many finger-like [[Pyloric Caeca]] near the stomach that aid digestion.
- Dark spots on a light background mark Atlantic Salmon, Brown Trout and Rainbow Trout. Brook Trout has light spots on a dark background.

**Species profiles**
- **Atlantic Salmon:** Up to 40 lb in New England and Canada, but locally seldom over 12 inches and a few ounces. Spawning males turn brownish-red and develop hooked jaws. Juveniles (parr) stay in fresh water for two years, then go to sea in their third summer. They may reach 7 lb after one year at sea.
- **Brown Trout:** Maximum about 27 inches and 8½ lb. Golden brown with dark spots. Mostly stocked in Maryland. Inactive at midday and feeds mainly in early morning and evening on insects, worms, small crayfish and fishes.
- **Rainbow Trout:** Maximum about 27 inches and 8½ lb. Marked with a pinkish-blue or red lateral band and black spots on the fins and back. Feeds on molluscs, insects and fishes.
- **Brook Trout:** Olive green with wormlike back markings and greenish spots. Prefers insects but also takes molluscs and fishes. Its existence in Maryland now depends largely on the state hatchery program.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical decline**
- Trout once occupied most upland streams in Maryland. Logging, strip mining, agriculture and pollution killed fish or drove them to the forested western counties.
- Early accounts describe five-pound brook trout in tributaries near Sang Run in [[Garrett County]], and a 1834 report of five-pound, 22-inch brook trout in the [[Youghiogheny River]].
- Rising human population pressure depleted natural stocks, so public stocking was needed by the late 19th century.

**Habitat threats**
- Spawning gravel (redds) became silted over.
- Industrial activity warmed waters above tolerance: 55°F for brook trout and 65°F for brown and rainbow trout.
- Water chemistry changed, with low oxygen and contamination.
- Shade-providing plant cover was lost.
- Dams removed the swift, clear water trout need for growth and reproduction.
- Changes in the [[Chesapeake Bay]] reduced the searun Atlantic Salmon population. Runs that once yielded many pounds of catchable fish now produce only occasional stragglers.

**Stocking history**
- Brook trout stocking began about 1875.
- Extensive stocking of [[Deep Creek Lake]] began in 1919.
- Rainbow trout eggs were shipped from California about 1883, and brown trout stocking began at about the same time.
- Atlantic Salmon introductions to the Gunpowder, Susquehanna, Patuxent and Youghiogheny rivers (1873–1880) were unsuccessful.
- Chinook salmon introductions (about 1873) to the Youghiogheny and [[Potomac River]] failed, as did lake trout introductions in the 1890s.

**Current management**
- More than 45 streams are stocked annually under a "put and take" policy, which the author considers necessary because original spawning habitat is degraded.
- Stocked streams are listed by county and include [[Savage River]], [[Gunpowder Falls]], [[Patuxent River]], [[Deer Creek]], [[Jones Falls]] and [[Little Seneca Creek]] (about 30 selected streams, summarized here rather than reproduced in full).

## Key Findings

- **Species status:** Maryland has one native trout (Brook Trout), two introduced trout (Brown and Rainbow), and one native salmon (Atlantic Salmon). Chinook Salmon and Lake Trout introductions failed.
- **Decline drivers:** Land-use practices, pollution, warming, siltation, loss of shade cover and dams drove trout out of most upland streams.
- **Stocking dependence:** Wild trout reproduction is insufficient, so the state relies on hatchery stocking of more than 45 streams per year.
- **Life history data:**
  - Brown Trout spawn October–December and hatch in 48–52 days; hatchery fish reach 17 inches in 16 months.
  - Rainbow Trout spawn late February to early April and hatch in about 50 days; hatchery fish reach 6 inches in 16 months.
  - Brook Trout spawn September–November and hatch in about 50 days at 51°F; wild fish reach 3–5 inches in a year, while hatchery fish reach 7–10 inches.
  - Atlantic Salmon juveniles spend two years in fresh water before migrating to sea.
- **Records:** Brook Trout world record is 32 inches; Maryland record is 23 inches.
- **Angling notes:** Brown Trout are best caught early or late in the day, with dry flies, spinners or bottom-fished baits. Rainbow Trout respond well to worms, and Brook Trout respond to live baits and flies.
- **Conservation recommendations:** Begin pollution abatement immediately, improve agricultural and forestry practices, and preserve the streams that still support big trout.
- **Future options proposed:** Introduce searun Brown Trout to the Bay and its tributaries, stock landlocked [[Kokanee Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka kennerlyi*) in Deep Creek Lake, or develop hybrid trout suited to changing environments.
