# Distribution of Fishes in North and South Dakota Basins Affected by the Garrison Diversion Unit

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #distribution

## Executive Summary

This 1981 monograph by [[John B. Owen]], [[Dean S. Elsen]] and [[Gordon W. Russell]] (University of North Dakota, Fisheries Research Unit) documents the present and historical distribution of freshwater fishes in the basins associated with the proposed [[Garrison Diversion Unit]] (GDU). The study covers the [[James River Basin]] (Missouri drainage) and the [[Wild Rice River]], [[Sheyenne River]], [[Devils Lake]] closed basin and [[Souris River]] (Hudson Bay drainage). Field collections were made in 1974 and 1975, and the report combines them with earlier records, glacial history, human impact accounts and species life histories. The central concern is that GDU water transfers could carry Missouri Basin fishes into the Hudson Bay drainage through canals and return flows. The authors could not calculate that probability, because the project was suspended and its features were under revision.

## Background & Description

The report is a baseline biological reference. Its stated objectives were to determine present and past fish distribution in the affected basins, identify the ecological factors controlling distribution, and evaluate the chance of fishes establishing themselves in new basins if they gained access through the diversion. The work was funded by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and the Garrison Diversion Conservancy District.

The document has five parts:

- **Chapter II, Drainage Relationships:** pre- and post-Pleistocene drainage history, including glacial [[Lake Agassiz]], [[Lake Souris]] and [[Lake Dakota]], and their role as dispersal routes.
- **Chapter II-C, Human Impact:** settlement effects such as lowered water tables, siltation, nutrient loading, [[Low-Head Dams]], and introduced [[Common Carp]].
- **Chapter III, Present Distribution:** basin-by-basin collection results and maps (Figures 8 to 105).
- **Chapter IV, Discussion:** controlling factors and the interbasin transfer risk for each basin.
- **Chapter V, Life Histories:** range, size, habitat, reproduction, food and utilization for each species, plus a key to North Dakota fishes.

The checklist covers roughly 100 species across more than 20 families. Individual species accounts are not reproduced here. Representative examples include lampreys, sturgeons, gars, [[Paddlefish]], [[Goldeye]], salmonids, [[Northern Pike]], minnows, suckers, catfishes, sunfishes, perches and the [[Walleye]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainage history.** Before glaciation, the Hudson Bay drainage and the Missouri system flowed northeast. Ice sheets then covered the eastern Dakotas and forced the Missouri to become a tributary of the Mississippi. The authors describe this as "of paramount significance" to fish distribution. Glacial lakes connected the basins at different times. Lake Dakota cut an outlet along the present James River. Lake Agassiz covered much of the Red River Valley and drained through River Warren into the Mississippi system at one stage. Lake Souris drained through the Sheyenne, Devils Lake and Pembina routes at different phases. The present continental divide is low and poorly defined, so it may never have been a complete barrier.

**Limiting factors.** The authors identify seasonal flow interruption, poor oxygenation, winterkill and summerkill as the principal limits in the eastern Dakotas. Conditions are harshest in headwaters and small streams. Human changes include lowered water tables, reduced spring flow, siltation that covered sand and gravel spawning beds, nutrient loading that raised biochemical oxygen demand, and the many lowhead dams. These dams form pools that trap silt and organic matter and may deoxygenate in winter. They also block upstream migration except at high flow.

**Basin conditions.**

- *James River:* about 21,000 square miles (8,400 in North Dakota). The authors identify four segments that differ in flow and fish community. Around 50 dams were listed by Elsen (1977).
- *Wild Rice River:* intermittent above Lake Tewaukon, with a silted bottom throughout and few habitats.
- *Sheyenne River:* about 550 miles long, with a drainage of 10,700 square miles. It crosses Drift Prairie, the Sheyenne Delta and old Lake Agassiz bed. [[Baldhill Dam]] is a barrier to upstream movement.
- *Devils Lake:* a closed basin of about 3,580 square miles. Lake levels fell from 1,438.3 feet (1867) to 1,400.9 feet (1940), and rose to 1,424.1 feet by 1979.
- *Souris River:* 357 miles long in North Dakota. Three national wildlife refuges and eight, four and five low-head dams in the Des Lacs, Upper Souris and J. Clark Salyer refuges form physical and chemical barriers. The river is heavily enriched with nutrients.

## Key Findings

**Species richness**
- The James River basin yielded 41 species in 1974 and 1975, against 60 previously reported.
- The Wild Rice River yielded 12 species in 1974, against 14 known in the river.
- The Sheyenne River yielded 32 species in 1974, against 53 previously recorded. Peterka (1978) found 31 species upstream of Baldhill Dam and 49 downstream.
- The Souris River yielded 18 species and the Devils Lake basin 5 species.

**Patterns**
- Larger rivers support more species than smaller rivers, and downstream stretches have more species than upstream ones.
- The James River count rises from 14 species above Jamestown Dam, to 20 from Jamestown to the South Dakota border, 28 from the border to Frankfort, and 44 from Frankfort to the mouth.
- Some species (Iowa darter, sand shiner, plains topminnow) are restricted to specialized habitats or a few tributaries.
- Historic losses are documented. Eight of the 20 species collected by Woolman (1896) in the North Dakota James River are now gone from that reach.

**Interbasin transfer risk**
- Routes of concern include the McClusky Canal and Lonetree Reservoir, the James River Feeder Canal, the Oakes area divide, and Lake Ashtabula's return flows.
- Missouri Basin fishes could reach the Hudson Bay drainage through failure of the fish screen, illegal bait release, public introductions, canal overflow or wasteways.
- Once in the Hudson Bay drainage, fishes could move downstream across the U.S.-Canada border.
- The authors could not give a probability, because alternative plans were under evaluation.

**Effects of flow change**
- GDU flows would raise winter flows and sustain low-water periods, improving winter survival in the James River.
- Some species that depend on large rivers could extend their range upstream. The authors name shortnose gar, river carpsucker, blue sucker, flathead catfish and gizzard shad.
- Specialized tributary species are expected to be unaffected.

**Introduced species**
- [[Carp]] have spread through all North Dakota basins except Devils Lake and the Souris.
- Largemouth and smallmouth bass, bluegill and white bass were introduced from more southerly regions.
- Grass carp were spreading northward in the Missouri system and were considered a monitoring priority.
- Rainbow smelt were introduced to Lake Sakakawea in 1971 and are now self-maintaining.

**Devils Lake**
- The small fauna reflects wet and dry cycles. Northern pike disappeared in 1889. Young (1924) found only stickleback in the lake at a low point.
- Recent introductions of northern pike, walleye, white bass and yellow perch have done well with higher water.

**Souris River**
- Bullheads, fathead minnows and northern pike were the most widespread species and are relatively tolerant of low oxygen.
- Carp occur occasionally, with no self-sustaining population.
- Increased GDU flows could improve oxygenation and reduce winterkill, but could also weaken the refuges as barriers.

**Limitations**
- The authors did not perform a comprehensive biological survey of the James River.
- The GDU was suspended and under redesign, so transfer risk could not be quantified.
