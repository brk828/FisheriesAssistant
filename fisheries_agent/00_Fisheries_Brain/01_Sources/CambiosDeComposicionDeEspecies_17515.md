# Changes in Fish Species Composition in Semiarid Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports changes in freshwater fish community composition at nine Mexican localities marginal to arid zones, documented over roughly 70 years or less. Comparisons are drawn between early collections by [[Seth E. Meek]] (1901–1904) and later surveys from 1960 to 1973. Most changes are losses of species, interpreted as degenerative environmental change. Several endemic species and subspecies are threatened with extinction. The author attributes the decline mainly to human activity and calls for systematic monitoring of fish communities.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Nine Mexican localities, with a further seven analyzed in a separate manuscript in preparation (Contreras, MS).
- **Rationale:** Species composition shifts in biological communities are recognized indicators of environmental degradation, often caused by people (CWQ, 1973). Comparable work existed for US fishes ([[Miller 1961]]; [[Minckley and Deacon 1968]]), but none had been published for Mexico.
- **Historical baseline:** The earliest ichthyological records come from [[Meek 1902]] and [[Meek 1904]], which serve as reference collections for the comparisons.
- **Field data:** Collections were made from 1960 onward, including surveys by [[Barbour and Contreras]] in 1963.
- **Presentation:** Nine tables list species by locality, with abundance and status codes. Status codes mark records as present, rare, assumed present, threatened, or extinct, and taxonomic status as species, subspecies, local population, or undefined.
- **Origin:** The paper was presented as comments at the end of a session, not as a scheduled talk.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The localities lie in the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] region and adjacent semiarid basins. Common threats recorded, in descending order of frequency, are:

1. [[Agricultural Disturbance]] (irrigation, erosion, and sediment)
2. [[Exotic Fish Introduction]]
3. [[Industrial Pollution]] (for example, cellulose mill effluent and factory discharges)
4. [[Urban Sewage]] and domestic wastewater
5. [[Dams and Reservoirs]]
6. [[Canalization]]
7. [[Reduced Stream Flow]], which leads to warming, turbidity, and loss of habitat

Native fauna tends to decline in clear, cold, or fast-flowing water and among bottom-dwelling species. Exotic species such as [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and bluegill sunfish (*Lepomis macrochirus*) often replace them.

## Key Findings

**Locality-by-locality results**

- **Laguna de Encinillas (Sauz), Chihuahua:** Meek (1901) recorded 4 species. In 1964 only 3 were found. A new *Gila* record was added in 1968, and the local *Cyprinodon* proved rare. The taxa are considered endemic and under study. The watershed has intensive agriculture and small dams, but no direct link to species loss was established. The fauna is considered threatened.
- **Laguna Bustillos, Cuauhtémoc, Chihuahua:** Meek collected 1 species in 1901, now considered undescribed. No fish were found in the lagoon in 1964. Three exotic species occurred in a lateral spring, and the native community of 5 species survived in tributaries. Cellulose mill effluent is the probable cause of lacustrine fauna loss, and exotics prevented recolonization of the spring.
- **Río Santa Isabel, General Trías, Chihuahua:** Meek recorded 11 species in 1901. By 1964, three had disappeared, all bottom-dwelling, while two midwater invaders were added. Sedimentation from nearby agriculture did not appear to have increased, so the loss could not be tied to a specific alteration. Both *Etheostoma* species are considered threatened.
- **Río Nazas, Santiago Papasquiaro, Durango:** Eight species were known in 1903. By 1964 one had disappeared and two were rare. An undescribed *Etheostoma* is considered threatened. Agricultural development is underway, but specific causes of decline have not been identified, and the decline is only beginning.
- **Río Mezquital, Presa Peña del Águila, Durango:** Surveys in 1963, 1964, and 1968 documented native *Chirostoma jordani* plus *Gila* sp. and *Moxostoma* sp., giving three confirmed native species. Introduced species include common carp, largemouth bass, bluegill, goldfish (*Carassius auratus*), and an unidentified *Chirostoma*. The original native fauna of the wider area was at least 10 species. Intensive cultivation and the reservoir are the most conspicuous disturbances.
- **Río del Tunal, Durango:** At least 10 species were known in 1903. In 1963, seven native species were recorded about 7 km south of the city, where disruption was minor during bridge construction. By 1968, an upstream dam and regional canals had turned clear water turbid, replaced sand and gravel with mud, and removed vegetation. Only a few *Gila* sp. specimens survived. Exotic carp (common, Israeli, and Japanese forms), *Lepomis*, and *Micropterus* had replaced the natives. At least six endemic forms, including three undescribed species and one subspecies, survive in small streams nearby.
- **Río San Juan, San Juan, Nuevo León:** Meek recorded 14 species in 1903, probably with one more present. A 15th species was added in 1961, and about 7 more were thought to be present. By 1964, six had disappeared and three new records were added, with four rare. By 1968, four more had disappeared (Table 7 lists about 21 species across the survey years). Lost species require fast, large channels or prefer bottom or clear water. Probable causes are reduced flow from water use, turbidity from agricultural erosion, warming from lower flow, and contamination from Monterrey and other city discharges.
- **Río Morelos, Montemorelos, Nuevo León:** Meek recorded 10 species in 1903, with more than four probably present. Six were collected in 1960, and six new records in 1964 raised the known total to 14. In 1969, collections produced only 9 species, two of them rare. Rotting orange waste on the banks and in the water, with whitish, foul-smelling water, indicated pollution, and additional unlocated dump sites are likely. Lost species were clean, cold-water, mostly bottom-dwelling fishes.
- **Río San Juan, San Juan del Río, Querétaro:** Meek collected 3 species in 1901. In 1964 the same species were found, two were rare, and two introduced species were present. By 1973 no native or introduced species remained. Contamination was noted in 1954 and worsened by 1973, with factory effluent and sewage entering the river. The endemics *Notropis mexicanus* and *Goodea gracilis*, and the local population of *Algansea affinis*, are considered threatened, with hope that they persist upstream.

**Overall conclusions**

- Most documented changes are declines, and several endemic taxa are at risk of extinction.
- Cause identification is difficult, but human-driven degradation is the most likely driver.
- The author recommends expanding observation areas, establishing long-term monitoring programs, identifying causes, and removing pollution sources where feasible. The goal is to protect the economic, aesthetic, and scientific value of these fish communities and the water quality on which they depend.

**Methodological notes**

- Species counts depend on collection effort and taxonomic uncertainty. Many listed forms were undescribed at the time.
- Some tables give 1901 and others 1903 for Meek's collections, reflecting differing references in the source.
