# Inland Commercial Fisheries Workshop

#ichthyology #marine-biology #commercial-fisheries #reservoir-ecology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This document records proceedings from the Inland Commercial Fisheries Workshop held April 15-16, 1971, at the [[University of Wyoming]] in Laramie. The workshop brought together fisheries researchers, state game and fish personnel, and [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] representatives from across the western United States to discuss commercial fishery research and development in inland reservoirs. Participants presented state-by-state project reviews covering population dynamics, harvest techniques, bait fish culture, and marketing challenges for non-game species including [[Smallmouth Buffalo]], [[Carp]], [[River Carpsucker]], [[Goldeye]], [[Channel Catfish]], and various sucker species. The workshop addressed fundamental questions about sustainable yields, efficient harvest methods, nutrient removal through fish harvest, and market development for underutilized reservoir fish populations.

## Background & Description

The workshop was organized under the Commercial Fisheries Research and Development Act (P.L. 88-309), which funded multiple state projects investigating commercial fishing potential in mainstem reservoirs and impoundments across the western United States. Participating states included Montana ([[Fort Peck Reservoir]]), South Dakota ([[Missouri River]] reservoirs), New Mexico ([[Elephant Butte Lake]]), Wyoming ([[Ocean Lake]]), North Dakota ([[Garrison Reservoir]], [[Lake Ashtabula]]), Colorado, Arizona, Nevada, Nebraska ([[Lake McConaughy]]), and Oklahoma.

Research presentations covered diverse topics including life history studies of commercial species, gear efficiency testing, population estimation through mark-recapture, sonar fish detection, cage culture experiments, and economic analyses of bait fish markets. The workshop featured four panel discussions: (1) New Research and Harvest Techniques, (2) Population Dynamics, (3) Bait Fish, and (4) Marketing. Species of primary commercial interest included [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*), [[Bigmouth Buffalo]], [[River Carpsucker]] (*Carpiodes carpio*), [[Quillback]] (*Carpiodes cyprinus*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Goldeye]] (*Hiodon alosoides*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Freshwater Drum]], [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*), and [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Commercial fisheries in western reservoirs operated within complex ecological systems where non-game species often dominated fish biomass. At [[Fort Peck Reservoir]], [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] comprised 75.6% of the 3.74 million pounds harvested commercially since 1957. Standing crop estimates varied widely: Arizona reservoirs showed 250-300 pounds per acre for buffalo populations, while New Mexico's [[Elephant Butte Lake]] yielded rotenone estimates of 400-600 pounds per acre total fish biomass.

Population dynamics studies revealed that intensive commercial harvest could alter community structure. At [[Elephant Butte Lake]], removal of 68% of the buffalo standing crop resulted in population shifts favoring [[River Carpsucker]] and [[Gizzard Shad]]. Growth rates of remaining buffalo increased following exploitation, with time to marketable size decreasing from 9 years to 7 years. South Dakota research demonstrated that intensive removal of [[Black Bullhead]] populations (using 1 net per 5 acres during June-early July peak vulnerability) could effectively control densities and promote development of commercially valuable sizes.

Seasonal movements and habitat use patterns proved critical for harvest efficiency. [[Goldeye]] in [[Garrison Reservoir]] exhibited strong migratory behavior, with multiple University of North Dakota theses documenting depth distribution, lateral distribution relative to limnological conditions, food habits, and growth variations across reservoir areas. Nebraska's [[Lewellen Weir]] on the [[North Platte River]] captured upstream spawning migrations, with [[Carp]], [[Quillback]], and [[River Carpsucker]] showing the strongest migration tendencies and vulnerability to weir harvest.

An innovative nutrient management approach was explored at [[Lake Ashtabula]], North Dakota, where researchers investigated removing nitrogen and phosphorus from the system through commercial harvest of [[Yellow Perch]]. The project estimated that harvesting perch could potentially remove half of the annual input of 10-11 metric tons of phosphate and 7-8 metric tons of nitrogen, though panel discussions noted that experimental work in Arizona achieved only 2-3% phosphate removal via [[Tilapia]] harvest in controlled conditions.

## Key Findings

**Montana - Fort Peck Reservoir:** Since 1957, commercial fishermen removed 3.74 million pounds of fish, with [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] comprising 75.6% of total catch. Nine species contributed commercially: [[Goldeye]], [[River Carpsucker]], [[Freshwater Drum]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Carp]], common and redhorse sucker, and [[Bigmouth Buffalo]]. Selective netting techniques for [[Goldeye]] harvest were developed and demonstrated. Tagging studies were underway to determine harvest rates and population estimates. Sonar equipment was being tested to locate fish concentrations.

**South Dakota:** Six projects under P.L. 88-309 yielded multiple findings. Carp preparation methods were judged acceptable but required further development. Intensive removal of [[Black Bullhead]] using 1 net per 5 acres during June-early July achieved large population reductions in short time periods. Control of bullhead densities to promote commercially valuable sizes appeared achievable at relatively low cost. Floating trap nets proved generally less effective than 7-inch diameter hoop nets with 100-foot leads. Annual commercial fish production statistics and individual contractor effort records were deemed invaluable for management planning, while voluntary production reports for bait fish were inaccurate.

**New Mexico - Elephant Butte Lake:** Project 6-11-R investigated biological potential of rough fish species for commercial harvest. Reduction in density and biomass of [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] through existing commercial fishery resulted in increases in several game fishes and [[River Carpsucker]], demonstrating that ecologically-similar species must be considered both individually and as groups for management. Experimental fishing revealed gill nets as most effective harvest method. Brown-colored 4 and 5-inch stretch-mesh nets set in areas of seasonal fish concentrations increased [[River Carpsucker]] catch rates by 400-800%. Growth rates of buffalo increased post-exploitation, reducing time to marketable size from 9 to 7 years.

**Wyoming - Ocean Lake:** Mark-recapture studies in 1969-1970 estimated populations of 1,299,000 [[Carp]] and 42,500 [[White Sucker]]. Studies planned for 1971-1972 included refining standing crop estimates, estimating production, evaluating methods and costs for efficient carp removal, and initiating large-scale removal to simulate commercial harvest and evaluate ecosystem effects. Mercury content measurements of carp from regional impounded waters were scheduled to begin in 1971.

**North Dakota - Garrison Reservoir:** Six completed Master's theses on [[Goldeye]] documented: commercial fisheries survey of Upper Missouri River mainstem reservoirs; depth distribution in Moccasin Bay; life history observations; lateral distribution relative to ecological factors; biological characteristics comparison across reservoir areas; and diurnal and seasonal food habits. Research on [[Channel Catfish]] documented age, growth rate, and ecological distribution, with population dynamics studies continuing as the population opened to commercial fishing in 1971. Additional studies covered age and growth of [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*), [[Sauger]] (*S. canadense*), [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), and [[River Carpsucker]].

**North Dakota - Lake Ashtabula:** Nutrient budget research investigated fish harvest as biological nutrient removal mechanism. Winter and summer creel census combined with commercial harvest data estimated total fish removal. Nutrient content of fish flesh was analyzed to estimate total nutrients removed. Watershed-scale nutrient sources and exchanges were measured with emphasis on nitrogen and phosphorus. Food chain analysis identified principal food items and their caloric and nutrient content to estimate energy flow. The project aimed to remove approximately half of annual phosphate (10-11 metric tons) and nitrogen (7-8 metric tons) inputs through fish harvest, though panel discussions questioned efficiency of this approach.

**North Dakota - Cage Culture:** Black bullhead (*Ictalurus melas*) cage culture feasibility was tested in a 120-day growing season. Nine cages (3×4×4 feet) held three replicates each of 250, 300, and 350 fish fed commercial diet (Purina Trout Chow) at 3% body weight daily. Fish gained weight steadily until late September when gains leveled and became negative with decreasing water and air temperature. Conversion ratios were lowest in higher-density cages but did not match channel catfish cage culture results from south-central United States. Total mortality was 2.62%, higher in lower-density cages.

**Colorado:** Research completed included life history studies of [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) and [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*), and fathead minnow culture methods. An extension bulletin on bait fish culture was produced. Ongoing research included sex determination in fishes, propagation of [[Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum*), culture, economics and catchability of [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*), and hybridization of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and [[Blue Catfish]] (*I. furcatus*).

**Arizona:** Research assumed fish distribute relative to food supplies, which distribute relative to nutrients. Studies emphasized intra- and inter-lake heterogeneity in central Arizona reservoirs. All data were computerized into transferable data retrieval system linked to multivariate analysis program providing correlation coefficients to direct field study emphasis. Remote sensing via echo-sounding and high-altitude aerial photography with various film types and filters was employed and evaluated against ground truth. Harvest of buffalo was only about 4 pounds per acre where standing crop was estimated at 250-300 pounds per acre. Poor returns from tagging buffalo and inability to find young buffalo were noted problems.

**Nevada:** In cooperation with [[National Marine Fisheries Service]], Nevada examined bait fish industry problems in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]. Economic analysis showed approximately $500,000 annual sales volume, with 40% concentrated in Las Vegas-Lake Mead area. Each sub-area was dominated by one wholesaler. Supply of minnows produced within the area met only 50% of demand. With sport fishing increasing at 10% per year, bait fish demand was expected to increase similarly. Supply of most preferred species (water-dog) was already limited and erratic. Methods were being developed to raise new bait minnow species suitable for the basin to replace scarce water-dog supply.

**Nebraska - Lake McConaughy:** Electric weir on [[North Platte River]] near Lewellen (constructed 1966-67) operated for three years to define seasonal distribution and availability of commercial fishes. Upstream movement from reservoir consisted mainly of sexually mature lacustrine specimens on spawning migrations. Of seven commercial species, only three ([[Carp]], [[Quillback]], [[River Carpsucker]]) showed large standing crops, strong migration tendencies, and vulnerability to weir removal. Total commercial fish removal increased yearly (1968: 5,160 lbs; 1969: 18,263 lbs; 1970: 26,290 lbs), reflecting efficiency improvements. These three species comprised 95-98% of total catch. Mark-recapture studies and gill-net data suggested carp segregated into sedentary and migratory sub-populations, while quillback and river carpsucker did not.

**Oklahoma:** Commercial fishing existed in river systems for decades but modern practices began with major reservoir creation in early 1940s. Early liberal regulations and substantial harvest led to numerous restrictions due to public sentiment. Research determined that intensive removal programs were required to significantly improve sport fishery. Studies indicated existing commercial fishery was inefficient as management unit and did not adequately utilize natural resources. New commercial fishery planning project was scheduled to begin July 1, 1971, to develop more efficient commercial fishery.

**Panel Discussions - Population Dynamics:** Preliminary consensus estimated standing crop at 200 pounds per acre for commercial species, with 25% harvestable surplus yielding approximately 50 pounds per acre sustainable harvest. Sustainable yields for buffalo were estimated at 100-200 pounds per acre. Growth rate increases following exploitation were documented. Question remained whether harvestable surplus reflected population dynamics (sustained yield) or simply failure of year class reproduction requiring waiting for good year classes.

**Panel Discussions - Harvest Techniques:** New techniques discussed included: bioaccoustics for aggregating conditioned fish; three-phase electric current forming triangular voltage gradient; phototaxis; computer analysis of sonar data to identify targets and value schooled fish; discrete depth sampling devices; time-lapse photography of fish schools; vertical depth fish samplers; magnesium destruct devices to test trap efficiency; CO₂ bubble fences to block fish movements; and selective breeding/"atomic farming" to produce larger individuals, faster growth, or herbivorous characteristics.

**Panel Discussions - Bait Fish Culture:** [[Fathead Minnow]] production yielded 1,200 pounds per surface acre with food conversions of 2:1 to 4:1. Colorado could produce salable fish in one year. Minimum salable size for fatheads exceeded size of maximum food conversion efficiency, requiring constant growth pushing; [[Golden Shiner]] offered more operational flexibility. Carrying capacity and minimum salable size relationships might force two-year production programs. Arkansas economics showed $1.00-1.50 per pound on-farm, transportation 40¢ per mile, gross income about $500 per acre, net about $200 per acre with efficient management. Fishermen preferences for bait minnows varied by area and target game fish. Pond construction for draining was recommended, though two-thirds of production required seine removal. Electroaxis was suggested for herding minnows into seines or traps.

**Panel Discussions - Marketing:** Critical question emerged whether goal was marketing available fish protein or least-cost method of controlling undesirable species. Possibility existed that carp and sucker production in cool, relatively unproductive Rocky Mountain lakes might be too low and dispersed for profitable exploitation competing with catfish farming in Kansas, Missouri, and Arkansas. Markets for South Dakota fish were through Chicago to St. Louis, New York, and Memphis. [[Elephant Butte Lake]] fishermen trucked fish to Los Angeles. Phoenix could use more fish than supplied. Distance was considered minor factor if stable supply existed. Need identified for broker to concentrate fragmented (seasonally and geographically) supply. [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] indicated willingness to provide marketing specialist when stable supply from Rocky Mountain reservoir lakes became evident. Lower Colorado River Basin bait fish market showed $500,000 annual volume with 50% demand unmet by local production.
