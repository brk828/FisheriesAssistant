# The Fishes of the Colorado Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This historical paper by [[Barton W. Evermann]] and Cloud Rutter, published in the *Bulletin of the United States Fish Commission*, summarizes the state of knowledge on the geographic distribution of fishes in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It describes the basin's extent and river course, lists its main tributaries, and gives brief taxonomic notes on the species collected there. The text is partly degraded by OCR errors, and several passages, including most of page 5, are unreadable. The numerical values below are reported as they appear in the scanned text, with uncertain readings flagged.

## Background & Description

The authors set out to present, in succinct form, the existing knowledge of fish distribution in the basin. The drainage area is given as approximately **66,023 square miles** on page 2. An earlier figure of "5,040 square miles" on page 1 appears to be a scan error, but this could not be confirmed. The basin covers:

- Most of Arizona
- A long strip along the western side of New Mexico
- Much of western Colorado
- Southwestern Wyoming
- The eastern half of Utah
- A small portion of southeastern California

The [[Colorado River]] is described as more than 1,200 miles long and, after the [[Columbia River]], the greatest river of the western States. It rises in the Wind River Mountains of western Wyoming, near the headwaters of the North Platte, Big Horn, Yellowstone, and Snake rivers. It flows south through Wyoming and Utah and is called the [[Green River]] until it is joined by the Grand River near latitude 39°20′ in Utah. The Green River drains about **47,222 square miles**. The river then enters Arizona, flows west through the [[Grand Canyon]] to the Nevada line, receives the Rio Virgen from the north, and turns sharply south to the [[Gulf of California]]. It forms much of the boundary between Arizona and California.

The main tributaries listed include:

- Tomichi Creek, White Mountain Creek, and Trapper Lake (upper basin)
- Eagle River, Roaring Fork, and Cation Creek
- Yampa River and Little Snake River
- Duchesne River, San Rafael River, Dirty Devil River, and Price River
- Virgin River

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors describe the water in much of the basin as follows:

- The streams of the Colorado and Utah highlands are described as clear, cold, and mountain-fed.
- The main river and the lower tributaries are described as shallow and turbid, and the water is reported as unfit for anything but mud-loving fish.
- The lower tributaries in Utah and Arizona are described as comparatively clear.

The fauna is characterized by:

- A large Cyprinidae (minnow) component. The text says "the bulk of the species of the Colorado Basin belong to this family."
- Several taxa that the authors regarded as local to the basin, including *Lepidomeda*, *Plagopterus*, and *Meda*. The text says these are not known to occur elsewhere.
- Notes on how widely certain species are distributed and how variable they are in morphology across localities.

## Key Findings

- **Basin extent:** The text reports about 66,023 square miles for the total basin, with 47,222 square miles for the Green River portion.
- **Species checklist:** The paper identifies several species from the basin and summarizes earlier collections, including specimens from [[Fort Bridger]] collected by Lieut. [[G. C. Beckwith]]. The count of species is partly illegible in the scan. A reading of "8 species" appears on page 2 but could not be verified.
- **Key taxa mentioned:**
  - *Gila robusta* and *Gila elegans*, both named in the collection discussion (page 3)
  - *Catostomus insignis* (described by Baird & Girard), a sucker
  - *Xyrauchen ancompahgre*, a sucker whose type specimens were taken in the Uncompahgre River
  - *Cyprinodon maculatus* (Baird & Girard), described from the Gila region
  - *Coregonus williamsoni* (Girard), a whitefish. The text notes that the only reference to it from this basin is in the work of Cope.
- **Endemic genera:** *Lepidomeda*, *Plagopterus*, and *Meda* are highlighted as occurring only in this basin.
- **Historical sources:** The paper cites work by Baird, Girard, Cope, Kennerly, Jordan, and Evermann, and collections made in the 1870s to 1890s, to document distribution.
- **Taxonomic variation:** The authors note that some species show wide variation in morphology across the basin. For example, some populations differ in gill raker counts and fin ray length.
