# Fisheries Management in New Jersey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1953 demonstration paper by the [[New Jersey Division Of Fish And Game]] describing the methods and principles of fisheries management in New Jersey. It was presented at the Schiffs Scout Reservation, Mendham, New Jersey, on June 19, 1953. The paper argues that fisheries management is a combined study of biological, chemical, and physical conditions in lakes and streams, and of the socio-economic factors that shape angler behavior. It describes the state's approach to trout stocking in streams, the stepwise [[Lake Survey]] process for warmwater lakes and ponds, the use of the [[Creel Census]] as an evaluation tool, and a general outline of survey procedures. The paper also lists recommended reading and the principal game and forage species of New Jersey waters.

## Background & Description

**Definition and scope.** Fisheries management is defined (attributed to Thorpe) as "A study of the complex and interrelating biological, chemical, and physical conditions in and around a lake or stream, in an attempt to produce good fishing." The author adds socio-economic aspects, meaning people, communities, industries, and individual angler preferences, as a major factor.

**Context.** The text is an instructional demonstration. It refers to a Fish Merit Badge Manual, a forthcoming Progress Report, and a planned set of fin-clipping and creel census projects at camps.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Streams.**
- Most New Jersey stream angling is for trout. The text states that probably over 95% of these are hatchery-reared fish.
- Larger streams support other fisheries. The [[Delaware River]] has walleye and smallmouth bass fishing.
- Coastal streams of southern New Jersey offer excellent [[Chain Pickerel]] fishing. Their lower, tidal reaches hold a variety of freshwater and marine species.
- Southern coastal streams are described as possible spawning areas for bay fishes.

**Lakes and ponds.**
- Lakes vary. Some are rich and highly productive, some are acidic and/or low in productivity.
- Some lakes support trout, some are good bass lakes, and others will only support pickerel.
- Warmwater lakes often contain prolific "pan" species and so-called forage species, which the text says are in need of reduction rather than increase.

**Survey framework.** The lake survey is compared to farm management: the biologist analyzes the water, fish growth, and related factors, treating the lake as "essentially a wet pasture or garden" from which an aquatic harvest is reaped.

## Key Findings

**1. Stream management**
- The Division of Fish and Game stocks over 500,000 trout annually.
- Stocked trout average between eight and ten inches, depending on the hatchery growing season.
- Trout fishing is maintained at its current level only through artificial propagation. Sportsmen bear the cost, which the author describes as "an exceptional value for their license dollar."
- Current stream management consists of trout stocking plus limited stream improvement on major streams.
- The Division is particularly interested in south Jersey coastal streams for their recreational and possible bay-fish spawning value.

**2. Lake management principles**
- Stocking warmwater species is "seldom the answer."
- Stocking legal [[Largemouth Bass]] would be prohibitive in cost.
- Small fingerling bass are unnecessary in most lakes that already hold ample populations of that size group.
- Prolific pan species and forage species generally need reduction rather than stocking increases.
- Alternatives to stocking are discussed in the forthcoming Progress Report.

**3. Four-stage management process**
- **Lake Inventory:** Analysis of water, fish growth, and related conditions before any program is attempted.
- **Recommendations:** Recommended actions for each lake or pond. These may include regulation changes or elimination, reclamation of overcrowded and stunted fish populations, [[Weed Control]], setting of brush piles, and changes in stocking.
- **Design and Operation of a Program:** Implementation shaped by market, transport, and socio-economic considerations. Some recommendations may be too costly, impractical, or slow to pay off.
- **Evaluation:** Determining whether the program achieved the desired results, measured by angler success through the [[Creel Census]].

**4. Creel census results (trout census on a New Jersey lake, spring)**
- 50% of anglers caught nothing.
- 17% of anglers caught 54% of the fish.
- Most anglers who caught nothing attributed this to insufficient stocking.
- The author notes that many anglers "just don't know how to fish," and that a sound program may not increase catches for such anglers.

**5. Lake and Pond Survey procedure (General Outline)**
- **Methods:** [[Seine]]s, [[Gill Net]]s, [[Fyke Net]]s, electric shocker, and [[Rotenone]].
- **Biological:** population ratios of species and overpopulation; growth studies (scale analyses, stunted populations, total length measurements only); stomach analyses, primarily for game fishes; parasite survey emphasizing game species, collecting all leeches seen on fish.
- **Physical:** bottom type (ledge, boulder, gravel, sand, clay, mud), depth, turbidity and color, and development (cottages, boat liveries, hotels and cabins, use).
- **Chemical:** oxygen, carbon dioxide, pH, alkalinity (M.O. and ph-th), total acidity, and temperature (including air temperature). Temperature stations are set at all major depressions in large lakes, at the mouths of major inlet streams, and at surface and bottom in small lakes.
- **General biological:** shoreline type (percent rocky, sandy, muddy; open, pasture, wooded, swampy), dominant vegetation (shore, emergent, submergent, plankton and blooms), and checks for pollution (sewage, industrial waste, natural erosion, agricultural sources, farm yards, gardens, and inlet streams as sources).

**6. Expected outcomes of the survey**
1. Types of fish in New Jersey lakes and ponds.
2. General condition and resources of the fisheries.
3. Management to be pursued by the Conservation Department regarding laws, future stockings, and general policies.
4. Closer integration of research and management across Department of Conservation agencies.
