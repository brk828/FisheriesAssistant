# Rarity, Fragmentation, and Extinction Risk in Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[William F. Fagan]] and colleagues tests whether spatial rarity and distributional fragmentation raise the risk of local extinction in the native freshwater fishes of the [[Sonoran Desert]]. Using the [[SONFISHES Database]] and a landscape-level, phylogenetically controlled analysis of the [[Lower Basin of the Colorado River]], the authors find that fragmentation of historic distributions, measured by a scale-independent [[Scale-Area Slope]], predicts local extirpation far better than the raw number of occurrences. Species with the most fragmented distributions were nearly five times more likely to suffer local extirpation since 1980.

## Background & Description

Rarity is commonly defined by geographic range, frequency of occurrence, or local abundance. Theory and small-scale experiments suggest that rare or spatially fragmented species should be more vulnerable to extinction, but large multi-species tests were lacking, and most prior work had focused on mammals and birds. The authors' focal fauna is the native freshwater fishes of the Sonoran Desert, described as a gravely endangered fauna (see [[Minckley and Deacon]]).

The study area is the Lower Basin of the Colorado River, covering 25,970 km of reach draining 331,500 km² of landscape, including Mexican and US reaches from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to the [[Gulf of California]]. The SONFISHES database contains more than 20,000 locality records for all 52 native freshwater fish taxa in the wider Sonoran ecoregion, and 18,771 georeferenced records for the 25 native species of the Lower Basin. Records were digitized to within about 1.0 km of collecting sites.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Native fishes here are adapted to extreme drought and flood and historically persisted regionally while fluctuating locally. Massive anthropogenic change, including [[Dam Construction]], water diversion, and introduction of exotic species, has profoundly disrupted the regional ecology and reduced surface water.

Dispersal is strongly leptokurtic: most individuals stay put, but a few travel long distances. This pattern supports rapid recolonization of defaunated reaches, especially during high discharge, so clustered occurrences aid recolonization. Connectivity among river patches, already limited compared with terrestrial habitats, has been severely reduced by barriers such as dams. Scale is a central methodological problem, since species presence depends on the spatial scale of observation, and habitat patchiness varies among species.

Phylogenetic relatedness was also considered, since Cyprinidae and Catostomidae, especially the genera [[Gila]] and [[Catostomus]], are particularly well represented in the ecoregion.

## Key Findings

- **Fragmentation outweighs occurrence count:** Historic scale-area slope was a stronger predictor of local extirpation than the number of historic occurrences at the 5-km scale. In stepwise logistic regression, scale-area slope alone gave r² = 0.63, and adding occurrence number gave no improvement (r² = 0.63 vs. 0.63). Adding occurrence number first gave r² = 0.44 versus 0.63 when slope was added second.
- **Magnitude of risk:** Species with the most fragmented historic distributions were nearly five times more likely to suffer local extirpations (since 1980) than species with the most continuous distributions.
- **Phylogenetically controlled confirmation:** Partial Mantel tests gave a correlation of 0.55 (P < 0.001) between scale-area slope differences and extirpation risk, versus 0.01 (P = 0.86) for occurrence number differences.
- **Distribution shrinkage:** Local extirpations further fragmented distributions. The mean scale-area slope of the 23 extant species rose by 0.06 ± 0.02 (mean ± 1 se), a 9% proportional increase.
- **Data design:** Historic distributions were compiled from records for 1843–1980 and modern distributions from 1981–1998. Extirpation probability was the proportion of historic 5-km reaches with no modern records. Three spring-dependent species and translocated records were excluded.
- **Scale-area fits:** All species had scale-area regressions with r² > 0.90, and 23 species had r² > 0.96.
- **Conservation implication:** The authors argue that if clustering of occurrences proves key to persistence across landscape scales, the interplay of fragmentation and habitat loss may be more damaging than currently predicted, and dam building, water diversion, and exotic introductions are key threats.
