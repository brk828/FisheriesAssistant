# Gila Topminnow Draft Revised Recovery Plan: Fish and Wildlife Service Comments

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a letter dated September 25, 1998, from the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] Arizona Ecological Services Field Office to [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] Director Duane Shroufe. It gives detailed comments on the draft revised [[Recovery Plan]] for the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*). The Service praises the draft's overall quality but argues that important elements were omitted or weakly explained. Its central requests are:

- Clearer treatment of the species' listing history and range.
- A fuller account of habitat loss, ongoing threats, and nonnative species impacts.
- A realistic recovery time frame of 20 to 25 years.
- Separate "[[Survival Criteria]]" to prevent extinction, ahead of "[[Downlisting Criteria]]".
- Stronger protection for natural and long-lived reestablished populations than for reintroduction efforts.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow was listed as endangered in the United States in 1967. A recording error in the Code of Federal Regulations listed it as endangered throughout its range, including Mexico, from the early 1970s until 1989, when the 1989 update (50 CFR 17.11) corrected the listing to the U.S. portion only. The Service asks the plan to explain this history, because the 1983 recovery priority guidelines are not widely available. The Service also notes that no [[Critical Habitat]] has been designated for the species.

The Service asks for several wording and terminology changes:

- Use "reestablished" consistently, not "reintroduced" (the Service's stated preference).
- Use "et al." for citations with more than two authors.
- Use "Gila (Sonoran) topminnow" for clarity and state that the plan applies only to the Gila subspecies.
- Include the scientific name and full common name "[[Western Mosquitofish]]" (noting that mosquitofish closely resemble the topminnow).
- Define "[[Evolutionarily Significant Unit]]" and other technical terms in a glossary.
- Correct the historic elevation figure from 1800 m to 1500 m (Minckley 1985 revised Minckley 1977).
- Correct the maximum male and female sizes, which conflict with the Description section.

The Service also asks for many more citations, updated literature, restored figures from the earlier draft, and a fix to the reference ordering (junior authors, then year).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The Service argues that the draft understates the historic habitat. The primary habitat was the marshes, sloughs, backwaters, and edgewaters of larger streams, not springheads. Most of this habitat was lost over the past 100 to 150 years through dams, diversions, groundwater pumping, and erosion. Remaining topminnow sites are marginal habitats. They persist mainly because they have not been altered enough to exclude the species, including by nonnative introductions.

**Habitat destruction.** The watershed damage that drove the decline includes woodcutting and livestock grazing, plus:

- Altered runoff and reduced base flows.
- Floodplain conversion to agriculture, urban, and suburban use.
- Channelization, impoundment, and dewatering of backwaters, marshes, and cienegas.
- Diversion of springs for water supply.

**Nonnative species.** Mosquitofish occupy much of the remaining backwater, slough, and edgewater habitat on larger rivers. The Service concludes this likely precludes recovery in places such as the [[San Pedro National Riparian Conservation Area]]. The draft should also address other nonnative threats:

- Largemouth bass, green sunfish, crayfish, and bullfrogs.
- Water cress (*Rorippa nasturtium-aquaticum*), which has altered small-stream and edgewater habitat.
- The [[Asian Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*), which is probably or soon to be introduced to the Santa Cruz River subbasin through the [[Central Arizona Project]] aqueduct. Its effects on nutrient cycling and food availability are unknown but likely.

**Reintroduction outcomes.** Many reintroduced populations disappear immediately, while others persist for 5 to 10 years before dying out. Causes include loss of water (natural or human-caused), dredging or bulldozing, and mosquitofish introduction, though many losses have no obvious cause. Fragmentation can bring genetic bottlenecks, founder effects, and poor recolonization. Disease, predation, and small habitat size also reduce resilience.

**Legal protection.** Potential reintroduction habitats have no statutory protection, so they can be degraded in ways that reduce their recovery value.

## Key Findings

**1. Threat analysis is incomplete.** The Service stresses that a recovery plan must name the factors behind the species' decline, because these justify the step-down actions. The draft's removal of the "future threats" section gives the false impression that decline has ended. The Service asks for a new section on ongoing and future threats. It should cover natural and reintroduced populations and potential sites, and list grazing, roads, development, mining, groundwater pumping, diversion, channelization, recreation, timber harvest, erosion, fire, pollution, and the fragility of artificial sites. It also recommends placing "Habitat Destruction" before "Interactions with Mosquitofish", because habitat loss preceded and enabled nonnative invasion.

**2. Why delisting is not yet possible.** The Service asks the plan to state seven reasons:

- Continuing decline of natural and reestablished populations.
- Very low reestablishment success.
- Destruction or mosquitofish contamination of most natural habitat.
- Difficulty protecting natural populations, most of which are on private land.
- Mosquitofish invasion that is hard to prevent, with removal only marginally successful.
- Continuing threats from habitat destruction and nonnative introductions.
- Limited existing tools and resources to address these threats.

**3. Recovery time frame.** The Service regards 15 years as the minimum time frame. It considers 20 to 25 years more realistic, given the difficulty of fish reintroduction, low success rates, and the time reintroduced populations need to contribute. The estimate should appear in the Executive Summary and Implementation Schedule.

**4. Priorities.** Securing natural and long-lived reestablished populations is the highest priority. Reintroduction is secondary and should proceed only when it does not divert resources from existing populations.

**5. Survival Criteria (five items, I–V).**
- **I:** Secure the natural populations, including Salt Creek, which the Service considers natural and part of the Bylas Springs metapopulation. Rename "lower" Santa Cruz River as "middle Santa Cruz River". Define the Cienega Creek metapopulation as the single population on BLM and State land.
- **II:** Continue searching for Ash Creek and Sheehy Spring populations, and automatically include any natural populations discovered after the plan is signed.
- **III:** Protect long-lived reestablished populations to the same standard as natural populations. These include AD Wash, Cold Springs Seep, Dutchman Grave Spring, Heron Spring, Hidden Water Spring, Johnson Wash Spring, Kayler Spring, Lime Creek, Lower Mine Spring, Mescal Warm Springs, Mud Springs, Tule Creek, an unnamed drainage (#68), Walnut Spring, and Watson Wash. Yerba Mansa Spring is excluded because it is outside the historic range.
- **IV:** Maintain captive stocks from each natural population.
- **V:** Require a credible monitoring program for all populations in items I to III.

The Service also asks for "secured" to be defined using the wording from the [[Desert Pupfish]] recovery plan. A population is secured only where mosquitofish and other detrimental nonnative aquatic species are absent. The Service calls the terms "extinction prevention criteria" negative and suggests "survival criteria" instead.

**6. Downlisting Criteria (four items).** Item 1 should read that the Survival Criteria I–V have been met. The Service says the replicate numbers in item 2 are too low. It cites the desert pupfish criteria (18.5 replicates per natural population) and the Recovery Team's 1989 discussions. It recommends an average of 9 replicates per natural population: 2 to 4 level 2 and 4 to 10 level 3. The Service accepts that the smaller listed range justifies fewer replicates than desert pupfish, but says the draft figures (5 per natural population in the downlisting criteria, and 3.5 in Task 2) are still inadequate and inconsistent. It recommends:

- Defining "established" differently for level 2 and level 3 populations, based on the level of human intervention needed.
- Moving the detailed replicate specifications into Task 2 and referring to them from item 2.

**7. Reestablishment and reintroduction.** The Service asks that:

- Level 2 sites receive priority over level 3 sites for stocking and protection.
- Level designation can occur at stocking or up to 10 years later, and can change with new information.
- Level 3 sites be defined as those that need regular or extensive human intervention.
- Known "failed" sites be recognized as suitable habitat.
- The plan explain why reintroduction has largely failed and describe the shift from the 1980s "Johnny Applefish" quantity-driven approach to a quality-driven approach that protects natural and successful reestablished populations.
- The San Pedro River be added to Table 2. The Service supports using upper Santa Cruz stock, such as from Sharp Spring or the Santa Cruz River, for San Pedro reintroductions.
- Stocking require permits, section 7 compliance, and [[National Environmental Policy Act]] review beforehand.

**8. Habitat and nonnative protections.** The Service asks for:

- A new Task 2.2 to designate [[Critical Habitat]] for reintroduction sites expected to support level 2 populations. It also asks that natural sites occupied within the past 50 years, such as Sabino Canyon, be designated.
- Special land-use designations on Federal lands, such as Areas of Critical Environmental Concern, Research Natural Areas, Wild and Scenic Rivers, and Quality Waters.
- Compliance with [[Endangered Species Act]] sections 7 and 9 and enforcement of applicable state and local laws.
- Retitling Task 1.5 as protection from "detrimental nonnative aquatic species" rather than only fish, with methods for sites such as [[Sharp Spring]] where removal is unlikely to succeed.
- A new Task 2.6 to prohibit mosquitofish release in reestablished or potential sites.

**9. Monitoring and data.** The Service asks for annual monitoring of reintroduced populations and for underwater inspections to be removed from routine monitoring. It asks that all data in the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] repository be available to cooperating parties. It agrees that extirpation criteria belong in monitoring reports rather than the plan.

**10. Genetics and captive propagation.** Genetic considerations should be a separate section, not a subsection of Conservation Measures. Gene flow from level 3 back to level 2 populations should be prohibited, as in earlier drafts. Captive propagation should be merged into Task 1.1. Captive populations should count toward recovery only when no natural sites are available and the captive site is quasi-natural.

**11. Research.** Task 5 should be retitled to cover life history, genetics, ecology, habitat, and interactions with all nonnative aquatic species. Genetic monitoring should move under Task 4.

**12. Implementation schedule and administration.**
- Tasks 1, 2, and 3 are priority 1. Tasks 4 and 5 (formerly 6) are priority 2. Task 6 (formerly 7) is priority 3.
- The [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] should be added to many tasks because of its role in section 7 consultation on the Central Arizona Project.
- Task 1.1 appears four times, and Task 2.3 is missing.
- The Appendix A title should state that data run only through 1996.
- The Service asks for clear guidance on the limits of the Endangered Species Act on take permits for educational displays.
