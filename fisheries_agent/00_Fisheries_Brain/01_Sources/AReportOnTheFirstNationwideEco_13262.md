# National Survey of Fishing and Hunting (1955): Economic Value of Sport Fishing and Hunting in the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #recreational-fishing

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (Circular 44, published October 1956) presents the first nationwide economic survey of sport fishing and hunting, covering calendar year 1955. Conducted by [[Crossley S-D Surveys]] under contract, the personal-interview survey projected results from a household sample to the national population of 118,366,000 persons aged 12 and older. It estimates that 20,813,000 people fished and 11,784,000 hunted, and that sportsmen spent about $2.85 billion. The report was intended to give [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and state agencies reliable figures for planning conservation, fish and game management, and [[Federal Aid to the States]] programs.

## Background & Description

**Rationale.** Before this survey, estimates of sportsmen's spending ranged from $1 billion to $10 billion and were described as "little more than guesses." License sales did not capture unlicensed anglers and hunters, and about a fourth of state agencies had run their own economic surveys, which could not be combined nationally. Conservation agencies also needed economic evidence to justify conservation provisions in land- and water-use projects.

**Origin.** Interest grew from 1953 among the Fish and Wildlife Service, state agencies, the [[Wildlife Management Institute]], the [[National Wildlife Federation]], and the [[Sport Fishing Institute]]. In September 1954 the [[International Association of Game, Fish, and Conservation Commissioners]] recommended a national survey funded from Federal Aid administrative funds.

**Funding context.** Excise taxes on fishing tackle and arms and ammunition, about $20 million a year, support [[Fish and Wildlife Restoration]] through state cooperative programs.

**Scope.** The survey measured participation, spending, travel distance, and days of recreation in 1955. It did not measure attitudes or intangible values, and people who wanted to fish or hunt but did not were excluded.

**Method.** Interviews were conducted January and February 1956 by about 300 interviewers using [[Area Probability Sampling]] from 1950 census records. Key sample figures:

- Approximately 20,000 homes were contacted in more than 1,000 neighborhoods across all 48 states.
- Interviews: 6,220 with fishermen and 3,108 with hunters.
- The average sampling rate was 1 in 2,135 households, ranging from 1 in 834 to 1 in 5,579.
- The survey sampled 48,389,000 households in 1955, and population was self-adjusted for growth since 1950.
- The sample was divided into 16 regional and population-density strata, justified by a 36 percent correlation between resident license counts and urbanization.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report is socioeconomic rather than ecological, but it establishes the human pressure on aquatic and game resources:

- **Freshwater fishing** covered inland streams, lakes, ponds, reservoirs, and coastal streams above tidal limits.
- **Saltwater fishing** covered the ocean, coastal bays and estuaries, and coastal streams below tidal limits.
- Saltwater fishermen fished mainly on the [[Atlantic and Gulf Coasts]] (3,420,000, 75 percent) and the [[Pacific Coast]] (about 1,187,000, 25 percent; a second table gives 1,137,000).
- Most coastal states did not require saltwater licenses in 1955, so the survey was the first reliable count of saltwater anglers.
- The report notes that economic benefits of recreation partly indicate the value of fish and wildlife conservation, which in turn depends on the resource base.

## Key Findings

**Participation (1955)**

- Fishermen: 20,813,000 (about 18 percent of persons 12 and older; 17.6 percent in the table).
- Hunters: 11,784,000 (10 percent).
- Both activities: close to 8 million (7,680,000 in the table). Combined fished-and/or-hunted total: 24,917,000 (21.7 percent).
- Freshwater fishermen: 18,420,000. Saltwater fishermen: 4,557,000.
- Hunters by type: big game 4,414,000; small game 9,822,000; waterfowl 1,986,000 (categories overlap).
- Men made up most participants: 12,938,000 male fishermen and 9,675,000 male hunters, against 4,689,000 female fishermen and 418,000 female hunters.

**Expenditures**

- Total: $2,850,979,000 (about $2.85 billion).
- Fishing: $1,914,292,000 (mean $91.98 per fisherman).
- Hunting: $936,687,000 (mean $79.49 per hunter).
- Saltwater fishing: $488,939,000 (Atlantic and Gulf $311,862,000; Pacific $177,077,000).
- Hunting by type: big game $323,909,000; small game $494,033,000; waterfowl $118,745,000.
- Largest category: equipment, with 18,068,000 people buying or receiving equipment (72.5 percent of participants).
- Spending ranged widely; one sportsman spent $12,190.

**Time and Effort**

- Fishing man-days: 397,447,000. Hunting man-days: 169,423,000 (big game 30,834,000; small game 118,630,000; waterfowl 19,959,000). Total: 566,870,000, which is "over half a billion days of recreation."
- The median fisherman fished 9.5 days and the median hunter hunted 8.5 days. The mean fisherman figure of 19.1 days is inflated by heavy users.
- Trips: 341,333,000 by fishermen and 154,370,000 by hunters.
- Car-miles: 7,166,486,000 for fishing and 2,684,914,000 for hunting (10,451,400,000 total). Passenger-miles totaled 23,982,730,000.
- About 1.2 percent of participants (roughly 5.5 percent of all fishermen and hunters) belonged to fishing or hunting clubs.

**Licenses**

- Fishing licenses held by 13,737,000 fishermen (66 percent), costing $37,240,000.
- Hunting licenses held by 9,951,000 hunters (84.4 percent), costing $39,935,000.
- State sales for fiscal year ending June 30, 1955: 19,625,387 fishing licenses ($39,501,838) and 16,241,931 hunting licenses ($42,790,687).
- The report concludes that license sales overstate individual participants, because many people bought multiple licenses and some bought licenses they did not use.
- Exemptions were common: under-16s in more than half of states, over-65s and disabled veterans in many, and property owners in many.

**Waterfowl and Duck Stamps**

- 1,662,000 waterfowl hunters purchased duck stamps. An additional 395,000 bought stamps without hunting, for a total of 2,057,000 stamps purchased by hunters. Reported sales were about 2,236,000, including collectors.
- The survey estimates about 324,000 waterfowl hunters did not buy stamps. Most were presumed under 16, but the report suggests some may have hunted illegally.

**Reliability**

- Participant counts are accurate within about 5 percent. Expenditure and regional figures carry greater variation.
- The 19-of-20 confidence intervals: 24,339,000 to 25,495,000 for all fishermen and/or hunters; 20,269,000 to 21,357,000 for fishermen; 11,386,000 to 12,182,000 for hunters.
- Expenditure intervals: $2,596,671,000 to $3,105,287,000 for all fishermen and/or hunters.

**Corrections (Supplement, October 1956)**

- Expenditure figures on page 28 were transposed, and those values are means, not medians. Both are needed for economic analysis.
- Mileage block labels on pages 24 and 25 and table captions on page 49 were corrected.
