# Preliminary Testing of the Role of Exercise and Predator Recognition for Bonytail and Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This USGS Open-File Report (2007-1423), prepared with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, tests whether physical exercise (flow conditioning) and prior predator exposure improve the predator avoidance and short-term survival of hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] before they are stocked into the wild. Predator/prey trials used [[Flathead Catfish]] (for razorback suckers) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (for bonytail) in the Willow Beach hatchery's satellite facility. The authors conclude that hatchery fish are naive to predators, that predator avoidance is a learned behavior, and that both conditioning treatments reduced predation losses, although the study was small and the authors call for field validation.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Hatchery-reared fish are typically released in very large numbers but survive poorly. Since 1980, more than two million bonytail and 15 million razorback suckers were stocked in the Colorado River basin, yet survival was extremely low (<10 percent or absent). Marsh and Brooks (1989) reported that entire truckloads of stocked razorback suckers were eaten by resident catfish within hours of release.
- **Size effect:** First-year survivorship was 10 percent for fish 30 cm long versus 26 percent for fish released at 35 cm (Marsh and others, 2005). The Lake Mohave Native Fish Work Group recommended raising the target stocking size from 35 to 50 cm, though fish this size remain vulnerable to striped bass and flathead catfish, and larger fish raise production costs.
- **Precedent:** Survival training is established for gray wolf, California condor, masked bobwhite, and black-footed ferret reintroductions, and for Pacific Northwest salmon (NATURES program). Such research is rare for warm-water species.
- **Hypothesis:** Ward and Hilwig (2004) found that exercised bonytail and razorback suckers had greater swimming stamina, so improved swimming performance might also improve predator avoidance.
- **Study species:** Juvenile hatchery razorback suckers (<25 cm TL) and bonytail, reared at the Achii Havasu satellite facility of Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery, Parker, Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Exercise regimen:** Treatment tanks (four rectangular 1.2 × 7 × 1 m fiberglass tanks, paired with controls) were driven by electric trolling motors. Current velocity rose from 7.5 cm/s by 2.5 cm/s weekly to 30 cm/s over 10 weeks, then held at 30 cm/s. Fish were exercised eight hours per day, Monday through Friday.
- **Predator exposure:** Large predators were captured from the lower Colorado River and held at least one month before use. Flathead catfish averaged 680 mm TL (range 572–838 mm) and 4.5 kg; largemouth bass averaged 1.3 kg. Predators were released unharmed after the study.
- **Stressors:** An ich (*Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*) outbreak in 2006 killed exercised bonytail, which prevented completion of bonytail predator-trial comparisons. Water depth was kept at 1 m and fish were fed commercial pellets.
- **Behavioral observations:** Naive razorback suckers approached flathead catfish and showed no avoidance. Bonytail with predator experience swam at the tank surface and sides, a defensive posture that limits attack angles.
- **Field context:** Telemetry shows stocked suckers disperse rapidly (Mueller and Marsh, 1998), which may expose them to predation before they learn to avoid it.

## Key Findings

**Swimming performance (flow chamber)**
- Critical swimming speed (Ucrit) was measured for 60 razorback suckers: 29 unexercised and 31 exercised. Exercised fish had somewhat higher Ucrit and endurance, but differences were not statistically significant.
- Mean Ucrit was 2.25 ± 0.11 BL/s for exercised fish versus 2.44 ± 0.11 BL/s for unexercised fish (shown in Table 1).
- Bonytail (control only, n = 30) averaged 3.36 BL/s, higher than razorback suckers, but the screen-modified chamber limited trials to 4.5 BL/s.
- Razorback suckers used a "sweet spot" near the flow filter to drift with little active swimming. A screen was added to shorten the chamber, though fish could still rest passively.

**Razorback sucker predation trials**
- **2006:** Exercised fish suffered significantly fewer mortalities than controls (mean 3.33 ± 0.62 SE vs. 5.11 ± 0.74 SE out of 20 prey per trial; one-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs test, n = 9, z = 1.69, p = 0.046).
- **2007:** Exercised and exposed razorback suckers were eaten at a mean of 30.98 percent versus 45.72 percent for control fish (one-tailed MRPP, n = 42, standardized test statistic = -2.35, p = 0.017). Twenty-three trials ran from April 10 to June 26, 2007.
- **Prior exposure:** Previously exposed razorback suckers had significantly fewer losses than predator-naive fish (p = 0.017, as reported in the summary).
- **Tank observations:** In the preliminary tank trials, fish given predator exposure moved toward the sanctuary side. In one trial, prey used the sanctuary zone about six times more often than the predator zone.

**Bonytail predation trials**
- In 2007, 88 treatment and 80 control bonytail were used across 29 trials. Losses were 18 (20 percent) for treatment fish and 26 (32 percent) for controls.
- Of 29 trials, 22 gave unequivocal results. Exercised bonytail survived in 14 trials and controls in eight (one-tailed goodness-of-fit, χ² = 1.636, df = 1, p = 0.143). This difference was not significant because of small sample size.
- Predator-exposed bonytail had 35 percent fewer losses than naive fish in the summary, while the summary's own results note that the difference was not statistically significant.

**Conclusions (as stated by the authors)**
- Hatchery-reared bonytail and razorback suckers are naive to largemouth bass and flathead catfish.
- At initial contact, hatchery fish freely approach predators.
- Predator avoidance is a learned behavior.
- Predation losses were reduced for flow-conditioned razorback suckers, for razorback suckers and bonytail with prior predator experience, and for all treatments combined.

**Future research questions**
- How long predator avoidance is retained
- The most effective way to mass-expose fish to predators, and which predators to use
- The optimal duration and velocity of flow conditioning
- The actual survival benefit in the wild and the economic savings

**Management implication:** The authors recommend integrating predator recognition and physical conditioning into hatchery culture, and suggest testing large groups of treated fish in the wild to measure actual predation rates.
