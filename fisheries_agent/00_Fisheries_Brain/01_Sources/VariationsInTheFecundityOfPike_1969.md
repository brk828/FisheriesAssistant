# Variations in the Fecundity of Pike (Esox lucius) in Windermere

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Esocidae #freshwater-ecology #fecundity #population-dynamics

## Executive Summary

This study by Kipling & Frost (1969) quantifies year-to-year variation in the fecundity of [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) in [[Windermere]], England, based on egg counts from 512 female pike collected over four consecutive spawning seasons (1963–64 to 1966–67). Using the standardized metric "eggs per gram of total fish weight," the authors found no significant fecundity difference between 1963–64 and 1964–65, but very significant increases in each subsequent year (1965–66, 1966–67). These changes were not explained by water temperature, growth rate, or food supply, but coincided with a multi-year decline in adult pike population density (numbers and biomass), suggesting a **density-dependent population regulation mechanism** operating through fecundity. The findings imply that fecundity changes alone accounted for 12–17% variation in total annual egg production, highlighting fecundity as a potentially important, under-appreciated lever in fish population regulation.

## Background & Description

- **Study system**: [[Windermere]] pike population, part of a long-term (1944–1968) Freshwater Biological Association investigation into lake fish populations, building on earlier work by Worthington (1949), Le Cren (1958), and Frost & Kipling (1959, 1967).
- **Definition of fecundity**: number of maturing (mature) eggs in the ovaries prior to spawning.
- **Sampling method**: 
  - Fine flax gill nets (2.5 in / 6.4 cm bar) set October–February, capturing pike ≥ ~50 cm (age 3+), almost all of which had spawned at least once.
  - Annual catch ~350 pike (~1 ton biomass).
  - Fecundity samples: 50 fish (1963–64), 140 (1964–65), 111 (1965–66), 211 (1966–67); plus historical reference data from 3 pike in January 1950.
  - Fork length, age (via opercular bones per [[Frost & Kipling 1959]] method), total weight, and ovary weight recorded per fish.
  - Egg counts: two independent 1-g samples per ovary, cross-validated; total egg estimate = mean eggs/g × ovary weight.
- **Egg distribution homogeneity test**: 10 samples (5 per ovary) from a single 64 cm pike (30 Oct 1964) showed egg distribution within ovaries was **more uniform than random**, with no significant difference between ovaries or sample locations — validating the two-sample subsampling method.
- **Standardized fecundity metric**: "eggs per gram of total fish weight" was adopted because:
  - Egg number is proportional to total fish weight (with individual variation).
  - Eggs/g of total weight is independent of fish length.
  - This metric remained stable across the Oct–Feb sampling window despite a ~5-fold increase in gonad weight over that period (confirmed via matched-fish comparisons in Table III and monthly means in Table IV), unlike "eggs per gram of body weight without gonads," which varied more — supporting the conclusion that gonad growth is additional to (not at the expense of) body weight.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: [[Windermere]] is a temperate freshwater lake in the English Lake District; long-term temperature records (since 1933) and standardized annual netting programs (since 1944) provide a rich environmental dataset.
- **Prey base**: [[European Perch]] (*Perca fluviatilis*) constitute the main summer food source for pike; a perch disease outbreak occurred in autumn 1963, but overall food supply was considered relatively stable 1962–1966.
- **Population dynamics context**: Adult pike biomass and numbers underwent a sustained decline starting in 1962, reaching low levels by 1965 — coincident with the onset of significant fecundity increases, supporting a **density-dependent regulation hypothesis**.
- **Comparative populations**: Fecundity (eggs/g total weight) was compared against other studied pike populations:
  - [[Houghton Lake]] (Michigan, USA) — mean 23.3–26.0
  - [[Siljön]] (Sweden) — mean 13.2 (during spawning; possibly some spent fish included)
  - [[Minnesota]], [[Aral Sea]], and [[Volga Delta]] populations — Volga fish showed ~2× the eggs/g of Aral Sea fish at equivalent lengths; smallest counts across all these populations occurred in pike <40 cm (likely first-time spawners).
  - Windermere's individual-level variation was comparable to Houghton Lake, but overall population means were significantly higher than both Houghton Lake and Siljön.
- **Comparable density-dependent fecundity findings in other species**: [[Long Rough Dab]] (*Hippoglossoides platessoides*) and [[European Plaice]] (*Pleuronectes platessa*) fecundity shifts linked to population size and food availability (Bagenal 1957, 1963); [[Roach]] (*Rutilus rutilus*) fecundity increased 50% under favorable feeding conditions (Woodhead 1960).

## Key Findings

- **Annual mean eggs per gram of total fish weight**:
  | Year | Mean | SD | n |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1963–64 | 27.3 | 3.9 | 50 |
  | 1964–65 | 26.4 | 3.7 | 140 |
  | 1965–66 | 30.0 | 4.6 | 111 |
  | 1966–67 | 31.9 | 4.5 | 211 |

- **Statistical pattern**: No significant difference between 1963–64 and 1964–65; **very significant increases** (P<0.01) between 1964–65→1965–66 and 1965–66→1966–67. This pattern held consistently across age groups (3, 4, 5 years), year-classes, and most length groups (50–79 cm).

- **Individual variation**: Within any single year, the most fecund fish had **at least twice** the eggs/g of total weight compared to the least fecund fish — substantial individual-level variability independent of year effects.

- **Historical baseline (1950)**: Three pike sampled in January 1950 gave eggs/g values of 20, 18, and 20 — significantly lower (P=0.0001) than the 1963–66 means, suggesting fecundity was considerably lower in 1950 than during the main study period (though methodological differences caution direct comparison).

- **Estimated total annual egg production** (biomass × mean fecundity):
  | Year | Mean eggs/g | Female biomass (kg) | Estimated eggs produced |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1964 | 27.3 | 2040 | 55,700,000 |
  | 1965 | 26.4 | 1420 | 37,500,000 |
  | 1966 | 30.0 | 1380 | 41,400,000 |
  | 1967 | 31.9 | 1750 | 55,800,000 |

  - The rise in fecundity accounted for an estimated **+5,000,000 eggs (12%)** in 1965–66 and **+9,600,000 eggs (17%)** in 1966–67 relative to a static 1964–65 fecundity baseline — indicating fecundity shifts alone can substantially alter total reproductive output.

- **No association found with**:
  - **Temperature** (degree-days >14°C) in either the 18–24 month or 6–12 month windows before spawning.
  - **Growth rate** (4th annual increment) — growth actually improved across years even when fecundity was flat or rising, showing no consistent covariation.
  - **Food supply** (perch abundance) — relatively stable across the study period.

- **Density-dependence hypothesis**: The very significant fecundity rise occurred only after several consecutive years of declining adult pike biomass/numbers (from 1962 onward), suggesting that population density needed to fall below some "optimum density" threshold before a compensatory fecundity increase manifested — consistent with [[Wynne-Edwards 1962]]'s homeostatic population regulation theory.

- **Biological mechanism (speculative)**: Authors propose that **overcrowding-induced stress** may physiologically suppress egg production in high-density years, while lower density removes this suppression, allowing higher per-gram fecundity — a hypothesized stress-mediated density feedback on reproduction.

- **Reproductive timing inference**: Based on Carbine's (1944) histological data (immature eggs = 4% of ovary volume, mature = 96%, with no intermediate size class), egg numbers for a given spawning are likely determined either at the previous spawning or during the preceding summer (~12 months prior) — unlike herring (*Clupea harengus*), which requires a ~2-year determination window with a resting phase (Bowers & Holliday 1961).

- **Downstream population implications**: Prior work ([[Kipling & Frost in press]]) showed 80% of year-class recruitment variation (1944–1962) was explained by early-life temperature; this paper suggests the residual ~20% unexplained variation could partly stem from year-to-year fecundity differences, warranting continued multi-lake sampling.

- **Methodological contribution**: Establishes "eggs per gram of total fish weight" as a robust, temporally and size-independent metric for cross-year and cross-population fecundity comparisons in pike, validated against alternative metrics and tested for confounds from gonad growth timing.
