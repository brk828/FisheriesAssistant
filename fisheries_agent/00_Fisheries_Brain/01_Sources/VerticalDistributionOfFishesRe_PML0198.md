# Vertical Distribution of Fishes Relative to Physical, Chemical and Biological Features in Two Central Arizona Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Clupeidae #Centrarchidae #Ictaluridae #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1973 Arizona State University master's thesis by Peter Olof Bersell examines how fishes are distributed vertically in two linked central Arizona reservoirs, [[Roosevelt Lake]] and [[Apache Lake]], on the [[Salt River]]. Vertical vertical gill netting was carried out on five runs in Roosevelt and four in Apache, with sampling timed to ecological rather than calendar conditions. Multiple linear regression (MLRP) identified [[Dissolved Oxygen]], [[Chlorophyll-a]], [[Net Plankton]] and, to a lesser degree, [[Temperature]] as the most consistent correlates of fish dispersion. Light penetration (depth of the [[Euphotic Zone]]) appeared to act indirectly. Most fishes in both reservoirs were netted in the upper 10 m of the water column.

## Background & Description

The Salt River reservoirs, a chain of four impoundments covering about 100 km of river, were built in the early 1900s for water storage and irrigation in the Sonoran Desert. Their recreational use grew with the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]]. Biological work began in 1954 through Arizona State University and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and the author's study (1970–71) was one part of a broader survey funded under P.L. 88-309.

Roosevelt Lake (behind Roosevelt Dam, completed 1911) is broad and open, with a mean width about five times that of Apache and a volume nearly six times greater. Apache Lake lies in a steep-walled canyon below Roosevelt Dam (Horse Mesa Dam, completed 1927) and has a basin slope of 32.0% versus 2.0% for Roosevelt. Apache was drawn down during the study and was essentially drained in autumn 1971.

Methods centred on three-net "gangs" of white nylon gill nets (mesh sizes 2.54, 3.81 and 7.62 cm), set vertically in multiple sets at transects. Water quality was measured with a [[Hydrolab]] II-B meter, a submarine photometer, and a Hach colorimeter, and water was sampled with a Kemmerer sampler. Chlorophyll-a was extracted with acetone and measured spectrophotometrically; net plankton was collected with a Clarke-Bumpus sampler.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime:** Both reservoirs are warm monomictic. Roosevelt's metalimnion sat at 10–15 m, while Apache's was at roughly 5–7 m. Apache was isothermal during run #4 because of drawdown through deep penstocks.
- **Oxygen:** Metalimnetic waters in both reservoirs fell below 1.0 mg/L in summer. The study adopted 2.0 mg/L as a lower tolerance limit, following [[Eley et al. (1967)]].
- **Light:** Mean euphotic zone depth was 9.2 m in Apache (clearer) versus 5.4 m in Roosevelt (more turbid). Roosevelt's turbidity was attributed to inflow from the Salt River and Tonto Creek.
- **Water chemistry:** Specific conductance ranged from 1,100 to 1,380 µmhos/cm (about 500 to 800 mg/L TDS). pH ranged from 5.7 to 9.4. Neither variable was judged limiting for fishes in the upper 10 m.
- **Trophic links:** [[Threadfin Shad]] are planktivores that graze on [[Phytoplankton]] and zooplankton, and shad were the main forage fish for bass, crappie and catfish.

## Key Findings

- **Dispersion drivers:** Dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll-a, net plankton and, to a lesser extent, temperature were the most frequently significant correlates. Variance explained by the MLRP models was generally low, often below 30%.
- **Depth distribution:** In Roosevelt, almost all species were above the thermocline. In Apache, most species were below it. Both lakes held most fishes within the top 10 m, and the average depth of capture was under 10 m for most species.
- **Between-lake comparison:** Run #1 showed low correlation of fish dispersion between lakes (r = 0.35), mainly because shad were distributed in a binodal pattern in Roosevelt. Run #4 showed the highest between-lake correlation (r = 0.88).
- **Threadfin shad:** Of shad netted in Roosevelt during run #1, 99% were above the thermocline, versus 30% in Apache. Depth of capture averaged 4.2 m in Roosevelt and 5.2 m in Apache. About 73% (Apache) and 61% (Roosevelt) of shad were within the euphotic zone.
- **Largemouth bass:** The most important game fish, with 222 netted in Roosevelt (mean depth 5.8 m) and 37 in Apache (mean depth 5.7 m). Most bass were in the top 10 m. About 58.5% (Roosevelt) and 54.1% (Apache) were above mean euphotic depth.
- **Carp:** Most common in the upper 15–20 m. Carp were the only species that occurred in greater numbers below the euphotic zone limit in both reservoirs, consistent with their bottom-feeding habits.
- **Catfish and sunfish:** Catfish averaged 7.7 m (Roosevelt, 46 fish) and 5.7 m (Apache, 35 fish). Sunfish (genus *Lepomis*) were mostly in shallow water along the perimeter. Black crappie were the third most abundant species in Roosevelt (110 netted, mean depth 3.4 m).
- **Buffalofishes:** Only 27 bigmouth buffalo were caught in Roosevelt and 7 in Apache. Average depth of capture was 6.2 m and 7.8 m, respectively. Black and smallmouth buffalo were too scarce for depth analysis.
- **Conclusion on controls:** Dissolved oxygen set outer limits on distribution. Within those limits, euphotic zone depth acted indirectly, probably through shad abundance, which in turn shaped predator distribution. Conductivity, pH and turbidity were judged not to have a demonstrable effect on depth distribution.
- **Food-chain signal:** Of 21 biological MLRP runs with a significant correlation, nine indicated a food-chain relationship.
