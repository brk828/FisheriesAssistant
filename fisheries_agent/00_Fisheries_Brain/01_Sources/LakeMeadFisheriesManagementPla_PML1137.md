# Lake Mead Fisheries Management Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 plan, prepared by fisheries biologist John A. Hutchings for the [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], sets out the ecological condition, fish community history, angler use and management strategies for [[Lake Mead]], a Colorado River reservoir on the Nevada–Arizona border. The plan finds that the reservoir is nutrient-poor, that its food web is simple and dependent on a single planktivore ([[Threadfin Shad]]), and that the [[Striped Bass]] fishery has replaced the earlier largemouth-bass-dominated fishery. It proposes a [[Nutrient Enhancement Program]], [[Cover Enhancement]] for [[Largemouth Bass]], stock and density studies, and a [[Black Crappie]] distribution effort, all within a ten-year frame.

## Background & Description

**Reservoir morphometry.** Lake Mead is the largest U.S. reservoir by volume and second only to Lake Powell in surface area. Key figures from the document:

- Volume at spillway levels: about 29.5 million acre-feet
- Maximum depth: 580 ft; mean depth: 55 m
- Shoreline: 550 miles; length: 110 miles
- Shoreline development index: 9.7
- Hydraulic retention time: 3–4 years
- Littoral zone (water 11 m or less touching land): ≤10% of area at 1,200 ft elevation, and ≤15% near the old 1,150 ft level

Only the volume has changed significantly, because of silting from the [[Colorado River]] before 1963. Projected Bureau of Reclamation operating levels near 1,180 ft were expected once the [[Central Arizona Project]] was online.

**Purpose and operation.** [[Hoover Dam]] was built mainly for flood control in the Imperial Valley, with irrigation, hydroelectric power, municipal and industrial water supply and wildlife mitigation as additional purposes. Flood regulation requires 5.35 million acre-feet of flood-control storage on 1 January each year. Discharge from Hoover Dam enters the epilimnion at 83 m depth at an operating level of 364 m.

**Watershed and inflows.** The Colorado River supplies about 90% of inflow. The Virgin and Muddy Rivers (Overton Arm) and the [[Las Vegas Wash]] (Las Vegas Bay) add smaller inputs. The only major withdrawal is at Saddle Island, which supplies municipal, irrigation and industrial water to the Las Vegas metropolitan area. The Colorado River drains 242,100 sq mi in the United States and 2,200 sq mi in Mexico.

**Geology and vegetation.** The lake floor is mainly Muddy Creek Formation sediments: moderately consolidated sand, silt and clay, with interbedded strata including gypsum, anhydrite and rock salt. A 1986 cover study of the inundation zone recorded about 58 plant species across 30 study sites. Three vegetation tiers were recognized, with the highest-inundation tier dominated by narrowleaf cattail, willow, and Bermuda grass, and the intermediate tier by salt cedar, willow and skeleton weed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Temperatures reach about 30 °C in August in the Overton and Temple Basins. A thermocline forms in June–July, and oxygen concentrations drop in the hypolimnion, with a clinograde profile in Gregg's Basin and Temple Basin in 1982. Light transparency (Secchi depth) varied widely, from 13.0 m in Boulder Basin to 18.0 m in Temple Basin in 1981.

**Nutrients.** The Colorado River was the main nutrient source, contributing 63% of total phosphorus inputs in 1981 (a second, OCR-garbled 1982 value is not reliably recoverable). Only 10–40% of Colorado and Virgin River phosphorus was biologically available because it binds to sediments. Las Vegas Wash carried a high share of bioavailable phosphorus, providing 57% and 45% of orthophosphorus in 1981 and 1982. Nitrate-nitrogen peaked in winter–spring (0.25–0.30 mg/l) and declined through summer.

**Primary productivity.** Lower-basin productivity fell 73% in 1981 and 82% in 1982 relative to 1977–78, after Las Vegas wastewater treatment began removing phosphorus. Carbon-14 estimates showed production shifting from the Upper Basin (1955–1962) to the Lower Basin during the Las Vegas Wash inputs (1962–1981). Middle Las Vegas Bay productivity fell from 2,239 mg C/m²/day (1977–78) to 1,309 mg C/m²/day (1982).

**Zooplankton.** Two studies were compared: Burk (1975–76, 23 species in Boulder Basin) and Sollberger (1984–85, 42 species). Copepods dominated from June to November, with rotifers in December–January. Density differences were large, and the document attributes them to planktivory: threadfin shad dominated in Sollberger's Las Vegas Bay station, while rainbow trout predation shaped Burk's results.

**Benthic macroinvertebrates.** A 1987 UNLV survey recorded 98 species in 69 genera and 10 phyla. Of these, 32 species were insects, including 22 Chironomidae. Mean densities per m² were highest in the Colorado River inflow area (about 3,860) and lowest in Virgin Basin (456); overall average near 1,500, with a range of 500–3,900. Crayfish densities were very low, at 0.02–0.04 per m², and the only species found was [[Red Swamp Crayfish]]. Green sunfish appear to be depressing crayfish and benthic populations.

## Key Findings

**Fish community history**
- **Native fishes:** Historically resident Colorado River species now largely lost. [[Humpback Sucker]] persisted only in the upper basin. [[Bonytail Chub]] has not been verified since 1967. Colorado squawfish and humpback chub were considered extirpated by 1978.
- **Introduced cold-water fishes:** [[Rainbow Trout]] was the main salmonid stocked. Several other trout and salmon species were stocked, and freshwater eel was documented in 1972.
- **Warm-water fishes:** [[Channel Catfish]] (first stocked 1892–1893), [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]] and black bullhead were established. [[Threadfin Shad]] became the dominant forage fish after its 1954–55 stocking. [[Striped Bass]] was introduced in the 1960s.
- **Stocking:** The document reports a total of 512,551,768 fish stocked over 91 years (as printed). Largemouth bass stocking totalled 111,151,860 (1935–1942 period, as printed). Rainbow trout stocking peaked at 801,861 in 1975.

**Angler use and harvest**
- The first documented creel survey (1953) estimated 139,241 angler days. The creel program has run continuously since 1958.
- Angler days grew from 139,241 (1953) to about 645,000 in the current five-year average, roughly a 460% increase over 33 years.
- The average catch rate was 2.1 fish per angler day, and the average annual catch was about 1,298,650 fish.
- In the most recent five-year period, striped bass made up 50.3% of the catch, channel catfish 22.98%, largemouth bass 10.7% and black crappie about 10%.
- Preference angler use: striped bass anglers were most common at 51.6%, followed by indiscriminate anglers at 26.4% and largemouth bass anglers at 13.9%.

**Regulations** changed repeatedly: a 10-inch minimum on largemouth bass (1952), a striped bass minimum of 16 inches (1965), and striped bass bag-limit increases to five (1979) and ten (1984). The black crappie limit was reduced to 15 in 1986 and the largemouth limit to six in 1987.

**Problems identified**
- Lake productivity is very low: only about 5% of surface area is classed as moderately productive, tied to low phosphorus.
- Threadfin shad is the only planktivore and the sole secondary consumer; its population crashes affect all sportfish.
- Largemouth bass recruitment is poor, likely from inadequate cover and forage, and harvest has declined.
- Black crappie has been reduced to satellite populations, formerly 26% of the sport harvest.
- Striped bass growth rates at the third annulus fell 29%, weight and condition declined, and winter forage conditions were poor.

**Management goals**
- Year-round sport fishery with a catch rate of three fish per preference angler day, or minimum biomass harvest of three pounds per angler day.
- Striped bass condition factors at least 50% above 1985 levels.
- A cold-water fishery catch rate of 1.5 fish per preference angler day without a weight restriction.

**Projections (ten years):** Angler demand grows 2.2–2.5% per year, reaching about 715,000–725,000 angler days in five years. Total harvest is projected above 2.8 million fish, with striped bass biomass harvest above 2 million pounds annually.

**Management strategies**
- **Nutrient enhancement:** Preliminary results indicate a potential increase of 20% in fish flesh. Target area is at least 60,000 acres (Overton Arm, Virgin Basin, Gregg's Basin), with potential harvestable biomass of 2.0–2.3 pounds per acre. Applications include discharge-tube deflectors, aircraft and watercraft nutrient application, and linkage to the proposed Spring Canyon Pumpback Storage Project.
- **Largemouth cover:** Plant material in at least 10% of identified aquatic habitat and 5% of terrestrial areas, aiming at 200,000 harvestable fish per year.
- **Stock recruitment and winter condition:** Six-month captive studies of over-wintering fingerling bass, plus candidate forage species evaluation if winter forage is limiting.
- **Striped bass:** Spring and fall studies of spawning stress, diet and condition, using captive holding at the [[Lake Mead Fish Hatchery]].
- **Black crappie:** Survey satellite populations, tag and track fish, and test whether reproduction can repopulate restored cover areas.
- **Creel program:** A ten-percent angler questionnaire to assess catch-and-release rates and to validate creel indices, with possible harvest restrictions depending on stock density results.
