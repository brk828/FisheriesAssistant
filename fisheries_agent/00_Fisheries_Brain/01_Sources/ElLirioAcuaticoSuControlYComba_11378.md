# Water Hyacinth Control and Management in Mexico (1963 Bulletin Article)

#aquatic-botany #pontederiaceae #weed-control

## Executive Summary

This section of the *Boletín de Piscicultura Rural* (Vol. XIII, 1963), published by the Dirección General de Pesca e Industrias Conexas and the Instituto Nacional de Investigaciones Biológico-Pesqueras, compiles information on the invasive aquatic plant [[Water Hyacinth]] (*Eichhornia crassipes*, "lirio acuático"). The plant has become a serious pest in Mexico since 1948, especially in the [[Lerma-Chapala Basin]]. It obstructs fishing, navigation and hydroelectric systems, contributes to sedimentation and transpires large volumes of water. The authors review its taxonomy, biology and reproduction, then assess three control approaches: [[Chemical Control]] (mainly [[2-4-D Herbicide]]), [[Mechanical Control]] and [[Biological Control]] with phytophagous fish. They conclude that an integrated, multi-method program is preferable to any single method, and that harvested biomass should be put to use.

## Background & Description

**Origin and spread.** The species is native to Brazil. It spread across the Americas, Asia and Africa, mostly after introduction as an ornamental plant. It is common in reservoirs, irrigation canals, and natural and artificial lakes in Mexico.

**Morphology.** The aquatic form has spongy, bladder-like petioles arranged in a rosette. Its leaves are oval and fleshy, with abundant stomata on both surfaces. A short stem extends underwater as a rhizome, reaching up to 8 cm in adult plants (up to 35 cm, per the text). Older parts rot as the plant grows.

**Flowers and seeds.** Flowers are borne in spikes, last about 24 hours, are bluish-mauve and zygomorphic, with three petals, three sepals, six stamens and a three-carpelled ovary. Fruit is a capsule containing at most 50 seeds. Fruiting is sensitive to frost and sometimes fails.

**Reproduction.** Propagation is mainly vegetative, through stolons and rhizomes. Mats up to 60 cm thick can form. Seed needs about two months to mature, and germination is favored at 28–36 °C with intense light. Vegetative multiplication yields flowers in about 30 days. The document cites a nursery in Africa where two isolated plants produced about "1 200" plants in 130 days (the scanned text reads "| 200", so the exact figure is uncertain). Fertilizer runoff into irrigation canals also promotes growth.

**Seasonality.** Growth declines in winter and spring because of low rainfall and temperature, and peaks in summer and autumn. Photogrammetric monitoring by the Secretaría de Recursos Hidráulicos on Lake Chapala over one year confirmed these seasonal highs and lows.

**Taxonomic placement.** The document places the genus in the monocotyledons, family Pontederiaceae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Impacts:** The plant obstructs fishing and navigation, hampers hydroelectric supply to Guadalajara, promotes silting through accumulation of organic matter, and transpires large, uncharacterized volumes of water.
- **Fish and plankton:** Inorganic chemicals (sodium chlorate, arsenic trichloride, mineral acids, caustic soda) were abandoned because they are toxic and corrosive, and they also destroy plankton and fish.
- **Herbicide safety:** The text states that 2-4-D does not harm fish and acts only on the target plant. It penetrates through the waxy cuticle, not the stomata. Ester solvents are preferred to protect neighboring crops.
- **Crop risk:** Cotton is highly sensitive to 2-4-D, so spraying should occur at least one kilometer from cotton fields.
- **Competing weeds:** In the Yaqui Valley survey of October 1960, invaded area was 85% [[Tule]] (*Typha*-type reeds), 10% water hyacinth and 5% carrizo (giant reed).
- **Potential vectors:** The text notes that introduced species can carry parasites and diseases, and that habitat factors (temperature, altitude, food, predators) can limit establishment.

## Key Findings

**1. Chemical control**
- Inorganic herbicides were abandoned. Before 1943, substances such as sodium chlorate and arsenic compounds were used. They were described as about 95% effective but too dangerous.
- After 1943, organic herbicides, especially 2-4-D, gave the best results. Reported doses ranged from about 1 to 9 kg/ha, and concentrations from 0.1% to 1% for motor pumps and up to 40% for aerial spraying.
- Other herbicides listed included aminotriazole, TCA, pentachlorophenol, kerosene/oil emulsions and sodium naphthalene acetate. Most achieved only about 50% destruction of total vegetation.
- **Yaqui Valley (Ciudad Obregón, Sonora):** A satisfactory result was reported. The mixture for 100 gallons of water included 4.560 kg of 2-4-D salt, 0.712 kg of a second component and 120 ml of emulsifier, enough to spray one acre. Tule required 3–4 applications per year. Water hyacinth was controlled almost completely after 55 days, with 3 applications per year sufficient.
- **Lake Chapala:** A trial on the eastern side of the pier used 6.350 kg of the salt, 0.960 kg of the second component and 0.237 kg of detergent per 140 gallons. Dilutions of 80% and 50% killed plants briefly, but new shoots reappeared, so these were judged non-lethal.
- **Cost:** A complete spray unit (truck, Wisconsin engine, centrifugal and pressure pumps, boom) cost $268,210.50. The 2-4-D amine cost $11.84/kg and TCA $6.89/kg. At nine kg/ha, herbicide alone costs $552.90 per hectare. The authors describe chemical control as costly.

**2. Mechanical control**
- Manual harvesting by work crews is effective but costly in some countries, though considered feasible in Mexico.
- A machine on Lake Chapala pushed plants into twin crushing rollers. Plants sank quickly, but cleanup was too expensive, so the method was dropped.
- Two chemical analyses of harvested plants were reported (sources: University of Guadalajara and the Dirección General de Pesca laboratory). Results included high fiber, ash and carbohydrate levels. The authors suggest uses as forage, cellulose source or soil conditioner. The Dirección General de Pesca report gave a moisture value of 92.20% and total ash of 15.60%, with hemicellulose, cellulose, lignin and related fractions at 24.38%.
- Flame throwers were tried, but although surface temperatures were reported as 2,000–3,000 °C (as printed), the heat did not reach rhizomes 2–3 cm below the surface.

**3. Biological control**
- In Malaysia, grass carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idellus*) and *Puntius javanicus* gave excellent results in fish ponds. [[Tilapia Mossambica]] and *Tilapia melanopleura* were also used, though these tilapias are less selective feeders.
- Dense stocking of about 4,000 carp per hectare cleared a pond infested with *Myriophyllum*, per Prugining and Lipschitz (1957, cited by Hickling).
- Ducks and nutria were also reported to help control aquatic weeds.
- In Mexico, the Dirección General de Pesca held *T. mossambica* under observation at the El Tejar ponds (Veracruz) for future culture and possible weed-control trials. Experiments with the [[Manatee]], an herbivorous coastal Gulf mammal, were also proposed.

**4. Recommended integrated program**
1. Obtain aerial photographs to map the invaded area before any control work.
2. Apply a multi-method plan: chemical (aerial or boat spraying with 2-4-D), mechanical (drains, irrigation canals, lakes and ponds, with reuse of harvested material), and biological (experimental introduction of phytophagous fish).
3. Use harvested biomass for forage, fertilizer or cellulose, pending laboratory tests.

**5. Summary conclusions**
- Complete eradication is judged very difficult and costly, so the goal should be a considerable reduction of invaded area.
- Combined methods are expected to outperform any single method, depending on local conditions.
- Grass carp and *T. mossambica* are judged feasible for biological control in neotropical and some temperate zones of Mexico. Manatee introduction requires testing.
- Other aquatic weeds merit similar study.
