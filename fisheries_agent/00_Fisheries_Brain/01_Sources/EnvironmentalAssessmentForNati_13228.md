# Environmental Assessment for Native Fish Management Activities on Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #endangered-species #habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

This Environmental Assessment evaluates proposed improvements to native fish rearing facilities at [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]], focused on recovering the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The [[Razorback Sucker]] population declined from an estimated 60,000 individuals in 1989 to approximately 25,000 in 1992, with no successful recruitment to juvenile or adult life stages despite successful spawning. The population consists entirely of aging fish (25-45 years old), with larval fish disappearing at 10-12 mm total length due to predation by non-native fishes. The proposed action includes constructing an earthen berm at [[Davis Cove]] to replace an ineffective barrier net, improving berms at existing backwater rearing areas ([[North Nine Mile]], [[Willow Cove]], [[Arizona Juvenile]], [[North Arizona Juvenile]]), and establishing an additional grow-out cove. The project is coordinated by the [[Colorado River Native Fish Work Group]], comprising multiple federal and state agencies.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system of Western North America. Once common throughout the mainstream and major tributaries from Wyoming to Mexico, the species is now reduced to remnant populations in [[Green River]] (Utah), upper [[Colorado River]] (Colorado), [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona and Nevada), and scattered individuals elsewhere. The species was listed as endangered in November 1991, with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] proposing critical habitat including [[Lake Mohave]].

[[Lake Mohave]] contains the largest remaining population of [[Razorback Sucker]]s. Monitoring over several years determined that this and other populations comprise old fish ranging 25-45 years in age. Although spawning, incubation, and hatching are successful, larval razorback suckers disappear at 10-12 mm total length. Recruitment to juvenile or adult life stages is non-existent, and remaining populations face extirpation through natural aging. Scientific consensus identifies predation by non-native fishes as the single most important factor responsible for recruitment failure.

The [[Colorado River Native Fish Work Group]] developed razorback sucker rearing facilities around [[Lake Mohave]] in backwater areas adjacent to the lake and in [[Davis Cove]]. The project serves as a pilot to test immediate recovery concepts and as a prototype for other potential native fish recovery efforts along the lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. The razorback sucker has proven amenable to hatchery propagation, but on-site natural reproduction and grow-out represents an attractive, fiscally responsible alternative to remote hatchery facilities.

Eight backwater areas and one cove are currently used to rear razorback suckers. All backwater areas except [[Yuma Cove]] are ephemeral. The rearing process involves: (1) collecting larval fish from [[Lake Mohave]] and holding them for up to three weeks at the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] laboratory in Boulder City, Nevada; (2) transplanting fish into backwater areas to produce 10 cm juveniles; (3) transplanting to [[Davis Cove]] to reach 30 cm prior to lake release, at which size predation risk is substantially reduced.

[[Davis Cove]] (approximately 3 acres, located at 35°12'30" N, 114°34'30" W) was selected for its size, sufficient water depth during low lake elevation, capacity to support nearly 1,000 pounds of fish, and narrow neck suitable for barrier placement. A net placed in February 1992 proved ineffective—fluctuating water levels caused the net to be buried under sand on the north shoreline, allowing it to rip and permitting predator fish entry. Less than 1% of over 10,000 fingerling razorbacks and 6,000 bonytail chubs placed in the cove were successfully salvaged.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] ecosystem has been dramatically altered by dam construction, water diversion, and introduction of non-native fish species. [[Lake Mohave]], formed by [[Davis Dam]], represents a highly modified aquatic environment where native fish species face intense predation pressure from introduced species. The reservoir's operational characteristics include fluctuating water levels (operational high water elevation: 647 feet) that affect backwater habitat stability.

The proposed improvements address critical limiting factors in native fish recovery:

**Predation Pressure**: Non-native predatory fishes are the primary cause of recruitment failure. Larval and juvenile razorback suckers are highly vulnerable at sizes below 30 cm. Effective isolation of rearing areas from predators is essential for juvenile survival.

**Habitat Requirements**: Razorback suckers require protected backwater habitats for early life stages. Natural backwaters adjacent to [[Lake Mohave]] provide suitable conditions but are vulnerable to breaching during high water or storm-induced wave action. The ephemeral nature of most backwaters (7 of 8) creates management challenges.

**Population Demographics**: The aging population structure (25-45 years) with complete recruitment failure indicates imminent population collapse without intervention. Natural mortality will eliminate the species from [[Lake Mohave]] within decades absent successful augmentation.

The [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), also endangered, faces similar challenges and could benefit from the same rearing methodology if the razorback sucker project proves successful.

## Key Findings

**Alternative A (No Action)**: Under this alternative, the existing barrier net at [[Davis Cove]] would remain in place as the only predator barrier. Backwater berms would not be modified, and no additional backwater areas would be developed. This alternative would result in continued decline and eventual extirpation of razorback sucker and bonytail chub populations from [[Lake Mohave]], decreasing biological diversity of native fish species.

**Alternative B (Proposed Action - Improvements to Backwater-Rearing Facilities)**:

*Davis Cove Berm Construction*: An earthen berm of approximately 1,500 cubic yards of local soils would be constructed across [[Davis Cove]] during fall 1994. The berm would be approximately 150 feet long, 56 feet wide at center, 10 feet wide at top, and 23 feet high total with 3-foot freeboard. Slopes would be 1:1. A geotextile material would provide erosion protection. Source material would come primarily from an existing service road used during [[Davis Dam]] construction (500-600 cubic yards), with supplemental material from adjacent areas (1,440 and 780 cubic yard volumes). Equipment (one or two front-end loaders with 5-yard bucket capacity and a D-7 bulldozer) would be barged to the site.

*Backwater Improvements*: Berms at [[North Nine Mile]] and [[Willow Cove]] would be improved using heavy equipment accessing via existing approved roads and beaches below operational high water elevation. [[North Arizona Juvenile]] and [[Arizona Juvenile]] coves would be accessed by boat with hand crews using sand and gravel from below operational high water elevation.

*Additional Grow-Out Cove*: An additional cove would be identified on [[Lake Mohave]] with rocky shorelines (to prevent sand-shifting problems) and fitted with a temporary barrier net.

**Environmental Impacts**:

*Natural Resources*: Short-term soil disturbance from heavy equipment use would be mitigated by using rubber-tired vehicles, limiting access to approved routes, and raking tracks. Temporary localized siltation would occur during construction, with water quality monitoring implemented. Source materials would be naturally replaced through wave action, water fluctuations, and flash floods. Vegetation impacts would be minimal, primarily affecting exotic tamarisk (which the park actively eradicates). Wildlife would be temporarily displaced during construction.

*Fish Populations*: Extensive salvage operations using electroshocking and seining would remove most fish from [[Davis Cove]] prior to piscicide (antimycin and rotenone) application. Remote possibility exists for incidental take of endangered razorback suckers or bonytail chubs not captured during salvage. However, long-term benefits to these species far outweigh potential short-term impacts. Non-native fish removed are widely distributed throughout Lower [[Colorado River Basin]] impoundments, making impacts minimal.

*Cultural Resources*: No impacts anticipated. Archaeological inventory would be conducted prior to construction in compliance with sections 110 and 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act. Construction would halt if cultural resources were unexpectedly discovered.

*Recreation*: [[Davis Cove]] would be temporarily closed to recreational use (swimming, fishing) for at least five years, though many alternative coves exist nearby. Motorized vessels already prohibited. Construction during low visitor use periods would minimize impacts. The berm would be covered with natural gravel and sand to blend with surrounding banks. Interpretive signage and brochures would explain the temporary closure.

**Project Goals**: The [[Lake Mohave Backwater-Rearing Facility Management and Research Plan]] (1989) and Draft Addendum (1994) established goals including: (1) stock 50,000 juvenile razorback suckers into [[Lake Mohave]] from naturally produced reservoir larvae, artificially spawned reservoir adults, and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]; (2) increase existing production levels; (3) adopt more effective culturing techniques; (4) analyze on-site rearing effectiveness.

**Compliance**: A U.S. Department of Interior Pesticide Use Permit was submitted by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. Biological assessment prepared under section 7 of the Endangered Species Act, with [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] conducting biological opinion on potential impacts to bonytail chub or razorback suckers. Section 404 Clean Water Act permit application forwarded to [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]]. [[Arizona Department of Environmental Quality]] notified for water quality review. Soil analysis for gradation and plasticity conducted according to Arizona water quality standards.

**Conclusion**: The National Park Service concluded no significant adverse effects on threatened, endangered, or candidate species within the project area. Mitigation measures would effectively reduce likelihood of mortality to razorback suckers and bonytail chubs from piscicide application. Long-term protection and population stabilization of endangered species would result from successful project implementation. The project represents a critical intervention to prevent extirpation of the largest remaining [[Razorback Sucker]] population and provides a prototype for native fish recovery throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]].
