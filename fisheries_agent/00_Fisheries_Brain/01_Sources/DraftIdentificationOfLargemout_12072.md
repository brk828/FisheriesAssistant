# Identification of Largemouth Bass Based on Non-invasive Tissue Samples: Electrophoretic Pre-genotyping

#ichthyology #marine-biology #micropterus

## Executive Summary

This study evaluates whether [[Electrophoretic Pre-genotyping]] can identify [[Largemouth Bass]] (*[[Micropterus Salmoides]]*) from non-invasive tissue samples. Earlier [[Isozyme]] methods required [[Liver Biopsy]] or autopsy, which killed or impaired the fish and so removed it from [[Brood Fish]] programs and from stocking or recapture. The authors tested erythrocytes, blood plasma, caudal and pelvic fin, and scale/epithelial tissue from hatchery-reared fish. They resolved 46 protein and enzyme systems covering a minimum of 51 loci, and identified seven well-resolved polymorphic loci. Caudal fin gave the best resolution and blood plasma the poorest. The authors conclude that non-invasive pre-genotyping can support [[Fish Culture]] and [[Fishery Management]] without loss of individual fitness.

## Background & Description

The document is a research article by J. Holt Williamson, Donald C. Morizot, and Gary J. Carmichael. Williamson and Morizot are affiliated with the United States Fish and Wildlife Service, National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center, San Marcos, Texas. Morizot and Carmichael are also affiliated with the University of Texas, Science Park, Research Division, Smithville, Texas.

The authors describe the use of gene frequency data to study the genetic structure of fish populations, which is now a standard approach. They note that [[Genetic Tag]]s, which are natural biochemical properties of a fish, are superior to artificial marks because they are permanent and do not alter behavior or fitness. The study species is the largemouth bass, which the authors used as a model. Their earlier work had identified at least 64 enzyme loci in:

- [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] (*M. s. floridanus*)
- [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] (*M. s. salmoides*)
- Two [[Intergrade Strain]] hatchery stocks (Marion, Alabama, and Inks Dam, Texas)

Applications the authors list include:

- Identifying brood fish for selective breeding, including for homozygosity or heterozygosity
- Screening brood fish for marker proteins
- Monitoring brood fish for inbreeding potential
- Producing offspring of known genotype for stocking
- Identifying rare, endangered, or threatened fishes from small populations
- Monitoring fish during fishing tournaments

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not describe natural habitat, wild population ranges, or environmental conditions. It refers to hatchery-reared fish from the San Marcos facility, and to domestic and native fisheries that need to separate natural recruitment from annual stock introductions. The comparison between Florida and northern subspecies is genetic rather than ecological.

Two loci separate the Florida and northern forms, according to the cited [[Biochemical Genetics]] work (Phillip et al. 1982). In the authors' discussion, caudal fin tissue can distinguish the two subspecies using the [[Isocitrate Dehydrogenase-B]] locus (IDH-B), which is expressed mainly in the liver. The authors suggest this matters for managing stocks of mixed or intergrade origin.

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- Fish were anesthetized before sampling. Blood was drawn from caudal vessels into heparinized syringes, centrifuged, separated into plasma and red cells, and stored at -90 °C.
- One pelvic fin and a caudal fin sample were removed. Scales and epidermis were scraped from the caudal peduncle and frozen at -90 °C.
- Analysis used [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]] (vertical gels) following Siciliano and Shaw (1976) and Brewer (1970). Histochemical stains came from Siciliano and Shaw (1976), Harris and Hopkinson (1977), and Shaw and Prasad (1970).
- Two buffer systems, TVB (pH 8.0) and TC (pH 7.0), resolved most proteins. Glyoxalase I resolved best on a tris-maleate buffer (pH 7.4).

**Results**

- 46 protein and enzyme systems were adequately resolved from non-invasive samples (Table 1).
- The authors estimate a minimum of 51 coding loci in the sample.
- Seven well-resolved polymorphic loci were obtained: [[Adenosine Deaminase]] (ADA), [[Isocitrate Dehydrogenase-2]] (IDH-2), [[Malate Dehydrogenase-1]] (MDH-1), [[Mannosephosphate Isomerase]] (MPI), [[Peptidase-B]] (PEP-B), [[Pyruvate Kinase-1]] (PK-1), and [[Triosephosphate Isomerase-2]] (TPI-2).
- Fin tissue resolved these seven polymorphisms. The abstract states that they can be resolved from fin tissue.
- [[Caudal Fin]] was the most useful tissue, meaning it resolved the largest percentage of loci. [[Blood Plasma]] was the least useful. MDH-A and MDH-B were expressed in fin, red cells, and scale/epithelium but not in plasma.
- Table 1 lists isozyme expression across tissues (blood/erythrocytes, plasma, caudal fin, pelvic fin, and scale-epithelium) using relative intensity scores from 0 to +++.

**Comparison with previous data**

- Compared with earlier electrophoretic data (Williamson et al. 1985), the non-invasive tissues yielded 76.1% of the enzyme systems, 82.2% of the gene loci, and 68.8% of the polymorphic loci.
- The authors estimate that concentrating samples could resolve at least seven more loci and four more polymorphisms from fin tissue.

**Conclusions and implications**

- The authors state that they can characterize the stock of largemouth bass with greater than 99% certainty using only tissue samples that do not alter individual fitness.
- Pre-genotyping can shorten breeding studies by one generation. Instead of genotyping offspring to infer parental genotype, aquaculturists can identify brood fish and still use them for breeding.
- The method allows genetic research on endangered species without the "killing to study" problem.
- The authors argue that pre-genotyping, when supported by appropriate background genetic information, is a useful tool for aquaculture and fishery management.
