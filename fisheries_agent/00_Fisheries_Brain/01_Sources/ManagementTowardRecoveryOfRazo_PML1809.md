# Management Toward Recovery of Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter reviews the decline of the [[Razorback Sucker]], a large, uniquely shaped catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of western North America, and describes the development of a recovery strategy based on propagation and reintroduction. The authors compile historical records (about half of the more than 350 cited works are "gray literature"), trace the collapse of wild populations from abundant mid-1800s to 1900s status, and document the establishment of a [[Hatchery Propagation Program]] at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Reintroductions into the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]] of central Arizona since 1981 have yielded only 118 recaptures through autumn 1988, and no self-sustaining population has been confirmed. The authors identify predator exclusion as the factor most strongly linked to successful recruitment and argue that recovery depends on resolving the recruitment failure, expanding monitoring, and improving information management.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described as *Catostomus texanus* by Abbott (1861); *Xyrauchen* was erected by Eigenmann and Kirsch (in Kirsch 1889) on specimens from the Gila River. It is the sole species of its genus.
- **Morphology:** Large, uniquely shaped body. Males are smaller, slimmer-bodied, with larger fins, nuptial tubercles on fins and caudal peduncle, and a more pronounced predorsal keel. Females are larger, thicker-bodied, with smaller fins and a lower, broader keel. Breeding adults are dark brown to black dorsally and creamy-yellow ventrally, with a variable lateral band.
- **Size:** Maximum known adult size about 70 cm TL in Lake Mohave; one historical specimen weighed about 4.5 kg (10 lb).
- **Distribution:** Once ranged from Wyoming to northwestern Mexico. Largely disappeared; natural populations are now absent from the Gila River basin, and only isolated individuals remain in most of the range.
- **Historical names and uses:** Named Tsa'xnap by Yuman peoples and sxJex by Cocopah. Harvested as food and for fertilizer; archaeological remains and [[Indigenous Fishing Weirs]] at ancient Lake Cahuilla shores attest to a major fishery.
- **Scientific attention:** Little studied until recently; regarded as inconsequential by sport-fishery managers and largely ignored in general works.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical abundance:** Jordan and Evermann described it as "very abundant" in parts of the basin in the 1890s; Gilbert and Scofield found it "extremely abundant" near Yuma in 1890. Large numbers appeared in the [[Salton Sea]] after 1904–1905 floods, with one resident reporting 147 fish taken in an hour (1909).
- **Lower basin decline:** Concern emerged after the closure of [[Hoover Dam]] (1935). Essentially all records since then are adults, with a historic absence of small fish in collections. Persisted in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]] into the 1970s; Lake Mohave held the last major population.
- **Upper basin:** Populations were widespread but less abundant. Major declines followed closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (1962) and [[Fontenelle Reservoir]] (1953), and rotenone treatment in 1962. Holden (1973) caught only 53 razorback suckers in 1957–1972 across 22 reaches.
- **Habitat use:** Spawning in Lake Mohave occurs over cobble and gravel, often in depressions 20 cm or more deep, with mean depth ~2.2 m at one site. Upper basin spawning occurs in broad, flatwater segments over gravel and cobble bars. Adults use backwaters, eddies, and gravel pits; radio-tagged fish also used sand-bottomed channels.
- **Movement:** Tagged upper-basin fish moved from less than 10 km to 256 km; of 74 recaptured fish, 24 (32%) traveled less than 10 km.
- **Reproduction:** Spawning in Lake Mohave runs December–May, with a peak in January–March; water temperatures during spawning ranged from 10.5 to 21°C. Fecundity estimates range from about 1,700 to 2,100 ova per cm SL in hatchery females.
- **Reasons for decline:** Dams, habitat alteration, altered water quality, cold hypolimnetic releases, competition, predation by non-native fishes, hybridization, and parasites were all considered; none was proven as the single cause. Predation is supported by experimental evidence.

## Key Findings

1. **Historical records are sparse and scattered.** About half of more than 350 cited works are gray literature; the authors personally examined each cited original work where possible.
2. **Recruitment has been absent for decades.** No natural recruitment into wild reservoir populations was detected between 1963 and the authors' study period, despite extensive sampling; Lake Mohave adults (oldest 44 apparent annuli) appear to be the last major population.
3. **Predator exclusion enables recruitment.** Stocked fish in predator-free habitats (an 8.9 ha urban lake in Tempe, Arizona; a Dexter NFH goldfish pond) grew rapidly and reproduced. Razorback larvae disappeared where predatory fishes were present.
4. **Hatchery production is effective.** Percentage hatch of eggs at Dexter NFH commonly exceeded 90%; unfed larvae survived 20.5–100% over 50 days in ponds.
5. **Reintroduction results were poor.** Through autumn 1988, 118 fish were recovered from about 12 million larvae and juveniles stocked into central Arizona; none was recovered from the Colorado mainstem despite 2.9 million stocked in 1987–1988.
6. **Juvenile stocking outperformed larval stocking.** Recaptures came only from fish stocked as juveniles; larger fish were thought to avoid predation.
7. **Hybridization concerns were partly resolved.** Allozyme analysis (Buth et al. 1987) found hatchery and wild-hatched fish genetically identical, with introgression at low levels; no increasing hybrid trend was supported.
8. **Legal status changed over time.** The species was listed as endangered in California, threatened in Colorado, and under legal protection in five states by 1987. A 1978 federal threatened listing proposal was withdrawn in 1980; a 1989 petition to list it as endangered was pending.
9. **Recovery criteria are undefined.** The authors call for research on recruitment, movement and habitat ecology, and genetics across remnant populations, and recommend centralized data management and peer-reviewed publication.
