# Limnological Factors Limiting the Distributions of Native Fishes in the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (May 1995), funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] through the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program, examines why native fishes are unevenly distributed in the terminal 21 km of the [[Little Colorado River]]. Speckled dace occur throughout the reach, while [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] are confined to the 14.2 km below [[Chute Falls]]. The study tested two of four hypotheses: that water chemistry and that food resources limit fish distributions above Chute Falls. Food resources and water chemistry were both found not to prohibit humpback chub and bluehead sucker from the reach above the falls. The authors conclude that during base flow Chute Falls acts as a physical barrier, and that the reach could support reintroduced populations.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The perennial 21 km of the Little Colorado River above its mouth, a deeply entrenched canyon reach that narrows to under 50 m in places. [[Blue Spring]], a perennial saline spring 21 km above the mouth, provides a mean base flow of 6.31 m³/s (cms) at the mouth.
- **Historical context:** The river was perennial until the early 1900s, when land and water use turned the reach between Holbrook and Blue Spring intermittent. Native fishes in the Southwest have declined since at least the early 1900s due to dams, diversions, groundwater pumping, overgrazing, and nonnative fishes.
- **Tufa dams:** Calcite precipitation from carbon dioxide degassing produces tufa dams, mainly between Chute Falls and Salt Trail Canyon. The [[Atomizer Falls Complex]] comprises Atomizer Falls (13.6 km), Upper Atomizer Falls (13.9 km), and Chute Falls (14.2 km).
- **Water temperature:** Waters at Blue Spring are about 20°C. Temperatures at the mouth average 9°C warmer than the [[Colorado River]].
- **Fish fauna:** Of the eight fishes indigenous to the Colorado River in Grand Canyon, [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Roundtail Chub]] have been extirpated from the region, and [[Razorback Sucker]] is rare. Four native species remain in the lower LCR. Two nonnative species, [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Common Carp]], have been recorded above Chute Falls.
- **Hypotheses (Gorman 1994):** (1) water chemistry limits fish above Chute Falls; (2) food resources limit fish above Chute Falls; (3) Chute Falls is a physical barrier; (4) suitable physical habitat is unavailable above the falls. This study addressed hypotheses 1 and 2.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Longitudinal water chemistry.** Sampling was conducted at six sites (21, 20, 15, 10, 5, and 0.6 km above the mouth) in October 1991, January 1992, June 1993, and August 1993. Reach 1 (0–14.2 km, below Chute Falls) and reach 2 (14.2–21 km, Chute Falls to Blue Spring) were compared.

- Dissolved oxygen, pH, and conductivity increased from Blue Spring to about 15 km, then leveled off.
- Alkalinity and free carbon dioxide decreased from Blue Spring to 0.6 km.
- Turbidity rose to 10 km, then declined toward the mouth. It exceeded nephelometer range (>1,000 NTU) during the January 1992 runoff.
- Nitrate increased to 20 km, then generally decreased. Phosphate showed no trend.
- Trends were consistent across seasons, except for phosphate.

**Primary producers and invertebrates.** Periphyton chlorophyll *a* and invertebrate biomass (ash-free dry weight) were greatest in reach 2. One hundred and thirteen algal taxa were identified, with 93 collected at the six sites. Eight invertebrate taxa were found only in reach 2.

**Relocation experiment (June 13–16, 1993).** Age-0 and age-1 [[Humpback Chub]] and age-1 [[Bluehead Sucker]] were captured near 10.5 km, then held in cages at 12.5 (control), 15, 17.5, and 20 km for 72 h.

**Carbon dioxide and fish health.** Authors noted that free CO₂ concentrations at the 20 km site (about 349 mg/L) were near the lethal limit for age-0 humpback chub. Concentrations at 15 km (179 mg/L) and 17.5 km (190 mg/L) were well below lethal limits reported for [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[White Sucker]] in [[Black et al. 1954]].

## Key Findings

1. **Distribution pattern:** Speckled dace occur throughout the 21 km reach. Humpback chub, bluehead sucker, and flannelmouth sucker are restricted to the 14.2 km below Chute Falls.
2. **Food resources are not limiting:** Invertebrate biomass was either greater in reach 2 or did not differ between reaches. The presence of abundant speckled dace in both reaches was also cited as evidence.
3. **Water chemistry is not prohibitive:** All age-1 humpback chub and bluehead suckers survived 72 h above the falls, and most age-0 humpback chub survived at 15 and 17.5 km.
4. **Survival by site (age-0 humpback chub):** Mortality was 70% at 20 km, 0% at 17.5 km, 30% at 15 km, and 20% at 12.5 km (G = 13.20, p < 0.01). At 20 km, all fish exhibited stress behaviors such as lethargy, gulping air, and loss of equilibrium.
5. **Physical barrier:** The authors believe Chute Falls is a physical barrier to upstream movement during base flow. Combined with near-saturation oxygen and rising CO₂ above the falls, this may restrict movement.
6. **Flood effects:** Four consecutive days of mean flows of 283–464 cms at Cameron in January 1993 may have eliminated the falls' barrier effect. No native large-bodied fishes have been observed above Chute Falls since then, though sampling has been sporadic.
7. **Historical transit:** Large-bodied native fishes were present below Grand Falls in the early 1900s, indicating they once transgressed the Atomizer Falls Complex.
8. **Habitat suitability:** Habitat data from [[Mattes 1993]] above the falls resembled conditions below, where humpback chub occur, and fit [[Habitat Suitability Index]] curves from [[Valdez et al. 1990]]. Deep pools (>4 m) were also observed.
9. **Management recommendation:** The authors believe humpback chub and the two sucker species could be successfully reintroduced above Chute Falls. Physically lowering the falls to reduce the vertical drop was proposed as a way to allow natural fish movement and avoid artificial stocking costs.
