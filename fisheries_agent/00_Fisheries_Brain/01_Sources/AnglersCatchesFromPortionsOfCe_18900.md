# Anglers' Catches from Portions of Certain Michigan Trout Streams in 1939 and 1940, with a Discussion of Indices to Angling Quality

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1943 study by [[David S. Shetter]], published by the [[Institute for Fisheries Research]] of the [[Michigan Department of Conservation]], reports weighed creel-census results from portions of seven trout streams in 1939 and five in 1940. It quantifies the yield of trout to anglers in pounds per acre and evaluates two indices of angling quality: legal trout caught per hour and pounds of legal trout caught per hour. The author concludes that pounds per hour is the more valid index because it combines the number and size of fish caught.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Most earlier [[Fish Yield Studies]] could not express catch per unit effort or per unit water area in pounds, because creel clerks (often relief labor) recorded only fish counts, species and estimated average size. Minimizing recorded data let census takers interview more anglers.
- **Study period and labor:** In 1939 and 1940, crews from the [[Civilian Conservation Corps]] and [[United States Forest Service]] camps, supplemented by clerks hired by the Conservation Department, carried out intensive censuses. Censuses covered stretches one to twelve miles long.
- **Weighing:** Balances arrived late, so weighing began June 15, 1939; fish were measured individually before that date.
- **Estimation procedure:** [[Weight-Length Relationship]] curves were built for each species and stream from weighed and measured fish. Catches with only counts were assigned the period-average length for each species. Anglers seen but not interviewed were assumed to have average catches.
- **Area measurement:** Stream areas were derived from plane-table maps or by chaining length and width.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hatchery supplementation:** Hatchery trout were planted as advanced fingerlings (3.75 to 6.00 inches) in late summer and fall of 1935, 1936 and 1938. Because these fish spent at least one year in the stream before capture, their catch was treated as stream production.
- **Wild versus hatchery yield:** Net yield of wild trout was estimated from jaw tags, fin clips, or returns from earlier marked plantings. Between 80.5 and 94.8 percent of the total poundage removed by anglers was of natural origin, despite generous legal-sized plantings.
- **Angling pressure:** Pressure ranged from 103 to 180 man-hours per acre per season in the Michigan streams.
- **Comparative streams:** [[Tarzwell (1938)]] reported 29.5 to 54.2 pounds per acre for rainbow trout in Tonto and Horton creeks, Arizona (1936–1937), at 125 to 241 man-hours per acre. [[Surber (1940)]] implied yields of 7.55 pounds per acre (brook trout) and 9.64 pounds per acre (rainbow trout) in the St. Mary River, Virginia (1938–1939). The author notes Tarzwell's figures may be too high because hatchery fish were not deducted.
- **Habitat variability:** The White River's yield fell from 30.6 pounds of trout per acre in 1939 to 8.5 in 1940, which the author attributes to a stream that is good habitat year-round in one case and only usable in spring and early summer in the other.

## Key Findings

**Yields in 1939 (seven streams, 15.7 lb/acre total):**
- White River: 30.6 lb/acre of trout
- Pine River: 26.4
- Hunt Creek: 15.6
- Little Manistee River: 14.1
- North Branch of the Au Sable River: 12.3
- Pigeon River: 11.1
- Canada Creek: 3.2

**Yields in 1940 (five streams, 15.3 lb/acre total):**
- Fishdam River: 68.3 lb/acre of legal trout
- East Branch of the Tahquamenon River: 27.4
- Hunt Creek: 12.8
- North Branch of the Au Sable River: 10.6
- White River: 8.5

**Indices to angling quality:**
- Hunt Creek had the highest catch rate in 1939 (0.63 legal trout per hour) but the smallest average trout (2.18 oz), placing it second from bottom on pounds per hour (0.086).
- The Little Manistee River had a lower catch rate (0.37 per hour) but larger fish, ranking first on pounds per hour (0.107).
- Rankings change with the criterion used (see Table II), so the choice of index matters.

**Average sizes (Table III):**
- Brook trout averaged roughly 7.5 to 8.9 inches, and rainbow trout about 8.2 to 9.5 inches.
- Average catch weights ranged from about 2.2 oz (Hunt Creek, 1939) to 5.3 oz (White River, 1940) for all trout.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- The author recommends pounds of fish per unit of angling effort as the most valid index for comparing waters, seasons, or parts of a season, and plans to use it whenever weights can be obtained.
- Yield estimates carry errors from measurement, seasonal weight-length variation and uninterviewed anglers, but these are assumed to be compensating and comparable across streams.
