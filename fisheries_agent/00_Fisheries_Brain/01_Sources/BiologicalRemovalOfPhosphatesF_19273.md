# Biological Removal of Phosphates from Aquatic Media

#ichthyology #aquatic-biology #tilapia

## Executive Summary

This master's thesis in [[Fishery Biology]] tested whether living organisms could remove dissolved [[Ortho-Phosphate]] from water, with the goal of controlling [[Eutrophication]] in [[Wastewater Reclamation|reclaimed wastewater]] intended for recreational fisheries. Several candidates were screened, including fish, macrophytes and clams. The most effective system combined a natural [[Algal Succession]] with filtration by beds of the oriental clam [[Corbicula Fluminea]]. Algae raised pH, which precipitated phosphate as [[Hydroxyl-Apatite]]; the clams then removed the colloidal apatite. In the tests, this system reduced phosphate to below 0.30 mg/L in 16 days or less and produced a clear effluent. The author concludes that the approach is most suitable for alkaline waters and that effluent should be released only once total phosphate is below 0.30 mg/L.

## Background & Description

The study addresses a recurring problem in reclaimed-water fisheries. Earlier projects at [[Santee Recreation Project]] (Santee, California) and Lancaster, California, along with work by Hallock (1969), recorded fish kills after algal blooms depleted oxygen. The author argues that phosphorus is the most practical nutrient to exclude, because oxygen and carbon are replenished from the atmosphere and some blue-green algae fix atmospheric nitrogen. Phosphorus enters aquatic systems only through added materials or mineral dissolution. A key source in municipal effluent is polyphosphates from detergents, which hydrolyze to the ortho-phosphate form that plants can use.

Experiments used well water with salt content similar to Tucson municipal wastewater, spiked with reagent-grade ortho-phosphoric and nitric acids. Most tests ran in outdoor pools of 22.7 kL each, about 1 m deep, with a surface area of 23.6 m². Candidate organisms were screened against four criteria: removal rate, ease of recovery of removed phosphate, dependability, and effect on overall water quality.

Analytical methods included the ammonium molybdate-stannous chloride method, X-ray diffraction and electron microscopy of sediments, atomic absorption for calcium and magnesium, and turbidity and chlorophyll measurements. Membrane filtration (0.45 µm) was used to remove colloidal apatite. The author notes that all initial ortho-phosphate values from the early surveys carry an undetermined positive bias.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Phosphate chemistry.** Removal depended on the carbonate buffering system. Algal photosynthesis removed dissolved CO₂, which raised pH. At pH above about 9.0, calcium phosphate precipitated as hydroxyl-apatite, and calcium carbonate converted to apatite in the presence of the HPO₄²⁻ ion. The author reports a peak effectiveness near pH 10.5, where the concentration of PO₄³⁻ is highest. Turbidity tracked pH elevation, and both were linked to algal blooms.

**Lime treatment comparison.** Conventional tertiary treatment at South Lake Tahoe, California, adds lime to raise calcium and hydroxide. The author notes that this produces apatite crystals from about 400 Å to visible sizes, which can form stubborn colloids that re-dissolve when pH falls. Alum and other flocculants are also used to speed precipitation.

**Shading and reversibility.** Clam filtration worked well only in sunlit systems, where algal metabolism kept pH above 9.0. In shaded units, pH dropped, the carbonate equilibrium shifted, and part of the phosphate returned to solution.

**Clam biology.** Prokopovich (1969) cited a pumping rate of over 0.5 L per hour for a 1-gram *Corbicula fluminea*, and a sedimentation rate of 5.4 g dry weight per year. Heinsohn (1958) showed the species lacks a parasitic larval stage and instead broods its young, so it poses no threat to fish in reclaimed-water fisheries. Clams trapped apatite in mucous and deposited it as pseudo-feces.

**Limits on biological removal.** Clams survived eutrophic conditions only with constant circulation and water temperatures below 30°C. Planktonic desmids, *Chroococcus* and *Oscillatoria* were the dominant algae observed. *Chara* grew on clam shells but seldom survived heavy blooms.

**Sea water.** The conclusions suggest that sea water, which contains an average of about 410 mg/L of a major cation (the element is garbled in the scan, likely calcium), could support automatic phosphate removal even more readily. The author proposes this for combating estuarine eutrophication, but this was not tested.

## Key Findings

### Screening of organisms

- **Mozambique tilapia (*Tilapia mossambica*):** Eight tanks were stocked at 175 fish per tank (74,148/ha) and fed no supplemental food. Four tanks received fish and four served as controls. Phosphate was added at 1.52 mg/L/week. Mean yield over 60 days was 1.7738 kg per tank (751.72 kg/ha). Mean phosphate removal was only 1.21% of the 216 g added per tank, so fish were excluded.
- **Water hyacinth (*Eichornia crassipes*):** Forty plants grew to 258 in fresh well water. In the test, four tanks each received 48 plants. Chlorosis appeared in all plants within two weeks, vegetative reproduction ceased, and the plants died. Chlorosis appeared only when algal blooms pushed pH above 8.8, which the author attributes to iron and manganese precipitation. Hyacinth was excluded because blooms are expected under eutrophic conditions.
- **Duckweed (*Lemna minor*) and giant Vallisneria (*Vallisneria gigantea*):** Duckweed was intended to shade the surface and prevent blooms. Wind concentrated it on one side, exposing about half the surface. An algal bloom developed, duckweed became chlorotic and died within two weeks, and Vallisneria appeared dead within six weeks. Roots and crowns, however, recovered in clear water. Both were judged unsuitable.
- **Water sprite (*Ceratopteris thalictroides*):** Plants grown indoors under 4600 lux became chlorotic and decayed after being moved outdoors in spring.

### Natural algal succession

- Four pools of enriched water developed algal blooms within three to four days. Phosphate was added every seven days at 1.52 mg/L.
- Over 60 days, phosphate fell from 15.2 mg/L to 1.19 mg/L (Figure 1).

- Clam filtration used about 2.73 kg of clams per 94.5 L aquarium. The author calculated that this volume was filtered 14.4 times per 24 hours, and that the clam bed could deposit 40.4 g dry weight of suspended material per day.
- Turbidity reached up to 21 mg/L (5.0 series), 24.0 mg/L (10.0 series) and 27.5 mg/L (15.0 series), with pH readings of 9.20 or higher.
- Mean precipitation rates for the six systems were 0.390, 0.394, 0.561, 0.618, 0.870 and 0.930 mg/day. The table gives these values without per-system labels.
- Sediment dry weight in the 5.0, 10.0 and 15.0 mg/L series was 4.30, 5.30 and 3.28 kg per tank. Control tanks held only trace amounts.
- The author concludes that clam filtration produced effluent below 0.30 mg/L more quickly and reliably than algae alone. Without clams, the 10.0 and 15.0 mg/L series would have needed an indefinite time to reach that level.

### Chemistry and mechanism

- X-ray diffraction confirmed that the removed phosphate precipitated as hydroxyl-apatite. The calculated free energy of the conversion of calcium carbonate to apatite is −15 kcal/mol.
- Conclusions state that removal occurs without added reagents in alkaline waters, and that clam-algae treatment raises pH without lime, which precipitates calcium and magnesium until a new carbonate equilibrium is reached.
- Dissolved calcium and magnesium showed no appreciable change relative to the effluents of other tanks.

### Method caveats

- The 0.45 µm filter does not remove all apatite crystals, which range from about 400 to roughly 4,500 Å. The author advises that literature phosphate values be treated as having an undetermined positive bias unless shown otherwise.
- Nitrate was kept above 1 mg/L so that nitrogen would not limit algal growth.

### Recommendations

- Reclaimed tertiary-treated water should be released only after total phosphate falls below 0.30 mg/L. This level allows high primary productivity while avoiding the anoxia caused by large algal standing crops.
- Clam beds should remain sunlit and continuously circulated, and water should stay below 30°C.
- The approach is suited to alkaline waters. The author suggests water hyacinth may be useful in humid regions, where transpiration can exceed open-water evaporation by a factor of 7.8 in arid areas, citing Holm, Weldon and Blackburn (1969).
