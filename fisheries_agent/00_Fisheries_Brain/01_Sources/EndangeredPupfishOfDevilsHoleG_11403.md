# Endangered Pupfish of Devils Hole Given New Lease on Life

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary
This paper documents the emergency conservation transplant of the critically endangered [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) from its sole natural habitat, [[Devils Hole]] in Death Valley, to an artificial refugium constructed below [[Hoover Dam]] in Black Canyon, Nevada. With a global population of fewer than 300 individuals, the species was threatened by a declining water table caused by groundwater pumping for agriculture in [[Ash Meadows]]. A cooperative effort among the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], [[Nevada Department of Fish and Game]], the [[National Park Service]], the [[Desert Fishes Council]], and the [[University of Nevada at Las Vegas]] resulted in the design and construction of a spring-fed refugium mimicking the physical and light conditions of Devils Hole. Initial transplants in October 1972 succeeded, with adult survival and the first documented reproduction of the species outside Devils Hole, representing a major milestone in desert fish conservation.

## Background & Description
- Extremely small body size, averaging **less than 1 inch (2.5 cm)** in length.
- Distinct morphology relative to related pupfish populations in [[Ash Meadows]], having diverged significantly due to long-term geographic and reproductive isolation in Devils Hole.
- Considered a genetically and morphologically distinct species (*Cyprinodon diabolis*), separate from other regional pupfish descended from a common ancestral lineage tied to a prehistoric Death Valley lake system fed by the [[Amargosa River]] and [[Carson Slough]].
- Concern noted that if refugium fish diverge morphologically or genetically under altered environmental conditions, they could evolve into a distinct subspecies, complicating conservation of the "original" Devils Hole population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Natural habitat**: [[Devils Hole]] — an isolated, warm-water pool with no surface outflow, situated at ~2,300 ft elevation atop a subterranean carbonate aquifer cavern system.
  - Upper pool reaches a maximum depth of 46 feet; associated cavern system exceeds 299 feet.
  - Water surface lies ~50 feet below ground level.
  - Reproduction and feeding activity are almost entirely confined to a **shallow shelf just below the water surface**, a critical microhabitat identified by researcher [[Carol James]] and corroborated by [[Robert Miller]] (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology).
- **Thermal requirements**: Optimum water temperature approximately **92°F**, necessitating a warm-spring-fed refugium site.
- **Trophic ecology**: Diet and habitat include algae (a major natural food source), along with associated fauna such as planarians, snails, and beetles — all transplanted alongside the fish to replicate the Devils Hole food web.
- **Refugium habitat**: A 10- by 20-foot rectangular concrete tank in [[Black Canyon]], fed by a natural warm spring, with a 10-foot deep end and a 3-foot shallow shelf, oriented along the same directional axis as Devils Hole to replicate sunlight exposure. Substrate, algae, and invertebrates from Devils Hole were introduced to mimic the natural ecosystem.
- **Threats**: Groundwater pumping from agricultural wells near [[Ash Meadows]] caused declining water levels in Devils Hole, reducing the shallow shelf habitat essential for feeding and reproduction, threatening total reproductive failure.
- **Biogeographic history**: Ancestors likely colonized the Death Valley region via ancient marine/lake connections prior to the Sierra Nevada uplift; subsequent aridification and geological isolation cut Devils Hole off from other Ash Meadows springs, driving speciation.

## Key Findings
- Global population of *Cyprinodon diabolis* was estimated at **fewer than 300 individuals** at the time of writing, confined to a single natural locality.
- U.S. Geological Survey field studies linked declining Devils Hole water levels directly to groundwater pumping from nearby agricultural wells, threatening reproductive habitat.
- Research by [[Carol James]] (M.S. thesis, UNLV) established that nearly all feeding and reproductive activity occurs on the shallow shelf near the surface — a finding validated by [[Robert Miller]].
- Legal action: The Department of the Interior requested cessation of pumping from four critical wells; when pumping continued, the Department of Justice was asked to pursue litigation (filed July 22, 1973), pending in Federal District Court in Nevada.
- A refugium site with matching warm-spring characteristics (~92°F) was identified below Hoover Dam in early 1971; construction ran January–August 1972.
- On **October 2, 1972**, seven pupfish were transferred to the refugium for a 96-hour bioassay; two died, five survived and adapted well.
- On **October 13, 1972**, 20 additional adult pupfish were transported via helicopter; one died two days later.
- By **October 21, 1972**, successful reproduction was documented in the refugium — the **first recorded instance of Devils Hole pupfish reproducing and surviving outside Devils Hole**.
- By **December 1, 1972**, all surviving adults appeared well-adapted, at least six juveniles were growing, and spawning activity continued; only one instance of adult-on-juvenile predation was observed.
- Management of the refugium was formally transferred from the Bureau of Reclamation to the Nevada Department of Fish and Game and Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife on November 13, 1972.
- Long-term success criteria identified: sustained annual recruitment of young, and periodic introduction of additional Devils Hole individuals to maintain genetic heterogeneity.
- Authors caution that if the refugium population evolves divergently (e.g., into a distinct subspecies) due to differing environmental conditions, it would not serve as an adequate conservation surrogate for the original Devils Hole population, requiring alternative conservation strategies.
