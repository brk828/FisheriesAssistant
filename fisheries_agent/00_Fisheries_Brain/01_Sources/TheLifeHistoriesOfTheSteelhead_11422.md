# Life Histories of Steelhead Rainbow Trout and Silver Salmon at Waddell Creek, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game bulletin (Fish Bulletin No. 98, 1954, by Leo Shapovalov and Alan C. Taft) presents quantitative life-history data for [[Steelhead Rainbow Trout]] and [[Silver Salmon]] derived from a nine-season (1933-34 through 1941-42) two-way trap and dam study on [[Waddell Creek]], a small coastal stream in Santa Cruz County. The study counted upstream and downstream migrants, sampled scales for age determination, marked juveniles to estimate survival, and used a paired stream, [[Scott Creek]], to quantify homing and straying. The authors also provide management recommendations. The document is a major reference for anadromous salmonid biology on the California coast.

## Background & Description

The two species are closely related salmonids with contrasting life histories. Silver salmon (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*) are semelparous: all adults die after a single spawning, and they must spend time at sea before spawning. Steelhead (*Salmo gairdneri gairdneri*) are iteroparous and may spawn more than once, and some individuals complete their life cycle entirely in fresh water. Both are anadromous, digging redds in gravel where females deposit eggs that are fertilized and covered.

The experiment was launched in 1932 as a cooperative project of the California Trout Investigations and the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries. A dam and two-way trap were built in summer 1933 about 7,250 to 9,250 feet above the stream mouth. Life-history categories were recorded with a notation system (e.g., "2/1" for two years of stream life and one year at sea; "S" for spawning). Age was designated by brood year, and fish were measured by fork length. Waddell Creek drains about 26 square miles, with mean annual rainfall of 55 to 60 inches in the headwaters and about 30 inches near the coast. Its mouth is typically closed by a sand bar for part of the year, which governs the timing of fish entry.

The stream fish fauna included [[Prickly Sculpin]], [[Aleutian Sculpin]], [[Three-spined Stickleback]], and [[Tidewater Goby]]; the Striped Bass was the only introduced species. Amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals, and an extensive invertebrate assemblage (about 30 pages of taxonomic listings, summarized here) were also documented.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and mouth dynamics:** Lagoon closure and opening strongly influence fish entry. Silver salmon and steelhead enter after the bar breaks with the first heavy rains. Mouth openings were recorded across seasons, and the lagoon area and depth varied widely.
- **Habitat:** The upper watershed lies in the Redwood belt (Big Basin); the lower stream passes Douglas fir, chaparral, and grassland. Spawning riffles were composed of medium and small gravel.
- **Predators and competitors:** The [[California Coon]] was the only mammal directly feeding on adult salmonids. Egg-eating sculpins and stickleback were present. Fish-eating birds such as the [[American Merganser]] were absent or rare.
- **Invertebrate food base:** Trichoptera, Ephemeroptera, Diptera, Plecoptera, and Coleoptera (riffle beetles) dominated, along with crayfish (*Pacifastacus klamathensis*) and lagoon crustaceans such as *Corophium* and *Neomysis*.
- **Disease and mortality:** Disease was uncommon in natural conditions. A suspected [[Furunculosis]] outbreak in 1933-34 caused abnormal mortality. Fungus (*Saprolegnia parasitica*) was a secondary infection, and the trap itself killed juveniles in 1933-34 until it was modified.

## Key Findings

**Silver salmon**
- 2,218 adults were counted in the upstream trap over nine seasons. Runs were highly concentrated: 96% were taken between December 10 and February 10. Spawning runs ranged from 84 (1937-38) to 583 (1934-35) fish.
- Almost all adults returned at age 1/1 (males only, one season at sea) or 1/2 (both sexes, two seasons at sea). A demarcation at 49 cm separated 1/1 from 1/2 fish with 99.1% accuracy.
- Juveniles migrated to sea as age 1 fish. Of 18,362 juveniles checked, 18,256 were age 1; migration peaked between April 22-28 and May 20-26, with over 95% of migration occurring April 8 to June 9.
- Egg production was estimated from the relationship Number of Eggs = 0.01153 × Length^2.903 (r = 0.682), based on Scott Creek fish. Total egg production over the study was estimated at about 2.9 million eggs.
- Over-all survival from eggs to returning adults averaged 0.13%; survival from marked downstream migrants to returning adults averaged 2.3%. An inverse relationship between egg production and survival percentage was noted.
- Straying was 14.9% from Waddell to Scott Creek and 26.8% in the reverse direction, far higher than in steelhead.

**Steelhead**
- 3,888 adult steelhead were counted in the upstream trap; the largest season count was 539 (1934-35) and the smallest 373 (1937-38). About 96% were taken between December 3 and May 5.
- Most adults were first-time spawners (82.8%). Second spawners made up 15.0% and third spawners 2.1%. The dominant life-history categories were 2/1 (29.8%), 2/2 (26.5%), 3/1 (10.5%), and 2/1S.1 (8.1%).
- Downstream migration of stream fish totaled 36,779 individuals over nine seasons, with age groups migrating in sequence during spring.
- Primary over-all survival from eggs to first-spawning sea-run adults averaged about 0.023%. Marked-fish survival averaged 2.4%, comparable to silver salmon.
- Straying was 1.9% from Waddell to Scott Creek and 2.9% in reverse, much lower than in silver salmon.
- Females outnumbered males among repeat spawners because males experienced higher post-spawning mortality.

**Shared findings and recommendations**
- The authors emphasize the importance of quantitative life-history data for management, and note that variation within populations and compensatory responses complicate prediction.
- Scale reading was validated with marked fish and two-observer checks; more than 95% of age assignments were judged correct.
- Management recommendations (in the later sections of the bulletin) cover regulation, habitat improvement, pollution control, fish rescue, and artificial propagation, including recognition that steelhead spawning extends past the winter season and that heavy fishing and hatchery holding can distort run composition.
