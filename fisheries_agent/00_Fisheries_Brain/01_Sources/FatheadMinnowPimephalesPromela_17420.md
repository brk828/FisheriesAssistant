# Fathead Minnow (*Pimephales promelas*) Life History in Iowa River Populations, 1965-1966

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by [[Dale R. Carlson]], published in the *Iowa State Journal of Science*, describes life history traits of [[Fathead Minnow]] populations in two Iowa river systems: the [[Des Moines River]] (3 locations, 1965) and the [[Skunk River]] drainage (21 locations, 1966). Fish were sampled with seines in the latter half of June and in July. The study covers sex ratio, spawning timing, fecundity, length-weight relationships, scale-based annulus formation, age structure, and competition with [[Bluntnose Minnow]]. Its main conclusion is that spawning peaks later than reported for pond and lake populations, and that the Des Moines River population appears to be sustained largely by immigration from tributaries or ponds during floods.

## Background & Description

The [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas* Rafinesque) previously had life history studies under rearing conditions ([[Markus]] 1934) and in lakes ([[Isaak]] 1961). Carlson's study addresses river and stream populations, which were less documented.

- **Range:** Southern Canada east of the [[Rocky Mountains]] to Maine, south to the Susquehanna River and the Gulf States; introduced west of the Rockies (Eddy 1957).
- **Iowa distribution:** Widespread in ponds, lakes, streams, and rivers. Prefers shallow, calm water and is rare to absent in flowing waters of larger rivers (Harlan and Speaker 1956). Tolerant of a wide range of turbidity.
- **Sampling methods:** A 15-ft common sense minnow seine (1/4-inch mesh) was used in 1965. In 1966, 1/8-inch mesh netting was added to the central portion of the seine. Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin, then weighed (triple-beam balance, nearest 0.01 g) and measured.
- **Measurement corrections:** Standard, fork, and total lengths were compared on 436 specimens (1956 measurements as reported). Relationships given as FL = 0.92 TL and SL = 0.75 TL. Live fish gained an average of 13% in weight and lost 2.2% in length after preservation (n = 31); preserved values are reported.
- **Aging:** Scales (10-20 per fish) were examined with a scale projector. The author judged scales reliable for aging and back-calculation despite irregularities noted by Noble (1964).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat associations:** In the Skunk River drainage, fathead minnows were most abundant in headwater creeks with slow current, 6 in. to 2 ft of water, and sand-silt bottoms with much floating and submerged algae. [[Bluntnose Minnow]] occupied slow-to-moderate current, 6 in. to 3 ft depths, over sand-gravel bottoms.
- **Flood influence:** High, unstable water levels in the spring and early summer of 1965 may have produced a transitory population in the Des Moines River. Recruitment there is thought to depend on fish washed in from tributaries or ponds.
- **Interspecific competition:** Trautman (1957) reported that fathead minnows are most abundant where bluntnose minnows are absent. This held in the Skunk River (1966) but not in the Des Moines River (1965), where both species were often collected together in similar proportions. An aquarium observation showed a guarding male fathead chasing other minnows, but no other aggressive interaction was detected.
- **Temperature cue:** Markus (1934) and Isaak (1961) linked spawning onset to water temperatures near 64°F.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction and sex ratio**
- Sex ratio was essentially 1:1 (Table 1). Most larger fish were male. Largest male measured 74 mm TL; largest female 66 mm.
- Smallest males with nuptial tubercles were 48 mm; smallest spent females were 40 mm.
- Fathead minnows are intermittent spawners, possibly spawning from May to mid-August.
- Late July was the period of greatest spawning in both study areas. Spawning was probably unsuccessful in the Des Moines River, where rugose (egg-care) pads were absent or poorly developed.

**Fecundity**
- Isaak (1961) reported 636 to 1,338 eggs per female for 10 fish (41-51 mm TL).
- Four Skunk River females collected July 5, 1966: 47 mm (802 eggs), 52 mm (2,328), 54 mm (2,622), and 55 mm (1,800). About one-third of eggs were ripe.

**Length-weight relationships**
- Mean weights were usually higher in late July than earlier in the Des Moines River (1965). In the Skunk River (1966), weights were usually higher in late June.
- No consistent weight differences were found between sexes at equal lengths. Skunk River fish were heavier at equal lengths than Des Moines River fish.
- Because the relationship changed through the season, no single equation was adequate. Four formulas were fitted (log W = a + b log L), for example:
  - July 16-30, 1965, males: log W = -2.9257 + 3.0064 (log L)
  - July 16-30, 1966, combined sexes: log W = -4.2717 + 3.2187 (log L)
- Weight increases faster than the cube of length, so condition improved with size.

**Annulus formation and aging**
- Fish collected June to early July either lacked an annulus or showed one with much growth beyond it. Mid- to late-July specimens often showed definite annulus formation at the scale edge.
- Annulus formation coincided with the peak of spawning. Hile (1948) was cited for assigning January 1 as the common birthday in the Northern Hemisphere.

**Age structure and growth**
- Age I and Age II fish overlapped broadly in size (1965 and 1966).
- Age II made up 33.4% of 1966 Skunk River collections. Most 1965 Des Moines River fish were Age II. This contrasts with [[Markus]] (1934), who reported only 15-20% reaching Age II, and [[Isaak]] (1961), who found no Age III fish.
- Mean lengths changed little from late June to late July (Table 5). The author attributed this to high, unstable water levels and considered it the probable reason for late annulus formation.
- Calculated length at first annulus for late-July Age II fish averaged 40.0 mm (1965, n = 80) and 42.7 mm (1966, n = 37).
- Males averaged larger than females at Age I, but similar sex ratios at Age II suggested no sex difference in longevity.

**Abundance changes in the Des Moines River**
- [[Starrett]] (1950) found fathead minnows in 14% of 1946-47 Des Moines River collections.
- Fathead minnows were abundant in some habitats in 1965, but only 6 were captured in 1966 at the same stations.

**Conclusions**
- Peak spawning is later than reported for pond and lake populations. Many late-spawned fish may not reach reproductive size the following summer.
- Des Moines River populations probably depend on immigration from tributaries and ponds during floods.
- Competition with bluntnose minnows may reduce fathead populations where both occur, though this was not consistent in 1965.
