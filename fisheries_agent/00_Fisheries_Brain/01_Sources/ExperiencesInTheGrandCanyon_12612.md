# Experiences in the Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This early twentieth-century account by [[Ellsworth Kolb]] and [[Emery Kolb]] recounts their photographic journeys through the [[Grand Canyon]] and its tributaries, culminating in a 1911 boat trip from [[Green River]], Wyoming, to [[Needles]], California, which repeated [[Major John Wesley Powell]]'s 1869 expedition. Fish appear only incidentally. The authors describe spawning "bony tail" fish in the [[Little Colorado River]] and mass fish kills caused by mud-laden flood water in [[Lodore Canyon]]. The text is primarily a travel and photography narrative, so its aquatic content is anecdotal rather than systematic. No sampling methods, sample sizes, or population data are given.

## Background & Description

The authors lived for twelve years at the head of the [[Bright Angel Trail]] on the south rim of the Grand Canyon. Their stated goal was a complete photographic record of the canyon's scenery. The article has three parts:

- **Cataract Creek Canyon ([[Havasu Canyon]]):** a trip to the "most beautiful" tributary, home to the Havasupai people.
- **The Cataracts of the Little Colorado:** a hard descent via the [[Tanner Trail]] to the mouth of the Little Colorado.
- **Shooting the Rapids of the Colorado:** a boat voyage from Green River, Wyoming, through seventeen canyons, which the authors describe as a duplication of Powell's journey.

The authors describe the fish of the Little Colorado as "Gila Elegans, or Gila Trout," a silvery-white species that "seldom exceed[s] 16 inches in length," with "a small flat head somewhat like a pike" and a large dorsal hump. They call it "bony tail" because of its numerous small, dividing bones. Modern taxonomy places *Gila elegans* as the bonytail; the article's common name "Gila Trout" is a historical label.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry and mineral deposition**
- Havasu Creek water is "heavily impregnated with mineral" (magnesium, lime, and silicate), which coats vegetation and builds travertine dams and falls. The [[Mooney Falls]] are about 180 feet high.
- Little Colorado water was "strongly mineral and very unpalatable," while the muddy Colorado was preferred for drinking.
- During the 1911 spring flood, a sample of the Lodore Canyon water "contained 20 per cent of an alkaline silt."

**Flow regimes**
- The Colorado and Green rivers were low in some reaches (for example, Red Canyon) and in flood in others. A 6-foot rise in Lodore Canyon was accompanied by mud-clogged water.
- Flood stages could raise the water "50 feet higher" at the [[Havasu Creek]] mouth and up to 107 feet above low water in Marble Canyon.

**Terrestrial and riparian features noted**
- Cottonwoods were cut down by [[Beaver]] in Brown's Hole.
- The authors saw mountain sheep trails on ledges, a mountain sheep on the rim, and wild burros descended from escaped prospectors' animals.
- Other wildlife mentioned includes a junco, a nuthatch, rats in a stone building, and barrel cacti.

**Canyon geology**
- The canyon's rock sequence includes the [[Marble Wall]] and the [[Granite Gorge]], which the authors call "perhaps the wildest and most forbidding part of the Grand Canyon."
- Rapids were formed by debris from side canyons or by single dikes or ledges, not by a uniform gradient.

## Key Findings

**Fish observations**
1. **Spawning in the Little Colorado (May):** At the junction with the Colorado, "the fins and tails of numerous fish could be seen above the water." The tail-striking produced a noise the authors first mistook for shale sliding. They caught "all we cared to use that evening" with hooks and lines.
2. **Edibility:** The fish are "nicely flavored," but "filled with countless small bones which divide and subdivide until they are almost like needle points."
3. **Flood kill in Lodore Canyon:** After the flood rise, "fish were swimming on the top of the water, gasping for fresh water." The authors struck 14 fish with oars for food. Catfish, suckers, and humpback bony tail were found at the surface, and "many dead fish, with their gills filled with this mud," were found afterward.

**Expedition facts (as reported)**
- The Kolbs' trip began September 8 (1911) at Green River, Wyoming, and ended at Needles on January 18 (1912), after 101 days on the river.
- The route covered about 850 miles and seventeen canyons, with a total descent of about 6,000 feet, through 365 "big rapids."
- Cataract Canyon contains 45 bad rapids in 41 miles, and nine parties lost their lives there.
- Lodore Canyon's river descends 425 feet in its short length. The authors took eight days to cover 20 miles.
- Cataract Canyon's "Smith" (a solo trapper) was never seen again by the authors, but later wrote that he survived after 17 days in the canyon.

**Historical and access notes**
- The Bright Angel Trail (the Cameron trail) carried about 7,000 people yearly on muleback and charged a toll of $1 per animal.
- Earlier prospectors built most completed trails to the canyon bottom. Trails without use "wash out" within a few years.
- A motor-boat route down the Green to the Grand River was described as free of rapid danger.

**Bottom line**
The article documents incidental fish behavior and mortality, but does not provide quantitative fish surveys. Its strongest aquatic evidence is the flood-related fish kill and the spawning Gila elegans of the Little Colorado. Both are useful as historical observations of the Colorado River system before major dam construction, but they should not be treated as systematic data.
