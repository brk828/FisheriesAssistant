# Fishes of the Verdigris River in Oklahoma

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae #river-survey

## Executive Summary

This master's thesis documents a comprehensive ichthyological survey of the [[Verdigris River]] drainage in Oklahoma conducted during summer 1956 and spring 1957. The survey sampled 88 locations using seines, rotenone, and gill nets to determine species composition and relative abundance. A total of 39,649 fish were collected, representing 68 species, 32 genera, 14 families, and 4 hybrid combinations. The [[Verdigris River]] rises in east-central Kansas and flows 351 miles before emptying into the [[Arkansas River]], draining 8,150 square miles. The river exhibits high seasonal variability, ranging from large swift flows in spring to disconnected pools in late summer. Water quality was rated excellent (dissolved solids <500 ppm), though localized pollution from industrial and municipal sources affected some sections. The study was conducted in anticipation of dam construction near Oologah, Oklahoma, to provide baseline data for future comparison of fish fauna changes post-impoundment.

## Background & Description

The [[Verdigris River]] system had received minimal attention from ichthyologists prior to this study. Early collections by Hubbs and Ortenburger (1929b) yielded only 19 species from one site on the river proper and one on [[Hominy Creek]]. Additional records came from Oklahoma fisheries biologists studying reservoir populations, including Jenkins (1949) reporting 16 species from [[Lake Claremore]], and Hall (1951) and Thompson and Hutson (1951) each listing 8 species from [[Lake Pawhuska]].

The river rises at 1,375 feet elevation in the Flint Hills of Kansas and descends to 480 feet at its mouth, averaging 2.6 feet per mile gradient (1.2 feet per mile in Oklahoma). The channel width varies from 150-500 feet, with well-defined banks 10-50 feet high. The stream bed consists primarily of rock or shale. The river experiences extreme flooding, with a 42-48 foot range between maximum high and minimum low water levels. The valley is 1.5-2 miles wide (occasionally 3-4 miles), with most land used for agriculture and oil production.

Geologically, the area consists of sedimentary formations with blue shale and limestone outcroppings. Alluvial soils in bottoms are heavy black loam resistant to erosion. The drainage basin forms 5.1% of the total [[Arkansas River]] drainage area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Verdigris River]] system offers diverse habitats ranging from clear eastern tributaries to muddy sluggish western streams. The main channel transforms seasonally from a large turbid swift stream in spring to small disconnected pools in late summer. During low flow periods, the stream develops a green hue from algal growth.

Pollution significantly impacts fish distribution. Immediately south of the Kansas-Oklahoma state line, industrial and municipal wastes create highly polluted conditions. These effects dissipate downstream, but [[Bird Creek]] and [[Caney River]] introduce additional organic wastes. Darters were collected between the northern pollution zone and Bird Creek entry but were rare below that point. The increased industrialization may have eliminated some species historically present, including [[Yellow Bass]] ([[Morone interrupta]]), [[Black Redhorse]] ([[Moxostoma duquesnei]]), and [[Kiamichi Shiner]] ([[Notropis ortenburgeri]]).

Eastern tributaries proved more productive in species diversity than western ones, possibly reflecting differential waste input. Clear streams typical of eastern drainage supported greater species richness compared to turbid western tributaries. This pattern follows trends observed in other Oklahoma river surveys: clearer eastern streams ([[Neosho River]], [[Poteau River]], [[Illinois River]], [[Little River]]) yielded more species than turbid western streams ([[Chikaskia River]]).

Aquatic vegetation varied by location and included Ceratophyllum, Dianthera, Spirodela, Potamogeton, Polygonum, and filamentous algae. Substrate types ranged from bedrock and shale to mud, gravel, and sand, supporting different fish assemblages.

## Key Findings

**Species Composition and Abundance:**
- Total catch: 39,649 fish representing 68 species, 32 genera, 14 families
- Four hybrid combinations documented
- Most abundant species: [[Red Shiner]] ([[Notropis lutrensis]]) with 9,670 individuals (8-75 mm), found at nearly every station with multiple spawnings indicated
- Second most abundant: [[Ghost Shiner]] ([[Notropis buchanani]]) with 6,425 individuals (16-39 mm)
- Other highly abundant species: [[Black Bullhead]] ([[Ictalurus melas]]) 2,725 individuals; [[Gambusia]] ([[Gambusia affinis]]) 2,441 individuals; [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis humilis]]) 2,158 individuals; [[Green Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis cyanellus]]) 1,905 individuals

**Family Representation:**
- Cyprinidae (minnows): 18 species, most diverse family
- Centrarchidae (sunfishes): 10 species including important game fish
- Ictaluridae (catfishes): 5 species
- Catostomidae (suckers): 7 species
- Percidae (darters): 9 species

**Notable Species Records:**
- [[Alligator Gar]] ([[Lepisosteus spatula]]): not collected but photographed specimen of 165 pounds taken January 1957; 160-pound specimen reported 1953
- [[Goldeye]] ([[Hiodon alosoides]]): 4 specimens from deep hole, all taken same day despite 10-day netting period
- [[American Eel]] ([[Anguilla rostrata]]): single specimen from pond in eastern drainage
- [[White Bass]] ([[Roccus chrysops]]): 3 specimens (54-88 mm), expected to establish in future impoundment

**Game Fish Populations:**
- [[Largemouth Bass]] ([[Micropterus salmoides]]): 305 specimens (18-171 mm), abundant throughout drainage; Jenkins (1957) estimated 30 bass per acre based on survey
- [[Spotted Bass]] ([[Micropterus punctulatus]]): 51 specimens (23-192 mm), well distributed in clearer waters
- [[Channel Catfish]] ([[Ictalurus punctatus]]): 459 specimens (20-145 mm)
- [[Flathead Catfish]] ([[Pylodictis olivaris]]): 210 specimens (20-248 mm); young-of-the-year with characteristic white splash on upper caudal fin trapped in summer pools
- [[White Crappie]] ([[Pomoxis annularis]]): 553 specimens (16-156 mm)
- [[Black Crappie]] ([[Pomoxis nigromaculatus]]): 22 specimens (25-113 mm)

**Forage Fish:**
- [[Gizzard Shad]] ([[Dorosoma cepedianum]]): 975 individuals (17-207 mm), very abundant and valuable potential forage for future impoundment
- [[Bluntnose Minnow]] ([[Pimephales notatus]]): 1,022 individuals (13-74 mm), abundant throughout with little habitat preference
- [[Stoneroller]] ([[Campostoma anomalum]]): 1,442 individuals (18-88 mm), very abundant in both turbid and clear water, higher concentrations near riffles

**Buffalofish:**
- [[Bigmouth Buffalofish]] ([[Ictiobus cyprinellus]]): 96 specimens (18-115 mm)
- [[Black Buffalofish]] ([[Ictiobus niger]]): 168 specimens (21 mm to 25 pounds), apparently more abundant than bigmouth
- [[Smallmouth Buffalofish]] ([[Ictiobus bubalus]]): 11 specimens (92-280 mm); difficult to separate from black buffalofish based on existing characters

**Suckers:**
- [[River Carpsucker]] ([[Carpiodes carpio]]): 194 individuals (24-261 mm), mainly from gill nets in deeper pools
- [[Golden Redhorse]] ([[Moxostoma erythrurum]]): 75 individuals (22-315 mm)
- [[Spotted Sucker]] ([[Minytrema melanops]]): 225 individuals (14-214 mm), fairly abundant

**Darters:**
- [[Orangethroat Darter]] ([[Etheostoma spectabile]]): 1,637 specimens (13-43 mm), most abundant darter
- [[Slenderhead Darter]] ([[Percina phoxocephala]]): 732 individuals (20-40 mm), found in deep swift water below riffles and sandy bottoms; notable exception at wave-washed rocky shore of [[Hulah Reservoir]]
- [[Logperch]] ([[Percina caprodes]]): 273 individuals (19-94 mm)
- [[Redfin Darter]] ([[Etheostoma whipplei]]): 90 individuals (19-59 mm)
- [[Channel Darter]] ([[Percina copelandi]]): 15 specimens (30-40 mm)

**Gar Species:**
- [[Longnose Gar]] ([[Lepisosteus osseus]]): 175 specimens (45-273 mm), larger individuals in main river, small specimens in tributary headwaters
- [[Shortnose Gar]] ([[Lepisosteus platostomus]]): 10 specimens taken in one week by gill net
- [[Spotted Gar]] ([[Lepisosteus productus]]): one young and one adult

**Sunfish:**
- [[Longear Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis megalotis]]): 617 specimens (20-118 mm), more numerous in clearer waters
- [[Bluegill]] ([[Lepomis macrochirus]]): 335 specimens (17-131 mm), collected throughout system but less abundant than other sunfishes
- [[Warmouth]] ([[Chaenobryttus coronarius]]): 103 individuals (19-145 mm)
- [[Redear Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis microlophus]]): 5 specimens (56-122 mm), introduced by Oklahoma Game and Fish Department

**Hybrid Combinations:**
1. [[Lepomis cyanellus]] × [[Lepomis megalotis]]: 5 specimens from stations 14, 44, 83; intermediate in body depth, pectoral fin length, maxillary length, lateral line scale count
2. [[Lepomis cyanellus]] × [[Lepomis humilis]]: 2 specimens from stations 13, 15; larger than L. humilis with characteristic large sensory cavities
3. [[Lepomis megalotis]] × [[Lepomis macrochirus]]: 3 specimens from stations 44, 83; intermediate in pectoral fin length, opercular flap length, mouth size
4. [[Etheostoma spectabile]] × [[Etheostoma whipplei]]: 2 specimens from station 14

**Anomalies:**
- [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] exhibited noteworthy anomaly: lateral-line system, especially on head, often lacked covering; ranged from small uncovered portions to almost wholly open system; planned for further study by M.R. Curd

**Introduced Species:**
- [[Carp]] ([[Cyprinus carpio]]): 60 specimens (24-170 mm), not abundant but present throughout drainage
- [[Yellow Perch]] ([[Perca flavescens]]): introduced to [[Lake Pawhuska]] before 1939; 190 individuals in 10-year creel census (1939-1948); 38 captured summer 1954
- [[Redear Sunfish]]: stocked by Oklahoma Game and Fish Department

**Species Possibly Present but Not Collected:**
- [[Sauger]] ([[Stizostedion canadense]]): reported by local residents
- [[Paddlefish]] ([[Polyodon spathula]]): reported by local residents
- Five species from Kansas portion: [[Moxostoma aureolum pigolabrum]], [[Moxostoma carinatum]], [[Notropis camurus]], [[Schilbeodes exilis]], [[Etheostoma zonale arcansanum]]
- Three additional Kansas records: [[Walleye]] ([[Stizostedion vitreum vitreum]]) from Fall River Reservoir, [[Carpiodes velifer]], [[Notropis cornutus frontalis]]

**Species Apparently Extirpated:**
- [[Yellow Bass]] ([[Morone interrupta]]): reported only from [[Vann's Lake]] (oxbow) which dried completely 1954-1955
- [[Black Redhorse]] ([[Moxostoma duquesnei]]): last collected 1950 from Osage Hills area; streams showed pollution evidence during 1956 survey
- [[Kiamichi Shiner]] ([[Notropis ortenburgeri]]): not collected since 1940; may have been bait-bucket introduction

**Comparison with Kansas Segment:**
- Schelske (1957) found 42 species at 4 Kansas stations over 12 months
- 38 of Kansas species also found in Oklahoma survey
- Species richness lower in Oklahoma portion, following west-to-east turbidity gradient

**Taxonomic Notes:**
- [[Notropis deliciosus]] name validity questioned; Dr. Royal D. Suttkus presented unpublished evidence at 1957 American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists meeting that types of Moniana deliciosa Girard 1856 do not represent species currently called N. deliciosus
- [[Notropis volucellus]] separated from [[Notropis buchanani]] using Bailey (1951) characters: N. volucellus has well-developed infraorbital canal extending around lower orbit to nostril; N. buchanani lacks infraorbital canal; additional character in N. buchanani: ring of melanophores around lower orbit (prominent in clear water, less so in turbid)
- [[Hybognathus nuchalis]] specimens were of "placita type" (smaller eye, smaller scales); Bailey (1956) considered H. nuchalis and H. placita conspecific with differences possibly caused by temperature and turbidity
- [[Pimephales]] species separated using caudal spot shape: P. promelas lacks spot; P. tenellus has vertical bar; P. notatus has rectangular spot; P. vigilax has triangle with anterior apex
- [[Chaenobryttus coronarius]] name retained despite Bailey (1956) reversion to C. gulosus, pending final taxonomic action
- Schilbeodes retained as genus name despite Taylor (1954) indication of merger with Noturus, pending published justification

**Management Implications:**
- Survey provides baseline for post-impoundment comparison after dam construction near Oologah
- Stabilized flow below dam expected to cause considerable change in fish fauna
- [[Gizzard Shad]] identified as valuable potential forage fish for future impoundment
- [[White Bass]] expected to establish successfully in impoundment (more abundant in lakes than streams)
- High abundance of sunfish, suckers, minnows, and catfish despite somewhat adverse conditions
- Species composition follows Oklahoma trend: fewer species than clearer eastern streams, more than turbid western streams
