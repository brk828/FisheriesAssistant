# Monitoring Study of Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) Reintroduced into the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 monitoring report, prepared under a one-year California Department of Fish and Game contract, tracks the post-stocking fate of hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae and juveniles released into backwaters of the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Larvae were released at several backwater sites in spring 1987 and vanished within about two weeks. Juveniles moved at night toward the main channel and were rarely recaptured. Predation by non-native fishes, chiefly [[Bluegill]] on larvae and [[Largemouth Bass]] on juveniles, was identified as a likely driver of losses, though the stomach data themselves show little evidence of large-scale consumption. The author recommends stocking larger fish, securing rearing areas, marking hatchery fish, and extending monitoring beyond one year.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was once widespread across the [[Colorado River Basin]], supporting commercial harvests and spawning runs, but it has been extirpated from much of its historic range. Decline was documented across both upper and lower basins from the 1970s onward. The species was proposed for federal endangered listing in 1978, but the proposal was dropped in 1980 because critical habitat was poorly understood. A 1981 Memorandum of Understanding between the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service set up a 10-year production and stocking program at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], with a 1991 review point. Approximately 10 million razorbacks, mostly fry, were stocked in Arizona from 1981 to 1986. California launched its own 10-year reintroduction program in 1986, using fry from Dexter and rearing at [[Niland Native Fish Ponds]] and the [[Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond]].

The study area covers about 206 km (128 mi) of river between Headgate Rock Dam and Imperial Dam, spanning the Parker, Palo Verde, Cibola, and Imperial operational divisions. The river has been channelized, riprapped, and dammed, and daily flow fluctuations below Parker Dam are substantial. Backwaters are warmer and more productive, and they offer shoreline cover such as [[Bulrush]], cattail, and [[Water Milfoil]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Backwaters formed by riprap training structures, isolated oxbows, and ponds. Substrates are mostly sand and silt, with cobble-gravel near training structures. Vegetation includes bulrush, common reed, dead mesquite, saltcedar, and dense water milfoil beds.
- **Hydrology:** Average monthly discharge below Parker Dam in 1987 ranged from 6,100 ft³/s (January) to about 6,100 ft³/s, with daily fluctuations that can flush or dewater backwaters. On April 2, 1987, a sharp release reduction left Draper (CA) Backwater dewatered by April 3.
- **Climate:** Sonoran Desert scrub; average annual rainfall at Blythe is about 8 cm (3 in), and summer temperatures can exceed 43°C (113°F).
- **Predator community:** Non-native centrarchids (bluegill, green sunfish, redear sunfish, warmouth, largemouth bass), threadfin shad, channel catfish, and flathead catfish.
- **Competing invasives:** Saltcedar dominates much of the floodplain, and Lernaea (anchorworm) infestation affected Niland pond stocks.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and larval dispersal (1987)**
- On March 31, 1987, about 1,098,804 one-week-old larvae (mean TL 9.3 mm) were stocked at four backwater sites and one isolated pond, and roughly 250,000 were planted at Niland Native Fish Ponds for rearing.
- Larvae stayed close to release points for about one to two weeks, then declined sharply. By two weeks after release, fewer than two larvae were found per spotlighting site, and surveys found virtually none.
- Larvae were associated with partially submerged shoreline cover, though not strictly confined to it.
- Two weeks after release, larvae collected averaged 14 mm TL (0.55 in), indicating growth.
- Daily water-level fluctuations were proposed as a cause of transport out of backwaters, although this was not demonstrated directly.

**Larval predation**
- Of 428 predator stomachs examined, ten contained larval razorbacks.
- [[Bluegill]] were the primary consumer: 8 of 153 (5.3%) contained larvae, with an average of 4.75 larvae (range 1–18) per stomach. Seven of these bluegill came from the Parker Backwaters one day after stocking.
- Threadfin shad and warmouth each had one occurrence.
- The author notes that rapid digestion (larvae reduced to unrecognizable fragments within about 4 hours in [[Green Sunfish]] per prior work) likely caused underestimation.

**Juvenile dispersal**
- Juveniles from the [[Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond]] and Niland ponds were released at Parker Backwater (AZ), Parker Backwater (CA), and Goose Flats Backwater.
- Nocturnal movement toward the main channel was consistent across sites. At Parker Backwater (AZ) on May 7, 1987, catch rose overnight, with eight fish in each of two nets by 0400 hrs.
- At Goose Flats (November 17, 1987), electro-shocking of 4,015 Niland juveniles (mean 126 mm TL) yielded 72 razorbacks one day after release, 12 one week later, and none two weeks after.
- Recapture rates were low; for example, 23 of 600 planted were caught (3.8%) on May 7, and 14 of 390 (3.6%) on June 17.

**Juvenile predation**
- Predation on juveniles smaller than 130 mm TL was much more apparent than on those over 190 mm TL. Largemouth bass were the primary consumer.
- Two largemouth bass examined on May 7 both contained razorbacks, averaging 151 mm TL.
- Juveniles were found in four of 23 largemouth bass (17%) examined at Goose Flats in the fall; one bass held two razorbacks.
- No razorbacks were found in predators sampled after the September 1987 Golf Course Pond stocking, possibly because the fish were larger.

**Golf Course Pond fish kill**
- On July 30, 1987, a fish kill occurred after a herbicide ("Roundup") was used during shoreline weed removal. 427 dead razorbacks were removed, and thousands survived in deep, vegetated areas.
- Larger fish appeared more susceptible based on length comparisons.

**Recommendations**
1. **Stock larger fish.** Juveniles over 190 mm TL appeared to escape detectable predation.
2. **Secure rearing areas.** Rear fewer larvae per pond to reduce crowding, control Lernaea, and consider isolated backwaters with blocking structures; remove predators with ichthyocide before stocking.
3. **Mark stocked fish.** Unmarked fish make it impossible to separate stocked from wild individuals.
4. **Extend monitoring.** One year is insufficient, since much of the lower river was unsampled and fish reach maturity in their second or third summer. Radio-telemetry is suggested for tracking movement and habitat.

**Limitations:** Predator sampling covered short periods in small areas, so stomach data may underestimate predation. Recovery efforts were limited to five minnow-trap sampling occasions during the 1986 post-stocking phase.
