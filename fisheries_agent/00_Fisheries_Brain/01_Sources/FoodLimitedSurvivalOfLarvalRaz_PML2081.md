# Food-Limited Survival of Larval Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Laboratory

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study tests whether food limitation contributes to recruitment failure in the [[Razorback Sucker]], a large endemic fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Adults in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada) reproduce each year, yet no recruitment to the adult population has been detected for about 30 years. Wild larvae disappear at roughly 10.6 mm total length (TL), with reduced yolk sacs and mostly empty stomachs, while lake [[Zooplankton]] densities are low and variable. Three experiments manipulated the absence, delay, and quantity of food for newly hatched larvae during the transition from yolk to external feeding. Larvae that were starved, fed late, or given insufficient food usually died between 20 and 30 days after hatching. The authors conclude that food-related mortality is a plausible contributor to year-class failure in Lake Mohave.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*, Abbott) is a large, endemic catostomid of the lower and upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its population is declining because of chronic year-class failure.
- **Theoretical framework:** Starvation of larvae has long been proposed as a cause of mortality that reduces recruitment (Hjort 1914, 1926; Cushing 1976; Leggett 1986). Larvae are most vulnerable during the [[Critical Period]] (May 1974), when they must switch from [[Yolk Absorption|endogenous yolk reserves]] to capturing sufficient food.
- **Observed decline:** Adults remain common in Lake Mohave and reproduce annually, but larvae do not survive to recruit. Wild larvae are typically captured at a mean of 10.6 mm TL, and the largest recorded larva was 12.2 mm TL.
- **Experimental broodstock and rearing:**
  - Females from Lake Mohave received three intramuscular injections of 220 IU chorionic gonadotropin per kg body weight at 24 h intervals, starting 11 February 1985.
  - Eggs were stripped about 24 h after the third injection and fertilized with milt from wild Lake Mohave males.
  - Eggs were incubated at 21 °C in Heath trays. Hatching began 18 February 1985, and larvae first swam actively on 23 February.
  - On 25 February, 10 larvae measuring 9.6 to 10.7 mm TL (mean 9.96 ± 0.04 SE mm, n = 50) were stocked into each jar. Studies lasted 50 days, until 16 April.
- **Feeding prey:** Live [[Artemia Nauplii]] (*Artemia salina*, [[Brine Shrimp]]) were hatched from commercial eggs and delivered three times daily at 8 h intervals. Densities were quantified with Sedgewick-Rafter counts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake conditions.** Zooplankton densities in areas of Lake Mohave where larvae are captured range from 1.5 to 26.3 organisms per litre (Marsh & Langhorst 1988; Paulson unpublished data). Only part of this biomass is likely available to larvae. Preferred larval prey are [[Cladocera]], which make up nearly 64% of total zooplankton density.

**Temperature.** Lake Mohave is cooler during egg incubation (9 to 15 °C) than the 21 °C used in the laboratory. Marsh (1985) found that razorback embryos hatched about two-thirds as quickly at 15 °C as at 20 °C (5.5 vs. 8.5 d), but TL at swim-up did not differ significantly (8.5 vs. 8.4 mm). After hatching, lake temperatures (15 to 17 °C) more closely match the 18 °C used in the experiments.

**Reproductive timing.** Lake Mohave razorbacks spawn from early January through April, with hatching from February to May (Langhorst & Marsh 1986).

**Experimental setup.** Experiments were run at Dexter National Fish Hatchery in glass jars (3.9 L) placed in indoor raceways. A constant 18 °C water bath and uniform lighting minimized position effects. Water flowed continuously at about 100 mL/min, and jars were screened to prevent loss of fish and food.

## Key Findings

**Experiment 1: Total food absence (six replicate jars)**
- Median time to 50% mortality of unfed larvae was 24 to 25 days post-hatch.
- Most larvae died between 20 and 30 days after hatching.
- Mean TL at death was 9.6 ± 0.2 mm (n = 28).

**Experiment 2: Delayed feeding (seven treatments, 3 jars each)**
- Food was withheld for 7, 11, 15, 19, 23, 27, or 31 days before brine shrimp were offered.
- Cumulative mortality by treatment:

| Food first offered (d post-hatch) | Mortality |
|---|---|
| 7 | 0.0% |
| 11 | 10.0% |
| 15 | 10.0% |
| 19 | 26.7% |
| 23 | 56.7% |
| 27 | 86.7% |
| 31 | 93.3% |

- No significant differences were found among feeding at 7, 11, 15, and 19 days.
- Starved larvae that survived until feeding began grew reasonably well compared with those fed from day 1.
- Mean TL at death in this experiment was 9.3 ± 0.1 mm (n = 35).

**Experiment 3: Ration size (six treatments, 3 jars each)**
- Food rations were 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, and 1000 nauplii per litre, delivered as nominal feeding rates.
- Mortality was highest and not significantly different at 5 nauplii/L (86.7 ± 3.3%) and 10 nauplii/L (73.3 ± 3.3%).
- Larvae fed 50 to 1000 nauplii/L had 20% or lower mortality, with no significant differences among those treatments.
- The threshold prey density beyond which mortality did not decrease significantly was 50 nauplii/L. The minimum density yielding less than 50% mortality was between 10 and 50 nauplii/L.
- Most deaths occurred between 20 and 30 days, at a mean TL of 10.1 mm (n = 25).
- Survivors at 50 days grew nearly as large at 5 to 10 nauplii/L as at about 50 nauplii/L. The authors estimate that 30 to 60 nauplii per fish per day is near the minimum needed to survive the critical period.

**Critical period timing**
- Yolk absorption was estimated at about 8 days post-hatch, based on the presence or absence of yolk in dead larvae and on Minckley & Gustafson (1982).
- Larvae lost fewer than 30% of individuals for 11 days after yolk absorption. Mortality rose above 50% after 15 days even when food was provided.
- The critical period therefore lies between about 8 and 19 days post-hatch, and the point of irreversible starvation lies between 19 and 23 days.

**Methodological notes**
- Mortality estimates were corrected for unrecovered dead larvae using the O'Connell & Raymond (1970) distribution formula.
- Kruskal-Wallis tests (p < 0.05) were used for comparisons between treatments, and means are reported ± SE.

**Conclusions**
- Starvation, late feeding, and insufficient food each lead to larval mortality, mainly at 20 to 30 days post-hatch.
- Larvae that are starved but then fed can still grow reasonably well, though very small or light larvae may be less reliable indicators of age when food is limiting.
- Lake zooplankton densities, especially at the lower end of the observed range, may cause more than 50% of larvae to die from starvation. The authors argue that this food-related mortality may contribute to the consistent absence of recruitment in Lake Mohave.
- The authors note knowledge gaps: hatching sizes in Lake Mohave, yolk utilization efficiency, and growth rates at ambient temperatures are unknown.
