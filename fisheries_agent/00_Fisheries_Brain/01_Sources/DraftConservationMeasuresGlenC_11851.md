# Draft Conservation Measures for the Humpback Chub, Glen Canyon Dam Biological Opinion (1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is a June 20, 1989 transmittal letter from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Phoenix Ecological Services) with an attached June 1, 1989 draft of seven conservation measures. The measures are actions that the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] would commit to in the biological opinion on operation of [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Their purpose is to eliminate a jeopardy finding for endangered or threatened species, chiefly the [[Humpback Chub]]. The draft consolidates comments from the Glen Canyon Dam Consultation Team: [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], Reclamation, the [[National Park Service]] and the Service. The measures cover:

- habitat protection in the Little Colorado River
- research on dam impacts
- long-term monitoring
- a second self-sustaining population
- sediment conservation
- Gila taxonomy
- a hatchery refugium

## Background & Description

- The letter, signed by Field Supervisor Sam F. Spiller, says the Consultation Team met on June 1, 1989 to consider written comments from AGFD, NPS and the Service.
- The draft was shared with [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) team members at a June 5 meeting with Dr. Duncan Patten (Senior Scientist).
- Reclamation had recently supplied draft study plans for the measures, which the Consultation Team will consider.
- The document says the humpback chub has been known to science for only about 40 years, so life-history information is limited. It also describes the species as long-lived (40+ years).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The Little Colorado River's perennial lower reach holds the only known spawning habitat of the humpback chub in the project area. The cold releases from Glen Canyon Dam appear to have restricted spawning and rearing of early life stages to this reach.
- [[Backwater Habitat]] in the mainstream is influenced by dam operations. It depends on sediment and is also important to other native fish and to riparian habitat.
- Beaches form the substrate for most riparian habitat in the project area. They also play a role in the food chain of the [[Peregrine Falcon]] that is not yet defined.
- Possible threats and stressors named include contaminant spills (e.g., from mines), environmental contaminants, recreational use, competition, parasitism and predation, and exotic fish competitors.
- Flow regimes of interest come from the Commissioner of Reclamation's December 1982 memorandum. They include low flows of 1,000, 3,000, 4,000, 5,000 and 8,000 cfs, plus fluctuating flows.

## Key Findings

**I. Little Colorado River protection plan**
- Define land, water and fish and wildlife responsibilities among federal, state, tribal and other entities in the basin.
- Develop a cooperative interagency plan to protect the chub and its habitat, formalized through a Memorandum of Understanding or Cooperative Agreement.
- Develop a contingency plan for catastrophic events such as a spill.
- Timing: a draft plan 18 months after the final biological opinion, and a final plan within 6 months of the draft.
- Reclamation leads and funds the plan. Participants may include the [[Navajo Nation]], the [[Environmental Protection Agency]], the [[Bureau of Land Management]], and Arizona agencies.

**II. Research on dam operations**
- Determine habitat use for all life stages (egg, larvae, juvenile, adult), movements and degree of isolation.
- Determine habitat availability and selection under the range of operating flows.
- Determine food resources, biological interactions, age/growth and age/fecundity relationships, and develop a population model for reproductive success, recruitment and mortality.
- A literature review will precede field work. Controlled-flow experiments may be needed. Studies should complement GCES, with final approval resting with the Service.

**III. Long-term monitoring**
- Define monitoring criteria and evaluate results at least every five years, coordinated with the Colorado River Storage Project review.
- Reinitiate formal [[Section 7 Consultation]] if monitoring shows likely jeopardy.
- Establish quantitative recovery goals for the Grand Canyon population.
- Monitoring is to be long-term, possibly for the life of the project, because of the species' longevity and variable releases.

**IV. Second self-sustaining aggregation**
- Determine spawning and rearing requirements, rank suitable sites, and, if appropriate, develop a reintroduction program (fish size, stocking rate, timing, success criteria).
- Stocking and monitoring would run for five years, after which the Consultation Team recommends whether to continue.
- Work is planned for Phase 2 of GCES, funded by Reclamation.

**V. Sediment longevity**
- Reclamation is to develop operating criteria that reduce spilling to less than once in twenty years and cap powerplant releases at 31,500 cfs.
- Exceptions would cover power emergencies and unforecasted spilling.
- High-flow strategies might benefit the chub if exotic fish competitors are disadvantaged.
- Reclamation funds this measure.

**VI. Gila taxonomy**
- Include project-area humpback chub, [[Bonytail Chub]] from [[Lake Mohave]], and other Lower Basin Gila in the Service's taxonomic study under the [[Upper Basin Recovery and Implementation Program]].
- Methods: electrophoresis, mitochondrial DNA and principal components analysis, plus analysis of gene-environment interactions related to damming.
- The study has already begun and is funded by the Service. Hybridization in other populations could raise the importance of the Grand Canyon population.

**VII. Refugium**
- Maintain approximately 50 to 100 project-area humpback chubs at a suitable lower-basin hatchery as insurance against a catastrophic or chronic event.
- Protocols will draw on the Service's fish culturists in Denver and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- Fish are to be procured within the first year, and Reclamation funds procurement. Holding continues until the Service, advised by the [[Colorado Fishes Recovery Team]], decides it is no longer needed.
