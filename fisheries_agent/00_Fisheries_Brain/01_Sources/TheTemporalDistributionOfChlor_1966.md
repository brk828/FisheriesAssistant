## Executive Summary

This 1966 study by Matheson and Anderson reports three years (1963–1965) of chlorophyll data collected from municipal water intakes at [[Hamilton, Ontario]] and [[Burlington, Ontario]] on western [[Lake Ontario]]. The authors argue that water treatment plant intake sampling is an inexpensive, continuous, and scientifically valuable method for characterizing lake-wide biochemical processes, since intake water is already tested routinely for parameters like ammonia, pH, alkalinity, and hardness. Total chlorophyll (chlorophyll *a* + chlorophyll *b*) was used as an indicator of [[Phytoplankton]] biomass and, indirectly, lake productivity.

Average total chlorophyll concentrations in Lake Ontario raw water (8.6 µg/L overall) were found to be substantially higher than contemporaneous reports from [[Lake Superior]] and [[Lake Michigan]] (0.4–2.8 µg/L) and somewhat higher than the [[Bay of Quinte]] (1.8–2.1 µg/L), though comparable to 1962 values from [[Lake Erie]] (6–12 µg/L). The elevated and highly variable chlorophyll levels at the Hamilton/Burlington intakes were attributed largely to intermittent, discrete plumes of highly fertilized water discharged from [[Burlington Bay]] and, less frequently, from a eutrophic impoundment at the mouth of [[Stoney Creek]], rather than to local in-lake eutrophication. The study establishes chlorophyll monitoring at water treatment intakes as a practical tool for tracing water-mass movement, pollution transport, and seasonal productivity trends in the Great Lakes.

## Background & Description

Not applicable — the source does not describe organismal morphology (fish or otherwise). The paper's "characteristics" of interest are chemical/optical rather than anatomical:

- **Analyte:** Chlorophyll *a* (Ca) and chlorophyll *b* (Cb), summed as total chlorophyll (Ct), expressed as µg per litre of lake water.
- **Method:** Water samples filtered through 47-mm membrane filters (0.8 micron pore size) sufficient to retain nearly all chlorophyll-bearing organisms (i.e., [[Phytoplankton]] cells, diatoms, and blue-green algae). Filtered material was macerated in 80% acetone, extracted 1 hr in darkness, and absorbance read spectrophotometrically at 665 and 649 nm, following [[Vernon 1960]] methodology.
- **Sample volumes:** Up to 2–3 litres for plankton-poor water, adjusted based on filterability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although no fish are discussed, the study characterizes the **primary producer compartment** of the western Lake Ontario ecosystem and the physical/chemical processes structuring it:

- **Basic biological populations** identified as most fundamental to lake trophic dynamics: [[Phytoplankton]] and bacteria, used as bio-indicators of the lake's biochemical state and nutrient supply.
- **Habitat zones sampled:**
  - Lake Ontario nearshore intake water (Hamilton: 2100–3000 ft offshore, ~35 ft depth; Burlington: 2500 ft offshore, ~20 ft depth).
  - [[Burlington Bay]] — a highly fertilized, semi-enclosed embayment receiving sanitary and industrial effluent, with theoretical detention time of 1.5 years.
  - [[Stoney Creek]] mouth pool — an intermittently sand-bar-sealed, highly eutrophic creek-mouth impoundment fed by secondary sewage effluent.
- **Thermal stratification dynamics:** In summer, warmer bay water enters and remains in the lake's epilimnion, sometimes forming a distinct warm surface layer; in unstratified conditions, bay water mixes through the full water column. The [[Thermal Bar]] phenomenon (Rodgers 1965) is cited as a mechanism producing abrupt spatial discontinuities in planktonic mass between coastal and deep waters.
- **Circulation:** No persistent circulation pattern in the western basin; a clockwise gyre is most common but easily disrupted by wind, producing intermittent counter-clockwise flow and irregular transport of bay-water plumes.
- **Nutrient dynamics:** Burlington Bay reduces nitrogen input to 26% and phosphorus input to 3% of loading before discharge to the lake, attributed partly to chemical phosphate precipitation by iron from steel-pickling liquor waste — despite this, the bay remains a significant source of episodic high-chlorophyll water exported to the lake.
- **Tracer relationships:** Bay-origin water masses are identifiable by co-occurring high ammonia-nitrogen (3–5 mg/L) and high chlorophyll; ammonia depletes faster than chlorophyll upon dilution/aging, allowing water-mass "age" since bay discharge to be inferred.

## Key Findings

- **Overall average total chlorophyll** across Hamilton and Burlington intakes (no significant difference between sites): **8.6 µg/L**, markedly higher than [[Lake Superior]]/[[Lake Michigan]] values (0.4–2.8 µg/L; Saunders 1964) and Bay of Quinte values (1.8–2.1 µg/L; Tucker 1949), but comparable to 1962 [[Lake Erie]] values (6–12 µg/L; Verduin 1962).
- **Annual Hamilton intake averages:** 10.4 µg/L (1963), 7.2 µg/L (1964), 9.0 µg/L (1965).
- **Annual Burlington intake averages:** 5.5 µg/L (1963), 9.6 µg/L (1964), 10.4 µg/L (1965).
- **Seasonal pattern:** Modest seasonal variation in monthly averages — summer ~10 µg/L, winter ~6 µg/L; when only monthly *minima* are considered (interpreted as "background" uncontaminated lake water), seasonal amplitude is greater (~6 µg/L summer to ~2 µg/L winter), still elevated relative to other Great Lakes reports.
- **Individual sample variability:** Fluctuations within months ranged from ~30% to 300% of the monthly average; annual peak values at Hamilton intakes were 33.3 µg/L (1963), 17.1 µg/L (1964), and 35.3 µg/L (1965); Burlington intake peak reached 37.8 µg/L (1965).
- **Burlington Bay chlorophyll levels** were far higher than the lake itself: Station 5 (near the canal, representing water likely to be exported to the lake) showed monthly averages of 20–40 µg/L and monthly maxima up to and exceeding 60 µg/L (Table 3), explaining the anomalously high intake spikes via limited-dilution transport of discrete bay-water plumes into the lake.
- **Stoney Creek pool** was extremely eutrophic: June–October 1963 average total chlorophyll ~180 µg/L, with peaks near 400 µg/L; sudden pool discharges (triggered by sandbar breach) produced identifiable localized chlorophyll spikes at the nearby Hamilton intakes.
- **Attribution of high lake chlorophyll:** The unusually high average lake chlorophyll (relative to other Great Lakes) is attributed primarily to intermittent inputs of high-chlorophyll water from [[Burlington Bay]] and [[Stoney Creek]], not to local in-lake eutrophication — though Lake Ontario's position as the most downstream lake in the Great Lakes chain confers natural nutrient enrichment (chloride ion increased from 10.4 to 23.5 ppm over 40 years per Schenk and Thompson 1965, implying a parallel rise in phytoplanktonic mass over time).
- **Methodological/monitoring recommendation:** Because chlorophyll measurement is inexpensive and effectively integrates photosynthetic biomass signals, the authors recommend incorporating routine chlorophyll sampling at 3–4 waterworks intakes per Great Lake to support lakewide productivity and water-mass tracing assessments.
- **No conclusions** could yet be drawn regarding a relationship between chlorophyll concentration and filter-clogging algae or taste/odor events, as no severe operational problems had occurred during the study period.
