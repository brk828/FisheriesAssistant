# Status of the Virgin Spinedace in the Virgin River Drainage, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This BIO/WEST report (PR-197-1, January 1991), prepared with the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] (UDWR), assesses the distribution, abundance and threats facing the [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*), a fish endemic to the [[Virgin River Drainage]]. Field work by UDWR and partners ran through six trips from September 1986 to January 1990.

The species has declined sharply. Its occupied range in Utah has fallen from an estimated 143.9 miles of stream to 87.0 miles, a 40% loss. None of the 13 populations is considered secure. Three are in good condition with existing threats, six show evidence of decline with persistent threats, and the [[Santa Clara River]] population is in rapid decline and in danger of extirpation. Three populations ([[Magotsu Creek]], [[Quail Creek]] and [[Leeds Creek]]) are extirpated. The main threats are water diversion, degraded water quality and non-native species. The authors recommend a monitoring program and consideration of federal threatened listing.

## Background & Description

**Morphology.** The fish has a silvery body with a brassy sheen and light sooty side blotches. The paired-fin bases are reddish-orange. The dorsal fin has two anterior hard spiny rays, eight dorsal rays and usually nine anal rays (range eight to ten). There are 77 to 91 lateral-line scales, and pharyngeal teeth are in two rows, typically 2,5-4,2.

**Taxonomy.** The species belongs to the endemic western cyprinid tribe Plagopterini, which includes [[Spikedace]], [[Woundfin]] and the genus *Lepidomeda*. Early workers first referred Virgin River *Lepidomeda* to *L. vittata*. [[Miller and Hubbs]] (1960) later described it as a new species with subspecific designations.

**Conservation status.** The species is a candidate (category 2) under the [[Endangered Species Act]], which gives it no federal protection. UDWR classifies it as threatened, and the American Fisheries Society lists it as threatened in Utah, Nevada and Arizona.

**Life history** (after [[Rinne 1971]]):
- Lifespan about three years; maturity after the first year.
- Maximum size about 125 mm standard length (SL), with a 128 mm SL maximum recorded by Rinne.
- Spawning April to June at mean daily water temperatures of 13 to 17°C (maximum 21°C), over gravel and sand at the downstream shallow end of pools in 15 to 30 cm of water.
- Mean relative fecundity: 459 eggs (age I), 788 (age II), 693 (age III).
- Sexual dimorphism is weak; females are plumper and the vent region is swollen during spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use.** Virgin spinedace prefer clear, cool, flowing streams with pools, runs and riffles. Pools with cover (undercut banks, boulders, lodged debris) are used most. They mostly occupy depths of 0.9 to 91 cm and velocities of 0.3 to 3.0 ft/s, often near shear zones between fast and slow water. In [[Beaver Dam Wash]] they avoided deep beaver ponds, which have silt substrate, little cover and low dissolved oxygen.

**Diet.** Fish feed at the surface on drifting material, mainly Diptera and Coleoptera (larval forms dominant), plant material and organic debris. Fish were in poorest condition when plant material dominated the diet, so insect availability likely regulates spawning and growth.

**Thermal niche.** The upper thermal preferendum is 23.1°C, matching greatest abundance in lower tributaries and the upper mainstem. Downstream occurrences depend mainly on cool inflows such as tributaries and springs. Among the six native Virgin River fishes, speckled dace have the coolest preferendum, Virgin spinedace are intermediate, and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] the warmest.

**Community interactions.** Competition from the [[Redside Shiner]] was reported by Rinne (1971) to reduce fecundity through food shortage, but shiners have not been recorded recently and their decline is unexplained. Predation by [[Brown Trout]] may occur in the upper reaches and by [[Largemouth Bass]] in the lower reaches. The [[Red Shiner]] invaded the lower Virgin River in 1986, contributing to woundfin decline and posing a threat to spinedace.

**Sampling methods.** Crews used backpack electrofishing ([[Electrofishing]]), 10 and 15-foot seines with 1/4-inch mesh, and bank electrofishing on the main Virgin River. Two-catch [[Population Estimate]]s were expanded to representative stream reaches. The 9-day January 8–16, 1990 cooperative trip with BIO/WEST covered 23 sites, mainly in Beaver Dam Wash, Moody Wash, Magotsu Creek and the upper Santa Clara River. Flows in January 1990 approximated minimum flows because of a dry winter.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and range loss**
- Historic distribution was probably common to abundant throughout the clear tributaries and upper mainstem of the Virgin River.
- The species now occupies portions of nine tributaries. Its Utah range fell from 143.9 to 87.0 miles (56.9 miles lost, 40%). Decline has occurred since the 1930s.
- Largest remaining areas (Table 5): [[Virgin River]] mainstem (14.5 miles occupied, 3.0 lost), [[East Fork Virgin River]] (9.2 miles, no loss), [[North Fork Virgin River]] (12.5 miles, no loss), and [[North Creek]] (8.1 miles, no loss).

**Threats**
- Dewatering by irrigation and mining diversions (Motoqua, Lytle Ranch, Iverson Ranch, Gunlock and Baker reservoirs, Quail Creek Diversion).
- Non-native species: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Bullfrog]]s, [[Crayfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], Brown Trout and Largemouth Bass, often from streamside ponds.
- Degraded water quality and siltation from cattle grazing, livestock yards, organic pollution and urban runoff.
- Inundation by reservoirs. Quail Creek Dam (1985) extirpated its population, and North Creek Reservoir is a planned threat.
- Beaver pond expansion converting flowing stream to impoundment.

**Management recommendations**
- Secure the Lytle Ranch population by preventing further diversions within the Lytle Ranch Preserve and by controlling beavers. Protect it from bass invasion from Nordin Ranch.
- If bass are removed from the two ponds and the stream, the East Fork of Beaver Dam Wash could be a suitable introduction site.
- Augment La Verkin Creek by introducing fish above the chute falls, after assessing habitat and non-native species.
- Develop a monitoring program to estimate population size and trends regularly.
- Consider proposing the species as threatened under the Endangered Species Act, given the 40% range reduction and continued decline.
- Address data gaps: upstream limits in The Narrows, North Fork upstream distribution (possibly to Orderville Canyon), South Creek and Cottonwood Creek status, and the effects of Baker Dam releases and Iverson Ranch diversion.
