# Radioecological Study of the Biota of Doe Run, Meade County, Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 final report from the University of Louisville, prepared under [[U.S. Atomic Energy Commission]] Contract No. AT-(40-1)-2595, documents the accumulation of [[Gross Beta Radioactivity]] from [[Fallout]] by the biota of [[Doe Run]], a spring-fed limestone stream in [[Meade County]], Kentucky. Data were gathered from May 1959 to October 1964. The study was unusual because pre-fallout baseline data existed for an entire stream system, so the effect of the Soviet tests beginning in September 1961 could be measured directly.

Radioactivity in all biota rose sharply after September 1961. Most organisms at least doubled their gross beta activity within two years, and many more than tripled. The banded sculpin, [[Banded Sculpin]] (*Cottus carolinae*), was the top carnivore of the stream, and fish generally accumulated less than their invertebrate prey. Seasonal differences could not be assessed with confidence.

## Background & Description

The study began as a preliminary survey of flora and fauna in early 1959. Because the stream lay far from any known radioactive or industrial source, the Atomic Energy Commission was asked to support a detailed study of the biota, the physical and chemical characteristics of the stream, and the accumulation of [[Strontium 90]] and [[Cesium 137]] from fallout. The work produced eight theses and dissertations, several journal papers, and a complete survey of the stream community.

Doe Run rises as a torrent spring near Ekron, Kentucky, and flows through [[Mississippian Limestone]] karst for 9.7 miles to the [[Ohio River]] at Ohio River Mile 642.2, about 36 miles below Louisville. Key features:

- **Elevation:** 575 ft at the source to 374 ft at the mouth (average gradient 20.7 ft/mile).
- **Discharge:** less than 5 to more than 600 cubic feet per second (cfs), governed mainly by rainfall. Discharge near Mile 3.1 probably exceeded 2000 cfs in March 1961.
- **Dams:** three old mill dams (Mile 1.65, 2.36, 2.82), silted to a fraction of their original depth. [[Doe Valley Lake]], an earthen-fill impoundment at Mile 7.6, closed on 9 July 1961 and reached about full pool by the following spring, backing water to about Mile 3.5.
- **Water quality:** source water at 13–14 °C; dissolved oxygen 75–80% of saturation at the source and at least 90% (often over 100%) downstream; pH circumneutral at the source and just over 8.3 downstream; methyl purple alkalinity 150–250 mg/L.

Five permanent stations (I–V) were established to represent the main community types. Station I was at the spring source, Station II in swift riffles between Miles 1.8 and 2.1, Station III near a highway culvert at Mile 3.1 (frequently disturbed by visitors), Station IV at Mile 4.7–4.9 (flooded in 1961), and Station V at Mile 7.7 in the Ohio River backwater.

Sampling yielded 5,996 gross beta assays: 5,456 from Doe Run (Stations I: 1,214; II: 1,882; III: 1,527; IV: 458; V: 375) and 540 from [[Sinkhole Ponds]] near the stream. Biota samples comprised 31 plant kinds (1,275 samples), 34 invertebrate kinds or groups (2,666 samples), and 22 vertebrate kinds (1,170 samples). Samples were digested in nitric acid, dried to planchets, and counted for 12.8 minutes on a proportional gas flow counter; results were reported in picocuries per gram (pCi/g) or per liter (pCi/L). Radiochemical analysis for Sr-90 and Cs-137 was performed on 184 composite samples (59 from Station I, 78 from Station III, 47 from Station V) by a commercial laboratory.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Doe Run supports a diverse community of roughly 120 plant kinds (about 50 algae, about 25 vascular plants, and 46 diatoms) and more than 120 invertebrate kinds. [[Minckley]] (1963) recorded 66 fish species, but only three were indigenous to the headwaters. Many of the fish were collected in Ohio River backwaters and were not regular inhabitants. The fish fauna belonged to 13 families, led by Cyprinidae (23 species) and Catostomidae (12 species).

Three fishes were resident year-round and formed the core of the fish community:

- [[Common Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*)
- [[Blacknose Dace]] (*Rhinichthys atratulus*)
- [[Banded Sculpin]] (*Cottus carolinae*), the most abundant fish throughout the stream and its top carnivore.

Stations I–III showed the richest sampling record. The sinkhole ponds differed markedly from the stream: they held more than 140 plankton kinds, were dominated by dinoflagellates such as *Peridinium cinctum* and *Ceratium hirundinella*, and supported far more rotifers and copepods than Doe Run. Any plankton found in the stream was presumed to have washed in from these ponds. The ponds also acted as concentration basins for radionuclides, with 73 of 81 post-1961 pond samples exceeding 5 pCi/L.

Organisms formed a food web with several levels:

- **Producers:** [[Vaucheria]] (alga), [[Fissidens Julianus]] (moss), and [[Nasturtium Officinale]] (watercress) and [[Myosotis Scorpioides]] (forget-me-not).
- **Primary consumers:** [[Goniobasis]] (snail), [[Asellus Bivittatus]] (isopod), amphipods (*Gammarus*), and crayfishes.
- **Secondary consumers and carnivores:** plecopteran larvae, [[Blacknose Dace]], and [[Banded Sculpin]].

Crayfish distribution was constrained by temperature: [[Cambarus Tenebrosus]] occupied the cooler upper reaches, while [[Orconectes Rusticus Rusticus]] was limited to the stream downstream of Dam 1.

## Key Findings

**Methodology and Scope**

1. The study is described as the first intensive radioecological survey of an entire stream system, with pre-fallout baseline data gathered from 1959 onward.
2. Seasonal differences in radionuclide accumulation could not be assessed with confidence because the activities were so low and the ranges so broad. No evidence of seasonal variation was found, although the highest plant concentrations occurred in spring and early summer.
3. Gross beta radioactivity was used as a general indicator of total contamination, but not of specific radionuclide doses. Sr-90 and Cs-137 were the only radionuclides sought in the radiochemical analyses.

**Response to Fallout**

4. Radioactivity in all components of the ecosystem increased shortly after the Soviet tests began in September 1961. Every increase was recognizable within three months of the start of testing.
5. By the end of the study (about two years later), all organisms at least doubled their activity from the July–August 1961 low, and the isopods, plecopterans, and all plants except watercress more than tripled.
6. Watercress showed no large increase and never exceeded 20 pCi/g. The authors could not explain this.
7. Pond water usually held more than twice the activity of stream water. Before September 1961, 27 of 115 Doe Run water samples exceeded 5 pCi/L, and the highest post-1961 stream value was about 69 pCi/L (late 1962 to early 1963). Sinkhole ponds exceeded 65 pCi/L in November and December 1961.

**Accumulation by Trophic Level**

8. The moss *Fissidens julianus* consistently accumulated more activity than any other plant. In five Station II samples it exceeded 100 pCi/g, and two exceeded 120 pCi/g. Only *Myriophyllum* and the red alga *Lemanea* also exceeded 100 pCi/g, and only in a few samples.
9. Isopods, amphipods, and plecopterans had the highest animal accumulation, but no sample exceeded 50 pCi/g, and amphipods and plecopterans never exceeded 30 pCi/g.
10. Accumulation declined up the trophic series. Fish accumulated less than their crayfish prey, which in turn accumulated less than the herbivores. The authors attributed this to fish diets based on animals that themselves accumulated little.
11. Snails accumulated little activity, mainly because the calcium-rich environment may prevent radiostrontium uptake. Most activity was in the soft tissues rather than the shell.
12. The authors proposed seven trophic "levels" in Doe Run, three of them at the producer level (algae, mosses, tracheophytes).

**Ecological Patterns**

13. Biological diversity peaked at Station II, about two miles below the source, and declined toward the mouth, where Ohio River organisms came to dominate.
14. Station I was the least diverse habitat (30 kinds sampled). Station II had 35 kinds not found at Station I.
15. Among the crustaceans, isopods, amphipods, and decapods were the dominant invertebrate groups.
16. [[Asellus Bivittatus]] was strongly tied to moss abundance and was sensitive to discharge. Its main diet was diatoms.
17. The banded sculpin spawned from late January through March, reached maturity in its second year, and rarely lived beyond four years. Its diet was mostly crustaceans, and its feeding time was early evening.
18. The northern hog sucker, *Hypentelium nigricans*, reached sexual maturity at 107–126 mm standard length. The oldest specimen was an eight-year-old male.
19. Doe Run's constant temperature and rapid reproduction allowed organisms to respond quickly to fallout, though the authors noted that the spring-fed environment was relatively stable.

**Unresolved Questions**

20. The authors called for further study of the amphipods and isopods, of the snail's role in the economy of the stream, and of the complete life histories of the common sucker and blacknose dace.
21. The mechanisms behind the high accumulation in the forget-me-not and the low accumulation in watercress remained unknown.
