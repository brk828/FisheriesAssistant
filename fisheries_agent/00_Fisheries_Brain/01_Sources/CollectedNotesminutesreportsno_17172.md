# Colorado River Endangered Fishes: Recovery Team Records, 1981–1988

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This compilation gathers recovery-planning records for the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]], plus the [[Razorback Sucker]]. It includes meeting minutes (March 1981, April 1987), a 1984 [[Sensitive Areas]] report, agency correspondence, a bonytail handling protocol, a [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] fish passage status report, a research proposal, and a 1987 field survey of the [[Virgin River]]. The records show the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] revising three recovery plans, reclassifying the bonytail as a species needing emergency action, and recommending that the razorback sucker be listed as endangered. They also document funding constraints, hatchery-based recovery, and concern over the decline of native fishes under water development and nonnative species.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team, led by [[Kent Miller]] in 1981 and [[Jim St. Amant]] in 1987, revised recovery plans with [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) oversight. The 1981 meeting (St. George, Utah) reviewed draft plans for the squawfish, humpback chub and bonytail. Part 3 (budget needs) was assigned to FWS, and the plans were due by June 1, 1981. The April 15–17, 1987 meeting in Las Vegas, Nevada, updated the plans to include new information gathered since 1984.

The four focal species are:
- **Colorado squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a large cyprinid of the upper basin that migrates long distances to spawn.
- **Humpback chub** (*Gila cypha*), a deep-canyon species whose taxonomy remained unresolved.
- **Bonytail chub** (*Gila elegans*), which had almost no reported wild recruitment by 1987.
- **Razorback sucker**, a catostomid with declining upper-basin adults and no recorded successful recruitment.

Other species named in the records include [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), along with roundtail chub, flannelmouth sucker and desert sucker.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use (1984 Sensitive Areas report).** The Biological Sub-Committee of the Upper Colorado River Basin Coordinating Committee (UCRBCC) defined "confirmed use" as documented, recurring activity and "suspected use" as activity not yet replicated. The report focused on the squawfish, with the bonytail excluded for lack of data. Sensitive-area criteria included:
- Adult squawfish electrofishing catch rate above 0.70 fish per hour (high concentration) or above 0.30 per hour (concentration).
- Young-of-year seine catch above 0.9 per 10 m² in nursery areas. (One instance in the source reads "0.09/10m²," likely an OCR error.)
- Spawning areas defined by deep pools interspersed with cobble/riffle habitat, ripe females, and radio-tagged adults.

**Lower Basin and reintroduction.** In 1987 the team identified priority lower-basin areas: the Salt River, Verde River and mainstem Colorado below Davis Dam. The Salt and Verde populations were designated experimental "nonessential." The team did not support that designation for the mainstem Colorado and judged the potential for recovering squawfish in the Lower Basin to be low, though it recommended a 10-year reintroduction effort.

**Virgin River (April 1987).** A survey by Burton and Coffeen at five sites in the Virgin River narrows (3 m seine, 1/8-inch mesh) found red shiners dominant at most sites. Woundfin were present at only two of the five sites (Samples 1 and 3, with 4 woundfin in Sample 3). The memo attributes the woundfin decline largely to red shiners, possibly enabled by changes in flow after [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] was built. It also reports Asian tapeworms in the reservoir and water-quality concerns (high gypsum). The memo recommends reinitiating consultation with Region 6 and removing red shiners by treating the river during low flow.

**Flow and nursery conditions.** Flows in the [[Yampa River]] were predicted at 50 percent of normal in 1981. An overwintering study on the [[Green River]] by Tyus (1987) found direct winter mortality of young squawfish was not a major survivorship factor, and high mobility allowed fish to find favorable temperatures. He proposed field studies over lab tank trials.

## Key Findings

**Recovery plan revisions**
- The 1981 team reviewed three draft plans and was directed to limit data in the Introduction of the squawfish plan to general references to the ongoing [[Colorado River Fishery Project]].
- The 1987 team changed humpback chub objectives to protect two lower-basin populations ([[Little Colorado River]] and [[Havasu Creek]]) and recognized [[Cataract Canyon]] as a potential upper-basin recovery site.
- The team stressed prevention of humpback declines in Gray Canyon and Dinosaur National Monument, and clarification of taxonomy (Gila cypha vs. other Gila).

**Humpback chub criteria (1987 draft)**
- Downlisting when five self-sustaining populations are established and two refugia of about 50–100 fish each (OCR reads "5U to 100") are established, one for Upper Basin and one for Lower Basin stock.
- Delisting when six self-sustaining populations (two in the Lower Basin) and two refugia are maintained and their habitats are legally protected.
- Known populations: [[Black Rocks]]/[[Westwater Canyon]] and the Little Colorado River, with the largest known adult aggregations.

**Bonytail emergency actions (1987)**
- Collect all wild bonytails encountered and place them in refugia for captive propagation.
- Release about 100 F1 adults from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] into Dinosaur National Monument, with telemetry on some fish.
- Use Lake Mohave as a rearing site for young from the roughly 1,400 F1 bonytails at Dexter, which had spawned in holding ponds.
- Field identification criteria: total length at least 300 mm; dorsal and anal fin rays 10/10 or more; caudal peduncle length/head length above 1.0; head length/caudal peduncle depth above 5.0.
- Holding and transport guidance: keep the fish cool and aerated, avoid unnecessary handling, and move it to a temporary facility within two weeks of capture. Temporary facilities were [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]]; permanent facilities were Dexter and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].

**Razorback sucker**
- The 1987 team estimated that perhaps about 1,000 adults remain in the Upper Basin, with no evidence of recruitment.
- The team recommended endangered (not threatened) status basin-wide and offered to prepare a recovery plan after listing.

**Redlands fish passage (status report, January 1987)**
- Nine ladder designs were evaluated by the Corps of Engineers. The preferred design was estimated at $783,000 for design and construction, plus about $15,000 per year to operate and maintain, and $70,000 per year for three years of evaluation.
- The total cost estimate of about $1,000,000 was six times the 1984 estimate. Biologists judged the Gunnison River to be marginal habitat with limited recovery value.
- The status report recommended suspending the project. The 1987 team agreed and recommended retaining funds for passage research.

**Taylor Draw Dam and Kenney Reservoir (1987)**
- The team deferred stocking squawfish for a sport fishery in [[Kenney Reservoir]] and recommended additional research on upper White River contributions to squawfish recovery.
- If stocking occurs, the team recommended marking all released fish and monitoring any movement into the Green River.

**Funding and planning**
- In 1981, a meeting cost about $1,500–$1,800, and most of an $8,000 budget for the three plans had been spent by the time of the meeting.
- A proposed study on morphological abnormalities in about 600 hatchery-reared juvenile squawfish (Modde and Tyus, 1987–1988) had a listed budget of $7,000. The line items (2,250 + 1,350 + 600 + 400 + 2,700) sum to $7,300, not $7,000.

**Scientific reviews**
- A 1987 review of a Lake Mohave razorback manuscript (Bozek et al.) recommended a major rewrite. The reviewer found only 3 recaptures (under 0.7 percent of marked fish), which cast doubt on the population estimate, and recommended reporting total catch and catch-effort in place of the mark-recapture estimate.

**Coordination and policy**
- FWS would prepare budget sections (Part 3) for all three plans, a change the team welcomed.
- The team recommended expanding the Sensitive Areas concept to the Lower Basin and noted that a separate [[Desert Fish Recovery Team]] had been formed for lower-basin species.
- The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program (1987) requested written comments on dam operations and fish impacts by November 4, 1987.
