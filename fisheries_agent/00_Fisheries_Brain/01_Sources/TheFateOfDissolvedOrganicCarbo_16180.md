## Executive Summary

This study by Lock and Hynes (1976) investigates the fate of "dissolved" organic carbon (DOC) derived from autumn-shed [[Sugar Maple]] (*Acer saccharum*) leaf leachate in a temperate hard-water stream system, the [[Speed River]] (Ontario, Canada). Using freeze-dried leaf leachate added to circulating water systems ("miniature streams") with and without natural stream sediment cores, the authors demonstrate that removal of DOC from the water column is overwhelmingly a **sediment-associated process** rather than a water-column process. While leachate remained largely stable in stream water alone (only 10-20% precipitation over 4 days), DOC over natural stream sediment cores dropped to just **15% of initial levels within 1 day**, and to ~2% by day 4. Using a Tyndalization sterilization technique, the authors estimate that **at least 40%, and possibly up to 100%,** of this sediment-based removal is a biotic (microbial) process. Notably, uptake rates did not differ significantly between July and October sampling, nor after experimentally "loading" sediments with repeated leachate additions — suggesting a stable, responsive standing microbial community rather than a leaf-fall-stimulated one. This work is foundational for understanding [[Detrital Food Chains]] and the [[Organic Matter Budget|carbon budget]] of temperate headwater streams.

## Background & Description

*Not applicable — this study concerns dissolved organic carbon (DOC) chemistry and microbial/sediment processes rather than the morphology of an organism. Key structural/methodological features described include:*

- **Leachate preparation**: Freeze-dried water-soluble extract from air-dried, milled (1-mm mesh) sugar maple leaf powder, leached in distilled water (15 g powder : 20 L water), freeze-concentrated and filtered (0.45 µm), then freeze-dried to a soluble powder.
- **Sediment cores**: Collected via a custom piston corer from the [[Speed River]] streambed; top 2 cm of undisturbed gravel/sediment retained in modified glass "bowl" apparatus with a central column and rotating paddle (simulating flow at ~12 cm/s, 20 rpm).
- **Particle size cutoff**: "Dissolved" operationally defined as passing a 0.45-µm filter (may include colloids, not only true solutes).
- **DOC measurement**: Beckman TOC analyzer, samples acidified to remove inorganic carbon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System**: Temperate hard-water lotic (stream) ecosystem — the [[Speed River]], Ontario — characterized by high background DOC (15-25 mg/liter) relative to distilled or well water controls.
- **Organic input pathway**: Autumn leaf fall is a major energy source in temperate streams; per [[Fisher and Likens 1972]], leaf litter comprised 22.7% of Bear Brook's annual energy budget. Leaching can remove up to 40% of leaf dry weight within hours of submersion (Kaushik and Hynes 1971; Cummins et al. 1972; Lush and Hynes 1973).
- **Microbial community/substrate interaction**: The stream sediment matrix — bacteria, fungi, extracellular material, and organic/inorganic particles coating stream surfaces (the "[[Organic Layer Around Stones]]" concept of Madsen 1972) — is identified as the principal site of DOC uptake, rather than suspended bacteria in the water column (contra Cummins et al. 1972; Wetzel and Manny 1972).
- **Trophic implications**: DOC incorporated into sediment microflora likely becomes available to stream grazers and detritivores ([[Benthic Invertebrates]]), forming a critical link in detrital food chains distinct from planktonic/water-column pathways.
- **Seasonal dynamics**: Rate of DOC uptake was **not significantly different** between July and October sediments, despite expectation of enhanced autumn microbial activity ("zymogenous microflora") from increased leaf input — suggesting existing standing microbial stocks rapidly adjust uptake rates to substrate availability rather than requiring population growth/stimulation.

## Key Findings

1. **Water column alone is a minor sink**: In natural stream water without sediment, DOC leachate levels dropped only to 80-95% of initial levels over 4 days — far less precipitation than reported by [[Lush and Hynes 1973]] in synthetic water (down to 50% by 2.5 days). Distilled water showed negligible change (~5% deviation); well water showed 20-25% reduction, attributed to low background organic matter (competitive/surface effects).

2. **Sediment is the dominant removal site**: Over natural sediment cores, DOC fell to **15% of initial level within 1 day** and to ~2% by day 4 — starkly contrasting with the ~90% still present in water alone after 1 day.

3. **Rate consistency across seasons**: Regression slopes of log DOC decline over 9 h were statistically indistinguishable between July (-0.083) and October (-0.066) cores (F = 3.05, df = 1,41, not significant).

4. **No significant stimulation from repeated leachate loading**: Cores subjected to four consecutive days of leachate "loading" showed only a slightly steeper uptake slope (-0.115) at final addition vs. initial (-0.083), not statistically significant (F = 2.0, df = 1,41).

5. **Biotic vs. abiotic partitioning**: Using Tyndalization (repeated heat-cooling sterilization cycles, chosen over autoclaving/formalin due to less DOC artifact release), DOC in sterilized-sediment experimental bowls only fell to ~80% of initial level (vs. near-total removal in live sediment) — indicating **at least 40%, and possibly up to 100%,** of sediment DOC removal is microbially mediated.

6. **Comparative uptake rates**: Calculated hourly uptake rates (0.118 g m⁻² h⁻¹ July; 0.112 g m⁻² h⁻¹ October) were in reasonable agreement with sucrose uptake rates in experimental stream channels reported by [[Wuhrmann 1974]] (0.017–0.060 g m⁻² h⁻¹), despite differing equilibrium assumptions.

7. **Supporting literature comparison**: Findings align with [[Paerl 1973, 1974]], who showed via ¹⁴C- and ³H-labeled substrates that <5% of DOC uptake by suspended lake detritus was direct adsorption, with the majority incorporated into microbial biomass — reinforcing a primarily biotic uptake mechanism.

8. **Ecological synthesis**: The results support the concept of an "[[Organic Layer Around Stones]]" (Madsen 1972) — a biofilm-like matrix on stream substrates — as the key interface for converting leaf-derived DOC into particulate/microbial biomass, which subsequently enters detrital food webs consumed by benthic grazers and detritivores.

9. **Caveat**: All experimental systems (this study, Cummins et al. 1972, Wetzel and Manny 1972, Lush and Hynes 1973) are artificial microcosms; findings require field verification. The study examined only one dissolved organic input (maple leachate); other potentially more reactive DOC sources likely also feed detrital food chains.
