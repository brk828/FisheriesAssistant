# Introduction to Power Plant Effects on Fish and Shellfish Behavior

#ichthyology #marine-biology #power-plants

## Executive Summary

This chapter, the introduction to a state-of-the-art review by [[Charles H. Hocutt]] (Academic Press, 1980, *Power Plants: Effects on Fish and Shellfish Behavior*), frames why the behavior of aquatic organisms near power plants matters. Fish and shellfish behavior toward stimuli created or altered by plant operation is presumed to influence susceptibility to [[Impingement]] and [[Entrainment]]. The chapter outlines the relevant U.S. legal framework ([[Pl 92-500]], Sections 316a and 316b), documents the heavy water demands of the electric industry, and forecasts national energy and generating-capacity trends through the mid-1990s.

## Background & Description

**Objective.** The book aims to provide a well-documented review of how power plant design and operation affect fish and shellfish behavior. Behavior is singled out because it mediates exposure to intakes; the authors note that fish behavior has been studied in far greater detail than shellfish behavior, so the book is organized accordingly.

**Organization.** Chapters II–VII cover general fish behavior by stimulus type. Chapters VIII–XII review important shellfish by phyletic grouping. The literature cut-off was June 1978, with later publications included when brought to the authors' attention.

**Regulatory context.**
- Most biological data on power plant effects were collected after the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (1969) and the [[Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments]] (PL 92-500, 1972).
- **Section 316a** allows variances from thermal effluent limits. Applicants may demonstrate (1) absence of prior appreciable harm, (2) protection of representative important species, or (3) provision of biological or engineering data, including preference and avoidance studies of temperature and biocides.
- **Section 316b** requires intake structures to reflect the best available technology for minimizing environmental impact, addressing impingement and entrainment. Attraction to intakes, orientation to currents, and intake velocities are cited as key behavioral influences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Cooling water demand.** The electric industry uses roughly 70–80% of all water required by industry for cooling. Table 1 (EPA 1976, 1967 data) lists steam-electric powerplants at 151,400 × 10⁶ m³/yr from 1,000 establishments (average 151.40 × 10⁶ m³/yr), far above petroleum refining (4,655), primary metals (13,739), chemical plants (13,361), and other industries.

**Cooling system type.** About 60% of 769 fossil-fuel facilities and 74% of 39 nuclear plants used once-through cooling in 1973 (Schubel and Marcy 1978). Nuclear facilities need roughly 50% more cooling water than fossil stations without closed-cycle cooling, because of lower steam pressures and less efficient operation.

**Energy and generation trends.**
- Total U.S. energy consumption rose 70%, from 44.5 to 75.8 quads, between 1960 and 1977.
- Oil-fired generation tripled in absolute terms, from 104,276 × 10⁶ kWh (1968) to 357,889 × 10⁶ kWh (1977); nuclear generation rose from 12,528 to 250,883 × 10⁶ kWh.
- Coal remained the top producer (985,450 × 10⁶ kWh in 1977), with a resurgence expected after the 1973 oil embargo and recent legislation.
- Installed capacity grew from over 263 GW (1970) to 512 GW (1977), against a 1977 peak summer demand of 388 GW.
- Table 2 (1960–1974) shows regional fuel mixes; nationally, coal fell from 53.5% to 44.5% of generation while nuclear rose to 16.1%.
- Table 3 (NERC 1978, 1977–1987 plans) projects a national mix of coal 39.2%, oil 42.7%, gas 25.5%, and nuclear 18.3% of expansion capacity, and notes that many planned nuclear units were postponed or cancelled because of cost, construction time, waste disposal policy, and safety concerns after the [[Three Mile Island Incident]].

## Key Findings

- **Behavior is central to impact.** Fish and shellfish behavior toward plant-generated stimuli is presumed to drive impingement and entrainment susceptibility, which is the rationale for the book.
- **Cooling water dominates industrial intake.** Steam-electric plants account for the largest share of industrial cooling water, and most plants used once-through systems in 1973.
- **Nuclear plants are more water-intensive.** They require about 50% more cooling water than comparable fossil plants without closed-cycle systems.
- **Energy demand and generation are growing.** U.S. consumption, capacity, and oil- and nuclear-fired generation all rose sharply between 1960 and 1977.
- **Policy favors coal and nuclear.** Federal and NERC forecasts emphasize coal and nuclear replacing oil and gas, although many nuclear projects were later postponed or cancelled.
- **Regulatory pathways shape research.** Sections 316a and 316b make behavioral data (preference, avoidance, intake attraction) directly relevant to permitting.
- **Scope and limits.** The review covers literature to June 1978; the authors acknowledge some documents may have been overlooked, and fish behavior is far better documented than shellfish behavior.
