# Mound Springs: South Australian Conservation Initiatives

#ichthyology #aquatic-ecology #hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by C.R. Harris (South Australian Department of Environment and Land Management, *Rangeland Journal* 14(2), 1992) reviews the [[Mound Springs]] of inland Australia, the natural outlets of the [[Great Artesian Basin]]. It assesses a decade of conservation work since [[Harris (1981)]], which had concluded on a pessimistic note. Key achievements include the acquisition of [[Dalhousie Springs]] and [[Finniss Springs]] for the national parks system, the fencing of ten high-priority springs, and substantial new biological research. The paper identifies persistent problems: remoteness, high costs, aquifer draw-down, visitor pressure, and limited monitoring. It concludes that long-term funding and cooperation are essential for the springs' survival.

## Background & Description

**Setting and hydrology.** The [[Great Artesian Basin]] covers 1.76 million km². Its springs occur mainly along the basin margins, with others as inliers along fault lines. Most of the South Australian springs are small, with flows ranging from seepages to a maximum of 166 L/s at the main [[Dalhousie Springs]] spring. The estimated total flow from all Australian mound springs is 1500 L/s, of which 62% (926 L/s) comes from South Australian springs. South Australia probably has more than 600 springs. Water is high in dissolved solids, from below 2000 mg/L at Dalhousie to around 8000 mg/L or more south-west of [[Lake Eyre]]. Precipitated carbonates and wind-blown sediment built the characteristic cones that give the springs their name.

**Human history.** The springs have been permanent water sources in a desert for at least 5000 years of Aboriginal occupation, and most are rich in archaeological and mythological material. Since European settlement they were important for exploration, pastoral stations, the [[Overland Telegraph]] and the old [[Ghan Railway]] from Marree to Oodnadatta, whose alignment was largely dictated by the springs.

**Biological character.** The springs are specialised aquatic habitats that behave like islands, with high levels of endemism and relict species. The endemic fauna is dominated by hydrobiid snails and crustaceans with limited dispersal ability. Mobile taxa (birds, mammals, reptiles, most insects) are widely distributed across the region. Fish are the exception: three of the eight native species present are endemic to Dalhousie, namely the [[Dalhousie Catfish]], [[Dalhousie Hardy Head]] and [[Dalhousie Goby]]. Their status as evolved-in-isolation or relict populations is unresolved (Glover 1989).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flora.** The spring-specific flora is small, about a dozen aquatic and semi-aquatic species, including endemics and relicts. [[Eleocharis Carsonii]] appears to be of tropical origin. [[Gahnia Trifida]] and [[Baumea Juncea]] are widely disjunct from their temperate main populations. Species diversity was related to the number of springs in a complex rather than the area of any one spring, which has important implications for conservation design. Spring surrounds support more common plants not dependent on the water.

**Invertebrates.** Hydrobiid snails show adaptive radiation with habitat partitioning and size displacement, typical of island faunas. Ten new hydrobiid species in two endemic genera, *Fonscochlea* and *Trochidrobia*, were described from springs between Marree and Oodnadatta (Ponder et al. 1989). Other endemics include the phreatoicid isopod [[Phreatomerus Latipes]], the ostracod [[Ngarawa Dirga]], and the phreatic amphipod [[Phreatochiltonia Anophthalma]], which may be a relict of a wetter central Australia. A macrostomid flatworm was recorded for the first time in Australia.

**Palaeobiology.** Peat deposits show pollen mostly from the current wetland flora. A Pleistocene conglomerate at Dalhousie holds fossil cones probably of *Allocasuarina*, a genus no longer at the springs. Fossil hydrobiids at Hermit Hill suggest early, rapid speciation. [[Diprotodon]] remains were found at Herrgott and Welcome Springs near Marree.

**Threats.**
- *Aquifer draw-down* is the most important threat. It can cause flow cessation and extinction of entire aquatic populations. Artesian bore extraction since the late 1870s lowered the potentiometric surface below ground in many areas. Around 15 ML/d is currently pumped from the existing [[Olympic Dam]] bore field, about half the amount estimated for full production.
- *Grazing and trampling* cause physical damage, organic pollution, algal growth and reduced oxygen. Removing cattle at Hermit Hill, however, allowed a large increase in *Phragmites australis* that displaced *E. carsonii*.
- *Visitor pressure* at Dalhousie and Finniss Springs brings trampling, compaction, pollution from washing and bathing.
- *Feral animals and breaches*: in May 1991 cattle broke into the Twelve Mile fenced exclosure and ten cattle died. Feral donkeys entered and damaged the spring.
- *Mining and petroleum*: the mining threat was judged limited, apart from draw-down. A 1986 low-impact seismic trial at Dalhousie kept environmental impact low, but cost over double conventional techniques.

## Key Findings

**Achievements (1981 to 1992)**
- Witjira National Park was created from Dalhousie Springs, covering 7769 km². Cabinet approved purchase in May 1984, livestock were removed by May 1985, and the park was proclaimed on 21 November 1985 under the *South Australian National Parks and Wildlife Act 1972*.
- Ten high-priority springs were fenced between 1986 and 1988 (see [[Spring Fencing Program]]). The first contract covered Blanche Cup, The Bubbler and Strangways. The remaining springs were fenced by National Parks and Wildlife Service field staff, finished in late 1988.
- Finniss Springs, including [[Hermit Hill]], was acquired in August 1991.
- Heritage listings: several Overland Telegraph, railway and cultural sites were entered on State and National Estate registers. A survey of non-Aboriginal heritage covered 78 items and sites.
- The 1985 [[Social and Ecological Assessment]] ranked 14 springs (high, medium, low). Dalhousie, Freeling, Hermit and Old Finniss Springs were at the top of the ranking.

**Management problems identified**
- Remoteness makes fencing capital costs two to three times those in settled areas and makes maintenance difficult. Contractors were hard to find.
- Fences were not rabbit-netted because of funding constraints.
- No permanent ranger is present at Witjira National Park, and none is expected at Finniss Springs in the foreseeable future. The nearest ranger base is about 370 km by road.
- Monitoring of vegetation is irregular, and a collaborative arrangement with tertiary institutions was proposed.
- Visitor information has been provided only at a few springs.

**Recommendations (Conclusions)**
- Acquisition within the national parks system is the surest form of conservation but is expensive and feasible only for the most important complexes.
- Fencing offers a cheaper alternative. Enclosing spring groups rather than single springs maximises biodiversity and reduces stock pressure on fencing, though initial costs are higher.
- Maintenance arrangements with local pastoralists should be considered.
- Monitoring of plant and animal responses to fencing is essential. Collaboration with tertiary teaching institutions could reduce costs.
- Research is needed on the ecology of plant and animal populations, especially the taxonomy and habitat needs of the invertebrate fauna.
- Systematic monitoring of flow rates is essential, as flow data are often the only reliable indicator of aquifer draw-down.
- Local Aboriginal communities should be drawn into cooperative management.
- Future park planning may use an "archipelago" model of several spring groups south-east of Oodnadatta, with intervening pastoral leases retained.
- The South Australian Government would seek Commonwealth support for World Heritage evaluation of the springs.

**Cost note:** The South Australian Government spent $92,000 on the natural and cultural heritage surveys of the mid-1980s, which were used as a guide for priorities.
