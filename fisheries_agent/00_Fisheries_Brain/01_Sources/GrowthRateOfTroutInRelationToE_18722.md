# Growth Rate of Trout in Relation to Elevation and Temperature

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 paper, by Clarence A. Puckett, Jr. of the [[Missouri Conservation Commission]] (Columbia, Missouri), tests whether trout in [[Mountain Stream]] systems grow more slowly at higher elevations because the water is colder. The study covers 758 trout from the [[West Gallatin River]] and 184 trout from [[Bridger Creek]] and [[Spring Creek]], all in the headwaters of the [[Missouri River]] system in Montana. The author concludes that growth in the West Gallatin declines with elevation, in step with lower water temperatures, while Bridger-Spring Creek showed no consistent differences in growth.

## Background & Description

The author notes that fisheries workers had observed larger trout in lower valley reaches than in upper canyon reaches, but little had been published. The size difference could reflect either variable [[Growth Rate]] or downstream movement of larger fish. If growth is responsible, it should correlate with [[Water Temperature]]. Prior work cited in the paper includes Hazzard (1932) on [[Eastern Brook Trout]] growth in cold headwaters and McHugh (1941) on [[Rocky Mountain Whitefish]] growth in relation to temperature and altitude.

The West Gallatin River rises in [[Yellowstone National Park]], flows about 90 miles, and joins the East Gallatin River to form the Gallatin River. The upper 25 miles are a meandering meadow stream with few pools and little cover. Below the park it enters [[Gallatin Canyon]], a roughly 35-mile reach of rapids and swift water with a gradient of about 43 feet per mile. The lower section has a gradient of about 34 feet per mile, more pools, and mostly riffles. The stream is 40 to 150 feet wide and has 26 tributaries.

For analysis the river was divided into three sections at roughly 1,000-foot elevation intervals:

| Section | Extent | Approximate Length | Elevation Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Mouth of East Gallatin to Spanish Creek | ~30 miles | 4,155 to 5,190 ft |
| II | Spanish Creek to West Fork | ~21 miles | 5,190 to 6,000 ft |
| III | West Fork to 3 miles above Fan Creek | ~30 miles | 6,000 to 7,100 ft |

Bridger Creek (18 miles, meadow stream) and Spring Creek (7 miles, small swift stream with heavy brush) were treated as one unit and split into five sections by 500-foot contours.

Species present in the West Gallatin, in descending order of abundance, were [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Rainbow-Cutthroat Hybrid]] trout, [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Eastern Brook Trout]]. Brown and brook trout were found only in the lower 30 miles. Hybrids could not always be distinguished, so some may have been recorded as rainbow or cutthroat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish movement.** The author assumed that trout did not move extensively. He cites Watts et al. (1942) on limited brook trout movement in Kettle Creek and Hazzard and Shetter (1938), who found most planted rainbow and brook trout within 1 to 3 miles of release.

**Temperature.** Weekly water temperature readings were taken in the West Gallatin in summer 1949. Minimum readings were taken between 6:30 and 9:00 A.M., and maximum readings between 2 and 5 P.M. Bridger and Spring Creeks were sampled at 2-week intervals in summer 1948. Thermometers were a Taylor pocket thermometer calibrated at 2° F intervals and checked against a certified chemical thermometer. Readings were taken in shade, and water readings were taken in current. Monthly morning averages declined at each higher elevation. The paper cites an optimum range of 55° to 60° F for trout (Davis, 1946). This range is reached in all sections during warm afternoons, but it persists for shorter periods at high elevations, where daily and seasonal fluctuations are also more extreme.

**Water chemistry.** Dissolved oxygen was near saturation and adequate in all sections. Methyl orange alkalinity indicated moderately hard water at all stations, and pH was in the alkaline range. No appreciable differences existed between sections in either stream system.

**Canopy and fluctuation.** The author attributes the pronounced daily temperature swings in the upper West Gallatin to a lack of shade. Needham (1938) and Belding (1928) are cited on temperature, metabolism and growth.

## Key Findings

**Methods.** Scale samples were taken from fish caught by anglers, from a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service creel census on the lower river in summer 1949, from a Montana Fish and Game survey (about 100 scales, August 1948), and from angling and electrofishing. Total length was measured in tenths of inches and weight in tenths of pounds. Condition was calculated as C = W × 10⁵ / L³, where W is weight in pounds and L is total length in inches.

**West Gallatin rainbow trout (n = 351).** Growth was fastest in Section I and slowed progressively upstream. The lower-upper differential in length increased with age:

| Annulus | Upper-Lower Difference (inches) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0.3 |
| 2 | 1.3 |
| 3 | 2.1 |
| 4 | 2.5 |
| 5 | 4.0 |

Using the t test (Pearson and Bennett, 1942), Sections I and III differed significantly at every annulus. At annulus I the difference was significant at the 5 percent level, and at annulus II it was significant at the 1 percent level in all comparisons. The Section I versus III difference at annulus III was significant at the 1 percent level. Annulus V was not tested because sample sizes were small.

**Rainbow-cutthroat hybrids (n = 121) and cutthroat trout (n = 113).** Both showed slower growth upstream, but the trend was weaker than in rainbow trout. For hybrids the author suggests this may reflect small upstream samples. For cutthroat trout the differences may not be significant because Section I and II samples were small.

**Brown and brook trout.** These were taken only in Section I, so no between-section comparison was possible. Within Section I, brown and brook trout grew faster than the other species, and brown trout exceeded all others in yearly increment after the first year.

**Condition.** No correlation existed between condition coefficient and section in either system. Average condition factors were no lower in the upper sections. The author concludes that temperature affects length but not condition.

**Abstract figures.** Summer morning temperatures differed by 9.6° F between the upper and lower West Gallatin stations. Afternoon temperatures differed by only 5° F. In the July 11 afternoon reading, Sections I, II and III measured 62.0, 60.0 and 58.0° F. Condition factors showed no significant variation between sections.

**Bridger-Spring Creek (n = 184).** This sample comprised 98 rainbow trout and 86 eastern brook trout from five sections. No consistent differences in annual length increments appeared, even between distant sections. Bridger Creek temperature differences between upper and lower sections were at most 7° F and at least 2° F. Temperature was not measured often enough to give firm conclusions, so the author calls the data "too limited to be more than suggestive."

**Conclusions.** The author concludes that at each higher West Gallatin elevation, annual length increments were smaller and water temperatures lower. The temperature differences may account for the growth differences, but unmeasured environmental factors may also contribute. In the Bridger-Spring system, no growth-temperature relationship could be established.
