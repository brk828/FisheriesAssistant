# Predator Recognition Training in Four Southwestern Native Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study tested whether four southwestern native fishes recognize introduced catfish as predators and whether they can learn to do so. Hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]], and wild-caught but predator-naive [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Roundtail Chub]], were exposed to [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] in a two-tank apparatus. Movement was tracked with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT tags). The fish did not inherently recognize catfish as a threat, but all four species learned to respond after a dead conspecific was paired with a catfish. Chubs swam away from the threat, while suckers reduced movement. The authors conclude that [[Predator Recognition Training]] could improve survival of hatchery fish, but only for species that already have an effective innate escape response.

## Background & Description

Many endangered-fish recovery programs depend on [[Hatchery Stocking]] to restore depleted populations. Hatchery-reared natives commonly suffer high mortality when stocked into waters with established non-native fishes. Documented cases include failed reintroductions of Razorback Sucker to the San Pedro River, Arizona, where catfish predation was responsible (Marsh and Brooks, 1989). Poor survival of Bonytail and Razorback Sucker stocked into [[Lake Mohave]] was attributed to non-native predators such as striped bass and Flathead Catfish. Managers therefore recommended stocking only large adults.

Two hypotheses were considered. Minckley and Douglas (1991) proposed that southwestern natives lack predator-avoidance defenses because they evolved with few or no predators. Other researchers argued that animals reared without predators must learn avoidance skills. The study was designed to test both explanations by measuring innate avoidance and the capacity for learned recognition.

The four study species differ in their native-fish status and in their documented responses to predators. The native fishes were compared with their introduced catfish predators, which were drawn from the [[Verde River]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predator-prey setting:** Introduced catfish are established predators in the Colorado River system. The natives involved had no shared evolutionary history with these species.
- **Sensory cues:** The study tested visual and chemical cues. Alarm cues from injured conspecifics, paired with predator cues, triggered antipredator behavior.
- **Behavioral ecology:** Freezing or reduced movement can be effective against predators that hunt by sight in clear water. It is less effective against catfish, which locate prey with chemosensory and tactile organs in low-visibility conditions.
- **Broader context:** Similar learned responses have been documented in trout, fathead minnows, and walleye. Some of these responses persisted for weeks to months, while others faded within days.

## Key Findings

**Study design and sample**

- Native fish came from three sources. Hatchery fish were 20 Razorback Sucker (mean 94.2 mm TL) from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], plus 20 Razorback Sucker (107.1 mm TL) and 40 Bonytail (95.3 mm TL) from the [[Arizona Bubbling Ponds Native Conservation Facility]]. Wild fish were 20 Sonora Sucker (85.4 mm TL) and 20 Roundtail Chub (98.8 mm TL), collected from [[Fossil Creek]], a site free of non-native fish.
- Predators were two Flathead Catfish (516 and 573 mm TL) and two Channel Catfish (479 and 430 mm TL) from the Verde River.
- Fish were held at 20 °C in 568-L recirculating tanks, PIT-tagged, and allowed 10 days to recover before trials.
- The apparatus had two 1,700-L circular tanks joined by a 1-m by 7.62-cm acrylic tube, with PIT antennas at each end. Trials ran overnight at 22 °C, with groups of 10 native fish per trial.
- Each species went through four treatments in a fixed order: no predator; catfish present; catfish carrying a dead conspecific; and catfish again two weeks later. Bonytail and Razorback Sucker had two replicates. Roundtail Chub and Sonora Sucker had one.
- Analyses used two-sample t-tests for time use and repeated-measures ANOVA for tag detections as an index of movement. A control trial with no predators was added for the two suckers.

**Innate and learned responses**

- Before exposure to a dead conspecific, no species showed a significant preference for one tank when catfish were present. This indicated no innate recognition of catfish as a threat.
- With catfish and a dead conspecific present, Roundtail Chub spent significantly more time in the tank without catfish. Bonytail showed a similar trend, but the difference was not significant.
- Sonora Sucker spent more time in the tank with catfish. Razorback Sucker showed no significant difference.
- Two weeks after the dead conspecific was removed, Roundtail Chub again favored the tank without catfish. Bonytail showed no preference.
- The Roundtail Chub result at two weeks was not expected. The authors interpret it as a sign that the initial avoidance did not persist for Bonytail.

**Movement changes in suckers**

- Tag detections for both suckers fell sharply when catfish carried dead conspecifics. Razorback Sucker in Trial 1 fell from 20.7 to a value reported as 1.0 tag hits. Razorback Sucker in Trial 2 fell from 30.4 to 9.3 tag hits. Sonora Sucker fell from 43.1 to 3.8 tag hits (P < .0005).
- Reduced movement continued for 14 days in Sonora Sucker and in Razorback Sucker Trial 1.
- Suckers often moved to the catfish side and then stayed still for the rest of the trial, or did not move at all.
- Sonora Sucker kept reduced movement in two later trials after the dead conspecific was gone. This makes it unclear whether the lasting effect came from learning or from predation during those trials.

**Losses during trials**

- Two Razorback Sucker and one Sonora Sucker were eaten by catfish during the trials held 14 days after exposure. The authors note that the catfish usually did not feed for several days after capture, which suggests they had become accustomed to handling.

**Conclusions**

- Native fish can learn to recognize novel catfish predators within about 12 hours by associating a dead conspecific with the predator.
- Chubs avoid predators by swimming away. Suckers avoid them by freezing or reducing movement. The authors argue that suckers' freezing response may make them more vulnerable to catfish, which locate prey by senses other than sight.
- Predator recognition training may help hatchery-reared Bonytail and Roundtail Chub, which already have an effective escape response. It is less likely to help Razorback Sucker and Sonora Sucker.
- Additional research is needed. Tests were conducted only with groups of fish, and Roundtail Chub and Sonora Sucker had only one replicate.
