# Symposium on Evaluation of Fish Populations in Warm Water Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #stream-survey #fish-population-evaluation

## Executive Summary

This document is the proceedings of a 1957 symposium, held at Iowa State College (Ames, Iowa) and convened by [[Kenneth D. Carlander]], on evaluating fish populations in warm water streams. The symposium treated any method giving population estimates, relative abundance, trends or condition as "evaluation." Its papers and discussions cover fish home range and homing, the effect of tagging on growth, catch dynamics of hoop nets and trap nets, comparison of survey gears, stream survey methods in the Southeast and Iowa, mark-and-recovery estimates, trout population methods in Michigan, and the design and efficiency of [[Electric Shocker]] equipment. The consensus was that warm water stream sampling is relatively inefficient and often yields only semi-quantitative data.

## Background & Description

The proceedings are organized in four parts (A: Distributional Behavior of Fishes; B: Use of Nets; C: Stream Survey Methods; D: Electric Shockers), followed by evening discussions on survey purpose, site selection, and stream classification. Appendices include an annotated bibliography on factors affecting catch per unit effort and a bibliography on electric shockers. The registered attendance list names roughly 60 biologists, managers and educators from state conservation agencies, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and universities, including [[Iowa State College]], [[Indiana University]], [[Missouri Conservation Commission]], and [[Illinois Natural History Survey]].

Warm water streams are described as the most difficult habitat for population study because they are less circumscribed than lakes, show greater seasonal variation in temperature, flow, and cover, and are more turbid and deeper than trout streams, which limits the transfer of trout techniques.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Home range and stability.** Gerking summarized evidence that 25 to 30 species, including the sunfish, bass, suckers and trout listed in the paper, show restricted home ranges. Longear sunfish often stayed within about 100 feet. Homing has been reported in walleye, smallmouth bass, cutthroat trout, lake trout, brown trout and brook trout. Territoriality and social hierarchy, documented in green sunfish, coho and sockeye salmon, and rainbow and brook trout, were proposed as stabilizing influences. Environmental fluctuation (for example, power-plant water level changes in Sweden) could destabilize populations.

**Gear selectivity.** Catch in stationary gear depends on fish movement, spawning behavior, baiting, water level and net location. Mesh size determined which size classes were retained: channel catfish in 3/4-inch wire traps averaged smaller than those in 1-inch hoop nets.

**Turbidity and water chemistry.** Turbid water reduced recovery of shocked fish from 50 to 75 percent in Tennessee. Shocking effectiveness depended on alkalinity (usable above 75 ppm, marginal at 20 to 75 ppm, impractical below 20 ppm in the Tennessee design).

**Tagging effects.** Several participants reported tagged fish losing length or weight, while others reported normal or increased growth. Carp in the Salt River study were the best adjusted to tags.

## Key Findings

**Distributional behavior (Gerking; Purkett).**
- Gerking found that longear sunfish transferred about 100 yards upstream returned to their original range at a rate of 74 percent. Smallmouth bass returned from transfers of 0.6 to 0.9 mile.
- Cutthroat trout confined in an enclosure (Miller 1954) showed strong escape attempts; 25 of 152 transplanted trout died against screens.
- Purkett's Salt River tagging study (about 1300 fish tagged over 6.5 years) recovered 100 percent of flathead catfish and green sunfish, 77 percent of carp, 33 1/3 percent of bigmouth buffalo and freshwater drum, 29 percent of channel catfish, and none of river carpsucker, white crappie or shortnose gar. Carp at 1140 days free were the longest-retained individuals.
- Discussants reported that intermediate-sized smallmouth bass (9 to 12 inches) were most mobile, and that about 10 percent of stocked rainbow trout behaved as "rovers."

**Nets and traps (Muncy; Cleary; Funk).**
- Channel catfish catches in the Des Moines River showed a sharp drop in net recaptures after spawning; baiting produced a significant difference at the 99 percent level by chi-square.
- Cleary compared trapnet catches with creel census data on three Iowa rivers (Cedar, Wapsipinicon, Maquoketa). Trends agreed only 42 percent of the time for catfish (number basis), 58 percent for crappies and 58 percent for all game fish. Cleary concluded that trapnet surveys are a dangerous basis for forecasting angler success.
- Funk's Missouri study (242 collections at 13 stations on four rivers) concluded that, under those conditions, the electric seine was the most effective single gear for most species, while catfish were poorly sampled. Funk reported that the electric seine was most effective for 15 of 19 species by frequency of occurrence. Of 21 rankings by catch rate, 13 agreed with frequency-of-occurrence rankings.

**Stream survey methods (Ruhr; Harrison; Purkett).**
- Ruhr's Tennessee statewide survey used a two-electrode AC shocker powered by a 230-volt, 6.7-ampere generator. Under optimum conditions, rarely more than 40 percent of fish were recovered. Smallmouth bass making up 7 to 10 percent of sample weight was adopted as a standard for good smallmouth streams.
- Harrison's Humboldt Pool (5.5 miles of the Des Moines River) mark-and-recapture estimates for channel catfish fell from 104,597 (fall 1953) to 35,742 (fall 1955). A fall 1956 rotenone treatment of the area yielded estimates of 69,174 carp and 25,062 quillback.
- Purkett's Salt River rotenone comparison showed that the direct census estimate of all species (1661) was 31.8 percent of the rotenone-based estimate (5229). Gizzard shad (5351 direct census) were the most abundant species, a result attributed to reproduction in isolated pools during drought.
- Discussants agreed that catch per unit effort is generally a more comparable measure than percentage composition, though this was debated.

**Electric shockers (Shetter; Funk; Larimore; discussion).**
- Shetter's Michigan trout method uses two complete trips with an electric shocker; the recovery trip follows at least 24 hours later. A five-man crew covered about 1 mile of stream per day. Michigan's trout shocker was 59 percent effective on trout over 5 inches.
- Larimore's Jordan Creek (Illinois) census covered 0.9 mile (2.4 acres). The shocker took 72 percent of minnow weight but only 8 percent of bullhead weight. The total population was 68,600 fish (445.7 pounds), or 28,580 fish per surface acre.
- Safety discussions emphasized that AC is more hazardous than DC. Discussants recommended waders, insulated gloves, positive power cutoff and one crew member assigned to the generator. A chemist reported that surface resistance at electrodes dissipates considerable energy.
- Equipment displays included electric seines from Missouri, Illinois and Iowa, a Kentucky control-box seine (estimated $800 to $1000), and a Florida "Spider" boat used to remove gar and dogfish (147,000 pounds in 201 hours).

**Evening discussions: survey purpose and classification.**
- Participants agreed that survey purpose must guide sampling design. Random or stratified site selection was favored for pollution studies, while managers preferred stable, fishable reaches.
- Missouri classifies smallmouth streams when smallmouth make up 5 to 10 percent of the creel census catch, with clear water and high gradient as supporting criteria.
- A 1956 Missouri fertilization study on a 35-mile Tennessee stream reported utilization per mile up 370 percent and total harvest up 380 percent, though catch per trip changed only from 6 to 6.5 fish.
