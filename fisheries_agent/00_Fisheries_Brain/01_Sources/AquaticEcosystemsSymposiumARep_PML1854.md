# Aquatic Ecosystems Symposium: Sustainability of Western Water Resources (1997)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This 1997 report, edited by [[Wendell L. Minckley]] at Arizona State University for the Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission, compiles five independent scientific chapters and a synthesis on the condition of surface waters in the American West. The contributors unanimously conclude that western aquatic ecosystems are "broken and must soon be fixed" if they are to be sustainable. The chapters cover physical landscapes and geomorphology, riparian ecosystems, nutrients and productivity, food webs, and native fishes. Common threads include dams and water diversion, groundwater mining, livestock grazing, mining, agricultural salinization, and introduced species. The report calls for watershed-based and flexible federal management, restoration of natural flow regimes, sediment and riparian protection, reform of the Endangered Species Act and the [[Clean Water Act]], "user pays" water pricing, and expanded monitoring and research.

## Background & Description

The symposium was organized to examine the past, present, and projected future of western surface waters across six interwoven categories: watersheds, channels, riparian zones, primary producers, secondary producers, and native fishes. The editor frames sustainability as meeting present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs, and notes that western water development is already unsustainable in arid and semi-arid areas.

The report's five chapters are:
- **Chapter II: Sustainability and Changing Physical Landscapes** by William L. Graf et al.
- **Chapter III: Sustainability of Western Riparian Ecosystems** by Duncan T. Patten et al.
- **Chapter IV: Sustainability of Western Watersheds: Nutrients and Productivity** by Nancy B. Grimm et al.
- **Chapter V: Sustaining Western Aquatic Food Webs** by Mary E. Power et al.
- **Chapter VI: Sustainability of Western Native Fish Resources** by W. L. Minckley et al.

A closing synthesis by Jack A. Stanford draws the chapters into policy messages. The report also includes biographical sketches, conversion tables, and a species appendix. The reference lists are extensive; they are summarized here rather than reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical landscapes.** Western hydroclimate is non-stationary, shifting between El Niño and La Niña patterns and several multi-decadal hydroclimatic periods. Land-use changes, including grazing, logging, roads, and urbanization, increased runoff, floods, and sediment yields. Beaver removal in the 1800s eliminated small dams that had stored water and sediment. High dams have reduced flood peaks, altered low flows, and caused channel narrowing and riparian expansion. Glen Canyon Dam controlled-flood experiments are cited as examples of restoration potential.

**Riparian ecosystems.** Riparian zones occupy a narrow band but support more than 75% of animal species in arid regions and about 75% of breeding birds in the Southwest are associated with riparian vegetation (Box 3 notes approximately 47% of birds breeding in the Southwest are riparian-restricted). Threats include impoundment, groundwater pumping, grazing, mining, urbanization, and invasive species such as tamarisk (saltcedar), which occupies over 500,000 ha of riparian habitat and up to 90% of the area originally dominated by cottonwood-willow forest near Yuma, Arizona.

**Nutrients and productivity.** Nitrogen is often the limiting nutrient in western streams, and river ecosystems retain nutrients through biological processes. Dams trap nutrients and sediments, disconnect floodplains, and promote eutrophication. Effluent-dependent rivers below cities demonstrate eutrophication and pH problems, and the Kesterson-like cases of mining runoff show how iron-oxide chemistry controls phosphorus availability.

**Food webs.** Western aquatic food webs depend on natural flow variability, especially floods that reset invertebrate communities and flush spawning gravels. Hydropeaking, thermal releases from deep reservoirs, and alien species disrupt these webs. Introduced bullfrogs, centrarchids, and mosquitofish displace native amphibians and fishes. The Flathead Lake opossum shrimp introduction collapsed the kokanee population and damaged the bald eagle tourism economy.

**Native fishes.** At least 40 North American freshwater fish species have gone extinct in the last century, more than half in arid western lands, and about 100 additional western species are threatened, endangered, or of special concern. Of 810 native North American freshwater fishes, 170 (21%) live west of the Rocky Mountains, and most western fishes are highly endemic. The Colorado River Basin has 32 native fishes, of which about 22 (69%) are endemic. Introduced species outnumber natives in the Colorado Basin (more than 60 introduced versus 32 native).

## Key Findings

- **Unanimous conclusion:** Western surface-water ecosystems are seriously degraded; restoring them requires coordinated federal, state, and local action.
- **Physical integrity** is under-protected in law. The chapter recommends increased emphasis on the physical integrity component of the Clean Water Act and establishing sediment quality standards.
- **Dam operations** should be altered to mimic natural flow regimes, including periodic flood releases, and existing dams should be reviewed for removal, modification, or relicensing with physical-integrity criteria. Hydropower should remain a component but not the primary objective.
- **Agency fragmentation:** Federal water agencies are organized by function rather than geography. The report recommends a commission to plan consolidation on a watershed basis and "flexible federalism" through watershed councils.
- **Riparian protection:** Recommendations include reform of the 1872 Mining Law, limiting grazing where riparian damage is severe, requiring riparian buffers for agriculture, discouraging floodplain development (including withdrawal of FEMA funding from repeatedly flooded properties), and designating a "natural" river class under the Wild and Scenic Rivers Act.
- **Nutrient retention:** Restoring flood pulses, reconnecting floodplains, minimizing diel flow variation, and phasing out agricultural subsidies that drive groundwater overdraft are recommended.
- **Food webs and invasives:** Restoring flushing flows, preventing introductions, and eradicating non-natives from isolated waters are key actions. Monitoring should emphasize ecosystem quality, not only water quality.
- **Native fish policy:** The report calls for an ecosystem-oriented management approach, "user pays" pricing for extractive uses, reauthorization or strengthening of the [[Endangered Species Act]], public education, and separation of native and non-native fish management. The authors describe the Virgin River basin case as an example of water development outweighing species recovery. Habitat Conservation Plans are viewed as long-term agreements that need science-based monitoring.
- **Synthesis:** Watersheds should be the basic units for water management. The synthesis calls for reduced per-capita water and energy consumption, a sediment standard, riparian protection, adaptive management, long-term monitoring, and research funding of roughly 10–20% of water-resource budgets (the authors note that far less than a few tenths of a percent is currently spent on basic aquatic research).
