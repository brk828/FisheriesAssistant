# Mitochondrial DNA Variability in the Endangered Razorback Sucker: Analysis of Hatchery Stocks and Implications for Captive Propagation

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Catostomidae #endangered-species #genetic-diversity

## Executive Summary

This study examines mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) diversity in the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] drainage. Researchers analyzed genetic variation in a source population from [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada) and three hatchery-produced year classes (1987, 1989, 1990) from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The wild population exhibited remarkably high genetic diversity (haplotype diversity h = 0.97, with 17 unique haplotypes in 25 individuals). While 1987 and 1989 hatchery year classes maintained relatively high diversity (h = 0.89 and 0.91), the 1990 year class showed significantly reduced diversity (h = 0.71), likely due to unequal contributions from broodstock females or differential progeny viability. The study evaluates three management alternatives for preserving genetic diversity: standard hatchery propagation, natural spawning in predator-free environments, and protective custody of wild-spawned larvae. The authors strongly recommend the third option as most cost-effective for maintaining maximum genetic variability while the species faces complete recruitment failure throughout its range.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large, long-lived catostomid fish historically widespread and abundant throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species has experienced dramatic population declines due to multiple anthropogenic impacts including water management practices, habitat modification, and introduction of non-native predatory species. The largest remaining populations occur in the upper [[Green River]] (Colorado-Utah, estimated <1,000 adults) and [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada, estimated <55,000 adults). Natural populations elsewhere are too small for reliable size estimates.

[[Lake Mohave]] is a mainstem impoundment created in 1954 by closure of Davis Dam. The reservoir consists of an upstream riverine reach (~60 km) grading into a wider lower basin (48 km long, maximum width 6.4 km). Surface area is 115 km², average depth 10.5 m (maximum ~43 m), with short retention time (0.24 year replacement at average annual discharge of 9.3 × 10⁹ m³). Tag-recapture data indicate fewer than 55,000 adult razorback suckers concentrated primarily in upper parts of the lower basin, most vulnerable to capture when aggregated near shore for spawning in winter-spring.

The species exhibits complete recruitment failure: all populations consist only of adults averaging 25 to >40 years of age, with juveniles virtually unknown and no detected recruitment to spawning stocks. Despite continued spawning and larval production in rivers and reservoirs, young fish disappear, likely to predation by non-native species, possibly mediated by nutritional constraints. The species was federally listed as endangered in 1991.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The razorback sucker's decline typifies the plight of western "big-river" fishes. Human impacts have combined to extirpate the species from most of its former range. Water management has reduced and extensively modified aquatic habitats, while non-native species (mostly introduced as sportfish or baitfish) have affected indigenous species through competition, predation, and hybridization. More than 20 native taxa became extinct in the past century in western North America.

Three hypotheses for recruitment failure have been examined: (1) transport of larvae from Lake Mohave by dam operations, (2) insufficient food resources (zooplankton densities sometimes fall below levels required for larval persistence), and (3) predation by non-native fishes on eggs and larvae. Evidence strongly supports predation as a primary factor—when stocked with predatory fishes, larval razorback suckers fail to survive to juvenile stage in all habitats studied, but typically mature successfully when stocked in diverse, fishless environments without food augmentation.

Twenty-three species have been introduced into Lake Mohave, with at least three-fourths known or predicted to consume eggs or larval fishes. Similar introductions occur throughout the Colorado River basin. Eradication or suppression of these predators appears unlikely, certainly not in time to benefit razorback suckers naturally.

The existing Lake Mohave population likely hatched around 1954 coincident with impoundment, prior to prevalence of non-native predators. Based on timing of disappearance of other reservoir stocks in the lower Colorado basin (~40 years after impoundment), this population faces imminent collapse. The population must represent direct descendants of an exceedingly large, diverse, panmictic population inhabiting the lower Colorado basin in pre-development times, as evidenced by remarkably high mtDNA diversity (average 0.68 haplotypes per individual).

## Key Findings

**Genetic Diversity in Source Population:**
- Lake Mohave population exhibited exceptionally high mtDNA diversity (h = 0.97)
- 17 haplotypes detected in 25 individuals (0.68 haplotypes per individual)
- Most haplotypes (11 of 17) were unique to single individuals
- Two most common haplotypes found in only three individuals each
- Diversity consistent with high fecundity (~2,000 ova/cm standard length) and large effective population size

**Hatchery Year Class Analysis:**
- Eight restriction enzymes produced ~160 fragments across 56 individuals, yielding 27 total haplotypes
- 1987 year class: h = 0.89, 6 haplotypes in 10 individuals (55 females spawned, 48.5% survivorship)
- 1989 year class: h = 0.91, 7 haplotypes in 11 individuals (14 females spawned, 46.0% survivorship)
- 1990 year class: h = 0.71, 4 haplotypes in 10 individuals (17 females spawned, 11.1% survivorship)
- Statistical resampling showed only 1990 year class significantly different from source (p < 0.001)
- Unique haplotypes not found in Lake Mohave sample present in all three year classes (4, 6, and 1 respectively)

**Factors Affecting Hatchery Diversity:**
- Reduction in 1990 diversity not explained by number of females used (17 vs. 55 and 14 for 1987/1989)
- Computer simulations demonstrated haplotype diversity (h) relatively insensitive to broodstock size above ~20 individuals
- Survivorship from fertilized ova to swim-up only 11.1% in 1990, substantially lower than 1982-1989 minimum of 22.5% (mean 40.1%, maximum 71.4%)
- Reduced diversity likely due to over-representation of some females or differential progeny viability
- Potential confounding by complex broodstock history involving multiple year classes and wild-caught fish

**Broodstock History Complications:**
- Initial broodstock from 1981-82 Lake Mohave collections (maximum 79 females)
- Most F₁ broodfish derived from only 6-8 wild females (two contributed 95.6% of 1981 production)
- Wild-spawned larvae collected in 1985 (85-WILD) added genetic material
- By 1990, broodstock mixed from multiple sources: 81+82YC-F₁s (>50%), 85-WILD (<20%), and 87YC-F₂s (33.5%)
- Specific origins of 1990 spawning females unknown, raising possibility of inbreeding with close relatives

**Management Alternatives Evaluated:**

*Option 1 - Standard Hatchery Propagation:*
- Can produce spectacular numbers: 100 females (45 cm SL) could yield 1.43 × 10⁷ ova
- With 71.4% survival to swim-up and 10% to 30 cm SL, could produce 1.02 × 10⁵ fish (nearly twice wild population) in one season
- Major limitation: space constraints on broodstock numbers dramatically reduce genetic variability
- Even under ideal conditions (equalizing contributions, preventing adaptation to captivity), can maintain only small proportion of existing variability
- Problems from unequal parental contribution, inadvertent selection, or inbreeding likely
- Most expensive option with least desirable outcome for genetic diversity

*Option 2 - Natural Spawning in Predator-Free Bays:*
- Involves removing predators from isolated backwaters, stocking adults to spawn unattended
- Five pilot bays used 145 females total between 1984-1992, with two of five years producing progeny large enough to escape predation
- If 10 bays established with 40% success, ~120 different females could contribute annually
- Maintains far more genetic variability than hatchery conditions over multiple years
- Limitations: space availability (number of bays) limits contributing fish; lack of control over reproduction prevents equalizing contributions or ensuring all individuals reproduce
- Intermediate cost and genetic diversity maintenance

*Option 3 - Direct Capture and Husbandry of Wild-Spawned Larvae (RECOMMENDED):*
- Larval razorback suckers appear along Lake Mohave shorelines December-January through April-May (rarely June)
- Positively phototactic, readily collected manually or by light traps
- Two dedicated collectors can harvest hundreds to thousands in favorable nights
- Tag-recapture data indicate large proportion of Lake Mohave individuals reproduce annually
- Annual larval production derives from far more adults than possible under hatchery or bay-culture
- Variability best conserved by sampling multiple time periods (early, mid-, late spawning) and widespread geographic locations over multiple years
- Accounts for potential seasonal/annual periodicity or geographic localization of genetic groups
- Represents far more parents than other alternatives despite inability to equalize contributions
- Most cost-effective option; diverse "grow-out" habitats can range from hatchery ponds/raceways to cutoff bays, livestock ponds, urban lakes (all proven successful)
- Authors strongly recommend immediate implementation before wild adult population collapses

**Critical Recommendations:**
- Do NOT replace existing Lake Mohave population with hatchery-cultured fish of reduced genetic variability
- Maintain existing inbred [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] broodstock as backup only; replace with wild-caught individuals
- Do NOT release progeny of current broodstock into wild, especially not to replace Lake Mohave population
- Continue and expand bay-culture program to provide rearing habitat for wild-caught larvae
- Keep all three management options open if economics allow, with continuing refinement
- Immediate action essential: hesitation may preclude capturing wild larvae in numbers sufficient to maintain genetic variation before old wild population disappears
- Avoid temptation to use traditional hatchery methods to quickly produce large quantities at substantial cost to genetic variability

**Conservation Genetics Implications:**
- Study demonstrates critical importance of monitoring genetic diversity in captive propagation programs
- MtDNA useful marker for assessing maintenance of genetic variation in hatchery stocks
- Haplotype diversity (h) measure has limitations: relatively insensitive to broodstock size above ~20 individuals, misrepresents levels of variation maintained
- Direct count of unique haplotypes provides better indicator of genetic composition changes
- Institutional goals of hatcheries must shift from efficient production philosophy to conservation philosophy for endangered species
- Geographic distribution of genetic variation essential consideration; isolated subpopulations may possess locally adaptive traits
- Loss of genetic diversity through drift or inbreeding potentially reduces ability to respond to changing environments upon reintroduction
