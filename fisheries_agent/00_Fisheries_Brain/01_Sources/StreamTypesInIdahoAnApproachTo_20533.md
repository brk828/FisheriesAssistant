# Stream Types in Idaho: An Approach to Classification of Streams in Natural Areas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study proposes a classification of small streams in [[Idaho]] for use in a [[Natural Areas]] programme. It describes five stream types: [[Ephemeral Streams]], [[Spring Streams]], and three types of [[Permanent Streams]] ([[Type 1 Streams]], [[Type 2 Streams]], [[Type 3 Streams]]). Types are defined by readily identifiable, non-variable features: stream order, gradient, pattern of flow and substrate. The types are supported ecologically by analysis of extensive collections of aquatic plants and invertebrates. Fish are described as weaker indicators than plants and invertebrates because of their mobility and range.

## Background & Description

Natural areas are tracts of land and water set aside to preserve undisturbed ecosystems for scientific study and as baseline sites for resource management. The [[Idaho Natural Area Organization]] was working with land managers to build a statewide network of such reserves, and this project was started to inventory aquatic ecosystems and develop a classification capable of covering the range of aquatic diversity.

The authors note that aquatic classification has lagged behind terrestrial classification because community characteristics are not immediately apparent. Existing stream typologies were often built around a single use, such as fishery value, aesthetic uniqueness or wild and scenic river status. The authors judged these use-oriented schemes unsuitable for natural areas. Many ecosystem-based systems sacrificed hierarchical development for wide applicability. [[Low-Order Streams]], the most ubiquitous aquatic features, were rarely defined in detail.

Classification criteria:

- **Order:** frequently shown on maps and a non-variable characteristic.
- **[[Gradient]]:** of secondary importance in ephemeral and spring streams but a key descriptor for permanent streams. It affects current velocity, flow pattern, [[Substrate]], dissolved oxygen, temperature and biota. It can be closely approximated from large-scale topographic maps.
- **[[Substrate]]:** linked to species distribution, abundance and composition. It can be inferred from gradient, has little seasonal variation and can be determined by direct observation.
- **Biological characteristics:** species associations were correlated with physical characteristics to provide the ecological basis for type designation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Ephemeral streams** carry water only during spring snowmelt (from about February to June, depending on area) or briefly after severe rainstorms. They are found mainly in small mountain streams of northern and central Idaho. In semi-arid southern Idaho, many higher-order streams are of this type. Because they are dry much of the year, typical aquatic communities do not establish.

**Spring streams** form where [[Groundwater]] aquifers discharge at the surface. They show less seasonal variation in flow than runoff-fed streams, and flow, velocity and substrate may be independent of order and gradient. A first-order spring stream may have torrential flow over boulders at very low gradient. In semi-arid regions such as southern Idaho, springs provide a perennial source of high-quality water for wildlife, recreation and other uses.

**Permanent streams** make up most low-order streams in the state. They show considerable seasonal variation, with high flow during snowmelt and intermittent flow in some cases later in the year.

- **Type 1 streams:** high sinuosity, low gradient, soft substrate, wide valleys. They often drain wet, open meadows or marshes in glaciated U-shaped valleys and cirque basins. Riparian vegetation is mainly sedges, grasses and forbs. Biota are adapted to minimal current, warmer temperatures, soft substrate and sometimes low oxygen. [[Rooted Vascular Plants]] are confined almost entirely to this type. Invertebrates include mud- and surface-dwelling insects, leeches (Hirudinea), freshwater shrimp (Gammaridae), fingernail clams (Sphaeriidae) and snails (Physidae).
- **Type 2 streams:** moderate gradient, with riffles, glides and deep pools. Substrate is coarse, and maximum particle size increases with gradient and flow volume. Flow is shallow relative to width, and streams usually occur in valleys with narrow flood plains. Shrubs usually dominate the riparian zone. These streams hold a diverse [[Aquatic Insects]] community, including mayflies (Ephemeroptera), stoneflies (Plecoptera), caddisflies (Trichoptera), two-winged flies (Diptera) and beetles (Coleoptera). Numerous moss species occupy the splash zone. The authors identify them as the most important fish habitat of the types.
- **Type 3 streams:** typically first- or second-order streams in young, V-shaped valleys on steep slopes, with a cascade-pool flow pattern. Log debris is an important substrate component and often forms the cascades. Pools below cascades hold sand, small gravel and coarse particulate organic matter. Bedrock is often exposed. Trees and heavy shading typify the riparian zone. Plants are uncommon and the rich aquatic community found in other types is absent. Insects with adaptations for swift current, such as [[Epeorus]] spp., [[Ephemerella Doddsi]] and Dioptopsis sp., occur here.

## Key Findings

- **Five stream types** are defined: ephemeral, spring, and three permanent types (Types 1–3). Classification rests on order, gradient, flow pattern and substrate.
- **Biological support:** type designation is backed by analysis of extensive collections of aquatic plants and invertebrates. Organisms are listed by permanent stream type in Tables 3–5.
- **Plant distribution:** rooted vascular plants occurred almost entirely in Type 1 streams, while most bryophytes were found in Type 2 streams.
- **Species associations:** computer analysis using phytosociological tables (after [[Mueller-Dumbois and Ellenberg]], 1974) produced seven groups. Each group had at least 50% of its species mutually associated within a stream set and no more than 10% in other streams (the "50-10 rule"). Some groups were confined to a single stream type, and groups 2, 3 and 5 occurred predominantly in one type.
- **Fish occurrence:** Table 5 lists six fish species: [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*S. gairdneri*), shorthead sculpin (*Cottus confusus*), longnose dace (*Rhinichthys cataractae*) and [[Mountain Whitefish]] (*Prosopium williamsoni*). Three amphibians are also listed: Oregon spotted frog (*Rana pretiosa*), tailed frog (*Ascaphus truei*) and California giant salamander (*Dicamptodon ensatus*). The OCR of the table does not reliably show which fish occur in which stream type, so type-specific fish records are not reported here.
- **Fish habitat:** Type 2 streams are described as the most important fish habitat. The authors state that fish are weaker stream-type indicators than plants and invertebrates because of their mobility.
- **Invertebrate indicators:** the text names Type 3 indicator invertebrates such as Epeorus spp. and Ephemerella doddsi. Type 1 indicators include leeches, freshwater shrimp, fingernail clams and snails.
- **Management significance:** the classification is intended as a use-independent, ecologically based basis for selecting and inventorying natural aquatic areas in Idaho.
