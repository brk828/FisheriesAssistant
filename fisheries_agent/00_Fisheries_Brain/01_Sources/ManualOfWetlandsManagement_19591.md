# Manual of Wetlands Management

#wetland-ecology #wetland-management

## Executive Summary

This manual, Wetlands Conservation Report Series No. 4 (July 1996), was prepared by the National Parks Service of the Victorian Department of Natural Resources and Environment, assisted by the Australian Nature Conservation Agency. It provides background and practical procedures for managing Victorian wetlands across nine chapters: wetland definitions and policy, survey and classification, landscape assessment, water management, livestock grazing, fire, restoration, aquaculture, and management guidelines. The document is a technical manual rather than a species study, so it contains no fish-specific data. Its central aim is to support the conservation of wetlands that are threatened by drainage, altered hydrology, grazing, fire, pollution and commercial development.

## Background & Description

**Definitions.** The manual adopts the [[Ramsar Convention]] definition of wetlands as areas of marsh, fen, peatland or water, natural or artificial, permanent or temporary, with static or flowing water that is fresh, brackish or salt, including marine areas no deeper than six metres at low tide. Victoria uses an adapted version, the [[Wetland Definition]], which excludes marine beaches, stream sections, impoundments under one hectare, and irrigated freehold farmland, and includes swamps, billabongs and floodplain depressions.

**Policy framework.** Australia is party to the Ramsar Convention and to the Japan–Australia (JAMBA) and China–Australia (CAMBA) migratory bird agreements. Ten Victorian wetland areas were declared Ramsar sites after nomination in 1982. Other instruments include the [[Register Of The National Estate]], the [[Wetlands Conservation Program]] (1988–92), and the [[Flora And Fauna Guarantee Act]] (1988). The state also manages more than 300 wetland-dedicated reserves.

**Survey and classification.** The manual sets out a six-step strategy: survey, inventory, classification, evaluation, management and monitoring. It focuses on the first three. Victoria's wetland inventory, based on [[Corrick Classification]] work by the Arthur Rylah Institute, covers an estimated 18,000 wetlands over one hectare. The [[Victorian Minimum Data Set]] records identity and location, land status and area, and ecological attributes (system, biophysical region, morphology, origin, water regime, conductivity and pH). Field data are standardised through a [[Wetland Field Survey Form]] and stored in the [[Regional Wetland Database]].

**Landscape assessment.** Chapter 3 proposes a [[Visual Resource Value]] methodology. Wetlands are compared only within the same Landscape Character Type, wetland setting and classification type. A high rating requires at least one significant positive variable in each of landform, vegetation and waterform, and no dominant "less manageable" negative feature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats.** The manual lists fire, grazing, altered hydrology, landscape change, commercial activity, drainage, land filling, irrigation-driven salinisation, pollution, sedimentation, mining, exotic species, acidification, overfishing and lead poisoning of waterfowl from shot as threats to wetlands.

**Loss.** Figures differ between sections:
- Section 1.2 reports a 22% decrease in wetland number and a one-third decrease in area since European settlement.
- Section 4.1.1 gives a 28% loss of area and 22% loss of number.
- Section 3.2.1 states that approximately one third of wetlands has been lost over about 150 years.

In California, over 90% of natural wetlands have been lost or highly degraded (Dugan 1988).

**Causes of change.** Drainage accounts for 55% of wetland change in Victoria, and less permanent watering regimes for 40%. More permanent watering accounts for 5%, and salinity for 1%. Shallow wetlands have suffered the greatest losses because they are easiest to drain.

**Value.** Wetlands provide products (water, fish, birds, timber, forage, energy), ecosystem functions (flood mitigation, erosion control, groundwater recharge, water purification, habitat for plants and animals), social services (recreation, tourism, education, research, cultural and landscape values), and intrinsic values. The manual notes that wetland creation generally costs more than protection.

**Hydrology.** Water is the main driver of wetland function. Chapter 4 distinguishes a hydrology-driven approach (restoring a past or natural hydrological regime) from an ecology-driven approach (managing for known biological communities). Chapter 4 also notes that some wetlands valued for their naturalness, such as River Redgum forests and Lake Pirron Yallock's floating islands, are products of past human alteration.

**Classification attributes.** The proposed ecological classification prioritises biophysical region, geomorphology, hydrology and water chemistry. Conductivity and salinity are described as major determinants of wetland ecology. The manual notes that salinity divides freshwater from saline wetlands and that tolerant species decline as salinity increases.

## Key Findings

- **Classification framework.** Victoria's ecological classification (seven attributes: system, biophysical region, geomorphic unit, morphology, origin, water regime, conductivity and pH) is designed to be hierarchical, flexible and not yet validated. The manual states that little research has tested whether the classes are ecologically meaningful, so testing is needed.
- **Survey standards.** Quantitative attributes such as pH and salinity should be recorded as direct measurements, since fluctuations over seasons and years mean that single readings are unreliable for classification.
- **Hydrology.** A wetland's water budget and water regime are fundamental to managing it. Regime categories range from permanent to episodic. Restoration of natural hydrology is recommended where feasible, and recommended water allocations should be based on ecological needs rather than cost.
- **Grazing.** Chapter 5 emphasises monitoring and baseline data to inform grazing decisions. Grazing regimes should be based on the appropriateness of grazing to each wetland.
- **Fire.** Chapter 6 treats fire as both a threat and a management tool. A case study examines using fire to regulate *Typha* spp. and *Phragmites australis* populations, and the chapter covers fire prevention, pre-suppression and suppression.
- **Restoration.** Chapter 7 addresses degradation processes, the ecology of wetland vegetation establishment, and planning steps including pre-planning investigations.
- **Aquaculture.** Chapter 8 identifies physical change, nutrient inputs, chemical use, effects on indigenous biota, landscape and water impacts, and socio-economic effects. It also considers conflicts with recreation and other commercial uses.
- **Visual management.** Chapter 3 recommends managing wetlands in Zones A, B and C according to sensitivity and visual value. Zone A (highest priority) requires low or inevident landscape change. Zone B permits apparent but not dominant change, and Zone C allows dominant change that harmonises with the landscape.
- **Management guidelines.** Chapter 9 consolidates evaluation criteria, water, grazing, fire and restoration guidance for Victorian wetlands, including criteria for nationally important wetlands.
